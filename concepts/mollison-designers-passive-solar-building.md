# Passive Solar Building Design

Passive solar building design uses the energy of the sun to heat, cool, and
light structures without mechanical systems. [[passive-solar-design]] are universal but their application varies
with climate.

## Fundamental Principles

Passive solar design rests on four interrelated principles:

1. **Collection**: Capturing solar energy through appropriately oriented and
   sized glazing (windows, skylights, sunspaces).
2. **Storage**: Absorbing and retaining [[phase-change-materials-thermal-energy-storage]] in high-density
   materials (thermal mass) for release when needed.
3. **Distribution**: Moving stored heat through the building by natural
   convection, conduction, and radiation.
4. **Control**: Managing heat gain and loss through shading devices,
   insulation, ventilation, and thermal shutters.

All four principles must be addressed for effective passive solar performance.
A building with large south-facing windows but inadequate thermal mass will
overheat during the day and freeze at night. Conversely, massive walls without
solar access provide no heating benefit.

## Orientation and Solar Access

In the northern hemisphere, the primary solar-collecting facade should face
within 15 degrees of true south. In the [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]], it should face
true north. This orientation maximizes winter solar gain when the sun is low
on the horizon while allowing easy shading in summer when the sun is high.

The building's long axis should run east-west, with the majority of glazing
on the equator-facing side. East and west walls should have minimal glazing
because low-angle morning and afternoon sun is difficult to control and
contributes disproportionately to summer overheating. North-facing walls (in
the northern hemisphere) should be well-insulated with small windows for
daylight and ventilation.

Solar access must be protected from shading by buildings, trees, and terrain.
Mollison advises designing the Mollison Designers [[mollison-course-zone-planning-and-design]] layout
so that deciduous trees are positioned to shade buildings in summer but allow
winter sun penetration after leaf fall. Evergreen windbreaks should be placed
to the north and west of buildings, never to the south.

## Earth-Sheltered Construction

Earth-sheltered or underground buildings use the earth itself as insulation
and thermal mass. Soil maintains a remarkably stable temperature at depth:
approximately equal to the annual average air temperature. At a depth of 2
to 3 metres, soil temperatures fluctuate by less than 2 degrees Celsius
throughout the year. Mollison describes several earth-sheltered approaches:

- **Bermed construction**: The building is set into a slope or partially
  covered with earth on one or more sides. The roof remains exposed for
  daylight and ventilation.
- **Underground construction**: Entirely below grade with a courtyard or
  atrium providing light and air. Excellent insulation but requires careful
  waterproofing and ventilation.
- **Earth-integrated design**: Walls built from rammed earth, earthbags, or
  cob, combining structural and thermal functions in a single material.

Waterproofing is the critical challenge. French drains, gravel drainage
layers, and waterproof membranes must be meticulously installed to prevent
moisture infiltration.
