## The Comparison Table

Hemenway presents a detailed comparison showing how ecosystems
change as they mature across several key attributes:

### Organic Matter
- **Immature:** Low. Most biomass is removed annually through
  harvest, and bare soil periods prevent buildup.
- **Mature:** High. Continuous leaf drop, root turnover, and
  woody accumulation build deep organic reserves.

### Source of Mineral Nutrients
- **Immature:** Nonliving sources dominate. Rock particles,
  rainfall, and imported fertilizers provide most nutrients.
- **Mature:** Biological sources dominate. Plants, animals,
  humus, and soil organisms cycle nutrients internally.

### Mineral Cycles
- **Immature:** Open cycles with many imports and exports.
  Fertility leaves the system with every harvest and must be
  replaced from outside.
- **Mature:** Closed cycles with extensive recycling.
  [[soil-life]] that can survive the harsh conditions.

Plant diversity is tightly controlled. True diversity is
unwelcome, defined as weeds, pests, and raiding birds. Nature's
spontaneity means trouble rather than improvement.

## The Mature Woodland Model

In contrast, the mature woodland holds soil in place year-round.
A multilayered canopy moderates temperature extremes. Rain
filters gently through leaves to the forest floor. Deep root
systems of trees and shrubs tap moisture and nutrients far
below the surface. [[food-forest-design]] seeks to emulate
in a garden context.

## The Paradigm Shift

Understanding immature versus mature ecosystems reframes the
entire project of gardening. Rather than struggling to maintain
an inherently unstable, work-intensive system, the ecological
gardener aligns with nature's tendency toward maturity. The
result is a landscape that is more productive, more beautiful,
more resilient, and far less work.

Our addiction to impeccable lawns and soldierly rows of
vegetables and flowers is counter to the tendency of nature and
guarantees constant work. But we do not need to wield trowel and
herbicide with resentment in an eternal war against the
exuberant appetite of chicory and wild lettuce for fresh-bared
soil. Instead we can create conditions that encourage the plants
we want, and let nature do the work.
