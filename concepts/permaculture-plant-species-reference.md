# Permaculture Plant Species Reference

[[bill-mollison]]'s *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* concludes with an extensive catalog of plant species used across permaculture systems worldwide. This reference list, compiled from the manual's appendix, represents a global toolkit of multipurpose plants selected for their roles in food production, soil building, fiber, medicine, microclimate modification, and ecological function. The species listed span virtually every climate zone and functional category relevant to [[permaculture-design]].

## Purpose of the Species Catalog

The plant catalog in the Designers' Manual serves several critical functions. First, it provides designers with a working vocabulary of species suited to different climatic and edaphic conditions. Second, it encodes Mollison's decades of observation about which plants perform multiple functions simultaneously — a core permaculture principle. Third, it preserves knowledge of useful plants that may be unfamiliar to Western agriculture but are well-established in indigenous and traditional land management systems.

Unlike conventional agricultural seed catalogs organized by single-yield crops, Mollison's listing emphasizes polycultural utility. A species may appear because it fixes nitrogen, provides fodder, yields edible fruit, acts as a windbreak, accumulates minerals from deep soil layers, or serves as a companion plant that deters pests for neighboring crops.

## Categories of Useful Species

The catalog can be broadly organized into several functional groups, though many species overlap categories:

### Food and Nut Crops
Staple food plants form the caloric backbone of permaculture systems. **[[zea-mays]]** (corn/maize) and **Triticum spp.** (wheat) represent the major grain crops, while **[[theobroma-cacao]]** (cocoa) provides both food and economic value in tropical systems. **Vaccinium spp.** (blueberry, huckleberry) and **V. macrocarpon** (cranberry) are important temperate fruit crops. **Vitis vinifera** (grape) serves as both a food and beverage crop across Mediterranean and temperate climates.

Leguminous food crops are especially valued for their dual role in nitrogen fixation and protein production: **Vicia faba** (fava/broad bean), **Vigna radiata** (mung bean), **V. sinensis** (cowpea), and **Trigonella foenum-graecum** (fenugreek). **Trifolium spp.** (clover) serves as both fodder and a green manure crop, with species like **T. repens** (white clover) and **T. semipilosum** (Kenya white clover) widely used in temperate pastures.

### Timber and Forestry Trees
Large-scale timber species in the catalog include **Tectona grandis** (teak), one of the world's most valued hardwoods, and **Tipuana tipu** (rosewood/tipuana), a fast-growing leguminous tree useful for shade, timber, and nitrogen fixation. **Xanthorrhoea australis** (blackboy/grasstree) is an Australian native with multiple traditional uses.

### Forage and Pasture Plants
Pasture species are critical for integrated animal systems. **Themeda australis** (kangaroo grass) is a native Australian perennial grass important for sustainable grazing. **Trifolium** species serve as nitrogen-fixing pasture companions, while **Vicia spp.** (vetch/tares) provide excellent cool-season forage and green manure.

### Medicinal and Culinary Herbs
The herb category includes **Thymus vulgaris** (thyme), valued both as a culinary herb and for its antimicrobial properties. **Zingiber officinale** (ginger) is a tropical rhizome crop with extensive medicinal and culinary applications. **Taraxacum officinale** (dandelion), often dismissed as a weed, is recognized in permaculture for its deep taproot (which mines minerals from subsoil), edible leaves and flowers, and value as early spring bee forage.

### Fiber and Industrial Plants
**Tilia spp.** (basswood/linden) produces both edible flowers and timber, with bark traditionally used for fiber. **Vetiveria zizanioides** (vetiver grass) is one of the most important plants in tropical permaculture for erosion control — its dense, deep root system holds soil on steep slopes and can be used to stabilize terraces, drainage lines, and embankments.

### Aquatic and Wetland Plants
**Typha spp.** (reedmace/cattail) and **Trapa spp. natans** (water chestnut) are key aquatic species. Cattail is remarkably versatile: all parts are edible, it filters water, provides wildlife habitat, and can be used for thatching and fiber. Water chestnut produces edible nuts and helps maintain water quality in ponds and marshes.

### Nitrogen-Fixing and Soil-Building Species
**Tephrosia candida** (tephrosia) is a tropical leguminous shrub widely used as a green manure and fallow crop. **Ulex europaeus** (gorse/whin), despite being invasive in some contexts, fixes nitrogen vigorously and can be managed as a productive component of temperate systems. **Tetragonia implexicona** (bower vine) serves structural and ecological functions in garden design.

### Ornamental and Functional Landscape Plants
**Tradescantia albiflora** (wandering jew), **Verbena spp.**, **Viola odorata** (violet), **Zantedeschia spp.** (arum lily), and **Yucca spp.** serve aesthetic roles while often providing additional ecological functions such as ground cover, insect habitat, or edge definition.

### Edge and Pioneer Species
**Ziziphus jujuba** (jujube) is a hardy fruit tree that thrives in marginal, arid conditions and serves as an excellent pioneer species. **Zostera spp.** (seagrass) represents the marine dimension of permaculture design, stabilizing coastal sediments and providing nursery habitat for fisheries.

## The Philosophy Behind the Selection

Mollison's species selections reflect several [[permaculture-design-principles]]:

1. **Multiple functions**: Every plant should serve at least three functions. A teak tree provides timber, shade, and leaf mulch. A clover fixes nitrogen, provides forage, and suppresses weeds.

2. **Elemental analysis**: Designers are expected to know each plant's intrinsic characteristics — its needs (sun, water, soil pH), its products (food, fiber, medicine), and its behaviors (growth rate, root depth, allelopathic effects). The catalog is a starting point, not a final list.

3. **Bioregional appropriateness**: The global scope of the list reflects Mollison's insistence that designers work with species adapted to their specific climate and soil conditions rather than importing unsuitable commercial varieties.

4. **Successional thinking**: Pioneer species like gorse and tephrosia prepare the ground for climax communities. The catalog includes species for every stage of ecological succession.

5. **Genetic diversity**: The listing of multiple species within genera (e.g., several Trifolium species, multiple Vaccinium species) encourages designers to maintain genetic diversity rather than monocultures of single cultivars.

## Using This Reference in Design

When designing a permaculture system, the species catalog should be consulted alongside climate data, topographic maps, and soil surveys. The designer's task is to select combinations of species whose needs are met by the site's conditions and whose products meet the inhabitants' needs. The goal is to create functional interconnections — where the output of one element becomes the input of another, forming self-maintaining guilds and polycultures that reduce or eliminate the need for external inputs.

The catalog also serves as a reminder that the plant kingdom offers far more options than conventional agriculture recognizes. Many of the species listed — from vetiver grass to cattail to dandelion — are undervalued or actively suppressed in mainstream land management, yet they perform critical ecological functions that no amount of synthetic input can replicate.
