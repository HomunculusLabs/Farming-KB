# Mycophiles and Mushroom Culture

The world of mushroom enthusiasts (mycophiles) occupies a unique position in natural history. Amateur mycologists have contributed enormously to fungal taxonomy, ecology, and conservation, often surpassing professional mycologists in field identification skills. The culture of mushroom hunting spans scientific curiosity, culinary passion, artistic inspiration, and spiritual practice.

## The Amateur Tradition

Amateur mycologists are unique among natural history enthusiasts in their direct contributions to science. While amateur astronomers and birders primarily observe and record, amateur mycologists routinely discover new species, publish taxonomic descriptions, and maintain critical reference collections. Historical figures like Curtis Gates Lloyd built personal herbaria that became essential institutional resources -- the Lloyd Library in Cincinnati remains one of the world's foremost mycological collections.

As Money observes, it is rare in the sciences for amateurs to play such an important role in the continuing vibrancy of a field, and "perhaps only astronomers can boast of such a partnership between academia and the enthusiast." Amateur mycologists are responsible for a great deal of our knowledge of mushroom distribution and diversity, and their ability to find and identify mushroom species in the field often "far exceeds the capacity of someone like myself with years of professional experience in mycology."

## Mycological Societies and Forays

Local and national mycological societies organize regular forays where members collect, identify, and share knowledge. The North American Mycological Association (NAMA) coordinates regional forays and maintains standards for identification. These events combine scientific rigor with social bonding -- the traditional mushroom banquet after a foray is a cultural institution. Money recounts arriving at a foray "in a suit and tie," poorly dressed for the fieldwork but reflecting the formal culture that once characterized these gatherings.

## Cultural Attitudes Toward Fungi

Western culture has an ambivalent relationship with mushrooms. Folk traditions across Europe feature both reverence and fear:
- **Fairy rings**: Circles of mushrooms associated with supernatural dancing
- **Kicking mushrooms**: A common impulse, especially toward *Amanita* species, reflecting an instinctive wariness
- **Victorian prudery**: Etty Darwin (Charles Darwin's daughter) famously destroyed stinkhorn specimens on sight, offended by their phallic appearance
- **Fairy tales**: Mushrooms appear as houses, furniture, and magical objects in folklore worldwide

Mushrooms have also inspired a rich vocabulary of poisons, fairy tales, and scientific wonders. The cultural associations are remarkably diverse -- from the deadly serious business of [[fungal-poisons-and-toxicology]] to the whimsical names given to common species.

## The Commercial Dimension

Global wild mushroom trade is estimated at several billion dollars annually:
- **Matsutake** (*Tricholoma matsutake*): The most expensive wild mushroom, with top-grade Japanese specimens selling for over $1,000/kg
- **Chanterelles**: Major commercial harvest in the Pacific Northwest, Europe, and Asia
- **Porcini**: Extensively harvested and exported from Eastern Europe and China
- **Truffles**: The epitome of fungal luxury; European truffles command extraordinary prices

Mushroom hunting has become a serious commercial enterprise in some regions. Over-harvesting is a growing concern, particularly for slow-growing mycorrhizal species. Commercial foraging can damage forest floors through soil compaction and disruption of mycelial networks. Tuscan truffles are a celebrated example of decline, and stories of dwindling numbers of certain mushroom species in forests of the Pacific Northwest cannot be ignored.

## Ecological Impact of Collecting

While collectors argue that picking fruiting bodies does no harm -- a single mushroom can disperse hundreds of millions of spores in a single day -- the ecological picture is more complex. The elimination of spore production by a significant proportion of fruiting bodies limits outcrossing through sex and diminishes genetic diversity. Over-picked mycelia may acquire "the mycological counterparts of bad teeth, cross eyes, and an unnatural interest in banjo playing" through inbreeding depression. Additionally, the elimination of mushrooms as a food source for various animals and as a breeding ground for specific insects may have serious consequences on forest ecosystem viability.

Habitat destruction and environmental pollution are probably far more catastrophic to fungi than collectors, but mushroom hunting cannot be regarded as an entirely benign activity.

## Foraging Ethics

- Take only what you need, leaving specimens for spore dispersal
- Avoid raking or disturbing forest floor duff
- Do not collect rare or threatened species
- Respect private property and collecting regulations
- Share knowledge and specimens with fellow foragers
- Photograph before collecting for documentation

## The Mycophile Mindset

What draws people to mushrooms? The appeal combines several factors:
- The treasure-hunt excitement of finding hidden organisms
- The intellectual challenge of identification in a poorly known kingdom
- The seasonal rhythm that connects foragers to natural cycles
- The culinary reward of wild flavors unavailable commercially
- The philosophical fascination with an organism that is neither plant nor animal
- The ecological awareness that comes from observing decomposition and nutrient cycling firsthand

People who would never venture into the woods for any other reason will get up before dawn to collect mushrooms in the pouring rain. This peculiar dedication -- captured in the German term *Schwaermer* for those obsessed with mushrooms -- reflects the unique grip that fungi exert on the human imagination.

## Digital Age and Citizen Science

The internet has transformed mycophile culture. Online forums, social media groups, and citizen science platforms have connected mushroom enthusiasts globally, enabling rapid identification assistance and knowledge sharing. Platforms like iNaturalist have generated millions of fungal observations, providing valuable distribution data for researchers. Mushroom cultivation has also gone mainstream, with home grow kits and online tutorials making species like oyster mushrooms ([[cotter-oyster-mushroom-cultivation]]) accessible to urban dwellers. The [[mushroom-cultivation-fundamentals]] has further expanded interest in psilocybin mushrooms, though this has also raised concerns about over-harvesting of wild populations and the ethics of foraging for psychoactive species. The tension between accessibility and conservation remains a central challenge for the mycophile community.

# History of Mycology as a Science

The scientific study of fungi has been shaped by a cast of remarkable personalities -- obsessive bachelor professors, millionaire amateurs, warring taxonomists, and patient experimentalists. As Nicholas Money recounts in "Mr. Bloomfield's Orchard," mycology's history is one of slow recognition that fungi constitute a kingdom of life unto themselves, fundamentally different from both plants and animals.

## Early Classification: Chaos fungorum

Carolus Linnaeus, who invented the [[mushroom-etymology-and-nomenclature]] for naming organisms in the eighteenth century and described more than 12,000 species himself, was baffled by the variety and plasticity of fungi. He banished a diverse selection to a species he named Chaos fungorum -- a frank admission that fungal taxonomy defied the orderly system he had imposed on the rest of nature.

As late as 1804, the idea that fungi were offspring of comets was not universally ridiculed. The living nature of fungi was disputed by some natural historians throughout the eighteenth century. Fungi seemed to appear spontaneously from decaying matter, and their mode of reproduction remained mysterious until the nineteenth century.

## The Tulasne Brothers

A pivotal advance came from Louis-Rene and Charles Tulasne, two bachelor brothers who, aided by an inheritance, dedicated themselves to mycological study in mid-nineteenth century France. Louis-Rene had studied law before pursuing research at the Jardin de Plantes in Paris. Their masterwork, Selecta Fungorum Carpologia (1861-1865), introduced the revolutionary concept that individual fungi could produce different types of spores in different phases of their life cycle -- a phenomenon termed pleomorphy.

This principle -- that the same organism might look completely different in its sexual and asexual phases -- was initially opposed, then universally accepted, then misapplied "to the point of lunacy" by Ernst Hallier, who claimed Saccharomyces yeast could transform into an insect pathogen, bread mold, or water mold depending on its environment.

Charles Tulasne's illustrations remain unsurpassed. "It is as if he shrank himself to the size of a spore and drew the fungal forest that towered around him." His three-dimensional depictions conveyed information about fungal structure that no photograph could match.

## Anton de Bary

Anton de Bary, whose landmark 1887 book discussed the Tulasne brothers' work, provided the comparative morphological framework that established mycology as a rigorous discipline. His diagnosis of Hallier's "pleomorphic extravagances" helped set the field on a scientific course. De Bary also made fundamental contributions to understanding plant disease, establishing that fungi were the causal agents of many crop diseases -- a conclusion that was far from obvious at the time.

## A.H.R. Buller: The Greatest Experimental Mycologist

Arthur Henry Reginald Buller (1874-1944) arrived in Winnipeg in 1904 and became, as Money writes, "the greatest experimental mycologist in history." His seven-volume Researches on Fungi (1909-1950) answered fundamental questions about spore production and dispersal.

Buller estimated spore output, measured spore velocity using ingenious apparatus, studied the surface tension mechanism of spore discharge (observing "Buller's drop" at the base of each basidiospore), investigated fungal luminescence, and explored the physics of spore movement. He rose at 4 AM, walked to his lab before sunrise with his head muffled in horse blinders to preserve dark adaptation, and published limericks in Punch.

His genius was matched by his eccentricity: he lived forty years in a two-room suite at the McLaren Hotel, wore a moth-eaten raccoon coat on forays, crossed the Atlantic sixty-five times, and never married. "His genius set him apart from all other mycologists, and most other scientists." On mushroom forays, Buller donned impeccable attire -- a habit that contrasted sharply with the fieldwork conditions but reflected his Edwardian sense of propriety.

## Curtis Gates Lloyd: Millionaire Mycologist

Curtis Lloyd (1859-1926) of Cincinnati was a pharmacist-turned-self-taught mycologist who abandoned the family business for fungi. He published seven volumes of Mycological Notes (1898-1925), served as a bully pulpit against academic pomposity, and under the pseudonym N.J. McGinty described fictional species to satirize colleagues he considered incompetent.

His feud with Cornell mycologist George Atkinson -- over whether a decomposing specimen of Simblum periphragmoides constituted a new genus, Dictybole -- became legendary. Lloyd established the Lloyd Library in Cincinnati, which remains a major repository of botanical and mycological works. Lloyd's approach was that of the irreverent outsider who used humor and sarcasm as weapons against what he saw as the pretensions of academic mycology.

## Terence Ingold

C.T. Ingold (1905-2010) discovered the remarkable spore forms of aquatic fungi (aero-aquatic fungi with air-bubble appendages, helical spore shapes) while peering into a babbling brook. His books introduced generations of biologists to fungi. He continued publishing research into his nineties, naming the yeast Bensingtonia ciliata in honour of his adopted home.

## The Role of Women in Early Mycology

The history of mycology, like most scientific disciplines, has been dominated by men, but several women made significant contributions despite facing institutional barriers. [[fungal-inventory-methods]]'s contemporary, the English mycologist Anne Hutchinson (not to be confused with the colonial religious figure), contributed to fungal taxonomy in the late 19th century. In America, mycological societies often included prominent women members who led forays and contributed to species identification. The British Mycological Society, founded in 1896, counted women among its founding members. The contributions of these women were often minimized or attributed to male colleagues, a pattern that modern historians of science are working to correct.

## Fungi and Plant Pathology

One of the most practically important branches of mycology emerged from the recognition that fungi cause plant diseases. Anton de Bary's demonstration that fungi were the causal agents of crop diseases was a landmark not only for mycology but for agriculture. The Irish Potato Famine (1845-1852), caused by the oomycete Phytophthora infestans, dramatically demonstrated the devastating potential of fungal pathogens and spurred investment in plant pathology. The development of fungicides, resistant crop varieties, and integrated pest management all grew from the mycological understanding of plant-fungal interactions. This applied branch of mycology saved countless lives and remains critical for global food security.
