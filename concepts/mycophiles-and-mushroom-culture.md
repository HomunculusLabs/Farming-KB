# Mycophiles and Mushroom Culture The world of mushroom enthusiasts (mycophiles) occupies a unique position in natural history. Amateur mycologists have contributed enormously to fungal taxonomy, ecology, and conservation, often surpassing professional mycologists in field identification skills. The culture of mushroom hunting spans scientific curiosity, culinary passion, artistic inspiration, and spiritual practice. ## The Amateur Tradition Amateur mycologists are unique among natural history enthusiasts in their direct contributions to science. While amateur astronomers and birders primarily observe and record, amateur mycologists routinely discover new species, publish taxonomic descriptions, and maintain critical reference collections. Historical figures like Curtis Gates Lloyd built personal herbaria that became essential institutional resources -- the Lloyd Library in Cincinnati remains one of the world's foremost mycological collections. As Money observes, it is rare in the sciences for amateurs to play such an important role in the continuing vibrancy of a field, and "perhaps only astronomers can boast of such a partnership between academia and the enthusiast." Amateur mycologists are responsible for a great deal of our knowledge of mushroom distribution and diversity, and their ability to find and identify mushroom species in the field often "far exceeds the capacity of someone like myself with years of professional experience in mycology." ## Mycological Societies and Forays Local and national mycological societies organize regular forays where members collect, identify, and share knowledge. The North American Mycological Association (NAMA) coordinates regional forays and maintains standards for identification. These events combine scientific rigor with social bonding -- the traditional mushroom banquet after a foray is a cultural institution. Money recounts arriving at a foray "in a suit and tie," poorly dressed for the fieldwork but reflecting the formal culture that once characterized these gatherings. ## Cultural Attitudes Toward Fungi Western culture has an ambivalent relationship with mushrooms. Folk traditions across Europe feature both reverence and fear: - **Fairy rings**: Circles of mushrooms associated with supernatural dancing - **Kicking mushrooms**: A common impulse, especially toward *Amanita* species, reflecting an instinctive wariness - **Victorian prudery**: Etty Darwin (Charles Darwin's daughter) famously destroyed stinkhorn specimens on sight, offended by their phallic appearance - **Fairy tales**: Mushrooms appear as houses, furniture, and magical objects in folklore worldwide Mushrooms have also inspired a rich vocabulary of poisons, fairy tales, and scientific wonders. ## Early Classification: Chaos fungorum

Carolus Linnaeus, who invented the fungal-inventory-methods's contemporary, the English mycologist Anne Hutchinson (not to be confused with the colonial religious figure), contributed to fungal taxonomy in the late 19th century. In America, mycological societies often included prominent women members who led forays and contributed to species identification. The British Mycological Society, founded in 1896, counted women among its founding members. The contributions of these women were often minimized or attributed to male colleagues, a pattern that modern historians of science are working to correct.

## Fungi and Plant Pathology

One of the most practically important branches of mycology emerged from the recognition that fungi cause plant diseases. Anton de Bary's demonstration that fungi were the causal agents of crop diseases was a landmark not only for mycology but for agriculture.
