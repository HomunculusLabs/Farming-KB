# Key to Edibility of Fungi

Julius A. Palmer developed an original classification system for determining the
edibility of large, fleshy fungi — one of the first such keys created specifically
for [[gartz-gymnopilus-purpuratus-south-american-mushroom-in-europe]] hunters. Published in his 1894 work *About Mushrooms*, this
key organized fungi by their [[spore]]-bearing surface structure and provided practical
field guidance for distinguishing edible species from dangerous ones.

## The Four Grand Divisions

Palmer's system begins with the most fundamental morphological distinction: the
nature of the surface bearing the reproductive spores. He identified four primary
categories that any forager could recognize by simply turning a mushroom upside
down and examining its underside:

### 1. Gill-Bearing Fungi (Agaricini)

The largest family of mushrooms, characterized by thin plates set on edge radiating
from a central stem like spokes of a wheel. The gills (lamellae) are the primary
identification feature. Palmer subdivided this group into multiple series based on
gill attachment, flesh color changes, and taste:

- **Free gills** (not attached to the stem): Includes both edible and dangerous
  species. The meadow mushroom and horse mushroom fall here, but so do the deadly
  Amanitas.
- **Attached gills** (running down the stem): Generally safer for beginners.
- **Brittle gills** that break into pieces: Characteristic of the Russulae and
  Lactarii families.

Palmer's Series 1 for [[pleurotoid-mushroom-morphology-gilled-fungi]] distinguished edibility based on whether gills
were free or attached, whether the flesh changed color when cut, and taste
reactions — acrid or burning taste indicated non-edibility, while mild or nutty
flavor suggested edibility.

### 2. Pore-Bearing Fungi (Boleti and Polypori)

Instead of gills, these have a spongy surface of tubes ending in pores. Palmer
distinguished between Boleti (ground-growing with distinct stems and easily
separable tubes) and Polypori (mostly wood-growing with abortive stems):

- **Edible indicators**: Brown, yellowish, or greenish pores; russet or brown cap;
  white flesh that remains white when cut.
- **Warning signs**: Brilliant red or yellow coloration; blue discoloration when
  bruised; red pore mouths — these indicated poisonous varieties.

This distinction between Boleti and Polypori was crucial because, while many
Boleti are excellent edibles, the Polypori (bracket fungi) are generally tough
and woody, with few culinary applications.

### 3. Teeth-Bearing Fungi (Hydnei)

These fungi bear small spines or teeth instead of gills or pores. Palmer considered
them among the safest for amateur experimentation, noting that Smith confirmed all
species of any size enjoyed a good reputation. The spreading hydnum (*Hydnum
repandum*) was his primary example: usually yellow, sometimes reddish, always
firm-fleshed with a deformed or eccentric stem.

### 4. Puffballs (Lycoperdaceae)

Spores enclosed within a globular body rather than borne externally. Palmer
identified these as the safest starting point for beginners:

- **Giant puffball** (*[[lycoperdon-giganteum]]*): Smooth-skinned, pure white interior
  when young. Any yellow stain from a knife indicated it was too old.
- **Smaller varieties**: All white puffballs considered safe if cooked promptly.
- **Key rule**: No poisonous fungus resembles a true puffball, making them ideal
  for first-time foragers.

Puffballs change rapidly after gathering and should only be eaten in their freshest
state. Palmer believed all white puffballs could be safely used if cooked at once.

## Color-Change Reactions

A critical diagnostic feature throughout Palmer's key was color change upon bruising
or cutting. Different reactions indicated different chemical properties:

- **[[mckenna-glaukos-myth-blue-staining-mushroom-symbolism]]**: Common in many Boleti. Some blue-staining species are edible,
  but Palmer generally advised caution with blue-color-changing fungi, as this
  reaction indicates the presence of oxidizing compounds.
- **Red staining**: Found in some Russulae; could indicate either edible or
  inedible species depending on accompanying features.
- **No color change**: Generally a positive sign for edibility, especially when
  combined with mild taste and white flesh.

## Taste as a Diagnostic Tool

Palmer employed taste testing as a supplementary identification method, though
with important caveats:

- **Acrid or burning sensation**: Indicated non-edibility in Russulae and
  Lactarii, though Palmer noted this test was unreliable for the deadly Amanitas,
  which could be mild-tasting yet lethal.
- **Mild, nutty, or chestnut-like flavor**: Suggested edibility among the
  Russulae.
- **Grass-like or tree-like taste**: Found in some Agarics that were edible but
  required broiling rather than stewing.

The taste test was most reliable within specific families and least reliable for
the gilled fungi overall, where the deadly Amanitas could taste pleasant.

## Limitations and Context

Palmer's key was pioneering but had acknowledged limitations:

- It covered only "large and fleshy" fungi, omitting smaller species, truffles,
  and microscopic fungi.
- Classification was based on macroscopic features visible to the naked eye,
  deliberately avoiding microscopic analysis for accessibility.
- The key was designed for American species, though based heavily on European
  classification frameworks from Berkeley, Fries, and Cooke.
- Notable omissions included truffles, which Palmer discussed separately as a
  subterranean genus of uncertain American distribution.

## Historical Bibliography of Mycological Works

Palmer included an extensive bibliography reflecting the state of mycological
knowledge available in the late 19th century:

- **British authorities**: Berkeley's *Outlines of British Fungology* (1860),
  Cooke's *Handbook of British Fungi* (1871) and *Illustrations* (1881),
  Stevenson's *British Fungi* (1896), Worthington G. Smith's *Toadstools*
  (1876).
- **French works**: Tulasne's *Fungi Hypogaei* (1851), Paulet's *Traité des
  Champignons*, Montagne's publications, and the *Revue des Deux Mondes* truffle
  review (1875).
- **American contributions**: Peck's annual Reports of the State Botanist of
  New York (Albany), Harkness and Moore's *Pacific Coast Fungi* (1880), and
  Palmer's own earlier works including his Prang & Co. plates (1885).
- **Government publications**: Pamphlets issued by the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] Department
  of Agriculture, which Palmer criticized as limited and derivative of Smith's
  incomplete work.

Palmer noted he could not evaluate German-language works, which represented a
significant gap given the prominence of German mycology (especially the Friesian
classification system that underpinned most [[fungal-taxonomy]] of the era).
