## Problems with Climatic Classification

1. **Instrument limitations**: Accurate measurement instruments are expensive and often specific to narrow ranges of the total spectrum
2. **Incomplete data**: Averages often refer only to one part of the total spectrum; few long-term records exist for fog precipitation, dew, long-wave radiation, ultraviolet incidence, or atmospheric gas composition
3. **Dynamic interaction**: Rain, sun, and wind interact continuously, so averages mean little to organisms subject to normally changeable conditions covering wide ranges of interactive measures

## Engines of the Atmosphere: Global Weather Systems

### Polar Outflow
Dense cold air flows continually off the polar ice caps. This high-pressure, down-draught air spirals out of polar regions as persistent **easterlies** affecting high latitudes (60–80°) near the ice caps.

### Hadley Cell Dynamics
Long spokes of cold polar air curve outward to Latitude 30°. As the spiral is caught up in (and generated by) Earth's rotation, these cold cells drive a series of contra-rotating low-pressure cells:
- Turning **clockwise** in the southern hemisphere
- Turning **anti-clockwise** in the northern hemisphere

### Mid-Latitude High-Pressure Cells
These low-pressure cells mesh with rotating spirals of high-pressure air that have risen at the equator and are falling at Latitudes 15–40°:
- Turning **anti-clockwise** in the southern hemisphere
- Turning **clockwise** in the northern hemisphere

This three-cell model (polar, Ferrel, and Hadley cells) drives the prevailing winds, ocean currents, and precipitation patterns that define regional climates.

## The Imperative of Local Knowledge

There is no substitute in any design for:
- **Local observation** and long-term weather records
- **Community anecdotes** about weather patterns and extremes
- **Detailed maps** of local topographic and climatic factors
- **Lists of locally successful** plant and animal species
- **Analysis of local soils** and their behavior under different weather conditions

Designers working across wide climatic ranges should study regional long-term human and agricultural adaptations to climate. Above all, avoid introducing temperate (European) techniques and species to tropical and arid lands on any large scale. Native agricultural and pastoral management practices are often finely tuned to survival, sometimes highly productive, and above all independent of outside aid.
