## Mycorrhizal Fungal Communities and Ecosystem Function

## Overview

Mycorrhizal fungal communities are not merely collections of individual plant-fungal partnerships — they form complex networks that mediate nutrient cycling, carbon flow, and plant community dynamics at the ecosystem scale. This concept page synthesizes research on mycorrhizal community composition, spatial and temporal dynamics, interactions with soil organisms, and their consequences for ecosystem-level processes.

## Diversity and Composition of Mycorrhizal Communities

### Host Plant Effects on Fungal Communities

The composition of mycorrhizal fungal communities is strongly influenced by host plant identity:

- **Eom et al. (2000)**: Demonstrated host plant species effects on AM fungal communities in tallgrass prairie, finding that different grass species supported distinct AM fungal assemblages.
- **Johnson (1998)**: Showed differential responses of *Salsola kali* and *[[panicum-virgatum]]* to [[mycorrhizal-fungi]], phosphorus, and soil organic matter, with implications for revegetation of disturbed sites.
- **Gehring and Whitham (1994)**: Compared ECM communities on pinyon pines (*[[pinus-edulis]]*) across extremes of soil type and herbivory, revealing that environmental gradients can override host effects on fungal community composition.

### Functional Diversity Within ECM Fungi

- **Cairney (1999)**: Reviewed intraspecific physiological variation in ECM fungi, arguing that understanding this variation is essential for appreciating functional diversity. Isolates of the same species can differ dramatically in their nutrient use efficiency, enzyme production, and stress tolerance.
- **Anderson et al. (2001)**: Documented variation in nitrogen source utilization among *Pisolithus* isolates maintained in axenic culture, showing that functional diversity exists even within a single genus.

### Negative Associations Among Mycorrhizal Types

- **Lodge and Wentworth (1990)**: Found negative associations between VA-mycorrhizal fungi and some ECM fungi inhabiting the same root system, suggesting competitive exclusion between [[mycorrhizal-types]]. This has implications for plant community dynamics, as shifts in the relative abundance of AM vs. ECM plants could alter [[mineralogical-controls-fungal-community-structure]].

## Spatial and Temporal Dynamics

### Fruiting Body Patterns

- **Dighton et al. (1986)**: Documented changes in basidiomycete fruit body occurrence during forest stand development, with specific reference to mycorrhizal species. Early-stage fungi dominate young stands, while late-stage fungi characteristic of mature forests appear later.
- **Matsuda and Hijii (1998)**: Studied spatiotemporal distribution of ECM fungal fruit bodies in an *Abies firma* forest, revealing complex patterns of above-ground sporocarp production that reflect below-ground mycelial dynamics.

### Seasonal Variation

- **Griffiths et al. (1990)**: Measured seasonal variation in nitrogen chemistry and nitrogen cycle transformation rates in ECM mat and non-mat soils of Douglas-fir forests, showing that fungal mats create hotspots of nutrient cycling activity.
- **Baar et al. (1994)**: Found both stimulatory and inhibitory effects of needle litter and grass extracts on the growth of ECM fungi, suggesting that seasonal litter inputs regulate fungal community activity.

### Succession Following Disturbance

- **Baar et al. (1999)**: Investigated ECM colonization of *Pinus muricata* from resistant propagules after a stand-replacing wildfire, finding that some ECM fungi survive fire as resistant propagules and rapidly recolonize seedlings.
- **Jonsson et al. (1999b)**: Described ECM fungal communities in late-successional Swedish boreal forests and their composition following wildfire, documenting both fire-sensitive and fire-adapted species.
- **Mahmood et al. (1999)**: Examined effects of repeated harvesting on the ECM community in a Swedish spruce forest, finding that intensive harvesting reduces ECM diversity and shifts community composition toward disturbance-adapted species.

## Interactions with Soil Fauna and Microbes

### Soil Animals and ECM Mats

- **Cromack et al. (1988)**: Studied interactions between soil animals and ECM fungal mats, finding that mats create distinct microhabitats that concentrate both fungal biomass and soil animal activity. The calcium oxalate accumulated in mats (Cromack et al., 1979) influences soil chemistry and weathering rates.

### Soil Aggregation and Mycorrhizae

- **Hamel et al. (1997)**: Found that indigenous populations of AM fungi and soil aggregate stability are major determinants of plant response to inoculation, highlighting the role of mycorrhizae in soil physical structure maintenance.

### Bacterial Communities in the Mycorrhizosphere

- **Mogge et al. (2000)**: Used fluorescence in situ hybridization and confocal laser scanning microscopy to determine bacterial community structure and colonization patterns in *Fagus sylvatica* ECM mycorrhizospheres, revealing spatially organized bacterial communities within the fungal mantle.

### Nitrogen Competition

- **Kaye and Hart (1997)**: Reviewed competition for nitrogen between plants and soil microorganisms, highlighting the central role of mycorrhizal fungi in mediating this competition by accessing N sources unavailable to plant roots alone.
- **Norton and Firestone (1996)**: Studied N dynamics in the rhizosphere of *Pinus ponderosa* seedlings, finding that mycorrhizal colonization alters rhizosphere N cycling patterns.

## Ecosystem-Level Consequences

### Carbon Transfer Between Plants

- **Finlay and Read (1986a,b,c)**: Demonstrated ¹⁴C translocation between ECM plants interconnected by a common mycelium, and showed that ³²P is also distributed among connected plants. This bidirectional resource sharing suggests that mycorrhizal networks can buffer resource heterogeneity.
- **Simard et al. (1997a,b)**: Provided definitive field evidence for carbon transfer between *Betula papyrifera* (paper birch) and *Pseudotsuga menziesii* (Douglas fir) through shared ECM networks, with net carbon flow from the shade-intolerant birch to the shade-tolerant fir.
- **Heap and Newman (1980a,b)**: Demonstrated links between roots by hyphae of VA mycorrhizas and the influence of these connections on phosphorus transfer between plants.

### Litter Decomposition and Nutrient Cycling

- **Gadgil and Gadgil (1971, 1975)**: Proposed the "Gadgil effect" — the observation that mycorrhizal roots can suppress litter decomposition, possibly by competing with free-living decomposer fungi for nutrients. This challenges the assumption that mycorrhizae always accelerate nutrient cycling.
- **Berg and Lindberg (1980)**: Investigated whether litter decomposition is retarded in the presence of mycorrhizal roots in forest soil.
- **Conn and Dighton (2000)**: Showed that litter quality influences decomposition rates, ECM community structure, and mycorrhizal root surface acid phosphatase activity, linking above-ground litter inputs to below-ground fungal community function.

### Functional Redundancy and Ecosystem Stability

- **Jonsson et al. (2001)**: Found context-dependent effects of ECM species richness on tree seedling productivity, suggesting that the relationship between fungal [[mycorrhizal-diversity-and-ecosystem-function]] is not linear and depends on environmental conditions.
- **Leake (2001)**: Questioned whether ECM diversity is important for ecosystem function, noting that functional redundancy among ECM species may buffer ecosystem processes against species loss — but also that unique functional traits of certain species may be irreplaceable.

## Lichen Primary Production in Ecosystems

Lichens, as fungal symbioses with photobionts, contribute directly to primary production, particularly [[fungal-biodiversity-in-extreme-environments]]:

- **Cooper and Wookey (2001)**: Measured field growth rates of forage lichens and implications for reindeer grazing in Svalbard, documenting lichen productivity in Arctic ecosystems.
- **Kärenlampi (1971)**: Studied relative growth rates of fruticose lichens in subarctic environments.
- **Palmqvist and Sundberg (2000)**: Compared light use efficiency of dry matter gain among macro-lichens, showing species-specific traits interact with microclimate to determine productivity.
- **Beymer and Klopatek (1991)**: Estimated potential carbon contribution by microphytic crusts (including lichens) in pinyon-juniper woodlands, highlighting the role of lichen-dominated [[biological-soil-indicators]] in arid and semi-arid ecosystem carbon budgets.

## Conservation and Management Implications

### Revegetation and Restoration

- **Michelsen (1993)**: Demonstrated growth improvement of Ethiopian acacias by addition of VA mycorrhizal fungi or roots of native plants, showing the practical value of mycorrhizal inoculation for restoration of degraded tropical lands.
- **Jasper (1994)**: Reviewed management of mycorrhizas in revegetation, concluding that maintaining native mycorrhizal inoculum is critical for successful ecosystem restoration.
- **Gemma and Koske (1997)**: Studied AM fungi in sand dune plants of the north Atlantic coast, documenting both field colonization and the potential for greenhouse inoculation of planting stock.

### Sustainability

- **Jeffries and Barea (1994)**: Placed mycorrhizal research in the context of sustainable soil-plant systems, arguing that understanding mycorrhizal function is essential for developing agricultural practices that maintain soil health over the long term.
- **Barea et al. (1997)**: Reviewed interactions between mycorrhizal fungi and rhizosphere microorganisms within the framework of sustainable soil-plant systems.

### Functional Complementarity

- **Allen et al. (1995)**: Described patterns and regulation of mycorrhizal plant and fungal diversity, arguing that understanding diversity-function relationships is essential for predicting ecosystem responses to global change.
- **Miller (1995)**: Discussed functional diversity in fungi more broadly, noting that the functional significance of fungal diversity extends far beyond mycorrhizae to encompass decomposition, pathogenicity, and food web support.
