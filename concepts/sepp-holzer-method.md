# Sepp Holzer Method

Sepp Holzer's approach to farming and gardening centers on working with nature rather than against it.
Operating the Krameterhof in Austria at altitudes up to 1,500m, Holzer has developed practical techniques that reject chemical fertilisers, pesticides, and monoculture.
The Krameterhof spans approximately 45 hectares of steep mountainous terrain in the Lungau region of Salzburg province, where winter temperatures can drop below -20°C and the growing season is dramatically shorter than in lowland areas.
Despite these challenges, Holzer produces citrus fruits, kiwis, figs, and other supposedly "Mediterranean" crops through careful microclimate creation and landscape design.

Holzer is sometimes called the "agricultural rebel" of Austria for his refusal to follow conventional farming practices.
He left school at age 15 to take over his family's farm and has spent over five decades developing his methods through direct observation and experimentation rather than academic study.
His approach is distinct from other permaculture practitioners in its emphasis on high-altitude mountain farming, its rejection of any form of ploughing, and its integration of aquaculture and large-scale earthworks.
Holzer's methods have been applied on projects across six continents, from restoring barren landscapes in Spain and Portugal to establishing food forests in Thailand and Brazil.

## Core Principles

### Soil Fertility

Every plant has its own requirements and affects its surroundings and the soil in a different way.
In monoculture the demands on the soil are unbalanced, and the nutrient content becomes lower and lower until it is completely exhausted.
To regenerate exhausted soil, the soil life must be looked after first.
The creatures living in the soil — earthworms, bacteria and fungi among others — are the key to healthy soil.
Avoiding pesticides and chemical fertilisers is essential, as is avoiding deep ploughing in autumn, which causes the soil to freeze and destroys soil life and the natural layering.

Holzer's approach to soil is fundamentally fungal rather than bacterial.
He observes that forest soils — the most fertile and self-sustaining systems on Earth — are dominated by fungal networks that break down woody material and transport nutrients over large distances.
By maintaining permanent plant cover and incorporating woody material into his raised beds and hugelkultur mounds, he encourages mycorrhizal networks that dramatically increase the effective root zone of his crops.
This fungal dominance also suppresses pathogenic organisms through competitive exclusion.
Holzer recommends spreading mature compost and woody material on the soil surface rather than digging it in, allowing soil organisms to incorporate it at their own pace.

### Green Manure

A good mixture of green manure crops is very important so that the individual plants can propagate themselves.
This increases the stability of the system and its value for the soil, soil life and beneficial insects.
When biomass is left on these areas it benefits the soil and soil life.
The slow decomposition of green manure crops in autumn and winter builds up a productive layer of soil.
The biomass and loosening of the soil (caused by the root systems) leads to a good soil structure.

Holzer's green manure mixtures typically include deep-rooted plants like lucerne (alfalfa), which can reach depths of 3-4 metres and bring minerals from deep subsoil layers to the surface.
These are combined with nitrogen-fixing legumes like clover and vetch, and fast-growing biomass producers like buckwheat and phacelia.
The key principle is that green manure crops should never be dug in — they are simply cut and left on the surface as mulch.
This preserves the soil structure, prevents erosion, and allows the soil organisms to incorporate the organic matter gradually through their own activity.

### Old Varieties and Plant Diversity

Old varieties of cereal, vegetable or potato make the best crops — better flavour, generally less demanding, and far better adapted to local conditions than overbred varieties.
Hybrid seeds (F1 varieties) are entirely unsuited to permaculture systems: they cannot reproduce true, are frequently sterile, and are not suited to varying local conditions.
Holzer selects seeds from the strongest plants growing on the worst soils and under the most extreme conditions, because these produce plants that can deal well with difficult conditions while still giving satisfactory yields.

This seed selection philosophy is essentially a form of participatory plant breeding conducted on a single farm.
By saving seed from the most resilient individuals year after year, Holzer creates landraces uniquely adapted to his specific microclimates and growing conditions.
He has developed his own varieties of potato, cereal, and vegetable crops that reliably produce on his mountain farm.
He argues that the modern seed industry's focus on uniformity and maximum yield under optimal conditions produces varieties that are brittle and dependent on chemical inputs, whereas his landraces are robust and self-reliant.

### Polycultures

Monoculture farming practices are completely unnatural, cause many problems and should be stopped.
Different crops can grow well as a group, be harvested at the same time, and processed together.
A balanced mixture of plants which improve the soil and fix nitrogen is required.
Polycultures will not exhaust the soil of any one nutrient because they have different requirements.

A classic Holzer polyculture example is his grain-legume-pumpkin combination: cereals (oats, rye, or wheat) are planted with climbing beans or peas and pumpkins or squash.
The legumes fix nitrogen, the cereals provide support for the climbers, and the large pumpkin leaves shade the soil, suppress weeds, and reduce evaporation.
All three can be harvested simultaneously and the mixed grain can be ground together for a nutritionally complete flour.
This type of polyculture mimics the Three Sisters system of Mesoamerican agriculture and demonstrates how complementary plant architectures can share the same space productively.

### Alpine Plants

Holzer's experience cultivating plants from Alpine regions has taught him that all events in nature should be observed closely.
For example, yellow gentians (Gentiana lutea) only germinate under extreme climatic conditions — repeated cycles of frost, warmth, rain, and snow.
Nature cannot be "improved" upon.
Alpine plants often require symbiotic fungi in the soil, which can be introduced by taking soil from near the roots of the parent plant.
The alpine plant philosophy extends beyond individual species to a general principle of working within ecological limits rather than trying to override them.

Where conventional agriculture would drain a wet area, install heating, or apply chemical fertilisers to grow crops outside their natural range, Holzer instead designs microclimates — using stone walls, water bodies, and windbreaks — that create conditions where the desired plants can thrive without external inputs.
His terraced ponds and [[sepp-holzer-permaculture-and-rebel-farming|raised beds]] are primary tools for this microclimate creation.
Stone walls absorb heat during the day and release it at night, creating warm microclimates that can extend the growing season by several weeks in mountain environments.

### Livestock Integration

Livestock play a large role in Holzer's permaculture system.
They provide high-quality produce and are also industrious workers.
Holzer breeds robust, hardy animals suited to the terrain — old, sometimes rare domestic breeds as well as wild animals.
Pigs are used as living ploughs to dig over and loosen soil.
Livestock are always kept outside in families, and paddock rotation ensures land is never overgrazed.
The integration of animals into the farming system closes nutrient loops, as animal manure returns fertility to the soil and animals process plant biomass into high-quality food.

### Water Management

Holzer's water management is one of his most distinctive contributions.
He constructs ponds and lakes at multiple elevations across his farm, using them as both aquaculture systems and microclimate regulators.
Water bodies absorb heat during the day and release it at night, moderating temperature extremes and reducing frost risk in adjacent growing areas.
The ponds are stocked with fish and provide habitat for amphibians and aquatic insects that contribute to pest control on the farm.
He also uses contour swales and terraces to slow water movement across slopes, preventing erosion and maximising infiltration.
The Krameterhof contains over 70 pond systems, ranging from small mountain pools to large lakes, all designed to serve multiple functions simultaneously.

### Observation as Method

Central to Holzer's method is the principle of careful observation before intervention.
He spends long periods watching natural processes — how water moves across the landscape, which plants colonise disturbed ground, how animals interact with vegetation — before designing any intervention.
This observational approach leads to solutions that work with existing natural tendencies rather than against them.
Holzer uses [[holzer-indicator-plants]] as a diagnostic tool: the presence or absence of certain plant species reveals information about soil conditions, moisture levels, nutrient availability, and microclimate characteristics that would otherwise require laboratory testing to determine.
This approach reduces dependence on external inputs and expert knowledge, making the method accessible to anyone willing to observe carefully.

## Scale and Adaptability

The Sepp Holzer method is notable for its scalability.
The same principles that operate at the Krameterhof — species diversity, closed nutrient loops, animal integration, water management, and minimal intervention — can be applied on any scale from a small balcony garden to a large agricultural enterprise.
Holzer has consulted on projects ranging from urban community gardens to thousand-hectare landscape restorations, demonstrating that the principles are not specific to mountain environments.
The method has been adapted to Mediterranean, tropical, and arid climates through the selection of locally appropriate species and the creative use of available materials.
