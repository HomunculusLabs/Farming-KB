# Fungal Biosorption of Heavy Metals

## Overview

Fungal biosorption refers to the passive uptake of metal ions by fungal biomass through physico-chemical mechanisms, primarily involving binding to cell wall components. This process occurs whether the biomass is dead or alive and represents one of several mechanisms by which fungi interact with toxic metals in the environment. Fungi and their by-products have received considerable attention as biosorbent materials for metal-contaminated aqueous solutions due to the ease of cultivation and the availability of fungal biomass as industrial waste products.

## Fungal Cell Wall Composition and Metal Binding

Fungal cell walls are complex macromolecular structures consisting of:

- **Chitin**: Linear polymer of N-acetylglucosamine with amino groups that can bind metals
- **Chitosan**: Deacetylated form of chitin with enhanced metal-binding capacity due to free amino groups
- **Glucans**: Beta-glucans with hydroxyl groups for metal coordination
- **Proteins**: Containing carboxyl, amino, sulfhydryl, and imidazole groups
- **Lipids**: Minor component contributing to metal binding
- **Melanin**: Pigment with strong metal-chelating properties

This variety of structural components ensures many different functional groups are available to bind metal ions to varying degrees depending on their chemical properties (Gadd, 1990, 1993a). The biosorption capacity depends on:
- Fungal species and strain
- Growth conditions and cell wall composition
- Metal ion properties (charge, ionic radius, electronegativity)
- Solution chemistry (pH, competing ions, temperature)
- Biomass pretreatment (physical, chemical, thermal)

## Biosorption Mechanisms

### Physico-Chemical (Metabolism-Independent) Mechanisms

1. **Ion exchange**: Metal ions exchange with counter-ions (H⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺) associated with cell wall functional groups
2. **Complexation**: Metal ions form coordination complexes with nitrogen, oxygen, or sulfur donor atoms in cell wall polymers
3. **Chelation**: Multiple binding sites simultaneously coordinate a single metal ion
4. **Electrostatic attraction**: Charged metal species attracted to oppositely charged cell surface groups
5. **Precipitation**: Metal ions precipitate on or within the cell wall matrix as hydroxides, sulfides, or other insoluble forms
6. **Surface adsorption**: Van der Waals forces contribute to metal retention

### Physiological (Metabolism-Dependent) Mechanisms

1. **Active transport and intracellular uptake**: Metal ions enter cells through transport systems; essential metals (Na, Mg, K, Ca, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn) have specific acquisition systems. Inessential toxic metals compete for these transporters (e.g., Cs⁺ competes for K⁺ transport systems)
2. **Intracellular sequestration**: Metal-binding molecules within the cell:
   - **Metallothioneins (MT)**: Low-MW cysteine-rich proteins (6000-10,000 Da) induced by toxic metals; central to Cu and Cd detoxification in fungi
   - **Phytochelatins**: Peptides with general structure (γGlu-Cys)ₙ-Gly (n up to 11); bind Cd²⁺ and Cu⁺. In S. pombe, high-MW complexes also contain acid-labile sulfide (CdS crystallite core) for greater stability and binding capacity
3. **Vacuolar compartmentation**: Metals sequestered in vacuoles; demonstrated in S. cerevisiae mutants where vacuolar dysfunction increases metal sensitivity
4. **Efflux mechanisms**: Regulated export of excess metals

### Extracellular Metal Binding

- **Siderophores**: Low-MW ligands (500-1000 Da) with high affinity for Fe³⁺; also bind Mg²⁺, Mn²⁺, Cr³⁺, Ga³⁺, and Pu⁴⁺
- **Extracellular polymeric substances (EPS)**: Polysaccharides and other macromolecules that bind significant amounts of metals
- **Organic acids**: Citric, oxalic, and other acids produced by fungi complex with metals

## Biosorption Performance

### Capacity and Modeling

Biosorption data typically fit Langmuir or Freundlich isotherm models:
- **Langmuir model**: Assumes monolayer adsorption on homogeneous surface; Wang & Yu (1998) applied to dye adsorption on live and dead mycelium
- **Freundlich model**: Empirical model for heterogeneous surfaces; Knapp et al. (1997) applied to Orange II adsorption on heat-killed mycelium

### Regeneration and Reuse

Fungal biosorbents can be regenerated using acids, alkalis, or chelating agents (EDTA, citrate). Many studies show retention of a high percentage of original sorption capacity after repeated regenerative stages (Gadd & White, 1992; Gadd, 1993a). This is important for practical applications.

## Metal Solubilization by Fungi

In addition to immobilization, fungi can solubilize metal compounds:

### Mechanisms

1. **Protonation**: Plasma membrane H⁺-translocating ATPase and organic acid production decrease pH, solubilizing metal compounds
2. **Organic acid complexation**: Citrate, oxalate, and other acids form soluble complexes with metal cations, increasing mobility
3. **Siderophore production**: Solubilizes Fe³⁺ and other metals

### Environmental Significance

- Approximately one-third of soil fungal isolates can solubilize at least one of Co₃(PO₄)₂, ZnO, or Zn₃(PO₄)₂; about one-tenth can solubilize all three (Sayer et al., 1995)
- Rock phosphate fertilizers containing Cd can release Cd during phosphate solubilization
- Mycorrhizal fungi increase phosphate availability partly through their high phosphate-solubilizing ability
- Fungal organic acids form pore networks (3-10 μm diameter) in weatherable minerals under European coniferous forests (Jongmans et al., 1997)

### Bioremediation Applications

- **Metal recovery**: Fungi can leach metals from industrial wastes, low-grade ores, and electronic scrap (Burgstaller & Schinner, 1993)
- **Soil remediation**: Metal-citrate complexes can be subsequently degraded for ultimate metal recovery
- **Lead remediation concern**: Aspergillus niger can solubilize pyromorphite (Pb₅(PO₄)₃Cl), a stable lead mineral used for soil remediation, releasing Pb into bioavailable forms and producing biogenic lead oxalate (Sayer et al., 1999)

## Metal Transformation Mechanisms

### Volatilization

Fungi can transform metals into volatile forms:
- **Mercury**: Organomercury lyase converts organomercury to Hg²⁺; mercuric reductase reduces Hg²⁺ to Hg⁰ (Tezuka & Takasaki, 1988)
- **Selenium**: Volatilization of selenite as dimethyl selenide demonstrated in Penicillium species (Brady, Tobin & Gadd, 1996; Huysmans & Frankenberger, 1991)
- **Arsenic**: Trimethylarsine evolution by Penicillium sp. (Huysmans & Frankenberger, 1991)
- **Tin**: Tributyltin degraded to mono- and dibutyltins; inorganic Sn(II) as ultimate product (Barug, 1981)
- **Lead**: Trimethyllead degradation by alkyllead-tolerant yeast and Phaeolus schweintzii (Macaskie & Dean, 1987, 1990)

### Precipitation

- **Oxalate precipitation**: Many fungi produce oxalic acid that precipitates metals as insoluble oxalates. Copper-tolerant fungi produce copper oxalate (Murphy & Levy, 1983; Purvis, 1984). Lead oxalate dihydrate produced during pyromorphite transformation by fungi (Sayer et al., 1999)
- **Sulfide precipitation**: Intracellular (γGlu-Cys)ₙ-Gly–Cd–S²⁻ complexes form CdS crystallite cores

## Challenges and Limitations

Despite extensive laboratory research, commercialization of fungal biosorption has been limited. Key challenges:

- **Lack of specificity**: Fungal biomass binds multiple metals non-selectively, making selective recovery difficult
- **Lower robustness**: Compared with ion-exchange resins, biomass-based systems are less robust and predictable
- **Variable performance**: Natural variability in biomass composition affects reproducibility
- **Competition**: In real waste streams, multiple metals compete for binding sites
- **Regeneration costs**: Although possible, regeneration adds complexity and cost
- **No commercial adoption**: Despite several attempts, biosorption has not been adopted as a commercially viable treatment method (Eccles, 1999)

## Genetic Engineering Approaches

Metal-binding proteins and peptides have been expressed in E. coli to enhance biosorption:
- Yeast metallothionein expression increased Cd²⁺ binding 15-20 fold in E. coli (Sousa et al., 1998)
- Engineered metal-binding peptides (Cys-Gly-Cys-Cys-Gly motif) enhanced Cd²⁺ and Hg²⁺ binding
- Surface display of CP peptide increased Cd²⁺ binding 4-fold (Kotrba et al., 1999)
