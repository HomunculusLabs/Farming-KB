# Living Mulches and Ground Covers

Living mulches and ground covers are plants grown specifically to protect and
enrich the soil surface. Unlike traditional mulches of dead organic material,
living mulches are actively growing plants that provide continuous soil
protection, weed suppression, and nutrient cycling. Bill Mollison regarded
living mulches as an essential component of permaculture design, particularly
in [[mollison-designers-climate-zone-2-orchard-food-forest]] and [[mollison-designers-climate-zone-3-farm-forestry]], where maintaining soil
health under tree crops is critical.

## Functions of Living Mulches

Living mulches perform multiple functions simultaneously in the permaculture
system. They protect the soil surface from raindrop impact and erosion, reducing
runoff and increasing water infiltration. They suppress weed growth by competing
for light, water, and nutrients at the soil surface. They moderate soil
temperature, keeping roots cooler in summer and warmer in winter. They add
organic matter to the soil through root turnover and leaf decomposition. They
can fix atmospheric nitrogen when leguminous species are used. And they provide
habitat for [[mollison-designers-mycorrhizal-fungi-and-soil-microbiology]] and [[mollison-designers-integrated-pest-management-design]].

## Selection Criteria

The ideal living mulch species for permaculture systems should meet several
criteria. It should be low-growing so it does not compete with crop plants for
light. It should have a shallow or non-competitive root system that does not
interfere with crop root zones. It should tolerate shade, traffic, and
mowing. It should be persistent but not invasive. And it should provide
additional benefits such as nitrogen fixation, beneficial insect habitat, or
edible harvest.

## Leguminous Living Mulches

### White Clover (Trifolium repens)

White clover is the most widely used living mulch in temperate permaculture
systems. It fixes nitrogen, tolerates mowing and grazing, tolerates partial
shade, and forms a dense mat that suppresses weeds effectively. Under fruit
trees in [[mollison-designers-mixed-orchard-design-species-selection]], white clover provides nitrogen that reduces or
eliminates the need for additional fertiliser. Its flowers attract bees and
other pollinators, supporting the [[mollison-designers-bee-forage-tree-planting]] strategy.

White clover is best suited to cool, moist climates. In hot, dry areas it may
die back during summer, leaving bare soil exposed. It can be combined with
drought-tolerant grass species to maintain year-round ground cover.

### Subterranean Clover (Trifolium subterraneum)

Subterranean clover is an annual legume that self-seeds readily, making it
low-maintenance once established. It dies back in summer, leaving a mulch of
dry plant material that protects the soil surface, then germinates with autumn
rains. This annual cycle provides nitrogen input and soil protection without
requiring mowing or management.

### Lucerne (Alfalfa, Medicago sativa)

Lucerne is a deep-rooted perennial legume that fixes large quantities of
nitrogen and produces abundant biomass. It can be used as a living mulch between
rows of fruit trees or vine crops, with periodic cutting to provide mulch
material. Its deep taproot accesses subsoil nutrients, functioning as a
[[mollison-designers-dynamic-accumulators-comfrey]] for potassium, phosphorus, and trace minerals.

## Non-Leguminous Living Mulches

### Sweet Woodruff (Galium odoratum)

Sweet woodruff is a shade-tolerant perennial that forms a dense carpet in the
understory of forest gardens. It is particularly useful under [[mollison-designers-climate-zone-2-orchard-food-forest]] trees
where shade levels are too high for clover. Its aromatic foliage may have some
pest-repellent properties.

### Creeping Thyme (Thymus serpyllum)

Creeping thyme forms a low, dense mat that tolerates foot traffic and releases
aromatic oils that may repel some insect pests. It is well-suited to paths and
between stepping stones in [[mollison-designers-climate-zone-1-intensive-garden]] areas.

### Yarrow (Achillea millefolium)

Yarrow is a [[mollison-designers-dynamic-accumulators-comfrey]] that accumulates potassium, phosphorus, and copper.
Its deep root system and drought tolerance make it suitable for dry sites where
other ground covers struggle. Yarrow also attracts beneficial insects including
hoverflies and ladybirds.

### Comfrey as Ground Cover

While comfrey is primarily valued as a [[mollison-designers-dynamic-accumulators-comfrey]], it also functions as a
living mulch under fruit trees. The large leaves shade the soil surface,
suppress weeds, and add substantial organic matter when they decompose. Comfrey
is best used in dedicated patches rather than as a general ground cover, as it
can become dominant.

## Tropical Living Mulches

In tropical permaculture systems, different ground cover species are used:

- **Peanut (Arachis pintoi)**: A perennial groundnut that forms a dense mat,
  fixes nitrogen, and produces edible nuts. Excellent for orchard understories.
- **Centrosema (Centrosema pubescens)**: A vigorous climbing and trailing
  legume that provides dense ground cover in tropical pastures and orchards.
- **Calopo (Calopogonium mucunoides)**: A fast-establishing annual legume for
  rapid soil cover on disturbed sites.
- **Pigeon pea (Cajanus cajan)**: A tall legume shrub that provides both living
  mulch and edible seeds. Used in [[mollison-designers-tropical-soils-and-cation-exchange-management]].
- **Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas)**: The [[mollison-designers-cassava-tropical-root-crops]] vine provides excellent
  soil cover while producing edible tubers, making it a dual-purpose living
  mulch in tropical gardens.

## Living Mulches in Orchards

The orchard floor is the most important application of living mulches in
permaculture design. A diverse ground cover under fruit trees provides nitrogen,
suppresses weeds, improves soil structure, and supports beneficial insects. The
[[mollison-designers-apple-guild-and-cool-climate-fruit-guilds]] design typically includes a living mulch layer of clover,
comfrey, and other low-growing species beneath the tree canopy.

Management of living mulches in orchards requires some attention. Mulches that
grow too tall can compete with trees for water and nutrients, especially during
establishment. Regular mowing or grazing with [[mollison-designers-poultry-forage-design-pastured-systems]] controls height while
returning nutrients to the soil surface. The living mulch should be maintained
in a ring around the tree trunk, with a mulch-free zone immediately around the
trunk to prevent rodent damage and collar rot.

## Living Mulches in Annual Systems

Using living mulches in annual vegetable systems is more challenging because of
competition between the mulch and the crop. Several strategies can reduce this
competition:

- **Suppressed living mulch**: Establish the living mulch early, then kill it
  back (by mowing, rolling, or partial herbicide application) before planting
  the main crop. The dead mulch residue continues to protect the soil.
- **Interseeded living mulch**: Plant the main crop first, then interseed the
  living mulch once the crop is established enough to compete. This delays
  mulch establishment but reduces competition during critical crop growth
  stages.
- **Strip intercropping**: Alternate strips of crop and living mulch rather
  than interplanting them. This simplifies management and allows separate
  cultivation of each strip.
