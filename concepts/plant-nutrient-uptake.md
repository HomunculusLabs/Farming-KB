# Plant Nutrient Uptake

Plants absorb 17 essential elements: C, H, O (from air and water, ~96% of plant mass) plus 14 mineral nutrients from soil. Understanding HOW plants access these minerals — through cation exchange, root exudates, mycorrhizal pathways, and foliar feeding — is the foundation of [[soil-fertility-management]] and [[cannabis-plant-nutrition]].

## Nutrient Uptake Mechanisms

### Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)
Soil particles (clay, humus) carry negative electrical charges that attract and hold positively charged nutrient ions (cations): Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺, Na⁺. Plant roots release H⁺ ions in exchange, swapping them for nutrient cations at the root surface. Higher CEC = greater nutrient holding capacity. Organic matter dramatically increases CEC.

### Root Exudates
Plants actively engineer their rhizosphere by secreting sugars, amino acids, organic acids, and enzymes from roots. These exudates:
- Feed specific beneficial bacteria and fungi (10-40% of plant photosynthate)
- Solubilize locked-up minerals (e.g., citric acid solubilizes phosphorus)
- Attract mycorrhizal fungi via strigolactone hormones
- Recruit plant-growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR)
- Suppress pathogens through antimicrobial compounds

### Mycorrhizal Pathways
Mycorrhizal fungi extend the effective root zone 100-1000x. The fungal hyphae absorb water and dissolved minerals (especially P, Zn, Cu) from soil micropores inaccessible to roots, then exchange them with the plant for carbon (sugars). Up to 80% of plant P and N can come through mycorrhizal pathways.

### Foliar Feeding
Leaves can absorb nutrients through stomata and cuticle pores. Foliar application bypasses soil chemistry constraints. Best for micronutrients (Fe, Mn, Zn) and rapid correction of deficiencies. Apply at dawn/dusk when stomata are open. Add a surfactant for better coverage. Foliar feeding supplements but does not replace root uptake.

## Nutrient Mobility

| Mobile in Plant (deficiency shows in OLD leaves) | Immobile in Plant (deficiency shows in NEW leaves) |
|---|---|
| N, P, K, Mg, Cl, Na, Zn (partially) | Ca, Fe, Mn, B, Cu, S, Mo |

Plants mobilize N, P, K, Mg from older tissue to feed new growth when deficient. Immobile nutrients cannot be relocated — new growth suffers first.

## pH Effects on Availability

Each nutrient has an optimal pH range for root uptake:

- **pH 5.5-6.5**: Optimal availability for most nutrients. Micronutrients (Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B) are most available at lower pH.
- **pH 6.0-7.0**: Best general range for living soil. Calcium, magnesium, and molybdenum are more available.
- **pH >7.5**: Iron, manganese, zinc, copper, and boron become locked out (deficiency likely even if present in soil).
- **pH <5.5**: Calcium and magnesium become deficient; aluminum and manganese may reach toxic levels.
- The [[soil-food-web]] buffers pH naturally through microbial respiration and organic acid production.

## Synergistic and Antagonistic Relationships

Key interactions that affect nutrient availability:
- **K-Ca-Mg antagonism**: Excess potassium competes with calcium and magnesium uptake. Balance all three.
- **N-Zn**: High nitrogen increases zinc demand. Zinc deficiency can appear in heavily N-fertilized crops.
- **P-Zn, P-Fe**: Excess phosphorus locks out zinc and iron. Common in soils amended with too much manure or rock phosphate.
- **Ca-B**: Calcium aids boron mobility. Boron deficiency can occur even with adequate B if Ca is low.
- **Fe-Mn**: High manganese can induce iron deficiency (and vice versa). Both compete for same uptake sites.
- **N-S**: Nitrogen and sulfur are synergistic — sulfur is needed for protein synthesis alongside nitrogen.
- **K-Cl**: Potassium and chlorine compete. High chloride can reduce K uptake.

## Deficiency vs. Toxicity Symptoms

| Nutrient | Deficiency | Toxicity |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| **N** | Pale yellow older leaves, stunted growth, poor protein synthesis | Excessive vegetative growth, soft tissue, delayed flowering, disease susceptibility |
| **P** | Dark green/purplish leaves (especially undersides), stunted roots, delayed maturity | Interveinal chlorosis of older leaves, Zn/Fe/Mn deficiency induced |
| **K** | Brown leaf margins/tips (scorch), weak stems, poor bud development | Mg deficiency induced, salt stress, leaf edge necrosis |
| **Ca** | Distorted new growth, blossom end rot, brown spots, root tip dieback | Competes with Mg and K uptake, can induce Mg/K deficiency |
| **Mg** | Interveinal chlorosis on older leaves (yellow between green veins) | Interferes with Ca uptake |
| **S** | Uniform yellowing of NEW leaves (unlike N which shows on old) | Leaf burn, acidification of soil |
| **Fe** | Interveinal chlorosis on NEW leaves, veins stay green | Bronzing of leaves, Mn/Zn deficiency induced, phosphorus precipitation |
| **Mn** | Interveinal chlorosis on new leaves with dead spots | Fe deficiency induced, black/brown spots on older leaves |
| **Zn** | Small distorted new leaves, short internodes, "rosetting" | Fe deficiency induced, leaf chlorosis |
| **Cu** | Wilting, darkened/bluish leaves, tip dieback | Fe deficiency induced, root damage, leaf chlorosis |
| **B** | Cracked/hollow stems, bud die-off, poor pollen tube growth | Leaf tip yellowing, necrosis |
| **Mo** | Similar to N deficiency, whiptail in brassicas | Similar to Cu toxicity (rare) |
| **Cl** | Wilting, chlorosis in new leaves, stubby roots | Leaf burn, premature leaf drop (rare in most soils) |

## Chelation

Chelates are organic molecules that bind mineral ions, keeping them soluble and available for plant uptake. Humic acids, fulvic acids, citric acid, and EDTA act as natural or synthetic chelators. In living soil systems, microbial exudates and humic substances provide natural chelation — this is a key advantage of biological [[soil-fertility-management]] over synthetic fertilizers. Mycorrhizal fungi also chelate nutrients, especially iron and phosphorus.

## Microbial Mineralization

The [[soil-food-web]] converts organic matter into plant-available nutrients:
1. Bacteria and fungi decompose organic compounds (proteins, cellulose, lignin)
2. They immobilize nutrients in their biomass (N, P, S locked in microbial cells)
3. Protozoa and bacterial-feeding nematodes graze on microbes, excreting excess N as ammonium (NH₄⁺)
4. Fungal-feeding nematodes release nutrients from fungal biomass
5. Predatory nematodes eat other nematodes, further cycling nutrients
6. This "microbial loop" provides a steady, slow-release nutrient supply matched to plant demand
