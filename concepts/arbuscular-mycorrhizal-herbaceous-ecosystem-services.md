## Overview

[[arbuscular-mycorrhizae]] (AM) are the most widespread type of mycorrhizal symbiosis, dominating in temperate grasslands, tropical forests, and tropical grasslands. In these warm, moist environments where nutrient cycling is rapid, the major forms of soil nutrients exist primarily as inorganic ions in soil solution. Arbuscular mycorrhizae enhance plant access to these mineral nutrients — particularly phosphorus — through mechanisms including expanded soil exploration, faster phosphate transport, and limited enzyme-mediated solubilization of poorly available phosphates.

## Ecosystem Context

In herbaceous-dominated plant communities, the environmental conditions favor rapid decomposition:

- Warm, moist conditions promote biological activity year-round
- Plant litter is readily decomposable (low C:N ratios)
- Organic matter is rapidly incorporated into the mineral soil matrix
- The nutrient supply for plants is predominantly inorganic, mineralized by saprotrophic activity on high-quality plant residues

**Phosphorus** tends to be the primary limiting nutrient in these systems (Read, 1991b), and arbuscular mycorrhizal associations enhance the efficiency of mineral nutrient acquisition (Hetrick, 1989).

## Mechanisms of Phosphate Uptake Enhancement

**Bolan (1991)** identified three primary mechanisms by which arbuscular mycorrhizae enhance phosphate uptake:

1. **Exploitation of a larger soil volume**: Extraradical hyphae extend far beyond the root's depletion zone, accessing phosphate that would otherwise be beyond the root's reach
2. **Faster phosphate transport**: Fungal hyphae have higher surface-area-to-volume ratios and faster diffusion kinetics than roots
3. **Solubilization of complex phosphates**: Through production of phosphatase enzymes and organic acids

These mechanisms address the three rate-limiting steps of phosphate uptake by plants:

| Rate-Limiting Step | Non-Mycorrhizal Condition | Mycorrhizal Enhancement |
|---|---|---|
| Release of P from soil particles | Slow dissolution | Chemical modification via enzymes and organic acids (1a) |
| Diffusion to root surface | Long diffusion distance | Decreased distance via extraradical hyphae (2a) |
| Uptake across cell membrane | High threshold concentration | Reduced threshold concentration for transfer (3a) |

## Organic Phosphorus Utilization

Although arbuscular mycorrhizae have **limited enzyme expression** compared to ectomycorrhizal and ericoid mycorrhizae, they are capable of producing phosphatase enzymes:

- **Azcón et al. (1976)** and **Singh and Kapoor (1998)** demonstrated phosphatase production by AM fungi
- **Jayachandran et al. (1992)** provided striking evidence with big bluestem grass (*Andropogon gerardii*):
  - **Non-mycorrhizal** plants could access phosphorus from glycerophosphate and adenosine monophosphate, but **not** from phytic acid, RNA, ATP, or CMP
  - With the AM fungus *Glomus etunicatum*, plants could access **all forms** of organic phosphorus
  - Uptake into mycorrhizal plants was **500- to 600-fold higher** than in non-mycorrhizal plants

## Phosphate Acquisition in Calcareous Soils

The ability of arbuscular mycorrhizae to solubilize phosphate is particularly important in **calcareous soils**, where phosphate is limited due to complexing with heavy metal ions (particularly iron and calcium).

### Calcifuge vs. Calcicole Adaptation

**Tyler (1994)** demonstrated that calcifuge plant species (e.g., *Carex pilulifera*, *Deschampsia flexuosa*, *Holcus mollis*, *Nardus stricta*, *Veronica officinalis*) cannot grow on limestone soils because of their inability to decouple iron-phosphate complexes. Calcicole species, however, have developed mechanisms for acquiring both P and Fe from these soils:

**Ström (1997)** showed that calcicole species produce significantly more organic acids in their rhizosphere, particularly dicarboxylic and tricarboxylic acids:

| Plant Strategy | Example Species | Total Organic Acid (mmol/m³) |
|---|---|---|
| Calcifuge | *Deschampsia flexuosa* | 17.3 |
| Calcifuge | *Viscaria* | 11.7 |
| Calcicole | *Gypsophila* | **40.8** |
| Calcicole | *Sanguisorba* | **32.6** |

The higher organic acid production by calcicoles helps solubilize phosphate and essential heavy metals from calcium-iron complexes. Mycorrhizal fungi may contribute to this organic acid production (Azcón et al., 1976; Bolan, 1991; Singh and Kapoor, 1998).

### Enhanced Micronutrient Acquisition

**Goh et al. (1997)** showed that AM colonization of wheat roots in calcareous soil **significantly increased availability of both phosphorus and zinc**, although the effect was not always reflected in plant growth. **Clark and Zeto (2000)** noted that enhanced nitrogen uptake has also been observed with AM associations, though this may be indirectly related to achieving greater plant size through overcoming phosphate limitations.

## Interaction with Nitrogen-Fixing Legumes

The interaction between arbuscular mycorrhizae and nitrogen-fixing leguminous plants is ecologically significant. AM fungi assist the delivery of **phosphate to plants**, which maximizes the energetically expensive process of nitrogen fixation in root nodules (Azcón-Aguilar et al., 1979; Peoples and Craswell, 1992; Herrera et al., 1993). There is also some evidence that AM fungi can directly utilize **organic forms of nitrogen** (Ames et al., 1983).

## Soil Structure Maintenance

Beyond nutrient acquisition, [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] contribute to **soil fertility maintenance** through:

- Production of chemicals (glomalin and other glycoproteins) that help develop soil aggregates
- Mycelial networks that bind soil particles together, maintaining aggregate stability
- These effects improve soil structure, water retention, and overall fertility (Wright and Upadhyaya, 1998; Jeffries and Barea, 1994)

## The Bluebell Case Study: Seasonal Dynamics and Fitness

The work of **Merryweather and Fitter (1995a, b; 1996)** on bluebell (*Hyacinthoides non-scripta*) provides one of the most detailed field studies of AM function in a natural ecosystem:

### obligate Mycorrhizal Dependency
- Bluebell appears to be **obligately mycorrhizal** — it cannot complete its life cycle effectively without the symbiosis
- The researchers incorporated the mycorrhizal association into the plant's complete carbon and phosphorus budget

### Age-Related Increase in Colonization
As bluebell bulbs age and descend deeper into the soil:
- Rooting depth increases into zones where phosphorus is increasingly depleted
- Roots become **progressively more colonized** by arbuscular mycorrhizae
- The enhanced phosphorus gained through this association **maintains fecundity** (measured as bulb diameter)

### Fitness Control by Mycorrhizae
**Merryweather and Fitter (1996)** applied **benomyl** (a fungicide) to soil to reduce AM colonization:
- Phosphorus concentrations in vegetative parts (leaves) were reduced
- Despite reduced mycorrhizal function, **preferential allocation of phosphorus to flowers and seeds was maintained**
- This suggests the plant exerts control over resource allocation to maintain reproductive fitness even under suboptimal mycorrhizal conditions

## Field vs. Laboratory Conditions

**Fitter (1985)** highlighted significant discrepancies between laboratory and field observations of AM function:

- **Sanders and Fitter (1992a, b)** found very little influence of AM association on P uptake or heavy metal acquisition in natural grasses
- However, a **seasonal benefit** was suggested, occurring during periods when phosphorus availability is low and plant demand is high
- This seasonal benefit may explain the **maintenance of the mycorrhizal association** in communities where year-round nutritional benefit is not apparent

Potential reasons for field-lab discrepancies include:
- **Interconnectedness of plants** via [[common-mycorrhizal-networks]] (CMNs)
- **Faunal grazing** on extraradical hyphae reducing mycorrhizal function
- **Differences in root longevity** between natural and artificial systems

## Differential Effects on Host Plant Species

**Koide et al. (2000)** showed that the effect of AM colonization varies significantly among host species:

| Host Species | Phosphorus Uptake Rate Increase | Phosphorus Use Efficiency Change | Growth Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Lactuca* spp. | +23% | Decreased | Slight increase |
| *Abutilon* spp. | +32% | No change | Significant increase |
| *Beta* spp. | No change | No change | No effect |

This demonstrates that mycorrhizal colonization has **species-specific effects** that may influence competitive fitness within plant communities, not just individual plant growth.

## Faunal Grazing Effects

Faunal grazing on AM extraradical hyphae can significantly reduce nutrient acquisition efficiency:

- **Warnock et al. (1982)** showed strong interaction between collembolan density and host plant growth
- **Brussard et al. (2001)** demonstrated that nematode feeding on mycorrhizal hyphae reduces effectiveness and alters a plant's competitive fitness
- These effects are likely **more important in agroecosystems**, where reduced faunal diversity allows high collembola densities in the absence of predators

## Summary

Arbuscular mycorrhizae provide critical ecosystem services in herbaceous communities primarily through enhanced phosphorus acquisition. While they possess more limited enzyme capabilities than ecto- or ericoid mycorrhizae, they compensate through extensive soil exploration, efficient phosphate transport, and contributions to soil structure. Their effects are context-dependent — varying with season, host species identity, soil type, and faunal interactions — and in some natural ecosystems, their benefit may be most pronounced during brief periods of peak nutrient demand rather than as a constant year-round advantage.
