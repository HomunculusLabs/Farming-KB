## The City Planning Law and Housing Land

Rises in land prices over the postwar decades were triggered first by
massive purchases of land for housing and public use. This arose from a
general perception, aided by publicity to this effect, that land in Japan
is in short supply, a limited resource that cannot be increased. Combined
with the convergence of people drawn by false rumors of economic growth
on the cities, this perception created explosive demand for housing land.

The City Planning Law divided land into zones according to use: forests,
farmland, housing land, and so forth. Lines were drawn and farmland
divided into areas within urban planning zones, areas within land
adjustment zones, and areas outside the lines. The conversion of farmland
to housing land was prohibited, which forced a sharp reduction in housing
land that pushed up prices. Enforcement of the National Land Use Law may
have made land easy to come by for those enforcing it, but it made land
even more inaccessible to the ordinary person.

As laws proliferate, they appear to move toward perfection, but they only
become more imperfect and preposterously complex, breaking man and the
land apart. Only those who know the law well and can change the official
category of a piece of land are able to buy land and later sell it at
profit. Every time housing land changes hands, the price goes up.

## The Agricultural Land Law as Barrier

The most direct obstacle to those wishing to become farmers is the
Agricultural Land Law. Not one square yard of land that goes by the name
of farmland is available for purchase by city people. Such land cannot be
bought by anyone other than a farmer. Legally, a "farmer" is defined as
someone who owns at least one and one-quarter acres of farmland. The
Agricultural Land Law has effectively brought a halt to the transfer of
farmland.

land nor formally lease and work it as tenant farmers. The law that was
