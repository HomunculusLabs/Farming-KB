# Cannabis Treatment for Spinal Cord Injuries

Spinal cord injuries represent one of the most devastating forms of
trauma, often resulting in permanent paralysis, chronic pain, and
uncontrollable muscle spasms. Research compiled in The Cannabis Health
Index assigns a [[cannabis-health-methods-of-administration]] of 19 across 5
studies reviewed, making this one of the better-supported therapeutic
applications of medical cannabis.

## Understanding Spinal Cord Injuries

The spine allows upright movement, bending, stretching, and reaching. It
is a solid load-carrying support system that is remarkably flexible while
housing and protecting the spinal cord. Solid support derives from
vertebral bone rigidity, and flexibility is achieved by intervertebral
discs between each of 26 spinal bones.

The brain and the spinal cord make up the [[cannabis-endocannabinoid-system]]. The spinal cord is a relatively thick bundle of nerves descending from the medulla oblongata through the center of the spine. Along its length, spinal cord nerves create junctions and exit between each vertebra to connect all parts of the body through ever-smaller branching nerve fibers.

An injury to the spinal column most commonly results from external trauma
(car crash, gunshot) but may also result from internal trauma (stroke,
aneurysm) or certain diseases (cancer). The location of damage determines
the degree of disability. Paraplegic patients are paralyzed from the
waist down after damage to the thoracic or lower spine. Quadriplegic
patients lose the ability to move arms and legs after damage to the
spinal cord in the neck or brain itself.

The management of spinal column injuries, where some sensation is still
present, is often accompanied by chronic pain and uncontrollable muscle
spasms of the back, arms, and legs.

## Cannabis Research on Spinal Cord Injuries

### Antispasmodic Properties

The time-proven antispasmodic properties of cannabis have been confirmed
by modern science in numerous human studies. As early as 1974, VA
hospital-based researchers began looking at data suggesting a practical
therapeutic antispasmodic benefit. Later studies confirmed the efficacy
of cannabinoids, especially THC.

### Study Results

**Basel, Switzerland (2007)** - REHAB Basel, Centre for Spinal Cord
Injury and Severe Head Injury: Researchers tested oral THC (average daily
dose 31 mg) and rectal THC (average daily dose 43 mg) in 25 patients
with spinal cord injuries and regular spasms. THC was confirmed as an
effective antispasmodic. The study recommends a minimum dose of 15-20 mg
daily to produce a therapeutic and measurable effect in reducing spinal
cord injury-related spasmodic activity. CHI: 5

**Medical College of Georgia (1995)** - Five patients with spinal cord
injuries and regular spasms received dronabinol 2 x 5 mg, gradually
increased to 3 x 20 mg. Reduced spasms were observed in 2 patients.
CHI: 3

**Zurich, Switzerland (1990)** - A single patient with spinal cord
injury and subsequent pains and spasms received placebo vs. 50 mg
codeine vs. 5 mg THC. THC produced significant reduction in spasticity.
CHI: 5

**National Survey (1982)** - Data collected from 43 patients with spinal
cord injury and related spasms showed that inhaled cannabis produced
significant reduction in spasticity. CHI: 3

**Miami VA Hospital (1974)** - Ten patients with spinal cord injuries
and resulting pain and spasms received inhaled cannabis. Decreased pain
and spasms were reported. CHI: 3

**Total CHI Value: 19**

## Mechanism of Action

Cannabis binds with both [[cannabis-cannabinoid-receptor-pharmacology]]. THC and dronabinol both bind with CB1 and CB2. The antispasmodic effect is primarily mediated through CB1 receptors in the central nervous system, which modulate motor neuron excitability and reduce the hyperreflexia that causes spasticity.

The [[cannabis-endocannabinoid-system]] is deeply
involved in the central nervous system's regulation of motor function.
CB1 receptors are densely concentrated in areas of the spinal cord that
control motor output, making them directly relevant to spasticity
management following spinal cord injury.

## Dosing Guidelines

The Basel study recommends a minimum dose of 15-20 mg per day of THC to
achieve a therapeutic effect for spinal cord injury-related spasticity.
Effective doses in studies ranged from 5 mg (single oral dose) to 43 mg
(rectal administration). Finding the optimal
[[cannabis-dosing-and-titration]] is
essential, as doses below 15 mg may be sub-therapeutic for this
condition.

## Strain-Specific Considerations

While both [[cannabis-sativa-vs-indica-guide]] and indica strains
contain cannabinoids that activate CB1 and CB2, sativas or sativa-heavy
strains tend to produce higher THC:CBD ratios. Since the antispasmodic
effect is primarily attributed to THC, sativa-dominant strains may be
preferred. However, some patients may benefit from the anti-inflammatory
properties of CBD-rich strains to address concurrent inflammation.

Clinical trials employed cannabinoids from various sources: whole-plant
cannabis, isolated THC, and synthetic dronabinol. The use of whole-plant
cannabis may provide additional therapeutic benefits through the
[[blesching-cannabis-cannabinoid-terpene-entourage-effect]] of
multiple cannabinoids and terpenes working together.

## Pain Management Component

Beyond spasticity reduction, cannabis addresses the chronic pain that
frequently accompanies spinal cord injuries. The analgesic properties of
cannabinoids work through multiple pathways including modulation of pain
signaling in the spinal cord, reduction of inflammation around the
injury site, and activation of descending pain inhibition pathways.

## Administration Methods

Studies used various administration methods including oral THC,
rectal THC suppositories, dronabinol capsules, and inhaled cannabis.
The choice of method should be guided by patient preference, the
specific symptoms being targeted, and consultation with healthcare
providers.
