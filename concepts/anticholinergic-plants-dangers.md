## Datura Stramonium (Jimson Weed)

Datura stramonium is perhaps the most commonly encountered dangerous anticholinergic plant in the United States. It grows wild throughout the country except Wyoming and Alaska, thriving in disturbed soil, roadsides, pastures, and waste areas. The plant is an annual that can reach 3-5 feet in height with distinctive trumpet-shaped white or purple flowers and spiky seed pods.

All parts of the Datura plant contain toxic levels of atropine and scopolamine, with the seeds being the most concentrated source. However, the leaves, flowers, and stems are also toxic. The concentration of active compounds varies significantly between individual plants, parts of the plant, and growing conditions, making it impossible to predict the dose from any given preparation.

Datura poisoning is a regular occurrence at emergency rooms across the country, particularly among adolescents and young adults who experiment with the plant based on information found online. The results are frequently catastrophic: prolonged delirium lasting days, dangerous self-injury, hospitalization, and occasional death.

The name jimson weed derives from Jamestown weed, referencing an incident in Jamestown, Virginia, in 1676 when British soldiers consumed the plant in a salad and spent eleven days in a state of delirium.

## Why Anticholinergics Are Not Psychedelics

A critical distinction must be drawn between the delirium produced by anticholinergic plants and the altered consciousness produced by classical psychedelics such as LSD, psilocybin, or mescaline.

Classical psychedelics generally produce an expanded state of awareness in which the user retains insight into their altered condition. The user typically knows they have taken a drug and can distinguish between the drug experience and ordinary reality, even while perceptions are dramatically altered. Psychedelics enhance sensory perception, pattern recognition, and emotional awareness.

Anticholinergic delirium produces a genuine break from reality. The user typically has no insight into their altered state and cannot distinguish between hallucinations and real events. The hallucinations are more likely to be frightening and paranoid. The experience is characterized by confusion, disorientation, and an inability to form new memories (anterograde amnesia).

The danger of anticholinergic intoxication is compounded by the complete loss of judgment and risk assessment. Users have walked into traffic, jumped from heights, and inflicted serious self-injury while in anticholinergic delirium, with no awareness of the danger.
