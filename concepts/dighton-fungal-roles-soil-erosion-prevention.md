# Fungal Roles in Soil Erosion Prevention

## Overview

Fungi play critical roles in preventing soil erosion through multiple
mechanisms including physical binding of soil particles, formation of
soil aggregates, creation of water-resistant surface crusts, and
stabilization of plant litter on sloping terrain. These functions
represent important ecosystem services provided by diverse fungal
groups including saprotrophs, mycorrhizal fungi, and lichen-forming
fungi. The erosion-preventing functions of fungi are particularly
important in environments where soils are vulnerable to displacement
by water or wind, including agricultural lands, steep forested slopes,
and arid regions with sparse vegetation cover.

## Mechanisms of Erosion Prevention

### Physical Binding of Soil Particles

Soils are prone to erosion by intense rainfall, particularly on sloping
ground, and by wind displacement of dry soils. In the development of a
soil, the balance of inorganic, organic, and biotic components is of
great importance to physical stability. Where bare mineral soils are
exposed, primary colonizing organisms are essential for stabilization.

Fungal hyphae penetrate between soil mineral particles and act as a web
to physically retain soil particles. Polysaccharide secretions of both
fungi and bacteria aid this process, acting as a glue to bind mineral
particles together. The longevity of fungi, compared with the rapid
turnover of bacterial cells, provides greater permanency to the
soil-stabilizing function. This is particularly important in exposed
conditions where rapid environmental changes occur.

### Cryptogamic Soil Crusts

Lichens and fungi are important constituents of cryptogamic crusts on
nutrient-poor or sandy soils in dry areas (States and Christensen,
2001). The fungal component of these crust communities physically holds
mineral soil particles together while also contributing to [[lichen-ecology-and-soil-formation]] processes. These crust communities are essential for
controlling the nutrient content and availability of soils for invasion
by other plant species.

### Hydrophobic Surface Effects

The hydrophobic nature of some fungal hyphae alters water flow through
soil colonized by cryptogamic crusts. Water tends to flow laterally
across the surface soil in the presence of these crusts rather than
downward as in uncolonized soil surfaces. This lateral flow prevents
the downward movement of soil particles and organic matter, reducing
the risk of erosion of developing soil structure.

The hydrophobicity of the lichen community also retains recently formed
nutrients in upper soil horizons and reduces leaching of essential
plant nutrients. This function represents a trade-off: while reducing
erosion, the hydrophobic surface may also limit water infiltration for
plant use.

## Erosion Prevention in Forest Ecosystems

### Tropical Forest Litter Binding

In wet tropical forests, Lodge and Asbury (1988) demonstrated that
fungal hyphae and cords binding leaf litter together on the forest
floor are important in preventing downslope loss of leaf litter. The
potential loss of organic matter containing nutrients for plant growth
is mainly prevented by the action of basidiomycete fungi that bind
leaf litter together. Species of Collybia, Marasmiellus, Marasmius,
and Mycena are the main fungi involved in forest floor litter trapping.

The effect of litter binding by fungi increases with increasing ground
slope. Lodge and Asbury (1988) concluded from field manipulation
experiments that loss of litter was reduced by 35% from shallow slopes
(less than 75% angle) and 45% at greater slopes (75 to 90%). The
reduction in leaf litter loss and subsequent incorporation of organic
matter into mineral soil is thought to prevent soil erosion during high
rainfall periods.

### Salt Marsh and Coastal Stabilization

In coastal ecosystems, fungal hyphae contribute to binding particles
together and reducing the possibility of erosion caused by tidal
action. The rhizomorph networks of fungi in these environments help
stabilize sediments and maintain the integrity of coastal landforms
against wave and tidal forces.

## Soil Aggregate Formation and Stability

### Microaggregate and Macroaggregate Development

The role of fungi and bacteria in the formation and stability of soil
aggregates is fundamental to both soil fertility and carbon storage
within soils. Microaggregates (less than 250 micrometers diameter)
combine to form macroaggregates (greater than 250 micrometers). An
aggregation of aggregates is referred to as a soil crumb, the size
and structure of which determines soil texture and porosity.

Tisdall and Oades (1982) described how soil aggregate development
spans five orders of magnitude, from cementation of clay particles
(0.2 micrometers) through interactions with microbial debris and
living bacteria and fungi (20 micrometer scale) to soil crumbs of
approximately 2000 micrometers. Fungi play a major role in binding
larger aggregates together through their hyphal networks, while
bacteria are more important at smaller scales.

### Water-Stable Aggregates

In addition to aggregate formation, soil organisms and their products
are important in aggregate stability, which is the resistance of
aggregates to being physically disrupted by water or mechanical forces.
Fungal hyphae enmesh soil particles, providing tensile strength to
aggregates, while fungal polysaccharides and glomalin-related proteins
contribute to cementation. The combined effects of physical binding
and chemical cementation create [[fungal-contributions-soil-structure]] that resist erosion.

## Implications for Land Management

Understanding fungal contributions to erosion prevention has
significant implications for land management practices. Activities that
disrupt fungal networks, such as intensive tillage, vegetation removal,
or pollution, can compromise soil stability and increase erosion risk.
Conversely, management practices that support fungal communities,
including reduced tillage, maintenance of vegetation cover, and
minimization of chemical inputs, can enhance the natural
erosion-preventing functions of fungi.
