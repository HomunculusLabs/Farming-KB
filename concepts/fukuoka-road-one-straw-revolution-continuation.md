# Fukuoka's One-Straw Revolution Continuation
[[fukuoka-road-back-to-nature-overview]] represents [[natural-farming-fukuoka]]'s mature continuation of the philosophy first articulated in [[fukuoka-the-one-straw-revolution-final-chapter]]. Written nearly a decade later, after his world travels, the book deepens, extends, and in some ways corrects the earlier work. It shows how Fukuoka's thinking evolved from a Japanese farming method into a global ecological and spiritual philosophy.
## From Farm to World
The One-Straw Revolution (1975) was primarily about farming in Japan — about growing rice and barley without plowing, chemicals, or compost. The Road Back to Nature expands this radically. Fukuoka traveled to America, Europe, and Africa, applying his methods to deserts, orchards, and refugee camps. The book's scope encompasses not just agriculture but diet, education, spirituality, war and peace, biotechnology, and the future of civilization.
During his travels, Fukuoka visited the United States, where he observed
American agriculture's dependence on chemical inputs and mechanization with
alarm. He spent time at the Zen Center near San Francisco, where students
were already practicing his methods after reading The One-Straw Revolution.
In Europe, he traveled through Germany, Switzerland, and other countries,
meeting with farmers and environmentalists who were drawn to his philosophy.
His visit to Africa was particularly significant: he saw firsthand how
Western agricultural "aid" programs were destroying traditional farming
systems that had sustained communities for generations. He attempted to
demonstrate natural farming methods in desertified areas of Somalia and
other regions, using seed balls and natural vegetation recovery techniques.
These experiences convinced him that the principles of natural farming were
universal and not limited to the temperate rice-growing regions of Japan.
The book documents both the successes and frustrations of these
international efforts, providing a candid assessment of what worked and
what did not in different cultural and ecological contexts.
## The Reception Worldwide
Fukuoka documents how The One-Straw Revolution was received around the world. In America, "My book had been translated and read by many people." At the Zen Center near San Francisco, the methods described in The One-Straw Revolution were being practiced. In Vienna, "the interpreter had read The One-Straw Revolution." People would "come to my farm having read only The One-Straw Revolution." Fukuoka acknowledges that "in that book I did not reveal everything."
## A Deeper Understanding of God and Nature
While The One-Straw Revolution touched on spiritual themes, The Road Back to Nature makes them central. Fukuoka reveals for the first time that "I received a revelation of God one moment fifty years ago." He describes being "so taken aback by the vision I saw that I failed to advance along the road I should have." The earlier book's farming philosophy is now explicitly grounded in a theology of nature: "The soul of nature is also the will of God who dwells in nature."
## The Green Philosophy
Fukuoka coins the term "green philosophy" to describe the mature expression of his thought. "Although there are still many different forms and names for it, it is clear that my 'green philosophy' serves as a foundation." He insists that this "must be more than a narrow technique; nor should it be adopted merely as a passing fad. The thinking and outlook of natural philosophy must be at the core of any successful effort to establish a form of farming that would be a truly permanent Great Way of agriculture."
## From Organic to Natural Farming
The Road Back to Nature draws a sharper distinction between organic farming and natural farming than the earlier book. Fukuoka notes that "advances from organic gardening to natural farming are likely to continue to be made by people with an affinity toward natural methods," but warns that "most people do not yet understand the distinction between organic gardening and natural farming. Both scientific agriculture and organic farming are basically scientific in their approach."
## The Critique of Science Matured
Where The One-Straw Revolution questioned agricultural science, The Road Back to Nature mounts a comprehensive critique of reductionism, Darwinism, biotechnology, and the entire scientific worldview. Fukuoka now writes about "the wild rampage of science" and warns that "scientists today are under the illusion that they can replace God as the Lord of all creation." This is the earlier book's skepticism carried to its logical conclusion.
Fukuoka's mature critique extends beyond agriculture to encompass medicine,
education, and technology. He argues that modern medicine treats symptoms
rather than causes, much as modern agriculture addresses pests with
chemicals rather than restoring ecological balance. He views the
fragmentation of knowledge into specialized disciplines as itself a symptom
of humanity's disconnection from nature: "When people try to understand
nature by dissecting it into its component parts, they destroy the very
thing they are trying to understand." His opposition to genetic engineering
is rooted not in technical concerns but in philosophical ones: he believes
that the fundamental arrogance of genetic modification lies in the
assumption that humans can improve upon nature by understanding and
manipulating its individual components, when the intelligence of natural
systems operates at a level that cannot be grasped through analysis. This
critique anticipates later arguments from environmental philosophers and
deep ecologists, though Fukuoka arrived at his position through farming
practice rather than academic philosophy.
## A Letter for Peace
In a remarkable passage, Fukuoka describes writing a letter to Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1941, and now preparing to write a similar letter to world leaders. He asks: "Is there someone today with the courage to be the first to press the nuclear button?" This political dimension — the connection between farming methods and world peace — is largely absent from The One-Straw Revolution.
## Not a Return to the Past
Fukuoka explicitly rejects nostalgia: "What I am talking about here definitely is not a return to the primitive." He proposes instead "a return to the present" — "living in the nature of today" with "neither attachment to the past nor expectations for the future." This philosophical maturation distinguishes the later work from the apparent primitivism some readers found in the earlier book.

## The Book as Confession
The Road Back to Nature differs fundamentally from The One-Straw Revolution in its personal tone. Fukuoka writes: "The reason I lay bare here everything about my past is that I dearly wish to atone for my errors and make the fullest possible use of the few remaining years I have left on this earth." He fears that "natural farming may vanish as just one transient farming technique" unless its spiritual foundation is made explicit. This confessional quality — the revelation of his youthful vision of God, his decades of anguish, his sense of failure — gives the book a depth and urgency absent from the earlier work.
## Reception: Japanese Indifference vs. Western Enthusiasm
Fukuoka documents a striking disparity. "People have been getting excited over natural foods and organic farming, and the 'back to nature' movement seems to have really caught on in Japan. But compared to thirty-five years ago, it appears to me as if people are simply moving farther away from nature." In contrast, "when Westerners talk of returning to nature, they tend to go right ahead and do so." Foreigners "return home content. And when they go back, they put natural farming into practice."
He attributes this to Japanese skepticism and social pressure: "One is subject to criticism and obstruction from all sides." In Japan, "even a small drop in yields draws laughter from the surrounding farmers; and if you don't spray your fields you are upbraided for being a nuisance to the farmers around you."
## The Green Philosophy as Foundation
Fukuoka articulated what he calls his "green philosophy" as the unifying foundation beneath all natural farming efforts. "Although there are still many different forms and names for it, it is clear that my 'green philosophy' serves as a foundation." He insists this "must be more than a narrow technique; nor should it be adopted merely as a passing fad. The thinking and outlook of natural philosophy must be at the core of any successful effort to establish a form of farming that would be a truly permanent Great Way of agriculture."
## The Neighboring Farmer's Silence
Fukuoka describes the isolation of being a natural farmer in a conventional community. "No one dares change his way of farming and follow my example. More precisely, no one has even the slightest idea of what I've been doing and how to do it." His neighbors see his fields as messy and weedy: "To him it looks like a disorder at all." Despite bad or irregular harvests, he kept experimenting: "I have yet to provide results good enough to inspire the people of my community." The fact that "not a single villager has visited my hilltop orchard and seen for himself the radishes growing there" speaks to the profound resistance to change.
## The Young Religious Leader Who Couldn't Abandon
Fukuoka recounted a telling encounter at his farm. "On a spring day a couple of years ago, a young man from a religious organization called Tendai Kyodan arrived at my farm. 'I felt that something was wrong,' he explained. 'I was put on a pedestal as leader of the sect, but I was nothing more than a figurehead. So I withdrew and came here.'" Fukuoka challenged him: "If you're saying 'I've renounced everything,' then do so. Isn't it enough to be merely a farmer?" When the man admitted he had never done any farming, Fukuoka responded: "I've been practicing this for fifty years, but I'm still not prepared to teach it. And I don't even have a single follower."
This exchange encapsulates Fukuoka's insistence that one cannot learn natural farming by accumulating knowledge about it — only by abandoning knowledge entirely and immersing oneself in the practice.
## From Organic to Natural: The Crucial Distinction
At an international symposium on the West Coast, Fukuoka stressed the difference between organic farming and natural farming. "Both scientific agriculture and organic farming are basically scientific in their approach. The boundary between the two is not clear." He argued that moving from organic to natural farming "must be more than a narrow technique; nor should it be adopted merely as a passing fad." The thinking and outlook of natural philosophy must be at the core of any truly permanent agriculture.
## The Struggle Within
Fukuoka's writings in this book reveal a man tormented by the gap between his vision and reality. "Tossed about by the contradictions within and without, I was a disagreeable presence even to those in my family." He feared that natural farming "may vanish as just one transient farming technique" — a trivialization of the profound spiritual truth it embodied. This anxiety drove him to write with an urgency and personal honesty not found in The One-Straw Revolution, producing a document that reads as much as spiritual confession as agricultural treatise.
## Philosophical Influences
Fukuoka's thinking drew on multiple philosophical streams, though he rarely
cited specific influences. His rejection of intellectual knowledge in favor
of direct experience echoes aspects of Zen Buddhism, particularly the
emphasis on "beginner's mind" (shoshin) and the limitations of conceptual
thinking. His reverence for nature as the ultimate teacher aligns with
Daoist principles of wu wei (non-action or effortless action), though he
insisted his approach came from farming practice rather than philosophical
study. The spiritual revelation he describes receiving fifty years before
writing The Road Back to Nature suggests a mystical experience comparable
to those described in various religious traditions, yet he framed it in
terms of farming and nature rather than religious language. His critique of
reductionist science anticipates arguments later developed by thinkers like
Fritjof Capra and Arne Naess, though Fukuoka's version is grounded in
concrete agricultural experience rather than theoretical physics or
ecology. His distinction between "discriminating knowledge" (bunkenchi)
and "non-discriminating knowledge" (mubunkenchi) provides the
epistemological foundation for his entire philosophy: the former divides
the world into subject and object, self and nature, while the latter
apprehends the unity underlying all apparent distinctions.
## Global Impact and Legacy
The Road Back to Nature helped establish Fukuoka as a leading figure in the
global sustainable agriculture movement. His influence extended beyond farming
into the broader environmental movement, permaculture (Bill Mollison cited
Fukuoka as a key inspiration), and the slow food movement. Natural farming
communities inspired by his work were established in Japan, the United States,
Europe, India, Thailand, and other countries. The concept of "do-nothing
farming" resonated with the growing critique of industrial agriculture in the
1980s and 1990s, and his seed ball technique (encasing seeds in clay
