# Conk Fungi — Iceman Polypore and Agarikon

The genera *Fomes*, *Fomitopsis*, and *Laricifomes* encompass some of the most iconic and historically
significant polypore mushrooms in the world. These woody, perennial conks include the iceman polypore
(*[[fomes-fomentarius]]*) — famously found among the possessions of Ötzi, a nomadic traveler who lived
around 3300 BCE and was discovered frozen in the Alps in 1991 — and the extremely rare agarikon
(*[[fomitopsis-officinalis-entity]]*, formerly *[[laricifomes-officinalis]]*). These mushrooms are primarily valued
for their [[amanita-muscaria-medicinal-properties-heart-anxiety-dreams]] rather than as food, and several species are endangered due to slow
growth rates and overharvesting.

## General Description and Ecology

These are pored mushrooms that lack stems, attaching to the sides of downed or standing trees and
logs. The fruitbodies are grayish and banded, extremely tough to hard in texture, and hoof-shaped to
beehive-like in appearance. They persist year-round and sometimes grow in layers, with new layers
added annually. A few known wild specimens have been growing for over fifty years. The spore prints
vary from white to creamy yellow.

The discovery of *Fomes fomentarius* in Ötzi's possession has profound implications regarding the
historical use of fungi for food, fuel, and medicine. The mushroom was used for making textiles,
fabric, fire-starting material, and medicinals. Taxonomically, *Laricifomes* has recently been merged
into *Fomitopsis* based on molecular data, reflecting ongoing revisions as phylogenetic studies
clarify relationships among fungal groups.

## Mycelium and Spawn

The mycelium of *Fomes* is white, cottony, delicate, and slow-growing, eventually oxidizing to dark
chocolate brown as it ages. *Fomitopsis* mycelium is also white but somewhat powdery, reminiscent of
[[chicken-of-the-woods]] (*Laetiporus*) mycelium. Both types have a sweet, pleasant smell. Commercial
spawn is rare but when available is typically in the form of wooden dowels for outdoor cultivation.

## Species Profiles

### Iceman Polypore (Fomes fomentarius)

The iceman polypore is one of the most recognizable mushrooms in the world. It fruits primarily on
dying birch trees at higher elevations but occupies a wide range of hardwoods in North America. New
fruitbodies emerge in late spring through [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]] at higher elevations. These are
perennial conks that add growth layers every season and can endure "stop and go" growing conditions
such as alternating rain and drought periods.

The tissue is extremely tough, with a hard outer rind, making tissue samples difficult to excise for
culturing. The best approach is to pop a conk off the tree, harvest the attachment point where wood
is exposed, surface-sterilize with diluted alcohol or 3% [[cervantes-hydrogen-peroxide-sterilization]], and transfer colonized
tissue bits to agar media.

### Agarikon (Fomitopsis officinalis)

Agarikon is an extremely rare and endangered species that hides out in old-growth forests,
particularly those of [[douglas-fir]] and larch. Specimens should never be removed from the wild. Instead,
small boring tools should be used to collect plugs of tissue, which can be dissected and cultured,
leaving the beautiful conks in place. This species is increasingly sought after for its medicinal
properties, contributing to its rarity.

## Cultivation

### Outdoor Cultivation

These species prefer standing dead trees (snags) or large-diameter downed trees. Spawn types include
sawdust, plug, or cardboard. Inoculation to fruiting time ranges from one to three years. After a
year of colonization, paddle-type boring bits can be used to drill 2-inch-deep holes around the wood,
where delicate primordia will slowly form over a few days before emerging.

### Indoor Cultivation

Indoor cultivation is not recommended for any of these species.

## Harvesting and Processing

Fomes fruitbodies are ready when conks slow growth and the pore layers on the underside lose their
distinctive white appearance. Gently tap conks upward with a soft rubber mallet — they will pop loose
more easily than prying. For grain-based harvesting, cultivate on sterilized grain until the mycelium
has gelled the grain together, then either cook it or powder it for supplements and extracts.

## Storage and Preservation

Fresh mushrooms keep for one month at 38–42°F (3–6°C), though molds can infect the tube layer under
high humidity. Dried conks can be used for powders, tinctures, and fire starter. Dehydration is the
preferred preservation method since these fungi do not contain much water.

## Medicinal Properties

These mushrooms are rich in compounds similar to turkey tail (*[[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]]*), including
polysaccharide-K (PSK), a protein-bound polysaccharide used in Chinese medicine for treating cancer
patients during chemotherapy. Documented benefits include:

- **Immune modulation:** Boosting and modulating immune system function
- **Metabolic regulation:** Regulating blood pressure and sugar levels
- **Cardiovascular support:** Lowering cholesterol, providing cardiovascular and digestive support
- **Antimicrobial activity:** Antiviral and antibacterial properties
- **Anti-inflammatory compounds:** Contributing to therapeutic effects
- **Anticancer activity:** Suppression of many cancer cell lines

## Mycoremediation

The [[amanita-muscaria-antibiotic-properties-nanobacteria-fungal-medicine]] of these fungi make them strong candidates for mycofiltration of bacteria
in contaminated soil and water. Colonized biomasses of myceliated wood chips can be deployed in
trenches downslope of livestock operations to absorb and filter surface runoff, providing biological
water treatment.

## Conservation Concerns

These conk fungi are extremely slow-growing and increasingly rare in the wild. Cultivating wild
patches through responsible inoculation of snags and downed wood helps maintain populations of these
valuable medicinal species. Creating sustainable supplies through cultivation reduces pressure on wild
populations, particularly for agarikon, which faces extinction pressures from habitat loss and
overharvesting.
