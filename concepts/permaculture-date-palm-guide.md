# Date Palm Guide

## Botanical Details

**Scientific name:** Phoenix dactylifera
**Family:** ARECACEAE

Native to the tropical and sub tropical deserts of the Middle East, this palm has been cultivated for about 6,000 years. It is now not known in the wild. It is an erect palm growing to 36 m.

## Nutritional Composition

Per 100 grams of dried date (edible portion): Water - 22.5%, Energy - 274 calories, Carbohydrate - 72.9 g, Fat - 0.5 g, Protein - 2.2 g, Ash - 1.9 g, Calcium - 59 mg, Potassium - 648 mg, Sodium - 1 mg, Magnesium - 58 mg, Iron - 3.0 mg, Phosphorous - 63 mg, Chlorine - 390 mg, Sulphur - 120 mg, Iodine - 0.001 mg, Vitamin A - 50 I.U., Thiamin - 0.09 mg, Riboflavin - 0.10 mg, Niacin - 2.2 mg, Ascorbic acid - 2 mg. Digestion time: 3.5 hours.

## Climate Requirements

The date palm requires full sun and warm climates where the temperature rarely falls below -6 degrees C. It is hardy to 30 degrees latitude, but requires a sheltered site. Rainfall is typically 250-800 mm.

The Arabs claim that it must have its feet in the water and its head in the fire of heaven. It usually grows on saline soils near brackish water. For date production, a rain free fruiting period is needed.

## Soil Requirements

Planting densities range from 100-500 per hectare depending on soil type and water availability. The plant does well in sand, loam and clay provided that drainage and aeration are good. It is tolerant of very alkaline soils and fairly high salt levels although the latter can affect growth and fruit production.

## Food Uses

The fruit is borne on large clusters. Dates are eaten out of hand fresh and dried. They are used in cakes, biscuits, breads, pastes, syrups, jellies, ice cream and alcohol. In the Sahara desert they are fed to camels, horses and dogs. The terminal bud is eaten but this kills the palm. The seeds are dried and ground finely and mixed with flour to make bread. The seed is also soaked in water and fed to livestock and is a good chicken forage. Palms can be tapped (no more than two or three times) for sugar and wine production.

## Other Uses

- The seed is burned to make charcoal for silver smiths
- The leaves are used to make thatch huts, mats, baskets, screens, packing crates and fans
- The stripped fruit clusters are used as brooms
- The trunks are used for posts and rafters in most constructions
- All left over wood is used as firewood

## Medicinal Uses

The fruit is used as a detersive and astringent for intestinal troubles. An infusion is used to treat sore throat, colds, bronchial catarrh. It is taken to relieve cystitis, gonorrhoea, oedema, liver and abdominal troubles. It is mixed with beer to counter alcoholic intoxication. The gum which exudes from wounds in the trunk is used to treat genito-urinary ailments and diarrhoea. It is demulcent and diuretic. The roots are used for toothache.

## Propagation

Although date palms grow readily from seed, fruiting can take ten years and the fruit quality is variable. Also half of the raised seedlings are likely to be male. Where suckers of known plants are available, they are usually preferred. Suckers weigh about 45 kg when they are transplanted. Where suckers are not available, large numbers of seedlings should be planted and suckers propagated from the best specimens. Males will be required for pollination.

## Related Phoenix Species

**Phoenix sylvestris** (wild date palm, silver date palm) -- India: tropical rainforest, to 1500 m. Wide range of soils and sites. Withstands light frosts. Edible fruit for wildlife forage; sap for wine, sugar (40 kg sugar/tree/yr); leaves for weaving. Potential fuel or sugar crop for cooler areas of Australia.

**Phoenix canariensis** (Canary Island date palm) -- An ornamental widely planted in Mediterranean and subtropical areas. Produces small but edible dates.

## Oil-Producing Palms

**Elaeis guineensis (oil palm)** — Native to West African tropical rainforest, semi-hardy to 16 degrees latitude. Produces edible oil at 5 tonnes per hectare per year, easily extracted by boiling and pressing. Yields begin at 2.5 years and continue for 50+ years, though harvest becomes difficult as palms exceed 20 m height. Smallholders intercrop with annual crops; useful [companion planting strategy.

**Orbignya speciosa (babacu palm)** — A staple palm of South American tropical rainforest uplands, producing 40 kg of edible oil per tree per year. Kernels contain 72% oil, similar in quality to coconut oil, suitable for margarine, soap, fatty acids, and detergents. Produces up to 1,500 nuts per hectare. Seedcake remaining after oil extraction provides 75% protein animal feed. Thick shells make excellent fuel. Grows readily from fresh seed to 20 m.

**Borassus flabellifer (palmyra palm)** — Native to South Asian dry forests and savannas, widely planted in India. Sap yields sugar, wine, and up to 20 litres per tree per day. Produces 40,000 litres of nectar per acre. Spiny trunk, toothed midrib, and prickly foliage make an effective fence barrier. Often grown with legume forage species. Deep rooting in early stages requires site planting.

**Jessenia bataua (seje palm)** — Northern South American rainforest upland species. Edible oil from fruit; fruit made into beverages; leaves used for weaving. Can be grown alongside Astrocaryum species. Semi-wild but with high potential for sustainable oil production.

## Sugar and Wine Palms

**Arenga pinnata (sugar palm)** — Native to South and Southeast Asian rainforest. Yields sap for sugar and wine (36 litres per tree per day), starch from trunk (75 kg per tree), and fibre from leaf sheath. Tall, spiny trunk provides effective fencing. Widely cultivated in India and Southeast Asia. Monocarpic — flowers once then dies.

**Mauritia flexuosa (moriche, buriti palm)** — South American rainforest swamp species, growing to 25 m. Produces edible oil, fruit, heart-of-palm, wine from fruit and sap. Fruit is high in vitamins A and C with clusters reaching 45 kg. Starch yield of 60 kg per tree. Leaf fibre used for rope, sacking, nets, and hammocks. Trunk provides light, tough wood similar to balsa. An indicator species for tropical rainforest swamps.

**Cocos nucifera (coconut palm)** — While not covered in detail in this source, the coconut is the benchmark multipurpose palm and the standard against which other oil palms are measured. See [coconut palm polyculture for detailed treatment.

## Date Palms (Phoenix)

**Phoenix dactylifera (date palm)** — Cultivated for approximately 6,000 years in Middle Eastern deserts. Grows to 36 m, hardy to 30 degrees latitude with shelter. Requires full sun, warm climates, and rain-free fruiting period. Tolerates very [alkaline soils and saline conditions near brackish water.

Dates are eaten fresh and dried, used in cakes, biscuits, breads, pastes, syrups, jellies, ice cream, and alcohol. Leaves provide thatch, mats, baskets, screens, and packing material. Trunks serve as posts and rafters. Traditionally propagated from suckers (45 kg at transplant) rather than seed, since seed-grown palms take ten years to fruit and half are male.

**Phoenix canariensis (Canary Islands date)** — Hardy to 45 degrees latitude, tolerant of sea winds. Potential source of sugar for cooler regions where date palms cannot produce fruit.

## Heart-of-Palm and Edible Species

**Bactris gasipaes (pejibaye, peach palm)** — Native to Central and South American rainforest, this multi-stemmed palm produces fruits high in protein and carbohydrate at 3.4 tonnes dry fruit per hectare per year. One plant yields up to 12 kg of fruit. Because it is multi-stemmed, heart-of-palm can be harvested from thinnings without sacrificing the whole plant. Thorns provide effective fencing. Begins bearing at 6–8 years with an economic life of 75+ years. Widely cultivated as forage for pigs and poultry.

**Euterpe oleracea (acai palm)** — South American rainforest species from flood-prone sites. Produces heart-of-palm (1 kg per tree) and fruit used for beverages (acai). Limited cultivation with heart-of-palm exported. Attractive ornamental; suckering growth habit allows sustainable heart-of-palm harvest from thinnings.

**Metroxylon sagu (sago palm)** — Southeast Asian rainforest swamp species. Produces sago starch from trunk (300 kg per tree) and leaves for thatch. Cultivated in managed stands with starch exported. Suckering and terminal flowering with a lifespan of 8–15 years, usually felled before death.

## Cold-Hardy and High-Altitude Palms

**Parajubaea cocoides (Quito palm)** — Native to the Andes at altitudes above 3,000 m. Tolerates temperatures well below 0°C and high temperatures. Extremely fast growing for a palm, producing first crop of coconut-like nuts within 3–4 years. Puts down very deep roots and survives months without water once established. Bears clusters of 30–50 edible nuts 4–5 cm in diameter.

**Butia capitata (jelly palm)** — Very hardy, yielding to 40 degrees latitude in coastal areas. Withstands temperatures to -5°C in rainfall between 250–800 mm. Fruit is edible with edible nut and oil. Potential forage species for coastal areas.

## Rattan and Fibre Palms

Multiple genera provide rattan for furniture and basket-making: Ancistrophyllum (West Africa), Calamus (largest rattan genus, widespread Indo-Pacific), Daemonorops, Korthalsia (31 species), Myrialepis, Plectocomia, Plectocomiopsis, and Retispatha. These climbing palms are harvested from semi-wild stands and represent significant economic resources in tropical regions.

**Raphia ruffia (raffia palm)** — Madagascar native, semi-hardy, source of commercial raffia fibre. Monocarpic with very large leaves.

**Lodoicea [[permaculture-design]]mer)** — Praslin Island, Seychelles. Rare and slow growing, producing the world's largest seed (double coconut) with very large leaves used for roofing.
