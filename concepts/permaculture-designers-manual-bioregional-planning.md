# Permaculture Designers Manual - Bioregional Planning
updated: 2026-04-18

Bioregional planning is addressed primarily in Chapter 14, Section 14.6 (extended families and bioregional networks), Section 14.8 (developmental property trusts), Section 14.13 (ethical investment), and implicitly throughout the manual as the scale at which [[permaculture-design]] connects to political and economic systems.

## What is a Bioregion?

The manual references Kirkpatrick Sale's definition from The Ecologist (1984) and George Tukel's watershed-based model (1982). Mollison acknowledges that many tribal and urban bioregions may not fit a pure watershed model, but the concept remains useful as a planning framework.

A bioregion is:
- A geographic area defined by natural boundaries (watersheds, mountain ranges, coastlines) rather than political borders
- A scale at which local resources can be assessed and managed
- A basis for cultural identity and economic self-reliance
- A planning unit that respects ecological processes rather than administrative convenience

## Bioregional Network Structure

### Scale of Organisation

Mollison proposes a nested hierarchy of social organisation:
- Family: 3-5 close friends, a support group of 30+ acquaintances
- Village: 30-200 houses supporting shared services and facilities
- Tribe: 200-500 households confederating for manufacturing and trade
- Nation: 20-40 tribes allied for mutual benefit
- Bioregion: overlapping with watershed or ecoregion boundaries

### Extended Family Networks

The "extended family" concept (Section 14.6) is not biological family but affinity groups of shared ethics:
- Groups of 1,000 or fewer members with common interests and ethical base
- Cooperative interdependence replaces competition
- Meta-networking (tribe to tribe) enables global cooperation
- Funds managed: common enterprise fund, annual membership fund, children's permanent fund, special venture funds
- Members maintain a registrar and news service, co-invest in property, rationalise resources, develop social contacts

### Open Corridors

"We need not only fixed villages and bioregions, but open corridors to other regions, other people, and across nations." The web of multi-racial, multi-cultural families "obliterates defended territories and suits peaceful lifestyles."

## Bioregional Planning Principles

### Resource Assessment

Every bioregion should assess:
- Water resources: catchments, aquifers, surface water, water quality
- Soil resources: types, fertility status, erosion risk, capability
- Forest resources: existing forests, reforestation potential, fuel wood supply
- Agricultural land: arable land, pasture, suitable crops and rotations
- Energy resources: solar, wind, water power, biomass potential
- Wildlife habitat: corridors, reserves, endangered species
- Mineral resources: local building materials

### Mixed Ecologies

"Any village which has access to or can develop forest, aquatic, marine, agricultural, and market areas has many more options open to it than a village marooned in a simple ecology." Bioregional planning should ensure access to diverse productive systems.

### Water as Primary Planning Element

The manual consistently places water first in the planning hierarchy. Every village must be able to "catch, store, reticulate, and clean up its own water supply." At bioregional scale, this means integrated [[watershed-management]] where upstream activities protect downstream water quality.

### Transport and Trade

Bioregional planning includes road, rail, boat, and air access for trade. While computer and telecommunications enable data networking, "real-object trade needs transport." Bioregional alliances of 200-500 households can make "a very viable manufacturing or trading alliance."

## Developmental Property Trusts at Bioregional Scale

Section 14.8 outlines how property trusts operate at bioregional scale:
- Purchase of threatened wildlife and forest habitat
- Purchase and rehabilitation of eroded, salted, or deforested land
- Development of energy-efficient, sustainable villages
- Bioregional clean energy systems or clean transport methods
- Bioregional development as purchase of community resources

Funds can be used for specific project areas ranging from individual property rehabilitation to whole-region planning.

## Ethical Investment at Bioregional Scale

Section 14.13 describes how bioregional funds direct local money to local needs:
- Short-term loans: insulation, water tanks, irrigation, dam building
- Medium-term: animal systems, aquaculture, nurseries, tourism
- Long-term: town reconstruction, fuel crops, farm development, orchards
- Permanent: forestry, wildlife reserves, remnant forest protection

The manual recommends proportional investment: 10% risk ventures, 10-20% community development, 10-20% clean power, 20-40% social investment, 10% public interest.

## Bioregional Governance

### Local Trusts Over Central Government

"About 18-20% of businesses may be non-profit trusts owned or operated by charitable trusts." Mollison proposes that local trusts can "completely replace the bureaucracy of public services." The Dutch model (stichtings) is cited where trusts run public operations and government becomes "simply a way of conveying tax capital back to the regions via local trusts."

### Confederation of Small Groups

"It is far better to set up many such local trusts than to risk the power-centred inefficiencies of a monstrous hierarchical system." Every dissenter or group should form independent trusts in a cooperative network.

# Permaculture Designers Manual - Designer Environment
updated: 2026-04-18

The concept of the "designer environment" synthesises the ethical, philosophical, and practical foundations of permaculture from Chapter 14, "The Strategies of an Alternative Nation." Mollison argues that the techniques of earth restoration described in Chapters 1-13 "all come to naught if we, as a people, continue to invest in arms and destruction, to permit land abuse, and to fail to tackle the social and political impediments to reclaiming desertified and abused lands."

## Ethical Basis (Section 14.2)

A people without an agreed-upon common basis to their actions is neither a community nor a nation. A people with a common ethic is a nation wherever they live. The ethical basis includes:

Sustainable societies emphasise the duties and responsibilities of people to nature equal to those of people to people. Any code relates equally to other lifeforms and elements of landscape. To conduct oneself only in terms of response to other people gives potential to evade responsibility for damage to the total resource base.

The "seventh generation" concept from the Iroquois nation frames decisions in terms of their benefit or disadvantage to descendants about 100 years ahead. This explains why tribally managed lands are found rich in natural life resources, while inherited lands have been largely ruined.

Right livelihood: people need to adopt an ethic of right livelihood, for if labour and skills are bent to destructive work, we are the destroyers. We lay waste to our lives in proportion to the way the systems we support lay waste to earth. We cannot profess or teach one ethic and live another without damage to ourselves and common resources.

## The Right Not to Be in Debt (Section 14.4)

Some of the most charming and climatically appropriate houses on earth are built without bank loans, architects, metals, concrete, or contractors. While Chile accumulated $12 billion in foreign debt in 1985, poor people acting without loans built at least $11 billion in housing in slum areas by local cooperation without incurring any foreign debt.

Stone, mud, bamboo, round timbers, rope, thatch, and even baked brick and tiles are the age-old durable building materials of mankind, all locally producible if energy from community forests and people is provided.

The real cause of lack of shelter (as with food) in any country is not finance but restrictive practices by a regulatory bureaucracy. State or private ownership of forests, small mines, and lands denies basic biological and earth resources to the people who work to produce or mine them.

## Against Political Complacency

Mollison describes all western political systems as "competing belief elites" that function in essentially short-term ways. They seek to impose policy control over as wide an area as possible, are power-centred (not life-centred), and the continuing programmes necessary to reverse forest loss and soil decline are usually sacrificed for the short-term policies of an elite maintaining power.

"The argument for simplicity is never a political argument... when people practice it in their lives... they don't even need any politics."
