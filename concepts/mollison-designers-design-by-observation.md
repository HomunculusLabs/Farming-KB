# Design by Observation

Design by observation is the method of expanding on direct observations of a
site to develop design strategies. Unlike the analytic method, this approach
starts on and around the site itself.

## Why Observation Matters

Short practice at refining field observation will convince you that no
complex of map overlays, library, computer data, or remote analysis will
ever supplant field observation for dependability and relevance. Observation
is not easily directed and is therefore regarded as largely unscientific and
individualistic, yet it is from the observation of processes and events that
we can devise strategies of "least change."

No static method can reveal processes or dynamic interactions. Process and
events, as we encounter them on a real site, are never revealed just by maps
or other fixed data.

## Tools for Observation

A camera and a notebook are great aids to observation, allowing re-examination
of information if necessary. A good memory for events helps. Video recorders
are very useful to review processes. These tools complement but never replace
direct sensory experience of the site.

## Four Approaches to Observation

As we approach the problem, we can adopt any or all of these attitudes:

1. **A Child-like and Non-selective Approach:** "I wonder why..." may
   preface our actual observation. This open-minded stance often reveals
   what directed inquiry misses.

2. **A Thematic Approach:** We try to observe a theme such as water,
   potential energy sources, or the conditions for natural regeneration.

3. **An Instrumental Approach:** We measure, perhaps using equipment, a
   factor such as temperature gradients, wind, or reflection from trees.

4. **An Experiential Approach:** Using all our senses as our instruments,
   trying to be fully conscious both of specific details, sensations, and
   the total ambience of the site.

## The Five-Stage Observation Process

### Stage 1: Value-Free Note-Taking

Make non-interpretative notes about what is seen, measured, or experienced.
For example, "moles have thrown up earth mounds on the field." Make no
guesses or judgements at this stage. This takes discipline but yields
primary data.

### Stage 2: Speculation

Select observations of interest and list SPECULATIONS as to possible
meanings. For the mole example: that molehills are only conspicuous on
fields; that they occur only on fields; that fields are particularly
attractive to moles. Many speculations can arise from one observation.

### Stage 3: Confirm or Deny Speculations

Use several strategies to examine speculations:

- Library research on the subject and allied species
- Asking others about field behaviour and local knowledge
- Devising more observations on one particular theme to test ideas
- Recalling all known information about the species in other areas

### Stage 4: Pattern Examination

Examine all evidence now to hand. Have we evolved any patterns, any mode
of operating? What other creatures burrow in fields? Are they predators,
prey, or allies? What connections exist between the observed phenomenon and
the broader system?

### Stage 5: Application to Design

How can we find a use for all this information? What design strategies does
any of it suggest? For example, we may now see the mole as a fine soil
aerator and seed-bed provider to be encouraged, or the opposite. We may
discover places where moles are beneficial and places where they should be
excluded.

## Observation Leads to Innovation

No analytic method can involve one in the world as much as observation, but
observation needs to be practised and developed. As an observer, you are
very likely to stumble on unique and effective strategies, and thus become
an innovator. The uses and strategies derived from observation are the basic
tools of aware, long-term residents.

## The End Result of Systematic Observation

The end result is twofold:

1. Strategies for application in design, many of them transferable to other
   locations
2. A personal and involved understanding of the totality of the
   interdependence of natural systems

Here, we have used nature itself as our teacher. That is the greatest value
of observation, and it will in time supply answers to all our questions.

## Observation vs Analysis

Observation and its methods need to be practised and developed, whereas
analysis needs no prior practice and requires less field research or
first-hand knowledge. However, observation reveals dynamic processes,
unique site conditions, and innovative strategies that no amount of
desk-based analysis can discover.
