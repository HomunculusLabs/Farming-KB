## Mycorrhizal Fungi in Ecological Restoration of Contaminated Sites

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] offer significant potential for the restoration and
reclamation of contaminated lands. Their ability to protect host plants from
toxic metals, enhance nutrient uptake in nutrient-poor substrates, and degrade
organic pollutants makes them valuable partners in ecological restoration
projects ranging from mine tailings to landfill sites.

The review literature by Senior et al. (1993) and Donnelley and Fletcher (1994)
established the conceptual framework for applying mycorrhizal fungi in
restoration ecology. The ability of the mycorrhizal fungal partner to protect
its host plant from toxic levels of heavy metal is one of the key advantages of
this symbiotic association.

## Mycorrhizal Benefits in Mine Reclamation

The inoculation of plants with arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi significantly
improves plant establishment on contaminated substrates. Call and Davies (1988)
demonstrated that AM inoculation of three grass species planted on lignite mine
overburden substantially increased survival, biomass, and nutrient content over
three years of monitoring.

Using the AM fungi *[[glomus-fasciculatum]]* and *[[gigaspora-marginata]]*, mycorrhizal
inoculation improved survival by 11–30 percentage points and biomass by 40–80%
compared to uninoculated controls across all three grass species.

**Sideoats grama** — Inoculated plants showed 97% survival with *G.
fasciculatum* and 94% with *G. marginata*, compared to 86% for uninoculated
controls. Biomass increased from 15 g/plant (uninoculated) to 25 g/plant (*G.
fasciculatum*). Nitrogen content rose from 0.72% to 0.82% and phosphorus from
0.07% to 0.14%.

**Indian grass** — Survival improved from 72% (uninoculated) to 92% (*G.
fasciculatum*). Biomass nearly doubled from 21 to 36 g/plant. Phosphorus
content increased from 0.07% to 0.12%.

**Klein grass** — The most metal-sensitive species showed the largest relative
improvement, with survival increasing from 47% (uninoculated) to 67% (*G.
marginata*) and biomass from 10 to 19 g/plant.

Phosphorus content approximately doubled across all inoculated treatments,
highlighting the critical role of mycorrhizal hyphae in nutrient acquisition
from nutrient-poor mine overburden where [[phosphorus-availability]] is typically
the primary limiting factor.

## Heavy Metal Detoxification by AM Fungi

AM fungi mediate heavy metal interactions through a dual mechanism: enhancing
cadmium uptake from soil through extraradical hyphae while simultaneously
reducing transfer from the fungal tissue to the host plant. This
compartmentalization retains Cd within the fungal component of the root system,
effectively detoxifying the plant.

Heavy metals (Zn, Cu, Cd, Ni) from contaminated soils can delay AM fungal
invasion of clover roots (Koomen and McGrath, 1990). However, Joner and Leyval
(1997) found no reduction of AM hyphal extension into soil at up to 20 mg
extractable Cd kg⁻¹ soil, suggesting that AM function persists at moderate
contamination levels.

## Organic Pollutant Degradation

Beyond heavy metals, mycorrhizal fungi contribute to the degradation of organic
pollutants. Many ectomycorrhizal fungi produce enzymes that degrade aromatic
compounds, including lignin (a polymer of phenolic compounds).

The mat-forming ectomycorrhizal fungus *[[hysterangium-setchellii]]* may account
for 45–55% of total soil organic biomass (Cromack et al., 1979; Fogel and Hunt,
1983), giving it considerable potential to influence pollutant hydrocarbons in
soil environments.

Specific examples of organic pollutant metabolism include:

- 2,4-D and atrazine incorporated into biomass of *Rhizopogon vinicolor* and
  the ericoid mycorrhiza *Hymenoscyphus ericae*
- PCB decomposition: Donnelley and Fletcher (1994) screened 21 mycorrhizal
  fungi, finding that 14 species (67%) metabolized some PCBs by at least 20%
- *[[radiigera-atrogleba]]* and *[[hysterangium-gardneri]]* degraded 80% of
  2,2'-dichlorobiphenyl
- Ericoid mycorrhizae *H. ericae* and *[[oidiodendron-griseum]]* were less
  effective than ectomycorrhizal species at PCB degradation

## Landfill Restoration Considerations

In landfill restoration, the survival of ectomycorrhizal fungi depends on their
ability to tolerate reduced oxygen tension. Senior et al. (1993) and Tosh et al.
(1993) showed that *Laccaria proxima* and *Hebeloma crustuliniforme* survived
the low redox potentials characteristic of landfill environments, but *Paxillus
involutus* could not survive these conditions.

Species selection is therefore critical in restoration projects on anaerobic
substrates — fungi must be screened for hypoxia tolerance in addition to metal
tolerance.

## Fertilizer Interactions

A critical balance must be achieved between fertilizer application and
mycorrhizal function in restoration projects. N.C. Johnson (1998) cautioned
against high applications of inorganic fertilizers in taconite mine tailing
restoration with *Salsola kali* and *Panicum virgatum*.

High phosphorus fertilization can suppress mycorrhizal colonization by reducing
the plant's dependence on the fungal partner for nutrient acquisition. Johnson
demonstrated that AM inoculum combined with organic soil amendment (papermill
sludge) was more beneficial for survival and growth of *Panicum virgatum*, and
was potentially more cost-effective than high fertilizer inputs.

## Heavy Metals in Aquatic Ecosystems

The impact of heavy metals on fungal-mediated decomposition extends to aquatic
ecosystems. Maltby and Booth (1991) assessed coal mine effluent effects on
stream leaf litter decomposition, finding that hyphomycete species diversity
dropped from 8 species upstream to only 4 downstream of the mine drainage.

Species including *Articulospora tetracladia*, *Centrospora aquatica*,
*Clavatospora longibrachiata*, and *Tricladium* disappeared downstream.
Decomposition rates and C:N ratio declines were significantly faster upstream,
reflecting the functional impairment of the fungal community.

Duddridge and Wainwright (1980) showed that aquatic fungi (*Pythium*,
*Dictyuchus*, *Scytalidium*) accumulate metals in the order Zn > Pb > Cd.
Cadmium and zinc at low concentrations (<1 mg L⁻¹ and <10 mg L⁻¹,
respectively) stimulated fungal growth, while lead had no effect.

Critically, Cd transferred from fungal hyphae to the grazing amphipod
*Gammarus pulex*, demonstrating food chain bioaccumulation. No shrimp survived
13 days when fed *Pythium* containing 150–170 μg g⁻¹ cadmium, whereas 60%
survived to 21 days on control hyphae. Dead animals contained 22 μg g⁻¹ Cd.

Bärlocher (1992a,b) showed that heavy metals can be detrimental to the growth
of [[aquatic-hyphomycetes-and-ingoldian-spores]] reduce sporulation. However, the effects of high
levels of Cd and Zn were reduced by the presence of calcium ions in water,
attributed to ion complexation providing greater [[mycorrhizal-fungi-heavy-metal-tolerance]] in hard
water.

## Marine and Lichen Ecosystem Responses

sediment) and methylmercury (190 ng g⁻¹), Newell and Wall (1998) found
increased rather than decreased fungal activity, with higher ergosterol content
and ascospore production. This contrasts with terrestrial patterns.
