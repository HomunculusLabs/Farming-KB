# Vitamin D Enhancement in Mushrooms Through UV Exposure

Mushrooms are unique among foods in their ability to generate
vitamin D when exposed to ultraviolet (UV) light. Unlike plants,
mushrooms contain ergosterol rather than cholesterol as their
primary sterol. When ergosterol absorbs UV-B radiation (typically
290 to 315 nanometers), it converts to provitamin D2
(ergocalciferol), a potent form of vitamin D. This conversion
mirrors the process in human skin where 7-dehydrocholesterol is
converted to vitamin D3 by UV light. However, the fungal pathway
produces vitamin D2 rather than D3, though both are biologically
active in humans and can raise serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels.

## The Ergosterol-to-Vitamin D Pathway

Ergosterol is a [[plant-cell-membrane-transport-proteins]] component found in all fungi.
Fresh mushrooms typically contain 0.3 to 4 percent fat by dry
weight, with ergosterol comprising up to 15 percent of total
lipids. In its native state within the [[hplc-analysis-psilocybin-psilocin-mushroom-tissue]],
ergosterol has no vitamin D activity. The photolytic conversion
requires UV-B photons to break the B-ring of the ergosterol
molecule, forming previtamin D2, which then thermally isomerizes
to vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol). This reaction occurs rapidly,
often within minutes to hours of UV exposure depending on
intensity. The resulting provitamin D2 acts as a strong
antioxidant in addition to its role as a vitamin D precursor.

## Stamets Vitamin D Experiments

[[paul-stamets]] conducted a series of controlled experiments at
Fungi Perfecti Research Laboratories in Washington State
(coordinates N. 47.14970, W. 123.03905) to quantify vitamin D
production across multiple mushroom species under various light
and [[query-how-do-i-build-a-medicinal-herb-drying-rack-and-optimize-drying-conditions]]. All mushrooms were subjected to
standardized high-pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC)
analysis conforming to AOAC International official methods.

### Shiitake Results

Shiitake ([[staycare-lentinula-edodes-in-bioremediation]]) showed the most dramatic response.
Freshly picked indoor-grown shiitake dried indoors contained
only 110 IU of vitamin D per 100 grams. When placed outdoors
to dry in summer sunlight, vitamin D soared to 21,400 IU per
100 grams, a roughly 200-fold increase. Shiitake grown outdoors
in sunlight but dried indoors produced 1,620 IU, demonstrating
that both growth-phase and post-harvest UV exposure contribute.

### Orientation Effects

The most striking finding was that mushroom orientation to
the sun dramatically affected vitamin D production. When
shiitake were dried with their gills (spore-bearing surfaces)
facing upward toward the sun, vitamin D reached 46,000 IU per
100 grams, the highest level recorded in the study. When the
same mushrooms were dried with gills facing downward, vitamin
D was only 10,900 IU, a four-fold difference. This suggests
that the gill or pore surface area exposed to UV light is a
primary determinant of conversion efficiency, likely because
ergosterol concentrates in these fertile tissues.

### Maitake Response

Maitake ([[grifola-frondosa-maitake]]) showed remarkable vitamin D
enhancement despite being grown entirely indoors in filtered
light and dried indoors in the dark. After sun exposure for
approximately 6 hours, maitake produced 31,900 IU of vitamin
D per 100 grams, up from a baseline of only 460 IU. This
69-fold increase demonstrates that even mushrooms with no
prior light exposure can rapidly convert ergosterol to
vitamin D2 when exposed to natural sunlight.

### Reishi Results

Reishi ([[ganoderma-lucidum-cultivation]]) showed a more modest but still
significant response. Indoor-grown, indoor-dried reishi
contained 66 IU per 100 grams. After 6 to 8 hours of sun
exposure, levels rose to 2,760 IU, a 42-fold increase.
