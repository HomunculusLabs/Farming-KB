## DMT in Amazonian Ecosystems

## The Amazon as a Treasure House of Psychoactives

The Amazon basin is the richest repository of psychoactive plants on Earth. Nowhere else on the planet is there such a concentration of plant species containing DMT and related tryptamines. The sheer diversity of DMT-containing plants in the Amazon suggests a long and intimate coevolutionary relationship between these plants and the human inhabitants of the region.

DMT occurs naturally in many plant genera found in the Amazon, including Psychotria (the leaves commonly added to ayahuasca), Diplopterys (the leaves of chacruna), Mimosa (yurema), and Virola (the resin used by several Amazonian tribes). The distribution of DMT across so many unrelated plant families is itself remarkable and suggests that the compound has significant ecological functions.

## Psychotria Viridis: The Ayahuasca Companion

Psychotria viridis, known as chacruna in the Amazon, is the most commonly used DMT-containing plant in [[entheogen-virola-snuff-ethnobotany-chemistry]] tradition represents a parallel pharmacological tradition to ayahuasca — a different route of administration for the same class of compounds. The snuff produces a rapid, intense, and short-lived experience compared to the slow, gentle onset of ayahuasca. Different tribes have developed different preferences for different administration routes.

## Mimosa Tenuiflora: The Yurema

Mimosa tenuiflora, known as jurema or yurema in northeastern Brazil, is another DMT-containing plant with a long history of shamanic use. The bark of this tree contains significant quantities of DMT and has been used in preparation of a visionary brew analogous to ayahuasca.

The use of Mimosa tenuiflora in northeastern Brazil represents an independent discovery of the DMT-harmine synergy — a further indication that the Amazonian pharmacological tradition is ancient, sophisticated, and widespread.

## Ecological Implications

The extraordinary concentration of DMT-containing plants in the Amazon raises important ecological questions. Why are so many plants in this particular ecosystem producing compounds that are powerfully psychoactive in humans? Is this a coincidence, or does it reflect a genuine coevolutionary relationship between these plants and the human inhabitants of the Amazon?

McKenna suggests that the abundance of psychoactive plants in the Amazon is not accidental but reflects a long-standing mutualistic relationship between plants and humans. The plants produce psychoactive compounds as a way of influencing human behavior — encouraging the humans who consume them to protect and propagate the plants and their habitats.
