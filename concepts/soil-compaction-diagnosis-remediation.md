## Soil Compaction — Diagnosis and Remediation

## Definition

Soil compaction is mechanical compression of soil that reduces total pore space,
particularly macropores (>0.08 mm), increasing bulk density and impairing the
physical, chemical, and biological functions of the soil profile. Compacted soils
exhibit higher penetration resistance, lower [[mushroom-cultivation-jar-lid-management-loose-fit-vacuum-gas-exchange]] relative to their uncompacted state.

## Causes

Compaction arises from both natural and anthropogenic forces. [[savory-livestock-grazing-planning]] on wet
pastures exerts pressures comparable to light machinery. Repeated wheel traffic
concentrates compaction in predictable patterns. Raindrop impact on bare soil
seals the surface, forming a thin crust.

## Types of Compaction

**Surface crust** — a thin (1–5 mm) sealed layer formed by raindrop splash or
dispersive irrigation water. Impedes water entry and seedling emergence.

can decline 10–25% on moderately compacted fields.

Biological and chemical effects follow. Anaerobic zones develop in waterlogged
micropores, promoting denitrification and nitrogen loss as N₂O — a potent
[[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-networks]] are disrupted, earthworm activity drops,
[[root-anatomy-structure-and-nutrient-uptake]] efficiency falls as root exploration is limited.

## Diagnosis Methods

### Penetrometer Resistance
A cone penetrometer measures soil strength in MPa. Readings above 2.0 MPa in
moist loam soils indicate root-limiting compaction. Soil moisture must be
standardized; measurements in dry soil overestimate resistance. Take multiple
readings across the field and at multiple depths to map the layer.

### Bulk Density Measurement
Undisturbed soil cores (5 × 5 cm) are dried and weighed to determine bulk
density. Compare against texture-specific thresholds from USDA-NRCS or regional
soil health cards. Sample at the suspected depth and at an uncompacted
reference depth.

### Visual and Tactile Assessment
Dig a soil pit to examine the profile. Compacted layers resist penetration,
appear platy or massive (no visible aggregates), and show few roots or biopores.
Compare root distribution above and below the suspected layer.

### Percolation Tests
Measure the rate water drains from a hole to reveal hydraulic restrictions.
Slow drainage in otherwise well-drained soils suggests a compacted layer
impeding vertical flow.

### Root Growth Patterns
Shallow, restricted root systems in crops that normally develop deep taproots
(corn, canola) are a strong indicator of subsurface compaction.
