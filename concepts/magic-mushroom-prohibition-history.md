# Magic Mushroom Prohibition History

The prohibition of [[archaeology-of-psychedelic-mushroom-use]] represents one of the more peculiar chapters
in the history of [[psychedelic-prohibition-and-the-war-on-consciousness]]. Unlike cannabis, opium,
or cocaine, magic mushrooms were largely unknown to Western legislators when they were criminalised. Their
proscribed status resulted not from any demonstrated social harm but from structural similarity to
[[pihkal-doet]] and the political dynamics of the era. In law, [[psilocybin-isolation-and-early-scientific-research]] is typically listed alongside heroin
as among the most dangerous of illicit drugs -- a classification that would strike most mycologists and
psychopharmacologists as bizarre.

## The UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971)

In 1971, the United Nations, under considerable American pressure, introduced its Convention on
Psychotropic Substances as an adjunct to its earlier Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs (1961). At the time,
illicit use of magic mushrooms in Europe and America was minimal, had not gone overground, and was probably
little known to policymakers. Nevertheless, [[psilocybin-isolation-and-early-scientific-research]] and [[psilocybin-neuroplasticity]] were included in the Convention
because of their structural similarity to LSD -- the great drugs menace of the time.

Following appeals by the Mexican government, signatories agreed to prohibit the synthetic chemicals but not
the plants that contained them. The Mexicans were understandably worried by the prospect of having to
prevent indigenous [[mesoamerican-mushroom-ceremonies-teonanacatl]]. This distinction -- between synthetic chemicals and
naturally occurring fungi -- opened the legal loophole that existed in Britain until 2005
and that persists in Holland, allowing the sale of fresh mushrooms.

## United States Federal Prohibition

In the United States, psilocybin and psilocin were classified as controlled substances under Federal Law in
1970 through the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act (Public Law 91-513), making the
unlicensed possession, sale, or use of a psilocybin mushroom a criminal offence punishable by fine or
imprisonment. Possession of LSD was made a federal crime in 1966, and overnight many hundreds of research
projects across the States were shut down. Thereafter, research into the effects of psychedelics became
impossible and unthinkable, a situation that only forty years later is beginning to change.

Yet the fact that mushrooms were illegal did little to halt their spread. Arrests were sporadic and more
often for trespassing than for possession of a prohibited substance. As one Florida school principal
complained during the mushroom craze of the 1970s, "on weekends, the place is covered with mushroom freaks."

## The British Loophole and Its Closure

The distinction between fresh and prepared mushrooms in British law meant that for decades fresh magic
mushrooms could be sold legally in shops across the country. This arose from the wording of the Misuse of
Drugs Act 1971, which controlled psilocybin as a chemical but did not explicitly criminalise the fresh fungi
containing it. This loophole was eventually closed in 2005 through new legislation. The speed with which the
law was changed was striking, given that the problem had been known about for years -- a sign of how
politically charged the issue had become.

## Prohibition's Cultural Consequences

The legal situation became, as Letcher describes it, "the predominant cultural force about which
contemporary mushroom enthusiasts must orientate themselves, rather like iron filings in a magnetic field."
Several consequences followed:

- **Invented histories**: Enthusiasts developed supposed lineages of mushroom use stretching back to the
  dawn of time, serving to legitimate the illicit hobby. Claims that [[history-psychedelics-ancient]] were integral to
  ancient civilisations -- from stonehenge to the [[plutarch-and-cicero-on-the-eleusinian-mysteries]] to
  medieval witchcraft -- proliferated. Marion Zimmer Bradley's bestselling The Mists of Avalon (1984)
  retold the Arthurian myths as a spiritual battle between ancient Druidic, goddess-worshipping paganism
  and militant Christianity, with magic mushrooms as a key tool of the pagan high priestess Morgan le Fay.
- **Underground identity**: Mushroom consumption became a [[1960s-counterculture-and-magic-mushrooms]] forming the kernel of an alternative identity -- a sense of alterity or opposition to the mainstream.
- **Harm amplification**: The greatest risk to health, liberty, and well-being came not from the mushrooms
  themselves but from their proscribed status. In Britain during the early 1980s, mushroom-related enquiries
  to the National Poisons Information Service at Guy's Hospital increased from 47 in 1979 to 142 in 1981. A
  survey in Scotland found that twenty per cent of school children in Tayside had at least heard of magic
  mushrooms, if not actually tried them.
- **Dangerous identification**: The black market created genuine risks. In Australia, one death from renal
  failure was almost certainly caused by picking the wrong mushroom. In America, [[psilocybe-stuntzii-profile]]
  is easily confused with the deadly poisonous galerina-autumnalis, with which it can happily grow side
  by side.

## The Free Festival Crackdown

In Britain, the government did everything it could to shut down the hippy festivals and emerging traveller
lifestyle where mushrooms were consumed. The Stonehenge Free Festival, running from 1974 to 1984, was
famously and violently shut down by the Thatcher government in the "Battle of the Beanfield." The law was
tightened with the Public Order Act and then the Criminal Justice Act of 1994, which effectively outlawed
free festivals. Festival-goers saw themselves as "some ancient pagan tribe, pitted against a wholly
fascistic regime," and the magic mushroom slotted neatly into this romantic mythology.

## The Dutch Pragmatic Model

The Dutch government's approach demonstrated that pragmatic regulation could work. Recognising that a
drug-free society was an unobtainable ideal, the Dutch acted to separate "drugs with acceptable risks" from
"drugs with unacceptable risks," both in law and in the marketplace. Putting cannabis in the former category
led to the famous coffee shops. The Convention on Narcotic Drugs (1961) tied the Dutch
government's hands on cannabis, but the half-measure of decriminalisation freed them to maximise efforts to
prevent unacceptable use of heroin, cocaine, LSD, and later Ecstasy.

It was within this liberal climate that a young man named Damien van den Huerk opened Holland's very first
"smart shop," Dreams, in Amsterdam in 1993. A disillusioned IT worker, drugs charity worker, and rave
enthusiast, van den Huerk spotted the loophole in the law and hit upon the novel idea of growing mushrooms
commercially. It proved successful beyond all expectations.

## Harm Reduction Arguments

If harm reduction is the principal concern, there is a persuasive argument for the legal sale of cultivated
mushrooms to prevent people from making potentially lethal mistakes. Enthusiasts' time would be better spent
arguing the case for mushrooms on health and medical grounds -- the culturally sanctioned criteria of our
time -- than on the grounds of some fantastical history dreamed up on the basis of wishful thinking and
overworked evidence. As Letcher argues, if mushrooms really are safe, if they really do deliver meaningful
insights, then the fact that the practice is a modern one is neither here nor there.

## The Spore Trade

A peculiar feature of mushroom prohibition is the spore trade. Because spores do not contain psilocybin,
they exist in a legal grey area in many jurisdictions. This allowed entrepreneurs like
Robert McPherson (Psilocybe Fanaticus) to operate openly, distributing spores and
cultivation instructions while growing itself remained a felony. While spores could be traded legally in
America, growing magic mushrooms, particularly with intent to supply, remained a felony. Like the secret
stills supplying moonshine in the days of prohibition, magic mushroom cultivation became celebrated as an
illicit folk art.
