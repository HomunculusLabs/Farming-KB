# Edge Effect in Garden Design

Toby Hemenway's detailed exploration of the edge effect as a design tool for increasing biodiversity, productivity, and microclimate variety in the garden. From Gaia's Garden, Chapter 3.

## What Is the Edge Effect

Edges are where things happen. Where a forest meets the prairie, where a river flows into the sea, or at any boundary between two ecosystems is a "cauldron of biodiversity." All species that thrive in each of the two environments are present, plus new species that live in the transition zone. The edge is richer than what is on either side.

Any fisherman knows this — they don't cast into the center of the lake, but toward the shoreline where fish gather to feed on the flourishing life in the shallows.

## When to Minimize Edge

Sometimes edge translates into waste space and more work:

- [[gaias-garden-herb-spiral-and-natural-patterns]] minimize edge to reduce path waste
- [[gaias-garden-keyhole-beds-and-mandala-gardens]] minimize edge for the same reason
- The decision depends on what lies on either side and what you want from it

## When to Maximize Edge

For biodiversity and productivity, maximize edge through:

- Lobes, notches, mounds, pits, crinkles, and crenellations
- Varying height and depth of plantings, soil, structures, and ponds (third dimension)
- Wavy planting patterns instead of straight lines
- Alternating tall and short plant rows

## Applying Edge in the Garden

### House/Yard Edge

The outside walls of a house create varied microclimates:

- **South wall:** Hottest and sunniest — heat-loving and cold-tender plants; can grow plants 1-2 USDA zones south
- **West wall:** Slightly cooler
- **North wall:** Coldest and darkest

### Pavement/Soil Edge

- Thirstier plants alongside sidewalks and driveways catch runoff
- Pavement stores heat on sunny days; adjacent soil is warmer
- Gravel gardens can use this thermal mass

### Fence/Yard Edge

- Fences act as filters, stopping some flows and allowing others
- Debris and snow pile up against fences, collecting mulch and moisture
- Zigzag fencing increases trellis area, wind resistance, and creates sheltered pockets in each concave "zig"

### Plant/Soil Edge

- Wavy planting patterns fit more plants than straight lines
- Plants at bed edges often yield more than center plants
- Alternating tall and short plant rows achieves the same effect

### Plant/Air Edge

- Keyhole beds pointed south create sun traps
- Bed edges and shrub/tree rows can be sculpted into undulating shapes
- Contains cooler, windier lobes and protected, warm bays

### Water/Soil Edge

- Round pond = least edge; lobed/starburst pond = enormous edge
- Fingers of land extending into pond: dry centers with squishy margins
- Varying pond depth creates room for more fish and water plant types
- Frogs bask in warm shallows while koi flash in the depths

## Edge as Connection

Edges are not static places — they are the result of relationships between two or more dynamic pieces of the living landscape. The importance of edges is "one more manifestation of the role of connections in the ecological garden."

## Design Implications

- Increase edge where you want more biodiversity and production
- Decrease edge where you want to reduce maintenance and path space
- Use the third dimension (height variation) to multiply edge effects
- Transition zones between different plant heights create habitat and variety
- Nature never takes the shortest distance between two points — curves and lobes are more efficient
