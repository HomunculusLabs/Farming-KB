# Soil Formation and Conservation

Soil formation is the slow geological and biological process by which rock is transformed into the living matrix that supports terrestrial life. Understanding this process is essential because modern agriculture removes soil at rates vastly exceeding its natural rate of formation—a fundamentally unsustainable trajectory that threatens long-term food security and ecosystem health.

## The Soil Formation Process

### Mechanical Weathering
Water physically breaks down rock through multiple mechanisms:

- **Hydration swelling** — water combines with common minerals like felspars, causing [[query-what-are-ec-and-tds-and-how-do-i-use-them-for-cannabis-nutrient-management|them]] to swell and fracture the parent rock
- **Ice expansion** — water freezing in rock cracks exerts enormous pressure, progressively splitting rock apart
- **Glacial grinding** — glaciers pulverize rock to fine mineral flour across vast landscapes
- **Plant wedging** — roots physically pry open rocks and mineral particles

### Chemical Weathering
Water drives chemical transformations that convert rock minerals into soil components:

- **Hydration of felspars** — produces kaolin (clay), potassium carbonate, and silicates
- **Carbonic acid formation** — carbonates released during weathering create carbonic acid stronger than rainwater alone, further dissolving minerals
- **Oxidation** — atmospheric oxygen dissolved in rainwater oxidizes iron minerals (pyrites), forming haematite and sulphuric acid, which in turn dissolves additional metals

### Biological Contributions
Plants actively accelerate soil formation through:

- **Root penetration** — wedging rocks apart and creating channels for water and air
- **Acid exudation** — roots recreate acids that dissolve mineral particles
- **Mineral transport** — dissolved minerals are drawn into plant sap and redistributed through leaf fall and decomposition to other locations
- **Organic [[humus-formation-and-soil-organic-matter|matter]] accumulation** — decaying plant material becomes humus, the living component of soil

### Soil Building Rate
Natural soil formation produces approximately **2–4 tonnes per hectare per year** of uncompacted topsoil. This rate represents the baseline against which all human soil management must be measured.

## The Erosion Crisis

### Soil Loss Rates
Modern agriculture removes soil at catastrophic rates:

- **Cropping and tillage**: 40–500 tonnes per hectare per year
- **Worst-case erosion events**: can remove 2000 years of accumulated soil in a single season of flooding or strong wind over cultivated soils
- Even the most ideal tillage practices merely **keep pace** with the most ideal soil formation conditions

### The Fundamental Imbalance
The ratio of soil formation to soil loss reveals a stark reality:

| Activity | Rate (t/ha/year) |
|----------|-------------------|
| Natural soil formation | 2–4 |
| Typical cropping/tillage | 40–500 |
| Ratio of loss to formation | 10:1 to 125:1 |

This means every year of conventional agriculture effectively "mines" decades of future soil productivity.

### Global Scale
Australia has approximately **30% of its original soils** in fair condition. The rest have been washed away, blown away, or depleted in structure and yield. This pattern holds true for most countries where extractive agriculture and forestry occurs.

## Sites of Soil Conservation and Accumulation

Only four types of environments actively conserve or build soil:

### 1. Uncut Forests
Forests represent the most effective soil-building systems:
- Continuous canopy reduces rainfall impact energy
- Root networks stabilize soil on slopes
- Leaf litter and woody debris provide constant organic input
- [[mycorrhizal-networks]] facilitate nutrient cycling
- Shaded conditions reduce evaporation and maintain soil moisture

### 2. Lakes and Ponds
Quiet water bodies allow sediment accumulation:
- Eroded particles settle in still water
- Organic matter accumulates in anaerobic bottom sediments
- Over geological time, lake beds become deep soil deposits
- Valley floors and deltas contain the deepest transported soils

### 3. Prairies and Meadows
Permanent grassland systems maintain soil through:
- Dense, perennial root systems that hold soil in place
- Continuous organic matter input from root turnover and above-ground growth
- Protection from rain splash and wind by standing vegetation
- Seasonal growth cycles that build soil organic matter

### 4. Mulched and No-Till Systems
Human-managed systems that approximate natural soil conservation:
- **Mulched systems** — organic cover protects soil surface from erosion, moderates temperature, and feeds soil organisms
- **No-till systems** — minimize soil disturbance, preserving [[crumb-structure]] and fungal networks

## Value Frameworks for Soil Management

### Health Framework
Soil management must prioritize both human and plant health:

- Approach must be **careful and conservative**, especially regarding biocides and high levels of artificial fertilizers
- Historical populations thrived on home-grown produce without herbicides, pesticides, or artificial fertilizers
- The goal is to **improve, not lessen**, the long-tested vitality integral to good food

Additional health factors associated with food gardening include:
- **Mild regular exercise** — gardening as a steady, non-stressing form of physical activity
- **Meditation** — the capacity to sit, observe, and reduce stress
- **Meaning and purpose** — food gardening as "right livelihood" with direct value to others
- **Lifelong interest** — every day and season brings change, observation, and learning

### Yield Framework
The yield debate reveals a critical tension in modern agriculture:

**The case for conventional agriculture:**
- Once-off yield of ploughed, fertilized monocultures supported by chemicals and large energy inputs can out-yield almost every other production system

**The questions that must be asked:**
- At [[query-what-are-root-exudates-and-how-do-they-shape-soil-life|what]] **public cost**?
- For **how long** maintained?
- With what improvement in **nutrition**?
- With what guarantee of **sustainability**?
- With what effect on **world hunger**, **soils**, and **health**?

Abundant evidence now demonstrates that forced yields are **temporary** and that plough cultures destroy both soils and societies.

**The systemic problem:**
- Modern agricultural systems carry the seeds of our own destruction
- These systems receive public financial support while destroying the countryside through deforestation, hedge removal, species loss, soil degradation, and poisoning
- Society is obliged by entrenched bureaucracies to pay for the destruction of the world

### Soil Life Framework
Soil organisms are a **major soil factor** with profound effects on:

- **pH** — microbial activity modifies soil acidity
- **Mineral content and availability** — organisms solubilize locked minerals
- **Soil structure** — fungal hyphae and [[root-exudates]] create and maintain crumb structure
- **Erosion resistance** — biological crusts and root networks stabilize soil surfaces

### Ethics Framework
The ethical framework recognizes that:

- It is **not** the purpose of humanity to reduce all soils to perfectly balanced market gardens
- Only **4% of the earth** is needed for food production
- **60% of all agricultural soils** have been ruined by the plough or polluted by emissions
- The largest job is **restoration of soils and forests** for a healthy earth itself
- Without naturally deficient, poorly drained, acidic, or alkaline sites, many plant species would disappear—they have evolved specifically for such conditions

### Land Recovery Potential
Various estimates suggest how much land could be returned to nature:

- **Colin Tudge** (New Scientist, 1986): conservatively estimated perhaps **60%** of the British Isles could be returned to nature
- **John Jeavons** (based on garden productivity data): estimated perhaps **94%** of land could be returned to nature while still meeting food needs
