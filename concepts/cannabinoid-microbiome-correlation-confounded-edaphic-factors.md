### The Causal Inference Challenge

Three causal models can explain the observed cannabinoid-microbiome correlation:

**Model A: Cannabinoids shape the microbiome directly**
- Cannabinoids and other [[medicinal-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties]] that selectively inhibit or promote specific taxa
- Different cultivars produce different cannabinoid profiles, leading to cultivar-specific microbiomes
- This model predicts that within the same soil type, plants with different cannabinoid profiles should harbor different microbiomes

**Model B: Soil drives both independently**
- Soil chemistry independently influences both cannabinoid biosynthesis (through nutrient availability, stress responses) and microbial community assembly (through the two-tier selection model)
- The observed cannabinoid-microbiome correlation is spurious, arising from shared dependence on soil conditions
- This model predicts that controlling for soil type should eliminate the cannabinoid-microbiome correlation

**Model C: Bidirectional interaction**
- Soil conditions influence both cannabinoid production and microbial community composition
- Cannabinoids in turn modulate the microbiome through antimicrobial or signaling effects
- The relationship is partially but not wholly confounded by soil
- This model predicts a partial reduction in cannabinoid-microbiome correlation after controlling for soil, but not complete elimination

## Why the Study Could Not Distinguish Between Models

The experimental design had insufficient statistical power to disentangle these models for several reasons:

1. **Small sample size for cannabinoid data**: Only four plants were tested for cannabinoids (three White Widow, one Maui Wowie), providing minimal statistical power for correlation analyses.

2. **Incomplete factorial design**: Not all cultivar × soil type combinations were tested. White Widow was grown in both soils, but Maui Wowie was only grown in one. A complete factorial design with multiple cultivars in each soil type, with sufficient replication, would be needed to partition the effects.

3. **Single time point**: Cannabinoid concentrations change throughout the flowering cycle. A single measurement two weeks before harvest may not represent the cumulative cannabinoid exposure experienced by the endorhiza microbiome over the entire growth period.

4. **Unmeasured root-zone cannabinoids**: The study measured cannabinoid content in buds, not in root tissue or rhizosphere soil. Root exudate cannabinoid concentrations may differ substantially from bud concentrations, and root-zone exposure is what would directly affect the microbiome.
