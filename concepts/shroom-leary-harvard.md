# [[timothy-leary]] and the Harvard Psilocybin Project

updated: 2026-04-24

Timothy Leary (1920-1996) was the Harvard clinical psychologist whose
encounter with magic mushrooms in Mexico in August 1960 launched him on
a trajectory from respectable academic to America's most infamous
psychedelic advocate. His Harvard Psilocybin Project (1960-1963), the
most unusual episode in American intellectual history, eventually led
to his dismissal from the university and helped catalyse the psychedelic
revolution.

## The Mushroom Experience in Mexico

In August 1960, Leary, a married father, model tenured academic, and
brilliant clinical psychologist who had revolutionised personality
testing, heard about magic mushrooms from his friend and colleague Frank
Barron, who had read Wasson's Life article. Judicious inquiries by
anthropologist Gerhart Braun led Leary to an elderly Mexican woman,
Senora Juana, who willingly parted with freshly picked mushrooms for a
few pesos.

Whereas Wasson had tramped through the hinterlands in search of a
primitive encounter with Sabina and her holy mushroom veladas, Leary,
rather tellingly, took his mushrooms by the pool, with cold beers and
dry martinis close at hand. He was joined by an indolent circle of
academics and their bikini-clad wives. The playboy environment did not
diminish the experience. Leary oscillated between intense sensual
encounters and the feeling that he was being dragged backwards through
his own evolutionary history, regressing until he was no more than a
single amoebic cell. "It was the classic visionary voyage and I came
back a changed man," he later wrote.

## The Harvard Psilocybin Project

Leary returned to Harvard reinvigorated and determined to abandon his
previous research in favour of further explorations into the "strange
deep realms" the mushrooms opened up. He learned from Aldous Huxley
that the mushrooms' chemical ingredients were readily available from
Sandoz, and initiated the Harvard Psilocybin Project.

Still at this point a rationalist, atheistic psychologist, Leary's
principal concern was to see how people might be socially changed through
psychedelic experiences. In a crucial decision, he rejected the clinical,
quantitative approach in favour of one that was subjective and
qualitative. "Our doctors and scientists... follow the sterile medical
model of giving drugs to cadavers and observing only external results,"
he proclaimed.

He assembled an ever-expanding team of willing volunteers -- academics,
poets, artists, musicians, graduate students, friends -- and, breaking
all academic rules, took psilocybin with them in a "supportive
environment." This typically meant a home setting where volunteers could
listen to music, browse books on art, or make love.

## From Science to Cult

Over the years the project ran (1960-1963), during which some two
hundred doses were administered, it began to look less like a scientific
experiment and more like a psychedelic tea party. Increasing numbers of
subjects returned with reports of religious, spiritual or mystical
experiences. The infamous Beat poet Allen Ginsberg, halfway through his
first psilocybin session, descended naked from his bedroom declaring
that he was the Messiah. The project became cliquey, with furtive nods
and winks between those that "had" and those that "hadn't."

Harvard, worried by the stain on its reputation, gave Leary his marching
orders in 1963. His controversial office on Divinity Avenue was removed
to make way for a new and larger psychology department in which
psychedelic research would have no place.

## From Harvard to Counterculture

Leary proved irrepressible. During the latter days of the Psilocybin
Project he had taken LSD, a drug that proved even more astonishing in
its effects than psilocybin. "I became initiated into an ancient company
of illumined seers," he later wrote. His colleagues feared for his
sanity, but after a day or so he returned to normal and thereafter
became perhaps the world's greatest proselytiser for the drug.

Together with [[richard-alpert]] and [[ralph-metzner]], two other Harvard
colleagues fired during the psilocybin furore, he founded IFIF (the
International Foundation for Internal Freedom) and then the Castalian
Foundation. The trio authored The Psychedelic Experience, a tripper's
manual loosely based on the Tibetan Buddhist text The Book of the Dead,
which inspired John Lennon to write "Tomorrow Never Knows."

Sponsored by wealthy patrons, Leary moved into Millbrook, a rambling
mansion in upstate New York that became the prototypical hippy commune
centred around LSD. He appeared on radio and TV, stood as candidate for
Governor of California, and was eventually jailed for cannabis
possession. Possession of LSD was made a federal crime in America in
1966, and overnight hundreds of research projects across the States
were shut down.
