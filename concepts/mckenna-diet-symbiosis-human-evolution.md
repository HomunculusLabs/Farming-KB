# McKenna's Diet Symbiosis and Human Evolution

## Overview

In Chapter 2 of *Food of the Gods*, titled "Magic in Food," [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]] presents his argument for what he calls **"The Real Missing Link"** — the possibility that the dramatic tripling of hominid brain size over approximately three million years cannot be adequately explained by natural selection acting on random mutations alone.

McKenna proposes that early hominids in the African tropics encountered psilocybin-containing mushrooms as part of their foraging behavior, and that the psychoactive compounds in these fungi acted as a powerful selective pressure driving neurological expansion and behavioral modernization.

This theory represents a specific formulation of the broader [[psychedelic-plants-and-civilization]], and connects directly to McKenna's [[tryptamine-consciousness-theory]] about the role of tryptamine compounds in the evolution of consciousness.

## The Explanatory Gap

McKenna opens his argument by highlighting what he considers a fundamental problem in conventional paleoanthropology. The hominid brain underwent an extraordinary transformation — increasing from roughly 400 cubic centimeters in *Australopithecus* to approximately 1,400 cubic centimeters in *Homo sapiens*. This represents a threefold expansion in cranial capacity.

He argues that standard models of natural selection struggle to account for the speed and magnitude of this change. Brain tissue is metabolically expensive, demanding a disproportionate share of the body's caloric budget. Natural selection would need a compelling adaptive advantage to justify such a costly investment.

McKenna suggests that psilocybin-containing mushrooms provided exactly this advantage: a set of behavioral and neurological benefits that made larger, more complex brains directly adaptive, thereby accelerating the selective pressures favoring neural expansion.

## The Three Big Steps for the Human Race

McKenna organizes his evolutionary argument around a sequence of three critical shifts in hominid behavior and neurochemistry, which he dubs the "Three Big Steps for the Human Race."

### Step One: The Fruit-Based Diet and Occasional Mushroom Encounters

The first major step involved the shift away from a purely scavenging diet toward a fruit-based foraging strategy. As early hominids moved into forest margins and grassland ecotones, they encountered a wider variety of food sources, including the dung-loving *Psilocybe* mushrooms that grew on the droppings of grazing herbivores.

At this initial stage, mushroom consumption was sporadic rather than systematic. The occasional ingestion of psilocybin-containing fungi at moderate doses would have produced effects including:

- Increased visual acuity and heightened pattern recognition

- Stimulated sexual activity and reproductive behavior

- Mild euphoria and a general sense of well-being

McKenna argues that even occasional exposure would have conferred enough adaptive benefit — through improved foraging success and increased mating opportunities — to create a selective pressure favoring individuals whose neurochemistry was most receptive to tryptamine compounds.

### Step Two: Low-Dose Psilocybin in the Regular Diet

The second step involved the incorporation of psilocybin mushrooms into the regular diet at low doses. McKenna draws an analogy to the concept of microdosing, suggesting that small, regular amounts of psilocybin improved visual acuity and edge-detection abilities.

For a hunting and gathering primate, enhanced visual perception would be directly adaptive. The ability to detect predators, track prey, and identify edible resources at greater distances would translate into significant survival and reproductive advantages.

This low-level dietary inclusion created what McKenna describes as a **chemical dependency syndrome** — not an addiction in the clinical sense, but a co-evolutionary relationship in which the mushrooms became an integral part of the hominid ecological niche. The behavioral advantages conferred by regular low-dose psilocybin use would have been substantial enough to shape the trajectory of hominid evolution over vast time scales.

### Step Three: Higher Doses, Ego-Dissolution, and Community Bonding

The third and most consequential step involved the discovery of higher doses of psilocybin. At elevated concentrations, the effects of psilocybin shift from the primarily sensory enhancements of low doses to profound alterations in consciousness and self-perception.

McKenna describes how higher doses would have produced:

- **Boundary dissolution**: The dissolution of ego boundaries between individuals, creating a sense of shared identity and collective consciousness

- **Enhanced social bonding**: The intensification of emotional connections and empathy within groups, strengthening the cooperative behaviors essential for early human society

- **Glossolalia and vocal expression**: At the highest doses, the emergence of spontaneous vocalizations — rhythmic, proto-linguistic utterances that McKenna suggests may have been precursors to language itself

- **Religious experience**: The generation of what would be experienced as visionary, numinous states — encounters with the sacred that formed the basis for proto-religious and proto-shamanic traditions

This third step connects directly to McKenna's ideas about [[psychedelic-set-and-setting]] and to his broader hypothesis about [[mckenna-human-cognition-and-the-stoned-age]] as a driver of cognitive evolution.

## Acquired Tastes and Dietary Symbiosis

### The Concept of Acquired Tastes

McKenna devotes significant attention to the phenomenon of "acquired tastes" — the process by which animals expand their dietary repertoire beyond instinctively preferred foods. He notes that many mammals, including primates, actively seek out and consume psychoactive plants in the wild.

This behavior is not unique to humans. Elephants ferment fruit to produce alcohol, reindeer seek out psychoactive *Amanita muscaria* mushrooms, and domestic cats deliberately ingest catnip. McKenna argues that this widespread pattern of intentional psychoactive consumption suggests that the interaction between animals and consciousness-altering plants is an ancient and evolutionarily significant phenomenon.

For early hominids, the development of a "taste" for psilocybin mushrooms would have represented a critical expansion of their dietary ecology — one that carried far-reaching consequences for the trajectory of their cognitive and cultural evolution.

### The Symbiosis Model

McKenna frames the relationship between hominids and psilocybin mushrooms as a genuine **symbiosis** — a mutually beneficial interaction between two species. The mushrooms benefit from being consumed because passage through a primate digestive system aids in spore dispersal. The hominids benefit from the psychoactive effects that improve their survival and reproductive fitness.

This symbiotic framing connects to McKenna's broader [[symbiosis-art-of-living]] philosophy, in which he argues that humanity's relationship with psychoactive plants represents one of the oldest and most fundamental partnerships in our species' history.

## Addressing Lamarckian Criticism

A common misunderstanding of McKenna's theory is the claim that it represents a Lamarckian argument — the discredited idea that acquired characteristics can be directly inherited. Critics have sometimes characterized McKenna as arguing that eating mushrooms literally caused genetic mutations that enlarged the brain.

McKenna explicitly rejects this interpretation. His argument is firmly grounded in standard Darwinian natural selection:

- The mushrooms did **not** cause mutations in the germ line

- The mushrooms did **not** directly alter DNA

- The mushrooms **did** produce behavioral effects — improved hunting, enhanced sexual activity, stronger social bonds, proto-linguistic vocalization — that created selective pressures

- Individuals whose neurochemistry was most responsive to psilocybin's effects would have enjoyed the greatest behavioral advantages, leading to preferential survival and reproduction

- Over millions of years, these selective pressures would have favored populations with increasingly complex and tryptamine-sensitive neural architecture

In this framework, psilocybin functions as an **environmental selective pressure** — analogous to climate change, predation pressure, or food scarcity — rather than as a direct agent of genetic change. The mushrooms shaped evolution not by rewriting DNA but by altering the fitness landscape in ways that favored neural complexity.

## Key Concepts and Terminology

**Dietary Symbiosis**: The mutually beneficial relationship between early hominids and psilocybin-containing mushrooms, in which spore dispersal was traded for psychoactive benefits.

**Chemical Dependency Syndrome**: McKenna's term for the co-evolutionary integration of a psychoactive substance into a species' ecological strategy — not addiction, but mutualistic dependence.

**Boundary Dissolution**: The psilocybin-induced breakdown of ego boundaries between individuals, facilitating social cohesion and collective identity formation.

**Glossolalia**: Spontaneous, rhythmic vocalization experienced during high-dose psychedelic states, proposed by McKenna as a precursor to syntactic language.

**Tryptamine Sensitivity**: The degree to which an organism's neural chemistry responds to tryptamine compounds like psilocybin and DMT, proposed as a trait under positive selection in early hominids.

## Connection to the Neuroscience of Psychedelics

McKenna's dietary symbiosis theory intersects with modern understanding of [[brain-neuroscience-and-psychedelic-mechanism]]. Psilocybin is a tryptamine alkaloid that acts primarily as a serotonin 5-HT2A receptor agonist, producing its effects by modulating activity in the prefrontal cortex, the default mode network, and visual processing centers.

The behavioral effects McKenna describes — enhanced visual acuity at low doses, emotional openness and social bonding at moderate doses, and profound ego-dissolution at high doses — map reasonably well onto what is now known about the dose-dependent effects of serotonergic psychedelics. Whether these effects actually drove human evolution remains speculative, but the neuropharmacological plausibility of McKenna's proposed mechanisms has been strengthened by contemporary research into [[psilocybin-neuroscience]].

## Significance and Legacy

McKenna's diet symbiosis theory remains one of the most provocative and widely discussed hypotheses about the role of psychoactive substances in human evolution. While it has not gained mainstream acceptance within the academic paleoanthropology community, it has been influential in:

- Stimulating interdisciplinary discussion about the co-evolution of humans and psychoactive plants

- Encouraging serious consideration of the role of altered states of consciousness in prehistoric human life

- Providing a narrative framework for understanding humanity's deep and ancient relationship with entheogenic fungi

- Connecting the study of human evolution to the broader field of [[cultural-evolution-and-psychoactive-substances]]

The theory represents a foundational element of [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]] and continues to inspire research and debate at the intersection of ethnobotany, neuroscience, and evolutionary biology.
