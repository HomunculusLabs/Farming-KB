Fungal mycelia are not passive decomposers — they are active foragers that
demonstrate sophisticated resource acquisition strategies, decision-making
capabilities, and translocation networks that rival those of animal nervous
systems in complexity. The study of mycelial foraging behavior reveals fungi as
intelligent biological agents that optimize resource capture, allocate biomass
strategically, and integrate information across large spatial scales through their
network architecture. This perspective has transformed our understanding of fungal
ecology and has implications for everything from forest management to computer
network design.

## Network Architecture

Fungal mycelia grow as branching networks of hyphae that explore and colonize
their environment. The branching pattern is not random — it follows mathematical
rules that optimize coverage of space while minimizing the total length of
connections. When a mycelium encounters a resource (organic matter, mineral
nutrients), it responds by redirecting growth toward the resource, thickening
the connecting hyphae, and establishing persistent transport pathways. Over time,
the [[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]] develops differentiated structures: thin, rapidly growing
exploratory hyphae at the colony margin for resource discovery, and thicker,
more persistent cords, strands, or rhizomorphs for long-distance translocation
between resource patches.

The network architecture of cord-forming fungi such as Marasmius and
Armillaria has been studied extensively using microcosm experiments and
mathematical modeling. These studies reveal that [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]] can
reorganize themselves in response to changing resource distributions —
strengthening connections to productive patches and abandoning connections to
depleted ones — in a manner analogous to the adaptive optimization observed in
neural networks and transportation infrastructure.

## Resource Translocation

One of the most remarkable capabilities of mycelial networks is the ability to
translocate materials — carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, water, and even heavy
metals — over distances of centimeters to meters. This translocation occurs
through cytoplasmic streaming within hyphae, bulk flow through hollow cords, and
osmotic gradients along concentration differentials. The [[brassinosteroid-physiological-mechanisms-cell-elongation-division]]
include both passive diffusion and [[diffusion-osmosis-and-active-transport-in-plants]] processes that require
metabolic energy.

Resource translocation enables several ecologically important functions. Carbon
can be moved from productive zones (where decomposition yields energy) to
exploration zones (where hyphae extend into new territory), subsidizing the
costs of foraging in nutrient-poor environments. Nitrogen can be translocated
from nutrient-rich to nutrient-poor patches, supporting growth in otherwise
unfavorable conditions. Water can be redistributed within the mycelial network,
buffering local drying events and maintaining hyphal turgor across heterogeneous
moisture conditions.

## Decision-Making and Learning

Experiments with saprotrophic fungi (particularly Physarum polycephalum, a
slime mold, and cord-forming basidiomycetes) have demonstrated that mycelial
networks can "solve" spatial optimization problems. In classic experiments,
Physarum placed in a maze with food at two exits reorganizes its network to find
the shortest path between the food sources, abandoning longer connections. When
food sources are distributed across a landscape at varying distances, the fungus
allocates biomass to create an efficient transport network that balances path
length against reliability, just as human engineers design transportation
grids.

These observations have led researchers to propose that mycelial networks
exhibit a form of biological computation — processing information about resource
distribution through the dynamics of growth, branching, and network reorganization.
The decision rules appear to be implemented through local interactions between
hyphal tips and their immediate chemical environment, without any centralized
control mechanism. This distributed processing architecture is being studied as
a model for developing resilient, self-organizing computational and
infrastructure systems.

## Patch Connectivity and Resource Sharing

In forest ecosystems, mycelial networks can connect multiple resource patches —
leaf litter accumulations, woody debris, and soil organic matter — into an
integrated foraging system. The ability to share resources across patches has
several ecological consequences. Carbon fixed in one location can be redistributed
through the network, supporting fungal growth and decomposition activity in areas
where local carbon supplies are insufficient. This resource sharing can buffer the
effects of spatial heterogeneity in resource distribution, enabling fungi to
persist in landscapes where resources are patchy and unpredictable.

[[dighton-mycorrhizal-networks-carbon-transfer]] extend this resource-sharing capacity to include plants.
Common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs) connecting multiple plant individuals can
facilitate carbon transfer from dominant canopy trees to shaded understory
seedlings, nutrient transfer from nutrient-rich to nutrient-poor plants, and
even signaling between plants through the fungal network. While the ecological
significance of CMN-mediated resource transfer remains debated, the capacity for
inter-plant resource sharing through fungal networks has been demonstrated under
controlled conditions.

## Foraging Strategies and Life History

Different fungal species employ distinct foraging strategies that correspond to
their life history strategies and environmental niches. "Phalanx" strategists
produce dense, slowly expanding networks with high biomass investment per unit
area, maximizing resource capture in stable, resource-rich environments. "Guerrilla"
strategists produce rapidly extending, sparsely branched networks that quickly
cover large areas, maximizing encounter rates with new resources in disturbed or
patchy environments. These strategies parallel the r-K selection continuum in
animal ecology and help explain the distribution of fungal species across
environmental gradients. See fungal-life-history-strategies for a comprehensive
treatment of fungal life history theory.

## Applications and Implications

Mycelial foraging behavior has practical implications for bioremediation (where
fungal networks can be directed toward contaminated zones), agriculture (where
mycelial networks contribute to soil health and plant nutrition), and materials
science (where mycelial growth patterns inspire self-organizing materials and
structures). The fungal mycelium is increasingly recognized as a model biological
system for studying distributed intelligence, network optimization, and
emergent collective behavior.
