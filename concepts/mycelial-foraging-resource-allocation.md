## Resource Translocation

One of the most remarkable capabilities of mycelial networks is the ability to
translocate materials — carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, water, and even heavy
metals — over distances of centimeters to meters. This translocation occurs
through cytoplasmic streaming within hyphae, bulk flow through hollow cords, and
osmotic gradients along concentration differentials. The [[diffusion-osmosis-and-active-transport-in-plants]] processes that require
metabolic energy.

Resource translocation enables several ecologically important functions. Carbon
can be moved from productive zones (where decomposition yields energy) to
exploration zones (where hyphae extend into new territory), subsidizing the
costs of foraging in nutrient-poor environments. Nitrogen can be translocated
from nutrient-rich to nutrient-poor patches, supporting growth in otherwise
unfavorable conditions. Water can be redistributed within the mycelial network,
buffering local drying events and maintaining hyphal turgor across heterogeneous
moisture conditions.
