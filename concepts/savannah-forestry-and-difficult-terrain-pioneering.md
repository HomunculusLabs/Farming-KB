# Savannah Forestry and Difficult Terrain Pioneering

Savannah forestry and difficult terrain pioneering are critical components of permaculture design in tropical and subtropical regions where overgrazing, fire, and destructive land-use practices have degraded native forests. These techniques focus on restoring productive tree cover on exhausted grasslands, landslide zones, volcanic areas, and lava fields.

## Overview

When overgrazing combined with fire or cut-and-burn forestry destroys native forests — particularly in the wet-dry tropics — closed grassland species develop, blocking tree seedbeds and preventing effective land management. The result is often a depauperate grassland with low stock carrying capacity, patches of bare and eroded soils, and acidic conditions (pH 4–4.5). Tough tropical forage grasses like blady grass (*Imperata cylindrica*) become nearly impenetrable barriers to reforestation.

## Pioneering on Difficult Terrain

### Landslide and Volcanic Areas

On man-made and natural landslide or volcanic areas in the tropics, the first step is to pocket the area with soil-mulch mixtures. Coconut and macadamia nut husks are excellent materials for establishing pioneer species. Key pioneer species for these conditions include:

- **Inga edulis** — fast-growing leguminous tree providing shade and mulch
- **Leucaena leucocephala** — versatile nitrogen-fixing pioneer
- **Acacia mearnsii** and other Acacia species — rapid canopy closure
- **Scalesia pedunculata** — Galápagos endemic pioneer
- **Prosopis pallida** — drought-tolerant legume
- **Dolichos** and **Desmodium** — climbing and trailing legumes

It is better to plant small assemblies of species rather than spacing out individual specimens. This grouped approach creates microclimates and mutual support that accelerates establishment.

### Lava Field Rehabilitation

A heavy spiked roller can crush new a'a lava (soft Hawaiian lava close to pumice in consistency). Crushed lava becomes accessible and easily weathered into soil. Soil pockets should be supplemented with trace elements:

- Boron
- Manganese
- Zinc
- Copper
- Molybdenum

### Moisture Harvesting from Sea Air

Near the sea and on islands, night air condenses on the sea-facing side of stones, which develop richer moss-algae-lichen flora than their inland sides. Pockets of vegetation similarly condense sea vapors. Islands and sea coasts therefore develop dry and wet sides suited to different plant species.

A ragged or spiky forest canopy — where palms and tall pines or fruit trees rise above the general canopy layer — captures more condensation from sea air than a level, closed canopy. The saturated winds sweeping off tropical seas carry heavy moisture loads available as dew on grasslands, but tree canopies trap this moisture far more effectively through myriad leaf surfaces.

Even small garden tree patches produce condensation "rain" on clear nights when a sea wind blows. Water channels down leaf midribs to fibrous trunks of palms and tree ferns. Once vegetation is cleared, effective precipitation drops dramatically, rivers cease to flow, and the land becomes truly dry.

## Savannah Forestry Techniques

Given annual rainfall of 60–150 cm, a set of rough pioneer legumes can rehabilitate exhausted sites, including mine spoils and road embankments. Direct seeding in scratch holes or chiselled strips leads to fast establishment of pioneer species.

### The Role of Pioneer Legumes

When legumes are established, leaf-drop and root nodulation re-establish soil fertility. The canopies of **Acacia auriculiformis** or **A. mangium** shade out and kill grasses, reducing fire intensity until it is eventually eliminated. These pioneer legumes serve multiple functions:

- Nurse crop for high-value timber seedlings
- Firewood source
- Pulp timber
- Mulch production
- Honey source (nectar for bees)

As nurse crops, they enable the introduction of high-value timber such as rosewood, mahogany, and ebony, planted in lines or clearings within the first crop. The system gradually transitions to either high-value forestry or sustainable strip cultivation.

### Nitrogen Diffusion Zones

Nitrogen-fixing tree legumes create zones of nitrogen intensity around their root systems. Nitrogen will diffuse from the site for up to 6 years after a tree is cut, so the soil fertility effects persist well after the life of the tree. Intensity of nitrogen concentration falls off in the outer root zones, creating a gradient that benefits surrounding plantings.

### Species for Nursery Establishment

Species such as **Pterocarpus indicus** or **P. erinaceus** can be first seed-planted in nursery stands, then coppiced for 2-meter quickset planting in bore-holes in the field. Some species can be set out at 10 cm diameter and develop into good timber trees.

## Key Pioneer and Grass-Excluding Species

### Acacia auriculiformis
The most important pioneer for exhausted savannah and tropical soils. Over a very wide range of soils and sites, it:
- Defeats blady grass (*Imperata cylindrica*)
- Restores soil fertility through nitrogen fixation
- Provides firewood
- Acts as a nurse crop for other tree species
- Reduces fire risk by shading out flammable grasses
- Provides good paper pulp
- Coppices and self-seeds readily
- Widely used as a shade and street tree in the tropics

### Acacia mangium
Similar to A. auriculiformis but straight-stemmed, making it better suited to forestry operations.

### Sesbania grandiflora
A fast tropical pioneer notable for:
- Rapid coppicing ability
- Excellent green manure in rice paddies
- Re-invigoration of worn-out land through exceptional nodulation
- Growth to 10 meters with good firewood production
- Wide soil tolerance, extensively used for eroded hill sites
- Edible young leaves, pods, and flowers (36% crude protein)
- Seeds at 40% protein
- Use as light shade crop and vine support
- Exceptional growth rate (frost and wind tender, ~20-year lifespan)

All food from this tree should be cooked before consumption.

### Calliandra calothyrsus
A stick wood coppicing species that:
- Defeats grasses effectively
- Provides abundant firewood
- Repairs exhausted soils and restores fertility

### Dalbergia sissoo
- Salt and frost tolerant
- Fast growing
- Defeats grasses
- Tolerates wide range of soil types
- Can be quickset from large cuttings (as practiced in India)

### Enterolobium cyclocarpum
A durable timber tree with large pods; defeats grasses in Central America.

### Mimosa scabrella
A subtropical pioneer from Brazil that provides good humus and functions as a living fence.

### Samanea saman (Rain Tree)
A very fast-growing large tree of the tropics and subtropics, with sugary pods. Grass grows well beneath its canopy. The wood is valuable and durable.

## Strategic Approach

The only significant barriers to savannah forestry are a lack of tree nurseries and seed sources of appropriate species. This gap itself presents an opportunity for enterprise in the humid tropics. A comprehensive selection of potential species can be found in the National Academy of Sciences publication on tropical legumes.

The broader strategy involves planting leguminous trees along swales and through areas designated for grassland, at 30–100 meter strip spacing. These trees are allowed to grow for 2–3 years with light grazing in the third year, after which managed rotational grazing begins. Forage legumes are drilled or broadcast into established grasses, with approximately 15–18 fields necessary for proper rotation.
