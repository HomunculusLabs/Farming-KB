# Low-Energy Refrigeration

Even the most energy-efficient refrigerators are significant energy consumers. Several strategies can dramatically reduce or eliminate refrigeration energy needs.

## Chest Freezer Conversion

The simplest solution: take a chest freezer and replace the thermostat with a regular refrigerator thermostat. Cold air is heavier and falls — every time you open a vertical fridge, cold air pours out the door. A chest freezer has the door on top, preventing this inefficient escape.

## Freezer Best Practices

- Best temperature: -5°F (-20°C); can go as high as 0°F (-17°C) to save energy
- Keep a few plastic jugs of water in the bottom — extends life during power outages and provides emergency water
- Keep freezer in the coolest room of the house, but NOT where it could freeze (kills the motor)

## Power Outage Strategy

1. **Short outage:** Keep fridge door closed, cover with blankets (except motor vent)
2. **Extended outage:** Pull everything out and build an outside refrigerator:
   - Dig a big hole in the ground
   - Stick a large camping cooler ("ice chest") in the hole
   - Stuff insulating materials (straw, bricks) around it
   - Cover with something heavy to keep animals out
3. **Meat:** Cook it on a fire/barbecue. Cooked meat lasts ~5 days in a fridge vs. ~3 days raw. Salt and dry the rest.

## Non-Electric Refrigeration (Evaporative Cooling)

A long-term, simple solution using clay pots or brick walls. Based on thermodynamics and evaporation, temperatures can stay below 68°F (20°C) without electricity.

### Clay Pot Method (Zeer Pot)
1. Put a large clay pot inside a slightly larger clay pot
2. Leave ~1 inch (2.5 cm) of space between them
3. Fill the space with wet sand — keep continuously wet
4. Cover with a damp cloth
5. The porous clay allows moisture to evaporate from the surface, cooling the interior

### Brick Wall Method
Same principle but larger scale:
1. Build a rectangular box within a slightly larger rectangular brick box
2. Fill space between with damp sand
3. Cover the whole thing with a damp cloth
4. Vegetables can last up to 28 days longer inside

## Additional Cooling Strategies

- Root cellar maintains 32-40°F (0-4°C) — see [[faires-house-design-and-retrofit]]
- Evaporative cooling from the shaderoom (trellis + water mist)
- Cook at night or outside to avoid heating the house
- Spring houses: store food in running water from a spring
- Earth berms: use the ground's stable temperature

## Practical Applications

The principles outlined on this page connect directly to hands-on work in gardens,
farms, and homesteads. Integrating these concepts with complementary techniques from
related disciplines can significantly improve outcomes. Start with small-scale
implementation and scale up as understanding deepens through direct observation.

## Design Considerations

Effective implementation requires careful attention to site-specific factors
including climate, topography, available materials, and household needs. Each
homestead presents unique challenges and opportunities that must be addressed
through thoughtful design rather than generic solutions. The goal is to create
integrated systems where each element serves multiple functions and supports
the others, reducing overall workload while increasing resilience and output.

## Materials and Resources

Sustainable homesteading prioritizes locally available and renewable materials.
This reduces both costs and environmental impact while creating systems that
can be maintained without dependence on distant supply chains. Understanding
the properties and appropriate uses of local materials is a foundational skill
for self-reliant living. Many traditional building and craft techniques were
developed specifically to work with regional materials and climatic conditions.

## Seasonal Planning

The rhythm of seasonal change drives all aspects of homesteading. Planning
activities around seasonal patterns maximizes efficiency and aligns human
effort with natural cycles. Spring preparation, summer production, autumn
harvest, and winter processing each have their own requirements and rhythms.
Understanding these patterns allows for more effective use of time and energy
throughout the year, reducing stress and improving overall system performance.

## Integration with Broader Systems

No homesteading element exists in isolation. Water systems connect to growing
areas, which connect to food preservation, which connects to waste management.
Designing these connections intentionally creates synergies that multiply the
benefits of each individual component. The most successful homesteads are those
where every output becomes an input for another process, closing loops and
minimizing waste while maximizing productivity and self-reliance.
