# Lichenized Fungi — Biology, Ecology, and Diversity
Lichens are composite organisms consisting of a fungal partner (mycobiont)
and a photosynthetic partner (photobiont) that grow symbiotically to form a
coherent structure (thallus). With >13,500 species — about 20% of all
described fungi — lichenized fungi represent one of the most successful
fungal lifestyles.

## The Lichen Symbiosis

### Nature of the Relationship
The fungal-photobiont relationship has been described as:
- **Mutualism:** Both partners benefit; fungus gains carbohydrates,
  photobiont gains desiccation protection
- **Controlled parasitism:** Fungus benefits; photobiont grows more
  slowly than when alone
- **Ecologically mutualistic:** The integrated lichen thallus survives
  in more habitats and is more abundant than either partner alone

### Partners
- **Mycobiont:** The fungal partner (names refer to the fungus)
- **Photobiont:** Alga or cyanobacterium; only ~150-200 species
  participate in lichen symbioses
- An individual thallus usually has one species of each
- Only the fungal partner reproduces sexually within the association
- The partners have independent phylogenies — lichen thallus is a
  product of coevolution

## Morphological Types
Most lichen thalli fall into five general categories:
1. **Leprose:** Mealy particles of intertwined fungal hyphae and algal
   cells
2. **Crustose:** Embedded on or in the surface of the substratum (most
   common)
3. **Squamulose:** Small flakes of thallus
4. **Foliose:** Leaflike, with distinct upper and lower surfaces
5. **Fruticose:** Shrubby or stringlike

## Ecological Characteristics

### Growth and Competition
- Slow growth; small size; long life
- Nonseasonal vegetative morphology
- Mineral nutrition mostly from airborne sources
- Greater tolerance of desiccation than most photosynthetic organisms
- Poor competitors — extreme examples of Grime's stress-tolerator
  lifestyle
- Compete most evenly only with bryophytes

### Distribution
- Found in every terrestrial habitat, including the most extreme
- Dominant primary producers in arctic tundra, Antarctic deserts, and
  arid landscapes
- Epiphytic lichens are abundant on tree bark in humid forests
- Saxicolous (rock-dwelling) species colonize surfaces from sea level
  to alpine summits
- Some species grow on soil, wood, or even artificial substrates

## Reproduction and Dispersal
Lichenized fungi reproduce both sexually and vegetatively. Sexual
reproduction involves [[brassinosteroid-fungal-fruiting-body-morphology]] bodies (apothecia or perithecia)
that produce ascospores. These spores must encounter a compatible
photobiont after germination to form a new lichen — a process that
limits successful establishment. Vegetative reproduction through
soredia (granules of algal cells wrapped in fungal hyphae) and isidia
(cylindrical or branched outgrowths) disperses both partners
simultaneously, ensuring successful reestablishment. Many lichens also
fragment mechanically, with each piece capable of developing into a
new thallus if conditions are favorable.

## Biochemistry and Secondary Metabolites
Lichens produce an extraordinary diversity of secondary metabolites,
known as lichen acids or lichen substances. Over 1000 unique compounds
have been identified, most of which are found nowhere else in nature.
These include usnic acid, atranorin, and various depsides, depsidones,
and dibenzofurans. Many lichen acids serve defensive functions,
deterring herbivory and microbial attack. Some have antibiotic,
antifungal, or antiviral properties. Others influence substrate
chemistry by dissolving minerals from rock surfaces. Lichen chemistry
has historically been important for species identification and remains
a valuable taxonomic character, though modern molecular methods
increasingly complement [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]].

## Sensitivity to Environmental Change
Lichens are widely recognized as bioindicators of environmental quality.
Their reliance on atmospheric sources of moisture and nutrients makes
them exceptionally sensitive to air pollution, particularly sulfur
dioxide and nitrogen compounds. Many species show clear distributional
responses to pollution gradients, declining or disappearing in areas
with poor [[cervantes-air-quality-ventilation]] and recovering as conditions improve. Climate
change is also affecting lichen distributions, with range shifts
documented for numerous species. Their slow growth rates make them
integrators of long-term conditions.

## Human Uses of Lichens
Humans have utilized lichens for millennia. Traditional uses include
dyes (Orcein from Roccella, cudbear from Ochrolechia), food (Iceland
moss as a tonic and thickener), and medicine. Modern applications include
perfume fixatives (oakmoss), fermentation starters in Asian cuisines,
and biomonitoring programs. Lichens contribute to soil formation through
weathering of rock surfaces, making them pioneer organisms in primary
succession. Their ecological importance far exceeds their modest biomass.
