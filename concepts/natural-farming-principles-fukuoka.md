## The Direct-Seeded No-Tillage Method

Fukuoka's core technique is "direct-seeded, no-tillage, winter grain/rice
succession in a clover cover." The annual cycle proceeds as follows:

1. **Autumn:** Broadcast clover and winter grain (barley or wheat) seed over
   the ripening rice heads. Seeds settle into the moist field during harvest.
2. **Rice harvest:** Harvest rice while treading on the young winter grain
   shoots, which are resilient enough to survive compression.
3. **Post-harvest:** Dry rice three days, thresh it, scatter all straw uncut
   over the field. The straw serves as mulch, moisture retainer, and
   eventually decomposes into organic matter.
4. **Optional:** Scatter chicken droppings over the straw if available.
5. **Before New Year:** Form clay pellets containing rice seed and scatter
   over the straw. The clay protects seeds from birds and rodents and
   prevents premature germination.
6. **Late May:** Harvest winter grain while clover grows luxuriantly and
   rice shoots emerge from the clay pellets.
7. **Post-harvest:** Dry, thresh, and scatter winter grain straw uncut,
   beginning the mulching cycle anew.
8. **June:** Flood the field for 4 to 5 days to weaken the clover and give
   rice seedlings a competitive advantage.
9. **June to July:** Leave the field unirrigated, allowing natural rainfall.
10. **August:** Run water through drainage ditches every 7 to 10 days.

With this method, Fukuoka consistently harvested close to 22 bushels (1,300
pounds) each of winter grain and rice per quarter-acre — roughly comparable to
conventional yields — while requiring only the labor of one or two people for
sowing seed and spreading straw. His goal was to eventually achieve 33 bushels
per quarter-acre through continued refinement.

## Philosophical Foundation

Fukuoka's farming practice is inseparable from his philosophical critique of
modern civilization. His arguments proceed from several fundamental premises:

**Nature cannot be understood through reductionist science.** The living
biosystem is an organic whole that cannot be dissected without killing it.
"Once broken down, it dies." [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]] studies dead, fragmented
nature and then claims to "know," "use," or "conquer" it. Man can never truly
know even a single leaf or a single handful of earth.

**[[fukuoka-illusions-scientific-farming-centrifugal-centripetal-model]] creates its own problems.** Plowing compacts soil,
creating the "need" for further plowing. [[cervantes-chemical-fertilizers-guide]] kill [[natural-diet]]. Traditional farming with draft animals
required ten times as much energy input; small-scale mechanization doubled that;
large-scale mechanization doubled it yet again in geometric progression.

**Even organic farming is insufficient.** Fukuoka was critical of the organic
farming movement, arguing that the elaborate processing and transportation of
[[coleman-compost-and-organic-fertility-management]] materials represented "just another type of scientific
farming." The gains were local and temporary; examined broadly, many efforts
to "protect" ecology through organic methods were actually destructive.
