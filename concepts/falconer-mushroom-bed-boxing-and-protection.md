# Boxing and Protecting Mushroom Beds

The boxing of mushroom beds is a practice employed to
protect the [[falconer-making-up-mushroom-beds]] from cold, draughts, vermin, and
excessive drying. It is especially important in cellars, sheds,
and other structures where the ambient temperature may
not be ideal for mushroom cultivation.

## Purpose of Boxing

Boxing serves multiple purposes in mushroom cultivation.
It confines the heat generated by the fermenting manure,
excludes cold currents and sudden atmospheric changes,
guards against the beds drying too quickly, and protects the
growing mushrooms from mice and rats. When the lid is
opened, the mushrooms are under observation and can be
gathered without any trouble; when shut, they are secure
from cold and vermin.

## Construction Materials

The boxing may consist of any kind of boards for ends and
sides. It should be built about six or ten inches higher than
the top of the beds, so as to give the mushrooms plenty of
headroom. The top of the boxing may be a lid hung on hinges
or straps, or otherwise arranged, to admit of being easily
raised or removed at will.

The lid should be made of light lumber, say one-half inch
thick boards, so that it can be handled with ease during
daily harvesting operations. A light wooden frame covered
with calico or oiled paper would also make a good top for
the boxing, only it would not be proof against much cold,
or rats or mice.

## Boxing in Dwelling House Cellars

In the cellar of a dwelling house, the boxing is particularly
valuable. If the atmospheric temperature of 50 to 60 degrees
cannot be maintained without impairing the usefulness of the
cellar for other purposes, box up the beds tightly, and from
the heat of the bed itself, when thus confined, there usually
will be warmth enough for the mushrooms.

If not, spread a piece of old carpet or matting over the
boxing. This additional insulation is often sufficient to
maintain adequate temperature for successful cropping
without requiring any fire heat.

## Boxing in Greenhouses

In [[falconer-growing-mushrooms-in-sheds]] cultivation, boxing takes different forms.
After the beds are spawned and cased with soil, a covering
of straw litter or hay prevents sudden drying out of the
surface. About the time the young mushrooms commence to
show themselves, the mulching is removed and replaced
with a covering of shutters raised another board's height
above the bed, or with strong calico or plant-protecting
cloth hung curtain-fashion over the beds.

The objection to mushroom beds on greenhouse benches is
their liability to frequent and marked changes of atmospheric
temperature and moisture, and to drying out. Boxing or
curtain-covering mitigates these problems.

## Mulching with Straw and Hay

Covering the beds with hay or straw is another protection
method, particularly valuable for maintaining proper
[[falconer-proper-temperature-for-mushrooms]]. In a cool greenhouse or shed, covering
the beds with hay or boxing over and covering the boxing
with hay or matting keeps a mushroom bed warm and free
from marked changes in temperature.

Mr. Gardner regards 57 degrees as the most suitable
temperature for a mushroom house or cellar, and maintains
that without the aid of fire heat by mulching his bed with
straw. However, this creates some inconvenience when
gathering the mushrooms, as the mulch must be carefully
parted and replaced.

## Frost Protection for Outdoor Beds

For beds in [[falconer-growing-mushrooms-in-sheds]] and outdoor situations, heavier
protection is required during freezing weather:

- Box the beds tightly with well-fitting boards
- Cover the boxing with heavy straw or hay
- Bank earth or litter against the outside walls
- Use mats or carpeting as additional insulation over the
  top of the boxing

The beds should be made deeper in a cool shed than in a
cellar or warm mushroom house, so that they may retain
their heat for a long time. This is essential because the
fermenting manure provides the primary heat source, and
without adequate depth and insulation, cold will penetrate
and stagnate the crop.

## Covering Against Condensation Drip

One of the chief troubles with flat-roofed mushroom cellars
is the drip from the condensed moisture rising from the
beds, and this is more apparent in unheated than in heated
cellars. The wet gathers upon the ceiling and, having no
slope to run off, drips down again.

Oiled paper or calico strung along wise above the upper beds
protects them perfectly; whatever falls upon the
passage-ways upon the floor does no harm. This simple
protection prevents damage to the mushroom crop from
cold drips that can chill and check the growth of the
mushrooms.

## The Importance of Ventilation with Boxing

While boxing confines heat and moisture, it must not
completely exclude fresh air. The cellars can be ventilated
without interfering with the welfare of the mushrooms when
proper boxing is employed. The lid arrangement allows the
grower to ventilate the general cellar space while keeping
the mushroom bed itself protected from cold draughts that
would harm the crop.
