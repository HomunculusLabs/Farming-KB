# Water Quality and Filtration for Cannabis Cultivation

## Overview

Water quality is one of the most critical and often overlooked factors in
cannabis cultivation. The chemical composition of irrigation water directly
affects nutrient availability, root health, and overall plant vigour.
Cervantes emphasises that understanding and managing water quality is
essential for achieving optimal growth, particularly in hydroponic systems
where the nutrient solution is the sole source of plant nutrition. Common
water quality issues include excessive mineral content, chlorine and
chloramine from municipal treatment, incorrect pH, and the presence of
contaminants that interfere with nutrient uptake.

## Water Quality Parameters

Several key parameters define water quality for cannabis cultivation:

### pH

pH is the measure of acidity or alkalinity on a scale of 0-14. Cannabis
generally prefers a root zone pH of 5.5-6.5 in hydroponic systems and
6.0-7.0 in soil. When pH drifts outside this range, essential nutrients
become unavailable to the plant even if they are present in the growing
medium. This condition is known as nutrient lockout and manifests as
deficiency symptoms despite adequate fertilisation.

### Electrical Conductivity (EC)

EC measures the total concentration of dissolved mineral salts in water.
In hydroponic systems, EC is used to gauge nutrient solution strength.
Cervantes provides EC targets for different growth stages, with lower EC
during seedling and early vegetative phases and progressively higher EC
during late vegetative and flowering stages.

### Total Dissolved Solids (TDS)

TDS is a related measure expressed in parts per million (ppm). While less
precise than EC for nutrient management, TDS gives a useful overall picture
of water mineral content. Tap water typically ranges from 100-500 ppm TDS,
while reverse osmosis water is near 0 ppm.

## Municipal Water Problems

Municipal water supplies present several challenges for cannabis growers.
Chlorine and chloramine are commonly added as disinfectants and can harm
beneficial soil microorganisms and root biology. While chlorine can be
removed by allowing water to sit in an open container for 24 hours,
chloramine is more stable and requires chemical neutralisation or
filtration. Cervantes recommends using a carbon filter or adding
ascorbic acid (vitamin C) to neutralise chloramine.

Municipal water may also contain elevated levels of sodium, calcium, and
magnesium that contribute to overall mineral content and can push pH and
EC to undesirable levels. Water softeners that use sodium ion exchange are
particularly problematic, as they replace calcium and magnesium with sodium,
which is toxic to cannabis at elevated levels.

## Reverse Osmosis Filtration

Reverse osmosis (RO) is the most effective method for producing clean
water for cannabis cultivation. RO systems force water through a
semi-permeable membrane under pressure, removing 95-99% of dissolved
minerals, contaminants, and impurities. The resulting water has near-zero
EC and provides a blank slate for precise nutrient solution preparation.

### RO System Components

A quality RO system typically includes a sediment pre-filter to remove
particulate matter, a carbon pre-filter to remove chlorine (which would
damage the RO membrane), the RO membrane itself, and often a deionising
(DI) post-filter for additional purity. Systems are rated by gallons per
day (GPD) of production capacity.

### RO Efficiency and Waste Water

RO systems produce purified water and a concentrated waste stream
(brine) that contains all the removed minerals. Typical systems have a
waste-to-product ratio of 3:1 or 4:1, meaning three to four gallons of
waste water are produced for each gallon of purified water. The waste
water can be directed to non-sensitive garden areas or laundry use.

### When RO Is Necessary

RO water is most beneficial in hydroponic systems where precise nutrient
management is critical, when source water has very high mineral content
(over 300 ppm TDS), or when specific contaminants are present. In soil
gardens with good-quality tap water, RO may be unnecessary. Many growers
blend RO water with tap water to achieve a moderate mineral content while
reducing problematic contaminants.

## Nutrient Solution Management

With clean water as a starting point, nutrient solutions can be precisely
formulated for each growth stage. Cervantes emphasises that the nutrient
solution should be prepared fresh regularly, as pH and EC drift over time
due to plant uptake and microbial activity. In recirculating hydroponic
systems, the solution should be monitored daily and changed completely
every one to two weeks to prevent the accumulation of inactive salts and
organic compounds.

## Salt Buildup and Flushing

Over time, salts accumulate in growing media from repeated nutrient
applications and water evaporation. This salt buildup can raise root zone
EC to toxic levels, causing nutrient lockout, leaf tip burn, and
stunted growth. Cervantes recommends periodic flushing with plain water
or a mild flushing solution to dissolve and remove accumulated salts.
In soil systems, flushing should be done two weeks before harvest to
improve the final smoke quality by removing residual nutrients from the
buds.

## Water Temperature

Water temperature affects dissolved oxygen levels and root health. Cold
water (below 15 degrees Celsius) slows metabolic processes and nutrient
uptake. Warm water (above 25 degrees Celsius) holds less dissolved
oxygen and promotes pathogenic organisms, particularly Pythium root rot.
The ideal irrigation water temperature is 18-22 degrees Celsius. In
hydroponic reservoirs, water chillers may be necessary to maintain this
range, especially during warm periods.
