# Mulch and the Soil Food Web

Mulch is anything placed on top of soil to reduce evaporation, prevent weed growth, and insulate plants. For [[teaming-with-microbes-soil-food-web-trophic-structure-and-succession]] gardeners, organic mulches serve an additional critical function: they feed and house soil organisms, allowing gardeners to establish [[teaming-with-microbes-bacterial-vs-fungal-dominance]] by choosing the right mulch materials and application methods.

## Organic mulch options

Organic mulches are materials that were once alive and can be recycled back into nutrients by soil food web organisms. These include leaves and leaf mold, aged pine needles, grass clippings, aged bark and wood chips, straw, well-rotted manure, seaweed, "almost compost," plant remnants, and paper.

Plastic sheeting may technically be mulch but provides no soil food web benefits.

## Standard mulch benefits

Most gardeners know the standard reasons to use mulch: weed suppression (smothering existing weeds and preventing germination), moisture retention (greatly reducing evaporation), temperature insulation (cooling in heat, warming in cold, preventing premature growth during freeze-thaw cycles), and compaction prevention (protecting soil from heavy rains).

## Soil food web benefits

Beyond the standard benefits, mulch provides nutrients and habitat for soil food web organisms:

- [[teaming-with-microbes-earthworm-ecology-and-vermicomposting]] pull mulch underground for shredding, producing nutrient-rich castings, more worms, worm tunnels, better water retention, and improved aeration
- Micro- and macro-[[teaming-with-microbes-overview]] live in mulches, speeding decay, adding organic content, and attracting other soil food web members
- [[teaming-with-microbes-bacteria-the-smallest-workers]] and [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-the-underground-network]] colonize mulch and begin decomposition
- [[teaming-with-microbes-protozoa-and-nematodes]] follow, completing the nutrient cycling loop

Mulch is not as effective as [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-biology]] for quickly adding diverse microbes — the decay process may not have started, so organic mulches lack compost's variety and numbers of organisms. But mulch excels at establishing [[teaming-with-microbes-bacterial-vs-fungal-dominance]].

## Bacterial vs fungal mulch (Rule #6)

The type of mulch determines which organisms dominate:

**Green mulches** (fresh grass clippings, fresh plant material) support bacteria. They contain simple sugars that attract and feed bacterial populations. Lawn clippings are the most readily available green mulch — avoid clippings from lawns treated with weed killers or pesticides, and from yards where dogs are part of the soil food web. Do not pile grass too thick or it can start to compost and go anaerobic, creating offensive odors or heat that interferes with the soil food web.

**Brown mulches** (aged leaves, wood chips, bark, straw, pine needles) support fungi. Leaves support fungal growth faster than wood chips. Bark chips last 3-4 years because lignin, cellulose, and waxes are difficult for microbes to decay; fungi dominate throughout. Leaves can decompose in 6 months; fungi dominate at the start, but bacteria increase once they get inside the material.

## Placement affects dominance (Rules #7-9)

How and where mulch is placed influences which organisms dominate:

- **Surface mulch** tends to support fungi (Rule #7) — it is easier for fungal hyphae to travel from soil to surface mulch than for bacteria to colonize dry surface material
- **Incorporated mulch** (worked into top few inches of soil) supports bacteria — contact with moist soil favors bacterial colonization
- **Wet, finely ground mulch** speeds bacterial colonization (Rule #8) — more surface area and moisture favor bacteria; some bacteria produce antibiotics that suppress fungal growth once established
- **Coarse, dry mulches** (less than 35% moisture) support fungal activity (Rule #9) — bacteria are more dependent on moisture than fungi

### Practical guidelines

If you want more bacteria: use green mulches that have been ground up and soaked. If you only have brown material and need bacterial dominance, chop it into fine bits and mix some into the top few inches of soil.

If you want fungal activity: use brown leaves or wood chips; do not pulverize or wet them much; place them on the surface.

## Nitrogen tie-up (C:N ratio)

If mulch has abundant carbon but little nitrogen (C:N ratio of 30:1 or greater), decaying microbes use nitrogen from the mulch first, then take nitrogen from soils touching the mulch. This nitrogen "robbing" usually occurs only at the thin soil-mulch interface and rarely affects the rhizosphere.

This is actually another reason mulch controls weeds so well: the biology in mulches ties up nitrogen, sulfur, phosphate, and other nutrients on the soil surface where mulch is put down — unavailable to shallow-rooted weeds while deeper-rooted plants are fine.

Using wood chips 3/4 inch or larger reduces bacterial colonization and minimizes nitrogen immobilization in underlying soils. Mixing brown mulch with organic nitrogen sources (green grass, alfalfa meal) ensures the C:N ratio is adequate.

## Application guidelines

- Apply 2 to 3 inches (5 to 7.5 cm) — thicker layers block moisture and air and can smother [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-and-mycorrhizal-relationships]]
- Do not place mulch snug against stems or trunks — this can cause microbial decay of the plant itself
- Combine with compost: put compost down first, then cover with mulch — compost organisms will inoculate the mulch and begin to decay it
- Pine needles need aging first — they contain terpenes (volatile chemicals) toxic to many plants
- Cedar chips should be avoided for the same reason — high terpene levels
- Peat moss is biologically sterile and should be mixed with other materials

## Growing protozoa with mulch

Soak fresh grass clippings, alfalfa, hay, or straw in dechlorinated water for 3-4 days (with aeration from an aquarium pump) to grow protozoa. This "protozoa soup" ensures nutrient cycling organisms are present alongside the bacteria and fungi in your mulch. Protozoa are essential — without them, nutrients tied up by bacterial and fungal feeding frenzies will not be cycled back to plants.
