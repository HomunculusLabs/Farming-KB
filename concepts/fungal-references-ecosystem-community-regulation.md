## Key References on Fungi and Population/Community Regulation

This page catalogs the foundational references cited in Chapter 5 of Dighton's *Fungi in Ecosystem Processes*, covering fungal roles in population dynamics and community structure regulation. These references span mycorrhizal ecology, biological control, endophyte-plant interactions, and fungal-mediated succession.

## Mycorrhizal Contributions to Community Structure

The role of mycorrhizal fungi in shaping plant community structure has been demonstrated through multiple lines of evidence:

- **Francis & Read (1994, 1995)** established that mycorrhizal fungi contribute significantly to the determination of plant community structure, showing both mutualistic and antagonistic interactions that impact community assembly.
- **Gange, Brown & Sinclair (1993)** demonstrated that vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal (VAM) fungi act as determinants of plant community structure during early succession, highlighting the importance of fungal-plant interactions from the earliest stages of community development.
- **van der Heijden et al. (1998)** showed that different arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal species are potential determinants of plant community structure, establishing that AMF identity matters for community outcomes.
- **Johnson, Tilman & Wedin (1992)** investigated plant and soil controls on mycorrhizal communities, linking belowground fungal assemblages to aboveground vegetation patterns.
- **Newsham, Fitter & Watkinson (1994, 1995)** explored multi-functionality and biodiversity in arbuscular mycorrhizas, and showed that root pathogenic and AM fungi together determine fecundity of asymptomatic plants in the field.

## Mycorrhizal Succession and Functional Ecology

Research on how mycorrhizal fungal communities change through time and across environmental gradients:

- **Hart, Reader & Klironomos (2001)** developed life-history strategies for [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] in relation to their successional dynamics, linking fungal traits to temporal patterns.
- **Last, Dighton & Mason (1987)** described successions of sheathing (ectomycorrhizal) fungi, establishing that fungal communities on roots change predictably through stand development.
- **Jumpponen, Trappe & Cázares (1999, 2002)** documented ectomycorrhizal fungi at Lyman Lake Basin, comparing primary and secondary successional sites and tracking fungal colonization of recently deglaciated terrain.
- **Ohtonen et al. (1999)** examined ecosystem properties and microbial community changes during primary succession on a glacier forefront, linking fungal community development to soil formation processes.

## Ectomycorrhizal Networks and Carbon Transfer

The discovery of common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs) connecting individual plants represents one of the most significant findings in fungal ecology:

- **Simard et al. (1997a,b,c)** demonstrated reciprocal transfer of carbon isotopes between ectomycorrhizal tree species (Betula papyrifera and Pseudotsuga menziesii) in the field, providing evidence for net carbon transfer between different plant species through shared mycorrhizal networks.
- **Read (1998)** popularized the concept of "plants on the web," highlighting the interconnectedness between trees and fungi.
- **Rayner (1998)** described trees and fungi as "fountains of the forest," emphasizing the interconnectedness of belowground fungal networks.
- **Onguene & Kuyper (2002)** demonstrated the importance of the ectomycorrhizal network for seedling survival and ectomycorrhiza formation in Cameroonian rain forests.

## Fungal Endophytes and Plant Defense

Fungal endophytes, particularly clavicipitaceous endophytes in grasses, have been extensively studied for their defensive mutualisms:

- **Marks & Lincoln (1996)** showed that antiherbivore defense mutualisms between fungal endophytes and grasses are enhanced under elevated CO₂ levels.
- **Lane, Christensen & Miles (2000)** reviewed the coevolution of fungal endophytes with grasses and the significance of secondary metabolites in these relationships.
- **Prestidge & Ball (1997)** discussed the "catch 22" of utilizing endophytic fungi for pest management — the same alkaloids that deter herbivores can be toxic to livestock.
- **Wilson & Faeth (2001)** investigated whether fungal endophytes result in selection for leafminer oviposition preference, examining the community-level consequences of endophyte infection.
- **Moy et al. (2000)** identified epiphyllous mycelial nets on leaves of grasses infected by clavicipitaceous endophytes, revealing the physical structures involved in endophyte-mediated defense.

## Biological Control and Mycoparasitism

Fungi serve as both agents and targets of biological control:

- **Jeffries (1997)** reviewed mycoparasitism as an ecological interaction, describing how parasitic fungi regulate populations of other fungi.
- **Kerry & Jaffee (1997)** documented fungi as biological control agents for plant-parasitic nematodes, with particular attention to nematophagous fungi.
- **Hay et al. (1998)** studied the invasion of sheep dung by nematophagous fungi and soil nematodes, illustrating fungal-nematode population dynamics in pastoral systems.
- **Janisiewicz (1996)** explored ecological diversity, niche overlap, and coexistence of antagonists used in biocontrol mixtures for postharvest diseases of apples.
- **Pieckenstain et al. (2001)** demonstrated Epicoccum purpurascens as a biocontrol agent against Sclerotinia head rot of sunflower.
- **Tsahouridou & Thanassoulopoulos (2002)** showed Trichoderma koningii proliferation in the tomato rhizosphere and suppression of damping-off by Sclerotium rolfsii.

## Fungal-Plant Pathogen Interactions

The complex interplay between fungi as pathogens, mutualists, and competitors shapes plant communities:

- **Gilbert, Hubbell & Foster (1994)** demonstrated density and distance-to-adult tree effects of canker disease in moist tropical forests, supporting Janzen-Connell hypothesis predictions.
- **Packer & Clay (2000)** showed that soil pathogens create spatial patterns of seedling mortality in temperate trees, providing a mechanism for maintaining tree species diversity.
- **Hansen & Goheen (2000)** reviewed Phellinus weirii and other native root pathogens as determinants of forest structure and process in western North America.
- **Holah, Wilson & Hansen (1997)** documented impacts of native root-rotting pathogens on successional development of old-growth Douglas fir forests.
- **van der Putten (2000)** discussed pathogen-driven forest diversity, emphasizing how fungal pathogens maintain plant species coexistence.
- **Liu (1995)** demonstrated that vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi reduce Verticillium wilt of cotton, illustrating the protective role of mycorrhizae against soil-borne pathogens.

## Allelopathy and Fungal Metabolites

Fungi produce a wide range of secondary metabolites that influence community interactions:

- **Hättenschwiler & Vitousek (2000)** reviewed the role of polyphenols in terrestrial ecosystem nutrient cycling, many of which are fungal in origin.
- **Lynch (1990)** cataloged microbial metabolites in the rhizosphere, describing the chemical ecology of root-fungus interactions.
- **Garbaye (1991)** described biological interactions in the mycorrhizosphere, including the helper effect where certain bacteria promote mycorrhizal formation.
- **Lawrey (1986, 1989)** documented the biological role of lichen secondary compounds, showing correspondence between antiherbivore and antimicrobial functions.

## Phylloplane and Leaf Surface Fungi

Fungi colonizing leaf surfaces play important roles in plant health and community dynamics:

- **Last & Deighton (1965)** pioneered the study of non-parasitic microflora on living leaf surfaces.
- **Whitham & Schweitzer (2002)** conceptualized leaves as "islands" of spatial and temporal variation, with consequences for herbivores, pathogens, and microbial communities.
- **Omar & Heather (1979)** showed that saprophytic phylloplane fungi can inhibit germination and development of the pathogen Melampsora larici-populina.
- **Schisler (1997)** reviewed the impact of phyllosphere microorganisms on mycoherbicide efficacy and development.

## Zoosporic Fungi and Animal Pathogens

Fungal pathogens affecting animal populations have significant ecological consequences:

- **Kaiser (1998) and Morell (1999)** reported on the chytrid fungus driving global amphibian declines (the fungus was later identified as *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*).
- **Lips (1999)** documented mass mortality and population declines of anurans at an upland site in western Panama.
- **Warkentin, Currie & Rehner (2001)** showed that an egg-killing fungus induces early hatching of red-eyed treefrog eggs, demonstrating host behavioral adaptation to fungal infection pressure.

## Mycorrhizal Effects on Plant Competition and Reproduction

The influence of mycorrhizae extends to plant fitness through effects on competition, growth, and reproduction:

- **Watkinson & Freckleton (1997)** quantified the impact of arbuscular mycorrhiza on plant competition, providing models for incorporating mycorrhizal effects into plant population dynamics.
- **Streitwolf-Engel et al. (2001)** demonstrated the ecological significance of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal effects on clonal reproduction in plants.
- **Heppell et al. (1998)** showed that mycorrhizal infection of Abutilon theophrasti affects competitiveness of offspring, demonstrating transgenerational effects of mycorrhizal colonization.
- **Stanley, Koide & Schumway (1993)** proved that mycorrhizal symbiosis increases growth, reproduction, and recruitment of velvetleaf in the field.
- **Koide, Suomi, Stevens & McCormick (1998b)** investigated interactions between needles of Pinus resinosa and ectomycorrhizal fungi, revealing host tissue-specific responses.

## Animal Dispersal of Mycorrhizal Fungi

The role of animals in dispersing mycorrhizal fungal spores links aboveground and belowground food webs:

- **Kotter & Farentinos (1984a,b)** demonstrated that tassel-eared squirrels form ectomycorrhizae after inoculation with squirrel feces and act as spore dispersal agents for hypogeous mycorrhizal fungi.
- **Trappe & Maser (1976)** showed that spores of Glomus macrocarpus germinate after passage through a rodent digestive tract, establishing that mammalian gut passage can enhance mycorrhizal spore viability.

## Invasive Fungi and Global Movement

The global movement of fungal pathogens threatens forest ecosystems worldwide:

- **Wingfield, Slippers, Roux & Wingfield (2001)** reviewed worldwide movement of exotic forest fungi, especially in the tropics and the southern hemisphere.
- **Rossman (2001)** highlighted a special issue on global movement of invasive plants and fungi.
- **Newbery, Alexander & Rother (2000)** investigated whether proximity to conspecific adults influences ectomycorrhizal tree establishment in rain forest, with implications for invasive pathogen impacts.
