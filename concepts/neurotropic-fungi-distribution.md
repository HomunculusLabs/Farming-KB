## Worldwide Distribution of Neurotropic Fungi

The worldwide distribution of neurotropic (hallucinogenic or psychotropic) fungi encompasses at least 216 known species distributed across all continents, with the greatest diversity concentrated in subtropical and tropical regions of the [[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]] and allies (5 species of Claviceps) plus 2 Cordyceps species. These produce ergot alkaloids including lysergic acid amide and related compounds that have been used medicinally and ritually.

## Biogeographic Patterns

### Southern Hemisphere Dominance

The majority of Psilocybe species occur in or near the [[guzman-panaeolina-foenisecii-psilocybin-controversy-conflicting-chemical-analyses]]
- **Northern hemisphere**: Amanita spp., Cordyceps spp., Psilocybe pelliculosa, P. silvatica
- **Tropical and subtropical**: Psilocybe cubensis, P. subcubensis, Copelandia spp.
- **Alpine and Arctic**: Psilocybe aztecorum at 4000 m elevation in Mexico
- **Endemic**: Many Mexican species (P. muliercula, P. chiapanensis, P. laurae), Colombian species (P. columbiana, P. guatapensis), Brazilian species (P. brasiliensis, P. paulensis)

### Trans-Pacific Connections

Species from Southeast Asia and the South Pacific show close relationships with Mesoamerican and South American species. Psilocybe samuiensis (Thailand) and P. makarorae (New Zealand) are closely related to P. mexicana (Mexico/Guatemala), all belonging to Section Mexicanae. Similarly, P. aucklandii (New Zealand) relates to P. zapotecorum (Mexico/South America) within [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] are volatile compounds that degrade over time. Ohenoja et al. (1987) found 0.014%, 0.67%, and 0.84% psilocybin (dry weight) in herbarium specimens from 1869, 1954, and 1976, respectively. Mexican indigenous knowledge reflects this: specimens kept dried for more than one year are considered ineffective and are discarded.

### Psilocybe cubensis Introduction

An intriguing biogeographic puzzle involves Psilocybe cubensis, which is common in the Americas growing on cattle dung but absent from Europe. Since cattle were introduced to the Americas by the Spanish in the 16th to 17th centuries, Guzman proposed that P. cubensis may have been introduced to the Americas through the slave trade during colonial times. If true, this would make P. cubensis a relatively recent introduction rather than an ancient native species.

## Problems in Identification and Chemistry

### Misidentification

Misidentification of species is a significant problem in the neurotropic fungi literature. Many chemical studies lack proper taxonomic foundations, and in some cases, mixtures of different species were analyzed together. This has led to contradictory reports about the neurotropic properties of several species, undermining confidence in the published chemical data.

### Contradictory Chemical Reports

Panaeolina foenisecii exemplifies the confusion: some studies found psilocybin while others did not. This cosmopolitan species is now considered more likely toxic than neurotropic. Similarly, several polypores and puffballs traditionally used in indigenous ceremonies have been shown to lack neurotropic properties upon chemical analysis.

### Confusion with Non-Neurotropic Species

The Mazatec name "nise" (little bird) is used for both Psilocybe mexicana (neurotropic) and Schizophyllum commune (a common edible species), despite their entirely different properties. Such naming confusion contributes to misidentification in ethnographic reports.
