# Carbon to Nitrogen Ratios

The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C:N ratio) is the single most important concept in [[composting]]. It determines whether your compost pile will heat up and decompose quickly into rich humus, or sit there as a stagnant, smelly mess for months. Understanding C:N ratios allows you to predict how any particular mixture of organic materials will behave in a compost pile and how well it will serve as fertilizer.

## What C:N Ratio Means

Every organic material contains both carbon and nitrogen. Carbon provides the energy that soil microorganisms need to work; nitrogen provides the protein they need to grow and reproduce. The C:N ratio expresses the balance between these two elements.

- **High C:N (carbon-rich)**: Materials like straw, dry leaves, sawdust, paper. These are "browns."
- **Low C:N (nitrogen-rich)**: Materials like fresh manure, kitchen scraps, grass clippings, seedmeal. These are "greens."
- **Ideal C:N for composting**: Around 25:1 to 30:1 — this is roughly the ratio found in the bodies of soil microorganisms themselves

## What Happens at Different Ratios

### Too Much Carbon (High C:N, e.g., 60:1 or higher)

When the pile has too much carbon relative to nitrogen:

- Microorganisms multiply slowly because they lack protein (nitrogen)
- Decomposition is very slow
- The pile may not heat up at all
- Finished compost may be low in nitrogen (nitrogen is "tied up" by microbes using what little N is available)
- Result: After months, you get a pile of partially decomposed material, not finished compost

### Too Much Nitrogen (Low C:N, e.g., below 15:1)

When the pile has too much nitrogen relative to carbon:

- Microorganisms have plenty of protein but not enough energy
- Excess nitrogen is released as ammonia gas (smell!)
- The pile may heat up impressively but much of the nitrogen is lost to the air
- Result: Compost that is lower in nitrogen than it should be, and a stinky process

### The Sweet Spot (25:1 to 30:1)

At the ideal ratio:

- Microorganisms have balanced food supply
- Decomposition proceeds rapidly
- The pile heats to proper thermophilic temperatures (130-160°F / 55-71°C)
- Nitrogen is conserved (used by microbes and released when they die)
- Maximum conversion of raw materials to stable humus
- Minimal odor

The formula involves balancing the total carbon and total nitrogen. A practical shortcut: for every 3 parts dry leaves by volume, add about 1 part fresh manure. This roughly achieves the target ratio.

### The Volume Rule of Thumb

- **3 parts brown to 1 part green** is a good starting point for most mixtures
- Adjust based on the specific materials — sawdust needs much more green than straw
- When in doubt, err on the side of more nitrogen (it's easier to add carbon later)

## Nitrogen as the Limiting Factor

In most home composting situations, nitrogen is the limiting factor. Most gardeners have plenty of carbon (fall leaves, straw, paper) but limited nitrogen sources. This is why:

- Seedmeal and manure are so valuable for composting
- Grass clippings are prized (high nitrogen when fresh)
- Kitchen scraps help but are usually insufficient in quantity
- Pure leaf piles compost slowly unless supplemented

## What This Means for Compost as Fertilizer

### The Nitrogen Tie-Up Problem

Materials with very high C:N ratios (sawdust, wood chips, paper) will actually take nitrogen FROM the soil as they decompose if mixed into the soil directly. Soil microorganisms use available soil nitrogen to break down the carbon, temporarily depriving plants. This is why:

- Sawdust should never be dug into garden soil as an amendment
- Wood chip mulches are fine on the surface but should not be incorporated
- Paper and cardboard are better recycled or composted properly before use

### Compost Quality and C:N

The C:N ratio of the starting materials directly affects the nutritional quality of the finished compost:

- Compost made from high-nitrogen materials will be richer in plant nutrients
- Compost made from mostly leaves and straw will be lower in nutrients
- Compost made from a balanced mix will have the best overall properties

## Practical Composting Guidelines

1. **Aim for 25-30:1** C:N ratio in your initial mix
2. **Use a variety of materials** for balanced nutrition
3. **Chop or shred** materials to increase surface area
4. **Moisten** the pile to about 50-60% moisture (like a wrung-out sponge)
5. **Turn the pile** regularly to introduce oxygen
6. **Monitor temperature** — proper composting should reach 130-160°F (55-71°C)
7. **Don't worry about exact ratios** — approximate is fine for home composting
