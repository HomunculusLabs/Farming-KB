# Winter Harvesting Techniques and Labor Efficiency

Harvesting crops from unheated winter greenhouses in cold climates presents unique challenges that differ fundamentally from summer production. The grower must manage narrow temperature windows, maintain quality through careful washing and packing, and sustain a pace of work that meets delivery commitments — all while dealing with cold fingers and frozen infrastructure. Success depends on systematic scheduling, purpose-built tools, and a culture of efficiency.

## The Winter Harvest Window

In unheated greenhouses in cold-climate areas, harvest timing revolves around the daily temperature cycle. After a below-zero night, growers must wait until the interior air temperature rises above 32°F before beginning work. Even on the coldest days, a reliable harvest window exists from approximately **10:00 AM to 3:00 PM** — before 10:00 everything is still thawing, and after 3:00 it starts to freeze up again.

On bright sunny days, the harvest window is wider and the temperature inside the houses can be downright pleasant. Having at least one **[[cool-greenhouse]]** (as opposed to a fully cold/unheated house) provides a significant efficiency advantage: the crew can begin harvesting salad crops in the cool house during early morning, then move to the cold houses for the main harvest, and return to the cool house in late afternoon for radishes and turnips.

### Supplemental Heat

A propane-fired salamander heater is kept in reserve for rare occasions when cold, snowy, and cloudy conditions prevent the sun from warming unheated houses above freezing. On average, this heater is used only about once per winter. It has never been run just to warm the crew — the financial discipline of not heating for comfort is notable.

## Staying Warm While Working

The practical realities of cold-weather harvesting require specific gear and techniques:

- **Clothing**: A vest is preferred over a parka — it keeps the core warm while allowing arm mobility for cutting. Bulky outerwear interferes with the precise, rapid hand movements required for [[mesclun]] harvesting.
- **Cutting tools**: Small, sharp Victorinox knives (7 inches overall, 3-inch blade), sharpened frequently.
- **Containers**: Food-grade five-gallon buckets for cut leaves.
- **Hand protection**: Neoprene gloves (cold-weather kayaking type) are available, but even the thinnest are awkward for picking. Many experienced harvesters wear one glove on the knife hand and leave the other hand bare, soaking it in warm water at every trip back to the packing area with full buckets.

### Re-covering Greenhouses

Inner covers must be replaced immediately after harvesting. Once temperatures drop below freezing, light row-cover material freezes to itself and becomes impossible to place back over supporting wickets without tearing. PVA covers (discussed in the broader greenhouse management context) are stronger and less problematic, but immediate re-covering remains best practice regardless of cover material.

## Washing and Packing

Food safety and presentation are paramount since the product is sold fresh and raw.

### Hygiene Standards

- The washing and packing area is scrubbed down with hot water after each harvest.
- All water comes from a drilled well tested annually.
- Hand washing is mandatory at all stages.
- Hats or hairnets are worn during washing and packing.

### Washing Technique

Each salad ingredient in a mix is washed **separately**. This makes it far easier to spot bad leaves or occasional detritus when there is only one shape and color of leaf to inspect, compared to trying to pick through a mixed pile.

### The "Pac Man" Mixer

After washing and spin-drying, individual salad ingredients are combined in a custom mixing barrel:

- **Dimensions**: 48 inches long, 36 inches in diameter, constructed from wood slats with plywood ends.
- **Design**: Mounted horizontally in a frame with a handle at one end. The barrel is divided into two halves, hinged on the long side, opening like a clamshell.
- **Capacity**: Up to 80 pounds of salad per batch.
- **Operation**: Fill the bottom half, close and latch the top, then turn ten slow revolutions in each direction (clockwise and counterclockwise). The ingredients are raised as the barrel side goes up and gently dropped to the bottom, producing a thorough but gentle mix.

## Working Efficiently

The economics of small-scale farming demand relentless attention to labor efficiency. Originally a two-person operation (Eliot and Barbara Coleman), the farm expanded to five summer employees (fewer in winter) while maintaining quality standards. The constant pressure is to avoid further staff expansion by becoming more efficient.

### Efficiency Analysis

Every aspect of the harvesting, washing, and packing operation is subject to continuous scrutiny. A notable example: installing larger-diameter pipes for sink filling and draining and raising water pressure **cut drain-and-refill time by 75%** between washing different leaf types. For maximum efficiency, sinks must be in continual use — one person begins washing as soon as the first buckets arrive from the field.

### Speed and Quality

Harvesting requires quick hands, a quick mind, and a no-nonsense attitude. New employees must understand:

- **Minimize hand travel**: Keep the harvest container as close as possible to the harvesting hand. On average, 40% of a harvester's time is spent moving the hand to the container — reducing this distance directly increases output.
- **Batch harvesting**: When cutting spinach leaf by leaf, hold as many cut leaves in the hand as possible before dropping them in the container. More leaves per drop means fewer trips.
- **Talk and hustle**: If employees are talkers, they must be able to converse and maintain pace simultaneously.
- **Quality checking**: Speed cannot come at the expense of quality. Each leaf should be visually inspected as it is cut.

### Managing Slow Workers

When verbal explanation, demonstration, and working alongside an employee do not improve their pace, piece-rate pay may be the only effective communication tool. While Coleman dislikes the idea of piecework, it can convey the business reality that labor costs must be proportional to output.

## The Planting-Harvest Cycle

The most important skill for a four-season grower is maintaining a **rigorous planting schedule**. As Coleman quotes from Andrew Marvell's "To His Coy Mistress": "But at my back I always hear / time's wingéd chariot hurrying near." The next harvest follows quickly after the previous one, and you cannot harvest what you have not planted. The daily or weekly planting schedule must be as integral to the grower's life as the harvest schedule itself.

Keeping careful notes on seed-to-harvest days throughout the year and preparing a day-by-day planting calendar for each crop is the foundation of consistent, timely harvests.

## Mechanized Harvesting for Small Scale

To compete with motorized harvesters used by large-scale producers, small growers are developing human-powered and battery-powered harvesting tools. Johnny's Selected Seeds sells the **Greens Harvester** — a cloth basket collector mounted behind a bow-saw frame holding a scalloped knife blade. The ideal tool for small-scale operations would be:

- Light enough for one-handed use
- Easy to carry between beds
- Adjustable cutting height above ground
- Wide enough to cover half of a 30-inch (75 cm) bed in one pass
- Reasonably priced

Development of such tools represents one of the most impactful areas for innovation in small-scale vegetable farming.
