# Fukuoka Natural Farming vs Organic Farming

Masanobu Fukuoka drew a sharp and fundamental distinction between his [[fukuoka-natural-farming]] and what is commonly called organic farming. While the two approaches share a rejection of synthetic chemicals, Fukuoka argued that organic farming is "just another type of scientific farming" -- an outgrowth of the same mechanistic, interventionist mindset that created the problems it seeks to solve. Understanding this distinction is essential to grasping Fukuoka's philosophy and practice.

## The Core Critique

Fukuoka's most direct statement on organic farming came early in "The Natural Way of Farming":

> "Even organic farming, which everyone is making such a big fuss over lately, is just another type of scientific farming. A lot of trouble is taken to move organic materials first here then there, to process and treat. But any gains to be had from all this activity are local and temporal gains. In fact, when examined from a broader perspective, many such efforts to protect the natural ecology are actually destructive."

Later, he wrote that organic farming "is an outgrowth of scientific farming that is oriented toward petroleum energy-intensive commercial agribusiness" and that it "only serves as a temporary stopgap, a brief respite" -- essentially a rehashing of the animal-based traditional farming of the past that would eventually be "swallowed whole and assimilated by scientific agriculture."

## Three Ways of Farming

Fukuoka articulated his classification of farming methods using a Buddhist framework of three paths:

### Mahayana Natural Farming (Pure Natural Farming)

This is Fukuoka's ideal: farming that relies entirely on the forces and resources of nature, requiring no human knowledge or action. It operates on a plane fundamentally different from scientific farming. As Fukuoka put it, scientific farming is "the sum of knowledge and forces extracted from nature by the human intellect," which amounts to "finite human knowledge." In contrast, nature encompasses "vast, boundless, perfect knowledge and power" that human knowledge can never equal.

### Hinayana Natural Farming (Organic Farming)

Fukuoka placed organic farming in the same "relative world" as scientific farming. Both are derived from nature as verified through discriminating knowledge. Organic farming attempts to cast off human knowledge and make the greatest possible use of natural forces, while scientific farming adds human knowledge and action. But both remain within the realm of relativity -- they still involve human intervention, just to different degrees.

Fukuoka compared Hinayana natural farming to the "one-sword school" directed toward the center, while scientific farming is the "two-sword school" directed outward. Mahayana natural farming is the "unmoving no-sword school" with which comparison is impossible. He described the difference using a swordsmanship analogy: scientific farming uses all possible means, increasing the number of swords; Hinayana natural farming tries to get the best results while reducing its swords; Mahayana natural farming does entirely without swords.

### Scientific Farming

Conventional agriculture that draws from natural forces and attempts, through human knowledge and technology, to produce results that "eclipse nature." Fukuoka argued this is inherently incomplete because human knowledge is always limited to small pockets of time and space. "Scientific farming blindly traces spiraling cycles in the tracks of science. Today's new technology will become the dated technology of tomorrow."

## The Compost Example

Fukuoka used compost preparation to illustrate the practical difference between organic and natural farming. In organic farming, organic materials such as straw, grasses, and seaweed are fermented through careful preparation -- frequent turning of the pile, addition of water, nitrogenous fertilizers, lime, superphosphate, rice bran, and manure -- to speed decomposition and produce a "safe, effective fertilizer."

Fukuoka argued that all this trouble achieves "at most ten to twenty percent" acceleration in decomposition compared to simply applying raw materials directly to the field. His [[fukuoka-compost-unnecessary-philosophy]] held that "while compost itself is not without value, the composting of organic materials is fundamentally useless." Instead, he scattered fresh straw and organic matter directly onto his fields, letting nature's decomposition proceed at its own pace.

## The Nitrogen Question

Fukuoka's analysis of nitrogen flows reveals the fundamental difference in approach. He observed that about seventy percent of the nitrogenous components absorbed by rice and barley are supplied directly by the soil, while about thirty percent are furnished artificially by fertilization. If all the straw and chaff from threshed grain were returned to the fields, farmers would need to apply at most fifteen percent of the nitrogenous components required.

Organic farming addresses this by composting organic materials and applying them as prepared fertilizer. Natural farming addresses it by returning all straw to the field and maintaining a [[fukuoka-white-clover-ground-cover]] that fixes nitrogen continuously. The result is the same -- adequate nitrogen for crop growth -- but the natural approach requires no processing, no transportation of materials, and no specialized knowledge.

## Philosophical Differences

The distinction goes deeper than technique. Fukuoka identified several fundamental philosophical divergences:

**Nature as perfect vs. nature as improvable**: Natural farming holds that nature is already perfect and complete. Scientific and organic farming both proceed from the assumption that nature is imperfect and requires human improvement through knowledge and effort. This connects to Fukuoka's [[fukuoka-nature-perfect-imperfect-philosophical-perspective]].

**Non-intervention vs. substituted intervention**: Organic farming substitutes organic inputs for synthetic ones, but still operates on the paradigm of intervening in natural processes. Natural farming seeks to eliminate intervention entirely, trusting that nature "grows the rice and wheat" without human help.

**Local/temporal gains vs. holistic balance**: Organic farming's gains, in Fukuoka's view, are "local and temporal." A compost pile feeds crops in one place at one time, but the organic materials were taken from elsewhere, depleting that source. Natural farming creates closed, self-sustaining systems where nothing is imported or exported.

**Knowledge-dependent vs. knowledge-free**: Organic farming requires considerable knowledge -- what to compost, how to compost it, when to apply it. Fukuoka's natural farming seeks to eliminate the need for knowledge altogether, operating instead on what he called the [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-philosophy-and-practice]] (nothingness).

## Practical Implications

The practical difference between organic farming and Fukuoka's natural farming can be seen in every aspect of field management:

| Aspect | Organic Farming | Fukuoka Natural Farming |
|--------|----------------|------------------------|
| Fertilizer | Prepared compost, organic amendments | None; straw returned directly to field |
| Tillage | Often reduced, but still practiced | Complete no-tillage |
| Weeding | Manual cultivation, flame weeding | No weeding; managed ground cover |
| Pest control | Organic sprays, beneficial insects | No pest control; ecosystem balance |
| Seeds | Often purchased organic seed | Farm-saved, locally adapted |
| Composting | Central practice | Called "fundamentally useless" |
| Labor | Significant, though chemical-free | Minimal; "sowing seed and spreading straw" |

## The Direction of Research

Fukuoka's critique extends to the very direction of organic farming research. While organic farming has developed elaborate composting methods, organic pest control sprays, and certification systems, Fukuoka argued that all of this represents movement in the wrong direction -- toward more complexity, more knowledge requirements, and more dependence on human intervention rather than less.

He contrasted this with his own approach of progressive simplification: "I eliminate unnecessary practices, expenditures, and labor by telling myself, 'I don't need to do this, I don't need to do that.' After thirty years at it, I have managed to reduce my labor to essentially just sowing seed and spreading straw." The ideal is not better organic farming but the elimination of farming as a separate human activity altogether -- a return to the recognition that nature grows the crops.

## The Energy and Knowledge Question

Fukuoka's critique of organic farming also involves energy economics. While organic farming reduces dependence on petroleum-based chemical inputs, it often increases dependence on human and animal labor. Composting, hand weeding, and organic pest management all require significant energy expenditure. Fukuoka asked whether this represents a genuine improvement or merely a substitution of one form of energy for another.

In natural farming, the energy for crop production comes entirely from the sun, the rain, and the biological activity of the soil ecosystem. The farmer's energy investment is limited to sowing, [[fukuoka-straw-mulch-philosophy]], and occasional water management. This creates what Fukuoka called "a farm that does not require energy inputs" -- a system that is not merely sustainable but truly self-sustaining.

Fukuoka also questioned the knowledge requirements. Organic farming demands that the farmer learn composting recipes, organic spray formulations, and complex crop rotation schedules. Natural farming, by contrast, seeks to reduce the farmer's knowledge requirements to near zero. "No farming method that is dependent on knowledge of techniques can be called natural," he wrote. The [[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-philosophy-and-practice]] aims at the dissolution of knowledge itself.

## Convergence and Divergence

Fukuoka drew a structural contrast between the trajectories of natural and scientific farming. The objective of natural farming is "non-action and a return to nature; it is centrifugal and convergent." Scientific farming "breaks away from nature with the expansion of human wants and desires; it is centripetal and divergent." Because this outward expansion cannot be stopped, scientific farming is "doomed to extinction." The addition of new technology only makes it more complex and diversified, generating ever-increasing expense and labor.

This framework applies equally to organic farming, which Fukuoka placed in the same divergent trajectory. Organic farming still moves outward -- toward new composting methods, new organic sprays, new certification standards -- even while claiming to move back toward nature. Natural farming, by contrast, moves inward through simplification and renunciation of technique. As Fukuoka summarized: "not only is natural farming simple, it is also economical and labor-saving."

## Yield and Quality Comparison

Fukuoka challenged the common assumption that scientific methods produce higher yields. "People believe that modern agriculture can both improve productivity and increase yields. What a misconception. The truth of the matter is that the yields provided by scientific farming are smaller than the yields attainable under the full powers of nature." What appear to be high-yield technologies are in reality "merely endeavors by man to artificially restore full productivity after he has hamstrung nature so that it cannot exercise its full powers."

He extended this critique to food quality: "Ever since man deluded himself into thinking that nature can be understood by being broken down and analyzed, scientific farming has produced artificial, deformed food." Modern agriculture, by making "quantitative and qualitative changes in certain aspects of nature, has managed only to fabricate synthetic food products that are crude, expensive, and further alienate man from nature." The fruit produced by scientific methods, he wrote, is "always large -- even unnaturally so -- in a relative sense, but invariably has grave flaws."

## Organic Farming and the Illusion of Food Quality

Fukuoka's critique extended to the quality of food produced by organic methods. While organic farming avoids synthetic chemicals, it still operates within the same paradigm of manipulating nature for human ends. The fruit produced by organic methods may avoid pesticide residues, but Fukuoka argued that the fundamental problem lies deeper -- in the very assumption that humans must "grow" crops rather than allow nature to produce them.

He noted that scientific farming constantly practices "the unnatural without the slightest concern," and that this unnaturalness "leads directly to incompleteness, which is why its results are always distorted and at best of only local utility." Organic farming, sharing the same underlying framework, produces results that are likewise limited and local -- even when they appear superior to conventional methods. The [[fukuoka-critique-organic-farming-ecology]] is that organic farming's achievements, like those of scientific farming, "may excel in a restricted sense but are invariably inferior in all other ways."

## The Economic Trap

Fukuoka observed that the mechanization championed by scientific agriculture had not truly reduced labor but merely displaced it. "Farmers must use the extra hours away from their fields to earn outside income to help pay for their equipment. All they have done is exchange their work in the fields for a job in some company; they have traded the joy of working outdoors in the open fields for dreary hours of labor shut up inside a factory." This economic dynamic applies with equal force to organic farming, which often requires more hand labor than conventional methods while producing food that commands premium prices -- prices that may not reflect genuine quality improvements.

The reduction in labor brought about by scientific farming "has succeeded only in forcing farmers off the land." The same risk exists for organic farming when it becomes dependent on costly inputs and certification systems that favor larger operations over small self-sufficient farms.
