# Mycoremediation of Pesticide and Herbicide Contamination

Fungi possess remarkable abilities to degrade a wide array of synthetic pesticides and herbicides through diverse biochemical mechanisms. Fungal degradation of these agrochemicals offers a biologically based approach to remediate contaminated soils, groundwater, and agricultural runoff.

## Fungal Transformation Mechanisms

Fungi transform pesticides through several major biochemical pathways:

- **Alkylation and dealkylation**: Transfer or removal of alkyl groups from pesticide molecules
- **Amide and ester hydrolysis**: Cleavage of amide and ester bonds by fungal hydrolases
- **Dehalogenation**: Removal of halogen atoms (chlorine, bromine) from halogenated pesticides
- **Dehydrogenation and hydroxylation**: Introduction of hydroxyl groups via cytochrome P450 systems
- **Ether cleavage**: Breaking of ether linkages in pesticide structures
- **Ring cleavage**: Oxidative opening of aromatic rings by ligninolytic enzymes
- **Oxidation and reduction**: Modification of oxidation states by peroxidases and reductases
- **Conjugate formation**: Binding of pesticide metabolites to fungal cell components

Cometabolism is the best-known transformation pathway for pesticides in fungi, where the pesticide is incidentally degraded during metabolism of a primary growth substrate.

## Insecticide Degradation

### Endosulfan

[[phanerochaete-chrysosporium]] metabolizes endosulfan through both hydrolytic and oxidative pathways, producing metabolites including a diol, hydroxyether, lactone, and ether. [[trichoderma-harzianum]] also degrades endosulfan efficiently and is considered an excellent candidate for rhizosphere introduction due to its ability to thrive in various plant root zones and tolerate fungicides like benomyl.

### Lindane

Lindane (gamma-hexachlorocyclohexane) is mineralized by P. chrysosporium and by a subtropical white-rot fungus designated DSPM95, with manganese peroxidase (MnP) and laccase involvement in the degradation pathway. Pleurotus ostreatus achieved 25.8 mg/g degradation of lindane. Tekere et al. (2002) demonstrated lindane biodegradation in both batch and packed-bed bioreactor systems using a subtropical white-rot fungus.

### DDT

DDT (1,1,1-trichloro-2,2-bis(4-chlorophenyl)ethane) and its metabolite DDE are biodegraded by P. chrysosporium. The rate of DDT degradation is influenced by culture parameters including nitrogen levels and temperature. The degradation pathway involves reductive dechlorination and oxidative steps.

## Organophosphorus Insecticide Degradation

### Malathion

Aspergillus oryzae transforms malathion to beta-monoacid and dicarboxylic acid metabolites. Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. pisi cutinase has been shown to biodegrade and detoxify malathion, providing an enzymatic pathway for its breakdown.

### Parathion

Parathion is degraded to aminoparathion by Penicillium waksmanii, isolated from flooded acid sulfate soil. This transformation reduces the toxicity of the parent compound significantly.

### Chlorpyrifos

Chlorpyrifos degradation has been demonstrated by P. chrysosporium and by mixed fungal populations, with mixed cultures proving more efficient than pure cultures. Coriolus versicolor performed best among tested fungi in biobed matrix systems for chlorpyrifos-containing pesticide waste.

## Herbicide Degradation

### Phenylurea Herbicides

Phenylurea herbicides including linuron, diuron, and isoproturon are degraded by multiple fungal species. Key degraders include Aspergillus niger, Geotrichum candidum, Trichoderma viride, and Cladosporium spp. Botrytis cinerea depletes linuron almost completely in culture. Rhizoctonia solani degrades diuron, chlorbromuron, fenuron, fluometuron, linuron, and metobromuron through a stepwise demethylation pathway. Khadrani et al. (1999) demonstrated degradation of chlortoluron, isoproturon, and diuron by soil micromycetes.

### Phenoxy Herbicides

2,4-D (2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid) and 2,4,5-T are mineralized by P. chrysosporium, with a higher rate of simultaneous mineralization when both are present compared to individual testing. The lignin-degrading enzyme system of white-rot fungi is responsible for the oxidative cleavage of phenoxy herbicide structures.

### Triazine Herbicides

Atrazine degradation has been demonstrated by Pleurotus pulmonarius, which produces a novel metabolite identified as 2-chloro-4-ethylamino-6-(1-hydroxyisopropyl)amino-1,3,5-triazine. Manganese enhances atrazine biotransformation by P. pulmonarius, correlated with its oxidation activity. Solid-state fermentation conditions improve atrazine degradation efficiency.

## Mixed Cultures and Field Considerations

Mixed fungal populations generally outperform pure cultures for pesticide degradation, reflecting the complex interactions in natural soils where multiple microorganisms attack pesticides simultaneously. Sometimes one fungus produces metabolic products that a second fungus or other microorganism further degrades, leading to more complete transformation. Soil enrichment techniques for introducing specific fungi into rhizospheres have not resulted in dramatic pesticide mineralization, but Trichoderma harzianum shows promise as a rhizosphere-compatible degrader due to its competitive abilities and fungicide tolerance.

## Fungicide Degradation

Fungal degradation of fungicides demonstrates the remarkable capacity of fungi to metabolize compounds specifically designed to kill them. Methyl benzimidazol-2-ylcarbamate (MBC) and benomyl are degraded by multiple fungal species through hydrolysis of the carbamate linkage. Penicillium strains isolated from treated soil show enhanced degradation capacity, suggesting adaptive enzyme induction. The organomercurial fungicides phenylmercuric acetate and ethylmercuric chloride are reduced to elemental mercury by Candida albicans and several Penicillium species. This mercury volatilization mechanism, while removing organic mercury compounds, can redistribute elemental mercury into the atmosphere, requiring careful management. Copper-based fungicides (Bordeaux mixture, copper hydroxide) are not degraded by fungi but can be immobilized through biosorption to fungal cell walls and extracellular polymeric substances. Trichoderma harzianum shows natural tolerance to several fungicides, making it a practical candidate for bioremediation of fungicide-contaminated agricultural soils.

## Biobeds and Biomixture Systems

Biobeds are engineered systems designed to intercept and degrade pesticide-containing agricultural wastewater before it reaches watercourses. Originally developed in Sweden in the 1990s, biobeds consist of a composite biomixture of straw (as lignocellulosic substrate to stimulate ligninolytic enzyme production), peat (for moisture retention and sorption), and topsoil (providing a reservoir of adapted microorganisms). White-rot fungi, particularly Coriolus versicolor (Trametes versicolor) and Pleurotus ostreatus, are key degraders in biobed systems. Ligninolytic enzyme activity in biobeds peaks during warm months and correlates with pesticide degradation rates. Field studies have demonstrated 90-99% reduction in pesticide concentrations passing through properly maintained biobeds. Optimizing the fungal component through inoculation with pre-adapted white-rot fungal strains has been shown to enhance degradation of recalcitrant pesticides including chlorpyrifos, metalaxyl, and atrazine. Biobed lifespan typically ranges from 5 to 10 years before the biomixture requires replacement.

## Soil Factors Affecting Fungal Degradation

Multiple soil parameters influence the efficiency of fungal pesticide degradation. Soil organic matter content is critical: higher organic matter increases pesticide sorption (which can reduce bioavailability) but also provides carbon sources for fungal growth and enzyme induction. Soil pH affects both fungal enzyme activity and pesticide speciation; most white-rot fungi prefer slightly acidic conditions (pH 4-6), while laccase activity can extend to near-neutral pH. Soil moisture must be maintained above 40% water holding capacity for fungal activity, but waterlogged conditions favor anaerobic bacteria over fungi. Temperature optima for most degradative fungi range from 25-30°C, though thermotolerant species like Thermoascus aurantiacus remain active at higher temperatures. Soil texture influences fungal hyphal penetration: sandy soils allow easier colonization but provide less surface area for enzyme-substrate contact compared to clay-rich soils. The presence of alternative carbon sources can either enhance degradation (by supporting fungal biomass) or inhibit it (through catabolite repression of degradative enzymes).

## Environmental Fate of Pesticide Metabolites

A critical consideration in fungal pesticide degradation is the fate and toxicity of transformation products. In many cases, fungal metabolism produces intermediates that are less toxic than the parent compound, but this is not guaranteed. For example, DDT transformation can produce DDD (via reductive dechlorination) or DDE (via dehydrochlorination), both of which are environmentally persistent and DDE is equally concerning as an endocrine disruptor. Endosulfan degradation by white-rot fungi produces endosulfan sulfate, which is equally or more toxic than the parent compound. Complete mineralization to carbon dioxide, water, and inorganic salts is the ideal outcome but is rarely achieved by fungal action alone; mineralization typically requires the combined activity of fungi and bacteria in a sequential degradation chain. Analytical monitoring should therefore target both parent compound disappearance and metabolite appearance, using GC-MS or LC-MS methods capable of detecting known transformation products.

## Integration with Sustainable Agriculture

Fungal pesticide remediation aligns closely with sustainable agriculture principles by offering biological alternatives to conventional cleanup methods. Reduced tillage and cover cropping practices that support soil fungal communities can enhance natural attenuation capacity, creating agricultural systems that are more resilient to pesticide contamination events.
The use of Trichoderma-based biocontrol products, already widely adopted in organic farming, provides a dual benefit of plant disease suppression and incidental pesticide degradation in the rhizosphere. Conservation tillage practices preserve the hyphal networks of both saprotrophic and mycorrhizal fungi, maintaining the biological infrastructure needed for continuous pesticide degradation.
Crop rotation schemes that alternate between mycorrhizal host crops and non-host crops can be optimized to maintain fungal diversity: including fungal-sustaining crops like maize, sorghum, and legumes between brassica or chenopod crops helps preserve degradative fungal populations.
Buffer strips planted with fungal-host vegetation along field margins can intercept and biodegrade pesticide runoff before it reaches waterways, combining erosion control with phytobioremediation. The integration of biobeds at farm scale, positioned to capture sprayer rinse water and equipment washoff, represents one of the most practical applications of fungal pesticide degradation in mainstream agriculture, with adoption rates highest in Scandinavian countries where regulatory requirements mandate point-source pesticide pollution controls.
Research from Sweden, Denmark, and Germany has demonstrated that biobeds can be adapted to local conditions by substituting regionally available lignocellulosic materials such as grape marc, olive pomace, or rice husks for the traditional wheat straw component, expanding the applicability of this technology beyond its Northern European origins.

## Monitoring and Analytical Methods

Effective monitoring of fungal pesticide degradation requires appropriate analytical methods tailored to both parent compounds and transformation products. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with UV or diode array detection is commonly used for herbicide analysis including triazines, phenylureas, and phenoxy compounds. Gas chromatography with mass spectrometry (GC-MS) is preferred for organochlorine and organophosphate insecticides due to their volatility and thermal stability. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) provides the most comprehensive detection capability, simultaneously quantifying parent pesticides and multiple metabolites at parts-per-billion concentrations. Enzyme activity assays for laccase (using ABTS or syringaldazine as substrates) and manganese peroxidase (monitoring Mn(III)-malonate formation) serve as indirect indicators of fungal degradative capacity in treated soils. Molecular biology tools including quantitative PCR of fungal laccase genes and terminal restriction fragment length polymorphism (TRFLP) analysis of fungal community structure allow researchers to track introduced fungal populations and their persistence in treated environments. Toxicity bioassays using Lumbricus terrestris (earthworm), Lactuca sativa (lettuce seed germination), and Vibrio fischeri (Microtox) provide complementary evidence of remediation success by measuring ecological recovery alongside chemical concentration reductions. Passive sampling devices including polyethylene membrane samplers and ceramic dosimeters can be deployed in situ to measure bioavailable pesticide concentrations over time, providing a more ecologically relevant metric than total soil extractions. Combining chemical analysis with biological endpoints gives a more complete picture of remediation effectiveness and helps identify situations where toxic transformation products persist even after parent compound removal.

## Fungal-Bacterial Consortia for Enhanced Pesticide Degradation

The combined action of fungi and bacteria often achieves more complete pesticide degradation than either group alone. White-rot fungi initiate degradation through oxidative attack on pesticide molecules, producing partially transformed intermediates that bacteria can then mineralize to carbon dioxide, water, and inorganic salts. This sequential degradation pathway is particularly important for recalcitrant compounds like DDT and lindane, where fungal transformation creates products more accessible to bacterial catabolism.
Laboratory studies have shown that co-cultures of Phanerochaete chrysosporium with Pseudomonas putida achieve up to 70% mineralization of DDT, compared to less than 20% by the fungus alone. Mycorrhizal fungi create a unique three-way interaction by connecting plant roots to bacterial communities in the mycorrhizosphere: the fungal hyphal network transports bacterial cells along root channels, extends the degradation zone beyond the immediate root surface, and supplies carbon from the host plant to fuel bacterial metabolic activity.
Constructed microbial consortia designed for specific pesticide mixtures are an active area of research, with strain compatibility, competitive interactions, and metabolic complementarity being key selection criteria. Bioaugmentation with pre-adapted fungal-bacterial consortia has shown promise in biobed systems, where the lignocellulosic matrix provides a structured habitat that supports both fungal and bacterial populations in close proximity.
The spatial organization of fungal-bacterial interactions is particularly important: fungal mycelia create physical channels through soil that facilitate bacterial dispersal, while bacterial biofilms on hyphal surfaces enhance enzyme stability and create localized zones of enhanced degradation activity. This hyphosphere effect extends the effective treatment radius well beyond the zone of direct fungal colonization.
