# Slime Molds

[[teaming-with-microbes-algae-slime-molds]] are a diverse group of protists that were historically classified
as fungi due to their similar appearance and ecological roles, but they belong
to entirely different kingdoms. They are divided into two major groups:
plasmodial slime molds (Myxomycetes) [[adenosine-triphosphate-and-cellular-energy]] slime molds (Dictyostelids).
Both groups fascinate biologists because they blur the boundary between single
organisms and multicellular life, and they exhibit remarkably complex behaviors
despite lacking a [[tompkins-bose-plant-nervous-system-hypothesis]].

## Plasmodial Slime Molds

Plasmodial slime molds begin life as microscopic spores that germinate into
swimming cells. When two compatible cells meet, they fuse to form a single
larger cell with multiple nuclei. This multinucleate mass, called a plasmodium,
creeps slowly over surfaces (often rotting logs) by cytoplasmic streaming,
engulfing bacteria, spores, and organic particles as it goes.

A plasmodium can be strikingly colorful: bright yellow, orange, or white, and
sometimes spanning over a foot in diameter. It is essentially one enormous cell
with no internal cell membranes separating its nuclei. When food becomes scarce
or conditions deteriorate, the plasmodium migrates to an exposed location and
transforms into fruiting bodies (sporangia) that release spores to start the
cycle anew. The genus *Physarum polycephalum* has become a model organism in
biological research.

The life cycle of plasmodial slime molds involves several distinct stages.
Spores germinate in moist conditions, releasing either biflagellated swarm
cells (in [[biodiversity-fungi-aquatic-environments]]) or amoeboid cells (on surfaces). These
haploid cells can reproduce asexually or find compatible mates for sexual
reproduction. Plasmogamy (cell fusion) produces a diploid zygote that feeds
and grows into the mature plasmodium through repeated nuclear division without
cell division (endomitosis). The plasmodium can be enormous, containing
millions of nuclei streaming through a single cytoplasmic mass. Under adverse
conditions, the plasmodium can form a hardened sclerotium, a dormant stage
that can survive desiccation and extreme temperatures for months. When
conditions improve, the sclerotium rehydrates and resumes active growth.
The transition to fruiting is triggered by light exposure, particularly
near-UV wavelengths, which stimulates the plasmodium to differentiate into
sporangia on stalks. The spores are dispersed by wind, [[water-management]], or animals,
completing the cycle. Common genera include Physarum, Fuligo (which produces
the "dog vomit slime mold" often seen on mulch), and Stemonitis (with
elegant, stalked fruiting bodies resembling tiny trees).

## Cellular Slime Molds

Cellular slime molds (Dictyostelids) spend most of their lives as individual
amoebae crawling through soil, feeding on bacteria. When food runs out, tens
of thousands of these independent cells aggregate into a multicellular "slug"
that migrates as a coordinated unit toward light and heat. The slug then
differentiates into a stalk supporting a spore capsule. The cells that form
the stalk die, sacrificing themselves so that the spore-bearing cells can be
elevated for better dispersal.

This altruistic behavior posed an evolutionary puzzle: why would individual
cells sacrifice themselves? Research revealed that the aggregating cells are
genetically related (clonal), so the sacrifice is explained by kin selection.
Strains that "cheat" by always taking the spore position have been identified
in wild populations, leading to an evolutionary arms race between cooperators
and cheaters.

The aggregation process of cellular slime molds is mediated by cyclic AMP
(cAMP), which serves as a chemoattractant signal. Starving cells begin
secreting pulses of cAMP, and neighboring cells detect these pulses through
G-protein coupled receptors on their surface. The cells move toward the cAMP
source and relay the signal outward, creating propagating waves that
coordinate the aggregation of up to 100,000 cells. Once aggregated, the
cells differentiate into at least four distinct cell types: prestalk cells
that form the stalk, prespore cells that become spores, and anterior-like
cells with regulatory functions. The ratio of stalk to spore cells is
regulated by a counting mechanism based on the ratio of two signaling
molecules, DIF-1 (differentiation-inducing factor) and cAMP. The entire
developmental cycle from starvation to mature fruiting body takes
approximately 24-48 hours in *Dictyostelium discoideum*, the most
extensively studied species. *Dictyostelium discoideum* has a 34 Mb genome
encoding approximately 12,500 genes, and its genome was sequenced in 2005,
revealing a surprisingly large number of genes shared with animals that are
absent in other protists, making it an important model for studying the
evolution of multicellularity and cell signaling.

## Intelligence Without a Brain

Slime molds, particularly *Physarum polycephalum*, have become famous for their
problem-solving abilities. In landmark experiments, researchers placed oat
flakes (food) at positions corresponding to major cities around Tokyo on a
agar plate, with the slime mold initially at a central point. The organism
grew to connect all the food sources, and its resulting network closely
matched the actual Tokyo rail system, including shortcuts and redundant routes
that engineers had arrived at through decades of optimization.

*Physarum* can solve mazes, find the shortest path between two points, and
remember where food was, even though it has no brain or neurons. It achieves
this through decentralized computation: the body-wide flow of cytoplasm
reinforces successful pathways and prunes unsuccessful ones, effectively
performing a form of natural optimization.

## Ecological Role

Slime molds play important roles [[teaming-with-microbes-bacteria-and-archaea-in-soil]] ecosystems. Their primary food is
bacteria, and they help regulate bacterial populations [[trace-elements-in-soil]] and on decaying
wood. By concentrating nutrients in their spores and fruiting bodies, they
also make those nutrients available to other organisms. Their plasmodia
contribute to soil structure and moisture retention. In forest ecosystems,
slime molds are remarkably diverse, with hundreds of species coexisting on a
single fallen log.

Slime molds occupy a unique trophic position in soil food webs as primary
consumers of bacteria and fungal spores. Their grazing activity influences
bacterial community composition, preferentially consuming certain bacterial
species while allowing others to proliferate. This selective predation can
have cascading effects on nutrient cycling and plant health in the rhizosphere.
Slime mold fruiting bodies provide food for various invertebrates, including
springtails (Collembola) and mites, which disperse spores to new locations.
The plasmodia of large slime molds like Fuligo septicum can cover areas
exceeding one square meter, temporarily coating the soil surface and
affecting the microhabitat conditions for smaller organisms. Slime molds are
particularly abundant in tropical forests, where biodiversity surveys have
documented over 500 species from a single hectare. Temperate forests harbor
fewer species but still support diverse communities, especially on coarse
woody debris in advanced stages of decomposition. Climate change may affect
slime mold distributions, as many species are sensitive to moisture levels
and temperature extremes.

## Research Applications

Beyond the famous problem-solving experiments, slime molds have found
practical applications in scientific research. *Physarum polycephalum* has
been used to design efficient transportation networks; researchers at the
University of Tokyo and Oxford University have applied Physarum's growth
patterns to optimize road and rail network designs. The organism has also
been used to develop biological computing devices, including Physarum-based
logic gates that process information through the organism's growth patterns.
In materials science, slime mold growth templates have been used to create
biomimetic vascular networks in synthetic materials. *Dictyostelium
discoideum* serves as a biomedical model organism for studying cell motility,
chemotaxis, and phagocytosis, with direct relevance to understanding human
immune cell behavior and the mechanisms of cancer metastasis. The organism's
social behavior has also made it a model for studying the evolution of
