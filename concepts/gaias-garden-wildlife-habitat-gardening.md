# Wildlife Habitat Gardening Principles

In Gaia's Garden, Toby Hemenway argues that conventional gardens are
fragmented and incomplete: an orderly vegetable plot here, flower beds
there, and a back corner for wildlife. Each fragment has its weaknesses.
A vegetable garden does not offer habitat to native insects, birds, and
other wildlife. A flower garden cannot feed the gardener. A wildlife
garden is often unkempt and provides little for people. The ecological
garden integrates these isolated pieces into a vigorous, thriving
backyard ecosystem that benefits both people and wildlife.

## Beyond Traditional Wildlife Gardening

Traditional wildlife gardens often mean setting aside a wild, brushy
area that looks unkempt and serves little human purpose. Hemenway
challenges this approach, advocating instead for gardens where wildlife
habitat is woven throughout the entire design. Ecological gardens are
filled with beautiful plants that have many uses, providing fruit and
vegetables, medicinal and culinary herbs, colorful blossoms, soil-
building mulch, protection from pests, and habitat for wildlife, all
within the same integrated landscape.

## The Problem of Fragmentation

Hemenway traces many pest problems directly to habitat destruction.
When hedgerows and wild places were removed from farmland, beneficial
insects lost the shelter and food sources they needed. Spiders, which
are superb predators, shelter in dried grass and mulch that provide
the high humidity and refuge from temperature changes they prefer.
Researchers found thirty times more spiders in mulched gardens than in
cleanly cultivated ones, with far less insect damage to plants.

The ecological gardener creates habitat not in isolated corners but
distributed throughout the landscape, ensuring that predators and
pollinators are always near the plants that need them.

## Creating Habitat Nooks

Hemenway provides specific guidance for creating wildlife habitat within
the garden design. Piling up stones, logs, or brush near guilds and
planting areas, and creating small ponds and puddles, will attract
lizards, frogs, snakes, and birds. Rocks tucked unobtrusively around
the yard harbor vast numbers of helpful garter snakes and lizards.
These predators are important for preserving balance: if any prey
species such as caterpillars, aphids, or slugs finds a home and begins
to reproduce exuberantly, a waiting population of predators will cull
their numbers with chilling efficiency.

## Plants for Wildlife Food

Hemenway organizes wildlife-supporting plants by season:

- Spring nectar plants: early-blooming species that feed migrating
  hummingbirds and emerging insects.
- Summer-fruiting plants: blackberry, blueberry, cherry, chokecherry,
  honeysuckle, raspberry, serviceberry, mulberry, elderberry, and wild
  plum.
- Fall-fruiting plants: dogwood, mountain ash, snowberry, sea
  buckthorn, buffaloberry, and cotoneaster, essential for migratory
  birds building fat reserves.
- Winter-fruiting plants: black chokecherry, snowberry, sumac,
  highbush cranberry, crabapple, barberry, hawthorn, and bittersweet.
- Nut and acorn plants: oaks, hickories, butternuts, walnuts,
  buckeyes, chestnuts, and hazels.

Many of these plants are multifunctional, offering food for people as
well as wildlife. Several, such as buffaloberry and sea buckthorn, are
nitrogen fixers that build soil. See [[gaias-garden-multipurpose-plants]]
for more on stacking these functions.

## The Wildlife Sector

In the zone-and-sector design framework, Hemenway identifies wildlife
as a sector that every yard is penetrated by, whether it brings
marauding deer, a skulking raccoon, or a flock of cedar waxwings.
Wildlife can be deflected or welcomed by plantings and structures.
Rather than treating wildlife as a problem to be fenced out, the
ecological gardener designs for it, creating corridors and habitat
connections that channel beneficial wildlife through the landscape
while managing potential damage.

## Multifunctional Wildlife Design

The key principle is that habitat features should serve multiple
functions. A pond provides wildlife habitat, irrigation water storage,
reflective heat for nearby plants, and aesthetic beauty. A hedgerow
provides wind protection, privacy, food, nitrogen fixation, and
wildlife corridors. A stone wall offers nesting sites, thermal mass
for microclimate modification, and a structural garden element. Every
habitat feature should connect to the broader garden system.

## Wildlife as Garden Partners

Hemenway encourages gardeners to see wildlife as partners rather than
pests. Birds scratch the soil, scatter seed, munch malevolent larvae,
and leave phosphate-laden droppings. Insects pollinate flowers and
control pest populations. Small mammals process organic matter and
aerate soil. Without animals, the gardener's labor is doubled and
redoubled. By creating a garden that nurtures wildlife, we close the
cycle and let nature carry her share of the work.

## Backyard Helpers: Beyond Insects

Hemenway extends the wildlife concept to include small domestic animals.
Before World War II, chickens, rabbits, and pigeons were common even
in urban backyards. Their recent resurgence reflects a growing
understanding that these animals provide fertilizer, tillage, pest
control, and food production as part of the garden ecosystem. See
[[gaias-garden-chicken-tractors]] and [[gaias-garden-rabbits-and-worm-composting]]
for detailed strategies on integrating small livestock.

The backyard ecosystem is deeply welcoming for the wild world and for
people, offering food and other products for self-reliance as well as
beauty and inspiration. When a garden supports pollinators, builds
soil, discourages pests, and moderates climatic extremes, its gifts
for people will also be marvelously abundant, flowing from the land.
