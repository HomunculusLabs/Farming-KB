# Convection Loops and Thermosiphons

## Overview

Convection is the transmission of low-grade heat through the movement of fluids — the passage of air and water over slightly heated surfaces. It is one of the three primary mechanisms of heat transfer (alongside conduction and radiation) and plays a fundamental role in both the design of built environments and the functioning of natural systems at every scale.

## Principles of Convection

Heat always flows from warmer to colder bodies. When a body is heated, it expands, its density decreases, and it rises — initiating convection currents. Cool air is heavier (more dense) than heated air, and the same principle applies to water, liquids, and gases generally. This density-driven movement is the engine behind all convection loops.

### Closed Loop Circulation

In a closed loop system, heated air or water circulates through pipes or ducts with heat applied to the lower part of the loop. A minimum height difference of approximately 40 cm is required to establish the thermosiphon effect — the passive circulation of fluid driven solely by density differences, without any mechanical pump.

## Thermosiphons in Practice

Thermosiphons are remarkably versatile passive devices:

- **Refrigeration**: Thermosiphon principles are used in absorption refrigerators driven by flames or other heat sources — heat applied at the bottom drives the circulation of the refrigerant through the cooling cycle.
- **Solar heating**: Thermosiphons are highly useful for transferring heat from solar ponds or flat plate collectors to home radiators and hot water tanks. The key siting rule is to place collectors at least 0.5 m below the storage or use points, ensuring gravity assists the natural circulation.
- **Building systems**: By eliminating the need for pumps, thermosiphon systems reduce complexity, cost, and energy consumption in residential and agricultural settings.

## Atmospheric Convection

### The Overbeck Jet

Over land, columns of heated air ascend in what are known as Overbeck jets. As this heated air rises, it cools and moisture condenses at the top, producing rain. These convectional rains create a mosaic rainfall pattern across desert landscapes — localized, scattered precipitation driven purely by thermal differentials between land surfaces.

### Limitations in Enclosed Spaces

Convection loops will not occur effectively in closed rooms where hot air (8–10 °C higher than ambient) stratifies below ceilings. This stagnant stratification means that air convection, while useful in open or properly designed systems, is not an efficient method for heating buildings directly.

Despite this limitation at the building scale, air convection remains the main engine of [[global-atmospheric-circulation-patterns]] globally, driving [[climatic-zones-and-global-weather-patterns]], wind systems, and the redistribution of heat across the planet.

## Heat Measurement and Solar Energy

### Units of Heat

- **Gram calorie**: The amount of heat required to raise 1 gram of water from 14.5 °C to 15.0 °C.
- **Langley**: A unit of solar radiation equal to 1 gram calorie per square centimeter (g·cal/cm²). The sun delivers approximately 2 Langleys per minute to the outer atmosphere.

### Attenuation Through the Atmosphere

The intensity of solar radiation at ground level varies significantly depending on:

- Latitude and season
- Slope angle and aspect of the receiving surface
- Atmospheric conditions including ice, water vapor, dust, and cloud cover

Only about 22% of the sun's radiant energy penetrates a single atmosphere of depth. In polar regions, where the sun may sit at only 5° elevation above the horizon (equivalent to passing through approximately 11 atmospheres), as little as 1% of the original energy is received at the surface.

### Siting for Solar Gain

Given the dramatic losses through atmospheric attenuation, siting houses on sun-facing slopes within the thermal belt is a critical energy conservation strategy. Proper solar orientation and slope position can dramatically increase the usable solar energy reaching a dwelling.

## Thermal Belts

Thermal belts are elevation zones where temperature conditions are most favorable, avoiding both cold-air pooling in valleys and excessive exposure on ridgelines. Their elevation ranges vary by climate:

| Climate Type | Thermal Belt Elevation |
|---|---|
| Hill country | 1,000–5,000 m |
| Lower slopes | 100–200 m |
| Hot deserts | 10–15 m |

In hot deserts, even a 10–15 m rise above the valley floor can provide meaningful relief from cold-air drainage and frost pockets.

## Advection: Wind-Driven Heat Transfer

### Advected Warmth and Cold

Advection refers to the horizontal transport of heat by winds from warmer or cooler regions. Two key phenomena arise from this:

- **Advection fogs**: Warm, moist air moving inshore from coasts encounters cooler land surfaces, producing persistent fog.
- **Advection frosts**: Cold air flowing downhill from higher elevations can settle into low-lying areas, causing frost damage even when surrounding regions remain frost-free.

### Condensation from Advected Warm Air

When warm, moisture-laden air is advected into cooler areas, condensation results. This process is critically important for forest precipitation — forests often derive significant moisture from advected air masses condensing within the canopy. However, the same process is a nuisance in enclosed buildings, where it leads to dampness, mold, and structural degradation.

## Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of heat of an intermediate grade via direct solid contact. It is the basis of the efficiency of slab-floored houses, where thermal mass in the floor absorbs, stores, and slowly re-radiates heat. Pipes embedded in hot solids (such as masonry stoves or earthen floors) conduct heat effectively to adjacent spaces or water.

## Radiation

Radiation is the transfer of intense heat from solids and liquids across space, requiring no medium. Key points:

- Radiant heaters are among the most efficient space heaters available, as they warm objects and people directly rather than heating air.
- Focused radiation (as with parabolic reflectors or magnifying lenses) can produce extremely intense local heat, useful for cooking, water purification, or industrial processes.

## Plants as Heaters

Some plants have evolved the remarkable ability to generate their own heat through metabolic processes — specifically by storing and oxidizing fats:

- **Arum lilies** (*Arum* spp.) and **[[philodendron-selloum]]** can reach internal temperatures of 46 °C when surrounding air is only 4 °C.
- **Crocuses** heat themselves approximately 15 °C above [[jadam-ambient-temperature-principle]].
- **Skunk cabbage** (*[[symplocarpus-foetidus]]*) generates enough heat to melt surrounding snow, enabling it to flower early in spring.

These thermogenic plants demonstrate that biological systems can exploit the same principles of heat generation and transfer that engineers apply in mechanical thermosiphon systems.

## Design Implications for Permaculture

Understanding convection, conduction, and radiation enables [[bill-mollison]] to:

1. **Site structures** on sun-facing slopes within thermal belts to maximize passive solar gain.
2. **Use thermosiphon loops** to passively move heat from solar collectors to storage without pumps.
3. **Design for advection**, accounting for frost drainage paths and advected moisture that sustains forest systems.
4. **Leverage thermal mass** through conductive materials in floors and walls for stable indoor temperatures.
5. **Employ radiant heating** for efficient, targeted space heating.
6. **Recognize natural thermogenesis** in plants as a model for decentralized heat production.

By working with these fundamental physical processes rather than against them, permaculture systems achieve greater energy efficiency, resilience, and self-sufficiency.

## Summary of Heat Transfer Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Grade | Medium | Key Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Convection | Low-grade | Fluids (air, water) | Thermosiphon solar water heaters |
| Conduction | Intermediate | Solids | Slab floors, pipes in masonry |
| Radiation | High-grade | Space (no medium needed) | Radiant space heaters, solar radiation |

Each mechanism operates simultaneously in most real-world systems. A well-designed permaculture structure harnesses all three: radiation from the sun warms conductive thermal mass in floors and walls, while convection loops distribute that stored heat through the living space.

## Key Design Principles

- **Height matters**: Thermosiphons require a minimum 40 cm rise; site solar collectors at least 0.5 m below storage.
- **Avoid stratification**: Design spaces with ventilation paths that prevent hot air pooling under ceilings.
- **Respect atmospheric depth**: Solar gain drops dramatically through the atmosphere — slope siting compensates for latitude losses.
- **Plan for advection**: Map frost drainage paths before placing frost-sensitive plantings or structures.
- **Use thermal mass strategically**: Conductive materials stabilize temperature swings in both heating and cooling seasons.
- **Consider biological models**: Thermogenic plants show that decentralized, chemical-based heat production is viable even in cold climates.
