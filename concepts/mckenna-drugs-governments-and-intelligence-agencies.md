# Drugs, Governments, and Intelligence Agencies

In the final chapters of Food of the Gods, McKenna advances one of his most controversial arguments: that modern governments, particularly through their intelligence agencies, have not merely failed to stop the drug trade but have actively participated in and profited from it.

This argument reframes the war on drugs not as a well-intentioned policy failure but as a deliberate mechanism of social control and illicit revenue generation by the same institutions that publicly condemn drug use.

## Modern Antidrug Hysteria

In the United States at the turn of the century, racist rumormongering fanned the hysterical fear that southern blacks, maddened by cocaine, might attack whites. In 1906 the Pure Food and Drug Act made cocaine and heroin illegal and set the stage for legally sanctioned suppression of addictive compounds from the opium poppy and coca bush.

In contrast to tobacco, tea, and coffee, which were initially resisted and then made legal, morphine, heroin, and cocaine began as legal substances but were suppressed once recognized as addictive. Why these drugs and not others? This is a question McKenna insists we must attempt to answer.

Was the addiction more virulent? Was the use of the hypodermic injection somehow offensive? Or was there some difference in the social and psychological effect of these drugs that made them scapegoats for the damage being done to society by alcohol and tobacco?

Part of the answer lies in the fact that by the early twentieth century nearly a hundred years of experience with the social consequences of addictive synthetic drugs was behind us. The cheerful folly of hailing each new pharmacological discovery as a universal panacea had been amply demonstrated.

## Government Involvement in the Drug Trade

Government involvement in and direct responsibility for the drug trade would diminish, with protection rackets replacing direct earnings, while retail prices would rise astronomically. The new price structure made the drug money pie large enough for all parties to profit handsomely, governments and criminal syndicates alike.

In effect, the modern solution has been for drug cartels to operate as proxies for national governments in the matter of supplying addictive narcotics. Governments can no longer participate openly in the world narcotic trade and claim legitimacy. Only pariah governments operate without fronts.

Legitimate governments prefer to have their intelligence agencies cut secret deals with drug mafiosi while the visible machinery of diplomacy seems all aflutter over the drug problem, a problem always presented in such terms as to convince any reasonable person of its utter insolubility.

The major production areas of hard narcotics are tribal zones. Modern imperialists would have us believe they have never been able to overrun and control these areas, in Pakistan and Burma for example, where major production of opium occurs. Faceless tribal leaders can be held responsible.

## Historical Patterns of Government Drug Dealing

From 1914 until World War II, drug distribution was largely in the hands of gangsters who directed other illicit operations: prostitution, loan sharking, and various rackets. The prohibition of alcohol in the United States created a vast windfall market for hard narcotics as well as easy profits from illegally manufactured alcohol.

Government manipulation of drug markets occurred elsewhere too. During World War II the Japanese occupiers of Manchuria produced vast amounts of opium and heroin for distribution inside China, not for profit but with the intent of creating so many addicts that the will of the Chinese people to resist occupation would be broken.

Later, during the 1960s, the CIA would use the same technique to smother political dissent in American black ghettos under an avalanche of No. 4 China White, heroin of extraordinary purity. This pattern of using drugs as a tool of political control is a recurring theme in McKenna's analysis.

## The Intelligence Connection

The virulence of addictions to synthetics like heroin and cocaine could not long escape the attention of the inheritors of the slave trade and the opium wars, the international intelligence agencies and secret police organizations. These shadowy groups have an insatiable need for untraceable money.

They require funding for private armies, terrorist cells, coups d'etat, and front groups that are their stock in trade. Involvement in and domination of the world narcotics trade has proven irresistible to groups such as the CIA, Opus Dei, and the French secret service.

The U.S. government's Mafia and narcotics connection goes back to World War II. ## The OSS Connections and Post-War Drug Networks

The U.S. government's Mafia and narcotics connection goes back to World War II. Two controversial joint operations between the OSS and Naval Intelligence established contacts with the Sicilian Mafia via Lucky Luciano and with the dope-dealing Green Gang of Shanghai via Tai Li.

Both connections were extended into the post-war period, creating the infrastructure for what would become the modern international drug trade. These wartime alliances between intelligence agencies and criminal organizations proved remarkably durable.

In the late 1970s, there was a move in American hard drug culture from emphasis on heroin to emphasis on cocaine. This move was in part a logical consequence of the American military defeat in Vietnam and retraction from Southeast Asia. It was soon reinforced when the Reagan agenda of contra support opened new frontiers for covert operations.

## The Transition from Heroin to Cocaine

In the late 1970s, there was a move from heroin to cocaine in American drug culture, in part a consequence of the military defeat in Vietnam.

It was soon reinforced when the Reagan agenda of contra support and narcoterrorism opened new frontiers for covert operations. The involvement of legitimate institutions remained essentially the same with certain exceptions.

Yet it is unlikely that the virulence or social cost of the cocaine epidemic was ever anticipated. Perhaps no one ever asked the question of what the consequences would be of hooking the American public on cocaine. Perhaps the development of smokable crack cocaine was unexpected.

## Cocaine as Escaped Technology

It is highly likely that the phenomenon of crack cocaine is an instance of technology having escaped from the control of its creators. In the 1980s cocaine assumed a form more virulent than any of its earlier victims and detractors could have possibly imagined.

This is a new and disturbing pattern in the evolution of human-drug interactions that cannot be ignored. If today we are confronted by a superaddictive form of cocaine, why not tomorrow a superaddictive form of heroin? In fact, such forms of heroin already exist.

Ice, a smokable form of highly addictive methamphetamine, has appeared in the drug underground. There will be other drugs in the future, more addictive, more destructive than anything now possible. The evolution of drug technology appears to be accelerating.

## The Calvinist Framework of Prohibition

How will law and society respond to this phenomenon? It is to be hoped the response won't be one of self-righteously holding addicts up as examples of contemptible behavior. From a historical point of view, restricting the availability of addictive substances must be seen as a peculiarly perverse example of Calvinist dominator thought.

It is a system in which the sinner is punished in this world by being transformed into an exploitable customer, who is punished for addiction by being relieved of cash by the criminal-governmental combine that provides the addictive substances.

The image is more horrifying than that of the serpent that devours itself. It is once again the Dionysian image of the mother who devours her children, the image of a house divided against itself.

## The Double Standard of Drug Policy

There is no question that a society that sets out to control its citizens' use of drugs sets out on the slippery path to totalitarianism. No amount of police power, surveillance, and intrusion into people's lives can be expected to affect the drug problem. Hence there is no limit to the amount of repression that frightened institutions may call for.

The contrast between the treatment of alcohol and tobacco, which kill millions annually, and the treatment of psychedelics, which have never been shown to cause death, reveals the irrationality at the heart of drug policy. The drugs that support dominator culture are embraced; those that threaten it are suppressed.

This is not a public health policy but a cultural policy, designed to maintain the pharmacological foundations of hierarchy and conformity that McKenna identifies as the hallmarks of dominator society.

## The Pattern of Drug Diplomacy

The pattern established by England's nineteenth-century opium diplomacy has been repeated in CIA collusion in the international heroin and cocaine trade. Just as tea dealers turned to opium when their market suffered, so did Western intelligence groups turn to cocaine after losing their heroin monopoly to Iranian mullahs.

The history of commercial drug synergies, the way one drug has been cynically encouraged and used to support the introduction of others over the past five hundred years, is not pleasant to contemplate. Perhaps that is why the exercise is so rarely undertaken.

The cycles began with sugar, whose existence depended on a savage slave trade. The seventeenth-century introduction of tea, coffee, and chocolate drove the craze for sugar to new heights. The invention of morphine in 1803 and heroin in 1873 carried the progression to the threshold of the twentieth century.

Alarmed social reformers who attempted to legislate drug use only succeeded in driving it underground. There it remains, controlled today not by robber baron corporations operating under public charter, but by international crime cartels often posing as intelligence agencies.

Since the Age of Exploration, drugs and plant products have become increasingly important factors in the equations of international diplomacy. No longer are distant tropical regions to languish unattended by the rapacious eye of the white man; they have become production areas populated by indentured labor forces.

Like the maenads lost in the transport of Dionysian fury, the sugar-intoxicated dominator economies of Europe sought to devour their own children. The drug trade became a mirror of the dominator impulse itself: extraction, exploitation, and endless expansion.

## Drugs and the National Security State

The involvement of intelligence agencies in the drug trade is not an aberration but a logical extension of the pattern McKenna describes. When drugs become instruments of geopolitical power, the lines between legitimate commerce, covert operations, and criminal enterprise dissolve entirely.

The OSS and CIA experiments with LSD represent one face of this phenomenon: the state's desire to control and weaponize psychoactive experience. The other face is the tacit tolerance of drug trafficking when it serves foreign policy objectives, from the Golden Triangle to Central America.

McKenna's broader point is that the state has always been deeply involved with drugs, not as a disinterested regulator but as an active participant seeking to shape and control the drug landscape for its own purposes. Prohibition is simply the most visible expression of this involvement.

The history of drugs and governments is ultimately a history of power, and of the lengths to which power will go to maintain its monopoly on consciousness.
