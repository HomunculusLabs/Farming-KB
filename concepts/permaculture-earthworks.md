## Permaculture Earthworks

Earthworks are the physical reshaping of land to manage water, control erosion, create growing space, and passively irrigate landscapes. In permaculture design, earthworks are typically the first major physical intervention on a site because they establish the water infrastructure that everything else depends on. Well-designed earthworks can transform degraded land into productive, resilient systems.

## Swales

Swales are shallow, level ditches excavated along the contour of a slope. Water collects in the swale during rain events and slowly infiltrates into the soil, recharging groundwater and hydrating the landscape downhill.

### Design Considerations

- Spacing depends on slope steepness: steeper slopes need swales closer together
- A typical spacing ratio is 3:1 to 5:1 (horizontal distance to vertical drop)
- The berm (mound) on the downhill side is planted immediately with trees, shrubs, and ground covers
- Swales are level on contour — use an A-frame level, laser level, or water tube level
- Overflow spillways are critical: swales must never fail catastrophically

### Construction

1. Mark contour lines across the slope
2. Excavate the swale — typically 1-3 feet deep and 2-6 feet wide depending on scale
3. Pile excavated soil on the downhill side to form the berm
4. Compact the berm gently and plant immediately to prevent erosion
5. Install an overflow spillway at one end, armored with rock or grass

### Function

- Captures runoff and allows infiltration over hours to days
- Reduces erosion by slowing water flow across slope
- Creates a moist microclimate on the berm for establishing trees
- Can passively irrigate downhill plantings for weeks after a single rain event

Swales integrate closely with [[keyline-design]] for whole-property water management.

## Ponds

Ponds serve as multi-functional water storage: irrigation reservoir, wildlife habitat, fire protection, aquaculture, and aesthetic/psychological value.

### Siting

- Place ponds high in the landscape to allow gravity-fed irrigation downhill
- Use natural depressions or excavate in areas with impervious clay subsoil
- Position where they collect runoff from multiple swales
- Ensure adequate watershed area to fill and maintain water level

### Types

- Excavated ponds: dug into the ground, lined with clay or geomembrane if needed
- Embankment ponds: dam across a valley or drainage
- Duck ponds: shallow edges for waterfowl, integrated with [[duck-keeping]] systems
- Chinampas-style: interconnected canal and island systems for intensive production

### Design Features

- Shallow edges (0-2 feet) for emergent aquatic plants and wildlife access
- Deeper center (6-10+ feet) for water storage, thermal mass, and overwintering fish
- Inlet and outlet structures to control water level
- Planting shelves at various depths for different aquatic species

## Keyline Plowing

Keyline design, developed by P.A. Yeomans in Australia, is a system of land management that uses the "keyline" — a contour line that intersects the convex/concave transition of a slope — to optimally distribute water across the landscape.

### The Keyline Concept

- Every valley has a point where the slope changes from convex (ridge) to concave (valley)
- The contour line through this point is the keyline
- Plowing parallel to the keyline (not parallel to contour) gradually moves water from wet valleys to dry ridges
- Subsoiling along these lines breaks compaction without inverting the soil profile

### Implementation

- Use a Keyline plow (Yeomans-style ripper with shanks)
- Plow along the keyline pattern just below the soil surface (4-8 inches)
- Do NOT plow when soil is saturated — shanks will smear and compact
- Spacing between passes is typically 3-5 feet
- Results improve dramatically over 2-3 years as infiltration increases

### Benefits

- Dramatically increases water infiltration and soil moisture retention
- Breaks hardpan and plow layers without destroying soil structure
- Moves soil moisture from saturated valleys to dry ridges
- Increases deep soil carbon storage
- Can be done with minimal machinery — even animal-drawn implements

See [[keyline-design]] for a full treatment of this system.

## Terraces

Terraces convert steep slopes into a series of level steps, making land agriculturally productive that would otherwise be too steep to work.

### Types

- Bench terraces: wide, flat platforms cut into the hillside (most common in traditional agriculture)
- Broad-based terraces: gentle, wide ridges across the slope (suitable for machinery)
- Contour bunds: simple earth banks on contour to slow runoff
- Rock wall terraces: dry-stacked stone retaining walls (common in Mediterranean and Andean agriculture)

### Design Principles

- The backslope (cut) should be stable in the local soil type
- Drainage must be provided — either through the terrace wall or via defined outlets
- Terrace width depends on slope: steeper slopes mean narrower terraces
- Plant the riser (front face) immediately to prevent erosion
- Consider the entire watershed above the terrace system

### Applications

- Hillside orchards and vineyards
- Rice paddy terraces
- Vegetable production on sloped land
- Pathways and access roads on steep terrain

## Chinampas

Chinampas are an ancient Mesoamerican agricultural technique sometimes called "floating gardens." They are artificial islands built in shallow lake beds, combining canal dredging with intensive food production.

### Construction

1. Stake out rectangular plots in shallow water (2-4 feet deep)
2. Build up boundaries with mud, reeds, and woven willow
3. Dredge nutrient-rich canal sediment onto the plot surface
4. Plant fast-growing trees (willow, ahuejote) at corners to anchor the island
5. Maintain by periodically dredging canal muck back onto the surface

### Productivity

- Chinampas were among the most productive agricultural systems ever developed
- Multiple harvests per year possible in tropical/subtropical climates
- Canal water provides constant irrigation and thermal moderation
- Aquatic plants and fish from canals provide additional protein
- The system is essentially self-fertilizing through sediment cycling
