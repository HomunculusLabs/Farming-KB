# Stock Density Versus Stocking Rate

The distinction between stock density and stocking rate is one of the
most important and least appreciated concepts in [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]]
Management. Confusion between these two fundamentally different measures
has led to decades of misguided policy and land degradation worldwide.
Understanding the difference is essential for anyone seeking to manage
grazing land effectively.

## Stocking Rate Defined

Stocking rate describes the number of animals continuously supported
by a given unit of land over an extended period, typically expressed as
animal units per section, hectare, or acre on an annual basis. It is a
long-term, average measure that tells you how many animals the land can
carry through the year.

Conventional [[mollison-pdc-range-management-wildlife]] has historically focused almost entirely
on stocking rate as the primary management variable. The standard
recommendation has been to calculate a "proper" stocking rate based on
estimated carrying capacity and then adjust animal numbers up or down
to match.

## Stock Density Defined

Stock density reflects the concentration of animals on the unit of land
where they actually are at a given moment. It is expressed as animals
per acre or hectare at a specific point in time. Unlike stocking rate,
stock density can vary enormously from moment to moment depending on
paddock size and the number of paddocks in a grazing system.

For example, three hundred cows on four thousand acres represents a
stocking rate of one cow per thirteen acres. But if those same three
hundred cows are concentrated in a single forty-acre paddock, the stock
density at that moment is seven and a half cows per acre, a dramatic
difference that profoundly affects both the animals and the land.

## The Critical Distinction

Neither stocking rate nor stock density alone describes whether animals
are feeding placidly or milling around in herd effect. That behavioral
dimension makes a critical difference to the land that neither metric
captures. However, stock density is far more relevant to understanding
what actually happens to individual plants and soil surfaces.

Stocking rate has little bearing on what happens to any individual
plant. One should discuss overgrazing only in regard to individual
plants. Applying the word to a whole area falls into the same
irrelevancies as trying to define proper utilization of key species.

As paddock numbers increase and paddock size decreases, stock density
increases. This means animals are concentrated on smaller areas for
shorter periods, producing more even utilization of available forage
while allowing longer recovery periods on the paddocks they have left.

## Stock Density and Animal Impact

Remember that stock density always equals the amount of forage consumed
per acre multiplied by the number of paddocks. As paddock numbers
increase and paddock size in each paddock decreases, stock density
increases, causing better distribution of grazing and animal impact.

High stock density, properly managed through time, produces several
critical effects. It ensures more even utilization of available forage
across all plants. It increases the proportion of plants that receive
some defoliation rather than concentrating on preferred species. It
creates the conditions for herd effect when animals are bunched. And it
provides adequate recovery time for plants after relatively brief but
intense grazing periods.

## The Stocking Rate Fallacy

Savory challenged the conventional emphasis on stocking rate through
extensive field observations. On rangelands across Africa, the Americas,
and the Middle East, he never saw prolonged land deterioration caused by
overstocking. Almost all sites commonly called overstocked did contain
overgrazed plants, but virtually always also contained a significant
quantity of overrested plants along with [[savory-bare-ground-and-soil-exposure]] from [[savory-partial-rest-and-algal-crusts]].

The presence of overrested plants belies the description "overstocked"
altogether. The blame for deterioration always fell on animal numbers,
when the real problem was the combination of partial rest (animals too
few and scattered to provide adequate disturbance) and overgrazing of
select plants that animals returned to too frequently.

## Stock Density and Recovery Periods

The relationship between stock density and recovery periods is
fundamental. Higher stock density means animals are concentrated on
smaller areas for shorter times, which in turn allows longer recovery
periods on the rested paddocks. This relationship is the key to
minimizing both overgrazing (by not staying too long) and overrest (by
not staying away too long).

When planning grazing, paddock size is ideally determined on the basis
of desired recovery periods rather than vice versa. The duration of
recovery periods is decided based on plant growth rates, which vary
with season, rainfall, temperature, and plant species composition.

## Economic Implications

Ranches running at far higher than conventional stocking rates on planned
grazing have demonstrated that stock density, properly managed through
time, can support more animals while improving land health. This occurs
because higher stock density with adequate recovery allows each acre to
produce more forage through healthier plants, better water cycles, and
improved mineral cycling.

The economic implication is profound: land productivity is not a fixed
quantity determined by carrying capacity. It can be increased through
management that addresses the time dimension and maintains all four
[[fungal-ecosystem-processes-overview]] at high function.
