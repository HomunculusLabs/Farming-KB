# In Vitro Compartmented Systems for AMF Transport Studies

## The Core Concept

Compartmented culture systems separate the root zone from the hyphal zone,
allowing researchers to study element transport by [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] (AM)
fungi without interference from direct root uptake. The fundamental design places
roots and AM fungi in one compartment (the root compartment, RC) while restricting
the other compartment (the hyphal compartment, HC) to the exclusive development
of the extraradical mycelium (ERM).

This separation enables the use of [[isotopic-tracers-mycorrhizal-research]] in the HC — any labeled
nutrient appearing in the plant must have been transported through the fungal
mycelium, providing direct evidence of [[dighton-ericoid-mycorrhizal-nutrient-acquisition]] uptake and transfer.

## Historical Development

The first bi-compartmented pot culture systems appeared in the late 1960s and
early 1970s, developed by researchers including Hattingh et al. (1973), Pearson
and Tinker (1975), Rhodes and Gerdemann (1975), and Cooper and Tinker (1978,
1981). These systems convincingly demonstrated the essential role of AM fungi in
phosphorus uptake and translocation to host plants — a finding that transformed
understanding of mycorrhizal function.

## Limitations of Soil-Based Compartmented Systems

Traditional soil-based compartmented pots suffer from five major drawbacks:

1. **Undesirable microorganisms** — soil carries diverse microbes that influence
   element bioavailability and transport processes
2. **Visualization difficulty** — the soil matrix obscures fungal and root
   development, making observation and recovery challenging
3. **Soil-element interactions** — elements interact with soil particles through
   adsorption, precipitation, and complexation, complicating transport analysis
4. **Tracer leakage** — diffusion and mass flow can carry isotopic tracers from
   the HC into the RC, confounding results
5. **Physiological complexity** — molecular and physiological studies on
   extraradical mycelium are difficult in soil systems

## The In Vitro Solution: Monoxenic Culture

To overcome these limitations, researchers developed monoxenic (single-symbiont)
in vitro systems using root-organ cultures (Ri T-DNA transformed carrot roots) or
whole plants as hosts. These axenic systems provide complete control over the
biological and chemical environment.

### Bi-Compartmented Petri Plate Design

The standard apparatus is a bi-compartmented Petri plate (90mm diameter) divided
by a physical barrier that allows only fungal hyphae to pass. The system
consists of:

- **Root compartment (RC)** — contains the culture medium and host roots with
  intraradical fungal colonization
- **Hyphal compartment (HC)** — accessible only to extraradical mycelium,
  where isotopic tracers or test compounds are applied
- **Barrier** — typically a physical divider with a narrow passage or membrane
  that excludes roots but permits hyphal passage

### Culture Media

Two media are most widely used for in vitro AMF culture:

**Modified Strullu-Romand (MSR) medium** — developed by Declerck et al. (1998)
from the original Strullu and Romand (1986) formulation. Contains both nitrate
(3.8 mM) and ammonium (0.18 mM) nitrogen sources, a full complement of
macroelements and microelements, and vitamins including biotin, thiamine,
pyridoxine, and cyanocobalamine. Solidified with 3 g/L Gelrite, Phytagel, or
8 g/L agar. pH adjusted to 5.5.

**Minimal (M) medium** — developed by Bécard and Fortin (1988). A simpler
formulation with nitrate as the sole nitrogen source (3.2 mM), fewer vitamin
supplements, and the addition of glycine (3 mg/L) and myo-inositol (50 mg/L).

### Critical Considerations for Gelling Agents

Gelling agents are not inert — they contain variable quantities of macro-,
micro-, [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]] that can significantly alter medium composition. For
example, Gelrite and Phytagel contain substantial quantities of K and P that can
affect experimental outcomes, particularly in phosphorus transport studies. The
choice and concentration of gelling agent should be validated for each experiment.

## Equipment Requirements

Essential equipment includes:

- [[comparison-laminar-flow-hood-vs-fruiting-chamber]] (horizontal or vertical)
- Stereo-microscope (10–40× magnification)
- Bunsen burner or bead sterilizer
- Temperature-controlled incubator
- Climatic chamber for whole-plant systems
- Rotating agitator for medium preparation
- Bi-compartmented Petri plates, sterile scalpels, forceps, cork-borers

## Applications in Transport Studies

These systems have been used to study:

- **Phosphorus uptake and transfer** — the classic application, using 32P or 33P
  isotopic tracers in the HC to quantify fungal-mediated P delivery to the host
- **Heavy metal transport** — studying how AM fungi affect plant uptake of Cd,
  Zn, Cu, and other metals from contaminated compartments
- **Nitrogen transport** — using 15N-labeled compounds to trace N movement
  through the mycorrhizal pathway
- **Water transport** — measuring hydraulic connections through fungal hyphae
- **Carbon allocation** — tracking photosynthate flow from host to fungus using
  14C labeling

## Advantages Over Soil Systems

1. **Axenic conditions** — no confounding microorganisms
2. **Complete visualization** — transparent medium allows [[fukuoka-direct-observation-method]] of
   hyphal growth, arbuscule formation, and spore development
3. **Precise chemical control** — known medium composition without soil
   interference
4. **No tracer leakage** — physical barriers prevent diffusion artifacts
5. **Reproducibility** — standardized conditions across experiments
6. **Molecular access** — clean tissue samples for gene expression and proteomic
   studies

## Limitations

- Root-organ cultures are not whole plants — some physiological processes
  differ from intact plant systems
- The artificial environment may not reflect field conditions
- Not all AMF species are amenable to in vitro culture
- Long-term experiments may be limited by medium exhaustion
- Scale is small compared to soil-based systems
