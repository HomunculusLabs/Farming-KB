# Fukuoka's Five Principles of [[masanobu-fukuoka]] (1913–2008) was a Japanese farmer and philosopher who developed a radical approach to agriculture he called "natural farming" (自然農法, shizen nōhō). After working as a plant pathologist at the Yokohama customs inspection station and experiencing a profound spiritual crisis in his twenties, he returned to his family farm on Shikoku Island in Ehime Prefecture. There he spent over fifty years refining a method that completely rejects the core practices of modern agriculture.

### 2. No Fertilizer (無肥料)

Neither chemical fertilizers nor prepared compost are used. Fukuoka observed that the ancient practice of applying manure and compost does accelerate crop growth in the short term, but it depletes the land from which the organic material was originally taken — a form of robbing Peter to pay Paul.

Chemical fertilizers, which became widely adopted only in the decades following World War II, create a dependency cycle: they stimulate rapid growth but degrade soil biology over time, requiring ever-increasing inputs to maintain yields. Instead of external fertility inputs, Fukuoka relied on the natural fertility maintained by three sources:

- Decomposing straw mulch returned to the field after each harvest
- Nitrogen-fixing white clover grown as a living ground cover
- The manure of free-ranging chickens that roamed through his orchard

The straw mulch was the most critical element — by returning all crop residues to the field, the nutrient cycle was closed and no fertility was exported from the system.

### 3. No Pesticides (無農薬)

Although thousands of diseases and insects affect plants in fields and forests, Fukuoka maintained that nature maintains its own dynamic balance without human intervention. He believed that what farmers call "pest damage" is largely a consequence of weakened plants grown in artificial conditions — plants that are over-fertilized, grown in monoculture, and denied the protection of a diverse plant community.

When crops are grown in healthy, living soil as part of a diverse ecosystem, pest problems are minimal and self-regulating. Fukuoka observed that in his own fields, which contained a rich diversity of plants including clover, weeds, and various vegetables growing semi-wild, pest outbreaks were rare.

When they did occur, they were quickly brought under control by predatory insects and birds attracted by the diverse habitat. Pesticides create the very problems they claim to solve by killing both pests and the natural predators that keep them in check, while also contaminating soil, water, and the crops themselves.

### 4. No Weeding (無除草)

Nature does not arbitrarily designate certain plants as "weeds" to be eradicated. So-called weeds serve critical ecological functions: they prevent soil erosion, fix atmospheric nitrogen, cycle minerals from deep soil layers, provide habitat and food for beneficial insects, contribute organic matter to the soil surface, and maintain soil moisture through shading.

The distinction between "crop" and "weed" is a human invention with no basis in natural ecology. Fukuoka's method used white clover (*[[bill-mollison]] and [[david-holmgren]] acknowledged his influence), the no-till farming movement, conservation agriculture, and the broader sustainable agriculture movement. His books have been translated into more than twenty languages.

## The Quarter-Acre Vision

Fukuoka believed that anyone could become self-sufficient on a quarter-acre of land using natural farming methods. He envisioned a world of decentralized, small-scale farmers who grew their own food without reliance on external inputs, fossil fuels, or global supply chains. This vision was not merely agricultural but political and spiritual — a rejection of the industrial system that he saw as fundamentally destructive to both nature and human community. The young people who came to his farm to learn were not just learning to grow rice; they were learning a different way of being in the world.
