# Fukuoka's Five Principles of Natural Farming

Masanobu Fukuoka (1913–2008) developed a revolutionary approach to agriculture based on five core principles: **no tillage, no fertilizer, no pesticides, no weeding, and no pruning**. Practiced on his farm in Ehime Prefecture, Japan for over fifty years, this "do-nothing" farming method consistently yielded 22 bushels (1,300 pounds) of winter grain and 22 bushels of rice per quarter-acre—comparable to or exceeding [[fukuoka-critique-scientific-agriculture-civilization]] creates the very problems it then claims to solve: plowing compacts soil and necessitates further plowing; [[non-discriminating-knowledge-and-critique-of-scientific-reductionism]], inevitably disrupts the organic wholeness of living systems. Once broken down, nature cannot be returned to its original state. Fukuoka called this the "methodless method of nature, the unmoving way of Bodhidharma."

### 3. No Pesticides

Nature maintains balance among thousands of plant diseases without human intervention. Fukuoka described the cascade of destruction caused by pesticide application in Japanese rice fields, where toxic chemicals are applied at least eight times per year:

- **First application**: Tens of thousands of young spiders per square yard vanish; swarms of fireflies disappear at once.
- **Second application**: Chalcid wasps—important natural predators—are killed; dragonfly larvae, tadpoles, and loaches are left victimized.

Yet insect damage in sprayed fields remains largely the same as in unsprayed fields. In Fukuoka's fields, whenever leafhopper outbreaks occurred, spider populations multiplied in response. As long as natural enemies are also present, a natural balance asserts itself. Fukuoka noted that pesticides ruin the soil and create pollution problems, while the blanket application of poisons kills beneficial organisms far more effectively than pests.

### 4. No Weeding

Nature does not arbitrarily classify one plant as a weed and attempt to eradicate it. The white clover growing in Fukuoka's fields served as a living mulch that fixed nitrogen, suppressed unwanted vegetation, and maintained soil moisture. Rather than fighting weeds, his method incorporated beneficial ground covers into the cropping system as integral partners. The clover was weakened temporarily by flooding but allowed to recover, creating a dynamic balance between the cover crop and the grain.

### 5. No Pruning

Fruit trees grow best in their natural habitat, where branches do not tangle, sunlight falls on every leaf, and the tree bears fully each year rather than in alternate years. Pruning is presented as an unnecessary human intervention that disrupts the tree's natural growth patterns and creates dependency on continued human management. Semi-wild vegetables grew in the clover among the trees in Fukuoka's orchard, and chickens ran free through the grove.

## The Direct-Seeded, No-Tillage Rice/Barley Method

Fukuoka's signature technique is called "direct-seeded, no-tillage, winter grain/rice succession in a clover cover." The annual cycle proceeds as follows:

1. **Autumn**: Broadcast clover and winter grain (barley or wheat) over the ripening rice heads before harvest. Harvest rice while treading on the young grain shoots. Scatter rice straw uncut over the entire field.
2. **Winter**: Form clay pellets containing seed rice and scatter them over the straw before the New Year.
3. **Spring (late May)**: Harvest winter grain while clover grows luxuriantly and rice seedlings emerge from clay pellets. Scatter all straw uncut.
4. **Early summer**: Flood the field for four to five days to weaken clover and give rice shoots a competitive advantage.
5. **Mid-summer**: Leave field unirrigated through June and July.
6. **Late summer (August)**: Run water through drainage ditches once every week or ten days.

The entire labor requirement for a quarter-acre amounts to sowing seed and spreading straw—the work of one or two people. Fukuoka summarized: "Were I to say that all my method of farming boils down to is the symbiosis of rice and barley or wheat in clover, I would probably be reproached. Yet, that is all there is to it."

## Energy Efficiency Comparison

Natural farming produces 130 pounds of rice (200,000 kilocalories) per man-day of labor, without outside material inputs—approximately 100 times the daily caloric intake of a farmer. By comparison, the energy input in calories was doubled with each technological leap:

- **Traditional farming** with draft animals: ~10x the energy input per calorie produced
- **Small-scale mechanization**: ~20x the energy input
- **Large-scale mechanization**: ~40x the energy input

This geometric progression has produced the energy-intensive methods of modern agriculture, where farmers must work additional hours off-farm to pay for equipment they were told would make their work easier. Fukuoka observed that they had "exchanged the joy of working outdoors in the open fields for dreary hours of labor shut up inside a factory."

## Critique of Scientific vs. Natural Agriculture

Fukuoka distinguished between two fundamental trajectories:

- **Natural farming**: Centrifugal and convergent—moving toward non-action and return to nature. Simple, economical, and labor-saving.
- **[[fukuoka-organic-to-natural-farming-transition]] hoping for higher yields, but this introduces the basic contradiction of human intervention into a system that works precisely because it lacks intervention.

## The Orchard Ecosystem

Beyond grain production, Fukuoka maintained a hilltop orchard overlooking the Inland Sea where chickens ran free and semi-wild vegetables grew among the trees. The orchard operated on the same principles: no pruning, no pesticides, no fertilization. Fruit trees in their natural state, he argued, do not require human shaping to bear abundantly.
