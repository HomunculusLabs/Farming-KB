## Psychedelic Plants and Human Evolution

[[terence-mckenna]] proposed that the interaction between early humans and
psychoactive plants was a decisive factor in the emergence of language,
culture, and self-reflective consciousness. His hypothesis, often called
the "stoned ape" theory, argues that psilocybin-containing mushrooms
ingested by hominids catalyzed cognitive developments that set humans on
a trajectory distinct from other primates.

## The Core Hypothesis

McKenna argued that early hominids foraging in African grasslands would
have encountered and consumed psilocybin-containing mushrooms, particularly
species of Psilocybe growing on dung of grazing herbivores. The effects of
psilocybin at different doses would have produced distinct adaptive
advantages.

At low doses, psilocybin increases visual acuity, particularly edge
detection. McKenna suggested this would have improved hunting success. At
moderate doses, psilocybin increases sexual arousal and social bonding,
potentially strengthening group cohesion. At higher doses, psilocybin
produces visionary experiences and dissolution of ego boundaries.

## Language and Symbolic Thought

The most significant claim is that psychedelic experiences catalyzed the
development of language. McKenna observed that under the influence of
psychedelics, people frequently experience synesthesia, the crossing of
sensory modalities. Sounds produce visual imagery, and visual objects seem
to emit sounds or meaning.

He proposed that this synesthetic experience provided the neurological
foundation for the development of symbolic thought. If a sound could
naturally evoke a visual image, the step to using sounds as symbols for
objects became shorter. The emergence of language from synesthetic
experience would represent a bridge between pre-linguistic primate
communication and fully symbolic human language.

## The Partnership-Dominator Transition

McKenna drew on Riane Eisler's framework of partnership versus dominator
societies. Partnership societies, which he associated with the Archaic
period of nomadic pastoralism, shamanism, and goddess worship, were
egalitarian and oriented toward ecological balance. Dominator societies
are hierarchical, patriarchal, and oriented toward control and conquest.

He argued that psychedelic plants sustained the partnership model by
providing regular access to ecstatic states that dissolved ego boundaries
and reinforced communal bonds. The suppression of psychedelic use
accompanied and enabled the rise of dominator culture, which required
rigid hierarchies and the suppression of individual direct experience of
the sacred.

## Shamanism as the Original Religion

McKenna characterized shamanism as the practice of the Upper Paleolithic
tradition of healing, divination, and theatrical performance based on
natural magic. Following Mircea Eliade, he emphasized that shamanism
maintains surprising internal coherence across all cultures and time
periods. The shaman undergoes symbolic death and resurrection, gaining
access to a superhuman plane from which they can heal and divine.

For McKenna, the shaman's use of psychedelic plants is the original form
of religious practice. All subsequent organized religions are
derivatives that replaced direct ecstatic experience with dogma and
priestcraft. The suppression of shamanic plant use is therefore the
suppression of humanity's oldest spiritual tradition.

## A World Made of Language

McKenna argued from shamanic experience that the world is in some
fundamental sense made of language. Drawing on linguistic theory, he
cited the proposition that language does not merely communicate ideas
about the world but actively brings the world into existence. For the
psychedelic shaman, reality resembles an utterance that becomes true as
it is told.

This perspective implies that human imagination can seize what McKenna
called the tiller of being. It connects the shamanic tradition to the
modern understanding of consciousness as constructive rather than
passively receptive. The shaman is, in this framework, the ancestor of
the poet and artist.

## Criticisms and Scientific Assessment

The [[stoned-ape-theory]] is not widely accepted in mainstream anthropology
or evolutionary biology. Critics note the absence of archaeological
evidence for systematic psilocybin use by early hominids, and the
difficulty of testing the hypothesis empirically. The neurological
effects of psilocybin on visual acuity and sexual behavior, while
documented, have not been demonstrated to produce evolutionary
advantages measurable on geological timescales.

McKenna's historical claims about the suppression of psychedelics by
dominator cultures are also debated. Some scholars argue that the
relationship between psychoactive plant use and social organization is
more complex and bidirectional than McKenna's framework allows.
