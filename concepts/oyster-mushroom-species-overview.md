# Oyster Mushroom Species Overview

Oyster mushrooms belong to the genus Pleurotus, one of the
most widely cultivated groups of edible fungi worldwide.
The genus contains over 30 described species found on
every inhabited continent, growing on dead or dying
hardwood in diverse ecosystems ranging from temperate
forests to tropical woodlands. Oyster mushrooms are valued
for their culinary versatility, nutritional content, rapid
growth rates, and remarkable ability to colonize a wide
range of agricultural waste substrate materials with
minimal processing.

## Taxonomy and Diversity

Pleurotus is classified in the family Pleurotaceae, order
Agaricales. Recent molecular phylogeny has reorganized the
genus considerably, with several species moved to new
genera based on DNA sequence data. The core commercially
cultivated species remain within Pleurotus sensu stricto.
The genus is characterized by eccentric (off-center)
stems, decurrent gill attachment (gills running down the
stem), and white to lilac spore prints depending on
species.

## Key Cultivated Species

### Pleurotus ostreatus (Pearl Oyster)
The most widely cultivated oyster mushroom globally, pearl
oysters produce shelf-like clusters with gray to brown
caps measuring 5-15 cm in diameter. They are extremely
aggressive colonizers, capable of fruiting on a remarkably
broad range of substrates including straw, sawdust, paper,
coffee grounds, and various agricultural waste materials.
Fruiting occurs at 12-25 degrees C. This species is often
the first choice for beginning cultivators due to its fast
growth rate and forgiving nature regarding environmental
conditions and substrate preparation.

### Pleurotus pulmonarius (Phoenix Oyster)
Similar in appearance to P. ostreatus but typically with
paler caps and a preference for warmer temperatures (18-30
degrees C). Phoenix oysters are popular in subtropical and
tropical regions where cooler-growing species perform
poorly. They tend to produce larger individual fruiting
bodies and have a slightly longer productive growing
season than pearl oysters. Some strains exhibit a
distinctive pink or salmon tint on young caps that fades
to white at maturity.

### Pleurotus eryngii (King Oyster)
King oysters are the largest members of the genus,
producing thick, meaty stems up to 10 cm in diameter and
caps 5-10 cm across. They are prized in high-end cuisine
for their firm, umami-rich texture that holds up
exceptionally well to grilling, braising, and other
cooking methods. King oysters require cooler temperatures
(12-18 degrees C) and longer colonization times (21-30
days) compared to other Pleurotus species. They grow best
on supplemented sawdust substrates rather than straw.

### Pleurotus citrinopileatus (Golden Oyster)
Named for their striking bright yellow caps, golden
oysters are native to East Asia and have become
increasingly popular among specialty growers and farmers
market vendors. They prefer warmer fruiting temperatures
(20-28 degrees C) and have a delicate, slightly nutty
flavor profile. Golden oysters are more sensitive to
contamination than other Pleurotus species and require
careful substrate preparation and handling during the
inoculation and colonization phases.

### Pleurotus djamor (Pink Oyster)
Pink oysters produce vivid pink to salmon-colored fruiting
bodies that gradually fade to pale orange or white as they
mature and approach harvest maturity. They are native to
tropical regions and require warm conditions (22-30
degrees C) for successful fruiting. Pink oysters have a
shorter shelf life than other oyster species and a more
intense, slightly pungent aroma. They are extremely fast
colonizers and fruit prolifically when conditions are
optimal.

### Pleurotus columbinus (Blue Oyster)
Blue oysters produce distinctive steel-blue caps that
develop gray tones as they mature and expand. They are
notably cold-tolerant, fruiting well at temperatures as
low as 10 degrees C, making them excellent candidates for
outdoor spring and autumn cultivation in temperate
climates. Blue oysters produce dense, substantial clusters
and have a robust, earthy flavor that is popular with
chefs and home cooks alike.

## Ecological Role

Oyster mushrooms are primary wood decomposers (white-rot
fungi) that secrete a suite of lignocellulolytic enzymes
including laccases, manganese peroxidases, and cellulases.
This ability to break down complex plant polymers makes
them effective agents for bioremediation, mycoremediation
of polluted soils, and bioconversion of agricultural waste
into protein-rich food and animal feed products.

## Nutritional Profile

Oyster mushrooms are low in calories but rich in protein
(containing all essential amino acids), dietary fiber, B
vitamins (especially niacin, riboflavin, and pantothenic
acid), and minerals including potassium, phosphorus, and
iron. They contain beta-glucans and other bioactive
polysaccharides with documented immunomodulatory
properties. Oyster mushrooms also naturally produce
lovastatin, a cholesterol-lowering compound, at
biologically significant concentrations.
