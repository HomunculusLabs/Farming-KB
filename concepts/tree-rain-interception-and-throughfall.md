## Tree Rain Interception and Throughfall

## The Problem of Bare Soil Under Rain

Rainfall delivers enormous energy to the earth — many tons may impact in an hour. On bare soils and thinly spaced crops, the impact of droplets carries away soil, typically removing **80 tonnes per hectare**, or up to 1,000 tonnes in extreme downpours. When we bare the soil, we lose the earth.

Water runoff and pan evaporation, estimated at **80-90% of all rain** falling on Australia, carries off nutrients and silt to the sea or to inland basins. As land is cleared, runoff initially increases and fills dams quickly, but dams eventually silt up, rivers cease to flow, and the clearing of forests results in **flood and drought** rather than a long-regulated and steady supply of clean water.

## The Forest Rain Interaction Process

When rain falls on a forest, a complex process begins that transforms the destructive energy of rainfall into a gentle, nutrient-delivering system.

### Step 1: Canopy Shelter and Impact Neutralisation

The tree canopy shelters and neutralises the impact effect of raindrops, reducing rain to a **thin mist below the canopy**, even in the most torrential showers. There is slight measurable silt loss from mature forests, which is exceeded by the creation of soils by forests.

### Step 2: Interception

If rain is light, little penetrates beyond the canopy. A film of water spreads across leaves and stems, trapped by surface tension. Tree cells absorb what they need, and the remainder evaporates to air. Where no rain penetrates through the canopy, this is termed **"total interception."**

**Interception** is the amount of rainfall caught in the crown. It is the most important primary effect of trees or forests on rain.

#### Factors Influencing Interception

- **Crown thickness** — deeper crowns intercept more rain
- **Crown density** — denser foliage captures more water
- **Season** — deciduous vs. evergreen state matters greatly
- **Intensity of rain** — light rain is almost entirely intercepted
- **Evaporation after rain** — sunny conditions re-evaporate intercepted water

#### Interception Rates

Broadly, interception commonly falls between **10-15% of total rainfall**:

| Condition | Interception Level |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Least interception | ~10% — thinned/deciduous forests, winter rain, heavy showers, cloudy weather |
| Greatest interception | Up to 100% — dense evergreen trees, light summer rain, sunny conditions |

### Step 3: Throughfall

When more rain falls, or heavy rains impact on the trees, water commences to drift as mists or droplets to earth. This water is called **throughfall**. Throughfall depends on the intensity of rain, and there is little interception effect in heavy downpours. As an average figure, throughfall is approximately **85% of humid areas' precipitation**.

#### Throughfall as Enriched Water

Throughfall is no longer just rainwater — it is a much richer brew containing:
- Dissolved salts
- Organic content
- Dust particles
- Plant cells and nutrients
- Plant exudates

Research (Murray and Mitchell, "Red Gum and the Nutrient Balance") found that "rain washes large amounts of potassium and smaller amounts of nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium, and magnesium from the canopies to the surface soil. Litter adds organic matter, and is a rich source of calcium and nitrogen and a moderately rich source of magnesium and potassium."

### Step 4: Directed Water Distribution

Throughfall cannot be measured in rain gauges because trees provide special receptors, conduits, and storages for such water. The random fall of rain is converted into **well-directed patterns of flow** that serve the needs and growth of the forest:

- **Stem bases** of palms, plantains, and many epiphytes hold water as aerial ponds, often rich in algae and mosquitoes
- **Flanged roots** of Terminalia trees and figs create water reservoirs
- **Stem mosses and epiphytes** absorb many times their bulk of water
- The tree itself **directs water via insloping branches and fissured bark** to its tap roots
- **Spiders** catch their share on webs
- **Fungi** soak up what they need
- Some trees trail **weeping branches** to direct throughfall to their fibrous peripheral roots

## Provision of Nuclei for Rain

### Organic Particles as Rain Nuclei

The upward spirals of humid air rising from the forest carry insects, pollen, and bacteria aloft. This is best observed in flights of gulls, swifts, and ibis spiralling up with warm air, actively catching insects lifted from the forest. Their gastric pellets consist entirely of insect remains.

These **organic aerial particles** (pollen, leaf dust, and bacteria) create the nuclei for raindrop formation.

### The Kenya Tea Hailstorm Case Study

The violent hailstorms that plague Kenya tea plantings may be caused by tea dust stirred up by local winds and the feet of pickers. Once above ground, particles are drawn up into thunderheads to form hailstorms. Kenyan organic tea leaf litter caused water to freeze in a test chamber at only **-5°C**, compared with:
- **-11°C** for eucalyptus grove leaf litter
- **-8°C** for indigenous forest litter from Colorado
- **-8°C to -10°C** for silver iodide (the artificial cloud-seeding agent)

Tea litter is thus a much better seeding-agent than silver iodide. The materials given up by vegetation may be a **critical factor in rainfall** inland from forests.

## Summary: Trees as Integrated Water Systems

Trees transform rainfall from a destructive force into a life-giving resource through a multi-stage process:

1. **Impact neutralisation** — canopy breaks raindrop energy
2. **Interception** — crown captures 10-100% of rainfall depending on conditions
3. **Throughfall enrichment** — water reaching the ground carries nutrients washed from canopy
4. **Directed distribution** — bark, branches, and roots channel water to where it's needed
5. **Atmospheric nucleation** — organic particles from forests seed rain formation downwind

This integrated system explains why deforestation leads simultaneously to increased flooding (from lost interception and impact buffering) and increased drought (from lost condensation, rehumidification, and nucleation). The tree is not merely a resident of the water cycle — it is one of its primary architects.

## Practical Applications
The principles discussed here have direct applications in agricultural systems, ecological restoration, and sustainable resource management.
Practitioners have demonstrated successful implementation across diverse climates and soil conditions.
Adaptation to local conditions and careful observation remain central to effective application.
