# Farming with Air, Water and Sunlight

"Farming with air, water and sunlight" is a natural farming philosophy articulated by Gil Carandang of Herbana Farms, Philippines. It holds that 96% of plant and animal growth derives from these three elemental forces of nature, with only 4% attributable to applied nutrients. The principle urges farmers to prioritize optimizing these natural elements before focusing on fertilization.

## The 96/4 Principle

Carandang observed that in healthy forests, plants grow vigorously without any deliberate fertilizer application. They achieve this by fully utilizing the powers of air, water, and sunlight available in nature. Nutrients are derived from natural soil fertility as organic matter decays and is processed by microorganisms. The implication is that conventional agriculture's fixation on NPK (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) inputs misses the dominant factors driving plant growth.

This framework parallels [[natural-farming-fukuoka]]'s observation that nature is complete and self-sustaining, and that human agricultural interventions often disrupt rather than enhance natural productivity. It also aligns with [[natural-farm-permaculture-ethics-principles]] emphasis on working with natural systems rather than against them.

## Air

### Soil Aeration

Air is vital to plant growth, yet its role is frequently overlooked. Carandang draws on biointensive mini-farming techniques, specifically double digging, which loosens soil to 24 inches compared to the 8-10 inches achieved by conventional plows and tractors. Deeper loosened soil allows more air to pass through, improving root respiration and microbial activity.

In [[fukuoka-natural-soil-fertility]] and [[fukuoka-soil-building-approach]], Fukuoka similarly avoided deep tillage, relying instead on soil biology to maintain structure. Carandang's approach differs in explicitly calling for mechanical loosening to maximize air penetration.

### Livestock Ventilation

For animal systems, air movement and ventilation are critical. Carandang mandates exhaust ventilation in poultry housing, with loose litter substrates (sawdust, wood shavings, compost) up to one yard deep to allow air circulation through the flooring material. The contrast with conventional high-density poultry operations — where crowding, decomposing manure, and lack of airflow create disease pressure requiring antibiotics — illustrates how air quality determines whether natural or chemical approaches succeed.

## Water

### Water Quality

Clean, chemically untreated water is essential for both plants and animals. Carandang specifically warns against using chlorinated water when working with beneficial microorganisms, as chlorine kills the live cultures in [[beneficial-indigenous-microorganisms-bim]] and [[lactic-acid-bacteria-lab]] preparations.

### Aquaculture Application

In aquaculture, poor water quality from fish excretions creates ammonia that stresses fish, stunts growth, and affects health. Regular addition of beneficial microorganisms to the water minimizes ammonia by completing the denitrification cycle, converting wastes into non-harmful forms. This approach is shared by [[knf-poultry-natural-farming]] and [[jadam-microorganism-solution-jms]] methods.

### Moisture Management

Carandang emphasizes that plants die without adequate moisture, but the form of water delivery matters. He advocates for soil that retains moisture through organic matter and microbial activity rather than relying solely on irrigation. This connects to [[urban-permaculture-rainwater-harvesting-graywater]] and [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] approaches to water management.

## Sunlight

### Plant Orientation

Carandang applies precise orientation principles for crop planting. East-west orientation maximizes direct sunlight exposure as the sun tracks across the sky. North-south orientation is used with interplanting or companion planting, where shorter plants are positioned toward the northern portion to maximize light utilization and shading patterns. See [[zone-sector-analysis-guide]] for sector-based solar design.

### Lunar Timing

Carandang incorporates lunar planting cycles, recommending transplanting a few days before the new moon. The rationale is that the moon reflects sunlight, and planting toward the new or full moon effectively increases available light energy for newly transplanted seedlings. This aligns with biodynamic planting calendars and traditional farming wisdom across many cultures.

### Livestock Housing Design

Poultry housing should be oriented east-west for maximum sunlight exposure, with transparent roofing sections to allow direct sunlight into the chicken house. Carandang observed that conventional poultry operations with no sunlight penetration are among the primary drivers of disease, necessitating antibiotic use.

### Seasonal Awareness

Carandang notes that full moons bring more nighttime light, influencing plant growth patterns. This sensitivity to natural light cycles extends beyond simple photosynthesis to encompass broader ecological rhythms that affect plant and animal development.

## Synthesis with Microbial Farming

The "farming with air, water and sunlight" philosophy integrates with Carandang's microbial farming approach. Beneficial microorganisms thrive when these elements are optimized — aerobic microbes need air, fermentation requires controlled moisture, and photosynthetic bacteria need sunlight. The three elements are not alternatives to microbial farming but prerequisites for it.

[[fukuoka-do-nothing-farming-and-spiritual-life]] similarly emphasizes alignment with natural forces, though framed in spiritual rather than elemental terms. [[permaculture-design-principles]] captures the same ethic of reading natural conditions before intervening.

## Critique of Conventional Agriculture

Carandang argues that conventional farmers have become "myopic" by focusing exclusively on NPK fertilization and synthetic pesticide application. When pests and diseases occur, the conventional response is to spray chemicals; for livestock, the emphasis is on feeds and antibiotics. This reductionist approach ignores the 96% of growth driven by elemental forces that cost nothing.

He further distinguishes between truly sustainable organic farming and "industrial organic" operations that still depend on purchased inputs. A large organic farm he visited in Watsonville, California, practiced soil-eroding methods while purchasing all inputs — technically organic but fundamentally unsustainable. This critique connects to [[natural-farming-vs-organic-farming]] and [[jadam-ultra-low-cost-agriculture]].

## Practical Applications

The principle translates into concrete farming decisions:

- Site selection prioritizing full sun exposure and natural airflow

- Deep soil preparation (double digging) to maximize root access to air

- Housing and infrastructure design oriented to capture sunlight and air movement

- Avoidance of chlorinated water in biological farming systems

- Lunar-aware planting and transplanting schedules

- Recognition that most growth challenges stem from elemental deficiencies rather than nutrient deficiencies
