# Insect-Fungi Interactions

Insects and fungi have interacted with one another for 400 million years
or more, forming relationships that range from mutually beneficial
symbiosis to lethal parasitism. These interactions have profoundly
shaped the evolution of both groups, driving the development of
specialized [[plant-hormones-and-chemical-signaling-systems]] systems, morphological adaptations, and
complex life cycles. Insects serve as vectors for fungal spore
dispersal, as food sources for [[biodiversity-nematophagous-predatory-fungi]], and as targets for
[[bloomfield-fungal-pathogens-and-parasites]].

## Insects as Fungal Pollinators

Rust fungi exemplify the intimate relationship between fungi and
insects. [[basidiomycete-mating-systems-sexual-reproduction]] in rusts depends on the active
participation of insects. When the rust *Puccinia graminis* has grown
within a barberry leaf for several days, it forms tiny flask-shaped
spermagonia that rupture the upper [[aact-foliar-sticker-spreaders-leaf-surface-attachment-ingham]]. Thin hairs protrude
through the opening, and each spermagonium exudes sugary nectar and
masses of spherical cells called spermatia that function as sperm.

Flies and other insects, attracted by the nectar, carry spermatia from
one spermagonium to another. During feeding, spermatia are brushed onto
the hairs of a compatible spermagonium, and the spermatial nucleus is
injected into the hair, migrates downward, and divides repeatedly. This
transforms the mycelium into a dikaryon, enabling the production of
aeciospores that infect the wheat host. The process is directly
analogous to insect pollination of flowers.

## Puccinia Monoica: The Flower Mimic

The rust *Puccinia monoica*, which infects plants in the cabbage
family, has evolved an extraordinary manipulation of insect behavior.
Clusters of infected leaves covered with yellow spermagonia are such
perfect mimics of the host plant's actual flowers that fewer surrounding
plants of other species with yellow flowers become pollinated and set
seed. The fungus satisfies its sexual needs by exploiting the insects'
insatiable appetite for yellow advertisements, altering the ecology of
its entire habitat in the process.

## Stinkhorns and Carrion Attraction

mass (gleba) at the tip of the [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]] produces volatile
chemicals including hydrogen sulfide, methylmercaptan, and compounds
called phallic acids that mimic the smell of rotting flesh. Flies swarm
upon the gleba, consuming the spore slime and becoming coated with
spores. Slugs have been observed traveling 20 or more feet toward the
odor source. Within hours, the insect activity cleans the head bare.

## Truffle Dispersal by Mammals

While not insects, mammals play a crucial role in truffle dispersal that
parallels insect-fungal interactions. Truffles evolved from cup fungi
that were originally dispersed by insects crawling through the open
fruiting body. When the ascocarp became buried, insects continued to
home upon the same chemicals that had attracted them to the ancestral
open form. Mammals subsequently became the primary dispersers, but
insects still hover above buried truffles today, their role in spore
dispersal now unclear.

## Fungi as Insect Pathogens

Fungi also attack insects. Certain zygomycetes parasitize aquatic
insects, producing [[bloomfield-ingoldian-aquatic-fungi-tetraradiate-spores-foam]] in creeks. Entomopathogenic
fungi infect and kill insects, releasing spores from the insect
cadaver to infect new hosts. This predatory relationship has driven the
evolution of [[plant-defense-mechanisms]] in both groups over hundreds of millions
of years.
