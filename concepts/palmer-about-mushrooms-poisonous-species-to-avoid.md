# Poisonous Species to Avoid

## The Three Categories of Mushroom Poisoning

Palmer classifies how mushrooms can harm the human system into three distinct types, each requiring different treatment:

1. **Indigestion**: Hard, tough varieties, or common mushrooms in a partly decomposed state generating hydrogen gas. Symptoms are soon manifest and cause no alarm -- relief comes naturally or through emetics, warm water, or the finger down the throat.

2. **Acrid or nauseous elements**: Slimy, acrid, or bitter species. Many mild Boleti are too slimy for food; Russule have very acrid varieties; Polyporus squamosus and others are bitter, tasting of leaves and grass. Effects are immediate; expulsion followed by emollients (sweet-oil, slippery-elm, gum-arabic) provides relief. A stinging sensation affects the tongue, throat and tonsils more, with the most effect upon the stomach and bowels.

3. **Amanitine (the deadly alkaloid)**: A subtle, tasteless, odorless poison found only in the Amanita family. This is the sole cause of fatal mushroom poisoning. "It is probable that most of the fatal cases of poisoning by toadstools are due to Amanitine." Physicians have confessed their "utter inability to find an antidote for this poison" throughout history.

## The Amanita Family -- The Only Deadly Killer

Palmer's most significant contribution to mycology was segregating the Amanita as the sole source of fatal mushroom poisoning. He found ten varieties near Boston alone and stated: "I have rarely seen a collection of these plants gathered at random that did not contain enough of this fungus to have killed a whole family."

The family has about thirty members; eight are known to be edible and at least five contain the deadly alkaloid designated under the names of muscarine, bulbosine, and amanitine. The danger lies in the fact that the poisonous varieties are "extremely common" and "nearly tasteless, yet noxious."

### Identifying Marks of the Amanita

Three marks must exist together to denote the sub-genus Amanita:

1. **Volva (wrapper/cup) at the base of the stem**: Encloses the young plant before it leaves the soil. To find it, let the fungus stand and poke away with care the leaves and earth around the base of the stem. From this sheath the poisonous Amanita invariably springs.

2. **Ring/veil around the stem**: About two-thirds the length from the ground, a membrane in the form of a veil or ragged ring encircling the stem. Stems are free from the gills.

3. **Warts/scurf on the cap**: An irregular collection of warts, quite defined in young plants, apt to be worn off in older specimens. These are easily removable by rubbing, leaving the smooth, satin-like skin intact.

Additional characteristics:
- Gills and stem are always pure white (one exception: the edible Amanita caesarea has yellow gills).
- Spore deposit on blue paper resembles delicate white shading.
- Cap colors: variable -- white, drab, golden, red, greenish, yellow -- soft and sticky when moist.
- Most common variety in New England: yellow spot in the center, shading to pure white at the edge.
- The flesh is white, tasteless, and almost scentless in young specimens.
- Older specimens develop a strong, unpleasant odor.
- In older plants the warts, ring, or volva may be evanescent -- but the volva is almost universally found if the plant is carefully removed from the ground.
- Caps range from bright scarlet (A. muscarius) to lemon-yellow (A. mappa) to pure satiny white (A. vernus, A. phalloides).

### Why the Amanita Is So Dangerous

- The poison has **no burning or unusual taste** -- it passes harmlessly through the stomach mixed with other food.
- **Delayed action**: symptoms begin 8 to 15 hours after ingestion (dizziness, nausea, purging, followed by fainting, delirium, and death).
- The alkaloid is **absorbed by the intestinal canal** via the villi, so even removing every vestige of the mushroom may leave the system fatally charged.
- One mushroom the size of a silver dollar mixed with a wholesome dish is enough to make five or six persons dangerously sick.
- The poison can be absorbed through **skin contact** (holding an Amanita in a warm, perspiring hand produces all the symptoms of poisoning, even to convulsions).
- It can be absorbed through the **sense of smell** alone (Palmer experienced vomiting and stomach oppression three hours after merely smelling specimens in a closed box).
- Swine are immune; rabbits swallow some non-esculent fungi unharmed. Yet Agaricus melleus (edible) reportedly killed a dog, and a cat died in convulsions from Marasmius oreades (edible). Animal resistance is no guide for humans.

### Specific Amanita Species Mentioned

- **Amanita vernus**: Pure white; the variety that poisoned the Farris family of Shenandoah, Pennsylvania (1885). Very common in this state.
- **Amanita phalloides**: Often included with A. vernus under the French name A. bulbosa; considered the most poisonous.
- **Amanita mappa**: Lemon-yellow cap; used by Currie and Vigier for pharmaceutical extraction of the alkaloid for treating catarrhal affections.
- **Amanita muscarius** ("poison-fly Agaric"): Bright scarlet cap with white warts. Used medicinally for chronic catarrh, profuse perspiration, skin diseases, and epilepsy. Inhabitants of northern Russia consume it for narcotic intoxication; their urine is in demand for future orgies similar to those produced by alcohol.
- **Amanita caesarea**: The one edible variety with yellow gills -- the only exception to the white-gill rule.

### Palmer's Personal Experiments with Amanitine

Palmer conducted multiple self-experiments to understand the poison:
- **Chewing a piece the size of a half-dime**: throat constriction, momentary vertigo, sea-sickness-like disgust. Seventeen hours later, vomiting and tenesmus, then recovery.
- **Smelling specimens in a closed box**: three hours later, vomiting and stomach oppression; whites of eyes became livid; leaden facial color lasted through the next day.
- **Carrying a large bunch while perspiring**: fearful nausea, all symptoms of seasickness, relieved only by putting distance between himself and the mushrooms.
- **Attending poisoning victims**: attendants and physicians all suffered simply from proximity to the patients.

## The Farris Family Case (1885)

Palmer's article prompted Dr. S.E. Shadle of Shenandoah, Pennsylvania to report five cases of Amanita poisoning. On August 30, Mr. F. and his brother-in-law Thomas R. gathered beautiful white toadstools under chestnut trees, stewing them in milk with butter, pepper, and salt. Symptoms began toward daybreak -- foul breath, coated tongue, stomach pain, nausea.

Thomas R. died in fifty-six hours and Mrs. F. in sixty-three hours. Dr. Shadle observed that the poison was "narcotic in its nature and spent its force on the nerve centres, especially selecting the one governing the function of respiration and the action of the heart." He administered sub-cutaneous injections of sulphate of atropia in doses of 1/8 to 1/6 grain, which invariably produced perceptible improvement. Through this remedy, three of five patients (60%) recovered.

## Absorption and Contamination

Palmer discovered that edible mushrooms can absorb the virus of poisonous fungi through contact:
- He was poisoned when edible mushrooms were shipped in the same box as decomposing Coprinus deliquescens for twelve hours. The virus permeated the edible specimens, causing dizziness, nausea, perspiration, and chills within thirty minutes.
- Mushrooms "inoculate each other, either with or without contact."
- If an Amanita is cut in sections and laid in vinegar, it may then be eaten -- but a very small dose of the contaminated vinegar causes death faster than the whole raw toadstool (one stage of absorption having been completed).
- Conversely, in the 1876 New Jersey case, one family member who put vinegar on her Amanita before eating survived (the alkaloid was neutralized on the plate).
- Once vinegar is administered after ingestion, however, it worsens absorption -- a dog given vinegar as antidote had symptoms "fearfully aggravated."

## Treatment of Amanita Poisoning

### What Does NOT Work
- Emetics and purgatives: worse than useless once Amanitine is absorbed; they only help the system absorb more poison by precipitating or amalgamating the alkaloid with gastric juices.
- Whites of eggs, coffee, tea, antimony, ether, laudanum, milk, leeches, ice: "None of the remedies were followed by the least sign of amelioration" (French physician's report).
- Whiskey and sweet-oil: useful for less serious mushroom poisoning but ineffective against Amanitine once absorbed.

### What May Work
- **Atropine (from Atropa belladonna)** via sub-cutaneous injection: The most promising antidote. A frog's heart stopped by muscarine resumes beating with atropine. Dr. Shadle's successful use in the 1885 Farris case saved 60% of patients (3 of 5). Doses ranged from 1/8 to 1/6 grain, frequently repeated.
- Other Solanaceae alkaloids: daturine, hyoscyamine, nicotine, stramonium -- all from the nightshade family. Dr. T.F. Wood of North Carolina confirmed successful use of daturine and hyoscyamine.
- Sweet-oil enemata: relieve tenesmus and assist natural expulsion of remaining material.
- The key diagnostic: if symptoms begin 6-18 hours after the meal, Amanitine is at work and atropine is indicated. The longer the delay before symptoms, the more danger, because the system has fully absorbed the alkaloid. Shorter onset times suggest less dangerous poisons treatable by conventional means.

## Non-Fatal But Troublesome Species

- **Emetic Russulas** (Russula emetica, R. rosacea): Very acrid, cause digestive disturbance; the acridity is subdued by cooking heat.
- **Bitter Boleti**: Change color to red or blue when cut; bitter taste accompanies the color change. No recorded fatalities.
- **Slimy Boleti**: Too slimy for food; cause nausea.
- **Boletus felleus** ("the gall boletus"): The one Boletus Palmer still avoids.
- **Coprinus deliquescens**: Not poisonous when fresh but becomes toxic in decomposition; Palmer was poisoned by edible mushrooms contaminated by its presence.
- **Lactarius piperatus**: Perfectly edible (contradicting government pamphlets that branded it "poisonous"); its acridity is removed by proper preparation.

## The Scale of Real Danger vs. Perceived Danger

Palmer estimated about 1,000 non-microscopic fungi in New England, of which the majority are non-esculent (tough, woody, bitter, slimy, or repellent) but only four varieties of the Amanita have caused authenticated fatal poisonings. The actual danger is thus small compared to the popular imagination, but the consequences of a mistake are severe.

He observed that the standard foreign textbooks "print the word 'poisonous' indiscriminately against any unpleasant plant" -- thus branding Boleti, Lactarii, and Russule as dangerous even though they are at worst disagreeable or having acrid properties subdued by heat. Only the Amanita "is deadly poison" among all the gill-bearing fungi.

The real tragedy is that many reported poisonings go unidentified: "Every year the public journals chronicle deaths from toadstool eating, but they never mention what species of toadstool occasions them. It would be of great practical value if, whenever possible, this information were given to the public, and fresh samples of the injurious fungus sent to some mycologist for positive identification." Without species identification, the same mistakes are repeated endlessly and no one learns from them.

## Practical Advice for the Beginner

Given the difficulty of Amanita identification, Palmer offers this simplification: if you can recognize the volva (the cup-like wrapper at the base of the stem), you can avoid all Amanitas. "Let the fungus stand and poke away with care the leaves and earth around the base of the stem" -- the volva will be found almost universally if the plant is carefully removed from the ground.

For the common mushroom (Agaricus campestris), the key distinctions from the deadly Amanita are: the common mushroom NEVER has a volva, is seldom found in woods, has pink or purple gills (not white), and a much shorter stem. These differences are sufficient to distinguish the most commonly gathered edible species from the most commonly encountered deadly one.

Palmer's final, pragmatic advice: "I may select twenty varieties, easily recognized, advise that all others be let alone, and follow my own counsel." This conservative approach -- learning a manageable number of well-characterized edible species and ignoring everything else -- is the safest path for the amateur mycophagist.
