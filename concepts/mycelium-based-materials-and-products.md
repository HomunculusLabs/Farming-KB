# Mycelium-Based Materials and Products

Mycelium — the vegetative body of fungi — is increasingly recognized as a versatile raw material for manufacturing sustainable consumer goods. From packaging and insulation to building materials and textiles, mycelium-based products represent a growing field that merges mushroom cultivation with materials science and product design. These innovations offer biodegradable alternatives to petroleum-based plastics, synthetic foams, and chemically treated materials.

## Packaging

The commercial use of myceliated biomass for packaging was pioneered by companies such as **Ecovative Design**, which demonstrated that fungal mycelium grown on agricultural waste could replace expanded polystyrene (Styrofoam) and other synthetic packaging materials. This work opened the door to broader experimentation by both research institutions and home growers.

### Living Packaging Systems

Infusing mushroom particles into a recycled cardboard slurry creates a living packaging system that is an upgrade over traditional packaging. The mycelium acts as a natural binder, helping glue paper particles together and potentially replacing the chemical binders used by the conventional paper industry.

A key advantage of myceliated paper products is their initial **hydrophobicity** — they repel water. The material only breaks down when thoroughly soaked, giving it a well-timed shelf life followed by the ability to be composted at end of use. Regular paper bags, by comparison, absorb water easily and lose their strength within seconds.

### Production at Scale

Mushroom cultivators can recycle a percentage of their spent growing media by shredding it, mixing it with waste paper, and hydropulping it into new cardboard or paper products. Spent oyster mushroom columns or sawdust-based fruiting substrate are ideal feedstocks. Hydropulpers can be purchased used, or cultivators can collaborate with existing box production companies to develop custom mycelium-infused packaging.

## Insulation

Dried mycelium possesses physical properties that make it suitable as a building insulation material. Its web-like architecture resembles that of fiberglass, and at a 2-inch thickness it achieves a comparable **R-value of 2.9** to commercially available insulation materials.

### Additional Properties

Beyond thermal insulation, dried mycelium offers several advantages over traditional insulation:

- **Fire-retardant**: Skeletonized chitin (dried mycelium) naturally resists ignition
- **Hydrophobic**: Beads water away unless completely saturated
- **Antifungal and insect-repelling**: Many mushroom species produce natural compounds that deter pests
- **Sustainable**: Made from agricultural waste that would otherwise be discarded

### Production Methods

Columns of spent oyster mushroom substrate can be dried and cut with a band saw into insulative boards of any desired thickness. The columns can also be shredded into a fluffy insulation material that can be blown into wall cavities and attics, similar to cellulose insulation.

**Critical caveat**: An exterior vapor barrier must be applied to keep the mycelial insulation from getting wet. If the dried mycelium absorbs enough moisture to revert to an active vegetative state, the fungus could begin attacking the very structure it was protecting. However, this would only occur under conditions of continuous, overwhelming moisture — the same conditions that would cause molds and rot in conventional building materials regardless.

## Building Materials

When air-dried or freeze-dried, fully colonized growing medium takes on the characteristics of a durable building composite. It can be formed or cut into blocks suitable for constructing structures.

### Assembly

Mycelium blocks adhere to one another with a light application of moisture between adjoining layers, rather than requiring chemical adhesives or mortar. Once naturally fused together (a matter of days), the assembled blocks can achieve tensile strength comparable to concrete and steel, with less tendency for fissures due to the slight flexibility of the mycelium, even when fully dried.

If kept dry, blocks of mycelium can last indefinitely. The most promising mushroom species for construction applications include:

- **Oyster mushrooms** (*Pleurotus* spp.): Fast colonization, good structural properties
- **Reishi** (*Ganoderma* spp.): Forms a dense, rubber-like material that holds its shape and repels water

### Rubber and Textile Applications

Some fungal strains produce fruiting bodies that are difficult to tear, resist moisture, and possess antibiotic properties — making them candidates for rubber replacements. By isolating these cultures and expanding them into biomass on sawdust, they can be formed into molds or sheets cut to shape. Prototypes have included flip-flops and other consumer goods that could replace short-life plastic products.

## Antimicrobial Cutting Boards

One innovative application is the **mushroom-infused cutting board**, which leverages the natural antimicrobial properties of fungi to create food preparation surfaces that resist bacterial contamination without synthetic antimicrobial coatings or nanoparticles.

### Why This Matters

Surfaces that spread infection are often treated with synthetic antimicrobial compounds or coated with antimicrobial nanoparticles such as silver. Products treated with nanoparticles include computer keyboards, handrails, hospital catheters, and cutting boards — and their use is increasing at a rate of 500 percent per year. No regulation currently requires manufacturers to disclose nanoparticle treatments, and the effects on human and environmental health are not well understood.

### Construction Process

Old hardwood cutting boards from thrift stores are ideal candidates — the wood is still biologically active and will support fungi, but will not rapidly degrade since it remains dry most of the time.

**Materials needed:**
- Pure mushroom spawn (sawdust, colonized cardboard, or colonized coffee grounds)
- Hardwood cutting board
- Large plastic bag
- Pot of boiling water (or heat-proof tray)

**Steps:**
1. Boil or soak the cutting board in boiling water until saturated (about 1 hour)
2. Spread a ¼-inch layer of spawn in a large plastic bag
3. Place the board on the spawn and cover the top with another ¼-inch layer
4. Tie the bag shut and poke small aeration holes around the perimeter
5. After 3–4 days, check that the spawn layers are adhering to the wood
6. Allow full colonization for several more weeks
7. Remove spawn layers with a scraper, wash the board, and it is ready for use

The mycelium drills its way into the wood from both surfaces, creating a naturally antimicrobial cutting surface. Once cleaned, the board can last for years with occasional washing, since mycelium can survive dehydration for up to a year.

### Expansion

Used spawn can be recycled to inoculate additional cutting boards, logs, stumps, or pasteurized media. Multiple boards can be stacked with spawn layers between them for efficient batch production.

## Mushroom Paper and Ink

### Paper from Polypores

Many mushrooms can be used to make paper, especially tough inedible polypores that have high fiber content, persist long in the environment, and are available year-round. The papermaking process involves blending mushrooms with water into a slurry, floating the slurry in a tub, and pulling sheets using a screen and deckle.

### Spore-Based Inks

Researchers have experimented with using mushroom spores as ink and infusing commercial inks with viable spores to create biologically active printing products. The resulting ink not only creates images but can germinate and colonize the paper product once discarded and wetted, accelerating composting and decomposition.

Colored spores of various species can be fermented in culture to produce dye and ink solutions. Dried spores can also be added to commercial inks just prior to printing to maintain spore viability. While not yet a marketable product, spore-based inks represent a promising avenue for biologically active packaging.

## Livestock Feed Applications

Medicinal mushrooms delivered as colonized grains, powdered coatings, or feed additives offer potential as veterinary and medicinal supplements for pets and livestock. The equine industry was the first to adopt these supplements for improving joint function and oxygen availability in racehorses. The same formulas are now entering the pet industry as adjunct anticancer therapies.

Supplementing livestock feed with spent mushroom growing substrate from primary decomposers (such as oyster mushrooms) provides nutrition and immune stimulation for ruminants. This is especially valuable in arid climates, where 30–40 percent of the water in a fungal colony resides in the substrate — feeding this "waste" to livestock converts that water into meat and milk while endowing the herd with disease resistance.

## Tempeh Culturing with Rhizopus oligosporus

Traditional tempeh is made by fermenting soybeans with *Rhizopus oligosporus*, a sporulating mold commonly called bread pin mold. Using sterile tissue culture techniques, tempeh cultures can be maintained on agar media indefinitely, eliminating the need to purchase new starter cultures.

Cultures can be expanded onto sterilized grain (such as organic rice), allowed to fully colonize and form black-spored tips, then spread in shallow pans under grow lamps to maximize spore production. Once covered with thick black spore growth, the grain is dehydrated, blended into powder, and stored refrigerated for months. This powder contains billions of spores ready for tempeh production.

## Future Directions

The field of mycelium-based materials is rapidly expanding. Opportunities exist for:

- **Bioprospecting** among thousands of fungal species for traits like high tensile strength or natural antibiotic properties
- **Developing mycelium-based alternatives** to plastic bottles, disposable cups, food packaging, and pet toys
- **Integrating mycelium products into existing production lines** at fruiting facilities
- **Creating closed-loop systems** where waste from one process feeds another

The versatility of mycelium as a manufacturing material makes it one of the most promising areas in applied mycology.
