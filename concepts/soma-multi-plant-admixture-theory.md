# Soma Multi-Plant Admixture Theory

James Arthur's "Mushrooms and Mankind" proposes a novel theory that the legendary Vedic Soma was not a single plant but a multi-plant admixture — a carefully prepared brew combining several psychoactive species to produce the full spectrum of effects described in ancient religious texts. This theory attempts to resolve longstanding debates about Soma's identity by suggesting that previous single-plant candidates each captured only part of the picture.

## The Problem with Single-Plant Candidates

Arthur observes that the search for Soma's botanical identity has produced several compelling but ultimately unsatisfying candidates. [[wasson-hofmann-ruck-collaboration]] case for the [[amanita-muscaria-in-eastern-religions]] is very convincing but "certainly not the end of the story." Flattery and Schwartz have made a good case for Syrian Rue. [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]] and others have exhaustively covered the ecstatic visionary states produced by psilocybin mushrooms.

The problem, as Arthur frames it, is that individually each candidate falls short of producing the full described state of consciousness found in the religious texts. The experience achieved by each proposed single plant, while significant, does not match the comprehensive and transformative effects attributed to Soma in the Vedas.

## The Five-Plant Recipe from Medieval Art

Arthur's key evidence comes from a twelfth-century artwork depicting Jesus above four plants underscored by a green vine. He interprets this image as an alchemical recipe for the ingredients of the true Soma, identifying five plant components.

The red plant on the right is clearly the Amanita muscaria, which Arthur says produces "the quickening of the spirit."

The next plant is a blue mushroom, indicating a Psilocybe species, which "opens the third eye."

A third depiction appears to be Syrian Rue (Peganum harmala), which contains telepathine, an MAO inhibitor that increases the properties of other compounds. Syrian Rue contains harmine and harmaline, [[mao-inhibitors-and-psychedelics]] that parallel the function of pinoline — a natural MAO inhibitor produced by the pineal gland.

A fourth plant appears to be the Opium Poppy (Papaver somniferum), whose euphoric effects "enable one to relax enough to let go" to fully experience the visionary state of this intensive plant combination. Arthur notes the linguistic link between "Soma" and "Somniferum."

The fifth element is the green vine underscoring the other four, which Arthur suggests represents green plants including Cannabis — used to prevent nausea and enhance other effects — and other plants containing DMT that have a history of indigenous usage worldwide.

## The MAOI Mechanism: Mimicking the Pineal Gland

Arthur draws a significant connection between the external plant admixture and the body's own internal chemistry. The combination of the pineal-secreted DMT (dimethyltryptamine) and pinoline (a natural MAO inhibitor) may be responsible for naturally occurring psychic experiences and even what some interpret as UFO contact visualisations.

The Soma brew, Arthur argues, mimics this natural DMT-pinoline combination. This is supported by the research of Jace Callway and others, who have shown that DMT-containing plants are mixed in brews with MAO inhibitor-containing plants (such as Banisteriopsis caapi and Syrian Rue) to produce entheogenic brews like [[mckenna-tryptamine-ayahuasca-and-dmt-combinations]].

These plants must be used in combination because their psycho-activity individually is limited. It is the synergistic interaction between the components that produces the full entheogenic effect — just as the natural pineal gland combination produces its effects through the interaction of endogenous compounds.

## The Ayahuasca Parallel

Arthur explicitly connects his Soma theory to the Amazonian Ayahuasca tradition. In both cases, an MAO inhibitor-containing plant is combined with DMT-containing plants to produce a visionary brew that would be inactive if the plants were consumed separately.

The Ayahuasca model demonstrates that ancient cultures understood and utilised complex pharmacological interactions long before modern science identified the biochemical mechanisms involved. Arthur suggests that the Vedic Soma brewers possessed similar sophisticated knowledge of plant combinations and their synergistic effects.

## Linguistic Clues

Arthur explores linguistic connections that support his multi-plant theory. The name "Papaver somniferum" (the opium poppy) contains "somn" — connected to "Soma." He notes "somnambulism" (sleep-walking), which interestingly occurs when DMT and pinoline are excreted by the pineal gland during sleep.

These linguistic connections, while not conclusive on their own, add another layer to the argument that the Soma tradition involved multiple plants working in combination, including plants with both stimulating and sedating properties that together produce a unique state of consciousness.

## Different Keys Open Different Locks

Arthur emphasises a principle that he considers crucial: "All roads do not lead to Rome. Different 'keys' open different doorways, and different combinations open different locks." He warns against the trap of thinking that any entheogenic experience is the same as any other.

A chemical is designated a specific name to distinguish it from all others, and different psychoactive compounds produce genuinely different experiences. The Soma brew, in his theory, was specifically formulated to produce a particular spectrum of effects that no single plant could achieve alone. Each component played a specific role in the overall experience.

## The Five-Color Theme

Arthur notes that the five-plant combination depicted in the medieval artwork involves five colours — a theme that recurs in many mystical traditions. This colour symbolism may represent an encoding of the admixture recipe in visual form, preserved in religious art long after the actual practice of preparing Soma had been suppressed or forgotten.

The five-colour theme, like the five plants themselves, represents a complete system — each element contributing something essential to the whole. This holistic approach to entheogenic preparation stands in contrast to the modern tendency to isolate single active compounds.

## Implications for the Soma Debate

Arthur's multi-plant admixture theory offers a potential resolution to the centuries-old debate about Soma's identity. Rather than requiring that any single candidate be rejected in favour of another, the theory suggests that all proposed candidates may be partially correct — each representing one component of a larger, more complex preparation.

The theory also has implications for understanding other ancient entheogenic traditions, suggesting that what appear to be competing single-plant identifications may actually represent fragments of more sophisticated multi-plant practices that have been lost to history.

## Evidence from the Rig Veda
The Rig Veda contains numerous passages that are difficult to reconcile
with any single-plant identification. Soma is described as simultaneously
golden and tawny (suggesting Amanita muscaria), green-growing and pressed
from a plant (suggesting a green herb), filtered through wool (a common
preparation technique for both mushroom extracts and plant juices), and
producing effects ranging from exhilaration and strength to visionary
insight and a sense of immortality. These diverse descriptions are among
the strongest arguments for a multi-plant interpretation. The [[wasson-brough-soma-debate]] centred partly on these textual
ambiguities, with Brough arguing for an Ephedra-based identification
based on the plant-filtering descriptions while Wasson maintained his
Amanita hypothesis based on the golden colour and visionary effects.

## Comparative Entheogenic Traditions
The multi-plant admixture theory finds support in the widespread use of
complex botanical combinations in other indigenous entheogenic traditions.
Amazonian Ayahuasca combines Banisteriopsis caapi (MAOI) with Psychotria
viridis or Diplopterys cabrerana (DMT source), sometimes with additional
admixture plants such as Datura, Nicotiana, or Brugmansia to modify the
experience. The Mazatec sometimes combine [[psychedelic-mushrooms-species-and-identification]] with tobacco and other plants in their ceremonies.
Central American mushroom stones from the pre-Columbian period often
depict composite figures that may encode multi-plant recipes. The [[mckenna-tryptamine-ayahuasca-and-dmt-combinations]] demonstrates that
sophisticated pharmacological knowledge of plant combinations existed
independently in multiple cultures, making it plausible that Vedic
priests possessed similar knowledge.

## Criticisms and Limitations
Arthur's theory faces several significant criticisms. The reliance on a
single medieval artwork as the primary evidence for the five-plant recipe
is a weak archaeological foundation. The linguistic argument connecting
"Soma" to "somniferum" (opium poppy) is superficial -- the Sanskrit root
"su" (to press, extract juice) is the generally accepted etymology.
The [[allegro-soma-mushroom-thesis]] and other single-plant
theories at least have direct textual evidence, even if ambiguous.
Arthur's theory, while pharmacologically sophisticated, requires a level
of interpretive latitude that some scholars find unacceptable.

## The Pineal Gland Connection
Arthur's linking of the Soma brew to the pineal gland's natural
DMT-pinoline production draws on the research of Rick Strassman, who
proposed in "DMT: The Spirit Molecule" (2001) that the pineal gland may
produce endogenous DMT during extraordinary states such as near-death
experiences, mystical states, and the peak of the psychedelic
experience. While Strassman's hypothesis remains controversial and the
pineal gland's capacity to produce significant quantities of DMT under
normal conditions is debated, the broader concept that entheogenic plants
mimic and amplify naturally occurring brain chemistry is well-supported.
The [[pineal-gland-dmt-entheogenic-experience]] provides an evolutionary framework for understanding why
humans are sensitive to psychedelic compounds -- these molecules may be
exploiting receptor systems that evolved for endogenous psychoactive
molecules.

## Modern Recreations and Experimental Archaeology
Several researchers and psychonauts have attempted to recreate multi-plant
Soma brews based on Arthur's recipe and similar theories. These
experiments typically combine Amanita muscaria (or psilocybin mushrooms),
Syrian Rue seeds (as MAOI), and various adjunctive plants. Reports
generally describe the combination as qualitatively different from any
single component, with the MAOI component potentiating and altering the
effects of the other psychoactive plants. The [[allegro-colour-and-consistency-of-the-amanita-muscaria]] documents several
traditional preparation methods that involve drying, heating, or urine
recycling to convert ibotenic acid to the more psychoactive muscimol.
