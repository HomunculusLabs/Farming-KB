# Keewaydinoquay and Native American Mushroom Traditions

Keewaydinoquay (approximately 1900-1999) was an Anishinaabe (Ojibwe) medicine woman from the Great Lakes region who preserved traditional knowledge about the sacred use of Amanita muscaria, the fly-agaric mushroom. Her story represents one of the most important — and most debated — cases of indigenous mushroom knowledge in North America.

## Background

Keewaydinoquay was a traditional Anishinaabe elder from Garden Island, Lake Michigan. She was trained as a medicine woman and preserved extensive knowledge of her people's botanical and ethnomycological traditions. Her work gained wider attention when she was visited by [[gordon-wasson-comparative-method-ethnomycology]] at her traditional wigwam.

## The Wasson Connection

Wasson learned of Keewaydinoquay in July 1975 through a recommendation from the University of Wisconsin, where she had just published an account of her ethnobotanical knowledge. A lively correspondence ensued between the two, culminating in Wasson visiting her at Garden Island. Though Wasson was a widower in his late seventies and Keewaydinoquay was significantly younger, they became close — "it would appear to have become lovers," as one biographer noted.

## The Amanita muscaria Tradition

Keewaydinoquay described a traditional Anishinaabe practice involving the ritual use of Amanita muscaria (the fly-agaric), the same mushroom used by Siberian shamans. Key aspects of her account:

- The mushroom was used ceremonially by medicine people

- It was regarded as a powerful spiritual medicine

- Specific protocols governed its collection, preparation, and use

- The tradition was closely guarded and transmitted orally

## Preparation and Ritual Details

According to Keewaydinoquay's accounts, the Anishinaabe preparation methods for Amanita muscaria involved careful drying and sometimes specialized cooking techniques. The mushroom was typically consumed in a ceremonial context, often during specific seasons or for particular spiritual purposes such as healing, divination, or communication with spirit beings. These preparation details bear some resemblance to [[siberian-fly-agaric-shamanism-urine-recycling]], where drying and specific processing methods are used to reduce toxicity and modify psychoactive effects. The knowledge was considered highly sacred and restricted to trained medicine people who had undergone appropriate initiation.

## Significance and Controversy

### Importance

If authentic, Keewaydinoquay's account represents the only well-documented case of Amanita muscaria use by Native North American peoples — a finding of considerable ethnobotanical significance that would extend the known geographic range of fly-agaric shamanism from Siberia across the Bering Strait.

### Debates

Some scholars have questioned the authenticity of the tradition, noting:

- The difficulty of verifying oral traditions recorded decades after the fact

- The possibility that Wasson's influence shaped the account

- The lack of independent corroborating evidence from other Anishinaabe sources

- Parallels with [[siberian-fly-agaric-shamanism-urine-recycling]] that might suggest diffusion or influence rather than independent tradition

### Defense

Supporters argue:

- Keewaydinoquay was a respected elder with decades of traditional training

- The account was published independently before Wasson's involvement

- The specific details of preparation and ritual use are consistent with other indigenous mushroom traditions

- The knowledge was part of a broader ethnobotanical system, not an isolated claim

## The Bering Strait Hypothesis

Keewaydinoquay's account, if accepted, lends support to the broader hypothesis that psychoactive mushroom use may have crossed from Asia to North America alongside human migration. This idea connects to the wider debate about the geographic extent of entheogenic mushroom traditions, paralleling discussions about whether [[mesoamerican-mushroom-ceremonies-teonanacatl]] represent an independent cultural development or are connected to Old World traditions. The question remains one of the most contentious in ethnomycology.

## Legacy

Keewaydinoquay's account, regardless of debates about its authenticity, highlights several important themes:

- The fragility of indigenous mycological knowledge

- The role of individual knowledge-keepers in preserving traditional practices

- The complex relationship between Western researchers and indigenous informants

- The question of whether [[soma-and-the-sacred-mushroom-debate]] extended across the Bering Strait to the Americas
