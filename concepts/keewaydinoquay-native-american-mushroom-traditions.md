## The Wasson Connection

Wasson learned of Keewaydinoquay in July 1975 through a recommendation from the University of Wisconsin, where she had just published an account of her ethnobotanical knowledge. A lively correspondence ensued between the two, culminating in Wasson visiting her at Garden Island. Though Wasson was a widower in his late seventies and Keewaydinoquay was significantly younger, they became close — "it would appear to have become lovers," as one biographer noted.

## The Amanita muscaria Tradition

Keewaydinoquay described a traditional Anishinaabe practice involving the ritual use of Amanita muscaria (the fly-agaric), the same mushroom used by Siberian shamans. Key aspects of her account:

- The mushroom was used ceremonially by medicine people

- It was regarded as a powerful spiritual medicine

- Specific protocols governed its collection, preparation, and use

- The tradition was closely guarded and transmitted orally

## Preparation and Ritual Details

According to Keewaydinoquay's accounts, the Anishinaabe preparation methods for Amanita muscaria involved careful drying and sometimes specialized cooking techniques. The mushroom was typically consumed in a ceremonial context, often during specific seasons or for particular spiritual purposes such as healing, divination, or communication with spirit beings. These preparation details bear some resemblance to [[siberian-fly-agaric-shamanism-urine-recycling]] that might suggest diffusion or influence rather than independent tradition

### Defense

Supporters argue:

- Keewaydinoquay was a respected elder with decades of traditional training

- The account was published independently before Wasson's involvement

- The specific details of preparation and ritual use are consistent with other indigenous mushroom traditions

- The knowledge was part of a broader ethnobotanical system, not an isolated claim

## The Bering Strait Hypothesis

Keewaydinoquay's account, if accepted, lends support to the broader hypothesis that psychoactive mushroom use may have crossed from Asia to North America alongside human migration. This idea connects to the wider debate about the geographic extent of entheogenic mushroom traditions, paralleling discussions about whether [[soma-and-the-sacred-mushroom-debate]] extended across the Bering Strait to the Americas
