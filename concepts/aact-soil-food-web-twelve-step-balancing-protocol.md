# AACT Soil Food Web Twelve-Step Balancing Protocol

Dr. [[elaine-ingham]]'s systematic protocol for assessing and restoring a balanced soil food web. The twelve steps address each major group of soil organisms sequentially, from bacteria through microarthropods, using compost tea, compost, and targeted inocula to bring all groups within desired ranges.

## Overview of the Protocol

The protocol begins with a comprehensive soil biology assessment — typically through direct microscopy at a Soil Foodweb Inc. (SFI) laboratory. Once the current state of [[the-soil-food-web]] is known, corrective steps are applied in sequence. Each step addresses one organism group or functional role, building from the foundation (bacteria and fungi) up through protozoa, nematodes, and finally microarthropods and earthworms.

The fundamental principle is that soil health is a function of the complete biological community, not any single organism group. Steps cannot be skipped — if protozoa are absent, nutrient cycling will stall regardless of bacterial biomass.

## Step One: Assess Total and Active Bacterial Biomass

The first step is to determine whether total and active bacterial biomass fall within desired ranges for the specific plant community being grown. Annual crops, perennial pastures, and mature forests each have different optimal bacterial levels.

If bacterial biomass is too low, the corrective action is to apply a bacterial-dominated compost tea. The tea should be brewed with foods that select for bacteria — primarily simple sugars like unsulfured molasses, along with bacterial inoculants if indigenous populations are severely depleted.

If bacterial biomass is adequate, proceed to step two.

## Step Two: Assess Total and Active Fungal Biomass

Fungal biomass must be evaluated alongside bacterial biomass, as the fungal-to-bacterial ratio is critical for determining which plant successional stage the soil supports. Weeds and early-successional annuals thrive in bacterial-dominated soils, while perennials, shrubs, and trees require increasingly fungal-dominated soil food webs.

The desired fungal-to-bacterial ratio varies by plant type:
- **Annual vegetables and row crops**: approximately 0.1 to 1:1 (bacterial-dominated)
- **Pastures and grasslands**: approximately 1:1 to 5:1
- **Shrubs and vines**: approximately 5:1 to 10:1
- **Conifer and deciduous forests**: approximately 10:1 to 100:1 (fungal-dominated)

If fungal biomass is too low, apply a fungal-dominated compost tea brewed with fungal foods such as humic acids, fish hydrolysate, and small amounts of rock dust. The compost used as the inoculum should itself be fungal-dominated — thermal compost with woody materials, or forest litter compost.

## Step Three: Assess Mycorrhizal Colonization

Mycorrhizal colonization is assessed by examining root samples under the microscope. The target range for healthy plants is typically 40% to 70% colonization of root length.

- **If colonization is between 0% and 15%**: An inoculum of mycorrhizal spores must be placed directly next to the roots or on and around the seed at planting time. [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] cannot be effectively delivered through compost tea because they are obligate symbionts that require direct root contact to establish.
- **If colonization is between 15% and 40%**: Adding humic acid as a fungal food source may be sufficient to support expansion of existing [[mycorrhizal-networks]] without additional spore inoculation.

Mycorrhizal fungi extend the effective root zone dramatically, improving access to phosphorus, micronutrients, and water. They also contribute [[glomalin]] — a sticky protein that binds soil particles into stable aggregates.

## Steps Six, Seven, and Eight: Assess Protozoa

Protozoa are the primary nutrient cyclers in the soil food web. They consume bacteria and release plant-available nitrogen and other nutrients through their excretions. Three groups are assessed:

1. **Flagellates**: The smallest protozoa, important in bacterial-dominated soils and compacted conditions.
2. **Amoebae**: Larger protozoa that can consume significant bacterial biomass; critical in many agricultural soils.
3. **Ciliates**: Typically indicate anaerobic or compacted soil conditions when present in high numbers. Elevated ciliate populations serve as a warning sign of soil compaction and oxygen limitation.

If any protozoan group is below minimum levels, an inoculum should be added. Good sources of protozoa include worm compost (vermicompost), which is typically rich in all three groups, and various commercial protozoan products.

**Critical diagnostic note**: If ciliates are above desired levels, the soil is compacted and anaerobic. In this case, fungal biomass, protozoa, nematodes, and mycorrhizal colonization all typically need improvement. The elevated ciliates are a symptom, not the problem — the underlying compaction and anaerobic conditions must be addressed.

## Steps Nine, Ten, and Eleven: Assess Beneficial Nematodes

Three functional groups of beneficial nematodes are evaluated:

1. **Bacterial-feeding nematodes**: Consume bacteria and release nutrients; require at least six species for functional diversity.
2. **Fungal-feeding nematodes**: Consume fungi and regulate fungal populations; require at least six species.
3. **Predatory nematodes**: Consume other nematodes and maintain population balance; require at least two species.

If any group is below desired levels, a commercial inoculum of beneficial nematodes should be applied. If species diversity is insufficient, targeted inoculants for the deficient group are needed.

**If root-feeding nematodes are present**, the corrective approach is not to apply nematicides but to improve the overall soil food web:
1. Increase beneficial nematodes (they compete with and consume root-feeders)
2. Improve mycorrhizal colonization (mycorrhizal fungi protect roots)
3. Increase total beneficial fungal biomass
4. Ensure all three groups are at least at minimum levels, preferably at maximum levels

When root-feeding nematodes were originally detected, the specific biological levels that allowed the problem should be noted as a warning threshold for future monitoring.

## Step Twelve: Assess Microarthropods, Earthworms, and Higher Predators

The final step examines the larger organisms that maintain pore space and process organic matter. When a handful of soil or litter is picked up, adequate numbers of microarthropods (springtails, mites) and earthworms should be visible.

If these organisms are absent, a worm compost or forest soil containing them must be found and added to the system. These organisms may not exist in conventionally tilled agricultural systems, but at least a few per square foot are required for good soil health.

Microarthropods create macropores as they move through soil, improving water infiltration and air exchange. Earthworms create burrow channels that serve as preferential flow paths for water and roots. Both groups contribute to the formation and maintenance of soil structure.

## Methods of Organism Delivery

The protocol specifies several delivery methods for introducing organisms:

1. **Compost tea**: For foliar and soil applications; can be tailored for bacterial or fungal dominance.
2. **Mulch**: Selects for fungi by maintaining spatial separation of carbon from soil nutrients.
3. **Plug trays**: Fill with 100% aerobic compost containing VAM spores; plant bare root or seed in trays to colonize roots before transplanting into unhealthy soil.
4. **Soil cores**: For perennial plants lacking mycorrhizal colonization, pull one to four cores from the root zone and fill with compost plus mycorrhizal spores. Roots grow toward these enriched zones and become inoculated.
5. **Incorporation**: In extremely compacted soil, or where wind or water erosion may remove surface mulch, incorporation may be necessary to protect organic matter.
6. **Drip irrigation**: Add compost tea into drip systems to deliver organisms directly to the root zone.
7. **Spray applications**: Deliver compost tea through any system that applies water to leaf or soil surfaces.

## Feeding the Beneficial Organisms

After inoculation, organisms must be fed to sustain populations:

- **[[cover-crops]]**: Living roots provide continuous exudates that feed bacteria and mycorrhizae.
- **Compost**: Provides both organisms and slow-release food resources.
- **Compost tea**: Delivers liquid food resources along with organisms.
- **Molasses and sugars**: Simple carbohydrates that feed bacteria rapidly.
- **Humic acid and fulvic acid**: Complex compounds that feed fungi.
- **Fish hydrolysate**: Provides both nitrogen and fungal foods.
- **Citrus products and garlic oil**: Secondary metabolites that support diverse communities.

## Monitoring and Follow-Up

After applying corrective steps, soil samples should be sent to SFI Labs for re-assessment. The food web is dynamic — seasonal changes, weather events, and management practices all affect organism populations. Regular monitoring, typically two to four times per year, ensures that the biological balance is maintained and corrective actions can be taken before problems become visible above ground.

## Typical Input Amounts and Timing

For reference, the protocol provides general application rates:

- **Compost**: 5 to 30 tons per acre, applied fall and spring; add VAM spores if needed.
  - Row crops: broadcast inoculum and non-soluble nutrients.
  - Vineyards: apply under the row.
  - Trees: apply from 2 inches out to the drip line, with vertical mulching for compacted soil.
- **Compost tea**: 5 gallons per acre foliar (for each 6 feet of canopy height); 20 gallons per acre as soil drench.
  - Row crops: apply at first true leaf, flower bud, and post-seed-set.
  - Vines and trees: 2 weeks before budbreak; every 7 to 14 days if disease is present, once per month if no disease history.
