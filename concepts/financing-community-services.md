# Financing Community Services

## Overview

Sustainable financing of public services is one of the most critical challenges in [[permaculture-village-development]]. Mollison presents a model based on the **30% village trust allocation**, where a portion of all land titles is vested in the community trust, generating ongoing capital and interest income for public infrastructure and services. This approach, inspired by the Mondragon Cooperative system in Spain, ensures that villages can fund their own development without reliance on external government subsidies.

## The 30% Trust Model

### How It Works

The developer vests **30% of all subdivision titles** in the village trust (Trust A). When these titles are sold, the monies generated ensure a large annual interest yield for village development. This effectively means:

1. **All necessary public services are funded** from the trust's capital and income
2. **Residents have effectively paid nothing for land** over a period of 20 years, as the trust income covers community costs
3. **Capital or interest becomes available** as soon as sales exceed 60% of total titles

### Capital Deployment Timeline

| Phase | Activity |
|---|---|
| Initial | Land purchase, survey, subdivision, roading, basic services |
| Year 1–4 | Sales generate trust capital; interest accumulates |
| Year 5+ | Capital deployed for wealth-producing assets: glasshouses, computers, machinery |
| Ongoing | Trust income funds maintenance and new development |

### Use of Trust Capital

By year 5, when residents have a clear idea of future needs, capital can be directed toward:

- Glasshouses and commercial food production
- Computers and information technology
- Machinery and manufacturing equipment
- Energy systems (solar, biogas, hydro)
- Schools and educational facilities
- Workshops and community buildings

### The Credit Union Connection

If a **credit union** has been established, much of the trust capital can be transferred to a loans account, allowing residents to draw on it at low interest for local occupational development. Conscientious use of a credit union by village residents would greatly increase capital flow to [[mollison-village-enterprises-occupations]].

A **building cooperative** is more effective than housing loans from the credit union for residential construction.

## Ongoing Income for the Trust

The Trust always needs income for maintenance of roads, fences, water supply, fire control, and other site infrastructure. Several revenue streams are available:

### Lease and Loan Revenue
- Small charges for leases and loans to residents
- Hourly rates for lease, use, or hire of machines and facilities

### Cooperative Profit Levy
- **10% levy on net profits** of locally-funded cooperatives
- This mirrors the Mondragon cooperative system, which uses the same levy to fund banks, schools, and research facilities
- Applies only to net profits of trust-funded cooperatives, not to all businesses

### Village Morale

The result of having capital and interest flowing into the Trust is that **village morale is greatly increased**, with every resident seeing long-term plans fairly rapidly achieved. Well-managed, the capital should actually increase over time, giving a large annual fund for village use.

## Income Generation Models

Mollison identifies four primary models for generating community income:

### 1. Levy (Flat Rate)

A yearly levy on each family or property, collected by the Community Council.

- **Advantage**: Predictable, simple to administer
- **Disadvantage**: Probably in addition to normal Shire Council rates; may create hardship on low-income households
- **Fairness concern**: Flat rate regardless of earning power

### 2. Tithe (Sliding Scale)

Individuals, groups, and businesses tithe **10% of net profit** to public services.

- **Advantage**: Fair — those who earn more contribute proportionally more; rates on a sliding scale
- **Precedent**: All [[mondragon-cooperatives]] do this, funding schools, libraries, churches, and recreational grounds
- **Disadvantage**: Requires accurate profit reporting from enterprises

### 3. Local Currency

The Community Services Council can print its own currency, **backed by a valuable community resource** such as firewood or clean bottled water.

- **Advantage**: Keeps capital circulating within the community; funds local projects directly
- **Resource backing**: A timber forest planted early and harvested at various stages of maturity can produce most income needed for subsequent years
- **Precedent**: Modern towns with social forestry programs use timber harvests to fund community services

### 4. Charges (User-Pays)

Charges by the school, community centre, and health services offset costs on a user-pays basis.

- **Advantage**: Fair — only those who use services pay for them
- **Disadvantage**: Covers only non-universal facilities; universally used services need pro-rated funding
- **Best used**: In conjunction with another model, not as the sole income source

### Comparison of Models

| Model | Fairness | Predictability | Local Benefit | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Levy | Low (flat) | High | Moderate | Basic infrastructure |
| Tithe | High (sliding) | Moderate | High | Ongoing services |
| Local currency | High | Variable | Very high | Capital projects |
| Charges | High | Variable | Moderate | Specific facilities |

## Community Services Council

### Structure

A **Community Services Council** is elected to administer policy on publicly owned or common assets. The council:

- Collects lease monies on utility plots
- Administers funds for schools and medical services
- Ensures rates are used for maintaining roads, water supplies, and public services

### Membership

The Council should be comprised of a **selection of those active in the service areas**, not an uninvolved group. Each area of action has an independent management sub-group, which appoints one representative to Council.

### Meeting Frequency

Council meets monthly or even less frequently. Sub-groups handle everyday business within their budget and allotted areas of operation. Council can call for and act on submissions or reports relating to specific policies and strategies.

### Sub-Group Autonomy

Sub-groups can:

- Raise their own funds (in addition to accessing public funds)
- Have an active business management role in income-producing areas
- Supply workers to carry out business operations
- Report to Council regularly
