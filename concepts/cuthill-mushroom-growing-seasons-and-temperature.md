The finest field crops were produced in dry summers, not wet ones: "Do
you ever see a crop of mushrooms in a field after a wet summer? On the
contrary, the finest crops are produced in a dry summer."

## Ideal Bed Temperature

Cuthill specified precise [[cuthill-mushroom-bed-preparation-and-manure]]: 60-70 degrees F, described as
  "September heat."
- This could always be maintained by hay and mats.
- Milk heat (approximately 98 degrees F) was the target when preparing
  to spawn using the old method.
- For the new (early-spawning) method, beds were spawned at 100 degrees
  F or more.
- The trial stick inserted into the manure heap should not be too hot
  for the bare hand to bear; if it was, the heat was too great and
  would destroy spawn.
- Dung near the surface (within four inches) should never be heated
  beyond blood heat.
- Winter droppings were allowed to heat gently to 80-90 degrees before
  turning.

## Seasonal Bed-Making Schedule

Cuthill practiced a continual rotation system that ensured year-round
supply:

- "Some seasons I begin earlier than others."
- Bed No. 1 (made and spawned approximately late September): uncovered
  November 3rd, coming fast into bearing after eight weeks spawned.
- Bed No. 2: spawned October 6th, mushrooms just coming up when
  uncovered in early November.
- Bed No. 3: spawned October 28th.
- Bed No. 4: made up with fresh stable dung in early November.
- Additional beds (Nos. 5, 6, 7) followed in sequence.

The typical timeline from spawning to first mushrooms was:

- Normal method: approximately two months (Cuthill states he "never
  like[d] to see the tops appearing sooner than two months after the
  bed is made").
- Forced early crop: four to five weeks (by spawning while heat was
  90-100 degrees F and using half the usual quantity of mould). This
  was not a lasting bed.
- New method (spawning at 100+ degrees): approximately six weeks
  minimum; two weeks earlier than the old method.

## Summer Cultivation

Cuthill was particularly proud of his [[cuthill-mushroom-house-construction-and-management]] fully
  exposed to the sun, entirely free from [[falconer-mushroom-insect-enemies]]s.
- His method: cover beds with straw, water the covering every night to
  keep the interior cool.
- The maggot could not breed at a surface temperature of about 60
  degrees F.
- In 1863, he had mushroom beds in glass houses fully exposed to the
  sun with shade temperatures of 80-90 degrees F, yet had fine crops
  free from maggots.
- He had mushrooms selling at five shillings per pound in July.
- His finest summer mushroom of one year weighed eleven ounces, grown
  on May 28th.
- A remarkable specimen from July 1856 had a circumference of three
  feet six inches and weighed four and a half pounds, valued at twenty
  shillings.

The key principle was: "mushrooms can be produced free from insects in
summer as well as in winter, by keeping low temperature upon the surface
of the beds." This was achieved not by watering the mould itself but by
sprinkling the mats and straw coverings.

## Winter Cultivation

Winter was the traditional mushroom season and commanded the highest
prices:

- "The price of mushrooms during winter is very high."
- Mushroom prices were compared to sea-kale and asparagus, all native
  British plants that were expensive because scarce.
- During the severe winter of 1864-65, Cuthill maintained bed heat
  successfully using his methods.
- Water used in winter always had the chill taken off.
- Beds were covered with glass, straw, and mats during cold weather.
- Brick pits were especially useful in winter because of the ease of
  covering them.

## The Maggot and Temperature

Temperature was directly linked to pest management:

- The mushroom maggot was "of spontaneous growth, similar to cheese
  mites," produced at temperatures above 70-80 degrees F.
- At approximately 60 degrees F the maggot would not breed.
- An analogy: "If in hot weather you place a piece of butter on a slab,
  reverse a basin over it, and put a cloth steeped in water over the
  basin, it will make the butter hard, however hot the weather may be."
- If watering of the straw coverings was neglected in summer, "failure
  must result."
- A second pest (a small dark-purple maggot, half an inch long) attacked
  newly-formed buttons regardless of temperature and had to be crushed
  by hand.

## Year-Round Supply Through Rotation

By adopting his practices with a continual rotation of beds, Cuthill
claimed: "I have not been without mushrooms for a day during the past
three years." This required:

- Staggered bed-making from September through November.
- A mix of normal and forced beds.
- Both indoor houses (for winter) and cooled houses (for summer).
- Careful attention to heat management at every stage.

## Temperature for Spawn Development

Spawn development had its own temperature requirements:

- Spawn "rejoices in a September heat" -- the warm days and cool nights
  of late summer and early autumn.
