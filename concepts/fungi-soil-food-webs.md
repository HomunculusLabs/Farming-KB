# Fungi in Soil Food Webs

Trophic structure, energy flow, biomass, and the role of fungi in soil food webs. Based on Gadd, Watkinson & Dyer, *Fungi in the Environment* (2006). See also [[fungal-ecology-decomposition-cycles]], [[fungal-nutrient-cycling-mineral-transformations]], [[fungal-bacterial-interactions]], and [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]].

## Overview

Soil food webs describe the complex network of feeding relationships among soil organisms, from bacteria and fungi (primary decomposers) through protozoa, nematodes, and microarthropods (consumers) to predatory nematodes, centipedes, and ground beetles (top predators). Fungi occupy a central position in these webs as the dominant decomposers of complex organic matter and as a major food source for higher trophic levels.

## Fungal Biomass in Soil

### Quantifying Fungal Biomass

Fungi typically represent a large proportion of total soil microbial biomass:

- **Forest soils**: Fungi constitute 60-90% of total microbial biomass. In boreal and temperate coniferous forests, fungal biomass can exceed 500 kg C per hectare in the organic horizon.
- **Grassland soils**: Fungi constitute 30-60% of microbial biomass
- **Agricultural soils**: Fungi constitute 10-30% of microbial biomass, lower due to tillage disturbance and high nitrogen inputs
- **Total fungal biomass**: Global estimates of soil fungal biomass range from 1-5 Gt C (gigatonnes of carbon), making the fungal kingdom one of the largest carbon reservoirs in terrestrial ecosystems

### Measurement Methods

- **Microscopic direct counts**: Using fluorescence microscopy after staining with fluorescent brighteners (e.g., calcofluor white) to visualize hyphae. Hyphal length is measured and converted to biomass using a standard conversion factor.
- **Ergosterol**: Ergosterol is a sterol found in fungal membranes (not in plants or most bacteria). Soil ergosterol concentration correlates with living fungal biomass. This is the most widely used biochemical method.
- **Phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) analysis**: Specific fatty acids (18:2omega6,9) are fungal biomarkers. PLFA analysis provides both total biomass and community composition data.
- **DNA-based methods**: Quantitative PCR targeting fungal ribosomal DNA provides estimates of fungal abundance and community composition.

## Trophic Roles of Fungi

### Primary Decomposers

Fungi are the dominant decomposers of recalcitrant organic matter:

- **Lignin**: Only white-rot basidiomycetes can fully mineralize lignin. This is ecologically critical because lignin is the second most abundant biopolymer on Earth.
- **Cellulose**: Fungi and bacteria both decompose cellulose, but fungi dominate in acidic forest soils. Fungal cellulase systems are often more efficient than bacterial ones for crystalline cellulose.
- **Chitin**: Fungal chitinases degrade insect exoskeletons and fungal cell walls, recycling nitrogen bound in these recalcitrant compounds.
- **Cutin and suberin**: Fungi produce cutinases that degrade plant cuticle and suberin (cork), important in the decomposition of leaves and woody tissue.
- **Humus formation**: Fungal metabolites (melanin, phenolic compounds) contribute to humus formation, the stable organic matter fraction that persists in soil for centuries to millennia.

### Mycorrhizal Symbionts

Mycorrhizal fungi form a direct trophic link between plants and soil:

- **Carbon pathway**: 10-30% of plant photosynthate flows to mycorrhizal fungi, entering the soil food web through fungal biomass and exudates
- **Nutrient pathway**: Mycorrhizal fungi channel phosphorus, nitrogen, and micronutrients from soil to plants
- **Network connections**: Common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs) link multiple plants, potentially transferring carbon and nutrients between individuals of different species

### Food for Higher Trophic Levels

Fungal hyphae and fruiting bodies are food for numerous soil organisms:

- **Fungal-feeding nematodes**: *Aphelenchoides*, *Aphelenchus*, and *Ditylenchus* species feed on fungal hyphae. They are abundant in forest soils and can consume significant amounts of fungal biomass.
- **Microarthropods**: Collembola (springtails) and oribatid mites are major consumers of fungal hyphae. Collembola selectively feed on preferred fungal species, influencing community composition.
- **Enchytraeids**: Potworms consume both fungal hyphae and decomposing organic matter.
- **Larger organisms**: Many mammals, birds, and insects eat fungal fruiting bodies. Truffle-feeding mammals (squirrels, deer, wild boar) are important spore dispersers for ectomycorrhizal fungi. See [[james-trappe]].

## Fungal-Based vs Bacterial-Based Energy Channels

Soil food webs can be conceptually divided into two energy channels:

### Fungal Energy Channel

- Energy flows from plant litter through fungi to fungal-feeding organisms
- Characterized by slower decomposition, more stable soil organic matter, and higher carbon retention
- Dominant in: forests, perennial grasslands, no-till systems, undisturbed ecosystems
- Associated with: acidic pH, high C:N litter, woody debris, recalcitrant organic matter
- Key consumers: fungal-feeding nematodes, collembola, oribatid mites
- Fungi in this channel include: saprotrophic basidiomycetes, ascomycetes, and mycorrhizal fungi

### Bacterial Energy Channel

- Energy flows from labile organic matter through bacteria to bacterial-feeding organisms
- Characterized by rapid nutrient cycling, less stable soil organic matter, and faster nutrient release
- Dominant in: agricultural soils, disturbed ecosystems, early succession
- Associated with: neutral-alkaline pH, low C:N organic matter, root exudates
- Key consumers: bacterial-feeding nematodes, protozoa (amoebae, flagellates, ciliates)

### The Fungal:Bacterial Ratio

The ratio of fungal to bacterial biomass (F:B ratio) is a useful indicator of:

- **Soil management**: Higher F:B in no-till, perennial systems; lower in tilled, annual systems
- **Successional stage**: F:B increases with ecosystem maturity
- **Decomposition pathway**: High F:B = slower, more efficient decomposition; Low F:B = faster, less efficient decomposition
- **Soil health**: Higher F:B generally associated with better soil structure (fungal hyphae bind soil aggregates), higher carbon storage, and more stable nutrient cycling
- **Typical values**: Forest soils F:B = 5-100; Grasslands = 1-10; Conventional agriculture = 0.1-1; No-till agriculture = 1-5

## Fungal Contributions to Soil Structure

### Aggregate Formation

Fungi play a major role in creating and stabilizing soil aggregates:

- **Hyphal entanglement**: Fungal hyphae physically bind soil particles together, forming macroaggregates (>250 um). This is especially important in sandy and loamy soils.
- **Glomalin**: AM fungi produce glomalin, a glycoprotein that acts as a biological glue. Glomalin persists in soil for years to decades and is a significant component of soil organic matter (1-5% of total SOC). See [[glomalin-and-soil-carbon]].
- **Hyphal residues**: Dead fungal hyphae contribute polysaccharides and other compounds that stabilize aggregates. Fungal necromass is a major source of stable soil organic carbon.
- **Water stability**: Fungal-stabilized aggregates are more water-stable than those stabilized by bacteria, reducing erosion risk.

### Soil Porosity and Water Relations

- Fungal hyphae create macropores (>30 um) as they grow through soil, improving water infiltration and gas exchange
- The mycorrhizal mycelium significantly increases the effective root-soil contact area, improving water and nutrient uptake
- Fungal decomposition of organic matter creates humus that improves soil water-holding capacity

## Fungal Energy Flow and Carbon Cycling

### Carbon Use Efficiency

Fungi convert a higher proportion of consumed carbon into biomass than bacteria:

- **Fungal CUE**: 40-60% of consumed carbon incorporated into biomass
- **Bacterial CUE**: 20-40% of consumed carbon incorporated into biomass
- **Ecological significance**: Higher CUE means more carbon is retained in soil as fungal biomass (living and dead), rather than being respired as CO2. This makes fungal-dominated systems more effective at carbon storage.

### Fungal Necromass and Stable Carbon

- Fungal cell walls contain chitin and melanin, which are highly resistant to decomposition
- Fungal necromass (dead hyphae, spores, and fruiting bodies) contributes disproportionately to stable soil organic matter pools
- Estimates suggest fungal necromass may account for 30-60% of total soil organic carbon in some forest soils
- The mean residence time of fungal-derived carbon in soil (decades to centuries) is longer than that of bacterial-derived carbon

### Priming Effect

Mycorrhizal fungi can influence decomposition of existing soil organic matter:

- **Positive priming**: Mycorrhizal activity accelerates decomposition of soil organic matter, releasing CO2. This occurs when mycorrhizal fungi produce enzymes or exudates that stimulate saprotrophic activity.
- **Negative priming**: Mycorrhizal fungi suppress decomposition by competing with saprotrophs for nutrients or resources.
- The "Gadgil effect" (named after the researchers who first described it): In ECM-dominated forests, decomposition of litter is slower than in AM-dominated forests, possibly because ECM fungi compete effectively with free-living decomposers.

## Fungal Responses to Environmental Heterogeneity

Fungal hyphae respond dynamically to spatial and temporal heterogeneity in soil resources:

- **Resource foraging**: Fungal hyphae grow preferentially toward nutrient-rich patches, a process mediated by tropisms and signaling. Some species show "translocation" -- moving resources from nutrient-rich to nutrient-poor parts of the mycelial network.
- **Hyphal fusion (anastomosis)**: Fungal hyphae of the same individual can fuse, creating an interconnected network that allows resource sharing across large areas. A single genet of *Armillaria* can cover hectares.
- **Foraging strategies**: Different fungal species employ different strategies:
  - **Phalangeal**: Dense, slowly extending front (e.g., many saprotrophs)
  - **Linear**: Rapid extension of individual hyphae with little branching (e.g., cord-forming fungi)
  - **Domain**: Moderately dense exploration with intermittent branching (e.g., many ECM fungi)
