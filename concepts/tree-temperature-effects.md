# Tree Temperature Effects

## Evaporation, Condensation, and Thermal Regulation

Trees are powerful regulators of local temperature through two primary mechanisms: **evaporation** causes heat loss locally, while **condensation** causes heat gain locally. Both effects can be strategically used to heat or cool air or surfaces in [[permaculture-design]].

### Evapotranspiration and Cooling

The USDA's Yearbook of Agriculture on Trees (1949) reports that an ordinary elm of medium size will transpire approximately **15,000 pounds of water on a clear, dry, hot day**. Evapotranspiration in a 40-inch rainfall area is generally not less than 15 inches per year. This massive evaporative cooling effect means that evaporation from tree canopies by day significantly cools the surrounding air in hot weather.

### Condensation and Nighttime Warming

Conversely, nighttime condensation of atmospheric water warms the surrounding air. Moisture will not condense unless it finds a surface to condense on — leaves provide this surface, as well as contact cooling. Leaf surfaces are likely to be cooler than other objects at evening due to evaporation from leaf stomata during the day. As air rises over trees, vertical lift cooling also occurs, and the two effects combine to condense moisture on the forest canopy.

Leaves are approximately **86% water**, giving them twice the specific heat of soil. As a result, trees remain **cooler than soil by day and warmer at night**. Plants generally may be 15°C or so warmer than the surrounding air temperature.

### Microclimate Moderation

Small open water storages or tree clumps placed upwind of a house have a pleasant moderating effect:

- **Summer**: Air passing over open water is cooled
- **Winter**: Air is warmed and has moisture added
- **Hot, humid tropics**: Trees captured water has a **dehumidifying effect**, as trees are capable of reducing humidity by direct absorption (except in the most extreme conditions)

## Leaf Colour and Temperature

### Reflective Properties of Different Leaf Colours

The colour of foliage has significant thermal implications:

- **Reddish-coloured leaves** (found in some vines and shrubs) reflect chiefly red light rays. Sharp temperature decreases of up to **20°C (36°F)** can result from interposing reddish foliage between a thermometer and the sun, compared with green-pigmented plants (Daubenmire, 1974)
- **Whitish plants** such as wormwood and birch may reflect up to **85%** of incoming light
- **Dark leaves** of shade plants may reflect as little as **2%** of incoming light

### Design Applications for Thermal Control

These reflectance properties have direct applications in building design:

1. **White or red-coloured roof vines** over tiles can effectively lower summer temperatures within buildings
2. **Trellis systems** with appropriate foliage can create cool microclimates
3. **Fine water sprays** and **damp mulch systems** fitted under trellises create cool areas of dense air through evaporation
4. This combined effect is highly effective for **moderating summer heat in buildings** and providing cool air sources for induced cross-ventilation

## Practical Design Strategies

### Cooling a Building with Trees

To maximize cooling benefits:

- Place **deciduous trees** on the south side (in the Northern Hemisphere) to provide summer shade while allowing winter sun through
- Use **red-leaved or white-leaved vines** on trellises over buildings for maximum solar reflection
- Combine vegetation with **water features** (small ponds, sprays) upwind of structures
- Install **damp mulch systems** under trellises for evaporative cooling
- Design for **cross-ventilation** that draws air through these cooled zones

### Winter Warming

- Evergreen trees placed as windbreaks reduce heat loss from buildings
- Trees placed upwind add moisture to cold winter air, increasing its thermal capacity
- The nighttime condensation on tree surfaces releases latent heat to the surroundings
- Forest canopy retains warmth compared to open ground

## The Forest as a Thermal Buffer

The forest interior maintains remarkably stable temperatures compared to open ground. The combination of high specific heat (due to high water content), evaporative cooling by day, condensation warming by night, wind reduction, and shade creates a buffered microclimate. This thermal stability extends to:

- **Reduced frost risk** in forest clearings and edges
- **Moderated diurnal temperature swings** compared to open fields
- **Extended growing seasons** in [[forest-gardens]] and agroforestry systems
- **Reduced heating and cooling costs** for nearby structures

The practical takeaway for permaculture designers is that trees and forests are among the most effective tools available for passive temperature regulation, and their placement should be a primary consideration in any site design.

## Ecological Significance
The principles described in relation to tree temperature effects
are fundamental to understanding how natural systems maintain equilibrium.
Forests and vegetation play critical roles in water cycling, temperature
regulation, and atmospheric processes that sustain regional climate patterns.

## Practical Applications
These concepts apply directly to permaculture design, agroforestry systems,
and watershed management. Understanding the interactions between vegetation,
water, and atmosphere enables designers to create more resilient and productive
landscapes that work with natural processes rather than opposing them.
