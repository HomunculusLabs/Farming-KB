# Mushroom Poisoning and Safe Foraging Practices

## Palmer's Theory of Taste as a Toxicity Indicator

Palmer advanced what he called his "theory: namely, that no fungus which, unspiced, being plainly cooked with dry heat, commends itself to the taste, can be, in the least, dangerous to human life." He proposed this with hesitation, acknowledging that "many people have so little sense in such matters."

The core argument was that [[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-mixed-extracts-synergism]] with gravies, fried in batter, simmered in fat, or seasoned with black pepper and parsley have their "delicate flavor destroyed by compounding them with other food." The cook thereby "disguises the very alarm which Nature has placed at the gateway." One of the most common signs of a harmful fungus was a stinging sensation affecting not just the tongue but also the throat and tonsils, with effects on the stomach and bowels.

## Palmer's Personal Testing Protocol

Palmer described a careful, incremental approach to testing new mushroom species:

1. **Trace the specimen to its family** using classification keys
2. **Broil with salt and butter only** (no pepper)
3. **Eat a small piece on an empty stomach**
4. **Increase the quantity in successive experiments**
5. **Continue until perfectly safe** or unpleasant symptoms appear

He reported that "usually, the non-edible fungus discloses its character over the charcoal: nauseous slime weeps from the stem, a grassy and disagreeable odor arises as it heats, or, on being tasted, there is no desire to take another mouthful."

Palmer claimed to have tested "perhaps forty varieties of toadstools" and ate regularly "considerably more than one-half," making "daily additions to the bill of fare." Despite this adventurous approach, he insisted he never acted carelessly: "In whatever cause, reckless disregard of consequences is not bravery, although no two elements are oftener confounded."

## The Coprinus Deliquescens Poisoning Incident

Palmer's own most serious poisoning occurred not from eating a toxic species but from cross-contamination. He had purchased common mushrooms that were shipped in a box containing a large number of *[[coprinus-deliquescens]]* specimens. These deliquescent fungi were in a state of decomposition — they "eventually turn to ink, independent of contact with any fluid substance."

The symptoms appeared almost immediately after swallowing the [[palmer-edible-mushrooms-north-america]]: "Dizziness, nausea, purging, perspiration, with alternate cold spells, all passed over me within an hour, so rapid is the effect of the mushroom-virus." Palmer noted that "my engines refused to reverse action" — the poisoning could not be stopped once begun.

## Palmer's Antidote: Sweet Oil and Whiskey

The remedy that neutralized the poison consisted of "two wineglasses of whiskey and sweet-oil (equal parts)." Palmer prescribed this remedy in all cases rather than emetics, noting that one could "omit the whiskey, if you please, or substitute vinegar for spirits, but take sweet-oil liberally in case of mistake."

He theorized that Italians could eat many dangerous fungi "with impunity" because "when fresh, their properties are changed by sweet-oil; preserved, they are neutralized by pickle." He believed that "either of these elements renders harmless the peculiar alkali, to a superfluity of which mushrooms owe their noxious qualities."

## Environmental Influence on Mushroom Safety

Palmer documented several cases demonstrating that environmental conditions, not species identity alone, determined edibility:

### Potash-Forced Horse Mushrooms
A gardener sent a basket of horse-mushrooms that had been forced with potash. "They cooked like pieces of soap, and the liquor resembled strong lye." The alkaline contamination from forcing agents rendered normally edible mushrooms inedible.

### Fishy-Scented Boletus
A delicious species (*Boletus astivalis*) was found "impregnated so strongly with the odor of decayed fish as to be unfit for food." The environmental absorption of off-putting odors made the mushroom unpalatable despite being a safe species.

### Flavor-Transferring Boletus
Specimens of *[[boletus-pachypus]]* "communicated their strong, nutty flavor, without contact, to some potatoes placed beside them in my refrigerator." This demonstrated the volatile nature of [[medicinal-mushroom-compounds-and-health-benefits]] their ability to contaminate nearby foods.

### Habitat-Dependent Quality
The maned mushroom (*[[coprinus-comatus]]*) was "delicious eating" when found in lawns, but specimens from "dust-heaps, brick-yards, or city offal" had no flavor and "in large quantities would doubtless prove deleterious."

## The Honey-Colored Mushroom (Agaricus melleus) Case

Palmer's experience with the honey-colored mushroom illustrated the confusion in mycological literature. After eating plentifully of this species, he consulted Greville, a well-known Scotch authority, and found a dire warning: the species was "said to be freely eaten on the Continent" according to Fries, but Person and Paulet gave it a bad character, with Paulet reporting that "the poor animal died twelve hours after receiving the poisonous fungus" in a dog-feeding experiment.

Palmer concluded: "I think it may be noxious raw, but that the heat kills the virus." He noted it "continues to be a favorite article of diet to this day."

## Cooking Methods

Palmer insisted there were "but two ways in which it is proper to cook mushrooms":

1. **Broiling**: On a fine-wire gridiron, sprinkled with salt and (if the species is well known) red pepper, buttered as the fire browns them. Best for elm-tree mushroom, honey-colored mushroom, and any species that tastes raw of grass or trees.

2. **Stewing in milk**: "Exactly as you would make an oyster stew." Best for meadow mushroom, horse-mushroom, and coprinus species.

## Mushroom Nutrition and Preservation

Palmer was enthusiastic about the nutritional value of mushrooms. During the season, he ate them "three times a day; sometimes of a half dozen kinds at a meal," and reported: "I never enjoy better digestion than during toadstool time." He attributed this to the "natural alkali which in some systems is greatly needed."

He also dried mushrooms for winter use, noting that "the Chinese make dried fungus a very general article of diet" based on his [[palmer-mushroom-cooking-and-personal-experience]] living in San Francisco, Honolulu, and Hong Kong. He declared: "No better substitute for meat than fungus can be found. Neither its odor when cooking, nor the gravy it makes, resembles any form of vegetable food." This was natural, he argued, "since the plant absorbs oxygen from the air, after the manner of animals."

## Mushroom Growth Rate

Palmer carefully observed growth rates from his own closet cultivation. The common mushroom, "supposed to spring up in a night," actually takes about ten days to mature "from a button the size of a pin-head to a disk three or four inches in diameter." This was the average period for most species, although the Coprinariae required "not half that time."
