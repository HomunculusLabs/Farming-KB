# Permaculture Principle 12 — Creatively Use and Respond to Change

The twelfth of David Holmgren"s twelve permaculture design principles. This principle has two threads: designing to make use of change in a deliberate and cooperative way, and creatively responding or adapting to large-scale system change that is beyond our control. The butterfly, which is the transformation of a caterpillar, conveys the idea of adaptive change that is uplifting rather than threatening.

> "Vision is not seeing things as they are but as they will be." — David Holmgren

## Core Concept

In Permaculture One, Holmgren and Mollison stated that although stability was an important aspect of permaculture, evolutionary change was essential. Permaculture is about the durability of natural living systems and human culture, but this durability paradoxically depends in large measure on flexibility and change.

Holmgren frames this understanding within the scale-dependent nature of change: small-scale, fast, short-lived changes of elements actually contribute to higher-order system stability. We live and design in a historical context of turnover and change at multiple larger scales, and this generates an illusion of endless change with no possibility of stability. A contextual and systemic sense of the dynamic balance between stability and change contributes to design that is evolutionary rather than random.

## Ecological Succession

The concept of ecological succession — where there is a pattern to changes in vegetation composition and structure — has been a central idea in ecological theory for nearly a century. The classic model describes a directional progression from pioneer species on bare ground through intermediate stages to a relatively stable climax community.

Holmgren describes how permaculture designers can accelerate succession toward desired endpoints. Rather than waiting decades for natural processes, we can plant fast-growing pioneer species, build soil rapidly with compost and green manures, and introduce climax species (fruit trees, timber trees) under the nurse canopy of pioneers.

## Top-Down and Bottom-Up Change

Holmgren distinguishes between change we can control (top-down) and change that emerges from the system itself (bottom-up):

- **Top-down**: When we design and manage gardens, farms, businesses, and communities, we exercise some control. The key is to use this power sparingly — "a little change goes a long way if used carefully"
- **Bottom-up**: Plants, animals, microbes, weather, and market forces also drive change in our systems. Our job is to observe these bottom-up changes and work with them

## Information as a Resource

Change brings information. When something in a system shifts — a plant dies, a pest appears, water flows differently — that change carries information about the state of the system. Holmgren argues that information is itself a resource, perhaps the most valuable one in a changing world. The more quickly and accurately we can read and respond to the information that change provides, the more effectively we can adapt.

## Permanent Structures vs Renewable Adaptation

Holmgren discusses the tension between building things to last and designing for change. He offers Stewart Brand"s "Six S"s of buildings" as a framework:

1. **Site**: The most permanent — location, orientation, relationship to land
2. **Structure**: The frame — should last centuries
3. **Skin**: The exterior cladding — lasts decades
4. **Services**: Plumbing, wiring, HVAC — updated every 10-20 years
5. **Space plan**: Interior layout — changes with each occupant
6. **Stuff**: Furniture and belongings — changes constantly

By not embedding ephemeral aspects in permanent structure, we maintain flexibility for current and future users to adapt buildings to their needs.

## Maintenance and Creative Change

Holmgren contrasts routine maintenance (a repetitive, uncreative reaction to inevitable change) with creative adaptation. Rather than always building things to last, the strategy of using cheap and abundant renewable materials has merit. The banana leaf used as a plate in South-East Asia is an excellent example of a simple, sustainable, throw-away solution.

In the garden, temporary structures from reused or easily renewable materials (trellises, animal hutches) can be more appropriate than expensive attempts at durability, especially when decay rates of wood and metal in compost-rich soil are high.
