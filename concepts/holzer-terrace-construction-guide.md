# Terraces and Paths - Construction Guide

From "Sepp Holzer's Permaculture" by Sepp Holzer.

## Benefits of Terraces

Human beings have known the benefit of using terrace systems for a very long time. In Asia, South America, Africa and Europe people have been using terraces for thousands of years. Making steps in steep slopes helps to prevent soil erosion. Valuable humus remains on the slope instead of being washed away. Terraces store and hold moisture, increase the area available for cultivation, are more pleasant to work and are far easier to access than a steep slope.

Well-designed terraces minimise the danger of landslides and mudslides and also greatly improve the microclimates in cultivated areas.

## Design as Dual-Use Paths

I tend to design terraces so that they can be used as paths and provide access for mechanised farming equipment. By combining the two uses, I have both a terrace that is a path and a path that is a terrace. It is still possible to access the terraces while they are under cultivation, but it is important to keep to the embankments.

## Width

The width required by any machinery you are planning to use should be taken into account. It is best to make sure that the entire terrace can be cultivated or harvested in a single trip. Managing two terraces each with a width of five metres is far easier and more profitable than managing a single terrace with a width of 10 metres.

When calculating the perfect width for a terrace, it is important to consider the current gradient of the slope. The steeper the slope, the narrower the terrace should be. Particular care should be taken with fine, loamy soil on very steep slopes, because this is where there is the greatest danger of erosion.

## Gradient

The gradient should be as low as possible and no more than 15 to 20 percent. Dead ends waste time and energy and should be avoided. The gradient of the terrace embankments can be 1:1 if the soil is stony. On sandy or loam soils the best results are with gradients of around 1:1.5 to 1:2.

## Risers and Embankment Construction

To stabilise the embankments risers need to be constructed. The topsoil and the layers of earth beneath should be removed until you reach solid and stable material. The riser forms the foundation and is angled slightly into the hill. Next, the embankment should be built up replacing the excavated layers. The topsoil forms the uppermost layer.

It is important to handle the topsoil very carefully. Mistakes are frequently made when the operator of the mechanical digger does not have enough experience and fails to preserve the topsoil.

## Working with Water

In areas where there is little rain, I try to angle the terraces into the hill slightly to help retain water. When stabilising the terraces against heavy rainfall, it is important to make sure that their alignment will not channel the water.

## Key Points

1. Sepp Holzer's approach to Holzer Terrace Construction Guide demonstrates the power of working with natural landscape features rather than against them.
2. Holzer's Krameterhof farm at 1,500m elevation in Austria proves that extreme climates can support abundant food production.
3. The method relies on creating diverse microclimates through strategic use of water, earthworks, and plant placement.
4. Raised beds and hugelkultur are central techniques that improve drainage, warmth, and soil fertility simultaneously.
5. Animal integration — particularly pigs and poultry — plays a crucial role in soil preparation and pest management.
6. Holzer emphasizes the importance of observing the land for at least a year before making major design decisions.
7. Water retention landscapes including ponds and swales are key infrastructure elements in Holzer's approach.
8. The method avoids synthetic inputs entirely, relying on natural cycles and biological diversity for fertility and pest control.
9. Fruit trees are managed without pruning, allowing them to develop natural forms suited to their environment.
10. Crop diversity and polyculture systems provide resilience against weather extremes, pests, and disease.

## Practical Applications

- Walk the land extensively during different seasons and weather conditions before beginning any earthworks or planting.
- Construct raised beds using available materials — logs, branches, soil, and organic matter in layered mounds.
- Install ponds and water features at high points to enable gravity-fed irrigation and create reflective microclimates.
- Integrate livestock grazing patterns with crop rotation to naturally fertilize and manage vegetation.
- Plant fruit trees and berry bushes on mounds and slopes where water drainage is optimal.
- Use stone walls and large rocks as thermal mass to create warm microclimates for heat-loving crops.
- Establish polyculture strips with diverse root depths, growth habits, and maturity dates.
- Allow natural succession to inform planting schemes rather than imposing rigid crop plans.

## Soil Stabilisation and Planting Techniques
Once the terrace embankments are constructed and the topsoil has been carefully replaced, stabilising the exposed surfaces is critical. Holzer recommends immediate planting of deep-rooted perennials along the riser faces to bind the soil and prevent erosion during the first rainy season. Comfrey, with its deep taproot system, is one of the most effective plants for terrace stabilisation because it anchors the soil structure while also providing nutrient-rich biomass that can be cut and used as [[holzer-green-manure-crops]] on the terrace surfaces above. Other effective stabilisers include raspberry bushes, which form dense root mats, and nitrogen-fixing trees such as black locust or sea buckthorn that can be planted at the base of risers to provide both root reinforcement and eventual shade.
The terrace tread — the flat cultivated surface — should be planted according to [[holzer-microclimates-permaculture]]. The upper edge of each terrace, where cold air drainage collects, is suited to cold-hardy crops and fruit trees that benefit from the moisture that accumulates there. The lower edge, which receives more warmth and better air circulation, is ideal for heat-loving crops and tender perennials. Holzer often places stone walls or large rocks along the lower edges of terraces to create thermal mass that radiates warmth at night, extending the growing season for adjacent plantings.
## Drainage and Overflow Systems
Proper drainage is essential for terrace longevity. Holzer designs each terrace with a slight outward slope (approximately 1-2 percent) so that excess water can drain off the tread without pooling and saturating the soil. Overflow water from one terrace should be directed onto the next terrace below via a stone-lined spillway or a planted swale, never allowed to fall freely over the riser face where it would cause erosion. This cascade principle mirrors the design of [[holzer-water-landscapes-retention]] and ensures that every drop of rainfall is captured and utilised as it moves down the slope.
In areas with intense summer storms, Holzer recommends installing additional drainage channels at the back of each terrace — the uphill side where water naturally collects. These channels should be lined with gravel and planted with moisture-loving species such as mint or watercress, creating a productive use for the drainage water. The combination of surface drainage and subsurface infiltration means that terraces can handle extreme rainfall events without catastrophic failure, a critical consideration in regions prone to flash flooding or prolonged monsoon rains.
## Integration with Raised Beds and Hugelkultur
Terraces provide an ideal framework for integrating [[sepp-holzer-terracing-and-hugelkultur]] structures. Holzer often builds hugelkultur beds directly onto terrace treads, using the terrace as a level platform for the mound. The woody core of the hugelkultur bed provides long-term moisture retention and nutrient release, while the terrace provides the structural support needed to maintain the mound on sloping terrain. This combination is particularly effective on steep slopes where conventional raised beds would be difficult to construct and maintain.
The [[holzer-raised-bed-design-principles]] that Holzer applies on flat ground — layering organic materials, incorporating animal manure, creating diverse microclimates through orientation and height variation — all translate directly to the terrace context. On terraces, raised beds can be oriented perpendicular to the slope to further slow water movement, or parallel to the slope to create distinct planting zones with different moisture and temperature characteristics. Holzer emphasises that the raised beds on terraces should be narrow enough to reach across from either side, eliminating the need to walk on the growing surface and compact the soil.
## Long-Term Maintenance and Evolution
Terrace systems require ongoing maintenance to remain productive and stable. Holzer recommends annual inspection of riser faces for signs of erosion, animal burrows, or root damage from large trees. Any damaged areas should be repaired immediately before small problems become large ones. The embankment plantings should be cut back periodically to prevent shading of the terrace tread, with the cut material used as mulch or compost on the cultivated surface. Over time, as the stabilising plants mature and the soil structure develops, the terraces become increasingly self-maintaining, requiring progressively less intervention.
Holzer also emphasises that terraces should be allowed to evolve over time. As trees planted on the terraces mature, they create shade patterns that shift the microclimate and open opportunities for understory plantings. The [[holzer-assessing-the-land]] process that Holzer recommends before initial construction should be repeated periodically to observe how the terraces are performing and to identify opportunities for improvement. This adaptive management approach — observing, responding, and adjusting — is fundamental to Holzer's overall [[sepp-holzer-terrace-systems-and-raised-beds]] and distinguishes it from conventional engineering approaches that seek permanent, fixed solutions.
## Terraces in Different Climate Zones
Holzer's terrace designs are adapted to the specific conditions of different climate zones. In Mediterranean climates with hot, dry summers and intense winter rainfall, terraces must be designed primarily for water capture and erosion control. The terrace tread should be angled slightly into the hill to maximise water retention, and overflow channels must be sized to handle the large volumes of water generated by winter storms. In these environments, terraces work synergistically with [[holzer-water-landscapes-retention]] by directing excess water into ponds and swales that store it for summer use.
In tropical and subtropical climates with high rainfall, the primary concern is managing water volume and preventing terrace failure during prolonged wet seasons. Terraces in these environments should have more gradual gradients, wider drainage channels, and more robust embankment plantings. Holzer has observed that in tropical environments, terraces stabilise more quickly because the rapid plant growth quickly binds the soil structure, but they also require more frequent maintenance because the aggressive root systems of tropical plants can displace terrace stones and crack embankment surfaces.
In cold mountain climates like those at the Krameterhof, terraces serve additional functions beyond erosion control. The south-facing terrace embankments absorb solar radiation during the day and release it slowly at night, creating warm microclimates that extend the growing season for heat-loving crops. Holzer often places his most demanding crops — including peppers, tomatoes, and melons — on south-facing terrace edges where the reflected heat and retained warmth give them the best conditions. The north-facing terrace risers, which receive less direct sun, are planted with shade-tolerant crops and berry bushes that benefit from the cooler, moister conditions.

## Mechanical Considerations and Earthmoving

Holzer's approach to terrace construction emphasises efficiency in earthmoving operations. When using mechanical excavators, he recommends working from the top of the slope downward, creating each terrace in sequence so that the excavated material from one terrace is used to build the embankment of the terrace below. This "cut-and-fill" approach minimises the distance that earth must be moved and reduces the total machine hours required. The excavator operator plays a critical role — an experienced operator who understands the principles of [[permaculture-water-harvesting-earthworks]] can shape terraces that closely conform to the natural contours of the land, reducing the need for subsequent manual finishing work.
Holzer cautions against over-engineering terrace structures. Unlike conventional agricultural engineering, which often specifies concrete retaining walls, engineered drainage systems, and imported fill material, Holzer's terraces rely on the natural stability of properly compacted earth embankments and the root-reinforcing effect of living plant systems. This approach dramatically reduces construction costs but requires greater skill in site assessment and construction technique. The key to stability is proper compaction of the embankment fill and the immediate establishment of a dense cover of stabilising vegetation on the riser face. When these principles are followed correctly, Holzer reports that his terraces have withstood extreme rainfall events that would have destroyed conventionally engineered structures.
For smaller-scale projects where mechanical equipment is not available, Holzer has developed techniques for terrace construction using hand tools and animal power. Stone retaining walls, built without mortar using the dry-stone technique traditional to European mountain agriculture, can stabilise terrace risers on slopes where earth embankments alone would be insufficient. The integration of [[sepp-holzer-terrace-systems-and-raised-beds]] into hand-built terraces allows even small-scale farmers to create productive growing areas on steep terrain without the need for imported soil or fertiliser.

## Costs and Economic Considerations

The economic case for terracing depends heavily on the local cost of labour and machinery. In developed countries where mechanical excavator hire is relatively affordable, the cost of constructing terraces is primarily determined by the machine hours required, which depends on the soil type, slope gradient, and the distance material must be moved. Holzer estimates that a skilled operator with a medium-sized excavator can construct approximately 50-100 linear metres of terrace per day, depending on conditions. For a typical smallholding of 1-2 hectares with moderate slopes, the total earthmoving cost is comparable to the cost of a single year's fertiliser and pesticide inputs on a conventional farm of the same size.
In developing countries where labour is inexpensive but machinery is costly, terraces can be constructed manually using hand tools. The labour investment is significant — typically 200-400 person-days per hectare for complete terrace systems — but the benefits accrue over decades. Terraces on Holzer's Krameterhof, some of which were constructed decades ago, continue to provide productive growing areas with minimal ongoing maintenance cost. The [[ultra-low-cost-jadam-microorganism-solution-and-soil-foundation]] to terrace-based farming complements Holzer's earthworks by providing ultra-low-cost pest management and soil fertility methods that eliminate the need for purchased inputs, maximising the economic return on the initial terrace investment.
The value of terraces extends beyond direct crop production. By preventing erosion, terraces preserve the soil resource that is the foundation of all agricultural productivity. On steep slopes without terraces, topsoil loss can exceed 50 tonnes per hectare per year during intense rainfall events — a rate of loss that is economically and ecologically unsustainable. Terraces reduce this loss to near zero, effectively preserving the productive capacity of the land indefinitely. When this long-term soil conservation value is factored into the economic calculation, the case for terrace investment becomes compelling even in situations where the short-term crop production benefits alone would not justify the cost.

## Traditional Terrace Systems as Holzer's Inspiration
Holzer's terrace methods draw on a deep well of traditional knowledge from terrace-building cultures around the world. The rice terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras (a UNESCO World Heritage Site), the Inca andenes of the Andes, the olive terraces of the Mediterranean, and the vineyard terraces of the Rhine and Moselle valleys all demonstrate the same fundamental principles that Holzer teaches: follow the contour, stabilise the risers, manage the water, and integrate the terraces into the broader landscape system. Holzer acknowledges these traditions and argues that modern permaculture practice should recover and adapt the best elements of traditional terrace engineering rather than reinventing them from scratch.
The key innovation that Holzer brings to traditional terrace practice is the integration of terraces with [[holzer-water-landscapes-retention]] and [[sepp-holzer-terrace-systems-and-raised-beds]]. Traditional terraces were designed primarily for erosion control and crop production; Holzer's terraces are designed as multi-functional landscape elements that simultaneously manage water, create microclimates, provide wildlife habitat, and produce food. This integration of multiple functions into a single structure is a hallmark of the [[sepp-holzer-method]] and distinguishes it from both traditional terrace agriculture and modern conventional engineering approaches.
Holzer has also innovated in the area of terrace planting design. Traditional terrace systems typically use a single crop or a simple crop rotation; Holzer advocates polyculture systems that include fruit trees, berry bushes, vegetables, herbs, and [[holzer-green-manure-crops]] growing together on the same terrace. This diverse planting design maximises the productive use of the terrace surface while also providing the root structures and organic matter inputs needed to maintain soil health and embankment stability. The result is a terrace system that is more productive, more ecologically integrated, and more self-maintaining than traditional monoculture terrace systems.
