# Terraces and Paths - Construction Guide

From "Sepp Holzer's Permaculture" by Sepp Holzer.

## Benefits of Terraces

Human beings have known the benefit of using terrace systems for a very long time. In Asia, South America, Africa and Europe people have been using terraces for thousands of years. Making steps in steep slopes helps to prevent soil erosion. Valuable humus remains on the slope instead of being washed away. Terraces store and hold moisture, increase the area available for cultivation, are more pleasant to work and are far easier to access than a steep slope.

Well-designed terraces minimise the danger of landslides and mudslides and also greatly improve the microclimates in cultivated areas.

## Design as Dual-Use Paths

I tend to design terraces so that they can be used as paths and provide access for mechanised farming equipment. By combining the two uses, I have both a terrace that is a path and a path that is a terrace. It is still possible to access the terraces while they are under cultivation, but it is important to keep to the embankments.

## Width

The width required by any machinery you are planning to use should be taken into account. It is best to make sure that the entire terrace can be cultivated or harvested in a single trip. Managing two terraces each with a width of five metres is far easier and more profitable than managing a single terrace with a width of 10 metres.

When calculating the perfect width for a terrace, it is important to consider the current gradient of the slope. The steeper the slope, the narrower the terrace should be. Particular care should be taken with fine, loamy soil on very steep slopes, because this is where there is the greatest danger of erosion.

## Gradient

The gradient should be as low as possible and no more than 15 to 20 percent. Dead ends waste time and energy and should be avoided. The gradient of the terrace embankments can be 1:1 if the soil is stony. On sandy or loam soils the best results are with gradients of around 1:1.5 to 1:2.

## Risers and Embankment Construction

To stabilise the embankments risers need to be constructed. The topsoil and the layers of earth beneath should be removed until you reach solid and stable material. The riser forms the foundation and is angled slightly into the hill. Next, the embankment should be built up replacing the excavated layers. The topsoil forms the uppermost layer.

It is important to handle the topsoil very carefully. Mistakes are frequently made when the operator of the mechanical digger does not have enough experience and fails to preserve the topsoil.

## Working with Water

In areas where there is little rain, I try to angle the terraces into the hill slightly to help retain water. When stabilising the terraces against heavy rainfall, it is important to make sure that their alignment will not channel the water.

In areas with intense summer storms, Holzer recommends installing additional drainage channels at the back of each terrace — the uphill side where water naturally collects. These channels should be lined with gravel and planted with moisture-loving species such as mint or watercress, creating a productive use for the drainage water. The combination of surface drainage and subsurface infiltration means that terraces can handle extreme rainfall events without catastrophic failure, a critical consideration in regions prone to flash flooding or prolonged monsoon rains.

## Terraces in Different Climate Zones
Holzer's terrace designs are adapted to the specific conditions of different climate zones. In Mediterranean climates with hot, dry summers and intense winter rainfall, terraces must be designed primarily for water capture and [[permaculture-earthworks-and-water-harvesting]] can shape terraces that closely conform to the natural contours of the land, reducing the need for subsequent manual finishing work.
Holzer cautions against over-engineering terrace structures. Unlike conventional agricultural engineering, which often specifies concrete retaining walls, engineered drainage systems, and imported fill material, Holzer's terraces rely on the natural stability of properly compacted earth embankments and the root-reinforcing effect of living plant systems. This approach dramatically reduces construction costs but requires greater skill in site assessment and construction technique. The key to stability is proper compaction of the embankment fill and the immediate establishment of a dense cover of stabilising vegetation on the riser face. When these principles are followed correctly, Holzer reports that his terraces have withstood extreme rainfall events that would have destroyed conventionally engineered structures.

The rice terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras (a UNESCO World Heritage Site), the Inca andenes of the Andes, the olive terraces of the Mediterranean, and the vineyard terraces of the Rhine and Moselle valleys all demonstrate the same fundamental principles that Holzer teaches: follow the contour, stabilise the risers, manage the water, and integrate the terraces into the broader landscape system. Holzer acknowledges these traditions and argues that modern permaculture practice should recover and adapt the best elements of traditional terrace engineering rather than reinventing them from scratch.
The key innovation that Holzer brings to traditional terrace practice is the integration of terraces with [[query-how-do-i-create-microclimates-in-my-garden]], provide wildlife habitat, and produce food. The result is a terrace system that is more productive, more ecologically integrated, and more self-maintaining than traditional monoculture terrace systems.
