# Entheogenic Eleusis Theory — Scholarly Reception

The scholarly response to the [Wasson-Hofmann-Ruck hypothesis that the [Eleusinian Mysteries involved the use of a psychoactive ingredient in the [kykeon.

## The Original Proposal

The entheogenic theory of the Eleusinian Mysteries was first published in 1978 as "The Road to Eleusis: Unveiling the Secret of the Mysteries," authored by [R. Gordon Wasson, Albert Hofmann, and Carl A. P. Ruck. The book proposed that the kykeon — the sacred barley potion consumed during the initiation — contained an ergot-based psychedelic compound.

Wasson, the ethnomycologist who had first identified the psychoactive mushrooms of Mesoamerica, provided the ethnographic and historical argument. Hofmann, the discoverer of LSD and an expert on ergot alkaloids, provided the chemical analysis. Ruck, a classical scholar at Boston University, provided the philological and archaeological interpretation.

## Initial Resistance from Classical Scholars

The proposal met with significant resistance from the classical studies establishment. Wasson acknowledged that some classical scholars would feel that the authors were intruding upon their territory with a hypothesis that challenged centuries of interpretation.

The resistance stemmed from several factors:

Classical scholars had traditionally interpreted the Eleusinian Mysteries as primarily symbolic, psychological, or theological phenomena. The introduction of a pharmacological element was seen as reductive and materialistic.

The secrecy of the Mysteries meant that no ancient source explicitly described the kykeon as psychoactive. The theory required reading between the lines of fragmentary and ambiguous evidence.

The association of the Mysteries with "drugs" was perceived as diminishing their spiritual and cultural significance.

## Arguments Against the Theory

Scholarly objections to the entheogenic theory have included the following:

The nature of the evidence is circumstantial. No ancient text explicitly states that the kykeon was psychoactive. The theory relies on inference from suggestive but inconclusive passages.

Ergot of barley, while theoretically capable of producing psychoactive effects, would be unpredictable in its potency and could be dangerous. The ergotism that caused "St. Anthony's Fire" in medieval Europe demonstrated the toxic potential of ergot contamination.

The Mysteries functioned successfully for nearly two thousand years. It seems unlikely that a preparation as variable and potentially dangerous as ergot-infused barley could have been used reliably over such a long period.

The ancient Greeks had access to other known psychoactive plants (notably wine) and did not need to resort to ergot for altered states.

The theory was proposed by scholars outside the classical mainstream — a mycologist, a chemist, and a relatively junior classicist — which raised questions about their command of the source material.

## Arguments Supporting the Theory

Proponents of the theory have offered several lines of supporting evidence:

The extraordinary reverence with which the Mysteries were held for two millennia demands an explanation. As Sophocles wrote, initiates were "thrice happy" compared to non-initiates, suggesting an experience of extraordinary power.

The description of the initiation experience in ancient sources — visions, encounters with divine beings, sensations of light emerging from darkness — closely parallels the phenomenology of psychedelic experiences.

The strict prohibition against revealing the nature of the Mysteries suggests that the secret involved something concrete and tangible rather than merely abstract theological concepts.

The refusal of Demeter to drink wine in favor of the kykeon symbolically elevates the barley potion above the known intoxicant, suggesting it contained something more powerful than wine.

A papyrus fragment from a comedy by Eupolis, written shortly after the profanation of the Mysteries in the fifth century BCE, confirms that the profanation involved drinking the kykeon and suggests the identification of its active ingredient. The fragment describes an informer explaining how he had caught someone who had obviously been drinking the potion since he had "barley groats on his moustache."

Hofmann's chemical analysis demonstrated that Claviceps (ergot) on barley was a plausible candidate. The water-soluble alkaloids of ergot, particularly those closely related to LSD, could produce psychoactive effects when prepared as a water-based beverage.

The preparation described in the Ruck chapter — removal of the sclerotia, mixing with water and mint — would selectively extract the water-soluble psychoactive alkaloids while leaving behind the toxic lipid-soluble ergopeptines responsible for ergotism.

## Changing Scholarly Landscape

Over the decades since its publication, the scholarly reception of the entheogenic theory has gradually become more open, though far from universally accepted.

Several factors have contributed to this shift:

The broader recognition that psychoactive substances played significant roles in many ancient religions has made the Eleusinian hypothesis seem less outlandish.

Advances in archaeobotany and archaeochemistry have opened new possibilities for detecting trace evidence of psychoactive substances at archaeological sites.

The ongoing revival of psychedelic therapy research has increased scientific understanding of the phenomenology of psychedelic experiences and their potential for producing mystical-type experiences.

## The Eupolis Fragment

One of the strongest pieces of evidence supporting the theory is the papyrus fragment from Eupolis's comedy "The Demes." The fragment, brought to Ruck's attention during the research for the book, preserves a portion of a scene in which an informer explains to a judge how he had come upon someone who had obviously been drinking the potion since he had barley groats on his moustache.

The accused had bribed the informer to say it was simply porridge and not the potion he had drunk. By a possible pun, the comedian may even indicate that the incriminating "crumbs of barley" were "purples of barley" — a reference to the purple color of ergot sclerotia.

This fragment confirms that the kykeon was understood to be psychoactive in classical Athens, and that its active ingredient was connected to barley.

## Assessment

The entheogenic theory of Eleusis remains a hypothesis rather than a proven fact. However, it provides the most parsimonious explanation for the extraordinary power and endurance of the Mysteries, and it is consistent with the available evidence from classical sources, archaeology, and chemistry.

As our understanding of ancient pharmacology and the role of psychoactive substances in human culture continues to develop, the theory continues to be refined and debated. It has stimulated productive research and has forced scholars to take the experiential dimension of ancient religion more seriously than was previously common.
