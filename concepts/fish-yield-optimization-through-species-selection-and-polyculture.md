# Fish Yield Optimization Through Species Selection and Polyculture

## Overview

Fish yield in aquaculture ponds is governed by several critical factors: species selection based on trophic level, selective breeding within species, and polyculture design that exploits complementary ecological niches. [[bill-mollison]]'s analysis, drawing heavily on Swingle (1966), demonstrates that yields can vary by orders of magnitude depending on how these factors are managed.

## Trophic Level and Species Selection

Fish positioned lower on the trophic scale — those feeding on plankton, algae, or vegetation — consistently produce higher yields per hectare than predators. In fertilized ponds, maximum production per hectare per year follows a clear hierarchy:

- **Bass** (predator) — lowest yield
- **Catfish** (omnivore) — moderate yield
- **Bluegill** (insectivore) — good yield
- **Java Tilapia** (plankton feeder) — highest yield

This ordering is not absolute, however. When pelleted food is supplied, the hierarchy can reverse: catfish may outperform bluegill, because food type and availability fundamentally alter conversion efficiency. Each trial must be conducted under the specific food management schedule proposed, as fish harvest different foods with varying efficiency.

## Selective Breeding Within Species

The variation within a species can exceed the variation between species. By stocking high densities of fingerlings (5,000–10,000 per hectare), feeding them, and then netting out only the largest individuals as brood stock after a few weeks or months, dramatic improvements are achievable:

- After only **one year of selection**, bass fingerlings from fast-growing strains converted food at **3.4:1**
- Unselected fish converted at **7.3:1** on average
- Worst cases reached **24.2:1**

This range of metabolic efficiency — a seven-fold difference between the best and worst within a single species — is greater than the differences between species themselves. In hatcheries where water supply is free of fish diseases, both selection and pathogen-free stock can be ensured through close attention to cleansing eggs and fry. Results can include doubling the number of fish growing to marketable size.

## Polyculture Design Principles

Yield increases of 40–200% are achieved through the judicious admixture of species. When designing polycultures, each species should serve one or more of the following functions:

### Rapid Nutrient Uptake

Species that breed in ponds, grow fast, and convert decomposers to food serve to fix fertilizers and convert wastes produced by dense stocking of other fish. Ideal selections include:

- **Shrimp**, especially when provided with vegetation or brush bundles for cover; also small crabs, scuds (amphipods), and molluscs
- **Minnows** or small free-breeding fish, provided with cover, screens, or shallow water refuges

Bluegill stocked alone at 3,900 per hectare yielded 186.4 kg. Bluegill with Gambusia fish yielded 3,449.4 kg — an **18-fold increase**. Swingle hypothesized that 50% of a pond devoted to cover or special habitat for small forage species would not reduce total pan fish yield, much as 30% tree shelter in fields does not reduce livestock weight yield.

Minnows have increased yields for trout, salmon, Tilapia, bass, bluegill, mullet, and other species. Prawns benefit from brine shrimp (*Artemia*) mixtures, and most fish benefit from shrimp in ponds.

### Water Quality Maintenance

Species that consume food waste — particularly **fresh or brackish-water mussels** and **plants** — serve dual functions. Mussels actively circulate water, and plants contribute to daytime oxygenation. Their yields are additive to fish yields and enable higher stocking rates:

- Fish alone: **316.6 kg**
- Fish plus mussels: **5,545 kg** (plus 864 kg of mussel meat)
- Adding plants could yield an additional 600 kg of water chestnut

Algae activates bacteria and improves oxygen content, as do higher plants.

### Predation for Stock Control

Small numbers of predators prevent over-stocking by culling excess small fish, which is essential for well-maintained growth to market size. Species used include bass, soft-shelled tortoises, snakeheads, and pike. Yield increases are modest (30–50%) but predators often fetch higher prices than prey species, so fiscal economics exceed what weight figures suggest:

- Bluegill alone: **316.6 kg**
- Bluegill plus bass: **484.4 kg**

### Utilization of Different Foods

This offers the greatest potential for yield increase. Carp ponds commonly contain the above species mixes plus 3–7 varieties of carp, all chosen for distinct food preferences:

- Common carp alone (2,500/ha): **314 kg**
- Buffalo fish alone (2,500/ha): **1,086 kg**
- Buffalo fish (2,500) plus 250 carp/ha: **925 kg** (300% increase on carp alone)
- [[channel-catfish]] alone (4,400): **1,400 kg/ha**
- Channel catfish (4,400) plus 1,250 Tilapia: **1,834 kg/ha**

In the latter case, the food conversion efficiency of catfish (1.7:1) was unaffected by Tilapia — there was no stunting of either species. Similarly, adding pangas (*Pangasius*) to ponds rich in molluscs does not affect carp or Tilapia yields. It is critical to choose fish with genuinely different food preferences for maximum polyculture yield.

## Key Principles for Practice

1. **Prefer low-trophic-level species** for baseline production — Tilapia, carp, and other plankton/detritus feeders
2. **Select aggressively within species** — even one season of size-based selection dramatically improves food conversion
3. **Design polycultures for functional complementarity** — each species should fill a distinct niche (nutrient cycling, water quality, predation control, or unique food source)
4. **Provide cover and refuge** for forage species — at least 50% of pond area can be devoted to habitat without reducing pan fish yield
5. **Maintain pathogen-free breeding stock** through egg and fry cleansing, especially in hatchery settings
6. **Test under actual management conditions** — yield hierarchies shift with food type, density, and environmental factors
