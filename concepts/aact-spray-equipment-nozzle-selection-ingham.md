# AACT Spray Equipment and Nozzle Selection

## Overview

Selecting the proper spray equipment is critical for successful [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] Compost Tea (AACT) application. The goal is to deliver living organisms to plant surfaces—both foliage and soil—without damaging them through mechanical shear, desiccation, or [[ingham-manure-antibiotics-chemical-residues-composting]]. Ingham emphasizes that the spray system must be designed to preserve the full diversity of organisms present in the tea, including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes. (see [[ingham-micronized-compost-aact-brewing-technique]]).

## Core Principles of AACT Spray Application

The living organisms in compost tea are the active ingredients. Unlike chemical sprays where the molecule is the active agent, AACT efficacy depends entirely on delivering viable, metabolically active microorganisms to the target surface. This means every component of the spray [[ingham-spray-nozzles-equipment-compost-tea]] system—from pump to nozzle to hose—must be organism-friendly.

Three factors determine spray equipment suitability for AACT:

1. **Organism survival through the pump**: Pumps that create high shear forces can shred fungal hyphae and damage bacterial cells. The pump must move tea gently enough to preserve organism integrity. (see [[query-what-are-predatory-mites-and-how-can-i-use-them-for-pest-control]]).
2. **Droplet size at the nozzle**: Droplets must be large enough to protect organisms from desiccation upon landing on the leaf surface, yet small enough to provide adequate coverage.
3. **Chemical compatibility**: All hoses, seals, tanks, and fittings must be free of chemical residues that could harm microbial life.

## Pump Selection

Transfer pumps used to move compost tea from the brewer to the spray tank must be evaluated for their effect on organism viability. Ingham recommends diaphragm pumps as generally acceptable for moving compost tea, as they create relatively low shear compared to centrifugal or piston pumps.

Key pump considerations:
- **Diaphragm pumps**: Preferred for their gentler action. Suitable for moving tea from brewer to spray tank.
- **Centrifugal pumps**: Can create significant shear forces that may damage fungal hyphae. If used, minimize impeller speed and avoid tight tolerances.
- **Piston pumps**: Generally too aggressive for fungal organisms. The compression cycle can destroy hyphal networks.
- **Peristaltic pumps**: Gentle but typically too low-volume for field-scale application.

The pump should never pull tea through a long intake line at high velocity. Keep intake lines short and wide-diameter to reduce flow velocity and shear.

## Nozzle Selection and Droplet Size

Droplet size is perhaps the most critical variable in foliar AACT application. Ingham specifies that fungal hyphae, which are essential for [[aact-banana-cultivation-tropical-crop-disease-suppression-ingham]] on leaf surfaces, measure approximately 200–400 micrometers in diameter. The spray droplets must be large enough to envelop these structures and protect them from rapid drying after contact with the leaf surface.

Recommended droplet sizes for AACT application:
- **Minimum droplet diameter**: 200 micrometers for foliar applications where fungal preservation is important.
- **Optimal range**: 300–500 micrometers provides good coverage while protecting organisms.
- **Fine mist sprays (<100 micrometers)**: Unsuitable. Droplets evaporate too quickly, leaving organisms exposed and desiccated on the leaf surface before they can establish.

Nozzle types rated for AACT:
- **Hollow cone nozzles**: Acceptable if operated at low pressure to produce larger droplets.
- **Flat fan nozzles**: Can work at reduced pressure settings. Avoid high-pressure flat fan configurations.
- **Flood nozzles**: Generally produce droplets in the acceptable range. Good for soil drench applications.
- **Rotary atomizers**: Typically produce droplets too fine for AACT. Not recommended.

Pressure management is directly linked to droplet size. Higher pressure produces finer droplets. Ingham recommends operating spray systems at the lowest pressure that still provides adequate coverage and reach. For most field applications, pressures of 20–40 PSI are sufficient and produce droplets in the acceptable range.

## Mesh Sizes and Filtration

Compost tea should never be filtered through mesh smaller than what the target organisms can pass through. Standard recommendations:
- **Maximum filtration**: 400-micrometer mesh for fungal-dominated teas. This allows fungal hyphae to pass through while removing large debris.
- **Bacterial teas**: Can be filtered through 200-micrometer mesh if needed, though filtration is generally unnecessary for soil drench applications.
- **Avoid fine filtration**: Screens below 100 micrometers will remove most fungal material and many protozoa.

If the spray system has built-in inline filters, these should be removed or replaced with appropriately sized mesh screens. Many commercial sprayer filters are designed for chemical application and are too fine for biological products.

## Sprayer Types and Capacity

Ingham provides guidance on matching sprayer capacity to the scale of application:
- **Backpack sprayers** (2–5 gallons): Suitable for small plots, home gardens, and targeted spot treatments. Ensure the pump mechanism is compatible (diaphragm-type backpacks preferred).
- **Tractor-mounted sprayers** (50–200 gallons): For field-scale operations. Pay close attention to the agitation system—gentle agitation is needed to keep organisms suspended without damaging them.
- **Pull-behind sprayers** (25–100 gallons): Good for market garden scale. Choose PTO-driven or electric diaphragm pump models.
- **[[cervantes-drip-irrigation-systems]]**: AACT can be injected into drip lines for root-zone delivery. Ensure the system does not have fine filters that would remove organisms. Emitters should be at least 400 micrometers to prevent clogging from fungal material.

## Damage to Organisms by Equipment

Ingham documents several modes of organism damage common in spray equipment:

1. **Fungal hyphae damage by impeller pumps**: High-speed impellers literally chop hyphal strands, reducing the effective fungal biomass delivered to the target surface.
2. **Compression damage in piston pumps**: The compression cycle can crush sensitive protozoa and nematode cysts.
3. **Desiccation from fine droplets**: Organisms deposited in micro-droplets dry out within minutes on the leaf surface, losing viability before they can colonize.
4. **Chemical contamination**: Residual herbicides, fungicides, or cleaning agents in sprayer tanks, hoses, or nozzles can kill or inhibit the very organisms the tea is meant to deliver.

## Pre-Application Equipment Cleaning

Before using any spray equipment for compost tea, clean the system thoroughly to remove chemical residues:
- Flush all hoses, tanks, and lines with clean water.
- Run a batch of clean water through the entire system including nozzles.
- If chemical residues are suspected, a dilute [[cervantes-hydrogen-peroxide-sterilization]] flush (3% solution) can help break down persistent residues. Flush thoroughly with clean water afterward.
- Never use chlorine-based cleaners, as chlorine residues are particularly harmful to [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]].
