## Cassava / Manioc (Manihot esculenta)
Family: EUPHORBIACEAE. Tropical shrub to 5 m producing starchy tuberous roots that are a staple food for over 500 million people. Extremely drought tolerant once established. Tubers must be processed (peeled, soaked, cooked) to remove cyanogenic glucosides. Leaves are edible when cooked and contain significant protein (up to 10% dry weight). Yields of 20-40 t/ha in good conditions. Propagation from stem cuttings -- one of the easiest crops to establish. Excellent for food security in tropical permaculture systems. Combine with [[permaculture-food-forest-design-principles]] with mulching-guide|heavy mulching.

## Yam Bean / Jicama (Pachyrhizus spp)
Family: FABACEAE. Leguminous vine producing large, crisp, sweet tubers (jicama). Unlike most legumes, the edible part is the tuber rather than the seed pod. Seeds and pods are toxic. The vine fixes nitrogen while producing food below ground -- a dual-function crop ideal for food forests. Propagation from seed.

The nitrogen-fixing ability of jicama makes it a valuable [[soil-fertility-management]] for neighbouring crops.

## Lesser-Known Tuber Crops for Permaculture

Several underutilised tuber crops deserve wider attention in permaculture systems due to their resilience, nutrition, and low maintenance requirements:

- **Mashua (Tropaeolum tuberosum)**: Andean tuber closely related to nasturtium. Produces peppery-flavoured tubers high in vitamin C and antioxidants. Extremely pest-resistant -- aphids and nematodes avoid it. Grows as a climbing vine, making it ideal for vertical stacking in food forests. Tolerates poor soils and high altitudes up to 4,000 m.
- **Oca (Oxalis tuberosa)**: Second most widely grown tuber in the Andes after potato. Tubers form in autumn as day length decreases. Acid flavour when raw, becoming mild and starchy when cooked. Sun-charging (exposing tubers to sunlight) converts bitter oxalates to sweet glucose. Hardy to moderate frosts once established.
- **Ulluco (Ullucus tuberosus)**: The most cold-tolerant of all root and tuber crops, surviving temperatures below -10°C. Brightly coloured tubers in reds, yellows, and pinks add visual appeal. Waxy texture when cooked, similar to beetroot. Leaves are also edible as a spinach substitute.
- **Apios / Groundnut (Apios americana)**: North American native leguminous vine producing chains of high-protein tubers (protein content roughly three times that of potatoes). Nitrogen-fixing while producing food -- an excellent dual-function crop. Thrives in moist, partly shaded conditions along pond edges or in food forest understories.

## Nutrient Cycling and Soil Health

Tuber crops play important roles in nutrient cycling within permaculture systems. Deep-rooted species like cassava and yam access mineral nutrients from subsoil layers unavailable to shallow-rooted crops, effectively mining nutrients and bringing them into the biological cycle through leaf fall and root decomposition. This dynamic nutrient mining is especially valuable in [[soil-fertility-management]] in polyculture arrangements. These dual-function crops reduce or eliminate the need for external nitrogen inputs in the root crop layer.

Harvesting tubers inevitably disturbs soil, so [[symbiotic-fungi-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-mechanisms]].

## Climate Adaptation Considerations

Tuber crops offer strategic advantages for climate-adaptive food systems. Many traditional tuber species are remarkably resilient to extreme conditions that would devastate grain crops. Cassava tolerates extended drought; oca and ulluco withstand freezing temperatures; taro thrives in waterlogged soils. This breadth of environmental tolerance makes tuber crops valuable for building [[faires-livestock-integration-systems]]

- Use spreading tuber crops (sweet potato, Jerusalem artichoke) as living [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]] to maintain [[permaculture-herb-plants]] as pest deterrents among tuber plantings

## Growing Tuber Crops in Polycultures

Tuber crops integrate well with tree crops through time-stacking and space-stacking strategies. Root crops can grow beneath fruit trees, utilizing the lower soil profile while trees access upper layers.
