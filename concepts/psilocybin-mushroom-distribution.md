# Psilocybin Mushroom Distribution

Global distribution of psilocybin-containing (neurotropic) mushrooms, based on the landmark 1998 study by [[gaston-guzman]], [[john-allen]], and [[jochen-gartz]] cataloguing 216 known species. See also [[mushroom-species-guide]] and [[fungal-biodiversity]].

## Overview

The 1998 worldwide survey identified 216 neurotropic fungal species across four groups:
1. **Psilocybin-containing species (186 species):** The largest group, spanning 13 genera
2. **Ibotenic acid species (3):** Amanita muscaria, A. pantherina, A. regalis
3. **Ergot fungi (7):** Claviceps (5 species) and Cordyceps (2 species)
4. **Sacred fungi without confirmed chemistry (20):** Boletes, Russula, gasteromycetes used ceremonially

## Distribution by Region

### Mexico (Center of Diversity)

Mexico has the highest diversity: **76 neurotropic species** (44 Psilocybe = 39% of world total). Species are concentrated in subtropical humid forests of Oaxaca, Puebla, and surrounding states. Indigenous peoples (Mazatec, Mixtec, Zapotec, Chinantec, Nahua) have used sacred mushrooms for centuries. Key species: P. mexicana, P. caerulescens, P. zapotecorum, P. cubensis (known locally as "san isidros"), P. muliercula.

### North America (USA/Canada)

Widespread distribution across temperate and subtropical regions:
- **Southeast/South:** P. cubensis common on cattle dung; Panaeolus subbalteatus across Southwest
- **Pacific Northwest:** P. cyanescens, P. azurescens on wood chips and dune grasses; P. stuntzii
- **Northeast/Great Lakes:** P. semilanceata in pastures; Gymnopilus species on hardwood logs
- **Gulf Coast:** P. cubensis abundant after rainy season; Inocybe aeruginascens reported

### Central and South America

- **Colombia:** P. cubensis and Panaeolus cyanescens abundant; archaeological park at San Augustin dubbed "heart of magic mushroom land" due to golden mushroom-shaped pendants
- **Peru, Brazil, Ecuador:** P. cubensis and Panaeolus species common in Amazon basin and Andes regions
- **Guatemala:** P. mexicana offered for sale by Indian children near the capital
- **Argentina, Brazil:** Active use communities documented since the 1970s
- **Caribbean:** Panaeolus cyanescens documented in Jamaica

### Europe

- **Great Britain:** P. semilanceata (Liberty Cap) most widely used; common across England, Scotland, Wales. Peak season September-October. British colloquial names: Liberty Cap, Magic Mushroom, Blue Legs, Pixie Caps.
- **Scandinavia:** P. semilanceata in Norway fjord areas; classified as "absolutely forbidden substance" in Norway (1981); usage in Finland since 1981.
- **Central Europe:** P. bohemica in Czech Republic (Brno region); P. cyanescens in the Netherlands and Germany; usage in Austria, Denmark, Sweden, Belgium, Switzerland.
- **Southern Europe:** Panaeolus species near Barcelona, Spain; P. semilanceata in Italy since ~1980.
- **France:** Prohibition against exhibiting Psilocybe species at scientific conventions; French mycologists use old name "Geophila" to avoid legal issues.
- **Eastern Germany:** Inocybe aeruginascens discovered near Potsdam (1968); Gymnopilus purpuratus found on compost heaps at Ribnitz-Damgarten (1983); Conocybe cyanopus found in Potsdam/Harz Mountains (Schaffer, ~1930)
- **Hungary:** Pluteus nigroviridis (extremely rare); Inocybe aeruginascens collected (1967)

### Asia and Pacific Islands

- **Thailand:** Largest consumption of psychoactive mushrooms; P. cubensis, P. samuiensis served in restaurants on Koh Samui and Koh Pha-ngan (omelettes, soups, teas, pizzas)
- **Cambodia/Vietnam:** P. cubensis found by Patouillard in Tonkin (1907); Panaeolus cambodginiensis endemic to Cambodia
- **Nepal:** P. cubensis and P. subcubensis widespread; large doses (40+ mushrooms) reported
- **Japan:** P. subcaerulipes, P. argentipes, P. venenata; 2,000-year mushroom cultivation tradition
- **Indonesia (Bali):** Panaeolus cyanescens omelettes served in restaurants since the 1970s; commercial cultivation on buffalo dung
- **Samoa, Philippines, Sumatra, Java:** Panaeolus cyanescens widespread
- **New Zealand:** Same species as Australia; not native, likely introduced

### Africa

- **Algeria:** Psilocybe mairei (= P. cyanescens) first collected by R. Maire (1912) at Chrea Pass near Blida; Psilocybe species found in Morocco's Central Atlas Mountains
- **Kenya:** Psilocybe cubensis suspected in Highlands; intoxications documented in Nairobi (1945, Cullinan and Henry described 22 cases)
- **South Africa:** P. natalensis discovered by Gartz, Reid, Ecker & Smith (1994) in Natal Province — first psychotropic Psilocybe found in the area; generally whitish color, no annular ring, comparable in size to P. cubensis but does not grow directly on dung
- **West Africa:** Panaeolus africanus and P. tropicales documented (Ola'h monograph)
- **Sahara (prehistoric):** Rock art at Tassili (7000-5000 BCE) depicts mushroom-using ceremonies — oldest known mushroom cult; discovered independently by Giorgio Samorini and Terence McKenna; rock drawings show anthropomorphic beings with mushroom-shaped heads, masked horned dancers holding mushrooms, and mushrooms rendered in white, ochre, and blue (possibly representing the bluing phenomenon)

### Australia

- **Subtropical east coast:** P. cubensis abundant on cattle dung in Queensland, NSW after rainy season; "gold tops" colloquial name
- **Southeast:** P. subaeruginosa in Victoria, NSW, South Australia — described by J. Cleland (1927); "gold top" colloquial name
- **Northern/Southern regions:** Panaeolus cyanescens widespread; "blue meanies" colloquial name
- **Tasmania:** Dried mushrooms sold in gelatine capsules on black market
- First Australian use documented in 1940s ("hysterical psychoses"); widespread recreational use from late 1960s

## Species-Level Detail from Gartz

### Psilocybe natalensis
Discovered in 1994 by Gartz, Reid, Ecker & Smith in Natal Province, South Africa. Generally whitish in color, lacks an annular ring, comparable in size to P. cubensis but does not grow directly on top of dung in cow pastures. First psychotropic Psilocybe species ever found in the area.

### Psilocybe samuiensis
Found on Koh Samui, Thailand, growing on a mixture of rye and horse dung. Described from Southeast Asian specimens collected during mycological surveys.

### Psilocybe mairei
Originally collected by R. Maire (1912-1926) at Chrea Pass near Blida, Algeria, growing on raw compost under cedar trees. Published as Hypholoma cyanescens nov. spec. (1928), later classified by Singer as Psilocybe mairei. Some authorities consider it identical with European P. cyanescens. Krieglsteiner also found similar specimens in Morocco's Central Atlas Mountains.

### Panaeolus cambodginiensis
Bluing mushroom described by Ola'h & Heim, found only in Cambodia. All fruiting bodies contain psilocybin. Part of the Panaeolus genus which includes several strongly psychoactive species.

### Psilocybe subcaerulipes and P. argentipes (Japan)
Bluing species found in Japan. Caused intoxications when mistaken for honey mushrooms. Led to isolation of psilocybin in crystalline form from dried fruiting bodies. Yokoyama (1973) published systematic experimentation with P. argentipes showing psychotropic effects comparable to Psilocybe species from other continents. No "urge to dance" was noted in Japanese experiments (contrary to some reports of Mexican species).

### Panaeolus africanus and P. tropicales (Africa)
Two strongly psychoactive Panaeolus species documented in Ola'h's Panaeolus monograph from the 1960s, both found in Africa.

## The Soma Hypothesis

Schroeder and Guzman proposed that "soma" — the substance revered as a deity by the ancient Aryan civilization — may have been a psychedelic Psilocybe species rather than Amanita muscaria (as initially proposed by Wasson). This hypothesis is based on the spectacular psychotropic effects and geographic distribution pattern of psilocybian species in the region. See [[entheogen-culture]] and [[mushrooms-and-mankind]] for broader discussion.

| Habitat Type | Typical Species |
|---|---|
| Cattle dung (tropical/subtropical) | P. cubensis, Panaeolus cyanescens |
| Temperate grassland/pasture | P. semilanceata, Panaeolina foenisecii |
| Wood chips/mulch (urban) | P. cyanescens, P. azurescens, P. stuntzii |
| Decomposing wood | Gymnopilus spp., Pluteus spp., P. subaeruginosa |
| Forest floor (tropical) | P. zapotecorum, P. caerulescens |
| Raw compost/garden waste | P. cyanescens (Europe), P. mairei (Africa) |
| Buffalo dung (monsoon Asia) | P. cubensis, Panaeolus cyanescens |

## Climate and Altitude

- **Tropical/subtropical (0-30 degrees latitude):** Highest diversity; species fruit year-round with peaks in rainy seasons. Altitude range: sea level to 3,000 ft in monsoon climates.
- **Temperate (30-55 degrees):** Lower diversity but P. semilanceata is extremely widespread; fruiting September-October in Northern Hemisphere. Gymnopilus species on hardwood logs.
- **Altitude effects:** In Mexico and Southeast Asia, species distributions shift with elevation; Psilocybe species found at higher elevations in tropical regions where temperatures are moderated.

## Documented Intoxication Cases

- **Nairobi, Kenya (1945):** 22 cases of hallucinatory intoxication from mistaking yellow Stropharia species for culinary mushrooms. Symptoms: emotional imbalance, fits of mirth alternating with depression, inability to sleep, nightmarish closed-eye visions. Consciousness and rational speech retained throughout.
- **Nakuru, Kenya (1949):** European couple experienced visual hallucinations, euphoria, delusions, pupil dilation, tingling extremities after eating mushrooms for lunch. Both recovered completely within six hours after stomach pumping.
- **Japan (1970s):** Systematic experiments by Yokoyama with P. argentipes — subjects experienced regurgitation, sweating, visual hallucinations, slight paralysis of extremities, depressed mood. Effects dissipated by following morning.

## Key Notes

- Mexico has 39% of world Psilocybe species despite being a small fraction of global land area
- Psilocybe cubensis is the most cosmopolitan psychoactive species, found pantropically on every continent
- P. semilanceata is the most widespread temperate species, found across Europe, North America, and parts of South America
- Many "lookalike" intoxications occur when species are confused with edible mushrooms (honey mushrooms, champignons)
- Psilocybin and psilocin degrade in dried specimens — indigenous knowledge confirms specimens over one year old lose potency
- In Bali, Panaeolus cyanescens was commercially cultivated by transferring dung with naturally-grown mycelia onto fresh buffalo manure
