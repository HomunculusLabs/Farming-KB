# Space-Time Niches and Schedules

Space-time niches are a third category of [[permaculture-design]], because
scheduling is often the most effective strategy for fitting additional species
into a system without conflict.

## Three Types of Niche

Mollison summarises niche occupation as falling into three broad categories:

- **Niche in space (territory):** An organism defends or occupies a specific
  physical area and excludes others from it.
- **Niche in time (cycles of opportunity):** An organism exploits a resource
  that is available only during a particular phase — a fruiting season, a wet
  period, a bloom window.
- **Niche in space-time (schedules):** Multiple organisms share the same space
  by using it at different times. No individual "owns" the area outright; each
  holds only a **time-space slot**.

## Human Examples: The Public Park

A familiar illustration of space-time scheduling is the daily rotation of users
through a public park. Over 24 hours the same ground hosts distinct groups:

- Early morning joggers
- Midday lunch-hour office workers
- Afternoon retired draughts (checkers) players
- Evening crowds and families
- Semi-legal, unemployed, or lonely people at night
- Lame strollers walking dogs at dawn

Each group occupies the park in its own time slot with minimal friction. When
one group departs and the next arrives, there is little fighting — conflict
arises mainly when groups **overlap** or appear outside their scheduled window.

## Mammalian Time-Slot Control

Studies of mammals confirm that many species control **time-slots in space**
rather than defending fixed territories:

- **Wallabies** exhibit two sharp activity peaks in every 24-hour cycle: a
  crepuscular peak just after sundown and an auroral peak before dawn. They
  are essentially absent from the grazing area during daylight hours.
- **Possums**, which are aggressive and nocturnal, actively displace wallabies
  from shared feeding grounds during the night-time slot, pushing wallabies
  further toward their dawn and dusk windows.
- When animals are **out of schedule** — present at the wrong time — fighting
  increases. When they vacate at their usual time, conflict ceases.

## Long-Cycle Schedules in Grazing Systems

Time-space scheduling operates at far longer scales than a single day. In
African savanna systems, large herbivore herds follow a grazing succession:

1. **Antelope** graze first, taking the tender top growth
2. **Wildebeest** follow, cropping the medium-height forage
3. **Elephant** arrive last, pushing over trees and opening the canopy

Each species modifies the pasture in ways that prepare it for the next. No
single species "owns" the land; each holds a **successional time slot**.

### Informed Grazier Practice

Perceptive graziers mimic these natural sequences in domestic livestock
rotations, scheduling one species to follow another:

- **Sheep** follow **cattle**, cleaning up the short grasses cattle leave
- **Cattle** follow **horses**, grazing the coarser material
- **Horses** follow **goats**, which have browsed the woody and weedy edges

This multi-species rotation breaks parasite cycles, makes fuller use of the
sward, and reduces the need for pasture "rest" periods between grazings.

## Within-Species vs Between-Species Scheduling

Scheduling functions at two organisational levels:

- **Within-species:** Dominant individuals claim prime grazing spots at peak
  activity times (e.g. early morning on the best pasture). Sub-dominant
  individuals are pushed to marginal areas or less favourable times. A
  hierarchy of space-time preference exists within the herd.
- **Between-species:** Different species partition the same ground by time,
  often with minimal overlap. No individual owns the area; each species
  occupies a niche defined by **when** it is there, not merely where.

## The Sports Ground Analogy

Mollison extends the park example to a sports ground:

- Cricket or football teams use the field at scheduled times
- Gulls and rodents scavenge the pitch between games
- Earthworms occupy the soil **continuously**, regardless of surface activity

This layered stacking — surface users rotating by time, subterranean occupants
holding permanent tenure — demonstrates how many niches can coexist in a single
space when schedules are respected.

## Territorial vs Cooperative Species

Species differ in how rigidly they enforce scheduling:

- **Territorial species** defend fixed boundaries and tolerate little overlap
  in time or space. Conflict is frequent and energetically costly.
- **Cooperative species** (including many herd animals and flocking birds)
  accept shared space and rely on loose scheduling conventions. The group
  gains more total access than any individual could secure alone.
