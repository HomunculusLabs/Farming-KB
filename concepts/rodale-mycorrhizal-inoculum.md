# Rodale Mycorrhizal Inoculum Production

The Rodale Institute, in partnership with the USDA, developed accessible systems for farmers and gardeners to produce their own arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal inoculum on-farm. These methods prioritize simplicity, low cost, and readily available materials over laboratory precision.

## Rodale Institute Background

Based in Pennsylvania, the Rodale Institute has been studying organic farming and gardening methods since it was established in 1947. Its founder, J. I. Rodale, was heavily influenced by the food-growing techniques of British organic gardening pioneers Eve Balfour and Albert Howard. He wanted to promote a holistic system of agriculture that offered benefits for human health and respect for the environment. In addition to publishing several magazines and books, the Rodale Institute engages in agricultural research, partnering with private groups, colleges and universities, and the USDA.

## Rodale/USDA Partnership

In 2002, the institute partnered with the USDA Agricultural Research Service to develop a system that would enable farmers to produce [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]. The system needed to be simple, to use readily available and inexpensive materials, and to produce a spore-rich inoculum that was less expensive than commercial brands.

Rodale and USDA scientists spent eight years studying and developing several ways to grow arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. In 2010, they published a description of a system they used to grow fungi that would make it more available and affordable than expensive commercial formulations. In addition, the group developed a series of recommendations with regard to standard practices that ensure the viability of the produced fungi once put to use.

## On-Farm System Design Principles

The On-Farm Arbuscular Fungus Inoculum Production System was created for farmers and designed to avoid the need for complicated equipment or expensive, hard-to-find materials. Key design principles include:

- Simplicity of process accessible to non-specialists
- Use of inexpensive, readily available materials
- Production of spore-rich inoculum at lower cost than commercial products
- Scalability from small garden to farm-scale operations

One critical finding of the Rodale/USDA study: soil transfers run the risk of introducing organisms such as root pathogens. Using a host plant that is different from the crop plant prevents the inadvertent growth of pathogens associated with the final crop.

## Grow Bag Method

The Rodale recipe produces 16 bags of inoculum — enough to make 200 or 400 cubic feet (5.66 or 11.32 cubic meters) of inoculated potting media, depending on the dilution ratio of inoculum-to-media (1:9 or 1:19). The process takes four months to complete and is started in early spring. The host plants are frost-killed and the fungal propagules overwinter in the mix right where they developed.

### Materials

- Bahiagrass (*Paspalum notatum*) seed
- Field soil (or commercial propagule mix if field soil is unavailable)
- Vermiculite
- Conical plastic pots
- Coarse-grained sand (swimming pool filter type)
- Ground cover fabric
- Black 7-gallon (26.5 liter) grow bags
- [[compost-building-guide]]

### Step-by-Step Process

1. Four months before the predicted last frost, germinate the bahiagrass in vermiculite.

2. Three months before the predicted last frost, transplant the seedlings into conical plastic pots filled with a 1:3 mixture of soil-to-sand. The conical pots provide enough space for roots to spread.

3. As soon as possible after the last frost, set up the inoculum production area by covering the ground with ground cover fabric. Then set up the grow bags. Fill the bags three-quarters full with a 1:4 mixture of compost-to-vermiculite. A 1:4 volume-based mixture of yard-clipping compost-to-vermiculite is a good start. Transplant five seedlings into each bag.

4. During the growing season, weed and water the plants in the bags as needed. Mycorrhizae will grow as the plants grow. In the winter, frost will kill the bahiagrass, and mycorrhizae will overwinter naturally outdoors in the bags.

5. The following spring, harvest the inoculum. Remove dead plant material. Shake the growing media from the root ball into a bin. This mix will contain the mycorrhizal spores and vesicle-laden hyphae. Cut the roots into short segments (less than ½ inch, or about 1 centimeter, each). Mix the inoculum into the potting media. Use a 1:9 mixture of inoculum-to-media for flats, or 1:19 mixture of inoculum-to-media for larger pots.

## Compost Mix Ratios

Rodale experimented with various mixtures in the grow bags. Pure compost, pure sand, and perlite or vermiculite all supported mycorrhizae, but these were not successful media when used alone — they were either too low in nutrients or, in the case of compost, too high, particularly in phosphorus and/or nitrogen.

Compost with high nitrogen, low phosphorus, and moderate potassium levels works best. The study referenced different composts made from yard clippings, dairy manure, and leaves. The best dilution rate with the compost varied depending on the mycorrhizal fungi used as well as the diluting media, but a 1:4 mixture of compost-to-diluting media worked best.

The 1:4 mixture of yard-clipping compost-to-vermiculite produced an average of 30 spores per cubic centimeter. The additional propagules come from the colonized roots, and the vesicles in them can develop mycorrhizae as well. This relates to the broader principles of [[mycorrhizal-inoculum-production]].

## Host Plant Alternatives

This method of developing inoculant can be adapted using smaller bags and different host plants. Suitable alternatives include:

- Rye (*Secale* spp.)
- Fescue (*Festuca* spp.)
- Corn (*Zea mays*)
- Other grasses

All will support mycorrhizae for collection of spores, propagules, and roots. With the ability to test results and identify specific fungi, you can tweak the process to specific needs.

## Greenhouse Recipe

For controlled indoor production, the Rodale/USDA team also developed a greenhouse method.

### Materials

- Plastic pots
- Host plant seeds
- Paper towels
- Plastic bags
- Sterile growing media
- Commercial inoculum
- Low-phosphorus fertilizer

### Steps

1. Sterilize plastic pots by placing them in a dishwasher with bleach-based detergent, or wash them in a solution of 1 part household bleach to 9 parts water for at least ten minutes. Then wash the pots in dish detergent and water before rinsing.

2. Soak host plant seeds for five minutes in a solution of 1 part bleach to 9 parts water (5% available chlorine). Alternatively, use 3% hydrogen peroxide heated to 140°F (60°C), cooled, then soak seed for five minutes. Rinse with sterilized water after soaking.

3. Place seeds between two damp paper towels in a plastic zipper bag to maintain sterile conditions until germination.

4. Plant seeds in 6- to 10-inch sterile pots in pathogen-free growing media, such as a 1:3 mixture of peat-to-vermiculite or peat-to-perlite. Rockwool, coarse sand, or sterilized soil also work. The media must not contain phosphorus, which prevents sufficient mycorrhizal formation.

5. Thoroughly mix commercial inoculum into the sterilized media at a 1:20 ratio (1 part inoculum to 20 parts growing media). Roll seeds in the mix or sprinkle over seeds. Plant three to six seeds per pot. Alternatively, use spores from known soil, diluted soil, transplants with established mycorrhizae, or soil from inoculated fields.

6. Feed host plants periodically with low-phosphorus fertilizer (P number of 6 or less). Examples include alfalfa meal (2-1-3), fish emulsion (5-2-2), soybean meal (7-2-1), compost, compost teas, or diluted fertilizer mixes.

7. After about six weeks, mycorrhizae will begin to form. This is a critical period. Root samples can be tested to determine colonization rates.

8. After about 14 weeks, stop watering to let the planting media dry out.

9. At around week 16, gently pull the plants out, being careful not to damage roots. Shake off media containing spores and fungal fragments, and store in a cool, dry location. Cut roots into small pieces containing vesicles and hyphae. Store in a little media in a cool, dry location. These propagules remain viable for about a year.

## Pot Culturing

Inoculant can also be produced in greenhouse conditions using various host plants. Over the years, nursery growers and farmers have had success using:

- Corn (*Zea mays*)
- Onion (*Allium cepa*)
- Strawberry (*Fragaria* spp.)
- Peanut (*Arachis hypogaea*)
- Sorghum (*Sorghum* spp.)
- Big bluestem (*Andropogon gerardii*)

Warm-weather plants can also serve as hosts, including pencilflower (*Stylosanthes* spp.), bahiagrass, and kudzu (*Pueraria phaseoloides*).

## Field Culturing

To grow mycorrhizal inoculum in field conditions, start with any commercial soil mix without added fertilizers or manures. Sterilize the commercial mix in an autoclave or oven for about an hour and a half at 260°F (121°C). After a week or two, mix 1 part field-collected soil with 3 parts sterilized soil, or use commercially available mixes of fungi instead of field soil to prevent pathogens.

The best soil and propagule collection site is a field in production with soil rich in organic material. The better the crop grown in the soil added to the mix, the more likely inoculated crops will thrive. Higher diversity of the field soil's mycorrhizae also improves results. Field soil can be tested for propagules before use.

You can also gather propagules from strawberry plants using field culture. Transplant a strawberry plant from the field to a pot filled with sterile soil, or establish a runner in potted field soil and transplant it into sterile soil. Nurture plants until mature and well established. Then remove plants and roots, save the soil clinging to the roots, and chop up the roots. The resulting products can inoculate plants started in sterile soil.

These methods are viable for other crops as well. Experimentation is encouraged.

## Ectomycorrhizal Production

[[symbiotic-fungi-ectomycorrhizal-network-carbon-trading]] are easy to produce provided you have an appropriate host plant. Several methods are available:

- **Soil transfer**: Studies show that soil containing ectomycorrhizal fungi taken from a forest at one location can successfully associate with host trees at another. One study used soils from New Zealand, India, Italy, Wisconsin, and the Alaskan tundra to establish successful mycorrhizal associations with the same tree hosts in one plot. Conifers and deciduous trees respond equally.

- **Fruiting body propagules**: Collect mushrooms of ectomycorrhizal fungi to use as propagules. The sporocarps are loaded with spores. Cut fruiting bodies into small pieces and mix them into potting soil. You don't need more than a few spores, and it is impossible to use too much. This is effective for growing a single strain or creating your own mix.

- [[mushroom-spore-printing-and-storage]]: Remove the stem and place the cap, spore side down, on paper or glass for 24 hours. Spores deposit onto the surface and can be collected for use.

To establish ectomycorrhizal colonies, add propagules directly adjacent to plant roots or spray seeds with spore suspensions.
