# Permaculture Designers Manual - Tropical Climates

Chapter 10 of the Designers' Manual covers the humid tropics, specifically excluding arid tropics (covered in the drylands chapter). The three chapters on climate-specific strategies should be read in total for any one site, as plants and techniques do not split neatly into climatic areas.

## Climatic Types

WET TROPICS: River basins and wet coasts from Latitudes 0-25 degrees (Amazon, Congo basins, Central America, Sri Lanka, Malaya, Borneo, New Guinea). About 10% of earth's surface. Temperature fluctuates little at 21-32C (70-90F). Humidity constantly high, frost unknown. Rainfall 152-328 cm (60-129 inches) with rain most days and 75-150 thunderstorms per year, usually towards evening. About 85% of nutrients are held in plants or animals; soils themselves are infertile if clear-cultivated.

WET-DRY TROPICS: Poleward of wet tropics, about 15% of earth's surface (Campos, Llanos, Gran Chaco of South America, parts of Central America, Hawaii). No frosts. Temperatures 21-27C wet season, 32-38C dry season. Rainfall 25-152 cm (10-60 inches), erratic towards desert margins. Contains vast savannah grasslands with thorn-bush and flat-topped Acacia trees. Soils generally more fertile and alkaline than wet tropics.

MONSOON TROPICS: Sub-type of wet-dry tropics influenced by continental land masses (Indo-Thailand region, northern Australia, India). About 8-10% of world land surface but large human populations. Late summer heating causes onshore sea winds and heavy rains (about 60% of rain falls in summer). Rain erratic, 102-1016 cm (40-400 inches). Soils lateritic, often very hard in dry season.

## Tropical Soils

Special problems: except in areas of recent vulcanism (Indonesia), soils are old, deeply leached, with silica and calcium in low supply. Once cleared, humic particles leach out to about 30% of prior levels, and infertility appears.

Strategies to restore soil nutrient-holding capacity:

- Restore humus with green crop, especially perennials such as Leucaena and tree legumes
- Add small quantities of superphosphate at frequent intervals before leaching occurs
- Add fine crushed basalt, cement powder, and shredded bamboo or cane mulches for silica and calcium
- Increase pH with lime after trees and green crop are growing well
- Give fertiliser as light spread all year at 6-week intervals
- Substitute perennial for annual crop; never practice frequent cultivation

In the long term, rely on tree and ground legumes to maintain soil health. Destructive approaches combining forest clearing, bare-soil cropping, and careless water run-off management create desolate baked clays out of once-rich tropical forests.

Silicon is an important part of cell walls in many grasses and bamboo, essential in soil cation exchange capacity of deep, red, heavily-leached tropical soils. Palms, bamboo, and Acacia species provide silica-rich mulch for tropical gardens.

## House Design

Optimum comfort is at dry-bulb temperatures of 20C (68F) in still air (winter) and 25C (77F) in summer. Above 40% relative humidity, effectively add 1C for every 4% increase in humidity. High humidity prevents cooling by sweating, so design must shade, cool surfaces, and above all induce cool air currents.

### Human Comfort Strategies

- SHADE: Light and heat both excluded as incoming radiation in shade. Critical on massive walls and water tanks near homes
- TRANSPIRATION: Plants assist cooling by transpiring; partial shade helps understory species
- COOL BODIES: Large heat reservoirs as water tanks and shaded stone, concrete, mud brick absorb heat from air
- AIR FLOWS: Even low air flows from shaded areas greatly aid cooling
- REMOVING HEAT SOURCES: Cook-stoves and hot-water systems best placed in semi-detached outdoor kitchens

The best cooling systems use a hot roof or metal chimney to draw cold air from earth-cooled underground tunnels or pipes (1 m deep, 15-20 m long trench, self-draining to lower slope).

### Equatorial House Essentials

- Orientation to prevailing winds, not to the sun
- Site sheltered from hurricanes, tsunami, and vulcanism
- Walls white or reflective, overshaded by wide eaves and palms
- Heat sources detached from main structure (outdoor kitchens)
- Wall-material light, even permeable to wind (woven matting and mosquito screens)
- Vertical louvres and window shutters for cross-ventilation
- In hurricane areas: strong central core, deep ground anchors, strapped timbers; large bamboo groves to windward bend without breaking

## The Tropical Home Garden

Seasons: The wet season is the "hungry gap" where plants are growing but too young to harvest. Early in wet season, soil is soft enough to plant and establish trees, but timing is critical as the dry season is long-lasting. Water storages are essential for garden, tree crop, and diversity in yield. Use resistant plant varieties or root-stocks against moulds, mildews, and root fungi.

Key species: Mango, papaya, sapote, banana, limes, coconut, cashew, macadamia nut, breadfruit, avocado, pineapple, durian as the garden and orchard framework. Large legumes (Inga, Gliricidia, Leucaena, Cajanus) are essential interplants. In the vegetable garden, yam and sweet potato yield better than potato. Amaranth is a good green and grain crop.

Structural species: Bamboos, balsa, teak, palms, mahogany. Rattans along waterways. Oil palm, jelly palm, Bactris, salak palm provide useful fruits. A scatter of Banksia, Casuarina with mycelial associates fixes phosphate and returns it via leaf mould.

Domestic animals: Pigeons and bees elevated on pole structures or over shallow ponds. Guinea fowl, francolin, pheasant, and bantams provide foraging and insect control. Guinea-pig aids small tree establishment. Small pigs of Taiwanese strains provide orchard scavenging. Waterfowl and aquatic species add yields to water storages.

Hurricane damage limited by raising large earth banks, selecting valley garden sites, screening with bamboo groves, establishing general tree canopy through garden, or combination of strategies.

## Design Essentials Summary

Wet tropics: hygienic faeces disposal; clean water sources; integrated insect control; gradual replacement of ground crops by trees; preservation of natural tree stands; development of river traffic; evolution of natural products; no-dig (mulch) techniques on root crops; domestic foragers for snail and insect pests; appropriate medicinal plants.

Wet-dry tropics: small domestic water storage; hedgerow against winds; in-crop tree legumes (Acacia albida); improved stock management; mulch use of grasses; increased tree crop of high forage value; decreased fire frequency; no-tillage grain techniques; low bunds for water retention; keyline flood control; soakage pits and swales across slopes; manures in gardens; domestic fuelwood systems near villages.
