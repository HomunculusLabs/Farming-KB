## Social Hierarchy Patterns and Tribal Art in Service of Life

## Universal World Beliefs and Their Degradation

Many world belief systems share an essential core rooted in nature observation and pattern understanding. However, Mollison identifies a historical drift from nature-based, universal systems toward personalized humanoid gods, dogma, fanaticism, and symbols without meaning or practical relevance.

This degradation follows a pattern: indigenous knowledge systems are destroyed less for monetary gain than for the replacement of public, accessible information by exotic, secretive, centralized belief systems. The fanatic cares not what is destroyed if it empowers the repressive hierarchy imposed afterward. Most tribal art has been burnt, looted, destroyed, and broken by invading belief systems — by those seeking secret power rather than open knowledge, or by the merely destructive.

Book-burning and image-breaking is the reaction of the alienated or intellectually deprived to the accumulated wisdom of their revelationary ancestors. We most damage ourselves when we destroy information and aids to understanding.

## Patterns of Society: Occupational Hierarchy

Mollison and his students studied social behavior patterns in Tasmania from 1969 to 1974, surveying approximately 35,000 people across organizations including the local army, a multinational company, churches, and small businesses. They identified a characteristic "boat" pattern representing occupational hierarchy:

### The Four Strata

**1. Low Occupational (Resource) Area — Manual and Unskilled Urban:**
- General dearth of material resources and low status
- Part-time occupations predominate
- Remarkable preponderance of male births and surviving male children (~140 males per 100 females)
- Large families with serial polyandry common or acceptable

**2. Central/Most Populated Level — The "Middle Class":**
- Adequate resources with nine-to-five jobs and some job tenure
- "Normal" birth ratio of 104 males per 100 females
- Mixed white-collar and skilled technical workers
- Average family sizes; monogamy as ideal, often expressed as serial monogamy

**3. Upper Levels:**
- Few people with extensive resources
- Flexible, often self-set working times
- High proportion of female children (~100 females to 70 males or less)
- Urban professionals or managers typify the group
- Small families; effective polygyny via concubines or mistresses

**4. Very High Levels:**
- Executive directors and landed nobility
- Variable family sizes with preponderance of female children
- Habit of lateral intermarriage for economic alliances, facilitated by exclusive schools and resorts

### Genetic Streaming Between Classes

The imbalance of sex ratios across strata ensures **genetic turnover or diffusion** between classes over generations. The excess males at the bottom stream upward through marriage; the excess females at the top create scarcity that draws from lower levels. This genetic streaming between occupational levels is a natural regulatory mechanism maintaining social cohesion over time.

The hierarchy seldom exceeds **seven major occupational levels**, even in rigorously stratified organizations like the military. The width of each level in the pattern represents population numbers, creating the characteristic boat shape — wide at the base (many workers), narrow at the top (few executives).

## Tribal Art as Functional Mnemonic

### Art"s Primary Purpose: Information Storage

Tribal art in the forms of song, dance, sculptural or painted objects, and designs served quite specific ends. Most (if not all) tribal art was a **public and ever-renewed mnemonic** — a memory aid for the community. Apparently simple spiral or linear designs could combine thousands of bits of information in a single, deceptively simple pattern.

The decorative function was **incidental** to the educational and sacred information function. "Decoration" is the trivial aspect of such art.

### Modern Art"s Divergence

Much modern art is individualistic and decorative; some "motif" art is plagiarized from ancient origins but no longer carries educational or sacred function. Entertainment and decoration are valid but minor functions. Social comment in theatre and song, and spirited dances that uplift, represent meaningful modern applications, but Mollison laments the monoculture of his own society — based on advertising jingles and purely decorative patterns, with education divorced from relevant long-term observations of the natural world.
