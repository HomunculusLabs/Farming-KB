# Wasted Human Resources and Traditional Knowledge

The ingenuity and skill of traditional, mostly rural people — predominantly in poorer countries — represents one of the most underutilised resources on the planet. [[david-holmgren]] argues that the global economy systematically devalues these skills, destroys people's capacity to apply knowledge productively, and at best treats them as uneducated factory fodder. This devaluation occurs at the precise historical moment when this knowledge base represents humanity's greatest asset in the transition to reduced energy availability.

## The Loss of Traditional Skills

The majority of people on the planet still have some personal or family experience of a culture of place and living from renewable local resources. Most are struggling to climb aboard the train of industrial affluence, primarily through migration from rural areas to cities. In the process, monetary incomes rise, but people lose access to unmeasured wealth and must discard their most useful technical skills and social values.

This is not a romanticisation of rural poverty. In some ways, rural poor are like the aerospace engineer at the other end of the scale — unprepared by their traditions to understand energy descent and carrying unrealistic expectations. But to classify these people as the most destitute and incapable on the planet is fundamentally wrong. They represent a vast pool of human resource and capability being debased and denigrated when it is most needed.

## Why Traditional Knowledge Is Being Lost

The devaluation of traditional knowledge occurs through a complex of interlocking factors:

- **Systemic breakdown**: Useful knowledge is bound within traditional integrated systems that no longer work as they once did due to overpopulation, war, resource degradation, or social change
- **Generational disconnect**: The knowledge is only understood by old people, sometimes in a language no longer spoken by the young
- **Cultural denigration**: Advertising and government propaganda promote modern methods and denigrate local knowledge
- **Aspirational pressure**: Keepers of traditional knowledge perceive that the rest of the world is affluent and uses modern methods

## Permaculture Principles in Development

Many progressive development projects by small NGOs working with the world's poor consciously or unconsciously apply [[permaculture-principles]]:

1. **Acknowledging and supporting existing local systems** that provide for people's needs, maintain cultural knowledge, and foster husbandry of natural resources
2. **Identifying undervalued or wasted local resources** — both human and natural
3. **Introducing limited new elements** — species, tools, and materials found useful in similar-scale traditional systems that can be maintained using local skills and resources

One of the most important side-effects of these projects is the re-evaluation of traditional knowledge that was being dismissed as old-fashioned and ineffective.

## The Reversal of Expertise

[[bill-mollison]] made the provocative suggestion that we need to get those with knowledge of traditional sustainable systems to come to the West to teach us how to feed ourselves. This emphasises that conservation and reinvigoration of traditional sustainable systems is not charity, but a path to survival for the global community.

Holmgren recounts presenting a slide show about Melliodora (his [[permaculture]] property) to a Bedouin community in the Negev desert in 1994. At the first slide of their mud brick house, an old man gestured excitedly and said, "Look, a Bedouin house!" — because earth was a traditional local building material. The status of a foreign expert living in an earth house encouraged younger men to see the value in traditional building materials over industrial ones.

## The Wineglass Model and Real Wealth

The wineglass model of global economic activity shows a staggering imbalance of measured economic wealth between rich and poor nations. However, if the value of free environmental services were added, the disparity would not be so extreme. Poor rural people often still have access to relatively more unmeasured goods and services through subsistence agriculture, hunting, and gathering — though these sources are diminishing rapidly.

EMERGY evaluations provide a more realistic measure of real wealth:

| Country | Per person EMERGY use |
|---------|----------------------|
| India | 1 |
| World average | 6 |
| Brazil | 15 |
| USA | 29 |
| Australia | 59 |

*(× 10¹⁵ solar emjoules/year)*

The surprisingly high EMERGY per person for Brazil and the extraordinary figure for Australia reflect the very high free services of nature, as well as mining of non-renewable resources and, in Australia's case, low population density.

## Self-Reliance as the Most Powerful Action

For people from rich countries not directly involved in development projects, the most powerful thing they can do is engage in the same process of self-reliance in their own homes and communities. With greater self-reliance, we:

- **Reduce demand** for corporate-controlled exploitative export development of Third World resources
- **Increase the status** of self-reliance in the emerging global culture
- **Free up capital** to flow into underdeveloped countries, gradually correcting the mismatch between overdeveloped and underdeveloped economies

Self-help development projects and fair trade cooperatives that allow people to maintain traditional skills while gaining modest incomes are the natural complement to greater self-reliance and disconnection from the corporate global economy in rich countries.

## "The Problem Is the Solution" Applied to Pests

The excessive surpluses of pest plants and animals represent a special opportunity. The slogan "the problem is the solution" challenges us to find creative ways to use what is otherwise regarded as worthless. Commercial harvesting of pest carp from Australian inland rivers for fertiliser is on the right track, but higher-value uses such as poultry feed should displace fertiliser use. Eventually, human consumption will be the natural and best control on carp in Australia.

Carp themselves are a response to inland waterways overloaded with algal growth, stimulated by excessive nutrients from farm fertilisers and town sewage. Beyond recognising our role in contributing to nature's plagues, learning to accept plant and animal plagues as opportunities to creatively provide for our needs while restoring greater balance epitomises our harmonious place in nature.

## Practical Applications

Theoretical understanding translates to practice through careful
observation, systematic experimentation, and iterative
improvement. Documenting results builds personal knowledge and
enables sharing successful approaches with others.
## Historical Context

Traditional knowledge systems worldwide have developed
sophisticated approaches over centuries. Modern science often
validates what indigenous and traditional practitioners have
long understood about ecological relationships and management.
## Future Directions

Emerging research continues to expand understanding of
complex interactions within biological systems. Climate change
adaptation strategies are increasingly important. Technology
and traditional knowledge combined offer promising pathways.
