# Savannah Forestry Rehabilitation

Savannah forestry is the systematic restoration of degraded tropical grasslands—often caused by overgrazing, fire, and slash-and-burn practices—back into productive forest systems. Wherever native forests have been ruined, particularly in the wet-dry tropics, closed grassland species of fire-prone and tough grasses develop, closing out tree seedbeds and preventing good management practices. Further degradation can result in depauperate grasslands with low stock carrying capacity, bare and eroded acidic soils (pH 4–4.5), and persistent barriers to reforestation.

## The Degradation Cycle

The typical pathway to savannah degradation follows a predictable pattern:

1. **Overgrazing** removes ground cover and compacts soil
2. **Fire or cut-and-burn forestry** eliminates tree regeneration
3. **Tough grassland species** colonize and dominate, forming near-impenetrable barriers
4. **Blady grass** (*[[imperata-cylindrica]]*) and other tropical forage grasses establish stubborn, persistent stands
5. **Soil quality declines** — nutrient states drop, acidity increases, erosion accelerates

Despite their toughness, these grasses actually provide good mulch material when cut or mowed, which becomes a key asset in the rehabilitation process. Tropical grasses scythed or mown 5–8 times annually make good mulch for trees and gardens.

## Pioneer Legume Species for Rehabilitation

Given rains of 60–150 cm, a set of rough pioneer legumes are available for rehabilitating exhausted sites, including mine spoils and road embankments. Direct seeding in scratch holes or chiselled strips will result in fast establishment. Providing enough seed can be obtained, multiple species will establish simultaneously. These species restore soil fertility through leaf-drop and root nodulation:

### Key Pioneer Trees

- **Acacia auriculiformis** — The most important pioneer for exhausted savannah and tropical soils. Defeats blady grass (*Imperata cylindrica*), restores fertility, provides firewood, acts as a nurse crop, reduces fire intensity, and produces good paper pulp. Coppices and self-seeds. Widely used as a shade and street tree in the tropics.
- **Acacia mangium** — Similar characteristics to *A. auriculiformis* but straight-stemmed, making it better suited to forestry operations.
- **[[sesbania-grandiflora]]** — Exceptionally fast-growing tropical pioneer reaching 10 m. Coppices readily, provides good forage, green manure in rice paddies, and firewood. Young leaves, pods, and flowers are edible (36% crude protein; seeds are 40% protein). Wide soil tolerance, extensively used for eroded hill sites. All food from this tree should be cooked. Frost and wind tender, lifespan about 20 years. Used as light shade crop and vine support.
- **Calliandra colothyrsus** — A stick wood coppicing species that defeats grasses, provides abundant firewood, and repairs exhausted soils.
- **Dalbergia sissoo** — Salt and frost tolerant, fast growing, defeats grasses. Tolerates a wide range of soil types and can be quickset from large cuttings (India).
- **Enterolobium cyclocarpum** — Durable timber tree with large pods, defeats grasses (Central America).
- **Mimosa scabrella** — Subtropical pioneer from Brazil, provides good humus and serves as a living fence.
- **Samanea saman** (rain tree) — Very fast-growing large tree of the tropics and subtropics with sugary pods. Grass grows well below. Wood is valuable and durable.

### Nitrogen Dynamics

The zones of nitrogen intensity around a tree legume extend significantly beyond the canopy. Nitrogen will diffuse from the site for up to 6 years after a tree is cut out, so the effects last long after the life of the tree itself. Intensity of nitrogen concentration falls in the outer root zones, creating a gradient that benefits understory plantings and companion crops.

## The Savannah Forestry Sequence

The rehabilitation process follows a structured progression from initial site preparation through to permanent productive systems.

### Phase 1: Site Preparation
- Choose a land-forming system such as swaling, interception banks, or pitting
- Establish dams for irrigation above good soil types
- Cut benches or roads on contour every 250–100 m as aids to regenerative forest processes and subsequent harvesting
- Road borders can be of dense, evergreen, wide-crowned trees for track shading and stability

### Phase 2: Pioneer Establishment
- Sow pioneer grasses such as molasses grass or *Sorghum almum* mixed with sawdust into slashed weeds
- Or drill selected grass and legumes after slashing
- Plant a mixture of leguminous trees along swales through the area to be grassed at 30–100 metre strip spacing
- Allow 2–3 years for trees to grow with light grazing to year 3
- It is better to plant small assemblies than to space out a lot of species on their own

### Phase 3: Canopy Closure and Grass Suppression
- The canopies of *Acacia auriculiformis* or *A. mangium* shade out and kill the blady grass
- Fire intensity is progressively reduced and eventually eliminated
- Deep shade keeps fences clear of grasses and weed crop
- These pioneer legumes serve simultaneously as nurse crop, firewood source, pulp timber, mulch, and honey source

### Phase 4: Advanced Species Introduction
- High-value timber such as rosewood, mahogany, and ebony can be introduced in lines or clearings within the first crop
- Species like *Pterocarpus indicus* or *P. erinaceus* can be seed-planted in a nursery stand, then coppiced for 2 m quickset planting in bore-holes in the field
- Some species can be set out at 10 cm diameter and make good timber trees

### Phase 5: Gradation to Permanent Systems
- The system graduates to either high-value forestry or sensible strip cultivation on a sustainable basis
- If *Leucaena*, *Samanea*, *Prosopis*, and *Inga* are planted, a long-term forage system will evolve
- Replanting or rest periods must be given for seedlings to re-establish

## Managing Perennial Grass Swards

Establishing perennial grass swards on weedy or eroded areas is a one to three year process. About 15–18 fields are necessary for rotational grazing. On irrigated areas, some strip grazing is possible using electric fences. On rocky knolls, leguminous tree pioneers followed by kikuyu sward may succeed. Early furrows of banna grass provide erosion and wind control at 30 m spacing until tree legumes establish.

## Overcoming Barriers to Adoption

The only thing preventing or delaying savannah forestry is a lack of tree nurseries and seed sources of appropriate species. This itself presents an opportunity for a small enterprise in the humid tropics. A very good selection of potential species can be found in the National Academy of Sciences publication on tropical leguminous trees referenced in the source material.

## Integration with Forage Systems

The rehabilitated savannah can support multiple productive uses simultaneously:

- **Mixed grazing systems** — Cattle and sheep on improved pastures beneath scattered tree legumes
- **Forage tree systems** — *Leucaena*, *Samanea*, and *Prosopis* providing pods, foliage, and food sugars
- **Long-term forestry** — High-value timber interplanted within pioneer nurse crops
- **Beekeeping** — Pioneer legumes serve as excellent honey sources during the rehabilitation phase
- **Perennial forage** — A sequence of species or a species polyculture planted to take best advantage of seasonal forages

## Pioneering on Difficult Terrain

On man-made and natural landslide or volcanic areas of the tropics, it is first necessary to pocket the area with soil-mulch mixtures (nut husks from coconut and macadamia are excellent) to establish any pioneer species. Species such as *Inga edulis*, *Leucaena leucocephala*, various *Acacias* (*A. mearnsii*), *Scalesia pedunculata*, *Prosopis pallida*, and legumes (*Dolichos*, *Desmodium*) will prepare the area for palms, figs, and useful fruit trees by providing shelter and mulch.

## Condensation and Moisture Harvesting

In extensive hill areas of gorse or blady grass, the benches or roads cut on contour serve additional functions beyond access and harvesting. The saturated winds that sweep off tropic seas carry a heavy moisture load which is available as dew on grasslands, but is much more effectively trapped on the myriad leaf surfaces of an uneven canopy of trees. Hence the importance of maintaining forested slopes of sea-facing mountains in tropic tradewind areas.

Even small garden tree patches "rain" softly on clear nights when a sea-wind is blowing, letting down drips in a steady stream to swell dried-out leaves and channel down leaf midribs to the fibrous trunks of palms and tree-ferns. Once plants are cleared, effective precipitation falls, rivers cease to flow, and the land becomes truly dry. A ragged or spiky forest canopy, where palms and tall pines or fruit trees lift above the general canopy layer, will ensure more condensation from sea air than will a level and relatively closed canopy.

Near the sea and on islands, the night air condenses on the sea-facing side of stones, which have a richer moss-algae-lichen flora than the inland side. Pockets of vegetation act in a similar manner to condense sea vapours for their use. Thus, islands and sea coasts will have dry and wet sides suited to different plant species — an important consideration for species selection in savannah rehabilitation near coastal areas.

## Climate and Rainfall Considerations

Savannah forestry approaches vary with rainfall gradients:

- **60–90 cm rainfall** — Focus on hardy pioneers (*Acacia*, *Prosopis*), drought-resistant grasses (makarikari), and careful water harvesting
- **90–150 cm rainfall** — Full species palette available; pioneer legumes establish readily; transition to high-value forestry faster
- **Above 150 cm rainfall** — Rapid establishment possible; concern shifts to species selection for market value and biodiversity

The transition zone between wet and dry tropics is where savannah problems are most acute and where forestry rehabilitation has the greatest impact. In these areas, fire management becomes critical — the shading effect of established tree canopies is the most reliable long-term fire suppression strategy.
