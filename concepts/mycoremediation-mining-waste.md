# Mycoremediation of Mining Waste and Acid Mine Drainage
Mining activities generate large quantities of waste materials and contaminated water that pose significant environmental challenges. [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]] offers potential biological approaches for the treatment of mining wastes, including the remediation of acid mine drainage, heavy metal contamination, and tailings stabilization. Fungal approaches to mine waste remediation exploit the remarkable metal tolerance and biosorption capacity of certain fungal species to capture, concentrate, and sometimes transform toxic metals.




## Acid Mine Drainage Treatment
Acid mine drainage (AMD) occurs when sulfide minerals (primarily pyrite, FeS2) in mine tailings or exposed rock are oxidized by air and water, producing sulfuric acid and dissolved iron. This acidic runoff mobilizes heavy metals (aluminum, manganese, zinc, copper, lead, arsenic) from surrounding rock, creating extremely toxic water that kills aquatic life and contaminates drinking water supplies. Conventional AMD treatment uses chemical neutralization (adding lime) and settling ponds, which is expensive and produces large volumes of toxic sludge. Fungal treatment approaches include sulfate-reducing fungi that can precipitate metals as sulfides, and fungal mats that filter and biosorb metals from drainage water. The [[mycoremediation-techniques|mycofiltration]] approach is particularly promising for passive AMD treatment systems.




## Fungal Biosorption of Mine Metals
Many fungal species demonstrate remarkable capacity to absorb heavy metals from solution. Species of Aspergillus, Penicillium, Rhizopus, Mucor, and Trametes have been extensively studied for metal biosorption. The fungal cell wall provides abundant binding sites through chitin, glucans, proteins, and melanin. Live and dead fungal biomass can both be effective — dead biomass avoids toxicity concerns and can be regenerated through desorption. [[singh-fungal-biosorption-heavy-metals]] documents biosorption capacities for multiple fungal species across different metals. The [[fungal-biosorption-mechanisms]] involve ion exchange, complexation, coordination, and adsorption processes.




## Tailings Stabilization and Revegetation
Mine tailings — the crushed rock remaining after ore extraction — are often unstable, barren of organic matter, and contaminated with metals. Establishing vegetation on tailings is essential for long-term stabilization (preventing erosion and dust), but plants struggle in the sterile, toxic substrate. Mycorrhizal fungi can be inoculated onto tailings to support plant establishment by improving nutrient uptake, providing protection from metal toxicity, and helping build soil structure. [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-in-tropical-forest-restoration|AMF restoration techniques]] adapted from forestry applications show promise for tailings revegetation. The combination of organic amendments (compost, biochar) with mycorrhizal inoculation significantly improves plant survival and growth on mine wastes.




## Biomineralization and Metal Recovery
Some fungi can transform dissolved metals into insoluble mineral forms through biomineralization — precipitating metals as oxalates, carbonates, or phosphates. Oxalic acid produced by fungi can leach and immobilize metals simultaneously. The fungus can precipitate copper as copper oxalate, or uranium as uranium phosphate minerals. This biomineralization approach has potential not only for remediation but for metal recovery — the precipitated minerals can sometimes be processed to recover valuable metals. The [[bioremediation-fungal-metal-metalloid-transformations|metal transformation]] research explores these biogeochemical pathways in detail.




## Case Studies and Field Applications
Field applications of fungal mine waste treatment have shown mixed but promising results. Passive treatment systems using fungal inoculation in constructed wetlands have successfully reduced metal concentrations in AMD at several sites. [[battelle-diesel-mycoremediation-study|Battelle's mycoremediation research]] demonstrated fungal degradation of petroleum hydrocarbons in contaminated mine soil. Challenges include maintaining viable fungal populations in harsh mine environments, ensuring treatment performance across seasonal variations, and scaling laboratory successes to field conditions. Long-term monitoring is essential to confirm that fungal treatment systems provide sustained remediation rather than temporary containment.
