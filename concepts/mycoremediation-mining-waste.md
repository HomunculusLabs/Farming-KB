# Mycoremediation of Mining Waste and Acid Mine Drainage

Mining activities generate large quantities of waste materials and contaminated water that pose significant environmental challenges. [[cotter-mycoremediation-overview]] offers potential biological approaches for the treatment of mining wastes, including the remediation of acid mine drainage (AMD), the recovery of heavy metals from mine tailings, and the stabilization of contaminated mine sites. Fungi contribute to mining waste remediation through both [[mycorrhizal-heavy-metal-uptake]] of heavy metals and [[staycare-fungal-degradation-of-synthetic-dyes]] of organic co-contaminants.

## Acid Mine Drainage

Acid mine drainage is one of the most significant environmental problems associated with mining activities. When sulfide-bearing minerals are exposed to air and water during mining, they produce sulfuric acid and dissolved metals. Release of industrial wastewater due to acid mine drainage over large areas is a major environmental problem.

Fungi can contribute to AMD treatment through several mechanisms. The fungal biomass can adsorb and concentrate dissolved metals from AMD through [[mycorrhizal-heavy-metal-uptake]] processes. Fungi can also raise the pH of acidic water through metabolic activities, precipitating metals as hydroxides or sulfides. The [[fungal-heavy-metal-uptake-and-translocation]] in AMD environments involve complex processes of biosorption, bioaccumulation, and biotransformation.

## Metal Contamination at Mining Sites

In the United States, 389 of 703 National Priority List sites contain toxic metal contaminants. At least 100,000 such sites are estimated in the European Union. Government agencies have developed metal discharge standards in a number of countries to regulate releases and contaminants in wastewaters entering waterways and sewerage systems. Coal-fired power plants also produce large quantities of metal releases at several points during combustion, further contributing to environmental metal contamination.

Industries including electroplating, electronic circuit production, steel and nonferrous processes, and chemical and pharmaceutical manufacturing discharge metal-laden wastewaters. Mining operations are among the most significant sources of metal contamination, releasing a wide variety of metals including copper, zinc, lead, cadmium, arsenic, and iron into the environment.

## Fungal Biosorption for Mining Waste

Fungal biosorption, the fungal-specific process of metal biosorption, is particularly relevant for mining waste treatment. A wide variety of living and dead fungal biomass is capable of sequestering toxic metals from mine waste streams. Dead fungal biomass offers significant advantages because it does not depend on requirements for growth, metabolic energy, or transport, and is unaffected by metal toxicity.

Fungal biomass can be generated as a waste by-product of large-scale industrial fermentation and is pretreated by washing with acids and/or bases before final drying and granulation. This approach converts an industrial waste product into a valuable biosorbent for mining waste treatment, representing an economically attractive circular economy strategy.

## Mechanisms of Metal Removal

The biological removal of metals from mining waste solutions can be divided into three categories: biosorption of metal ions on the surface of fungi, intracellular uptake of metal ions, and chemical transformation of metal ions by fungi. Living fungal biomass is required for the last two categories. Biosorption involves ion exchange, chelation, adsorption, crystallization, and precipitation, followed by ion entrapment in inter- and intrafibrillar capillaries and spaces of the polysaccharide material.

Cell walls of fungi are composed of chitins, chitosans, and glucans, and also contain proteins, lipids, and other polysaccharides. These cell wall components provide numerous functional groups for metal binding, including carboxyl, sulfate, phosphate, hydroxyl, amino, imino, sulfonate, imidazole, sulfhydryl, carbonyl, and thioether moieties. The biomass usually contains a larger number and variety of functional groups than monofunctional ion-exchange resins.

## Biosorption Isotherms and Performance

The Langmuir and Freundlich equations are commonly employed in evaluating biosorption isotherms for metal removal from mining waste streams. The Langmuir model is applicable to monolayer sorption onto a surface with a finite number of identical sites, while the empirical Freundlich model is based on sorption on a heterogeneous surface. Other biosorption isotherms include the Scatchard-Langmuir, Redlich-Peterson, Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET), and Dubinin-Radushkevich models.

Rapid uptake of metals by fungal biosorbents is desirable for mining waste treatment applications. External factors affecting biosorption include the type of metal, ionic form in solution, and the functional site. pH, temperature, biomass concentration, type of biomass preparation, initial metal ion concentration and metal characteristics, and concentration of interfering ions are also important in evaluating the extent of biosorption.

## Marine Fungi in Metal Accumulation

Marine fungi have been found to accumulate heavy metals, which may have applications for mining waste in coastal environments. The marine fungi Corollospora lacera and Monodictys pelagica have been found to accumulate lead and cadmium extracellularly in mycelia. Aquatic fungi are also known to accumulate heavy metals, suggesting potential applications in water-based mining waste treatment systems.

## Mining-Industry Yeast Isolates

Different yeasts isolated from the gold mining industry in Brazil have demonstrated the ability to bioaccumulate metals from mining waste streams. During growth of Aspergillus niger on gold mining industry substrates, significant metal removal was observed. These findings suggest that mining environments themselves can be sources of fungi already adapted to high metal concentrations, which may be particularly effective for bioremediation applications at similar sites.

## Phytomining and Mycorrhizal Approaches

[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] play a role in rhizosphere remediation at mining and metal-contaminated sites. [[singh-mycorrhizal-fungi-in-heavy-metal-phytostabilization]] can enhance plant tolerance to heavy metals and facilitate the phytoextraction of metals from contaminated soil. This synergistic approach combining plants and fungi may offer sustainable solutions for the rehabilitation of mine tailings and contaminated land.

Fungi can play a key role in determining the fate of metals in soil environments through [[fungal-heavy-metal-interactions]] with metal species. The movement of the fungal sheath in mycorrhizal associations can play an important role in determining metal bioavailability and distribution in contaminated soils, further supporting integrated fungal-plant remediation strategies.

## Comparison with Conventional Treatment

Conventional treatment methods for mining waste include chemical precipitation, ion exchange, reverse osmosis, and electrodialysis. These methods are often expensive and generate secondary waste streams. Fungal biosorption offers a potentially more economical and environmentally friendly alternative, particularly for low-concentration metal waste streams where conventional methods are not cost-effective.

Conservative estimates of new biosorbents in the North America environmental market amount to $27 million per year. The use of fungal biosorbents derived from industrial fermentation waste could further reduce costs while addressing waste management objectives simultaneously. The [[cost-effectiveness-fungal-remediation]] approaches continues to drive research in this area.

## Challenges and Future Directions

Despite the promise of fungal biosorption for mining waste treatment, several challenges remain. Scale-up from laboratory to field applications requires optimization of biomass production, pretreatment, and reactor design. The selectivity of fungal biosorbents for specific metals may be limited in complex multi-metal waste streams. Desorption and recovery of metals from loaded biosorbents, along with regeneration of the biosorbent material, are important for economic viability.

Future research directions include genetic engineering of fungal strains with enhanced metal-binding capacities, development of composite biosorbent materials combining fungal biomass with other sorbents, and integration of fungal biosorption with other treatment technologies in comprehensive mining waste management systems. The [[fungal-bioaugmentation]] of contaminated sites with adapted fungal strains represents another promising avenue for in situ remediation.

## Integrated Remediation Strategies

The most effective approaches to mining waste remediation often combine fungal biosorption with other biological and physical treatment methods. biostimulation-techniques|Biostimulation of native fungal communities through nutrient amendment can enhance metal immobilization at contaminated sites. Combining fungal treatment with bioaugmentation-strategies|bioaugmentation using metal-resistant fungal strains can further improve remediation outcomes.

The treatment of mining waste often requires a multi-stage approach that addresses both the aqueous and solid phases of contamination. Fungal biosorption can effectively treat metal-laden water, while solid-phase treatments such as landfarming or biopile techniques address contaminated soils and sediments. The integration of these approaches within a comprehensive treatment train maximizes the overall remediation efficiency.
