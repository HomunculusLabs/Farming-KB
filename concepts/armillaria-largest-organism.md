# Armillaria - The Largest Living Organism

## Overview

*Armillaria* is a genus of [[basidiomycota-classification-major-clades|basidiomycete]] fungi that includes
some of the largest and oldest living organisms on Earth. These wood-decay
fungi form massive subterranean mycelial networks that spread through forest
soils, invading tree roots and feeding on organic matter for thousands of years.

Individual *Armillaria* mycelia have been discovered covering hundreds of acres
and weighing as much as a blue whale, making them contenders for the title of
the world's largest organism by mass. The genus represents one of the most
important groups of wood-decay fungi in forest ecosystems worldwide.

## Mycelial Architecture

### Hyphal Structure

Like all fungi in Kingdom Fungi, *Armillaria* grows as networks of filamentous
cells called **hyphae**. These are eukaryotic cells with chromosomes housed in
nuclei, rich in ribosomes, mitochondria, and an internal cytoskeleton of protein
strands. The cytoplasm is contained by a plasma membrane and sheathed in a
sugar-based polysaccharide cell wall that allows the cell to become pressurized
(turgid).

Fungal hyphae are not hollow tubes but are filled with pressurized cytoplasm
that behaves like a water-filled balloon. This turgor pressure, generated by the
osmotic uptake of water, is essential for hyphal extension and for maintaining
the structural rigidity of the mycelium. Without internal pressure, hyphae
collapse into flaccid threads.

*Armillaria* hyphae are septate, meaning they are partitioned by cross-walls
(septa) into interconnected compartments. Each septum is perforated with a pore
that allows cytoplasm to shuttle between compartments. Close to the extending
tips, cytoplasm flows actively from one compartment to the next. Older
compartments show less motion and are eventually sealed when their septal pores
are plugged.

### Growth Pattern

Fungi grow by extending the tips of their hyphae and by branching to initiate
new hyphae. The tip of each hypha is a site of intense metabolic activity where
new wall material is continuously synthesized and the cell pushes forward. The
resulting network, or **mycelium**, is the feeding phase of the organism.

When food is plentiful, the mycelium adopts a doughnut or torus shape, spreading
outward from its point of origin and leaving an ever-widening circle of dead
and dying cells at its core. This distinctive growth pattern is responsible for
creating **fairy rings** in pastures and lawns.

## Record-Breaking Individuals

### The Michigan Giant

A large *Armillaria* mycelium was discovered in the United States with an outer
rim that spread itself over 15 hectares (37 acres) and equaled the mass of a
blue whale. Because different fungi can mingle in the same area, tracking
individual mycelia requires collecting and genetically analyzing mushrooms,
which duplicate the genetic makeup of their soil-bound parents.

The genetic fingerprinting technique allows researchers to distinguish one
individual from another in the tangled subterranean world. Without this tool,
the full extent of *Armillaria* clones would remain invisible.

### The Oregon Monster

More recently, a monster *Armillaria* was found in the Blue Mountains of
eastern Oregon covering more than 2,200 acres (approximately 890 hectares). From
the size of the mycelium and its current rate of expansion, the age of the
Oregon fungus is estimated to be between 2,400 and 7,200 years.

*Armillaria* currently holds the title of World's Largest Organism, surpassing
its nearest competitor, the quaking aspen (*Populus tremuloides*). While aspen
groves can also be immense clonal colonies spreading from single trees, with
some forest patches potentially 10,000 years old, *Armillaria* outclasses them
in terms of living mass because dead wood accounts for much of the bulk of an
aspen grove.

The fungal mycelium, by contrast, is entirely living tissue, making its
enormous mass all the more remarkable. If one were to assign a single identity
to the Oregon specimen, it would be the oldest and heaviest living thing on
the planet.

## Rhizomorphs: Fungal Cables

*Armillaria* produces specialized structures called **rhizomorphs**, which are
rope-like aggregations of hyphae that function as root-like organs. These dark,
shoelace-like structures are among the most distinctive features of *Armillaria*
and play a crucial role in its ability to spread through forests as a pathogen.

Rhizomorphs allow the fungus to:

- Travel through soil from one food source to another over distances of
  several meters
- Invade healthy tree roots by direct penetration through the bark
- Transport water and nutrients over considerable distances within the mycelial
  network
- Survive periods of food scarcity by maintaining dormant mycelium in a
  desiccation-resistant state

Rhizomorphs represent an advanced form of hyphal organization that few other
fungi achieve. Their internal structure is more complex than simple bundles of
hyphae, with differentiated cell types arranged to maximize both structural
integrity and transport efficiency.

## Forest Pathology

*Armillaria* species are among the most important root rot pathogens of trees
worldwide. They attack a vast range of woody hosts and can kill trees directly
or predispose them to attack by other pathogens and insects.

The fungus can persist in stumps and roots for decades after a tree dies,
serving as a source of infection for newly planted trees. In managed forests,
*Armillaria* root rot is a significant economic concern.

The fungus spreads from infected stumps to adjacent healthy trees through root
contact or rhizomorph growth, gradually killing the stand. Control strategies
include stump removal, chemical treatments, and the use of resistant tree
species. In some forests, the removal of stumps after logging has reduced
*Armillaria* losses by up to 50 percent.
