# Cohoba Snuff

Cohoba is the Taíno name for a psychoactive snuff prepared from the
seeds of [[anadenanthera-peregrina]], a tree native to the Caribbean and
northern South America. The use of cohoba was one of the most
important entheogenic practices of the indigenous peoples of the West
Indies at the time of European contact, and the custom is believed to
have been imported to the Caribbean from the Orinoco region of South
America. The snuff contains powerful tryptamine alkaloids,
principally bufotenine, that produce intense visionary experiences.

## Historical Background

When Columbus arrived in the Caribbean in 1492, the Taíno people of
Hispaniola and other islands were actively practicing the ritual use
of cohoba snuff. Early Spanish observers documented the practice
extensively, noting that the indigenous priests (behiques) inhaled
the powdered seeds through special tubes to enter visionary states
for healing, divination, and communication with the spirits.

The custom was widespread throughout the Antilles and was also
practiced by indigenous groups in the Orinoco basin of South America,
from whom the Caribbean peoples likely inherited the practice. The
snuff was prepared from the toasted, pulverized seeds of the cohoba
tree, which were mixed with lime or ashes derived from certain shells
to facilitate the release of the active alkaloids.

Friar Ramon Pane, who accompanied Columbus on his second voyage,
provided one of the earliest detailed descriptions of the cohoba
ceremony. He reported that the behiques (shamans) would inhale the
snuff through bifurcated tubes, entering a trance state during which
they communicated with the cemis (spirits or gods) and received
messages for the community.

## Botanical Source

Cohoba is prepared from Anadenanthera peregrina (also known as
Piptadenia peregrina), a leguminous tree of the family Fabaceae. The
tree produces characteristic bean-like pods containing numerous small
seeds that are rich in psychoactive tryptamine alkaloids. A related
species, [[anadenanthera-colubrina]], provides the seeds for the South
American snuffs known as cebil, villca, and huilca. See
[[anadenanthera-entheogens]] for comprehensive coverage of the genus,
and [[yopo-snuff]] for the South American counterpart of cohoba.

## Preparation

The traditional preparation of cohoba snuff involves a careful
multi-step process that reflects an empirical understanding of the
chemistry involved:

1. Harvesting the ripe seed pods from the Anadenanthera tree
2. Toasting or roasting the seeds over a fire to develop the psychoac
  tive properties
3. Grinding the toasted seeds to a fine powder using a mortar and pes
  tle
4. Mixing the powder with calcined lime or shell ash (a source of cal
  cium hydroxide)
5. The resulting snuff is stored in ornate containers, often carved f
  rom wood, bone, or ceramic

The addition of lime serves a critical chemical purpose: it converts
the free-base tryptamine alkaloids into a form more readily absorbed
through the nasal mucosa. This technique parallels the preparation of
other South American snuffs like [[yopo-snuff]] and demonstrates a
sophisticated traditional understanding of bioavailability.

## Administration and Ceremonial Context

The cohoba snuff was administered through a special apparatus
consisting of a pair of Y-shaped tubes (known as a tabaco or tabanu).
The snuff was placed in one end and inhaled through the other, often
with the assistance of an attendant who blew the powder into the
nostrils of the initiate. The tubes were often elaborately carved and
decorated, reflecting the sacred status of the practice.

The ceremonial context of cohoba use was deeply integrated into
Taíno religion. The behiques (shamans or priests) used the snuff to:

- Communicate with the spirit world (cemis), the Taíno gods and ances
  tral spirits
- Diagnose and treat illness by entering a diagnostic visionary state
- Practice divination and prophecy on behalf of the community
- Seek guidance from ancestors for important decisions
- Conduct rituals of protection, healing, and communal welfare

The cohoba ceremony was typically conducted in the bohio (ceremonial
house) and was accompanied by chanting, music, dancing, and other
ritual elements. The ceremony served as a bridge between the human
and spirit worlds, enabling the behique to act as an intermediary for
the community.

## Chemistry

The psychoactive constituents of cohoba seeds are tryptamine
alkaloids, principally:

- **Bufotenine (5-hydroxy-DMT)**: The major active compound, a seroton
  in analog
- **DMT (N,N-dimethyltryptamine)**: Present in some varieties, adding 
  to the visionary intensity
- **5-MeO-DMT**: Found in some seed samples, contributing to the overa
  ll effects
- **Bufotenine oxide**: A related compound that may be pharmacological
  ly active

The snuff is no longer used in the West Indies, as the indigenous
Taíno populations were decimated following European colonization.
However, the practice continues among indigenous groups in the
Orinoco basin, where the snuff is known as yopo or niopo. See
[[yopo-snuff]] for the continuing South American tradition.

## Archaeological Evidence

Archaeological excavations throughout the Caribbean have uncovered
elaborate snuff paraphernalia, including carved snuff tubes, snuff
tablets, and ornamental containers made from wood, bone, ceramic, and
shell. These artifacts provide evidence of the antiquity and cultural
importance of the cohoba practice. The iconography of the Taino
people frequently depicts the cohoba ritual, with figures shown
inhaling through Y-shaped tubes while in trance states.
