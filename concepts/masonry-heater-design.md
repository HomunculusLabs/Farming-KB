# Masonry Heater Design

A masonry heater (also called a masonry stove, kachelofen, or Russian stove) is a high-efficiency wood-burning heating appliance that stores heat in a large [[comparison-windbreak-vs-thermal-mass]] of brick, stone, or [[cob-building-basics-detailed|cob]]. Unlike conventional wood stoves that burn continuously and radiate heat directly, a masonry heater burns a hot, fast fire and stores the heat in its mass, releasing it slowly over 12-24 hours. A single intense fire in the morning can heat a home all day.

## How Masonry Heaters Work

### The Burn Cycle

1. A hot, fast fire is built in the firebox
2. Wood burns at high temperature (1000-1500F) with excess air
3. Hot gases travel through a system of internal channels (baffles)
4. Heat is absorbed by the masonry mass at every turn
5. By the time gases exit the chimney, they are cool (250-350F)
6. The masonry mass radiates stored heat into the room for 12-24 hours

### Why Efficiency Is So High

- Complete combustion: High temperatures and excess air ensure wood burns completely, producing minimal creosote, smoke, and particulates
- Heat extraction: Internal channels force gases to contact maximum masonry surface area
- [[coleman-soil-heat-mass-passive-thermal-storage]]: Heat that would go up the chimney in a conventional stove is captured in mass
- Overall efficiency: 80-90% (vs. 40-60% for conventional wood stoves)

## Types of Masonry Heaters

### Contraflow Heater (Most Common Design)

Developed in Finland, the contraflow design is the most popular for modern masonry heaters.

- Hot gases rise from the firebox to the top of the heater
- Flow downward through side channels
- Exit at the bottom through a chimney connection
- The "contra" (against) flow means the gas path reverses direction at the top
- Produces even surface temperatures on all sides of the heater

### Downdraft Heater

- Hot gases rise from the firebox
- Are forced downward by a restricted throat at the top
- Flow down through internal channels
- Exit at the bottom
- Simpler internal geometry than contraflow

### Bell Heater

- Hot gases rise from the firebox into a large chamber (bell)
- Cool gases settle to the bottom of the bell and exit
- No internal channels — the bell itself is the heat exchange chamber
- Simpler to build but less efficient at heat extraction
- Can be stacked — multiple bells increase efficiency

### Russian Stove (Traditional)

- Large horizontal masonry mass
- Gases travel through a labyrinth of horizontal channels
- Very large thermal mass — can weigh 10,000+ lbs
- Takes 2-3 days to fully heat up
- Historically used as a sleeping platform on top

### Kachelofen (German Tile Stove)

- Masonry heater faced with decorative ceramic tiles
- Tiles have air channels behind them that enhance heat radiation
- Often highly decorated and considered art pieces
- Very common in Central Europe

## Design Considerations

### Sizing

Masonry heaters are sized by the square footage they need to heat:

| Heated Area | Heater Weight | Firebox Volume |
|-------------|--------------|----------------|
| 500-800 sq ft | 3,000-5,000 lbs | 1.5-2.0 cu ft |
| 800-1,200 sq ft | 5,000-8,000 lbs | 2.0-3.0 cu ft |
| 1,200-2,000 sq ft | 8,000-14,000 lbs | 3.0-4.5 cu ft |
| 2,000+ sq ft | 14,000+ lbs | 4.5+ cu ft |

### Firebox Design

- Firebox floor area: approximately 1 sq ft per 500 sq ft of heated space
- Firebox depth: 16-24 inches (for standard 16-18 inch firewood lengths)
- Firebox height: 12-18 inches
- Load door: 8x12 inches minimum for convenient loading
- Firebrick lining: Required to withstand temperatures over 2000F
- Insulating firebrick: Optional inner layer for even higher temperatures

### Baffle/Channel Design

- Channel cross-section: 4x6 inches to 6x8 inches
- Minimum 30-50 feet of total channel length for adequate heat extraction
- Smooth interior surfaces reduce friction and improve draft
- Cleanout doors at the bottom of every channel for ash removal

### Chimney Requirements

- Minimum 15 feet of chimney height above the heater
- Interior chimney preferred (stays warmer, better draft)
- 6-8 inch flue diameter for most residential heaters
- Stainless steel liner required for safety
- Chase outside the building requires insulated chimney pipe

## Materials

### Core Materials

- Firebrick (refractory brick): Lines the firebox and high-temperature areas
- Insulating firebrick (IFB): Optional inner layer in the firebox
- Regular brick or stone: Outer shell and mass
- Refractory mortar: For firebox construction (withstands 3000F)
- Type S or N mortar: For outer masonry

### Sealing and Gaskets

- Ceramic fiber rope gaskets around doors
- High-temperature silicone for joints
- All joints must be airtight to prevent smoke leakage

## Building a Masonry Heater

### Foundation Requirements

- Weight: 5,000-14,000+ lbs requires a reinforced concrete pad
- Minimum 8-inch reinforced concrete slab on compacted gravel
- Foundation must be independent of the building foundation (separate footings)
- Floor framing must be engineered to support the weight

### Construction Steps

1. Design: Create detailed plans with firebox dimensions, channel layout, and chimney path
2. Foundation: Pour reinforced concrete pad
3. Base course: Lay first courses of brick, establishing the footprint
4. Firebox: Build firebox with firebrick and refractory mortar
5. Baffles: Build internal channels or bell chambers
