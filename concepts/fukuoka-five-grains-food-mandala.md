# Fukuoka Five Grains and Food Mandala

In his discussion of the [[fukuoka-japanese-diet-worse]], [[natural-farming-fukuoka]] celebrates the traditional food culture built around the "five grains" and introduces his concept of a "food mandala" -- a comprehensive map of seasonal eating that once sustained Japanese farming communities.

## The Five Grains

Fukuoka identifies the traditional Japanese staple foods:

- Rice, barley, foxtail millet, proso millet, and barnyard millet -- known collectively as the "five grains"
- These served as the basic dietary principle in the traditional Japanese farming community
- Today, "little of these five grains is grown anymore, except for rice and barley"
- The minor grains have been "all but forgotten" due to their small seeds and low yields
- Production shifted almost exclusively to rice and barley

Fukuoka insists that the primitive, small-seed crops are best for human health:

- "It is the primitive, small-seed crops closest to nature, such as the millets, the azuki bean, and buckwheat, which are best for human health"
- "Energy is more highly condensed in smaller things" -- he would "venture to say that it is probably better to go with what is smaller than bigger"
- The modern preference for large, tasty fruits and grains represents a departure from natural food principles

## The Food Mandala

Fukuoka describes his vision of complete local food self-sufficiency:

- By adding together the grains, vegetables, fruits, and edible wild herbs that can be gathered from the fields and hills about his [[fukuoka-village-self-sufficient-community]], one can create a "food mandala of the four seasons"
- This mandala represents the full spectrum of natural foods available in each season
- The farmers of old were said to be poor, "eating only rice cooked with barley, pickled vegetables, and the sour pickled plum (umeboshi), but in a sense their's was a marvelous diet"
- No imported or out-of-season supermarket selection could create "a true feast" to compare with the seasonal abundance of the Japanese countryside

## Western Root Vegetable Deficiency

Fukuoka was surprised by the poverty of Western vegetable consumption:

- Europeans and Americans eat almost no root vegetables
- "About the only root vegetables that Europeans and Americans seem to eat in any quantity are carrots"
- Burdock grows wild in the West "but no one eats it"
- Japanese soldiers who fed Americans burdock at prisoner-of-war camps were accused of war crimes for "forcing prisoners to eat 'tree roots'"
- Westerners grow potatoes and some tubers, "but these are very hard and not what I would consider edible fare"
- "No one eats taros, no one eats burdock, no one eats lotus root or bamboo shoots"

## Root Vegetables and Health

Fukuoka argues that root vegetables are essential for health:

- "The deeper roots penetrate into the soil, the more primitive they are and the better for the body"
- "Yams ought to be the best"
- Eating root vegetables "good for the body and vegetables high in fiber such as burdock, sweet potatoes, and bamboo shoots, one will never be constipated"
- The absence of root vegetables in Western diets contributes to widespread constipation
- He composed a humorous verse: "Sweet potato turds, left behind, make fit and strong the touring pilgrim"

## Sweet Potatoes in Europe

Fukuoka actively promoted sweet potato cultivation during his European travels:

- He found that sweet potatoes "could be grown quite easily" in Europe
- "Almost no one seemed to know about this vegetable"
- He began a talk to a group of Italian farmer's wives by describing his "special high-yield method of sweet potato cultivation"
- The sweet potatoes common to Japan are "delicious, high-yielding" compared to those occasionally seen in Europe
- "There does not seem to be any other country with such a rich variety of foods for cooking as Japan"

## Rice and Wheat as Calorie Crops

Fukuoka provides a global analysis of staple grains:

- Rice, barley, and wheat are the best crops for calorie production per acreage
- Corn and potatoes also have high calorie output in colder climates
- "Roughly half of the world's population uses wheat as its food staple, while the other half uses rice"
- Nomadic peoples of cold northern countries hunted and ate meat to compensate for grain shortages
- Wheat spread first among nomadic peoples because it could be preserved and carried
- In warm, humid subtropics, upland rice was grown in hilly locations and paddy rice on flat land

His fundamental principle: "The question to ask is not what to grow here, but what grows here."

# Nature's Food Mandala and Seasonal Eating

From [[fukuoka-the-one-straw-revolution-final-chapter]] (1978), Book IV.

## The Food Mandala

Fukuoka drew circular diagrams (mandalas) to explain the natural diet that transcends science and philosophy. The first mandala brings together the foods people can most easily obtain, arranged in groups. The second shows foods as they are available during the various months of the year. From this mandala, the sources of food provided on the earth are shown to be nearly limitless.

Fukuoka's thinking on natural food is the same as on natural farming: natural diet is a way of eating in which foods gathered in the wild or crops grown through [[fukuoka-natural-farming-philosophy-of-mu]], and fish caught by natural methods, are acquired without intentional action through the non-discriminating mind.

Even though he speaks of non-intentional action and non-method, wisdom acquired over time in the course of daily life is acknowledged. The use of salt and fire in cooking could be criticized as the first step in the separation of man from nature, but it is simply natural wisdom as apprehended by primitive people, and should be sanctioned as wisdom bestowed by heaven.

Crops evolved over thousands of years by dwelling together with human beings are naturally occurring foods. But instantly altered varieties developed by agricultural science that has drawn far away from nature fall outside that category. Farming, fishing, animal raising, the everyday realities of food, clothing, shelter, spiritual life -- everything must form a union with nature.

The fishermen and farmers in a Japanese village have no particular interest in the logic of these diagrams. They follow nature's prescription by selecting seasonal foods from their immediate area.

## The Year-Round Cycle

### Early Spring

From early spring, the seven herbs sprout from the earth, and the farmer can taste seven flavors. Along with these come pond snails, sea clams, and turban shellfish.

### The Season of Green (March)

Horsetail, bracken, mugwort, osmund, and other mountain plants appear. Young leaves of persimmon and peach trees and sprouts of mountain yams make delicious tempura and seasonings. At the seashore, kelp, nori, and rockweed are abundant during the spring months.

### Bamboo Shoot Season

Grey rock cod, sea bream, and striped pig fish are at their most delicious. The iris blossom season is celebrated with slender ribbonfish and mackerel sashimi. Green peas, snow peas, lima beans, and fava beans are eaten right from the pod or boiled with brown rice, wheat, or barley.

### End of the Rainy Season

Japanese plums are salted. Strawberries and raspberries are gathered. The body desires scallions with watery fruits such as loquats, apricots, and peaches. Loquat seeds can be ground into "coffee," and the leaves brewed as tea are among the finest medicines. Mature leaves of peach and persimmon trees produce a tonic for longevity.

### Midsummer

Under the bright sun, eating melons and licking honey in the shade of a big tree is a favourite pastime. Summer vegetables -- carrot, spinach, radish, cucumber -- ripen. The body needs vegetable or sesame oil to hold off summer sloth. Barley noodles of various sizes and shapes are prepared. Buckwheat grain, an ancient wild plant, is harvested in summer.

### Early Fall

Soybeans and small red azuki beans, many fruits and vegetables, and yellow grains ripen together. Millet cakes are enjoyed at the autumn moon viewing. Parboiled soybeans are served with taro potatoes. Maize, rice steamed with red beans, matsutake mushrooms, and chestnuts follow. The rice that absorbed the sun's rays all summer ripens in the fall, providing calories for the cold winter.

### First Frost

Deep-water blue fish such as yellowtail and tuna can be caught. It is interesting that the Japanese radish and the leafy vegetables abundant during this season go well with these fish.

### New Year

Cooking is prepared from food pickled and salted for the celebration: salted salmon, herring eggs, red sea bream, lobster, kelp, and black beans have been served every year at this feast for many centuries.

### Winter

Radishes and turnips left in the ground, covered with a blanket of soil and snow. Grains and beans grown during the year, miso and soy sauce, are staples. Cabbages, radishes, squash, and sweet potatoes stored in the autumn. Leeks and wild scallions pair with oysters and sea cucumbers.
