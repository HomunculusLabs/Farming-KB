# Geomycology and Mineral Transformations

Geomycology is the study of fungal interactions with minerals and geological
substrates — encompassing the roles fungi play in mineral weathering,
biomineralization, element solubilization and immobilization, and the
transformation of [[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]] hyphal tip extension can wedge apart mineral
grains and expand fractures, increasing surface area for chemical
attack.

### Extracellular Enzymatic Action
Fungi produce [[fungal-oxalate-secretion-and-mineral-dissolution]] leads to precipitation of calcium oxalate
crystals (whewellite and weddellite) in and around mycelium. These
crystals can persist long after the fungus dies, contributing to soil
calcium pools and affecting soil structure. Calcium oxalate accounts
for significant carbon sequestration in some soils.

### Carbonate Precipitation
Some fungi induce calcium carbonate precipitation through metabolic
processes that raise local pH or concentrate calcium ions. This process
contributes to the formation of calcareous soil horizons, cave
speleothems, and cemented soil layers (calciche).

### Metal Oxidation and Reduction
Fungi can oxidize manganese (II) to manganese (III/IV) oxides, reduce
iron (III) to iron (II), and transform other redox-sensitive metals.
These transformations affect metal bioavailability and toxicity in soil.

## Metal Interactions

Fungi interact with metals through several mechanisms:

### Biosorption
Fungal cell walls, rich in chitin, glucans, and proteins, bind metal
cations through ion exchange, complexation, and electrostatic
interactions. Dead fungal biomass can be even more effective at metal
binding than living mycelium, making fungal waste products potentially
useful for bioremediation.

### Bioaccumulation
Active transport systems can concentrate metals inside fungal cells to
levels far exceeding environmental concentrations. Some fungi
hyperaccumulate specific metals (cadmium, lead, uranium, cesium).

### Volatilization
Certain fungi can transform metals into volatile forms — for example,
methylation of arsenic, selenium, and mercury — affecting their
environmental mobility and toxicity.

### Extracellular Precipitation
Fungi can precipitate metals as insoluble oxides, sulfides, phosphates,
or carbonates, effectively immobilizing them in soil.

## Phosphorus Solubilization

Phosphorus is often the limiting nutrient in terrestrial ecosystems, yet
most soil phosphorus is locked in insoluble mineral forms. Fungi are among
the most effective biological agents for phosphorus solubilization:
- Organic acid secretion dissolves rock phosphate and iron/aluminum
  phosphates
- Phosphatase enzymes release phosphate from organic compounds
- Mycorrhizal fungi extend plant access to soil phosphorus beyond the
  root depletion zone

## Ecological Significance

Geomycological processes are fundamental to soil development, nutrient
cycling, and ecosystem functioning:
- Soil formation from rock: Fungi are primary agents of biological
  weathering that converts rock into soil over geological time scales
- Nutrient availability: Mineral transformations control the release of
  plant-essential elements from insoluble forms
- Carbon sequestration: Fungal biomineralization contributes to long-term
  carbon storage through oxalate and carbonate precipitation
- Metal detoxification: Fungal immobilization of toxic metals reduces their
  bioavailability and protects soil food webs
- Soil structure: Fungal filaments and exopolymers bind soil particles,
  maintaining aggregation and water-stability

## Bioremediation Applications

Geomycological knowledge has practical applications in environmental
remediation: fungal biofilters for metal-contaminated water, mycoremediation
of polluted soils, fungal-based biosorbents for industrial waste treatment,
and bioleaching of valuable metals from low-grade ores.
