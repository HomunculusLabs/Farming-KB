# Phytomining

Phytomining (also called agromining) is an agricultural technology that uses hyperaccumulator plants to extract valuable metals from low-grade ore bodies, mineralized soils, or anthropogenically contaminated substrates. The harvested plant biomass, enriched with target metals, is then processed to recover the metal content for commercial use. Phytomining represents a novel intersection of phytoremediation and extractive metallurgy, offering a sustainable alternative to conventional mining for certain metal resources.

## Principles of Phytomining

The concept of phytomining exploits the natural ability of certain plant species, called hyperaccumulators, to concentrate metals in their above-ground tissues at concentrations far exceeding normal plant levels. While most plants contain trace amounts of metals (typically 1 to 100 mg/kg dry weight), hyperaccumulators can concentrate specific metals to levels exceeding 1,000 mg/kg (0.1% dry weight), with some species reaching concentrations of 1% to 5% for certain metals.

Phytomining operates on the same fundamental principles as phytoextraction, a mechanism of [[phytoremediation-mechanisms]]. However, while phytoextraction aims to remove contaminants for environmental cleanup, phytomining targets metal recovery for economic value. The two goals often overlap, as many contaminated sites contain economically significant concentrations of metals such as nickel, cobalt, thallium, and gold.

## Target Metals and Hyperaccumulator Species

The economic viability of phytomining depends on the market value of the target metal and the ability of hyperaccumulator plants to concentrate it from the growth substrate. Research and pilot-scale operations have focused on several metals.

### Nickel Phytomining

Nickel is the most commercially advanced phytomining target. Over 500 nickel hyperaccumulator species have been identified worldwide, predominantly in the Brassicaceae (mustard), Euphorbiaceae (spurge), Flacourtiaceae, and Buxaceae (boxwood) families. Many of these species are endemic to ultramafic (serpentine) soils, which are naturally enriched in nickel, chromium, cobalt, and magnesium.

Alyssum species (especially A. murale and A. corsicum) are among the most studied nickel hyperaccumulators for phytomining applications. These species can accumulate nickel to concentrations exceeding 2% of their above-ground dry biomass when grown on ultramafic soils. Phaerosperma oxyphylla from New Caledonia can accumulate up to 4% nickel.

Pilot-scale nickel phytomining trials have been conducted in Albania, New Caledonia, Spain, and the United States using Alyssum species. These trials have demonstrated nickel concentrations in biomass ash of 10% to 20%, comparable to or exceeding the grade of many conventional nickel ores.

### Thallium Phytomining

Thallium is a rare and valuable metal used in electronics, medical imaging, and specialized alloys. The plant Biscutella laevigata has been identified as a thallium hyperaccumulator, concentrating thallium to levels exceeding 1% in leaves when growing on thallium-contaminated soils near mining and smelting operations.

Thallium phytomining is particularly attractive because thallium is toxic at very low concentrations, and phytomining can simultaneously remediate contaminated soil while recovering a valuable byproduct. The high market price of thallium makes even relatively low biomass concentrations economically interesting.

### Gold and Precious Metal Phytomining

Gold phytomining is more challenging because gold is largely insoluble and therefore not readily available for plant uptake. However, research has demonstrated that applying chemical amendments such as thiocyanate or cyanide to gold-bearing substrates can solubilize gold ions, which are then taken up by certain plant species.

Brassica juncea (Indian mustard) has been shown to accumulate gold at concentrations up to 57 mg/kg dry weight when grown on cyanide-amended gold ore tailings. While these concentrations are far lower than for nickel, the very high market price of gold means that even modest accumulation rates could be economically viable at large scale.

### Cobalt Phytomining

Cobalt is a critical metal for battery technology and superalloys. Several nickel hyperaccumulator species also accumulate significant cobalt, particularly when grown on cobalt-rich ultramafic soils. Species of Haumaniastrum from the Democratic Republic of Congo can accumulate cobalt to concentrations exceeding 1% dry weight, making them promising candidates for cobalt phytomining operations in Africa.

## The Phytomining Process

A complete phytomining operation involves several sequential stages from site selection through metal recovery.

### Site Selection and Characterization

Suitable sites for phytomining include naturally metal-enriched soils (ultramafic soils, mineralized outcrops), mine tailings and waste rock dumps, smelter-contaminated soils, and urban or industrial sites with metal contamination. Soil analysis determines total and bioavailable metal concentrations, pH, nutrient status, and physical properties that affect plant growth.

Economic viability assessment considers the market value of the target metal, estimated biomass yield, expected metal concentration in biomass, and costs of cultivation, harvesting, processing, and refining.

### Cultivation and Agronomy

Hyperaccumulator plants are cultivated using agricultural techniques adapted to the specific species and site conditions. This may involve direct seeding, transplanting of nursery-grown seedlings, or vegetative propagation. Agronomic practices include fertilization (with careful attention to nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, and avoidance of metals that compete with the target metal for uptake), irrigation, weed management, and pest control.

Intercropping with non-accumulator species has been investigated as a strategy to improve soil structure and nutrient cycling while maintaining the primary focus on metal accumulation by the hyperaccumulator crop.

### Harvesting and Biomass Processing

Above-ground biomass is harvested at peak metal accumulation, which typically occurs during flowering or early seed set for many hyperaccumulator species. Harvesting may involve mowing, cutting, or whole-plant removal depending on the growth habit of the species.

The harvested biomass is dried to reduce moisture content and then incinerated to produce a metal-rich ash (bio-ore). The ash represents a concentrated form of the target metal, with metal concentrations typically 5 to 20 times higher than in the dried biomass. For nickel phytomining, ash nickel concentrations of 10% to 25% have been achieved.

The bio-ore is then processed using conventional hydrometallurgical or pyrometallurgical techniques to recover the purified metal. For nickel, the ash may be smelted directly or subjected to acid leaching followed by solvent extraction and electrowinning.

## Economic Considerations

The economics of phytomining depend on achieving sufficient metal yield per hectare per year to offset cultivation and processing costs. For nickel phytomining with Alyssum species, metal yields of 100 to 200 kg of nickel per hectare per year are considered economically viable at current nickel prices, depending on local labor and energy costs.

Phytomining offers several economic advantages over conventional mining. It operates on low-grade resources that are not economically mineable by conventional methods. It has lower capital costs, lower energy requirements, and generates less waste. It can be integrated with existing agricultural operations and land management practices.

Revenue may also be generated from carbon credits (for carbon sequestration during plant growth), from ecosystem services (soil stabilization, habitat creation), and from the sale of residual biomass for energy generation after metal recovery.

## Environmental Benefits

Phytomining simultaneously addresses environmental contamination and resource recovery. It provides a sustainable approach to managing metal-contaminated sites that might otherwise require costly excavation and disposal of hazardous waste. The process improves soil quality through the addition of organic matter from root systems and crop residues.

Phytomining on ultramafic soils can generate economic value from land that is otherwise unproductive for conventional agriculture due to the naturally toxic soil chemistry. This provides an economic incentive for conservation of ultramafic ecosystems and their unique biodiversity.

## Challenges and Limitations

Phytomining is currently limited to a relatively small number of metals for which suitable hyperaccumulator species exist. Many hyperaccumulator species are slow-growing and produce low biomass yields, limiting metal productivity per unit area. The geographic distribution of suitable species and substrates constrains where phytomining can be practiced.

Long-term sustainability of metal extraction from soil requires that bioavailable metal pools are replenished through weathering of soil minerals. If bioavailable metal is depleted faster than it is released from the soil matrix through natural weathering, phytomining yields will decline over successive cropping cycles.

Regulatory frameworks for phytomining are still developing, as the technology does not fit neatly into existing mining or agricultural regulations. Market acceptance of bio-ore as a feedstock for metal refining requires demonstration of consistent quality and competitive production costs.
