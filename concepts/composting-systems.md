# Composting Systems

Composting converts organic waste into stable, nutrient-rich humus through biological decomposition. Different systems suit different scales, inputs, and timelines. On a homestead with [[duck-keeping]] and [[goat-keeping]], manure management makes composting essential. See [[vermicomposting]], [[bokashi]], [[compost-tea-aact]], and [[sepp-holzer-methods]] for specialized methods.

## Hot Composting (Berkeley Method)

Fast, thermophilic composting that produces finished compost in 2-4 weeks:

- **C:N ratio:** 25-30:1 (greens = nitrogen-rich, browns = carbon-rich). Mix roughly 2-3 parts browns to 1 part greens by volume.
- **Pile size:** Minimum 3x3x3 ft (1 m³). Smaller piles can't sustain thermophilic temperatures. Maximum practical size ~5x5x5 ft for manageability.
- **Moisture:** 50-60% — feels like a wrung-out sponge.
- **Aeration:** Turn every 2-3 days. Each turn reintroduces oxygen and redistributes microbes.
- **Temperature stages:**
  - Mesophilic phase (70-100°F / 21-38°C): Days 1-3. Initial decomposition.
  - Thermophilic phase (110-160°F / 43-71°C): Days 3-14. Pathogen and weed seed kill. Optimal around 130-150°F. If exceeding 160°F, turn to cool — beneficial microbes die off.
  - Cooling and curing (below 100°F / 38°C): Days 14-28. Earthworms and other mesophiles recolonize. Cure 2-4 weeks minimum before use.

## Cold/Slow Composting

Minimal-effort method: pile materials as they become available, no turning required.
- Takes 6-12 months to a year or more.
- Does not reliably kill weed seeds or pathogens — avoid seeding with mature weeds.
- Layer greens and browns as available; keep covered to shed excess rain.
- Good for homesteads with continuous low-volume organic waste.

## Vermicomposting

Worm-based composting ideal for kitchen scraps and small spaces. See [[vermicomposting]] for full details: bin setup, feeding rates, harvesting castings, species selection (Eisenia fetida).

## Bokashi Fermentation

Anaerobic pre-composting using [[query-how-do-you-make-effective-microorganisms-em-for-agriculture]]. See [[bokashi]] for full details: bran inoculation, two-week fermentation, soil burial of finished pre-compost.

## [[sheet-composting|sheet]] Mulching / Lasagna Gardening

In-place composting that builds garden beds:
1. Mow or sheet-mulch existing vegetation (cardboard without glossy ink, overlapping edges).
2. Layer nitrogen materials (manure, grass clippings, kitchen scraps) 2-4 inches.
3. Layer carbon materials (leaves, straw, wood chips) 4-6 inches.
4. Repeat 2-3 layers. Top with finished compost and mulch.
5. Plant directly into top layer or let decompose 3-6 months first.

## Hugelkultur

Buried wood mound beds. See [[sepp-holzer-methods]] for detailed technique: logs and branches buried under soil create long-term moisture reservoirs and slow-release carbon. Combine with compost layering for rapid fertility.

## Composting Duck and Goat Manure

Both manures are "hot" (nitrogen-rich) and compost well:

| Manure | N-P-K (approx) | C:N Ratio | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Duck | 0.6-1.4 - 0.4-1.3 - 0.3-0.6 | 10-15:1 | Wet, high nitrogen; mix with plenty of carbon (straw, wood chips) |
| Goat | 0.7-1.5 - 0.5-1.2 - 0.9-3.0 | 12-25:1 | Pelletized, easier to handle; mix with bedding for C:N balance |

- Mix manure with carbon bedding (straw, leaves, wood shavings) at 3:1 to 4:1 carbon-to-manure ratio by volume.
- Hot compost for 2+ months minimum to kill pathogens (especially for goat manure used on food crops).
- Duck bedding from coops is often already well-mixed — pile and turn.

## Humanure Safely

Human manure can be composted but requires strict protocols:
- Use a dedicated composting toilet system (thermophilic compost or vermicompost).
- Must reach 120°F+ for sustained period to destroy pathogens (helminths, bacteria, viruses).
- Compost for 1-2 years minimum. Do not use on root crops or crops eaten raw.
- Follow local regulations — many jurisdictions have specific rules. See the Humanure Handbook (Jenkins) for comprehensive guidance.

## Compost Tea (AACT)

Actively [[actively-aerated-compost-tea-aact-brewing]] Tea extracts and multiplies beneficial microbes. See [[compost-tea-aact]] for full brewing method and [[compost-tea-guide]] for the complete AACT manual.

### Compost Tea and Composting Systems Integration

Different compost types produce different biology for AACT (per Ingham):

| Compost Type | Bacteria:Fungi | Best Tea Use |
|-------------|---------------|--------------|
| [[ingham-thermal-compost-versus-worm-compost]] (green-heavy) | Bacterial | Vegetable/annual crops |
| Thermal compost (brown-heavy) | Fungal | Perennials, trees |
| Worm castings | Highly bacterial | General inoculant, seedlings |
| Forest humus | Highly fungal | Trees, shrubs, [[cervantes-cannabis-flower-anatomy]] |
| Static compost | Mixed (may be anaerobic inside) | Use with caution — test |

### Key Compost Tea Principles (from Ingham's Guide)
- AACT must stay aerobic (DO >6 ppm) — if it goes anaerobic, beneficial fungi, protozoa, and nematodes die
- True compost contains no human pathogens; AACT brewed aerobically maintains that safety
- Compost tea is NOT leachate, NOT manure tea, NOT anaerobic fermented tea
- Tea biology peaks at 18-36 hours then declines — use immediately
- Cannot be stored long-term — brew fresh on-site for each application
- Food resources (molasses, kelp, [[comparison-fish-hydrolysate-vs-fish-emulsion]]) select for specific organism groups
