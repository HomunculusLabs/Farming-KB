# Mold Identification Guide

Molds are rapidly growing, filamentous fungi that reproduce primarily by producing airborne spores (conidia). They are among the most common organisms encountered in indoor environments and are significant concerns for human health, food safety, and building maintenance. Most indoor molds belong to the phyla [[ascomycota]] and Zygomycota.

## Understanding Mold Growth

### Conditions for Growth
Mold requires four things to grow:
1. Moisture: the most critical factor; most molds need water activity (aw) above 0.65
2. Food source: [[cellulose]] (wood, paper, drywall), organic matter, dust
3. Temperature: most molds grow between 4-38 C, with optimal range 20-30 C
4. Oxygen: virtually all molds are aerobic

### Common Indoor Substrates
- Drywall paper (cellulose-based)
- Ceiling tiles
- Carpet and carpet padding
- Wood framing and subflooring
- Wallpaper and wallpaper adhesive
- HVAC systems and ductwork
- Window sills and bathroom grout
- Books and paper products
- Clothing and textiles

## Major Indoor Mold Genera

### Aspergillus

One of the most important genera in indoor environments and the [[ascomycete-phylum-guide|Ascomycete]] phylum.

#### Common Indoor Species
- A. niger: black spores, common on decaying vegetation, produces ochratoxin in some conditions
- A. flavus: yellow-green colonies, producer of aflatoxins (potent carcinogens)
- A. fumigatus: blue-green, thermotolerant (grows at 37 C), major cause of aspergillosis in immunocompromised patients
- A. versicolor: produces sterigmatocystin (a precursor of [[aflatoxin]]), commonly found in water-damaged buildings
- A. clavatus: found on stored grain, produces patulin

#### Identification
- Colonies are typically fast-growing, velvety to powdery
- Colors range from green to yellow-green, black, brown
- Microscopic: conidiophores with vesicle bearing phialides in radiating or columnar heads
- Spores (conidia) are spherical to ellipsoidal, 2-5 um

### Penicillium

Closely related to [[aspergillus]], found in soil, decaying vegetation, and indoor environments.

#### Common Indoor Species
- P. chrysogenum: the original penicillin producer, very common indoors
- P. brevicompactum: frequently found on damp building materials
- P. expansum: causes blue mold of fruits, produces patulin
- P. citrinum: produces citrinin (nephrotoxic)

#### Identification
- Colonies are usually blue-green to gray-green, velvety with a white edge
- Microscopic: brush-like (penicillus) conidiophore structure
- Phialides are borne on metulae (secondary branches) which arise from the conidiophore
- Spores are smooth or rough-walled, globose, 2-5 um
- Often produces a characteristic musty odor

### Stachybotrys chartarum (Black Mold)

The most notorious indoor mold, often called "toxic black mold" in media.

#### Key Facts
- Requires sustained high moisture (water activity above 0.93) to grow
- Grows slowly compared to other molds (7-12 days to produce spores)
- Dark brown to black, slimy colonies
- Produces [[mycotoxins]]: satratoxins, roridins, and verrucarins (trichothecenes)
- Spores are sticky, not easily aerosolized unless disturbed
- Found on cellulose-rich materials: drywall paper, ceiling tiles, cardboard

#### Health Concerns
Stachybotrys gained notoriety in the 1990s during the Cleveland infant pulmonary hemorrhage outbreak, though subsequent CDC review found insufficient evidence for a causal link. Current scientific consensus:
- Can cause respiratory irritation and allergic reactions
- Mycotoxin exposure risk is debated; spores contain mycotoxins but inhalation risk is not well-quantified
- Individuals with compromised immune systems, allergies, or asthma are at greatest risk
- Should be remediated professionally due to handling risks

### Cladosporium

The most common airborne mold genus worldwide.

- Olive-green to brown-black colonies
- Found on dead plant material, soil, and indoors
- Major component of outdoor air spore counts
- Can grow at cooler temperatures (some species active near 0 C)
- Usually does not produce mycotoxins
- Common allergen causing hay fever and asthma symptoms

### Alternaria

Common outdoor mold, frequent indoor contaminant.

- Dark olive-brown to brown colonies with velvety texture
- Large, multicelled, club-shaped conidia (muriform spores) with transverse and longitudinal septa
- Major allergen, associated with allergic rhinitis and asthma
- Plant pathogen on many crops
- Commonly found in bathrooms, damp areas, and on window frames

### Chaetomium

Indicator mold for chronic water damage.

- Cottony white colonies that turn gray to olive-brown
- Produces distinctive perithecia (small, dark, flask-shaped fruiting bodies) visible to the naked eye
- Often found on water-damaged drywall, paper, and cellulose materials
- Some species produce mycotoxins (chaetoglobosins)
- Presence usually indicates prolonged moisture problem

### Trichoderma

Fast-growing green mold, often found on water-damaged building materials.

- Bright green, rapidly spreading colonies
- Some species are aggressive colonizers of other fungi
- T. harzianum is used as a biocontrol agent against plant pathogens
- Can cause opportunistic infections in immunocompromised individuals
- Common in soil and on decaying wood

### Mucor and Rhizopus

Zygomycete molds (see [[zygomycete-phylum-guide]]).

- Very fast-growing, cottony to gray-white colonies
- Fill petri dishes quickly
- Produce dark sporangia at the ends of stalks
- Can cause mucormycosis in immunocompromised patients
- Common on bread, fruit, and other food items

## Health Effects

### Allergic Reactions
Most common health effect of indoor mold exposure. Symptoms include:
- Sneezing, runny nose, nasal congestion
- Red, itchy, or watery eyes
- Coughing, wheezing, throat irritation
- Skin rash in sensitive individuals

### Asthma Exacerbation
Mold spores and fragments can trigger asthma attacks. Sensitization to specific mold species (Alternaria, Cladosporium, Aspergillus) is a risk factor for severe asthma.
