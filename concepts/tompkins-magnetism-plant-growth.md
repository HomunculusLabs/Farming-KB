## Magnetism and Plant Growth

The influence of magnetic fields on plant growth has been observed
across centuries and cultures, from folk wisdom about planting seeds
in particular orientations to modern scientific experiments with
magnetized soil amendments. Research documented in The [[fukuoka-inductive-deductive-intuitive-reasoning-agricultural-research]] Station in

a garden plot where it contacted the roots of red and white radishes.

The results exceeded his wildest expectations. Not only were the
"activated" radishes on average twice as large as controls, but their
tap roots were three to four times as long, indicating that root
stimulation had produced the increased growth. The same remarkable
effect was obtained for rutabagas, turnips, carrots, beans, lettuce,
broccoli, and oyster plant.

## Remarkable Reports from Users

When Cox's Electroculture Corporation began selling the product in
ten-pound cans in 1970, users reported not only much larger yields but
far better flavor in their vegetables. This corroborated earlier
reports from Lemstrom's experiments with strawberries and Sir Oliver
Lodge's observations about bread quality.

Other users reported that irises doubled the number of blossoms on a
single stalk regardless of whether they were planted with or without
fertilizer. A plastic surgeon told Cox that when he put the magnetized
ore among the roots of one of two ponderosa pine seedlings, the little
tree grew in one summer four times as high as its neighbor.

## The Earth Connection Mystery

One puzzling finding was that the magnetized powder produced no results
when shaken into flower pots or greenhouse flats. To work, it had to go
into the soil of the earth itself. Cox could not explain this anomaly,
but one explanation suggested was that the ferrous oxide, when
magnetized as lodestone, radiates its power only when in contact with
what the Elizabethan physicist William Gilbert called "its animate
mother" -- the living earth.
