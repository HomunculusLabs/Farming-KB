# Eliot Coleman and The Winter Harvest Handbook

## Eliot Coleman: A Biography

[[eliot-coleman]] is one of the most influential figures in modern organic agriculture, with nearly 40 years of experience spanning all aspects of organic farming: field vegetables, greenhouse vegetables, rotational grazing of cattle and sheep, and range poultry. His work has fundamentally shaped how small-scale farmers and gardeners approach year-round food production.

### Career Highlights

- **Author** of *The New Organic Grower* and *Four-Season Harvest*, two of the most widely-read books on organic vegetable production
- **Contributor** to three scientific books on organic agriculture; published extensively on organic farming since 1975
- **Executive Director** of the International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements (IFOAM) for two years
- **USDA Advisor** during the landmark 1979–80 study, *Report and Recommendations on Organic Farming*
- **European study tours** of organic farms, market gardens, orchards, and vineyards — bringing European intensive cultivation ideas back to American agriculture
- **Tool developer** who created and popularized a complete system of tools and equipment for organic vegetable growers, including broadforks, flame weeders, and precision seeders
- **Tool consultant** to Johnny's Selected Seeds, one of the premier seed companies for market gardeners
- **Television host** of *Gardening Naturally* on The Learning Channel, alongside his wife Barbara Damrosch

### Four Season Farm

Coleman and his wife Barbara Damrosch operate **[[four-season-farm]]** in Harborside, Maine (USDA Zone 5, latitude 44°N). The farm serves as both a commercial year-round market garden and a horticultural research station where Coleman develops and refines his winter harvest techniques. Located on a coastal peninsula, the farm benefits from maritime climate moderation — winters are cold but not as extreme as inland Maine locations.

### Philosophy and Influence

Coleman's work bridges historical European agricultural traditions and modern organic practice. He draws heavily on:

- **French intensive gardening** (*la culture maraîchère*) — the 19th-century Parisian system of year-round vegetable production on small urban plots
- **British market gardening** traditions that adapted French techniques to cooler, cloudier climates
- **Scientific research** on soil biology, plant nutrition, and protected cultivation

His approach is characterized by meticulous experimentation, honest reporting of results (including failures), and a commitment to practical, economically viable organic farming. He is frequently cited alongside Wendell Berry, Wes Jackson, and Joan Gussow as a leading voice in the sustainable agriculture movement.

## Winter vs. Summer Crops: The Fundamental Distinction

Coleman's system is built on a complementary pairing of summer and winter crop groups:

**Summer crops** (warm-season, long-day requiring):
- Tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, eggplants
- Require temperatures above 50–60°F and 12+ hours daylight
- Grown in greenhouses during summer months
- Vertical production on trellises to maximize space

**Winter crops** (cold-tolerant, short-day adapted):
- Leafy greens, root vegetables, alliums
- Tolerate freezing temperatures and grow in daylight under 10 hours
- Harvested from cold houses and quick hoops
- Cut-and-come-again harvesting maximizes yield per planting

This complementary system allows a single piece of land (or a mobile greenhouse) to produce two distinct crops per year, dramatically increasing total annual productivity and farm income.

## Washing, Packing, and Quality

### Post-Harvest Handling (pp. 8–9, 73, 158–159, 161–162)
Washing and packing are done inside the cool greenhouse using cold water. Cold water helps maintain product freshness and extends shelf life. Coleman emphasizes that washing and packing infrastructure should be integrated into the greenhouse system, not treated as a separate operation.

### Nutritional Quality (pp. 170, 172–173, 210–213)
Coleman argues that winter-grown vegetables are nutritionally superior to produce shipped long distances. Key factors:
- **Anthocyanins** (pp. 172) — cold temperatures increase anthocyanin production in crops like Bull's Blood beet leaves
- **Sugar content** — cold converts starches to sugars in carrots and other root vegetables
- **Freshness** — harvest-to-delivery time measured in hours rather than days or weeks
- **Soil quality** — biologically active soils produce nutritionally denser crops

## Practical Topics from the Index

### Wind Effects (pp. 1, 59)
Wind is a major factor in protected cultivation. Cold houses must be sited and designed to manage wind exposure — excessive wind accelerates heat loss and can damage structures. Windbreaks and proper structural design are essential.

### Weed Control (pp. 84, 100, 134, 151–156, 188, 191)
A multi-pronged approach:
- **Prevention** through dense planting that shades out weeds
- **Flame weeding** for pre-emergence control
- **Mechanical cultivation** with collineal hoes and wire weeders
- **Stale seedbed technique** — prepare beds, allow weeds to germinate, then flame or cultivate before planting
- **Cover crops** to suppress weed seed germination between cash crops

### Summer Solstice and Winter Solstice (pp. 34–35, 47)
The solstices mark the extremes of the daylight cycle and define the boundaries of the growing season. Summer solstice (June 21) initiates the declining daylight period; winter solstice (December 21) marks the midpoint of the Persephone Period when daylight begins increasing again.
