# Chamovitz: Photoperiodism and Phytochrome in Plants

## Overview

[[daniel-chamovitz]]'s *What a Plant Knows* presents the science behind
how plants perceive and respond to light, demonstrating that plants
possess sophisticated visual capabilities far beyond simple
photosynthesis. Through the discoveries of phototropism,
photoperiodism, and the remarkable phytochrome pigment system, plants
are revealed to sense not only the direction of light but its color,
intensity, and duration, enabling them to measure time and coordinate
their life cycles with extraordinary precision.

## Darwin's Discovery of Phototropism

It is not widely known that for the twenty years following his
publication of *On the Origin of Species*, Charles Darwin conducted
plant experiments that still influence research today. In his final
book, *The Power of Movement in Plants*, Darwin wrote: "There are
extremely few plants, of which some part does not bend towards
lateral light." This behavior, called phototropism, was shown by
Darwin and his son Francis to be the result of light hitting the tip
of a plant's shoot, which sees the light and transfers information
to the plant's midsection to tell it to bend in that direction.

The two Darwins grew canary grass in a totally dark room for several
days, then lit a very small gas lamp twelve feet from the pot, so dim
they "could not see the seedlings themselves, nor see a pencil line on
paper." But after only three hours, the plants had obviously curved
towards the dim light. Their classic experiment proved that
phototropism is the result of an inherent sensitivity to light in the
seedling tip, not photosynthesis.

## Photoperiodism: The Maryland Mammoth

Several decades later, a tobacco strain cropped up in southern
Maryland that never seemed to stop growing. Named Maryland Mammoth,
it could reach four and a half metres in height and produce almost a
hundred leaves, but rarely flowered. In 1918, Wightman W. Garner and
Harry A. Allard at the US Department of Agriculture discovered that
simply limiting the amount of light the plants saw was enough to
cause Maryland Mammoth to stop growing and start flowering.

If exposed to the long days of summer, it would keep growing leaves.
But if it experienced artificially shorter days, then it would
flower. This phenomenon, called photoperiodism, gave the first
strong evidence that plants measure how much light they take in.
Other experiments revealed that many plants flower only if the day is
short, called "short-day" plants, including chrysanthemums and
soybeans. Some need a long day to flower, called "long-day" plants,
such as irises and barley.

## Measuring Darkness, Not Daylight

Around the time of World War II, scientists discovered they could
manipulate when plants flowered by quickly turning lights on and off
in the middle of the night. They could take a short-day plant like
the soybean and keep it from making flowers in short days if they
turned on the lights for only a few minutes in the middle of the
night. Conversely, they could cause a long-day plant like the iris to
flower even in winter by a brief nighttime illumination.

These experiments proved that what a plant measures is not the length
of the day but the length of the continuous period of darkness. This
has practical applications: chrysanthemum farmers can keep plants from
flowering until just before Mother's Day by turning on lights for a
few minutes at night throughout autumn and winter. When they stop,
all the plants start to flower at once.

## The Red and Far-Red Light Switch

Scientists discovered that plants only responded to a flash of red
during the night. Blue or green flashes would not influence flowering,
but only a few seconds of red would. Plants were differentiating
between colours: they were using blue light to know which direction to
bend in (phototropism) and red light to measure the length of the
night (photoperiodism).

In the early 1950s, Harry Borthwick and colleagues made the amazing
discovery that far-red light, light with wavelengths a bit longer
than bright red and most often seen at dusk, could cancel the effect
of red light. If you give irises a shot of red light in the middle of
the night, they will flower. But if you shine far-red light on them
right after the pulse of red, it is as if they never saw the red
light. Hit them with red again after far-red, and they will flower.
It is like a light-activated switch.

## Phytochrome: The Plant Color Sensor

By the time John F. Kennedy was elected president, Warren L. Butler
and colleagues had demonstrated that a single photoreceptor in plants
was responsible for both the red and far-red effects. They called
this receptor "phytochrome," meaning "plant colour." In its simplest
model, phytochrome is the light-activated switch. Red light activates
phytochrome, turning it into a form primed to receive far-red light.
Far-red light inactivates phytochrome, turning it into a form primed
to receive red light.

Ecologically, this makes sense. In nature, the last light any plant
sees at the end of the day is far-red, and this signifies to the
plant that it should "turn off." In the morning, it sees red light
and it wakes up. In this way a plant measures how long ago it last
saw red light and adjusts its growth accordingly.

## The Leaf as the Eye for Photoperiodism

Surprisingly, the "eye" for photoperiodism is not in the tip of the
plant, as it is for phototropism. If in the middle of the night you
shine a beam of light on different parts of the plant, you discover
that it is sufficient to illuminate any single leaf in order to
regulate flowering in the entire plant. If all the leaves are pruned,
leaving only the stem and the apex, the plant is blind to any flashes
of light. If the phytochrome in a single leaf sees red light in the
middle of the night, it is as if the entire plant were illuminated.
Phytochrome in the leaves receives the light cues and initiates a
mobile signal that propagates throughout the plant.
