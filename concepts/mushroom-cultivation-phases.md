# Mushroom Cultivation Phases

Successful mushroom cultivation follows a defined sequence of phases, each
with specific environmental requirements. For oyster mushrooms (*Pleurotus*
spp.), the entire cycle from inoculation to harvest typically spans 4-8
weeks depending on species, substrate, and conditions.

## Phase 1: Strain Selection and Maintenance

Cultivation begins with selecting a genetically stable strain suited to the
target species, substrate, and environment. Strains are maintained on agar
media (malt extract agar or potato dextrose agar) and stored under
refrigeration. Regular subculturing every 3-6 months prevents senescence.

Key considerations include fruiting temperature range, colonization speed,
biological efficiency, and market characteristics (color, size, shelf life).

## Phase 2: Spawn Production

Spawn is the living fungal inoculum used to transfer mycelium into bulk
substrate. Quality spawn is the foundation of successful cultivation.

### Grain Spawn

The most common spawn type. Rye, wheat, or millet grain is hydrated,
sterilized, and inoculated with agar culture. Mycelium colonizes the grain
over 10-14 days at 22-25 degrees C. Grain spawn offers rapid colonization
due to numerous inoculation points.

### Sawdust Spawn

Hardwood sawdust mixed with bran is sterilized and inoculated. Slower to
produce but less prone to contamination during handling. Preferred for log
inoculation and some commercial operations.

## Phase 3: Substrate Preparation

The chosen substrate is prepared through chopping, hydrating, and
pasteurization or sterilization. Moisture content is adjusted to 60-75%,
and pH is typically corrected to 6.5-7.5 for oyster mushrooms. See
[[oyster-mushroom-substrates]] for detailed preparation methods.

## Phase 4: Inoculation

Inoculation (spawning) introduces spawn into the prepared substrate. This
is the highest-contamination-risk step and must be performed as cleanly as
possible.

### Inoculation Rates

- **Grain spawn**: 5-15% by weight (10% is standard)
- **Sawdust spawn**: 10-20% by weight

Higher rates speed colonization and reduce contamination risk but increase
material costs.

### Inoculation Methods

- **Mixing** -- Spawn thoroughly mixed into substrate (most common for
  bag cultivation)
- **Layering** -- Spawn layered between substrate in the container
- **Plug insertion** -- Spawn plugs inserted into drilled holes (log
  cultivation)

## Phase 5: Incubation (Colonization)

Mycelium spreads through the substrate, digesting lignocellulosic material.
Requirements:

- **Temperature**: 22-28 degrees C depending on species
- **Darkness or low light**: Light can trigger premature pinning
- **Fresh air exchange**: CO2 below 5000 ppm to prevent stalling
- **Humidity**: 70-85% to prevent substrate drying

Incubation takes 14-21 days on straw, or 21-28 days on sawdust. Full
colonization is indicated by complete white mycelial coverage and substrate
consolidation (the block becomes firm).

## Phase 6: Fruiting Induction

Once fully colonized, fruiting is triggered by environmental changes:

- **Temperature drop**: 5-10 degree C reduction from incubation
- **Fresh air exchange**: CO2 drops below 800 ppm
- **High humidity**: 85-95% relative humidity
- **Light**: Indirect light (12 hours on/off cycle) for orientation

## Phase 7: Primordia Formation (Pinning)

Small pin-like structures (primordia) emerge from the substrate. This is
the most delicate phase since pinsets are easily damaged by temperature
fluctuations, low humidity, excessive CO2, or physical disturbance. A good
pinset with even distribution is the primary determinant of final yield.

## Phase 8: Fruit Body Development

Pins develop into mature mushrooms over 3-7 days:

- **Watering**: Mist regularly; avoid direct spraying on developing pins
- **Air circulation**: Gentle air movement prevents CO2 buildup
- **Temperature**: Maintain stable fruiting temperature for the species
- **Harvest timing**: Pick before full cap expansion for best texture

## Phase 9: Harvest and Post-Harvest

Mushrooms are twisted or cut from the substrate base. Multiple flushes are
typical: 2-4 flushes for oyster mushrooms, with the first flush producing
50-70% of total yield. Subsequent flushes have lower yields but still
provide significant production.

## Phase 10: Spent Substrate Management

After the final flush, spent substrate is composted, used as soil
amendment, or re-inoculated for additional cycles. See
[[spent-mushroom-substrate]] for detailed management approaches.
