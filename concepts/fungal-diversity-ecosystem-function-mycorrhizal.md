# Mycorrhizal Diversity and Ecosystem Function

## Overview

The relationship between mycorrhizal fungal diversity and ecosystem function has been a
subject of growing research interest. While the broader question of
[[fungal-biodiversity-ecosystem-function]] has been
extensively studied for plant communities, the role of mycorrhizal diversity specifically
is less well understood. Evidence suggests that mycorrhizal diversity influences plant
performance, nutrient acquisition, and competitive interactions in ways that cannot be
predicted simply from total colonization levels.

## Evidence from Ectomycorrhizal Systems

Multiple studies have demonstrated that greater ectomycorrhizal diversity enhances plant
performance. Chu-Chou and Grace (1985) showed that radiata pine seedlings performed
better in the presence of three ectomycorrhizal symbionts than with one. Similarly, two
ectomycorrhizal species produced larger host plants than one (Parlade and Alvarez, 1993;
Reddy and Natarajan, 1997). The yield of competing Douglas fir seedlings was enhanced as
the number of ectomrhizal partners was increased (Perry et al., 1989).

Under low fertility conditions in Sweden, Jonsson et al. (2001) showed that the growth of
birch trees was higher when associated with eight ectomycorrhizal species than comparable
plants associated with single fungal species. This suggests that diverse mycorrhizal
communities provide complementary functions that become particularly important under
nutrient limitation.

## Functional Plasticity of Mycorrhizal Communities

Evidence from the evaluation of enzyme production by mycorrhizal fungi suggests that
there are significant differences in the ability of different fungal species to produce
enzymes (Dighton, 1983; Antibus et al., 1992, 1997; Leake and Read, 1990). The
availability of inorganic forms of nutrients in soil has a negative feedback on enzyme
production (Sinsabaugh and Liptak, 1997).

Given that the root system of individual forest trees can maintain a community of many
ectomycorrhizal species simultaneously, the ectomycorrhizal community on root systems may
be functionally plastic and able to change locally at spatial and temporal scales to
optimize resource utilization as local environmental conditions change. This plasticity
makes mycorrhizal communities ideally suited for exploitation of patchily distributed
nutrient resources.

## Diversity vs. Species Composition

Baxter and Dighton (2001) demonstrated that changes in plant performance were related to
the diversity per se of the ectomycorrhizal community rather than the actual species
composition. As the community of mycorrhizae increased, the proportional representation
of each species declined, but the total number of mycorrhizal root tips per plant
increased. In response to increased mycorrhizal diversity, plant shoot biomass declined
but root biomass increased.

Using stepwise multiple regression analysis, they showed that changes in plant biomass
(root and shoot) and plant phosphate content were significantly correlated with
ectomycorrhizal diversity rather than the level of root colonization. This suggests that
the interaction of ectomycorrhizal fungi on the same root system has an influence on
plant performance beyond either the total level of mycorrhizal infection or the species
composition.

## Species-Specific Differences in Nutrient Uptake

Different ectomycorrhizal species show markedly different efficiencies in accessing soil
nutrients. Using radiotracer phosphorus, Dighton et al. (1990) demonstrated that
phosphorus uptake into the canopy of birch trees was significantly higher in root zones
colonized mainly by Hebeloma mycorrhizae (134 ng P/g leaf) compared to Lactarius (55 ng
P/g) or Laccaria (52 ng P/g).

These species-specific differences in nutrient acquisition efficiency mean that the
composition of the mycorrhizal community directly influences the nutrient status of the
host plant. Loss of particular fungal species from a community could therefore have
cascading effects on plant nutrition and ecosystem-level
[[fungal-nutrient-cycling-mineral-transformations]].

## Context Dependence of Diversity Effects

The effects of mycorrhizal fungal diversity may depend on the context in which they
exist. The community structure may be beneficial, detrimental, or neutral depending on
the nutritional conditions of the soil, plant age, and other factors. Fitter (1985, 1991)
noted that it is not always obvious what the function of mycorrhizae is in a natural
ecosystem, though a clear nutritional benefit accrues in agricultural contexts.

Cairney (1999) discussed the range of ectomycorrhizal species and their varied
physiological functions, suggesting that relatively little is known about variation in
the physiology of the few fungi studied extensively, let alone the myriad of other
species. Heterogeneity of function within the mycelia of the same ectomycorrhizal fungus
as it exploits different resource patches in soil further complicates predictions
(Cairney and Burke, 1996).

## Implications for Ecosystem Management

The demonstrated importance of mycorrhizal diversity for plant performance has
significant implications for ecosystem management and
[[fungal-biodiversity-conservation-status]]. The function of mycorrhizae appears to
have a more dramatic effect on plant growth in oligotrophic systems than in fertile
systems. In volcanic fields and glacial outwash, for example, the multiple effects of
mycorrhizae in overcoming multiple stresses can double tree growth compared to their
effect in adjacent more fertile soils.

Leake (2001) correctly points out that laboratory studies may not fully represent plant
and fungal responses in more realistic field situations. This caveat highlights the need
for field-scale experiments to validate the diversity-function relationships observed
under controlled conditions. Understanding these relationships is essential for
predicting how [[dighton-fungal-responses-climate-change]] and
[[dighton-mycorrhizal-diversity-ecosystem-function]] may alter ecosystem function
through effects on mycorrhizal communities.
