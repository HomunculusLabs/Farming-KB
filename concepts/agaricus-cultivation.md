# Agaricus Cultivation

## Overview

The genus *Agaricus* includes some of the most commercially important and widely recognized [[fungi-cultivated-mushrooms-pathogens-competitors]] in the world. The white button (*A. bisporus*), portabella, and crimini are all the same species harvested at different maturity stages—white button is picked young, crimini during the second flush at smaller size, and portabella is allowed to mature fully. The almond portabella or royal sun agaric (*A. blazei*, *A. brasiliensis*, *A. subrufescens*) is a highly sought-after tropical species with the aroma and sweet flavor of almond extract or anise.

## Ecology and Natural Habitat

*Agaricus* species are common worldwide, growing naturally on composted livestock manure, grassy fields, wood chip piles, and forest leaf litter. Most species are specific to their food sources with very little adaptability to different substrates. All mushrooms in this genus function primarily as **secondary decomposers**—they are not efficient at [[lignin-degradation]]. It can be beneficial to grow a primary decomposer such as [[oyster-mushrooms]] first, then cultivate *Agaricus* on the spent media after the oysters have broken down the lignin.

*Agaricus* mushrooms require interaction with the microbial community in their natural environment to initiate [[pf-tek-primordia-formation-and-pinning-process]] fruitbody development. This makes them dependent on a microbe-enriched **[[casing-soil]]** applied after colonization of the fruiting substrate.

The royal sun agaric has a unique affinity for compost piles in [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]] and can naturalize easily if compost is added to its bed periodically throughout the year. The heat generated by the composting process creates a safe haven for mycelium during colder months.

## Common Species

- **A. augustus** (the prince)
- **A. bisporus** (white button, portabella, crimini)
- **A. bitorquis, A. campestris** (forest mushroom, field mushroom, pink bottom, rose bottom)
- **A. blazei, A. brasiliensis, A. subrufescens** (almond-scented agaric, almond portabella, royal sun agaric)

## Difficulty Level

- Outdoor cultivation: **2** (intermediate)
- Indoor cultivation: **3** (advanced)

## Mycelium and Spawn

The mycelium is white and linear, with irregular margins. It appears cottony in pure culture but becomes rhizomorphic when it collects microbial partners. Overcolonized spawn or fruiting substrates exude a light yellow metabolite. The growth rate is much slower than genera like *Pleurotus* (oyster) and *Volvariella* (paddy straw). The fragrance of the mycelium is species-specific, ranging from musty to sweet like almond extract.

**Commercial spawn** is normally available as cereal grains or supplemented sawdusts. Grain is the carrier of choice for most commercial cultivators. Sawdust spawn is slower to colonize unless supplemented with wheat bran or cereal grains, but it is more useful for home cultivators unable to fully pasteurize their compost, since insects can consume grain spawn before the mycelium spreads through the substrate.

## Fruitbody Development

The cap is centered on the stem with **free gills** (not attached to the stem) that are pinkish white at first, darkening at maturity as spores develop and stain the gill blades. The cap is white, covered with a fine matting of fibrils. Stems of some species stain bright yellow when cut or bruised. The **spore print is chocolate black**.

## Fruiting Conditions by Species

| Species | Spawn Run Temp | Fruiting Temp |
|---------|---------------|---------------|
| The prince (*A. augustus*) | 70–80°F (21–27°C) | 75–85°F (24–29°C) |
| White button, portabella, crimini (*A. bisporus*) | 70–80°F (21–27°C) | 60–65°F (16–18°C) |
| Forest/field mushroom (*A. bitorquis, A. campestris*) | 70–80°F (21–27°C) | 60–65°F (16–18°C) |
| *A. bitorquis* (heat-tolerant strain) | 77–91°F (25–33°C) | 75–91°F (24–33°C) |
| Almond portabella (*A. blazei, A. brasiliensis, A. subrufescens*) | 70–80°F (21–27°C) | 75–85°F (24–29°C) |

*A. bitorquis* is the most heat-tolerant commercial strain and a good candidate for tropical climates over *A. bisporus*.

### Indoor Fruiting Cycle

Indoor strains follow a well-orchestrated timing sequence: higher spawn run temperatures, a lower "middle" temperature phase to initiate primordia following colonization, and a slightly higher temperature to promote mushroom formation and maturation.

### Outdoor Fruiting

Outdoor bed cultivation requires choosing a strain that matches your climate and substrate availability, since environmental controls are relinquished. Outdoor beds generally produce **two flushes** a few weeks apart, depending on temperature fluctuations. Harvests are less predictable from a timing standpoint than indoor, climate-controlled cultivation.

## Wild Spawn Expansion Techniques

*Agaricus* is compost-loving and relies on soil microbes to stimulate fruiting, making it difficult to perpetuate using natural expansion methods. Options include:

- **Stem base transplants**: Transplant rhizomorphic stem bases or harvest plugs of colonized compost to start new beds (note: multiple transfers result in strain degeneration).
- **Microbial slurries**: Use stem bases to make a slurry mixed into fresh compost, bringing key microbial players needed for mushroom formation. Store stem bases under refrigeration until needed.

## Laboratory Isolation and Spawn Culture

This genus is slow to grow in culture. **Potato dextrose yeast agar** (PDYA) is preferred, created by adding 2–3 grams of yeast per liter of boiled potato water mixed with agar. The pH should be closer to neutral (7.0) for *Agaricus* species, compared to the 5.8–6.2 preferred by most other mushroom species.

Because growth is slow on agar, blending petri cultures with sterilized water and adding the liquid culture directly to sterilized grain is preferred. Grain spawn is enhanced by adding calcium in the form of limestone or gypsum powder.

### Storage Considerations

Tropical strains (*A. blazei, A. brasiliensis, A. subrufescens*) are extremely sensitive to prolonged refrigeration and may die after several weeks. Store these strains at room temperature or frozen in a glycerol solution for prolonged storage. An even 50–55°F (10–13°C) works well for maintaining cultures that can be subcultured even when they dry out. Dehydrating grain spawn also works well for tropical strain storage.

## Preferred Fruiting Substrates

*Agaricus* species prefer **composted mixtures of manure and cereal straws**, supplemented with cotton hulls or agricultural by-products such as bean hulls, cornstalks, cotton waste, beet pulp, and sugarcane bagasse. Almond portabellas prefer a complex media matrix, so provide a nice variety of supplements to enhance fruiting and development.

## Casing Soil

A **microbial casing soil is required**. When the entire surface of the substrate is a continuous white mat (check by scratching around in a few spots), dress the bed with microbial casing soil to stimulate primordia formation.

## Outdoor Cultivation

- **Media preparation**: Follow composting procedures to prepare growing medium. Fill rows or raised beds approximately 8–10 inches deep in a shady location. Cover beds with chicken wire or plywood to deter digging animals. Use supplemented sawdust spawn outdoors to minimize pest risk (upgrade to grain spawn for increased yields if pest pressure is low).
- **Spawn type**: Sawdust, plug, cardboard, or grain spawn
- **Inoculation to fruiting**: 80–100 days
- **Expected yields**: Fair to very good; typically 2–3 pounds per square foot for outdoor planted beds

## Indoor Cultivation

- **Media preparation**: Fill containers or trays 8–10 inches deep with growing medium. Poke narrow holes in a diamond pattern every 6–8 inches across the surface, fill holes with spawn, and cover with wet compost. Cover substrate with perforated plastic or drilled plywood for moisture retention and gas exchange.
- **Spawn type**: Grain
- **Inoculation to fruiting**: 45–60 days
- **Expected yields**: Good; 5–7 pounds per square foot

## Harvesting and Storage

Mushrooms form singly or in small groups scattered across the casing surface. Cut close to the casing soil with a sharp knife and trim the base. Fresh mushrooms keep up to **two weeks at 38–42°F (3–6°C)**. They do not rehydrate well but can be dried and powdered. *Agaricus* mushrooms pickle well and can be canned.

## Marketing

This meaty, flavorful mushroom is excellent sautéed. Common species sell for $3–5 per pound. The less common almond-flavored species can fetch $15–20 per pound, though this requires educating consumers about their superior nutritional profile.

## Nutritional Value

*Agaricus* mushrooms average ~93% moisture. Common species contain 25–33% protein (dry weight). The almond-flavored agarics are among the most protein-rich of all cultivated mushrooms at **35–49% protein (dry weight)**, comparable to [[paddy-straw-mushroom]] (*[[paddy-straw-mushroom-volvariella-volvacea-cultivation]]*). Medicinal properties include [[blesching-cannabis-immune-system-modulation]], blood pressure and sugar regulation, cholesterol reduction, cardiovascular and digestive support, antiviral and antibacterial activity, anti-inflammatory compounds, and suppression of many cancer cell lines.

## Mycoremediation Applications

*Agaricus* species have been shown to **hyperaccumulate cadmium, copper, and zinc**. The almond-flavored agarics also contain high levels of [[amanita-muscaria-antibiotic-properties-nanobacteria-fungal-medicine]].
