# Salinity Control Strategies and Cold Montane Deserts

## Overview

Salinity is one of the most pervasive land-degradation problems in arid and semi-arid agricultural regions. Rising water tables bring dissolved salts to the surface, poisoning soils and killing productive vegetation. Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual* outlines a hierarchy of strategies for controlling salinity, alongside design principles for cold montane and high-altitude desert environments where extreme conditions demand specialised approaches.

## Salinity Control Strategies

### Strategy #2: Interceptor Drains

Interceptor drains are cut at midslope positions to capture saline groundwater before it reaches lowland agricultural areas. These drains lead to natural waterways at midslope and lowland positions, diverting saline water away from productive land. The captured water must be discharged into accepting waterways or evaporation basins; it cannot be recycled onto already-stressed land without treatment.

Interceptor banks effectively leave more land available for cropping by containing the spread of salt-affected zones. By intercepting water at the break of slope, they prevent saline seepage from ruining valley-floor agriculture. The design must account for seasonal flow variations and ensure drains do not become channels that spread salt further during flood events.

### Strategy #3: Intensive Tree Planting at Recharge Areas

Perhaps the most effective long-term salinity control is planting deep-rooted trees at identified recharge areas — the zones where water percolates downward and raises the regional water table. Recommended species include:

- **Eucalyptus camaldulensis** — River red gum, widely tolerant across many soil types
- **Eucalyptus sideroxylon** — Ironbark, suited to drier sites with heavy soils
- **Eucalyptus wandoo** — Dryland specialist for Western Australian conditions
- **Eucalyptus globulus** — Best for cool, wet upland sites with reliable rainfall
- **Casuarina cunninghamii** — River she-oak, excellent for water uptake on alluvial soils

Trees are valuable not only over recharge areas but also as energy crops, providing fuelwood and timber income while performing the essential ecological function of lowering water tables. A single mature eucalypt can transpire hundreds of litres per day, drawing the water table down and preventing salt from rising into the root zones of crops and pastures. They may ultimately supplant mechanical bores and pumps as the primary control method.

### Natural Tea-Tree Vegetation and Recovery

Indigenous vegetation including *Leptospermum* (tea tree), *Melaleuca* (paperbark), and *Banksia* is highly sensitive to salt and is readily killed by rising saline water tables. These species are often the first indicators of a spreading salinity problem, with dieback visible along drainage lines and low-lying flats.

However, if uphill recharge effects are controlled — through tree planting and interceptor drainage — these species can re-establish naturally from seed banks in the soil, restoring native biodiversity and stabilising soils. The recovery of tea-tree communities is a reliable indicator that salinity management is succeeding.

### Strategy #4: Subsurface Drains for High-Value Crops

Subsurface drainage (slotted pipes or gravel-filled trenches installed below the root zone) is an expensive but effective strategy reserved for high-value crop areas. By maintaining the water table below the root zone, salts are prevented from concentrating in the crop root layer. This approach is economically justified only for intensive horticulture or orchards where the per-hectare return justifies the capital cost of installation and ongoing maintenance.

### Strategy #5: Bores and Pumps as Last Resort

Pumping saline groundwater from bores is the least preferred strategy because it is energy-intensive, costly, and ongoing. It should only be deployed where tree planting and drainage cannot act quickly enough to protect critical infrastructure or remnant vegetation. In many cases, well-established tree plantings eventually make pumping unnecessary, providing a permanent biological solution that requires no external energy input.

### Irrigation Efficiency and Water Loss

A staggering amount of water is lost before it reaches the crop. Only about **26% of channel waters** actually reach the farm gate. Of that remainder, on-farm losses may waste **60% or more** through evaporation, seepage from leaky channels, and inefficient application methods. Strategies to address this include:

- Eliminating leaky open channels in favour of **impermeable pipes**
- Using **sophisticated irrigation at root level** (drip or sub-surface delivery)
- Shifting from shallow-rooted annual pastures to deep-rooted **forage tree crops** and **fuelwood** species that access water at depth and recycle nutrients

### Salt-Tolerant Plant Species

Where salinity cannot be fully controlled, productive salt-tolerant species can still provide forage, fuel, and land stabilisation. Species thriving in soils with **greater than 0.4% NaCl** include:

| Species | Common Name | Role |
|---------|-------------|------|
| *Atriplex* spp. | Saltbush | Forage, land stabilisation |
| *Acacia* spp. | Wattle | Fuelwood, nitrogen fixation |
| *Prosopis* spp. | Mesquite | Fuelwood, forage pods |
| *Puccinellia* spp. | Alkaligrass | Forage grass |
| *Maireana* spp. | Bluebush | Forage, drought tolerant |
| *Tecticornia* spp. | Samphire | Halophyte ground cover |
| *Thinopyrum ponticum* | Tall wheat grass | Forage |
| Secale spp. | Wimmera rye grass | Winter forage |

### Stream Salinity and Fresh Water Supply

As soils are flushed to remove salt, stream salinity levels rise significantly. During the decades-long recovery period, households and livestock may need to rely on **rainwater tanks** and **hill sand storage dams** to secure fresh water supplies independent of saline surface water. Planners should anticipate that fresh surface water may be unavailable for decades and design water storage accordingly.

## Cold and Montane Deserts

### Climate Characteristics

Cold and montane deserts occupy arid plateau interiors and high valley floors at elevation. They are defined by **extreme temperature variations**: very hot days followed by icy nights. The diurnal range can exceed 40°C. Growing seasons are extremely short — typically **40 to 70 days** — and frosts may occur during most of the year. In these environments, **cold hardiness is far more important than drought hardiness** for plant survival.

### Snow Hydrology and Water Management

At high altitudes, snow may sublimate directly to vapour rather than melting into usable runoff. It is therefore essential to **[[cold-montane-deserts]] requires heavy thermal mass and wind protection:

- **Trombe walls** for passive solar heating
- **Double entries** (airlocks) to reduce heat loss
- **Glazing over courtyards** to create sun traps and extend the growing season
- **Earth-sheltering** (berming) for insulation against extreme cold
- **Massive fire-flue walls** for radiant heat storage and distribution throughout living spaces

## Integrated Design Synthesis

Salinity control and cold montane design share core [[water-management]], appropriate species selection, and earth-integrated structures are universal strategies that translate across these seemingly different environments.

### Key Takeaways

- Prioritise **biological solutions** (tree planting) over mechanical interventions (pumps, drains)
- Design for the **full spectrum of extreme conditions** — heat, cold, salinity, and aridity
- Use **stone, mulch, and earth-sheltering** as low-cost thermal moderators
- Secure **independent fresh water** supplies during long salinity recovery periods
- Select species for **functional roles** first — water uptake, frost tolerance, forage value
