# Chelation and Metal Binding in Plants

Chelation is a fundamental biochemical process in which organic molecules
(ligands) form multiple coordinate bonds with a single metal ion, creating a
stable ring structure called a chelate. In plants, chelation controls metal
bioavailability, transport, compartmentalization, and detoxification, serving
as the central mechanism underlying both essential nutrient homeostasis and
heavy metal tolerance.

## Endogenous Chelators

**Phytochelatins** are small, cysteine-rich peptides synthesized from
glutathione by the enzyme phytochelatin synthase (PCS). They are rapidly
induced in response to heavy metal exposure and form complexes with cadmium,
arsenic, lead, and other toxic metals. The phytochelatin-metal complexes are
transported into vacuoles by ABC-type transporters, effectively sequestering
the metals away from sensitive metabolic processes. Phytochelatins are not
directly encoded by genes — their production is regulated post-translationally
by metal-activated PCS enzymes.

**Metallothioneins** are gene-encoded, cysteine-rich proteins that bind
metals through thiolate bonds. They play roles in copper and zinc
homeostasis, [[staycare-phytochelatins-and-intracellular-metal-detoxification-in-fungi]].
Unlike phytochelatins, metallothioneins are constitutively expressed at
low levels and can be transcriptionally upregulated by metals, hormones, and
oxidative stress. Different plant metallothionein isoforms show preferences
for specific metals.

**Organic acids** including citrate, malate, and oxalate serve as
important metal chelators in both roots and shoots. Citrate forms stable
complexes with iron and aluminum, facilitating long-distance xylem transport
of iron and contributing to aluminum tolerance in acid soils. Malate is
involved in aluminum detoxification through root exudation, forming
non-toxic aluminum-malate complexes in the rhizosphere.

**Amino acids** participate in metal binding and transport. Histidine is
particularly important for nickel transport and tolerance in nickel
hyperaccumulators, where free histidine concentrations in xylem sap can be
orders of magnitude higher than in non-accumulating species. Nicotianamine,
a non-protein amino acid derived from methionine, chelates iron, copper,
and zinc for [[2c-family-compounds-overview]] exuded by graminaceous
(grass family) plants to acquire iron from alkaline soils. These compounds
have extremely high affinity for Fe(III) and form soluble complexes that
can be taken up by specific transporters (YELLOW STRIPE proteins). Some
phytosiderophores also chelate zinc, copper, and nickel.

## Exogenous Chelators

Synthetic chelators are used in agriculture and remediation. EDTA
(ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) strongly chelates most divalent and
trivalent metals, increasing their solubility and bioavailability in soil.
DTPA (diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid) is widely used in micronutrient
fertilizers to maintain iron and zinc in plant-available forms. EDDS
(ethylenediamine-N,N'-disuccinic acid) is a biodegradable alternative to
EDTA that has gained favor in phytoremediation applications.

Natural exogenous chelators include [[query-how-does-phosphorus-availability-change-across-soil-ph-levels]] and as a biodegradable
chelator in remediation. These natural compounds generally provide lower
but more sustainable metal mobilization compared to synthetic chelators.

## Metal Transport and Chelation Coupling

Metal-chelate complexes are recognized by specific membrane transporters.
The NRAMP (Natural Resistance-Associated Macrophage Protein) family
transports divalent metals, the ZIP (ZRT-IRT-like Protein) family mediates
zinc and iron uptake, and the HMA (Heavy Metal ATPase) family transports
heavy metals including copper, zinc, cadmium, and lead. The coupling of
chelation with selective transport allows plants to control metal fluxes
with high specificity.
