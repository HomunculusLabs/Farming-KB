# staycare cyanide biodegradation by fungi

Cyanide is one of the most rapidly acting metabolic inhibitors known,
and its release into the environment through industrial processes creates
a serious remediation challenge. Fungi have evolved remarkable
enzymatic systems for cyanide detoxification and utilization,
particularly through the enzyme cyanide hydratase. These systems offer
significant potential for the biological treatment of cyanide-
contaminated waste streams and soils.

## Cyanide Chemistry and Toxicity

Hydrogen cyanide (HCN) is a weak volatile acid (pK 9.36) that
inhibits respiration by binding to Fe3+ in cytochrome c oxidase,
blocking electron transfer to oxygen. Inhalation of concentrations
greater than 0.3 ppm is toxic, and 300 ppm is fatal unless treated
promptly. HCN can be absorbed through skin as well as by inhalation.

Cyanide exists in several chemical forms with varying toxicity:

- **Free cyanide** (HCN/CN-): The most toxic form, comprising
  hydrogen cyanide and simple dissociable cyanides such as KCN and
  NaCN. Below the pK of 9.36, the majority exists as volatile HCN.
- **Weak metal-cyanide complexes**: Include complexes with cadmium,
  lead, nickel, and zinc. Stable under alkaline conditions but
  dissociate to free cyanide at neutral and acidic pH.
- **Strong metal-cyanide complexes**: Include hexacyanoferrates and
  hexacyanocobaltates. Most stable and least toxic cyanide species.
  Iron cyanides can form Prussian Blue (ferriferrocyanide) at low
  pH, which is highly stable and relatively non-toxic.
- **Organic cyanides (nitriles)**: Compounds with covalent C-N bonds
  (RCN), used widely as industrial chemical precursors.

## Industrial Sources of Cyanide

Major industrial sources of cyanide contamination include:

- **Former gaswork sites**: Coal carbonization produced town gas
  containing cyanide impurities. Spent oxide waste from gas
  purification contains up to 50 g/kg cyanide, primarily complexed
  with iron as Prussian Blue. As many as 5000 former gaswork sites
  exist in the UK alone.
- **Metal mining and recovery**: Cyanide solutions extract gold and
  silver from ores by forming soluble metal-cyanide complexes.
- **Chemical manufacturing**: Production of acrylonitrile,
  adiponitrile, and methylmethacrylate for polymer manufacturing.
- **Electroplating industry**: Plating baths produce wastewaters with
  total cyanide concentrations up to 100 g/L.
- **Steel and iron industry**: Blast furnace gas scrubbers and coke
  production generate cyanide-containing by-products.

## Cyanogenesis by Fungi

Despite its toxicity, several fungal species produce cyanide as a
secondary metabolite. Cyanogenesis is widespread within the
Basidiomycetes and also occurs among Ascomycetes and Zygomycetes.
[[fusarium-solani]], [[fusarium-lateritium]],
Gloeocercospora sorghi, and Leptosphaeria maculans.

### Enzyme Properties

Cyanide hydratase is a large oligomeric protein with a native molecular
mass of at least 300 kDa, composed of subunits of 43-45 kDa. Key
properties vary among fungal sources:

| Species | pH Optimum | Subunit (kDa) | Km (mmol/L) |
|---------|-----------|---------------|-------------|
| F. solani | 7.5 | 45 | 4.7 |
| F. lateritium | 8.5 | 43 | 43 |
| G. sorghi | 7-8 | 45 | 12 |

The Km values are high (4-43 mmol/L), indicating a low affinity for
cyanide. This may limit commercial potential for effluent treatment
but is adequate for environmental concentrations. Phylogenetic
analysis groups cyanide hydratase with nitrilase enzymes rather than
nitrile hydratases, and nucleic acid sequences show 65-82% homology
among fungal sources.

## Cyanide Utilization Pathway

Some fungi go beyond simple detoxification to utilize cyanide as a
nitrogen source for growth. The complete pathway involves three steps:

1. **Cyanide hydratase**: HCN + H2O -> HCONH2 (formamide)
2. **Formamidase**: HCONH2 -> HCOO- (formate) + NH4+ (ammonia)
3. **Formate dehydrogenase**: HCOO- -> CO2 (utilized for growth)

Fusarium solani IHEM 8026, isolated from contaminated alkaline wastes,
grows on cyanide at pH 9.2-10.7 with associated biomass increase.
Radiolabelling confirmed the complete pathway from cyanide through
formamide and formic acid to carbon dioxide, with ammonia utilized as
a nitrogen source (Dumestre et al., 1997a; Barclay et al., 1998a).

## Metal-Cyanide Complex Degradation

Fusarium solani strains can degrade both free and metal-complexed
cyanides as sole nitrogen sources. Degradation efficiency depends on
complex stability:

- **Nickel cyanide** (K2Ni(CN)4): Complete cyanide removal and growth
  in 3-5 days at neutral pH.
- **Iron cyanide** (K4Fe(CN)6): Complete removal requires up to 28
  days at pH 4; no degradation occurs at pH 7 due to complex
  stability.

The mechanism likely involves dissociation of free cyanide from the
metal complex rather than direct enzymatic attack on the intact
complex. Evidence includes: the same cyanide hydratase gene is
induced by both KCN and metal-cyanide complexes (confirmed by
RT-PCR), and growth rates correlate with complex stability and free
cyanide availability rather than total cyanide concentration.
