# Hamilton: Garden Layout — Beds, Borders, Paths, and Terraces

**Source:** Geoff Hamilton, *Organic Gardening* (Nick Hamilton edition)

**Updated:** 2026-04-18

The layout of a garden determines its functionality, beauty, and suitability for organic cultivation. Working out a plan is challenging because the possibilities are endless, but taking time to put ideas on paper before starting work ensures cohesion in the finished product.

## Planning Process

1. Make lists of essential features (clothesline, storage, paths, gates) and desirable features (vegetable plot, ornamental area, pond, greenhouse).
2. Measure the garden using triangulation from two fixed points (house corners).
3. Transfer measurements to grid paper at a scale of 1in:1ft (1cm:1m).
4. Draw the outline and existing features in ink, then overlay tracing paper to experiment with designs.
5. Transfer the final plan to the garden using stakes and string.

Be prepared to be flexible when executing the plan—if a curve looks wrong when cut from lawn, alter it. If it looks right, it is right.

## Paths

Paths should only be used where absolutely essential because they tend to cut gardens into pieces. In a small garden, that is a disaster. If a path is necessary, make it curved so it disappears from view here and there, creating the illusion of hidden nooks. Never make a dead straight path in a small informal garden or allow a path to run across the plot or straight down the middle.

Essential paths include those to clotheslines (which must often be straight—place at the garden edge and hide with a flower border). An extremely attractive alternative, especially in small gardens, is stepping stones set in gravel, spaced to deliberately slow passage and planted with alpines in the gaps.

Path materials include gravel, grass, paving slabs, or stepping stones. Choose materials that blend with the house and garden style. Soften stark paving by leaving spaces between slabs for low-growing plants such as alpines.

## Terraces and Paving

A terrace for sitting out need not be against the house. If the back of the house faces north, a corner terrace may be more pleasant; in hot climates, a shaded area may be preferred.

A narrow strip of paving along the house is almost useless—too little room for a table and chairs. A square or triangular area in one corner is more practical and requires no more paving. Paving must finish at least two rows of bricks below the damp-proof course or airbricks to prevent water creep.

## Beds and Borders

The lawn area simultaneously shapes the flower borders. In cottage-garden style, use long, sweeping curves producing borders of varying widths. This makes the garden look bigger and is easier to mow.

**Raised beds** can be created by removing topsoil, leveling the subsoil, replacing topsoil evenly, and building a supporting wall at the front. This is essential for terracing slopes into stepped borders.

**Edging** paved areas with raised flower beds brings color to the house; edging with a hedge provides both privacy and windbreak. Allow space for softening hard surfaces with raised beds or plant-filled pots.

## Vegetable Plot Planning

Position the vegetable plot in a sunny part of the garden. Never plant a screening hedge between vegetables and their sun source. If space is limited, use the [[hamilton-deep-bed-system]]—vegetables grown in blocks rather than rows, with beds about 4ft (1.2m) wide and 12-18in (30-45cm) paths between. Leave extra room for crops not suited to deep beds (Brussels sprouts, runner beans).

The vegetable plot need not be rectangular—irregular shapes blend better with the rest of the garden. In very small gardens, grow fresh greens and ornamental vegetables among flowers in the borders.

## Fruit Garden Planning

Reserve sunny walls for fan-trained peaches, nectarines, and apricots. Use north walls for acid cherries. Train apples and pears as fans or espaliers on east- or west-facing walls. Grow cordons 2-3ft (60-90cm) apart at 45 degrees to form a productive hedge. Stepovers (single-tier espaliers only 12in/30cm high) make ideal low hedges around the vegetable plot. Plan a fruit cage if space allows—protecting both fruit and vegetables from birds is well worthwhile.

## Greenhouse and Cold Frame

A greenhouse should be as sunny as possible and as near to the house as convenient (for electricity/gas installation and winter visits). Rectangular is most common; hexagonal suits small gardens. A [[hamilton-greenhouse-management-organic]] is essential for hardening off plants—site it close to the greenhouse.

## Water Features

A pond attracts birds, insects, frogs, and other pest predators. Plan with rounded edges for informal gardens and shallow edges or ledges for marginal plants. Even miniature ponds (buried plastic containers) attract wildlife. Moving water (waterfall, fountain) requires underground electricity laid before paving or lawns.

## Utility Area

Organic gardeners accumulate materials: compost bins (at least two, preferably three), a manure pile, leaf mold containers, and storage for tools, string, and recycled containers. Screen the utility area behind tall shrubs, hedging, or a trellis with fast-growing [[hamilton-climbing-plants-organic]]. A garden shed can be transformed with climbing plants.

## Cottage-Garden Style

The cottage-garden design offers distinct advantages for organic gardeners: it combines ornamental and vegetable plants in the same beds, maximizing space; its informality encourages native plants that attract useful insects and pest predators; and its close-planting technique suppresses weeds. It is endlessly adaptable to modern architecture.

## Managing Slopes

Steep slopes should be terraced into plateaus linked by paths or steps. Remove all topsoil, level the subsoil, then replace and spread topsoil evenly—simply leveling the topsoil creates uneven depth. Connect levels with stone steps, wooden steps (railroad ties on stakes), or ramps. Slopes also affect frost: cold air flows downhill and accumulates in hollows, creating frost pockets.
