# Seed Quality and Germination Testing (Solomon)

From [[solomon-gardening-when-it-counts-index]] by Steve Solomon.

## Why Germination Matters

When gardeners depend on their gardens for food, they cannot take chances with poor seed. The worst disappointment occurs when seeds germinate but grow poorly due to lack of vigor -- gardeners rarely realize what is happening in time to resow. The next worst is seeds that do not germinate at all.

For farmers, seed failure represents a $16,000 loss per 80-acre field. Farmers therefore require minimum laboratory germination standards for each vegetable species. The home gardener should be equally demanding.

## Germination Standards

USDA minimum standard germination must be met for seed packages over one pound (454 grams) in the US. Smaller home-garden packets are exempt but often fall below these levels.

Representative minimum germination percentages (USDA standard vs. commercial quality):

| Crop type | USDA minimum | Johnny's Selected Seeds minimum | Commercial quality | Storage potential (years) |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| Cabbage family | 75% | 80% | 85% | 4-5 |
| Beans | 70% | 75% | 80% | 3 |
| Corn | -- | 75% | 80% | 2 |
| Peas | 75% | 80% | 85% | 3 |
| Lettuce | 80% | 85% | 85% | 4-5 |

Johnny's Selected Seeds tests all seed lots twice a year and sells only seed that exceeds federal minimums. When buying commercial seed, insist on at least 85% germination for cabbage -- if it sprouts below that figure, it is almost certainly too weak to depend on.

## How Germination Testing Works

A germination lab places seeds on moist paper in a temperature-controlled cabinet at the ideal temperature for each species. After a set number of days the lab counts what sprouted.

Germination percentage in the field is always lower than in the lab because:
- Outdoor soil is never at the perfect moisture content for germination
- Soil moisture is never stable
- Temperature fluctuates
- Field testing uses a set number of days; some seeds emerging after that cutoff are not counted

To get the best outdoor germination:
- Sow when soil moisture is adequate and stable
- Avoid sowing too early when soil is cold
- Sow at proper depth for the seed size

## Signs of Low-Vigor Seed

A seedling from low-vigor seed may:
- Emerge with two small fat leaves (cotyledons), sit there stunned, and be unable to produce a true leaf
- Fall over and die -- this may seem like damping-off disease but is actually death by starvation
- The embryo consumes its food reserves in dormancy; if stored too long, there will not be enough remaining for the seedling to fully develop

## The "Heirloom" Trap

Many heirloom varieties sold by enthusiast seed companies are degraded. The owners often have little idea of how to grow, select, and store seed. Their varieties are irregular, with many nonproductive individuals, and germination levels tend to be sub-par. When a bargain-price packager's variety gets too degraded, they buy a few pounds of commercial-quality seed of a similar-looking variety and use that to start their seed production field, then label it with the heirloom name. Much of what is sold as "heirloom" has nothing in common with the original variety.

## Evaluating Seed Companies

Ask seed companies:
- Do you conduct variety trials? How large a trials ground?
- What percentage of your catalog is evaluated in your own trials vs. accepted from suppliers?
- Do you have your own germination laboratory?
- How often do you test seed lots?
- What germination percentage do you require?

A company serious about quality:
- Maintains at least a half-acre trials ground (2,000 sq meters)
- Grows enough plants of each variety to evaluate uniformity, maturity timing, yield, flavor, frost tolerance, and disease resistance
- Tests germination of all seed lots at least twice yearly
- Performs variety trials organically to accurately assess disease resistance

## Buying Seed Packets vs. Bulk

To save money, buy larger quantities when you are certain of two things:
1. You will grow that variety for more than one year
2. The seed will not die before you use most of it

Example: Johnny's sells kohlrabi mini-packets at $2.85 for ~130 seeds, while 1,000 seeds costs $7.60. If you use 300 seeds per year, 1,000 seeds lasts over 3 years and costs one-third the mini-packet price. The key is understanding how to preserve germination during storage.
