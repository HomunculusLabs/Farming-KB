# Pleurotus sajor-caju Taxonomic Confusion and Identity

## Overview

The name "Pleurotus sajor-caju" has been one of the most widely
misapplied names in mushroom cultivation history. Cultivators around
the world have used this name for decades to refer to what is actually
a strain of Pleurotus pulmonarius, the Phoenix Oyster. The true
Pleurotus sajor-caju (Fr.) Singer is a completely different species
that has been returned to the genus Lentinus and is now called
Lentinus sajor-caju (Fr.) Fries. Resolving this confusion is
essential for accurate [[growing-gourmet-tree-species-guide-mushroom-cultivation]]
and scientific communication.

## The True Pleurotus sajor-caju

The true Pleurotus sajor-caju, as originally described by Fries, has
a distinct veil, a persistent ring on the stem, and trimitic or
dimitic hyphae composing the flesh. These features clearly separate it
from P. pulmonarius, which has monomitic hyphal structure and no
persistent veil. Pegler (1975) transferred this species to the genus
Lentinus, where it resides today as Lentinus sajor-caju (Fr.) Fries.

Singer (1986, p. 178) commented on the similarity of P. sajor-caju
and Lentinus dactyliophorus and Lentinus leucochrous, which is now
understandable given that he was describing a mushroom completely
different from the one cultivators have been growing and calling
"P. sajor-caju."

## The Misapplication

The name "P. sajor-caju" has been mis-applied so frequently that
confusion will likely reign for a considerable time. Many of the
scientific papers published on the extraordinary yields of
"P. sajor-caju" on straw, cotton wastes, coffee residues, and other
substrates are undoubtedly referring to a strain of P. pulmonarius
(see Hilber, 1989, p. 246). Since the name has become so entrenched
by cultivators, naming a new variety -- i.e., Pleurotus
pulmonarius var. sajor-caju -- seems like a good compromise.

Until a formal resolution, cultivators should refrain from calling
this commercially cultivated Oyster mushroom "Pleurotus sajor-caju,"
as it is incorrect.

## Distinguishing Features

Pleurotus pulmonarius (the actual species in cultivation) differs
from the true P. sajor-caju in several key respects:

- **Hyphal system:** P. pulmonarius is monomitic; true P. sajor-caju
  is trimitic or dimitic
- **Veil and ring:** P. pulmonarius lacks a persistent veil or ring;
  true P. sajor-caju has a distinct veil and persistent stem ring
- **Habitat:** P. pulmonarius is primarily a spring mushroom on
  hardwoods at various elevations, including conifers at higher
  altitudes in western North America
- **Spore characteristics:** P. pulmonarius spores are white to
  yellowish to lavender grey, cylindric, 7.5-11 x 3-4 microns

## Impact on Cultivation Literature

The widespread misapplication has significant consequences for the
mushroom cultivation literature. Many published studies, particularly
those from India, Southeast Asia, and tropical Africa, describe
"Pleurotus sajor-caju" when in fact they were working with strains of
P. pulmonarius. This affects:

- Interpretation of yield data and substrate suitability studies
- Cross-referencing between published research papers
- Strain identification in culture collections
- Comparison of cultivation parameters across studies

Authors such as Bano and Rajarathnam (1982) described the nutritional
aspects of this mushroom identified as "P. sajor-caju." This mushroom
is more widely cultivated than any other Oyster mushroom in tropical
and subtropical regions, making the taxonomic confusion particularly
impactful.

## Commercially Available Strains

Culture libraries frequently distribute strains mis-labelled as
"Pleurotus sajor-caju." A nearly sporeless strain, known as "3300
INRA-Somycel," produces about 1/100th of the spore load of normal
strains, but is less productive. The development of high-yielding,
low-sporulating strains of Pleurotus is essential to limit the impact
spores have on the health of workers in indoor cultivation
facilities.

## Relationship to Other Oyster Species

P. pulmonarius is closely related to P. ostreatus (the Tree Oyster)
and P. populinus. All three share a preference for hardwoods, with
P. pulmonarius and P. populinus particularly favoring aspen and black
poplar. P. pulmonarius can be separated from P. ostreatus by its
generally darker pigmentation and slightly shorter spores (under 10
microns compared to up to 15 microns for P. populinus).
