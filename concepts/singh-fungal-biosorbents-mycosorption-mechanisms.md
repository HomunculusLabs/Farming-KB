# Fungal Biosorbents and Mycosorption Mechanisms

Mycosorption refers to the biosorption of metal ions by fungal biomass,
with the fungal material used in the process termed a mycosorbent. The
biosorption phenomenon, in which microorganisms bind metals from aqueous
solution, provides a promising and economical alternative for treating
metal-containing industrial effluents. Singh's review provides a
comprehensive examination of fungal biosorbents, their mechanisms of
action, and their application in heavy metal removal from waste streams.

## Fundamentals of Mycosorption

Microorganisms have the ability to bind metals from aqueous solution,
and those responsible for the process are considered biosorbents. A wide
variety of living and dead biomass of bacteria, algae, fungi, and plants
is capable of sequestering toxic metals from waste streams. Yeasts and
fungi are unique in metal biosorption, and this process is known as
mycosorption. The fungal biomass used in mycosorption is termed a
mycosorbent. Conservative estimates of new biosorbents in the North
America environmental market amount to $27 million per year (Volesky,
2001). The biological removal of metals from solutions can be divided
into three categories: biosorption on fungal surfaces, intracellular
uptake, and chemical transformation of metal ions by fungi.

## Advantages of Nonliving Fungal Biomass

Living fungal biomass is required for intracellular uptake and chemical
transformation of metals. However, nonliving fungal biomass offers
significant advantages for biosorption applications. Nonliving biomass
does not depend on requirements for growth, metabolic energy, and
transport. Nonliving biomass shows a strong affinity for metal ions due
to the lack of protons produced during metabolism. The problem of metal
toxicity does not affect nonliving biomass, which is one of the major
advantages of biosorption. Fungal biomass can be generated as a waste
by-product of large-scale industrial fermentation and is pretreated by
washing with acids and/or bases before final drying and granulation.
All these factors contribute to reducing the final cost of the process.

## Biosorption as Ion Exchange

Biosorption is a pseudo-ion-exchange process in which metal ions are
exchanged for counterions in the biomass or resin. Filamentous fungi
generally possess higher adsorption capacities for heavy metal removal
than unicellular organisms. Biosorption involves external factors
including the type of metal, ionic form in solution, and the functional
binding site, and tends to be exothermic. Other important factors
include pH, temperature, biomass concentration, type of biomass
preparation, initial metal ion concentration, metal characteristics,
and concentration of interfering ions. Biosorption and recovery can be
intensified in the presence of stirring induced by magnetic field.

## Cell Wall Binding Sites

The fungal cell wall is the primary site for metal biosorption. Chitin
and chitosan are the predominant targets for zinc biosorption by
Rhizopus arrhizus. Carboxyl groups of acetone-washed yeast biomass are
involved in lead biosorption. Carboxyl groups also contribute to the
binding sites of metals in the cell walls of Mucor rouxii. Scanning
electron microscopy coupled with x-ray energy dispersion analysis
indicated that potassium and calcium are replaced by lead on the cell
wall of Mucor rouxii (Lo et al., 1999). The thickness of the mannan
layer and the larger surface area of different yeast strains influence
their metal uptake capacity. FTIR spectroscopic analysis confirms the
involvement of carboxyl, amino, and hydroxyl groups in metal binding.

## Fungal Biomass Types as Biosorbents

A wide variety of fungal biomass types have been evaluated as
biosorbents. These include baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae),
filamentous fungi (Rhizopus arrhizus, Aspergillus niger, Mucor rouxii,
Penicillium spp.), white-rot fungi (Phanerochaete chrysosporium,
[[staycare-trametes-versicolor-bioremediation-applications]]), and various yeast species (Debaryomyces hansenii,
Candida utilis, Kluyveromyces marxianus). Fungal biosorbents have
different specificities: some are nonspecific and bind multiple metals,
while others are specific for certain metals. Some fungal biosorbents
are particularly good for uranium, while others show high enrichment
selectivity for chromium or lead. The choice of biosorbent depends on
the target metal, wastewater composition, and process requirements.

## Pretreatment and Modification

The sorptive capacity of fungal biomass improves when pretreated with
formaldehyde, dimethyl sulfoxide, sodium hydroxide, and detergent
compared to live biomass. Alkali treatment exposes additional binding
sites on the cell wall by removing surface impurities and modifying the
conformation of cell wall polymers. A FeCl3-pretreated waste tea fungal
mat is an effective biosorbent for As(III) and As(V), and an autoclaved
fungal mat is effective for Fe(II) removal from groundwater samples.
Immobilization of fungal biomass in polysulfone or alginate matrices
enhances mechanical stability and facilitates recovery from treatment
systems. The regeneration and reuse of biosorbents using eluants such as
sodium carbonate or nitric acid further improve process economics.

## Bioreactor Configurations for Biosorption

Based on appropriate contact between the metal solution and biosorbent,
various bioreactor configurations have been developed for biosorption.
Packed-column reactors are among the earliest used for evaluating the
effectiveness of metal-biosorbent systems. A plug-flow column reactor
with a continuous feed of fresh biosorbent can be harvested continuously
from the reactor, providing a semi-continuous treatment process. The
removal efficiency in packed-column reactors depends on the flow rate,
initial metal concentration, bed depth, and particle size of the
individual biosorbent particles. Increased removal efficiencies are
achieved with smaller particle sizes that provide greater surface area
for metal binding. Continuous-flow stirred tank reactors handle large
concentrations of multimetal solutions with greater biosorption yields.

## Organic Pollutant Biosorption

Beyond heavy metals, fungal biosorbents also adsorb organic pollutants.
The living mycelia of Emericella nidulans adsorb 15% of atrazine, 18%
of 2,4-D, 29% of 2,4-dichlorophenol, and 30% of 4-chlorophenol. A
macrofungus has shown the ability to adsorb 80% of endosulfan from
water at 10 mg/l after 24 hours. Due to their large surface area,
fungal hyphae can aggregate to form a mycelium that acts as a biosorbent
for organic pollutants. Inactivated nonliving fungal biomass shows rapid
adsorption on cell wall surfaces for organic compounds. Different species
of Basidiomycetes display differences in their adsorbed amounts of
pentachlorophenol, with uptake ranging from 5 to 18% on mycelia.

## Future Directions for Mycosorbents

Extensive data is now available on different types of fungal biomass and
biosorbents for the removal of toxic metals from waste streams. The
question remains whether biosorbents of new generation with higher
specificities can be produced. Genetic engineering, directed evolution,
and chemical modification of fungal cell walls offer potential routes to
improved biosorbent materials. The development of composite biosorbents
combining fungal biomass with inorganic supports or polymeric matrices
could enhance both capacity and reusability. Integration of biosorption
with other treatment processes in hybrid systems may provide the most
practical approach for industrial wastewater treatment applications.
