# Fungal Biosorbents and Mycosorption Mechanisms

Mycosorption refers to the biosorption of metal ions by fungal biomass,
with the fungal material used in the process termed a mycosorbent. The
biosorption phenomenon, in which microorganisms bind metals from aqueous
solution, provides a promising and economical alternative for treating
metal-containing industrial effluents. Singh's review provides a
comprehensive examination of fungal biosorbents, their mechanisms of
action, and their application in heavy metal removal from waste streams.

## Fundamentals of Mycosorption

Microorganisms have the ability to bind metals from aqueous solution,
and those responsible for the process are considered biosorbents. A wide
variety of living and dead biomass of bacteria, algae, fungi, and plants
is capable of sequestering toxic metals from waste streams. Yeasts and
fungi are unique in metal biosorption, and this process is known as
mycosorption. The fungal biomass used in mycosorption is termed a
mycosorbent. Conservative estimates of new biosorbents in the North
America environmental market amount to $27 million per year (Volesky,
2001). The biological removal of metals from solutions can be divided
into three categories: biosorption on fungal surfaces, intracellular
uptake, and chemical transformation of metal ions by fungi.

## Advantages of Nonliving Fungal Biomass

Living fungal biomass is required for intracellular uptake and chemical
transformation of metals. However, nonliving fungal biomass offers
significant advantages for biosorption applications. Nonliving biomass
does not depend on requirements for growth, metabolic energy, and
transport. Nonliving biomass shows a strong affinity for metal ions due
to the lack of protons produced during metabolism. The problem of metal
toxicity does not affect nonliving biomass, which is one of the major
advantages of biosorption. Fungal biomass can be generated as a waste
by-product of large-scale industrial fermentation and is pretreated by
washing with acids and/or bases before final drying and granulation.
All these factors contribute to reducing the final cost of the process.

## Biosorption as Ion Exchange

Biosorption is a pseudo-ion-exchange process in which metal ions are
exchanged for counterions in the biomass or resin. Filamentous fungi
generally possess higher adsorption capacities for heavy metal removal
than unicellular organisms. Biosorption involves external factors
including the type of metal, ionic form in solution, and the functional
binding site, and tends to be exothermic. Other important factors
include pH, temperature, biomass concentration, type of biomass
preparation, initial metal ion concentration, metal characteristics,
and concentration of interfering ions. Biosorption and recovery can be
intensified in the presence of stirring induced by magnetic field.

## Cell Wall Binding Sites

The fungal cell wall is the primary site for metal biosorption. Chitin
and chitosan are the predominant targets for zinc biosorption by
Rhizopus arrhizus. Carboxyl groups of acetone-washed yeast biomass are
involved in lead biosorption. Carboxyl groups also contribute to the
binding sites of metals in the cell walls of Mucor rouxii. Scanning
electron microscopy coupled with x-ray energy dispersion analysis
indicated that potassium and calcium are replaced by lead on the cell
wall of Mucor rouxii (Lo et al., 1999). The thickness of the mannan
layer and the larger surface area of different yeast strains influence
their metal uptake capacity. FTIR spectroscopic analysis confirms the
involvement of carboxyl, amino, and hydroxyl groups in metal binding.
