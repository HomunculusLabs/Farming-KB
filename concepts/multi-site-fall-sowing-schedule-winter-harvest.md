# Multi-Site Fall Sowing Schedule for Winter Harvest

Eliot Coleman's winter harvest system relies on precise, staggered sowings across multiple growing environments. The core principle is that as daylight diminishes after the autumn equinox, plant growth slows dramatically. By the winter solstice (around December 21), most cold-hardy vegetables enter a state of semi-hibernation, only to resume growth as days lengthen again in late January. This means **everything harvested between November and March must reach harvestable size before the Persephone Period begins**.

## The Four Growing Sites

Coleman's Four Season Farm in Harborside, Maine (USDA Zone 4b–5a) utilizes four distinct growing environments for fall and winter production, each offering progressively more protection and therefore a later sowing window:

### 1. Outdoor Fields
The least protected environment. Harvests continue until early November. Sowings must be completed earliest here because plants receive no supplemental shelter from frost or wind. Cold-hardy crops like arugula, spinach, and certain Asian greens can be sown in outdoor beds through mid-September for a final pre-winter harvest.

### 2. Early Greenhouses
The first cohort of moveable greenhouses to be positioned over field crops. These houses are typically moved into place in early to mid-October, giving plants an extra 4–6 weeks of protection compared to outdoor fields. Sowings here can occur roughly 2–3 weeks later than outdoor dates.

### 3. Late Greenhouses
The second cohort of moveable greenhouses, positioned later in the season (typically late October to early November). These provide a mid-range sowing window. Crops sown here must still achieve adequate size before the solstice, but the greenhouse environment buys additional growing time through heat retention and wind protection.

### 4. Cool Greenhouses
A stationary greenhouse maintained at cool temperatures (above freezing but without active heating, typically 35–50°F / 2–10°C). This environment provides the most protection and the latest sowing window. Cool greenhouses allow sowings into late October, November, and even early December for the hardiest species like mache and claytonia.

## Key Crop Sowing Windows

The following summarizes the sowing progression for major winter-harvest crops across the four sites:

### Arugula
| Site | Sowing Dates |
|------|-------------|
| Outdoor | September 4–16 |
| Early House | September 22 – October 2 |
| Late House | September 10 |
| Cool House | October 20 – December 10 |

Arugula is one of the most cold-tolerant salad greens. Multiple succession sowings are planned across all sites to ensure continuous harvest. The cool house receives the widest spread of sowings, extending into December for the hardiest selections.

### Lettuce
| Site | Sowing Dates |
|------|-------------|
| Outdoor | August 29 – September 6 |
| Early House | September 13–17 |
| Late House | September 19 – October 12 |
| Cool House | October 24 – November 11 |

Lettuce requires more growing time than arugula and therefore must be sown earlier. The late house receives the most intensive succession planting program with sowings every 3–4 days throughout late September and October.

### Mâche (Corn Salad)
| Site | Sowing Dates |
|------|-------------|
| Outdoor | August 26–30 |
| Early House | September 2–6 |
| Late House | September 10 |
| Cool House | October 1, 5 |

Mâche is extremely cold-hardy and one of the few crops that can germinate and grow through winter. Sowings in the cool house as late as October still produce harvestable plants by late winter.

### Spinach
| Site | Sowing Dates |
|------|-------------|
| Outdoor | August 10–16 |
| Early House | August 20 |
| Late House | (covered by late house succession) |
| Cool House | October 6 – November 5 |

Spinach is sown earliest because it requires substantial vegetative growth before winter. Despite being cold-hardy, spinach plants need to reach a minimum size before day length drops below 10 hours.

### Miscellaneous Greens
- **Minutina (Plantago coronopus):** Outdoor August 20–25; Late House September 18–26; Cool House October 10–20
- **Claytonia:** Cool House October 10–20
- **Scallions:** Cool House October 10–20

## The Succession Principle

Dates shown in **bold** in Coleman's schedule indicate sowings planned for **multiple harvests**. This is a critical distinction: rather than a single cut, these plantings are intended for cut-and-come-again harvesting, where the same plants are harvested repeatedly over weeks or months. This approach:

- Maximizes yield per square foot
- Reduces the number of sowings needed
- Extends the harvest window from a single planting date
- Works best with leafy greens (lettuce, arugula, Asian greens)

## Regional Adaptation

Coleman explicitly notes: *"We are not entirely satisfied with the precision of these dates yet and will be adding more dates as we fine-tune our production in the years to come. No dates are given for crops where we are not sure of our recommendations. The best advice is to sow often and keep notes."*

For growers in different climates, the schedule should be adapted by:

1. **Latitude:** Growers further south have a longer pre-solstice growing season and can shift dates later. At 40°N latitude vs. Coleman's 44°N, add roughly 1–2 weeks to sowing dates.

2. **USDA Zone:** Each half-zone shift warmer allows approximately 1 week of additional growing season. A grower in Zone 6 can start sowings roughly 2–4 weeks later than Zone 4.

3. **Microclimate:** Urban heat islands, coastal moderation, and elevation all affect local frost dates and growing degree days.

4. **Greenhouse design:** High tunnels with no end walls provide less protection than fully enclosed structures. The type of cover material (single poly, double poly with inflation, polycarbonate) significantly affects heat retention.

## The Persephone Period Constraint

The entire scheduling system is built around one immutable constraint: **the Persephone Period**, defined as the time when day length drops below 10 hours. At Coleman's latitude (44°N), this runs approximately from November 5 to February 5. During this period:

- Photosynthesis is severely limited by short days
- Plant growth essentially stops regardless of temperature
- The goal is to have **harvestable-sized plants already in place** before this period begins
- The greenhouse's role during the Persephone Period is protection (preventing freezing damage), not promotion of growth

This is why sowing dates are so critical and why the multi-site approach works: by staggering sowings across environments with different protection levels, the grower ensures that plants in each environment reach adequate size before growth essentially halts.

## Practical Implementation Notes

- **Sow often, keep notes:** Coleman's most repeated advice. Climate variability from year to year means rigid adherence to dates is less valuable than careful observation and record-keeping.

- **Bold dates = multiple harvests:** Succession planting within a single sowing date is designed for repeated cutting, not single harvest.

- **No dates given when uncertain:** If Coleman doesn't provide a date for a crop in a particular site, it means the combination hasn't been sufficiently validated at Four Season Farm.

- **Four sites create redundancy:** If an early outdoor sowing fails (pest damage, weather), later greenhouse sowings provide backup harvest potential.

- **The system is iterative:** Coleman presents these dates as a work in progress, refined over decades but still being adjusted based on annual results.
