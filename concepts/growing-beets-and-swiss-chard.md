## Growing Beets and Swiss Chard

Beets and Swiss chard (silverbeet) are the same species — *[[beta-vulgaris]]*. Beets were selected for a succulent sweet root; chard was selected for large, tender leaves. Dig up a beet and you"ll see the same pattern of development, just at a different stage. Both are exceptionally drought-tolerant and well-suited to low-maintenance gardens.

## Drought Tolerance

Beta's deep root system is excellent at handling long-lasting dry spells. A beet root system about 110 days after sowing can spread 4+ feet (120+ cm) across and 7 feet (215 cm) deep. This makes beets one of the best crops for gardens without irrigation.

Beta does not need hugely fertile soil to make a large root, as long as there is nutrition accessible in the subsoil. Their deep root system acts as a huge reservoir for moisture storage.

## Seed Biology

Technically, beet seeds are fruits — each usually produces multiple seedlings. The commercially produced seed can be multigerm (producing clusters). This is important for thinning strategy.

### Germination

- Sow slightly deeper than many other vegetables
- Each "seed" may produce 3-5 seedlings
- Seed longevity: 6 years is normal, 10 isn't unusual
- If seed sprouts poorly, it was very old — beet seed is reliable when fresh

## Growing Details

### Thinning Strategy

Careful thinning is essential for well-formed beets:

1. **Shortly after germination**: Reduce the thickest of the clumps, but do so cautiously — there will usually be a fair number of mysterious seedling disappearances
2. **When plants are about 4 inches (10 cm) tall**: Do the final, precise thinning to the spacing you want for mature beets

### Spacing

- Closer spacing: Smaller but more numerous beets
- Wider spacing: Larger beets, better storage potential
- Wide spacing example: 1 foot (30 cm) apart in rows 4 feet (120 cm) apart
- After 5 entirely rainless months, widely spaced beets can reach volleyball size and still be delicious

### Soil Preparation

- Beta doesn't need hugely fertile soil to make a large root
- As long as there is nutrition accessible in the subsoil, deeply adventitious roots will find it
- Double-digging or deep bed preparation helps by putting nutrients deeper where the root system can reach them

## Varieties

### Winter Storage Beets

Where winter consists of only frosts or chilly weather, two historic and virtually identical varieties are essential:
- **Lutz** (also called Winterkeeper)
- Both bred to make enormous roots that hold for months without becoming woody

### Other Types

- **Cylinder varieties**: Bred for canneries that want uniform slices and quick-cooking vegetables; have little beet flavor
- **White beets** (sugar beet crosses): Sweeter flavor
- **Classic red beets**: Best all-around for flavor and versatility

## Harvest and Storage

### Harvesting

- Most varieties continue enlarging without becoming woody or tasteless if they have room to grow
- Pulling every second beet in the row helps the patch remain in better eating condition
- In mild climates, beets may overwinter in the garden with crowns under a few inches of soil
- Store in a barrel of moist sand for longest keeping

### Storage

Because this crop is so well-suited to being a reliable staple, storage quality matters:
- Best in root cellar at 32-40°F (0-4°C) in moist sand
- Can remain in the ground through light freezes with soil mounded over crowns
- Lutz/Winterkeeper types hold for months

## Culinary Uses

### Baking

Beets may be baked like potatoes and are delicious. This method retains more of their nutrition than boiling.

### Raw Beet Salad

A grated-beet salad can be delicious when:
- The subsoil offers good fertility (balanced fertility produces a sweet, mild beetroot)
- Mix with bits of navel orange and minced mild onion
- Dress with lemon juice and black pepper

### Chard

Swiss chard has tender leaves but a somewhat tougher midrib. The midribs are often cooked, tops and all. Chard can be used as a pot green similar to spinach or kale.

## Saving Seed

### Beets

- Must overwinter before making seed the next spring
- Wind-pollinated — need at least 1/4 mile (400 m) isolation for rough purity
- Seed-making plants occupy a lot of space and inevitably make lots of seed
- Replant overwintered roots to the depth of their crown, 1 foot (30 cm) apart, in rows 3-4 feet (90-120 cm) apart
- In midsummer, when seed is dry on the stalk, harvest whole plants and dry to a crisp under cover
- Rub seeds off stalks and winnow to clean

### Maintaining a Variety

- Include at least 25 plants in the gene pool to maintain through more than one generation
- 4-6 plants will make several pounds of seed lasting a decade if kept dry during the drying-down period
- Commercial beet seed is grown in dry-summer regions (like the Pacific Northwest) because summer rain during seed drying is problematic

### Chard (Silverbeet)

To make chard seed where the snow flies:
1. Dig half a dozen plants with as much root as possible
2. Cut off larger leaves
3. Plant in large tubs or beds of earth in the root cellar over winter
4. Transplant back outside in spring
5. Otherwise they are like beets and will cross with beets

### The Cross-Pollination Warning

Beets and chard cross freely — you cannot save seed from both in the same season without isolation. Plan accordingly.
