# Psilocybe baeocystis Knobby Tops Guide

## Overview

psilocybe-baeocystis Singer and Smith, commonly known as "baeos"
or "knobby tops," is a moderately to highly active psilocybin mushroom
of the Pacific Northwest. First reported from Oregon, this species is
common throughout the Pacific Northwest region of North America. It is
named for its association with the discovery of the alkaloid [[baeocystin]],
which was first isolated from collections of this species. The species
prefers bark mulch or wood-enriched soils under conifers, particularly
Douglas fir, and is often found in landscaped areas near newly
constructed buildings.

## Macroscopic Features

**Cap:** 1.5-5.5 cm broad. Conic to obtusely conic to convex,
expanding to plane only in extreme age. Margin incurved at first, and
distinctly undulated when convex; translucent striate and often tinted
greenish. Dark olive brown to buff brown (occasionally steel blue),
becoming copper brown in the center when drying, hygrophanous, fading
to pallid white and easily bruising bluish. Surface viscid when moist
from a gelatinous pellicle, usually separable.

**Gills:** Attachment adnate to sinuate, close. Color grayish to dark
cinnamon brown with the edges remaining pallid.

**Stem:** 50-70 mm long by 2-3 mm thick. Equal to subequal. Pallid to
brownish surface, sometimes covered with fine whitish fibrils, while
often more yellowish towards the apex. Brittle, stuffed with loose
fibers. Distinct rhizomorphs present about stem base. Partial veil
thinly cortinate, rapidly becoming inconspicuous.

## Microscopic Features

Spores are purplish brown, mango-shaped, 10-12 by 6-7 microns. Basidia
are 4-spored. Pleurocystidia are absent. Cheilocystidia measure 20-30
(40) by 4.5-6 (9) microns, fusoid with a narrow neck.

## Habitat and Distribution

P. baeocystis grows solitary to subcespitose on decaying conifer mulch,
in wood chips, or in lawns with high lignin content. Occasionally grows
from fallen seed cones of Douglas fir (Pseudotsuga menziesii). Found in
the fall to early winter and rarely in the spring, with documented
collections as early as June 20. The species is abundant where wood
chips and bark from Douglas firs have been used for landscaping.
Stamets often finds P. baeocystis amongst ivy as it encroaches over
cultivated landscapes near newly constructed buildings. The dark-colored
cap is well disguised against the background of wood chips, making it
difficult to spot. In one rhododendron garden, P. baeocystis and
[[psilocybe-cyanofibrillosa]] are found growing side by side.

## Potency and Chemistry

Moderately active, losing significant potency in drying or from damage.
Reported alkaloid concentrations vary considerably:
- Psilocybin: 0.15-0.85%
- [[psilocin]]: up to 0.59%
- Baeocystin: up to 0.10%

The incurved, sometimes inrolled cap margin and irregular form are
important diagnostic features. The species bruises readily, which
accounts for some potency loss during handling and collection.

## The Baeocystin Discovery and Misconception

The discovery of baeocystin is directly tied to P. baeocystis. In 1960
and 1961, eight individuals were unintentionally intoxicated from
mushrooms purported to be P. baeocystis. One child died after three
days of hospitalization with a 106-degree fever and cerebral edema.
This spurred the search for another alkaloid similar to psilocybin but
possibly toxic. Soon, baeocystin was discovered (Leung and Paul 1968;
Benedict et al. 1962).

However, re-examination of the case revealed that the photographs of
the alleged toxic species clearly showed mushrooms resembling
[[psilocybe-cyanescens]], not P. baeocystis. The identifying
mycologist, Alexander Smith, retracted his identification upon
confrontation with the photographic evidence. Other mushroom species
were present in the yard but were not kept for analysis. Most
researchers are unaware that baeocystin has not been proven to be toxic
in the dosages commonly consumed. The name "baeocystis" (from the
Greek for "false bladder") is thus somewhat of a misnomer regarding
toxicity concerns.

## Taxonomic Relationships

Taxonomically, the closest relative to P. baeocystis is psilocybe-aztecorum, its subtropical Mexican cousin. Both species fade in
drying to a light color, often bleaching white from sun exposure. The
species is related to the broader group of wood-inhabiting Psilocybes
that includes psilocybe-cyanescens, [[psilocybe-azurescens]], and
allies. P. baeocystis shares habitat preferences with several other
Pacific Northwest species.

## Ecological Notes

P. baeocystis is closely associated with Douglas fir ecosystems in the
Pacific Northwest. Its ability to grow directly from fallen seed cones
demonstrates an adaptation to utilizing specific woody substrates. The
species appears to benefit from human landscaping activities that
introduce conifer bark mulch into garden environments. Stamets has also
documented P. baeocystis and psilocybe-cyanofibrillosa growing
directly from Douglas fir cones and in three-inch moss islands with
wood buried underneath.
