# Permaculture Design in Landscape

Permaculture Two (1979) establishes seven foundational reasons for designing plant systems, framing permaculture as "primarily a consciously designed agricultural system" combining landscape design with perennial plants and animals for sustainable yields with low energy inputs.

## Seven Reasons for Designing Plant Systems

1. Save energies -- reduce external energy inputs across the system

2. Cope with external energies -- sun, wind, fire, and frost management

3. Mutual plant assistance -- companion planting and guild design

4. Optimal placement of all units -- zonation for efficiency

5. Suit climate and site -- species selection matched to conditions

6. Integrate with man/society -- save heating, cooking fuel, and human labor

7. Provide wide range of necessities -- achievable by every person on small areas

These seven reasons form the philosophical foundation that [[permaculture-two-design-principles]] elaborates into practical design methodology. They connect directly to the [[permaculture-design-principles]] framework used in modern design courses.

## Slope-Based Design Principles

Ridge-top: Storage dams ("turkey nests") for gravity-fed water, fire protection water reserves, covered tanks as basement or foundation of buildings (thermal buffer).

Keyline: Water systems following Yeomans' keyline principles. Diversion channels around high ridges. Covered tanks reserved strictly for drinking water at lower settlement levels. See [[keyline-design-water-management]] for detailed applications.

Midslope: Gardens using water from high dams on the keyline. Above keyline, select dry-country permaculture needing only spot watering. Below keyline, plant species with higher water requirements.

Dwellings: Site behind lower dams for fire protection (fire is most intense advancing upslope). Small emergency tanks (ca. 22,000L for a family of five). Wastewater run to small ponding systems for algae, nutrients, duck food, and fish food.

High slopes: Forests coupled with "thermal belt" create remarkable difference to midslope climate and soil temperatures. Warm down-draught from high forests on frosty nights. Functions of erosion control and water retention.

Downslope: Reflection from dams adds warmth. Solar collectors transmit heat via thermal siphoning. Even slight slopes of 1:150 function to collect water and heat.

Access: High slope roads/tracks embody drainage or diversion to midslope dams, fire control, and cargo/harvest access to forest and service buildings. Slatted floors under upslope sheds enable dry storage and easy access to manures.

## Thermal Belts and Frost Protection

Anyone doubting the effect of high forests on midslope climate should walk towards an uphill forest on a frosty night and measure or experience the warm down-draught. If forests are above Zones I and II, they present little or no fire danger. This principle is critical for [[cold-climate-permaculture-guide]] and [[mollison-cold-climate-strategies-detailed]].

## How Much Land

"Admire a large estate if you like: but farm a small one." -- Virgil

The concept of being "land poor" from NW Tasmania: men with too much land become poorer as holdings increase. The answer to "How much land?" is "As much as you can control." Any more and you lose self-sufficiency.

The ideal plot is about 750-1000 m2 for the home garden in terms of annual agriculture. The smaller the managed annual system, the more is left for perennial planting and free-range animals on forage crop. In Russia, 4% of land around peasant houses produces 60% of the food. See [[mollison-how-much-land-for-self-sufficiency]] for detailed analysis.

## The Stacking of Plants

Williams' evocative description of multi-tier agriculture: palm tree above, olive beneath, fig under the olive, pomegranate and vine below the fig, then grain and vegetables. The stacking intensity is determined by environment -- rainfall sets the main limitation. Strategies to increase density:

- Prevent water loss via high shade, hedge, and trenched wall systems

- Introduce drought-resistant species like cacti as windbreak hedges

- Use vertical buried sheet-plastic moisture barriers between garden and dry soil

A well-occupied system resists invasion by rampant forms. Initial diversity plus lack of disturbance preserve the diversity-stability dynamism. At about 60 years for long-lived elements, systems begin to simplify and need intervention.

## Edge Effect

The interface between two ecosystems represents a third, more complex system combining both. At interfaces, species from both systems can exist. Gross photosynthetic production is higher at interfaces. Estuaries and coral reefs show the highest production per unit area of any major ecosystem. A landscape with complex edge is interesting, beautiful, and more productive. See [[edge-effect-in-permaculture]] for expanded treatment.
