## Low Island and Coral Cay Strategies in Permaculture Design

## Overview

Low islands and coral cays present unique [[permaculture-design]] challenges:
limited freshwater, poor or absent soils, exposure to salt spray and storm
surge, and extreme isolation from mainland resources. Mollison addresses
these environments as specialized design zones requiring distinct strategies
for water capture, soil building, food production, and settlement design.
Despite their apparent limitations, low islands can be made productive and
self-reliant through careful application of permaculture principles.

## Environmental Characteristics

Low tropical islands and coral cays share several defining features that
constrain and shape all design decisions. Freshwater is typically absent or
limited to a thin lens of rainwater floating above saltwater intrusion in
the coral substrate. Soils are generally poor, shallow, alkaline, and
derived from decomposed coral and coral sand with low organic matter and
poor water retention. Exposure to salt spray, cyclones, and tidal surge
creates harsh growing conditions for most species. The small land area
limits the scale of production and the diversity of systems that can be
implemented.

## The Freshwater Lens

The freshwater lens is the single most critical resource on a low island.
Rainfall percolates through the porous coral substrate and accumulates as a
lens-shaped body of fresh water floating atop denser salt water. The depth
and extent of this lens depends on island size, rainfall patterns, and
extraction rates. Mollison emphasizes that protecting and enhancing the
freshwater lens is the primary design imperative for any low island system.

### Strategies for Lens Protection

- Maximise vegetative cover to reduce evaporation and promote infiltration.
- Avoid deep-rooted species near the lens that could breach the saltwater
interface.
- Limit groundwater extraction to sustainable rates.
- Direct all roof runoff to infiltration areas rather than to the sea.
- Establish swales and contour bunds to slow runoff and maximise
percolation.

## Water Harvesting Systems

Given the limitations of the freshwater lens, supplementary rainwater
harvesting is essential. Mollison recommends roof catchment systems with
above-ground storage tanks as the primary water supply for domestic use.
Every roof surface should be directed to storage, and storage capacity
should be sized for the longest expected dry period.

### Desalination Considerations

For islands where freshwater is severely limited, Mollison discusses simple
solar desalination techniques. Solar stills can produce modest quantities
of fresh water suitable for drinking, though the energy cost per litre
makes them impractical for broad agricultural use. The priority should
always be rainwater capture and lens protection over desalination.

## Soil Building on Coral Substrates

Building productive soil on coral sand and limestone is one of the greatest
challenges of low island permaculture. The alkaline pH (often 8.0-8.5)
limits the availability of many plant nutrients, particularly iron,
manganese, and phosphorus. Mollison recommends several soil-building
strategies:

### Organic Matter Accumulation

The primary strategy is massive organic matter addition. All vegetable
matter, seaweed, fish waste, and animal manures should be composted and
incorporated into garden beds. Mulching is essential to retain moisture
and moderate soil temperatures. [[green-manure-crops]] and nitrogen-fixing
species should be rotated through beds to build nitrogen and organic matter.

### Raised Bed Gardens

Raised beds filled with imported organic matter and compost provide the
best growing conditions on coral substrates. The raised position improves
drainage while the organic fill provides nutrient availability and water
retention that the native coral soil lacks. Beds should be regularly
top-dressed with compost and mulch.

### Biochar and Charcoal

Mollison notes the traditional practice of incorporating charcoal into
tropical soils. Biochar improves water retention, provides a buffered
nutrient exchange medium, and creates [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] soil
microorganisms. On coral islands, biochar can be produced from coconut
shells and other woody materials.

## Species Selection for Low Islands

Species selection must account for salt tolerance, drought tolerance,
alkaline soil tolerance, and wind resistance. Mollison identifies several
key species groups:

### Coastal Trees and Shrubs

Coconut palms (Cocos nucifera) are the foundational species of most
tropical low islands, providing food, building materials, and thatch.
Pandanus species provide fruit, fibre, and wind protection. Suriana
maritima, Scaevola taccada, and Tournefortia argentea are valuable coastal
pioneers that stabilize sand and provide windbreaks.

### Food Crops

Breadfruit ([[artocarpus-altilis]]), taro ([[colocasia-esculenta]]), sweet potato
([[ipomoea-batatas]]), and cassava ([[manihot-esculenta]]) form the staple
carbohydrate base. Citrus species, bananas, papaya, and guava provide
fruit. Pandanus and [[terminalia-catappa]] provide additional food resources.

### Salt-Tolerant Vegetables

Few conventional vegetables tolerate saline conditions. Coastal peoples
have traditionally relied on Ipomoea aquatica (kangkong), Abelmoschus
manihot (slippery cabbage), and selected cultivars of sweet potato leaves
for leafy greens. These should be prioritised in low island garden design.

## Storm and Cyclone Resilience

Low islands are extremely vulnerable to tropical cyclones and storm surge.
Mollison emphasises that settlement and infrastructure should be sited
above the maximum historical storm surge line. Wind-resistant species
should form the primary windbreak structure around settlements and gardens.
Building design should incorporate cyclone-resistant features including
reinforced structures, secure roofing, and elevated positions.
