# Mollison on Keyline, Soil Conditioning, and Humid Landscape Water

**Source:** Bill Mollison, Permaculture Design Course, Pamphlet II (Permaculture in Humid Landscapes), 1981
**Last Updated:** 2026-04-18

## Overview

Mollison provides detailed practical instruction for the keyline system, soil conditioning with the Wallace soil conditioner, dam construction, swale design, and the complete water management sequence from ridge to valley in humid landscapes. This extends [[mollison-keyline-water-conservation]] and [[mollison-pdc-keypoint-keyline-humid]].

## The Thermal Belt

- Frost behaves like treacle, not water -- moves over tree tops pushing warm air down
- Warm thermal belt exists between frost above keypoint and valley floor below
- Several degrees warmer than surrounding areas
- First buds of spring break here; last area of autumn productivity
- House sited just below keypoint gets "a racing start on thermal efficiency"

## Soil Conditioning System

- **Wallace soil conditioner**: disc cuts soil, shank follows with shoe widening slit at base; 9 inches max depth
- Creates thousands of grooves running faintly across slope
- Water absorption: conditioned soils hold water to 9+ inches depth, absorb ~1 inch per foot as interstitial water
- **Soil temperature**: conditioned soils average 19 degrees F above surrounding soils in winter; 15-19 degree frost differential
- Olives bearing in 3 years vs 17-18 years in compacted soil
- Never need more than one treatment every 3-4 years under heavy grazing
- Don't use in: very free sandy soils, forested landscapes, soils with >90% rock

## Biological Alternatives to Soil Conditioner

- **Daikon radish**: spikes soil to 2 feet; broadcast after slashing; biennial (rots in place, never need to pull)
- **Western red cedar**: natural soil spike tree
- **Compost holes**: dig holes, put little logs in, plant vegetables where logs rot underneath
- Superphosphate is a "type one error" -- creates insoluble tri-calcium phosphate concrete block 15 inches down

## Dam Design Principles

- **"The flatter the floor you flood, the more water per dollar spent"** -- applies on open field, ridge, or valley floor
- Walk valley floor to find where it levels and constricts -- logical dam site
- Don't dam valleys as first resort; use for emergencies or productive systems
- Large-surface, shallow, easily constructed lower dams are best

## Dam Construction Details

- **Small dams (6-8 feet)**: 2.5:1 front slope, 3:1 rear slope; broad crown (car-width); tamp every foot; bump rocks to side (they cause leaks)
- **Large dams (200+ feet)**: trench to good clay at base, pack whole core with selected clay; line vertical center to top
- **Spillways**: cut into solid hillside, wind along contour, let shallow out and fail; never bring below dam
- **Earth tanks**: excavated on flat land, soil thrown up; spinner drains can lead into them
- Everything leaks a little -- this is normal

## The Complete Water Sequence

1. **High point**: clean water for domestic use
2. **Keypoint**: soil conditioning parallel to keyline stores water in soil
3. **Mid-slopes**: biological nutrient capture (fodder plants, algae, mussels, snails in ponds)
4. **Lower dams**: fish, wild rice, duck manure -- nutrient flow through duck/manure into pond systems
5. **Release**: water leaves property clean after passing through biological net
6. **Total vertical drop**: can accomplish complete system in 6 feet

## Additional Water Systems

- **Dieu-ponds**: hand-dug monastic catchments (3-20 feet diameter); taper inward so surface area decreases as water evaporates (never dry); 2-3 people dig in a day; traditional high country stock watering
- **Mini-terraces**: waist-high, 2 feet wide top, 3-4 feet base, walkway between tiers; stagger for runoff distribution; max 40 feet long per tier
- **Reverse siphon irrigation**: buried pipes between swale levels; only need to run hose at highest point; whole system floods section by section
- **Spinner drains**: large wheel with cups behind tractor; throws dirt aside creating nearly invisible drain
