# Mineral Transformations and Biogeochemistry

Fungi play significant roles in the transformation of minerals and the cycling
of elements in terrestrial environments. As geomycological agents, fungi
contribute to rock weathering, mineral dissolution, metal mobilization, and
the formation of secondary minerals. Their filamentous growth habit, metabolic
versatility, and ability to thrive in extreme environments make them
particularly effective agents of biogeochemical change. This field, termed
geomycology, examines the diverse ways in which fungi interact with the
mineral world.

## Elements and Their Bioavailability

Many elements have essential functional potential: for the synthesis of
biological macromolecules and energy capture (C, N, H, O, P, S), for the
transmission of information (Na, K, Ca), for catalysis (Fe, Cu, Zn, Mo),
for transfer of electrons (Fe), and for building solid structures (Ca, P,
Si). All these elements must be released into bioavailable forms that can be
assimilated by the biota. Their release occurs via weathering of rock
substrates and mineral constituents through physical, chemical, and biological
processes.

In addition to mobilization of essential nutrients during lithospheric
weathering, non-essential toxic metals (Cs, Al, Cd, Hg, Pb) may also be
mobilized. Metals can exert toxic effects by blocking functional groups of
enzymes, displacing essential metal ions, disrupting cellular membranes, and
interacting with protective antioxidant systems. Fungi employ a variety of
mechanisms for tolerance and survival, as described in
[[gadd-fungal-metal-interactions]].

## Bioweathering

Bioweathering is defined as the erosion, decay, and decomposition of rocks
and minerals mediated by living organisms. Fungi can dissolve minerals and
mobilize metals at higher pH values, and over a wider redox range, more
efficiently than bacteria. Fungi are well suited as weathering agents because
they can be highly resistant to extreme environmental conditions such as metal
toxicity, UV radiation, and desiccation; they can adopt varied growth,
metabolic, and morphological strategies; they can exude protons and metal-
complexing metabolites; and they form mutualistic symbiotic associations with
plants, algae, and cyanobacteria.

The fungal filamentous explorative growth habit and high surface-area-to-mass
ratio, together with the ability to translocate nutrients within the mycelial
network, ensures that fungal processes are integral components of
biogeochemical change. [[gadd-fungal-rock-weathering]] communities in soil are
very diverse, with [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] being particularly important in element
cycling.

## Fungi in Rock Environments

Many oligotrophic fungi can scavenge nutrients from air and rainwater,
enabling them to grow on rock surfaces. Rock-inhabiting communities must also
deal with varying extremes in microclimatic conditions, including light,
salinity, pH, and water potential. Fungi may achieve protection by melanin
pigments and mycosporines in their cell wall, and by embedding colonies into
mucilaginous polysaccharide slime that may entrap clay particles.

The symbiotic lichen association is one of the most successful means for
fungi to survive in extreme sub-aerial environments and is an extremely
biogeochemically active fungal growth form. Rock- and stone-dwelling
microorganisms are classified as epilithic (on surfaces), hypolithic
(associated with pebbles), endolithic (within the rock sub-surface),
chasmolithic (in pre-existing cracks), cryptoendolithic (inside cavities
among crystal grains), and euendolithic (actively boring into rock).

## Mechanisms of Weathering

### Biomechanical Weathering
Direct biomechanical degradation occurs through extensive hyphal penetration
into decayed rocks and tunnelling into otherwise intact mineral matter along
crystal planes, cleavage, cracks, and grain boundaries. Fungal hyphae can
exert considerable mechanical force derived from osmotically generated turgor
pressure. Biomechanical penetration is facilitated by thigmotropic reactions
and lubrication with mucilaginous slime.

Indirect biomechanical weathering occurs through shrinking and swelling
effects of hydrated mucilage produced by many fungi. Thigmotropism, a directed
mode of fungal growth towards grooves, ridges, and pores in solid material,
may explain how hyphal tips explore and exploit weakened sites in mineral
surfaces.

### Biochemical Weathering
Biochemical processes are believed to be much more important than mechanical
mineral degradation. The main mechanisms of mineral solubilization by fungi
are:

**Acidolysis**: Fungi acidify their microenvironment through excretion of
protons and organic acids and the formation of carbonic acid from respiratory
CO2. Many fungi excrete organic acids, including oxalic, citric, gluconic,
and fumaric acids, which solubilize metal cations from minerals and
precipitate them as metal oxalates.

**Complexolysis**: Fungi produce a wide range of metal-complexing agents
including organic acids, amino acids, phenolic compounds, and siderophores.
These ligands can chelate metal ions from mineral lattices, enhancing
dissolution.

**Redoxolysis**: Some fungi can mediate redox transformations of metals,
particularly iron and manganese, through the production of reducing compounds
or through direct enzymatic action. These redox transformations can
destabilize mineral structures containing reducible metals.

## Fungal Communities on Rock Surfaces

Filamentous fungi, including zygomycetes, ascomycetes, and basidiomycetes,
occur on rock surfaces and in cracks, fissures, and pores of the rock
sub-surface. Fungi have been found in many rock types including limestone,
soapstone, marble, granite, sandstone, andesite, basalt, gneiss, dolerite,
amphibolite, and quartz. They are likely ubiquitous components of the
microflora of all rocks and building stone across wide geographical and
climatic zones.

## Mineral Transformations and Secondary Mineral Formation

Interactions of fungi with rocks and minerals can directly and indirectly
lead to bioweathering. Microorganisms can influence mineral replacement
reactions and dissolution-re-precipitation processes by mineral dissolution,
precipitation of secondary minerals, and alteration of mineral surface
chemistry and reactivity. Fungal bioweathering of basaltic outcrops in
sub-polar areas is believed to be chronologically the first process of
weathering, followed by subsequent cryogenic processes.

Alkaline rocks seem more susceptible to fungal attack than acidic rocks.
Along with other organisms, fungi contribute to the weathering of silicate-
bearing rocks (mica and orthoclase) and iron- and manganese-bearing minerals
(biotite, olivine, and pyroxene). [[aspergillus-niger]] and Penicillium
expansum can degrade olivine, dunite, serpentine, muscovite, feldspar,
spodumene, kaolin, nepheline, and basalt.

## Ecological Significance

Fungal mineral transformations have profound ecological implications for
[[gadd-fungal-nutrition-metabolism]] and ecosystem nutrient availability.
By solubilizing minerals, fungi release essential nutrients into bioavailable
forms for plant uptake. Mycorrhizal fungi are particularly important in this
regard, as they directly connect plant roots to mineral soil horizons.
