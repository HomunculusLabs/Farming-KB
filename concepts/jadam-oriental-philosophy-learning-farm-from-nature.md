# JADAM Oriental Philosophy — Learning to Farm from Nature

## Overview

JADAM's technical methods are grounded in an explicit philosophical framework drawn from East Asian thought, particularly the concept of non-dualism found in Daoism and Buddhism. This concept page examines the philosophical foundations of JADAM and how they inform practical farming decisions.

## The Way and Virtue

### Daodejing as Farming Guide

JADAM draws on the Daodejing (Tao Te Ching), which "teaches us that the way a person shall live, the wisdom which he/she shall follow is readily found in nature. Consequently, we, as organic farmers, should be thinking 'the way I shall farm, the wisdom of farming that I shall follow is found in nature.'"

The greatest teaching is not found in agricultural textbooks or research papers but in the observation of natural ecosystems. Nature has been "farming" for billions of years; humans have been doing it for a few thousand.

### Trust Nature

"Trust her; she is much more experienced than you. Let's consult nature when we find ourselves in trouble."

This principle reverses the conventional approach to agricultural problem-solving. Instead of reaching for a chemical solution or a research paper, the JADAM farmer asks: how does nature handle this problem?

## Asking Nature — Case Studies

### Monoculture and Soil Degradation

One of JADAM's key teaching techniques is to pose agricultural problems to nature and observe how natural ecosystems solve them. For soil degradation from repeated monoculture, the natural answer is biodiversity: natural ecosystems never grow a single species in the same place year after year.

### The Grass and Microorganism Connection

"You will observe that the soil beneath the grass turns soft. This is sign of indigenous microorganisms propagating." The simple observation that grassland develops soft, fertile soil leads to the JADAM technique of using grass-covered soil as a starter for microorganism culture.

### Optimum Fertility

"What is optimum soil fertility to grow perfect crops? Many farmers wrestle over this issue endlessly." JADAM's answer: ask nature. A natural forest achieves perfect fertility without any external inputs. The principle is not a specific nutrient ratio but the self-sustaining cycle of growth, death, and decomposition.

## Non-Dualism in Agriculture

### Good and Bad Are One

"Anything that is good will turn bad if not applied in the appropriate amount. This holds true for everything. Soil nematodes are not always bad, as adequate number of them helps maintain soil health. Ladybugs are not always good; too many and they will become pests."

JADAM's non-dualistic philosophy rejects the categorization of organisms as "good" or "bad." In natural systems, every organism has a role; problems arise only when populations become unbalanced.

### The Mystery of Life

"No life can be labeled good or evil; this is the mystery of life. Do not look at nature through the lens of dualism—labeling this as good and that as evil. Look at nature with the view that 'the good and the bad are one.'"

This perspective has practical implications for pest management: the goal is not to eliminate pests but to maintain balanced populations where no single organism dominates.

### Against the Dualistic Mindset

"Humans like to look at the world through the eyes of dualism. People prefer to draw a line between good and evil, not knowing that such line does not exist in nature." The chemical pesticide mindset—kill all pests—is the ultimate expression of agricultural dualism.

## The Family as Farming Unit

### The Central Role of the Wife

"If you are a husband, the changes that are occurring is not simple; it cannot be handled by yourself alone. You need the participation and support from your wife. Your wife will actually be playing a central role in your farming."

JADAM explicitly addresses the gender dynamics of farming families, arguing that the wife's role is central—not peripheral—to the farm's success. This reflects the Korean farming tradition in which women have always been essential to agricultural production.

### The Pesticide Expert

"Make your wife into a JADAM pesticide expert who can make natural, cheap and effective pesticides by herself. She will be a treasure to your farm."

By training the wife to produce the farm's pesticide inputs, the family achieves self-sufficiency in one of the most expensive categories of farm expenditure.

### Breaking Into the Post-2020 Era

"Without your wife or life mate, it is impossible to break into the post 2020-era. Divide and give out your wife's household chores to your children."

JADAM envisions a future in which the family farm is a self-sufficient production unit, with all members contributing to both household and farm tasks.

## The China Opportunity

JADAM identifies China as "the largest organic market in the world" and positions Korea as "in the fastest and easiest position to access it." The development of high-speed rail connecting Seoul to Beijing and Chinese bullet train networks creates logistical possibilities for Korean organic produce to reach Chinese consumers within days.

This market vision provides the economic motivation for JADAM adoption: farmers who transition to ultra-low-cost organic production will be positioned to supply a rapidly growing market with premium produce at competitive prices.
