## The Three Essential Tools

**The spade:** A proper spade has a flat, narrow blade designed to be
pressed straight down into the earth with your foot. Of all digging
implements, spades penetrate compacted soil most effectively because
the gardener's full body weight presses down on them. Spades are best
for turning soil and preparing beds. People who use such tools all day
long cannot afford to be breaking one every other day, so professional
quality tools, while not cheap, work out to be economical over months
of use.

**The [[combination-shovel]]:** The combination shovel does what both the
spade and the shovel do, but in one tool. The blade is somewhat pointed
with a curved edge that is as effective as a spade for cutting into
soil, but will move soil better than a spade. It can also scoop up and
move loose materials like [[compost]] or sand.

**The [[garden-hoe-techniques-and-weeding]]:** The primary [[flame-weeding-and-cultivation]] tool. Solomon
emphasizes that a sharp hoe transforms weeding from exhausting labor
into quick, easy work. A dull hoe from the store will have you dripping
with sweat in two minutes.

## Shovels Versus Spades

There are different kinds of shovels and spades, each designed for a
different task. Industrial hardware stores and landscapers' suppliers
usually display a wall showing each design, weight, and size. Some
shovels are designed to move piles of loose material from one place to
another. Blades on these are broad and deeply curved so as to hold a
lot, like a soup bowl. These are designed to be thrust into a heap of
soft material with the foot, not for cutting into compacted earth.

A combination shovel, on the other hand, has a blade that is still
narrow enough that it does not take an unreasonable effort to press it
into uncompacted earth, yet can also flip soil and move loose material.

## Choosing Quality Tools

Solomon strongly advises against buying discount store tools. Where can
you find quality new tools? He recommends looking at a landscaper's or
nursery supply house, or a large, well-stocked commercial hardware
store. Inspect what they sell carefully.

Key indicators of quality include: a handle solidly attached; the tool
light and well balanced, yet strong; and for combination shovels or
spades, the top of the blade should be rolled over so you can press
hard with your foot without pain. [[emcdda-brand-names-user-terms-smartshop-marketing]] used by tradespeople are
generally reliable indicators of quality.

## Sharpening the Shovel

It is highly unlikely that any shovel or hoe will be sharp when you
purchase it. In fact, it will almost certainly be entirely blunt,
squared off instead of honed into an acute bevel like a chisel's edge.

To sharpen a shovel, place it on the edge of a porch or on the earth.
Put one foot on the handle, just behind where it sockets into the
blade, and press down with enough weight to hold it still. Hold the
file so that the angle between the blade and the file is about 15
degrees. Maintain that angle firmly. Stroke the file along the edge,
pressing down firmly. If the cutting edge is curved, always start the
stroke in the center of the blade and work out toward one side, then do
a few strokes going to the other side.

After a few hundred strokes (it may seem an endless task the first
time), you will have produced a chisel-like bevel, and the shovel will
have a sharp edge, nearly sharp enough to cut your finger on.

## Steel Quality and Tool Longevity

Making tool steel involves compromise. The harder the steel, the
sharper the edge it can take and the longer it will stay sharp.
