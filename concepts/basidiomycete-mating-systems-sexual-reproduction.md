# Basidiomycete Mating Systems: Sexual Reproduction and Genetics

Basidiomycetes (club fungi) encompass mushrooms, bracket fungi, puffballs, rusts, and smuts. Their sexual reproduction is unique among fungi and involves the formation of a dikaryotic mycelium — a stage where each cell contains two genetically distinct nuclei that divide synchronously. Understanding mating systems is critical for mushroom cultivation, strain breeding, and the genetics of commercially important species like Agaricus bisporus (button mushroom), Pleurotus ostreatus (oyster mushroom), and Lentinula edodes (shiitake).

## Overview of Mating Strategies

Basidiomycetes employ two fundamental mating strategies:

**Homothallism (self-fertile)**: A single spore can complete the sexual cycle without mating with a genetically different individual. The spore carries both mating-type alleles and can form a self-fertile dikaryon. Homothallic species include Agaricus bisporus (predominantly secondary homothallism), Coprinopsis cinerea (has both homothallic and heterothallic strains), and some Volvariella species.

**Heterothallism (outcrossing)**: Mating requires the fusion of two genetically compatible mycelia derived from different spores. This promotes genetic recombination and outcrossing. Most cultivated mushroom species are heterothallic. Heterothallism is subdivided into two types based on the number of mating-type loci controlling compatibility.

## Bipolar Mating Systems

In bipolar mating systems, compatibility is controlled by a **single mating-type locus** with multiple alleles (multiallelic). Two monokaryons (haploid mycelia) can form a dikaryon only if they carry different alleles at this locus.

- **Mating type locus**: Designated as the A locus (historically, or MAT locus in modern terminology)
- **Number of alleles**: Typically 50-300+ alleles in natural populations, providing high outcrossing potential
- **Compatibility formula**: A₁ × A₂ = compatible; A₁ × A₁ = incompatible
- **Example species**: Coprinellus disseminatus, Ustilago maydis (corn smut), Pholiota nameko (nameko mushroom)

In bipolar species, approximately 50% of random spore pairings are compatible (assuming a large number of alleles with roughly equal frequencies in the population). The A locus encodes homeodomain transcription factors (HD1 and HD2) that, when derived from different alleles, form a functional heterodimer that activates dikaryon-specific gene expression.

## Tetrapolar Mating Systems

In tetrapolar mating systems, compatibility is controlled by **two unlinked mating-type loci**, both of which must differ between mating partners. This is the more common system among Agaricales (gilled mushrooms):

- **A locus**: Controls nuclear pairing, clamp connection formation, and dikaryotic growth. Encodes homeodomain transcription factors (HD proteins).
- **B locus**: Controls nuclear migration and plasmogamy (fusion of hyphal cells). Encodes pheromone receptors (STE3-like) and pheromone genes.
- **Compatibility formula**: A₁B₁ × A₂B₂ = compatible; A₁B₁ × A₁B₂ = incompatible (same A); A₁B₁ × A₂B₁ = incompatible (same B)
- **Example species**: Schizophyllum commune (split gill), Coprinopsis cinerea, Pleurotus ostreatus, Lentinula edodes, Flammulina velutipes

### Tetrapolar Mating Dynamics

In tetrapolar species, the probability of compatibility between two random monokaryons depends on the number of alleles at each locus:

- If A has 100 alleles and B has 100 alleles: compatibility = (1 - 1/nA) × (1 - 1/nB) ≈ 98% of random pairings
- If A has 50 alleles and B has 50 alleles: compatibility ≈ 96%
- If A has 9 alleles and B has 9 alleles: compatibility ≈ 80%

In nature, many tetrapolar species have dozens to hundreds of alleles at each locus, resulting in very high outcrossing rates. Schizophyllum commune has one of the most extreme systems known, with an estimated 288 A alleles and 81 B alleles, making nearly 99.95% of random matings compatible.

### Sequential Steps in Tetrapolar Mating

1. **Pheromone signaling (B locus)**: The B locus encodes multiple pheromone genes and pheromone receptor genes. A monokaryon produces pheromones that bind receptors on a compatible partner (different B alleles). This reciprocal signaling triggers:
   - Cell wall dissolution at points of hyphal contact
   - Plasmogamy (fusion of cytoplasm, not nuclei)
   - Formation of a conjugation pore between hyphae

2. **Nuclear pairing (A locus)**: After plasmogamy, the A locus-encoded homeodomain proteins form heterodimers only between different A alleles. These heterodimers activate the dikaryotic genetic program:
   - Synchronized nuclear division
   - Clamp connection formation
   - Dikaryon-specific gene expression
   - Formation of fruiting body primordia

3. **Dikaryon establishment**: A stable dikaryotic mycelium forms where each compartment contains exactly two nuclei (one from each parent) that divide in synchrony. Clamp connections ensure each daughter cell receives one nucleus of each type.

## The Dikaryotic Phase

The dikaryon is the dominant vegetative phase of most basidiomycetes and has several distinctive features:

### Clamp Connections

Clamp connections are hook-shaped lateral branches unique to many basidiomycete dikaryons. They ensure the precise distribution of nuclei during cell division:

1. A terminal cell of the dikaryotic hypha begins dividing
2. One nucleus migrates into the developing clamp connection
3. A septum forms at the base of the clamp
4. The two nuclei in the terminal cell undergo simultaneous mitosis (conjugate division)
5. The clamp connection fuses with the subterminal cell, delivering its nucleus
6. A second septum forms, creating two dikaryotic daughter cells

Not all basidiomycetes form clamp connections. Some (like certain Agaricus species) use other mechanisms to maintain the dikaryon, and some yeasts in the basidiomycete lineage (like Cryptococcus) have lost the dikaryotic stage entirely.

### Dikaryon Advantages

The dikaryotic state offers several advantages over both haploid and diploid states:
- **Genetic buffering**: Each nucleus is haploid, maintaining high mutation rates and adaptability, while the paired state provides functional redundancy
- **Hybrid vigor**: Complementation between two different genomes allows expression of beneficial recessive alleles
- **Regulation of sexual reproduction**: The dikaryon is pre-adapted for immediate karyogamy (nuclear fusion) when conditions favor fruiting

## Karyogamy and Basidium Formation

Sexual reproduction culminates in the fruiting body (mushroom), where the basidia — the reproductive cells — develop on the gills (lamellae):

1. Karyogamy: The two nuclei in each terminal dikaryotic cell of the basidium fuse, forming a diploid nucleus (2n)
2. Meiosis: The diploid nucleus undergoes meiosis, producing four haploid nuclei
3. Basidiospore formation: Each haploid nucleus migrates into a developing sterigma and basidiospore
4. Spore discharge: Mature basidiospores are forcibly discharged from the basidium (ballistospore discharge mechanism)

In most species, each basidium produces exactly four spores (tetrasporic). However, some species are bisporic (two spores per basidium) and others are monosporic. Agaricus bisporus (the common button mushroom) is bisporic, which contributes to its predominantly homothallic breeding system.

## Secondary Homothallism

Secondary homothallism is a special case where a single basidiospore contains two sexually compatible nuclei. This occurs when:

- The basidium produces spores that each receive two post-meiotic nuclei (as in bisporic Agaricus bisporus)
- Or when spores contain dikaryotic mycelium (as in some homothallic Coprinopsis strains)

Agaricus bisporus is primarily secondarily homothallic: most spores contain two nuclei of different mating types and can form a fertile dikaryon without mating. However, approximately 1% of spores are homokaryotic (single nucleus), and these can be used for crossbreeding. This makes breeding Agaricus bisporus significantly more challenging than tetrapolar species.

## Mating Systems in Cultivated Mushrooms

Understanding mating systems is essential for mushroom breeding and strain development:

| Species | Mating System | Notes |
|---------|--------------|-------|
| Agaricus bisporus | Secondary homothallism | Bisporic; breeding difficult |
| Pleurotus ostreatus | Tetrapolar | Multiple alleles; breeding straightforward |
| Lentinula edodes | Tetrapolar | Shiitake; extensive breeding programs |
| Flammulina velutipes | Tetrapolar | Enoki; cultivated strains selected |
| Volvariella volvacea | Primary homothallism | Rapid lifecycle |
| Agrocybe aegerita | Tetrapolar | Pioppino; edible and medicinal |
| Ganoderma lucidum | Tetrapolar | Reishi; medicinal mushroom |

For tetrapolar species, strain improvement involves:
1. Isolating monokaryons from single spores
2. Performing pairwise matings between compatible monokaryons
3. Selecting dikaryons with desirable traits (yield, quality, disease resistance)
4. Maintaining selected strains through vegetative subculture or cryopreservation
