# Methylophilus in Cannabis Endorhiza

## Overview

Methylophilus is a genus of methylotrophic bacteria that emerged as a striking
example of cultivar-specific microbial enrichment in the cannabis (Cannabis
sativa L.) root microbiome. In a landmark study examining the endorhiza (root
interior) communities across three chemically distinct cultivars — Bookoo Kush,
Burmese, and Sour Diesel — Methylophilus displayed dramatic variation in
relative abundance that could not be explained by soil or [[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]]
alone. This finding provided key evidence supporting a two-tier model of root
microbiome selection, where host genotype acts as a secondary filter on top of
soil-driven [[biodiversity-fungal-community-assembly]].

## Taxonomic Background

Methylophilus belongs to the family Methylophilaceae within the class
Betaproteobacteria. These organisms are obligate methylotrophs, meaning they
exclusively utilize single-carbon compounds such as methanol and methylamine as
their carbon and energy sources. They are unable to grow on multicarbon
substrates like glucose or acetate. In soil environments, methanol is
primarily generated through the demethylation of pectin, a major structural
polysaccharide in plant cell walls. As plant roots undergo cell wall turnover
and remodeling, pectin degradation releases methanol into the surrounding
rhizosphere and potentially into the root endosphere itself. Methylophilus
species are well-adapted to exploit this niche, possessing methanol
dehydrogenase enzymes that oxidize methanol to formaldehyde, which then enters
central carbon metabolism via the ribulose monophosphate pathway.

## Cultivar-Specific Enrichment Pattern

The [[otu-differential-abundance-cannabis-microbiome]] of Methylophilus across the three studied cannabis
cultivars was one of the most pronounced taxonomic signals in the entire
experiment. Bookoo Kush harbored approximately 13% Methylophilus sequences in
its [[proteobacteria-dominance-cannabis-endorhiza-community]], making it one of the dominant genera in that
cultivar's root interior. In stark contrast, Burmese showed only 0.13%
Methylophilus — a 100-fold reduction. Sour Diesel had no detectable
Methylophilus sequences whatsoever in its endorhiza. This three-tier gradient
(13% / 0.13% / 0%) was highly statistically significant even after false
discovery rate (FDR) correction, with an adjusted p-value of 0.012. The fact
that all three cultivars were grown in the same controlled environment with
identical soil substrates eliminates soil chemistry as a confounding variable,
strongly implicating host plant genotype as the driver of this differential
enrichment.

## The Two-Tier Selection Model

The Methylophilus pattern fits neatly into the two-tier selection model for
root microbiome assembly. In the first tier, soil conditions — including pH,
organic matter content, moisture, and mineral composition — select for a broad
pool of potentially root-associated bacteria from the vast soil metacommunity.
This tier explains the shared core microbiome observed across all cannabis
cultivars, which includes taxa like Pseudomonas, Cellvibrio, and members of
the Oxalobacteraceae. In the second tier, host plant factors — including root
exudate composition, cell wall chemistry, immune system activity, and
root anatomy — act as selective filters that differentially enrich or deplete
specific taxa from the available pool. Methylophilus appears to be primarily
shaped by this second tier, as its presence is entirely dependent on cultivar
identity rather than soil conditions.

## Pectin Turnover and Methanol Availability

The metabolic connection between Methylophilus and plant pectin turnover
provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for cultivar-specific enrichment.
Different cannabis cultivars are known to produce root systems with varying
architectures, cell wall compositions, and rates of root growth and turnover.
A cultivar with higher rates of root cell wall remodeling — or one that
produces pectin with a higher degree of methylesterification — would release
more methanol into the endorhiza. Bookoo Kush may simply produce a root
environment that is richer in methanol, thereby favoring Methylophilus
colonization and proliferation. Conversely, Sour Diesel's root interior may be
a methanol-poor environment, making it inhospitable to obligate methylotrophs.
This hypothesis could be tested by measuring methanol concentrations in the
endorhiza of different cultivars or by comparing pectin methylesterification
levels in root cell walls across genotypes.

## Ecological Implications

The presence or absence of Methylophilus in the cannabis root microbiome may
have functional consequences for the host plant. While Methylophilus is not
typically classified as a plant growth-promoting rhizobacterium in the
conventional sense (it does not fix nitrogen, produce siderophores, or
synthesize phytohormones at notable levels), its [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] may
indirectly benefit the plant. By consuming methanol — which can be toxic at
elevated concentrations — Methylophilus may help detoxify the root
microenvironment. Additionally, formaldehyde produced during methanol
