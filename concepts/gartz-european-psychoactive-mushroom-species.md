# European Psychoactive Mushroom Species

Jochen Gartz's "Magic Mushrooms Around the World" provides the most
comprehensive survey of psychoactive mushroom species found across the
European continent. While Central and South America are traditionally
associated with ritual mushroom use, Europe possesses a surprisingly diverse
mycoflora of psilocybin-containing species. These include members of the
genera [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]], Panaeolus, Inocybe, Gymnopilus, and
Pluteus, distributed from the Atlantic coast of Norway to the steppes of
eastern Europe.

## Psilocybe semilanceata: The Liberty Cap

The most important European psychoactive species by far is
[[psilocybe-semilanceata]], known colloquially as the
"Liberty Cap." This small, conical mushroom fruits in pastures and meadows
throughout the British Isles, Scandinavia, central Europe, and as far east as
Siberia. It is probably the most potently psychoactive mushroom among the
European species. Its effects are characterized by impressive intensity and
rapid onset -- as little as twenty minutes after ingestion.

The fruiting bodies are extremely hygrophanous, meaning their cap color
changes dramatically with moisture content. When wet, the caps turn dark
olive black-brown. The species possesses a remarkable and rarely-reported
attribute: under the light of a quartz lamp, Psilocybe semilanceata specimens
turn fluorescent. The substance responsible for this fluorescence has not
yet been identified.

Gartz's own field research documented extensive natural fruitings in
Germany. At one former cow pasture near a creek, he and colleagues harvested
2,800 mushrooms (approximately 140g dry weight) over three years. The
majority of specimens were concealed inside clumps of grass rather than
visible on the surface. In the Pacific Northwest of North America, where the
species was introduced, it has become the most widely used psychoactive
mushroom, with the habitat expanding into pasture lands east of the Cascade
Mountains.

## Psilocybe bohemica: The Czech Species

[[psilocybe-bohemica-central-european]] (syn. Psilocybe serbica) is a
significant European species found primarily in the former Czechoslovakia
and surrounding regions. It is more commonly used in that country than
Psilocybe semilanceata. The species is notable for displaying the most
impressive bluing reaction among the species Gartz examined -- the caps
stain very quickly in reaction to pressure, despite containing low or
non-existent levels of free psilocin. This paradox is explained by the
rapid enzymatic removal of the phosphate group from psilocybin, which
produces psilocin that immediately oxidizes into blue-colored compounds.

Psilocybe bohemica has a distinctive flour-like smell reminiscent of
radishes or poppies. Cross-breeding experiments demonstrated that mycelia
from Psilocybe bohemica can never be fused with those of
[[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]] collected in the United States,
confirming that these are distinct species despite some morphological
similarities.

## Psilocybe cyanescens: The Wavy-Capped Species

[[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]], known colloquially as "Blue
Wavy" or "Cyan," has been found at numerous locations in Europe, including
Germany (Saarland, the Black Forest, Vogtland, Rheinland, Hamburg, and
Bremen from 1982-1983), Switzerland (1972), Austria (Steiermark, 1976), and
Corsica (1972, 1984). This species is particularly associated with bark
mulch and wood debris in parks and gardens. It develops very intensely blue
stains on its stems, while its caps exhibit a characteristic wavy margin.

The species has thick, visible mycelial strands (rhizomorphs) that can
readily colonize wood debris substrates. In the Pacific Northwest of North
America, Psilocybe cyanescens is the second most important species in terms
of usage after [[psilocybe-semilanceata]], and its
European populations appear to be expanding.

## Inocybe aeruginascens: The Fibrehead

Inocybe aeruginascens is a psychoactive member of the fibrehead genus that
was first discovered in Berlin in the 1970s. Unlike most other psychoactive
species, it forms mycorrhizal associations with trees rather than growing as
a saprophyte on decaying matter. The species produces only trace amounts of
psilocin but contains significant quantities of psilocybin and baeocystin.

Gartz documented several cases of accidental intoxication with this species.
In one instance, a mushroom hunter collected what he believed to be common
fairy ring mushrooms, while in another, specimens were picked as white
champignons. These incidents underscore the danger of misidentification --
the hallucinogenic Inocybe species can easily be mistaken for muscarine-
producing Inocybe species that are extremely poisonous. Iron chloride
applied to Inocybe aeruginascens fruiting bodies causes a deep blue
discoloration, while muscarine-producing species of the same genus do not
change color.

## Gymnopilus purpuratus: The Spectacular Find

Gymnopilus purpuratus was discovered growing on bark and wood chips in
Eastern Germany (Ribnitz-Damgarten) and represents one of the most visually
striking European psychoactive species. The cap can reach up to 20cm in
diameter, covered with distinctive purplish-ruby pointy scales on a yellow
background. A sulfur-yellow cortina (partial veil) is characteristic of the
species.

Biochemically, Gymnopilus purpuratus is remarkable for containing the
highest psilocin concentration of any European species. Smaller mushrooms
showed higher alkaloid concentrations, with approximately 0.1% each of
psilocin and psilocybin in large specimens. The species also contains
baeocystin. A bluing reaction develops on the stem base when the mushroom
is damaged.

## Pluteus salicinus: The Neglected Genus

Pluteus salicinus is a wood-inhabiting species that grows singly or in small
groups on dead hardwood. It is not particularly attractive in appearance but
has been confirmed to contain psilocybin. The species was first reported
from North America in 1981 and Norway in 1984. Gartz's analyses of
non-bluing European specimens from Thuringia, Germany yielded surprisingly
high psilocybin concentrations -- up to 1.57% in caps and 1.14% in stems --
along with urea, a substance also found in Panaeolus species.

Interestingly, European and American collections of Pluteus salicinus
turned out not to be identical, and the American material contained no
alkaloids at all. This finding demonstrated that mushrooms of the same name
from different continents are not necessarily the same species, highlighting
the need for careful taxonomic work.

## Panaeolus subbalteatus: The Early Fruiter

Panaeolus subbalteatus, known by the slang name "Red Cap," is significant
for fruiting earlier in the year than most Psilocybe species -- it begins
appearing in spring. In Europe, it was responsible for a notable poisoning
incident in Leipzig in 1970, when specimens growing in a commercial
cultivation area were mistaken for the Giant Stropharia (Stropharia
rugoso-annulata). The species grows rapidly and often fruits in artificial
growing areas before the cultivated species, making it a frequent "invader"
of mushroom farms.
