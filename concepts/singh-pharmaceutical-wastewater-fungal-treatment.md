# Pharmaceutical Wastewater Treatment by Fungi

Pharmaceutical industry wastewaters represent a complex and challenging
class of environmental pollutants. Little attention has historically been
paid to the release of pharmaceutical substances into the environment,
which are recognized as environmental micropollutants with specific
biological effects. Singh's review documents [[singh-biopile-fungal-treatment-contaminated-soil]] approaches
for pharmaceutical wastewaters, including biofilm processes, anaerobic
biodegradability assessment, and the broader challenges of treating these
complex waste streams.

## Pharmaceutical Wastewater Characteristics

Pharmaceutical wastewaters are characterized by high chemical oxygen
demand (COD), toxicity, and the presence of persistent organic materials
and phosphorus. The Swedish Environmental Protection Agency investigated
pharmaceutical plant wastewaters and found them to be highly toxic. At
the Snackviken plant in Sweden, approximately 1000 cubic meters per day
of wastewater with a COD of 3 to 4 g/l is discharged. At the Gartuna
plant, the flow is approximately 100 cubic meters per day with a COD of
0.8 to 1.5 g/l. Production of the majority of drugs leads to large
variations in wastewater composition. Continuous development of new drugs
and production methods also causes changes in wastewater characteristics
over time, complicating treatment process design.

## Environmental Fate of Pharmaceuticals

Several substances of pharmaceutical waste persist in the environment.
Antibiotics employed as growth promoters or feed additives for a variety
of animals often end up in the environment. Few studies have reported
results of medical substances in field samples other than those of
sediment or treated wastewater. Current knowledge of the occurrence,
fate, and effects of pharmaceutical wastes in the environment, including
different exposure routes and legislation, has been described by
Halling-Sorensen et al. (1998). The persistence of pharmaceutical
compounds in environmental matrices raises concerns about the development
of antibiotic resistance, endocrine disruption, and chronic ecological
effects at low concentrations.

## Conventional Treatment Approaches

At present, activated carbon is used to treat the most toxic portion of
pharmaceutical wastewater. Wastewater from the Snackviken plant was
previously treated by evaporation in combination with reverse osmosis;
today, it is incinerated. The final forms of pharmaceutical wastes have
been characterized through studies of formulation and preparation plant
processes, which include mixing of solid or liquid substances, pelletizing,
encapsulating, and packing. These operational processes do not produce
excessive wastes. The overall BOD of pharmaceutical waste ranges from
44 to 752 mg/l. Treatment typically includes pH control, 12-hour holding
in an equalization basin, primary clarification, trickling filtration,
and secondary clarification. Sludges are removed, dewatered, and disposed
of in a landfill. The BOD of the final effluent is reduced to 30 mg/l.

## Multistage Biofilm Process

In three pilot-scale tests, the multistage biofilm process showed the
elimination of 90% of the COD and 95% of the total organic carbon (TOC)
with better removal of toxicity (Rosen et al., 1998). This fungal-based
biofilm process represents a promising approach for pharmaceutical
wastewater treatment. The disadvantage of this process is that the pH
must be adjusted to 4 in the fungal stages and then raised to 7 for
subsequent treatment stages. This pH adjustment adds operational
complexity and cost but enables the sequential exploitation of fungal
and bacterial degradation capabilities within a single treatment train.

## Anaerobic Biodegradability Testing

Methanogenic activity tests are used for the determination of anaerobic
biodegradability of concentrated pharmaceutical wastes. Specific waste
streams studied include waste biomass from threonine production, mycelium
after Penicillium extraction, and excess activated sludge from the
treatment of other pharmaceutical wastewaters (Zabranska et al., 1994).
Anaerobic biodegradability of each waste and mixtures of wastes was
evaluated. An optimum substrate concentration and methane yield for each
waste and mixture were determined. These tests provide essential data for
designing anaerobic treatment stages following aerobic fungal treatment.

## Role of Fungal Treatment

Organic material, phosphorus, and toxicity can be removed to a higher
degree by the incorporation of fungal treatment stages. [[query-how-do-fungi-contribute-to-carbon-sequestration]]
to pharmaceutical wastewater treatment through several mechanisms: the
production of extracellular enzymes that degrade complex organic
molecules, biosorption of toxic compounds by fungal biomass, and the
ability to tolerate and degrade xenobiotic compounds that are resistant
to bacterial attack. The fungal mycelial structure provides extensive
surface area for both enzymatic activity and biosorption, making fungal
biofilm reactors particularly effective for complex waste streams.

## Process Development Considerations

Under conditions of variable wastewater composition, it is necessary to
develop stable and cost-effective treatment methods. Treatability studies
for the degradation of specific compounds in batch and continuous
laboratory-scale processes are recommended before application in
pilot-scale tests. When researching and developing new drugs and synthetic
methods for compounds of pharmaceutical interest, it is important to
consider the biodegradability of discharges to wastewaters. This proactive
approach to green chemistry design could reduce the environmental burden
of pharmaceutical manufacturing waste streams.

## Integrated Treatment Strategies

The most effective pharmaceutical wastewater treatment likely involves
integrated strategies combining multiple biological and physicochemical
processes. A fungal biofilm stage can be followed by conventional
activated sludge treatment, with [[biogas-digester-basics]] of the resulting
sludges for biogas production. The multistage biofilm process
demonstrates that sequential fungal and bacterial treatment can achieve
high removal efficiencies for both COD and toxicity. Future developments
may include membrane bioreactors with [[gadd-fungal-biofilms-quorum-sensing]], constructed
wetlands with fungal-augmented plant systems, and advanced oxidation
processes coupled with biological treatment.
