## Wild Rice Aquaculture

Wild rice (Zizania spp.) represents one of the most recently domesticated grain crops and a uniquely valuable component of cold-climate aquacultural systems. Native to the regions of Canada and the United States from Minnesota to Florida, this tall annual grass (reaching 2.5 m) has been gathered as a grain for centuries by Amerindian tribes and brought into formal cultivation only within the last 30 years.

## Species and Provenance

Several perennial species exist within the Zizania genus. For culture trials, seed should be collected from selected plants at the climatic and water provenance suited to the culture site. Of the approximately 3 million kg produced annually, about 50% is still wild-gathered in traditional fashion by Amerindians, who beat out the grains into a canoe. The remainder comes from extensive fields of up to 100 hectares created by modern growers.

Wild rice is peculiarly suited to cold swamps and shallows with intense summer heat. The original culture was for duck flyway (wildlife) seed, and wild ducks harvest perhaps 70% of the total production, as the seed ripens slowly over 3–4 weeks.

## Yield Characteristics

Commercial yields are relatively low at 50–100 kg/ha, but prices are high ($10/kg in 1984), meaning fields of 100 hectares or so can generate annual cash returns of up to $100,000 from rice alone. The problem with yield is not in the plant itself but in the mechanical limitation of harvesting all at one time. Home plots gathered over 3–4 weeks are in fact very productive, and only 200–800 plants should supply a family if well cared for.

Ideal spacing is 20 × 35 cm, and for small plots with selected heads, yield can reach 200–500 g per head. Mechanical harvest gathers only the seed which is ripe at harvest time, leaving significant yield on the table.

## Traditional Harvest Strategies

Amerindian peoples developed a strategy to save grain from predation and shattering: bundle and tie 10–20 heads together, then untie them periodically for threshing into canoes. This prevents seed loss from shattering while allowing staged harvest.

## Seed Handling and Planting

Seed is saved from selected plants and held over winter in bags under water in icy lakes, or in water in refrigerators at 40°F. The seed requires cold stratification to germinate. It is sown in spring in fields flooded to about 16–20 cm deep and harvested from mid-autumn. Fields are dried out for 2–3 weeks before machine harvesting and must be rested or sown to another crop to clean the ground if a new cultivar is to be introduced, as self-seeding otherwise perpetuates the first-sown variety.

## Associated Polyculture

In the large fields and deep margin ditches of Minnesota, a self-generated polyculture has evolved alongside wild rice culture:

- **Crayfish** (Homarus spp.) live in ditches and browse 5–10 m into the crop, causing some damage but serving as a harvestable crop themselves or as food for mink
- **Mink and beaver** inhabit the system edges
- **Thousands of waterfowl** utilize the fields during migration and breeding
- **Coyote** range the bunds as predators
- **Packrats** store significant quantities of cleaned grain

### The Packrat Harvest Strategy

An intriguing potential lies in the propensity of packrats to carry and store large quantities of sound, cleaned grain into artificial shelters (like engine manifolds), insulated and plugged by Typha (cumbungi, cattail) seed silk. Narrow fields with artificial storage sites may be the most efficient harvest method. Packrats could be allowed to keep 15% of their stores. They may well be the most efficient, as well as the cleanest, harvesters for the crop—certainly an improvement over machines costing $300,000 or more that gather less than 10% of available grain.

A similar strategy is used by [[bill-mackently]], who employs grey squirrels to collect hickory nuts and acorns, providing them with buried pipes to store the nuts and leaving them about 15% of the harvest.

## Supplementary Products

Duck fattening, crayfish harvest, mussel meats, fish production, and vine crops of hops or silverberries ([[actinidia-arguta]]) are obvious supplementary products. Together with restricted fur production, specialty forestry, and recreational fishing or tour potential, wild rice culture can support a diverse farm enterprise.

## Undeveloped Potential

An obvious but undeveloped potential exists for coldwater fish-rearing in the canals and ditches of the system. Species such as trout and bullheads could thrive in these waterways. Manuring by [[azolla]] is not currently practiced, and the fields are rested dry in winter—a condition that could potentially be modified for year-round production.

## Duck Potato as Companion Weed

Duck potato (Sagittaria) can become a weed of the system, but it serves dual purpose as waterfowl food. It prefers somewhat deeper water than the rice plant and can be managed through cultural practices or hand weeding if necessary.

## Recommendations

A small terrace or pond of wild rice should be the aim of every home gardener who can obtain seed and who enjoys a nutritious grain. For wildlife refuges, especially those serving waterfowl, there is no better autumn fodder crop. The integration of wild rice into polycultural aquacultural systems represents one of the most promising yet underutilized opportunities in temperate [[permaculture]].

## Environmental Considerations

Water management decisions affect downstream ecosystems and
communities. Sustainable approaches balance human needs with
ecosystem requirements for maintaining aquatic habitats and water
quality. Wetland restoration provides natural filtration and flood
control services. Riparian buffer zones protect waterways from
agricultural runoff. Integrated water resource management considers
the entire watershed as an interconnected system.

## Ecological Integration

Water features in sustainable systems serve multiple functions beyond
irrigation. Ponds and wetlands support beneficial insect populations
that provide pest control. Aquaculture integration produces protein
while nutrient-rich water fertilizes crops. Edge plantings around
water features create wildlife corridors and windbreaks. Water storage
moderates microclimate temperatures, reducing frost risk in adjacent
growing areas. These multi-functional designs exemplify permaculture
principles of stacking functions and beneficial connections.

These integrated systems demonstrate the potential of aquaculture to produce nutritious food while maintaining ecological integrity.

## System Design

Water management integrates multiple strategies for capture, storage,
and efficient distribution. Swales slow surface runoff promoting
infiltration. Ponds store water for dry-season irrigation while
providing habitat. Keyline design optimizes distribution across
landscapes by identifying natural drainage patterns. Drip irrigation
delivers water directly to root zones, minimizing evaporation losses.

## Water Quality

Maintaining quality requires attention to biological, chemical, and
physical parameters. Biological filtration through wetlands removes
pathogens and excess nutrients. pH management ensures nutrient
availability. Dissolved oxygen supports aerobic decomposition.
Regular testing for contaminants protects system integrity.
