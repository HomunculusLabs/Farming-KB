## A Tree as a Universe

Every large tree is a universe in itself, offering many specialty-forage niches to bird, mammal, and invertebrate species. Mollison provides a detailed example from Tasmanian forests:

- **Yellow-throated honeyeaters** search knot-holes for insects
- **Treecreepers** probe bark fissures
- **Strong-billed honeyeaters** work the rolls of branch bark and hanging bark strips
- **Black-headed honeyeaters** scan the foliage for scale insects
- **Pardalotes** specialise on scale insects as their exclusive field

These birds also partition their time differently:
- Yellow-throats are permanent, territory-holding residents
- Treecreepers are migrants
- Strongbills and blackheads are roving flock species
- All scatter as breeding pairs in spring and summer

It is rare to find any one tree fully occupied at any one time. There is a pronounced post-breeding tendency for several bird species to form **consociations** for [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-animal-systems-in-permaculture]] provide thousands.

## Design Implications

For permaculture designers, niche theory suggests:

- **Design for multiple vertical layers** — root zone, ground cover, understory, canopy, and above-canopy niches
- **Include species that occupy different time-slots** — diurnal and nocturnal, seasonal and permanent
- **Use animal schedules** to maximise forage utilisation without overgrazing
- **Create edge environments** where multiple niche types overlap
- **Avoid territorial species** when adding new elements — choose cooperative species that yield space when the time is right
- **Observe natural scheduling patterns** before designing interventions
- **Remember that those who try to hold on to all things at all times prevent their use by others**
