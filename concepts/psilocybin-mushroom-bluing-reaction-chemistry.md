# Psilocybin Mushroom Bluing Reaction Chemistry

## Overview of the Bluing Reaction

A feature common to many [[stamets-african-psilocybin-mushroom-distribution]] species is the
bluing reaction. Many [[tropisms-psilocybe-cubensis-gravitropism]] and [[stamets-panaeolus-cyanescens-copelandia-identification-ecology]] species turn
bluish or bluish green when bruised. This occurs either as a normal
response to growing conditions or when the mushrooms are handled
during picking. The bluing reaction is of great interest to
chemists, pharmacologists, and mushroom taxonomists alike. It is
considered a valuable characteristic for delineating groups of
species within the psilocybin-containing genera.

## Biochemical Mechanism

The blue pigmentation appears to result from a phenomenon
paralleling the degradation of unstable psilocin (dephosphorylated
psilocybin) to presently unknown compounds by enzymes within the
mushroom cells. When a Psilocybe or Panaeolus bruises bluish, the
color reaction serves as a co-indicator that psilocin is or was
present. Since the bluing phenomenon appears to be a parallel
decomposition sequence, the more the mushrooms are bruised, the
less potent they become. Despite decades of study, no one has been
able to definitively pinpoint the chemical structure of the bluing
compound. Its elusive nature has been surprising to the chemists
who have attempted to characterize it.

## The Psilocin Connection

The bluing reaction is specifically linked to psilocin rather than
psilocybin. Psilocin (4-HO-DMT) is the dephosphorylated, active
metabolite of psilocybin (4-PO-DMT). Species that are high in
psilocin tend to show strong bluing reactions, while species that
are high in psilocybin but low in psilocin may show little or no
bluing. For example, [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] is high in
psilocybin but low in psilocin, and rarely bruises bluish. In this
species, the strength of the bluing reaction is not an indication
of activity, unlike the majority of potent species that are
comparatively higher in psilocin.

## Potency Implications

Because bluing represents oxidative degradation of psilocin,
extensive bruising directly reduces the potency of affected
mushrooms. Careful handling during harvest is therefore important
for preserving the psychoactive constituents. This degradation
pathway also suggests that psilocin-rich species may have shorter
shelf lives than those primarily containing psilocybin, as the
more labile psilocin is both responsible for bluing and more
susceptible to breakdown during storage.

## Limitations as an Identification Tool

The bluing reaction has limited importance from the taxonomist's
point of view for precise [[mushroom-identification]]. Many active
Psilocybe and Panaeolus species will not blue regardless of how
much they are handled, and there are several poisonous and suspect
species outside these genera that exhibit near-bluing reactions
without containing any psilocybin or psilocin. The bluing reaction
alone is never sufficient for species-level identification.

## Dangerous Look-alikes That Blue

Several non-active or toxic species exhibit bluing or bluish-black
discoloration that could be mistaken for the psilocybin-related
bluing reaction. Hygrophorus conicus and allies turn brilliantly
bluish black when disturbed and can be toxic, causing severe
diarrhea. Inocybe Calamistrata has a stem that is often blue
black at the base, though it does not contain psilocybin. Many
poisonous muscarine-producing Inocybe species appear nearly
identical to the untrained collector. Mushrooms of the deadly
genus [[stamets-psi-galerina-deadly-look-alikes-safety]] may have stems that darken blackish from the
base upwards, which could be called bluish black, especially at
the base. Many mushrooms darken in this region from handling, so
collectors should not be too eager to imagine a bluing reaction
when the mushroom is simply darkening naturally.

## Species with Blue Tones and Unknown Activity

Several mushroom genera contain species with bluish tones whose
psilocybin status remains unresolved. In the genus Mycena, which
produces white spores, about a half dozen species have bluish
tones or turn bluish at the base. Despite repeated analyses,
no psilocybin or psilocin has been detected in Mycena amicta,
Mycena cyanorhiza, Mycena pelianthina, or Mycena pure. However,
anecdotal accounts of activity from ingesting blue-footed Mycenas
have been reported by experienced observers. A luminescent Mycena
from Malaysia, M. chlorophos, produces white mycelium that bruises
blue when cut, yet analyses have failed to detect psilocybin,
psilocin, or baeocystin. Whether the bluing in these Mycenas is
chemically similar to that in psilocybin mushrooms remains an open
question warranting further research.

## Practical Field Application

With appropriate caveats, the bluing reaction can serve as a
primary parameter for narrowing the field of candidates to
psilocybin-containing varieties with dark purplish brown to black
spores. The bluing reaction is not very useful for determining the
identity of individual species, nor is it useful for identifying
all active species. However, as an easy-to-observe feature, the
bluing reaction in combination with other characteristics,
particularly spore color, greatly narrows the field to a small
pool of candidates. The genus [[tropisms-psilocybe-cubensis-gravitropism]] is further
characterized by a separable gelatinous pellicle, nut-brown
hygrophanous caps, whitish gill edges, and typically collyboid or
mycenoid aspect. The genus [[stamets-panaeolus-cyanescens-copelandia-identification-ecology]] consists of primarily
grassland and dung-inhabiting species with mottled gills and
blackish spore deposits. Combining bluing reaction observation
with spore print color assessment provides the most reliable
practical field screening method for separating potentially
active mushrooms from inactive or toxic species in the wild.

## Research Directions

The identity of the bluing compound(s) remains one of the enduring
unsolved problems in [[psilocybin-mushroom-chemistry]]. Modern
analytical techniques including HPLC-MS and NMR spectroscopy may
eventually resolve this question. Understanding the bluing
chemistry could have practical implications for assessing mushroom
potency and for forensic identification of psilocybin-containing
materials. The observation that some non-[[tropisms-psilocybe-cubensis-gravitropism]] genera
produce similar bluing reactions raises fundamental questions about
whether a common biochemical pathway exists across unrelated
fungal lineages.
