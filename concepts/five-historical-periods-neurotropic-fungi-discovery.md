# Five Historical Periods of Neurotropic Fungi Discovery

## Overview

The study of neurotropic (hallucinogenic) fungi has progressed through
five distinct historical periods, as outlined by Stamets (1978, 1996)
and updated by Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (1998) in their comprehensive
worldwide distribution study. These periods span from ancient indigenous
use through to modern taxonomic and chemical research, reflecting the
evolving relationship between human cultures and psychoactive fungi.

## Period 1: Ancient Indigenous Use

The earliest period encompasses the traditional use of neurotropic
fungi by ancient peoples across multiple continents. This period is
defined by pre-scientific, ceremonial, and shamanic use of
hallucinogenic mushrooms. The most significant centers of ancient use
include:

- **Mesoamerica**: The Aztec, Mazatec, Mixtec, Zapotec, Chinantec,
  Trique, and other indigenous peoples of Mexico used psilocybian
  mushrooms in [[metzner-shamanic-healing-and-divination-paradigm]] ceremonies. The Nahuatl name
  "teonanacatl" (divine mushroom), first recorded by Sahagun
  (1569-1582), is the most famous term, though many other indigenous
  names existed including "apipiltzin" (kid), "atkad" (mayor/leader),
  "di nize taaya" (fungus of the genius), and "shi tho" (that
  eruptions thing).

- **Siberia**: The Chukchee and Koryak peoples used Amanita muscaria
  (fly agaric) as a sacred intoxicant. Wasson and Wasson (1957)
  documented the central role of this mushroom in Siberian shamanism.

- **New Guinea**: Several aboriginal groups in Papua New Guinea used
  species of Psilocybe, Boletus, and Heimiella as sacred mushrooms,
  as reported by Singer (1958, 1960a), Heim and Wasson (1965), and
  Heim (1965a, 1978).

- **North America**: Evidence suggests possible pre-Columbian use of
  neurotropic fungi by indigenous peoples, though documentation is
  more limited than for Mesoamerica.

## Period 2: Early Erroneous Studies (Early 20th Century)

The second period was characterized by uncertain or erroneous studies
about the identification of neurotropic fungi. Key milestones include:

- **Schultes (1939)** initially stated that the neurotropic fungi in
  Mexico belonged only to [[panaeolus-campanulatus]] var. sphinctrinus,
  a position later followed by Singer (1949).
- **Singer (1949)** also considered Psilocybe cubensis based on
  some unidentified materials from collections by Schultes in
  Mexico, contributing to early [[growing-gourmet-pleurotus-sajor-caju-taxonomic-confusion]].
- The scientific community initially believed that neurotropic fungi
  only occurred in Mexico, a misconception that persisted until
  specimens were documented from other continents.

This period was marked by the fundamental challenge of connecting
[[psilocybin-chemistry-stability-indigenous-knowledge]] with Western taxonomic frameworks, complicated
by the complexity of fungal taxonomy and the difficulty of obtaining
reliable field collections.

## Period 3: Scientific Investigations (1950s Onward)

The third period began with the groundbreaking studies of R. Gordon
Wasson in the 1950s, which launched modern ethnomycological and
chemical research on neurotropic fungi. Major contributors included:

- **Wasson and Wasson (1957)**: Brought international attention to
  the sacred mushrooms of Mexico through their Life magazine article.
- **Heim (1956a, 1956b, 1957a, 1957c, 1958a, 1958b)**: Roger Heim
  conducted extensive taxonomic and cultivation studies, working with
  Wasson to describe and cultivate Mexican neurotropic species.
- **Singer (1949, 1958, 1959, 1960a)** and **Singer and Smith
  (1958)**: Rolf Singer provided critical taxonomic revisions.
- **Hofmann**: Albert Hofmann isolated and characterized psilocybin
  and psilocin, the active compounds, and determined their chemical
  structures.
- **Guzman (1959, 1977a, 1978b, 1982, 1983, 1990a, 1990b)**:
  Gaston Guzman conducted extensive fieldwork and published the
  definitive monograph on the genus Psilocybe.

Guzman (1990a, 1990b) divided the development of Mexican mycology
into two periods: before Wasson, Heim, and Singer's studies (before
1954-1958) and after their studies, underscoring the transformative
impact of this period on mycology as a whole.

## Period 4: Recreational Drug Use Era (1960s)

The fourth period saw the utilization of neurotropic fungi as a
recreational drug, accompanied by a degeneration of traditional
ceremonial use, particularly in Mexico. Key developments included:

- The spread of psychedelic culture in the 1960s popularized
  hallucinogenic mushrooms beyond their traditional cultural
  contexts.
- Commercial cultivation techniques, particularly the PF TEK
  developed by Psilocybe Fanaticus (McPherson), made home
  cultivation accessible.
- An underground market developed for the illegal trade of these
  fungi in the USA, Canada, Europe, and Australia.
- Governments of many countries responded by prohibiting the use
  and commerce of hallucinogenic fungi.
- The tropical species Psilocybe cubensis began appearing in Europe
  as illegally cultivated or dried specimens imported from tropical
  countries.

This period represented both the broadening of awareness about
psychoactive fungi and the beginning of their commercialization and
associated legal restrictions.

## Period 5: Modern Recapitulation (Present)

The current fifth period involves a recapitulation of knowledge, with
renewed descriptions of new species and more sophisticated chemical
analyses. Developments include:

- Discovery of new species of neurotropic fungi from the USA, Mexico,
  Colombia, Puerto Rico, Spain, Thailand, New Zealand, and South
  Africa (Gartz et al., 1995; Stamets and Gartz, 1995; Guzman
  1998a, 1999b).
- Improved chemical analytical techniques (HPLC, GC-MS) providing
  more reliable detection and quantification of psychoactive
  compounds.
- Critical reassessment of earlier chemical studies, with many
  previously reported species being re-evaluated.
- Growing recognition of the diversity and complexity of neurotropic
  fungi distribution, with acknowledgment that many regions remain
  poorly explored (southeastern Russia, Mongolia, Arabia, Turkey,
  and much of Africa).

The five-period framework illustrates how the study of neurotropic
fungi has evolved from indigenous knowledge through scientific
discovery, popularization, and into a mature phase of critical
reevaluation and continued exploration.
