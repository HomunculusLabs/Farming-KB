# Psychedelic Plants and Human Coevolution

Terence McKenna proposed that the relationship between human beings and psychoactive plants was not merely cultural or recreational but fundamentally evolutionary. In *Food of the Gods* (1992), he argued that the early hominids' adoption of an omnivorous diet and their discovery of the power of certain plants were decisive factors in moving early humans out of the stream of animal evolution and into the fast-rising tide of language and culture. This coevolutionary hypothesis places plant-human relationships at the center of human origins.

## The Omnivore's Advantage

The mutation-inducing influence of diet on early humans and the effect of exotic metabolites on the evolution of their neurochemistry and culture remains largely unstudied territory. This gap in scientific knowledge is itself remarkable, given the centrality of diet to every aspect of human biology.

Early hominids' adoption of an omnivorous diet opened access to an enormous variety of plant [[forensic-identification-methods-psilocybin-mushrooms-tlc-gc-hplc]] grow in the dung of grazing herbivores—precisely the environments where early hominids foraged on the African savanna.

The hypothesis proceeds as follows: early hominids following herds of grazing animals would have regularly encountered psilocybin mushrooms growing in dung. These mushrooms are visually distinctive and would have attracted attention. The question is not whether early humans encountered them—they certainly did—but what happened when they ate them.

The answer, according to McKenna, depends critically on dosage:

- **Low doses (approximately 0.5–1.0 grams dried)**: Psilocybin enhances visual acuity and edge detection. Colors become more vivid, patterns more discernible. This is a direct practical advantage for hunting, tracking, and predator detection. A hunter who can see slightly better has a survival advantage.
- **Moderate doses (approximately 2–3 grams dried)**: Psilocybin increases sexual arousal and social bonding. The mushroom experience at this level promotes pair bonding, group cohesion, and reproductive behavior—factors that enhance the evolutionary fitness of individuals and groups that use the mushrooms.
- **High doses (approximately 5+ grams dried)**: Psilocybin produces full-blown visionary experiences—hallucinations, mystical insights, encounters with seemingly autonomous entities. These experiences may have laid the foundation for religious thought, art, music, and the concept of the sacred. The emergence of the idea of the sacred—of a realm beyond the material—may be directly traceable to plant-induced visionary experiences.

This dose-dependent range of effects means that the same mushroom could provide immediate practical benefits at low doses (better hunting) and long-term cultural benefits at higher doses (religious experience, social bonding through shared visions). Natural selection would favor individuals who sought out these mushrooms, creating a feedback loop between mushroom consumption and cognitive capacity. Over thousands of generations, this feedback loop could have driven the expansion of the human neocortex and the emergence of language, art, and religion.

## Addictions and the Human-Plant Relationship

McKenna argued that addictions and obsessions are unique to human beings among animals. While anecdotal evidence supports a preference for intoxicated states among elephants, chimpanzees, and some butterflies, these animal behaviors are enormously different from the systematic, culturally embedded use of psychoactive plants characteristic of humans. Human chemical dependence plays a more conscious role than ever before in the formation and maintenance of cultural values.

The rise of a global information culture has led to the ubiquity of information on the recreational, aphrodisiacal, stimulating, sedative, and psychedelic plants discovered by inquisitive human beings living in remote and previously unconnected parts of the planet. At the same time, great strides have been made in the synthesis of complex organic molecules and in understanding the molecular machinery of genes and heredity. These new technologies are contributing to a culture of psychopharmacological engineering—designer drugs such as MDMA and anabolic steroids are harbingers of an era of ever more frequent and effective pharmacological intervention in how humans look, perform, and feel.

The human relationship with psychoactive plants is not merely pharmacological but symbiotic. Plants produce psychoactive compounds for their own evolutionary reasons—typically as defenses against herbivores or as attractants for seed-dispersing animals. The fact that these compounds affect human consciousness so profoundly is, in McKenna's view, evidence of a deep coevolutionary relationship. The plants "want" to be consumed by humans and other animals because human use ensures their propagation. Psychoactive plants are among the most widely cultivated and traded plant species on Earth precisely because of the effects they produce.

This symbiotic relationship raises profound questions about the nature of plant intelligence and communication. If plants can produce compounds that interface so precisely with animal nervous systems, is this merely coincidence, or does it suggest a form of cross-kingdom signaling that science has yet to fully appreciate?

The evidence for some form of coevolution is strongest in the case of compounds like caffeine. Plants produce caffeine as an insecticide, yet humans have evolved to not merely tolerate it but to seek it out and derive cognitive benefits from it. The near-universality of caffeine consumption across human cultures—coffee, tea, chocolate, kola, guarana—suggests a deep coevolutionary history that McKenna's hypothesis seeks to generalize to all psychoactive plant compounds.

## The Shift from Symbiosis to Suppression

The immense changes in human evolution occurred largely as a result of synergies between human beings and the various plants with which they interacted and coevolved. An honest appraisal of the impact of plants on the foundations of human institutions would find them to be absolutely primary. Yet in historical times, this symbiotic relationship was systematically disrupted.

McKenna traced this disruption to the transition from partnership societies to [[mckenna-paradise-regained-dmt-hyperspace]]?"—frames human history as a story of gaining, losing, and potentially recovering the transformative relationship with nature that psychoactive plants represent.

McKenna identified the Ur-myth of Western culture—the Garden of Eden story—as a distorted memory of the loss of this plant-human relationship. The "fruit of the Tree of Knowledge" was, in his reading, a psychoactive plant that conferred expanded consciousness.

This interpretation is not unique to McKenna. R. Gordon Wasson proposed that the Soma of the Vedic scriptures was the fly agaric mushroom (*[[mckenna-consciousness-expansion-and-drug-war]] and its suppression. The serpent, traditionally identified with evil, becomes in McKenna's interpretation a benefactor—the agent that introduced humanity to the transformative plant. God's prohibition against eating the fruit becomes, in this reading, the dominator culture's prohibition against direct access to expanded consciousness.

## Implications for the Future

The mutation-inducing influence of diet and exotic plant metabolites on human neurochemistry remains an underexplored frontier. If McKenna's coevolutionary hypothesis has merit, it suggests that the human brain evolved in a chemical environment rich with plant psychoactives, and that modern human consciousness may be maladapted to the chemically impoverished environment of industrial civilization.

The recovery of this relationship—as McKenna proposed through what he called the "Archaic Revival"—would not mean a simple return to prehistoric conditions but a conscious integration of ancient plant wisdom with modern knowledge. The application of botanically inspired steady-state solutions, such as zero population growth and massive recycling programs, might help reorganize societies along more holistic, environmentally aware, neo-Archaic lines.
