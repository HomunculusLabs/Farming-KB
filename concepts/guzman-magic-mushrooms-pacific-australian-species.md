# Pacific and Australian Neurotropic Mushroom Species

## Overview

The Australasian and Pacific regions harbor a significant and distinctive
neurotropic mycoflora. Australia, New Zealand, and New Guinea together
support numerous species, many of which show close biogeographic
relationships to American taxa, supporting the hypothesis of Gondwanan or
trans-Pacific [[guzman-global-distribution-patterns-neurotropic-fungi]].

## Australia

Australia has a moderately rich [[mexico-center-neurotropic-fungal-biodiversity]] flora, documented
primarily by Hall (1973), Southcott (1974), Margot and Watling (1981),
and Allen et al. (1991).

### Australian Psilocybe Species

- **P. subaeruginosa**: The most common and widespread Australian
  neurotropic Psilocybe; found in southern Australia from autumn
  through winter; grows on decaying wood and wood chips; confirmed to
  contain psilocybin (Picker and Richards, 1970); extensively studied
  by Chang and Mills (1992)
- **P. australiana**: Described from Australia by Margot and Watling
  (1981); related to the subtropical species complex
- **P. cubensis**: Common in tropical and subtropical regions of
  Australia; found on bovine dung in northern regions
- **P. cyanescens**: Present in Australia (Margot and Watling, 1981)
- **P. semilanceata**: Found in southeastern Australia; confirmed by
  multiple studies (Allen et al., 1991)
- **P. eucalypta**: Described from Australia; associated with Eucalyptus
  forests
- **P. collybioides**: Found in Australia; however, Guzman (1983)
  noted this is not the same species as reported from Argentina;
  Australian material is synonymous with P. zapotecorum
- **P. subcubensis**: Present in Australia
- **P. tasmaniana**: Named for Tasmania; found in Tasmania and
  southeastern Australia

### Australian Non-Psilocybe Species

- **[[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]]**: Introduced and naturalized; documented by
  Cleland (1976)
- **Copelandia cyanescens**: Present; confirmed by Pollock (1976)
- **[[gerronema-fibula]]**: Documented from Australia

## Tasmania

Tasmania supports several neurotropic species:
- **P. australiana**: Found in Tasmania
- **P. cubensis**: Present in Tasmania
- **P. semilanceata**: Confirmed from Tasmania
- **P. subaeruginosa**: Common in Tasmania (Picker and Rickards, 1970)
- **P. tasmaniana**: Named for and found in Tasmania
- **Copelandia cyanescens**: Present in Tasmania (Allen et al., 1991)

## New Zealand

New Zealand has a notable neurotropic mycoflora documented by Allen et al.
(1991), Guzman et al. (1991, 1993b), and Johnston and Buchanan (1995).

### New Zealand Endemic and Notable Species

- **P. aucklandii**: Endemic to New Zealand; described by Guzman,
  Bandala, and King (1991); closely related to P. zapotecorum from
  Mexico and South America, demonstrating trans-Pacific biogeographic
  connections
- **P. makarorae**: Endemic to New Zealand; described by Johnston and
  Buchanan (1995); a bluing species unique to New Zealand
- **P. kumaenorum**: Also found in New Guinea; demonstrates New
  Zealand-New Guinea connection

### Other New Zealand Species

- **P. australiana**: Shared with Australia
- **P. collybioides**: Present in New Zealand (synonymous with P.
  zapotecorum)
- **P. cubensis**: Present in New Zealand
- **P. eucalypta**: Shared with Australia
- **P. semilanceata**: Confirmed from New Zealand
- **P. subaeruginosa**: Present in New Zealand (Johnston and Buchanan,
  1995)
- **P. subcubensis**: Present in New Zealand
- **P. tasmaniana**: Shared with Australia and Tasmania
- **Amanita muscaria**: Present in New Zealand (introduced)
- **Copelandia cyanescens**: Present in New Zealand

## New Guinea Neurotropic Mycoflora

New Guinea represents a critically understudied region for neurotropic
fungi despite its extraordinary biodiversity. The island's vast
equatorial rainforests and highland ecosystems likely harbour numerous
undescribed species. Documented taxa include *Psilocybe kumaenorum*
from highland regions, first collected from the Kuman area, and
*Psilocybe subcubensis* reported from lowland tropical habitats. The
biogeographic affinities of New Guinean species are complex: some show
relationships to Australian taxa, others to Southeast Asian species,
and at least one (*P. kumaenorum*) connects to New Zealand flora.
Guzman and colleagues hypothesised that New Guinea may serve as a
biogeographic bridge between Asian and Australasian neurotropic fungal
distributions. The remoteness of many New Guinean collecting sites and
the difficulty of fieldwork in dense rainforest terrain have limited
systematic surveys, making this region a priority for future
mycofloristic exploration. Indigenous knowledge of psychoactive fungi
in New Guinea remains largely undocumented.

## Pacific Island Species and Distribution Patterns

The Pacific Islands have a comparatively sparse documented neurotropic
mycoflora, though this likely reflects collection effort rather than
true absence. *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]]* and *Copelandia cyanescens* are
the most widely reported species across tropical Pacific islands,
found on bovine dung in pastoral areas from Fiji to Samoa. In Hawaii,
which shares many biogeographic connections with the broader Pacific,
*Psilocybe cubensis*, *P. cyanescens*, and *P. subcubensis* are
established. The [[global-distribution-of-neurotropic-fungi]] fungi across the Pacific
poses interesting biogeographic questions: many species appear to have
arrived relatively recently through human-mediated dispersal,
particularly the dung-inhabiting species associated with introduced
livestock. Indigenous Pacific cultures generally lack well-documented
traditions of [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushroom-clubbing-surveys-and-prevalence]] use, unlike the rich ceremonial
traditions of Mesoamerica. This may reflect genuine cultural absence
or simply the loss of traditional knowledge through colonisation and
missionary activity over the past two centuries.
