What this book is
Hello. My name is William WR Ozier,
my friends call me Wes. As of the
writing of this book I have been
studying Sustainability, ecological
design and Permaculture since the year
2000. I have worked and taught at the
Arcosanti Project, the Ecosa Institute,
and the Lost Valley Educational Center.
I have earned a Permaculture Design
Certificate, an Ecovillage Design
Education Certificate and I am a
LEEDAP. I have worked on four
backyard scale EcoMachines and
various eco-building projects.
Me with the Summer 2009 Lost Valley PDC Class

This book is a distillation of my
understanding of Permaculture Design
based on the standard 72 hour
Permaculture course curriculum. To
write this book I simply wrote down
everything I would say if asked to
teach a 2 week long course by myself.
Reading this book alone does not
qualify one to have a Permaculture
Design Certificate. For that you need to
take a class with an instructor who has
earned a PDC and has a few years
experience. However this book will
serve as a text book perfect for those
either looking to run a Permaculture
Design Course, or for those who can't
make it to a course but are still
interested in learning the content of a
course.

Throughout the book you will see, in
addition to the text descriptions of the
topics, discussion points,
recommended readings, internet search
keywords and learning exercises.
These additional learning tools will
help you to digest and understand the
material beyond it just being words on
paper.

Of course let's not forget the BEST
research tool for understanding the
ecology...the outside. One of the
'media' this book uses for education is
Nature itself and you will be asked to
go outside from time to time.
Parts of this book are fact, parts of it
are perspective. A one thousand square
foot surface collects six hundred
gallons of water per inch of rain that
falls on it is a fact. The claim that the
International Style of Architecture is a
manifestation of Communist ideals is a
perspective. It is not necessary for you
to agree with all of my perspectives,
but it is important that you absorb the
facts.

What I am presenting to you is food for
thought on the subject, and I hope it is a
hearty and tasty meal. I do not ask that
you walk away from this book
believing what I believe, only that you
walk away from this book empowered
to develop your own understanding on
the subject. The longest journey begins
with the first step and reading this
book can be your first step, but it will
by no means be your last. Truly
understanding Permaculture, and
Permaculture design is a life time
endeavor. This book will help you to
have a framework for incorporating
that further learning into a
comprehensive world view and skill
set.

A note about gardens and plants:
Gardens and plants are specific
elements in a Permaculture system but
they are by no means the totality of
Permaculture. While gardens and
plants will be topics covered in this
book they will be covered from their
place in a Permaculture system. In
other words this is not a book about
gardening, or about plants, though this
book will give you guidance on how
gardens and plants can be used to
create holistic self sustaining
ecological systems.

Understanding Sustainability
As Permaculture is about creating
'sustainable' agricultural systems, and
ultimately a 'sustainable' culture, it is
important to take some time to
understand what 'Sustainability' means.
Below is a sound bite I like to use to
serve as a way to approach the
complex topics of Sustainability and
Permaculture:

Sustainability is the goal,
Ecological Design
is the method by which we can
achieve it,

Permaculture
is a set of guiding principles
on how to get there,
Green architecture and sustainable
landscapes are what we will build,
Intentional Community is where we
will live.

Once the buildings and infrastructures
are created the people of the culture
are committed to living out that
philosophy every day. Through
architecture the philosophical system
becomes engrained in them and they
accept it without conscious thought or
realization because they have no other
physical choice.

Before you can make a design that is
sustainable, you have to understand
what Sustainability means and what it
is. If your philosophy of sustainability
is incomplete, or incohesive your
designs and through them what you
manifest into the world will be as
well.

Before we discuss what Sustainability
is let's explore the history of how we
became nonsustainable.

I use an axiom with my students that
architecture is literally the physical
manifestation of philosophy. Let me
assure you that I do not take the word
'literally' lightly. When I say literally, I
mean that architecture is literally the
manifestation of philosophy. This
axiom is not a metaphor, or a
philosophical position. This is a solid,
real world fact. When I say
architecture I mean any buildings
and/or infrastructure system designed
and built by humans. All architecture
begins with a belief in how humans
should be living on this planet. Based
on this belief some force in society
commissions the creation of a building
or infrastructure system that will
enable people to live in accordance
with that belief. Skeptical? Think about
structures such as the Great Pyramids
or gothic cathedrals. Entire cultures
were shaped around these construction
projects, massive structures requiring
massive resources and had massive
impacts on their respective ecologies.
They made their belief a real physical
fact of the world.

I wonder what future archaeologists
who study our most prominent
buildings will determine was the
paramount belief of our culture?
The History of Modern Non-
Sustainability

When discussing how we became
nonsustainable I always point a square
finger of blame at the 'International
Style' of architecture as popularized by
the Bauhaus School of Architecture
and their contemporaries. It is the
International Style which has brought
us an explosion of concrete, steel,
brick, right angled structures, the
International Building Code and the
Modern world as we understand it.
The International Style has resulted in
an ecological and cultural catastrophe
for the world and it is important to
understand the philosophy behind it,
and why it was embraced as a positive
step forward for humanity.




The International Style was
predominantly born in Europe. What
many of these modern architects saw in
European culture was a history of
architecture being used to create
aggrandized manors and castles to
create a class stratified society. The
structures allowed the ruling class to
enjoy an opulent lifestyle and to horde
resources, such as food, medicines,
books, wealth and weapons, away
from the masses. This was dressed up
as Classical European Architecture. It
can be easily and well argued that
without this architectural paradigm
there could have been no monarchies
as we understand them as without the
architecture to support it they could not
have created the fortress' necessary to
enforce their rule.

It is a matter of historical controversy,
but some, myself included, believe that
the Bauhaus architects and their
contemporaries were driven by
Communist philosophy. It was their
political aim to use architecture to
change the European cultural
paradigm. They sought an architecture
that would eliminate class distinctions
and create a society of equality with
the highest possible standard of living
for all. Regardless if they were exactly
Communist or not, their new form of
architecture was literally the
manifestation of their humanitarian
philosophy.

To create an architecture that would
bring equality for all they rejected the
notions of regionalism and
traditionalism in design. Based on their
observations they believed regional
and traditional thinking held cultures
back from embracing new concepts,
keeping resource poor areas in
squallor while enabling a decadent
standard of living for resource rich
areas. With the rejection of regional
and traditional design came the
rejection of local resource use in
architecture. Modern construction
materials had to be shipped from far
off industrial centers which required
the creation of a massive transportation
infrastructure. Modern designs did not
allow for traditional cultural habits
and lifestyles. Can you imagine a group
of American Indians trying to have a
'pow-wow' in the lobby of a modern
day apartment building? With the loss
of these traditions we also lost the
wisdom of how to live best in harmony
with the environment.

One of the main functions of
architecture is to create comfortable
temperatures inside of the structure.
Across the world and throughout time
cultures developed traditional
architecture based on local materials
and appropriate strategies to create
these comfortable environments. With
the rejection of regionalism and
traditionalism by the International
Style, modern architecture has
replaced environmental design wisdom
with HVAC (Heating Ventilation and
Air Conditioning) systems that are
made from industrialized materials and
require electricity to run. Buildings are
no longer comfortable by design. They
are designed so that their comfort
requires a constant input of electricity
from a power plant that needs fuel to
be constantly harvested and burned.

The International Style incorporated
new feats in steel, concrete, glass and
chemical engineering to create
industrially mass produced
construction materials. Along with
these new industrialized materials
came strip mining, deforestation, and
an increased consumption of fossil
fuels to both produce and transport
these materials. Instead of living off
their own resources and traditional
livelihood those plugged into the
Modern architecture were now being
'paid' to reap their own natural
resources and send them away so they
could 'afford' to purchase and power
the modern architecture they had been
sold. Where empirial powers used to
accomplish this with martial force it
was now being done with architecture.
On an international scale laws and
government policies shifted and this
new, modern, standard of living was
exported all across the globe.

Not all of the architects associated
with the International Style came from
the Bauhaus school. Also instrumental
in the creation of the modern
International Style was the Swiss
architect LeCorbusier. His main area
of focus was on the design of a new
type of urban environment based on
these Modern principles. He pretty
much single handedly created what we
now know and recognize as the typical
skyscraper and road grid ridden
metropolitan down town. Ironically his
urban designs garnered ALOT of
criticism at the time and were rejected
by his culture, yet the basics of his
designs have become the template for
the contemporary redesign of almost
all major urban centers since his time.
An unforseen consequence of his
design theory was that as commercial,
business, industrial and apartment
complexes took over the city center
land prices became prohibitive to the
concept of owning a homestead within
the city.

Seeing the death of the American
homestead as a cultural travesty
American architect Frank Lloyd Wright
unveiled his design for 'Broad Acre
City', a design which called for single
family homes surrounded by a one acre
plot of land for food production, and a
surrounding road grid to allow
automobile access from the residents
to far off urban centers. These homes
would be affordable because the land
would be outside of the expensive city
center. While Broad Acre City only
existed on paper it presented an
influential vision of how to develop
housing to accommodate the newly
returning GIs of WWII and the
expanding baby boomer generation.

In the early 1940s the first modern
suburb called Levittown was
constructed. The land for the suburb
was viable agricultural land near the
city that was bought up and converted
to land for housing, a trend that would
become a standard strategy in suburban
expansion. This strategy destroyed the
local food resources of city after city,
and was the death knell of American
agrarian and naturalistic culture.
Levittown shared many design features
with Wright's Broad Acre City, but
instead of an acre of viable gardening
land each home had a small yard filled
only with lawns. Farm lands were
pushed further and further away from
the city, requiring petroleum dependent
food delivery infrastructures to feed
the populations. As the suburbs
extended out they chewed up the
ecosystems that ringed the cities, often
being named after the very ecological
features they destroyed such as Shady
Pines, Hidden Lakes, or Oakwoods.
As the Levittown model proved
financially viable it spread ravenously,
creating urban sprawl.

Working together the International
Style of Architecture, LeCorbusier
inspired city centers and the
bastardized version of Broad Acre
City that was the urban sprawl of the
Levittown model created a resource
hyper-consumptive system, reliant on
the automobile, that destroyed both
food security and local ecologies, and
caused a cultural degeneration. This
perfect storm of well intended but
destructive architecture is not the only
factor that has created our modern non-
sustainable world.
You cannot determine the
'Sustainability' of a society without
looking at how they produce their food.
The current philosophy of the modern
architecture of food production is
based on the 'Green Revolution', an
agricultural revolution started in the
late 50s and early 60s to increase the
world food supply.

This revolution had some
philosophical similarities with the
development of the International Style
of architecture. This revolution
depended on abandoning regional and
traditional food sources and relying on
the application of modern, industrial
'improvements' to food production.
One of the strategies of the Green
Revolution was to re-purpose the war
industry left from WWII into a food
industry, it was literally a swords to
plowshares card. Chemical plants that
produced explosive gun powder could
be modified to produce fertilizer (this
is why Timothy McVeigh was able to
buy fertilizer and blow up a building
with it), assembly lines that produced
tanks could be retooled to produce
combines and heavy machinery,
chemical weapons were modified to
become herbicides and pesticides.
Such a massive shift in food
production was financially viable
because much of the infrastructure was
already in place and paid for by the
war generation who helped by doing
such things as recycling metal and
rubber and growing their own food in
'victory gardens'. By the application of
large scale machinery, row farming,
chemical fertilizers, chemical
herbicides and pesticides food
production could be engineered to
produce greater yields. Later came the
application of laboratory driven
genetic engineering to modify plant
yields.
These 'improvements' did drastically
increase the exportable food supply of
modernized countries, and many
believe that this panacea of food was
one of the major factors why the world
population spiked! These new food
systems has caused massive ecological
impacts, all negative. The massive
mono-crop fields that stretch out over
the horizon destroy local ecologies, the
chemical run offs pollute local water
tables, the systems 'burn out' the
nutrient cycle in the local soil
requiring higher and higher chemical
inputs, and the food production systems
are completely dependent on gas
powered vehicles and equipment. With
this came the destruction of local
ecologies, resource hyper-consumption
and the destruction of hundreds of
generations of tried and true cultural
ecological wisdom.

In addition to all of this one of the main
culprits in creating a non-sustainable
world was the invention of waste, one
of the few true human inventions.
Synthetics as well as perfectly natural
materials that could decompose, such
as paper, wood and food are 'dumped'
in ways that do not allow the to
decompose. The ecology is an
inherently 'up-cycling' process, that is
it gets stronger over time, but by the
creation of waste we are down-cycling
the ecology, making it weaker and
weaker as time goes on.
So we can see that we are non-
sustainable due to our architecture and
infrastructure. It is quite obvious that to
become sustainable we need to change
our architecture and infrastructures.
However, our current architecture and
infrastructures are expressions of
philosophical systems about how
humans should live on the planet that
believe we should use engineering and
industry rather than ecological wisdom
to provide our standard of living.
While it may seem that I am being very
critical of the philosophy of the
modern era that is far from the truth.
With the exception that Levittown was
'for whites only', the international style
of architecture, the green revolution
and even the development of suburbs
all began from very humanitarian
positions that I agree with. These
design paradigms were created by
people attempting to provide food,
housing and improved lifestyles for all
equally. What the design concepts of
these systems missed was a
philosophical system based on the
notion of how humanity should live in
accordance with the environment. This
is where Permaculture Design comes
in, providing a design system that is
ethically similar to those that have
guided the modern era, yet integrating a
belief that humans should live in
harmony with the fluctuations of
Nature.

Where the Modern age rejects the
notion of regionalism and
traditionalism, Permaculture calls to
incorporate regionalism,
traditionalism, ecological wisdom, as
well as Modern thinking into one
design paradigm. For example,
Aquaponics systems are a modern
technology yet they are completely
Permaculture, from a Permaculture
point of view the Arcosanti Project is
in many ways a modern American
India Pueblo. So, don't yet reject any of
the things from the Modern era. Rather
hold on to them, and after reading this
book re-focus them through this new
lense, a Permaculture lense.

Defining Sustainability
So those are some, but by no means all,
of the factors that make the modern era
nonsustainable, but still the question
remains, what is Sustainability?

The current accepted and oft used
definition of Sustainability is:
development that meets the needs of
the present without compromising the
ability of future generations to meet
their own needs. This definition was
created in 1983 by the United Nation's
World Commission on Environment
and Development, known more
commonly as the Bruntland
Commission, so called after it's
chairperson Gro Harlem Brundtland.
While it is the recognized definition of
Sustainability, still some people feel it
is a little vague and needs further
clarification. So let's take it step by
step and fill in the gaps of the
definition.
The first part of the definition is
'development'. What is 'development'?
In it's simplest terms when the
Bruntland Commission used the phrase
development they were referring to the
creation of new architecture and
infrastructure systems. So we can
expand the definition by replacing
'development' with 'architecture and
infrastructure'. This gives us
Sustainability is: architecture and
infrastructure that meets the needs of
the present without compromising the
ability of future generations to meet
their own needs.

Based on a conversation with Tony
Brown, Director of the Ecosa Institute
when I look at this definition the thing
that sticks out the most to me as
requiring clarification is the concept of
'needs'. It seems to me in the modern
society we often confuse what we want
with what we need. Just as often when
people tell you what you need, really
they are just trying to sell you
something. So how do we determine an
objective standard of 'needs'? To
answer this question I turn to a theory
from psychology called Maslow's
Hierarchy of Needs, put forth in the
early 40s by a psychologist named
Abraham Maslow. While Maslow's
theory has many criticizable aspects, it
works for me as one of the best
objective standards of determining
what are truly needs as opposed to
what are wants.
At the bottom of Maslow's Hierarchy
are the physiological needs such as
healthy food, clean water to drink, rest,
health, etc. These are the needs of the
body to survive. Once the body has all
of it's physiological needs met the
psyche will then instinctively seek the
next level of the Hierarchy.
The second tier of Maslow's Hierarchy
is that of security. Essentially once the
body has it's needs the mind will want
to know these needs will be met into
the foreseeable future. An important
nuance to understand about the need for
security is that it is not about actual
security, it is about the perception of
security. While in the best case
scenario we would always hope that
the security one seeks is true security
in reality, however we must remember
the psyche can be fooled. One could in
actuality be perfectly secure yet still
feel insecure, one could be in imminent
danger yet believe they are secure and
safe.

Unfortunately in the case of this Need,
perception is everything and this
perception can be wrong. So while
what the psyche truly needs is the
perception of security, we also hope
that one is in a smart enough situation
that they have true security.

Once the body has it's physiological
needs met, and the psyche is
comfortable in it's perception of
security the psyche then seeks
relationships. The human psyche needs
a full range of relationships,
friendship, family, acquaintances,
romantic relationships and a sense of
belonging in a group. I interpret this
need as a need for belonging in a
community, because in a community
you will find the opportunity for all of
these relationships. Also I believe that
these relationships extend to
relationships with more than just
humans, and includes relationships
with animals and even plant species.
While this may seem a stretch I believe
it explains the desire for pets, and
houseplants, why zoos are so exciting,
the love of gardens in the yard, bird
watching, and many other relationships
with animals and plants that humans
seek out. So when I translate this as a
need for community, it is not just a
community of humans, but the need to
have community with a whole diverse
grouping of species. Part of belonging
in a community is also having a
unifying cultural identity that denotes
one as a member of a community.

Maslow' Hierarchy believes that as a
social animal all humans have not only
a need to be in a community, but an
evolved need to know that they are a
positive contributor to the community.
This need translates as the need for ego
gratification. While this may seem
shallow to many people since we
villify excessive egotism, this is not
the self interested form of ego but
rather the need for the feedback that
builds a positive sense of self esteem.
As individuals we seek positive
esteem in many different ways, some of
them healthy, some not, but they all
stem from the same need. Any one
remember the gold star when you were
a kid? Children in school freak out to
get the gold star, and when they get it
are insufferably pleased, why? The
gold star isn't of any tangible benefit, it
doesn't taste good, it isn't even fun to
play with, all in all it's pretty
frivolous. However it feeds the ego's
need for positive self esteem because
the gold star says you did something of
value, and in saying that the gold star is
invaluable. Metaphorically speaking
this need for ego gratification is the
need for gold stars. Metaphorically
speaking our society offers many types
of gold stars for children and adults. A
question for you the reader, what is the
latest gold star you have received?
Understanding this need may help you
to understand the actions and
motivations of others better, and why
they seem to seek and place such
importance on seemingly frivolous
things.

Once we have our physiological needs
met, our need for security satisfied, we
have entered into community and
developed relationships, and are
recognized for our positive
contributions to our community the
final need will be that of self-
actualization. Simply put, this is the
ability to do what you want, but not in
a capricious manner, but in a manner of
making one's self a 'better person'.
Perhaps you want to be a painter, or
write a novel, be an ideal parent, or a
teacher of youth. Self-actualization is
about making yourself the best version
of you you can. Unfortunately too many
people believe that to actualize
themselves they need to control
others...those people probably did not
get some lower level need met and are
now overcompensating.
When one's needs are not met the
instinctive response is anger, and
violence. Think about, if you were
trying to eat lunch and I kept grabbing
your food away from you, I don't care
how pacifist you are you would
eventually get angry at me and try to
violently push me away. Imagine what
your reaction would be if I were
holding my hand over your mouth and
nose and cutting off your air supply.
The reaction of anger and violence is
the same for all levels of need,
however sometimes the anger and
violence does not have as easily
identifiable a target. For example if I'm
taking your lunch you have an easy
target to get angry at and focus your
violence on. If lunch just never shows
up on the table because a delivery
truck never brought the food then
perhaps a target for your anger would
not be so easy to identify, so now you
would have to conjecture and guess
who the target is. If you never could
conjecture and guess who the target is,
still your psyche would have the need
to be angry at someone for something.

Also the psychological mechanism for
violence has a subconscious governor
that does a risk-benefit analysis. For
example if it was me who took your
lunch you might try to swat me away, if
it was a grizzly bear probably not. Yet
though you may not swat away the
grizzly bear your psyche may still
contain the anger and try to let it out in
some other way. It is by this
psychological mechanism that we
sometimes miss-target our anger and
violence and instead of attacking the
true target in an effective way, we lash
out at a target of opportunity, or target
of least risk. For example, perhaps you
get passed up at work for a promotion,
well you can't hit your boss and get the
promotion, so maybe you come home
and hit your kids. Maybe your need for
violence urges you to vote for an
unnecessary war because by doing so
you get an outlet for your un-targeted
anger knowing that your tax dollars are
bombing people. In many cases the
anger and violence gets turned against
one's self and one develops a self
destructive neurosis, such as
alcoholism, anorexia or other
potentially fatal habits that in their own
way enact violence on your own body.

Understanding this quirk of the human
psyche has greatly helped me to
understand people's seemingly
senseless actions a lot better. Before
you read on I want you to stop and go
for a walk and think about this point or
engage someone in a discussion about
it because understanding this point may
be one of the most important things in
this book. Seriously, put the book

To sum up the Maslow's Hierarchy,
'Needs' are: Physiological needs of the
body, security, community, positive
esteem, and selfactualization. The
expanded definition of Sustainability
would then be: architecture and
infrastructure that provides the
physiological needs of the body,
security, community, opportunities for
positive esteem, and selfactualization
for the present generation without
compromising the ability of future
generations to do the same.

This leaves the notion of 'generations'.
How much time is a generation, how
many future generations are we talking
about? Nailing down an objective and
legitimate definition of 'generation' is
very tricky as it is a somewhat vague
phrase. I found that what was easier
than trying to define generation was to
look at our resource usage. In other
words if we use our resources in a
way that leaves an equal amount of
available resources when we are done
then no matter what your definition of
'generations' there will be resources
available to them. To that end we need
to use resources that are either
renewable, recyclable or re-usable.

Putting this all together we can create a
new, expanded definition of
Sustainability as thus: architecture and
infrastructure systems that use
renewable, recyclable, or re-usable
resources to provide the physiological
needs of the body, security, community,
positive esteem, and selfactualization.
With the caveat of 'renewable
resources' added into the definition I
believe we can, for purposes of
linguistic efficiency, leave the present
and future generation clauses out of the
final definition.
Many people may look at this
definition and think that the inclusion
of positive self esteem and self-
actualization enter into a realm of
personal satisfaction and life choices
that are outside the pervue of a
designer. For the most part I agree with
this. Designers do have some
responsibility to create infrastructures
that provide for all of the other needs
that lead up to this. As Maslow's
Hierarchy shows us, if you don't have
your basic needs met your psyche
cannot pursue the higher needs. So if
designers do not make architecture and
infrastructure that meets these lower
needs then the culture that lives within
those designs will not be able to meet
their higher needs, right? Right.

For sake of expedience I will jump
ahead here, and summarize that what I
came to realize is that achieving
positive self esteem and self
actualization are what I call lifestyle
choices. In other words those are
endeavors that are up to you to pursue
on your own responsibility within the
architecture and infrastructure systems
that provide your physiological,
security and community needs. Brining
up this point of lifestyle made me
realize another thing as well.

You could have the most eco-friendly
sustainable structures and
infrastructures possible, but the people
within them could still live in ways
that are non-sustainable. People still
need to develop the right habits, and
have the proper knowledge, to live in a
sustainable way.

Putting these two realizations about
'lifestyle' together we can come to
understand that, when talking about
sustainability, lifestyle encompasses
one's daily habits, livelihood and
interactions with other people and the
environment and how they are used on
a regular basis to uphold the
sustainable infrastructures as well as
the personal pursuit for positive self
esteem and self actualization. So any
definition of Sustainability would also
need to include an awareness of
lifestyle and it's place in creating a
Sustainable future.

The Bruntland Definition of
Sustainability is not the only
widespread definition of
Sustainability. There is also a concept
called the Triple Bottom Line. This
concept stemmed from finding a model
for sustainable business' and
economics. In modern economics the
most important aspect of business is
the 'bottom line', money. The more
money the more successful the
business. The environment, the Earth's
natural systems, and it's people,
become expendable to increasing the
bottom line. Many environmentally
minded endeavors failed because the
people of conscience in charge of the
projects did not prioritize, or value,
the role of proper business practices.
The Triple Bottom Line was
established as a sound business
principle, giving a guide as to how the
environmentally conscious can also,
ethically, be business conscious. The
Triple Bottom Line identifies three
'bottom lines' that are necessary for an
endeavor to be 'sustainable'; the
financial, the social, and the
environmental. For an endeavor to be
sustainable it must be positive in all
three bottom lines. The manager of the
sustainable endeavor must balance the
three factors, ensuring that all three are
roughly as equally positive as possible
to ensure the greatest sustainable
longevity of the project. While many
use the Triple Bottom Line as a
definition of Sustainability. I find it
more to be a guiding principle on how
to make things sustainable rather than a
definition of Sustainability itself,
though the two can be melded into one,
creating a more comprehensive
definition for us to follow.

To bring it all together 'William's
Definition' of Sustainability would be
as follows: Sustainability is the
creation of architecture, infrastructure
systems, and lifestyles that, in
accordance with the triple bottom line,
use renewable, recyclable, or re-
usable resources to provide the
physiological needs of the body,
security, community, positive esteem,
and selfactualization.

If Sustainability is the goal, as stated
above, then we now have a definition
that gives us a clear articulation of
what that goal is. The rest of this book
will now deal with Permaculture, the
design principles that will enable us to
literally, physically manifest these
goals into the world and into our lives.

What is Permaculture?
At it's most basic Permaculture is
about ecologically sound agriculture
and there have been many who
pioneered these concepts in their own
ways. Some of the early names in the
origins of Permaculture are Sepp
Holzer, Howard Odum, and Rudolph
Steiner's Biodynamic Farming. The
coining of the phrase and creation of
Permaculture as an organized
curriculum was the combined work of
Bill Mollison and David Holmgren.
Through their work together they
organized and codified their concepts
and created the Permaculture Design
Certificate curriculum upon which this
book is based. By providing a
packaged set of lessons and
standardized terminology they were
able to create a new educational
system that allowed them to
communicate these complex and
esoteric concepts to others.

The world “Permaculture” combines
the two words Permanent+Agriculture.
Later the portmanteau was re-
envisioned to be Permanent+Culture.
This was done to emphasize the
understanding that all cultures rely on
their agriculture base, and all things
cultures do effect their environment
which in turn effects their agricultural
base, which in turn effects the culture.
It's all one big interlinked cyclic
system and those who don't understand
that are doomed to self-extinction. The
good news is Permaculture isn't about
the 'doom and gloom', it's about the fact
that we have the techniques,
knowledge and capability to create the
exact opposite of doom and gloom, we
have the techniques, knowledge and
capability to create abundance and
prosperity for all.

In simple terms Mollison and
Holmgren (and the other early
pioneers) observed that for eons the
ecological systems of nature have
provided food systems that have fed
billions of beings. These ecological
systems operated without machinery,
artificial inputs, or hours of labor by
'farm hands'. As the systems continued
to operate over time they got stronger
and stronger, more productive and
robust. Then along came humanity with
modern agriculture. We can now see
that modern agricultural systems
require an increasing amount of
artificial inputs, as well as more and
more labor. As the systems grow over
time they become weaker and less
productive, and destroy the local
natural ecology. Modern industrialized
agricultural systems are inherently
self-extincting, and logic dictates that a
culture based on these systems would
also be self-extincting. Permaculture is
modeled on nature's systems to create
food production systems that can
maintain themselves indefinitely and
become stronger over time,
permanently sustaining sustaining

Holmgren and Mollison were able to
expand the understanding of the
complex ecological web that supports
these food systems to take into account
other human systems that seem un-
related to agriculture, such as
architecture, business practices, and
social norms. Working together they
created a system of design principles,
terminology and design methods that
could be communicated to the
uninitiated to teach them how to do
design their own self sustaining
cultural infrastructure.

By applications of Permaculture
Design principles to our personal and
social infrastructures we can
theoretically create infrastructures that
create stronger and more robust local
and global ecological systems.
Permaculture is not about conservation
as many ecologists envision it.
Permaculture is about the active
transformation of one's local and
global ecology. However it ultimately
is also about conservation because the
more we impact our local areas in
active Permaculture ways the less we
impact far flung places, thus allowing
them to be conserved.

Permaculture is not about the specific
elements, it is about the connections
between the elements. Most people
think that a Permaculture course is
about plants. While plants are an
important part of any Permaculture
system, still themselves,

the culture and permanently they
support. they are only an element of
any system. There are many courses
about plants one could, and should
take, but a Permaculture course will
not be about plants. A Permaculture
Design course will teach you how to
connect those plants ecologically to
other elements in the system, and the
Permaculture course would also cover
those other elements as much as it does
plants.
Permaculture means different things to
different people. Like architecture
people use Permaculture to create the
structures and infrastructures that
support life in accordance with their
beliefs. Christians build churches,
Capitalists build banks. Those who are
spiritual will see Permaculture as an
expression of spirituality, those who
believe that economics drives society
will see Permaculture as a sound
financial strategy, environmentalists
will see as a means of protecting the
environment, humanists will see it as a
way to provide for people's happiness.
It is important to understand they are
all correct because the exact purpose
of a Permaculture Design depends
upon the goals of the designer. Being a
system that aspires for as inclusive as
a holistic picture as possible
Permaculture crosses many
disciplines, philosophies, and goals.
The one thing that all Permaculture
Designers agree on though is that
Permaculture is about designing
architecture, infrastructure, technology
and lifestyle habits that are in harmony
with the natural cycles of the Earth.
This can be believed from a deep
ecologist point of view, caring for the
Earth for it's own sake, or as an
anthropocentric view, caring for the
Earth because without the Earth there
will be no humanity.

The Permaculture Ethics
There are three accepted central
Permaculture ethics adapted from the
work of Mollison and Holmgren: Earth
Care, People Care, Fair Share.

Earth Care
All things, even humans depend upon
the

Earth. Therefore Permaculture begins
with the care for the health of the
ecology and all it's plants and animals
solely and altruistically for it's own
sake. Therefore Permaculture, while an
anthropocentric discipline, is not only
about the human animal. All
Permaculture ethics, principles, goals
and systems are to benefit the rest of
the Earth as much as they benefit the
people of the Earth.

People Care
Showing compassion for all people.
Putting the health and well being of
people as our first priority. People are
not expendable! A system is only as
good as it's service to the needs of the
people.

Fair Share
A fair share means that everything and
everyone receives enough of the
resources to meet their needs. Anything
past, or superfluous to, your needs is
considered 'abundance'. Abundance is
to be shared as well. Remember the
first two Permaculture ethics, Earth
Care and People Care. All things on
the Earth receive their Fair Share, and
all people on Earth receive their Fair
Share. This means we humans get to
share in the Earth's bounty and beauty,
it also means that we endeavor in
doing so that the Earth in general
receives it's fair share as well.

Principles
These 12 Permaculture Design
principles were coined by David
Holmgren. There are other lists of
Permaculture Principles out there that
are just as valid. It is not about
understanding these principles word
for word that is important. What is
important is that you understand the
underlying logic of the principles and
how to apply that logic as a designer,
and as a human being. These principles
are mainly to be used as design
principles, but they can also be applied
to many aspects of life. Holmgren
coined the 12 Permaculture Principles,
I am presenting here my understanding
of them. I challenge you to research
these principles on your own and
challenge my understanding.
The 12 Permaculture Principles
1. Apply self-regulation and accept
feedback 2. Catch and store energy
3. Creatively use and respond to
change 4. Design from patterns to
details
5. Integrate rather than segregate
6. Observe and interact
7. Obtain a yield
8. Produce no waste
9. Use and value diversity
10. Use and value renewable resources
and
services
11. Use edges and value the marginal
12. Use small and slow solutions

Permaculture Principle #1:
Apply self-regulation and accept
feedback

Nature is a self-regulating system. If a
herd of animals over graze their food
supplies, the herd dwindles, if a
deciduous tree does not receive enough
nutrients it drops a bunch of its leaves.
By this process of regulation plants
and animals maintain the peak health of
the individual, allowing the species
itself to prosper over time. It is at this
point obvious that humans have the
capacity to NOT self-regulate when it
comes to achieving a harmonious
balance with the environment...or at
least we humans think they can live
without ecological harmony. The
problem with this human attitude is that
eventually nature wins, one way or the
other. When humans ignore the natural
balance, the methods by which nature
achieves equilibrium can be very
painful and devastating to both the
human and natural world.

One of the goals of Permaculture is to
achieve a harmonious balance with
nature that is healthy and positive for
both the humans and the Earth. To
create these healthy sustainable
systems we must consciously control
our natural instincts for ecological
responsibility. We must consciously
choose to regulate ourselves in
accordance within the limits of our
natural environment. Part of that
process of selfregulation is accepting
the feedback from your actions and
changing in response to that feedback,
without excepting the feedback there
can be no self-regulation, without self-
regulation there can be no true
ecological coherence. From analysis,
to concept, to implementation, to on
going usage, the Permaculture Designer
must be open to accepting feedback
and self-regulating the actions and
impacts of our designs and habits.
Altruism and inherent respect for all
things wise and more evolved aside,
one must remember that if we do not
regulate ourselves we will break the
ecological systems which support us
and Nature will regulate us in it's own
harsh ways.

Permaculture Principle #2:
Catch and store energy

The ecology around you is filled with
enormous potential energy. Some forms
of this energy are obvious, such as
catching solar power to generate
electricity, or heat water. Windmills
can harness the potential power of the
wind, micro-hydro systems can catch
the potential energy of running water.
These powers systems create
renewable, ecologically sound,
electricity and heat. While solar, wind
and hydro are the most commonly
perceived forms of natural energy they
are by no means the only forms of
energy available around you.
Generating heat and electricity is not
the only way to use these energies
either. Energy can be used for motion
that is to move something like water,
you can even energy to transform
things.
Lost Valley Educational Center Permaculture
student Inger-Lise Burns at the Cougar Mountain
Community, photo by William Ozier.

In a Permaculture sense 'energy' isn't a
quantifiable scientific form such as
watts, or BTUs...though those are
forms of energy. Energy is more of a
statement of functionality, and using an
event or phenomenon to accomplish
some goal or purpose.

For example, almost every serious
Permmie I know keeps chickens. Why?
Obviously chickens provide eggs and
meat. Food. Of course food energy is
one type of energy to be harnessed for
an effect besides generating electricity
or heat (unless of course you snuggle
up on a cold night under a blanket with
your sweetie and catch and store body
heat). In addition to the obvious food
energy chickens are a bevy of other
potential energies! I am going to stop
there, you will find a further
explanation of the available forms of
'chicken energy' in the discussion on
Animals. In the meantime, as an
assignment try to think of three other
forms of potential energy and what you
would do with them that can be
harnessed from a chicken.

Aside from animals there are still yet
more forms of potential energy that
surround you. It is difficult to give a
true list of these energies, as 'energy'
can mean many, many things. In
essence energy is present in any
change, interaction, movement, or
transformation...think about that again,
any change, interaction, movement or
transformation. Determining what is an
'energy' is more a matter of your
perception and design imagination,
than reading words off a list. Energy
can be said to be embodied in any
proper noun. Shade energy, cold
energy, kid energy, duck energy, sound
energy, the energy of the slope of a hill,
the energy of a party, the energy of a
brick, the energy of a Sunrise, of a
block of coal, any of these, and more,
can be considered 'energy' for
purposes of Permaculture function and
design.

This principle has two parts, one is to
catch the energy, and the other is to
store the energy. Just catching it and
using it once is not reaching the
maximum potential of a system. The
ambient energy of the ecology is
around you always, twenty four hours a
day, every day of the year. A good
system would in some manner be
catching these energies, storing them in
some manner, and then using them
when needed later, such as composting
for example.

Permaculture Principle #3:
Creatively use and respond to change

Things change. We cause a lot of
environmental damage by fighting
change. A Permaculture Designer
understands that ecosystems evolve.
Even systems you have designed and
installed perfectly will still change
over time. Any good Permaculture
Design will begin to alter it's
environment. If the system is healthy
and robust 'volunteers', plants and
animals you did not design for, will
show up. This is a good thing. swales,
berms and other earthworks will begin
to slowly alter the topography, which
will alter the function of your designs.
This is a good thing. There is no 'bad'
ecological evolution as long as you are
prepared to adapt and change, and
make the best you can of the new
conditions. Some change is predictable
and a good design will have to account
for, enable and follow that change. For
example you plant some trees with a
garden around their base now, in 20
years those trees will be much bigger
and the whole system will function
completely differently. You can make
an evolving design that takes
predicable change into account, and is
able to adapt to the unpredictable.

Permaculture Principle #4:
Design from patterns to details

The whole point for a Permaculture
Design is for it to fit into the 'bigger'
picture, so before you even dig that
first hole you need to understand that
bigger picture. What is a pattern? In
this context a pattern is a repeating
phenomenon in nature, we have wind
patterns, flow patterns, rain patterns,
migration patterns. These patterns
overlap and interact and create the
bigger picture your design will fit into.
For example your local rain patterns
will tell you how much rain water you
can catch and when, and that will tell
you how big your tanks need to be,
which will further tell you how big the
foundation will have to be to hold them
up when they are full and where that
foundation has to be. Once you know
how big the foundation will be, and
where, you are now ready to attend to
the detail of digging that first hole,
which started with first understanding
a pattern. In addition to patterns like
rain and wind, a 'pattern' can be an
understanding of behavior, or
reactions. These could be reactions
from plants, animals or anything else.
For example, how do chickens react
when a car passes by? Understanding
that pattern of interaction may tell you
details that need to go into your
chicken coop design, or driveway
design. Once you have the details of
your design then you can start digging
holes. Patterns will be explored in the
section on Pattern Understanding. The
Design Process will walk you through
a recommended process on how to
analyze patterns and from them discern
details.

Permaculture Principle #5:
Integrate rather than segregate

Monocropping is the practice of using
only one type of plant in a specific
garden or agricultural area.
Polycropping is the practice of using
several different plant species in a
specific garden or agricultural area.
Polycropping is an example of
integrating, where as monocropping
would be an example of segregating.

This principle however applies to a lot
more than just plants and agricultural
systems. Like all of the Permaculture
principles it can be applied to
anything, plants, building materials,
design philosophies, social norms,
anything.

A good Permaculture Design tries to
integrate together as many elements as
possible. Locking them all together so
that they achieve more working as a
team than they could working
separately. In Permaculture this way of
thinking applies in all things.

Permaculture Principle #6:
Observe and interact

The hardcore will tell you that you
should spend one year observing
before you make any design
interventions. This way you can
observe the site across the seasons and
through many different conditions. The
real hardcore will tell you that you
should make observations for three
years before doing anything, because
that first year may have just been a
fluke. This advice chafes most novices
and clients because in our quick fix,
short term thinking society nobody
wants to wait a year, or three before
doing anything, they want to start doing
cool stuff to their site NOW. That's
where the interaction comes in.

This principles isn't just about looking
at a thing, it is also about interacting
with the environment you are
observing. Interactions can also be
small experiments to see how they go.
You could plant a small crop of some
plant and observe how it reacts to the
local area. You could put in a small
swale or berm, divert some water, and
see what happens. Don't forget the
simple interactions such as going for
hikes and walks across the property,
sitting in the grass, playing frisbee.
Interact! But all the while you are
observing.




Arcosanti Land Manager Adam Nordfors
observes before designing the next step
developing this field. Photo from the Soleri
Archives.

Observation involves much more than
just the eyes. You observe with all of
your senses and all of your mind. How
does it smell, how does it sound, what
does it remind you of. Take the time to
observe with each of your senses.
Close your eyes and just listen to the
site, take long slow inhales through and
learn what the site smells like, and
what makes it smell that way.

But be careful, observation is not the
same as interpretation! Make sure you
observe, a lot, long before you begin
interpreting. This grass is brownish
yellow, that is an observation, it is
dying is an interpretation. Just observe
and interact and observe the
interactions for now. And observe
under all different circumstances! Go
at night, at dawn, when it's Sunny,
when it's rainy, when it's windy. You
want to observe the full spectrum of
the life of your site! Observe and
interact and observe the interactions.
Permaculture Principle #7:
Obtain a yield

Every system in nature is prolific in its
bounty. Nature never produces anything
that is inert or non-useful. In fact
nature's system become more and more
bountiful over time. Not only is nature
productive it's productivity is prolific
in the variety of products it yields.
Fruit trees not only produce edible
fruit, and more trees, they also produce
oxygen, clean water, and provide
habitat for a variety of insects and
animals. Being modeled after Nature's
systems Permaculture systems should
produce useful things be they edible,
medicinal, construction materials,
beneficial micro-climates, or any other
type of useful out put. A well designed
Permaculture system would be able to
produce all these different products at
the same time.

Permaculture Principle #8:
Produce no waste

Permaculture systems are based on the
principles of nature, and in nature there
is no waste only nutrients to be
constantly re-used in an endless cycle.
Everything, everything is used and then
re-used, in an endless cycle. When a
bear poops in the woods the poop
becomes nutrients which feed plants,
which in turn feed the bear and when
the bear itself dies it's body
decomposes and becomes nutrients
which feed the system, supporting life
for it's progeny. On an on. Waste is a
completely human invention, in fact it
may be the only true human invention.
Every other species throughout the
entire history of the Earth uses
resources for it's own purposes and
then one way or another puts them back
in the system so they may be used by
another species. On and on. We are the
only species which uses resources in a
way that they can no longer participate
in nature's cycle. This didn't just
happen accidentally. We actively put
tons of intellect and effort into creating
waste. From a Permaculture
perspective waste can be seen as a
resource management issue, or rather a
resource mis-management issue. Most
importantly 'waste' is a choice. We
choose to waste resources and
materials. We choose to throw
something away as waste. The notion
of throwing something away as waste
is a complete falsehood of a statement
since technically there is no 'away' to
throw the things to.

Everything that is thrown away is still
somewhere, and still impacting our
environment. There is no true thing as a
'waste material', only wasted
opportunities to produce a yield. A
Permaculture Designer will
understand, with the possible
exception of nuclear waste, all things
in their system are resources that can
be used to help create an ecologically
beneficial system. After their first
function is done almost any material
can be re-used, re-cycled, or
repurposed into something beneficial.
The only limit to it's potential is the
imagination and skill of the designer.

Waste is not only a choice we make to
mis-manage resources, but it is also a
design issue. With some forethought
designers could in fact design products
that when their initial use is over the
product is feasible to re-cycle, or re-
use, thereby making it not waste but a
re-purposed resource. Producing no
waste is a design issue, and a resource
management issue. Most importantly it
is a choice. It is a choice in lifestyle
yes, but it is also a choice in how you
design.

Permaculture Principle #9:
Use and value diversity

In nature bio-diversity is strength. The
more elements a system has the more
robust the system. Climatic conditions
can fluctuate from year to year
resulting in droughts or downpours,
new plants or animals can move in.
Monocropped systems, that is systems
which have only one type of element,
are too vulnerable to the unexpected. If
you only use one type of plant in your
system to produce Vitamin C then a
cold snap could destroy your entire
system. However if your system uses
multiple plants to produce Vitamin C
then a cold snap may kill off some of
the species, yet the other will live on
and your Vitamin C supply continues
unabated. While plants make an
excellent example of this principle, it
is by no means limited to only thinking
about plants. This principles calls for
the using and valuing of diversity in
ALL things. It can apply to plants, it
can apply to building materials, it can
apply to economic systems, it could
apply to methods of transportation to
get to work in the morning. For any
type of system, diversity is strength,
and the greater the diversity the more
resilient, robust and the system.
Permaculture Principle #10:
Use and value renewable resources
and services

What I find to be the key word in this
principle is 'use'. I see it being not just
a suggestion but an imperative. It is in
essence saying 'Don't just value them,
go out and use them'. This principle
entreats us to value these resources,
their value is in their usefulness. The
more useful something is, the more
valuable. Permaculture is about
maximizing what is being given to us
by nature. Use what is given. It is not
ecologically un-ethical to use
renewable resources. What is
ecologically un-ethical is to use non-
renewable resources and then throw
them away, or to over harvest or over
use resources in a way that is non-
renewable.

Architect and author William
McDonough coined a phrase 'inter-
generational tyranny'. That is when the
people of the current generation build
infrastructures that rely on
nonrenewable resources. This is a
tyranny because by consuming the
resources now we condemn future
generations to live in a state of
resource scarcity, where they will be
unable to meet their needs. We
condemn them to that life by the
resource choices we make now, before
they are even born. By valuing and
using renewable resources we will
leave as many resources for those
generations as we had for ourselves.

Also, in terms of a strategy of social
change by actively seeking out and
using these renewable services and
resources when they present
themselves you lead by example. Not
only can you lead by example, anyone
who is politically savvy should
understand that we vote with our
dollars as well as our ballots. By
frequenting and using these renewable
resources and services we can help
support and accelerate the economic
shift our society needs to make
'Permaculture' financially viable, i.e.
support the Triple Bottom Line.

Permaculture Principle #11:
Use edges and value the marginal

What is an edge? An edge is where
two different things meet. Some edges
are where something meets nothing, we
often call those cliffs. Where grass
meets a sidewalk is an edge. Where a
field of grass gives way to a grove of
trees is an edge. We quite often
overlook the edges of things. Most
yards have feet of empty space lining
their edges. Permaculture entreats us to
produce no waste, well this extends to
not wasting available space.
Working with Paolo Soleri at the
Arcosanti project I came to realize
how the vertical marginal, and empty
space are under utilized. Even many
Permaculture Designers miss the
potential of vertical space, or elevated
horizontal space such as roof tops.
Food forests and many plant guilds are
excellent examples of taking advantage
of marginalized vertical edge. Green
roofs, and rain water catchment as
well, valuing the elevated, top, edge of
structures. Trellises, and vine covered
walls also take advantage of the often
overlooked vertical edge and
marginalized open vertical space.

A Permaculture designer must open
their mind to the potential of all things,
and maximize that potential. This
includes space.

Permaculture Principle #12:
Use small and slow solutions
This principle ties in strategically well
with the interact portion of the
principle to observe and interact. By
making your interactions, 'small and
slow', you will have time to observe
their effects and performance in your
ecology before committing major
ecological and personal resources to a
large scale implementation. This
principle also ties in strategically well
with the principle creatively use and
respond to change. As you will recall
that principle required the knowledge
that your system will evolve over time,
and will have to adapt to change.
Smaller solutions that have a slower
and steady growth rate will be able to
fit in better with new changes, or
easier to adapt to changing situations.

Additional Principle:
Stacking of functions

While not one of Holmgren's
Principles, the principle of Stacking of
functions is an oft recited Permaculture
Principle. This principle ties in
strongly with produce a yield, and
expands upon it. Instead of producing
just A yield, stacking of functions
implies that you try to produce as
many yields as possible from any one
design element.

Synonyms for this principle would
include the phrases 'multi-use' and
'multifunctional'.

By stacking functions and maximizing
the potential functions of any one thing
you achieve a much greater efficiency
in your system. With this greater
efficiency you can either then use
fewer resources to achieve the same
level of productivity, or accomplish a
greater many things with the resources
you use.
The more productive each individual
element of your personal system, i.e.
the more functions it stacks, the fewer
resources we as a group have to use.

Additional Principle:
The problem is the solution

There are many ways to look at and
deal with 'problems'. In a Permaculture
sense a 'problem' means something that
is decreasing the ecological activity in
your system. The problem may have
been something you designed/created
or it may have been a pre-existing
element. Either way anything that is a
'problem' represents a significant, or at
least some, amount of embodied energy
in your system. The Permaculture
Principles tell us to catch and store that
energy. Therefore some amount of
energy can be caught and harnessed
from a 'problem'. The principles also
tell us to produce no waste. If you
encounter a 'problem' in your system
and do not re-purpose it into a
beneficial element then you have
wasted an opportunity. The
Permaculture principles also tell us to
use the edges and value the marginal. A
'problem' is only something that has
been de-valued and marginalized.

This principle calls for a lot of
imagination and a lot of adaptability on
the part of the Permaculture Designer.
It is also calls for the designer to have
the most open of minds. The first
instinct with a problem is to remove it.
What this principles is calling for is
for you to drop the judgement of a thing
being a 'problem' and to see it for it's
potential. Instead of looking at it for
what it doesn't do, look at it for what it
can do.

This principle can apply to a lot more
than just physical design elements in an
agricultural or architectural system.
This principle can be applied in your
whole life. Have a problem? Try to
figure out how that problem can be
harnessed to provide it's own solution.
Do you have a crappy job? Try using
the money from that job to take a class
in something you want to learn. Is your
life too hectic and you're always on the
run? Try turning that rat race into a
jogging schedule to get in better shape.
These are just some utterly random
suggestions, and they may not be
feasible, but the point is not the
specific suggestions themselves but
their representation of how this
principle can be applied not to just all
aspects of your design, but to any
aspect of your life. Permaculture
Design is about more than just plants,
and infrastructures it is about how you
design your life habits, just remember
your life habits are shaped by your
infrastructures, which is why
Permaculture is about design.

Additional Principle:
Everything is an ecology

When we use the word ‘ecology’ most
people assume we mean the Earth’s
biosphere, and it is fine to use the
word in this assumptive way. The
Permaculture Designer however
understands an ecology is a system
where in each element effects and
supports each other element. Together
they create a dynamic stable system.
Your car is an ecology, your living
room is an ecology, your interactions
with your friends are an ecology, your
stomach and in fact your whole body is
an ecology. Everything is an ecology. If
you mess with one element in the
system, all other elements are effected.
However once you have read this book
and have a better understanding of the
atmospheric cycle, the hydro-cycle, the
nutrient cycle and microclimates it will
be clear to you how anything, no matter
how innocuous seeming, has a
continuous effect on the ecology of the
Earth’s biosphere. So each of these
micro-ecologies effects every element
of the Earth. Now, if everything is an
ecology, and Permaculture Design is
about designing ecologies, then the
principles and techniques of
Permaculture Design can be used to
design anything, from a house, to a car,
to your living room, to your
relationship with your friends,
anything!

Understanding something as an ecology
is about understanding it's cycles,
particularly it's consumption/excretion
cycles.

Animal Systems
As you are probably aware animals
are integral part of Nature's system.
Animals are integral in the atmospheric
cycle, they are active participants in
the hydro-cycle. Through the cycles of
birth, eating, excreting, and
decomposing upon death animals are
integral part of the nutrient cycle.
Animals improve the health of soil and
help plants to propagate.

Let’s take a minute to look at how the
Permaculture Design principles inform
us to use animals in a system.
Permaculture Principle #2 calls for us
to catch and store energy, and animals
generate a lot of energy which can be
harnessed. Look at a chicken for
example. Chickens spend all day
generating a lot of energy as they run
around scratching and pecking in the
dirt, and their poop even represents
energy in the form of fertilizer which
helps provide bio-chemical energy for
plants, and for humans who eat those
plants. Permaculture Principle #5 calls
for us to integrate rather than segregate,
so animals should be integrated right
into the design, not kept separate from
it. Animals should be active
participants in the ecology of a
Permaculture system integrated right
alongside all other ecological
functions in the design. Permaculture
Principle #7 informs us to obtain a
yield and as you may already know
animals by their very nature produce
all sorts of yields. However don’t just
think of an animals yield in terms of
meat, eggs and milk, but in terms of
work, and ecological activity the
animal can yield for your design.
Permaculture Principle #9 calls for us
to use and value diversity. When many
people think of Permaculture they think
of plants, but Permaculture is about
using any and all ecologically sound
resources available, that includes
animals, and a variety of them for a
variety of purposes. Permaculture
Principle #10 tells us to use and value
renewable resources and services. As
long as your animals are well fed they
are a renewable resource and provide
many renewable services. In fact they
can reproduce and provide even more
animals.

Actually using animals for meat is
probably the least sustainable and
desirable way to use them in a design.
While they may be tasty and provide
you with a full belly, an animal can
only serve as dinner once. While alive
an animal can continue to function in an
ecology for months, if not years.
Animals can work the soil for you
preparing garden spaces. An oft used
Permaculture strategy is to have flocks
of chickens and fence them in to an
area where you wish to start a food
system. While there they eat away
weeds, aerate the soil by their
scratching and digging, they fertilize
the soil with their poop, and eat up all
the bugs that could damage plants. By
moving the flock from place to place
you can harness the chicken energy to
prepare varying tracts of land for
gardens. Goats and other animals can
be used to do the same. Of course you
have to be careful and make sure that
the animals are not going to eat plants
you do want, and you have to make
sure they are protected from predators.
Animals can eat bugs and weeds and
convert them into poop, which can then
be used to fertilize beneficial crops.
They can’t do this if they’re dead.
These are only some of the way
animals con contribute to a system. I
am not trying to give you a
comprehensive list of what animals
can do, rather open your mind to be
able to explore, for yourself, the
possibilities of what animals can do.

Not only can animals provide a variety
of services, your design can harness a
variety of different animal types that
most people usually don’t think of
when they think of animals. There is a
type of design called an Aquaculture
system, which houses fish and the dirty
water from the fish is then used on
plants, and occasionally the fish and
the plants can be eaten. Bees help to
pollinate plants and make honey, how
is that for producing a yield! Bat boxes
can attract bats and their guano
harnessed as fertilizer, plus they keep
flying night time insects down. Bird
houses bring birds to your system
which can introduce more poop, and
help to keep down certain insect
populations, and possibly even help
pollinate certain types of plants, and
bring volunteer edible plants by
dropping off their seeds. Worms can be
used in composting systems to help
break down food scraps. Permaculture
systems can also be designed to attract
animals to 'volunteer' for your system,
that is bring in wild animals that can
benefit your system with their work.

The point I am making is that when it
comes to animals in a Permaculture
Design you should not think of them as
farm animals to be herded and
harvested. You should think of them as
components in an ecological system,
performing the same roles in your
design as they do in nature. The only
difference is that in your design they
will be domesticated natural
components in an ecological system,
which makes sense considering that a
Permaculture Design is in essence
domesticated nature.

Be very careful when designing large
animals into a system. Not everyone
will be able to properly care for goats
or chickens, and the cost of feed may
be burdensome. Many Permaculture
Designers like to incorporate bee hives
into their systems, but again, be careful
not everyone can tend bees. Attracting
birds or building bat houses is an easy
way to integrate animals into a system.
Vermiculture systems are compost
systems that incorporate red worms
into them, creating a more powerful
composting system that is not much
more difficult than a normal compost
system. If the design is within an urban
area and you want chickens or goats
you will have to check your local
codes, though many people are
surprised by how many chickens one is
allowed to keep in many urban areas.
Not only do many people, myself
included, think that ducks lay better
eggs than chickens, a raised duck pond
can be integrated into a system in a
way that enables the duckpoop ridden
water to be flushed into gardens
providing nutrient rich water for the
plants.

Animals can also serve as what you
call 'indicator species'. An indicator
species tells you something about the
local environment. Indicator species
can be anything from birds, to insects,
to ground animals, to crustaceans, on
and on. For example, Brown-headed
Nuthatch bird lives in pine forests, so
if you see one you know a healthy pine
forest is near. Some animals need
specific plant or animal prey to live,
so the presence of the predator would
be an indicator of the presence of the
prey. Some animals have specific
environmental needs for their life
cycle, so the presence of this animal
would indicate the presence of those
environmental factors. Just as the
presence of an animal can be an
indicator, so can sometimes the
absence of a particular animal can
serve as an indicator of the local
ecology. It is not always the presence
of absence of the animal per se that is
the indicator, sometimes upon
examination the animal can express
indicators of the local ecology. For
example some crustacean shells will
display odd colors in the presence of
specific toxins, or pollutants may be
build up in the livers or muscle tissues
or certain animals. All in all learning
to read animals as indicators takes
research and experience. As a
Permaculture Designer you don't
necessarily need to know ALL of the
indicator species and what they mean,
but you do need to understand the
concept of, and how to research,
indicator species for your local
environment.

There’s one animal that deserves
special attention when talking about the
role of animals in a Permaculture
system, and that is the animal known as
human. If you think humans are the
problem, then as a Permaculture
Designer you understand that humans
are the solution. A Permaculture
Designer needs to understand that
humans are an integral animal system
in any Permaculture design. So you
design to maximize the ecological
potential of the human animal for the
health of your design. So what role
does the human animal play in the
ecology? In a Permaculture system the
humans are the shepherds of that
system's ecology. This does not mean
that humans are the shepherds of the
whole world's ecology, only the
shepherds of the ecology of the
Permaculture system of which they are
a part. How do humans fulfill that
role? Well dear reader understanding
that is exactly the point of this entire
book, so please read on.

Appropriate Technology
"Any intelligent fool can make things
bigger, more complex, and more
violent. It takes a touch of genius --
and a lot of courage -- to move in the
opposite direction."

- Albert Einstein
"Not mass production, but production
by the masses."
- Mohandas K. Gandhi

One of the main goals of Permaculture
is to do things more in accordance with
nature. Well tool use is a natural
instinct among animals, not all of them
of course. Of the animals that do use
tools they don't use much more than
sticks and stones, but they are tools
nonetheless. In nature orangutangs have
been observed using sticks to bash
fish, ravens use sticks to spear grubs in
small holes in trees, and otters use
rocks to bust open the shells on
mussels. The human also shares this
instinct for tool use. However it does
seem that humans do this instinct better
than any other animal, in fact tool use
is the primary evolutionary advantage
of humans. In the long course of their
evolution humans have refined and
evolved their tool usage to be able to
conquer the limitations of their
environment and prosper on the planet.
However after the industrial revolution
the human instinct for tool use has
begun to cause extensive, and possibly
permanent damage to the very bio-
sphere that spawned them. Humans
now call their tools 'technology'. If
Permaculture is a call for humans to do
things more in accordance with nature,
how do we reconcile that goal with the
notion that tool use is a natural human
instinct? How do we make 'technology'
that is in accordance with nature? This
is the role of 'Appropriate Technology'.

While Permaculture seeks to reduce
humanities environmental impact,
modern high technology tends to
increase that impact. In general high
technology is resource consumptive,
pollutive, expensive and requires
dependency on an outside agent to
properly maintain, repair or upgrade.
Therefore a new technological
paradigm is needed, one that fits with
the goals and principles of
Sustainability. When using ecological
design, the design method for a
sustainable future, one turns to natural
solutions first, seeing if the local
natural environment can be harnessed
to provide for ones needs. Depending
on many different factors not all
challenges can be overcome by purely
natural means. There will be
challenges that cannot be overcome by
a change in habits. There are times
when you will need some device or
machine, a technology, to accomplish
the necessary task. When these times
arise one can then turn to find a
solution made of Appropriate
Technology.

The phrase Appropriate Technology
does not apply to any one particular
device or set of devices, but rather is a
new paradigm in finding technological
solutions to meet your needs.
Appropriate Technologies are devices
or machines that are at the Do-It-
Yourself scale, meaning that they can
be built by the layman with easily
available resources and techniques. To
reduce fuel consumption, Appropriate
Technology seeks to harness as much
readily available 'ecological energy' as
possible. Energy can be 'harvested'
from the local environment in the form
of mechanical forces, people or animal
power, Sunlight, thermal forces or
wind, just to name a few. Appropriate
Technology is almost always small
scale and meant for personal use, or
use by only a close few. One of my
favorite examples of Appropriate
Technology was made in South
America where a see-saw was used to
create a pump for a well, the children
playing on the see-saw provides the
mechanical energy to pump the water
out of the well. Brilliant, and
appropriate!

Appropriate Technology is usually
mechanical not electrical in nature,
(though it is not necessarily exclusive
of electricity). Examples include
bicycle powered blenders or washing
machines, solar cookers, solar hot
water heaters, water wheels and wind
mills. By harnessing existing kinetic
energy in the local environment, such
as in wind or your legs, mechanical
systems can be operated without the
need for electricity. Yes, photo voltaic
systems that convert Sunlight to
electricity can be considered
Appropriate Technology, however it is
the role of passive solar power that
plays a greater role in Appropriate
Technology. Passive solar is uses of
solar energy that do not create
electricity but use the ambient heat
energy in every Sun beam to
accomplish a task. The most ubiquitous
use of passive solar Appropriate
Technology is for heating, such as in
solar cookers which heat food by
channeling the Suns rays into an oven
like chamber. Though solar food
dehydrators are quite popular as well.

Being a Do-It-Yourself scale project,
in other words made by the people on
the site who will use it, Appropriate
Technology is imminently repairable,
maintainable and upgradeable by the
people on site. This ensures that no
matter what, the devices or machines
can always be maintained without
great expense or wait time.
Appropriate Technologies should be
made from local materials that follow
the 4Rs (reduce, reuse, recycle,
renew). This contributes to its nature
as a 'do-it-yourself' project, ensures
that it can be maintained by the people
who use it with readily available
resources, and reduces our impact on
the environment.

For example if you need electricity,
instead of buying a factory produced
wind generator or solar panel you
could use your own two hands, a few
tools and some elbow grease to create
a wind turbine out of an old alternator
from a broken down car. Of course
such a homemade rig would bring up
questions of efficiency. It can easily be
argued that a DIY scale wind generator
will not be as 'efficient' as one made
from modern engineering. I counter
though with the question which is truly
more efficient in a holistic sense, to
work and save and buy a $5000 high
power unit which has to be shipped
across the continent to you and if it
breaks will cost thousands of dollars
to fix, or to take $50 and an afternoon
to build a lower power version
yourself ? I wonder how much energy
could be generated at by all the
alternators sitting inert in all of the
worlds' junked cars?

Like Permaculture Design in general,
Appropriate Technology seeks to
empower people to be able to design
their own solutions that fit their unique
situations. Like Permaculture, what is
most important about Appropriate
Technology is not the specific devices
themselves, but rather the users
understanding of the basic mechanical
and ecological principles that underly
the specific devices. One should
understand the basics of mechanics, as
well as the basics of passive solar, and
wind. One should have some basic
mechanical skills, about on par with
what it takes to know how to build and
repair bicycles or some such. One
should also study Permaculture. Armed
with this knowledge each person
would be able to design and create
original devices using Appropriate
Technology that most perfectly fit their
situation, goals and beliefs.

The first recognized proponent of what
we now call Appropriate Technology
is considered to be Sri Aurobindu, the
man who's teachings later influenced
the creation of the ecocity Auroville.
Sri Aurobindu called it 'swadeshi', a
word which basically means self-
reliance. Aurobindu's teachings then
greatly influenced the Mahatma
Mohandas Gandhi. In his strategy of
passive resistance to British
oppression Gandhi advocated the use
of smaller technological systems
appropriate to the lives of the Indian
villagers, such as using looms to make
clothes and creating little solar
distilleries to harvest salt from sea
water. By taking advantage of their
own skills and local ecological
resources the citizens of India were
able to break many of the systems of
oppression imposed upon them by the
British Empire. Appropriate
Technology as we envision it today
was popularized in the seventies by a
man named EF Schumacher, the author
of the highly influential book, "Small is
Beautiful" and creator of the concept of
"Buddhist Economics". An economic
advisor, Schumacher proposed the shift
towards local Appropriate Technology,
which he called Intermediate
Technology, as a means for
'developing' countries to bolster
themselves and for developed nations
to have greater resilience to economic
down turns.

Since the 70s hundreds of people the
world over have made developments
in Appropriate Technology. Some
current favorites are pedal powered
blenders, pedal powered sewing
machines, pedal powered washing
machines, and passive solar food
cookers. Rocket stoves, cob ovens and
hayboxes are other forms of
Appropriate Technology used for
cooking. Play pumps are an innovative
form of Appropriate Technology used
in some '3rd world' countries. A play
pump is a water pump attached to a
children' playground equipment, such
as teeter-totters and carousels. As the
children play their activity
mechanically powers the pump,
bringing up the water. Through the use
of appropriate technologies such as
grey-water systems, Eco-Machines,
and sand filters people have created
cheap systems, sometimes from 'junk'
to provide life sustaining clean water.
Home composters such as the Sun-
Frost Scrap Eater, or the Green Cone
use Appropriate Technology thinking to
make composting simple, and easily
accessible to the masses. There have
even been developments in
Appropriate Technology to create food
production systems such as do-it-
Yourself scale hydroponics systems,
aquaponics systems and even using 55
gallon drums to create self-contained
gardening systems.
There are Appropriate Technology
solutions for vast array of goals
including, building construction and
environmental maintenance, cooking,
energy production, food preparation,
food production, lighting, refrigeration,
transportation, ventilation and air
conditioning, and water supply and
treatment. While the sustainable future
so many people envision may still have
micro-chips and electricity, and solid-
state-electronics it will also have a
ubiquitous amount of Appropriate
Technology carrying most of the load
of day to day living, with high tech
machines fewer and further between
used only when necessary.

Appropriate Technology is often
discussed only in the context of how it
can be used in the '3rd world' to to
help them 'develop'. The 'developed'
world is the main cause of the
ecological destruction that is wracking
our planet, so therefore isn't
Appropriate Technology MOST
appropriate for the developed world?
For example, pedal power can be
combined with low energy electronics
and have a major impact on reducing
pollution, while at the same time
helping to increase the health of your
average American. I mean, what if TVs
were all pedal powered? We could
still be a nation of couch potatoes and
instead of being the world's most
obese nation we could be the world's
most in shape nation! Pedal powered
laptops. Hand crank flashlights.
Imagine if gym treadmills fed into the
power grid! What if EVERY
playground in America fed child-play
produced energy into the electrical
grid? How much potential electricity is
out there, literally right in our own
hands, being wasted? Why can't
modern homes have grey water
systems, Eco-Machines and solar
cookers built right into them? With
Appropriate Technology we could
actually keep many of the 'modern
conveniences' developed by the
industrial age, yet have them in a way
that respects the environment and
empowers people to build their own
lives, rather than destroys the
environment and enslaves it's people
into debt.

How do you learn to make Appropriate
Technology? Good question. Given that
AT is not a specific list of gadgets, you
can't just open up a manual that will
tell you what to build, or a text book
with lesson plans. Being that
Appropriate Technology is made of
local resources to meet your specific
needs and situation there is no book of
parts lists you can read. Because
Appropriate Technology can be used
for so many different functions the
question isn't what types of
Appropriate Technology are there. The
question isn't how do I build
Appropriate Technology. The question
is what is it you need done, then what
resources do you have on hand, and
then using those resources what is it
you can think to build? Making
Appropriate Technology is like being
part MacGyver, part Mythbusters, part
Professor from Gilligan's Island and
ALL Permaculture!

Climates
Understanding your climate is perhaps
the most important thing to understand
for Permaculture Design. The vast
majority of factors that effect your
design will come from the local
climatic conditions. A Permaculture
Design that works well in an arid
climate could be an ecological disaster
in a tropical climate for example.
Climate effects architecture which
effects your everyday life, it effects
food, cultural habits, on and on. In
many ways climate is one of the most
important factors in shaping every
aspect of a culture. To get an
understanding of how important
climate is in shaping culture just look
at the difference between say the Inuits
of the arctic north and the Hadza of the
Tanzanian deserts. Climate determines
what types of plants and animals can
survive in an area which directly
effects food sources and availability.
Climate determines the factors that
must go into building life saving
shelters, and climate will even effect
available building materials. For
example Inuits do not build with
bamboo like asian cultures. Why? For
one you would freeze to death in a
bamboo structure in the arctic circle,
and two bamboo will not grow in the
arctic climate. Unfortunately with the
proliferation of the International Style
of architecture and it's cultural impact
the Modern world now develops
culture divorced of it's climate. We do
not build, dress, eat, or behave
appropriately for our climate and the
ecology is paying the price.

The major factors that contribute to
creating a 'climate' are latitude,
altitude and land mass. Latitude
determines how much direct Sunlight
will reach the area over the year,
which determines the temperature
range. The land mass determines wind
patterns and humidity. The altitude
effects temperature and oxygen
concentrations. Areas with similar
factors will have similar climates. For
example all mountain tops tend to have
similar climates because of their
altitude, most deserts in the world fall
within a latitude range that puts them
near the equator.
Types of climates
Every biome has a unique climate,
however climatologists have identified
some basic climate types:

• Arid - dry, and hot.
• Mediterranean - have mild winters
and dry hot summers
• Polar - cold and dry all year round
• Temperate - cold winters and mild
summers with varying rainy seasons.
• Tropical - hot and wet all year round.
• Tundra - very cold all year.

Biomes

Within a climatic zone there will be
distinct biomes. A biome is a large are
with a unique ecology. What makes
something a distinct biome? Biomes
are defined for the most part by their
plant and animal types. For examples a
savannah is a type of biome dominated
by grasses with thin forests or medium
sized trees spread throughout, and they
usually support grazing animals. An
oak savannah is a biome characterized
by grasslands with oak trees spread out
among them, the oak trees will support
a range of animals different than that
found in other types of savannahs. Rain
forests would be another type of
biome.

Biomes are created by a host of forces,
including climate, soil composition,
ground water, migration histories and
too many other factors to name here.
Biomes are not necessarily static, they
can change and evolve over time,
though it may take decades it not
centuries or millennia for a biome to
naturally evolve into a different type of
biome. Of course the interventions of
mankind can increase this evolution to
an unhealthy rate.

Biomes may have very specific names
that describe their ecologies in detail
such as temperate deciduous forest, or
Sonora desert.

Anthropogenic biomes are simply
biomes created by humans. For
example the wheat fields of canvas
could be considered an anthropogenic
biome. Suburbs and cities could be
considered an anthropogenic biome.
What other types of anthropogenic
biomes can you think of?

Ecotones and the edge effect
An ecotone is a 'clash' zone where two
(or more) biomes come together and
interact. Ecotones will have species of
plants and animals from both biomes
within them, plus possibly a mix of
unique plants and animals that may be
adaptable to both biomes.

Ecotones increase what is called the
edge effect, which is an important
concept in Permaculture Design. The
edge is the physical line where two
biomes, or in smaller scales any two
different conditions, meet. The edge
effect always produces a greater
variety of bio-diversity and ecological
activity as the biomes interact and
attempt to strike a balance which
drives the evolutionary forces of
adaptation. Ecotones are one of the
best natural examples of integration
rather than segregation. The greater the
edge the greater the bio-diversity.
While the edge effect is in effect in
large scale phenomenon such as
biomes, it applies at any scale, from an
entire ecological region to a small
garden. Permaculture prefers curvy
edges as it increases the surface area
for bio-diversity. In most, but not all,
circumstances in Permaculture Design
it is a good idea to increase the edge
effect as much as possible. For
example in garden or pond design it is
good to increase the edge effect
because you increase the surface area
for bio-diversity, but in designing a
home increasing the edge effect of your
walls would be bad because your
home would bleed more heat.

Permaculture is modeled after nature,
nature has no one caretaker. Natural
systems are maintained by communities
of animals and plants working together
in harmonious symbiosis. Nature
benefits from and is dependent on the
‘network effect’. The more individuals
involved in the network, the more
value the network has for each
individual. Most plants and animals
inherently involve themselves in the
natural networks around them. Humans
either lack or have lost this instinct,
and therefore we need an
organizational system to enable us to
assume our role as an animal feature in
a natural system. With a community
around your PC system you can swap
seeds with others, trade and better
forage for supplies, you can coordinate
food strategies with other gardeners
and grow coordinated crops, you can
pull together people for work parties,
you can pull upon pools of talent,
knowledge and skill greater than your
own to design and build. Some systems
may require steps of succession over a
period of time that may outstretch your
involvement, and when you are no
longer there to see these steps through
the community can do so. Also just as
birds and bees can help natural
systems propagate and spread humans
can do the same for PC systems.

Here are some recommended steps on
how to go about creating community,
be it an ecovillage, a community of
people living dispersed within a city, a
business, a club, people trying to
accomplish an activity or put on an
event, or even a Permaculture Design
group.

1) Setting Intention

At the heart of an Intentional
Community is, of course, the intent of
the community. Don’t just assume that
because it has the label of ‘intentional’
community that the intent can be
assumed. It benefits the community to
have an organized and agreed upon
intention. To set the intention of your
community your group should create a
Mission and Vision Statement. Mission
statements are goal oriented, they
articulate what you want to accomplish
and why. The Vision Statement
articulates what this mission will look
like when manifest, vision often covers
the ‘how’ of the mission statement. I
recommend that you get everybody
together, they each put up one sentence
on the board encapsulating what they
think the mission should be. Reduce
down any redundant ones. Then
synthesize what is left into a mission
statement about three sentences long
articulating the mission and another
three or four sentences articulating the
vision. Unless you plan on being in a
community of one you won’t
completely get your way in setting the
intention of the community. Remember
in Permaculture you should endeavor
to use and value diversity, as well as
integrate rather than segregate. There
may very well be intentions you have
for your personal life that you wish for
the community. The question for you is
can you still live out your intention
within the intention of the community?
For example if your intention is to be a
vegetarian but the rest of the
community does not wish to adopt this
for the intention of the community, will
that actively stop you from being a
vegetarian? If not then be the
vegetarian you wish to be, even though
it’s not part of the stated Mission and
Vision and others can follow your lead
if they so choose. Setting the intention
of a community is about creating a
space where you can all live in
harmony. Even if the final Mission and
Vision is different than the one you had
in mind the question is can you live
within it? If so, then you have created
community.

2) Organizational Structure

Just because everyone has agreed to a
set intention and created a Mission and
Vision Statement, do not assume that
means everything will be smooth from
here on in. No matter how harmonious
a group people still to be organized to
enable activity, and need a mechanism
for making group decisions. There are
many organizational and decision
making models that can be used for
your community. What I am presenting
here is an organizational and decision
making structure I call a Stewardship
Mandala. This model is a non-
hierarchal organizational structure that
provides both autonomy for
individuals, giving them the necessary
freedom to work on their 'slice of the
pie' without being bogged down in
committee, yet still provides oversight
and team work.

The organizational structure is
composed of individual lobes each
headed up by a 'Steward'. How the
Steward is chosen remains a question
for your community, but they should be
chosen based on experience,
leadership skill and willingness to do
it. These people are called 'Stewards'
because stewardship is a core tenet of
sustainable land management. A
Steward is not one 'in charge',
stewardship is not a position of
personal gain. A Steward is one
entrusted with the responsibility of
caring for a resource for the benefit of
the whole group. Each lobe represents
a specific function that needs to be
done for the community, analogous to
departments in a business structure.

The Steward doesn’t necessarily have
to do all of the jobs in their lobe
themselves, but it is the Steward’s job
to see it that the jobs are getting done.
If the Steward cannot accomplish the
tasks of their lobe themselves then it is
the Steward’s responsibility to recruit
and assemble a team under their
guidance who can. With a team in
place it is the Steward’s responsibility
to set the goals, strategies, policies,
and necessary outputs of the team
based on the agreed upon goals and
strategies of the community.

Within each lobe their can be sub-
circles, each representing a more
specific function within that lobe. It is
the duty of the individual Steward to
define and arrange the sub-circles
within their lobe, in essence each sub-
circle is its own mandala within the
mandala, and has it’s own ‘sub-
steward’ to oversee it’s functions.
There can be multiple sub-circles
within a lobe that each operate
independently of each other, but under
the care of the lobes Steward. There
can even be sub-mandalas within sub-
mandalas, like individual cells within
individual organs within the body of an
individual being that operates
cooperatively within a larger
community. For




example a community may have a lobe
that is Food Strategy. It could be the
mission of this lobe to oversee the
food security of the entire community,
and it would be headed up by a Food
Strategy Steward. Within the Food
Strategy Lobe there may be sub-circles
of Gardens, Kitchen, the Food Co-Op,
and Food Not Bombs. Perhaps the
Steward of this lobe has experience as
a chef and therefore acts as the head
chef for the Kitchen, but the Steward
must then oversee assembling the right
team for the other functions of the lobe.
The Garden Circle would be in charge
of designing, creating and maintaining
gardens for the community, and may
very well have several sub-circles
each representing an individual garden,
each garden would have it’s own
substeward. The Kitchen Circle would
be a community kitchen run by a
Kitchen Steward that may store food in
the pantries and fridges, make
preserves and fermented foods, and of
course cook group meals. The Food
Co-Op circle headed up by the Co-Op
Steward may use group purchasing
power to buy and distribute food to the
members of the community who pay in.
The community may decide to work
with their local chapter of Food Not
Bombs and the ‘interface’ between the
community and their local FNB chapter
would be it’s own Circle. The kitchen,
gardens and co-ops may very well sell
meal plans, participate in farmer’s
markets and engage in other activities
that generate income. That income
could be used to pay for all their
activities, and any excess funds
generated would then go to the General
Fund to be overseen by the
Stewardship Council. Other lobes may
handle functions such as building
projects, events, outreach, or
administration.

Together all of the Stewards of the
main lobes, the organs of the body if
you will, form a Stewardship Council.
The Council is a consensus body. The
Stewardship Council sets the
'deliverables' of each Lobe. For
example the Stewardship Council may
decide that the Food Lobe needs to
provide 10 lbs of kale every month, or
that the Education Lobe should run 5
courses a year, or that the Construction
Lobe needs to build 1 Earthship a
month. The individual Stewards are
then responsible to the Council for
meeting those goals. The goal of the
Steward’s Council is not to micro-
manage each lobe, but to enable
communication and coordination
between all of the different lobes to
help them achieve their goals, as well
as to provide 'quality check' and
accountability to each Steward for
reaching their goals. In a worst case
scenario the Stewardship Council is
empowered to remove or replace a
Steward if their lobe is not performing
it’s function or cannot account for it's
activities, decisions and the
consequences of those decisions under
the Steward’s guidance.

It is not necessary for the ‘sub-
stewards’ to be present at the
Stewardship Council meetings,
however that doesn’t mean they can’t,
or shouldn’t. Remember it’s not about
creating an elite group ‘in control’, it’s
about creating efficient meetings.

If finances are an issue, each lobe
generates it’s own income if
appropriate. The Steward oversees the
raising of funds, makes sure that all
things within their lobe that need to be
paid for are covered, keeps 10% of the
net for a discretionary fund in the lobe
and then the financial excess is
‘deposited’ into a general fund
overseen by the Stewardship Council.
Individual Stewards can then make
proposals for how the General Funds
are used. Sub-circles can also generate
their own income and follow the same
process, depositing the excess funds
into their Lobe's discretionary fund
under the Steward's supervision. Of
course a sub-circle in a sub-circle
would deposit it's excess into the
discretionary fund of the sub-circle
above it. So on.

It is the intention of this diagram that
EVERYONE in the community be in it.
That is everyone in your community
has to fit into one of the functions of the
community, being a part of some team.
This is part of the Permaculture ethic
of fair share. Fair share not only means
you get a fair share of the bounty, it
means you participate in a fair share of
the work. Membership in the
community should be contingent upon
that agreement. The exact details of
what is required of a team member is
up to the individual Steward.
3) Synergistic Communication

Now, just because you have a set
intention, and an organizational
structure that still does not mean
everything will work out smooth. You
all still need to be able to come
together and create action, to do that
you need to communicate in a way that
blends the best of everyones ideas into
one. Synergistic Communication is
about using and valuing the diversity of
individual ideas your community can
generate, and integrating rather than
segregating these ideas into a cohesive
whole.

This proposed method of synergistic
communication may seem overly
formalized. First off, it is being given
in the context of creating formalized
procedure for an organized community,
secondly just keep in mind that this
'formal' procedure can be serve as just
more of a guide for you in your
informal, personal life. In the
'procedures' of Synergistic
Communication I refer to 'the circle'.
The circle I am referring to could be a
formal circle at a meeting where
minutes must be taken, or it could just
mean a circle of friends hanging out, it
could even be applied to just two or
three people.

Choose a facilitator for the circle,
someone who will keep the
conversation on track and organized.

First is the 'call in'. This is the formal
opening of the circle. One by one you
go around and basically every one says
something they find focusing and
inspirational and then calls themselves
by name 'into the circle'. The main goal
of this is to focus everyone on being
present and attentive. In touchy feely
crowds the call in is usually performed
while holding hands.

Open the circle with a 'Check In'. A
check is just taking a minute to see how
everyone is doing, a slightly more
formal version of asking 'how are
you?' There are many different ways
you can do check ins, they don't always
have to be the question how are you.
I've done check ins before where
everybody in the circle gets to lead us
all in one yoga pose, where everyone
picks a lyric from a song that typifies
how they currently feel, where
everyone just makes a noise that
typifies how they feel, or shares a
dream. The point of the check in is just
to get a feel for how the group is.

Review what has been accomplished
by the circle since the last meeting.
Each Steward in the circle gives a
brief report about their lobe, this way
the whole group is informed about
what has gone before and now has
context for this meeting. Now review
or set the goals for this meeting, why is
the circle getting together, what is to be
accomplished? It is the facilitator's
responsibility to make sure that the
group discussion is moving towards
this goal.

Now that you know how everyone is,
you know what has gone before and
you know what you are here to
accomplish the members of the group
can start making proposals on what the
group should be doing. Give everyone
a chance to make their proposal and
explain it to the group, and let there be
one round of clarifying questions. Then
the group decides on which proposal
they would like to adopt and enact.

Now when listening to proposals it is
important to understand your own
disagreements with the proposal. If you
think a proposal is a bad idea, why?
Do you find the proposal to be 'off
mission'? Do you believe the proposal
will lead to a demonstrable bad end,
for example will it empty out the bank
account, or result in a structure that
will fall down, or take too long to be
accomplished? Now, be honest with
yourself, is it just that you don't like the
proposal because you would rather see
it done your way, do you just not like
the person making the proposal, do you
feel the person making the proposal is
'peeing in your pool' so to speak or do
you truly find the proposal to be, on it's
own merits, a bad idea? When
objecting to a proposal it is of utmost
paramount importance that you be
honest with yourself, and with the
group about why you don't like the
proposal. There is no place for egos in
synergistic communication. Proposals
should be rejected for legitimate
reasons, not just because you aren't
getting your way, or for a personal
grudge. Yes, we all need self
actualization and positive self esteem,
but seeking those needs does not justify
tearing down someone else's attempts
for the same. Proposals are supposed
to be made for the good of the group,
not the good of the individual, and they
are supposed to be rejected because
they are bad for the group, not because
they are bad for the individual...unless
the proposal is for the group to do
something for the good of an
individual, that is of course up to the
group.

Whatever your objection to a proposal,
it's not enough to just to object. You
need to share why so the group can
attempt to combine the ideas into a
something that works well for the
group. In Permaculture the problem is
the solution. If you can see the problem
with a proposal you should also be
able to go that extra step and see a
solution. Voice both, the problem and a
proposed solution. However if you just
see no solution to the problem with a
proposal then you should make a
counter proposal of your own that
avoids the problem you just identified.
Remember that all conflicting opinions
should be expressed and handled with
Non Violent Communication.

It is a fact of human nature that
sometimes people talk for much too
long during meetings, drawing them out
from being a meeting into a test of
endurance and patience. It is
recommended that discussions be
moderated to be fair, yet concise and
expedient. Towards that aim I
recommend that the facilitator guide
the discussion on a proposal through
three rounds. First the proposal is
made. Then everyone gets their initial
statements. The facilitator consciously
makes sure that each person has a fair
amount of air time to make their
comments or ask questions. After
everyone has had their initial say the
facilitator then guides another round so
that everyone has a chance to make
clarifying statements or questions.
Then finally everyone gets to make one
last final comment. People should not
waste their air time just reiterating
something that was already said. So
your group should develop some sort
of silent hand signal that basically
means "I agree". Perhaps the signal
might be to give a thumbs up or the
like. That way when someone is
talking if you wish to show that you
totally agree with them you just give
the signal while they are talking. Based
on the three rounds the facilitator can
call for a vote, extend another round of
discussion or possibly table the
discussion.

Now that the proposal is on the table
the group decides. The aim should be a
consensus decision, but again
objections should be based on
'reasoned and paramount objections'
not just whim, opinion or hurt feelings.
Reasoned and paramount objections
means you can articulate some
objective, solid reason why the
proposal should be objected, perhaps
you feel it does not accomplish the
goals of the meeting, it is too
expensive, or in your past you have
tried similar things and they didn't
work and this proposal does not over
come any of the problems you see.
Either way, if the preponderance of the
group feels this is a good idea you
should agree still to support it. At it's
heart synergistic communication is
about you giving other people in your
community the support, personal
respect and opportunity for self-
actualization that you yourself will
someday want in return. It's not about
creating a tit-for-tat culture, it is about
creating a culture of mutual support.

Once an idea is adopted by the group
the implementation of the idea should
be nested in an appropriate lobe of the
organization under the care of a willing
Steward to ensure it's implementation.

5) Non Violent Communication

Now you have your community
intention, your community organization
mandala is in place, and you have your
procedures for synergistic
communication. Even with all of this,
conflict and disagreements may still
result and they need to be resolved in
peaceful means. In other words yelling
at each other, hurting each others
feeling and tearing down each others
esteem will tear a community apart.
There are many techniques and tips for
NVC, Non Violent Communication. At
it’s heart is the notion that instead of
increasing the anger and hurt feelings
to resolve a conflict, instead of trying
to ‘win’ the argument, all parties
involved are agreed that they will try
to resolve the issue in a way that
leaves both parties feeling positive. At
least, they should agree to come to a
resolution they can both agree to live
with. In NVC you avoid using
insulting, threatening or belittling
remarks to one another. One of the core
tenets of NVC is not throw blame at
people for how you feel, but rather to
try and get them to understand how they
are making you feel. The hope being
that they will not wish to cause you
emotional pain and once they
understand how they have effected you
they will work with you to change
that...it is of course expected that you
are doing the same in equal good faith.
The idea being that once all parties
involved are endeavoring not to
emotionally trigger the other they can
now discuss the issue calmly,
rationally and come to a mutually
agreed resolution. One common
technique in NVC is ‘reflecting’, that is
saying back to a person your
interpretation of what is going on in
their head. The idea being that if you
can reflect back to them in a way they
agree with then some understanding
has been reached. Of course NVC
requires a level of personal honesty,
honesty with one’s self, that some may
find difficult. It helps in NVC to have
an agreed upon neutral party present to
help facilitate the communication
between the parties involved. One
common technique in NVC that I also
like is for each person to say the issue
they have with the other, but then to
also say something they think is a
positive thing about the other person.
This is not just an effort to mollify the
other person with flattery, the goal is to
remind yourself that this one issue does
not define the entirety of the person’s
character. The goal is also for the other
person to hear something about
themselves that can resonate positively
with you, opening the door for the
building of future positive interactions
that will hopefully overshadow this
negative one. NVC requires a level of
restraint, compromise, compassion,
honesty and strength that many find
challenging at first. However in a
community, it is a necessary function,
because human nature is not perfect
and conflict will arise and when it
does the people involved need to be
able to express themselves but they
also need to be able to come together
in a positive way to be able to function
as a community. This paragraph is not
adequate to be able to truly apply
NVC, there are more techniques and
concepts that need to be learned and
applied. This paragraph is only to
make you aware of the concept and of
its necessity.

6) Create Community Goals and
Strategy
Now that you have your shared
community intention, your community
organization, the procedures for
synergistic communication and engage
in NVC you are now ready to get to
work! Your community now needs to
set goals and enable actions to achieve
it’s mission. The organizational
mandala should help with this
endeavor greatly as each lobe is
empowered with a mission, function
and purpose that should guide them.
However it is not enough for each lobe
to be acting independently. The
question to be put forth to the Steward
Council is what are our group goals?
What is it our community as an entity
wishes to do and accomplish? Not only
do you want to answer the question of
the what, you want to know the when,
the how and the why. When your
community knows it’s goals it will be
easier, if not inevitable for all of the
individual lobes to function
independently but in a coordinated
manner, each moving autonomously
toward a common goal that will benefit
all. Once you know your community
goals you can then develop step by
step strategies that each lobe can
engage in to move towards that goal.

7) Create Your Community Standards,
Norms, Agreements

The standards, agreements or norms of
a community set the acceptable
behaviors in a community. The goal
here is not to tell people how to act or
behave, but to have an agreed upon
way of interacting that enables the
social and psychological health of the
community. What those standards are
may vary from community to
community. Some typical ones are
physical violence will not be tolerated,
an adherence to non-violent
communication, respect of people,
property and the environment. Share
air space, meaning let everyone have a
fair amount of time to talk is common.
Things like that. Depending on how
your community is set up this may
include something like paying dues, or
providing space for meetings or
something like that. Again, the idea of
the standards, norms and agreements
isn't to tell people how to act in your
community, nor is it to try and make
sure that everyone around you only
likes to do the things you like to do, or
only believes the things you believe,
it's to establish a fair standard of
behavior necessary to make your
community work.

Here I would like to make a comment
about creating community standards
and agreements. I've lived in two
communities, known people who have
live in communities such as Arcosanti,
Auroville, Damanhur, the Farm,
Findhorn and others, and I took a
Masters level class on community
studies at Northern Arizona University.
From those studies I have learned to
caution people to avoid what I call
XYZ communities. Meaning
communities that have agreements like
'we are all vegetarian, neopagan,
polyamorous, income sharers' or
something like that. It has long been a
meme in the communities movement
that this is how one makes 'community'.
For one, from my studies it seems that
these type of communities basically
just don't work. There are many
reasons why. To sum up, they tend to
be very exclusionary to a vast variety
of good people, also they tend to fall
apart after awhile because they
prohibit cultural evolution of the
community. Frankly I believe that it
sends the wrong message that there is
only one way to be sustainable. There
is a common belief that building
communities based on XYZs reduces
conflict and increases synergy, frankly,
that is just not true. I have seen plenty
of vegetarians, and neo-pagans, and
eco-christians get into just as many
conflicts and have as much disharmony
as groups composed of people with
varied beliefs and personal habits. I
have seen chain smokers sit down with
non-smokers and establish good
rapport with each other. I have seen
vegetarians and meat eaters live
together harmoniously. It's not about
the habits and beliefs, it's about the
character of the person.

The practical, utilitarian argument that
XYZ communities doesn't work aside,
it also to me seems very non-
Permaculture. If in our physical
Permaculture systems, such as gardens
and buildings, we are supposed to
value diversity, and integrate rather
than segregate than shouldn't the
invisible, social structures of a
Permaculture community do the same?
XYZ communities tend to falter
because they prohibit social, and
personal evolution. What if a person in
a vegan community one day has an
epiphany that it's more important to be
a local-vore than a vegan? What, now
they have to leave the community that
has been their home? That doesn't seem
much like People Care to me. Frankly,
XYZ communities in my observations
and interactions tend to lend
themselves towards the queen-bee
syndrome (this is not a gender phrase,
a man can attempt to be a queen bee as
well), where one person tries to call
the shots of who is a 'proper'
community member. Permaculture tells
us to creatively use and respond to
change, if we should do so in our
physical systems shouldn't we do so in
our invisible, social systems as well?
As long as you as a community can stay
focused on your similarities, have
respect and tolerance for each other,
agree to work out your conflicts, and
agree to synergize on your
commonalities then you don't need
XYZ to bond your community together.
Of course, ultimately it is your
community so make of it what you will.

When creating your agreements and
standards it is also important to create
some process of escalating
consequences for those who won't
follow the community agreements and
standards. This process can vary, but a
good model is the 'three strikes your
out policy', and it should be decided by
some form of community council, not
just by individuals. In other words, if
you would want an escalated
consequence levied against someone
you would take it to the Council, make
your case and the Council would then
decide to levy the consequence or not.
This ensures that the behavior is
something that does offend the
community, not just rubs one person the
wrong way. The whole process should
be fair, transparent, written down and
communicated to any prospective
member. However before anything is
taken to any Council for any official
action I recommend that people in your
community are first encouraged to use
NVC to try and come to an agreement
on the issue. THEN if one of the
parties will not do this, or will not
respect that agreement let the
consequences begin.

Creating community isn't about
excluding those who believe
differently than you. It's about
including those with whom you have
something in common. It's about being
respectful and tolerant. It's about
creating a space that every one has a
chance to share in. Sometimes they
each go off to be their own thing, that's
OK, everyone in the community doesn't
have to be with everyone all the time.
Sometimes they get to be in the middle
and everyone in the community is
respectful, tolerant and even
supportive of that. And when the time
comes for synergy they all put their
differences aside and come together as
a community. Your community
standards, norms and agreements are
their to provide a social and personal
structure to enable all of that to
happen.

The Bigger Agenda

With this community section,
especially the Organizational Diagram,
I have a bigger agenda I am pursuing. It
is to create a larger network of inter-
connected communities. I believe one
of the main things the community
movement has suffered from is that
each community has been solely it's
own entity, separate from the others.
Many people who have sought to live
in community have found that each
community is so completely different
that it is very difficult to transition
from one to another, and while they all
share similar beliefs and goals there is
no inter-connectivity between them. So
I am sharing my community
organization diagram in the hopes of
helping to create a sort of standardized
way to create community. Keeping in
mind that standardized does not mean
conformist or homogenized. If multiple
communities follow the organizational
mandala it will make it much easier for
them to connect together easily and
efficiently. Not only that, but the
mandala can be used to create much
larger organizations of communities. I
am proposing that each community
would have it's own Stewardship
Council. Then each community would
send a representative to a larger Local
Community Council. This Council
would be composed of representatives
from each local community, I leave it
to you to decide what constitutes
'local'. Just as in the organizational
mandala each Lobe represents a
function of the community, each Lobe
of the Local Community Council would
represent an entire Community. I highly
recommend that no Local Community
Council have more than ten
communities within it. Then each Local
Community Council would outreach to
other Local Community Councils in
their same watershed, yes it is
important that they be in the same
watershed.
Each Local Community Council then
forms it's own Lobe in what would
become a Watershed Council. Then
each Watershed Council could
outreach to ther Watershed Councils
and form the Lobes for a Geo-regional
Council, this would be on the scale of
a State, but not necessarily dependent
on State boundaries. This system may
need to be tweaked here and there
depending on the exact parameters of
the Watersheds and Communities
within them of course. Then each Geo-
Regional Council forms the Lobes for
a Bio-Regional Council.

A Bio-Regional Council would be
something like 'The American
Southwest' in scale. Then each Bio-
Regional Council would form the
Lobes for a Continental Council, so on
and so on, until finally the formation of
a Global Council. Again, each Council
should be formed of no more than ten
individual lobes, so again the system
may need some tweaking. For example
there may be a two or three Bio-
Regional Councils in the same bio-
region, they would simply form a
'Greater Bio-Regional Council'. By
using the simple pattern language of the
organizational mandala we can create
a much larger organization of
communities across the country if not
the globe.

As was said, Permaculture benefits
from the network effect, the more
involved the stronger the system.
While all individual communities need
the freedom and autonomy to be what
they are and live in accordance with
their own principles my hope is that by
following this greater organizational
motif they can all come together,
interconnect and interact to form a true
social movement.
Ecovillage Design Diagram

So we've talked about the Invisible
Structures of Community Design now
let's talk a bit about the Physical
Structures of Community Design. To
aid us in this discussion I have created
the Community Design Diagram as a
visual tool. Now, not all Permaculture
Designers will be designing whole
eco-villages, and not all communities
will be eco-villages, but I wanted to
share a pattern language for eco-
village design I have developed during
my studies. This pattern language will
help you when designing ecovillages
true, you may also be able to adapt it to
apply to any type of physical
community lay out. This diagram
should not be taken as a literal design
diagram, in other words I am not
suggesting that communities be built in
perfect concentric circles. This
diagram is a useful tool though in
helping you to plan out an eco-village.

The diagram is divided into four
Community Layers.
The outermost ring represents the
Public Layer. This is the area(s) of the
Community that are open to the general
public. This may represent tour routes,
offices, parks and the like. The 'rules'
and uses of this area should be crafted
to take into account the public face of
the Community.
The next ring represents the
Community Layer. These are the areas
of the Community that are open to any
and all community members, but not
the general public. Here 'rules' and
uses are designed to make these spaces
open and comfortable for all members
of the community to use freely.
The third ring represents the Intimate
Space Layer. Intimate Spaces are those
which are not open to the general
community to use freely but are close
enough to private spaces that they are
sort of 'invite only' kind of areas. Here
'rules' and uses are designed for the
various 'subcommunities' within the
larger community. An example of an
Intimate Space in modern design may
be a porch or front yard. These are
spaces that are near the public but no
one in their right mind would just enter
willy nilly unless invited by the
proprieter of the private space they
attach to. While this may seem counter
intuitive to community design, it is
important to have such spaces where
one can sub-divide out of the
community once in awhile and
congregate with a more intimate circle,
or by one's self.
The final inner circle represents the
Private Layer, the personal private
space of an individual. The dark grey
box in the middle represents the home,
or Permaculture Zone 0, surrounded by
Permaculture Zone 1, which would be
that individuals private yard space.
The private space should also be
surrounded by visual barriers to
provide visual privacy as well. Of
course friendly barriers cordon the
area off.




This diagram should not be taken to
imply that all of the intimate and
community spaces are in one area in
the middle. There can be clusters of
these spaces within the community
area. The second diagram below gives
a more accurate depiction of what the
community design diagram should look
like. The black box in the middle
represents the Community House.




In the larger diagram the grey rings in
between each layer represent a
'friendly barrier'. A friendly barrier is
something that deters the 'wrong'
people from wandering from one Layer
into another Layer. For example a
chain link with a sign that says KEEP
OUT, or VISITOR'S NOT WELCOME
is not friendly. Perhaps a row of
hedges and trees could serve as a
barrier. While it would keep people
out and maintain some modicum of
privacy it would also seem very nice,
pleasant and natural. Friendly barriers
could be other things such as cob
benches with big walls so you can't see
past the wall but you can stop and have
a seat on the nice bench. Perhaps you
could use hoop houses to form friendly
barriers'. One of the best ways to think
up original friendly barriers is to think
multifunctionally. Being a barrier is
only one function of this design
element, so ask yourself what else
does this design element do besides
being a barrier? Like any Permaculture
element it can be multi-functional.
The line marked 'pathways' has two
arrows, one which says 'formal' and
the 'informal'. The most 'formal' of
pathways is a sidewalk with lights
lining it, the most informal of pathways
would be a dirt trail. Signage can also
help mark formal and informal
pathways. There is no science to what
makes a pathway formal and informal,
this is left to your imagination as a
designer.




This cob bench at the Lost Valley Educational
Center serves as a friendly barrier. Photo used
with permission of Inger-Lise Burns

You will also see on the pathway line
blocks marked 'gate'. The most
common, and unfriendly threshold, is a
gate. These don't have to be a
traditional gates per se, but more of a
threshold that just needs to look like
you are now going through something
and entering a new area. These could
be trellised arches, or just two posts
sticking up, maybe one with a small
mailbox or bird house on it. Again, no
true design science here, but I believe
you get the gist. If you are trying to
contain animals or children though true
gate might be appropriate.

The two wedges marked 'food systems'
represent a recommended way to
arrange gardens, greenhouses,
orchards, etc in the community design.
They flank the pathway to represent
that pathways should take one through
the food system spaces, integrating the
food systems into the aesthetic effect of
your landscaping, but with traffic
control so people don't tromp all over
your groundcovers and prepped beds.
The wedge furthest out, in the Public
and Community Layer, is labeled
'production area'. This would be where
you make food systems that are run for
'commercial purposes'. Perhaps they
are the systems for your communities
CSA, provide food for your
communities cafe, or produce crops
sold at Farmer's Markets. The next
layer of the food systems wedge is
labeled 'shared community'. This is
where you have food systems that, as it
sounds, are shared by community
members solely for the consumption of
the community and it's individual
members. The wedge marked Personal
Systems are those used by individuals
to create their own food systems.

The wedge labeled 'commercial
interface' is where community
members, or the community in general
can have spaces to do business with
the public without this public roaming
freely in the community, intimate and
private spaces. In general the
Commercial Interface should be on the
edge of the Community Layer, directly
connected to the Public Layer on one
side, and the Community Layer on the
other. Commercial Interfaces could be
studios classrooms, galleries,
storefronts, what have you. They may
also include any business offices or
tourist centers.

Of course this is only one way of
looking at how communities could be
designed. There are many other
schools of thought on community
design. Again, this diagram is not
intended to tell you how to design your
community. The goal is to help you to
organize your thoughts on it, and to
hopefully provide some inspiration for
your community design.

Composting
There are several key cycles that
support the world's ecology. The
atmospheric cycle exchanges oxygen
for carbon-dioxide, providing the
necessary gases all living things need
to breath. The hydro-cycle circulates
and cleans water for all living things to
drink. Just as importantly, the nutrient
cycle provides the raw building blocks
that all life needs. The driving
component of the nutrient cycle is the
action of plants. From the standpoint of
the nutrient cycle plants use Sunlight
(and water and atmospheric gases) to
convert inert minerals in the ground
into nutrients. The plant incorporates
the nutrients into it's body, and then
other plants and animals can then eat it
to access and live off those nutrients.
However that is not the whole story of
the nutrient cycle. The rate of
converting minerals into nutrients is
not enough to support the entire
ecology. In addition to 'mining'
nutrients from the Earth the ecology
needs to constantly recirculate
nutrients through local biomes. A berry
bush converts minerals into berries, a
bear eats the berries. As the bear
walks around in the woods it poops the
digested berries back out, which break
down and become 'fertilizer' for future
bushes. When the bear that has been
living off these berries dies it's entire
body will decompose and become
'fertilizer' for future berry bushes as
well. This is the essence of the nutrient
cycle. Plants and animals don't truly
use up nutrients, they constantly
recycle them. Living things use the
nutrients and then when finished places
them back into the soils whence they
came to continue the process anew. So
in a nutshell, plants use solar energy to
mine the inert minerals of the Earth,
make nutrients out of them and then put
those nutrients into circulation,
basically, forever.

Modern infrastructure systems have no
true nutrient management systems. We
grow food in large agri-business farm
plots fertilized by chemicals made in
factories. The food is then harvested
and shipped out to grocery stores and
restaurants around the country, if not
the world. People consume the food.
Any wasted food is 'thrown away' in
ecologically inert landfills along with
hordes of other waste. Once the food is
eaten it is then pooped out into toilets
which then flush the excreted nutrients
out to sewer systems. Put it all together
and you can see that our current
infrastructure system is actually
designed to break down the nutrient
cycle. Maybe that wasn't their intention
but that is what it is does by design.
Nutrients are harvested from one
location, transported away and then
discarded. As we continue to consume
and discard nutrients the ecology of
our food producing biomes breaks
down requiring vast amounts of
synthetic fertilizers to be manufactured
and shipped in to replace them. Our
nutrient cycle becomes dependent on
fossil fuels, and industry. Even with all
these industrial inputs our system still
weakens the ecology, requiring higher
and higher levels of industrial inputs
just to maintain a constant level of
output.

Composting is the technique of the
controlled, and sanitary,
decomposition of food scraps and
organic waste to be used as fertilizer.
By composting we maintain the nutrient
cycle. While there is industrial
composting happening in America,
composting is something that can be
done at the do-it-yourself scale right in
the home. When composting is
combined with home grown food
systems you can maintain your own
local nutrient cycle and create an up-
cycling ecology right in your own
backyard!

There are many different techniques for
composting. Many people who
compost create compost piles in their
yards, where food scraps, and plant
materials are layered on in a way that
encourages their controlled
decomposition. The compost is
occasionally 'turned', tossed almost
like a salad, which mixes up the
material within and allows oxygen into
the process. Oxygenation is necessary
for proper and non-smelly composting.
Some practitioners create a compost
'tea', where the compost is soaked in
water, dissolving the nutrients into a
fertile solution which can then be
applied to crops and soils. If not
properly tended though a compost pile
can become smelly and un-sanitary
which is why it is important to
understand how composting works
before attempting to make large scale
compost piles. Some people seeking to
live a sustainable lifestyle compost on
a small scale, which makes it much
easier to design and manage. Compost
can break down at different rates
depending on the system. In essence,
the faster the compost breakdown the
more work and care by humans the
system will take. If one does not have
the proper skill and knowledge and
tries to create a fast compost system
one could accidentally create quite a
mess.

In recent years there has been a
veritable explosion of composting
devices designed for the layperson.
These devices are basically containers
designed to be easy to use, to allow
proper aeration, and to easily allow
the compost to fall into soil. There is
no real technology involved in these
composters. Contained within the
device the food scraps break down in
the enclosed container and become
fertilizer. Most of them are essentially
barrels that are designed to be easy to
rotate with a handle. In some of them
such as the Green Cone, and the Sun
Frost Scrap Eater one simply opens a
lid and throws the waste food in. One
can compost any organic material can
be composted, from food, to leaves
and sticks, to all natural paper
products and clothing fibers, allowing
any natural, organic material to be
broken down and used as nutrients.

Composting is an integral part of
maintaining one of the most important
natural cycles necessary for a
sustainable system, the nutrient cycle.
Once a designer understands how
composting works there are many
different ways a composting system
can be designed. One could even forgo
the designing and use pre-fabbed
composters in the design. The trickiest
part is that the designer MUST design
a compost system that works at the
level the client understands. Even
when the designer is designing for
themselves there are still many tricks
and traps to designing compost systems
that many designers fall into. For one
large scale or fast compost systems
require a lot of labor, skill and time on
the part of the human in the system.
While these large scale or fast systems
hold a lot of philosophical and 'cool
factor' appeal for many Permaculture
Designers, if the system takes too much
time, skill or labor it will not function
properly and will create a mess. While
an efficient composting system should
be part of any Permaculture Design, for
many reasons it is better to be
conservative in designing composting
systems, and always remember Design
Principle #1: The K.I.S.S. Principle
Keep It Simple Stupid!

Digging a Hole
Nine point five times out of ten the first
step in any Permaculture project is to
start digging a hole. If you want to
study Permaculture, you might as well
go out and by your own shovel now.
You will dig holes and you will move
Earth. As a Permaculture Designer you
will develop a whole new respect for
and love of the often taken for granted
hole. A hole can be a many layered,
marvelous thing when you begin to
look at it from a Permaculture
perspective. A hole is much, much
more than just a nothing that is
accomplishing something.

A single hole seems like a simple
thing, but before you can dig that first
simple hole think of all the work you
have to do in analyzing your
environment! You have to understand
your patterns, analyze your
environment, sample the soil, inventory
the wildlife, do a zone and sector
analysis, this is all after the client
interview, and the site selection and
the budget discussion, etc. All of this
knowledge combines into the one most
crucial piece of information for the
success of any project...where to dig
the first hole. There is a lot of
embodied knowledge in that one little
humble hole, many patterns have been
analyzed to create this one, first,
crucial detail.

Digging a hole is also an amazing
philosophical moment. Think about it.
The sound of a shovel going into earth
is the sound of imagination becoming
reality. That imagination comes from
your understanding of Permaculture.
You studied Permaculture because of
your beliefs. Your beliefs are a product
of your philosophy, and that sound of
the shovel head digging into the earth
is the sound of that philosophy
manifesting into and becoming a part of
the real world. That is amazing
transcendental moment to be a part of.

One of the beauties of digging a hole is
the inherent stacking of functions,
whenever you are digging you are also
mining. Every hole generates a little
pile of earth. Usually the earth
excavated from a hole is totally taken
for granted and just thoughtlessly slung
aside. The Permaculture student knows
better. The Permaculture student knows
that every shovel full is a shovel full of
usable resource. No Permaculture
Designer ever just slings dirt. The
excavant from the hole is always
placed somewhere. With every shovel
full you are working on two projects at
once, one that needs a hole, one that
needs some earth.

If you start out only needing a hole then
this shovel full of excavant becomes an
inspiration. Since you got it, what are
you going to do with it? Based on your
soil sample what is it good for? Is it
good topsoil which can go on a
garden? Does it have good clay content
and therefore could be used to make
earthen bricks, or a cob oven? Would it
be good earth to make a berm which
diverts rain water? Before you ever
dig a hole you should know what you
are going to do with the material that
comes out. The possibilities are
limited only by your own imagination
and it all began with digging a humble
little hole.

Be careful, the humble little hole is
also one of the great destroyers of man.
Digging a hole has ruined many a
career and livelihood. Holes have torn
back muscles, torn ligaments, twisted
ankles, and resulted in numerous
shovels upside the head. Ha, ha that
sounds funny, but shovel edges can
slice deep into skin, and crack skulls.
A wildly swinging shovel can be very
dangerous and you must be very
careful when using one. You must be
very aware of your body, and your
surroundings, and your movements.
You should never take one second for
granted while shoveling, because it
only takes one second to twist your
ankle, herniate a disc or slice your
partner across the face.

Digging a hole is also great exercise!
You will have a rock hard buff bod if
you shovel a lot of holes and all sorts
of hotties will be throwing themselves
at you. Never thought the humble little
hole could improve your love life as
well as your health did you? As long
as it doesn't destroy you, digging holes
will make you smarter, stronger, and
better looking.

Never thought there was so much in the
humble little hole did you?
Understanding the beauty of the hole is
only one small way in which
Permaculture can your open your eyes
to the multi-layered beauty of even the
simplest thing in the world.

Disaster Preparedness
Prolific earthen builder Nadir Kalili
points out that most natural disasters
are actually man made disasters. Most
'disasters' result because we build
inappropriate, weak systems that do
not take into account nature's patterns.
Every year a hurricane hits the
southern east cost of America. Every
year these hurricanes knock down
houses, power systems and destroy
roads, and every year we build the
exact same type of houses, power
systems and roads right back in the
same place and get surprised when it
happens all over again, usually laying
the blame on God. Wildfires are a part
of the natural cycle, there are even
trees who's entire reproductive life
cycle depends on wild forest fires. Yet
we build homes made from wood and
petroleum products near fire prone
areas and then get surprised that the
homes went up in smoke. Most floods
in urban areas occur not because of the
overall amount of rain that falls, but
because the storm sewer systems get
backed up, yet we keep building the
same storm sewer systems that keep
causing floods and then call those
floods 'natural' disasters. How many
lives are lost every year due to bad
design? How many billions of dollars
are spent designing the same disaster
again and again? As a wise man once
said, some mistakes are built to last, so
that's what you get. Hurricanes,
tornadoes, wildfires, floods, these
things aren't 'disasters' they are just
nature. The disaster is the ignorance
and arrogance of our design
philosophy.
Permaculture Design is about
designing for Sustainability, which is
about designing for people's needs.
One of those needs is for health and
freedom from injury, another need is
for security. Therefore Permaculture
asks us to design in a way that protects
people from these 'disasters'. Currently
the Modern response to disaster
preparedness is to have various
emergency services handy to patch up
the victims and pick up the bodies,
which is great, but rarely does the
Modern response include designing the
solutions to prevent people from
becoming victims in the first place.
That is what disaster preparedness is
about.

An integral part of the Permaculture
Design process is to do an analysis of
the ecology of the site. This analysis
should include researching the common
'disasters' and dangers in the area.
Once you know what to expect you can
now do some design work to prepare.
Yes, it is true that sometimes nature
will just unleash a can of whoop ass on
us and it's patterns will be more
extreme than predicted. Sometimes
tsunamis are twice as powerful as
predicted, sometimes hurricanes are
twice as powerful, sometimes
wildfires start in un-predicted sectors.
However that is no excuse to be
unprepared for the predictable.

One of the most common disasters are
wildfires. While wildfires are a
perfectly normal part of the natural
cycle, statistics show that where ever
you have humans you have a greater
chance of wildfires. Therefore you
should always plan some fire
prevention in your design to offset the
odds of a human started wildfire. In the
section on the design process you will
learn about an analytical tool called
the 'sector analysis'. Your sector
analysis should always include a fire
sector, which illustrates to the designer
where the greatest fire hazards are
likely to come from.

To determine a fire sector requires
knowing a few things about how
wildfires propagate. For one, unlike
water fire flows up hill. The steeper
the hill the faster the fire will move
and the hotter it will get. So you can
backtrack up any slopes in your
topography to determine the most
likely path a fire will travel. Fires also
need fuel. Wildfires burn best when
they have small vegetation to burn.
Very few wildfires start by burning
trees. They first burn dry grasses, then
shrubs, then dried low hanging tree
canopies. So look for these elements in
your design. Combining the topography
and the fuel analysis will tell you the
most likely fire sectors .
Where ever there are humans it is an
unfortunate inevitability that at some
point there will be cars. If there are
grasses in the parking area for cars that
is definitely a fire hazard! Parking lots
are especially dangerous if there are
shrubs nearby or low hanging tree
canopies over the grassy areas. Adjust
your designs accordingly.

Also unfortunately humans often keep
flammable things on their sites, such as
propane tanks, extra gasoline, oily
rags, or trash. These things should be
isolated from a fire prone area as much
as possible. It may be a good idea to
make a raised slab for your trash
receptacles for these things to sit on.
Also do not use plastic trash
receptacles, metal ones are much safer.
However the BEST way to fire proof
trash is to follow Permaculture
Principle #8: Produce no waste.
Waste paper, the number one
flammable trash component can either
be used to make papercrete, or
possibly mulched or composted.

The best way to stop plastic, such as
bottles, from catching fire is to NOT
put a plastic trash bag liner in their
container. The liners catch fire much
easier than the bottles themselves, but
once the liners ignite they burn hot
enough to further ignite plastic.
However perhaps you can find uses for
the plastic bottles that make them
fireproof, such as planting small plants
in them...just an idea.

NEVER pile old tires up! Piles of tires
are fire disasters just waiting to
happen. Instead, there are many great
ideas for how to use wasted tires that
prevent them from going up in flames.
You can create gardens in old tires.
You can use old tires as the form work
and boundary for raised walkways. To
do so you line the tires along the path
you want, and then fill them with dirt,
and possibly gardens, and then fill up
the area in between with dirt and tamp
it down. You will now have a nice
solid raised pathway. You can also cut
up old tires to make sandals, make
enough and you can possibly sell them
or gift them away (right livelihood
anyone?). Of course if you're super
ambitious you can study how to build
an Earthship and use your old tires to
start constructing your new home.
There are many, many more ideas here,
but the basic idea is that the easiest
way to fire proof your trash is to not
have any.

The actual number one cause of man
made forest fires is, you guessed it,
those silent killers, cigarettes. A
carelessly flung butt can bring down an
entire biome. Are there any smokers in
your design? Well you have a few
options. You can be self righteous and
tell them they shouldn't smoke, like
they never heard that before. You can
be fascist and create a nonsmoking
community and then tear your hair out
as people break the rules and smoke in
totally inappropriate places, increasing
the danger. Or you can design a
smoking area that is made to reduce the
risk of runaway butts. Just a note, often
people design smoking areas to be
uncomfortable and somewhat
inhospitable, which to me is just an
obvious passive aggressive move
mostly made not by non-smokers, but
by EX-smokers. The best way to
ensure a smoking area gets used and
used properly is to design it nice, so
that smokers want to congregate there,
make butt disposal easy and people
will do it. Ignore this advice at your
peril.
As was said fires need fuel so you
should make sure your design does not
put fuel in the form of dried grasses,
shrubs and low hanging canopies
directly in contact with buildings and
structures. Also your land stewardship
plan should include an occasional
'sweep' of the forest floor and other
areas to remove dried flammable
natural material such as dead grasses,
brush, twigs and the like. You should
also make sure any wood sheds are
holding the wood in a way that reduces
the risk of fire. In a best case scenario
wood sheds will always be near a
water source or at least away from fuel
or structures. If you are doing rain
water catchment in cisterns see if it is
feasible to design the cistern so that
they can provide water near fire prone
areas. If you have a structure in a fire
prone area do you best to design the
fire side of the structure from non-
flammable materials.
Another consideration in fire proofing
a site is to make fire breaks in fire
prone areas. Roads and pathways make
EXCELLENT fire breaks! swales can
be possibly be good fire breaks if you
expect them to be moist during the fire
season, otherwise they are
questionable for this purpose. There's
nothing wrong with putting in a few
extra swales, pathways or a winding
road through fire prone areas for the
purposes of making firebreaks.

Gardens can also make decent
firebreaks if you plan on them being
moist during fire season, other wise,
like a dry swale, they are questionable
for this purpose. So if appropriate,
there's nothing wrong with going out of
your way to put in a garden somewhere
to act as a firebreak.

Wind events are also common 'natural
disasters'. You should make sure that
everything in your design is battened
down against massive wind events. Do
your best to make sure that structures
and systems are not underneath
possibly falling branches. If you have
power lines in your design for god's
sake make sure they are not positioned
so that if they fell they could
electrocute someone or start a fire!

Floods are also a common natural
'disaster'. Honestly many floods
happen because humans keep insisting
on building below water lines, and
then they build dams and levees that
break. So the best advice here is to do
your best not to have a site below a
water line. When during your site
analysis you will be checking for your
local building zone ordinances.
Another piece of information your city
or municipality should be able to
provide you with is your 'hundred year
flood plain' analysis. A hundred year
flood plain does not mean that the area
floods exactly every one hundred
years. What it means is that
statistically speaking the odds of a
destructive flood are 1% a year,
meaning that, statistically speaking, it
is inevitable that the area will flood at
least once every hundred years. Some
flood plains may be rated even more
frequent, like a 20 year flood plain or
the like. Basically don't build in flood
plains! In some places that is even
building code, but even if it is not, try
not to house people and vital systems
in hundred year (or less) flood plains.
Also if your area is prone to flood, or
if you just want to be safe try to raise
your structure up whenever you can in
your design. In addition berms and
raised pathways can be used as flood
control earthworks on a site to help
reduce the destruction from minor
flooding. When thinking about flooding
there are two main things to keep in
mind. One, a MAJOR flood will
probably destroy your design not
matter what I'm sorry to say. Unless
you are able to design some sort of
house-boat kind of thing then no matter
how prudent you are there is just no
way to make a truly flood proof design,
especially if you live below a water
line. However the second thing to
remember, while big tsunamis and such
get all the press the VAST majority of
flood damage is actually caused by
minor floods. It only takes a few inches
of flood water to destroy crops and
leave whole populations starving, it
only takes a few inches of flood water
to ruin a home, to wash out a road, or
clog up a storm sewer. A few inches of
flood water here equates to a major
torrent of water over there. So when
planning for floods I'm not saying you
gotta be prepared for a Noah like
flood, that may very well be
unfeasible, but your design should
definitely be prepared for minor
flooding, and that will probably get
you through 90% of the flood disasters
that may happen. If this thinking was
applied broad scale we'd probably
prevent most of the other 10% as well.

Keep in mind, most natural disasters
are actually human made disasters, this
includes floods. It is therefore not
surprising the vast majority of flood
damage occurs in cities. Modern
architecture designs city-scapes so that
water drains off of everything into the
streets. The ubiquitous hardscape of
pavement covering hundreds of square
miles prevents the water from
infiltrating into the ground. Storm
sewers get clogged or over-filled and
back up. The long straight streets
enable torrential flows. Put it all
together it's almost as if modern cities
were purposefully designed to flood. If
cities used Permaculture designs that
caught rain water, combined with
permeable pavement, and broadscale
rain sensitive landscaping most floods
would be averted by design.

While speaking about floods I wanted
to add a point I find usually missing in
discussions of disaster preparedness,
that is making flood proof food
systems. One of the main damages of
floods is not just that they damage
homes, they also destroy crops, leaving
the victim with no food. It doesn't take
much to flood and destroy gardens in
the ground, an inch or so will do it.
Using Permaculture food systems
design it is possible to make food
systems on roofs, or in raised container
gardens, and other vertical tricks to
keep food production above flood
height. This way if you do flood, at
least some of your food production can
continue unabated. Also it may be
prudent to make sure you have a
versatile crop of medicinals in flood
proof food systems. If the roads wash
out you may not be able to get to the
place where they keep the penicillin,
yet there are many types of plants that
are extremely effective against fighting
infection if used expediently! If you are
concerned about disaster preparedness
I can't recommend this little detail
enough. There are also several great
plants for fighting fever, and stomach
ailments. A flood and fire proof crop
of these medicinals may very well be
the difference between life and death.

Clean drinking water should also be
available in case of disaster. There are
many ways to make this happen, #1
being rain water catchment of course.
Depending on the situation you may
want to know how to make a sand
filter, charcoal filter, or eco-machine
in case you need clean water.
So we're prepared for fires, floods,
wind events, and medical emergencies.
Another topic often missed in disaster
preparedness is the role of deadly
animals. Now this one is going to take
some research on your part, but then
again all Permaculture Design does.
This is the flip side of another
Permaculture strategy. As was
discussed in Animal Systems and in
Plants, the Permaculture Designer can
design systems that attract beneficial
animals and insects into your design.
So a design can attract certain types of
animals, you need to remember that
when thinking about deadly animals in
your area. For example, rattlesnakes
like to sleep underneath rock
outcroppings because they are cold
blooded and the thermal mass of the
rocks creates a warmer micro-climate
for the snake (bet you didn't think
rattlesnakes were instinctive
Permaculture Designers). This means
if your live in a rattlesnake prone area
and your design has something like
that, say, some concrete steps with
space underneath or a porch with a
concrete overhang, then you may have
just inadvertently created a nice little
rattlesnake habitat right at your front
door. I wager this would go badly for
both you and the snake. Research the
deadly animals in your area and make
sure you are not inadvertently
designing habitat for them, inviting
disaster. You will also want to
research what type of venoms and
poisons are in your area, research and
see if there are plants or other natural
remedies for them that you can design
into your system and then make sure
they are CLEARLY marked so in an
emergency everyone doesn't have to
run around trying to remember or
figure out which plant it was that
counters Brown Recluse venom or
what have you.
In many ways Permaculture Design is
inherently disaster preparedness
design. A disciplined Permaculture
designer will do their best to have
appropriate designs. Many disasters
occur because we are dependent on
centralized infrastructures. If cities and
homes had Permaculture food systems
intertwined throughout them then
starvation and hunger, one of the most
common consequences of a disaster
would be greatly reduced. If our
electrical infrastructures were
powered by systems such as solar
panels and wind generators located on
the very site itself then there would be
no such thing as a widespread power
outage. If people were culturally
accustomed to homes built from local
materials with do-it-yourself scale
techniques then in case of disaster
reconstruction would be affordable,
easy and quick. The creation of
community networks educated in
Permaculture Design would make it
much easier for local areas to organize
themselves to recoup from disasters
rather than waiting for 'help to arrive'.
The use of Appropriate Technology
would make it so much easier for life
saving infrastructures systems to be
quickly built, or re-built and
maintained through times of disaster.
Keep in mind that during most
disasters one of the most horrific
consequences is not the disaster itself,
but the reaction of human panic,
looting, and violence. When the poop
hits the fan we start acting like we're in
a Mad Max movie. You may think to
yourself this represents some
fundamental flaw in humanity but
remember Maslow's Hierarchy.
Humans need their physiological needs
met, and they need security needs met.
When needs aren't met the result is
often mis-targeted violence. So from
this stand point the human reaction of
looting and violence during a disaster
makes perfect sense. If localized, do-
it-yourself Permaculture systems were
in widespread use then during times of
disaster people would still have a
sense of security if they could but focus
on working together as community to
recoup from the disaster themselves.
Remember most disasters are man
made disasters because the Modern
philosophy relies on inappropriate,
centralized, weak infrastructures and
does not engender community self
reliance. Permaculture inherently
reverses all of these trends.

Earthworks
So much of the ecology is effected by
the shape of the land. Just as soup is
contained within a bowl, the ecology is
contained with geological features
such hills, valleys, grottos, cervices,
soil composition, and so. The shape of
the ground effects the local hydro-
cycle, it creates micro-climates, it
determines what plants can grow
where, and through these effects the
animal populations. A Permaculture
Designer cannot take the geo-
morphology of the site for granted. In
Permaculture Design terms,
humanmade geo-morphology is
referred to as 'earthworks'. Any
Permaculture Design will probably
require some extent of earthworks,
some more than others. Keeping in
mind that in Permaculture you aren't
just moving the earth around for the
sake of moving the earth around. You
are not a painter and the earth your
canvas! The point of Permaculture
earthworks is to create geo-
morphological conditions (how's that
for a big smart sounding phrase) that
increase the ecological health of the
site. This can be accomplished by a
host of design strategies.

Earthworks can have many roles in a
Permaculture Design, perhaps the most
important is using earthworks to slow
erosion and to create the proper
conditions for plants to grow. In the

55 section on rain water you will learn
about berms, swales, keylining and
other landscaping techniques in
regards to harvesting rain water.
However berms, swales and keylines
are hardly the whole story of
Permaculture earthworks.

Terracing is another common strategy
with earthworks. Just like swales,
terraces help reduce water flow and
create garden space on slopes and
hills. Berms and swales can be used to
slow surface water flow, and terraces
should be used when you need to
create garden space, or otherwise need
a large flat plane of ground for some
reason.

The use of bio-swales is a common
earthwork strategy. A bio-swale is just
like a swale but with plants chosen
specifically for their function as a bio-
remediator rather than as a food crop.
Swale on contour at the Ampersand Sustainable
Learning Center. Photo used with permission of
Amanda Bramble.
As you study other design elements of
Permaculture such as gardens, or
building you will learn more about
their specific Earthworks
requirements. However Earthworks
can also exist as it's own discipline,
shaping the ecology of your site. But
you must be careful! More so than any
one other element earthworks is
probably the single most
transformative, or destructive, element
in any design. As you put in berms and
swales for your gardens, and
foundations for your structures you
must constantly be aware of the effect
all of this earthmoving will have on the
overall ecological health of your site.

LEARNING EXERCISE:
Make a swale around the drip line of a
tree, house, or other appropriate
locale. To create a typical swale you
dig down into the earth creating a
trench about the depth and width of
your shovel head.
You then collapse in the sides of the
trench so that it creates a V shaped
trench.
The slope of the sides is very gentle,
meaning your swale should not look
like some mancatching trap, but rather
a gentle dip in the landscape.




You then fill the swale with mulch. You
can also put cut grasses, leaves, and
even bunches of sticks and twigs in the
swale, basically any woody or leafy
thing that will break down into mulch.
You can also plant a swale, typically
with summer and winter perennial
ground covers, and possibly some
edibles as well.




You will want to use a level as you dig
your swale to make sure that it is level.
A typical technique for using a level to
make a level swale is to get the longest
architects level you can and two
bricks. As you dig out the swale you
place the bricks in it, and then lay the
level on top of the bricks. In this way
you will be able to determine if your
swale is level. Another great method
for leveling swales is to create a water
level. However due to limits of length
and scope of this book I will not
include the directions on how to
construct or use a water level; I do
however suggest that you do some
internet research if you are more
interested in using water levels. You
can also use an A-Frame level.

Ecological Design
Permaculture Design is the design
principles and methods for ecological
design...even if most ecodesigners
don't know that. Ecological Design
isn't about designing an individual
thing, a garden, a building, etc.
Ecodesign is about designing
comprehensive local ecological
systems. Another way of saying it is
that ecological design is about creating
sustainable, beneficial micro-climates
that meet the needs of it's human
inhabitants, up to and including the
need for shelter. In the same way a
beehive is a natural habitat, or a bird's
nest is a natural habitat, ecodesign is
about creating natural habitats for
humans. Ecodesign is not just about
structures and buildings, it is about the
whole life support infrastructure, as
well as quality of life provided by the
environment. This means ecological
design is about the creation of habitats
that provide, food, water, shelter, and
safe space.

Ecodesign is about human habitats,
ecodesign is an anthropocentric
endeavor. Basically, nature doesn't
need us to design for it, nature can
design itself through natural
evolutionary forces. They only thing
we need to do in regard to Nature is
endeavor to not impact it, and to
remediate any impact we do have.
However, primitive living skills
notwithstanding, humans can't just live
out in nature. We need shelter, reliable
food, cleaned water, tools (a house
counts as a tool), and places for culture
and socialization. We need areas we
CAN impact, and we need to ensure
that those areas do not detract from the
health of the greater ecology. Keep in
mind that while ecodesign is an
anthropocentric mind set, ecologically
speaking, humans need plants and
animals. Therefore a good ecological
design would support a variety of
plants and animal species as well.

Because the goal of Permaculture
Design is to be Sustainable, and
Sustainability means we must meet all
of our Needs, an Ecological Design
should endeavor to provide all of a
human's Needs wrapped up within one
design site.
Bio-shelters designed by the New Alchemy
Institute are a prime example of ecological
design

One of the goals of ecodesign is to
reduce our impact on Nature. For that
goal our ecodesigned, anthropocentric
habitats will need to be usable by
Nature as well. All in all even though
your ecodesign is anthropocentric it
will still need to take into account
Nature's needs and allow Nature's
systems to flow through your
ecodesign. Deer may need to walk
through your area to get to their food
supplies. Birds may need to rest in
your area as they fly back and forth
over the seasons. Seasonal water
courses may need to meander through
your site once a year. Ecological
Designs must also support Nature's
cycles.
Simon Wyld's Ecosa Plus Project, 2010. Image
used with permission of the Ecosa Institute.

A good ecological design maintains
resource loops within the boundaries
of the ecodesign, i.e. it should be as
closed loop a system as possible, with
the loops flowing through all of the
structures and into the landscape itself,
and back into the structures. You will
learn more about loops, such as the
hydrocycle, the nutrient cycle, and the
atmospheric cycle through out this
book. There is another important loop
for ecological design, the materials
loop. The materials loop involves
recycling, and re-using materials.
Building materials can be made from
natural elements such as bamboo,
sustainably harvested trees, and earth.
All of these materials can be returned
to the Earth whence they came and
maintain an ecological loop that way.
Non-natural materials can be recycled,
and re-used, maintaining an industrial
loop. By maintaining material loops
we reduce the impact on the natural
systems.

The design principles of Permaculture
will help you to do ecological design,
however it is important that you
understand what ecological design is.
Just studying Permaculture without
understanding the context of ecological
design does not give you a full picture
of the goals of a Permaculture Design.

Being a comprehensive ecosystem and
habitat a true ecodesign would address
all of an inhabitants needs, guided by
Permaculture. So let's review the
Maslow's Hierarchy, and Permaculture
but here use it as a checklist.

Here is a checklist of the elements that
should be taken into consideration :

• use as little energy use as possible
• provide comfortable shelter for
habitation
• generate and/or harness as much
energy as possible
• provide as much clean water as
possible
• provide as much food as possible
• provide space for social interaction
and meaningful interaction with nature
• have the appropriate support
structures for any endeavors planned
for in the design
• include habitat for other species as
much as possible
• be easily maintained by the
inhabitants
• be designed for maintenance, upgrade
and disposal/obsolescence
• manage all of it's own 'waste'
• follow the Triple Bottom Line

When choosing resources for the
project they should be chosen in this
order of importance:

• taking advantage of as many on site
resources as possible
• use as many locally produced
resources as possible
• use as many re-used resources as
possible
• use as many recycled resources as
possible
• use as many rapidly renewable
resources as possible
• use as many green products as
possible
• be re-designed to be more in
accordance with the principles of
ecodesign as possible
• have as much bio-remediation of its
impact as possible done on it's behalf

All plants should be indigenous,
ecologically compatible or contained
with growing systems such as
greenhouses, or container gardens
designed in accordance with
Permaculture Principles.

Electricity
Yes, it can be argued quite successfully
that humans don't "need" electricity.
However I pretty much guarantee you
that if you present a design to client
that has no electrical power in it and
you tell them to cook on open fire and
read by candle light you will be fired.
Electricity does exist abundantly in
nature but electrical systems as humans
you use them are not natural. We don't
even truly capture the electricity from
the environment like we can rain or
nutrients. We use machinery to convert
some other form of energy into
electricity, taking this energy from
nature, then use the electricity which
converts it into radical heat. This is
why I consider electricity one of the
few things we truly "take" from nature
because there is no loop by which we
return it. Well, here is where I am
forced to recite one of my more
controversial axioms...sometimes
nature has to take a hit for the team.
Make all of the primitive living
arguments you want, modern humans
need electricity. The goal though of a
Permaculture Designer is to take
electricity from renewable sources in
nature in such a way that doesn't
degrade the ecosystems we are taking
the electricity from.

In any Permaculture electrical design
the first and foremost strategy is to
reduce, reduce, reduce. The best way
to generate electricity in a way that
doesn't harm the environment is to not
need as much in the first place. A
Permaculture electrical designer
should use every trick they can to
reduce the electricity requirement of
the design. The most prominent
strategy in this is through passive
design, which is covered later. Though
a passive design may not be able to
replace 100% of the electricity needs
of a human, every watt saved is a watt
saved.

Natural lighting is another important
strategy in Permaculture electricity.
During the day the Sun is out and bright
and provides all of the light you need.
So why should we have to turn on
power burning light bulbs, even if they
are CFLs, to provide light in a building
during the day? There are many design
tricks for natural lighting. Skylights are
a well known design element that
allows sunlight in. There is another
development called a Light Tube,
which is essentially a metal tube in the
roof that concentrates and focuses
sunlight into a room. Lightshelves are a
design feature added to windows that
bounce light from the window up to the
ceiling, which then bounces back down
deeper into the room. There has even
been some very interesting work done
with using fiber optics to harness
natural sunlight. Of course interior
design color choices help to effect
natural lighting as well.

Another great strategy for reducing
electricity usage is to use solar water
heaters. Solar water heaters do not use
any electricity, the heat from the Sun
directly heats up the water. There are
many solar water heaters on the market
you can purchase for use in your
design, the company Sun-Mar makes
some excellent and affordable models.
Yes, solar water heaters can even work
in moderate to cold climates. The trick
with them is to allow the Sunlight to hit
the water storage, but to have the water
storage in an insulated envelope so the
ambient cold doesn't draw the heat out.
There are even some excellent do-it-
yourself solar water heater designs
using water tanks held within
refrigerators with glass fronts. You
open the door in the morning, the
Sunlight comes in through the glass and
heats the water, and you close the door
at night to keep the water insulated.
Now it is true in some cases that the
solar heater is a 'pre-heater', meaning
you still use a standard water heating
system, but because the water is
already heated by the Sun your
electrical water heater doesn't use as
much energy to bring the water up to
temperature...a watt saved, is a watt
saved.

Another electricity saver are
Earthtubes, the technical phrase for
which is 'ground source heat
exchanger'. Several feet below the
ground, below what you call the 'frost
line', the ground the Earth is at a
constant temperature. Though this
temperature varies slightly around the
world it is typically a constant
temperature around 50 degrees F.
Earthtubes, being buried below the
ground are always at this constant
temperature. By pumping the air out of
the tubes and into a structure you help
to heat or cool the structure. If the
ambient temperature inside your
structure is above 50deg then an
earthtube will help to cool, if the
temperature is below 50deg then the
earthtube will help to warm. This
system helps to moderate temperature
without using any real amount of
electricity.
Me in front of solar panels at the Cougar
Mountain Community. Photo taken by Inger-Lise
Burns

Of course when most people think of
sustainable electricity they think of the
ever popular photo-voltaic panel, often
simply called solar panels. Photo-
voltaic panels are a great source of
renewable electricity, however they
can be cost prohibitive. It is often a
good strategy to design a phasing in of
solar panels, having the entire structure
for the array built, but filled in one or
two panels at a time over time...much
like ecological succession. In the
hands of a good Permaculture
Designer, who always attempts to
maximize the ecological potential of
any design element, solar panels can
be used for more than just power. My
favorite use I have seen for solar
panels in Arizona is to place them over
parking lots, there they generate power
AND shade the cars, as well as keep
the asphalt of the lot cooler thereby
reducing the local heat island effect.
What a great stacking of functions! I
have seen passive solar designs where
the solar panels are used as the
overhang to keep the Sun out of the
structure. Stacking functions! As a
Permaculture Designer try to stack the
functions of any solar panels used in
your design.

Photo-voltaics are not the only
renewable sources of electricity of
course. If you are in a windy area you
can also use wind generators. If you
have an appropriate water course on
your site perhaps you could integrate a
micro-hydro system, small water
powered generators that can fit in
small water courses. Don't forget that a
diesel generator could also be
powered by veggie oil or bio-diesel.

In addition to designing sustainable
sources of electricity your should also
choose energy efficient appliances to
reduce consumption.

As you well remember, lifestyle
choices are a part of being sustainable,
and there is a direct connection
between lifestyle and electricity
consumption. If you truly wish to be
sustainable you will also need to
adjust your lifestyle habits in a such a
way as to reduce your energy
consumption.

The ultimate goal of pretty much any
person who envisions designing
anything Permaculture is to be entirely
off-grid, that is to not be hooked into
the grid of your local power company.
While this is a laudable goal it is not
always a feasible one. So don't beat
yourself up if you can't afford or other
wise design in any of these nifty
electricity gadgets. However you can
always, always, always, design to
reduce electricity consumption! There
is no one magica wand which solves
electricity consumption. Use and value
diversity! Use as many systems as
possible, all working together in
unison, this includes passive design
strategies, lifestyle habits and a host of
other strategies all brought together.

Forests
Forests are dense, three dimensional
environments teeming and squeeming
with life and are perhaps the pinnacle
achievement of the Earth's ecology.
Technically a 'forest' is a specific type
of biome where the keystone species
are long lived trees. However a forest
is not made by trees alone. Forests are
uncountably vast populations of plants,
animals, insects, fungi, and microbes
all working together in an
unfathomably complex, inscrutably
dynamic, harmonious and perfectly
balanced pattern of competition,
predation and symbiosis. Forests
recycle 100% of their resources. They
are solar powered and hence will
never run out of energy, and are
completely thermodynamically
efficient. They are the cradles of
evolution, transforming life itself. They
are multi-layered and three
dimensional, fitting as many active
systems as they possibly can into the
smallest area, maximizing every square
inch they impact. They produce food at
rates per square acre far greater than is
even conceivably possible for a human
made modern agricultural system.
Forests create the Earths atmosphere
as we know it by exchanging oxygen
for carbon dioxide in vast amounts in a
balanced cycle. They alter the hydro
cycle on a planetary scale, and are
even able to literally break down and
transform geology, altering the very
surface of the planet. Forests are the
engines that power that Earth's
biosphere. If Permaculture systems are
to be modeled after nature then the
forest is the greatest model that we
have to follow. Given what you now
know imagine the power of a
Permaculture system based on the
forest!

Forest types are often identified by
their dominant tree type, or greatest
ecological effect. For example forests
can be referred to as pine forests, rain
forests, cloud forests, temperate
needleleaf, etc.

Forest Layers
While every forest is unique, they all
follow the same basic structure
composed of seven layers.

1) Canopy, the topmost level,
sometimes called the overstory is
composed of large, dominant trees that
usually grow best in full Sun. Oaks,
hickories, maples, and basswood are
some trees that form the overstory.

2) Understory or second level down is
shaded by the overstory. It is made up
of smaller versions of overstory trees
and shade-tolerant (can grow well
under low light conditions) species
such as dogwood or hornbeam.

3) Shrub layer contains woody shrubs
such as gooseberry, coralberry, and
highbush cranberry.

4) Herbaceous layer is the most
diverse and has mostly non-woody
species-grasses, sedges, and
wildflowers.

5) Ground layer (forest floor) holds the
soil builders and stabilizers-fungi,
mosses, liverworts, and lichens.

6) Substrate – basically the area below
the soil, underground
Forest Succession

Not only do all forests follow the same
basic seven layer structure, they follow
the same process pattern of evolution
and growth, called succession.

There are two types of succession,
Primary and Secondary. Primary
Succession is when a forest develops
on a blank slate. If there was a pre-
existing forest or ecosystem who's
ecology collapsed and the process of
succession begins anew it is called a
Secondary Succession.

Whether it is Primary or Secondary
Succession follow five basic stages:

1) Pioneering Phase - Pioneer species
such as grasses, ground covers, and
sub-surface fungi come in and they
begin to 'work the soil' increasing the
health of the soil enough for the
development of larger plants. This
stage usually allows for populations of
small ground animals, a lot of them
burrowers, and populations of grazing
animals. The ecological activity of the
Pioneers begins to build up top soil,
setting the stage for future evolution.

2) Shrubs – The ground cover layers
have provided nutrient rich soil, water
retention, and reduced soil erosion.
This sets the stage for shrubs to begin
growing. Shrubs provide habitat for
small birds, greater insect activity and
a more diverse and dense population
of small ground animals, however
large grazing animals begin not to fit.
Shrubs add nutrients to the soil,
increase water retention, bring in more
animals who in turn aerate the soil
more, and drop more fertilizer in the
form of urine and feces. This all
prepares the soil to be able to support
more complex plants. The vulnerable
saplings of dominant trees take root,
growing under the protective umbrella
of the shrubs and fed by their
ecological activity.

3) Young Forest – A forest is 'young'
when the saplings of dominant tree
species have finally broken through the
Shrub layer. Along side of these will
also be many other variety of trees that
are both shorter lived and shorter in
mature stature. Together these trees
will then cause significant shifts in the
micro-climate. They shade out the
shrubs and ground covers that once
protected them and set the conditions
for the growth of a whole new pallette
of shade tolerant flora. The flora and
fauna which pioneered this area can no
longer survive here in great numbers.
The young forest is a unique type of
eco-tone. Instead of being a geographic
ecotone, where two biomes lay
physically side by side and overlap, it
is a temporal eco-tone where one
biome from the past is now giving
away to a new biome of the future.

4) Mature Forest – Eventually amid the
ground cover of the young forest the
first generation of large, long lived
trees, such as dug fir and oak, trees
reach their mature state and their wide
branches form a canopy layer that
covers the forest. The mature forest
canopy causes drastic changes in the
micro-climate, some can create cloud
forests or full rain forests depending
on the exact ecological conditions. The
canopy itself becomes an ecology
supporting a wide range of very
specific bird, aboreal animal, insect
and even secondary plant life. The
Mature forest begins to create several
layers of ecological activity as major
tree dwelling animals species now
have habitat, and vines, mosses, lichen,
etc can now find purchase among the
bark and branches of the tall trees.
5) Climax Forest - The main hallmark
of a Climax Forest is that there are
now multiple generations of the
dominant tree types growing side by
side. But a Climax Forest is about
more than the trees. The ground is
covered in essentially a forest mulch
which is building true topsoil. Where
in a Mature Forest most plants are still
growing in the ground itself, in the
Climax Forest many plants are now
growing in the forest mulch layer itself.
The systems and cycles of the forest
have stabilized into their own
multilayered biome with the dominant
trees as the keystone species. Yes it
may have disasters and random
ecological occurrences but the mature
trees provide a strong and stable
environment, and there is by now a lot
of nutrients held in the soil to help it
recover. A mature forest may have to
go through several major disasters
before it's system can adapt and
develop into a Climax Forest.

So now that we have some
understanding of what forests do and
how they do it, let's look at forests
specifically in terms of Permaculture
Design. To break it down a system
modeled after a forest would be
composed of many three dimensional
layers containing various plants,
animals, insects, fungi, and microbes in
a dynamic and a harmonious balance,
provide shelter for varied animals,
powered by renewable energy,
maximize it's space usage by taking
advantage of layering and guilding,
recycle 100% of its resources, produce
as much food as possible, and maintain
hydrological and nutrient loops.

Agro-Forestry

This is the practice of integrating
human planted crops into an existing
forest. This method preserves the
existing forest and harnesses the
ecological power of the forest for the
crops. Of course one has to be very
careful about what crops are integrated
into an agro-forestry system, as they
must fit within the available niches
within the various forest layers. A
Permaculture Designer should not go
out into the forest just to plant gardens
for the sake of doing it, but if the forest
exists on the site in an area where a
garden should be, then agro-forestry
becomes an appropriate strategy.

Eco-Forestry

While humans obviously don’t design
forests, we can play a role in them.
One of the Permaculture ethics is that
of fair share. All things are entitled to
a fair share of what the Earth has to
offer, even humans. Therefore humans
are just as entitled to the bounty and
benefits of the forest as any other
animal. However, as we have seen
humans are capable of breaking the
ecological balance. Lacking the
instinct for ecological balance, we
need a guiding philosophy and
principles. For Permaculture
Designers this is eco-forestry, the
sustainable management of forest
systems which allow us to benefits
from existing natural forests in a
sustainable way. Eco forestry allows
us to help forests reach their climax
state and stay there in a healthy way.

Food Forests

We can apply the examples of Forests

take advantage of the vertical

up to seven 'layers' of production.

in understanding why I say
Permaculture is not about making
designs that reduce our impact, but
rather increase it. Take a minute to
contemplate that before reading on.

The Hydro Cycle
There are three major cycles that
support the ecology, in fact one could
say these three cycles ARE the
ecology. These three cycles are the
atmospheric cycle, the nutrient cycle,
and the hydro-cycle. The atmospheric
cycle exchanges carbon dixiode for
oxygen and creates the very fresh air
we all need to live. The nutrient cycle
creates healthy food to give us energy
and building blocks for our bodies and
minds. The hydro-cycle provides us
with fresh clean water to drink. 70% of
the surface of the planet is water, but
it’s water we can’t drink. The ecology
of our planet uses a vast system called
the hydro-cycle to create fresh water
by simultaneously moving and cleaning
water across our planet.

There are essentially six ways water
moves in the hydro-cycle; evaporation,
condensation, infiltration,
precipitation, runoff, and subsurface
flow.

Evaporation is when water changes
from a liquid to a gas and becomes a
vapor. Evaporation is driven by heat,
wind and agitation. The higher the
combination of those forces the higher
the rate of evaporation. It can be said
that the hydro-cycle begins with
evaporation when ocean water
evaporates. The evaporation of the
water leaves the salt and ambient
minerals which make ocean water
nondrinkable behind. The evaporated
water forms clouds in the sky, which
eventually float above land masses.

Condensation occurs when water
vapor comes back together and forms a
liquid again. Water never just
condenses out of thin air, it only occurs
on solid surfaces that must be colder
than the water vapor itself. Even for
rain to occur water vapor must
condense on dust particles suspended
in the air.

Precipitation is when water falls from
the sky, i.e. rain, which is a function of
condensation. Water vapor in the
atmosphere, usually appearing as
clouds, condenses onto dust particles
suspended in the atmosphere and the
droplets fall down to the Earth
gathering more and more water as they
fall. Most of the rain in the world of
course falls directly into the oceans,
however a large amount of rain falls
onto the land masses.

Infiltration is when water works its
way into the small spaces within solid
objects through capillary action. Water
enters through small pores, thin tubes
or other extremely small spaces such
as micro-cracks in rocks or concrete.
Solid surfaces have an 'infiltration
rate', that is how much water will
infiltrate into the object over a certain
amount of time. If the amount of water
falling onto the surface exceeds the
infiltration rate, that is more water is
coming down than can be absorbed by
the surface, then the un-absorbed water
becomes 'run-off.

Runoff is simply when water collects
into large amounts on a surface and
then flows across that surface and runs
off. Water can runoff the ground, off
roofs or off any surface. When running
off water tends to try and collect into
streams, and of course runs down hill.
The run off drives the force of erosion
and carves channels into the ground,
creating larger and larger streams,
eventually forming full rivers. If the
water cannot run off it will collect into
pools, and can even build up into full
lakes.

Subsurface flow is the movement of
water below the surface of the Earth.
Below the ground the earth is not
completely solid, lacing through the
geology of the world are small caverns
and basins. As water runs off from the
surface, or sits in lakes some amount of
it infiltrates into the ground and
collects into subsurface cracks and
features below the ground, becoming
subsurface flow. Some sub-surface
water flows back to the ocean, but a
large amount of it ends up being stored
in aquifers. Aquifers are essentially
lakes underground that hold the water.
Being well away from the heat of the
Sun or the winds, the evaporation rates
of aquifers are miniscule and water
can be stored in aquifers for literally
thousands of years; some water in
aquifers is referred to as 'fossil water'
because it has been stored in aquifers
for literally millions of years (or 5,000
years according to the Creationists).

Most surface run off collects on the
tops of mountains where clouds and
rain gathers into runoff that runs down
the mountain with gathering force,
carving out streams on the side of the
mountain. The streams eventually meet
up and concentrate more water until it
forms into larger running bodies of
water called rivers. All rivers
eventually lead back to the ocean,
depositing the run off back whence it
came so the process can begin all over
again.

However these five forces are not the
whole story of the hydro-cycle:
The seasons drastically control
temperature, and wind patterns, both of
which have a more than massive
impact on the hydrocycle. Given what
you know so far of the hydrocycle, and
of the seasons, you can probably think
of many of the ways the seasons effect
the hydrocycle.
Geological morphology, that is the
shape of the land, forms a major
component in the hydro-cycle.
Mountains, hills, valleys, and other
geological formations shape the
patterns of how water flows across the
surface, creating streams, rivers and
lakes.
Soil composition is also important to
the hydro-cycle. Softer soils have
higher infiltration rates and can absorb
more of the water run-off. The more
water that infiltrates into soft soils the
more gets stored in sub-surface
aquifers.
Of course trees and plants use water.
They primarily live off water that has
infiltrated into the ground, this
phenomenon of course ties in heavily
with soil composition. Also keep in
mind that plant colonies change soil
composition over time, making it
softer, which raises its infiltration rate.
All plants give off water from their
leaves through a process called evapo-
transpiration which simply means that
water evaporates out of the plants
leaves. Put it all together this means
that plants break up the earth, creating
softer soil which then absorbs more
water. The plants then pull that water
up into their bodies and then when it is
warm or windy essentially sweat that
water out, creating water vapor. This
water vapor can later condense and
form precipitation. Plants can also
cause water vapor to condense pulling
it out of the air. While this may all
seem like a big waste of time since the
water just ends up going back on its
way, remember this all a big cycle.
That's how the hydro-cycle supports
life on this planet, by moving water in
vast cycles that support plants and
animals along its way. Many people
are completely unaware of the role that
plants, especially trees, play in rain
patterns. Forests can account for
upwards of 30% of the rain fall in the
local area, deforestation can destroy
the local precipitation cycle and lead
to desertification.
Animals also play an important role in
the hydro-cycle. First off animals can
have a massive impact on plant
colonies. Animals help plants
propagate, and the plant propagation
follows the migratory patterns of the
animals. As we discussed plants have
a massive impact on the local hydro-
cycle. Not only that, animals eat plants.
When an animal consumes a plant, it
not only consumes the flesh of the
plant, it consumes the water the plant
has pulled out of the ground. The
animal then travels on and through
many process, such as breathing,
sweating, pooping and peeing
redistributes that water to other
locations in the ecology. Fish live in
the ocean, here on dry land, we ARE
the ocean. An ocean encapsulated in
our bodies.

Watersheds

Simply put a watershed is a large basin
in the landscape that collects all local
precipitation, and run off into one area.
Usually all of the water in a watershed
ends up deposited into a large body of
water such as a lake or river.
Watersheds are most often bounded by
mountains, hills or crests and funnel
down to rivers or lakes. Watersheds
can be as small as a city park or large
enough to encompass several states.
Some states have several watersheds
within them. According to the US
Geological Survey there are
approximately 2,110 individual
watersheds in the continental U.S.
Each supports particular and specific
ecological systems, or biomes.
Diagram of a watershed and it's hydro-cycle
To recap the hydro-cycle:

The sun beats down on the ocean and
the water evaporates from the oceans
and forms clouds. Wind blows those
clouds over the land masses of the
Earth. When the clouds enter cooler air
they condense and create precipitation.
This precipitation then rains down,
collecting into watersheds. The
watersheds funnel the rain water run
off into streams and then rivers. When
the land forms depressions in the
topography the water collects into
lakes, other wise it continues to flow
until it reaches the ocean. Along the
way, depending on how soft the soil is
water infiltrates into the ground where
it collects into aquifers where it is
stored for eons. Plants pull the water
up into their bodies, where that water
than evapotranspires back out, creating
rain, fog, humidity and other water
vapor phenomenon. Animals eat the
plants, absorbing their water and then
migrate away and breath, sweat, pee
and poop that water into a new
location. As water moves across or
under the ground it also moves
dissolved nutrients, and water flows
have a major impact on the animals
and plant populations which constitute
the biome fed by the watershed. All
excess water not absorbed by an
aquifer eventually works its way back
to the ocean where the process starts
all over again, often depositing
nutrients within the coastal regions
around the landmasses.

Now that we have recapped and
understand the natural hydro-cycle we
are now going to add another layer of
understanding the realities of the
global hydro-cycle; we are going to
discuss the role of human water
infrastructure.

As we learned animals play a large
role in the hydro-cycle, and humans are
animals. We create massive water
infrastructures that have massive
impacts on our local watersheds, and
when trying to understand the hydro-
cycle it is important to understand
human's role in it.

Humans typically get water by either
damming up water ways and creating
reservoirs or digging wells down into
aquifers. Both of these have major
effects on the watershed, and on the
whole hydro-cycle. It is often the case
that water pumped out of aquifers or
reservoirs is shunted away through
hundreds, if not thousands of miles of
pipe, where it is distributed to and
used in cities or on vast agricultural
fields. In cities water is used for
domestic purposes and for the care and
maintenance of lawns and ornamental
landscaping, evapotranspiration from
this landscaping raises the local
humidity. Some of the water is shunted
into aesthetic water features such as
fountains or misters where it
evaporates into the air. Domestic water
is typically flushed out into the sewage
system. Water runs off streets and
rooftops, washing down oil from cars
and other urban contaminants into
sewer systems which eventually flush
the water into rivers. Agricultural and
landscaping water tends to evaporate,
infiltrate back into the ground or is
runs off into streams depositing
chemicals and excess nutrients into the
watershed.

This modern approach to infrastructure
design has major ramifications on the
hydrocycle. When dams are built and
reservoirs made they create new lakes.
They also tend to dry up the rest of the
water shed down river, causing
massive ecological changes. Many
cities use their wells to pull water out
of their aquifers faster than the natural
hydro-cycle can re-fill them, draining
the water supply. In either case vast
amounts of water are displaced from
their natural locations and then flushed
to some other location having a major
impact on far off watersheds. The
water from our infrastructure is infused
with many contaminants from human
usage, changing the nutrient cycles of
the watershed.
However dams and wells are by no
means the only way human built
infrastructures impact the hydro-cycle
of your watershed. One of the major
contributing factors to the
characteristics of a watershed is the
geological morphology. To build their
towns, cities, and roads humans move
tons and tons of earth, literally re-
shaping the landscape. As the
landscape is re-shaped the entire
watershed is effected.

We now understand that plants and
trees also have a major impact on the
hydro-cycle. Humans harvest trees at
an alarming rate, clear cutting huge
swaths of forest. Deforestation has a
major impact on the local water shed
and in many cases can lead to
desertification. As was discussed trees
can provide precipitation to local
areas, and when the trees are gone
local precipitation patterns can change.
When the trees are gone water run off
can flow faster over the land reducing
infiltration, and increasing erosion.

The presence of large cities bring with
it a massive amount of concrete and
asphalt. These materials have the
capacity to soak up large amounts of
heat from the Sun and from cars. This
creates what is called the 'heat island
effect'. In essence the heat island effect
changes the local weather conditions
by raising the local temperature. By
raising the temperature the heat island
effect can, and in all large cities does,
change the local precipitation patterns.

When all of the effects of the human
infrastructure on the hydro-cycle are
added up it cannot be denied that
human infrastructure is an important
part of the hydro-cycle. This
phenomenon is nothing new, even long
before the invention of the modern age
humans were engaging in massive
water infrastructure projects that
effected their local watersheds.
Understanding the effects of the total
hydro-cycle are extremely important to
any Permaculture Designer. Humans do
and will always need water, therefore
humans will always require water
infrastructures, and these
infrastructures will always impact the
local watersheds and global hydro-
cycle.

Other sections of the book will cover
how to design hydro-cycles, in
particular the sections on rain water
catchment, and water cleansing.

Invisible Structures
The world around us is made up of
many things, some of them visible,
some of them not. There are many
physical structures that shape our
world. The shape of the land, the trees,
the flows of water, our buildings and
streets, all of these physical structures
govern our lives. There are just as
many ‘invisible' structures that shape
our world and govern our lives as
well.
Invisible structures are called so
because you can't really point at them
and see them as a thing. They are the
forces that govern the behavior of the
both the man made and natural physical
world around you. While these
structures are invisible, they manifest
themselves through the physical, so
every invisible structure has a
concurrent physical structure. Some of
the invisible structures are man made,
some are made by nature. The seasons
are a good example of a natural
invisible structure which manifests
through physical structures such as
snow, wind and leaves falling from
trees. While we all know and
recognize the seasons you can't really
point at a discrete thing and be like
'there's Spring over there'. Invisible
Structures are like the Matrix from the
movie, they are the world behind the
world you see. An excellent example
of a man made invisible structure is the
8 hour work day. It's concurrent
physical structure is the freeway
system which must be able to absorb
the ebbs and flow of commuter traffic.
What do you think are some of the
other physical effects and physical
structures governed by the invisible
structure of the 8 hour work day?

In addition to patterns such as seasons
or work days, invisible structures can
also be systems of governance, rules,
or even cultural traditions. As was
said all invisible structures have a
concurrent physical structure and all
physical structures create an invisible
structure, like the ripples of cause and
effect. Placing a physical structure in a
design will create an invisible
structure around it. Let me say that
again - placing a physical structure in a
design will create an invisible
structure around it. P.S. for purposes of
this conversation a human counts as a
physical structure because they are an
animal system, hence every human
creates an invisible structure around
themselves. The designer must be able
to recognize this fact. Just as the
Permaculture Designer must be able to
identify the micro-climates generated
by physical structures and create a
design that integrates those micro-
climates into an ecologically
harmonious whole system, the designer
must be able to do the same with
invisible structures.
Picture of an Invisible Structure...ha ha, just
kidding

As with many aspects of understanding
Permaculture Design, understanding
invisible structures is not a laundry list
of things, it is a method of thinking, a
way to understand the logic which
underlay how the world works. In fact
invisible structures ARE the
underlying logic which governs the
world. If you haven’t guessed by now
there is a strong correlation between
the concepts of invisible structures and
behavioral patterns. By reading
patterns one can come to ‘see’ the
invisible structures which govern our
world and shape our lives. While it is
impossible to make a list invisible
structures a Permaculture Designer
must be able to both identify the
invisible structures of the world, as
well as design new ones that will
impact design in a positive ecological
way. Invisible structures are easiest to
represent with charts, diagrams, lists,
schedules and other media displays.
However the Permaculture Designer in
an almost zen like way must be able to
‘see’ these actual structures as they are
out in the world, like Neo being able to
read the code of the Matrix. ‘Seeing’
invisible structures is tightly inter-
related to pattern understanding and
observation skills.
Designing invisible structures entails
understanding not only the patterns that
govern nature such as the seasons,
behavior of animals, and micro-
climates but also the types of patterns
that govern human behavior such as
economics, community organization,
and social interactions. When you
begin to do your Permaculture Design
you will have to be able to identify
existing invisible structures on your
site and to understand their role in the
site’s ecology. The designer must be
able to assess which of those invisible
structures will be beneficial to the new
design, and which have to go. You will
design new invisible structures and
you must be sure that they and their
necessary physical structures are
feasible within the new design.

life, and their parallel physical
structures, and the environmental
impact they have.

Passive Design
For millennia human architecture
excelled at passive design to keep
buildings cool in the heat and warm in
the cold. That is until the modern age
when the International Style of
architecture fully embraced the usage
of HVAC (Heating Ventilation and Air
Conditioning) systems. With the advent
and proliferation of HVAC dependence
modern humanity has all but forgotten
the art of passive design, though there
are still many structures still standing,
heating and cooling themselves without
the use of so much of an milliwat of
electricity or a single drop of oil.
Permaculture Principle #2 calls for us
to catch and store energy, this is the
essence of passive design. Passive
designs are able to catch, store and
harness the ambient energy that
naturally exists in the environment.
When you talk of passive design most
people’s thoughts turn to passive solar
design. While passive solar design is
an integral component, passive design
is much more than just the ever popular
passive solar design, it is a whole
systems approach attempting to modify
the environment within a structure by
harnessing natural existing forces and
energies.

The Permaculture Principles call for us
to design from p first understand the
behavioral patterns of heat. First a
brief side note, there is actually no
such thing as ‘cold’, there is only heat.
Hot and cold are not things in nature,
they are words that express an
emotional reaction to the flow of heat
in your own body. When the ambient
temperature is higher than your internal
body temperature then heat is flowing
into you and you feel hot; when the
ambient temperature is lower than your
internal body temperature then your
body is bleeding heat out and you feel
cold. Now with that said, let’s continue
with the discussion about heat.

There are three ways that heat moves:
radiation, conduction, and convection.
There are two types of radiation that
move heat, microwaves and infrared.
Microwaves don’t truly move heat per
se. When microwave radiation hits
solid matter the energy of the
microwaves is absorbed and excites
the molecules of the solid object
(we’re taking a few scientific shortcuts
here, but in general this is how the
process works). The excited solid
matter then gives of infrared radiation
which is what we interpret as heat.
Mankind has learned how to produce
microwaves with technology, but for
purposes of Permaculture Design we
will deal with the single largest source
of microwaves for planet Earth, the
Sun. The direct rays of the Sun carry
many different forms of radiation,
including microwaves which beam
down onto our planet heating up solid
matter that is exposed to direct
Sunlight. The excited material then
heats up and bangs out infrared
radiation. For practical purposes we
can just consider the direct rays of the
Sun to produce massive radiant heat.
The color of the solid object effects
how much of the radiation it absorbs.
The darker the object the more
radiation is absorbed, the more the
object heats up. Just as the microwaves
travel through space and heat things up,
infrared radiation also travels through
space and heats things up, infrared just
doesn’t travel as far or as with much
intensity as microwaves. When people
think of radiation they tend to think of it
traveling in beams, moving like
billiard balls, traveling in a straight
line from one point to another. The
designer must understand that this
image is completely false. Radiation
does not travel in beams it travels in
spherical pulses; that isatterns to
details. Before one can design the
details of a system that passively
moves heat one must radiation pulses
out in all directions from its source,
like throwing a stone in a pond, not just
in the direction the designer wants it to
go.
Conduction is another way that heat
moves from one object to another.
Conduction, a derivative of the word
‘conduct’, occurs between any two
solid objects that are in direct physical
contact. Conduction is attempting to
reach thermal equilibrium, that is heat
transfers from one object to another
until they are both the same
temperature. The exact rate of heat
transfer depends on many factors, such
as the density of the materials, their
atomic composition, and in general the
more of their surface area in contact
the greater the heat transfer.
Convection is the transfer of heat
through the movement of medium, such
as water or air. Technically convection
is similar to conduction. That is the
medium, such as air or water, comes
into physical contact with a heat source
and the heat conducts into the medium.
As most of us know heat rises, so when
the medium heats up it rises, the
medium then transfers its heat into
whatever it flows over or into. For
example if you have a hot rock sitting
in a room, the air that touches the rock
will heat up and create a convection
current, that is it will heat up and rise
through the air. The top of the room
will then heat up due to the convection
current.
When you put these three forces
together you have a description, and
understanding of how heat moves.
When the designers understands these
forces of radiation, conduction and
convection they can begin to design
systems that move heat to where it is
needed most. Another important thing
to understand about heat is that it tries
to reach thermal equilibrium, that is all
things are seeking to have an equal
temperature. Heat flows by these three
forces, radiating, convecting and
conducting from hot things into cold
things. In this process the hot thing
cools off, and the cold thing heats up.
When the temperature between the two
is equal heat no longer flows between
them, the system has reached thermal
equilibrium. Keep in mind that a
system trying to reach thermal
equilibrium can be, and in almost all
cases of reality will be, more complex
than just two items trying to reach
thermal equilibrium. In every system
heat will be bouncing around between
the many varied elements, attempting to
finally reach thermal equilibrium. You
have to understand no system ever will
reach thermal equilibrium (until the
theorized ‘heat death’ of the universe in
a few billion years when the entire
universe finally reaches thermal
equilibrium) however it is the attempt
to reach thermal equilibrium that
creates the flow of heat in all things.
When the designer understands and can
visualize this pattern of action they can
begin to design systems that will
distribute heat effectively throughout a
design.
As when designing water systems one
must know how to concentrate water
where it is wanted, and divert it from
where it is not wanted. One must also
learn how to create designs that will
harness the three ways heat moves, and
the flow of thermal equilibrium to
concentrate heat where it is wanted
and to bleed it off from where it is not
wanted. One must keep in mind that
you cannot perform miracles with
passive design. In extreme climates
passive design may still have it’s
limits. The challenge of the
Permaculture Designer is to distribute
and control as much heat as is
environmentally possible. Once the
limits of what is possible are reached
by passive design then the designer
may need to turn to other methods such
as fireplaces, swamp coolers, or other
appropriate technology to make up the
difference. However the Permaculture
Designer needs to be disciplined
enough, and imaginative enough to not
turn to these alternatives until their
design has reached it’s full passive
design potential.
In nature almost all heat begins with,
you guessed it, the Sun. So passive
design begins with harnessing the heat
contained within direct Sunlight. The
four most important aspects to proper
passive solar design are orientation,
insulation, glass and mass.

Orientation

As the year progresses the Sun keeps
rising higher and higher into the sky
until it reaches its summer peak, called
the Summer Solstice which is on June
21. After June 21 the Sun seems to get
lower in the sky every day until it
reaches its lowest point, called the
Winter Solstice on December 21. A
proper passive design keeps the direct
rays of the Sun out of the interior of the
structure during the summer times
when the Sun is high in the sky; the
same design then lets the direct rays of
the Sun into the structure during the
winter time, when the Sun is lowest in
the sky.

For this to occur the designer must
know three things about the Sun; it’s
direction, the solar altitude and the
solar azimuth. Direction is easy, the
Sun is in the southern sky unless you’re
in the southern hemisphere, for sake of
conversation this book assumes you
are in the northern hemisphere.
Basically you want to make sure that
you’re structure has it’s longest wall
facing solar south.

The solar altitude is the ‘height’ of the
Sun in the sky expressed as an angle.
For example if the solar altitude is 36
degrees then that means that the Sun is
at an angle of 36 degrees above the
horizon. You can find your solar
altitude by looking it up on a ‘Sun
chart’. In this day and age the best
place to find a Sun chart is on the
internet. Based on your latitude a Sun
chart will tell you the solar altitude for
each day. For practical purposes all
you really need to design for is the
altitude for the summer and winter
solstices. Now the reason you need to
know the solar altitudes is so that you
can properly design the overhangs of
your structure so that they keep the Sun
out of your structure in the summer and
let the direct rays of the Sun into the
structure during the winter.
Now dear reader this is the point
where you need to break out those high
school geometry skills you have
probably happily forgotten. That's
right, when you were in high school
and said to your geometry teacher,
'when is this crap ever going to apply
in real life', well here is that moment.
To properly design overhangs you need
to know the formula for calculating the
length of a shadow. The length of a
shadow is equal to the height of the
structure divided by the tangent of the
solar altitude. For purposes of
conversation an ‘overhang’ can mean
anything which keeps the Sun out. An
overhang can be a roof, an awning, or
a window setback into a thick wall
such as in adobe buildings. Once you
know how to calculate the length of
shadows you will then be able to
design overhangs so that the Sun stays
out in the summer and penetrates in the
winter.

Where the altitude is the vertical angle
of the Sun, the azimuth is the horizontal
angle of the Sun. The azimuth is also
measured in degrees and measures the
angel of the Sunrise and Sunset off of
solar south. The azimuth is expressed
as one angle. For example if the solar
azimuth is 36 degrees, that means that
if you were facing due solar south and
turned your head to watch the Sunrise
you would turn your head 36 degrees
to the east, to watch the Sun set you
would turn your head 36 degrees to the
west. Yes, the angle of the Sunrise and
the Sunset are always equal, so you
can express the azimuth with just one
angle. Now much in the same way that
you use the solar altitude to figure out
how the vertical shadow will fall on a
design, you can use the azimuth to
figure out how the shadows are cast
horizontally at Sunrise and Sunset.

By putting together the altitude and the
azimuth you can ‘describe’ exactly how
the Sun tracks through your design.
This information will not only be
important when making the structural
design, it will be of utmost importance
when deciding where to place the
thermal masses in the design, it will
also effect the exact placement of
windows to determine where shadows
fall, this knowledge will also be of
utmost importance to your interior
design. All of these factors will
become clearer to you as you read on.

Insulation

Insulation is a material that stops the
flow of heat. Insulation blocks
radiation, conduction and convection.
Insulation is measured in a number
called R-Value. Technically R value is
a measure of thermal resistance and is
calculated from a very complex
thermodynamic formula. However as a
designer it is not important to know
that formula, all you need to know is
the higher the R Value the better the
insulator. Insulating materials will
often be rated by R Value per inch. For
example if a material is said to have an
R value of 4 per inch, and if you made
a wall from this material that was 4
inches thick, it would have a total R
Value of 16. A way to design is to
choose the R Value target you wish to
achieve. When you know the target R
Value and then you know the R Value
per inch of a material you know how
many inches thick of material you will
need. Knowing this will tell you so
much about your design. Or it may
work the other way around, in that
perhaps you know the maximum
thickness you can make a wall due to
various physical constraints of the
design site, knowing the maximum
thickness you can have and your target
R Value may help you determine the
best material to use.

In general for a good passive design it
is recommended to have an R value of
20 or higher, many designers shoot for
an R Value of 24. The goal of a design
is to create an ‘insulating envelope’
around the core of the design. That is
you want to make sure that the entire
interior is surrounded by an insulating
layer that has your target R Value.
Designers must be careful to look out
for ‘thermal bridges’ in their envelope.
A thermal bridge is an area of the
design where there is a ‘hole’ in the
insulation. The hole may not be a
literal hole, but a section of the design
that has significantly less than the
target R Value. For example let’s say
your target R Value is 20, so you
design a home with straw-bale walls
with an R value of 24 and you design a
section of the wall made from brick
and that brick section only has an R
value of 10. That brick section would
be a thermal bridge. Thermal bridges
are for the most part a bad thing,
though there may be some instances
when an thermal design may be
desirable. For example if you have an
interior wall separating two rooms and
you want the heat source in one room
to help heat the other room, then in this
case the thermal bridge may be OK.
Another example when a thermal
bridge might be desirable might be if
you had say a greenhouse that collected
solar heat, and you wanted some of the
solar heat to ‘bleed’ into your house
then perhaps a thermal bridge between
that greenhouse and the interior of your
structure might be desirable. To avoid
thermal bridges many designers like to
use a concept called ‘outsulation’ or
‘exsulation’. Neither of which are real
words per se, but what they mean is
that the insulating envelope completely
encloses the interior of the design.

Glass Also of critical importance to a
proper
passive design is to have the proper
proportions
of glazing, the technical phrase for
windows, to
solid walls. To little glazing in the
proper place
and your design will not collect enough
Sunlight,
to much glazing and you will loose
more heat out
of the glazing than you are collecting.
First off,
the glazing needs to face south, the
direction of
the Sun. Now that you know which
direction it
must face, how much glazing should the
design
have? The surface area of south facing
glazing in
a passive design should be equal to
12%-20% of
the surface area of the design. For
example if
your design has 2,000 sq.ft, your
design should
have anywhere from 240 to 400 sq.ft of
south
facing glazing. So how do you
determine the best
percentage?
Think of it this way: If you had an
optimal design, perfectly oriented, a
great R
Value, a perfect thermal envelope and
glazing
only on the south side then you could
get away
with the bare minimum of only12%
glazing.
Now as you make that design ‘less
perfect’, the
R Value gets lower, you put more
windows on
the west, east and north side, etc, then
you would
increase the percentage of south facing
glazing to
compensate. The limiting factor is that
you never
want more than 20% south facing
glazing, lest
you loose too much heat.
The exact science of how you
determine
these exact percentages is beyond the
scope of
this book, but these broad strokes I’ve
outlined
will suffice for your basic
understanding of how
to determine the percentage of south
facing
glazing your designs should have.
Fortunately
passive solar design is somewhat
forgiving as
long as you follow these rules of
thumb.

Sunny day the sidewalk and any brick

hot well until the night and long after
the Sun has
set? That is because the concrete of
sidewalks
and the bricks in walls are a great
‘thermal
mass’. A thermal mass is any material
which
naturally stores heat. Because thermal
mass
depends on density the more dense the
material
the better, this is why stone, concrete,
and bricks
make excellent thermal mass. Catch
and Store
Energy! This is what a thermal mass
does,
Permaculture in action.
As we have discussed above a passive
design is designed so that during the
daytime
during the winter the Sunlight enters
into the
structure. By placing a thermal mass
within the
thermal envelope of the structure the
heat of the

mass and then
stays within the structure due to the
insulating
envelope. When the ambient
temperature drops

it then

had a
thermal mass four inches thick it would
take it

the Sun during the day, once the Sun set
the
thermal mass would ‘bleed’ heat back
out for
about four more hours. By

masonry
walls, floors or other design elements
in this path
so that it can catch and store the energy
from the

One of the ways heat moves is by
convection, the movement of a heated

control of
convection currents, moving heat by
creating
designs that let the air move. Of course
being
that it is natural ventilation means that
we are

Heat rises, that's how it moves.
However for hot
air to rise there must be an ‘intake’.
Let’s say you
had a closed room that was heating up.
If you
just cut a vent in the top the hot air
would not
rise out. However if you then cut a vent
in-take
in the bottom of the room the hot air
would rise out of the top and would
pull fresh air in through the bottom, this
act of pulling the air in is called
drafting. By looking at your design you
can create ventilation points that will
bleed hot air out the top and draft cool
air in, but it is the line of flow that the
designer must be cognizant of. You
want the line of flow to move through
the design in a way that moves the air
in the way you want it to move. The
line of flow can be designed
to take heat from one area and move it
to another. In convection heated air
rises, while at the
same time cool air sinks. If you are
trying to
create a cool area in a design you can
design a
‘bowl’ where the cool air will settle
into. Windows can be designed so that
when
they are opened they create a cross
breeze. For a
cross breeze the windows must be
across from
each other in the design. The air of a
cross breeze
will follow the path of the prevailing
wind. For
example if your prevailing wind is
from the east
then your structure will 'want' to cross
breeze
from east to west. This does not mean
that to
make a cross breeze you have to face a
window directly into the prevailing
wind. You just need to understand how
the wind will want to move through
your design. This will even work if
you have wind breaks in place, as even
with the wind breaks in place the
prevailing wind will create an area of
higher air pressure that will equalize
by flowing through the design to the
other side. In fact it is recommended
that you have wind breaks on the same
side as cross breezing windows to
prevent high gusts of winds from
blasting through your design. Now that
you know how to design a cross breeze
the question is when do you open the
windows and let them cross breeze?
The answer is very, very simple. When
the temperature and conditions outside
are more comfortable than those
inside, you open the windows. When
the temperature outside starts to
get uncomfortable, you close the
windows. Easy. So far in passive
design we have covered

Landscaping

By now you should understand the
concept of micro-climates, that within
the greater climate their can be areas
where the ambient weather conditions
are different. Well it is the local
landscape that creates micro-climates.
Factors such as forests, lakes, rivers,
hills, and other features of the
landscape effect the flows of heat in a
smaller area, creating a micro-climate.
Situating a structure in a specially
designed micro-climate makes it easier
to passively control the environment
inside of the design.

Plants are an important factor in
creating a micro-climate. Trees are
especially good at creating wind
blocks, as well as generating shade.
Evergreens are good for this in that
they will provide wind breaks and
shade all year round. However
deciduous trees are also very useful
because in the summertime they will be
full with leaves, providing shade and
cooling, and in the winter time they
will drop their leaves, letting Sun light
in. While evergreens provide a stable
set of conditions year round, deciduous
trees provide a specific set of changing
conditions that can be harnessed to
great effect. Bushes can do the same,
they just don’t cast as large a shade
zone, but where the trunks of deciduous
trees may not block wind, bushes
around them can fill in that gap.
Grasses and ground covers can shade
the very ground itself, reducing the
ambient temperature. Like deciduous
trees, plants can be chosen to either
provide that shade year round, or you
can choose plants that act like
deciduous trees and change their
conditions over the seasons. Water
features such as ponds also provide
cooling effects, and can even bounce
Sun light into a structure. Sands, rocks,
stones, and concrete or brick
walkways can function as thermal
masses in the landscape, which can be
exposed to the direct Sun year round or
bordered by plants that provide shade
in the summer time. In natural micro-
climates hills can create shade zones,
and alter wind breaks. A Permaculture
Design probably won’t be upheaving
enough earth to create true hills, but
secondary structures like chicken
coops, greenhouses, gazebos, garages,
yurts, what have you, can fill the same
role as hills with some good design
planning.

Roofscaping is also an important
aspect of this landscape design. Roofs
being on the exterior of the design are
also part of the landscape. The
Permaculture Designer understands
that there is a lot of potential uses for
roofs, and each use effects the
designed micro-climate. If the roof is
left bare then the designer must be
aware of the roofs SRI, which stands
for Solar Reflective Index, a measure
of how much Sunlight the roof material
reflects. The lower the SRI, the less
reflective the roof, the more heat it
will absorb and heat up the structure. If
you want to keep the interior of the
structure cooler, the roof will need a
higher SRI. If the roof is a flat roof
with a deck, then perhaps the decking
itself can protect the roof from
absorbing Sunlight. If the roof has
photo-voltaic or solar water heater
panels these features will also provide
shade which will stop the roof from
absorbing heat.

One of the goals of designing
microclimates is to make sure that the
structure is, during the summer time,
completely shaded so that no Sun light
falls on the walls or into the structure;
during the winter time you the structure
to receive as much direct Sun as
possible. Of course your landscaping
for creating the micro-climate will
interact with your designs for food
production, aesthetics, and all the other
factors of your Permaculture Design.
By the end it will all weave together
into one grand, intricate, design.

Pattern Understanding
As Permaculture Principle #4 says,
design from patterns to details. Nature
works on a vast array of simple
patterns which result in highly complex
systems. For example DNA is based on
a very simple patterns, four
nucleotides that combine in simple
ways, which give rise to a double
helix, which gives rise to life in all its
complexity. Simple patterns are the
stuff of nature. As a Permaculture
Designer you must be able to read the
patterns that exist in nature, not only to
understand the systems that exist, but
also to design systems of your own.

In nature there are two types of
patterns, physical patterns and
behavioral patterns. Physical patterns
are how things are shaped, behavioral
patterns are how things behave under
given conditions. For example, a
physical pattern is that trees grow in a
branch pattern, a behavioral pattern is
that when the temperature drops below
a certain level the tree drops its leaves
and when the temperature comes back
up the tree grows leaves back. A
freeway system is based on a physical
pattern that fits the parameters of cars
traveling at 60 mph. Rush hour on the
same freeway is a behavioral pattern.
When does rain fall, how does water
flow down a hill, how do plants grow
in shade, what plants attract birds,
mating seasons, migrations, these are
all patterns. Humans, being products of
nature, are themselves ruled by
patterns. Even the artificial systems of
humanity form patterns,when do you go
to work, how often does your car need
gas, what games do children play, all
patterns. The ecology of Earth is one
vast interaction of material and
behavioral patterns.

A Permaculture Designer must learn to
‘read’ patterns. Permaculture Principle
#6 calls for us to observe and interact,
both parts are necessary to properly
‘read’ patterns. Observation allows
you to see the pattern, and interaction
allows you to understand how the
pattern changes in response to external
stimulus. If you do X to the existing
patterns what new patterns emerge on
the site, what happens to the energy of
the old patterns?

A designer not only learns how to read
patterns, a designer learns to create a
'pattern language' to aid in the design
process. By creating a pattern language
a designer can make simple, small
patterns that when combined can create
a larger more complex design. The
pattern language of a Permaculture
Design is created in response to the
Permaculture analysis'. A pattern
language becomes like building the
legos, taking simple repeating patterns
and connecting them together to create
larger intricate structures. In
Permaculture Design the legos will be
ecological.

By reading the patterns of a site and
creating a pattern language a designer
can then figure out how design
elements can be inserted into the
existing site in a way that is coherent
with the Permaculture Principles. For
example, to catch and store energy one
has to understand the patterns of energy
on the site, and what physical and
behavioral patterns will best catch and
store that energy. By reading patterns
and creating a pattern language a
designer can determine how best to
integrate rather than segregate the
forces on their site.

Reading patterns and determining the
proper pattern language may enable a
design to produce the greatest yield. If
you want to creatively use and respond
to change it would help to understand
what patterns exist and what patterns
they can be changed into with the
energy available on the site. When a
designer does not understand the
patterns on their site waste will almost
always be the result. Observing the
effects that your designs have on
existing patterns on the site may be one
of the best methods of obtaining
feedback, in other words if your design
disrupts the patterns on your site in
UN-expected ways

In a very broad sense there are two
types of plants, perennials and annuals.
Perennials are plants that live for many
years, adapting across the seasons. You
plant a perennial once, and it will
continue to live on through the winter
and come back the following spring.
Annuals only live for one year,
typically dying off in one season with a
new generation being born to replace
them and start the cycle all over again.
There are summer annuals and winter
annuals. Summer annuals prosper
through the summer months and die off
in the winter months, the winter
annuals prosper in the winter months
and die off in the summer months. Each
type has it’s place in a design. For
example winter annuals can be used
because they provide ground cover
during the winter months to prevent
erosion but then die off so they do not
compete with summer food crops.

Don't think of a plant as only having
one function. By choosing the right
plants and putting them in the right
locations in the design you can harness
multiple effects from plants. Plants can
provide more than food, they can also
be medicinal, and even be resource
plants such as bamboo or hemp which
can be used as building materials or as
materials for other crafts. The
usefulness of plants does not end there!
The Permaculture Designer
understands the full ecological
potential of plants. Plants also have an
effect on the micro-environment.
Evapotranspiration lowers micro-
climate temperature, they can function
as windbreaks depending on the size
and shape of the plant, and can even be
positioned to function as shade
screens. You will learn later in this
book that plants can be used in waste
water treatment systems to create a
nigh infinite supply of fresh water.
Plants are an integral part of the
atmospheric cycle, and they always
help to improve air quality. When they
die they can be used to help build
mulch to strengthen gardens and grow
more future plants. They can be used to
attract beneficial insects, birds, and
other animals, and some plants can
even repel pests. Erosion can destroy
the soil quality of a site, and certain
types of plants can be used to stabilize
earth works and combat erosion.
Through their biochemistry and life
cycle, and the physical action of their
roots plants can even be used to change
the composition of soil.

So you see plants can accomplish
much, much more than just being food

be edible, medicinal or craft
resources. Above and beyond these
anthropocentric uses a good
Permaculture Designer understands

potential in the design, fulfilling a
variety of functions. A really good
designer will be able to have plants
that perform ALL of these functions at
once. Imagine that, using a plant that
provides food, cleans water, stabilizes
soil, reduces temperature and attracts

In nature plant’s don’t grow in isolated
rows, they grow in clusters called
plant communities. In a plant
community various plants all grow
together in a symbiotic and mutually
beneficial relationship. In
Permaculture plant communities that
you have designed to be appropriate
for your site are called Guilds.
Creating plant guilds is also referred to
as companion planting or polyculture.
Creating plant guilds demonstrates the
Permaculture principles integrate don't
segregate,value diversity, and stacking
functions all at once. Designing plant
guilds is more of an art than a science.
There are many, many, MANY factors
that go into answering the question of
what plants work together well in
guilds. There may be physical factors,
related to the ‘architecture’ of the
plants. For example a guild may be
composed of large leafy plants that
provide shade for shorter plants that
need shade to grow in. Tall plants with
strong stalks like corn may act as
trellises for creeping vine plants such
as beans. Short leafy plants may help
retain moisture in the soil to benefit a
plant that requires more water. The
factors for guild design may be
biochemical. For example there are
many plants that are ‘nitrogen fixers’,
which means they convert atmospheric
nitrogen into powerful nutrients for use
by other plants. Some plants form
guilds because one plant will attract
animals and insects that are beneficial
for another plant in the guild, or
conversely repel pests that prey on
another plant in the guild. Just to make
matters even more difficult I should
point out that creating a plant guild may
involve more than just plants, there
may be animals as part of the guild,
greywater systems, shade screens,
wind breaks, micro-climates, etc. Like
anything designing a guild is a whole
systems design process limited only by
the imagination and knowledge of the
designer.

Of course plants require soil and

Plants need to be environmentally
appropriate. The less appropriate the
plant the more resources it will require
to keep alive, and the less renewable it
will be. So to choose plants you
shouldn't choose the plant first. You
should first think of the function that
you want, then research plants
indigenous to your area to find the
appropriate plants that perform as your
system needs. Look at all the plants
you have to choose from to determine
if they can work together in guilds. If
not, do a little more research to
determine if there are good potential
guild partners that can help support
your primary plant. Analyze the effects
of the micro-climate created by the
plants and determine how that
microclimate interacts with your
design.

Of course the most prized type of plant
in a Permaculture system is a native
plant, meaning it performs the function
you want and grows naturally in the
area of your design. Pioneer species
are plants that tend to be small, hardy
perennial species with rapid
propagation rates. They are able to
move into relatively barren areas and,
after some number of generations,
bring greater ecological activity that
prepares the area for 'secondary
succession', the establishment of more
complex plant species. Invasive
species aka weeds, tend to have high
propagation rates, they enter into pre-
existing ecologies and destabilize them
by out-competing the existing plants.
Niche species tend to be complex
plants that thrive within narrow micro-
climates. Appropriate species are not
native, but grow well within the local
conditions and do not destabilize the
ecology.

Plants don't have to be just grown in

containers. They can be grown on

a small number of species. As a rule of

and create guilds to support those
plants.

sewer. It really wouldn’t be so hard to
just have that gutter deliver that water
into some sort of barrel, cistern or
other thing to hold that water, would it?
No, it wouldn’t. There are just a few
things you have to get right to collect
the water in a safe and healthy way.

First off there is the type of roofing
itself. Never, ever, ever catch and store
water off a roof with tar tiles. There
are toxic chemicals that will leach into
the water making it unsuitable for
usage. The best roofs to catch water off
of are metal roofs, or possibly even
slate tiles. The best surface to catch
water from is a roof covered in what
you call an elastomeric paint.
Elastomeric paints are rubberized
paints that when dry create a water
proof barrier, and the elastomeric paint
is designed so that toxins do not leach
out of it. In other words, elastomeric
paints form a perfectly safe water
proof barrier.

So how much water can a roof catch?
To find the answer to that is a simple
mathematic formula; this formula
applies to more than roofs, it tells you
how much rain water ANY surface can
collect, and it can be used for roofs,
the ground itself, anything. In verbal
terms the formula is this; for every one
thousand square feet a surface will
catch six hundred gallons of water per
inch of rain. Go back, read that
sentence again and make sure you
understand it.

Here it is as a formula:
Square Feet x Inches of rain x 600 gal.
= Gallons 1,000

So if you have a one thousand square
foot roof and it rains one inch you will
collect six hundred gallons of water. If
you have a two thousand square foot
roof and it rains five inches you will
collect...that’s right six thousand
gallons of water. So, with a little bit of
mathematics and knowing your way
around a calculator you can determine
how many gallons of rain water any
surface can collect. You just need to
know one more thing...how much does
it rain? This is where a little bit of
research comes in, you have to
research and find the average annual
rain fall in your area. There are many
ways you can find this information, of
course in this day and age, the easiest
way is to use the internet, but if you
want to do it old school try a Farmer's
Almanac. Once you know how many
gallons you can expect off a surface
you can now begin planning cisterns,
and rain water collection to your
design. Remember, rain water
collection tanks are Permaculture
structures, so all design considerations
from that standpoint apply. In other
words don't just think of them as tanks
that have to sit there and be tanks. Ask
yourself how can you maximize the
ecological potential of the tank itself.
Can you take advantage of it's
microclimate, use it to grow food
vertically. Can it serve an architectural
purposes such as helping to increase
privacy, or block an undesirable view.
The possibilities are limited only by
your imagination and skill as a
designer.

The other main strategy for rain water
collection is referred to as earth
storage. Earth storage simply means
getting the water to infiltrate into the
ground on your site. Modern design
typically treats rain water as something
that should be drained off the site and
shunted away. A Permaculture Design
attempts to create a design that holds
all of the rain water on the site!
However it is not enough to just hold
the water on the site, the water must
infiltrate, that is soak into the ground.
The section on Soils points out that
soft, loamy soils absorb and hold
water. So to hold water on your site
you would wish to create areas of soft
soil and aim the water toward them.
Gardens, berms and swales are
integral to this strategy.

The purpose of a swale is to catch rain
water and slow it down, therefore a
swale should cut across the path of
rain water flowing down hill. Berms
are similar to swales, except where a
swale is dug into the ground a berm is
built on top of it. Typically a berm and
a swale are made at the same time. You
simply take the earth you dig out of the
swale and pile it up to be a berm.
Often the berm is put on the down hill
side of a swale to help catch rain
water. Also when you put in plants it is
typical to put a berm on the down hill
side of the plant to help catch water
and keep it so the plant can use it.

Berms and swales can be used to guide
water across your site into garden
beds. Garden beds designed to hold
rain water should be dug down into the
ground about six inches or so so they
can hold the water. If berms, swales
and gardens aren’t enough to hold the
water you can also dig infiltration pits.
An infiltration pit is simply, as it
sounds, a pit. These aren’t big, deep
pits like in an Indiana Jones movie. In
your typical design an infiltration pit
needs only to be about a foot or two
deep, a few feet across, with gentle
sloping sides, and filled with mulch.
Of course infiltration pits aren’t just
haphazardly dug around the yard, they
are strategically placed and designed
to catch any water that would
otherwise run off the site.

The key to designing earth storage
systems is understanding how much
water can infiltrate into a given area of
soil, this is called the ‘infiltration
rate’. There is a simple way to
determine the infiltration rate of soil,
you can create a device called an
‘infiltrometer'. To create a homemade
infiltrometer simply get a coffee can.
Take off the lid, and then cut out the
bottom of the coffee can so you have a
cylinder. Now work the bottom of the
cylinder about an inch or so into the
soil. Now fill a one gallon container
with water. The rest is easy, on your
mark, get set, go, and start slowly
pouring the water into the infiltrometer.
use a stop watch to time how long it
takes to empty the entire gallon into the
infiltrometer, and for all of the of the
water inside to soak away into the
ground and disappear. Once you know
how much rain falls on your site, and
you know the infiltration rates of the
soil you can determine how much of
the rain water will be able to infiltrate
onto your site.

By a combination of collecting rain
water in cisterns off roofs, and doing
your earthworks design you can make
sure that your design catches and
collects 100% of the rain water from
the sky. By calculating exactly how
much rain water you can reasonably
expect you can now begin to do
detailed planning for sustainable water
consumption for you systems.
However, remember all of your rain
water calculations are based on
average rain fall. This means
sometimes you will catch more than
calculated, sometimes less. Make sure
that the water consumption habits of
your system are flexible enough that
they can deal with this fluctuating
water amounts.

2) Determine the infiltration rate for
the soils of your practice site.

Right Livelihood
Living in a sustainable way is more
than just an issue of architecture and
infrastructure systems, it is also an
issue of lifestyle choices and personal
habits. One of the most important
lifestyle choices we all make is how
we earn our livelihood. In these
modern times most people find
livelihood to be synonymous with
earning money. However the true
definition of livelihood means
"securing the necessities of life".
While in the modern system money is
the means by which we acquire
necessities, there are many more ways
to acquire these necessities than by the
exchange of money, with Permaculture
Design one can actually create systems
that generate the necessities of life.

A Permaculture economist understands
that there are five economic input
streams: market economy, barter
economy, gift economy, locally
produced natural resources, and skill
shares. The market economy is the
typical economy we all think of, goods
and services traded for legal currency.
Participating in the market economy
typically entails having a paying job or
some other form of gaining legal
tender. The barter economy is simply
trade, you give me that and I will give
you this. Trades are often a 'tit-for-tat'
equal exchange, though sometimes
what constitutes 'equal' is in the eye of
the beholder, as they say one man's
junk is another man's treasure. The gift
economy looks like a barter economy
but is of a wholly different ethical
nature. The gift economy is simply an
act of taking something you have in
abundance and giving it to someone
else. Typically in the gift economy one
never asks for a thing, things are
simply given. If one can produce
natural resources such as food, water,
medicines and building materials
through the use of high (or low) yield
Permaculture systems than one gains
the necessities of life. Skill shares are
simply when people get together and
share their skills with others, in
essence teaching and passing on
skillful knowledge. With skills one is
also able to acquire the necessities of
life.

Permaculture Principle #5 calls for us
to integrate not segregate therefore a
person trying to live a right livelihood
would attempt to integrate all five
forms of economic input streams into
the livelihood. You should not eschew
any of them, HOWEVER you should
also not be solely dependent on any
one of them. Permaculture is all about
harnessing multiple input streams into
your system, these five economies are
all input streams. You should utilize
them each as much as possible without
being dependent on any one of them.

A Permaculture Design should
endeavor to have systems that yield the
resources that one can base their
livelihood on. The idea is not to pull
from the system, but to have the system
yield at a sustainable rate for your
livelihood. The ultimate goal of any
Permaculture Design is first to ensure
the health of the earth, then take care of
one's personal needs, then generate
abundance. You can use the abundance
to create stores for yourself for the
lean times, when you have enough
stores then share your abundance with
others in a gift economy. The more of
your immediate needs your system can
provide the less money you need to
make to survive. For example grow as
much food as you can, harvest water,
etc. Create your own local
infrastructures that support your needs.

There are no easy answers to what
livelihood will be exactly right for
you. Hopefully this section will at least
help you to begin to give serious
thought to this question.

Hunter Lovins, and Paul Hawken.

Soils
Soil is the cradle of the nutrient cycle
which feeds the entire ecosystem.
From soil all things come, and to soil
all things return. Notice I did not say
that soil is the cradle and the grave of
the entire ecosystem, because in the
ecosystem there is no true death. While
an individual organism may die, the
nutrient cycle of the soil lives on, each
life, and each death, making it stronger
and more full of life. It is a continuous
cycle of birth and re-birth.

I can say without agenda, or hyperbole,
the simple facts is that all civilizations
depend on healthy soil. The
civilization of the modern world is no
exception. Food comes from soil, and
without food no civilization will
survive. The plants that produce
oxygen for us to breathe depend on
healthy soil, without healthy soil, no
plants, without plants, no atmosphere.
Soil cleans water, without healthy soil
no clean water to drink. For the most
part without soil there is no shelter,
considering the most common building
materials used throughout human
history have, and even into the modern
era are wood, and earthen construction
materials. Without healthy soil there
can be no healthy sustainable
civilization, period.

Soil is the living skin on the surface of
the Earth. It is literally the interface
between geology and Sunlight, that
converts the minerals of the earth into
living things, literally bringing our
planet to life. Soils is an immensely
complex topic, and there is a whole
discipline of ‘soil sciences’. In fact
soils are so complex a topic there is no
way to delve fully into the topic within
this book, so we will narrow the
discussion of soils to the three factors
most important to the Permaculture do-
it-yourselfer, agriculture, earthen
construction and impact on micro-
climates.

‘Soil’ has many layers to it, each with
their own unique characteristics and
ecological importance. For agricultural
purposes the most important is the top
soil. This is the uppermost layer of the
soil, it can be less than an inch thick
and still support life, however you
generally need at least 2 inches of top
soil if you really want to support an
ecology. Fertile top soil is not ‘dirt’
and should never be referred to as such
lest you invoke the ire of your nearest
organic gardener. Soil is itself an
active living eco-system. Within the
soil are micro-organisms and a host of
other living things all acting together to
keep the nutrient cycle alive in the soil.
One of the hallmarks of healthy soil is
the presence of insects, and mycellia
which look like white threads. Good
soil also has a dark, rich black color to
it, feels soft in the hands, and is moist.
Healthy soil is soft, and almost sponge
like and can absorb and hold water.

The ecological activity within the soil
keeps producing the micro-nutrients
that plants need to uptake into their
roots to live. Also as things die the
micro-ogranisms in the soil decompose
them into their micro-nutrient buildings
blocks which then feed the plants.
However you cannot take this system
for granted, soil can still only support
so much plant life. Basically if the rate
of nutrient uptake exceeds the rate of
nutrient replenishment then your soil
can ‘die’. This is why soil sometimes
needs amendments, that is nutrients
added to it, to maintain it’s health. In
Nature, a bear does not wait until the
local soil is depleted of nutrients
before it poops in the woods, thereby
adding amendments to it. No, the bear
just always poops in the woods, in
other words it is always adding
amendments so to speak. This is how
the living nutrient cycle of soil is
maintained. Plants and animals are
constantly adding ‘amendments’ to
their local soils in many ways.
Animals poop, pee, shed, molt and die,
returning their micronutrients to the
soil when they are done with them.
Plants also return micro-nutrients to the
soil when they are done with them by
shedding leaves, dropping twigs, and
eventually falling over when they die.
The Permaculture Designer
understands this, and themselves
endeavor to return micro-nutrients to
the soil whence it came when they are
done with them. Through techniques
such as composting old food, mulching
prunings and clippings, and even
harvesting their own pee and poop a
good Permaculture Designer becomes
a part of the nutrient cycle of soil by
constantly adding these ‘amendments’.
A good designer has to understand this
and design systems that enable the
human animal in the system to
efficiently, and in a sanitary fashion
return their nutrients whence they
came.

In nature top soil can be anywhere
from one to about eight inches, and it
can take anywhere from three hundred
to one thousand years for natural
process’ to build one inch of stable top
soil. For all of its importance top soil
is very fragile because it is a very thin
layer and because it takes so long to
replenish by natural means. Because
top soil is so thin the greatest natural
danger to it is erosion. The loss of top
soil in a biome can lead to rapid
desertification. Erosion is caused
mainly by two culprits, water and
wind, with water being the greater
threat of the two. Unchecked water
flows can literally wash your top soil
away. To protect your top soil you
need to slow the flow of water across
your top soil. There are many
techniques for controlling water flow
on your soil. Ground covers are plants
such as grasses, shrubs, ground vines,
etc. The root systems of ground covers
serve to physically hold the soil in
place, the leaves protect the ground
from wind as well provide shade
which stops the ground from drying out
and literally blowing away. The tangle
of the ground covers also slows water
down, preventing out of control water
flows. Another method of combating
erosion is to create berms and swales,
which are basically like small ditches.
The technique of using berms and
swales for erosion control is called
‘keylining’. Keylines are berms and
swales that run across the slope of a
hill. Essentially catching and slowing
the water as it flows down. By filling
berms and swales with ground covers,
and soft loamy soil it serves to soak up
the water and control the flow so you
don’t get erosion. Wind breaks also
help to reduce erosion. The wind can
dry soil out, and when the soil
becomes dry it begins to literally blow
away in thin slices. Wind breaks help
to reduce the wind load thereby
reducing erosion. As you also should
have deduced by now, evaporation
also contributes to erosion.
Evaporation is caused by heat and
wind, so to reduce evaporation on soil
also reduces erosion.

Put all of these factors together and you
can begin to develop a complex, yet
harmonious strategy for erosion control
by choosing various plants to provide
ground cover, wind breaks, and
shades, combined with systems of
berms and swales. One of the beauties
and harmonies of this approach is that
the more plants in the soil the healthier
the ecosystem of the soil. By using
plants to protect the soil from erosion
you also make the soil healthier.

For eons humans have been building
structures made from earthen materials.
In ancient times there were many
different techniques developed for
earthen construction, and in the new
Permaculture age many of these
techniques have been re-discovered
and new ones developed as well. Soils
used for construction need to have a
(relatively) high clay content,
depending on the technique anywhere
from 20%-30% of the earthen material
used needs to be clay. The rest needs
to be sand and aggregate.

Here is a brief list of some of the
techniques in earthen construction:
One of the more popular earthen
construction methods is called Cob. To
make cob the clay rich soil is mixed
with sand and straw (mixed in water of
course) and then made into a little hand
made ‘loaf’ called a cob, the loaves
are then stacked until you have your
structure. The higher the amount of
straw in your cob mix the better its
performance as an insulator. A lower
straw content means a higher earthen
content, which means the mix will
perform better as a thermal mass. This
means by controlling the mix the cob
designer can make insulators or
thermal mass as needed. Adobe is a
similar mix to cob, clay rich soil, sand,
and straw mixed in water. The main
difference is that adobe is made into
bricks and dried in the Sun, and the
bricks are then stacked in conventional
brick laying techniques. Adobe bricks
have a higher load bearing capacity
than cob, and make a better thermal
mass. The technique of rammed earth
has also been used since antiquity. In
rammed earth the earthen mixture is put
into a form work and then rammed
down with a weighty mallet or modern
tamping machine; sometimes a
‘stabilizer’ such as a little bit of
cement, or other natural stabilizer such
as lime, dung, or animal blood, are
added to the mix before tamping.
Historically speaking the most recent
addition to earthen construction
techniques has been the development
of the earth bag. Earth bags are very
similar to sand bags, a bag is filled
with the earthen construction materials,
and then the bags are stacked on top of
each other like bricks.
Soils also effect the micro-climate of
your site. Of course good agricultural
soil can, and will support plant life
which will provide shading and
cooling by evapotranspiration. The
converse is of course true, soils that
won’t support life will often be bare,
and compacted, and function as a
thermal mass in the topography. Let’s
say for example you had a yard with
nice lush soil that supported a lawn
full of grass. The soil would be soft
and loamy, shaded and moist, it would
stay rather cool in the summer and
freeze deeply in the winter. Now let’s
say in the middle of this yard you have
a large pile of sand, of course being
that you are now a great designer it
wouldn’t just be a pile of sand; it may
be something like a zen rock garden or
a sand box or some other aesthetically
designed sand feature. The sand would
act as a thermal mass, not only that
water would run off it. So right around
the sand pile, you’d have higher
temperatures and more water. This
would form a unique micro-climate in
the middle of your yard which could be
harnessed for myriad of effects. Can
you think of some of those effects?
Where soft, ‘healthy’ soils absorb
water and slow it’s movement,
dry,compacted soils cause water run
off. Remember, it's not about judging if
one of these is good or bad, it is about
understanding it so you can harness it's
effects for the ecological health of your
site.
To determine what types of soil you
have on your site you can take soil
samples. There is a very easy
technique for soil sampling to learn the
basic information about the soil
composition of your site. To take a soil
sample use a clear glass jar, like a
mason jar or something. Fill it about
half the way up with the soil you wish
to sample. Then fill it the rest of the
way with water, close the lid tight and
then shake it vigorously for a little
while. After a day or so the soil will
settle into layers. The heavier the soil
types will settle to the bottom. Then to
determine the percentages of soil types
in the sample you simply measure them
with a ruler. You can then compare the
thickness of each layer to determine the
proportions and determine the
percentages of each soil layer. For
example if the soil is a total of 4 inches
high in the sample, and one inch of it is
clay then your sample is roughly 25%
clay. If you wish a more detailed
analysis you can carefully then scoop
each layer off and examine each soil
layer independently. Another simple
test to determine if soil is suitable for
construction, just take a handful of the
soil and ball up your fist. When you
open your hand if the sample is still
holding the shape of the inside of your
fist then it has a high clay content.
When you take soil samples remember
to clearly mark where the sample is
from! There is a lot more to
understanding soils then presented
here, but this sampling technique will
give you some basic information about
your soils, and as you gain greater
experience you will be able to glean
greater and greater information from
your soil samples.

Structures
Structures is very broad word, for
purposes of this conversation a
structure is a human built piece of
architecture or infrastructure, anything
from a home, to a barn, to an oven, an
awning, a mailbox, so on and so on.
So, what is a Permaculture structure?
Like appropriate technology talking
about Permaculture structures is not a
laundry list of any sort, it is a method
of thinking.
Photo of Cody Lundin's property near Ashfork.
Photo used with permission of the Ecosa
Institute.

Permaculture structures don’t have to
follow any preconceived notions of
what structures should be like.
Permaculture structures, like any
‘Permaculture’, should fit into it’s
environment, in terms of morphology
(shape), materials, functions and
ecological impact. In accordance with
the principles of ecological design any
Permaculture structure should, as much
as possible, be a product of and
maintain material and industrial loops.
Undoubtedly each and every structure
will have a primary function, that is
being a house for people to live in, an
oven for cooking food, a coop for
housing chickens, a dam for holding
back water, so on and so on. Of course
a designer will identify needs and then
determine the specific structures and
their primary functions needed to fulfill
the purposes. Though for a good
Permaculture Designer the story
doesn’t end there. Remembering that
Permaculture is not about the elements,
it is about the connections between the
elements, and remembering the
Permaculture ethics and design
principles, regardless of the primary
function of the structure a good
Permaculture Designer will maximize
the full ecological potential of any
structure. Remember the William
McDonough building as a tree
concept? Any structure, from an office
building, to a tool shed, to a bus stop
can follow this concept. In addition to
their primary function any and all
Permaculture structures can harness the
power of the Sun, create healthy
micro-climates, create healthy soil,
clean water, improve air quality,
produce food, and provided natural
habitat for diverse species.

Because of all these points this
discussion will deal with how to
maximize the ecological potential of
ANY structure. For specific structures
such as straw-bale, cob ovens, cold
frames, Earthships, chicken coops, etc,
you will need to learn their specific
design and construction techniques and
then apply Permaculture Design.

Let’s start with one of the basics, be
aware of the prevailing wind patterns
that will effect the structure. You need
to be aware of any door and window
openings in the structure and their
relations to prevailing winds and make
sure they are interacting in the way you
want them to. You also need to be
aware of how those wind forces will
effect any roof overhangs, awnings,
and any other parts of the structure that
may effected adversely by the wind.




Students of the Ecosa Institute analysis of the
interaction of sun and architecture for the
Mission Lane design
project. Image used with permission of the Ecosa
Institute

Be aware of the solar angles, the
altitude and azimuth. You must
understand how the Sun will penetrate
and interact with both the exterior and
interior of the structure. I am not saying
if these interactions are good or bad,
the point is that you the designer must
be aware of these interactions. You the
designer must make sure that these
interactions are the ones that you wish
to have happen for specific purposes.

A good Permaculture Designer should
always calculate the amount of rain
water your structure will collect so
you know it’s rain water potential.
Once you know the amount of rain
water your building will catch, you can
then begin to fashion the best strategy
for what to do with that water.

It is highly recommended that you put
berms and swales around the base of
the structure. The main goal here is to
make sure that the surface water run off
is under control and going to where
you feel the water would serve best.
Never just let the water run
haphazardly around the base of your
structure. In addition to just controlling
the water flow, you can plant in the the
berms and swales, taking advantage of
the structure's micro-climate to
increase and diversify the plant
populations of your design.

The north sides of most structures will
be too shady and cold to support
plants, leaving this side unused.
However you can always plant trees
on the north side to maximize the
potential of this often un-used space as
trees will grow up and out of the shade
zone. Make sure to choose a correctly
sized tree.

Also trees and shrubs can be used as
windbreaks to reduce the wind load on
the structure, and as part of the passive
design strategy. Often the micro-
climate of the structure itself will help
the trees and other plants to last longer.

Remember structures themselves
function as shade screens, privacy
barriers and wind breaks, so one
structure can be used to help out
another (as long as their primary
functions don’t clash).

The micro-climate right against the
structure's walls may help to extend
growing seasons or make conditions
right for crops that would not
otherwise grow there. Food systems
such as vines, trellises and other
vertical gardening techniques can be
built on any structure to take advantage
of the micro-climate. Green roofs can
be used to maximize growing space,
replacing the ecological foot print of
the buildings.

Is the structure itself a thermal mass in
your landscape? If so how does it
effect the micro-climate of your site?
How can it be harnessed?

If you understand PC design real well
then you would realize that you know
how to design your own original
structures. Just remember that
designing a structure is enclosing a
space and creating an internal
environment. After studying micro-
climates, passive design, and PC in
general you should realize how to
harness specific effects for the
interiors, and exteriors, of structures.

However you do not have to re-invent
the wheel every time you want to
design a structure. You can turn to
other designers to look for design
precedents. That is, if you have a
function you want accomplished you
can look around to see what other
designers have come up with to
perform those same functions. Things
like cob ovens, Earthships, Eco-
Machines, herb spirals, etc, these are
all design precedents that you can learn
from and adapt to your designs.
A chicken coop designed and built by students of
the Ecosa Institute. Photo used with permission
of the Ecosa Institute.

Your structure, be it a house, mailbox,
cob oven, or even a whole Arcology,
will need to have the proper boots and
hat. Boots and hat is an analogy for the
roof and foundation of a structure. Any,
all and every structure will need
proper boots and hats, not just homes.
Roofs must be durable enough to
withstand the wind and any snow
loads, they must also be water tight.
Withstanding wind is not just a matter
of durability, it is a matter of having a
proper angle, so that the roof is

Water Courses
Many sites may already have existing
water courses on them such as rivers
and streams, and for purposes of this
conversation we will consider ponds
and lakes to be a water course as well.
Dealing with water courses from a
Permaculture stand point can be a bit
tricky. I say that because I do not want
someone to read this book and run out
and immediately start mucking around
with every naturally occurring body of
water they see on a site. Many of them
can be left alone! As long as you
promise to use this advice cautiously I
will share with you some of the things I
have learned about dealing with water
courses in a Permaculture Design.

First off if there is a natural occurring,
flowing water course on your site such
as a small stream or river I would say
your first instinct should be to leave it
alone...unless it has been messed with
by mankind previous to your arrival
OR if it will be messed with by your
design, and yes as much as you love
the Earth just the way it is your design
may mess with a pre-existing water
course. Modern design has long held
the notion that water should be drained
off a site as fast as possible, therefore
many sites have big drainage ditches
that have eroded away into becoming
small streams, and sometimes not so
small. A straight flowing, fast running
water course like this is an ecological
disaster waiting to happen, the Grand
Canyon not withstanding. In cases like
this you will want to slow this water
course down by altering the shape of
the bank.
Instead of straight fast flowing water
course you will want a meandering S
curved water course. Now don't worry,
you actually don't have to do a lot to
accomplish this, you only have to help
it along and Nature will do the rest. To
turn a straight flowing water course
into a meandering one you need to
place something in the stream that
deflects the water in alternating
patterns next to the banks. In some
cases you may be able to simply use a
big rock for this. You just place big
rocks along the bank in alternating
patterns so that the water will deflect
off one bank towards the other. The
natural forces of erosion will take over
from there and eventually carve out the
other side of the bank. If you don't have
big enough rocks you can create a
gabion, which is made by enclosing
rocks in a cage made usually of
chicken wire and placing them in
alternating patterns along the banks.
Another way to slow the flow of a
water course is to place small dams in
it. Not enough to actually dam it up,
unless of course you are trying to
create a pond, but enough to drastically
slow the water. It is not recommended
in this case to necessarily make a dam
out of earth as it will most likely erode
away. The best two techniques are to
either place logs from felled trees in
the water, or to put big rocks in the
water. Remember these are not
attempting to completely dam the water
course, unless of course you are trying
to make a pond, so they should be
shallow enough that the water will
flow over them without flooding out of
the banks.

If you have a water course that was a
man made drainage ditch, or some
other man made ecological disaster of
a water way, but there is another case
where you may want to use these
techniques. When doing the earthworks
on your site to slow surface water run
off all of your earthwork designs
should take into account the overflow,
that is what happens to the water that
doesn't infiltrate, where does it go? It
is a good idea to use your earthworks
to aim the overflow towards a pre-
existing water course if you have one.
If you do, you can easily see that you, a
Permaculture Designer, will be
messing with the natural flow of that
naturally occurring body of water. The
water you are adding to it will
increase the flow rate of the water
course and may turn it into an erosion
making machine. So, in that case you
may want to add some of the above
techniques to compensate for the
addition you will be adding to the
flow. If you don't have a pre-existing
water course on your site for the over
flow, well you are going to have to
make one. You can make a water
course by putting, essentially a large
swale at the bottom of your system that
will catch all of the run off and send it
somewhere. If you do this I would
recommend that you put in the rocks,
gabions, or dams ahead of time to cut
off erosion from the get go. On a side
note, it you have no where to have the
water course run to, then you should
probably be making infiltration pits at
the bottom of the design.

If you have a pre-existing pond on your
site I have one main piece of advice
for it, increase the edge effect.
Increasing the edge effect will
likewise increase the amount of
ecological activity within the pond. To
increase the edge effect you simple go
around the edge of the pond and dig out
chunks. You may also want to plant
reeds within these dug out chunks, or
plantings around the edge that will
grow out over it and provide shade
areas for fish and other aquatic
denizens to rest in. You can also
channel the run off from your site into a
pre-existing pond, or dig out a new
pond which will fill during the rainy
season.
There are of course many other options
and ways of thinking about water
courses on your site. Inventive
Designers may be able to include
water courses as part of sustainable
irrigation systems, or micro-hydro
systems. They can be used to support
populations of fish, ducks or other
animal and food systems. Water
courses can also possibly be used as
part of microclimate design.

Of course whenever working with a
water course on your site it is of
utmost importance that you always,
always, always, think about what it
happening downstream and design
accordingly.

Water Treatment
“Since water is used and not
consumed, we plan to use the same
water in three successive steps:
domestically, aesthetically and
agriculturally.”
- Dr. Paolo Soleri, “Arcosanti: An
Urban Laboratory?”, pg. 16, Arid
Lands and Habitat

The world has a finite amount of water,
and for millions of years billions of
organisms have been drinking, bathing,
peeing in, and pooping in this finite
supply of water. Yet after all these
eons the world is still filled with fresh,
clean, healthy water. This is one of the
beauties of the hydro-cycle. Not only
does the hydrocycle move water
around the planet, it cleans the water
as well. Through the continuous loop
of the hydro-cycle nature provides the
ecology with an effectively infinite
supply of clean drinking water. Then
along came mankind. Since the advent
of the modern industrial age mankind
has polluted almost every body of
fresh water available for drinking in
our habitat. The goal of Permaculture
Design is to meet mankind's needs by
harnessing the ecological forces of
nature. As discussed in the beginning
of this book one of mankind's needs is
for fresh, clean water. So how do we
harness the ecological forces of the
hydrocycle to create a continuous, and
nigh infinite supply of fresh water from
a finite supply of water in the world?

Before we discuss how to design these
systems we must address Water Lesson
#1: There is no such thing as 'waste
water'. From here on in if you ever call
it 'waste water' I will personally show
up at your door and take back your
PDC! As the opening quote in this
section from Dr.Soleri tells us, water
is used but NOT consumed. Water can
be cleaned and recycled nigh
indefinitely therefore there is no such
thing as 'waste' water, there is only
water that is wasted, and as
Permaculture principle #8 tells us,
produce no waste. Therefore if there is
any waste water from your design then
it is not a Permaculture Design. So,
from here on in there is no waste
water, the technical phrase is
effluent....effluent. Effluent, is the
water that comes out of a system after
it has been used. There are two main
types of effluent, grey water and black
water.

Grey water is effluent that was used
for domestic purposes, usually bathing,
laundry, and the dishes. Black water is
effluent that has been used and is now
suffused with dangerous bacteria. The
most common source of black water is
the toilet, where fecal matter infests the
water with dangerous bacteria called
coliforms. In some municipalities the
kitchen sink is also considered black
water because of the presence of
bacteria in the blood of raw meat that
may be washed down the sink.

In this section we will deal mostly
with greywater, as blackwater is
dangerous to work with and more for
'advanced' Permaculture Designers. A
common strategy for dealing with the
black water of toilets is actually to use
composting toilets, which do not use
water and belong more in the section
on composting than water. There have
been designs for EcoMachines which
can cleanse toilet water and recycle it
back into the toilet tank, and there is a
design called a Watson Wick which
can process black water, but again
these are 'advanced' Permaculture
Designs and will not be touched on
here...sorry.

There are many different types of
design for grey water systems, such as
simple reed beds, constructed
wetlands, branch drain systems,
Watson Wicks, and a host of others. No
matter the exact design they all have
two things in common, cleansing
medium, and plants. Cleansing medium
is usually sand, pea gravel, pumice
stone, or sometimes mulch. Essentially
the cleansing medium does the job in a
grey water system that soil does in
nature. Within the cleansing medium
your grow plants. The roots of the
plants feed off the 'contaminants' in the
effluent, again just like in nature. You
can use edible plants in grey water
systems, essentially plumbing your
grey water effluent out into gardens,
however I find that to be a somewhat
ill recommended strategy. The problem
with plumbing grey water directly onto
edible plants is that if something does
go wrong with the system, or
something weird goes down the drain
you could contaminate the edible
plants. A better recommended strategy
is to create a grey water system that
reliably cleanses all of the effluent
BEFORE it is ejected onto the edibles.
For this strategy the most commonly
used plants are reeds, and other water
loving plants. Cleansing medium, and
plants. As you study specific grey
water system designs you will see that
all of them are some design variation
on combinations of cleansing medium
and plants.

By the use of grey water system no
water need be 'flushed' off your site.
All water used domestically, ends up
being used agriculturally as well. The
hydro-cycle of your design is
maintained and the human animal
becomes another component of the on
site ecology. How so? In nature
animals play an important part in the
maintenance of the hydro-cycle. They
do it naturally, by instinct. A squirrel
never stops what it is doing to go
water the acorn tree, it just goes about
it's business of being a squirrel and in
doing so participates in the cycles of
it's local eco-system. So humans can
also do with grey water systems,
though lacking the instinct, for humans
it has to be done by design. In a well
disciplined Permaculture Design the
majority of the plants in the system
would be chosen appropriately to their
ecology and designed so that they only
needed the amount of water naturally
provided by the local hydro-cycle
through rain, dew, fog and other hydro-
cycle events. A grey water system
'plugs' into domestic uses such as
sinks, laundry, and bathtubs. The
Permaculture Designer can then
guesstimate the amount of effluent these
systems will produce. Don't worry
about being exactly exact, best
guesstimates will do. In this day and
age you can do some internet research
to find statistics on average domestic
water usage for different systems such
as bathing, laundry, dishes, etc. Based
on this 'water budget', how much water
will be produced by the average
domestic goings on in the design, you
can then design gardens appropriate to
that water source and 'plug' the grey
water systems into them. Once this is
done the humans now just go about the
business of being human, and their
domestic water activities provide the
water for oasis like 'hot spots' in their
landscaping. Instead of having to stop
what you are doing and going out to
water the garden you just have to do
your dishes, brush your teeth, do your
laundry, take a bath, and in doing so
you are participating in the healthy
hydro-cycle of your design. Grey water
systems also help cut down on over all
water usage by a design. In normal
designs domestic water is flushed
away, and then other water is used for
plants and landscaping. With greywater
systems the same water can be used in
multiple steps just as Dr. Soleri says in
the opening quote. Additionally if you
use the grey water to grow edible
plants and then you eat the plants, you
are actually even reclaiming the water
back into your own body, using it
again! Then you pee or sweat it out and
ends up back on the plants, which you
then eat and reclaim the water again!
The first step in any water system
design is actually the ecological
discipline of the people who use the
system. Perhaps the one inherent, and
forgivable weakness of Permaculture
water treatment systems is that these
systems cannot handle water
contaminated with toxic chemicals.
They can only handle systems
contaminated with natural
contaminants. This means that the user
of the system must only use natural
soaps, detergents, and other products
that end up down the drain. DO NOT
USE ANTI-BACTERIAL SOAPS! The
word of the day here is
'biocompatible'. There are many
wonderful cleansing products for
ablutions, laundry, and other water
usage that are composed of all natural
components. As of the printing of this
book the two biggest manufacturers of
these types of products are Toms, and
Dr.Bronners. You can find them at your
local Trader Joe's, health food store,
or on the internet.

Before you are ready to do any grey
water system design you will have to
do some additional research as the full
range of options is too extensive for
this book. The recommended reading
below will help greatly, but you should
also search the internet for some of the
key words used in this section such as
reed beds, constructed wetlands, and
grey water. However the first basic
step in any grey water system design is
to determine what domestic water
systems need to go into the home. Then
you have two options, you can either
have all of them feed into one
greywater system, or you can create a
specific grey water system for each. I
personally recommend the latter, as it
provides a more easy to digest design
parameter, and ultimately gives you
more versatility as to where the water
can go in your landscape. What ever
you decide you should follow the
advice of Art Ludwig and remember
that any source of effluent should be
looked at as a an opportunity for
landscape design. Remember as Rick
Valley, Land Steward for the Lost
Valley Educational Center likes to
remind us, Permaculture is not about
the individual elements it is about the
connections between the elements.
Don't just slap a grey water system into
your design for the sake of it. A good
Permaculture Designer uses the grey
water system to connect the human
animal in the design into the landscape
of the design by way of a hydro-cycle.
Capisce?

THE PERMACULTURE
DESIGN PROCESS
Now that you understand the various
elements that can go into a
Permaculture Design it is time to put
them all together. There is no one true
'design process', design is a creative
endeavor and can vary from individual
to individual. However it is good to
have a design process outlined for you
to follow for two main reasons. For
one if you have never designed before
you may have no idea how to go about
it and therefor need some ideas on how
to proceed and this Permaculture
Design process can provide that
structure for you. Two, if you are in a
design team of several individuals you
all need to agree on a group process so
that you can integrate all of your design
ideas in a timely and efficient manner.
Instead of you all trying to convince
everyone else that your method is the
best way, you can all just agree to
follow this process.

GET A CLIENT AND A SITE

The best way to look for Permaculture
clients is to be part of a community.
You can join your local Permaculture
guild and go from there.

ASSEMBLE TEAM

If you are taking a PDC class this will
be the easiest part, the team will be
your classmates. However if you are
not in a class then assembling the team

may very well be a team of one. Teams

elements, gardeners, builders,

organizers, etc. If they do not have the
expertise per se, they should at least
have the interest and passion to do the

is not necessary that everyone on the
design team have a PDC, though it
would help.

Once you have the team assembled you
should all go over the design process
and understand all of the steps. You
should also all go over the
Permaculture elements. Once you as a
group understand both the elements and
the design process you should then
organize your team, deciding who will
do what.

ANALYSIS

Before one can make ANY design
decisions you must first analyze the
site. Permaculture Design is all about
making choices Base Map

The Base Map is the map of the entire
site within the legal property limits.
You can most likely get an existing
base map for your site from your local
county or city office. If you cannot,
then a great resource in this day and
age is to create your own base map
using Google Earth.

It is recommended that you keep an
original of the Base Map aside as
reference, and create copies to work
on. You can either draw data directly
onto the copy of the base map or create
data overlays using transparencies or
tracing paper. If you use tracing paper
it is recommended that you put all
analytical data on one sheet, as
multiple layers of tracing paper will
make it difficult to see the base map
underneath. With transparencies you
may want to make multiple
transparencies with different analytical
data on each, but still try to create as
few as possible for sake of clarity.
Each transparency should have related
data on it, for example one
transparency may have your sectors
and zones, another may have your
surface water flow and soil types, etc.
Also in this day and age you can use
any number of computer programs to
create a layered digital base map.
Broad Scale Site

The analysis of a site begins at the
macroscale and then works it's way in
to smaller and smaller detail...does
this remind you of a particular
Permaculture Principle? Before you
analyze the specific site of your project
you need to understand it's place in the
broader context. How 'broad' a context
should you start in? Well, I would
recommend that you start globally,
literally. Look at the biggest broadest
issues first, what hemisphere are you
in, what continent? Then 'zoom in',
what country? Then which region of
the country? Then which state? Don't
forget to consciously analyze what bio-
region and what water shed the site is
in. Then which county? Then finally
zoom in on the site itself. A highly
disciplined Permaculture Designer
would do a full Permaculture analysis
at each step. Imagine doing an
inventory and zone and sector analysis
of the United States of America?! This
may seem redundant but far from it.
For some of these larger scales by
doing a Permaculture analysis of them
once you will then have data which can
apply to dozens, it not hundreds of
future projects. For example if you do
a full Permaculture analysis of your
county, then you will have that data to
be used for any future project in that
county.

When you are 'zoomed in' on your site
you want to look at all the local
factors. Nearby roads, nearby towns
and cities. Are there trash dumps
nearby or other sources of toxins? Are
there large tracts of forests nearby?
What is the situation with neighbors?
You want to garner as much
information as you can to understand
where the site sits in the larger picture
of things.

You can acquire a great many large
scale maps of the water shed, county
and state at your local County or City
Office.

Client Interview

A Permaculture Design will always be
done for a client. Even if you are doing
a design for yourself, think of yourself
as your own client and treat yourself
accordingly. To design for a client you
need to conduct a client interview. The
goal of the interview is for you the
designer to get into your client's head
and learn to think like them when you
sit down to design. Remember, design
is a manifestation of philosophy, when
you sit down to design you are
attempting to manifest your client's
philosophy not yours! If you make a
design that manifests your philosophy
then most likely it will not fit your
clients needs as it should manifest their
philosophy. Remember, Permaculture
Design is a service for others, not an
indulgence for one's self.

One of the main goals of being a
Permaculture Designer is not to design
FOR a client, it is for you and the
client to co-create the design. To
enable this co-creative process you
need to establish a rapport with your
client, to enable you to get into each
other's head and be able to
communicate synergistically. This is
what the client interview enables.
• The client interview is typically
conducted as a questionnaire, or
question and answer period.
Conducting a client interview is more
of an art than a science. You the
designer will craft a set of guiding
questions to illicit the type of
perspectives and information you need
from your client to enable you to
manifest their philosophy.
• The exact questions can vary, but the
below is a suggested list of the type of
information you should try to illicit
from your client:
• How well does your client
understand Permaculture? The goal
here is to be able to ensure that you
speak to your client at a level they will
understand and that you are able to 'fill
in' for a client the knowledge they are
missing.
• Are they willing to have a design that
naturally progress' through the stages
of ecological succession or do they
want the design to 'jump to the end'.
• Aside from general health, does the
client have any particular health
needs? Aside from what they need
does the client have any health
practices they wish to uphold?
• Aside from general nutrition
requirements, does the client have any
particular dietary needs?
• Aside from dietary needs, just what
type of foods does the client like or
want?
• Does the client posses any physical
or mental limitations or issues?
• Get the client's budget and time
frame.
• Get the client's vision. Ask them to
describe what they see life in their
design being like.
• Have your client make a mission
statement for this design. What is the
client's goals in creating this
Permaculture Design?
• How 'hands on' does the client want
to be in the design once it is installed
and 'running'?
• How many people will the design
serve? Do they have or want pets?
• What are the client's social habits?
Will they have social events that cause
intermittent, or regularly occurring,
"spikes" in impact and resource
consumption?
• Are there any other considerations
your client has that you didn't think to
ask.

General Climate Data

You will want to know as much about
the local climate as possible. Any
Permaculture analysis should include
the solar altitude and azimuth,
prevailing seasonal winds, flood
plains, average winter and summer
temperatures, and the average annual
rainfall.
You will also want to identify any
microclimates you can on the Base
Map.
History of the Site

Permaculture Principle #1 calls for us
to design from patterns to details, this
includes understanding the historical
patterns of the site over time. You
should research as much history of the
site as you can, especially as it
pertains to major climactic events
AND human habitation. You should be
looking for historical plants and crops
that were used on the site, design
precedence from the past, and other
historical factors that may influence
your design decisions.

Putting the history together you can tell
the 'story of the site' to gain an
understanding of it's patterns over time.

Indicator Species
Observe the site for plant and animal
indicator species.

Inventories

Inventories are lists of available
resources that already exist on the site.
You will want to create lists of plants,
animals, potential building materials,
potential salvageable materials, water
sources, available people power, and
'community' resources. Plus lists of any
other resources you think are relevant
to the project at hand.

Observation and Interaction

Permaculture Principle #1 calls for
observation and interaction to learn
about a site. Many hardcore
Permaculture Designers will tell you
that you should not do any design
implementation on a site until you have
observed it for a full year so that you
may see how the site acts in each
season. The REAL hardcore
Permaculture Designers will say you
should observe for three years because
the first year may have just been a
fluke. However, most people cannot
wait one, never mind three, years
before getting to work. That's OK, we
understand. Still before making any
design decisions the designer should
observe the site as often as possible
and under as many different conditions
as possible. If possible you should
camp out on the site several times. You
will want to observe it during the day,
during the afternoon, the evening, in the
morning, during the rain, after the rain,
when it's dry, and any and all other
conditions you can. Permaculture
Principle #1 calls not only for
observation but interaction, do not be
afraid to 'interact' with the site. Do
small projects to see what effect they
have, go for long hikes, fish, make
noise, be quiet, whatever small actions
you can do to see how the site
responds to stimulus.




Sectors

Permaculture Sectors are another
analytical tool to help you understand
the flow of forces across your site. A
Sector analysis is presented as a circle
over your site, with the circle centered
on your Zone 0, or the center of your
lowest zone. 'Pie slices' out of that
circle represents forces that flow
across your site. For example if
easterly winds blow in cold air you
would draw a wedge that points to the
east and mark it as the 'cold easterly
winds' sector. There is no exhaustive
laundry list of what type of sectors
your analysis should include, that is up
to the designer to determine what
forces there are to be identified that
effect the design. Here is a list of some
of the typical sectors employed by
Permaculture Designers:

• Fire hazard - it is worth noting on
every design which direction a wild,
or other fire, would most likely come
from.
• Summer wind sector - Typically in
most ecologies the winds blow from a
predominant direction during the
summer time. Some simple climactic
research should tell you this. The
wedge should probably be about 80
degrees wide on the circle.
• Winter wind sector - Typically in
most
• ecologies the winds blow from a
different predominant direction during
the winter time than the summer time.
Some simple climactic research should
tell you this. The wedge should
probably be about 80 degrees wide on
the circle.
• Noise Sectors - if there are large
noise generators near your site such as
roads, neighbors or drum schools, then
you should create a noise sector for
each. The angular width of the wedge
is at your discretion.
• Summer Sun sector - this wedges
denotes the solar azimuth during the
summer solstice. Given that the Sector
analysis is graphically presented as a
circle, you should make the actual
angles of the wedge match the actual
angles of the solar azimuth
• Winter Sun sector - this wedge
denotes the solar azimuth during the
winter solstice. This wedge should
also reflect the actual angles of the
solar azimuth.
• Undesirable views - if there are
things you just don't want to see from
your site then you should make a
wedge that covers how much angular
space the undesirable view covers.
• Desirable views - if there is some
particular view that you want in your
design than by creating a sector for it
that will help ensure that you do not
accidentally block that view with your
new design.

Again, there is no exhaustive list of the
types of sectors you should have, it is
up to your discretion. Before beginning
your Sector Analysis the designer
should sit down and make a list of
factors to be analyzed. Once you have
the sector analysis complete you can
now look at it and let it tell you where
various elements should go to either
block some force across your site,
allow that force access, or catch and
harness that force.

Institute. Photo used with permission of the

While this analytical tool looks easy to
make Sector Diagrams can sometimes
be frustrating because your information
visually overlaps. Trust me, once you
start to draw one you will see what I
mean. A recommended way to make
your Sector Diagram is to start with a
large circle centered on Zone 0, in the
middle of your plan, or centered on
whatever is of importance to this
particular Sector Analysis. Then make
a number of concentric rings equal to
the total number of Sectors you plan on
drawing in. For example if you were
going to have a summer Sun sector,
winter Sun sector, fire hazard sector,
and noise sector, that would be four
sectors, so four concentric rings. Then,
starting from the mid point you draw
the angle lines out to the appropriate
concentric ring and label it. It is
typical, but by no means necessary, to
put the summer Sun sector on the outer
most, the winter Sun second, the
prevailing winds third, and then the
rest in what ever order suits your
fancy. Then you have two options, you
can either just color in the outermost
arc of each sector, or you can color in
the whole wedge. If you choose to
color in the whole wedge I still suggest
that you first only color in the arcs then
go back to fill in the wedges to make
sure you don't overlap the colors
wrong. Try it a couple of times, you
will see what I mean.

Soil Samples

You should take soil samples and test
infiltration rates from around the site.
If you wanted to be really systematic
you would create a grid across the site
and test at regular intervals/ However
that disciplined of an approach may not
be feasible or necessary. To 'wing it'
you should take soil samples where
ever you seem to observe differences
that may be caused by differing
infiltration rates. For example if you
see two different types of grasses or
ground covers naturally occurring on
the site that may indicate to you a
difference in infiltration rate. If you see
two patches of the ground with
different colorations that may indicate
different types of soil. If the site slopes
you may want to take a sample at the
top of the slope, the middle and the
bottom. Be sure to clearly number your
samples and keep track of where they
are from. You can color in areas on the
base map to denote certain types of
soils, or place the numbers from your
soil samples on your working copy of
the base map. You should also put the
infiltration rate data on the working
copy of your base map.

Surface Water Flow

Following the topographical
information of your working base map
you will want to chart the flow of
water across your site. This will be
instrumental in helping you determine
what earthworks will be needed to
slow, spread and drop the flow of
water on your site. The ultimate goal of
this is to do the feasible best to ensure
that all rain water which falls on the
site stays on the site and infiltrates into
the ground, or at least that any water
which drains from your site, after
being cleansed by bio-swales or some
such, is deposited into a naturally
occurring water way.

Zones First off a Permaculture zone is
not to be
confused with ‘zoning’ used in modern
urban
planning. In modern urban planning
zones will
be given phrases like residential
zoning,
commercial zoning, etc. These have
nothing to
do with Permaculture.
In Permaculture Design Zones are an
analytical graphic tool used to help one
understand the impacts on a site, and to
help one
design. Being a ‘graphic tool’ means
zones are
something you draw on paper, they
don’t really
exist out on the ground per se. By
drawing the
Permaculture zones onto a base map or
design it
helps the designer to understand the
site.
Permaculture Zones are ranked from 0-
5, with
each rank denoting the amount of
human impact
on an area. Zones are usually used in
planning
gardens and crops, but they have many
more
implications than just gardening.
Understanding
zones is important to designing the
‘relative’
location of elements of the
Permaculture
Design.
When being described for students and
beginners zones are often represented
as concentric circles, however please
be aware that the concentric circle
zones are just for demonstration and
explanation purposes only! In reality
zones will almost never, ever be
concentric circles. They can be any
shape in a design but will usually be
big oblong, or globular areas. Again,
Zones are a tool, they are a rough
estimate of impacted areas not the
literal truth of it. While Zones will be
represented by lines on a piece of
paper, in reality they will not have
sharp
lines of delineation like they will on
paper. Also be very careful when
making Zones
in an urban environment. Many
neophyte
Permaculture Designers believe that
EVERY
Zone analysis has to have all five
zones, and that
is just not true. In dense urban
environments your
site for a home with a typical yard may
only have
a Zone 0 and a Zone 1. Perhaps it may
have a
Zone 2 and 3, but rarely will an urban
site
actually have a Zone 4 and it will
NEVER have a
Zone 5. Larger urban neighbors may
have up to
Zone 4, but the odds of their being a
Zone 5, true
wilderness in an urban area are pretty
much zero.

Zone 0 This is the zone for permanent
built
structures, usually the house or other
buildings.
This zone is considered to be
permanently
impacted, basically because the
building will
never move, and so is often considered
ecologically inert. However with
systems such as
green roofs and living walls some of
the
ecological activity of this zone can be
remediated
by a skillful designer.

Zone 1

This zone is the area impacted by
humans on a daily basis, directly
around the Zone 0 and around any other
place where humans come and go.
Zone 1 is where a designer should
place systems that either require or
will attract daily human impact.
Designers will usually put gardens in
Zone 1, anticipating that humans will
be coming and going to the garden to
pick things, and to tend to the garden.
Other features in Zone 1 may include
something like a cob oven, yard space
for playing or parking for automobiles.
Zone 2

This Zone is for systems that require or
will attract human impact only a few
times a week at most. The systems of
Zone 2 should be fairly self supporting,
requiring only occasional human
intervention or occasional harvesting,
or receive sporadic human attention
over the course of a week. Some
examples of zone 2 systems might be
beehives, compost piles, food
producing bushes, or pump houses for
water systems. There is no exact
science to exactly what goes in Zone 2,
the main factor is if humans need to or
will visit it about once or twice a
week then it is probably a Zone 2 type
of thing.

Zone 3 Whereas Zone 1 is impacted
daily, and
Zone 2 is impacted weekly, Zone 3 is
impacted
roughly monthly or seasonally. Zone 3
might be
a good place for orchards, crops that
will be
seasonally harvested, ceremonial
sweat lodges,
or something like that.

Zone 4 This is the area where human
impact is
present but minimal. Zone 4 may have
some light
impact such as bird watching, camping,
hiking,
or wildcrafting, and some sustainable
forestry
practices such as thinning and
coppicing, or the
like. Zone 4 really has no human built
structures
or systems. This is where humans
interact with
the natural world as is. Zone 4 may
also be areas
that were heavily impacted by humans
in the past
but now lay 'fallow', such as brown
fields (the
fancy term for vacant lots of dirt) or
old logging
areas. Zone 4 may also be wilderness
corridors
that cut through urbanized areas, or
wooded
areas within suburbs.

Zone 5 Zone 5 is the wilderness. I
mean true
wilderness, no structures, no roads, no
human
impacted forests. For the most part you
cannot
have wilderness within most urbanized
areas. For
example the edges of a city park do not
count as
Zone 5, those would be, at best, Zone
4. Zone 5
is out there, with nothing but the birds
and the bees, and perhaps the
occasional human hiker. Be careful
when labeling something Zone 5, it
needs to be true wilderness. A park
within a city will not be a true
wilderness, even a large untamed patch
of woods surrounded on all sides by
urban development and human impact
will not be true wilderness. These
things would most likely count
as a Zone 4.
Now that you understand the Zones you
can see they can be used as both an
analytical
tool and a design tool. You can analyze
an
existing area and determine the human
impact
and determine what type of Zones there
are
across the existing site. The designer
then has
two options, you can either place
appropriate
systems within the existing zones, or
decide that
the site may need some serious zoning
overhaul
and redesign the Permaculture zones of
the site. You should create an overlay
layer on
your base map that shows the Zones.

check with the city or county for your

CHARETTE

The charette is where the proverbial
rubber begins to hit the road design
wise. The charette is the brain
storming session of design. Your team
will undoubtedly have multiple
charettes before coming up with a final
design. As a team you should get
together and work out a charette
schedule, determining how many times
you will get together as a team. The
goal of each charette is to move closer
and closer to a final design, not just
spin your wheels on brainstorming.
Teams should bring better and better
presentation materials to each charette,
refining the specifics of their design
elements each time.

The client should be invited to each
charette, however if the client cannot
make it the team should meet with out
them.

At the first charette everyone shares
the results from their analysis and
produce an agreed upon copy of the
base map data. Each team member
should then be given a copy of the
working base map, and a copy of the
client interview. As a team you should
then spend some time brainstorming as
to what the design should be like.

After this first charette all members of
the team go back to the proverbial
corners where they work
independently, or inter-dependently if
you understand that phrase, on their
specific elements. I recommend that at
this phase the team members
concentrate on doing the research for
their individual elements, finding the
right type of plants, researching design
precedence, looking for structures in
analogous climates, etc. Once every
one has their research the team should
get back together and share their
findings so that everyone is on the
same page as to what elements the
design will have.
Now each team member should again
retire to their respective corners and
begin to work out the specific designs
of their small slice of the pie. How
large will structures need to be, how
should gardens be laid out, etc. Then
everyone gets back together for another
charette where these individual
designs are all shared.

At this charette it is time to try and
connect all of the design elements
together. How do the gardens interact
with the structures, where exactly
should the greywater systems go, etc.
Essentially you are playing Tetris, so
to speak, with your design elements.
Each team member MUST check their
ego at the door and be prepared to
make whatever changes are necessary
to make the design fit together. If need
be there should be a final design
arbiter to settle any design disputes,
and all team members MUST respect
the arbiters final decisions. In the best
case scenario the arbiter may be the
client if they can make it to the
charette. When the team has reached
the decision of how to fit everything
together each member should update
their working copy of the base map
with all of the design decisions.

Each team member now retires to their
respective corners to work on
finalizing their aspect of the design so
that it fits in with the rest. Due to the
limitations of both space and intent this
book does not cover the particulars of
design for each individual
Permaculture element, how to design a
garden is totally different than how to
design a grey water system, which is
totally different than how to design a
straw-bale house. The recommended
readings for each specific
Permaculture element cover the design
particulars and it is beyond
recommended that each team member
have a copy of the book appropriate to
their element. It is also beyond
recommended that each team member
have their own copy of the
Permaculture Designer's Manual by
Bill Mollison which covers many
aspects of the design particulars. In
this day and age the internet is also an
excellent resource for design research
and should be used.

There should be on final charette to
ensure that all designs fit together and
meet all criteria of the client interview.
If all has gone well you are now ready
to begin making the final presentation
materials.

PRESENTATION

Now that you have finished your
design it is time for the presentation.
This is where the rubber really hits the
road for if you cannot communicate
your design, then it was for naught.
Without communication there can be no
implementation. The goal of the
presentation is not to try and wow
people with how clever you are, it is
to present your complex ideas in as
understandable a way as possible.
Here are the recommended steps for a
good Permaculture Design
presentation:

First off invite the client, and the
community in general.
Choose an MC for your group. The MC
will introduce the presentation and the
group. Identify each person in the
group, and which part of the analysis
they worked on and each part of the
design they did.
Show the original base map of the site.
Not the actual original one you all
worked off, but a nice copy of the
original. Share the general climactic
data and identify for the audience any
noteworthy micro-climates. Then tell
the story of the site.
Share the results of the client
interview. Here you don't word for
word share the client interview, but
sum up your clients major goals, needs
and wants. Each member of the group,
when they speak should be able to
identify some element of the client
interview that was the most influential
on them.
Share the results of the analysis'. You
don't necessarily have to share every
scrap of information from the analysis
but you need to have all of the
information handy. Display your zone
and sector analysis, show the surface
water flows. Have a written list of
your inventories, etc. Just because
these lists are written doesn't mean
they can't be pretty, don't be afraid to
be artistic, however you do not want
your artistry to overshadow the
information. A common presentation
strategy is to have all of the
information of the analysis written and
displayed in the presentation along
with the base map. The MC explains a
general picture of the analysis. Then
each group member describes any part
of the analysis that was of particular
use to them. This way you don't bore
the audience with too much detail, but
the detail is there if anyone in the
audience wishes to ask follow up
questions.
Now that you have introduced the site,
as is, along with your client interview
and analysis, it is time to introduce
your design.
Ecosa students design presentation....don't be
intimidated by the elaborate set-up, Ecosa
students are some of the best. Photo used with
permission of the Ecosa Institute.

Show the new base map with your
design changes. Along with this show
any new zone and sector
considerations, changes in surface
water flow, etc. Again, you don't have
to explain every single detail, but get
across what is significant in your
proposed changes. After the MC
introduces the new Base Map each
member of the group should then go
over the details of the part they
designed.

Individual members may present their
own plans, drawings, etc which 'zoom
in' on their part of the design. However
before you introduce your 'zoom in'
make sure that you identify it's place on
the base map. Each member should
explain how their detail addresses
some need of the clients or of the site.
Remember to go over any plans of
succession in your design, to represent
this you may want to use overlays that
show the different steps.

After the individual members have
presented their parts the MC then
opens the floor to feedback and
questions from the client. The client
should not only ask questions, but give
the design team a critique of both their
presentation skills and a critique of the
design itself. The client then should ask
any follow up questions, each question
should be answered by the appropriate
team member. After the client has
asked their questions the MC opens the
floor to the general audience to share
their critiques and ask their questions.

With that you thank the audience for
coming, and wrap up the presentation.

A presentation should be no more than
about 45 minutes long. The team
should definitely get together and
practice a dry run at least twice before
the main event to work on their
presentation 'choreography', that is
who is doing what when. Ultimately
the main point of any presentation is
communication! Don't let the steps and
descriptions of this presentation
process inhibit your ability to
communicate in ways that are effective
for you and for your client.
FINAL WORDS

So there you go, a Permaculture course
in a nutshell as I would teach it. Other
instructors may have different material,
exercises, and perspectives on a great
many things. However I am confident
that we would all agree on the major
points and the major elements.

Writing this book was very difficult
because there is SO much I didn't say.
There just isn't room in any one book
to truly go over everything in
Permaculture. Like I said, and showed
with the recommended readings, each
section could be and is it's own book.
But without understanding the basic
principles of Permaculture, without
understanding how to connect it all
together you would not be able to
realize the true ecological potential of
those elements.
They say the journey of a thousand
mile begins with one step. Reading this
book was only one step. There is still,
literally, a whole world of information
out there for you. A whole world of
learning ahead of you. No one knows
everything about Permaculture Design.
Each person gravitates towards those
elements that speak to them the most.
Many 'Permaculture Designers'
specialize in specific areas, but still
they must know a little bit about each
area because what makes one a
Permaculture Designer is knowing how
to bring elements together in a way that
maximizes their ecological potential.

Devoting your self to Permaculture is
not easy, and rarely is it lucrative.
However it is immensely rewarding
and there are definitely people who
make their living at it as teachers
and/or designers. Very few people are
'professional Permaculture Designers'.
Most people who know Permaculture
use it to 'inform' some other profession
they are engaged in, such as
landscaping, architecture, or working
at a nursery. Some people use
Permaculture to inform professions that
have nothing to do with design work,
but they are still able to run their
professional life in a more sustainable
way. For some people they want to
know Permaculture solely for their
own personal lives and it has nothing
to do with their job. How you use
Permaculture in your life is up to you.

Of course you may now want to go out
and take an actual PDC. However
being a PDC teacher and knowing that
you have read this book I need to give
you a word of caution. I can tell you
from experience as both a teacher and
a student the worst students are those
who think they already know the topic.
It doesn't matter if they actually do or
don't. If a student walks into a class
thinking 'I already know all of this
stuff', nine times out of ten they will be
the student who learns the least,
disrupts the class the most, and hurts
the educational experience for all of
the other students and themselves. And
yes you reading this, thinking 'that
would never be me', YES THAT
COULD BE YOU! So my word of
advice is that if after reading this book
you take a PDC, empty your mind and
go in fresh. Listen to your teacher not
yourself and not to me. Do not think to
yourself that because you have read
this book you already know it all. If
you do I guarantee you will miss what
it is your teacher and fellow students
have to offer.

professional passion. So I owe you a
thanks for affording me this opportunity
to share and teach. I find that every
time I teach I also learn, and in writing
this book I have actually learned even
