## Overview

**Prunus spinosa** is a deciduous shrub or small tree in the family Rosaceae, widely known as blackthorn or sloe.

It is native to Europe, western Asia, and northwest Africa, where it forms extensive thickets in hedgerows, forest edges, and scrubland.

The species is famous for its tart, astringent sloe berries used to make sloe gin, and its dense, thorny branches that make it an impenetrable hedging plant.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Kingdom: Plantae → Tracheophyta → Magnoliopsida → Rosales → **Rosaceae → Amygdaloideae → Amygdaleae → Prunus → P. spinosa**.

The species was described by Linnaeus in 1753 and is the type species of subgenus *Prunus* section *Prunospina*.

Its relationship to *P. domestica* (cultivated plum) is debated; blackthorn is either a direct ancestor or a close relative of domesticated plums.

**Growth habit:** Dense, much-branched deciduous shrub or small tree, 2–5 m tall, forming extensive thickets through root suckering.

**Bark:** Dark brown to black, developing fissures with age; young branches are dark and typically spine-tipped at nodes.

**Thorns:** Sharp, modified stipular spines 1–3 cm long, formed at branch nodes, making the plant an effective barrier hedge.

**Leaves:** Alternate, ovate to elliptic, 2–4 cm long, finely serrate, pubescent beneath when young, becoming glabrescent; dull green above.

**Flowers:** Solitary or paired, white, 1.5–2 cm diameter, 5 petals, appearing March–April on bare branches before or with the leaves.

The [[equatorial-photoperiod-early-flowering]] makes blackthorn an important early nectar source; however, late frosts frequently damage the blossoms.

**Fruit:** Drupes (stone fruits), 10–15 mm diameter, round to slightly oblong, blue-black with a waxy bloom, ripening September–October.

**Flesh:** Green, very astringent and tart when raw; the stone is large relative to fruit size, comprising roughly 30% of fruit weight.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native across Europe from the British Isles and Scandinavia (south of 63°N) to the Mediterranean, eastward through Turkey to the Caucasus and northern Iran.

It also occurs in northwest Africa (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia) and has been widely naturalized in New Zealand and parts of [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America.

The species occupies a broad ecological range from sea level to 1,600 m, tolerating both acidic and calcareous soils.

It is a characteristic component of European hedgerows, scrub communities, forest edges, and [[comparison-primary-vs-secondary-succession]] on abandoned [[fukuoka-textdoc-agricultural-land-law-obstruction-return-farming]].

Blackthorn is highly tolerant of exposure, wind, and moderate shade, though it fruits most abundantly in full sun.

## Edible and Culinary Use

Sloe berries are far too astringent to eat raw and are almost exclusively consumed after processing, most famously as sloe gin (sloe berries steeped in gin with sugar).

The [[kava-traditional-preparation-ceremonial-context]] involves picking sloes after the first frost (which softens the astringency) or pricking each berry before steeping.

Sloe gin, sloe jelly, sloe jam, and fruit preserves are traditional across Britain, Ireland, France, Germany, and central Europe.

Sloes are also used in country wines, cordials, and liqueurs, and the fruit can be dried and ground as a flavoring for baked goods.
