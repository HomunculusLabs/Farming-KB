# Soil Weathering

## Description

Soil weathering is the sum of all natural forces that break down rocks into the mineral particles that form the foundation of soil. Weathering operates through three primary mechanisms — physical, chemical, and biological — which work simultaneously and reinforce each other. Over geological time, weathering transforms solid bedrock into the loose mineral component that, when combined with organic matter, air, and water, becomes soil capable of supporting plant life.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Soil science / Geology
- **Category:** Soil formation processes
- **Subcategory:** Rock decomposition

## Key Facts

- **Physical weathering:** Wind, rain, snow, temperature fluctuations, glacial grinding, river tumbling, and ocean wave action physically break rocks into smaller particles. Water freezing in cracks expands by 9% and exerts approximately 2,000 PSI, splitting rocks apart. Thermal expansion of rock surfaces during hot weather causes outer layers to peel away from cooler inner rock.
- **Chemical weathering:** Water, oxygen, and carbon dioxide dissolve molecular bonds in rock. Materials go into solution, destabilizing the rock structure and making it more susceptible to physical breakdown. Fungi produce acids and bacteria produce alkaline substances that chemically dissolve rock. Microbes also produce ammonia and nitric acids that act as solvents.
- **Biological weathering:** Mosses and lichens (specifically the fungi within them) attach to rocks and produce acids and chelating agents that dissolve mineral fragments for nutrients. Plant roots penetrate rock crevices and physically force rocks apart. Freeze-thaw cycles exploit root-created fractures.
- Although nearly 90 different chemical elements exist in soil, only eight constitute the majority of mineral content: oxygen, silicon, aluminum, iron, magnesium, calcium, sodium, and potassium.
- All elements carry electrical charges at the molecular level and combine in different ratios to form the variety of minerals found in soil.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Weathering is the origin story of all mineral soil. Understanding it helps cultivators appreciate why certain minerals are naturally present or absent in their soil. The chelating agents produced by fungi during biological weathering are the same mechanisms mycorrhizal fungi use to solubilize phosphorus and micronutrients for plant uptake. In mycology, saprophytic and lithophilic fungi demonstrate the power of fungal enzymes to break down not just organic matter but mineral substrates — a principle applied in bioremediation and mineral extraction research.
