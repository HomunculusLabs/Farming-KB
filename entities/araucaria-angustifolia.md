# Araucaria angustifolia

## Overview

Araucaria angustifolia, commonly known as the Brazilian pine or Paraná pine, is a large evergreen conifer in the family Araucariaceae. It is the most economically and ecologically important tree species in southern Brazil.

The species once dominated the Mixed Ombrophilous Forest (Araucaria forest) across the southern Brazilian plateau. Intensive logging during the [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]] reduced its original range by approximately 97%.

Brazilian pine produces large, edible seeds called pinhão that have been harvested by indigenous peoples for millennia. These seeds remain a culturally significant food source across southern Brazil, Paraguay, and Argentina.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Araucaria angustifolia (Bertol.) Kuntze was first described by Antonio Bertoloni in 1819. The species epithet refers to the narrow, scale-like leaves characteristic of the genus.

Mature trees reach 30–40 meters in height with a straight, cylindrical trunk reaching 1–2 meters in diameter. The crown is distinctive, forming a characteristic umbrella or mushroom shape with horizontal whorled branches.

Bark is thick, deeply fissured, and dark grey to brown. Inner bark is reddish and resinous, producing aromatic exudates when damaged.

Leaves are leathery, linear-lanceolate, 3–6 cm long and 5–8 mm wide, arranged in dense, overlapping spirals. They persist on branches for 8–15 years before abscission.

The species is dioecious, with separate male and female individuals. Male cones are cylindrical, 10–15 cm long, while female cones are massive and globular, reaching 20–25 cm in diameter and weighing up to 5 kg.

Pollination occurs by wind during the spring months from September to November. Female cones require approximately 18–24 months to mature, with seed release occurring during the following autumn.

Each female cone contains 100–200 large, wingless seeds measuring 3–5 cm in length. The seeds are rich in starch, lipids, and protein, making them one of the most nutritious conifer seeds known.

## Distribution and Habitat

Araucaria angustifolia occurs naturally in southern Brazil, eastern Paraguay, and northeastern Argentina. Its historic range covered approximately 200,000 km² of the southern Brazilian plateau.

The species occupies the Mixed Ombrophilous Forest biome between 500 and 1,800 meters elevation. It grows on well-drained, acidic soils derived from basaltic parent material.

Optimal growth occurs in regions with 1,200–2,000 mm annual rainfall and mean annual temperatures of 13–18°C. The species is frost-tolerant but sensitive to prolonged drought.

Mixed Araucaria forests are among the most biodiverse forest types in southern [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]]. They serve as critical habitat for endangered species including the red-spectacled parrot (Amazona pretrei) and the brown howler monkey (Alouatta guariba).

The forest type transitions ecologically with altitude, forming mosaics with grasslands (campos) and subtropical Atlantic forest. These ecotonal zones support exceptionally high plant species richness.

Altitudinal gradients create distinct vegetation bands, with pure Araucaria stands at higher elevations giving way to mixed associations with [[ilex-paraguariensis]] (yerba mate) and Podocarpus lambertii at lower slopes.

## Ecological Role

Araucaria angustifolia is a dominant canopy species that structures the entire forest community. Its emergent crown creates a distinct vertical stratification beneath which a rich understory develops.

The massive seed cones (piñas) provide food for numerous vertebrate species. The azure jay (Cyanocorax caeruleus) is the primary seed disperser, caching pinhão seeds in the forest floor where many germinate.

Indigenous communities including the Kaingang and Xetá peoples have co-evolved with Araucaria forests for thousands of years. Traditional pinhão harvesting practices contribute to [[clay-pellet-seed-dispersal]] and forest regeneration.

The species supports diverse epiphyte communities including bromeliads, orchids, and lichens. Fallen branches and decomposing needles create microhabitat heterogeneity essential for ground-dwelling invertebrates.

Araucaria forests regulate regional hydrological cycles, maintaining streamflow in the Paraná and Uruguay river basins. Their role in [[mycofiltration-watershed-protection]] is increasingly recognized in conservation planning.

The species exhibits a unique growth pattern among conifers, with juveniles passing through a shaded "candelabra" phase before developing the mature umbrella crown. This developmental shift typically occurs between 15 and 25 years of age.

Fire regimes have historically shaped the distribution of Araucaria forests. Low-intensity surface fires create canopy openings that facilitate regeneration, while severe fires kill mature trees and reset succession.

## Timber and Economic Uses

Brazilian pine timber is straight-grained, lightweight, and easily worked. Heartwood is pale yellow to reddish with a fine, even texture and moderate natural durability.

Wood density ranges from 450–550 kg/m³ at 12% moisture content. The timber is valued for construction, furniture-making, plywood production, and paper pulp manufacturing.

Export of Araucaria timber reached peak volumes in the mid-twentieth century, with Brazil exporting over 15 million cubic meters annually. This exploitation was the primary driver of population collapse.

Pinhão seeds are harvested commercially and sold in markets throughout southern Brazil during the autumn season. Annual harvests are estimated at 40,000–60,000 tonnes from natural stands.

The species has been planted in subtropical regions worldwide as an ornamental and timber tree. Plantations exist in South Africa, Australia, New Zealand, and parts of [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]].

Resin extracted from the bark has traditional applications in folk medicine and varnish production. The aromatic quality makes it a potential source of terpenoid compounds for pharmaceutical use.
