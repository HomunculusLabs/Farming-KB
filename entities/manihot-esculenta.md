## Overview

*Manihot esculenta* (cassava, manioc, yuca) is a woody shrub in the family Euphorbiaceae, cultivated throughout the tropics for its starchy tuberous roots, which are a major carbohydrate source for over 800 million people worldwide.

Cassava is the third-largest source of carbohydrates in the tropics after rice and maize, and is exceptionally drought-tolerant, thriving on marginal soils where few other crops survive.

## Taxonomy

Eudicots → Rosids → Malpighiales → Euphorbiaceae → Crotonoideae → Manihoteae → ***Manihot* → *M. esculenta***

- **Authority:** Crantz (1766)
- **Basionym:** *Jatropha esculenta* Crantz (1766)
- **Synonyms:** *Manihot utilissima* Pohl, *Manihot dulcis* J.G.M. Pers., *Manihot aipi* Pohl, *Manihot diffusa* Pohl
- **Common names:** cassava, manioc, yuca, mandioca, tapioca plant, Brazilian arrowroot, mogo (East Africa), kamoteng kahoy (Philippines)

## Morphology

Cassava is a perennial shrub cultivated as an annual, reaching 1–5 m in height depending on variety and growing conditions.

- **Stems:** Erect, cylindrical, woody at base; green to reddish-brown, up to 5 cm diameter; prominently lenticellate; may branch at higher nodes.
- **Leaves:** Palmately compound, 5–9 leaflets per leaf; leaflets ovate to lanceolate, 8–20 cm long, 2–6 cm wide, with entire to shallowly lobed margins; petioles 5–25 cm long.
- **Roots:** The economically important organ — tuberous, tapering storage roots, 5–15 per plant, 15–100 cm long and 3–15 cm diameter; outer periderm brown and corky; inner flesh white, yellow, or cream.
- **Flowers:** Monoecious; male flowers in terminal panicles, female flowers in lower axillary racemes; petals yellowish, 5–10 mm; pollinated by insects.
- **Fruit:** Capsule, 1.5 cm diameter, six-angled, containing three seeds.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cassava originated in the Cerrado and Amazon regions of Brazil and was domesticated approximately 8,000–10,000 years ago by indigenous peoples of South America.

The species is now cultivated across the global tropics between approximately 30°N and 30°S latitude.

- **Major producers:** Nigeria (world's largest), Democratic Republic of Congo, Thailand, Indonesia, Brazil, Ghana, Mozambique, Cambodia, Vietnam, Angola.
- **Climate:** Grows best at 25–29 °C with 1,000–1,500 mm annual rainfall; tolerates prolonged drought (3–6 months) due to deep root system and stomatal control.
- **Soils:** Highly adaptable — grows in sandy loams to heavy clays, acidic to neutral pH (4.5–8.0); tolerates low fertility better than most staple crops.
- **Altitude:** Sea level to approximately 2,000 m in equatorial regions.

## Cultivation

Cassava is propagated vegetatively from stem cuttings, making it accessible to subsistence farmers who do not need seed or specialized inputs.

- **Propagation:** Stem cuttings 15–25 cm long, planted horizontally or at a 45° angle; 10,000 cuttings per hectare is standard.
- **Planting season:** Beginning of rains in seasonal climates; can be planted year-round in equatorial regions.
- **Growth cycle:** 8–24 months depending on variety and conditions; sweet varieties mature in 8–12 months, bitter varieties in 12–24 months.
- **Yield:** 10–40 tonnes fresh roots per hectare; average world yield ~12 t/ha; improved varieties can exceed 50 t/ha.
- **Nutrient requirements:** Low compared to cereals; benefits from moderate potassium and phosphorus application; nitrogen needs are modest due to efficient canopy architecture.
- **Pest resistance:** Generally resistant to most pests; the cassava mealybug (*Phenacoccus manihoti*) and cassava green mite (*Mononychellus tanajoa*) are the most significant pests.
- **Diseases:** Cassava mosaic disease (CMD, caused by begomoviruses), cassava brown streak disease (CBSD), and bacterial blight (*Xanthomonas axonopodis* pv. *manihotis*) are the most damaging.

## Nutritional Profile

Cassava roots are primarily composed of starch and water, with relatively low protein and micronutrient content compared to cereals.

- **Macronutrients (per 100 g raw root):** 160 kcal, 38 g carbohydrates (primarily starch), 1.4 g protein, 0.3 g fat, 1.8 g dietary fiber.
- **Vitamins:** Vitamin C (20 mg), thiamine (0.09 mg), folate (27 μg), niacin (0.4 mg).
- **Minerals:** Potassium (271 mg), phosphorus (27 mg), calcium (16 mg), magnesium (21 mg), iron (0.3 mg).
- **Protein limitation:** The protein content is insufficient to serve as a sole protein source; populations dependent on cassava require complementary protein from legumes, fish, or animal sources.

## Toxicity and Processing

Raw cassava contains cyanogenic glycosides (primarily linamarin and lotaustralin) that release hydrogen cyanide (HCN) upon tissue damage and enzymatic hydrolysis.

- **Bitter vs. sweet varieties:** Bitter varieties contain 100–500 mg HCN/kg fresh weight; sweet varieties contain less than 50 mg/kg. Bitter varieties are generally preferred for processed products due to higher dry matter content.
- **Acute toxicity:** Consumption of inadequately processed cassava can cause acute cyanide poisoning (konzo, a neurological disorder characterized by spastic paraparesis); chronic low-level exposure is linked to ataxic neuropathy and goiter.
- **Processing methods:** Peeling, grating, fermenting, sun-drying, and cooking all reduce cyanogenic content; traditional processing methods (fermentation followed by roasting or frying) reduce HCN by 90–99%.
- **Safe threshold:** The WHO recommends maximum HCN intake of 10 mg/kg body weight per day for acute exposure; properly processed cassava products contain <10 mg HCN/kg.

## Permaculture and Agroforestry Applications

Cassava is one of the most versatile permaculture crops in the tropics, valued for its resilience, low-input requirements, and multiple functions in designed ecosystems.

- **Food forest integration:** Functions as an upper-shrub layer between ground covers and canopy trees; provides shade for understory crops during establishment.
- **Soil improvement:** Deep root system breaks compacted subsoil and cycles nutrients from deep soil layers; leaf fall contributes organic matter.
- **Erosion control:** Effective slope stabilizer when planted as contour hedgerows; the dense root mat holds soil on steep terrain.
- **Companion planting:** Pairs well with leguminous trees (gliricidia, leucaena, sesbania) for nitrogen fixation; intercropped with cowpea, peanut, and maize in traditional systems.
- **Multi-use crop:** Leaves are edible (rich in protein and vitamins when cooked); stems provide fuelwood and propagation material; starch extracted from roots produces tapioca, bioethanol, and industrial adhesives.
- **Drought resilience:** Often planted as a famine-reserve crop due to its ability to remain in the ground for up to 3 years without deterioration, providing food security during crop failures.
