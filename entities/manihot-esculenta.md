## Overview

*Manihot esculenta* (cassava, manioc, yuca) is a woody shrub in the family Euphorbiaceae, cultivated throughout the tropics for its starchy tuberous roots, which are a major carbohydrate source for over 800 million people worldwide.

Cassava is the third-largest source of carbohydrates in the tropics after rice and maize, and is exceptionally drought-tolerant, thriving on marginal soils where few other crops survive.

## Taxonomy

Eudicots → Rosids → Malpighiales → Euphorbiaceae → Crotonoideae → Manihoteae → ***Manihot* → *M. esculenta***

- **Authority:** Crantz (1766)
- **Basionym:** *Jatropha esculenta* Crantz (1766)
- **Synonyms:** *Manihot utilissima* Pohl, *Manihot dulcis* J.G.M. Pers., *Manihot aipi* Pohl, *Manihot diffusa* Pohl
- **Common names:** cassava, manioc, yuca, mandioca, tapioca plant, Brazilian arrowroot, mogo (East Africa), kamoteng kahoy (Philippines)

## Morphology

Cassava is a perennial shrub cultivated as an annual, reaching 1–5 m in height depending on variety and growing conditions.

- **Stems:** Erect, cylindrical, woody at base; green to reddish-brown, up to 5 cm diameter; prominently lenticellate; may branch at higher nodes.
- **Leaves:** Palmately compound, 5–9 leaflets per leaf; leaflets ovate to lanceolate, 8–20 cm long, 2–6 cm wide, with entire to shallowly lobed margins; petioles 5–25 cm long.
- **Roots:** The economically important organ — tuberous, tapering storage roots, 5–15 per plant, 15–100 cm long and 3–15 cm diameter; outer periderm brown and corky; inner flesh white, yellow, or cream.
- **Flowers:** Monoecious; male flowers in terminal panicles, female flowers in lower axillary racemes; petals yellowish, 5–10 mm; pollinated by insects.
- **Fruit:** Capsule, 1.5 cm diameter, six-angled, containing three seeds.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cassava originated in the Cerrado and Amazon regions of Brazil and was domesticated approximately 8,000–10,000 years ago by indigenous peoples of [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]].

The species is now cultivated across the global tropics between approximately 30°N and 30°S latitude.

- **Major producers:** Nigeria (world's largest), Democratic Republic of Congo, Thailand, Indonesia, Brazil, Ghana, Mozambique, Cambodia, Vietnam, Angola.
- **Climate:** Grows best at 25–29 °C with 1,000–1,500 mm annual rainfall; tolerates prolonged drought (3–6 months) due to deep root system and stomatal control.
- **Soils:** Highly adaptable — grows in sandy loams to heavy clays, acidic to neutral pH (4.5–8.0); tolerates low fertility better than most [[grain-and-staple-crops]].
- **Altitude:** Sea level to approximately 2,000 m in equatorial regions.

## Cultivation

Cassava is propagated vegetatively from stem cuttings, making it accessible to subsistence farmers who do not need seed or specialized inputs.

- **Propagation:** Stem cuttings 15–25 cm long, planted horizontally or at a 45° angle; 10,000 cuttings per hectare is standard.
- **Planting season:** Beginning of rains in seasonal climates; can be planted year-round in equatorial regions.
- **[[query-how-do-i-use-knf-inputs-through-the-plant-growth-cycle]]:** 8–24 months depending on variety and conditions; sweet varieties mature in 8–12 months, bitter varieties in 12–24 months.
- **Yield:** 10–40 tonnes fresh roots per hectare; average world yield ~12 t/ha; improved varieties can exceed 50 t/ha.
- **[[cannabis-soil-and-nutrient-requirements]]:** Low compared to cereals; benefits from moderate potassium and phosphorus application; nitrogen needs are modest due to efficient [[mollison-shade-systems-and-canopy-architecture]].
- **[[cannabis-disease-pest-resistance-breeding-clarke]]:** Generally resistant to most pests; the cassava mealybug (*Phenacoccus manihoti*) and cassava green mite (*Mononychellus tanajoa*) are the most significant pests.
- **Diseases:** Cassava mosaic disease (CMD, caused by begomoviruses), cassava brown streak disease (CBSD), and bacterial blight (*Xanthomonas axonopodis* pv. *manihotis*) are the most damaging.

## Nutritional Profile

Cassava roots are primarily composed of starch and water, with relatively low protein and micronutrient content compared to cereals.
