# Broussonetia papyrifera

## Overview

Broussonetia papyrifera, commonly known as paper mulberry, is a fast-growing
deciduous tree in the family Moraceae, native to East Asia. It has been
cultivated for millennia across Asia and the Pacific for its bark fiber, which
is the traditional raw material for tapa cloth and handmade paper — hence the
common name. The tree grows rapidly in disturbed sites and has become a
problematic invasive species in eastern North America and parts of South Asia.
Despite its invasive reputation, paper mulberry has significant ethnobotanical
importance, medicinal applications in traditional Chinese medicine, and
potential as a fodder and fiber crop in managed systems.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae → **Division:** Angiosperms → **Class:** Eudicots
- **Order:** Rosales → **Family:** Moraceae → **Genus:** *Broussonetia*
- **Species:** *B. papyrifera*
- The genus is named after Pierre Marie Auguste Broussonet (1761–1807), a
  French naturalist and correspondent of Joseph Banks.
- Closely related to mulberry (*Morus*) and fig (*Ficus*), all within
  Moraceae.
- Considered polyploid (2n = 2x = 26, and higher ploidy levels); wild
  populations exhibit significant morphological variation, suggesting
  hybridization and introgression with related Asian *Broussonetia* species.

## Morphology

- **Growth habit:** Deciduous tree, 10–20 m tall (up to 25 m in optimal
  conditions); trunk 20–50 cm diameter, often short and crooked; crown broad
  and spreading, 8–15 m wide.
- **Bark:** Smooth, grey-brown on young trees, becoming rough and fissured
  with age; inner bark fibrous, white to light tan — the paper-making layer,
  2–5 mm thick.
- **Leaves:** Highly variable on the same tree — ovate on short shoots to
  deeply 3–5 lobed on vigorous shoots; 7–20 cm long × 5–12 cm wide; upper
  surface rough (scabrous), lower surface densely white-pubescent; leaf shape
  polymorphism is a key diagnostic feature.
- **Flowers:** Dioecious (separate male and female trees). Male
  inflorescences are pendulous catkins, 3–8 cm long, appearing before leaves
  in early spring; female flowers in compact spherical heads, 1–2 cm.
- **Fruit:** Aggregate of small drupes (syncarp), 1.5–2.5 cm diameter, orange
  to red, sweet and fleshy when ripe; each contains a single seed; matures
  July–September.
- **Roots:** Shallow lateral roots with strong root-suckering ability; forms
  dense sprout thickets when cut or damaged from a single parent tree.

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Native range:** China, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, and Southeast Asia.
- **Introduced/naturalized:** Widespread in the Pacific Islands (carried by
  Austronesian voyagers ~3,000 years ago), eastern and central US (New York
  to Texas, heaviest in the Southeast), South America, South Asia, and
  eastern Africa.
- Highly adaptable — grows in full sun to partial shade on a wide range of
  soils including poor, rocky, and disturbed sites; tolerates pH 4.5–8.0.
- Tolerant of drought, air pollution, heat, and compacted urban soils;
  frequently colonizes abandoned lots, roadsides, and forest edges.
- Hardy to USDA zones 6–10; tolerates winter temperatures to -20°C when
  dormant.

## Cultural Significance

- **Tapa cloth (kapa):** Inner bark beaten into thin sheets to make
  traditional cloth throughout Polynesia (Hawaii, Samoa, Tonga, Fiji); one
  of the most important canoe plants carried by Austronesian settlers. In
  Hawaii, kapa-making was a highly developed art with regional patterns.
- **Paper production:** Used in China for papermaking since at least the Han
  Dynasty (105 CE); the species was the dominant paper source before bamboo
  and wood pulp. The Chinese name (構樹, gòushù) literally means "paper tree."
- **Japanese washi:** Used for calligraphy, printmaking (ukiyo-e), and shoji
  screens; paper mulberry (kōzo) is one of three principal washi fibers.
- **Korean hanji:** Bark fiber mixed with mulberry (*Morus alba*) to produce
  durable, archival-quality paper for books, calligraphy, and windows.

## Uses

- **Fiber:** Inner bark yields long, strong bast fibers (4–12 mm length,
  10–20 µm diameter) used for paper, cloth, rope, and fishing nets; tensile
  strength comparable to jute.
- **Medicinal (TCM):** Bark (構皮, gòupí) used as a diuretic and to treat
  edema, jaundice, and hepatitis; leaves applied as poultices for wounds and
  inflammation; fruit used for dysentery; roots used for rheumatism.
- **Fodder:** Leaves are high in protein (15–28% crude protein, dry weight)
  and digestible by ruminants; used as supplementary fodder for cattle, goats,
  rabbits, and poultry; leaf meal can replace up to 30% of concentrate feed.
- **Soil conservation:** Rapid growth and root sucker formation make it useful
  for slope stabilization, mine reclamation, and reforestation of degraded
  land.

## Invasive Impact

- Listed as invasive in the eastern US (especially Southeast and mid-
  Atlantic), parts of Pakistan and India (Pakistan's most problematic
  invasive tree), and some Pacific islands.
- Forms dense thickets via root suckering that shade out native understory
  and prevent forest regeneration.
- Pollen is a significant allergen; ranked among the top aeroallergens in
  urban areas of Pakistan, India, and the eastern US; male trees produce
  enormous quantities of wind-dispersed pollen in early spring.

## Permaculture Applications

- Useful in managed food-forest systems for rapid biomass production and
  chop-and-drop mulch.
- High protein content makes it valuable as a fodder tree in silvopastoral
  systems, particularly for goats and rabbits.
- Can be coppiced repeatedly for fiber harvest; regrows from stumps within
  a single growing season, yielding 3–5 m of new growth.
- Best suited to controlled settings where spread can be managed — not
  recommended for wild planting in regions where it is invasive.
