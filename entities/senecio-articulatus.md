## Overview

**Senecio articulatus** (L.f.) Sch.Bip., commonly called candle plant or sausage plant, is a succulent shrublet in the family Asteraceae. It is native to the Eastern Cape and Western Cape provinces of South Africa, where it grows in semi-arid thicket and rocky slopes.

The species is widely cultivated as a houseplant and ornamental specimen for its distinctive segmented stems and silvery-green foliage. Its fleshy, articulated stems resemble candles or sausages, giving rise to its common names.

During the winter growing season, it produces clusters of small yellow disc florets held above the foliage. These flowers attract pollinators and add seasonal interest to container plantings.

The plant reaches a mature height of 30–60 cm with a similar spread. It is well-suited to hanging baskets and shallow containers due to its relatively compact root system.

In cultivation, it is often confused with *Senecio talinoides* and *Senecio mandraliscae*, which have similar growth habits but different leaf morphology.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Senecio articulatus belongs to the tribe Senecioneae within Asteraceae. The basionym is *Othonna articulata* L.f., later transferred to *Senecio* by Sch.Bip. in 1844.

Key synonyms include *Senecio cylindraceus* DC. and *Senecio roberti-henrici* auct. The species epithet "articulatus" refers to the jointed, segmented appearance of the stems.

Stems are erect to decumbent, fleshy, and prominently jointed at the nodes, reaching 30–60 cm in height. The stem surface is pale green to glaucous with darker longitudinal striations.

When bruised or broken, stems exude a faint aromatic resin. This resin has a slightly bitter scent and may serve as an additional herbivore deterrent.

Leaves are pinnately lobed or deeply incised, succulent, and covered with a powdery white bloom. Leaf blades measure 3–8 cm long and 2–5 cm wide.

Leaves are deciduous, persisting only during the active growing season. They are sessile or shortly petiolate with a somewhat fleshy texture.

Flower heads are terminal corymbs of 3–8 capitula, each with yellow disc florets and no ray florets. Flowering occurs in late autumn to mid-winter (May–July in the southern hemisphere).

The root system is fibrous and shallow, adapted to absorb moisture from brief rainfall events. Roots emerge primarily from the lower stem nodes where contact with substrate occurs.

Plants are deciduous in summer, shedding leaves and entering dormancy as temperatures rise. Stems persist as succulent storage organs through the dry season, resprouting with autumn rains.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is endemic to South Africa, occurring primarily in the Eastern Cape and Western Cape provinces. Its range extends from the Little Karoo through the Albany Thicket biome.

It grows on rocky slopes, dry riverbeds, and in semi-arid thicket vegetation at elevations of 200–800 m. Plants favor well-drained, mineral-poor soils derived from shale and sandstone substrates.

The species is relatively fire-tolerant, regenerating from surviving stem bases after low-intensity burns. High-intensity fires may kill entire plants, relying on seed bank regeneration.

The climate in its native range is semi-arid with hot, dry summers and cooler, moister winters. Annual rainfall ranges from 250–450 mm, falling predominantly in the winter months.

Populations are scattered but locally abundant in suitable habitat. The species is not currently considered threatened, though habitat degradation from overgrazing affects some populations.

It co-occurs with other succulent shrublets including *Portulacaria afra*, *Crassula ovata*, and various *Aloe* species in thicket vegetation.

## Cultivation

Senecio articulatus thrives in bright, indirect light to partial shade. It prefers well-draining succulent potting mix with added perlite or pumice for aeration.

Water moderately during the active winter growing season, allowing the soil to dry between waterings. Reduce water significantly during the summer dormancy period when stems may shed leaves.

The species is frost-tender and should be protected below 5 °C. It grows well in containers on bright windowsills, greenhouses, or sheltered patios in frost-free climates.

Propagation is by stem cuttings or seed. Stem cuttings root readily in sandy medium within 2–3 weeks when taken during the active growing season.

Seed germination occurs at temperatures of 15–20 °C with surface sowing on moist sandy substrate. Fresh seed has higher viability than stored seed, which loses vigor after 12 months.

Pests include mealybugs and aphids, which may colonize stem joints and leaf axils. Good air circulation and regular inspection prevent infestations from becoming established.

Overwatering is the most common cause of decline in cultivation. Stems become soft and translucent before rotting at the base, requiring immediate removal of affected tissue.

## Ecological Role

In its native Eastern Cape thicket, Senecio articulatus contributes to the understory succulent layer. It provides shelter for small invertebrates and contributes organic matter through leaf senescence.

The yellow flower heads attract generalist pollinators including flies, small bees, and beetles during the winter flowering season. Its seasonal growth cycle aligns with the winter rainfall pattern of the Eastern Cape.

Fruit is a small achene approximately 2 mm long with a pappus of fine white hairs. The pappus facilitates wind dispersal across the open thicket landscape.

Like many Senecio species, it contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids that deter herbivory by mammals and insects. These compounds render the plant toxic if ingested in quantity.

The species shows limited vegetative spread in the wild, typically forming compact clumps. Seed dispersal is by wind, aided by the pappus attached to each achene.

## Uses and Ethnobotany

Senecio articulatus is primarily valued as an ornamental succulent for container culture and rock gardens. Its unusual stem form and silvery foliage make it a distinctive accent plant.

It has no significant history of medicinal or culinary use due to its pyrrolizidine alkaloid content, which is hepatotoxic. Traditional uses in the Eastern Cape are not well documented for this species.

In horticulture, it is appreciated for its low water requirements and tolerance of neglect. It is suitable for xeriscaping and water-wise garden designs in Mediterranean and subtropical climates.

The Royal Horticultural Society has awarded Senecio articulatus the Award of Garden Merit (AGM) for its reliable garden performance. This recognition highlights its value as a container-grown ornamental.

It has become naturalized in parts of Mediterranean Europe and coastal California where conditions mimic its native habitat. In these regions it is occasionally found growing on banks and disturbed ground.

The plant has received the RHS Award of Garden Merit, confirming its reliability in cultivation. It performs best in USDA hardiness zones 10–11 when grown outdoors.

Several cultivars exist in the horticultural trade, including forms with more pronounced stem variegation. Variegated specimens are slower growing but highly sought by collectors.
