# Plinthite

## Description

Plinthite is an iron-rich, humus-poor soil material that hardens irreversibly when exposed to repeated wetting and drying cycles. It is a form of ferricrete (ironstone) that occurs in tropical and subtropical soils, often capping desert hills or appearing as veins alongside silcrete. Plinthite represents a transitional soil material — soft enough to be dug when moist, but once exposed and hardened, it becomes a permanent barrier to roots and water.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil material / duricrust
- **Composition**: Iron oxides with low organic matter content
- **Synonyms**: Ironstone, laterite (partial overlap), ferricrete
- **Formation context**: Tropical and subtropical zones, areas with alternating wet-dry seasons
- **Behaviour**: Hardens irreversibly upon repeated wetting and drying

## Key Facts

- Iron-rich and humus-poor — distinguishes it from organic iron pans like ortstein
- Soft when first exposed but hardens permanently through wet-dry cycling
- Common in tropical and subtropical soil profiles
- May overlie pale bauxite deposits
- Often found capping desert hills with veins of silcrete
- Forms part of the ferricrete family of iron-cemented soil materials
- Thickness varies considerably across landscapes

## Management in Permaculture

- Avoid exposing plinthite to surface conditions where possible — exposure triggers irreversible hardening
- If plinthite is near the surface, maintain permanent vegetation cover to prevent exposure
- Ripping or shattering may be needed to establish trees where plinthite has already hardened
- Deep mulch pits can help establish trees through plinthite layers
- In design, map plinthite zones and allocate them to permanent forest or non-cultivation uses

## Relevance to Cultivation

Plinthite is significant because of its irreversible hardening behaviour. Unlike other soil limitations that can be ameliorated, once plinthite hardens it creates a permanent impermeable layer. This makes it a critical factor in tropical site assessment — land with plinthite near the surface must be treated as permanently constrained. The relationship between plinthite exposure and deforestation is a key permaculture lesson: maintaining forest cover may be the only way to keep plinthite from destroying the productive capacity of the land.
