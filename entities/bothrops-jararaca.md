# Bothrops jararaca

## Overview

Bothrops jararaca, commonly known as the jararaca, is a highly venomous pit viper species endemic to southeastern South America. It is one of the most medically important snakes in Brazil, responsible for the majority of snakebite envenomations in the densely populated states of São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, and Minas Gerais.

The species has played a pivotal role in the history of antivenom production and cardiovascular pharmacology. The discovery of bradykinin-potentiating peptides (BPPs) in its venom led directly to the development of captopril, one of the first angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors used to treat hypertension.

Jararacas are moderate-sized pit vipers that inhabit a range of ecosystems from coastal Atlantic Forest to agricultural landscapes. Their adaptability to human-modified environments brings them into frequent contact with rural and suburban populations.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Bothrops jararaca belongs to the family Viperidae, subfamily Crotalinae. It was first described by Maximilian zu Wied-Neuwied in 1824. The genus Bothrops contains approximately 45 species distributed throughout Central and South America.

The specific epithet jararaca is derived from the Tupi-Guarani language, meaning large snake. This name has been applied broadly to several Bothrops species by indigenous and colonial communities across South America.

Adult jararacas measure 80 to 150 cm in total length, with females typically larger than males. The maximum recorded length is approximately 180 cm. The body is moderately stout with a prehensile tail.

The dorsal ground color varies from grey to brown, often with a series of dark triangular or trapezoidal blotches along the midline. A distinctive dark postocular stripe extends from the eye to the angle of the mouth. The ventral surface is cream to yellowish with scattered dark pigment.

The head is triangular and distinct from the neck, covered with small keeled scales. A loreal pit is present between the nostril and the eye, functioning as a thermoreceptive organ. The pupils are vertically elliptical.

Dorsal scale rows number 21 to 27 at midbody. The ventral scales number 166 to 186 in males and 170 to 192 in females. The subcaudal scales are predominantly paired in both sexes.

## Distribution and Habitat

Bothrops jararaca is endemic to southern and southeastern Brazil, eastern Paraguay, and northeastern Argentina. In Brazil, it occurs from southern Bahia and Minas Gerais southward through Rio de Janeiro, São Paulo, Paraná, and Santa Catarina.

The species is strongly associated with the Atlantic Forest biome, one of the most biodiverse and threatened ecosystems on Earth. It occurs from sea level to approximately 1,500 meters in elevation within forested and forest-edge habitats.

Habitat preferences include tropical and subtropical moist forests, secondary growth forest, banana and coffee plantations, and rural agricultural areas. The species is frequently encountered near human dwellings, particularly in areas where forest has been converted to agriculture.

Jararacas are primarily nocturnal and terrestrial, though juveniles may be partially arboreal. During daylight hours they shelter under leaf litter, fallen logs, rocks, and in rodent burrows.

Activity patterns are influenced by temperature and rainfall. Peak activity occurs during the warm wet season from October to March. During cooler months, the species becomes less active and may enter periods of reduced metabolism.

The Atlantic Forest has been reduced to approximately 12 percent of its original extent, placing significant pressure on forest-dependent species. Despite this, Bothrops jararaca has demonstrated considerable adaptability to fragmented and modified landscapes.

## Venom Composition

Bothrops jararaca venom is primarily hemotoxic, proteolytic, and coagulopathic. The murine LD50 is approximately 1.1 to 2.6 mg/kg by intravenous injection, making it moderately potent among viper venoms.

The venom is a complex mixture of snake venom metalloproteinases (SVMP), serine proteases, phospholipase A2 enzymes (PLA2), C-type lectins, and bradykinin-potentiating peptides. SVMPs are the most abundant toxin family, comprising up to 50 percent of total venom protein.

Snake venom metalloproteinases are responsible for local tissue damage, hemorrhage, and inflammation. They degrade components of the basement membrane and extracellular matrix, disrupting capillary integrity and producing the characteristic swelling, ecchymosis, and blistering seen in envenomations.

Serine proteases include thrombin-like enzymes and factor X activators that produce defibrinogenation and coagulopathy. These procoagulant effects consume clotting factors, paradoxically leading to both thrombotic and hemorrhagic complications.

Bradykinin-potentiating peptides (BPPs) inhibit angiotensin-converting enzyme, potentiating the hypotensive effects of endogenous bradykinin. These peptides were the basis for the development of captopril by Squibb Pharmaceuticals in the 1970s.

The identification of BPPs by Sergio Ferreira and colleagues at Instituto Butantan was a landmark discovery in pharmacology. Ferreira demonstrated that jararaca venom fractions could potentiate bradykinin activity, which led John Vane and Miguel Ondetti to design captopril as a synthetic analog.

Phospholipase A2 enzymes contribute to myotoxicity, local pain, and inflammation. Myotoxins can produce rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury in severe cases. The combined effects of coagulopathy, hemorrhage, and myonecrosis make Bothrops jararaca envenomation a complex clinical challenge.

## Clinical Significance

Bothrops jararaca is the most important cause of snakebite envenomation in southern and southeastern Brazil. The Instituto Butantan in São Paulo reports treating approximately 20,000 snakebite cases annually, with Bothrops species accounting for approximately 85 percent of these cases.

Envenomation produces intense local pain, progressive swelling, ecchymosis, and blistering at the bite site. Systemic coagulopathy develops in 30 to 50 percent of cases, manifested by prolonged clotting times, thrombocytopenia, and spontaneous bleeding from gums, nose, and urinary tract.

Local tissue destruction can be severe, with necrosis requiring surgical debridement in approximately 10 to 15 percent of cases. Compartment syndrome occurs in severe envenomations and may necessitate fasciotomy. Permanent functional impairment of affected limbs is a common long-term outcome.

Bothropic antivenom produced by Instituto Butantan is the standard treatment. It is raised by immunizing horses with a mixture of Bothrops venoms and is effective at neutralizing the coagulopathic and hemorrhagic effects. Dosage recommendations depend on the severity of envenomation.

The discovery of BPPs in Bothrops jararaca venom represents one of the most significant contributions of venom research to clinical medicine. Captopril, approved by the FDA in 1981, was the first ACE inhibitor and has been prescribed to hundreds of millions of patients worldwide for hypertension, heart failure, and diabetic nephropathy.

This pharmaceutical legacy has made Bothrops jararaca one of the most studied venomous snakes in the world. Research on its venom continues to yield novel bioactive compounds with potential therapeutic applications in cardiovascular medicine.

## Ecology and Conservation

Bothrops jararaca is a generalist predator that feeds primarily on small mammals, particularly rodents. Lizards, frogs, and occasionally birds and other snakes supplement the diet.

The species is an ambush predator that relies on its cryptic coloration and thermoreceptive loreal pits to detect and strike passing prey. Venom is delivered through hollow hinged fangs that fold against the roof of the mouth when not in use.

Prey is struck and released, then tracked by scent using the forked tongue and Jacobson's organ. The venom rapidly immobilizes small mammals through coagulopathy and tissue damage, preventing escape.

Reproduction is viviparous. Mating occurs primarily during the autumn months. Females give birth to 10 to 40 neonates between February and April, coinciding with the onset of the warm wet season.

Neonates measure 15 to 25 cm in length and are capable of delivering envenomating bites from birth. Juvenile jararacas are known to use caudal luring, wiggling the bright yellow tail tip to attract frog and lizard prey.

The bright yellow tail tip is lost as the snake matures, coinciding with a dietary shift from ectothermic prey toward endothermic mammals. This ontogenetic color change is a common feature among juvenile pit vipers and reflects changing foraging strategies.

Bothrops jararaca is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN. The species remains common throughout much of its range despite the extensive loss of Atlantic Forest habitat. Its adaptability to agricultural and suburban environments has buffered population declines.

However, ongoing deforestation and urbanization continue to fragment populations. Road mortality is a significant source of mortality in areas where the species crosses roads between forest fragments. Long-term monitoring is recommended to assess population trends.

Despite its medical importance, Bothrops jararaca receives limited conservation attention compared to more charismatic species. Its role as a model organism in drug discovery highlights the broader ecological and scientific value of venomous snake conservation.
