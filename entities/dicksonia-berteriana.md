## Overview

**Dicksonia berteriana** (Hook.) Hook., known as the Juan Fernández tree fern, is a rare arborescent fern endemic to the Juan Fernández Islands (Robinson Crusoe Island and Alejandro Selkirk Island) off the coast of Chile. It is one of the most geographically restricted tree fern species in the world.

The species forms a short, stocky trunk 1–3 meters tall, considerably shorter than mainland Dicksonia species. Its fronds reach 2–3 meters in length and form a spreading crown.

Dicksonia berteriana is classified as Endangered by IUCN due to its extremely limited distribution, small population size, and ongoing threats from invasive species. It is a priority species for Chilean island conservation.

The Juan Fernández Islands are a UNESCO World Biosphere Reserve, and the endemic flora ranks among the most threatened island plant assemblages on Earth. Dicksonia berteriana exemplifies the conservation challenges facing island endemics.

The Juan Fernández flora has an endemism rate exceeding 60%, making it one of the most unique island plant communities globally. Dicksonia berteriana is part of an ancient fern lineage that colonized the archipelago millions of years ago.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Polypodiophyta
- **Class:** Polypodiopsida
- **Order:** Cyatheales
- **Family:** Dicksoniaceae
- **Genus:** Dicksonia
- **Species:** Dicksonia berteriana (Hook.) Hook.

The species was initially described by William Jackson Hooker and later reclassified within Dicksonia. The specific epithet honors Carlo Bertero, an Italian botanist who collected on the Juan Fernández Islands in the early 19th century.

Dicksonia berteriana is phylogenetically closest to the South American Dicksonia sellowiana and the Australasian Dicksonia antarctica. Its isolation on the Juan Fernández archipelago has produced subtle morphological divergence from mainland relatives.

No synonyms are currently accepted. The species has sometimes been treated as a variety of Dicksonia sellowiana, but molecular and morphological data support its recognition as a distinct species.

The island isolation has led to reduced genetic diversity compared to mainland Dicksonia populations. This genetic bottleneck increases vulnerability to environmental change and disease.

## Morphology

The trunk is erect to leaning, typically 8–12 cm in diameter, reaching 1–3 meters in height. This dwarf habit likely reflects adaptation to the windy, exposed conditions of the islands.

Fronds are bipinnate, 2–3 meters long, forming a broad crown. The stipe is dark brown at the base, covered with reddish-brown hairs and narrow scales.

Pinnae are oblong-lanceolate with deeply lobed pinnules. The rachis bears scattered hairs similar in color to those on the stipe but becoming sparser toward the frond apex.

Sori are marginal, with a bivalvate indusium characteristic of Dicksonia. The outer indusial valve is formed from modified leaf tissue, and the inner valve is a cup-shaped structure.

Spores are trilete, golden-brown, and produced during the austral spring (October–December). The species is characterized by relatively small spore size compared to mainland Dicksonia species.

The root mantle is thinner than in mainland congeners, typically 3–5 cm thick. This may reflect reduced water storage needs in the island's persistently humid climate.

On exposed ridge crests, the trunk may lean or become prostrate, with fronds emerging horizontally rather than vertically. This growth form is an adaptation to the strong prevailing westerly winds that buffet the islands.

## Distribution and Habitat

Dicksonia berteriana is endemic to the Juan Fernández Islands archipelago, located approximately 670 km west of central Chile. It occurs on Robinson Crusoe Island (Isla Róbinson Crusoe, formerly Más a Tierra) and Alejandro Selkirk Island (formerly Más Afuera).

The species inhabits montane forest and fern-grassland ecotones at elevations of 400–1,000 meters. It grows in the island's cloud zone, where persistent fog provides moisture even during dry periods.

Primary habitat includes the native forest remnants dominated by endemic trees such as Luma apiculata and Fagara mayu. Dicksonia berteriana is most abundant in shaded ravines and south-facing slopes.

The total population is estimated at fewer than 1,000 mature individuals, distributed across several fragmented subpopulations. Population density varies from scattered individuals to small groves of 10–30 trunks.

The species grows on volcanic soils derived from basaltic parent material. Soil is typically shallow and organic-rich, with excellent drainage on the steep island terrain.

The Juan Fernández Islands are volcanic in origin, approximately 4–6 million years old. Dicksonia berteriana likely colonized via long-distance spore dispersal from mainland South America and subsequently diverged in isolation.

## Ecology and Conservation

Dicksonia berteriana is classified as Endangered by IUCN. The species qualifies under criterion D (population size estimated at fewer than 250 mature individuals on Robinson Crusoe Island).

Invasive species are the primary threat. Introduced maqui (Aristotelia chilensis), murtilla (Ugni molinae), and blackberry (Rubus ulmifolius) have invaded native forest understories, competing with tree fern regeneration.

Feral goats, now largely eradicated from Robinson Crusoe Island, historically caused severe habitat degradation. Goat browsing destroyed understory vegetation and prevented tree fern recruitment for decades.

The species provides habitat for endemic invertebrates, including several Juan Fernández endemic beetles and land snails. Its frond axils collect water and organic debris, creating microhabitats for these organisms.

Conservation actions include habitat restoration through invasive plant removal, fencing of key populations, and ex situ propagation at the Chilean National Botanical Garden. Spore banking has been initiated as a genetic insurance measure.

Climate change poses an additional threat through altered fog regimes. Research suggests that cloud base heights have risen in the southeastern Pacific, reducing moisture input to island montane habitats.

Landslides triggered by heavy rainfall events are a natural disturbance factor on the steep island slopes. Dicksonia berteriana can survive small slides but large events may eliminate entire subpopulations.

## Cultivation and Uses

Dicksonia berteriana is extremely rare in cultivation. A few specimens are maintained at botanical gardens in Chile and internationally as part of conservation collections.

The species requires cool, humid conditions with protection from frost and direct sun. Its compact size makes it potentially suitable for container cultivation in temperate greenhouses.

In cultivation, the species has proven difficult to maintain long-term outside of specialized fern houses. High humidity, stable temperatures, and protection from air movement are essential for frond health.

Propagation from spores has been achieved at the Chilean National Botanical Garden. Germination occurs in 6–10 weeks, but growth is very slow, with sporophytes requiring 5–7 years to form a visible trunk.

There is no recorded traditional use of Dicksonia berteriana by the island's inhabitants. The species was likely always too rare and inaccessible to be harvested for practical purposes.

Reintroduction programs using spore-grown stock are planned for suitable habitat on Robinson Crusoe Island. These efforts require invasive species removal at planting sites to ensure seedling survival.
