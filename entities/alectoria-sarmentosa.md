## Overview

Alectoria sarmentosa, commonly known as witch's hair lichen or horsehair lichen, is a prominent fruticose lichen in the family Alectoriaceae. It forms long, pendulous, dark grey to brownish-black strands that drape from the branches of coniferous trees in old-growth forests.

The species is widely distributed across boreal and montane regions of the Northern Hemisphere. It serves as an important bioindicator of forest continuity and air quality, declining rapidly in fragmented or polluted landscapes.

Witch's hair lichen is one of the most conspicuous epiphytic lichens in Pacific Northwest old-growth forests. Its dramatic hanging curtains can extend over a meter in length on ancient trees.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Alectoria sarmentosa (Ach.) Ach. was described by Erik Acharius and later validated within the genus by the same author. It is the type species of the genus Alectoria. Recent molecular phylogenetics places Alectoria within the family Alectoriaceae (order Lecanorales).

The thallus is fruticose, forming long, filiform to subcylindrical branches that arise from a single holdfast. Branches are typically 0.3–1.0 mm in diameter and can reach 30–100 cm in length on undisturbed trees.

Coloration ranges from dark olive-grey to blackish-brown when dry, becoming darker when wet. The surface is glossy to matt, with frequent anastomosing (net-like) branching patterns. Pseudocyphellae (tiny pores for gas exchange) are sparse to absent.

Soralia and isidia are absent in typical material. Apothecia are rare, lecanorine, up to 3 mm in diameter, with a dark brown disc and a well-developed thalline margin. Ascospores are ellipsoid, 8 per ascus, colourless and simple.

The photobiont is a unicellular green alga, typically from the genus Trebouxia. This distinguishes Alectoria from many cyanolichen genera in similar habitats.

## Distribution and Habitat

Alectoria sarmentosa has a circumpolar boreal and [[dighton-nitrogen-deposition-mycorrhizal-community-shifts]]. It disappears rapidly from forests near industrial areas or in regions with significant agricultural ammonia inputs.

## Ecology and Symbiosis

As an epiphytic lichen, Alectoria sarmentosa plays a significant role in forest canopy ecosystems. It intercepts moisture from fog and rain, creating microhabitats for invertebrates including mites, springtails, and nematodes.

The hanging thalli provide nesting material and forage substrate for birds and small mammals. The northern flying squirrel (Glaucomys sabrinus) and the red squirrel (Tamiasciurus hudsonicus) both incorporate Alectoria into their nests.

Alectoria contributes to [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] populations. Selective retention of large conifers during timber harvest can partially mitigate impacts, but full recovery requires decades of forest regrowth.
