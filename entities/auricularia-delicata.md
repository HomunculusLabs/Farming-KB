## Overview

*Auricularia delicata* is a species of jelly fungus in the family Auriculariaceae, order Auriculariales. It is widely distributed across tropical and subtropical regions and is commercially cultivated as an [[bloomfield-boletus-satanus-edible-mushroom-hazards]] in parts of Southeast Asia.

The species is closely related to the more commercially prominent *Auricularia polytricha* (wood ear mushroom) but can be distinguished by its paler coloration, finer surface hairs, and slightly thinner gelatinous fruiting body.

## Taxonomy

Basidiomycota → Agaricomycetes → Auriculariales → Auriculariaceae → ***Auricularia* → *A. delicata***

- **Authority:** (Fr.) P. Karst. (1879)
- **Basionym:** *Tremella delicata* Fr. (1822)
- **Synonyms:** *Auricularia fibrillosa* (Berk. & M.A. Curtis) Farl., *Auricularia tenuis* (Berk. & M.A. Curtis) Underw.
- **Common names:** white wood ear, snow ear mushroom, pale jelly fungus

## Morphology

Fruiting body is gelatinous to rubbery, ear-shaped or cup-shaped, 2–8 cm in diameter.

- **Upper surface:** Light tan to pinkish-brown, covered in fine short hairs (setae) visible under magnification; surface is smooth to slightly wrinkled.
- **Hymenial surface (underside):** Smooth, lighter in color (whitish to pale tan), often with a frost-like or pruinose appearance; strongly gelatinous when fresh.
- **Flesh:** Translucent, gelatinous, 1–3 mm thick; rubbery and elastic when fresh, becoming brittle and horny when dried.
- **Spore print:** White to cream.
- **Spores:** Allantoid (sausage-shaped), 12–16 × 5–6 μm, smooth, thin-walled, hyaline, inamyloid.
- **Basidia:** Cylindrical to clavate, transversely septate (phragmobasidia), 50–65 × 4–5 μm, with four sterigmata.

## Distribution and Habitat

*A. delicata* has a [[copelandia-cyanescens-pantropical-distribution-and-ecology]], found throughout Southeast Asia, Oceania, sub-Saharan Africa, and tropical regions of the Americas.

The species fruits year-round in warm, humid climates, with peak fruiting during the rainy season.

- **Substrate:** Saprotrophic on dead hardwood, especially broadleaf trees including *Acacia*, *Mangifera*, *Hevea*, *Durio*, and *Eucalyptus* species.
- **Habitat:** Lowland and montane tropical forests, plantations, orchards, and agroforestry systems; commonly found on fallen branches, stumps, and logs.
- **Altitude:** Sea level to approximately 1,500 m elevation.

## Cultivation

*A. delicata* is cultivated on a modest commercial scale, primarily in Thailand, Vietnam, and southern China, often alongside the more common *A. polytricha*.

- **Substrate:** [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]] (rubber wood, mango wood, or mixed hardwood), rice straw, or bagasse in polypropylene bags.
- **Spawn:** Grain spawn (wheat or sorghum) inoculated from [[comparison-seed-saving-techniques-vs-tissue-culture-cloning]] or spore print.
- **Fruiting temperature:** 25–32 °C; unlike temperate species, it requires consistently warm conditions.
- **Relative humidity:** 85–95% during fruiting.
- **CO₂ tolerance:** Tolerates moderately elevated CO₂ levels, making it suitable for enclosed growing structures.
- **Crop cycle:** 25–35 days from inoculation to first flush; total yield 200–350 g fresh per kg dry substrate.
- **Harvest:** Fruit bodies are twisted off by hand when fully expanded but still soft and gelatinous.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

*A. delicata* is edible and widely consumed across its native range. Its culinary properties closely resemble those of *A. polytricha*.

- **Texture:** Crisp and crunchy when cooked, with a mild gelatinous snap; retains some firmness in soups.
- **Flavor:** Very mild, almost neutral, absorbing flavors of accompanying ingredients.
- **Preparation:** Typically rehydrated if dried, then added to stir-fries, soups, hot pots, and salads; often combined with garlic, ginger, soy sauce, and chili.
- **Nutrition:** Low in calories; contains dietary fiber, polysaccharides, small amounts of protein, iron, and B vitamins.
- **Dried shelf life:** Excellent — dried fruiting bodies store for 12+ months at room temperature in sealed containers.

## Bioactive Compounds

Like other *Auricularia* species, *A. delicata* produces several bioactive metabolites with documented pharmacological properties.

- **Polysaccharides:** Water-soluble β-glucans with reported immunomodulatory, antitumor, and antioxidant activities; structural characterization shows primarily (1→3)- and (1→6)-β-D-glucan linkages.
- **Melanin:** Dark pigment found in the upper pileal surface with documented free-radical scavenging activity.
- **Triterpenoids:** Minor constituents with anti-inflammatory properties reported in related *Auricularia* species.
- **Phenolic compounds:** Caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid, and ferulic acid derivatives contributing to antioxidant capacity.

## Pharmacology

In vitro and animal studies on *A. delicata* extracts have demonstrated several promising bioactivities, though clinical evidence in humans remains limited.

- **Immunomodulation:** Polysaccharide fractions stimulate macrophage activation, increase phagocytic activity, and enhance production of nitric oxide and cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) in murine models.
- **Antioxidant activity:** Methanol and hot-water extracts show significant DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging, with IC₅₀ values comparable to standard antioxidants.
- **Antihyperlipidemic effects:** Polysaccharide supplementation in high-fat-diet rats reduced serum total cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL cholesterol while raising HDL levels.
- **Anticoagulant activity:** Heparin-like polysaccharides exhibit mild anticoagulant effects via inhibition of thrombin and factor Xa.

## Ecological Role

*A. delicata* is a primary decomposer of hardwood [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-in-tropical-forest-restoration]] ecosystems, playing an important role in [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]].

The species is among the first colonizers of freshly fallen branches and contributes significantly to early-stage wood decomposition in moist tropical forests.

- **Decomposition:** White-rot decay type, capable of degrading both cellulose and lignin; contributes to carbon and nutrient release from woody debris.
- **Succession:** Typically an early successional species on fallen wood, often replaced by more competitive polypores as decay progresses.
- **Associations:** Fruiting bodies serve as habitat and food source for various invertebrates, including dipteran larvae and collembolans.
