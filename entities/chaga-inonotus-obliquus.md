## Taxonomy and Identification

**Kingdom:** Fungi → **Division:** Basidiomycota → **Class:** Agaricomycetes → **Order:** Hymenochaetales → **Family:** Hymenochaetaceae → **Genus:** *Inonotus* → **Species:** *I. obliquus*

**Synonyms:** *Boletus obliquus* (Pers.), *Fomes obliquus* (Fr.), *Poria obliqua* (Pers.), *Polyporus obliquus* (Fr.)

The visible chaga conk is a dark, cracked, charcoal-like mass protruding from the trunk or branches of birch trees, typically ranging from 10–40 cm in diameter but occasionally exceeding 50 cm. The exterior is black and deeply fissured, resembling burnt bark. Internally, the tissue ranges from rust-brown to golden-yellow, with a corky texture.

**Melanin:** The black exterior crust is extraordinarily rich in melanin pigments (15–20% dry weight), giving chaga one of the highest melanin concentrations in the fungal kingdom. Chaga melanin has demonstrated potent free-radical scavenging activity, UV-protective properties, and heavy metal chelation capacity.

**Betulin and betulinic acid:** Because chaga draws its substrate from birch trees, it concentrates betulin (a triterpenoid abundant in birch bark) and converts it to betulinic acid through biotransformation. Betulinic acid has shown antitumor, anti-inflammatory, and antiviral properties in preclinical studies. This compound is essentially absent from fungi growing on non-birch substrates, making host tree identity critical for therapeutic quality.

**Polysaccharides:** β-glucans and other immunomodulatory polysaccharides constitute a significant portion of the inner conk tissue. These compounds activate macrophages, stimulate natural killer (NK) cell activity, and modulate cytokine production.

**Other constituents:** Chaga contains oxalic acid, ergosterol (vitamin D₂ precursor), inotodiol, trametenolic acid, hispidin, and various polyphenols including protocatechuic acid, vanillic acid, and syringic acid.

The total triterpenoid content of chaga (2–8% dry weight) is notably high compared to other medicinal mushrooms, contributing to its distinctive bitter taste.

## Traditional and Ethnobotanical Uses

Chaga has one of the longest documented histories of continuous use among medicinal fungi:

- **Siberian Khanty people:** Used chaga tea for general vitality, gastrointestinal health, and as a topical application for skin conditions. The conk was also dried and carried as a portable fire-starting material due to its smoldering properties.
- **Russian and Finnish folk medicine:** Consumed as a decoction ("chaga tea") for stomach ailments, tuberculosis, and as a general tonic during long winters. The 1968 Russian novel *Cancer Ward* by Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn popularized the belief that chaga had treated a character's cancer, though this was fictional.
- **Indigenous North American peoples:** Several First Nations and Native American groups used chaga for similar purposes — as a tea for digestive health and a poultice for wounds and skin conditions.
- **Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM):** Known as *bái huān bèi* (白桦菌), chaga is classified as a tonic herb that supports the spleen, stomach, and kidney systems.

In 1955, the Soviet Ministry of Health approved chaga as a cancer treatment adjunct under the name *Befungin*, a standardized alcohol-water extract. This commercial preparation remained in pharmacological use in Russia and Eastern Europe for decades.

## Modern Research

While clinical evidence remains limited, in vitro and animal studies have explored several pharmacological activities:

- **Antioxidant activity:** Chaga extracts demonstrate exceptional ORAC (Oxygen Radical Absorbance Capacity) values, among the highest recorded for any natural substance. The melanin-glucan complex is primarily responsible.
- **Immunomodulation:** Polysaccharide fractions stimulate macrophage activation via TLR-2 and TLR-4 receptors and enhance NK cell cytotoxicity against tumor cell lines.
