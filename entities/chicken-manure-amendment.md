## Description

[[chicken-manure]] is a [[hamilton-concentrated-organic-fertilizer-products]] widely used in [[dryland-vegetable-gardening]]. In bagged form (somewhat composted but not labeled "compost"), it is significantly more nutrient-dense than steer manure and serves as the intermediate-tier amendment in Steve Solomon"s [[soil-fertility-gradient-approach]]. Solomon used bagged chicken manure (NPK 4-3-2) as his primary fertilizer before switching to his proprietary [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof]] (COF) blend.

## Classification

- **Category:** Soil amendment / organic fertilizer
- **Type:** Poultry manure (partially composted)
- **Source:** Chickens
- **Typical NPK:** 4-3-2 (bagged product)
- **Related concepts:** Steer manure, compost, COF, nitrogen fertilizer

## Key Facts

- Chicken manure is "far better stuff than weak steer manure for fertilizing the veggie patch" (Solomon).
- The concentrated nutrient content means careful application is required — overuse can burn plants and unbalance soil minerals.
- In Solomon"s fertility gradient: 1/8 inch (3 mm) layer for medium-demand vegetables, 1/4 inch (6 mm) for high-demand vegetables per 1,000 sq ft.
- Fresh chicken manure is too "hot" (high in ammonia) for direct garden use and must be composted or aged first.
- The bagged commercial product has been partially composted, reducing burn risk but also lowering some [[chelation-and-nutrient-availability]].
- Chicken manure is particularly rich in nitrogen and phosphorus, making it valuable for leafy greens and fruiting vegetables.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Chicken manure serves as a bridge between low-grade steer manure and the full COF fertility program:

- **Medium-demand crops:** Adequate when combined with steer manure and lime
- **High-demand crops:** Best supplemented with additional amendments
- **Budget-conscious gardeners:** More cost-effective per unit of nutrient than steer manure, since less volume is needed
- **Soil biology:** Like all manures, feeds [[soil-food-web]] organisms while providing plant-available nutrients

## Precautions

- Do not exceed recommended application rates — nitrogen burn is a real risk
- Avoid direct contact between fresh manure and plant stems/roots
- Composted chicken manure is safer but has lower nutrient concentrations than the partially-composted bagged product
- May contain residual medications from poultry operations (a concern in conventional, not organic, poultry)

## Nutrient Cycling
Organic amendments play a vital role in closing nutrient loops
on farms and in gardens. Manure and compost return nutrients
to the soil that were removed through harvest. The slow release
of nutrients from organic sources provides sustained feeding
for crops throughout the growing season.

## Application Methods
Proper application ensures maximum benefit and minimizes
nutrient loss. Incorporate amendments into the soil surface
or apply as side-dressing during the growing season. Avoid
applying fresh manure directly to edible crops. Composting
or aging manure before application reduces pathogen risks.

## Safety and Best Practices
Always wear gloves when handling animal manures. Age or
compost manure for at least six months before garden use.
Keep amendments covered during storage to prevent nutrient
runoff. Follow local regulations regarding manure application
rates and timing near waterways.

## Integration with Garden Systems
Combine amendments with cover cropping and crop rotation for
optimal soil building. Monitor plant response to guide future
amendment schedules. Soil testing helps determine appropriate
[[ingham-compost-tea-application-rates-and-timing]] identifies nutrient imbalances that need
correction.

## Additional Notes
These principles apply across a range of growing conditions and
can be adapted to suit specific local requirements. Continued
observation and record-keeping help refine techniques over time.
