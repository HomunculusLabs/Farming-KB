# Valeriana officinalis L.

*Valeriana officinalis*, commonly known as valerian, is a perennial flowering plant native to Europe and parts of Asia. It is one of the most widely used medicinal herbs in Western herbalism, prized for its sedative, anxiolytic, and sleep-promoting properties. References to its medicinal use date back over 2,000 years to ancient Greece and Rome, and it remains one of the best-researched herbal sedatives in modern phytotherapy.

**Synonyms:** *Valeriana officinalis* var. *officinalis* (autonym), *Valeriana exaltata* Mikan ex Schult., *Valeriana tiliifolia* Troch.
The genus *Valeriana* comprises approximately 200 species distributed across temperate and cold regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

## Etymology and Common Names

- **Genus *Valeriana*:** Derived from the Latin *valere* ("to be strong, healthy"), referencing its medicinal potency.
- **Species epithet *officinalis*:** Latin designation for plants with established medicinal use in apothecaries and official pharmacopeias.

## Morphology and Identification

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Habit** | Hardy herbaceous perennial, 60–150 cm tall at maturity |
| **Roots** | Thick, cylindrical rhizome system with numerous slender secondary roots; strong, characteristic odor when dried |
| **Stems** | Hollow, erect, furrowed, branching in upper portion, glabrous to sparsely hairy |
| **Leaves** | Opposite, pinnate or pinnatisect, 5–20 cm long, with 7–21 lanceolate leaflets; lower leaves long-petiolate, upper sessile |
| **Flowers** | Small, fragrant, arranged in dense terminal and axillary cymose panicles; individual flowers 3–5 mm across |
| **Corolla** | Tubular, 5-lobed, white to pale pink, funnel-shaped |
| **Calyx** | Inconspicuous in flower, developing into feathery pappus in fruit |
| **Fruit** | Oblong achene, 3–4 mm, crowned with a tuft of feathery pappus hairs aiding wind dispersal |
| **Flowering** | June–August (Northern Hemisphere) |
| **Fruit** | August–October |

**Identification keys:** Distinguished from other *Valeriana* species by its tall stature, pinnatifid lower leaves with numerous leaflets, and the feathery pappus on mature achenes. The dried root has a distinctive pungent, earthy aroma often compared to aged cheese.

## Distribution and Habitat

**Native range:** Europe (widespread from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean), Western and Central Asia (Turkey, Caucasus, Iran, Siberia), North Africa (Morocco, Algeria).
**Naturalized:** North America (eastern Canada and northeastern USA, introduced and widely escaped from cultivation), New Zealand, parts of South America.

**Habitat:** Damp meadows, woodland edges, stream banks, moist hedgerows, marsh margins, and disturbed ground. Prefers partial shade to full sun in moist, nitrogen-rich soils. Elevation range: sea level to approximately 2,000 m in montane European habitats. A classic species of tall-herb fen and damp pasture communities.

## Traditional and Historical Use

**Antiquity:** Discussed by Dioscorides in *De Materia Medica* (c. 50–70 CE) as *phu*, noted for its diuretic and medicinal properties. Galen prescribed it for insomnia.
**Medieval Europe:** Universally known as "all-heal" (Latin *panacea*); used for digestive complaints, palpitations, epilepsy, and nervous conditions. Hildegard of Bingen (12th century) documented its use.
**16th–18th century:** Official in the London Pharmacopoeia from 1620; extensively used for nervous disorders, hysteria, and convulsions.
**Folk medicine:** Root preparations used as a sedative, antispasmodic, carminative, emmenagogue, and topical wound treatment across European folk traditions.

## Phytochemistry

The pharmacological activity of valerian derives from a complex mixture of volatile and non-volatile constituents. No single compound fully accounts for its effects — the whole extract exhibits greater activity than isolated fractions, suggesting synergistic action.

### Major Constituents

| Compound Class | Key Compounds | Activity |
|---------------|---------------|----------|
| **Valepotriates** | Valtrate, isovaltrate, acevaltrate | Cytotoxic iridoids; unstable, degrade during drying/storage to baldrinals |
| **Valerenic acids** | Valerenic acid, acetoxyvalerenic acid, hydroxyvalerenic acid | GABA_A receptor positive allosteric modulation; primary anxiolytic/sedative compounds |
| **Volatile oils** | Bornyl acetate, isovaleric acid, valerenal, camphene | Sedative; contribute to characteristic odor |
| **Lignans** | Hydroxypinoresinol, pinoresinol | Antioxidant, GABA-modulating |
| **Flavonoids** | Hesperidin, linarin (linarin = acaciin = linarin-aglycone) | Anxiolytic activity |
| **Amino acids** | GABA, glutamine, arginine | Direct GABAergic contribution |

The volatile oil content of dried roots ranges from 0.2–2.5%, with bornyl acetate and isovaleric acid as major components. Valepotriate content is highest in fresh roots but drops significantly upon drying and storage.

## Pharmacology and Mechanisms

### GABAergic System
- **GABA_A receptor positive allosteric modulation:** Valerenic acid binds to the β-subunit of GABA_A receptors at a site distinct from benzodiazepines, enhancing chloride ion influx and producing anxiolytic and sedative effects without the dependency risk associated with benzodiazepines.
- **GABA reuptake inhibition:** Valerian extracts inhibit the synaptic GABA transporter (GAT-1), increasing extracellular GABA concentrations in the brain.
- **GABA transaminase inhibition:** Reduced GABA catabolism prolongs inhibitory neurotransmission.

### Adenosine System
- **Adenosine receptor interaction:** Valerian extracts show affinity for adenosine A1 receptors, contributing to sleep-promoting effects through modulation of the sleep-wake cycle.

### Serotonergic System
- **5-HT_5A receptor:** Valerenic acid acts as a partial agonist at 5-HT_5A receptors, a serotonin receptor subtype implicated in circadian rhythm regulation and sleep architecture.

### Pharmacokinetics
Valerenic acid reaches peak plasma concentration approximately 1–2 hours after oral administration. The half-life of major constituents is approximately 2–4 hours. Chronic administration (2–4 weeks) is typically required for full anxiolytic effects, though acute sedative effects can occur after single doses.

## Cultivation

- **Hardiness:** USDA zones 4–9; fully cold-hardy
- **Soil:** Moist, well-drained, loamy soils rich in organic matter; pH 5.5–7.0
- **Propagation:** Seed (stratification required), root division in spring or autumn, or crown divisions
- **Spacing:** 30–45 cm between plants
- **Harvest:** Roots harvested in autumn of the second or third year when active compound concentrations peak
- **Drying:** Roots cleaned, sliced, and dried at temperatures below 40°C to preserve volatile compounds
- **Yield:** Approximately 2–4 tonnes of fresh root per hectare under cultivation

## Modern Clinical Evidence

Multiple meta-analyses and systematic reviews support valerian as a mild to moderate sleep aid, particularly for subjective sleep quality and latency. Effects on objective polysomnographic measures are more modest. Evidence for anxiolytic effects is suggestive but less robust than for sleep. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) recognizes valerian root as a well-established traditional herbal medicine for the relief of mild nervous tension and sleep disorders.
