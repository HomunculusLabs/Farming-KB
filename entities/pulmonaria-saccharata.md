## Overview

Pulmonaria saccharata, commonly known as Bethlehem sage or sugar lungwort, is a clump-forming perennial in the family Boraginaceae. It is native to southeastern France and northern Italy, where it grows in woodland settings on limestone substrates.

The plant reaches 20–30 cm in height and spreads to form clumps 30–45 cm across. Its leaves are ovate to cordate, heavily spotted with silver-white blotches, giving the foliage a distinctive frosted appearance.

Funnel-shaped flowers open pink and mature to blue-violet, creating a bicolor display in spring. The color change is due to pH shifts in the corolla cell sap as flowers age.

The specific epithet "saccharata" means "sugared," referring to the white-spotted leaves that appear dusted with sugar. This silver patterning has made it one of the most popular Pulmonaria species in ornamental horticulture.

It is sometimes confused with Pulmonaria officinalis, which has similar spotting but generally smaller flowers and a wider native distribution across Europe.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Clade: Tracheophytes
- Clade: Angiosperms
- Clade: Eudicots
- Clade: Asterids
- Order: Boraginales
- Family: Boraginaceae
- Genus: Pulmonaria
- Species: P. saccharata

Basal leaves are ovate-cordate, 10–20 cm long, with a rough-hairy surface. The silver-white spotting is caused by air spaces beneath the upper epidermis that reflect light. Stem leaves are smaller, narrower, and less spotted.

Flowers are 10–15 mm long, tubular-campanulate, borne in terminal cymes. The corolla is five-lobed with a short tube. Stamens are included within the corolla tube.

The plant is diploid (2n = 14). It hybridizes freely with other Pulmonaria species, particularly Pulmonaria officinalis, and many cultivated "Pulmonaria saccharata" plants are actually interspecific hybrids.

The bristly hairs covering leaves and stems are characteristic of the genus. These trichomes are made of silicified cells and serve as a defense against herbivory and excessive transpiration.

## Distribution and Habitat

Endemic to the calcareous woodlands of southeastern France (from the Alps to the Cévennes) and adjacent northwestern Italy. It grows at elevations of 200–1,500 m in mixed deciduous forests, particularly under beech, oak, and ash.

The species favors damp, humus-rich soils on limestone substrates. It tolerates deep shade but flowers best in dappled light. Winter hardiness extends to zone 4 in cultivation.

The limestone association reflects a calcicole physiology. Plants grown on acidic soils may show chlorosis and reduced vigor. Liming or adding crushed limestone to the planting area improves performance in acid gardens.

Natural populations are locally common but restricted to a relatively small geographic area. Habitat loss from quarrying and urban expansion has reduced some populations, though the species is not currently listed as threatened.

In cultivation, it has spread far beyond its native range and is a staple of shade gardens worldwide.

It has become naturalized in parts of the UK, Germany, and the northeastern United States where garden escapes have established persistent populations. These naturalized occurrences are typically restricted to shaded, limestone-influenced sites.

## Cultivation and Uses

**Ornamental value:** The silver-spotted evergreen foliage provides year-round interest in shade gardens. Flowers in March–May offer early nectar for emerging pollinators.

**Cultivars:** Numerous cultivars exist, with selections for leaf spotting intensity, flower color, and plant vigor. Notable cultivars include 'Mrs. Moon' (pink-blue flowers, heavy spotting) and 'Sissinghurst White' (pure white flowers).

**Growing conditions:** Prefers moist, well-drained, humus-rich soil in partial to full shade. Tolerates clay soils if not waterlogged. Avoid hot afternoon sun, which scorches the foliage.

Mulching in autumn with leaf mold or compost helps maintain soil moisture and mimics the forest floor conditions of its native habitat. Established plants are moderately drought-tolerant once the root system is fully developed.

**Propagation:** Division in early spring or autumn. Also from seed, though cultivars do not come true. Self-seeding occurs in optimal conditions but is not aggressive.

**Traditional medicine:** Like other Pulmonaria species, it was historically used for respiratory ailments. The Doctrine of Signatures linked the spotted leaves to diseased lungs. Modern use is exclusively ornamental.

The leaves were traditionally brewed into a tea for treating coughs, bronchitis, and sore throats. This use has been largely abandoned due to the availability of more effective modern remedies and lack of clinical evidence.

## Ecological Role

Pulmonaria saccharata is one of the earliest spring nectar sources in the shade garden. Its flowers are visited by early-emerging bumblebee queens (Bombus spp.), hive bees (Apis mellifera), and hoverflies (Syrphidae).

The flower color change from pink to blue serves as a signal to pollinators. Pink (fresh) flowers offer more nectar, while blue (older) flowers are less rewarding, directing pollinators to the most productive blooms.

This color-change mechanism is shared with other Pulmonaria species and some members of Boraginaceae. It increases pollinator efficiency by reducing visits to already-pollinated flowers.

In its native range, the species contributes to the spring ephemeral layer of deciduous forests. The evergreen basal rosette photosynthesizes through winter when the canopy is open, storing energy for early spring flowering.

After flowering, the basal leaves persist through summer and autumn, maintaining ground cover in shaded areas. This evergreen habit makes it valuable as a year-round ground cover in ornamental plantings.

Slugs may feed on young foliage in spring, though the rough hairs provide some deterrence. Deer and rabbits generally avoid Pulmonaria due to the bristly leaf texture.

## Chemistry

Pulmonaria saccharata contains several bioactive compound classes. Allantoin is present in the leaves at concentrations of 0.5–1.2% dry weight, contributing to the traditional wound-healing applications of Pulmonaria preparations.

Rutin and other flavonoids have been identified in leaf extracts. These compounds exhibit antioxidant activity, though the species has not been subjected to rigorous pharmacological study.

Mucilage polysaccharides in the leaves account for the demulcent properties historically exploited in cough remedies. The mucilage content is highest in young spring foliage.

Tannins are present at low concentrations. The combination of allantoin, mucilage, and tannins gives Pulmonaria its traditional "lung-healing" properties, though clinical evidence for efficacy is lacking.

Pyrrolizidine alkaloids have not been reported in Pulmonaria saccharata, distinguishing it from several other Boraginaceae genera (Symphytum, Cynoglossum) where PAs are a significant concern.

This absence of toxic PAs makes Pulmonaria one of the safer Boraginaceae genera for garden use, though internal consumption of any plant part is still not recommended without proper identification and preparation.
