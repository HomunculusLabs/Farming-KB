# Compost Leachate

## Description

Compost leachate is the liquid that oozes out of compost when it is pressed or when water runs through it and leaches out soluble components. It is one of several compost-derived liquids sometimes confused with [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT), but it is fundamentally different in both composition and microbial value.

## Classification

- **Category:** Compost derivative / liquid amendment
- **Related to:** Compost tea, [[compost-extract]], [[manure-tea]]
- **Distinction:** Not aerated; minimal microbial content

## Key Facts

- Leachate forms passively when water contacts compost — bacteria and fungi in compost are attached to organic matter and soil particles with biological glues and do not simply wash off into the liquid.
- May have some nutrient value and color but does little to impart microbial life to soils.
- The microbial population in leachate is negligible compared to actively aerated compost tea.
- Should not be confused with AACT, which actively extracts and grows microbial populations through aeration.
- Leachate is an uncontrolled, passive byproduct rather than a deliberate biological inoculant.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Compost leachate is important to understand primarily as a point of confusion. Gardeners and cultivators may assume that the dark liquid draining from a compost pile is equivalent to compost tea, but it lacks the concentrated, diverse microbial populations that make AACT effective as a [[soil-food-web]] tool. For genuine microbial inoculation, actively aerated compost tea is required — leachate cannot substitute.

The distinction matters because relying on leachate as a "compost tea" would give poor results in building soil biology, suppressing foliar pathogens, or improving nutrient cycling — all functions that depend on the high populations of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes found only in properly brewed AACT.
