# Myristica fragrans

**Myristica fragrans** is an evergreen tropical tree in the family Myristicaceae, native to the Maluku Islands (the Spice Islands) of Indonesia. It is the sole source of two commercially important spices: **nutmeg** (from the seed kernel) and **mace** (from the bright red aril covering the seed). The plant is also notable for its psychoactive properties when consumed in large quantities, producing deliriant and mildly hallucinogenic effects attributed primarily to the compound myristicin.

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

*Myristica fragrans* Houtt. is the type species of the genus *Myristica*, which comprises approximately 120 species distributed across the tropics. The family Myristicaceae includes about 18 genera and 300 species of [[mollison-designers-moringa-and-multipurpose-tropical-trees]]. Several other *Myristica* species produce edible seeds, but *M. fragrans* alone is commercially cultivated for spice production.

The English word "nutmeg" derives from the Latin *nux muscata*, meaning "musky nut," referring to the aromatic seed. The word "mace" comes from the Latin *macir*, itself borrowed from an Arabic or Sanskrit source. In Sanskrit, nutmeg was known as *jatiphala*, and in Arabic as *jawz buwa*.

## Botany

*Myristica fragrans* is a medium-sized evergreen tree that typically grows 5 to 18 meters tall, though specimens in cultivation can exceed 20 meters with a trunk diameter of up to 60 cm. The tree is dioecious, with separate male and female individuals; both sexes must be present for fruit production. It features dark green, leathery, elliptical to oblong leaves 5 to 15 cm long and 2 to 7 cm wide, arranged alternately along the branches.

The flowers are pale yellow to creamy white, bell-shaped, and somewhat fleshy, borne in pendulous clusters. Male flowers are borne in axillary panicles, while female flowers are usually solitary or in small groups. Pollination is primarily by insects, though wind pollination may also occur.

The fruit is a fleshy, pendulous drupe, roughly the size and shape of a small apricot or plum (6 to 9 cm long), which turns yellow when ripe and splits open along a ventral suture to reveal the seed. The single large seed (the nutmeg) is approximately 2 to 3 cm long and 1.5 to 2 cm in diameter, with a hard, brown, shiny shell enclosing the aromatic, oily kernel. Surrounding the seed is the aril (mace), a bright red, lacy, net-like structure that is carefully removed and dried. Dried mace ranges in color from orange-yellow to reddish-brown.

The tree begins bearing fruit at approximately 7 to 9 years of age and can remain productive for 60 to 80 years or more. A mature female tree produces 1,500 to 2,000 fruits per year, yielding approximately 3 to 4 kg of dried nutmeg and 0.75 to 1 kg of dried mace. The ratio of nutmeg to mace is approximately 4:1 by weight. Both products are harvested when the fruit splits naturally, indicating ripeness. The mace is carefully peeled from the seed, flattened, and dried in the sun or in drying rooms until it becomes brittle. The nutmeg seed is dried for 6 to 8 weeks, during which time the kernel shrinks enough to rattle within its shell. The shells are then cracked and the kernels sorted and graded for market.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is endemic to the Maluku Islands of eastern Indonesia, specifically the Banda Islands (also known as the Spice Islands), including Banda Neira, Banda Besar, and Run, as well as the nearby islands of Ambon, Seram, and Ternate. It has been widely cultivated throughout the tropics over the past several centuries, including the West Indies (especially Grenada, which is now the world's second-largest producer), Sri Lanka, the Malabar Coast of India, Penang (Malaysia), Papua New Guinea, and Zanzibar.

The tree requires a hot, humid tropical climate with temperatures between 20°C and 30°C, abundant and well-distributed rainfall (2,000 to 3,500 mm annually), and high atmospheric humidity. It grows best at elevations between sea level and 700 meters in rich, deep, volcanic or alluvial soils with good drainage. The tree is sensitive to drought and strong winds but is relatively tolerant of occasional flooding.

## Chemical Composition

Nutmeg contains a complex mixture of volatile oils, fixed oils, and psychoactive alkaloids that have been extensively studied:

### Psychoactive Alkaloids

- **Myristicin** (4-methoxy-6-propyl-1,2-methylenedioxybenzene) — the primary psychoactive compound, comprising approximately 0.2% to 1.3% of the seed by weight. Myristicin is a phenylpropene compound structurally related to [[mescaline]] (sharing the 3,4-methylenedioxyphenyl group) and to safrole. It is believed to be metabolically converted to MMDA (3-methoxy-4,5-methylenedioxyamphetamine), an amphetamine derivative, though this metabolic pathway remains debated.
- **Elemicin** (3,4,5-trimethoxyallylbenzene) — a phenylpropene compound comprising approximately 0.1% to 0.4% of the seed. Elemicin is structurally related to [[mescaline]] (sharing the 3,4,5-trimethoxyphenyl group) and is believed to be metabolically converted to TMA (3,4,5-trimethoxyamphetamine). It contributes to the overall psychoactive profile of nutmeg.
- **Safrole** (4-allyl-1,2-methylenedioxybenzene) — a precursor compound present in the essential oil at approximately 0.2% to 0.5%. Safrole is a controlled substance precursor used in the illicit synthesis of MDMA.
- **Elimicin** and **isoelemicin** — additional phenylpropene compounds present in trace amounts.

### Fixed Oils

- **Trimyristin** — the major fixed oil component, comprising 15% to 25% of the seed. Trimyristin is a triglyceride of myristic acid (tetradecanoic acid) and is used commercially in the synthesis of myristic acid and its derivatives.
- **Myristic acid** — a saturated fatty acid widely used in soap making and cosmetics.
- **Additional [[stable-carbon-isotope-labelling-mycorrhizal-fatty-acids]]** — including oleic, palmitic, linoleic, and stearic acids.

### Volatile Oils

The essential oil of nutmeg (approximpinene5% to 15% of the seed) contains numerous terpene alimonenenoid compounds, primarily:

- **Sabinene** (15% to 30%) — the dominant constituent
- **Alpha-pinene** (10% to 20%)
- **Beta-pinene** (5% to 15%)
- **Limonene** (2% to 5%)
- **Terpinen-4-ol** (2% to 8%)
- **Gamma-terpinene** (1% to 5%)

These volatile compounds give nutmeg its characteristic warm, sweet, aromatic flavor and fragrance.

## Psychoactive Effects

When consumed in doses significantly exceeding culinary amounts (typically 5 to 20 grams of ground nutmeg), nutmeg produces pronounced psychoactive effects classified as **deliriant** [[fukuoka-textdoc-no-cause-and-effect-in-nature]]:

- **Onset**: 2 to 6 hours after ingestion, reflecting the slow absorption and metabolic conversion of the active compounds
- **Peak effects**: 5 to 8 hours after ingestion
- **Duration**: 12 to 24 hours, with residual aftereffects sometimes persisting for 48 to 72 hours
- **Physical effects**: Dry mouth, flushed skin, increased heart rate, nausea and vomiting (common), blurred vision, pupil dilation, urinary retention, ataxia, and in severe cases, tachycardia and hypotension
- **Psychological effects**: Altered perception of time and space, visual and auditory hallucinations, disorientation, confusion, mild to moderate euphoria followed by dysphoria, paranoid ideation, and a characteristic sense of unreality or detachment. The deliriant quality means users typically cannot distinguish hallucinations from reality, and the experience may include interactions with non-existent people or objects.

The experience is generally considered unpleasant by most users due to the intense physical side effects (particularly the prolonged nausea) and the disorienting, often frightening nature of deliriant hallucinations. The extremely long duration and delayed onset also contribute to negative experiences. Recreational use is most commonly reported in institutional settings (prisons, military facilities) where access to preferred psychoactive substances is limited.

## Historical and Cultural Use

### Ancient Spice Trade

Nutmeg was one of the most valuable commodities in the ancient and medieval spice trade. Arab traders kept the source of nutmeg secret for centuries, spreading legends that it grew in a mythical land guarded by monstrous birds. The spice was traded to Europe through Venetian and Genoese merchants and commanded extraordinarily high prices — a single bag of nutmeg could finance an entire voyage. In medieval Europe, nutmeg was believed to ward off the plague and was used as a charm and talisman. It was also prized as a flavoring agent and digestive aid.

### Dutch Monopoly

The Dutch East India Company (VOC) seized control of the Banda Islands in 1621 under Governor-General Jan Pieterszoon Coen, establishing a brutal monopoly on nutmeg production. The native Bandanese population was largely exterminated or enslaved during the conquest. The VOC restricted cultivation to a few islands, enforced production quotas, and maintained the monopoly through violence and strict trade controls. The British acquired the island of Run in a trade, and the exchange of Run for New Amsterdam (Manhattan) in the 1667 Treaty of Breda is one of the most famous real estate deals in history. The Dutch monopoly persisted for nearly two centuries until the British established nutmeg plantations in Grenada and other Caribbean islands in the early 19th century.

### Recreational Use

Recreational use of nutmeg as an intoxicant has been documented since at least the 19th century. It was described in medical literature as early as 1576 by Lodovico Sinistari, and systematic reports appeared in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The practice has been documented in prison populations, among soldiers during wartime, and in communities where other psychoactive substances are unavailable. Use tends to increase during periods when other substances become scarce or expensive.

## Culinary and Commercial Uses

Nutmeg and mace are among the most widely used spices in global cuisine. Nutmeg is a key ingredient in many cuisines, particularly in baked goods (pies, cakes, cookies), custards, cream sauces, béchamel sauce, eggnog, mulled wine, and potato dishes. It is a component of many spice blends including garam masala, ras el hanout, and pumpkin pie spice. Mace has a similar but more delicate, refined flavor and is preferred in some applications including light-colored sauces, fish dishes, and pickling.

Beyond culinary use, nutmeg oil is used in perfumery (as a fixative and fragrance component), pharmaceutical preparations (traditional digestive and carminative remedies), and aromatherapy. In traditional Indonesian and Malaysian medicine, nutmeg has been used to treat digestive disorders, rheumatism, respiratory complaints, and as a general tonic.

## Toxicity

Nutmeg poisoning, while rarely fatal, is a recognized clinical condition documented extensively in medical literature since the 19th century. The earliest systematic report was published by the German physician E. Ott in 1881, who described cases of nutmeg intoxication observed in a psychiatric setting. Subsequent case reports have appeared regularly in medical journals worldwide.

Symptoms match the deliriant profile described above and follow a characteristic timeline: initial nausea and gastrointestinal distress (1 to 3 hours), followed by central nervous system effects including tachycardia, hypertension, agitation, visual and auditory hallucinations, and disorientation (3 to 8 hours), and a prolonged recovery phase with fatigue, headache, and lingering cognitive effects (12 to 72 hours). In severe cases, complications can include seizures, cardiovascular collapse, and organ damage.

Fatalities are extremely rare but have been documented in cases of massive ingestion (estimated at greater than 50 grams of ground nutmeg). The LD50 of myristicin in rodents is approximately 200 mg/kg (oral), and the estimated lethal dose in humans is approximately 5 grams of myristicin (corresponding to roughly 400 to 500 grams of nutmeg, far exceeding typical recreational doses of 5 to 30 grams). No deaths from nutmeg alone have been reported in modern medical literature.

Chronic heavy use of nutmeg has been associated with nutmeg dependence syndrome, characterized by craving, tolerance development, and withdrawal symptoms including anxiety, insomnia, irritability, and tremor. Psychiatric complications including nutmeg-induced psychosis have been documented in susceptible individuals, particularly those with pre-existing psychiatric conditions. The deliriant nature of the intoxication makes it particularly hazardous for individuals with psychotic vulnerability.

## Legal Status

Nutmeg is uncontrolled worldwide as a food spice. It is sold freely in grocery stores and markets globally. However, several jurisdictions have enacted restrictions specifically targeting the sale of nutmeg in quantities suggestive of recreational use. In some Middle Eastern countries, importation of large quantities of nutmeg is restricted. The plant and its extracts remain legal to possess and purchase in virtually all countries. In the [[psychedelic-research-legal-history-united-states]], myristicin itself is not a scheduled substance, though some states have considered regulatory action.

## Research and Pharmacology

Research into the pharmacology of myristicin has revealed a complex mechanism of action. Myristicin inhibits monoamine oxidase (MAO), particularly MAO-A, at moderate concentratioserotonin may contribute to its psychoactive effects by increasing synaptic levels of monoamine neurotransmitters. It also exhibits weak agonist activity at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, a pharmacological profile shared with many classical psychedelics. The metabolic conversion of myristicin to MMDA (3-methoxy-4,5-methylenedioxyamphetamine), an amphetamine derivative with psychedelic properties, has been demonstrated in animal studies, though the extent to which this conversion occurs in humans remains unclear.

In addition to its psychoactive properties, nutmeg extracts have demonstrated antibacterial, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, and hepatoprotective activities in preclinical studies. The essential oil has been investigated for its potential as a [[mollison-designers-neem-tree-as-natural-insecticide]] and as a food preservative due to its antimicrobial properties. These pharmacological activities are attributed to the combined effects of myristicin, elemicin, safrole, and the terpene constituents of the essential oil.
