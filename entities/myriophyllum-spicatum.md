## Overview

**Myriophyllum spicatum**, commonly known as Eurasian watermilfoil, is a submerged aquatic plant in the family Haloragaceae native to Europe, Asia, and North Africa.

It is one of the most aggressive invasive aquatic plants in [[dighton-mycorrhizal-diversity-ecosystem-function]].

Its feathery submerged foliage and terminal emergent flower spikes make it readily identifiable in the field.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Myriophyllum spicatum** L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753 in *Species Plantarum*.

It belongs to the order Saxifragales, family Haloragaceae, genus *Myriophyllum*, a group of approximately 60 species of aquatic plants.

Common synonyms include *Myriophyllum spicatum* var. *exalbescens* and *Myriophyllum spicatum* var. *muricatum*.

The plant is a perennial aquatic herb with a robust creeping stem rooted in bottom sediments.

Stems are slender, 1–4 meters long, branching profusely near the water surface to form dense canopies.

Submerged leaves are pinnately divided into 12–21 pairs of thread-like leaflets per leaf, each leaflet 7–15 mm long.

Leaf arrangement is whorled, with 4–5 leaves per node along the stem.

The leaflets give the foliage a delicate feathery appearance that distinguishes it from coarser native milfoils.

A key diagnostic feature is that the leaflets of *M. spicatum* are compressed and held at an upward angle when removed from water.

Flower spikes emerge 5–10 cm above the water surface from the stem tips, blooming from June through September.

Flowers are small and inconspicuous, with reddish bracts and four tiny petals, arranged in a dense terminal spike.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to Europe, temperate Asia, and North Africa, from the British Isles and Scandinavia to Japan.

In [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], Australia, and New Zealand.

The species colonizes a broad range of freshwater habitats including lakes, ponds, reservoirs, slow rivers, and irrigation canals.

It tolerates water depths from 0.5 to 10 meters, with optimal growth in 1–4 meters of clear to moderately turbid water.

Preferred substrates include fine sand, silt, and organic mud, though it can root in coarser gravels.

The plant is highly tolerant of a wide [[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]].

These monospecific stands reduce habitat complexity compared to diverse native plant communities, negatively affecting fish and invertebrate diversity.

However, dense beds do provide structural refuge for juvenile fish and some invertebrate taxa during certain life stages.

The plant reproduces primarily through vegetative fragmentation — even small stem fragments can root and establish new colonies.

A single fragment as short as 2 cm with a single node can produce a viable new plant under favorable conditions.

Boat propellers, water currents, and waterfowl are the primary vectors for fragment dispersal between water bodies.

Chemical allelopathy has been documented, with *M. spicatum* releasing polyphenols that inhibit the growth of competing algae and macrophytes.

Decomposition of massive seasonal die-offs can deplete [[ingham-dissolved-oxygen-anaerobic-aact-brewing-management]], leading to localized fish kills.

## Human Uses

Despite its invasive status, *M. spicatum* has been investigated for phytoremediation of water contaminated with heavy metals and nutrients.
