## Overview

**Sanghuangporus vaninii** is a perennial polypore fungus belonging to the family Hymenochaetaceae. It is one of the primary species collectively referred to as "sanghuang" in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), a term historically applied to multiple morphologically similar bracket fungi growing on mulberry and other hardwoods.

The species was originally described as *Phellinus vaninii* by Ljubarsky in 1955. It was reclassified to *Sanghuangporus vaninii* following molecular phylogenetic studies that established the genus *Sanghuangporus* as distinct from *Phellinus* sensu stricto.

Sanghuang has been used in Chinese medicine for over 2,000 years, documented in the earliest Chinese pharmacopeia, *Shennong Ben Cao Jing*.

Modern research has validated several of its traditional uses, particularly its immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor properties. The species is increasingly recognized as a source of bioactive polysaccharides and triterpenoids with therapeutic potential.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Fungi > Basidiomycota > Agaricomycetes > Hymenochaetales > Hymenochaetaceae > *Sanghuangporus* > *S. vaninii*.

The basidiocarp is perennial, sessile to effused-reflexed, and woody-corky in consistency. The upper surface is dark brown to black, often with concentric furrows and radial cracks. The pore surface is brown to rust-brown, with round to angular pores numbering 4–6 per millimeter.

The context is cinnamon-brown and distinctly zonate. The hyphal system is dimitic, comprising generative hyphae with clamp connections and thick-walled skeletal hyphae. Basidiospores are broadly ellipsoid, hyaline, thick-walled, and measure 4.5–6 × 3.5–4.5 µm.

Setal hyphae are present in the context and tubes, typically lanceolate to ventricose, 20–40 µm long. These are a key diagnostic feature distinguishing *Sanghuangporus* from morphologically similar genera. The species lacks a true stipe, attaching directly to the substrate.

## Distribution and Habitat

*S. vaninii* is found predominantly in temperate and subtropical forests of East Asia. Its range extends across China (especially Heilongjiang, Jilin, and Liaoning provinces), the Russian Far East, Japan, and the Korean Peninsula.

The species is a heartwood rot fungus, causing a white rot of standing and fallen hardwood trees.

Preferred hosts include mulberry (*Morus alba*), oak (*Quercus* spp.), elm (*Ulmus* spp.), and poplar (*Populus* spp.). The white rot decay selectively degrades lignin while leaving cellulose largely intact.

It typically fruits in cooler conditions, with basidiocarps developing slowly over years. Wild specimens are often collected from mature trees in old-growth or secondary forests.

The increasing demand for sanghuang has led to overharvesting concerns in parts of its natural range. Sustainable management and cultivation are active areas of research.

## Bioactive Compounds

Polysaccharides are the most extensively studied bioactive constituents of *S. vaninii*. Water-soluble β-glucans with (1→3) and (1→6) linkages demonstrate significant immunomodulatory activity, stimulating macrophage proliferation and enhancing natural killer cell cytotoxicity.

Triterpenoids isolated from *S. vaninii* include inotodiol, trametenolic acid, and lanosterol derivatives.

These compounds exhibit anti-inflammatory activity through inhibition of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways. Several triterpenoids also show cytotoxic effects against cancer cell lines in vitro.

Polyphenolic compounds, including hispidin and its oligomers, contribute to the antioxidant capacity of sanghuang extracts. Hispidin analogs also demonstrate antitumor activity via induction of apoptosis in cancer cell lines.

Optimal fermentation conditions include 28°C, pH 5.5–6.0, with agitation at 150 rpm. Polysaccharide yields in submerged culture reach 2–4 g/L.

Conservation concerns have prompted research into sustainable harvesting practices and improved cultivation techniques for all sanghuang species.
