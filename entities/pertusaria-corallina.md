## Overview

Pertusaria corallina is a crustose lichen in the family Pertusariaceae, widespread across temperate and boreal regions of Europe and North America.

It forms a distinctive white to pale grey thallus densely covered with wart-like protuberances called pseudopertusariae, which give the surface a rough, coral-like appearance.

The species is moderately pollution-sensitive and serves as a useful bioindicator of [[cervantes-air-quality-ventilation]] in forested landscapes.

It is one of the more conspicuous members of the genus Pertusaria due to its large, prominent apothecia and robust thallus.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Pertusaria corallina (L.) Arnold is classified in the order Pertusariales, family Pertusariaceae.

The thallus is crustose, epilithic or epiphytic, forming a continuous to patchy crust up to 10 cm in diameter.

Surface colour ranges from white to grey-white, often with a pale greenish tinge when moist.

The surface is densely covered with convex warts or pseudopertusariae measuring 0.3–0.8 mm in diameter.

Apothecia are prominent, immersed to adnate, with a thalline margin and a reddish-brown to dark brown disc 0.5–1.5 mm wide.

Ascospores are ellipsoid to broadly ellipsoid, 60–120 × 30–50 μm, muriform (divided by both transverse and longitudinal septa), colourless, and commonly 16 per ascus.

The photobiont partner is a trebouxioid green alga, typical of many crustose lichens in temperate zones.

No soredia or isidia are produced; reproduction is primarily through ascospore dispersal.

The genus name Pertusaria derives from the Latin "pertusus," meaning pierced or perforated, referencing the pore-like appearance of the apothecia in many species.

## Distribution and Habitat

Pertusaria corallina has a broadly circumpolar distribution across temperate and boreal latitudes.

In Europe, it ranges from Scandinavia and the British Isles south through central Europe to the Mediterranean region.

In North America, it occurs across Canada and the northern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], extending into the Appalachian Mountains.

The species is epiphytic on the bark of mature deciduous and coniferous trees, particularly oak, beech, and spruce.

It is also found growing on siliceous rocks in sheltered, humid microhabitats such as ravines and north-facing cliff faces.

Optimal habitats are old-growth forests with high humidity, moderate shade, and relatively clean air.

It is less common in heavily polluted or disturbed landscapes, reflecting its moderate sensitivity to sulphur dioxide and [[dighton-nitrogen-deposition-mycorrhizal-community-shifts]].

## Chemistry

Pertusaria corallina produces a characteristic suite of lichen [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] detectable by thin-layer chromatography.

The principal compound is stictic acid, a depsidone that fluoresces blue-white under UV light.

Stictic acid is often accompanied by norstictic acid and cryptostictic acid as secondary metabolites.

Some specimens also contain trace amounts of connorstictic acid and consalazinic acid.

These depsidone compounds are of chemotaxonomic significance, helping to distinguish P. corallina from morphologically similar species such as P. coccodes and P. hemisphaerica.

The chemistry of Pertusaria species is particularly important for species-level identification, as morphological characters alone are often insufficient.

Spot tests typically show K+ yellow turning red (stictic acid reaction) and C− or C+ faint pink.

## Ecology

As a crustose epiphyte, Pertusaria corallina contributes to the micro-epiphyte communities on mature tree bark.

It provides substrate and microhabitat for various invertebrates, including tardigrades and mites, which shelter in its irregular thallus surface.

The species participates in [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] by intercepting atmospheric deposition and slowly releasing trapped minerals back to the forest floor through leaching and thallus fragmentation.

Pertusaria corallina is relatively slow-growing, with radial expansion rates estimated at 1–3 mm per year under optimal conditions.

Its sensitivity to air pollution makes it a reliable bioindicator species for monitoring long-term air quality trends in forest ecosystems.

Declines in P. corallina populations have been documented in areas with elevated atmospheric sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides.

The species shows intermediate tolerance compared to other Pertusaria species, making it useful for gradient studies along pollution transects.

## Traditional Uses

While not a major economic lichen species, Pertusaria corallina has limited documented use in traditional dye-making.

The stictic acid content can yield yellow to brown dyes, though other lichen species such as Xanthoria parietina and Parmelia saxatilis are more commonly used for this purpose.

Some Pertusaria species in European folk traditions were reportedly used as a bitter tonic or stomach remedy, though specific records for P. corallina are sparse.

No modern pharmaceutical applications of P. corallina metabolites have been developed.

Extracts of P. corallina have shown weak antimicrobial activity in preliminary bioassays, though this has not been pursued commercially.
