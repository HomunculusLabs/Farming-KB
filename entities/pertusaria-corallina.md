## Overview

Pertusaria corallina is a crustose lichen in the family Pertusariaceae, widespread across temperate and boreal regions of Europe and North America.

It forms a distinctive white to pale grey thallus densely covered with wart-like protuberances called pseudopertusariae, which give the surface a rough, coral-like appearance.

The species is moderately pollution-sensitive and serves as a useful bioindicator of [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], extending into the Appalachian Mountains.

The species is epiphytic on the bark of mature deciduous and coniferous trees, particularly oak, beech, and spruce.

It is also found growing on siliceous rocks in sheltered, humid microhabitats such as ravines and north-facing cliff faces.

Optimal habitats are old-growth forests with high humidity, moderate shade, and relatively clean air.

It is less common in heavily polluted or disturbed landscapes, reflecting its moderate sensitivity to sulphur dioxide and [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] detectable by thin-layer chromatography.

The principal compound is stictic acid, a depsidone that fluoresces blue-white under UV light.

Stictic acid is often accompanied by norstictic acid and cryptostictic acid as secondary metabolites.

Some specimens also contain trace amounts of connorstictic acid and consalazinic acid.

These depsidone compounds are of chemotaxonomic significance, helping to distinguish P. corallina from morphologically similar species such as P. coccodes and P. hemisphaerica.

The chemistry of Pertusaria species is particularly important for species-level identification, as morphological characters alone are often insufficient.

Spot tests typically show K+ yellow turning red (stictic acid reaction) and C− or C+ faint pink.

## Ecology

As a crustose epiphyte, Pertusaria corallina contributes to the micro-epiphyte communities on mature tree bark.

It provides substrate and microhabitat for various invertebrates, including tardigrades and mites, which shelter in its irregular thallus surface.

The species participates in [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] by intercepting atmospheric deposition and slowly releasing trapped minerals back to the forest floor through leaching and thallus fragmentation.

Pertusaria corallina is relatively slow-growing, with radial expansion rates estimated at 1–3 mm per year under optimal conditions.
