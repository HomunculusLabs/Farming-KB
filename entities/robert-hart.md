# Robert Hart

**Robert A. de J. Hart** (1913–2000) was a British pioneer of **forest gardening** and one of the most influential figures in the temperate-zone permaculture movement. He is best known for creating the **Forest Garden** at Wenlock Edge in Shropshire, England — a small-scale (approximately 0.12 hectare / 0.3 acre) demonstration of perennial polyculture food production that became one of the most visited and influential examples of sustainable agriculture in the temperate world. Hart's work demonstrated that the principles of tropical forest gardening could be adapted to temperate climates, and his 1991 book *Forest Gardening: Cultivating an Edible Landscape* became a foundational text for the movement.

## Biography

Robert Hart was born in 1913 in England and spent much of his early life working as a journalist and writer. He served in the British military during World War II, and his wartime experiences — including exposure to the food insecurity and environmental destruction that accompanied the conflict — shaped his later commitment to sustainable, self-sufficient food production.

Hart's journey toward forest gardening began in the 1970s when he became concerned about the environmental and social consequences of industrial agriculture. Influenced by the work of Robert Rodale (organic farming), Masanobu Fukuoka (natural farming), and the emerging permaculture movement of Bill Mollison and David Holmgren, Hart began experimenting with perennial food systems at his smallholding at Wenlock Edge in Shropshire, on the western edge of England near the border with Wales.

The Wenlock Edge site was a steeply sloping, north-facing piece of land that would have been considered marginal or unsuitable for conventional agriculture. Hart's approach was to work with the land's natural conditions rather than against them, establishing a multi-layered perennial food system modeled on the structure of a natural forest.

## The Concept of Forest Gardening

Hart's forest garden was based on the observation that natural forests are among the most productive and self-sustaining ecosystems on Earth. In a natural forest, multiple layers of vegetation occupy distinct vertical niches — from the canopy trees down through understory trees, shrubs, herbaceous plants, ground covers, and root crops — each capturing sunlight and resources at a different level. This layered structure maximizes the total productivity of the system while minimizing competition between plants.

Hart identified **seven layers** in a mature forest garden, a classification that has become standard in the movement:

1. **Canopy layer:** Tall fruit and nut trees (standard or semi-standard), including apples, pears, walnuts, chestnuts, and other large tree crops that form the overstory.
2. **Understory (low-tree) layer:** Smaller fruit and nut trees, typically dwarf or semi-dwarf, including damsons, plums, cherries, hazelnuts, and mulberries that occupy the space below the canopy.
3. **Shrub layer:** Fruit bushes and berry-producing shrubs, including blackcurrants, redcurrants, gooseberries, raspberries, blueberries, and other soft fruit that thrive in partial shade.
4. **Herbaceous layer:** Perennial herbs and vegetables that die back to the ground each winter and regrow in spring, including comfrey, sorrel, mint, lovage, and perennial brassicas.
5. **Ground cover layer:** Low-growing, spreading plants that cover the soil surface, suppress weeds, retain moisture, and can be harvested for food or medicine, including strawberries, creeping thyme, and various creeping herbs.
6. **Rhizosphere (root) layer:** Edible root crops and tuberous plants, including Jerusalem artichokes, perennial potatoes, and various root vegetables that occupy the soil volume.
7. **Vertical (climbing) layer:** Climbing and vining plants that ascend through the other layers, including grapes, kiwis, hops, climbing beans, and various perennial vines.

Hart argued that by designing a food production system that mimics the structure and function of a natural forest, it was possible to create a garden that was:
- **Highly productive:** Multiple crops from each square meter, harvested throughout the year
- **Self-sustaining:** Minimal external inputs required once established; the system generates its own fertility through leaf fall, root exudates, and nitrogen fixation
- **Low-maintenance:** Perennial plants eliminate the need for annual tilling, planting, and much of the weeding required in conventional vegetable gardens
- **Biodiverse:** The multi-layered structure creates habitat for a wide range of beneficial organisms, including pollinators, pest predators, and soil microorganisms
- **Resilient:** The diversity of species provides insurance against crop failure; if one species underperforms in a given year, others compensate

## The Wenlock Edge Forest Garden

Hart's demonstration garden at Wenlock Edge became a living laboratory and pilgrimage site for permaculture practitioners and sustainable agriculture advocates from around the world. The garden was established on a steep, north-facing slope that Hart transformed from a degraded pasture into a productive perennial food system over approximately two decades.

Key features of the Wenlock Edge garden included:

- **Diverse tree fruit:** Multiple varieties of apples, pears, plums, damsons, and cherries, selected for reliability, disease resistance, and extended harvest periods
- **Soft fruit plantings:** Extensive ribbons of blackcurrants, redcurrants, gooseberries, raspberries, and strawberries
- **Nut trees:** Hazelnuts and walnuts for protein-rich harvests
- **Herbaceous perennials:** Comfrey (used for mulch and as a dynamic accumulator of minerals from deep soil layers), sorrel, perennial onions, and various culinary and medicinal herbs
- **Wildlife habitat:** Dead wood piles, hedgehog houses, bird boxes, and unmowed areas that supported a rich community of beneficial organisms

The garden was intentionally small — less than a third of an acre — to demonstrate that the principles of forest gardening could be applied on a domestic scale. This was a deliberate choice by Hart, who wanted to show that forest gardening was not just a theoretical concept for large landholders but a practical approach for ordinary householders with modest plots of land.

## Major Works

- **"Forest Gardening: Cultivating an Edible Landscape"** (1991) — Hart's most influential work, providing a comprehensive guide to designing and maintaining temperate forest gardens. The book combines practical horticultural advice with philosophical reflections on the relationship between humans and the natural world. It remains in print and is widely considered essential reading for anyone interested in sustainable food production.
- **"Forest Gardening: Rediscovering Nature and Community in a Post-Industrial Age"** (1996) — A revised and expanded edition that broadened the scope to address social and philosophical dimensions of forest gardening.
- **"The Inviolable Hills"** (1975) — An earlier work reflecting Hart's environmental philosophy.

## Influence and Legacy

Robert Hart's influence on the sustainable agriculture and permaculture movements has been profound and far-reaching:

- **Toby Hemenway's *Gaia's Garden*:** In his influential book, Hemenway credits Hart as one of the key visionaries who recognized the value of tropical forest gardens and "transplanted many of their concepts to temperate gardens." Hemenway's *Gaia's Garden* (2001, updated 2009) became the most widely read permaculture book in North America and introduced forest gardening principles to a generation of gardeners.
- **Patrick Whitefield's *How to Make a Forest Garden*:** Whitefield, a British permaculture teacher and author, wrote the most detailed practical guide to temperate forest gardening, building directly on Hart's foundation.
- **Martin Crawford's work at the Agroforestry Research Trust:** Crawford established a more research-oriented forest garden at Dartington in Devon, England, systematically evaluating hundreds of plant species for their suitability in temperate forest gardens.
- **Global movement:** The forest gardening concept has spread worldwide, with demonstration gardens and research projects established across Europe, North America, and beyond. The principles have been adapted to local conditions in climates ranging from Mediterranean to continental to maritime.

Hart's emphasis on small-scale, household-level food production was prescient in an era of growing concern about food security, climate change, and the environmental costs of industrial agriculture. His demonstration that a productive, diverse, and self-sustaining food system could be created on a modest suburban plot has inspired countless individuals to begin their own forest gardening experiments.

## Philosophical Approach

Hart's work was motivated by more than practical horticulture. He saw forest gardening as a form of environmental activism and a spiritual practice. His writing frequently addressed themes of ecological responsibility, the interconnectedness of all life, and the need for a fundamental shift in humanity's relationship with the natural world — from domination and exploitation to cooperation and stewardship.

He was particularly influenced by Mahatma Gandhi's philosophy of self-sufficiency (*swadeshi*) and non-violence (*ahimsa*), and he saw forest gardening as a practical expression of these principles — a way to produce food that was simultaneously nourishing to the body, gentle on the earth, and conducive to social justice.

## Connection to the Broader Permaculture Movement

While Hart developed his forest garden independently, his work became closely associated with the permaculture movement after he encountered the work of Bill Mollison and David Holmgren in the 1980s. Forest gardening is now considered one of the core design elements of temperate permaculture, alongside other techniques such as swales, keyline design, and zone-and-sector analysis.

Hart's seven-layer model for forest gardens has been integrated into the permaculture design framework as a specific application of the broader principle of "stacking functions" — designing systems in which each element serves multiple purposes. In a forest garden, each plant serves not only as a food producer but also as a habitat creator, soil builder, microclimate modifier, and aesthetic element.

## Key Plant Species in Hart's System

Robert Hart's forest garden design incorporated a wide range of plant species selected for their productivity, resilience, and ecological functions. Some of the most important species in his system included:

- **Apple (Malus domestica):** The primary canopy tree, with multiple varieties selected for different harvest times, disease resistance, and culinary uses (dessert, cooking, cider). Hart emphasized heritage and local varieties over modern commercial cultivars.
- **Pear (Pyrus communis):** A secondary canopy tree complementing the apples. Pears are long-lived and relatively disease-resistant in the English climate.
- **Plum and damson (Prunus domestica, Prunus insititia):** Understory fruit trees providing reliable crops and serving as pollinator forage in early spring.
- **Hazelnut (Corylus avellana):** A multi-purpose understory tree providing nuts, serving as a nitrogen-fixer through associated mycorrhizae, and providing structural support for climbing plants. Hazelnut was one of Hart's most emphasized species.
- **Raspberry (Rubus idaeus) and blackberry (Rubus fruticosus):** Shrub-layer fruit producers that crop prolifically in partial shade conditions.
- **Blackcurrant and redcurrant (Ribes nigrum, Ribes rubrum):** Shade-tolerant soft fruit bushes producing vitamin-rich berries.
- **Comfrey (Symphytum officinale):** Perhaps Hart's most frequently recommended plant. Comfrey's deep taproot mines minerals from deep soil layers, and its fast-growing foliage can be cut multiple times per season for use as nutrient-rich mulch. It also serves as a dynamic accumulator, drawing up potassium, calcium, and other minerals.
- **Herbs:** A diverse selection of culinary and medicinal herbs including mint, thyme, rosemary, sage, lovage, sorrel, and chives occupying the herbaceous layer.
- **Strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa):** Ground cover providing fruit while suppressing weeds.
- **Jerusalem artichoke (Helianthus tuberosus):** A root crop in the rhizosphere layer that produces prolific tubers and serves as a windbreak with its tall stems.

## Design Principles

Hart's approach to forest garden design was guided by several key principles that have since been adopted and refined by the broader movement:

- **Observation before intervention:** Hart emphasized spending time observing the site — its microclimates, soil conditions, water flows, sun exposure, and existing vegetation — before making design decisions. This principle, shared with Fukuoka's natural farming, ensures that the garden works with natural processes rather than against them.
- **Minimal disturbance:** Avoiding digging, tilling, and other soil-disturbing practices that damage the soil food web. Hart practiced "no-dig" gardening, adding organic matter to the soil surface as mulch rather than incorporating it.
- **Closed-loop fertility:** Designing the system to generate its own fertility through nitrogen fixation (leguminous plants), dynamic accumulation (deep-rooted plants like comfrey), mulch production, and composting of on-site materials. External inputs were minimized.
- **Edge effect maximization:** Creating diverse interfaces between different plant communities and habitat types, as edges tend to be the most productive and biodiverse areas in any ecosystem.
- **Succession management:** Working with natural ecological succession rather than against it, guiding the development of the plant community toward a mature, stable, and productive state.
- **Functional diversity:** Selecting plants not only for their direct food yield but also for their ecological functions — nitrogen fixation, pest control, pollinator attraction, soil building, microclimate modification, and aesthetic value.

## Influence on Modern Food Forestry

Hart's work has been particularly influential in the development of **food forestry** as a distinct discipline within permaculture and sustainable agriculture. Several major initiatives trace their intellectual lineage directly or indirectly to Hart's demonstrations:

- **Agroforestry Research Trust (Martin Crawford):** Crawford's systematic evaluation of hundreds of plant species for temperate forest gardens represents the scientific maturation of Hart's empirical approach.
- **Beacon Food Forest (Seattle, USA):** One of the largest public food forest projects in North America, directly inspired by Hart's seven-layer model and the temperate food forest concept.
- **UK Forest Garden Network:** A network of demonstration gardens across the United Kingdom that traces its origins to Hart's work at Wenlock Edge.
- **Academic interest:** Forest gardening has attracted increasing attention from academic researchers in agroecology, with studies examining the productivity, biodiversity, carbon sequestration, and resilience of perennial polyculture food systems compared to conventional agriculture.
