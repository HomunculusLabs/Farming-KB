The species has a long history of use among Indigenous peoples of the Great
Plains for medicinal purposes, including treatment of coughs, fevers, and
digestive complaints. In contemporary use, anise hyssop is valued as a culinary
herb, a tea plant, a garden ornamental, and a component of polyculture and
permaculture plantings. It received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of
Garden Merit in 1993.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomic classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Order: Lamiales
- Family: Lamiaceae
- Subfamily: Nepetoideae
- Tribe: Mentheae
- Genus: Agastache
- Species: A. foeniculum (Pursh) Kuntze

**Morphological characteristics:**

Agastache foeniculum grows 60–120 cm tall with an upright, square-stemmed habit
characteristic of the mint family. The leaves are opposite, ovate to lanceolate,
3–8 cm long, with serrate margins and a grayish-green coloration. Both leaves
and stems are covered in fine pubescence.

Flowers are arranged in dense, terminal spikes 5–15 cm long. Individual flowers
are tubular, 8–12 mm long, with two-lipped corollas ranging from lavender-blue
to violet. Blooming occurs from mid-summer through early fall (July–September
in its native range). The root system consists of a branching caudex with fibrous
roots, occasionally producing short rhizomes.

## Cultivation

Anise hyssop is easily propagated by seed, division, or stem cuttings:

- **Seed**: Cold stratification for 30 days improves germination; seeds
  germinate in 14–21 days at 20 °C. Direct sow in spring or start indoors
  6–8 weeks before last frost
- **Division**: Divide established clumps in [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] or fall; each
  division should contain 3–5 growing points
- **Cuttings**: Softwood cuttings root readily in 2–3 weeks under mist
