## Overview

**Piper longum**, commonly known as long pepper, is a flowering vine in the family Piperaceae, native to the Indian subcontinent and [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]].

It is one of the oldest known spices in human history, predating [[comparison-black-pepper-vs-cardamom-tropical-spice-cultivation]] (*[[piper-nigrum-entity]]*) in international trade.

The dried fruit spikes are used extensively in Ayurvedic medicine as a rasayana (rejuvenative) and in traditional culinary preparations across South and Southeast Asia.

The species is pharmacologically significant as the richest natural source of piperine, a bioavailability-enhancing alkaloid that increases absorption of numerous drugs and phytochemicals.

It holds a central place in classical Ayurvedic texts including the Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Magnoliophyta → Magnoliopsida → Piperales → Piperaceae → **Piper** → **P. longum**.

Authority: Carl Linnaeus, Species Plantarum (1753).

P. longum is a slender, climbing or creeping herbaceous vine that can reach 2–3 meters in length with supportive vegetation.

Leaves are alternate, simple, ovate to elliptic, 7–12 cm long and 3–6 cm wide, with entire margins and prominent lateral veins.

The inflorescence is a cylindrical spike, 3–7 cm long, bearing numerous minute flowers arranged in a catkin-like structure.

The fruit is a small drupe approximately 1 mm in diameter, embedded in a fleshy spike that darkens to blackish-green when dried.

The dried fruit spike (the commercial product) is 2–5 cm long, irregularly cylindrical, with a rough, gritty texture and pungent aroma.

Roots are thick, tuberous, and also used medicinally in Ayurveda (known as pippali moola).

## Distribution and Habitat

P. longum is native to the Indo-Malayan region, occurring naturally in India, Nepal, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Indonesia, and southern China.

It grows in tropical and subtropical moist deciduous forests at elevations from sea level to 1500 meters.

The species prefers well-drained, loamy soils rich in [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]], with partial shade and annual rainfall of 1500–2500 mm.

Wild populations are increasingly rare due to overharvesting; most commercial supply comes from cultivated sources in India (especially the northeastern states and foothills of the Himalayas).

It is often found as an understory climber in forest edges and disturbed habitats, colonizing through bird-dispersed seeds.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

The principal bioactive alkaloid is **piperine** (1-piperoylpiperidine), constituting 3–9% of the dried fruit weight.

Piperine inhibits hepatic and intestinal drug-metabolizing enzymes (CYP3A4, P-glycoprotein), significantly increasing the bioavailability of co-administered drugs including [[curcumin]], resveratrol, and antiretrovirals.

The fruit also contains piperlongumine, a distinctive amide alkaloid with demonstrated anticancer activity through inhibition of the STAT3 signaling pathway.

Piperlongumine selectively induces [[blesching-cannabis-oxidative-stress-and-antioxidant-defense]] in cancer cells while sparing normal cells, showing activity against breast, colon, and [[blesching-cannabis-pancreatic-cancer]] lines.

Additional compounds include piperettine, piperlonguminine, piperstachine, and volatile oils composed primarily of sesquiterpenes.

Immunomodulatory activity includes enhancement of macrophage function, stimulation of splenocyte proliferation, and modulation of Th1/Th2 cytokine balance.
