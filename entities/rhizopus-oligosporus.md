## Rhizopus oligosporus

## Description

[[rhizopus]] oligosporus is a sporulating mold commonly referred to as bread pin mold. It appears as a furry growth on decomposing fruits, breads, and vegetables. This fungus is the primary organism used in the production of traditional tempeh, a fermented food made by inoculating cooked soybeans with its spores. The mycelium binds the soybeans into a firm, cake-like product with improved nutritional profile and digestibility. Tempeh has been a staple food in Indonesia and other parts of Southeast Asia for centuries, and its production represents one of the oldest known applications of deliberate fungal fermentation for human food.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Mucoromycota
- **Class**: Mucoromycetes
- **Order**: Mucorales
- **Family**: Mucoraceae
- **Genus**: Rhizopus
- **Species**: R. oligosporus

Rhizopus oligosporus is a zygomycete fungus, distinct from the basidiomycete mushrooms commonly cultivated for food. It is a rapid colonizer that thrives at warm temperatures (approximately 30C/86F) and produces a dense white mycelial mat that binds substrates together. Unlike many Rhizopus species that are spoilage organisms, R. oligosporus is specially adapted for food fermentation and produces minimal spores during the tempeh production window.

## Key Facts

- The primary culture organism for making tempeh from soybeans and other legumes
- Produces black-spored sporulating heads when mature, visible as dark fuzzy tips on colonized grain
- Can be maintained indefinitely on agar media through subculturing, similar to mushroom mycelium
- Commercial tempeh starter cultures are inexpensive and widely available
- Can be expanded on sterilized grain such as cooked organic rice to produce large quantities of inoculum
- Grain colonized with R. oligosporus can be dried and powdered to create a shelf-stable spore powder containing billions of spores per gram
- The powdered spore culture can be stored in jars in the refrigerator for months without loss of viability
- Mycelial binding strength allows it to hold food substrates together firmly into a solid cake
- Tempeh fermentation improves protein bioavailability and produces vitamin B12 in the substrate
- The mold breaks down anti-nutritional factors in soybeans including phytic acid and trypsin inhibitors
- Optimal fermentation temperature is approximately 30C (86F) with a typical fermentation time of 24-48 hours
- The resulting tempeh cake can be sliced and cooked fresh, or dried and compressed for long-term storage

## Culturing and Spawn Production

To produce tempeh starter from a purified culture, the process begins by transferring R. oligosporus from agar media to sterilized grain such as cooked organic rice. The grain is allowed to fully colonize until black-spored tips form on the surface. The colonized grain is then spread in a shallow pan approximately one-half inch thick and covered with an inverted clear pan, offset slightly to create a breathing dome that allows gas exchange while providing space for sporulating heads to form.

Placing the pan under a grow lamp significantly increases spore production. Once the top of the grain is covered with thick black fungal fur, the grain is transferred to a food dehydrator at low heat and dried completely. The dried grains are then blended into a fine powder containing billions of spores and dehydrated mycelium fragments ready to make tempeh at a moments notice. This powder stores well in sealed jars in the refrigerator for months.

## Tempeh Production Process

Traditional tempeh is made by cooking soybeans, dehulling them, and inoculating with R. oligosporus spores. The inoculated beans are packed into perforated plastic bags or banana leaves that allow gas exchange while maintaining humidity. Fermentation proceeds at 30C for 24-48 hours, during which the white mycelium grows through the beans, binding them into a solid cake. The mycelium knits the beans together so firmly that the cake can be sliced like a solid food.

The fermentation process transforms the substrate in several important ways: it increases protein digestibility by breaking down complex proteins into simpler forms, synthesizes B-vitamins including B12, reduces anti-nutritional compounds like phytic acid and trypsin inhibitors, and creates a firm texture that holds together during cooking. Tempeh can also be made from other substrates including chickpeas, black beans, lentils, and various grains.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Rhizopus oligosporus occupies an important niche in fungal fermentation technology and food production. For mushroom cultivators, this organism represents an accessible entry point into sterile tissue culture techniques. The skills required to maintain, subculture, and expand R. oligosporus on agar and grain are directly transferable to mushroom [[spawn-production]]. Cotter (2016) recommends mastering these techniques to produce tempeh year-round without purchasing new starter cultures.

The workflow mirrors [[grain-spawn]] expansion used in [[mushroom-cultivation]]: culture on agar, transfer to sterilized grain, allow full colonization, and process the resulting biomass. This parallel makes tempeh production an excellent training exercise for aspiring mushroom cultivators who want to develop their sterile technique before working with more demanding mushroom species.

Additionally, R. oligosporus is relevant to the concept of mycelial space cakes for space cultivation. Unlike tempeh cultures, which sporulate prolifically and would pose air quality risks in enclosed environments, nonsporulating mushroom cultures are preferred for space applications. However, the tempeh model of mycelium-bound food cakes directly inspired the development of mushroom-based space food products using shiitake, oyster, and reishi mycelium. The space cakes concept involves growing mushroom mycelium on substrates like beans, grains, or rice, then cooking the colonized substrate fresh or drying it for storage.

A comprehensive overview of tempeh culture can be found in The Art of Fermentation by Sandor Katz (2012), which describes the process of culturing tempeh from purchased strains as well as perpetuating and expanding the culture indefinitely.
