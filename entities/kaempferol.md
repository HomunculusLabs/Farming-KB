## Overview

**Kaempferol** (3,5,7,4′-tetrahydroxyflavone) is a natural flavonol polyphenol found widely in edible plants, fruits, vegetables, and herbal medicines. It is one of the six major dietary flavonoid subclasses and ranks among the most abundant flavonols in the human diet alongside quercetin and myricetin.

The compound was first isolated from the rhizome of Kaempferia galanga (lesser galangal), from which its name derives. It has attracted extensive pharmacological research for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties demonstrated across hundreds of in vitro and in vivo studies.

## Chemical Properties

- **Molecular formula:** C₁₅H₁₀O₆
- **Molecular weight:** 286.24 g/mol
- **IUPAC name:** 3,5,7-trihydroxy-2-(4-hydroxyphenyl)-4H-chromen-4-one
- **CAS number:** 520-18-3
- **SMILES:** C1=CC(=C(C=C1O)O)C2=C(C(=O)C3=C(C=C(C=C3O2)O)O)O
- **Appearance:** Yellow crystalline powder

Kaempferol belongs to the flavonol subclass (3-hydroxylated flavones). Its structure consists of a chromen-4-one backbone with hydroxyl groups at positions 3, 5, 7, and 4′. The absence of the 3′-OH group distinguishes it from quercetin.

The compound is sparingly soluble in water (1.3 mg/L at 25°C) but soluble in ethanol, DMSO, and alkaline aqueous solutions. It undergoes typical flavonoid chemistry: complexation with aluminum chloride, bathochromic shift with sodium acetate, and susceptibility to oxidation at the catechol-like positions.

## Dietary Sources and Bioavailability

Kaempferol is concentrated in capers, kale, onions, berries, tea, grapes, and citrus fruits. Capers (Capparis spinosa) contain the highest documented levels at approximately 234 mg/100 g fresh weight. Other rich sources include dill weed, chives, and broccoli.

Average daily intake in Western diets is estimated at 10–20 mg, though significant inter-individual variation exists depending on fruit and vegetable consumption patterns. Populations with high tea intake (East Asia) may consume 30–60 mg daily.

Oral bioavailability is low (approximately 2%), limited by extensive phase II metabolism (glucuronidation and sulfation) in intestinal epithelial cells and hepatic first-pass metabolism. Peak plasma concentrations of 0.1–1 µM are reached 1–2 hours after ingestion of a 50 mg dose.

## Pharmacology and Mechanisms of Action

Kaempferol modulates multiple signaling pathways relevant to inflammation and cancer. It inhibits NF-κB activation by blocking IκB kinase (IKK) phosphorylation, reducing expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, COX-2).

The compound activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a central metabolic regulator. AMPK activation contributes to its documented insulin-sensitizing, lipid-lowering, and anti-obesity effects in rodent models.

Kaempferol induces apoptosis in cancer cells through both intrinsic (mitochondrial) and extrinsic (death receptor) pathways. It upregulates pro-apoptotic Bax and Bak, downregulates anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, and activates caspase-3, -8, and -9.

Additional pharmacological targets include inhibition of PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling, suppression of VEGF-mediated angiogenesis, and modulation of estrogen receptor signaling. These multi-target effects underlie its broad-spectrum anticancer activity.

## Anti-inflammatory and Antioxidant Activity

Kaempferol scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) including superoxide anion, hydroxyl radical, and peroxynitrite. Its direct antioxidant capacity (ORAC value) is comparable to quercetin but lower than myricetin.

Anti-inflammatory effects are mediated through inhibition of lipoxygenase (LOX) and cyclooxygenase (COX-2) enzymes, reducing prostaglandin and leukotriene production. Kaempferol also inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation, a key driver of chronic inflammatory diseases.

In animal models of arthritis, kaempferol (50–100 mg/kg) reduces joint swelling, inflammatory cell infiltration, and cartilage degradation. These effects are comparable to indomethacin at equivalent doses, with fewer gastrointestinal side effects.

## Cardiovascular and Metabolic Effects

Epidemiological studies associate high dietary kaempferol intake with reduced cardiovascular disease risk. The Zutphen Elderly Study (n=805, 10-year follow-up) reported a 66% reduction in coronary heart disease mortality in the highest quartile of kaempferol intake.

Vasoprotective effects include enhancement of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activity, reduction of endothelial adhesion molecule expression (VCAM-1, ICAM-1), and inhibition of platelet aggregation. These actions improve vascular function and reduce atherosclerotic plaque formation.

Metabolic benefits include improved glucose uptake in skeletal muscle, enhanced insulin signaling, and reduced hepatic gluconeogenesis. In high-fat diet-fed mice, kaempferol supplementation (0.1% w/w) reduces body weight gain, hepatic steatosis, and serum triglycerides.

## Anticancer Evidence

Preclinical evidence supports kaempferol activity against multiple cancer types including breast, lung, colon, prostate, pancreatic, ovarian, and gastric cancers. IC₅₀ values in cancer cell lines typically range from 20–100 µM.

In breast cancer, kaempferol inhibits ERα-positive cell proliferation and synergizes with tamoxifen. In [[cannabis-and-colon-cancer-research]], it suppresses Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

In vivo studies demonstrate tumor growth inhibition of 30–60% in xenograft models at doses of 50–200 mg/kg. No significant toxicity has been observed at therapeutic doses in animal studies. [[blesching-cannabis-safety-adverse-effects-evidence]] have been reported at normal dietary intake levels.

At pharmacological doses (>200 mg/day), mild gastrointestinal discomfort has been reported. High-dose intravenous administration in animal studies showed no organ toxicity at doses up to 500 mg/kg. No mutagenic or genotoxic effects have been observed in standard Ames test and micronucleus assays.
