## Overview

*Anchusa officinalis*, commonly known as common bugloss or alkanet, is a herbaceous perennial in the family Boraginaceae. It is native to Europe and western Asia, where it grows in dry grasslands, scrub, and disturbed ground.

The species produces striking deep blue to violet-blue flowers in coiled cymes that uncurl as they bloom. These vivid blue blossoms make it a valued ornamental plant for cottage gardens and wildflower meadows.

The specific epithet *officinalis* indicates its historical use in medicine. The roots were formerly used to produce a red dye and were thought to have medicinal properties.

Common bugloss is closely related to the dyer's alkanet (*Alkanna tinctoria*) and the Italian bugloss (*Anchusa azurea*). These species share similar flower morphology and habitat preferences.

The plant has naturalized in parts of North America, where it is sometimes considered a minor weed in pastures and along roadsides.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantate → Phylum Tracheophyta → Class Magnoliopsida → Order Boraginales → Family Boraginaceae → Genus *Anchusa* → Species *A. officinalis* L.

The genus *Anchusa* comprises approximately 35 species distributed across Europe, North Africa, and western Asia. *Anchusa officinalis* belongs to a group of perennial species with tubular, bright blue flowers.

Plants grow 30–80 cm tall with erect, branched stems that are covered with stiff, spreading hairs. The entire plant has a coarse, bristly texture characteristic of many Boraginaceae.

Basal leaves are lanceolate to oblong, 8–20 cm long and 2–5 cm wide, with prominent midribs and rough-hairy surfaces. Stem leaves are smaller, narrower, and sessile, clasping the stem at the base.

Flowers are tubular, 8–12 mm in diameter, with five rounded lobes in deep blue to violet-blue. A white or cream eye ring at the center of the corolla provides a contrasting nectar guide.

Flowers are arranged in scorpioid cymes — coiled inflorescences that uncurl as flowers open progressively. This distinctive arrangement is characteristic of the Boraginaceae family.

Blooming occurs from May through September. Individual flowers last 2–3 days, with new flowers opening continuously over a long season.

The fruit consists of four nutlets (achenes), each 3–4 mm long, wrinkled, and gray-brown. The nutlets are enclosed by the persistent calyx after maturity.

The chromosome number is 2n = 16, which is typical for the genus *Anchusa*. Polyploid forms are rare but have been reported in some southeastern European populations.

## Distribution and Habitat

Common bugloss is native across most of Europe, from the British Isles and Scandinavia south to the Mediterranean and east to the Caucasus and Iran. It is widespread and locally common within this range.

The species grows in dry grasslands, scrub margins, road verges, field edges, and disturbed ground. It prefers well-drained, calcareous to neutral soils in full sun to partial shade.

It tolerates a wide range of soil conditions, including poor, stony soils and dry banks. This adaptability contributes to its success as a colonizing species on disturbed sites.

In North America, *Anchusa officinalis* has naturalized in scattered locations across the northeastern United States and southeastern Canada. Populations are most established in pastures and along transportation corridors.

In the British Isles, it is locally frequent in southern England and Wales but rare in Scotland and Ireland. It occurs primarily on chalk and limestone grasslands.

The species occurs from sea level to approximately 1,800 m elevation in mountainous regions of central Europe. Alpine populations are generally more compact with shorter flowering stems.

Mediterranean populations in Spain, Italy, and Greece occupy drier, more open habitats than northern European plants. These southern populations show some genetic differentiation from central European stocks.

## Chemistry and Toxicity

*Anchusa officinalis* contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs), including intermedine and lycopsamine. These compounds are hepatotoxic and potentially carcinogenic with chronic ingestion.

The roots contain anchusin, a red-brown coloring matter that was formerly used as a dye. The dye produces shades of red, brown, and purple depending on the mordant used.

Traditional medicinal uses included treating skin conditions, respiratory ailments, and inflammatory disorders. However, modern herbalism discourages internal use due to PA toxicity concerns.

The plant is toxic to livestock if consumed in large quantities. Horses and cattle are most susceptible to PA-induced liver damage, though grazing animals typically avoid the bristly foliage.

Despite these toxicity concerns, the plant has a long history of use in European folk medicine. The species was listed in many medieval herbals as a treatment for melancholy and skin disorders.

## Cultivation

Common bugloss is cultivated as an ornamental perennial for its vivid blue flowers and long bloom season. It thrives in full sun with well-drained, moderately fertile soil.

Hardy to USDA zones 4–8. Plants are drought-tolerant once established and perform well in xeriscaping schemes. They are relatively short-lived, persisting 3–5 years.

Propagation is by seed sown in spring or autumn. Seeds germinate readily without pretreatment. Plants also self-seed freely in suitable garden conditions.

Division is possible but difficult due to the deep taproot. Root cuttings taken in late winter provide a more reliable vegetative propagation method.

The species combines well with other blue-flowered perennials such as *Centaurea montana* and *Salvia nemorosa*. The bristly foliage deters deer and rabbit browsing.

Deadheading prevents excessive self-seeding in garden settings. Allow some seed heads to mature if naturalizing is desired.

Plants benefit from being cut back to basal growth after the first flush of flowers fades. This encourages a second flush of blooms in late summer and fresh basal foliage.

## Ecology

The flowers are pollinated by long-tongued bees, particularly bumblebees (*Bombus* spp.) and leafcutter bees (*Megachile* spp.). The deep tubular corolla restricts access to shorter-tongued insects.

The coarse, hairy foliage provides shelter for predatory insects such as ground beetles and rove beetles. These beneficial insects contribute to biological pest control in garden settings.

Seeds are dispersed by ants (myrmecochory), attracted by a lipid-rich elaiosome on the nutlet surface. This ant-mediated dispersal helps establish new plants near parent colonies.

The species is a food plant for the larvae of several moth species. The scarlet tiger moth (*Callimorpha dominula*) has been recorded feeding on *Anchusa* foliage in southern Europe.

The long bloom season makes common bugloss a valuable late-summer nectar source. In wildflower meadows, it fills the gap between early-summer and autumn-flowering species.

The deep taproot helps stabilize soil on dry banks and roadside embankments. This erosion-control function contributes to its persistence in semi-natural habitats across Europe.
