# Candida albicans

Candida albicans is a dimorphic fungal species that exists as both a
commensal organism and an opportunistic pathogen in humans. It is a
member of the Hemiascomycetes and is one of the most common causes of
fungal infections worldwide, capable of causing both superficial
mucosal infections and life-threatening systemic disease.

C. albicans normally colonizes the human gastrointestinal tract, oral
cavity, and genital mucosa without causing disease, but can transition
to pathogenic growth when host defenses are compromised. This
commensal-pathogen duality makes it a clinically important organism
studied extensively in medical mycology.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Hemiascomycetes (Saccharomycetes)
- **Order:** Saccharomycetales
- **Family:** Saccharomycetaceae (Candidaceae)
- **Genus:** Candida
- **Species:** C. albicans

C. albicans is classified among the Hemiascomycetes alongside
Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Ashbya gossypii. Unlike S. cerevisiae, C.
albicans does not possess hex-1 homologues or Woronin bodies,
consistent with the absence of these organelles outside the
Euascomycetes.

Comparative analyses have been conducted across the Candida genus,
including C. glabrata, C. shehatae, C. tanzawaensis, and C. ambrosiae.
The genus contains over 200 species, but C. albicans remains the most
clinically significant member.

## Dimorphism

C. albicans can switch between several morphological forms central to
its biology and pathogenicity. The yeast form consists of oval budding
cells approximately 3-5 micrometers in diameter, the predominant form
during commensal growth and bloodstream dissemination through the
host.

The hyphal form comprises elongated, tube-like filaments that
facilitate tissue invasion and escape from phagocytic cells. The
pseudohyphal form consists of chains of elongated cells with
constrictions at septal junctions, representing an intermediate
morphology between yeast and true hyphae.

Chlamydospores are large, thick-walled resting structures produced
under certain environmental conditions. The yeast-to-hypha transition
is a critical virulence factor triggered by temperature (37 degrees
C), pH, serum, and nutrient availability. This morphological
plasticity enables adaptation to diverse host niches during both
commensal and pathogenic growth phases.

## Quorum Sensing

C. albicans possesses a sophisticated quorum-sensing system that
regulates morphological transitions and population density-dependent
behaviors, allowing the fungus to coordinate its behavior across a
population of cells within the host.

- **Tyrosol:** A quorum-sensing molecule accumulating at high cell density that promotes filamentous growth, acting as an autoinducer stimulating germ tube formation
- **Farnesol:** An extracellular quorum-sensing molecule produced at high population densities that inhibits hyphal formation, maintaining the yeast form
- **Chk1p:** A two-component signal transduction protein regulating quorum sensing in C. albicans

The interplay between tyrosol and farnesol allows coordination of
morphological state with population density, a key adaptation for the
commensal-pathogen lifestyle. Release from quorum-sensing molecule
suppression triggers hyphal formation during resumption of growth from
stationary phase.

## Cell Cycle and Chlamydospores

The cell cycle dynamics and quorum sensing of C. albicans
chlamydospores are distinct from those governing budding yeast growth
and hyphal development. Chlamydospore formation is regulated
independently from the yeast-hypha transition, suggesting a separate
developmental program activated under specific environmental
conditions.

Chlamydospores are produced under conditions including nutrient
limitation and microaerophilic environments. Their biological
significance remains unclear, but they may represent a survival or
dispersal strategy during periods of environmental stress or host
immune pressure.

## Pathogenicity

C. albicans is a major human pathogen responsible for a spectrum of
diseases. Oral and genital candidiasis (thrush) are superficial
mucosal infections common in immunocompetent individuals. Cutaneous
candidiasis affects skin and nails in moist intertriginous areas.

Invasive candidiasis causes systemic infections with high mortality
(30-50%) in immunocompromised patients including neutropenic patients
and those in intensive care units. Candidemia is the fourth most
common nosocomial bloodstream infection in the United States,
representing a significant hospital-acquired infection burden.

The yeast-to-hyphal switch is central to pathogenicity: hyphae
facilitate tissue invasion through secreted aspartyl proteases and
lipases, and enable escape from macrophages by piercing phagocytic
cell membranes. The yeast form promotes dissemination through the
bloodstream to colonize distant organs.

## Genomics

C. albicans has a sequenced genome that has been the subject of
extensive comparative genomics studies. Its diploid genome lacks the
hex-1 gene found in Euascomycetes, consistent with its classification
outside this group. The species has a highly heterozygous genome that
has complicated genomic analyses.
