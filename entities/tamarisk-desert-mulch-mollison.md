## Tamarisk (Tamarix spp.)

## Description

Tamarisk is a genus of drought-adapted shrubs and small trees in the family Tamaricaceae, prized in [[permaculture]] as one of the most productive mulch-generating plants for arid environments. Mollison identifies tamarisk as "one of the best plants" for desert windbreaks and barrier plantings due to its extraordinary capacity to produce organic matter from minimal water input.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Tamaricaceae
- **Genus**: [[tamarix]]
- **Common names**: Tamarisk, salt cedar, athel pine (T. aphylla)
- **Growth form**: Shrub to small tree, 2-15 m tall
- **Habitat**: Arid and semi-arid regions, saline soils, wadi banks

## Key Facts

- Produces a very large amount of leaf litter mulch, making it one of the top mulch-producing species for desert permaculture systems.
- Can be propagated as live stakes: cut branches soaked in water for three days, then pushed directly into desert soil where they root and grow.
- Used extensively in Chinese desert reclamation programs, planted through rice mats alongside Australian acacias in large baskets of humus.
- Deep rooting system allows access to permanent water tables, enabling survival and rapid growth in extreme aridity.
- Accumulates salt in specialized glands on its leaves, excreting excess salinity — a trait that influences soil chemistry beneath the canopy.
- The Chinese afforestation strategy uses tamarisk as a primary species for stabilizing moving sand dunes in desert margins.
- Serves dual function as windbreak barrier and mulch source, combining erosion control with soil improvement.
- Provides critical shade in desert gardens, reducing evapotranspiration from understory plantings.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Tamarisk occupies a central role in Mollison's arid landscape strategies:

1. **Mulch production**: In deserts, mulch is essential to prevent rapid pH rise in soils receiving [[drip-irrigation]]. Without mulch, mineral soils become toxic to plants. Tamarisk generates the bulk organic matter needed to buffer soil chemistry through humic acid release.

2. **Windbreak establishment**: Planted on raised earth banks, tamarisk forms the backbone of desert windbreak systems that protect gardens, reduce evaporation, and deflect hot winds.

3. **Sand stabilization**: The Chinese model demonstrates that tamarisk planted through permeable matting into sand dunes arrests dune migration and creates conditions for permanent vegetative cover.

4. **Propagation simplicity**: The ability to establish from cuttings pushed directly into soil eliminates the need for nurseries, potting media, or transplanting — critical advantages in remote desert settlements.

5. **Rapid establishment**: Once planted, tamarisk grows quickly, providing mulch and wind protection within the first growing season.

## Ecological Considerations

In some regions (notably the American Southwest and Western Australia), certain Tamarix species have become invasive along riparian corridors, displacing native cottonwood-willow communities. This invasiveness is context-dependent: it thrives where natural flood regimes have been altered by dams and water extraction. In [[permaculture-design]], careful species selection and placement away from natural waterways is advised.

The Western Australian declaration of certain mesquite-tamarisk complexes as noxious reflects grazing industry priorities rather than ecological assessment — Mollison notes these plants actually stabilize and enrich desert landscapes while cattle degrade them.

## Cultivation Notes

- **Planting**: Live stakes, 3-day water soak, direct push into soil
- **Spacing**: Dense hedgerows on earth banks for windbreak; scattered for mulch production
- **Water requirements**: Minimal once established; benefits from wadi proximity
- **Associations**: Plant with casuarinas and desert pines for multi-species windbreak
- **Maintenance**: Coppice or pollard for continuous mulch production
