## Overview

Rhus copallinum, commonly known as winged sumac, shining sumac, or flameleaf sumac, is a deciduous shrub in the family Anacardiaceae. It is native to [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]], occurring from Ontario south to Florida and west to Texas.

The species is named for the winged rachis between its leaflets, a distinctive morphological feature that separates it from other [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] sumacs. It is valued for its brilliant scarlet autumn foliage and persistent ornamental fruit clusters.

Winged sumac forms extensive thickets through clonal spreading, making it effective [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]] on slopes and disturbed sites. It is an important species in early-successional plant communities across the southeastern United States.

The species spreads primarily through rhizomatous root systems that can extend several meters from the parent plant. A single individual may produce a thicket covering dozens of square meters over time.

Despite its spreading habit, winged sumac is not considered invasive. It is easily controlled by mowing or cutting and does not form monocultures that exclude other native vegetation.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Rhus copallinum was described by the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The species epithet "copallinum" refers to the copal-like resin historically extracted from the bark.

The species epithet has been the subject of nomenclatural debate. Some authorities accept Rhus copallinum while others use Rhus copallina. The current consensus favors the neuter ending "copallinum."

The shrub typically grows 2 to 5 meters tall with an open, spreading crown. Young stems are densely pubescent, becoming glabrous and brown with age.

Leaves are pinnately compound with 9 to 21 leaflets, each 4 to 8 centimeters long. The rachis between leaflets is conspicuously winged, a key diagnostic character.

Leaflets are lanceolate to elliptic with serrate margins and dark green upper surfaces. Fall color ranges from orange to brilliant scarlet, often persisting for several weeks.

Panicles of greenish-yellow flowers appear in midsummer, typically from June through August. Each panicle is 10 to 20 centimeters long and densely flowered.

Plants are dioecious, with male and female flowers borne on separate individuals. Female plants produce the showy fruit clusters, so both sexes are needed for fruit production in cultivated settings.

The fruit is a small, spherical drupe 3 to 5 millimeters in diameter, covered with short glandular hairs. Ripe fruits are deep red to crimson and persist on the plant through winter.

## Distribution and Habitat

Rhus copallinum is widely distributed across eastern North America. Its range extends from southern Ontario and New York south through the [[bolete-fungi-appalachian-region]] to Florida, and west to eastern Texas and Oklahoma.

The species is most abundant in the southeastern United States, where it occurs in old fields, roadside thickets, woodland edges, and dry upland forests. It is less common in the northeastern portion of its range.

Winged sumac prefers well-drained, acidic to neutral soils. It tolerates poor, rocky soils and shallow depths, making it common on ridges and hillsides.

The species is intolerant of deep shade and is typically found in full sun to partial shade. It colonizes disturbed sites rapidly and is one of the first woody species to establish on abandoned agricultural land.

USDA hardiness ranges from Zone 3 to Zone 9, reflecting its broad latitudinal distribution. Southern populations are evergreen in mild winters.

The species is fire-adapted and resprouts vigorously from the root crown after top-kill by wildfire. This trait contributes to its abundance in fire-prone ecosystems of the southeastern coastal plain.

## Cultivation and Uses

Rhus copallinum is cultivated as an [[hamilton-ornamental-shrub-care]] for its fall color and architectural form. It is planted in naturalistic landscapes, highway plantings, and native gardens.

The species requires minimal maintenance once established. It tolerates drought, poor soils, and urban conditions, making it a low-input landscape choice.

Sumac-ade, a tart beverage, is made by steeping the ripe fruit clusters in cold water. The drink has a flavor similar to lemonade and is rich in vitamin C and antioxidants.

[[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] peoples used the fruit for food, medicine, and dye production. The Cherokee used root bark tea as a remedy for dysentery and other digestive complaints.

The dried fruit clusters are sometimes used in decorative arrangements. Their persistent red color and structural form make them attractive through the winter months.

Tannins extracted from the leaves and bark have been used historically in leather tanning and dye production. The tannin content gives the foliage an astringent quality that discourages heavy browsing by livestock.

## Ecology and Wildlife Value

Rhus copallinum provides food and cover for a wide range of wildlife species across its range. The fruit is consumed by over 90 species of birds.

Game birds including wild turkey, ruffed grouse, bobwhite quail, and mourning dove rely on sumac fruit during fall and winter. The persistent fruit provides a critical food source when other berry supplies are depleted.

Mammalian frugivores including white-tailed deer, black bear, raccoon, and opossum consume the fruit. Deer also browse the foliage, though palatability is moderate compared to other forage.

The flowers attract a diversity of pollinators, including native bees, butterflies, and syrphid flies. The species serves as a late-summer nectar source when many other woody plants have finished flowering.

Winged sumac thickets provide excellent cover for small mammals and ground-nesting birds. The dense, low canopy structure creates sheltered microhabitats within early-successional landscapes.

The species serves as a nurse plant for establishing tree seedlings in open fields. The partial shade and wind protection provided by sumac thickets improves survival rates of oak, hickory, and pine seedlings.
