# Carum carvi

## Overview

**Carum carvi**, commonly known as caraway, is a biennial herbaceous plant in
the family Apiaceae. It is cultivated primarily for its aromatic fruits,
commonly called caraway seeds, which are used as a spice and traditional
medicine.

Caraway is native to Europe, western Asia, and North Africa. It has been used
as a culinary spice for at least 5,000 years, with evidence of use in
Neolithic settlements across central Europe.

The species is one of the oldest cultivated spices in Europe. Its distinctive
warm, anise-like flavor derives from [[herb-growing-guide]] 40–70 cm tall in its second year. The
first year produces a rosette of finely divided, pinnate leaves arising from
a thick, fusiform taproot.

In the second year, an erect, branched flowering stem emerges. Leaves on the
stem are alternate, pinnately compound, with narrow, feathery leaflets 2–5
cm long. The foliage resembles that of carrots or dill.

Flowers are arranged in compound umbels 3–7 cm across. Individual florets
are small, white to pale pink, with five petals of unequal size. Flowering
occurs from May to July in the second year.

The fruit is a schizocarp (cremocarp) approximately 5–7 mm long, elliptical
and slightly curved. Each mericarp contains a [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of Europe, the Caucasus, Anatolia,
and parts of Central Asia. It has become naturalized in [[query-how-do-fungal-volatile-compounds-affect-plant-growth-and-other-microorganisms]] include carveol, dihydrocarvone,
perillaldehyde, and trans-anethole. The essential oil yield from dried
fruits ranges from 3–7% depending on cultivar and growing conditions.

Carvone exhibits documented antispasmodic activity on smooth muscle,
supporting the traditional use of caraway for digestive complaints. In
vitro studies demonstrate inhibition of intestinal cramping and reduction
of gastrointestinal transit time.
