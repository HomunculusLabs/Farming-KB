Scattered populations have been reported from the Pacific Northwest of North America, but the species is far less common west of the continental divide.

The species is characteristically found on the bark of deciduous trees, especially Acer, Quercus, Fraxinus, and Fagus. It shows a strong preference for smooth to moderately rough bark with moderate light levels.

Occasional records exist from coniferous bark and siliceous rock surfaces, but these are considered atypical substrate occurrences.

Lecanora chlarotera is moderately pollution-tolerant and persists in suburban and rural settings where more sensitive species have declined. It is less abundant in heavily industrialized urban cores.

The species has shown modest recovery in parts of Europe following reductions in sulfur dioxide emissions since the 1980s, though it remains less abundant than before the industrial period.

The lichen occurs from lowland to montane elevations, typically below 800 meters. It is most common in humid oceanic and suboceanic climates with moderate annual precipitation.

In continental climates with colder winters, Lecanora chlarotera is replaced by more cold-tolerant Lecanora species on similar substrates.

## Chemistry and Secondary Metabolites

Lecanora chlarotera contains atranorin and zeorin as its primary cortical and medullary compounds. The medulla gives K+ yellow and C- reactions, distinguishing it from several chemically similar Lecanora species.

The presence of atranorin in both cortex and medulla is typical of the Lecanora subfusca group. Thin-layer chromatography profiles are used to separate Lecanora chlarotera from morphologically overlapping taxa that produce additional secondary metabolites.

Usnic acid is absent, which helps differentiate Lecanora chlarotera from yellow-green Lecanora species in the L. subaurifera group.

The absence of usnic acid also means that Lecanora chlarotera does not contribute to the yellow staining commonly associated with other bark-inhabiting lichens in the Lecanoraceae.

## Ecology and Symbiosis

Lecanora chlarotera associates with Trebouxia photobionts, primarily Trebouxia arboricola and related lineages. This photobiont partnership is shared across many Lecanora species occupying similar bark niches.

The specificity of the mycobiont-photobiont pairing in Lecanora is moderate, with the same Trebouxia clades partnering across multiple Lecanora species in mixed bark communities.

The crustose growth form allows Lecanora chlarotera to establish on bark surfaces that foliose and fruticose lichens cannot colonize effectively. It contributes to the early successional stages of bark lichen communities.

As an early colonizer, Lecanora chlarotera modifies bark pH and moisture retention, creating conditions that facilitate the later establishment of more specialized crustose and foliose species.

The species provides microhabitat for bark-dwelling invertebrates and can serve as a substrate for the establishment of other crustose lichens. Its persistent thallus modifies bark surface chemistry over time.

The crustose growth strategy of Lecanora chlarotera allows it to persist on bark that experiences periodic shedding, as it can regenerate from surviving thallus fragments.
