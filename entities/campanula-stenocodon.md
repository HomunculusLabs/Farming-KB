## Overview

**Campanula stenocodon** Loret & Barrandon is a narrow endemic perennial in the family Campanulaceae. It is restricted to the southeastern French Alps, primarily in the departments of Hautes-Alpes and Alpes-de-Haute-Provence.

The species is characterized by its narrow, elongated bell-shaped flowers, which distinguish it from the broader-campanulate flowers of most Alpine Campanula species. The corolla is pale violet-blue, 20–30 mm long, and distinctly tubular.

Plants grow 10–25 cm tall, with a loose cespitose habit. Basal leaves are ovate, crenate, and persist through the growing season.

Campanula stenocodon is one of several French Alpine endemics with extremely restricted ranges. Its limited distribution makes it of conservation concern despite occurring in remote mountain habitats.

The species was described by Loret and Barrandon in 1876 from material collected near Gap in the Hautes-Alpes. It has remained poorly known outside French botanical circles.

It is occasionally confused with Campanula spicata but differs in its solitary or few-flowered inflorescence and narrower corolla. No cultivars or horticultural selections have been named.

The species exemplifies the high endemism of the southern French Alps. This region harbors over 150 narrow endemic plant taxa, many restricted to single mountain massifs.

Its closest relative appears to be Campanula macrorhiza, another narrow endemic from the nearby Cévennes. Both species share the elongated calyx lobes characteristic of section Megalocalyx.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Campanula stenocodon is placed in Campanula sect. Megalocalyx, a group of Alpine species with large calyx lobes. The section includes several other narrow endemics from the southern Alps.

The calyx is distinctive, with long, narrow lobes that are nearly as long as the corolla tube. This character is diagnostic and separates it from all sympatric Campanula species.

Basal leaves are ovate to subcordate, 2–5 cm long, crenate-dentate, and sparsely pilose. Stem leaves are smaller, lanceolate, and sessile.

The flowering stem bears 1–3 flowers in a loose terminal cluster. The corolla is narrowly tubular-campanulate, pale violet-blue, 20–30 mm long, and 5–8 mm wide at the mouth.

The capsule is ovoid, ribbed, and dehisces by three small basal pores. Seeds are approximately 1 mm, ellipsoid, and pale brown with a finely reticulate surface.

Chromosome number has not been definitively reported but is expected to be 2n = 34 based on related species. The species shows no evidence of polyploidy.

## Distribution and Habitat

Campanula stenocodon is restricted to the Dauphiné and Provencal Alps of southeastern France. Its global range spans less than 200 km from north to south.

It occurs on limestone cliffs, scree, and rocky grasslands at 1200–2400 m elevation. The species is a calcicole, found exclusively on calcareous substrates.

Populations are small and scattered, often comprising fewer than 100 individuals. The species is locally common only at a few favored sites near Gap and Embrun.

Associated species include other limestone specialists such as Globularia cordifolia, Teucrium montanum, and Sesleria caerulea. These communities are characteristic of the southern French alpine limestone flora.

The species prefers warm, south-facing cliffs with some shade from adjacent rock. It avoids the coldest, north-facing exposures despite occurring at high altitude.

Microhabitat selection favors rock fissures with accumulated fine soil and moss. These sites retain sufficient moisture during the dry Mediterranean-influenced summers.

Geological mapping shows populations are concentrated on Urgonian limestone formations. These Cretaceous-era limestones produce the fissured cliff habitats the species prefers.

Annual precipitation in the species' range is 800–1200 mm, with a pronounced summer drought period. This Mediterranean influence distinguishes its habitat from the wetter northern Alps.

## Ecology

Flowering occurs from June to August, with peak bloom in July. The narrow tubular corolla suggests adaptation to long-tongued pollinators.

Primary pollinators are likely bumblebees (Bombus hortorum and related long-tongued species). The narrow corolla tube excludes short-tongued visitors.

Seed production is modest, with each capsule containing 20–40 seeds. Germination in the wild is likely limited by summer drought.

Plants are long-lived perennials, with individuals estimated to persist 10–20 years. They spread slowly through basal branching.

The species shows no tendency toward vegetative reproduction. All population growth depends on seedling establishment.

Drought tolerance is moderate but the species is sensitive to prolonged summer desiccation. Its restriction to fissures and mossy crevices reflects this moisture requirement.

## Cultivation

Campanula stenocodon is very rare in cultivation, grown primarily in a few European alpine collections. It requires full sun, excellent drainage, and alkaline soil.

Propagation is by seed, though germination is unreliable without cold stratification. Sow in autumn in a cold frame for best results.

The species is hardy to approximately USDA Zone 5 but requires protection from winter wet. It is unsuitable for humid maritime climates.

No commercial sources are known. Plants must be obtained from seed exchanges or specialist alpine collections.

Given its extreme rarity in cultivation, it should be prioritized for ex situ conservation. Botanic gardens in the French Alps region are the most logical custodians.

## Conservation

Campanula stenocodon is listed as "Vulnerable" (VU) on the French national Red List. Its small global range and low population sizes make it inherently susceptible to threats.

Climate change represents the most significant long-term threat. Warming temperatures may allow competitive lowland species to colonize its high-altitude habitats.

The species occurs within the Ecrins National Park and several regional nature reserves. Legal protection in France prohibits collection from wild populations.

A conservation action plan was initiated by the Conservatoire Botanique National Alpin in 2018. This includes population monitoring and seed banking of representative populations.

Genetic analysis of six populations showed moderate differentiation, suggesting limited gene flow between sites. Conservation efforts should prioritize maintaining connectivity between the largest populations.
