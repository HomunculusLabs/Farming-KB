## Deep-Organic Farming

## Description

Deep-organic farming is a philosophical and practical framework coined by Eliot Coleman to distinguish between organic growers who work to understand and mimic natural biological systems (deep) versus those who merely substitute organic inputs for chemical ones without changing their underlying approach (shallow). The concept borrows terminology from the ecology movement"s "deep ecology" versus "shallow ecology" distinction.

## Classification

- **Category**: Concept / Farming philosophy
- **Domain**: Organic agriculture, soil science, pest management
- **Contrasted with**: Shallow-organic farming

## Key Facts

- Deep-organic farmers reject agricultural chemicals AND seek better ways to farm through understanding natural systems
- Core practices: freely available natural soil foods from deep-rooting legumes, green manures, and composts
- Pest philosophy: the underlying cause of pest problems is plant stress, not the pest itself
- Management focuses on: soil tilth, nutrient balance, organic-matter content, water drainage, airflow, crop rotations, varietal selection
- Deep-organic farmers free themselves from dependence on the industrial supply network
- Shallow-organic farming mimics chemical agriculture patterns using organic inputs -- still dependent on purchased products
- The difference is fundamentally a "difference in life views" about whether natural systems are adequate or need constant correction

## Principles

1. Cause correction over symptom treatment: Pests and diseases indicate plant stress from suboptimal growing conditions
2. Feed the soil creatures: Add organic matter to nurture soil organisms, which in turn feed plants
3. Learn from the plant: A plant bothered by pests is communicating that soil conditions need improvement
4. Nature makes sense: If something is not working, the farmer has made the error, not Nature
5. Reject palliatives: Both chemical and "organic" sprays merely mask symptoms without addressing causes

## Relevance to Cultivation

This framework is central to understanding Coleman"s entire approach to farming and is directly applicable to all cultivation systems. For any grower the deep-organic approach means investing in soil biology and environmental conditions rather than purchasing pest control products. It requires more observation, knowledge, and patience, but produces more resilient, self-sustaining growing systems with lower input costs and higher nutritional quality.

## Source Context

Defined and elaborated across Eliot Coleman's "The Winter Harvest Handbook," particularly in the chapters on Insects and Diseases and Deep-Organic Farming and the Small Farm.
