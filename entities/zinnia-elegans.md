# Zinnia elegans (*Zinnia elegans*)

## Overview

Zinnia elegans, commonly known as the common zinnia or elegant zinnia, is an
annual herbaceous plant in the family Asteraceae. Native to Mexico, it is one
of the most commercially important ornamental annuals grown worldwide, valued
for its vibrant, long-lasting flowers and exceptional heat tolerance.

The species has been extensively hybridized, producing hundreds of cultivars in
nearly every color except true blue. Flower forms range from simple single
daisies to fully double, cactus-flowered, and dahlia-flowered types. Zinnia
elegans blooms continuously from early summer until frost, making it a reliable
source of garden color and a major cut flower crop.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes, Angiosperms, Eudicots, Asterids
- **Order:** Asterales
- **Family:** Asteraceae
- **Tribe:** Heliantheae
- **Genus:** Zinnia
- **Species:** Z. elegans

The species was first described by Jacquin in 1792 and later reclassified by
de Candolle. The genus name honors Johann Gottfried Zinn, an 18th-century
German anatomist and botanist. The specific epithet elegans means "elegant,"
referring to the showy inflorescences.

Zinnia elegans is a diploid species with 2n = 24 chromosomes. Molecular
phylogenetic analyses place the genus Zinnia within Heliantheae, closely
related to Heliopsis and Sanvitalia. Approximately 20 species comprise the
genus, distributed from the southwestern United States to South America.

## Morphology

### Growth Habit

Zinnia elegans is an erect, branching annual herb growing 20–100 cm tall
depending on cultivar. The stems are stout, green to purplish, and typically
glabrous or sparsely hairy. The habit ranges from compact and bushy in dwarf
cultivars to tall and open in large-flowered types.

### Leaves

Leaves are opposite, ovate to lanceolate, 3–10 cm long and 1–4 cm wide, with
entire to slightly dentate margins. The leaf surface is rough to the touch due
to short, stiff trichomes, a trait that deters some herbivores. Leaf color is
medium to dark green, occasionally with a purplish tinge in certain cultivars.

### Flowers

Capitula are solitary at stem terminals, 4–15 cm in diameter depending on
cultivar. Wild-type flowers have 8–12 ray florets surrounding a prominent
central disc of yellow tubular florets. Cultivated double forms have ray
florets that are transformed into petaloid structures, sometimes completely
obscuring the disc.

Ray floret colors span white, yellow, orange, salmon, pink, red, crimson,
lavender, and purple. Some cultivars exhibit bicolor or striped patterns.
Individual flowers persist 7–14 days on the plant. The involucral bracts are
broadly ovate, 8–15 mm long, and united at the base.

### Fruit and Seed

Achenes are narrow, 7–12 mm long, compressed, and crowned with a persistent
pappus of two unequal awns. Seeds are brown to gray and weigh 3–5 mg each.
A single plant produces 500–3,000 seeds. Germination occurs in 5–7 days at
21–26°C, with seed viability lasting 3–5 years.

## Distribution and Habitat

Zinnia elegans is native to Mexico, occurring from the states of Nuevo León
and Tamaulipas in the northeast to Guerrero and Oaxaca in the south. It grows
naturally in open grasslands, scrubby hillsides, and disturbed areas at
elevations of 100–2,000 meters.

The species is cultivated globally and has naturalized sporadically in warm
temperate and subtropical regions, including the southern United States,
Central America, parts of Africa, and South Asia. Naturalized populations
typically persist in disturbed habitats, roadsides, and abandoned fields.

It requires full sun and warm conditions, performing poorly in cool, wet
climates or partial shade. Well-drained, moderately fertile soils produce the
best results. The species is notably sensitive to powdery mildew (Golovinomyces
cichoracearum), especially in humid conditions with poor air circulation.

## Ecology

Zinnia elegans is a valuable nectar resource for butterflies, bees, and
hoverflies. The flat flower heads provide landing platforms for diverse
pollinators, and the nectar production is relatively high compared to other
ornamental annuals. Monarch butterflies (Danaus plexippus) are particularly
attracted to the flowers during migration.

Syrphid flies are frequent visitors and important biological control agents
in gardens where zinnias are grown alongside vegetable crops. The rough,
hairy foliage provides some resistance to deer browsing. In its native range,
specialist Lepidoptera including the sunflower moth (Homoeosoma electellum)
occasionally use zinnias as host plants.

Major diseases include powdery mildew, bacterial leaf spot (Xanthomonas
 campestris pv. zinniae), and Alternaria leaf spot. Good air circulation,
resistant cultivars, and avoiding overhead irrigation are the primary
management strategies.

## Cultivation and History

Zinnia elegans is direct-seeded after the last frost, germinating in 5–7
days at 21–26°C. It flowers approximately 60–75 days from sowing. Thin
seedlings to 15–30 cm spacing depending on cultivar size. The species responds
well to moderate fertility but excess nitrogen produces excessive foliage at
the expense of flowers.

Zinnias are among the most popular annual bedding plants worldwide, widely
used in mass plantings, cutting gardens, containers, and pollinator gardens.
The global cut flower trade includes significant zinnia production, especially
in India, where the species is extensively cultivated for garlands and
religious offerings. The genus was introduced to Europe in the late 18th
century and rapidly became a garden favorite.
