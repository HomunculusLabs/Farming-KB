## Overview

Swertia chirayita is an erect annual or biennial herb in the family Gentianaceae, renowned across South and [[childrens-herbal-medicine]] markets in India, Japan, and Europe. This has led to severe population declines across much of its range.

The species is listed as Critically Endangered in several Indian state Red Lists and is included in CITES Appendix II review. Cultivation efforts remain insufficient to meet commercial demand.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Swertia chirayita (Roxburgh ex Fleming) H. Karsten belongs to the genus Swertia within the family Gentianaceae. The basionym is Gentiana chirayita Roxburgh ex Fleming.

The species is an erect annual or occasionally biennial herb, growing 60–150 cm tall. The stem is solitary, branching in the upper portion, cylindrical, and greenish-purple with characteristic purple stripes.

Leaves are opposite, sessile or subsessile, lanceolate to ovate-lanceolate, 3–10 cm long and 1–3 cm wide. They are entire-margined with three to five prominent parallel veins and a conspicuously acuminate apex.

Inflorescences are large, lax, paniculate cymes occupying the upper half of the plant. Individual flowers are small, 8–12 mm in diameter, tetramerous, with greenish-yellow corolla lobes.

Each corolla lobe bears two prominent, fringed nectary glands (fascicles) — a diagnostic character of the genus Swertia. These glands are conspicuously bordered by stiff, hair-like fimbriae.

Flowering occurs from August to October. The calyx is deeply four-lobed, with lobes equal to or slightly shorter than the corolla tube.

Capsules are ovoid to ellipsoid, 6–8 mm long, dehiscing loculicidally into two valves. Seeds are numerous, minute, angular, and brown with a reticulate seed coat surface.

The entire plant has an intensely bitter taste due to high concentrations of amarogentin and other secoiridoid glycosides. Modern pharmacology has confirmed antimalarial activity, with swerchirin showing activity against Plasmodium falciparum strains in vitro. This supports the traditional use for malarial fever in Himalayan regions.

Hepatoprotective effects are well-documented, with whole-plant extracts reducing serum transaminases and improving liver histopathology in animal models. Mangiferin is the primary hepatoprotective constituent.
