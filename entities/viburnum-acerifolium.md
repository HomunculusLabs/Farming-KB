The species typically grows 1.5 to 2 meters tall and forms loose, open thickets
in the understory of deciduous forests. It is valued in native plant landscaping
for its fall foliage color and wildlife appeal.

Mapleleaf viburnum produces flat-topped clusters of white flowers in late spring,
followed by showy dark-blue to black drupes in late summer and fall.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Viburnum acerifolium was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 and placed in the
family Caprifoliaceae. The specific epithet refers to its maple-like foliage.

The leaves are opposite, simple, and three-lobed, measuring 5 to 12 centimeters
long and nearly as wide. Leaf margins are coarsely toothed, and the upper surface
is dark green while the lower surface is paler with fine hairs along the veins.

Fall foliage color is exceptionally showy, ranging from pink to rose-red and
purple. This autumn display is among the most reliable of any native eastern
shrub.

Flowers are arranged in flat-topped cymes 3 to 8 centimeters across, with each
individual flower measuring about 5 millimeters in diameter. The five petals are
white and the stamens extend well beyond the corolla.

Fruit is a drupe approximately 6 to 8 millimeters in diameter, ripening from red
to blue-black. Each drupe contains a single flattened seed.

The bark is thin, grayish-brown, and develops a slightly warty texture with age.

## Distribution and Habitat

Viburnum acerifolium ranges from southern Maine and Quebec west to Wisconsin
and Iowa, and south to northern Georgia and eastern Texas. It is most abundant
in the [[query-how-to-attract-native-bees-and-beneficial-insects]],
syrphid flies, butterflies, and moths. The extended bloom period of roughly two
weeks provides an important nectar source in late spring forest understories.

The fruit is consumed by numerous bird species, including eastern bluebirds,
robins, cedar waxwings, wood thrushes, and ruffed grouse. Mammals such as
white-tailed deer, foxes, raccoons, and squirrels also feed on the drupes.

Several species of Lepidoptera use Viburnum acerifolium as a larval host plant,
including the spring azure butterfly. The foliage provides browse for deer,
though it is not considered a preferred forage species.

The shrub provides cover and nesting habitat for songbirds and small mammals in
the forest understory. Its rhizomatous growth habit helps stabilize soil on
slopes and disturbed sites.
