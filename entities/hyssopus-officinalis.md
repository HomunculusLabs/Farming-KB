Hyssop has a long history in traditional European herbalism, referenced in the Bible, the writings of Dioscorides, and the medieval monastic pharmacopoeias. The genus name derives from the Hebrew "ezob," though the biblical hyssop was likely a different species (Majorana syriaca).

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Magnoliophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Lamiales
- **Family:** Lamiaceae
- **Genus:** Hyssopus
- **Species:** H. officinalis L.
- **Authority:** Linnaeus (1753)

Hyssop is a compact, bushy subshrub growing 30–60 cm tall and 30–45 cm wide. The stems are square (characteristic of Lamiaceae), woody at the base, and bear numerous ascending branches. The habit is upright and tidy, forming a rounded mound.

Leaves are opposite, lanceolate to linear, 1–3 cm long and 4–8 mm wide, dark green above, greyish-green beneath due to fine pubescence. The leaf margins are entire or slightly toothed near the tip. When crushed, the foliage releases a strong camphoraceous-minty aroma.

Flowers are borne in whorled spikes at stem terminals, each spike 3–8 cm long. Individual flowers are bilabiate, 8–12 mm long, typically blue-violet but with cultivars in pink, white, and deep blue. Flowering occurs from mid-summer to early autumn (July to September).

The fruit consists of four small nutlets (mericarps), each approximately 2 mm long, dark brown when mature. The root system is fibrous and relatively shallow.

## Distribution and Habitat

*H. officinalis* is native to the Mediterranean region, extending from southern Europe (Spain, Italy, Greece) through the Balkans and Turkey to central Asia (Iran, Afghanistan, Pakistan). It has naturalized in parts [[staphylococcus-aureus]]*, *Escherichia coli*, and *[[cannabis-for-inflammatory-conditions]] of the upper respiratory tract.

## Cultivation

Hyssop is easily propagated by seed, cuttings, or division. Seeds require light for germination and should be surface-sown in spring at 15–20°C, germinating in 14–21 days. Softwood cuttings taken in early summer root readily under mist.

Plants should be spaced 30–45 cm apart in well-drained soil. Hyssop is drought-tolerant once established and requires minimal irrigation. Overwatering is the most common cause of plant failure, promoting root rot in poorly drained soils.

Pruning after flowering maintains a compact shape and encourages a second flush of blooms. Plants should be divided every 3–4 years to maintain vigor. Hyssop is relatively pest-free; its aromatic oils deter most insect herbivores, though it can be affected by [[cervantes-spider-mites-identification-control]] under dry conditions.

## Culinary and Traditional Use

Hyssop leaves and flowers have a strong, slightly bitter, minty-camphor flavor. The herb is used sparingly in European cuisine — in soups, stews, salads, and stuffings. It pairs well with fatty meats, robust cheeses, and legumes.
