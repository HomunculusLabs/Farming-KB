## Venturia

## Description

Venturia is a genus of ascomycete fungi that are significant plant pathogens, most notably causing apple scab (Venturia inaequalis) and pear scab (Venturia pirina). The genus is of major concern in [[fruit-tree-care-and-orchard-management]] and is one of the fungal diseases that Elaine Ingham discusses as being preventable through properly made [[actively-aerated-compost-tea-aact-brewing]] tea (AACT).

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Division**: Ascomycota
- **Class**: Dothideomycetes
- **Order**: Pleosporales
- **Family**: Venturiaceae
- **Genus**: Venturia

## Key Facts

- Venturia inaequalis is the causal agent of apple scab, one of the most economically important diseases of apples worldwide
- The fungus overwinters in fallen leaves on the orchard floor, producing ascospores in spring that initiate primary infections
- Infection occurs during wet conditions when ascospores or conidia germinate on leaf and fruit surfaces
- According to Elaine Ingham's research, compost tea with adequate fungal biomass can prevent Venturia from establishing on leaf surfaces
- The mechanism of suppression is competitive exclusion — beneficial organisms occupy infection sites before Venturia spores can germinate
- Proper orchard floor management, including spraying compost tea on fallen leaves in autumn, can reduce overwintering inoculum

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Disease Suppression via Compost Tea
- If Venturia is escaping and causing disease despite compost tea applications, it indicates insufficient coverage with the right beneficial organisms
- A well-made compost tea with the full food web needed does not allow Venturia to grow on leaf surfaces
- Both [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-bacterial-and-fungal-dominant-soil]] components in the tea are needed for effective suppression
- Fungi must constitute at least 5% of a minimum 70% total organism coverage on leaf surfaces

### Orchard Management Protocol
- Spray the orchard floor and downed leaves in the fall to break the disease cycle
- Apply [[ingham-foliar-compost-tea-application]] at least two weeks before bud break
- Continue applications at 10-14 day intervals depending on disease severity
- Soil drenches in fall and spring complement foliar applications

### Integrated Approach
- Compost tea should be viewed as part of a biological management system, not as a pesticide replacement
- Proper [[aact-compost-quality-standards-ingham]] is essential — the organisms must be present in the compost to transfer to the tea
- Monitoring beneficial organism levels through microscopy helps ensure adequate coverage
