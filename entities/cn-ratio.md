## Description

The Carbon-to-Nitrogen ratio (C:N ratio) is the fundamental measurement of the relative proportions of carbon and nitrogen in organic materials. It is the single most important parameter in composting, mulching, and soil organic matter management. The C:N ratio determines whether microbial decomposition proceeds efficiently, whether nitrogen is released or immobilized, and whether the resulting compost or soil environment favors fungal or bacterial dominance. Understanding and manipulating C:N ratios is essential for producing targeted compost and managing soil food webs.

## Classification

- **Type**: Chemical ratio / Soil science parameter
- **Units**: Mass ratio (dimensionless)
- **Application**: Composting, mulching, soil management, [[substrate-preparation]]

## Key Facts

### Optimal Composting Ratio
- The **ideal C:N ratio for composting is 25:1 to 30:1** — this provides sufficient carbon for microbial energy and nitrogen for enzyme and protein synthesis.
- At this ratio, decomposition proceeds rapidly and completely.
- **Too much carbon** (high C:N): Nitrogen is quickly consumed by microbes, decay slows dramatically, and nitrogen becomes **immobilized** (tied up in microbial biomass, unavailable to plants).
- **Too much nitrogen** (low C:N): Organisms consume available nitrogen rapidly, excess carbon is vented as CO₂ or washed away with water, and nitrogen may be lost as ammonia gas.

### C:N Ratios of Common Materials

| Material | C:N Ratio |
|----------|-----------|
| Sawdust | ~500:1 |
| Paper | ~170:1 |
| Tree leaves | 40:1 – 80:1 |
| Wood chips | ~400:1 |
| Grass clippings | ~19:1 |
| [[alfalfa-meal]] | ~12:1 |
| Kitchen scraps | ~15:1 |
| Straw | ~80:1 |

### Brown vs. Green Materials
- **Brown materials** (aged, dry): Leaves, bark, wood chips, twigs, branches — high carbon, support **fungal dominance**.
- **Green materials** (fresh, moist): Grass clippings, fresh weeds, kitchen scraps — high nitrogen, support **bacterial dominance**.
- **Mixed grass clippings (19:1) + tree leaves (40–80:1)** produce near-optimal composting ratios when combined.

### Manipulating Compost Biology via C:N
- **Fungal compost recipe**: 5–10% alfalfa meal, 45–50% fresh grass clippings, 40–50% brown leaves or small wood chips.
- **Bacterial compost recipe**: 25% alfalfa meal, 50% green grass clippings, 25% brown leaves or bark.
- Higher carbon content → more fungal activity → lower pH (5.5–7.0)
- Higher nitrogen content → more bacterial activity → higher pH (7.0–7.5)

### Nitrogen Immobilization ("Nitrogen Robbing")
- When mulch or amendment with C:N ratio > 30:1 is applied to soil, decomposing microbes consume all available nitrogen in the material and then **borrow nitrogen from surrounding soil**.
- This nitrogen immobilization primarily occurs at the thin interface between mulch and soil surface.
- Usually does not affect the rhizosphere (root zone) deeper in the soil.
- Risk can be reduced by using wood chips ≥⅜ inch or larger, which limits bacterial colonization.
- Mixing a nitrogen source (green grass, alfalfa meal) with high-carbon mulches prevents this problem.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

1. **Compost formulation**: The C:N ratio is the primary lever for producing compost with the desired microbial dominance (fungal for perennials and woody plants; bacterial for annuals and vegetables).
2. **Substrate preparation**: In mushroom cultivation, C:N ratio of substrates directly affects mycelial colonization speed and fruiting performance.
3. **Mulch management**: Understanding C:N ratios prevents nitrogen immobilization under mulch layers and allows targeted fungal or bacterial dominance.
4. **Soil fertility**: Soil organic matter with appropriate C:N ratios ensures steady nutrient release; imbalanced ratios lead to either nitrogen lock-up or nitrogen loss.
5. **Thermophilic composting**: Proper C:N ratio ensures rapid heating to 135°F (57°C) within 24–72 hours, critical for pathogen and weed seed destruction.
6. **Vermicompost**: Lower C:N feedstocks produce faster worm processing; high-carbon materials must be pre-processed.

## Practical Application

- **Monitor**: A pile that won't heat up likely has too much carbon — add green, nitrogen-rich material.
- **Adjust**: Add newspaper, fruit pulps, or commercial compost inoculants to jumpstart slow piles.
- **Test**: Finished compost should have a C:N ratio of approximately 15:1–20:1.
