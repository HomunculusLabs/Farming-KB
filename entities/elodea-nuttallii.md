# Elodea nuttallii

## Overview

Elodea nuttallii is a submerged freshwater aquatic plant in the family Hydrocharitaceae. It is commonly known as Nuttall's waterweed or narrow-leaved waterweed. The species is native to North America and has become widely naturalized in Europe and parts of Asia as an invasive aquatic plant.

It is distinguished from the closely related Elodea canadensis by its narrower leaves and more elongated leaf tips. E. nuttallii has become the dominant Elodea species in many European waterways since its introduction in the mid-20th century.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Elodea nuttallii was described by J.T. Planchon in 1848. The specific epithet honors the English botanist and entomologist Thomas Nuttall, who extensively surveyed North American flora in the early 19th century. It belongs to the order Alismatales, family Hydrocharitaceae.

The genus Elodea contains approximately 12 species distributed primarily in the Americas. E. nuttallii was long confused with E. canadensis in European botanical records. The two species were only reliably distinguished in European herbaria beginning in the 1960s.

Stems are slender, brittle, and heavily branched, typically reaching lengths of 60–100 cm. They root at the base in substrate but can also survive as free-floating fragments.

Leaves are linear-lanceolate, 6–15 mm long and 1–3 mm wide. They are arranged in whorls of three along the stem, with conspicuous serrations toward the leaf tip. The leaves are sessile and lack a differentiated petiole.

Leaf morphology varies with water conditions. Plants in deeper or turbid water produce longer, narrower leaves compared to those in shallow, clear water. This phenotypic plasticity is a key adaptation to variable light environments.

The leaf margin is finely serrate, visible under magnification. Each leaf has a single midvein with no lateral venation. Leaf tissue is thin and translucent, typically 1–2 cell layers thick.

Root systems are relatively sparse compared to other submerged macrophytes. Adventitious roots arise from stem nodes at irregular intervals. Root development is more prolific in nutrient-rich substrates.

Flowers are unisexual and arise from a tubular spathe at leaf axils. Male flowers detach and float to the water surface; female flowers have a long slender pedicel that draws them to the surface.

In Europe, E. nuttallii reproduces almost exclusively vegetatively through stem fragmentation. Sexual reproduction is rarely observed outside its native North American range. A single stem fragment with a single node can generate an entirely new plant.

Vegetative propagation rates are exceptionally high during the growing season from May through September. Fragment dispersal occurs through water currents, boat traffic, and animal movement. This efficient dispersal mechanism underlies the species' invasive success in European waters.

## Distribution and Habitat

Elodea nuttallii is native to North America, ranging from southern Canada through much of the United States. It occurs across the Great Plains, the Mississippi River basin, and Atlantic coastal states. It was first recorded in Europe in 1939 in Belgium and has since spread to most of the continent.

The species is now established across the British Isles, western and central Europe, Scandinavia, and parts of eastern Europe. It has also been reported in Japan and New Zealand. Its spread has been facilitated by the international aquarium and water garden trade.

It inhabits slow-moving freshwater environments including ponds, lakes, canals, ditches, and slow-flowing rivers. It tolerates a wide range of nutrient conditions, from oligotrophic to eutrophic waters. Populations are most vigorous in moderately enriched water bodies.

E. nuttallii grows at depths from 0.5 to 7 meters. It prefers fine-grained substrates but can root in gravel, clay, or organic mud. In very shallow water it may extend above the surface.

The species is tolerant of moderate water pollution and can colonize disturbed aquatic habitats rapidly. It is often among the first submerged macrophytes to establish in newly created or restored water bodies. Seasonal dieback occurs in temperate regions during winter months.

## Ecology and Interactions

Elodea nuttallii provides important habitat structure for aquatic invertebrates, juvenile fish, and amphibians. Dense beds offer shelter from predators and spawning substrate for several fish species.

The species is a significant oxygen producer through photosynthesis. However, dense monocultures can deplete dissolved oxygen at night through respiration, particularly during summer algal conditions.

E. nuttallii competes aggressively with other submerged macrophytes for light and nutrients. Studies show it can outcompete the earlier-invading E. canadensis through higher photosynthetic efficiency and faster nutrient uptake.

It has been observed to displace native macrophyte communities in European waterways. This displacement reduces plant diversity and can alter invertebrate community composition.

Competitive displacement follows a predictable pattern in European waters. E. canadensis typically colonizes first, followed by E. nuttallii which gradually replaces it through more efficient resource capture. Both species may eventually coexist at reduced densities in mixed stands.

Waterfowl including mallards, coots, and swans feed on E. nuttallii shoots and roots. The plant also serves as a substrate for periphyton and biofilm growth.

Several species of aquatic snails graze on E. nuttallii epiphytes and leaf tissue. These grazing interactions can reduce excessive periphyton accumulation that would otherwise limit photosynthesis.

Macroinvertebrate diversity is generally higher in E. nuttallii beds than in unvegetated substrate. The plant provides attachment surfaces, refuge from predation, and a food source for detritivores.

## Aquarium and Horticultural Use

Elodea nuttallii is widely cultivated in the freshwater aquarium trade. Its fast growth rate and tolerance of varied conditions make it a popular choice for coldwater and tropical aquaria alike.

In aquaria, it serves as an effective oxygenator and nutrient sink. Dense plantings help control algal growth by competing for dissolved nutrients and light.

The species is frequently used in planted aquarium layouts as a background or midground plant. Its branching habit and translucent green foliage provide good visual contrast with broader-leaved species.

E. nuttallii is sometimes employed in classroom and laboratory settings for photosynthesis experiments. Its rapid oxygen production under light is easily observed, making it a standard teaching specimen.

E. nuttallii is readily available from aquarium retailers and online suppliers worldwide. Care should be taken to dispose of aquarium waste water properly to prevent introduction into natural waterways.

Temperature tolerance ranges from approximately 5°C to 30°C. Optimal growth occurs between 15°C and 25°C in moderate to high light conditions. The species does not require supplemental CO₂ injection in aquaria.

## Invasive Potential and Management

Elodea nuttallii is classified as an invasive species in several European countries. Its rapid vegetative spread and tolerance of varied conditions make it difficult to control once established.

Mechanical cutting is the most common management approach but can inadvertently spread fragments downstream. Cutting must be repeated annually as plants regenerate quickly from remaining stem sections.

Herbicide treatments using fluridone or diquat have shown limited effectiveness against E. nuttallii. Biological control methods are not currently available for this species. Integrated management combining mechanical, chemical, and preventive approaches is recommended for best results.

Water level manipulation has been explored as a control technique in managed waterways. Drawing down water levels can expose and desiccate rooted plants, though fragments may survive and re-establish.

Prevention of spread through cleaning of boats, fishing equipment, and aquatic plant trade is considered the most effective long-term strategy. Educational programs targeting waterway users have been implemented in several countries.

Despite its invasive status, E. nuttallii does provide some ecosystem services. It contributes to water clarity through nutrient uptake and can help reduce phytoplankton blooms through light competition.

The species has been studied as a potential phytoremediation agent for nutrient-rich waterways. Its high uptake rates for nitrogen and phosphorus make it effective at reducing dissolved nutrient concentrations. Harvested biomass can potentially be composted or used as green manure.
