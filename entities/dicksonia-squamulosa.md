## Overview

**Dicksonia squamulosa** (Brause) Holttum is a tree fern species in the family Dicksoniaceae, native to the montane tropical forests of New Guinea. The species epithet refers to the distinctive scaly (squamulose) covering on the stipe and rachis that distinguishes it from other Dicksonia species in the region.

This species is part of the diverse pteridophyte flora of New Guinea, which hosts one of the richest fern assemblages in the world with over 3,000 species and varieties. Dicksonia squamulosa occupies the higher-elevation montane forests where tree ferns form a conspicuous component of the vegetation structure.

The species is poorly known in cultivation and has been collected only sporadically since its original description. Much of its basic biology, including population size and reproductive ecology, remains undocumented.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Division: Polypodiophyta
- Class: Polypodiopsida
- Order: Cyatheales
- Family: Dicksoniaceae
- Genus: *Dicksonia* L'Hér.
- Species: *D. squamulosa* (Brause) Holttum

**Basionym:** *Balantium squamulosum* Brause

The trunk is erect and relatively short for a tree fern, typically reaching 1–3 m in height and 5–8 cm in diameter. The crown bears a compact rosette of arching fronds 1.5–2.5 m long.

Stipes are dark brown to nearly black at the base, densely covered with prominent, glossy brown scales. These scales are broadly lanceolate, 5–12 mm long, and are the most distinctive feature separating this species from the co-occurring Dicksonia lanigera.

Fronds are tripinnate at the base, becoming bipinnate-pinnatifid toward the apex. The lamina texture is firm and leathery, dark green above and paler beneath. Pinnules are sessile, oblong to lanceolate, with crenate margins.

Sori are marginal, positioned at the tips of pinna lobes, enclosed in a bivalve indusium characteristic of the Dicksoniaceae. The outer indusium valve is formed from the reflexed lamina margin, while the inner valve is a separate membranous structure.

Spores are tetrahedral-globose, trilete, and measure approximately 35–45 µm in diameter. The perispore surface is granulate with low, irregular ridges.

## Distribution and Habitat

Dicksonia squamulosa is endemic to the island of New Guinea, occurring in both Papua New Guinea and the Indonesian provinces of Papua and West Papua. Collections have been made from the Central Range, the Huon Peninsula, and several outlying mountain ranges.

The species inhabits montane rainforests at elevations of 1,500–2,800 m above sea level. It occurs in mossy montane forest, upper montane cloud forest, and the transition zone between lower montane and upper montane vegetation types.

At these elevations, cloud cover is near-constant and annual rainfall typically exceeds 3,000 mm. Temperatures are cool, with daily ranges of 8–18°C and rare frost occurrence at the highest sites.

The species grows on steep forest slopes, along stream banks, and on moist roadside embankments in montane areas. It is frequently found growing as an understory component beneath Nothofagus-dominated canopy at upper elevations.

Associated tree fern species include other Dicksonia taxa and members of Cyatheaceae. The fern flora at these elevations is exceptionally diverse, with 80–120 species commonly recorded in single-hectare plots.

## Ecology

Dicksonia squamulosa reproduces via wind-dispersed spores. Gametophyte development requires continuously moist, shaded conditions typical of the cloud-forest floor. Sporulation timing is poorly documented but likely occurs year-round in the perhumid montane environment.

Growth rates are likely slow, consistent with other montane Dicksonia species. The short trunk stature suggests that energy allocation favors frond production over vertical growth at these cool, cloudy sites.

The persistent stipe bases and fibrous trunk surface provide important substrate for epiphytic bryophytes and small ferns. In New Guinea montane forests, the epiphytic load on tree fern trunks can be substantial, supporting diverse communities of mosses, liverworts, and filmy ferns.

The species plays a role in montane forest hydrology by intercepting cloud moisture through its frond canopy and directing it to the forest floor via throughfall and stemflow. This fog-capture function is ecologically significant in cloud forests.

## Cultivation and Uses

Dicksonia squamulosa is essentially unknown in horticulture outside of a few specialist collections. Its montane habitat preferences suggest it would require cool, humid, shaded conditions in cultivation.

Based on related montane Dicksonia species, cultivation requirements would include consistently moist, humus-rich soil, protection from direct sun, and cool temperatures (10–20°C optimal). The species would likely be intolerant of heat and drought.

Propagation would follow standard Dicksonia spore-propagation techniques. No documented ethnobotanical uses are recorded for this species, though other New Guinea Dicksonia species have been used for construction materials and as emergency food.

The species has no known commercial applications. Its conservation status has not been formally assessed by the IUCN, but habitat loss through logging and agricultural conversion in montane New Guinea represents a potential threat to some populations.

## Conservation

Dicksonia squamulosa has not been assessed by the IUCN Red List. The species occurs across a broad elevational range in New Guinea but population density and total population size are poorly known.

Montane forests in New Guinea face increasing pressure from logging, agricultural expansion (particularly smallholder gardens), and infrastructure development. Climate change may shift the cloud-base altitude upward, reducing the extent of suitable habitat.

Several protected areas in Papua New Guinea and Indonesian Papua encompass montane forest habitat suitable for the species. These include Mount Wilhelm National Park, the Lorentz National Park World Heritage Site, and various wildlife management areas.

Further field surveys are needed to document the species' full distribution, population ecology, and conservation status. The remote and rugged terrain of New Guinea's montane interior means many areas remain botanically unexplored.
