## Overview

**Centaurea nigra** L., commonly known as common knapweed, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Asteraceae. It is native to Europe and western Asia, where it is a characteristic component of species-rich meadows and grasslands.

The species is highly valued as a nectar source for pollinators, particularly bees and butterflies. Its dark thistle-like flower heads bloom throughout summer, providing sustained forage in grassland habitats.

Common knapweed is widely used in wildflower seed mixes for meadow restoration and rewilding projects across northern Europe. It establishes readily from seed and persists in semi-natural grassland once established.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Centaurea nigra** was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in *Species Plantarum*.

The genus name derives from the Greek *kentauros* (centaur), referencing the mythological association of Centaurea species with Chiron the centaur. The specific epithet *nigra* refers to the dark appearance of the flower heads.

Plants grow 30–80 cm tall from a stout taproot. Stems are erect, branched in the upper portion, and covered in short hairs.

Leaves are alternate, lanceolate to oblong, 4–12 cm long. Basal leaves form a rosette and are petiolate; stem leaves are sessile and clasping.

The capitula (flower heads) are 15–25 mm in diameter, solitary or in clusters of 2–3. Involucral bracts are dark brown to black at the tips, a key distinguishing feature from *Centaurea scabiosa*.

Florets are all tubular (no ray florets); outer florets are enlarged and sterile, inner florets are fertile and hermaphrodite. Flower colour is typically rose-purple, rarely white.

Achenes are 3–4 mm long, brownish, with a short pappus of unequal bristles.

**Centaurea nigra** is frequently confused with *C. scabiosa* (greater knapweed) and *C. jacea* (meadow knapweed). *C. scabiosa* has larger flower heads (25–40 mm) with distinctly fringed bract tips, and a preference for calcareous soils.

*C. jacea* hybridises freely with *C. nigra* where their ranges overlap, producing intermediate forms. These hybrids are common in disturbed grassland and along road verges in central Europe.

The chromosome number is 2n = 22, consistent with many diploid Centaurea species. Polyploid races have not been documented in this species.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Centaurea nigra* is native to most of Europe, from the British Isles and Scandinavia south to the Iberian Peninsula and east to western Russia. It has been introduced to [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]].

In its native range, it occupies a wide ecological amplitude, from lowland to montane zones up to approximately 800 m elevation.

The species is a strong indicator of unimproved grassland. It tolerates moderate [[comparison-parasitoid-wasps-vs-chemical-pesticides]].

Goldfinches (*Carduelis carduelis*) feed extensively on knapweed seeds in autumn and winter, making the species an important component of the winter food web in grassland landscapes.

The root system contributes to soil structure in permanent pasture. Its deep taproot helps break compacted soil layers and draws nutrients from lower soil horizons.

*C. nigra* serves as a larval food plant for several moth species, including the knapweed plume moth (*Pterophorus pentadactyla*). The foliage is also browsed by rabbit and deer in the absence of preferred forage species.

## Cultivation

Common knapweed is easily grown from seed sown in autumn or [[hamilton-wildflower-meadow]] mixtures. It typically germinates within 2–4 weeks under warm, moist conditions.

Plants usually flower in their second year from seed, though early-sown seed may produce flowers in the first autumn. Flowering period extends from June to September.

Established plants are drought-tolerant once mature and require no irrigation [[query-how-to-grow-and-use-oca-in-temperate-climates]]. They persist for many years in permanent grassland without needing reseeding.

## Uses

In traditional agriculture, *C. nigra* was considered a moderate-quality forage plant. Livestock will graze young growth, but mature stems become fibrous and are generally avoided.

The species is a core component of native wildflower seed mixes marketed for meadow creation, pollinator strips, and highway verge management in the UK and continental Europe.

It has limited ethnobotanical use. Dried flower heads have been used ornamentally, and the plant has occasionally been used in folk medicine as an astringent and wound herb.
