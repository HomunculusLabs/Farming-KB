The African oil palm is a large, single-stemmed, monoecious palm reaching 20 to 30 meters in height under plantation conditions. The trunk is robust, 40 to 75 cm in diameter, with prominent leaf scar rings and a crown of 30 to 40 pinnate leaves.

Leaves are pinnate, 5 to 7 meters in length, with 200 to 300 leaflets per side. Leaflets are arranged in groups along the rachis, giving the frond a characteristic plumose appearance.

The petiole is stout, 1 to 2 meters long, armed with sharp spines along the margins. Spine density varies among genetic stocks, with some commercial varieties selected for reduced spine length.

Spineless (pisifera) and semi-spineless (tenera) varieties have been developed through breeding programs and are preferred for plantation management because they reduce worker injury during harvest operations.

Inflorescences are produced in the leaf axils on a continuous cycle throughout the year in [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]] margins, freshwater swamp forests, and riparian corridors.

The species has been introduced and widely cultivated throughout the tropics, with major plantation regions in [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] content. Plantations are established on a wide range of soil types, including deeply weathered tropical ultisols and oxisols.

## Cultivation and Uses

### Palm Oil Production

Oil palm is cultivated primarily for two distinct oils extracted from the same fruit. Crude palm oil (CPO) is extracted from the fleshy mesocarp and is rich in palmitic and oleic [[biodiesel-production]].

The crop's exceptionally high land-use efficiency has driven its expansion as a replacement for less productive oilseed crops. Oil palm produces approximately 4-5 times more oil per hectare than soybean, rapeseed, or sunflower.

This productivity advantage means that meeting global vegetable oil demand with alternative oilseed crops would require substantially more land conversion, potentially displacing other [[legal-responses-to-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-in-the-european-union]]. The use of palm oil as a biofuel feedstock has generated additional controversy regarding competition with food production and net carbon benefits.
