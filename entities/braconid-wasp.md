## Description

Braconid wasps (family Braconidae) are a vast family of small parasitoid wasps that are among the most important biological control agents in agriculture and gardening. Unlike social wasps (hornets, yellowjackets), braconids are tiny — most are less than 1 cm long — and do not sting humans. They are parasitoids, meaning they lay their eggs on or inside other insects, and their larvae consume the host from within, ultimately killing it. The most iconic image in biological control is a tomato hornworm covered in the white cocoons of a braconid wasp.

## Classification

- **Order**: Hymenoptera (wasps, bees, ants)
- **Family**: Braconidae
- **Common genera**: *Cotesia*, *Bracon*, *Aphidius*, *Diaeretiella*, *Chelonus*
- **Type**: Parasitoid wasp (beneficial)

## Key Facts

- Over 1,000 genera and approximately 17,000 described species worldwide; actual diversity likely much higher
- Primary parasitoids of caterpillars (Lepidoptera larvae), aphids, flies, beetles, and other insect pests
- The species *Cotesia congregata* is the well-known parasitoid of tomato and tobacco hornworms
- *Aphidius* species are important aphid parasitoids used commercially in greenhouse and field settings
- Adults are tiny (2-15 mm), often dark or brown, and feed on nectar and honeydew
- Parasitized hornworms become sluggish and stop feeding long before the wasp larvae emerge
- After consuming the host internally, larvae spin white silken cocoons on the host body surface
- Multiple generations per year; overwinter as larvae inside host insects or as pupae in cocoons
- Highly sensitive to broad-spectrum insecticides

## Role in the Soil Food Web

Braconid wasps operate as parasitoids rather than predators, representing a distinct control strategy within the garden food web. Rather than consuming prey directly, they convert pest insects into next-generation biological control agents. This makes them exceptionally efficient at population regulation — each parasitized host produces multiple new wasps.

In the Lowenfels [[soil-food-web]] framework, braconid wasps exemplify the complex interrelationships that develop when chemical interventions are eliminated. Their sensitivity to pesticides means they are among the first beneficials lost when chemicals are applied and among the last to return. Their presence is a strong indicator of a mature, functioning garden ecosystem.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Hornworm control**: The single most effective natural control for tomato/tobacco hornworms — a major garden pest
- **Aphid parasitism**: *Aphidius* species are commercially reared and released for aphid control in greenhouses and fields
- **Caterpillar management**: Attack a wide range of caterpillar pests including cabbage worms, armyworms, and fruit worms
- **Vegetable garden value**: Critical beneficial in tomato, pepper, cabbage, and cole crop plantings
- **Compatible with soil food web practices**: Thrive when compost teas replace chemical sprays and diverse plantings provide nectar
- **Synergy with mycorrhizae**: By reducing defoliation from caterpillars, they help maintain the photosynthetic capacity that drives root exudate production for [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]

## Practical Notes

- **Do not kill parasitized caterpillars**: A hornworm covered in white cocoons is already dying and is producing the next generation of wasps — leave it in the garden
- Provide nectar sources: small-flowered plants like dill, fennel, yarrow, alyssum, and coriander attract and sustain adult braconid wasps
- Maintain some flowering weeds in garden borders as wasp nectar sources
- Avoid all insecticides during the growing season — even Bt (*[[bacillus-thuringiensis]]*) can kill braconid larvae inside caterpillars
- Diverse plantings (intercropping, [[companion-planting]]) increase braconid wasp effectiveness

## Relationships

- **Hosts/Prey**: Hornworms (*Manduca* spp.), cabbage worms (*Pieris* spp.), aphids (multiple species), armyworms, fruit worms, various beetle larvae
- **Adult food**: Nectar from small flowers, honeydew from aphids
- **Allies**: Ladybird beetles, green lacewings, spined soldier bugs, trichogramma wasps (another parasitoid family)
- **Threats**: Broad-spectrum insecticides, Bt applications, loss of nectar plant diversity
