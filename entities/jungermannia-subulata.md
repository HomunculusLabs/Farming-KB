## Overview

**Jungermannia subulata** is a leafy liverwort in the family Jungermanniaceae, commonly known as theawl-leaved jungermannia or **subulate leafy liverwort**. It is a small but distinctive species found in temperate and boreal regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

The species is recognized by its narrow, subulate (awl-shaped) leaves that curve toward one side of the stem, giving the plants a characteristic asymmetrical appearance. It is an obligate calcicole, restricted to calcium-rich substrates.

Jungermannia subulata typically grows on damp limestone rocks, mortar in old walls, and calcareous soil banks. Its strong substrate specificity makes it a reliable indicator of base-rich conditions.

The genus *Jungermannia* was one of the first liverwort genera described and served as the original type for the order Jungermanniales. The specific epithet *subulata* refers to the awl-shaped leaf form.

The genus is named in honor of Ludwig Jungermann (1572–1653), a German botanist and early pioneer of bryology who assembled one of the first significant herbaria of liverworts and mosses.

Plants are typically dark green to olive-brown when dry, becoming brighter green when hydrated. The color change is useful for locating populations in the field after rain events.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Jungermannia subulata was described by August Emanuel Fürnrohr in 1836. The taxonomy of the genus *Jungermannia* has undergone substantial revision, with many species transferred to other genera.

**Classification:**
- Division: Marchantiophyta
- Class: Jungermanniopsida
- Order: Jungermanniales
- Family: Jungermanniaceae
- Genus: *Jungermannia* L.
- Species: *J. subulata* A. Evans (syn. *J. subulata* Fürnr.)

The gametophyte forms small, creeping patches 0.5–2 cm in extent. Stems are slender, 0.3–0.5 mm in diameter, and irregularly branched with lateral and occasionally terminal branches.

Leaves are succubous, closely imbricate when dry, and spreading when moist. They are narrow-ovate to subulate, 0.8–1.5 mm long, with the apex curved strongly toward the stem in a characteristic falcate orientation.

Leaf cells are thin-walled, trigones absent or poorly developed, and approximately 20–30 μm in diameter. Oil bodies are present, typically 2–4 per cell, hyaline, and homogenous.

Underleaves are small, orbicular to reniform, and approximately one-third the size of the lateral leaves. They are inserted on the ventral surface of the stem at regular intervals.

Rhizoids are confined to the ventral stem surface near the underleaf insertions. Perianths are ovate, strongly inflated at the base, and contracted to a ciliate mouth. Spores are 14–18 μm in diameter, reddish-brown, and coarsely papillose.

## Distribution and Habitat

Jungermannia subulata has a scattered but widespread distribution across Europe and North America. In Europe, it is recorded from the British Isles, Scandinavia, central Europe, and the Mediterranean region.

In North America, the species occurs from eastern Canada and New England west through the Great Lakes region. Disjunct populations are reported from the Rocky Mountains and [[spawn-storage-conditions]]. Fresh spores sown on calcareous agar substrates germinate within 2–4 weeks under [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] within its thallus, including nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria that contribute to [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]] on otherwise nutrient-poor limestone surfaces.

## Conservation Status

Jungermannia subulata is not globally threatened but is considered rare or uncommon across much of its range. It is listed in several national red lists as a species of conservation concern.

The primary threat is habitat loss through quarrying of limestone formations and demolition of old stone walls. Both activities directly destroy the calcareous substrates the species requires.

Air pollution, particularly acid deposition, threatens populations by reducing the pH of calcareous substrates over time. Acidified substrates become unsuitable for this obligate calcicole, leading to local population declines.
