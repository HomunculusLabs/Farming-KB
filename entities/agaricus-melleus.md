## Description

Armillaria mellea, historically referred to as **[[agaricus]] melleus** in early
taxonomic literature, is a honey-colored agaric mushroom commonly known as the
**honey mushroom**. It is a widespread parasitic fungus that grows in dense
clusters at the base of trees, on stumps, and on buried roots. Palmer (1894)
describes it as very acrid when raw but perfectly wholesome when cooked, noting
that heat destroys the noxious properties present in the uncooked state.

The species is notable for its bioluminescent mycelia and its role as one of
the most significant plant pathogens in temperate forests worldwide, causing
root rot in a very wide range of woody hosts.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Physalacriaceae
- **Genus:** Armillaria
- **Species:** A. mellea
- **Synonyms:** Agaricus melleus (original Friesian name)

## Key Facts

- Grows in large clusters (fasciculate habit) at tree bases and stumps
- Cap is honey-yellow to brown with darker scales toward the center
- Possesses a distinct ring (annulus) on the stem
- Very acrid and peppery taste when raw — should never be consumed uncooked
- The heat of cooking destroys the acrid compounds, rendering it safe to eat
- Bioluminescent [[mycelium]] observable in darkness ("foxfire")
- One of the largest living organisms on Earth when measured by clone size
- Acts as a white-rot decomposer and aggressive tree pathogen
- [[spore-print]] is white to cream-colored
- Gills are white initially, darkening with age, often developing rust spots

## Historical Context

Julius A. Palmer notes that this species occupies an ambiguous position in the
older mycological literature. The Scottish authority Greville records conflicting
reports: Trattinick states it is freely eaten on the European Continent, while
Persoon gives it a bad character, and Paulet demonstrated its toxicity by feeding
it to a dog, which died twelve hours later. Palmer himself ate it plentifully
without ill effect, attributing the contradictory reports to the difference between
raw and cooked consumption. He writes: "Notwithstanding such a warning, it
continues to be a favorite article of diet to this day. I think it may be noxious
raw, but that the heat kills the virus."

This historical debate highlights a central theme in Palmer's work: that many
supposedly poisonous fungi are merely unpalatable or mildly toxic when raw but
perfectly safe when properly prepared — a distinction lost in the crude binary
of "edible" versus "poisonous" that characterized earlier mycological texts.

## Culinary Notes

- Must be thoroughly cooked; never eat raw
- Palmer recommends broiling on a fine-wire gridiron with salt and butter
- Best gathered young before the caps fully expand
- Has a mild, pleasant flavor when properly prepared
- Some modern foragers parboil before cooking to reduce bitterness
- Known to cause gastrointestinal upset in some individuals even when cooked

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Armillaria mellea presents a dual significance for cultivation:

1. **Pathogen concern:** It is among the most destructive forest pathogens,
   killing trees through root infection. Cultivators of trees and woody
   perennials must be vigilant about Armillaria presence in soil.

2. **Edible species:** Despite its pathogenic nature, the fruiting bodies are
   prized edibles in many cultures, particularly in Eastern Europe and East
   Asia. It is commercially foraged rather than cultivated.

3. **Research organism:** Its enormous clonal colonies (some spanning hectares)
   make it a subject of ecological and genetic research into fungal
   individuality, somatic mutation, and clonal growth strategies.

4. **Bioluminescence:** The species is studied for its bioluminescent
   properties, which may have applications in biological imaging and
   ecological monitoring.

## Morphology

- **Cap:** 3–10 cm, convex expanding to flat, honey-yellow to brown, covered
  with fine dark scales or fibrils, especially at the center
- **Gills:** Adnate to slightly decurrent, white to cream, sometimes with
  reddish-brown spots in age
- **Stem:** 5–15 cm long, fibrous, with a well-developed ring; above the ring
  the stem is whitish, below it is brownish and scaly
- **Flesh:** White, firm, with a slightly acidic odor
- **[[spore]] print:** White to cream

## Habitat and Season

Found throughout temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere. Fruits from late
summer through autumn. Grows on living trees, dead wood, stumps, and roots of
deciduous and coniferous trees. Especially common in oak, beech, and pine
woodlands.

## Cautions

Must be distinguished from the deadly **[[galerina-marginata]]**, which can grow in
similar cluster formations and contains amatoxins. Proper identification by an
expert is essential before consumption.
