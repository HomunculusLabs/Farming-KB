# Symphytum officinale
**Common Comfrey** · Knitbone · Boneset · *Symphytum officinale* L.

## Taxonomy

|------|------|
| Kingdom | Plantae |
| Clade | Tracheophytes |
| Clade | Angiosperms |
| Clade | Eudicots |
| Order | Boraginales |

The genus *Symphytum* contains approximately 35 species native to Europe and western Asia. Several species and hybrids are cultivated, with the Russian comfrey hybrid (*Symphytum × uplandicum*, 'Bocking 14') being the most widely planted in permaculture systems. The species epithet *officinale* indicates its long history of use in official (apothecary) medicine.

## Etymology and Common Names

The genus name derives from Greek *symphyo* (to unite/grow together), referencing its traditional use in healing wounds and mending broken bones. Common names across languages reinforce this association:

- **English:** Comfrey (from Latin *con firma* — "with strength"), knitbone, boneset, bruisewort
- **French:** Consoude (from *con solidare* — "to consolidate")
- **German:** Beinwell ("bone well")
- **Spanish:** Consuelda
- **Chinese:** 聚合草 (jùhé cǎo — "uniting grass")

## Botanical Description

*Symphytum officinale* is a vigorous, herbaceous perennial growing 60–150 cm tall with a deep, branching taproot system that can extend 1.5–3 meters into the soil.

### Morphology
- **Leaves:** Large, broadly lanceolate to ovate, 15–40 cm long, covered in coarse trichomes (hairy). Lower leaves petiolate, upper leaves clasping the stem. Arrangement is alternate, becoming smaller toward the inflorescence.
- **Stem:** Erect, stout, hollow, branched above, covered in bristly hairs. Contains mucilaginous sap.
- **Flowers:** Bell-shaped (campanulate), 12–18 mm long, arranged in scorpioid cymes (characteristic of Boraginaceae). Color varies from creamy-white to purple-pink, sometimes blue. Each flower has 5 fused petals, 5 stamens, and a superior ovary. Flowers from May to July (Northern Hemisphere).
- **Root:** Massive, thick, black-skinned taproot with white, mucilaginous interior. The root is the primary medicinal part and the key to comfrey's role as a dynamic accumulator.
- **Fruit:** A cluster of 4 nutlets (schizocarps), each glossy black, containing one seed.

### Reproduction

Primarily spreads vegetatively through root fragments — even small root pieces can regenerate into new plants. Sexual reproduction produces abundant seeds that persist in soil seed banks for decades. This dual reproductive strategy makes comfrey a vigorous and potentially invasive colonizer.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to Europe and temperate western Asia, from Ireland and the UK eastward to the Caucasus and Iran. Naturalized across much of North America, temperate South America, New Zealand, and southeastern Australia.

Habitat includes damp meadows, riverbanks, ditches, forest edges, and disturbed ground. Prefers moist, nitrogen-rich soils in partial shade to full sun. Tolerates pH 5.0–7.5 and a wide range of soil types, including heavy clay.

## Phytochemistry

### Allantoin

The signature compound, constituting 0.3–1.2% of dried root and 0.1–0.8% of leaves. Allantoin is a cell proliferant that promotes wound healing through stimulation of epithelial cell division and tissue regeneration.

### Pyrrolizidine Alkaloids (PAs)

**Critical safety note:** *S. officinale* contains hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids, primarily:
- **Symphytine** — major PA in roots
- **Echimidine** — carcinogenic, present in aerial parts
- **Lycopsamine** — hepatotoxic
- **Intermedine/N-oxide derivatives**

PA content varies significantly between plant parts, cultivars, and harvest times. Roots contain the highest concentrations (up to 0.3% dry weight). The Russian hybrid 'Bocking 14' (*S. × uplandicum*) contains lower PA levels than wild *S. officinale*, but is NOT PA-free.

### Mucilage

Constitutes 20–30% of the root dry weight. Responsible for the soothing, demulcent properties. Composed primarily of acidic polysaccharides (galacturonic acid, rhamnose, galactose).

### Other Compounds
- **Rosmarinic acid** — anti-inflammatory, antioxidant
- **Tannins** — astringent, wound-healing
- **Silica** — promotes bone and connective tissue repair
- **Vitamins B12** (trace amounts) — one of few plant sources
- **Protein** — dried leaves contain 20–30% crude protein

## Permaculture Role

Comfrey is arguably the single most important plant in temperate permaculture systems. Its value derives from several integrated functions:

### Dynamic Mineral Accumulator

Comfrey's deep taproot mines minerals from subsoil layers inaccessible to most garden plants. It is particularly noted for accumulating:
| Mineral | Accumulated Amount (mg/kg dry leaf) |
|---------|--------------------------------------|
| Potassium (K) | 30,000–80,000 (3–8%) |
| Nitrogen (N) | 30,000–50,000 (3–5%) |
| Calcium (Ca) | 20,000–40,000 (2–4%) |
| Phosphorus (P) | 3,000–5,000 (0.3–0.5%) |
| Magnesium (Mg) | 4,000–8,000 (0.4–0.8%) |
| Iron (Fe) | 200–500 |
| Manganese (Mn) | 50–200 |
| Zinc (Zn) | 30–100 |
This mineral-rich biomass is accessed through:
- **Comfrey tea (liquid fertilizer):** Leaves steeped in water for 2–4 weeks produce a nutrient-dense liquid feed (~NPK 1.5–0.5–8.0)
- **Compost activator:** Layered into compost piles to accelerate decomposition and enrich finished compost
- **Mulch:** Fresh or wilted leaves used as surface mulch release nutrients slowly
- **Chop-and-drop:** Cut 4–6 times per season; plants regrow within 2–3 weeks

### Biomass Production

Established comfrey plants produce enormous biomass yields:
- **Year 1:** 1–2 kg fresh leaf per plant (4–6 cuts)
- **Year 2+:** 3–5 kg fresh leaf per plant (4–6 cuts)
- **Per hectare (30,000 plants/ha):** 90–150 tonnes fresh biomass/year

### Companion Planting

- **Fruit trees:** Comfrey planted in the root zone of fruit trees provides a living mulch and nutrient source. The "comfrey circle" — a ring of plants around the tree's drip line — is a classic permaculture technique.
- **Potatoes:** Comfrey mulch reduces potato scab and increases yields
- **Tomatoes:** Comfrey tea fed to tomatoes reduces blossom end rot (calcium supply) and boosts potassium for fruit production
- **Comfrey under fruit bushes:** Suppresses weeds, retains moisture, and provides ongoing nutrient cycling

### Pollinator Support

The bell-shaped flowers are highly attractive to bumblebees (*Bombus* spp.) and long-tongued bees. Comfrey provides early-to-mid season nectar and pollen when other forage may be scarce.

### Erosion Control and Soil Stabilization

The extensive root network binds soil on slopes, stream banks, and swale edges. Comfrey is planted on earthworks, dam walls, and terraces to prevent erosion while producing a harvestable crop.

## Cultivation

### Propagation

- **Root cuttings (most common):** 5–8 cm root sections planted horizontally 5 cm deep; sprout in 2–3 weeks. A single mature root crown can yield 50+ propagules.
- **Crown divisions:** Divide established plants in spring or autumn.
- **Seed:** Direct sow in spring; slow and less reliable than vegetative methods.

### Recommended Cultivar: Bocking 14

Developed by Lawrence D. Hills at the Henry Doubleday Research Association (HDRA), Bocking 14 (*Symphytum × uplandicum*) is the standard permaculture comfrey:
- **Sterile hybrid** — does not set seed, preventing unwanted spread
- **High biomass** — larger leaves and more vigorous growth than *S. officinale*
- **Deep roots** — superior mineral accumulation
- **Lower PA content** — though still not PA-free

### Growing Conditions
- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 3–9
- **Light:** Full sun to partial shade (produces most biomass in full sun)
- **Soil:** Tolerates most soils; performs best in deep, moist, fertile loams
- **Spacing:** 60–90 cm between plants
- **Establishment:** Plant crowns 5 cm below soil surface in spring or autumn
- **Harvest:** Begin cutting in second year; cut 5 cm above ground; stop cutting 6 weeks before first frost

## Medicinal Uses (Historical and External Only)

### Traditional Use

Comfrey has a 2,500+ year history of medicinal use. Dioscorides (c. 40–90 CE) prescribed it for wounds and respiratory complaints. Culpeper (1653) recommended it for "inward hurts, bruises, wounds, and ulcerated lungs."

### Modern Internal Use — CONTRAINDICATED

**Internal use of comfrey is strongly discouraged** due to pyrrolizidine alkaloid hepatotoxicity. PAs cause hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD), a potentially fatal condition. The German Commission E, UK MHRA, and FDA have all issued warnings against internal use. PAs are also genotoxic and carcinogenic in animal studies.

### External/Topical Use

External application remains widely practiced and is supported by clinical evidence:
- **Wound healing:** Allantoin and rosmarinic acid promote tissue regeneration
- **Bone and joint injuries:** Traditional use for fractures, sprains, and osteoarthritis; some clinical evidence for topical pain relief
- **Skin conditions:** Eczema, psoriasis, minor burns, insect bites
- **Form:** Comfrey ointment/cream containing <0.1 µg PA/g (PA-free preparations available from cultivated hybrid roots)

A 2012 Cochrane review found moderate evidence for topical comfrey reducing acute musculoskeletal pain. Several RCTs have shown efficacy for osteoarthritis of the knee (Grube et al., 2007) and acute ankle sprains (Predel et al., 2005).

## Ecological Considerations

Comfrey's vigorous growth and root-fragment regeneration make it a potential invasive species outside its native range. In permaculture systems, this is managed by:
- Planting sterile Bocking 14 to prevent seed spread
- Containing root systems with physical barriers (root barriers, raised beds)
- Regular harvesting to prevent excessive spread
- Avoiding planting near waterways where root fragments could spread downstream
