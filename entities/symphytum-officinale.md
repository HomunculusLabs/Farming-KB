# Symphytum officinale (Common Comfrey)

## Overview
**Symphytum officinale**, commonly known as **comfrey**, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Boraginaceae that occupies a central role in both traditional herbal medicine and modern permaculture systems. Native to Europe and temperate regions of Asia, comfrey has been cultivated for centuries as a medicinal herb, fodder crop, and soil-building plant. In permaculture design, it is valued as one of the most effective **dynamic accumulator** plants, capable of mining deep soil minerals and making them available to other plants through its fast-decomposing biomass.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Boraginales
- **Family:** Boraginaceae
- **Genus:** *Symphytum*
- **Species:** *S. officinale*
- **Common names:** Comfrey, common comfrey, knitbone, boneset, bruisewort, slippery root
- **Note:** Russian comfrey (*S. × uplandicum*, a hybrid of *S. officinale* × *S. asperum*) and 'Bocking 14' (a sterile cultivar) are more commonly grown in permaculture settings

## Botanical Description
Comfrey is a robust, clump-forming perennial growing 60–150 cm tall and 60–120 cm wide. It produces large, hairy, lance-shaped leaves up to 30 cm long that form a rosette. In late spring to early summer, it bears bell-shaped flowers in nodding clusters, typically blue-purple (occasionally white or pink), each 1–2 cm long. The root system is extensive and remarkable—**taproots can extend 2–3 meters deep**, with a thick, dark, mucilaginous main root that is brittle and difficult to eradicate once established.

## Dynamic Accumulation Properties
Comfrey's permaculture value derives from its exceptional ability to accumulate nutrients from deep subsoil layers and concentrate them in its leaves:

| Nutrient | Concentration in Dry Leaves | Significance |
|----------|---------------------------|--------------|
| Nitrogen | 4–7% | Comparable to chicken manure |
| Potassium | 3–7% | Exceeds most organic fertilizers |
| Phosphorus | 0.3–0.8% | Moderate accumulator |
| Calcium | 2–4% | Important for cell wall strength |
| Iron | Moderate | Trace mineral replenishment |
| Silica | Moderate | Improves disease resistance in neighboring plants |

This nutrient profile makes comfrey leaves one of the richest sources of organic potassium and nitrogen available to home growers.

## Permaculture Applications

### Compost Activation
Comfrey leaves are one of the best natural compost activators. Their high nitrogen content and moisture-rich tissues accelerate decomposition. Adding a 5–10 cm layer of comfrey leaves between compost layers dramatically speeds up the composting process.

### Liquid Fertilizer (Comfrey Tea)
**Comfrey tea** is a potent liquid fertilizer produced by steeping comfrey leaves in water:
- **Method:** Fill a container with comfrey leaves, add water (or use without water for concentrated "comfrey concentrate"), cover, and ferment for 3–6 weeks
- **Dilution:** Dilute 1:10 with water before application
- **Nutrient profile:** High in potassium, making it ideal for fruiting and flowering plants (tomatoes, peppers, cucurbits, fruit trees)
- **Application:** Every 2 weeks during the growing season

### Mulch
Comfrey leaves can be cut and laid directly around plants as a nutrient-rich mulch. They decompose rapidly (typically within 2–3 weeks), releasing nutrients directly to the soil surface. This "chop and drop" approach is a cornerstone of permaculture soil management.

### Nutrient Mining
Comfrey's deep taproots mine minerals from subsoil layers inaccessible to most garden plants. When the leaves are cut and used as mulch or compost, these deep-mined nutrients become available in the topsoil for shallow-rooted crops.

### Companion Planting
- **Fruit trees:** Comfrey planted in the drip line of fruit trees provides ongoing potassium supplementation
- **Potato beds:** Potassium-rich mulch improves potato yield and disease resistance
- **Comfrey barriers:** Dense planting creates weed-suppressing barriers along garden edges

### Pollinator Support
The bell-shaped flowers are highly attractive to bees, particularly long-tongued bumblebee species (*Bombus* spp.), providing early-season nectar and pollen.

### Livestock Fodder
Historically, comfrey was widely grown as fodder for cattle, pigs, and poultry due to its high protein content (20–30% of dry weight). However, concerns about pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) have led to reduced use as feed in many countries.

## Cultivation

### Site Requirements
- **Sun:** Full sun to partial shade
- **Soil:** Tolerates most soil types; prefers moist, fertile, well-drained soils
- **pH:** 6.0–7.5
- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 3–9

### Propagation
- **Root cuttings:** Most common and reliable method. Cut 2–5 cm root sections and plant horizontally 5 cm deep
- **Crown divisions:** Divide established clumps in spring or autumn
- **Seed:** Rarely used; 'Bocking 14' is sterile and produces no viable seed
- **Recommended cultivar:** 'Bocking 14' is preferred for permaculture as it is sterile (won't become invasive), higher-yielding, and has higher nutrient content than wild comfrey

### Harvest
- Cut leaves 5–8 cm above the crown
- First cut typically 6–8 weeks after spring emergence
- Subsequent cuts every 4–6 weeks during the growing season
- Total annual yield: 3–5 cuts, producing 2–5 kg of fresh leaves per plant per year
- Cease cutting by late summer to allow root reserves to rebuild before winter

## Safety and Toxicity

**Important:** Comfrey contains hepatotoxic **pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)**, primarily symphytine and lycopsamine. These compounds can cause veno-occlusive disease of the liver, particularly with internal use or prolonged topical application on broken skin.

- **Internal use:** Not recommended. Several countries (Australia, Germany, Canada) have restricted or banned internal use of comfrey products
- **Topical use:** Generally considered safe for short-term application to unbroken skin for sprains, bruises, and minor wounds
- **Permaculture use:** External use (fertilizer, mulch, compost) is completely safe as PAs are not taken up by other plants from decomposing comfrey material
- **Gloves recommended:** Handling comfrey can cause skin irritation in sensitive individuals

## Allantoin Content

Comfrey is one of nature's richest sources of **allantoin** (0.6–1.5% in roots), a cell-proliferating compound that promotes wound healing, tissue repair, and cell regeneration. This is the basis of its traditional name "knitbone" and its historical use as a poultice for fractures, sprains, and wounds. Allantoin is widely used in commercial cosmetics and skin care products.

## Traditional and Historical Uses
Comfrey has an extensive history in European herbal medicine spanning over 2,000 years:

- **Ancient Greece:** Dioscorides (c. 40–90 CE) recommended comfrey root poultices for wounds and fractures in *De Materia Medica*
- **Medieval Europe:** Central to monastic physic gardens; used by Hildegard of Bingen (1098–1179) for healing wounds
- **Traditional Chinese Medicine:** Known as *shang cai*, used externally for burns and ulcers
- **19th century:** Widely prescribed as "knitbone" for fractures and "bruisewort" for soft tissue injuries
- **Modern herbalism:** Still used topically in poultices, salves, and creams for sprains, strains, and minor wounds

## Permaculture Design Placement
Comfrey is strategically placed throughout permaculture designs for maximum utility:
- **Fruit tree guilds:** Plant 3–5 comfrey plants around each fruit tree at the drip line. Deep roots mine calcium and potassium from subsoil, and regular "chop and drop" cycling feeds shallow tree roots
- **Compost station edges:** Border compost piles with comfrey for easy access as an activator and to capture nutrient leachate
- **Pond edges:** Comfrey thrives in moist conditions near water features and helps filter runoff
- **Garden bed borders:** Dense comfrey plantings create effective weed barriers and windbreaks
- **Banks and slopes:** Deep roots stabilize soil on slopes and prevent erosion

## Propagation Guide
The most reliable propagation method for 'Bocking 14' is root cuttings:
1. **Source roots** from an established plant in early spring or autumn
2. **Cut root sections** 2–5 cm long with at least one bud
3. **Plant horizontally** 5 cm deep, 60 cm apart
4. **Keep moist** until established (2–3 weeks)
5. **First harvest** in the second year; avoid cutting in the first year to allow root establishment
