# Comfrey (*Symphytum officinale*)

## Overview

Comfrey is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Boraginaceae, native to Europe and naturalized across temperate regions worldwide. Widely regarded as one of the most valuable plants in temperate permaculture systems, comfrey serves multiple functions: dynamic nutrient accumulation, high-yield biomass production for mulch and liquid fertilizer, medicinal application (externally), bee forage, and companion planting. The most widely cultivated variety in permaculture is the sterile hybrid 'Bocking 14' (*S. × uplandicum*), developed by Lawrence D. Hills at the Henry Doubleday Research Association (HDRA) in the 1950s.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Boraginales
- **Family:** Boraginaceae
- **Genus:** *Symphytum*
- **Species:** *S. officinale* L.
- **Common names:** Comfrey, common comfrey, boneset, knitback, bruisewort
- **Key cultivars:** 'Bocking 4' (deep-rooted, high protein), 'Bocking 14' (sterile, highest nutrient yield), 'Bocking 2' (drought-tolerant)
- **Hybrid note:** *S. × uplandicum* (Russian comfrey) is a naturally occurring hybrid of *S. officinale* × *S. asperum*; the Bocking strains are selections within this hybrid complex.

## Botanical Description

Comfrey forms a robust clump 60–150 cm tall. The root system is the most notable feature: a thick, deep taproot extends 1.8–3.0 m into the subsoil, branching extensively. This root architecture is the basis of comfrey's dynamic accumulator function. Stems are hollow, winged, and covered in stiff hairs. Leaves are large (up to 40 cm long), broadly lanceolate to ovate, rough-textured with bristly trichomes, and form a rosette at the crown. Flowers are bell-shaped, pendulous, 12–18 mm long, ranging from creamy-white to purple-violet, arranged in cymose scorpioid inflorescences. Flowering occurs May–July in temperate zones. 'Bocking 14' is triploid and produces minimal viable seed; it spreads exclusively vegetatively.

## Historical Use

Comfrey has been cultivated for at least 2,500 years. Dioscorides (c. 40–90 AD) prescribed it for wounds and broken bones under the name *symphyton* (from Greek *symphyo*, "to unite"). In medieval European herbalism, comfrey was the primary treatment for fractures, sprains, and bruises—hence folk names "knitbone" and "boneset." The 16th-century herbalist John Gerard recommended comfrey root poultices for joint inflammation. Indigenous European communities used comfrey internally for gastrointestinal complaints and externally as a compress for wounds, ulcers, and insect bites. Commercial cultivation for fodder peaked in 19th-century England and again during WWII, when it was promoted as a high-protein livestock feed.

## Phytochemistry

Key bioactive constituents include:

- **Allantoin (0.6–1.5%):** Cell-proliferant and wound-healing compound; promotes granulation tissue formation.
- **Mucilage (up to 30%):** Polysaccharide hydrocolloid responsible for soothing, demulcent properties.
- **Rosmarinic acid:** Anti-inflammatory antioxidant.
- **Tannins:** Astringent, contribute to wound-drying effects.
- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs):** Hepatotoxic compounds including lycopsamine, intermedine, and their N-oxides; present at 0.01–0.1% in leaves, higher concentrations in roots. PA content varies significantly by cultivar and growing conditions.
- **Symphytine and echimidine:** Specific PAs of toxicological concern.
- **Vitamins:** B12 (reportedly present, though disputed), A, C.
- **Minerals:** High in potassium, calcium, phosphorus, iron, magnesium.

## Permaculture Applications

Comfrey is considered a keystone species in temperate permaculture design due to its multi-functionality:

1. **Biomass production:** Yields 3–5 kg of fresh leaf material per plant per cutting, with 4–5 cuts per season in ideal conditions. Annual fresh biomass yields of 50–100 tonnes/ha are achievable.
2. **Nutrient cycling:** Deep roots mine subsoil minerals and deposit them in leaf tissue; when cut and used as mulch, nutrients become available to surface-feeding plants.
3. **Ground cover:** Dense canopy suppresses weeds beneath fruit trees and in guild plantings.
4. **Erosion control:** Extensive root network stabilizes banks and slopes.
5. **Bee forage:** Flowers are highly attractive to bumblebees and long-tongued bees; prolonged bloom period.
6. **Compost activator:** High nitrogen and microbial activity accelerate compost decomposition when added in layers.
7. **Grazing barrier:** Foliage is unpalatable to livestock due to rough hairs, protecting comfrey from grazing pressure in mixed plantings.

## Dynamic Accumulation

Comfrey is the archetypal dynamic accumulator in permaculture literature. Its deep taproot accesses minerals below the root zone of most annual crops. Reported leaf mineral concentrations (dry weight basis): potassium 3.0–4.5%, calcium 1.5–3.0%, phosphorus 0.3–0.8%, nitrogen 3.0–5.0%, magnesium 0.3–0.5%, iron 200–500 ppm. The N-fixation question remains contested; Crouch (1983) documented modest nitrogenase activity in root-associated microbial communities, but most nitrogen accumulation is attributed to deep soil mining rather than biological fixation. Potassium accumulation is the most practically significant: comfrey leaves contain 3× the potassium of farmyard manure, making comfrey tea particularly valuable for fruiting and flowering plants. Accumulation rates are highest in plants grown on mineral-rich subsoils.

## Companion Planting

- **Fruit trees:** Planted in the drip line of apple, pear, plum, and cherry trees as a nutrient-mining ground cover. The "comfrey apple tree guild" is a foundational pattern in forest garden design.
- **Tomatoes and potatoes:** Comfrey mulch provides potassium needed for fruit and tuber development without excessive nitrogen that would promote foliage over fruit.
- **Beans and legumes:** Avoid direct competition; comfrey's dense growth can shade out low-growing legumes.
- **Berry bushes:** Useful mulch source for currants, gooseberries, and raspberries.
- **Comfrey accumulates potassium but does not significantly compete with shallow-rooted companions for phosphorus due to its deep root strategy.
- **Spacing:** Plant at 60–90 cm intervals in guild plantings; allow 45–60 cm clearance from tree trunks.

## Comfrey Tea & Fertilizer

**Comfrey tea (liquid fertilizer):** Pack comfrey leaves into a container, weigh down, cover with water, and steep for 3–6 weeks. The resulting dark liquid is diluted 1:10 to 1:15 with water and applied as a potassium-rich liquid feed. Typical application: 500 ml diluted feed per plant, weekly during fruiting. Odor is extremely pungent; sealed containers or anaerobic fermentation methods reduce smell. NPK ratio of comfrey leaves averages approximately 3.0-1.0-8.0, reflecting the high potassium and nitrogen content.

**Compost additive:** Fresh leaves layered into compost piles at 5–10% by volume accelerate decomposition. Dried leaves can be stored for winter use.

**Mulch:** Freshly cut leaves applied as a 5–10 cm mulch layer break down in 2–4 weeks, releasing nutrients directly. Suitable around vegetables, fruit trees, and ornamentals.

**Potting mix amendment:** Dried, shredded comfrey leaves incorporated at 5–10% volume add slow-release nutrients to potting mixes.

## Medicinal Controversy (PAs)

Comfrey's internal use is controversial and restricted in many countries due to hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs). PAs cause hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD), a potentially fatal condition involving obliteration of small hepatic veins. Documented cases of VOD from comfrey consumption prompted regulatory action: Germany banned internal comfrey products in 1990; the FDA issued advisory warnings in 2001; Australia and the EU restrict PA-containing herbal products. The EFSA (2011) established a tolerable daily intake for PAs at 0.007 µg/kg body weight. External application is widely considered safe due to minimal systemic absorption, though caution is advised on broken skin or over large surface areas. 'Bocking 14' has been reported to contain lower PA levels than wild *S. officinale*, but no cultivar is PA-free. Homeopathic preparations (D1 and above) are permitted in some jurisdictions as PA content falls below detectable thresholds.

## Cultivation

- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 3–9; tolerates temperatures to -35°C once established.
- **Soil:** Adaptable to most soils including heavy clay, poor loam, and acidic soils (pH 5.0–8.0). Performs best in deep, moist, fertile soils. Tolerates intermittent waterlogging.
- **Light:** Full sun to partial shade; tolerates significant shade (50%+ reduction) with reduced biomass yield.
- **Water:** Moderate to high water needs; drought-tolerant once established due to deep root system but produces significantly less biomass under water stress.
- **Fertilization:** Comfrey generally does not require additional fertilization. A single application of compost at planting is sufficient.
- **Maintenance:** Cut to 5–10 cm above ground at each harvest. Remove flowering stems if biomass production is the primary goal. Plants remain productive for 20+ years.
- **Pests/diseases:** Generally pest-free. Powdery mildew can affect plants in poor air circulation. Comfrey rust (*Melampsorella symphyti*) is the primary pathogen; resistant cultivars exist.

## Propagation

- **Root cuttings (preferred):** Cut root pieces 2–5 cm long in spring or autumn, plant horizontally 2–5 cm deep. Emergence occurs in 2–4 weeks. Each root piece produces 1–3 new plants. Root cuttings are the standard method for Bocking cultivars.
- **Crown division:** Divide established crowns in early spring or autumn with a spade, ensuring each division has roots and at least one bud.
- **Offset plants:** Established plants produce offsets that can be separated and transplanted.
- **Seed:** Viable only for non-sterile wild types; stratification at 4°C for 30 days improves germination. Germination rate typically 60–80%. Seeds remain viable for several years.
- **Establishment timeline:** Root cuttings reach full production capacity in the second growing season. First-season yields are 30–50% of mature plant output.
- **Nursery practice:** Root cuttings can be pre-sprouted in trays of damp potting mix before field planting to improve establishment rates.
