## Anthracnose

## Description

Anthracnose is a group of fungal diseases caused by various species in the genus Colletotrichum (and related genera such as Apiognomonia and Discula) that affect a wide range of economically important plants.
The disease is characterized by dark, sunken lesions on leaves, stems, flowers, and fruits.
The name derives from the Greek words anthrax (coal) and nosos (disease), referring to the dark, charred appearance of infected tissue.
[[elaine-ingham]]'s field guide includes anthracnose among the plant diseases that can be suppressed through applications of [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT), placing it in her disease management framework alongside other major fungal pathogens.

## Classification

- **Disease type**: Fungal disease (foliar, fruit, and twig phases)
- **Primary causal organisms**: Colletotrichum spp. (teleomorph: Glomerella cingulata), also Apiognomonia veneta, Discula campestris, Discula fraxinea
- **Taxonomy**: Ascomycota, Sordariomycetes, Glomerellales
- **Host range**: Extremely broad — affects mango, banana, citrus, avocado, strawberry, tomato, pepper, beans, turf grass, shade trees (sycamore, ash, oak, maple), and many ornamental plants
- **Symptoms**: Irregular dark, sunken spots or lesions on leaves and fruit; fruit rot with orange-pink spore masses in sunken lesions; defoliation; dieback of twigs and branches
- **Transmission**: Water-splashed conidia from acervuli, also spread by insects and contaminated equipment

## Key Facts

- Anthracnose is one of the most common and economically significant fungal diseases in both tropical and temperate agriculture worldwide
- The disease spreads primarily through water-splashed spores, making it particularly problematic in wet, humid conditions and in overhead [[irrigation-systems]]
- Ingham identifies anthracnose as a target disease for AACT management in her comprehensive field guide to compost tea
- Biological control through compost tea works by establishing competitive beneficial organisms on plant surfaces before infection occurs, occupying potential infection sites
- Anthracnose can cause complete crop loss in susceptible varieties when environmental conditions favor the pathogen, especially during extended wet periods
- The fungi produce acervuli (pad-like fruiting bodies) that exude conidia in gelatinous masses, which are efficiently spread by rain splash and irrigation water
- Post-harvest anthracnose is a major cause of fruit loss during storage and transport, particularly in tropical fruits like mango and banana, where latent infections from the field become active after harvest
- Cultural practices (sanitation, proper spacing for air circulation, drip irrigation instead of overhead) combined with biological controls offer integrated management
- Colletotrichum gloeosporioides is the most widely distributed species, affecting over 470 host plant species
- The fungus can exist as a quiescent (dormant) infection in unripe fruit, becoming active as the fruit ripens — this makes preventative compost tea applications during the growing season critical

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

1. **Tropical fruit production**: Anthracnose is the single most important disease of mango worldwide and a major concern for banana, avocado, and papaya production in tropical regions
2. **Compost tea application**: Anthracnose is used as a model system for demonstrating compost tea efficacy in field trials, with documented success in both research and commercial settings
3. **Preventative strategy**: As with most biological controls, compost tea must be applied before infection for maximum effectiveness — protective applications during susceptible growth stages are key
4. **Spore competition**: Beneficial organisms in AACT compete with anthracnose spores for nutrients and infection sites on plant surfaces, reducing the probability of successful infection
5. **Post-harvest implications**: Pre-harvest compost tea applications may reduce latent infections that cause post-harvest anthracnose, extending shelf life and marketability of fruit
6. **Colletotrichum biology**: The genus is extensively studied in mycology as a model for understanding fungal-plant interactions, appressorium formation, and hemibiotrophic pathogenesis
