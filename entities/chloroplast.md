## Chloroplast

## Description

Chloroplasts are specialized organelles found in plant cells that serve as the primary sites of photosynthesis — the process by which light energy is converted into chemical energy stored in sugar molecules. They are the most familiar members of the plastid family of organelles and are responsible for the green coloration of plants.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Organelle (membrane-bound)
- **Type**: Plastid (chloroplast subtype)
- **Related organelles**: Leucoplasts (colorless), Chromoplasts (pigmented)
- **Evolutionary origin**: Endosymbiotic — descended from free-living cyanobacteria

## Physical Characteristics

- Shaped like tiny lenses, 2 to 8 microns in length
- An average plant cell contains 10 to 100 chloroplasts
- A typical leaf has approximately 500,000 chloroplasts per square millimeter
- Surrounded by a double phospholipid membrane
- The inner membrane is folded to increase surface area for photosynthetic reactions

## Internal Structure

The chloroplast contains several specialized internal components:

### Thylakoids

- Membrane-bound, flattened hollow discs stacked on top of one another
- Green, chlorophyll-filled antennae are attached to these discs
- Light hits chlorophyll molecules' electrons, causing them to jump to a higher energy orbit
- Souped-up electrons flow through the thylakoid and accumulate in one membrane compartment

### Stroma

- The fluid-filled space inside the inner membrane surrounding the thylakoids
- Site of the Calvin cycle (light-independent reactions of photosynthesis)
- Contains enzymes necessary for sugar synthesis

### Grana

- Stacks of flattened thylakoids
- Only grana contain the chlorophyll pigments that give plants their green coloration

## Photosynthetic Function

The chloroplast operates a two-stage [[energy-conversion-efficiency]] process:

1. **Light reactions**: Chlorophyll absorbs light energy, exciting electrons. The concentration gradient of electrons inside the thylakoid compartment drives them through the enzyme ATPase, creating ATP from ADP. Electrons receive a second dose of light and are picked up by NADP.

2. **Sugar synthesis**: ATP created in the first stage is used to form sugar molecules. Each chloroplast can create thousands of sugar molecules every second, provided there is sufficient light.

The overall reaction converts carbon dioxide and water into sugars and starches using light energy.

## Key Facts

- Chloroplasts carry their own DNA, similar to that of cyanobacteria
- They divide independently of the cell, like bacteria
- The double membrane structure is evidence of their endosymbiotic origin
- Chloroplasts are thought to have originated from free-living cyanobacteria that were ingested by or entered plant cells as parasites
- Over evolutionary time, parts of the cyanobacterial DNA were incorporated into the plant cell's own genome
- Starch produced during photosynthesis appears as dark circles within each chloroplast

## Evolutionary Significance

The close similarity between chloroplasts and cyanobacteria is a cornerstone of the endosymbiotic theory. Both share a double membrane, possess their own DNA, and reproduce by binary fission. This suggests that ancient free-living photosynthetic bacteria were assimilated into plant cells, eventually becoming indispensable organelles.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Chloroplasts determine the photosynthetic capacity of plants, directly influencing growth rate and yield
- [[hamilton-nutrient-deficiencies-and-treatment]] that affect chlorophyll production (such as nitrogen, magnesium, or iron deficiency) manifest as chlorosis — yellowing of leaves
- Understanding [[photosynthesis-and-chloroplast-function]] helps cultivators optimize light exposure, CO2 supplementation, and nutrient feeding schedules
- In mycology, chloroplasts are absent from fungal cells — fungi are heterotrophic and do not photosynthesize, relying instead on external organic matter
- The glucose produced by chloroplasts forms the base of the food web that sustains [[endorhiza-rhizosphere-bulk-soil-microbial-communities]], including [[beneficial-fungi-trichoderma-and-mycorrhizae]] and bacteria
