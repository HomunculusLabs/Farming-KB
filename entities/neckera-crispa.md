## Overview

Neckera crispa, commonly known as the crisped feather-moss or curled Neckera,
is a robust epiphytic moss in the family Neckeraceae. It forms conspicuous
pendent curtains on the trunks and branches of old-growth trees in humid
temperate forests, creating one of the most visually striking bryophyte
displays in European woodlands.

The species is distinguished by its large, crisped leaves with a strongly
bordered margin, glossy yellow-green appearance when moist, and dark brown
to nearly black coloration when dry. It is strongly associated with ancient
trees and is considered an indicator of long-term forest continuity.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Neckera crispa was described by Johann Hedwig in 1801 and serves as the type
species of the genus Neckera. The Neckeraceae comprises approximately 30 genera
of predominantly epiphytic mosses distributed worldwide in humid forest regions.

Plants form dense, interwoven mats that drape from tree trunks and large
branches. Stems are robust, 5 to 15 centimeters long, irregularly branched,
and covered with paraphyllia that enhance water retention.

Leaves are large for a moss, reaching 3 to 6 millimeters in length, broadly
ovate to obovate, with a short, double costa. The leaf margins are distinctly
bordered by elongated, thick-walled cells that give the leaf a rigid texture.

Laminal cells are prosenchymatous, 40 to 70 micrometers long, with thickened
cell walls that contribute to the glossy appearance. The cellular architecture
provides mechanical strength and reduces desiccation on exposed bark surfaces.

The seta is short, 5 to 10 millimeters, emerging from a lateral perichaetium.
The capsule is erect and cylindrical, 1.5 to 2.5 millimeters long, with a
reduced or absent peristome, a characteristic feature of the Neckeraceae.

## Distribution and Habitat

Neckera crispa has a primarily European distribution, ranging from the British
Isles and Scandinavia through central Europe to the Mediterranean basin and
eastward to the Carpathians and Balkans. It reaches its greatest abundance in
oceanic and sub-oceanic climate regions.

In North America, the species occurs in disjunct populations in the
Appalachian Mountains and the Pacific Northwest, where it is considered rare
and of conservation concern. It is absent from continental Asia.

The moss is an obligate epiphyte, growing almost exclusively on the bark of
mature broadleaf trees, particularly [[quercus-robur]], [[fagus-sylvatica]], and
[[betula-pendula]]. It shows strong preference for old-growth trees with rough,
moisture-retaining bark and is rarely found on trees under 80 years of age.

Optimal habitats are old-growth deciduous and mixed forests with high atmospheric
humidity, closed canopies, and minimal disturbance. The species is highly
sensitive to air pollution and forest management practices that remove old trees.

## Ecology

Neckera crispa plays a significant role in the epiphytic microhabitat of ancient
trees, creating moisture-retaining mats that support diverse invertebrate
communities. These mats provide shelter for springtails, oribatid mites, and
small insects that form the base of arboreal food webs.

The species contributes substantially to canopy water storage, intercepting
fog drip and rainfall that slowly percolates to the forest floor. This water
redistribution function is especially important in oceanic forests where
throughfall contributes significantly to soil moisture.

[[orthotrichum-anomalum]] and [[orthotrichum-diaphanum]] commonly grow within
Neckera mats, forming multi-species epiphytic communities on ancient tree trunks.
The moss canopy provides a buffered microclimate that benefits these smaller
and more desiccation-sensitive species.
