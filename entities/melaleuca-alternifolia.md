## Overview

**[[melaleuca]] alternifolia**, commonly known as narrow-leaved paperbark or tea tree, is a small tree in the family Myrtaceae native to eastern Australia.

The species is the primary commercial source of tea tree oil, one of the most widely used essential oils in dermatological and cosmetic products.

Tea tree oil is renowned for its broad-spectrum [[eucalyptus-globulus]], [[pimenta-dioica]].

Melaleuca alternifolia is a small tree or tall shrub, typically 4–7 meters in height, occasionally reaching 10 meters.

Bark is papery, layered, and white to light brown, peeling in characteristic sheets — a feature shared with other Melaleuca species.

Leaves are alternate, narrow-lanceolate, 10–35 mm long and 1–3 mm wide, with a prominent midrib.

The leaves are aromatic when crushed, releasing the characteristic fresh, medicinal scent of tea tree oil.

Flowers are borne in dense, creamy-white terminal spikes, 2–5 cm long, composed of numerous small stamens.

Flowering occurs from spring to early summer, with each flower spike containing 20–60 individual flowers.

Fruit is a small, woody, cup-shaped capsule approximately 3–4 mm in diameter, clustered along the stems.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is endemic to a relatively narrow coastal strip of eastern Australia, from the Clarence River district of northern New South Wales to southern Queensland.

It grows naturally in swampy lowland areas, along stream banks, and in seasonally inundated coastal wetlands.

Native populations are found at elevations from near sea level to approximately 200 meters.

The preferred habitat consists of acidic, sandy or peaty soils in areas of high rainfall and poor drainage.

Natural stands often occur in heathland or open forest communities, sometimes forming dense thickets in waterlogged areas.

The species tolerates occasional waterlogging and periodic flooding, reflecting its riparian and wetland origins.

## Cultivation and Oil Production

Commercial cultivation of M. alternifolia for essential oil production began in the 1920s in northern New South Wales.

The industry declined after World War II with the advent of synthetic antibiotics but experienced a major resurgence from the 1980s onward.

Plantations are now established across northern New South Wales and southern Queensland, with Australia producing over 80% of the world's tea tree oil.

Propagation is by seed or vegetative cuttings, with clonal propagation preferred for maintaining high-oil-yielding chemotypes.

Harvesting is typically done by mechanical cutting of the aerial biomass, which regenerates from the cut stumps.

Oil is extracted by [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], Europe, and Asia.

Optimal oil yield occurs when plants are harvested every 12–18 months, maintaining active vegetative growth.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

Tea tree oil is a complex mixture of approximately 100–115 identified terpene compounds.

The primary constituent is terpinen-4-ol, which should constitute 30–48% of the oil according to the ISO 4730 standard.

γ-terpinene typically accounts for 10–28% of the oil, and α-terpinene for 5–13%.
