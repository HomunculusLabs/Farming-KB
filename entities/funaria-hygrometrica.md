## Overview

**Funaria hygrometrica**, commonly known as cord moss, is a widespread acrocarpous moss in the family Funariaceae.

It is among the most recognizable bryophytes due to its distinctive asymmetric, pear-shaped capsules that bend sharply at the seta when dry.

The specific epithet *hygrometrica* refers to its hygroscopic setae, which twist and untwist in response to atmospheric humidity changes.

This species is a classic early-successional pioneer, colonizing disturbed soils, burned ground, and anthropogenic habitats worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Funaria hygrometrica* was described by Hedwig in 1801 and remains the type species of the genus *Funaria*.

The gametophyte is small, typically 3–10 mm tall, with a sparse mat of erect, sparsely branched stems.

Leaves are ovate to spatulate, 2–4 mm long, with a distinct costa extending to or near the leaf apex.

The leaf margins are entire or slightly denticulate near the tip, and leaf cells are rectangular to rhomboidal in the upper lamina.

The sporophyte is the most distinctive feature. The seta is 10–25 mm long, yellowish, and strongly hygroscopic.

The capsule is asymmetric, pyriform (pear-shaped), and strongly curved or nodding, measuring 2–3.5 mm in length.

The operculum is conical and falls readily when mature, revealing a double peristome of 16 teeth.

Spores are spherical, 12–20 μm in diameter, papillose, and yellowish-brown at maturity.

A well-developed calyptra covers the young capsule, splitting irregularly as the capsule expands.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Funaria hygrometrica* has a cosmopolitan distribution, occurring on every continent including Antarctica.

It is one of the most widely distributed moss species in the world, found from sea level to alpine elevations.

The species colonizes disturbed substrates including burned soil, old campfire rings, recently exposed earth, and brickwork.

It frequently appears on neglected garden pots, greenhouse surfaces, and along paths in urban parks.

Preferred substrates include bare mineral soil, ash, loam, and compacted earth with high nutrient content.

This moss is particularly abundant after wildfires, where it can form extensive bright green carpets within weeks of a burn event.

It is shade-intolerant and typically disappears as taller vegetation and competing bryophytes establish.

## Ecology and Interactions

As an early-successional pioneer species, *F. hygrometrica* plays a significant role in soil stabilization after disturbance.

Its dense protonemal mats bind loose soil particles, reducing erosion on recently exposed surfaces.

The species contributes to initial soil formation by trapping organic debris and facilitating microbial colonization.

[[aquatic-fungi-spore-dispersal-ingold]] is aided by the hygroscopic seta, which twists in dry conditions to scatter spores over greater distances.

The capsule's asymmetric shape and operculum mechanism further enhance dispersal efficiency during dry, windy conditions.

*Funaria* species serve as hosts for various bryophyte-associated microarthropods, including tardigrades and mites.

The protonemal stage is relatively long-lived compared to other mosses, enabling persistent colonization of unstable substrates.

## Traditional Uses and Applications

The hygroscopic movement of the seta has made this species a historical curiosity and teaching tool in botany classrooms.

In traditional European folk medicine, *F. hygrometrica* was occasionally used as a mild diuretic and anti-inflammatory agent.

Some [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] groups used the moss as a poultice for minor wounds, though documentation is limited.

The species has been investigated for its ability to accumulate [[fungal-biosorption-of-heavy-metals-by-filamentous-fungi]], particularly lead and copper, from contaminated soils.

Bioaccumulation studies suggest potential utility as a passive biomonitor for atmospheric heavy metal deposition.

Its rapid [[bloomfield-fungal-life-cycle-spiral-versus-circle]] (germination to sporophyte maturation in 8–12 weeks) makes it a model organism for bryological research.

The species has been used in laboratory studies of gravitropism, phototropism, and spore dormancy in bryophytes.

## Cultivation and Conservation

*Funaria hygrometrica* is easy to cultivate and is sometimes grown as a laboratory specimen for teaching and research.

Cultivation requires minimal [[cotter-compost-substrate-preparation-manure-mushroom-cultivation]] — spores germinate readily on damp soil, peat, or even filter paper.

Optimal conditions include moderate light, high humidity, and temperatures between 15–25 °C.

The species is not considered threatened; its cosmopolitan distribution and pioneer ecology ensure stable populations.

No specific conservation measures are needed, though its presence is often cited as an indicator of soil disturbance.

In greenhouse and horticultural settings, it is sometimes regarded as a minor nuisance when it colonizes pot surfaces.
