## Description

Capillary water is the water held in small soil pore spaces by forces of adhesion (water attracted to soil particle surfaces) and cohesion (water molecules attracted to each other). Unlike gravitational water that drains rapidly through soil, capillary water is retained against gravity and forms a thick film on particle surfaces. It is the primary source of water available to plant roots and is critical for sustaining soil microbial life between rainfall or irrigation events.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Soil physics / Hydrology
- **Category**: Soil water type
- **Distinguished from**: Gravitational water (drains through), Hygroscopic water (too tightly bound for root uptake)
- **Retention force**: Surface tension from adhesion and cohesion

## Types of Soil Water

### Gravitational Water
- Moves through large pore spaces under the force of gravity.
- Drains rapidly through soil like water poured through gravel.
- Temporarily displaces air as it moves through; fresh air is pulled in behind it.
- Mostly unavailable to plants — drains away before roots can absorb it.
- Large pores (sand-sized) promote gravitational water flow.

### Capillary Water
- Held in smaller pore spaces by adhesion (water-to-particle attraction) and cohesion (water-to-water attraction).
- Creates surface tension that forms a thick film on particle surfaces.
- Can move against gravity ("uphill") through capillary action.
- Remains in soil after gravitational water has passed through.
- **Primary water source for plant roots.**
- Retained in silt and clay-sized pore spaces.

### Hygroscopic Water
- Extremely thin film of water (only a few molecules thick) on very small soil particles.
- Held by strong electrical bonds — too concentrated and strong for roots to break.
- Roots cannot absorb this water.
- Critical for microbial life: even in dry conditions, soil particles hold hygroscopic water that allows many microbes to survive and travel.
- Can only be removed by applying significant heat (boiling it off).

## Key Facts

- In good garden soil, approximately half the pore spaces are filled with water (capillary + hygroscopic) and half with air.
- Water movement through soil creates an air exchange: as gravitational water pushes stale air out, fresh air is drawn in from the surface.
- Capillary water availability depends on soil texture: clay has the most capillary water (many small pores), sand the least (few small pores), silt intermediate.
- Loam soil provides the ideal balance of gravitational drainage (from sand) and capillary retention (from silt and clay).
- The presence of carbon dioxide in soil air is a good indicator that soil life is metabolically active and healthy.
- Poor soil porosity leads to anaerobic conditions: all oxygen is consumed by aerobic organisms, and anaerobic organisms take over, producing alcohols and other substances toxic to plant roots.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Understanding capillary water dynamics explains why soil texture matters for both plant growth and fungal colonization: fungi require consistent moisture but also adequate oxygen.
- Overwatering fills all pore spaces with gravitational water, displacing air and creating anaerobic conditions that kill beneficial aerobic fungi and bacteria.
- Mulch and organic matter dramatically increase capillary water retention by adding humus particles with enormous surface area and creating additional micro-pores.
- In mushroom cultivation, substrate moisture content must be carefully managed to maintain capillary water while preserving air spaces — the same balance as in healthy soil.
- Mycorrhizal fungal hyphae effectively extend the plant's capillary water access by transporting water from distant pore spaces back to the root.
- Compost improves both water-holding capacity (more capillary water) and drainage (better aggregate structure for gravitational water movement), achieving the ideal dual function.
- No-till practices preserve the pore structure that holds capillary water; tilling destroys soil aggregates and initially increases drainage at the expense of long-term water retention.

## Practical Applications

- **Finger test**: Squeeze a handful of soil. If it holds together but crumbles when poked, capillary water is adequate. If water drips out, it's saturated with gravitational water.
- **Mulching** reduces evaporation of capillary water from the soil surface.
- **Adding organic matter** (compost, leaf mold) is the single most effective way to increase capillary water retention in sandy soils.
- **Raised beds** improve gravitational drainage while the organic-rich fill material provides excellent capillary water retention.
