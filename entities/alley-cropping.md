## Overview

Alley cropping is an agroforestry practice in which rows of trees or shrubs are
established at regular intervals with agricultural crops cultivated in the
alleys between them. It integrates woody perennials with annual or perennial
crops on the same land unit, optimizing resource use and diversifying farm
production.

The system originated from traditional shifting cultivation practices in
tropical regions and has been refined into a formal agroforestry technology
applicable across temperate and tropical climates. It represents one of the
most widely adopted agroforestry practices globally, promoted for its
economic resilience and environmental benefits.

## Design Principles

Alley cropping systems are defined by their spatial arrangement — trees are
planted in single or multiple rows with alleys of 4–15 meters between tree
rows, depending on tree species, crop requirements, and machinery access.

Key design variables include tree-row orientation (ideally north-south to
minimize shade competition in temperate zones), alley width, tree species
selection, and root-pruning management. The ratio of tree to crop area
determines the balance between woody and [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]], common choices include nitrogen-fixing
species like black walnut (*Juglans nigra*), honey locust (*Gleditsia
triacanthos*), and various *Robinia* species. In the tropics, leguminous
trees such as *Gliricidia sepium*, *[[calliandra-calothyrsus]]* are widely used.

Inter-row crops include cereals (maize, wheat), legumes (soybeans, beans),
forages (alfalfa, clover), and specialty crops (vegetables, medicinal
herbs). The choice depends on shade tolerance, root competition dynamics,
and market demand.

Compatible species combinations minimize below-ground competition. Deep-
rooted trees paired with shallow-rooted crops reduce direct resource
competition. Some tree species also provide allelopathic weed suppression,
reducing crop management inputs.
