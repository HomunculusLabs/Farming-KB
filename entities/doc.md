## DOC

## Overview

DOC (2,5-dimethoxy-4-chloroamphetamine) is a potent psychedelic amphetamine belonging to the DOx family of substituted amphetamines. It is one of the most well-known and widely encountered members of this class, which also includes DOI, DOB, and DOM. The compound is distinguished by its combination of a chloro substituent at the four-position of the phenyl ring with methoxy groups at the two and five positions.

DOC was first synthesized by [[serotonin-receptor-interference-psychedelic-mechanism]], the same receptor responsible for the psychedelic effects of LSD and psilocybin. DOC exhibits high binding affinity for this receptor, which accounts for its potency at very low doses. The compound also shows activity at other serotonin receptor subtypes, contributing to its complex pharmacological and subjective effect profile.

The extremely long duration of DOC effects represents both its most distinctive characteristic and its greatest practical liability. Users frequently underestimate the duration, leading to difficult experiences when effects persist well beyond the expected timeframe. The extended duration also means that adverse effects, once they begin, may continue for many hours without the possibility of early termination. This characteristic has made DOC one of the more challenging psychedelics to use responsibly.

DOC typically appears as a white crystalline powder or is encountered on blotter paper, where it is applied in solution and allowed to absorb into the paper matrix. The blotter paper format is common because the active dose is so low that weighing individual doses on standard equipment is impractical. Liquid formulations in dropper bottles have also been encountered, allowing for volumetric dosing.

The compound has appeared sporadically in recreational drug markets since the early 2000s, often sold as LSD or misrepresented as other psychedelics. This misrepresentation carries significant risks, as the dose-response curve and duration of DOC differ substantially from those of LSD. Several hospitalizations have resulted from users expecting a standard LSD experience but receiving DOC at doses far exceeding the expected range.

Harm-reduction organizations including the DanceSafe and Erowid networks have documented multiple incidents of DOC misrepresentation. In some cases, blotter paper containing DOC was sold at doses of 2 to 3 milligrams per tab, representing a full psychedelic dose. Users accustomed to LSD doses in the 100 to 200 microgram range who consumed these tabs experienced unexpectedly intense and prolonged effects. Testing services using reagent kits can differentiate DOC from LSD, as the two compounds produce distinct color reactions with Ehrlich and Marquis reagents.

## Chemical Properties

DOC has the molecular formula C11H16ClNO2 and a molar mass of 229.70 g/mol. The IUPAC name is 1-(4-chloro-2,5-dimethoxyphenyl)propan-2-amine, reflecting the substitution pattern on the phenyl ring and the amphetamine backbone. The CAS Registry Number for DOC is 24686-79-9.

The molecule is built on an amphetamine core with three substituents on the phenyl ring: a chlorine atom at the four-position and methoxy groups at the two and five positions. This 2,5-dimethoxy-4-substituted pattern is the defining structural motif of the DOx class and is critical for 5-HT2A receptor affinity. The chlorine atom at the para position is responsible for the compound's high potency and extended duration compared to bromine-substituted (DOB) or iodine-substituted (DOI) analogues.

The hydrochloride salt of DOC is a white crystalline solid with moderate water solubility. The freebase form is an oily liquid. The compound has a calculated log P of approximately 3.0 for the freebase, indicating substantial lipophilicity that supports efficient blood-brain barrier penetration. The methoxy and chloro substituents both contribute to the compound's overall lipophilicity and metabolic stability.

The SMILES notation for DOC is CC(N)CC1=C(OC)C=C(C=C1OC)Cl, encoding the complete substitution pattern on the aromatic ring. The electron-donating methoxy groups and the electron-withdrawing chlorine create a push-pull electronic system across the aromatic ring, which influences both [[spawn-storage-conditions]] when protected from light and moisture. The compound is not volatile and does not decompose at room temperature. [[optical-isomers-psychedelic-phenethylamines]].

Radioligand binding studies have demonstrated that DOC has a Ki value in the low nanomolar range at the 5-HT2A receptor, comparable to or exceeding that of LSD. The compound shows approximately 10-fold selectivity for 5-HT2A over 5-HT2C and variable affinity for 5-HT2B. The 5-HT2B activity is of concern because chronic agonism at this receptor has been associated with valvular heart disease, though the acute use pattern of DOC makes this a theoretical rather than practical risk.

The methoxy groups at positions 2 and 5 of the phenyl ring are critical for high-affinity 5-HT2A binding, as established by extensive structure-activity relationship studies across the phenethylamine class. The 4-substituent modulates both potency and duration, with larger halogens (iodine in DOI, bromine in DOB) generally producing longer durations but similar potencies. The chlorine in DOC occupies a middle position in this series, providing high potency with a somewhat shorter duration than DOI or DOB.

DOC also exhibits weak releasing activity at serotonin and dopamine transporters, though this effect is minimal at psychedelic doses and contributes little to the overall [[psychedelic-phenethylamines-and-pihkal]], which may contribute to the intensity of the subjective experience at relatively low doses. This high intrinsic activity distinguishes DOC from partial agonists that produce milder effects even at saturating receptor occupancy.

## Effects

The onset of effects following oral administration occurs within 1 to 3 hours, which is notably slower than LSD or psilocybin. Peak effects are typically reached at 3 to 5 hours after ingestion. The total duration of action ranges from 12 to 24 hours, with residual effects sometimes persisting for an additional 6 to 12 hours. This extended duration makes DOC one of the longest-acting classical psychedelics.

Visual effects are the hallmark of the DOC experience and include geometric patterning, color enhancement, trailing and afterimagery, and at higher doses, immersive hallucinatory scenarios. The visual quality is often described as more crystalline and less organic than psilocybin, with a distinctive angular and geometric character. Open-eye visuals may become prominent enough to interfere with normal perception, while closed-eye visuals can be highly elaborate.

Cognitive effects include profound alterations in thought patterns, ego dissolution at higher doses, enhanced introspection, and synesthesia. Users frequently report philosophical insights and novel perspectives on personal issues during the experience. However, the extended duration means that difficult psychological material may surface and persist for many hours, requiring sustained psychological resilience from the user.

Physical effects include mydriasis, increased heart rate, elevated blood pressure, mild hyperthermia, increased perspiration, and reduced appetite. Pupil dilation is typically pronounced and may persist for the entire duration of the experience. Nausea is sometimes reported during the onset phase but usually subsides as peak effects are reached. Vasoconstriction may produce cold extremities in some users.

The active dose range for DOC is exceptionally narrow by psychedelic standards. Threshold effects begin at approximately 0.5 mg, with a light dose at 1.0 to 1.5 mg, a common dose at 1.5 to 2.5 mg, and a strong dose at 2.5 to 4.0 mg. Doses above 4.0 mg are considered high and carry significant risk of overwhelming [[blesching-cannabis-hypertension-cardiovascular-effects]] including tachycardia and hypertension are generally manageable in healthy individuals but may pose risks to those with pre-existing cardiac conditions.

Hyperthermia has been reported with DOC use, particularly in settings involving physical activity and warm environments. While the hyperthermic potential of DOC is substantially less than that of stimulant drugs such as MDMA, the combination of 5-HT2A agonism and mild sympathomimetic activity can produce significant temperature elevation. Severe hyperthermia is rare but has been documented in case reports involving high doses or polydrug use.

Vasoconstriction related to 5-HT2A receptor activation in peripheral vasculature has been reported and may contribute to extremity numbness and coldness. In severe cases, this vasoconstriction could theoretically contribute to ischemic complications, though such events have not been frequently documented with DOC specifically. The vasoconstrictive effects are generally less pronounced than those of stimulant amphetamines.

The potential for DOC to trigger persistent psychotic episodes in susceptible individuals is a significant concern. Individuals with a personal or family history of psychotic disorders, particularly schizophrenia, are at elevated risk for drug-induced psychosis that may not resolve with abstinence. The extended duration of DOC means that any psychotic decompensation will also be prolonged, potentially requiring extended psychiatric hospitalization.

No fatalities have been definitively attributed to DOC alone in the medical literature, though the compound's rarity makes comprehensive toxicological assessment difficult. The absence of significant cardiotoxic or neurotoxic mechanisms at recreational doses distinguishes DOC from more dangerous substituted amphetamines. However, the behavioral toxicity of the drug, particularly the potential for accidental injury during altered states lasting 12 to 24 hours, represents a real and documented risk.

**Legal status:** DOC is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance in the United States. It is controlled under the United Nations Convention on Psychotropic Substances as a member of the substituted amphetamine class. Many countries have scheduled DOC either individually or as part of broader phenethylamine or DOx analogue controls. China has banned its production and export. The United Kingdom classifies it as a Class A drug under the Misuse of Drugs Act.

Drug interactions of particular concern include serotonergic agents, which may increase the risk of serotonin syndrome when combined with DOC. MAO inhibitors should be strictly avoided due to the risk of hypertensive crisis and exaggerated psychedelic effects. The combination with stimulants may increase cardiovascular strain. Lithium has been reported to potentiate the effects of psychedelics unpredictably and should be avoided.

DOC was first synthesized by Alexander Shulgin and introduced to the public through PiHKAL, published in 1991. Shulgin assigned the compound the number 34 in his phenethylamine numbering system.
