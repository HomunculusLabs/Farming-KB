## Description

The [[brush-turkey]] (*Alectura lathami*), also referred to as the scrub turkey or bush turkey, is a large ground-dwelling bird of the family Megapodiidae native to eastern Australia. It is one of the most well-known megapode species, recognised for its remarkable nest-building behaviour in which the male constructs and maintains a large composting mound from decomposing vegetation to incubate eggs. The species plays a significant ecological role in forest nutrient cycling and soil formation, and has adapted well to suburban environments along Australia's eastern coast.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Animalia
- **Phylum**: Chordata
- **Class**: Aves
- **Order**: Galliformes
- **Family**: Megapodiidae
- **Genus**: *Alectura*
- **Species**: *A. lathami*
- **Common names**: Australian brush turkey, scrub turkey, bush turkey

## Key Facts

- The male compacts a heap of rain-wet vegetation in an excavated pit, covers it, and allows females to approach and lay eggs when the mound temperature reaches approximately 35 degrees C (95 degrees F).
- The male actively tends the nest, adding material to increase temperature or opening cooling vents to regulate incubation conditions.
- The nest section functions as a large underground compost pile, maintaining a steady internal temperature of about 30 degrees C.
- In documented cases, brush turkey nests have persisted for thousands of years. One rainforest clump in Central York Peninsula (Australia), approximately one mile across, was found centred on a megapode compost-mound nest carbon-dated to about 5,000 years.
- The species distributes organic material and creates nutrient-rich soil microsites that support plant colonisation in surrounding areas.
- Commonly found in rainforest margins, wet sclerophyll forests, and suburban areas along Australia's eastern coast.
- Mounds can be enormous — related species *Megapodius freycinet* builds mounds up to 12 x 5 metres.

## Ecological Role

The brush turkey is an ecosystem engineer. Its large composting mounds create zones of intense biological activity, concentrating nutrients and microbial life. Over time, these mounds become nuclei for vegetation establishment. Mollison describes how abandoned brush turkey nests in arid savannahs have served as the foundation for rainforest clumps, with the oldest vegetation found closest to the nest and progressively younger growth radiating outward.

Fruit pigeons and other birds deposit seeds at these nutrient-rich sites during migration, establishing diverse plant communities. The mounds also serve as thermal regulators in the landscape, maintaining warm, humid microenvironments that support microbial decomposition and mycelial networks even during dry periods. This creates persistent soil fertility that accumulates over centuries.

## Relationship to Other Megapodes

The brush turkey is one of approximately 20 megapode species worldwide. Related species mentioned by Mollison include:

- **Scrub turkey** (*Megapodius freycinet*): Constructs mounds measuring up to 12 x 5 metres.
- **Mallee fowl** (*Leipoa ocellata*): Builds smaller but deeper mounds in arid zones, 5-6 x 1-2 metres, buried in sand.

All megapodes share the characteristic of using external heat sources — either microbial decomposition or geothermal activity — rather than body heat for egg incubation. This represents one of the most unusual reproductive strategies in the avian world and demonstrates a deep evolutionary adaptation to specific environmental conditions.

## Permaculture Design Lessons

The brush turkey's behaviour provides several specific design lessons:

- **Compost heat utilisation**: The bird's mound demonstrates how organic decomposition can be harnessed for temperature regulation, applicable to greenhouse heating, hotbed construction, and seedling propagation systems.
- **Nucleus planting strategy**: The pattern of vegetation expanding outward from the compost mound mirrors the permaculture strategy of establishing small, intensive nuclei and expanding the perimeter over time.
- **Passive energy systems**: The self-regulating nature of the compost mound offers a model for passive temperature management in designed systems.
- **Nutrient concentration in arid zones**: The bird demonstrates how organic matter can be concentrated to create fertile oases in otherwise resource-poor environments.
- **Mycelial web development**: The mound's interior provides ideal conditions for fungal colonisation, supporting the mycelial networks that are critical for tree establishment.
- **Swale-compost integration**: Bury organic matter in contour swales to replicate the compost mound strategy at landscape scale.
- **Multi-species recruitment**: The natural pattern of birds depositing seeds at compost sites suggests designing systems that attract seed-dispersing wildlife to planting sites.

## Desert Reforestation Application

Mollison uses the brush turkey's compost nest as a direct model for desert reforestation. The strategy involves:

1. Grade wide hollows on contour in desert landscapes.
2. Bury mixed organic matter (chips, logs, manure, straw) under 1.0-1.5 m of sand.
3. Wait for rain to initiate decomposition.
4. Plant pioneer legume trees into the buried organic matter.
5. The deep mulch holds water at tree roots while developing mycelial networks.
