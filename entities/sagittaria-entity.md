## Sagittaria (Duck Potato / Arrowhead)

## Description

Sagittaria is a genus of aquatic and marginal perennial plants commonly
known as arrowhead (for the distinctive leaf shape) or duck potato (for
the edible tubers produced by several species). Found in wetlands, shallow
ponds, marshes, and slow-moving waterways across much of the world,
Sagittaria species are valued in [[bill-mollison]] specifically
identifies duck potato (Sagittaria) as a summer-dry forage resource,
indicating its value in seasonal water systems where receding water levels
expose previously submerged tubers for animal harvest.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Alismatales
- **Family:** Alismataceae
- **Genus:** Sagittaria
- **Notable Species:** S. latifolia (wapato, duck potato), S. sagittifolia
  (arrowhead), S. cuneata (arumleaf arrowhead)

## Key Facts

- Aquatic or marginal perennial, typically 30-100 cm tall
- Distinctive arrow-shaped leaves (sagittate), especially in mature plants
- Produces edible tubers (corms) at the ends of underground stolons
- Tubers are starchy and nutritious, comparable to potatoes in caloric value
- Native peoples across North America harvested wapato as a staple food
- Grows in shallow water (5-50 cm depth) and saturated mud
- White three-petaled flowers bloom in whorls on emergent stalks in summer
- Dies back to tubers in autumn; regrows from tubers in spring
- Valuable waterfowl food—ducks, geese, and swans consume tubers and seeds
- Supports diverse aquatic invertebrate populations

## Relevance to Cultivation

### Summer-Dry Forage

Mollison highlights Sagittaria as a forage resource available during
summer-dry conditions. In [[permaculture-water-systems]] designed with
seasonal fluctuation, water levels recede through summer, exposing the
mud flats where Sagittaria tubers grow. Poultry, particularly ducks, can
then access and harvest the tubers directly. This creates a self-harvesting
feed system timed to coincide with periods when other forage may be scarce.

### Food Production

Sagittaria tubers have been a significant human food source for millennia:

- **Tubers:** Harvested by hand or foot from mud, then baked, boiled, or
  roasted. Nutty flavour, texture similar to chestnuts or potatoes
- **Nutritional profile:** High in starch, moderate protein, good source of
  potassium and iron
- **Harvest timing:** Best harvested autumn through early spring when
  plants have stored energy in tubers

The traditional harvesting method involved wading into shallow water and
dislodging tubers with the feet, allowing them to float to the surface.

### Permaculture Design Roles

- **Duck forage:** Tubers and foliage consumed by domestic ducks and geese
- **Summer-dry feed resource:** Available when other forage is limited
- **Water purification:** Root systems filter nutrients from water
- **Habitat:** Provides cover and food for aquatic organisms and waterfowl
- **Food crop:** Productive starchy tuber for human consumption
- **Dam and pond planting:** Useful in designed water storage edges

### Companion Planting

Sagittaria integrates naturally with other wetland species in permaculture
water systems:

- **Typha (cattail):** Shares similar habitat; both useful in rested
  pig wallows and pond margins
- **Water chestnut:** Similar niche in shallow water cultivation
- **Watercress:** Occupies flowing water edges alongside Sagittaria
- **Duck systems:** Planted in ponds managed for duck foraging

### Cultivation Notes

- Plant tubers 5-10 cm deep in saturated soil or shallow water
- Requires at least 10-30 cm of water during active growing season
- Prefers full sun; tolerates partial shade
- Adaptable to various substrates from mud to sandy loam
- Propagates readily from tuber division
- Can spread aggressively in ideal conditions—manage by harvesting
- Hardy in USDA zones 3-10, depending on species
- Winter-dormant in cold climates; evergreen in frost-free regions

## Summary

Sagittaria, or duck potato, is a productive aquatic perennial that
Mollison identifies as a valuable summer-dry forage resource in
permaculture systems. Its starchy tubers provide food for both humans and
waterfowl, while its growth in shallow water and seasonal mud flats makes
it an ideal component of designed wetlands, duck ponds, and integrated
animal-water systems.
