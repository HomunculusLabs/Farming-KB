    10|    10|# Caragana arborescens
    11|    11|
    12|    12|**Common names:** Siberian peashrub, Siberian pea tree, pea shrub, caragana
    13|    13|
    14|    14|## Overview
    15|    15|
    16|16|**Caragana arborescens** is a deciduous, nitrogen-fixing shrub or small tree in the family Fabaceae, native to Siberia, Mongolia, and northern China.
    17|It is one of the hardiest and most versatile woody legumes in temperate permaculture systems, valued for its exceptional cold tolerance, windbreak performance, edible pods and seeds, nitrogen fixation, and drought resilience.
    18|Widely planted across the northern hemisphere for shelterbelts, erosion control, and as a fodder crop.
    19|    17|
    20|    18|## Botanical Description
    21|    19|
    22|20|Siberian peashrub typically grows 3 to 6 meters tall as a multi-stemmed shrub, occasionally reaching 8 meters as a single-trunked small tree.
    23|The bark is smooth and olive-green on young stems, aging to gray-brown with longitudinal fissures.
    24|Branches are slender, often slightly pendulous, with paired thorns at the nodes that are 3 to 8 mm long.
    25|Leaves are pinnately compound with 8 to 14 pairs of oblong leaflets, each 1 to 3 cm long, bright green above and paler below, giving the foliage a fine, feathery texture.
    26|The most striking feature is the bright yellow, pea-like flowers, 2 to 3 cm long, borne singly or in pairs from the leaf axils in May to June.
    27|Flowers are followed by linear pods 3 to 5 cm long that turn brown at maturity, containing 4 to 8 reniform seeds 2 to 3 mm in diameter.
    28|    21|
    29|    22|## Distribution and Habitat
    30|    23|
    31|24|Native to the steppes, forest-steppes, and river valleys of Siberia, Mongolia, northern China, and Kazakhstan, occurring naturally from the Altai Mountains to Lake Baikal and eastward to Manchuria.
    32|Introduced to North America in the 1750s and now naturalized across the northern Great Plains, Canadian Prairies, and parts of the northeastern United States.
    33|Extremely cold-hardy, surviving winter temperatures below minus 45 degrees Celsius, making it suitable for USDA Zone 2 and colder.
    34|Thrives on well-drained sandy to loamy soils, tolerating alkaline, saline, and nutrient-poor conditions.
    35|Drought-tolerant once established but performs best with moderate moisture.
    36|    25|
    37|    26|## Nitrogen Fixation
    38|    27|
    39|28|As a member of the Fabaceae, Siberian peashrub forms root nodules with Rhizobium bacteria for atmospheric nitrogen fixation.
    40|Nitrogen fixation rates of 20 to 60 kg N per hectare per year have been reported, comparable to or exceeding many other temperate woody legumes.
    41|The nodules are visible as pink to reddish clusters on fine lateral roots.
    42|Effective nodulation requires compatible Rhizobium strains; in regions where caragana is not native, inoculation may improve establishment.
    43|The nitrogen contribution benefits neighboring plants through root exudation, leaf litter decomposition, and root turnover.
    44|    29|
    45|    30|## Edible Uses
    46|    31|
    47|32|The young pods are edible when harvested green and tender, before the seeds harden, typically 2 to 3 weeks after flowering.
    48|They can be eaten raw, steamed, boiled, or added to stir-fries, with a mild pea-like flavor.
    49|The mature seeds can be cooked as a pulse similar to lentils or chickpeas, though they require boiling in two changes of water to remove mild bitterness.
    50|Seeds contain approximately 30 percent protein, 10 percent fat, and 35 percent carbohydrate on a dry weight basis.
    51|Flowers are edible and can be added to salads. Caragana was historically an important food plant in Siberia and Mongolia.
    52|    33|
    53|    34|## Fodder and Animal Feed
    54|    35|
    55|36|Siberian peashrub is a high-quality fodder species, particularly valued in cold, arid regions where other forage options are limited.
    56|Leaves and young stems contain 16 to 22 percent crude protein and are readily browsed by cattle, sheep, goats, deer, and rabbits.
    57|It provides palatable, nutritious forage during late autumn and early winter when other browse is scarce.
    58|In shelterbelt plantings, it doubles as livestock wind protection and emergency feed.
    59|    37|
    60|    38|## Permaculture Functions
    61|    39|
    62|    40|### Windbreak and Shelterbelt
    63|41|Caragana is one of the premier windbreak species for cold, windy, open sites across the northern Great Plains and boreal regions.
    64|Its dense, low-branching habit provides effective ground-level wind reduction.
    65|It tolerates severe wind exposure, ice loading, and heavy snow accumulation.
    66|It is a standard component of prairie shelterbelt systems across the Canadian Prairies and northern US states.
    67|    42|
    68|    43|### Nitrogen-Fixing Canopy Support
    69|44|In food forest systems, caragana serves as a nitrogen-fixing overstory or middle canopy element, feeding understory fruit and nut crops.
    70|Its dappled shade is compatible with berry bushes and shade-tolerant perennials.
    71|    45|
    72|    46|### Erosion Control
    73|47|The extensive, deep root system stabilizes slopes, roadcuts, and degraded soils.
    74|It is planted on mine spoils, gully walls, and sand dunes for reclamation.
    75|    48|
    76|    49|### Hedgerow and Barrier
    77|50|The thorny branches and dense growth form effective livestock barriers, privacy screens, and wildlife corridors.
    78|Caragana tolerates shearing and can be maintained as a formal hedge at 1.5 to 2 meters.
    79|    51|
    80|    52|### Bee Forage
    81|53|The abundant yellow flowers are a major nectar and pollen source for honey bees and bumblebees in early summer, producing a light, mild-flavored honey.
    82|    54|
    83|    55|## Cultivation
    84|    56|
    85|57|Propagation is straightforward from seed after scarification and cold stratification for 30 to 60 days.
    86|Direct seeding in early spring is successful on prepared sites. Hardwood cuttings root at 50 to 70 percent under mist.
    87|Transplants establish rapidly with a 60 to 80 percent survival rate. Plant spacing is 1.5 to 3 meters within rows.
    88|Growth is rapid, reaching 1 to 2 meters in the first three years.
    89|Caragana responds well to coppicing and can be cut back hard to rejuvenate aging plants.
    90|    58|
    91|    59|## Longevity and Management
    92|    60|
    93|61|Individual plants live 40 to 60 years in favorable conditions.
    94|Productivity declines after 25 to 30 years but can be extended through periodic coppicing at 10 to 15 year intervals.
    95|Caragana can become weedy in disturbed grasslands through seed dispersal and root suckering, though it is less aggressive than many other nitrogen-fixing shrubs.
    96|    62|
    97|    63|## Companion Planting and Polyculture Integration
    98|    64|
    99|65|Siberian peashrub serves as an effective nitrogen-fixing nurse tree for establishing fruit and nut [[food-forest-design]]ood forest design, it is positioned as the upper canopy in early succession, providing shade, wind protection, and nitrogen to establishing layers below.
   101|Compatible companions include apple, plum, cherry, and hazelnut as the eventual main canopy, with currants, gooseberries, and herbs in the understory.
   102|Caragana is also combined with sea buckthorn and autumn olive in multi-species windbreaks for enhanced biodiversity and resilience.
   103|Its early leaf emergence in spring and late leaf retention in autumn extend the period of photosynthesis and nitrogen contribution.
   104|    66|
   105|    67|## Pruning and Coppice Management
   106|    68|
   107|69|Caragana responds vigorously to coppicing, producing dense regrowth from the stump.
   108|Coppice cycles of 5 to 8 years maintain vigorous growth, maximize pod and biomass production, and extend plant lifespan beyond the natural senescence point.
   109|Coppiced material provides excellent woody mulch high in nitrogen and can be chipped for path surfacing.
   110|Pollarding at 2 to 3 meters produces a high canopy that allows underplanting while maintaining windbreak function.
   111|Thinning of multi-stemmed clumps improves air circulation and reduces disease pressure.
   112|    70|
   113|    71|## Seed Production and Collection
   114|    72|
   115|73|Pods are harvested when fully dry and brown, typically in July to August.
   116|Mature pods can be threshed by hand or with a flail thresher.
   117|Clean seed stores well for 3 to 5 years at cool, dry conditions.
   118|Seed yields average 20 to 40 kg per hectare from established plantings.
   119|Germination after cold stratification typically exceeds 80 percent for fresh seed.
   120|The seed coat is hard and benefits from scarification with hot water or mechanical nicking before stratification.
   121|    74|
   122|    75|## Propagation Techniques
   123|    76|
   124|    77|### Seed Propagation
   125|78|Scarify seeds by soaking in 80 degree Celsius water for 12 hours, then cold stratify at 1 to 5 degrees Celsius for 30 to 60 days.
   126|Sow in nursery beds at 2 cm depth, 5 cm spacing in rows 30 cm apart. Germination occurs in 7 to 14 days.
   127|Seedlings are transplanted after 1 to 2 years at 30 to 60 cm height.
   128|    79|
   129|    80|### Vegetative Propagation
   130|81|Hardwood cuttings of 15 to 25 cm with 3 to 5 buds, taken in late autumn after leaf fall, root at 50 to 70 percent under mist with hormone treatment.
   131|Root cuttings of 5 to 10 cm planted horizontally in spring produce new shoots reliably.
   132|Division of established clumps in early spring is effective for small-scale propagation.
   133|    82|
   134|    83|## Historical and Cultural Significance
   135|    84|
   136|85|Caragana has been used in Siberian and Mongolian agriculture for centuries as a windbreak, fodder, and food source.
   137|The genus name derives from the Mongolian word caragan meaning black.
   138|Russian settlers in Siberia relied on caragana pods as a protein supplement during long winters.
   139|It was introduced to North America by Eurasian immigrants in the 18th century and adopted by prairie agricultural agencies in the 20th century as the primary shelterbelt species for the Great Plains.
   140|The Prairie Farm Rehabilitation Administration planted millions of caragana seedlings across the Canadian Prairies from the 1930s through the 1970s.
