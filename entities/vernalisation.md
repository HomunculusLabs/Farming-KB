# Vernalisation

## Description

Vernalisation (also spelled vernalization) is the process by which prolonged exposure to cold temperatures induces flowering in plants. It is a critical technique in horticulture and permaculture for ensuring that cold-climate seeds and biennial crops properly germinate, break dormancy, and proceed to flowering. Related to but distinct from **stratification**, vernalisation specifically refers to the cold-induction of the [[mollison-designers-day-length-and-flowering-response]] rather than mere dormancy-breaking.

## Classification

- **Category**: Horticultural technique / Plant physiology
- **Related processes**: Stratification, seed dormancy, thermoperiodism, photoperiodism
- **Temperature range**: Typically 0–5°C (32–40°F)
- **Duration**: Weeks to months depending on species

## Key Facts

- Cold-area seeds, especially tree and berry seeds from boreal or cold climates, must spend autumn-to-spring periods in refrigeration when moved to warmer climates.
- Apple seeds stored in sand and chestnuts in peat sprout through cold stratification, mimicking natural litter-level exposure in cold forests or marshes.
- Wild rice and other aquatic seeds are stored in open ponds or under water in refrigerators.
- Vernalisation reproduces the natural cold exposure (0–5°C / 32–40°F) that seeds experience at litter level in cold forests or marshes.
- It can be accomplished by keeping seeds in sand, peat, or water in cold shaded valleys, or under open cool trellises in warm climates.
- Seeds should be checked in late winter and spring for signs of germination.

## Relationship to Stratification

Stratification is the broader process of subjecting seeds to moist cold conditions to break dormancy. Vernalisation is a more specific term referring to the cold-induced transition from vegetative to reproductive growth. In practice, the two processes often overlap in seed treatment workflows. Many perennial and biennial plants require vernalisation to flower — without sufficient cold hours, they remain vegetative indefinitely.

## Heat Treatment Contrast

The opposite technique is heat treatment, applied to many tree legume seeds:
- Oven heating at 95°C (200°F) for 10–20 minutes
- Pouring near-boiling water over seeds
- Burning seeds in a light straw fire

These methods crack hard seed coats and promote germination in species adapted to fire-prone ecosystems.

## Relevance to Cultivation

- **Essential for**: Apple, chestnut, berry seeds, wild rice, cold-climate tree species
- **Practical application**: Refrigerator stratification in warm climates; outdoor sand/peat beds in cool climates
- **Design implication**: [[bill-mollison]] must account for vernalisation requirements when selecting species for warm-climate installations that originate from cold climates
- **Companion technique**: Works alongside scarification (acid/alkali treatment, mechanical cracking, passage through animal digestive systems) for comprehensive seed preparation

## Species Examples Requiring Vernalisation

| Species | Method | Notes |
|---------|--------|-------|
| Apple (Malus) | Sand storage, 0–5°C | Pot out as shoots emerge |
| Chestnut (Castanea) | Peat storage, cold | Mimics forest litter conditions |
| Wild rice (Zizania) | Underwater, cold | Open pond or refrigerated |
| Boreal tree seeds | Sand/peat, cold | Autumn to spring cycle |
| Berry seeds (Rubus, Vaccinium) | Moist cold, 0–5°C | Extended periods often needed |
