## Overview

**Ophiopogon japonicus** (Thunb.) Ker Gawl. is an evergreen perennial herb in the family Asparagaceae, widely known by its Chinese name **mai men dong** (麦门冬). The dried tuberous roots are a fundamental herb in traditional Chinese medicine.

The species is native to East Asia and is also extensively cultivated as an ornamental ground cover, particularly the dwarf varieties sold as "mondo grass." It forms dense, low-growing tufts of arching grass-like leaves.

In TCM, *mai men dong* is classified as a yin-tonifying herb that nourishes the lungs, stomach, and heart. It is a key ingredient in classical formulas including *Mai Men Dong Tang* and *Sheng Mai San*.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

O. japonicus was originally described as *Convallaria japonica* by Carl Thunberg in 1784 and later transferred to the genus *Ophiopogon* by John Bellenden Ker Gawler. The genus name derives from Greek *ophis* (snake) and *pogon* (beard), referring to the snake-like rhizomes.

The plant forms dense clumps 15–30 cm tall from a network of creeping rhizomes and fleshy tuberous roots. Linear leaves are 10–40 cm long and 2–4 mm wide, dark green and arching.

Small, lilac to white flowers are borne on short racemes 3–8 cm long, typically hidden within the foliage. Flowering occurs in mid-summer, with each flower approximately 5–7 mm long.

The fruit is a small blue berry 5–8 mm in diameter containing 1–2 seeds. The tuberous roots, which are the primary medicinal organ, are spindle-shaped, 1–3 cm long, and pale yellow-white when dried.

## Distribution and Habitat

O. japonicus is native to China, Japan, and Korea. In China, wild populations occur in provinces south of the Yangtze River, particularly in Sichuan, Zhejiang, and Guizhou.

The species grows naturally in moist forests, grassy slopes, and along stream banks at elevations up to 2000 meters. It tolerates a wide range of light conditions from full sun to deep shade.

Cultivated production is centered in Sichuan and Zhejiang provinces, where the plant is grown in well-drained sandy loam soils. Major cultivation areas use raised beds to prevent waterlogging of the tuberous roots.

The ornamental varieties, especially dwarf forms, are widely planted in gardens globally. The species has become naturalized in parts of the southeastern [[permaculture-designers-manual-essential-oil-plants]] from leaves contains linalool and various sesquiterpenes.

## Pharmacology and Traditional Use

In TCM theory, *mai men dong* targets the lung, stomach, and heart meridians. It is indicated for dry cough, chronic bronchitis, diabetes with thirst, insomnia, and constipation caused by intestinal dryness.

Modern pharmacological research supports several traditional uses. Steroidal saponins exhibit expectorant, anti-inflammatory, and cardioprotective effects in animal models.

Homoisoflavonoids from the roots demonstrate COX-2 inhibitory activity comparable to non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. These compounds also show protective effects against [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] or late autumn. Yields of dried tubers average 1500–3000 kg per hectare.

Post-harvest processing involves washing, removing fibrous roots, and drying. The roots are traditionally processed by brief steaming or sweating before drying, which reduces bitterness and improves storage quality.

## Classical Formulas

*Mai Men Dong Tang* is the principal formula featuring O. japonicus as the chief herb. Originally recorded in the *Jingui Yaolue* (Essentials from the Golden Cabinet, circa 200 CE), it combines *mai men dong* with raw gypsum, rehmannia, licorice, and other herbs.

The formula is traditionally prescribed for lung heat with yin deficiency, presenting as chronic cough with thick sputum, thirst, and a dry mouth. Modern applications include chronic bronchitis and pharyngitis.

*Sheng Mai San* (Pulse-Generating Powder) is another classical formula containing *mai men dong* alongside ginseng and schisandra. It is used to treat qi and yin deficiency with symptoms of fatigue, spontaneous sweating, and a weak pulse.
