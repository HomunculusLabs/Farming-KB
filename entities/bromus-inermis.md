# Bromus inermis

## Overview

**Smooth brome** (*Bromus inermis* Leyss.) is a cool-season perennial rhizomatous grass native to Europe and temperate Asia. Introduced to North America in the 1880s for forage and erosion control, it has become both an important conservation grass and a problematic invasive species.

The species is widely planted across the northern United States and Canadian prairies for hay, pasture, and soil stabilization. Its extensive rhizome system makes it exceptionally effective at controlling erosion on disturbed sites, roadcuts, and waterways.

Smooth brome has been declared invasive in several US states and Canadian provinces, where it displaces native tallgrass prairie communities. Its aggressive rhizomatous spread reduces plant diversity and alters ecosystem function in remnant prairies.

The species was first collected in North America near Halifax, Nova Scotia, in 1884. By the 1930s, it was widely promoted by the US Soil Conservation Service as a solution to Dust Bowl-era erosion, accelerating its spread across the continent.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Bromus inermis* belongs to the family Poaceae, subfamily Pooideae, tribe Bromeae. The specific epithet *inermis* means "unarmed," referring to the lack of awns on the lemma — a trait distinguishing it from most other *Bromus* species.

The **inflorescence** is an open, nodding panicle, 10–25 cm long, with 5–10 flowered spikelets. Spikelets are slender, 15–25 mm long, with glumes unequal in length and lemmas that are prominently 7–9 nerved.

**Leaf blades** are flat, 15–50 cm long and 5–10 mm wide, with a prominent midrib and hairless upper surface. The lower surface may be sparsely hairy. The sheath is closed for most of its length.

The **ligule** is short (1–2 mm) and membranous, with an irregularly toothed margin. Auricles are absent. The collar region is distinct and often yellowish-green.

The root system is exceptionally well-developed, with coarse, scaly rhizomes extending 30–100 cm from the parent plant. Rhizome biomass can exceed aboveground biomass, contributing to the species' drought tolerance and aggressive spread.

Stems are erect, 60–150 cm tall, smooth and hairless — hence the common name "smooth brome." Growth is vigorous in spring and autumn, with a pronounced summer dormancy period under heat stress.

Chromosome number varies, with both diploid (2n = 28) and tetraploid (2n = 56) cytotypes occurring in natural populations. Tetraploid types are more vigorous and dominate commercial cultivar development.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to Europe, the Caucasus, and central Asia, smooth brome was introduced to North America as a forage crop in the 1880s. It is now naturalized across much of southern Canada and the northern United States.

It is widely planted in the US Midwest, Great Plains, and intermountain West for conservation purposes. The USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service has released numerous cultivars for erosion control, wildlife habitat, and mine reclamation.

Optimal growth occurs on deep, fertile, well-drained loam soils in regions receiving 400–800 mm annual precipitation. It is more drought-tolerant than timothy and less heat-sensitive than perennial ryegrass, giving it a broad climatic adaptation.

Smooth brome tolerates soil pH from 5.5 to 8.0 and performs well on both acidic and alkaline soils. It is one of the few cool-season grasses that establishes successfully on semi-arid rangeland sites receiving as little as 350 mm annual precipitation.

It is widely planted in the US Midwest, Great Plains, and intermountain West for conservation purposes.

The species is hardy to USDA Zone 3 (−40 °C), making it one of the most cold-tolerant introduced forage grasses. It survives winterkill events that devastate perennial ryegrass and orchardgrass in continental climates.

## Cultivation

Seed is drilled at 10–15 kg/ha in pure stands or 5–8 kg/ha in mixtures with alfalfa. Optimal sowing depth is 10–20 mm, deeper than many other cool-season grasses due to the larger seed size.

Smooth brome establishes more slowly than perennial ryegrass, typically requiring a full growing season before first harvest. Spring sowing is preferred in continental climates; autumn sowing is recommended in milder maritime regions.

Hay management requires careful timing, as quality declines rapidly after heading. Optimal cutting stage is early heading to boot stage, typically yielding dry matter of 5–10 t/ha/year depending on precipitation.

Nitrogen applications of 80–120 kg N/ha/year sustain productive hay stands. Under rotational grazing, rest periods of 3–4 weeks between defoliations maintain stand vigor and rhizome carbohydrate reserves.

The species persists indefinitely under low-input management, making it popular for marginal lands and conservation plantings. Stands can remain productive for 10+ years without renovation when managed for erosion control rather than intensive forage production.

Major cultivars include 'Lincoln,' 'Carlton,' 'Rebound,' and 'Baylor.' These differ in maturity date, winter hardiness, and disease resistance, allowing regional adaptation across the species' broad geographic range.

## Uses and Conservation Value

Smooth brome is one of the primary species used in Conservation Reserve Program (CRP) plantings across the US Great Plains. It provides rapid ground cover on highly erodible lands and critical wildlife habitat in agricultural landscapes.

CRP contracts using smooth brome typically enroll 10–15 year terms, during which the species establishes dense, persistent cover. Upon contract expiration, converting brome-dominated fields back to native prairie vegetation requires intensive management including herbicide, tillage, and native reseeding.

As forage, smooth brome produces hay of moderate quality with crude protein of 8–14% and digestibility of 60–70%. It is less palatable than timothy or perennial ryegrass, and livestock typically prefer other grass species when available in mixed swards.

Maturity at harvest strongly influences nutritional value. Early-boot-stage cutting yields the best balance of digestibility and dry matter production, while delaying harvest to soft-dough stage increases yield by 20–30% but reduces relative feed value substantially.

The dense root and rhizome network makes smooth brome highly effective at stabilizing streambanks, roadcuts, mine spoils, and other disturbed sites. It tolerates moderate soil salinity and a wide range of soil textures from sands to heavy clays.

Smooth brome provides nesting habitat and winter cover for upland game birds, including pheasants and quail. However, its tendency to form monocultures reduces overall biodiversity compared to native prairie plantings.

Several state DOTs use smooth brome in highway right-of-way seedings for its reliability and low maintenance requirements. It establishes quickly on compacted subsoil and tolerates road salt better than many native grass alternatives.

## Ecological Impact and Invasiveness

In native tallgrass prairie remnants, smooth brome is among the most damaging invasive plants. It spreads through both rhizomatous growth and seed dispersal, forming near-monocultures that displace diverse native plant communities.

Invasion reduces native plant species richness by 50–80% in heavily infested prairie sites. This loss of plant diversity cascades through the food web, reducing habitat quality for native insects, birds, and small mammals.

Pollinator communities are particularly affected, as smooth brome monocultures provide limited floral resources compared to diverse native prairies. Studies in the US Midwest have documented 40–60% reductions in native bee abundance in brome-dominated sites.

Control methods include prescribed fire, herbicide application (glyphosate, imazapic), and intensive grazing management. Integration of fire with herbicide treatment is most effective, as fire alone may stimulate rhizome sprouting and increase brome dominance.

Prevention is the most cost-effective management strategy. Avoiding smooth brome seed in prairie restoration mixes and maintaining buffer zones between planted brome fields and native remnants are critical conservation practices.

Long-term monitoring studies in Minnesota tallgrass prairie have shown that even after 20 years of active management, smooth brome cover remains significantly elevated compared to pre-invasion baselines. Complete eradication from established stands is generally considered impractical.

Research into biological control agents has identified several European pathogens and insects that selectively damage *Bromus* species. However, none have been approved for release due to concerns about non-target impacts on native *Bromus* taxa.

Despite its invasiveness, smooth brome remains a valuable tool for emergency erosion control on severely disturbed sites. The key management challenge is using it appropriately in degraded landscapes while preventing spread into high-quality natural areas.
