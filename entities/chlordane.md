## Description

Chlordane (also spelled chlordan) is a **persistent organochlorine insecticide**
that was widely used in the United States from the 1940s until its cancellation
for most uses by the EPA in 1983 and full ban by 1988. It belongs to the
cyclodiene class of chlorinated hydrocarbon pesticides. Chlordane is notorious
for its extreme environmental persistence — with a soil half-life of 1–20+ years
— and its tendency to bioaccumulate in food chains. In the context of compost
and soil biology, chlordane represents a significant contaminant concern because
it can persist in soils and compost feedstocks for decades after application.

## Classification

- **Chemical class**: Organochlorine insecticide (cyclodiene)
- **Chemical name**: 1,2,4,5,6,7,8,8-Octachloro-2,3,3a,4,7,7a-hexahydro-4,7-
  methano-1H-indene
- **CAS number**: 57-74-9 (technical chlordane)
- **Molecular formula**: C10H6Cl8
- **Regulatory status**: Banned in the U.S. since 1988; listed under the
  Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs)

## Key Facts

### Environmental Persistence

- Chlordane is one of the most persistent pesticides ever manufactured. It binds
  strongly to soil particles and resists chemical and biological degradation.
- Half-life in soil ranges from 1 year to over 20 years depending on conditions,
  with some studies detecting chlordane residues 30+ years after application.
- Commonly found as a residual contaminant in urban soils, particularly near
  foundations of older homes where it was applied for termite control.

### Health and Environmental Concerns

- **Toxicity**: Classified as a probable human carcinogen (EPA Group B2). Affects
  the nervous system, liver, and digestive system.
- **Bioaccumulation**: Accumulates in fatty tissues of animals and moves up the
  food chain. Found in fish, wildlife, and human breast milk.
- **Endocrine disruption**: Evidence suggests chlordane may disrupt endocrine
  function at low doses.

### Bioremediation Potential

- Some soil bacteria and fungi have demonstrated the ability to **degrade
  chlordane** through enzymatic processes. However, these organisms are not
  universally present in all soils or compost starting materials.
- Ingham notes that while chlordane-degrading organisms exist, they are not
  always found in compost feedstocks, making bioremediation unreliable without
  deliberate inoculation.
- This contrasts with **clopyralid** (another persistent herbicide), for which
  no known decomposing organisms have been identified in typical compost
  environments.

### Relevance to Compost Quality

- Chlordane contamination of compost can occur through:
  - Grass clippings from lawns previously treated with chlordane
  - Soil mixed into compost feedstocks from contaminated sites
  - Urban yard waste from properties with historical termite treatments
- Unlike some contaminants that degrade during composting, chlordane"s
  persistence means it can pass through the composting process largely intact.
- Compost testing for chlordane and other organochlorine residues is recommended
  for operations using urban feedstocks on former residential properties.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Soil Food Web Implications

- Persistent pesticides like chlordane can harm soil organisms including
  beneficial fungi, bacteria, nematodes, and microarthropods that are essential
  to [[soil-food-web]].
- Recovery of soil biology on contaminated sites may require active remediation
  through compost tea applications, [[mycorrhizal-inoculation]], and introduction of
  diverse microbial communities.

### Remediation Strategies

- **Bioremediation**: Introducing chlordane-degrading microorganisms via compost
  tea or bioaugmentation products may accelerate degradation, but results are
  inconsistent.
- **Phytoremediation**: Some plants can take up chlordane from soil; combined
  with microbial partners, this approach shows promise but is not yet reliable.
- **Dilution and isolation**: Physically removing contaminated soil or diluting
  with clean compost reduces plant exposure risk.

### Historical Context in Agriculture

- Before its ban, chlordane was extensively used on corn, citrus, deciduous
  fruits, nuts, vegetables, and for termite control around building foundations.
- Legacy contamination remains a concern in agricultural soils near former
  application sites, particularly in the southeastern and midwestern US.
- Organic certification requires testing for persistent pesticide residues when
  there is reason to suspect historical contamination.
