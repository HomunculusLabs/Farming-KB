# Scott Nearing

## Description
Scott Nearing (1883–1983) was an American economist, educator, and homesteader who, with his wife Helen Nearing, pioneered the modern back-to-the-land movement. Their 1954 book *Living the Good Life* documented their self-sufficient homesteading practice in Vermont and later Maine, inspiring generations of organic farmers including Eliot Coleman. In the mid-1960s, Coleman visited Scott and Helen Nearing, became friends with them, and in 1968 they sold him the back part of their Harborside, Maine farm at the same price they had paid twenty years earlier — a transformative act of generosity that enabled Coleman's farming career.

## Key Facts

### The Farm at Harborside, Maine
- Scott and Helen Nearing established their homestead on Cape Rosier, near Harborside, Maine
- The land was wooded with spruce and fir trees, had very sandy acid soil (pH 4.3), and was strewn with glacial stones
- They sold Coleman the back portion of their farm in 1968 at their original purchase price from the 1940s
- Coleman describes this as "truly a gift" since he had very little money at the time
- The land required enormous effort to convert from acidic podzol to productive market-garden soil

### Living the Good Life
- Published in 1954, co-authored with Helen Nearing
- Documented their approach to self-sufficient living, organic gardening, and simple living
- Became a foundational text of the back-to-the-land movement
- Influenced thousands of young people in the 1960s and 1970s to pursue homesteading
- Coleman credits the book with inspiring his interest in organic farming

### Philosophy and Influence
- Advocated economic self-sufficiency, simple living, and manual labor
- Practiced organic gardening methods before the term "organic" was widely used
- Emphasized living in harmony with natural systems
- Their approach was both practical (how to build, grow, preserve) and philosophical (why to live simply)
- Scott Nearing's background as an economist informed his systematic approach to self-sufficiency

### Connection to Coleman's Development
- The Nearings' generosity in selling affordable land made Coleman's farming career possible
- The challenging soil conditions (sandy acid podzol, pH 4.3, rocky) forced Coleman to develop soil-building expertise
- Coleman eventually transformed 1.5 acres of this poor land into "really decent loam" — what he calls "anthropogenic" (human-made) soil
- The connection to Kropotkin's observation that Parisian growers "make the soil themselves" is drawn by Coleman

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Soil Building Legacy
- The Nearings' rocky, acidic Maine land forced development of intensive soil-building techniques
- Coleman's soil building on Nearing land produced the composting and amendment systems he now teaches
- These soil-building techniques (compost, rock powders, seaweed, alfalfa meal) support the soil food web including beneficial fungi
- The transformation from podzol to loam demonstrates that any soil can be improved with sufficient organic matter inputs

### Homesteading and Self-Sufficiency
- The Nearing model of producing one's own fertility (compost, manure) aligns with sustainable mushroom cultivation
- Their emphasis on low-input, locally-sourced materials is relevant to small-scale mushroom operations
- The philosophy of working with natural systems rather than against them applies to working with fungal biology

### Land Access and New Farmer Support
- The Nearings' model of affordable land access for beginning farmers remains relevant
- Their act of selling at original price rather than market value enabled Coleman's career
- This model of mentorship and land access could support new mushroom cultivators entering the field

## Historical Significance
- Scott Nearing's century-long life spanned from the Gilded Age through the early 1980s
- Fired from academia for his anti-war and socialist views, he channeled his energy into homesteading
- The Nearing homestead at Forest Farm became a pilgrimage site for aspiring homesteaders
- Their influence extends through Coleman to the modern organic farming and local food movements
