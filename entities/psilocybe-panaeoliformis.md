# Psilocybe panaeoliformis

**Psilocybe panaeoliformis** is a psilocybin-containing mushroom species in the family strophariaceae, originally described from New Zealand. The species epithet reflects its resemblance to members of the genus Panaeolus, particularly in the mottled or speckled appearance of its gills as spores mature at different times.
It is one of several Australasian Psilocybe species documented in the global survey of magic mushrooms by Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz. The species is of particular interest due to its intermediate morphological features between typical Psilocybe and Panaeolus phenotypes.

## Taxonomy and Etymology

The name *panaeoliformis* is derived from the combination of the genus name *Panaeolus* and the Latin suffix *-formis*, meaning resembling, highlighting the species' superficial similarity to Panaeolus species in its gill coloration pattern.
The species was described from New Zealand collections and has been discussed in the context of Australasian Psilocybe diversity by Guzmán and collaborators. It is placed in Psilocybe section psilocybe-sect.-psilocybe, though some authors have noted intermediate morphological features between this section and other groups within the genus.
The distinctive mottled gill pattern is atypical for section Psilocybe and may indicate an evolutionary convergence with Panaeolus-like gill maturation patterns, or it may reflect a transitional morphology between sections. Molecular phylogenetic data for this species remain limited, and its precise placement within the genus would benefit from additional sequencing work.

## Description

Basidiocarps are small. The pileus is hemispherical to convex, 8 to 22 mm in diameter, hygrophanous, and dark brown to olive brown when moist, fading to pale tan or yellowish buff upon drying. The pileus surface is smooth and may be slightly wrinkled when dry.
The margin is initially incurved, becoming straight, and is translucently striate when wet. The lamellae are adnate to adnexed, moderately close, and exhibit a characteristically mottled appearance due to uneven spore maturation, with areas of grayish white intermixed with dark purplish brown patches, reminiscent of Panaeolus gill patterns.
The stipe is 25 to 55 mm long, 1 to 2.5 mm thick, central, equal, pale brown to whitish, pruinose near the apex, and bruises blue when handled or injured. A fibrillose partial veil is sometimes present.
Spore prints are dark purplish brown. Basidiospores are ellipsoid to slightly lemon-shaped, approximately 10 to 13 µm by 6.5 to 8 µm, with thick walls and an apical germ pore. Cheilocystidia are lageniform to ventricose with elongated necks. Pleurocystidia are less frequently observed.

### Microscopic Features
Basidiospores measure 10 to 13 µm long by 6 to 8 µm wide, ellipsoid to subamygdaliform in face view, with a distinct apical germ pore and moderately thickened walls. The spore surface is smooth.
Cheilocystidia are ventricose, measuring 15 to 22 µm in total length, with a narrow, cylindrical neck and often a slightly inflated apex. They form a sterile band along the lamellar edge.
Basidia are 4-spored, clavate to narrowly clavate, measuring 17 to 24 µm long. The pileipellis is an ixocutis, characterized by a gelatinous layer of narrow, hyaline hyphae 1.5 to 3 µm wide that become embedded in a gelatinous matrix when wet, giving the pileus its slight viscidity.
Clamp connections are present. The lamellar trama is regular to subregular. The combination of an ixocutis pileipellis and mottled gill pattern makes this species microscopically distinctive among Australasian Psilocybe taxa.

## Distribution and Habitat

### Geographic Range
*Psilocybe panaeoliformis* is primarily known from New Zealand, with confirmed collections from both the North and South Islands. The species has been documented from pastoral regions across both main islands, though its full distribution within New Zealand remains incompletely mapped.
Similar taxa have been reported from southeastern Australia, but whether these represent true *P. panaeoliformis* or distinct but related species requires molecular confirmation. The possibility of a broader Australasian distribution cannot be ruled out pending further study.

### Ecology
The species is coprophilous, growing primarily on the dung of herbivores including sheep, cattle, and horses in open pastures and grasslands. It has also been recorded on enriched soil where dung has decomposed and been incorporated into the substrate.
Fruiting occurs during the cooler, wetter months of the Southern Hemisphere, from April through August, with peak fruiting in late autumn to early winter. The species favors well-grazed pastures with abundant dung deposits and moderate to high rainfall.
It is typically found at low to moderate elevations. The coprophilous lifestyle of *P. panaeoliformis* aligns it with other dung-inhabiting Psilocybe species worldwide, though its Panaeolus-like gill mottling is unusual within this ecological group. The species may play a role in nutrient cycling in pastoral ecosystems, contributing to the decomposition of herbivore dung.
Fresh specimens should be carefully handled to observe the blue bruising reaction, as this is the most reliable field character distinguishing *P. panaeoliformis* from truly non-psychoactive Panaeolus species with similar gill patterns.

## Chemistry

The species contains [[psilocybin-compound]] and [[psilocin]], confirmed by chemical analysis and indicated by the blue bruising reaction. The bluing can be moderate to strong, most evident on the stipe and base.
Both major psychoactive compounds have been detected using chromatographic methods, indicating moderate potency comparable to other coprophilous Psilocybe species from the Australasian region. [[baeocystin]] has also been detected in fruiting body tissue, though quantitative data for all alkaloids remain limited.
No unusual or atypical tryptamine compounds have been reported from this species. Further analytical work is needed to establish precise alkaloid concentrations and variability across different populations and substrates, as environmental factors and substrate composition can significantly influence tryptamine profiles in coprophilous fungi.
The psychoactive effects reported for *P. panaeoliformis* are consistent with those of other moderate-potency psilocybin mushrooms, including visual and auditory perceptual changes, altered time perception, and shifts in mood and cognition.

## Similar Species

The mottled gill pattern that gives *P. panaeoliformis* its name also makes it superficially similar to various Panaeolus species, particularly [[panaeolus-cinctulus]] and [[panaeolus-subbalteatus]]. The critical distinguishing feature is the blue bruising reaction, absent in most Panaeolus species.
Among Psilocybe taxa, it may be confused with [[psilocybe-coprophila]], a non-psychoactive species that does not bruise blue, and with [[psilocybe-fimetaria]], which also grows on dung but has different spore morphology.
In New Zealand, [[psilocybe-makarorae]] and [[psilocybe-aucklandii]] are additional lookalikes found in different habitats. Careful attention to the combination of mottled gills, blue bruising, and coprophilous habit is key to correct field identification. Microscopic examination of spore morphology provides additional confirmation, as *P. panaeoliformis* spores are consistently larger than those of most Panaeolus species.

Collection of voucher specimens for microscopic study is strongly recommended when encountering this species, as the intermediate morphology between Panaeolus and Psilocybe makes confident field identification challenging even for experienced mycologists.

## Cultural Significance

New Zealand has a relatively well-documented history of recreational use of psychoactive mushrooms, with several indigenous and introduced species known to foragers. *P. panaeoliformis* is among the species that may be collected alongside more commonly recognized taxa such as [[psilocybe-subaeruginosa]] and [[psilocybe-cyanescens]].
Its small size and coprophilous habit may make it less conspicuous to casual collectors. The species is not among the most widely sought-after psychoactive fungi in New Zealand, but it contributes to the overall diversity of entheogenic mushrooms available in the region's pastoral landscapes.
The mottled gill pattern that distinguishes *P. panaeoliformis* is relatively uncommon among psilocybian fungi, making it an interesting subject for studies of gill development and spore maturation patterns in psychoactive mushroom species.

## Conservation Status

*Psilocybe panaeoliformis* has not been formally assessed for the IUCN Red List. As a coprophilous species dependent on herbivore dung in pastoral landscapes, its distribution is closely tied to agricultural land use patterns.
While New Zealand's extensive pastoral farming provides abundant habitat, changes in livestock management practices, including the use of anthelmintic drugs and the conversion of pastoral land to dairy or forestry operations, could potentially affect populations.
The species is not currently considered threatened, though no systematic population monitoring has been conducted. Its restricted known range to New Zealand, with unconfirmed reports from Australia, highlights the need for further survey work to assess its true distribution and abundance.
The New Zealand fungal conservation framework does not specifically address psychoactive species, and the legal status of psilocybin-containing fungi in the country may complicate conservation research efforts. Citizen science mycological records from platforms such as iNaturalist could help improve understanding of the species' distribution.
