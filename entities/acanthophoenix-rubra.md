## Acanthophoenix rubra

Acanthophoenix rubra, the Mauritius barbel palm or red palm, is a large, solitary palm endemic to the Mascarene Islands of Mauritius and Réunion in the Indian Ocean. It is one of the most iconic and endangered endemic plants of Mauritius.

The species is named for the distinctive barbed spines that cover the trunk and petioles, and for the reddish coloration of new leaf growth. It is the national palm of Mauritius and features prominently in the island's cultural heritage.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Acanthophoenix rubra was described by (Bory) H.Wendl. in 1865. The genus Acanthophoenix contains three species, all endemic to the Mascarene Islands: Acanthophoenix rubra from Mauritius and Réunion, Acanthophoenix crinita from Réunion, and Acanthophoenix rousselii from Rodrigues.

The genus is placed in the subfamily Arecoideae, tribe Areceae. Molecular phylogenetic studies place Acanthophoenix within a clade of Indian Ocean palm genera, reflecting the ancient Gondwanan biogeographic history of the Mascarene Islands.

Stems are solitary, reaching 12–20 meters in height and 25–40 cm in diameter. The trunk is covered with dense, sharp, black spines arranged in irregular whorls, giving the palm a formidable defensive appearance.

Pinnate leaves are 3–5 meters long, arching, with 80–120 leaflets per side. The rachis and petiole are heavily armed with black spines 1–3 cm long. New emergent leaves are conspicuously red to bronze, fading to dark green with maturity.

Inflorescences are infrafoliar, 0.8–1.5 meters long, and pendulous. The species is monoecious, producing both male and female flowers on the same inflorescence. Flowers are cream-colored and fragrant.

Fruits are ovoid drupes, 2–3 cm long, ripening from green through red to purplish-black. Each fruit contains a single spherical seed approximately 1.5 cm in diameter.

## Distribution and Habitat

Acanthophoenix rubra is native to the islands of Mauritius and Réunion. On Mauritius, wild populations are restricted to the remaining native forest fragments in the mountainous interior, primarily between 200 and 800 meters elevation.

On Réunion, the species occurs in humid forest on the windward slopes of the island, from lowland to montane elevations. The Réunion populations are generally larger and more vigorous than those on Mauritius.

The species grows as an emergent or canopy tree in humid [[coleman-sunlight-and-winter-crop-growth-rates]] are significantly reduced compared to primary forest conditions.

On Mauritius, the palm is most common on the wetter western and southwestern mountain slopes that receive the full impact of southeast trade winds. The leeward (northern and eastern) slopes are too dry for the species at most elevations.

The species has been extirpated from several lowland sites where it historically occurred. These lowland populations were the first to be cleared for agriculture during the French colonial period beginning in the 18th century.

## Ecology and Interactions

Acanthophoenix rubra is a keystone species in Mascarene [[clay-pellet-seed-dispersal]] agents, carrying fruits to feeding roosts and facilitating seed deposition away from parent plants.

Several endemic reptile species, including the Mauritius ornate day gecko (Phelsuma ornata), use the palm crown for foraging and shelter. The spiny trunk provides nesting sites for endemic birds such as the Mauritius grey white-eye.

The species is a host plant for several endemic insect species. The palm flower weevil (Diocalandra frumenti) feeds on the inflorescence and developing fruit, though it rarely causes significant damage to healthy palms.

## Conservation Status

Acanthophoenix rubra is classified as Endangered on the IUCN Red List. Total wild population on Mauritius is estimated at fewer than 1,000 mature individuals, distributed across several fragmented forest reserves.

Historical deforestation for sugarcane cultivation reduced the species' habitat on Mauritius to less than 5% of its original extent. Remaining populations are confined to protected areas including Black River Gorges National Park, Brise Fer, and Macchabée forest reserves.

Invasive species pose the most significant ongoing threat. Introduced rats (Rattus spp.) and macaques (Macaca fascicularis) consume large quantities of palm fruit and seed, dramatically reducing natural regeneration rates.

Competition from invasive plant species, particularly [[psidium-cattleianum]] (strawberry guava) and Ligustrum robustum, suppresses seedling establishment. These invasive trees form dense thickets that shade out palm seedlings in the forest understory.

Conservation efforts include invasive species control, seed collection and banking, and supplementary planting in protected areas. The Mauritian Wildlife Foundation has established several in situ conservation sites where Acanthophoenix seedlings are planted and monitored.

Captive populations exist at several botanical gardens including the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, and the Sir Seewoosagur Ramgoolam Botanical Garden in Mauritius. These collections serve as genetic reservoirs for potential reintroduction programs.

## Cultivation and Uses

Acanthophoenix rubra is cultivated as an ornamental palm in tropical and subtropical gardens, valued for its attractive red new growth, elegant form, and dramatic spiny trunk. It is commercially available from specialty palm nurseries but remains relatively uncommon in cultivation.
