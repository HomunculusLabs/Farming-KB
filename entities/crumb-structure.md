## Description

Soil crumb structure refers to the aggregation of soil particles into small, porous
crumbs of 0.2–2 mm diameter that permit good gaseous exchange, free root and water
penetration, and prevent waterlogging. It is one of the most critical physical properties
of productive soil, determining whether a soil can support healthy plant growth and
active soil biology. Without crumb structure, even nutrient-rich soils produce poor crops.

## Classification

- **Category:** Soil physical property
- **Component of:** Soil structure and tilth
- **Measured by:** Percentage of soil volume in stable crumbs of 0.2–2 mm diameter
- **Optimal range:** 92–95% crumb content in well-managed soils

## Key Facts

- Crumbs of 0.2–2 mm forming as little as 10% of total soil volume can still produce crop.
- Below 10% crumb content, crop yield drops dramatically and becomes impoverished.
- Well-structured, unploughed soils can contain 92–95% crumb structure (Leeper, 1982).
- Crumb structure develops from soil organism activity — gums and gels bind particles.
- Adequate humus (10–40%) is essential for maintaining crumb structure via colloid formation.

## What Destroys Crumb Structure

1. Destroying permanent vegetation (deforestation, overgrazing)
2. Flooding soils for prolonged periods
3. High-speed or heavy vehicle cultivation
4. Stocking with sheep or cattle during wet periods (pugging and compaction)
5. Using fertilisers that deflocculate soils (excess potassium where sodium is already high)
6. Burning vegetation in hot periods (destroys colloidal properties of surface clays)
7. Ploughing and turning soil (oxidises humus, collapses aggregates)
