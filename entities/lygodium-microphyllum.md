## Overview

Lygodium microphyllum, commonly known as the Old World climbing fern, is a highly invasive climbing fern species native to tropical and subtropical regions of Africa, Asia, and Australia.

The species has become one of the most destructive invasive plants in the Florida Everglades and other wetland ecosystems in the southeastern United States.

Unlike most ferns, Lygodium microphyllum produces an indeterminate climbing rachis that can extend continuously, enabling it to smother trees and form dense mats over large areas.

Its capacity to alter fire regimes, displace native plant communities, and threaten endangered species has made it a priority target for [[lygodium-japonicum]].

The species epithet "microphyllum" refers to the relatively small leaflets, which distinguish it from larger-leaved Lygodium species.

Like other Lygodium species, the climbing rachis functions as a stem, twining around supports and producing pinnae along its length without terminating growth.

The climbing rachis can reach lengths exceeding 30 meters, making it one of the most extensive fern growth forms known.

Sterile pinnae are approximately 5 to 12 centimeters long, broadly ovate, and divided into 2 to 6 pairs of lobed segments.

Fertile pinnae are narrower and more finely divided, with the sporangia borne on the undersurface of specialized reflexed lobes.

The indusia covering the sporangia are membranous and inconspicuous, dehiscing to release large quantities of wind-dispersed spores.

A single mature plant can produce millions of spores annually, facilitating rapid colonization of new areas through long-distance dispersal.

The root system is adventitious, with roots emerging from nodes along the climbing rachis where it contacts moist soil or organic substrates.

Vegetative reproduction through rhizome fragmentation also contributes to local spread, particularly along waterways where plant fragments are transported by flowing water.

## Distribution and Habitat

Lygodium microphyllum is native to a broad range across tropical and subtropical Africa, Southeast Asia, Australia, and the [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]] by eliminating native plant communities that provide food and shelter for wading birds, mammals, and reptiles.

[[water-management]] infrastructure in south Florida has been affected by dense fern growth that clogs drainage canals and water control structures.

Economic costs associated with Lygodium microphyllum management in Florida have exceeded tens of millions of dollars annually.
