## Overview

**Sticta sylvatica**, commonly known as the tree lungwort or felt lichen, is a large foliose cyanolichen in the family Stictaceae. It is widely distributed in temperate and [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]].

Apothecia are produced on the upper surface of lobes. They are reddish-brown, 1–4 mm in diameter, with a concave disc that becomes plane with age. Spores are colorless, ellipsoid, and multiseptate.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Sticta sylvatica* has a broad distribution across temperate and boreal regions of Europe, Asia, and North America. In North America, it occurs from the Pacific Northwest through British Columbia to Alaska, with disjunct populations in the Appalachian Mountains.

The species is predominantly corticolous (bark-dwelling), growing on the trunks and branches of deciduous trees. Preferred hosts include alder (*Alnus* spp.), maple (*Acer* spp.), ash (*Fraxinus* spp.), and oak (*Quercus* spp.). Occasional growth on conifers has been recorded.

It requires high humidity and is most abundant in old-growth forests with deep shade and constant moisture. Riparian corridors, foggy coastal zones, and deep ravines provide particularly favorable microclimatic conditions.

The species is highly sensitive to air pollution and habitat disturbance. It has disappeared from much of its historical range in central and western Europe due to industrial air pollution and forest management practices.

In the Pacific Northwest of North America, *S. sylvatica* reaches its greatest abundance in the hypermaritime coastal forests of southeastern Alaska and British Columbia. These rainforest environments provide the constant moisture the species requires.

### Reproductive Biology

*Sticta sylvatica* reproduces both sexually through ascospores and vegetatively through isidia and soredia. Vegetative diaspores are the primary mode of local dispersal, allowing colonization of nearby suitable substrates.

The lichen provides microhabitat for diverse invertebrate communities. Tardigrades, mites, and springtails colonize the thallus, using its moisture-retaining structure as shelter and feeding on associated microbial communities.

As an epiphyte, *S. sylvatica* does not parasitize its host tree. However, heavy lichen loading on branches can reduce photosynthetic surface area and may contribute to branch breakage during storms.

## Conservation and Research

*Sticta sylvatica* is considered threatened or declining across much of its range. In Europe, it is red-listed in several countries and is legally protected in others. In North America, it is used as an indicator species for old-growth forest conservation.

Habitat loss through logging is the primary threat. The species' dependence on old-growth conditions means that second-growth forests typically do not support viable populations for decades or centuries after disturbance.
