## Overview

Amanita mappa is a basidiomycete fungus in the family Amanitaceae, commonly known as the false parasol or European false [[query-how-to-identify-and-avoid-the-death-cap-amanita-phalloides]].

It is closely related to and frequently confused with *[[amanita-phalloides]]*, the [[amanita-phalloides-death-cap]], which is responsible for the majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide.

The species was originally described by Jean-Baptiste François Pierre Bulliard in the late 18th century and later reclassified by Lamarck.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Amanitaceae
- **Genus:** Amanita
- **Species:** A. mappa (Bull.) Lam. (1783)

The epithet "mappa" derives from the Latin word for map or cloth, likely referring to the cap's appearance when expanded.

### Cap

The cap measures 5–12 cm in diameter, initially ovate to hemispherical before expanding to plano-convex or flat in maturity.

The cap surface is smooth and slightly sticky when wet, colored pale olive-green to yellowish-green, often with a paler margin.

Remnants of the universal veil may persist as one or a few thin, membranous patches on the cap surface, though these are often washed away by rain.

### Gills and Stipe

Gills are free, crowded, and white, becoming cream-colored with age. The lamellulae are truncate.

The stipe is 6–12 cm tall and 1–2 cm thick, white, cylindrical, and bears a membranous, skirt-like ring near the apex.

Below the ring, the stipe surface is typically smooth or faintly fibrillose.

The volva at the base forms a free, sack-like structure that encloses the stipe base, white to cream in color, sometimes with a slight olive tinge.

### Spores and Microscopic Features

Spores are white in deposit, broadly ellipsoid to subglobose, 7.5–10 × 6–8 μm, thin-walled, amyloid.

Basidia are clavate, four-spored, 35–45 × 10–14 μm. Cheilocystidia are abundant, clavate to saccate.

## Distribution and Habitat

Amanita mappa is found across temperate Europe, particularly in the Mediterranean region, France, Italy, and the Iberian Peninsula.

It also occurs in parts of North Africa and has been reported from [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America, though records there may represent distinct taxa.

The species forms [[ectomycorrhizal-associations]] with broadleaf trees, especially oaks (*Quercus* spp.), beech (*Fagus* spp.), and chestnut (*Castanea* spp.).

Fruiting occurs from late summer through autumn, typically August to November, in both deciduous and mixed woodlands.

## Toxicity

Amanita mappa contains amatoxins, the same hepatotoxic peptide compounds found in the death cap (*A. phalloides*).

The primary toxins are alpha-amanitin and beta-amanitin, which inhibit RNA polymerase II, leading to irreversible liver [[blesching-cannabis-apoptosis-and-cancer-cell-death]].

Symptoms of poisoning follow the characteristic amatoxin syndrome: a delayed onset of 6–12 hours after ingestion, with initial gastrointestinal distress (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea), followed by a temporary remission period of 1–2 days, then fulminant hepatic failure.

Fatalities are documented, though *A. mappa* poisoning is somewhat less frequently reported than *A. phalloides*, possibly due to lower toxin concentrations or less common consumption.

There is no specific antidote; treatment is supportive with aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation, and silibinin or N-acetylcysteine may be administered.
