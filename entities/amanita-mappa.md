## Overview

Amanita mappa is a basidiomycete fungus in the family Amanitaceae, commonly known as the false parasol or European false death cap.

It is closely related to and frequently confused with *Amanita phalloides*, the death cap, which is responsible for the majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide.

The species was originally described by Jean-Baptiste François Pierre Bulliard in the late 18th century and later reclassified by Lamarck.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Amanitaceae
- **Genus:** Amanita
- **Species:** A. mappa (Bull.) Lam. (1783)

The epithet "mappa" derives from the Latin word for map or cloth, likely referring to the cap's appearance when expanded.

### Cap

The cap measures 5–12 cm in diameter, initially ovate to hemispherical before expanding to plano-convex or flat in maturity.

The cap surface is smooth and slightly sticky when wet, colored pale olive-green to yellowish-green, often with a paler margin.

Remnants of the universal veil may persist as one or a few thin, membranous patches on the cap surface, though these are often washed away by rain.

### Gills and Stipe

Gills are free, crowded, and white, becoming cream-colored with age. The lamellulae are truncate.

The stipe is 6–12 cm tall and 1–2 cm thick, white, cylindrical, and bears a membranous, skirt-like ring near the apex.

Below the ring, the stipe surface is typically smooth or faintly fibrillose.

The volva at the base forms a free, sack-like structure that encloses the stipe base, white to cream in color, sometimes with a slight olive tinge.

### Spores and Microscopic Features

Spores are white in deposit, broadly ellipsoid to subglobose, 7.5–10 × 6–8 μm, thin-walled, amyloid.

Basidia are clavate, four-spored, 35–45 × 10–14 μm. Cheilocystidia are abundant, clavate to saccate.

## Distribution and Habitat

Amanita mappa is found across temperate Europe, particularly in the Mediterranean region, France, Italy, and the Iberian Peninsula.

It also occurs in parts of North Africa and has been reported from eastern North America, though records there may represent distinct taxa.

The species forms ectomycorrhizal associations with broadleaf trees, especially oaks (*Quercus* spp.), beech (*Fagus* spp.), and chestnut (*Castanea* spp.).

Fruiting occurs from late summer through autumn, typically August to November, in both deciduous and mixed woodlands.

## Toxicity

Amanita mappa contains amatoxins, the same hepatotoxic peptide compounds found in the death cap (*A. phalloides*).

The primary toxins are alpha-amanitin and beta-amanitin, which inhibit RNA polymerase II, leading to irreversible liver cell death.

Symptoms of poisoning follow the characteristic amatoxin syndrome: a delayed onset of 6–12 hours after ingestion, with initial gastrointestinal distress (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea), followed by a temporary remission period of 1–2 days, then fulminant hepatic failure.

Fatalities are documented, though *A. mappa* poisoning is somewhat less frequently reported than *A. phalloides*, possibly due to lower toxin concentrations or less common consumption.

There is no specific antidote; treatment is supportive with aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation, and silibinin or N-acetylcysteine may be administered.

## Similar Species

Amanita mappa is most easily confused with *A. phalloides*, from which it differs in having a more yellowish-green cap, more persistent volval patches, and a slightly more robust volva at the base.

*Amanita phalloides* typically has a greenish to olive cap with a distinctly silky-smooth surface and a volva that is more firmly attached to the stipe base.

*Amanita vittadinii* is another similar species found in Mediterranean regions but has a white to cream cap and a distinct, deeply grooved volva.

*Amanita citrina*, the false death cap, is non-lethal but shares the general amanita habit; it has a strong phenolic or raw potato odor that aids identification.

*Amanita virosa*, the destroying angel, is another deadly amanita that can be distinguished by its entirely white cap and more elongated stipe.

## Ecology and Role

As an ectomycorrhizal fungus, *Amanita mappa* plays a significant role in forest nutrient cycling by facilitating nutrient exchange between tree roots and soil.

The species contributes to phosphorus and nitrogen uptake by host trees in exchange for photosynthetically derived carbohydrates.

In Mediterranean oak ecosystems, *A. mappa* is part of a diverse ectomycorrhizal community that supports forest health and resilience to drought stress.

The fruiting bodies are occasionally consumed by slugs and insect larvae, though mammalian predation is minimal due to the amatoxin content.

## Cultural and Historical Notes

*Amanita mappa* was historically included in early European mycological texts as a poisonous species to be avoided during mushroom foraging.

In regions where it co-occurs with *A. phalloides*, the two species are often discussed together in field guides and foraging manuals as a cautionary group.

The species has not been documented in traditional ethnobotanical use, consistent with its recognized toxicity since the earliest mycological descriptions.

Modern molecular phylogenetic studies have confirmed *A. mappa* as a distinct lineage within section Phalloideae of the genus Amanita.
