## Description

Gregory M. Mueller is an American mycologist and editor who served as co-editor and lead organizer of the landmark reference *Biodiversity of Fungi: Inventory and Monitoring Methods*. Based at the Field Museum of Natural History in Chicago, he is recognized for his work on fungal biodiversity, macrofungal inventory methods, and conservation mycology.

## Classification

- **Profession**: Mycologist, editor, biodiversity scientist
- **Affiliation**: Department of Botany, Field Museum of Natural History, Chicago, Illinois, USA
- **Specialty**: Macrofungal biodiversity, inventory methods, conservation mycology
- **Era**: Late 20th century–present

### Editorial Philosophy (with [[gerald-bills|Gerald Bills]])

- Co-authored the Introduction chapter establishing the scientific rationale for fungal inventory
- Emphasized that fungi are essential to decomposition, nutrient cycling, nutrient transport, and sustainable development
- Noted that fungi have been ignored in biodiversity conservation due to perceived intractableness
- Estimated that as many as 80% of species in species-rich groups (endophytes, soil fungi) are widely distributed
- Argued that shared species make broad-scale inventory work possible while unique species contribute to understanding biodiversity

### Scientific Contributions

- Documented changes in macrofungal species composition in Europe over 20–30 years
- Noted that several previously common species are no longer encountered
- Highlighted that species have been designated as rare or endangered (Arnolds 1988, 1991; Pegler et al. 1993)
- Connected fungal inventory to ecosystem health monitoring and land management assessment

### Organizational Approach

- Organized *Biodiversity of Fungi* by technique and functional ecological group rather than taxonomic unit
- Justified this approach because organisms in different taxonomic groups sharing habitats require similar sampling techniques
- Designed chapters to be autonomous for use by different specialist teams in inventory projects

## Research on Fungal Conservation

Mueller's work has been instrumental in raising awareness of fungal conservation issues worldwide:

1. **Population decline documentation:** His research documented that macrofungal species composition in European forests has changed significantly over two to three decades, with several previously common species no longer encountered.
2. **Conservation status assessment:** Mueller supported the application of formal conservation categories (rare, endangered, threatened) to fungal species, following frameworks established by Arnolds (1988, 1991) and others.
3. **Habitat connectivity:** His work emphasized that fungal diversity is closely linked to habitat quality and connectivity, making fungi effective bioindicators for ecosystem health.
4. **Sustainable harvesting:** As wild mushroom harvesting became economically important in many regions, Mueller's inventory methods provided tools for assessing whether harvesting levels are sustainable.

## Methodological Innovations

The *Biodiversity of Fungi* volume organized under Mueller's editorial leadership introduced several methodological innovations:

1. **Standardized plot design:** Permanent plots for long-term monitoring of macrofungal fruiting, enabling comparison across sites and time periods.
2. **Multi-taxon sampling:** Protocols designed to capture the full range of fungal diversity at a site, including macrofungi, microfungi, endophytes, and soil fungi, rather than focusing on a single taxonomic group.
3. **Voucher specimen standards:** Detailed guidance on collection, preservation, and documentation of fungal specimens to ensure reproducibility and future taxonomic verification.
4. **Quantitative inventory methods:** Standardized measures of abundance, frequency, and diversity appropriate for fungal communities.

## Connection to Cultivation Science

Mueller's biodiversity work intersects with mushroom cultivation in several important ways:

1. **Wild strain discovery:** Systematic inventory methods can identify new strains or species with cultivation potential, expanding the pool of cultivatable fungi.
2. **Conservation through cultivation:** For species under pressure from over-harvesting (like some [[ganoderma|Ganoderma]] and Tricholoma species), cultivation provides an alternative to wild collection.
3. **Substrate ecology:** Understanding the natural habitat and substrate associations of fungi informs substrate selection for [[synthetic-log-cultivation|synthetic-log cultivation]] systems.
4. **Genetic diversity:** Inventory data reveals the genetic diversity within species, which is valuable for breeding programs and strain improvement.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Mueller's work on macrofungal inventory and monitoring is directly relevant to cultivation, particularly for species collected from the wild. His documentation of declining macrofungal populations in Europe underscores the importance of cultivated alternatives for species under pressure. His organizational framework — grouping fungi by ecological function rather than taxonomy — parallels how cultivators categorize species by substrate and habitat requirements.
