## Lathyrus sativus

## Overview

Lathyrus sativus, commonly known as grasspea, chickling pea, or Indian pea, is an annual legume crop in the family Fabaceae. It is among the most drought-tolerant [[cho-fish-amino-acid-preparation]] β-N-oxalyl-L-α,β-diaminopropionic acid (β-ODAP), which causes the irreversible neurological disorder lathyrism when consumed in excess.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Lathyrus sativus L. is classified in the tribe Fabeae (formerly Vicieae), subfamily Faboideae, family Fabaceae. The genus Lathyrus contains approximately 160 species, with L. sativus being the most widely cultivated.

Grasspea is an annual climbing or trailing [[allegro-fertility-philosophy-of-the-ancient-near-east]] dating to approximately 8000–6000 BCE. It was a staple food in ancient Egypt, Greece, and the Indian subcontinent.

The crop spread throughout the Mediterranean, Central Asia, and the Indian subcontinent through early agricultural trade. It became particularly important in regions where environmental conditions limited the productivity of other pulse crops.

In Spain and Italy, grasspea was widely grown until the mid-20th century but has since declined due to lathyrism concerns. It persists in traditional cuisine as a flour ingredient in flatbreads and porridges.

Today, grasspea is cultivated on approximately 1.5 million hectares globally, primarily in South Asia (India, Bangladesh, Nepal), the Horn of Africa (Ethiopia, Eritrea), and Central Asia (Afghanistan, Pakistan). It persists as a subsistence crop in marginal agricultural environments.

Lathyrism epidemics have been documented since antiquity, with the earliest clinical descriptions attributed to Hippocrates. Major outbreaks occurred during famine conditions when grasspea became the dominant or sole food source for affected populations.

The most devastating recent epidemics occurred in Ethiopia during the 1990s drought and in Bangladesh following the 1998 floods. Modern public health responses include promotion of low-ODAP varieties and diversification of food sources.

## Cultivation

Grasspea is exceptionally hardy and tolerates conditions that defeat most other food crops. It survives prolonged drought (200–300 mm annual rainfall), waterlogged soils, moderate salinity, and temperatures from -10°C to 40°C.

This extreme tolerance makes it a reliable crop in marginal environments where other pulses and cereals fail. It is often the only crop that produces a harvest under severe drought conditions, earning it the name "famine food" in some regions.

The crop performs best in neutral to alkaline soils (pH 6.0–8.5) with moderate fertility. Its deep root system (up to 1.5 m) enables access to subsoil moisture unavailable to shallower-rooted crops.

Sowing rates of 40–80 kg per hectare are standard, with [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]] via Rhizobium symbiosis.

Weed competition is most severe during early establishment, as seedling growth is initially slow. Once the crop canopy closes, grasspea competes effectively with weeds through its climbing and scrambling growth habit.

Growth duration ranges from 100–180 days depending on variety and environmental conditions. Harvest occurs when pods [[livestock-feed-formulation]].

Grasspea fodder is palatable to ruminants and has a crude protein content of 18–22%. The crop's dual-purpose nature (grain and fodder) makes it particularly valuable in mixed farming systems where [[faires-livestock-integration-permaculture]] is essential.

Intercropping with cereals (barley, wheat, or teff) is common practice in Ethiopian and South Asian agriculture. The combination improves total system productivity and reduces risk of total crop failure.

## Nutritional Value and Toxicity

Grasspea seeds are nutritionally comparable to other pulse crops, providing 26–30% protein, 55–60% carbohydrate, and 1–2% fat. They are rich in lysine, an amino acid often limiting in cereal-based diets.

The seeds also contain significant amounts of dietary fiber, iron, zinc, and B vitamins. When properly processed (soaking, cooking, fermentation), grasspea provides high-quality protein nutrition for populations dependent on cereal staples.

In Ethiopian cuisine, grasspea flour is mixed with teff or sorghum to make injera and other flatbreads. In South Asia, the seeds are consumed as dal, boiled whole, or ground into flour for chapaties and snack foods.

The primary safety concern is β-ODAP (also called BOAA), a non-protein amino acid concentrated in the seed coat and embryonic tissues. Concentrations range from 0.2–2.5% of seed dry weight depending on variety and growing conditions.

β-ODAP causes neurolathyrism, an irreversible upper motor neuron disease characterized by spastic paralysis of the lower limbs. The toxin excites glutamate receptors, leading to excitotoxic neuronal death. Risk is dose-dependent: consumption of grasspea as more than 30–50% of caloric intake for 2–3 months produces symptoms.
