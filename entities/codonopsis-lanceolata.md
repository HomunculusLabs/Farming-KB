## Codonopsis lanceolata

## Overview

**Codonopsis lanceolata**, commonly known as deodeok (더덕) in Korean, is a perennial climbing herb in the Campanulaceae family. It is native to [[panax-ginseng]] and superficial resemblance to ginseng roots, though the two plants are unrelated. The species is commercially cultivated in South Korea, China, and Japan.

The name "deodeok" derives from the Korean words for "mountain" and "virtue," reflecting its traditional reputation as a strengthening food. Annual production in South Korea exceeds 15,000 tonnes of fresh roots, with a market value of approximately 50 billion won.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Family:** Campanulaceae
- **Genus:** Codonopsis
- **Species:** C. lanceolata
- **Authority:** (Siebold & Zucc.) Benth. & Hook.f. ex Trautv.
- **Synonyms:** Campanumoea lanceolata Siebold & Zucc.
- **Common names:** Deodeok (Korean), sha shen (Chinese), tsuruninjin (Japanese)

The plant is a twining, herbaceous perennial vine reaching 1.5–3 m in length. Stems are slender, green to purplish, glabrous or sparsely pubescent, and climb by twining around supports.

Leaves are alternate, ovate to lanceolate, 4–10 cm long, 2–5 cm wide, with entire or shallowly toothed margins. Leaf tips are acuminate, and the base is cordate to rounded.

Flowers are campanulate, 2–3 cm long, pale green-yellow with purple spotting inside the corolla tube. They appear solitarily or in pairs at branch tips from July to September.

The root is the economically important part: fusiform to cylindrical, 10–30 cm long, 1–3 cm thick, with milky white flesh and thin, yellowish-brown skin.

When cut, the root exudes a milky latex that is characteristic of the genus. This latex contains the triterpenoid saponins responsible for many of the plant's medicinal properties.

The chromosome number is 2n = 16, which is the base number for the genus Codonopsis and consistent across most East Asian species.

## Distribution and Habitat

Codonopsis lanceolata is native to Korea, Japan, eastern China, and the Russian Far East. It grows in mountain forests, forest margins, and scrubland at elevations of 100–2,000 m.

The species prefers partial shade, well-drained [[humus]]-rich soils, and cool, moist growing conditions. In the wild it is typically found climbing through shrubs and small trees on forest edges.

Korea is the center of both wild diversity and commercial cultivation. Major production areas include Gangwon-do and Gyeongsangbuk-do provinces, where it is grown on trellis systems in mountain fields.

In China, the species occurs primarily in the northeastern provinces of Jilin, Liaoning, and Heilongjiang, where it grows in mixed deciduous forests alongside Quercus, Acer, and Tilia species.

Wild populations have declined due to overharvesting. The species is now listed as "Vulnerable" on the Korean Red List of threatened plants.

## Culinary and Medicinal Use

Deodeok roots are a staple ingredient in Korean cuisine. The most common preparation is *deodeok-gui* (grilled deodeok), where roots are pounded flat, marinated in gochujang, and grilled.

Young shoots and leaves are also edible, consumed as namul (seasoned vegetables) in spring. The flavor is described as sweet with a slightly bitter, ginseng-like undertone.

The roots are typically peeled and soaked in salt water before cooking to reduce bitterness. In addition to grilling, they are used in jeon (pancakes), kimchi, and as a wrap for seasoned meats.

In Korean traditional medicine, deodeok is classified as a lung and spleen tonic. It is used to treat coughs, bronchitis, fatigue, and digestive weakness.

Modern pharmacological studies have identified triterpenoid saponins, flavonoids, and polysaccharides as the primary bioactive constituents. Anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory activities have been demonstrated in vitro and in animal models.

A 2018 meta-analysis of 23 clinical studies found moderate evidence for deodeok's efficacy in reducing respiratory inflammation. However, most studies were small and further randomized controlled trials are needed.

The roots contain significant levels of inulin-type fructans, which serve as prebiotics supporting gut microbiome health. This may explain some of the traditional digestive benefits attributed to the plant.

## Cultivation

Commercial cultivation uses seed or root segment propagation. Seeds require 60–90 days of cold stratification at 2–5 °C to break dormancy before spring sowing.

Plants are grown on trellises or support structures in rows spaced 30–40 cm apart. The crop requires 2–3 years of growth before roots reach marketable size and quality.

Optimal [[soil-ph]] is 5.5–6.5 with [[fukuoka-rice-straw-mulch-central-technique]] helps maintain soil moisture and [[extending-growing-season]].

Harvesting occurs in autumn after the aerial parts die back. Yields average 8–12 tonnes per hectare of fresh roots under standard cultivation practices.
