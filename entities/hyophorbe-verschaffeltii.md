The trunk reaches 4–6 meters in height with a characteristic spindle shape: swollen at the midpoint and narrowing toward both the base and the crownshaft. Maximum trunk diameter at the swollen portion is approximately 30–40 centimeters. The surface is smooth and light gray, marked with prominent leaf scar rings.

[[coleman-hardy-winter-vegetables-cold-tolerance-mechanisms]] is limited to approximately 30 degrees Fahrenheit, restricting outdoor cultivation to USDA zones 10B through 11.

Container culture is feasible for younger plants, and the palm is popular as a patio specimen in frost-free climates. Plants in containers require more frequent watering and fertilization than those in the ground.

Propagation is from seed, which typically germinates within 3–6 months under warm, humid conditions. Seedling growth is slow initially but accelerates once the trunk begins to form. Plants reach flowering size in approximately 8–12 years from seed.

Regular fertilization with a complete palm fertilizer is essential for optimal growth. The species is susceptible to lethal yellowing disease in regions where the phytoplasma is present, including parts of Florida and the Caribbean.

Irrigation requirements are moderate; established plants tolerate short dry periods but perform best with consistent moisture. Good drainage is critical, as waterlogged conditions promote [[savory-livestock-grazing-planning]], and human settlement. Invasive plant species, particularly [[acacia-nilotica]] and Litsea glutinosa, outcompete native vegetation and degrade remaining habitat fragments.

Free-roaming goats and cattle on Rodrigues browse seedlings and saplings, preventing natural forest regeneration in many areas. Fencing of remaining forest fragments is a key conservation measure.
