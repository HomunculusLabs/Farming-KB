## Overview

Hyophorbe verschaffeltii, commonly known as the Mascarene palm or spindle palm, is a distinctive pinnate palm endemic to Rodrigues Island in the Mascarene archipelago of the Indian Ocean. The species is named in honor of Ambroise Verschaffelt, a nineteenth-century Belgian horticulturist.

It is recognized by its spindle-shaped trunk, swollen in the middle and tapered at both ends, which gives rise to the common name "spindle palm." The crown is composed of arching, dark green pinnate leaves with a graceful, symmetrical form.

Hyophorbe verschaffeltii is critically endangered in the wild, with fewer than 50 mature individuals remaining on Rodrigues. Despite its rarity in nature, it is widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical gardens worldwide as an ornamental specimen.

The species was first introduced to European horticulture in the mid-nineteenth century and quickly became popular in conservatory and greenhouse collections. Today, it is one of the most commercially available Hyophorbe species in the international palm trade.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Hyophorbe verschaffeltii H. Wendl. belongs to the family Arecaceae, subfamily Arecoideae, tribe Chamaedoreeae. It was first described by Hermann Wendland in 1866 based on cultivated material originating from Rodrigues Island.

The genus Hyophorbe contains five species, all endemic to the Mascarene Islands. Hyophorbe verschaffeltii is closely related to Hyophorbe lagenicaulis (bottle palm) of Round Island, Mauritius, and the extinct Hyophorbe amaricaulis of Mauritius.

The trunk reaches 4–6 meters in height with a characteristic spindle shape: swollen at the midpoint and narrowing toward both the base and the crownshaft. Maximum trunk diameter at the swollen portion is approximately 30–40 centimeters. The surface is smooth and light gray, marked with prominent leaf scar rings.

Growth rate in cultivation is moderate, with well-maintained plants producing approximately 3–5 new leaves per year. The distinctive spindle shape becomes apparent only after several years of trunk development.

The crownshaft is distinctively colored, ranging from waxy gray-green to pale yellow-green, and is swollen at the base. Leaves are pinnate, 2–3 meters long, with 100–150 leaflets per side arranged in a graceful arching pattern.

The crownshaft is a defining feature that distinguishes Hyophorbe from morphologically similar genera. It measures 30–50 centimeters in length and tapers from a swollen base to the point where the emerging leaf unfurls.

Leaflets are lanceolate, 30–60 centimeters long and 2–4 centimeters wide, with drooping tips that give the crown a weeping appearance. The petiole is short, 10–20 centimeters, and the rachis is slightly arching.

Inflorescences are infrafoliar, emerging below the crownshaft, and bear both male and female flowers. The fruit is an ovoid drupe, approximately 2 centimeters long, turning from green to orange-red at maturity.

Flowering occurs primarily during the warm season, with inflorescences producing creamy-white to pale yellow flowers. Each fruit contains a single round seed surrounded by a thin fleshy mesocarp. Mature palms may produce multiple inflorescences simultaneously.

## Distribution and Habitat

Hyophorbe verschaffeltii is endemic to Rodrigues Island, a volcanic island approximately 560 kilometers east of Mauritius in the Indian Ocean. Rodrigues is the smallest of the three main Mascarene Islands, with an area of approximately 108 square kilometers.

The species historically occupied lowland tropical dry forests on the island's calcareous plateau and coastal slopes. Today, remaining wild populations are restricted to a few fragmented sites in the central and southwestern portions of Rodrigues.

Rodrigues was once densely forested, with a diverse assemblage of endemic plant and animal species. Deforestation began with early Portuguese and French colonization and accelerated under British rule in the nineteenth century.

Elevation range in the wild is from 50 to 350 meters above sea level. The palm grows on well-drained limestone-derived soils in areas receiving approximately 1000–1500 millimeters of annual rainfall.

Soils in its native habitat are shallow and alkaline, derived from coralline limestone bedrock. The palm's ability to thrive in these nutrient-poor conditions contributes to its adaptability in cultivation.

Native habitat on Rodrigues has been extensively cleared for agriculture and grazing since the seventeenth century. Less than one percent of the island's original forest cover remains, and most surviving patches are severely degraded.

The species now persists primarily in remnant forest fragments, along valley bottoms, and on steep slopes that were too rugged for agricultural conversion. Climate on Rodrigues is tropical maritime, with a pronounced dry season from May to November.

## Cultivation

Hyophorbe verschaffeltii is one of the most popular ornamental palms for tropical and subtropical landscapes, prized for its distinctive spindle trunk and compact crown. It is commonly used as a specimen tree, accent planting, and container specimen.

It thrives in full sun to light shade in well-drained soils with good organic content. The species is moderately salt-tolerant and suitable for coastal gardens. Cold tolerance is limited to approximately 30 degrees Fahrenheit, restricting outdoor cultivation to USDA zones 10B through 11.

Container culture is feasible for younger plants, and the palm is popular as a patio specimen in frost-free climates. Plants in containers require more frequent watering and fertilization than those in the ground.

Propagation is from seed, which typically germinates within 3–6 months under warm, humid conditions. Seedling growth is slow initially but accelerates once the trunk begins to form. Plants reach flowering size in approximately 8–12 years from seed.

Regular fertilization with a complete palm fertilizer is essential for optimal growth. The species is susceptible to lethal yellowing disease in regions where the phytoplasma is present, including parts of Florida and the Caribbean.

Irrigation requirements are moderate; established plants tolerate short dry periods but perform best with consistent moisture. Good drainage is critical, as waterlogged conditions promote root rot.

The species is relatively pest-free in cultivation, though coconut scale (Aspidiotus destructor) and red palm mite (Raoiella indica) may occasionally infest the foliage. Regular inspection and horticultural oil applications provide effective control.

## Conservation Status

Hyophorbe verschaffeltii is listed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List and is protected under Mauritian law. The wild population on Rodrigues is estimated at fewer than 50 mature individuals distributed across several small, fragmented subpopulations.

Habitat loss is the primary threat, driven by centuries of forest clearance for sugarcane cultivation, livestock grazing, and human settlement. Invasive plant species, particularly Acacia nilotica and Litsea glutinosa, outcompete native vegetation and degrade remaining habitat fragments.

Free-roaming goats and cattle on Rodrigues browse seedlings and saplings, preventing natural forest regeneration in many areas. Fencing of remaining forest fragments is a key conservation measure.

Rodrigues fruit bats (Pteropus rodricensis) are important seed dispersers for the species, but the bat population has itself declined and is classified as Critically Endangered. Loss of this mutualistic relationship further threatens natural regeneration.

Conservation programs led by the Mauritian Wildlife Foundation and the Rodrigues Forestry Service include habitat restoration, invasive species removal, and ex situ cultivation. Seedlings are grown in island nurseries and outplanted into protected forest reserves.

Genetic studies have revealed low genetic diversity in the remaining wild population, raising concerns about long-term viability. Managed breeding programs aim to maintain genetic representation from all surviving subpopulations in ex situ collections.

## Cultural Significance

Hyophorbe verschaffeltii is a symbol of Rodrigues' unique natural heritage and appears in local conservation awareness campaigns. The palm's striking silhouette makes it one of the most recognizable endemic species of the Mascarene Islands.

The species is featured on Rodrigues tourism materials and conservation education programs aimed at raising awareness about the island's threatened biodiversity. Its cultivation in botanical gardens worldwide provides a genetic safety net for the wild population.

In horticultural circles, the spindle palm is considered a collector's species and commands premium prices among palm enthusiasts. The Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, and several other major botanical institutions maintain living collections.

The genus name Hyophorbe derives from the Greek words "hyos" (pig) and "phorbe" (fodder), referring to the historical use of palm hearts as pig feed on Rodrigues. This practice is no longer practiced due to the species' endangered status.

The Mascarene Islands once supported a rich palm flora with at least ten endemic species across several genera. Human colonization since the sixteenth century has driven three species to confirmed extinction and left most survivors critically endangered.
