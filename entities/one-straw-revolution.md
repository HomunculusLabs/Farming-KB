## Description

*The One-Straw Revolution* (1978) is the seminal work by Japanese farmer and philosopher **[[masanobu-fukuoka]]**, presenting his philosophy of **[[natural-farming]]** (also called "do-nothing farming"). The book describes a method of grain and citrus cultivation that uses no machines, no prepared fertilizer, and no chemicals, yet achieves harvests equal to or greater than average Japanese farms. The title derives from Fukuoka's belief that a revolution can begin from a single strand of rice straw.

## Core Philosophy

The book articulates several interconnected philosophical principles:

### The Fundamental Insight
Fukuoka's central realization, which came to him on the morning of May 15th during a period of deep personal crisis, was: **"Humanity knows nothing at all. There is no intrinsic value in anything, and every action is a futile, meaningless effort."** This was not nihilism but a recognition that intellectual knowledge is insufficient to understand nature.

### The Do-Nothing Approach
Rather than asking "How about trying this?", Fukuoka's method asks "How about not doing this? How about not doing that?" — systematically removing agricultural interventions until only the essential remains.

### The Four Principles of Natural Farming

1. **No Cultivation** — No ploughing or turning of the soil; the earth cultivates itself through root penetration and microorganism activity
2. **No Chemical Fertilizer or Prepared Compost** — The soil maintains fertility naturally through the cycle of plant and animal life
3. **No Weeding by Tillage or Herbicides** — Weeds should be controlled, not eliminated; straw mulch and clover ground cover provide effective control
4. **No Dependence on Chemicals** — Sturdy crops in a healthy environment resist disease naturally

## Practical Methods Described

### Rice and Winter Grain Cultivation
- Broadcast rye and barley seed in fall while rice is still standing
- Harvest rice and spread straw back over fields
- Two weeks before winter grain matures, broadcast rice seed
- After harvest, spread rye and barley straw over field
- White clover is sown as permanent ground cover
- All planting can be completed by New Year's Day

### Clay Pellet Seed Enclosure
Seeds are enclosed in clay pellets to protect against birds and animals:
- Seeds spread on flat pan, dusted with powdered clay
- Fine mist of water added, forming pellets ~½ inch diameter
- Alternative method: soak unhulled rice, mix with moist clay, push through chicken wire screen
- One day of work produces enough pellets for several acres

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The One-Straw Revolution is directly relevant to [[mushroom-cultivation]] in several ways:

1. **Straw as substrate**: Fukuoka's emphasis on returning all straw to the field demonstrates the value of straw as a decomposing organic medium — the same properties that make straw an excellent mushroom substrate
2. **Natural decomposition**: His observation that straw decomposes into rich humus within months mirrors the decomposition process that mushroom mycelium drives
3. **Soil microbiome**: Fukuoka's fields demonstrate the critical importance of undisturbed microbial communities — directly relevant to maintaining healthy mycelial networks
4. **Non-intervention philosophy**: The principle of minimal interference has been adopted by some mushroom cultivators who favor natural outdoor methods over sterile laboratory techniques

## Key Facts

- Published 1978 by Masanobu Fukuoka
- Title derives from belief that revolution starts from a single straw
- Describes farming method using no machines, no fertilizer, no chemicals
- Achieves yields equal to or exceeding average Japanese farms (22 bushels/1,300 lbs per quarter acre)
- Four principles: no cultivation, no fertilizer, no weeding, no chemicals
- Fukuoka's fields had not been ploughed for 25+ years
- Book is both practical agricultural guide and philosophical treatise
