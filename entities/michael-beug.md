# Michael W. Beug

Michael W. Beug is an American mycologist and chemist at The Evergreen State College (Olympia, Washington), known for his collaborative analytical work with [[jeremy-bigwood]] on [[hplc-analysis-of-psilocybin-and-psilocin]] quantification, and for his extensive contributions to mushroom toxicity education and identification.

## Key Research

### Psilocybin and Psilocin Quantification (with Bigwood)

**Beug & Bigwood (1982):** "Psilocybin and Psilocin Levels in Twenty Species from Seven Genera of Wild Mushrooms in the [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], U.S.A." (*Journal of Ethnopharmacology* 5:271-285) — The first systematic quantitative survey of psychoactive alkaloid levels across multiple species of wild mushrooms. Surveyed 20 species from 7 genera, establishing baseline potency data.

**Bigwood & Beug (1982):** "Variation of Psilocybin and Psilocin Levels with Repeated Flushes" (*Journal of Ethnopharmacology* 5:287-291) — Demonstrated that psilocybin levels vary unpredictably across flushes while psilocin builds from zero to maximum by the fourth flush. Found 4× variation in controlled cultures and 10× variation in street samples.

### Key Findings from Flush Variation Study

- **Psilocybin:** Varied unpredictably across flushes (4× range in controlled cultures, 10× range in street samples), generally similar in first and last flush
- **Psilocin:** Typically absent in first 1-2 flushes, reached maximum by fourth flush, then declined — suggesting biosynthetic maturation of the fungus
- **Cap vs stem distribution:** Caps contained ~2× the psilocybin of stems; small amounts of psilocin present were entirely in stems (in some strains)
- **Amazon strain:** Trace of psilocin in cap but not in stem; equal psilocybin in cap and stem
- **Street samples:** Highly variable psilocybin (0.7–6.2 mg/g), consistently low psilocin — users cannot predict dosage from dry weight
- **Methods:** Rye-grain miniculture in half-pint jars, cased with peat/calcium carbonate/perlite; 4-5 weeks from inoculation to first flush; average 2.7g dry weight per jar; continued fruiting 20+ weeks

### Species Survey (20 species, 7 genera)

- Surveyed Pacific Northwest wild mushrooms for psilocybin and psilocin levels using HPLC calibrated against NIDA standards
- Confirmed *[[guzman-allen-gartz-psilocybe-semilanceata-temperate-cosmopolitan-distribution]]* as the most potent Pacific Northwest species (up to 0.93% psilocybin in Northwestern US samples)
- *[[conocybe-cyanopus]]* contained 0.33–0.55% psilocybin with trace psilocin (confirmed by Norwegian researchers 1982/83)
### Earlier Biosynthetic Work

Co-authored "Psilocin, Bufotenine and Serotonin: Historical and Biosynthetic Observations" (Chilton, Bigwood & Beug 1979, *Journal of Psychedelic Drugs* 11:61-69) — tracing the biosynthetic relationships between these indole compounds.

## Role at The Evergreen State College

Beug taught chemistry and mycology at The Evergreen State College for decades, where
the interdisciplinary teaching model suited his combined expertise in analytical chemistry
and field mycology. He supervised student research projects on [[mushroom-identification]] and
alkaloid analysis, training a generation of mycologists in rigorous lab techniques.

## Mushroom Toxicity Expertise

Beug has been a prominent educator on mushroom poisoning and identification safety. His photographic contributions appear in [[paul-stamets]]'s *Psilocybin Mushrooms of the World*. He has served as a resource for poison control centers and mycological societies on the identification of toxic mushroom species. Beug developed identification keys distinguishing
psychoactive species from their toxic look-alikes, particularly in the *Galerina* and
*Conocybe* genera where deadly amatoxin-containing species can be easily confused.

## Analytical Methods

Beug's work employed reversed-phase HPLC calibrated against National Institute on Drug Abuse standards, with TLC confirmation using butanol-acetic acid-water (12:3:5). This methodology became a standard for psychoactive mushroom analysis. His insistence on rigorous quantification against certified reference
materials set a benchmark that later researchers adopted worldwide.

## Impact on Psychedelic Research

Beug's quantitative data on [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]] directly informed harm reduction practices
in the psychedelic community. By demonstrating that psilocybin content varies by 4-10× even
within a single strain, his work underscored the impossibility of accurate dosing from dry
weight alone — a finding still cited in clinical protocols for psilocybin therapy.

## Field Identification Contributions

Beyond the laboratory, Beug became one of the Pacific Northwest's most respected field
mycologists. He led forays for the North American Mycological Association and contributed
photographic documentation to several field guides. His dual expertise in chemistry and
taxonomy made him uniquely qualified to bridge the gap between laboratory analysis and
practical mushroom identification — translating HPLC data into guidance for foragers.
