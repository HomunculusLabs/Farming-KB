# Buddleja davidii

## Overview

**Buddleja davidii**, commonly known as butterfly bush, summer lilac, or orange eye, is a
fast-growing deciduous shrub in the family Scrophulariaceae (sometimes placed in
Buddlejaceae or Orobanchaceae by modern classifications). Native to central and southwestern
China, it is one of the most widely planted ornamental shrubs in temperate gardens worldwide.

The species is named after the French missionary and naturalist Père Armand David, who
collected the type specimen in Sichuan province in 1869. It was introduced to European
horticulture in the 1890s and rapidly became a garden favorite due to its prolific, fragrant
flowering and attractiveness to butterflies and other pollinators.

The inflorescence is a terminal, paniculate thyrse, 10–40 cm long, bearing numerous
tubular flowers crowded along the branches of the panicle. Individual flowers are
tetramerous, 8–12 mm long, with a funnel-shaped corolla. The corolla color is highly
variable among cultivars, ranging from deep purple and violet through pink, magenta, red,
and white. The throat of each flower bears an orange-yellow "eye."

Fruit is a small, two-valved capsule, 4–6 mm long, containing numerous minute seeds. Seeds
are wind-dispersed and can travel considerable distances, contributing to the species'
invasive spread. A single inflorescence can produce thousands of seeds.

## Distribution and Habitat

Buddleja davidii is native to the mountainous regions of central and southwestern China,
including Sichuan, Hubei, Gansu, and Yunnan provinces, at elevations of 800–3,000 m. In its
native range, it grows on rocky slopes, stream banks, and forest margins.

The species has been introduced to cultivation throughout temperate and subtropical regions
worldwide. It has naturalized extensively in western Europe (especially the British Isles,
France, and Germany), New Zealand, Australia, and [[dmt-maoi-plants-united-states]], it
is established along the Pacific Coast and in the mid-Atlantic and southeastern states.

Naturalized populations typically colonize disturbed sites including railway embankments,
roadsides, abandoned quarries, rubble, river gravels, and waste ground. The species prefers
well-drained, nutrient-poor soils and full sun exposure but tolerates a wide range of
conditions. It is highly frost-tolerant, regenerating from the base after winter dieback.
