Leaves are broadly ovate, 3–6 cm long, with 3–5 shallow lobes and a rounded or slightly cordate base. The margins are irregularly toothed, and the upper surface is dark glossy green.

Flowers appear in May to June in corymbose clusters of 5–12. They are white or pale pink, 12–15 mm across, with 2–3 styles per flower, distinguishing the species from the single-styled *C. monogyna*.

Fruits are deep red to crimson pomes, 8–12 mm in diameter, ripening in September to October. Each contains 2–3 nutlets (stones), compared to the single stone of *C. monogyna*.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Crataegus laevigata* is native to western and central Europe, from the British Isles and northern Spain eastward to Poland, the Balkans, and Turkey. It extends into western Asia as far as the Caucasus.

The species occurs naturally in deciduous woodlands, particularly oak-ash-hazel woodlands and ancient hedgerows. It prefers heavier, moisture-retentive soils compared to the more drought-tolerant *C. monogyna*.

It grows at elevations from sea level to approximately 800 metres, tolerating both full sun and partial shade. In mixed hedgerows it often grows alongside *C. monogyna*, producing natural hybrids.

The woodland hawthorn is less common in purely agricultural landscapes than *C. monogyna*, as it is more sensitive to grazing and hedgerow management practices. However, it is a characteristic species of old-growth hedgerows in lowland England and parts of France.

The hybrid *Crataegus × media* (C. monogyna × C. laevigata) is common where the two species co-occur. It displays intermediate morphology, including 2-styled flowers and partially lobed leaves.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

Hawthorn preparations from *C. laevigata* (and related species) are among the best-studied herbal medicines for cardiovascular conditions. The primary active constituents include oligomeric procyanidins (OPCs), flavonoids, and triterpenic acids.

Flavonoid content is concentrated in the leaves and flowers, with hyperoside (quercetin-3-O-galactoside), vitexin, and rutin as major compounds. OPCs are particularly abundant in the fruits.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

Hawthorn berries are edible when cooked and are traditionally used in jams, jellies, fruit leathers, and preserves. The raw fruit has a mealy texture and mild apple-like flavour.

In Britain, hawthorn berries were historically used [[query-how-to-make-fermented-plant-extracts-for-natural-fertilizer]] hawthorn wine and a fruit-based sauce similar to cranberry sauce. The young leaves are also edible raw and have been used as a salad green.
