# Arctium minus (*Lesser Burdock*)

## Overview

Arctium minus, commonly known as lesser burdock, is a biennial herbaceous
plant in the family Asteraceae. Native to Eurasia, it has become widely
naturalized across North America and temperate regions worldwide. The
species is valued both as a medicinal herb (its root is used in
traditional European and Asian medicine) and as a practical permaculture
plant with deep taproots for soil improvement and bioremediation.

The plant is perhaps best known for its burrs — hooked involucral bracts
that cling tenaciously to animal fur and clothing, a trait that inspired
the invention of Velcro. Lesser burdock is closely related to and often
confused with greater burdock (*A. lappa*), which is the primary species
used commercially.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Magnoliophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Asterales
- **Family:** Asteraceae
- **Genus:** *Arctium*
- **Species:** *A. minus* (Hill) Bernh.
- **Synonyms:** *Lappa minor* Hill, *Arctium vulgare* (Lam.) Léveille

**Growth habit:** Biennial, forming a rosette in the first year and
a flowering stem (1–2.5 m tall) in the second year.

**Leaves:** First-year rosette leaves are large (up to 50 cm long),
ovate to cordate, with finely toothed margins and a woolly gray-green
undersurface. Petioles are solid (not hollow, distinguishing from
*A. lappa* which has hollow petioles). Second-year stem leaves are
smaller and alternate.

**Stem:** Erect, stout, branching above, green to purplish,
covered with short glandular hairs.

**Flowers:** Purple to pink, composed of numerous tubular florets
packed into globular flower heads (1.5–3 cm diameter). Heads are
subtended by hooked bracts (the burrs). Blooms July–October.

**Root:** Thick, fleshy taproot extending 0.5–1.5 m deep, creamy
white when cut, with a sweet, earthy flavor.

**Fruit (achene):** Cylindrical, 5–6 mm long, brown, with short
pappus. Enclosed within the persistent burr of hooked bracts.

## Distribution and Habitat

**Native range:** Europe and temperate Asia. Widely naturalized in
North America, Australia, New Zealand, and South America.

**Habitat:** Disturbed areas, roadsides, fence lines, waste ground,
forest edges, stream banks, and pastures. Tolerates a wide range of
soil types including compacted, clay-rich, and nutrient-poor soils.
Prefers full sun to partial shade and moderate moisture.

**Invasiveness:** Considered a noxious weed in some jurisdictions due
to its vigorous growth and burr dispersal. However, it is less
aggressive than some thistle species and can be managed through
grazing and cultivation.

## Phytochemistry

The root, leaves, and seeds of *A. minus* contain a range of
bioactive compounds, many shared with *A. lappa*:

- **Lignans:** Arctigenin, arctiin, matairesinol, and secoisolariciresinol.
  Arctigenin demonstrates anti-inflammatory, antitumor, and
  neuroprotective activity in preclinical studies.
- **Polyacetylenes:** Including arctinone and dihydroxymatricaria ester,
  with reported antibacterial and antifungal properties.
- **Flavonoids:** Quercetin, luteolin, and apigenin glycosides
  contributing to antioxidant activity.
- **Inulin:** A fructan polysaccharide comprising 30–50% of root dry
  weight; acts as a prebiotic, supporting beneficial gut microbiota.
- **Sesquiterpene lactones:** Including arctinolide A and B, with
  anti-inflammatory activity.
- **Phenolic acids:** Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, and cynarin.

## Medicinal and Traditional Uses

**Traditional European medicine:** Burdock root was used as a "blood
purifier" (alterative) in European folk medicine, prescribed for skin
conditions (eczema, psoriasis, acne), rheumatism, and urinary tract
complaints. Leaves were applied as poultices for wounds and burns.

**Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM):** *A. minus* and *A. lappa*
roots (niú bàng gěn) are used to clear heat and relieve toxicity,
treat sore throats, and reduce swelling. Seeds (niú bàng zǐ) are used
for wind-heat cold symptoms and skin eruptions.

**Modern herbalism:** Burdock root is used as a gentle diuretic,
cholagogue, and hepatoprotective agent. The high inulin content
supports digestive health and blood sugar regulation.

**Evidence level:** Most uses supported by traditional evidence and
in vitro studies. Limited clinical trials suggest benefits for mild
inflammatory skin conditions.

## Permaculture and Ecological Applications

*A. minus* has several practical applications in permaculture systems:

- **Deep taproot:** Penetrates compacted subsoil, bringing minerals
  (calcium, magnesium, potassium) to the surface. Functions as a
  dynamic accumulator similar to comfrey (*Symphytum* spp.).
- **Bioremediation:** Accumulates heavy metals (lead, cadmium) from
  contaminated soils; used in phytoremediation trials.
- **Companion planting:** Large rosettes shade soil, reducing
  weed competition. Flowers attract pollinators.
- **Food source:** Roots are edible when harvested in the first year
  (before they become woody). Cooked like carrots or parsnips, or
  dried for tea. Young leaves and stalks can be eaten after boiling
  (discard the water to remove bitterness).
- **Soil building:** When slashed and used as mulch, the nutrient-rich
  foliage returns accumulated minerals to the soil surface.
