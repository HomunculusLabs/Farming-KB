# Psilocybe caerulipes

**Psilocybe caerulipes** is a psilocybin-containing mushroom in the family Hymenogastraceae. Commonly known as the **blue-foot** or **blue-footed psilocybe**, it is named for the distinctive blue bruising that develops on the stipe base. It is one of the more widely distributed [[forensic-identification-methods-psilocybin-mushrooms-tlc-gc-hplc]] in [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America, though it is relatively uncommon and easily overlooked due to its small size.

## Taxonomy and Etymology

The species was first described by American mycologist Charles Horton Peck in 1880 as *Agaricus caerulipes*, later transferred to the genus *Psilocybe*. The specific epithet *caerulipes* derives from Latin *caeruleus* (blue) and *pes* (foot), directly referencing the characteristic blue staining at the base of the stem.

## Description

- **Cap**: 1 to 3.5 cm in diameter, convex to broadly convex, sometimes with a slight umbo in age. Surface is hygrophanous, brownish to ochraceous when moist, fading to a pale tan or buff as it dries. Margin is often translucent-striate when moist.
- **Gills**: Adnate to adnexed, close to subdistant, grayish at first, becoming dark purplish brown with spore maturity.
- **Stipe**: 3 to 7 cm long, 1 to 3 mm thick, equal to slightly enlarged at the base. Pale yellowish to brownish, developing intense blue bruising particularly at the base when handled. Surface is pruinose near the apex. Partial veil is evanescent, rarely leaving a fibrillose annular zone.
- **Spore print**: Dark purplish brown to nearly black.
- **Spores**: Ellipsoidal, 8 to 12 by 5 to 7.5 µm, thick-walled with a germ pore.
- **Taste and odor**: Farinaceous (mealy or flour-like).
- **Chemical reaction**: Bruises blue to blue-green, especially at the stipe base.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Psilocybe caerulipes* is found in eastern North America, ranging from Canada (Ontario, Quebec) south through the eastern United States to the Gulf Coast states and west to the Great Lakes region. It is also reported from Mexico.

It grows as a saprotroph on decaying hardwood debris, particularly on logs, sticks, and woody litter of oaks, maples, beech, and other hardwoods. Fruiting occurs from late spring through autumn, typically during warm wet periods. It is most commonly encountered in deciduous forests along stream banks, ravines, and other moist, shaded habitats.

## Psychoactive Compounds

*Psilocybe caerulipes* contains [[psilocin]] as its primary psychoactive alkaloids. Baeocystin has also been detected in some collections. The alkaloid content is generally moderate, though significant variation has been reported between collections. Potency is considered lower than that of *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]]* but comparable to several other woodland psilocybin species.

## Similar Species

Several non-psychoactive species may be confused with *P. caerulipes*:

- **Psilocybe silvatica**: Similar habitat and appearance but typically lacks significant blue bruising and has a different spore morphology.
- **Psilocybe montana**: A small brown species found in similar habitats but non-psychoactive.
- **Gymnopilus spectabilis**: Much larger, orange-colored, grows on wood; also psychoactive but in a different genus.
- **Mycena species**: Many small woodland Mycenas resemble *P. caerulipes* but lack blue bruising and purplish brown spores.

## Cultivation

*Psilocybe caerulipes* has been cultivated on hardwood sawdust substrates supplemented with bran, though it is rarely cultivated compared to more robust species like *P. cubensis*. Its slow growth and relatively low potency make it primarily of interest to collectors and researchers rather than cultivators.

## Legal Status

Like all psilocybin-containing mushrooms, *P. caerulipes* is controlled in most jurisdictions. In the United States, it is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under the Controlled [[controlled-substances-act-and-psychedelic-research]]. Legal status varies internationally.

## Microscopic Features

[[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] of *Psilocybe caerulipes* reveals features consistent with the genus. Basidia are four-spored, clavate, measuring 14–20 × 4–6 µm. Cheilocystidia are abundant on the gill edges, forming a conspicuous sterile band, predominantly ventricose to lageniform with narrow necks, 14–28 × 4–7 µm. Pleurocystidia are absent or rare. The pileipellis is a cutis of narrow (2–4 µm), hyaline to pale brown, gelatinized hyphae. Clamp connections are absent at the basidial bases. The spore morphology — particularly the thick walls and distinct germ pore — helps distinguish *P. caerulipes* from morphologically similar non-psychoactive woodland species that lack these features.
## Identification Notes

The combination of small size, woodland habitat on hardwood debris, and distinctive blue bruising at the stem base makes *P. caerulipes* identifiable with experience, though several features complicate field identification:
- The blue bruising can be slow to develop and may be subtle, especially in drier specimens
- The hygrophanous cap means color changes dramatically with moisture, potentially confusing identification
- The species is small and easily overlooked among forest floor debris
- Several non-psychoactive species share the general brown-capped, small-statured appearance

The farinaceous (mealy) odor and taste are helpful supporting characters, as many similar-looking woodland mushrooms lack this distinctive smell. Spore print color (dark purplish brown to nearly black) provides an additional diagnostic character that can be obtained in the field with minimal equipment. For definitive identification, microscopic examination of spore morphology and cheilocystidia is recommended.
