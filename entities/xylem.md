## Xylem

## Description

The xylem is a system of specialized cells connected end to end that transports water and dissolved mineral nutrients upward from the roots to the leaves of the plant. This one-way vascular system operates without any expenditure of energy by the plant — a remarkable feat given that water must sometimes travel hundreds of feet to reach the canopy of tall trees. Xylem tissue runs throughout the plant and is placed so efficiently that no single plant cell is further than about 0.5 millimeters from a xylem vessel.

## Classification

- **Type**: Vascular tissue
- **Category**: Conducting tissue
- **Direction**: One-way upward flow (roots → stems → leaves)
- **Contents**: Water and dissolved mineral nutrient ions
- **Structural material**: Lignin (makes up wood)

## Cell Types

### Tracheids

- The dominant xylem cell type in gymnosperms (conifers, cycads, ginkgoes — softwoods)
- Tapered cells that overlap each other
- End walls have pits — thin areas through which water flows from one cell to the next
- About the width of a human hair
- Water moves through pit-to-pit connections between overlapping tracheid cells

### Vessel Elements

- The dominant xylem cell type in hardwoods (angiosperms)
- End walls are perforated with actual holes (not just thin pits)
- Connected directly to each other (not just overlapping)
- About six times wider than tracheids (~500 microns)
- When linked together, form vessels up to 10 cm long
- Much less resistance to water flow than tracheids

Both cell types have walls strengthened with lignin — the material that makes wood. Lignin provides structural support, prevents tube collapse, and waterproofs the xylem to create a continuous plumbing system.

## Water Transport Mechanism

The xylem's ability to transport water upward without expending energy relies on several physical principles:

1. **Root pressure**: Osmotic pressure in roots pushes water upward into the xylem
2. **Capillary action**: Surface tension and adhesion of water to xylem walls draws water upward in narrow tubes
3. **Transpirational pull**: Evaporation of water from leaf surfaces creates negative pressure that pulls water up through the xylem column
4. **Cohesion-tension**: Hydrogen bonds between water molecules create continuous columns that can be pulled upward

Water's journey: Roots → root cortex → Casparian strip check → xylem vessels → stem → branches → leaf veins → mesophyll cells → evaporation through stomata

## Position in the Plant

- Xylem and phloem run side by side throughout the plant
- Separated by the vascular cambium (lateral meristem that generates new vascular cells)
- In roots, the xylem is typically central
- In stems, xylem is interior to the phloem
- In leaves, xylem and phloem form veins with xylem on the upper side

## Key Facts

- Xylem cells die at maturity — they are hollow tubes with no living contents
- Lignin preserves their shape and function after cell death
- The system is entirely passive — no energy expenditure required for water transport
- No cell is more than 0.5 mm from a xylem vessel, ensuring efficient water delivery
- Xylem connections are much wider than plasmodesmata, providing less resistance to water movement
- Lignin in xylem cells is what constitutes wood in woody plants

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Xylem function determines how efficiently water and mineral nutrients reach all parts of the plant — any blockage (from disease, air embolism, or physical damage) causes wilting and tissue death
- Vascular wilt diseases (Fusarium, Verticillium) infect through the xylem, clogging vessels and causing rapid plant decline
- Understanding xylem transport explains why root zone conditions (moisture, oxygen, nutrient availability) directly affect canopy health
- In mycology, some fungi are xylem-inhabiting endophytes that can protect plants from vascular diseases
- The lignin that strengthens xylem is decomposed by specialized fungi (white-rot fungi), making these organisms essential for carbon cycling in forest ecosystems
- Drought stress creates air bubbles (cavitation) in xylem vessels that block water transport — a key mechanism of drought damage
