## Overview

Eleocharis obtusa is an annual sedge in the family Cyperaceae, commonly known as blunt spikerush. It is native to North America, occurring from coast to coast in the United States and across much of southern Canada.

The species is one of the most widespread spikerushes in North America. It is characteristic of seasonally wet habitats including mudflats, pond margins, vernal pools, and disturbed wet ground.

It is often one of the first vascular plants to colonize exposed substrates in newly created or disturbed wetlands. This pioneer role makes it ecologically important for initiating plant succession in ephemeral habitats.

Eleocharis obtusa is an obligate wetland species that thrives in the drawdown zone of ephemeral wetlands. Its annual life cycle allows it to exploit temporary habitats that dry out by midsummer.

The specific epithet obtusa refers to the blunt or truncate tubercle at the apex of the achene, which distinguishes it from several closely related Eleocharis species with pointed tubercles.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Eleocharis obtusa was described by Carl Ludwig Willdenow in 1805. It belongs to Eleocharis subgenus Limnochloa, a group of annual spikerushes with reduced scale-like leaves.

The species is an annual growing 5 to 30 cm tall. Culms are slender, filiform, 0.3 to 0.8 mm in diameter, and arise individually or in small loose tufts. Leaf sheaths are reduced to a basal scale without a blade.

The spike is ovoid to ellipsoid, 4 to 10 mm long and 3 to 5 mm wide. It is borne at the apex of the culm and appears as a compact brown to dark brown head.

Glumes are ovate, 2 to 2.5 mm long, and brown to dark brown with a green midrib. They are distichously arranged and overlap tightly around the rachilla.

The glume arrangement and coloration distinguish Eleocharis obtusa from the similar Eleocharis microcarpa, which has narrower glumes with a less conspicuous midrib.

Perianth bristles are absent or reduced to a single short bristle in some populations. The style base is persistent and forms a tubercle at the achene apex.

Achenes are 1 to 1.3 mm long, biconvex, and pale brown. The tubercle is broad, depressed, and approximately one-third the width of the achene, giving it the characteristic blunt appearance.

## Distribution and Habitat

Eleocharis obtusa ranges across North America from British Columbia to Nova Scotia and south to California, Texas, and Florida. It also occurs in northern Mexico.

The species is most abundant in the interior of the continent, particularly the Great Plains and Upper Midwest. It is less common along the Atlantic and Gulf coasts, where perennial Eleocharis species dominate.

In California it occurs primarily in the Central Valley and in vernal pool complexes of the Coast Ranges and Sierra Nevada foothills. These populations are genetically distinct from eastern populations.

It occurs in vernal pools, pond margins, reservoir drawdown zones, muddy ditches, and disturbed wet ground. It frequently colonizes exposed mudflats following water level recession in late spring and early summer.

The species is equally common in natural wetlands and anthropogenic habitats such as agricultural ditches, construction sites, and stormwater retention areas. This adaptability contributes to its wide distribution.

The species is tolerant of a wide range of soil textures including clay, silt, and sand. It establishes readily on mineral soils with low [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-arid-regions]] of the southwestern United States.

At high elevations it is restricted to snowmelt-fed wetlands and alpine pool margins. Populations above 2000 m are typically smaller in stature and produce fewer achenes than lowland populations.

## Ecology

Eleocharis obtusa is a pioneer species that colonizes bare wet substrates following disturbance or water level drawdown. Its annual life cycle allows rapid establishment and [[fimbristylis-autumnalis]], and [[juncus-bufonius]] in ephemeral wetland communities.

In vernal pools of California it grows with Downingia, Lasthenia, and Plagiobothrys species. These annual communities are among the most threatened wetland types in North America due to agricultural and urban development.

## Conservation

Eleocharis obtusa is considered globally secure (G5) with no significant conservation concerns. It is common and widespread throughout most of its range.

Local populations can fluctuate dramatically from year to year depending on water levels and timing of drawdown. In drought years, populations may be absent from sites where they were previously abundant.

Drainage of ephemeral wetlands for agriculture has reduced habitat availability in some regions. Vernal pool conservation programs in California and the Pacific Northwest have helped protect important populations.
