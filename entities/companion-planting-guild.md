# Companion Planting Guilds

## Overview

A **planting guild** is a permaculture design concept — a deliberately assembled community of plants, fungi, and animals that work synergistically to support each other's growth, health, and productivity. Guilds replicate the mutually beneficial relationships found in mature ecosystems while maximizing food, medicine, and material yields. The concept extends beyond simple companion planting by designing multi-layered, multi-functional polycultures fulfilling seven essential roles.

## The Seven Guild Roles

Every well-designed guild incorporates elements that fulfill these ecological functions:

### 1. Food Producers
Plants that provide edible yields — fruits, nuts, vegetables, herbs, tubers, seeds, or greens. These are the primary reason for the guild's existence and typically form the canopy or primary harvest layer.

**Examples**: Apple tree, tomato, potato, corn, beans, squash, berry bushes, fruit trees

### 2. Nitrogen Fixers
Leguminous plants that form symbiotic relationships with rhizobia bacteria in root nodules, converting atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) into bioavailable forms (ammonia, NH₃). This reduces or eliminates the need for external nitrogen fertilization.

**Examples**: Clover (*Trifolium* spp.), lupine (*Lupinus* spp.), beans (*Phaseolus* spp.), peas (*Pisum sativum*), alfalfa (*Medicago sativa*), vetch (*Vicia* spp.), seaberry (*Hippophae rhamnoides*), goumi (*[[elaeagnus-multiflora]]*), Russian comfrey ([[nutrient-dynamic-accumulator-reference]], not a true fixer but deep nitrogen miner)

### 3. [[dynamic-accumulators]]
Deep-rooted plants that mine minerals from subsoil layers and bring them to the surface through their leaf litter and biomass. When these plants are cut and used as mulch ("chop-and-drop"), the accumulated minerals become available to shallow-rooted plants.

**Examples**: Comfrey (*[[symphytum-officinale]]* — accumulates potassium, calcium, phosphorus, silica, magnesium), yarrow (*[[achillea-millefolium]]* — potassium, phosphorus, copper), dandelion (*[[taraxacum-officinale]]* — potassium, iron, calcium, copper), chicory (*Cichorium intybus* — potassium, phosphorus, calcium), nettle (*Urtica dioica* — iron, nitrogen, calcium, magnesium, sulfur), borage (*Borago officinalis* — potassium, calcium)

### 4. Pest Confusers and Repellents
Plants that disrupt pest insect life cycles through aromatic compounds, physical barriers, or by providing habitat for predatory insects. They work by masking host plant scents, creating physical barriers, or actively repelling pests.

**Examples**: Alliums (garlic, onions, chives — repel aphids, cabbage moths, carrot rust flies), marigolds (*Tagetes* spp. — repel nematodes, whiteflies; produce alpha-terthienyl), nasturtiums (*Tropaeolum majus* — trap crop for aphids; repel squash bugs), lavender (repels moths, fleas, mosquitoes), mint (repels cabbage moths, ants, flea beetles), basil (repels tomato hornworms, aphids, flies)

### 5. Habitat Plants (Insectaries)
Flowering plants that provide nectar, pollen, and shelter for [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] — pollinators and predatory insects that control pest populations. A continuous bloom succession from early spring through late fall is ideal.

**Examples**: Umbellifers (dill, fennel, cilantro — parasitic wasps, ladybugs, lacewings), alyssum (hoverflies, parasitic wasps), buckwheat (bees, predatory wasps, ladybugs; green manure), phacelia (best all-purpose insectary), clover, echinacea, sunflowers.

### 6. Mulch Producers
Plants that generate abundant biomass for [[comparison-sheet-mulching-vs-chop-and-drop-mulching]], suppressing weeds, retaining soil moisture, and building soil organic matter. In guild design, mulch producers are strategically placed so their cut material falls where it's needed.

**Examples**: Comfrey (produces 4–5 cuts of biomass per season), hostas (shade-tolerant mulch production), cardoon (*Cynara cardunculus* — massive biomass producer), rhubarb (large leaves for mulch), [[comparison-jerusalem-artichoke-vs-potato]] (tall stalks for winter mulch), sorghum sudangrass (cover crop mulch)

### 7. Ground Covers
Low-growing plants protecting soil from erosion, suppressing weeds, retaining moisture, moderating temperature. Occupy the lowest guild layer.

**Examples**: White clover (nitrogen-fixing ground cover), creeping thyme, oregano, sweet woodruff (*Galium odoratum*), ajuga, strawberries (dual-purpose food + ground cover), vinca, dwarf comfrey, nasturtium (edible + ground cover)

## Classic Guild Examples

### The Three Sisters (Indigenous American)

The most famous companion planting guild, developed by Indigenous peoples of Mesoamerica over thousands of years:

- **Corn** (*Zea mays*): Structure provider — tall stalks serve as living trellis for beans
- **Beans** (*Phaseolus vulgaris*): Nitrogen fixer — rhizobia on roots convert atmospheric nitrogen, fertilizing corn and squash
- **Squash** (*Cucurbita* spp.): Ground cover/living mulch — large leaves shade soil, suppress weeds, retain moisture, and deter pests with prickly stems

The synergy: Corn provides structure, beans provide nitrogen, squash provides weed suppression and moisture conservation. Together they produce more calories per unit area than any of the three grown alone.

### The Apple Tree Guild

A classic permaculture tree guild: **Apple** (primary food), **Comfrey** (dynamic accumulator; mines K/Ca from deep soil; mulch), **Clover/alfalfa** (N-fixer; ground cover; bee forage), **Dill/fennel/cilantro** (insectary — parasitic wasps for codling moth/apple maggot), **Nasturtium** (aphid trap crop), **Garlic/chives** (repel apple scab; deter borers; accumulate S), **Strawberries** (ground cover; edible; moisture retention), **Yarrow** (K/P/Cu accumulator; ladybugs/hoverflies).

### The Tomato Guild

**Tomato** (main crop) with: **Basil** (repels hornworms/aphids; may improve flavor), **Marigold** (repels root-knot nematodes/whiteflies), **Carrot** (different soil depth; breaks soil), **Borage** (pollinators; trace minerals; hornworm repellent), **Nasturtium** (aphid trap crop).

### The Medicinal Herb Guild

A guild designed for herb production with integrated support species: **Echinacea** (*Echinacea purpurea* — primary medicinal crop; butterfly/bee attractant), **Yarrow** (dynamic accumulator; insectary; wound healer), **Lavender** (pest repellent; drought-tolerant; aromatic mulch), **Chamomile** (ground cover; calcium/potassium accumulator; antifungal), **Comfrey** (mulch producer; accumulator), **Calendula** (edible flowers; pest deterrent; skin healing), **Valerian** (deep accumulator; sedative; earthworm attractant), **Thyme** (ground cover; aromatic pest repellent).

## Allelopathic Interactions to Avoid

Some guild designs must account for negative chemical interactions:

| Plant | Incompatible With | Reason |
|-------|-------------------|--------|
| Black walnut | Tomatoes, potatoes, eggplant, peppers | Juglone toxicity |
| Fennel | Most vegetables and herbs | Allelopathic growth inhibition |
| Sunflower | Pole beans, potatoes | Seed germination inhibition |
| Wormwood | Most plants | Absinthin inhibits neighbors |
| Eucalyptus | Most understory plants | Allelopathic litter and exudates |
| Mint | Most plants | Aggressive runners; competition |

## Design Principles for Effective Guilds

1. **Observe before designing**: Study existing plant communities, microclimates, and soil conditions before assembling guilds.
2. **Stack functions**: Every element should serve at least three functions (e.g., comfrey: accumulator, mulch producer, medicinal, bee forage).
3. **Use biological resources**: Design guilds that self-fertilize through nitrogen fixers and [[comparison-dynamic-accumulators-vs-cover-crops]].
4. **Create edge effect**: Maximize interfaces between canopy, understory, and ground cover layers.
5. **Plan for succession**: Design for early, mid, and late succession phases.
6. **Close the loops**: Cycle biomass back via chop-and-drop mulching to keep nutrients on-site.
7. **Maintain biodiversity**: Include 7–15 species per guild for resilience against pests and climate variability.
8. **Design for harvest access**: Integrate pathways without compacting root zones.

## Guild Maintenance

- **Chop-and-drop**: Cut dynamic accumulators 2–3 times per season, leaving material in place
- **Successional planting**: Replace annuals seasonally while maintaining permanent guild members
- **Pruning**: Manage canopy to ensure light reaches understory layers
- **Soil monitoring**: Test annually to assess guild [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] effectiveness
