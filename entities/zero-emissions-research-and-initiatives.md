# Zero Emissions Research and Initiatives

## Overview
Zero Emissions Research and Initiatives, usually abbreviated **ZERI**, is a decentralized
sustainability network associated with [[gunter-pauli]].
- Full name: Zero Emissions Research and Initiatives.
- Common abbreviation: ZERI.
- Related frame: blue economy.

## Historical Background
Wikipedia summarizes ZERI as a decentralized global network founded in 1994 at the
UNU after support from the Japanese government.
It was initially headquartered at the UNDP in Geneva and later associated with IUCN
in Gland.
The network developed during the 1990s, when sustainable development thinking was
moving from pollution control toward redesign of production systems.
Instead of treating waste as a disposal problem, ZERI treats it as evidence that a
productive link is missing.

## Design Philosophy
ZERI's slogan-like premise is that **zero emissions means zero waste**.
In this usage, "emissions" means more than carbon dioxide.
It includes unused heat, wastewater, spent biomass, sludge, trimmings, and other
outputs normally treated as externalities.
The design task is to identify organisms, technologies, or enterprises that can use
those outputs productively.
This makes ZERI adjacent to permaculture, industrial symbiosis, ecological economics,
and applied mycology.

## Ecological Model
Natural ecosystems rarely have permanent waste streams.
Leaves become fungal substrate, fungal biomass feeds animals and microbes, and the
resulting nutrients return to soil and plants.
ZERI applies this logic to factories, farms, breweries, coffee regions, villages, and
regional development projects.
The emphasis is not only on reducing harm but on creating additional value from the
same local resource base.
A good ZERI design produces several linked products instead of one product plus waste.

## Fungal Relevance
Fungi are central to many ZERI-style cascades because they digest lignocellulosic
materials that humans and many animals cannot use directly.
Straw, sawdust, coffee pulp, spent grain, corn residues, and woody debris can become
mushroom substrate.
After fruiting, the spent mushroom substrate may become animal feed, vermicompost,
soil amendment, biogas feedstock, or mycoremediation material.
This is why ZERI appears in concept pages on fungal sustainability, packaging,
spent mushroom substrate, and ecological recovery.

## Brewery Cascade
A recurring example is the brewery cascade described in this wiki's fungal recovery
and sustainable-materials pages.
Traditional brewing leaves large quantities of spent grain.
In a ZERI-style system, that grain can feed oyster mushrooms such as [[fungal-roles-in-mass-extinction-recovery]] mentions a ZERI brewery
project in Tsumeb, Namibia.
That example matters because it demonstrates ZERI's preference for practical cascades
rather than abstract sustainability language.
The project links brewing, mushroom cultivation, animal feed, methane production, and
soil-building outputs.
For the wiki, it is a useful bridge between fungal decomposition in ancient ecosystems
and modern circular production systems.

## Coffee Project
Wikipedia notes that in 2009 the Specialty Coffee Association of America recognized
ZERI for a 15-year project in Colombia.
The project worked with coffee farms to use unharvested coffee plant material and
trimmings for shiitake mushroom production.
It was reported to have created substantial employment.
This case is important because coffee systems produce large quantities of residues
that can become either pollution or substrate.
ZERI frames those residues as a basis for local enterprise.

## Relationship to the Blue Economy
ZERI is one institutional root of Pauli's later Blue Economy framing.
The blue economy emphasizes business models that meet needs using locally available
resources and cascading value.
Where some green business models add environmental premiums, ZERI seeks lower cost
through multiple uses of the same resource stream.
It is therefore both ecological and entrepreneurial.

## Relationship to Permaculture
ZERI overlaps with permaculture principles such as "produce no waste" and "integrate
rather than segregate."
It links farms, factories, fungi, animals, water systems, and communities.
The difference is emphasis: permaculture often begins with land design, while ZERI
often begins with industrial or regional resource flows.
Both approaches value biological cycling and local resilience.

## Strengths
ZERI's strength is making circularity concrete.
It asks where every material stream goes and what organism or enterprise could use it.
It can reveal hidden value in low-status residues.
It also supports development strategies for places with biomass, labor, and ecological
knowledge but limited capital.
Fungal cultivation is a natural fit because it can begin with simple local substrates.

## Cautions
ZERI examples should not be copied mechanically.
A cascade that is biologically possible may still fail because of contamination,
transport cost, labor constraints, regulation, or weak markets.
Claims about job creation and replicability should be checked case by case.
The durable lesson is the design method, not any single universal recipe.
