## Overview

Bidens pilosa is an annual herbaceous plant in the family Asteraceae, commonly known as hairy beggar-ticks, Spanish needle, or cobbler's pegs. It is native to the Americas but has become one of the most widespread cosmopolitan weeds in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide.

The species is recognized for its distinctive barbed seeds that readily attach to animal fur and human clothing, facilitating rapid dispersal. In many traditional medicine systems, particularly across Africa, Asia, and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], the aerial parts are used to treat infections, inflammation, and digestive disorders.

B. pilosa demonstrates notable allelopathic properties, releasing compounds that suppress the germination and growth of neighboring plant species. This characteristic contributes to its success as an invasive species in agricultural and disturbed habitats.

Modern pharmacological research has partially validated several traditional uses, particularly the antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activities of leaf extracts. The species is now being investigated for potential applications in managing antibiotic-resistant [[blesching-cannabis-bacterial-infections]].

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Bidens pilosa L. was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum. The genus name derives from Latin "bis" (two) and "dens" (tooth), referring to the two-toothed awns on the achenes.

The species belongs to the tribe Coreopsideae within the family Asteraceae. Several varieties are recognized, including var. pilosa (typical form), var. minor (more compact), and var. radiata (with prominent ray florets). Some authorities separate the radiate form as B. alba.

Chromosome number is 2n = 48 for diploid populations, though polyploid forms exist. Molecular phylogenetic studies place B. pilosa within a clade that includes B. alba, B. bipinnata, and B. biternata, with the B. pilosa complex showing considerable [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] across its global range.

### Vegetative morphology

The plant grows 30–150 cm tall with erect, branching stems that are green to purplish and covered in fine hairs. Leaves are opposite, pinnately compound with 3–5 ovate to lanceolate leaflets.

Leaflets are 2–8 cm long with serrate margins and acute apices. The terminal leaflet is typically larger than the lateral ones. Both leaf surfaces may be pubescent, particularly along the veins.

Stems are square in cross-section when young, becoming more rounded with maturity. The root system consists of a relatively shallow taproot with extensive lateral branching, which facilitates rapid [[aact-microbial-foliar-nutrient-uptake-co2-stomata-ingham]] in disturbed soils.

### Reproductive morphology

Inflorescences are terminal or axillary capitula, 5–10 mm in diameter. Each head bears 5–8 white or pale-yellow ray florets surrounding numerous yellow disk florets.

Ray florets are pistillate and fertile, while disk florets are bisexual. The involucre consists of two series of bracts, with the outer bracts shorter and more herbaceous than the inner ones.

The fruits are achenes, linear and flattened, 6–16 mm long, with 2–4 retrorse barbed awns. These awns are the mechanism by which seeds attach to passing animals and fabric, enabling long-distance dispersal.

## Distribution and Habitat

B. pilosa is native to tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas, from the southern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] through [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-caribbean-central-america]] and into South America as far as northern Argentina. It has been introduced to virtually every tropical and warm-temperate region globally.

The species occurs throughout sub-Saharan Africa, South and [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]], Oceania, and parts of southern Europe. It is particularly abundant in disturbed sites, including roadsides, cultivated fields, pastures, waste areas, and forest clearings.

Germination is stimulated by light and fluctuating temperatures, favoring the bare soil conditions found after [[urban-permaculture-pig-land-clearing]] or tillage. Seeds germinate within 7–14 days under optimal conditions of 20–30°C with adequate soil moisture.

Elevation ranges from sea level to approximately 2,500 meters in tropical montane regions. It thrives in full sun to partial shade and tolerates a wide range of soil types, from sandy to clay-loam, though it prefers well-drained, nutrient-rich soils.

The plant exhibits pronounced phenotypic plasticity, varying in height, leaf size, and branching pattern in response to environmental conditions. Populations in shaded understories tend to be taller with larger leaves, while open-site plants are more compact and heavily branched.

## Bioactive Compounds

B. pilosa contains a diverse array of phytochemicals, with polyacetylenes and flavonoids being the most studied. The principal polyacetylenes include phenylheptatriyne and 1-phenyl-1,3,5-heptatriyne, which exhibit antimicrobial and antifungal properties.

Flavonoids identified in the aerial parts include quercetin, luteolin, and their glycosides. These compounds contribute anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and hepatoprotective activities demonstrated in vitro and in animal models.

Quercetin-3-O-glucoside and luteolin-7-O-glucoside are the predominant flavonoid glycosides. Their concentrations vary seasonally, peaking during flowering and declining as seeds mature.

The species also contains sesquiterpene lactones, including 4α,5α-epoxy-germacra-2-en-6β,12-olide. These compounds are associated with anti-inflammatory and cytotoxic effects against certain cancer cell lines.

[[permaculture-designers-manual-essential-oil-plants]] composition varies by provenance but commonly includes α-pinene, β-caryophyllene, and germacrene D. Aerial parts also contain phenolic acids such as chlorogenic acid and caffeic acid.

Coumarins including umbelliferone and scopoletin have been isolated from root extracts. These compounds display documented antifungal and antiplasmodial activity, supporting some traditional antimalarial uses.

## Ethnobotanical Uses

In traditional Chinese medicine, B. pilosa (known as xian feng cao) is used to clear heat, detoxify, and resolve swelling. Preparations from the whole plant are applied to sore throats, diarrhea, and snakebite.

Clinical studies have reported hypoglycemic effects of B. pilosa extracts in diabetic patients, supporting its traditional use for blood sugar management. The mechanism appears to involve enhanced insulin sensitivity and inhibition of intestinal glucose absorption.

Across sub-Saharan Africa, the leaves are consumed as a vegetable and used medicinally for malaria, diabetes, and wound healing. In Uganda and Nigeria, leaf decoctions are taken for gastrointestinal complaints and respiratory infections.

In some West [[mushroom-industry-african-countries]], the aerial parts are dried and powdered for use as a condiment. The bitter flavor is considered to stimulate appetite and aid digestion.

Brazilian traditional medicine employs infusions of the aerial parts as an anti-inflammatory and for treating liver disorders. The species is also used in folk remedies across Southeast Asia, particularly in Vietnam and the Philippines.

Young leaves are edible when cooked and provide a source of vitamins A and C. The plant is also used as animal fodder in some regions, though mature stems become fibrous and unpalatable.
