# Campanula trachelium

## Overview

Campanula trachelium, the nettle-leaved bellflower or throatwort, is a hairy, upright perennial in the family Campanulaceae. It is native to Europe, North Africa, and western Asia, growing in woodlands, hedgerows, and shaded banks.

The species is distinguished by its coarse, nettle-like leaves and large, pendant violet-blue flowers. The common name "throatwort" derives from the historical use of the plant in treating throat ailments.

Plants reach 40–80 cm in height with stiff, spreading hairs on the stems and leaves. The specific epithet "trachelium" refers to the neck-like shape of the corolla, from the Greek "trachelos" meaning neck.

Unlike many bellflowers, C. trachelium thrives in shaded and semi-shaded positions. This shade tolerance makes it valuable for woodland gardens and north-facing borders.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Campanula trachelium belongs to section Trachelium of the genus Campanula. The species is diploid with 2n = 32.

Several subspecies are recognized, differing primarily in hairiness and flower size. Subspecies trachelium is the widespread European form, while subsp. mauritanica occurs in North Africa.

The basal leaves are ovate to triangular-ovate, 5–12 cm long, with a cordate base and coarsely serrated margins. The [[urtica-dioica]]).

Stem leaves are smaller, sessile, and more lanceolate, clasping the stem at their base. The flowering stem is robust, angular, and densely hairy.

The inflorescence is a loose, leafy raceme of 3–15 flowers. Each flower is 2–4 cm long, narrowly campanulate, and held horizontally or slightly nodding.

The corolla is deep violet-blue to purplish-blue, covered externally with fine hairs. The calyx lobes are linear-lanceolate and spreading, also densely hairy.

The fruit is an ovoid capsule that opens by three basal pores near the stem. Seeds are light brown, flattened, and approximately 1.5 mm long.

The plant's resemblance to true nettles (Urtica dioica) may provide Batesian mimicry protection against herbivores. However, unlike nettles, C. trachelium lacks stinging hairs entirely.

## Distribution and Habitat

Campanula trachelium is distributed across most of Europe, from southern Scandinavia to the Mediterranean and from the British Isles to western Russia. Its range extends into North Africa (Algeria, Morocco) and western Asia (Turkey, the Caucasus).

It is primarily a woodland species, growing in deciduous and mixed forests, along hedgerows, and on shaded banks. It prefers calcareous soils but tolerates a range of substrates.

The species is shade-tolerant, growing in semi-shaded to fully shaded positions. It is one of the few Campanula species that naturally occurs in forest understory habitats.

In the British Isles, it is widespread in southern England and Wales, becoming scarcer in northern England and rare in Scotland. It is classified as a species of Least Concern across its range.

The species is often found along ancient woodland boundaries and hedgerows, where it can persist for decades. These long-lived populations serve as indicators of undisturbed habitat in landscape history studies.

## Ecological Role

The pendant, hairy flowers are pollinated primarily by bumblebees, which can navigate the narrow corolla tube. The flowers produce nectar at the base of the ovary, accessible to long-tongued insects.

In European woodland communities, C. trachelium is a characteristic species of theQuercus-Fraxinus woodland type. It contributes to the ground flora diversity of these ancient woodland habitats.

The species provides late summer nectar for bumblebee queens preparing for hibernation. This late-season resource is ecologically important for maintaining pollinator populations.
