## Description

Cryptogamic soil crusts (also called biological soil crusts, microbiotic crusts, or cryptobiotic crusts) are complex communities of mosses, lichens, fungi, green algae, cyanobacteria, and other bacteria that form a living carpet on the soil surface in arid, semiarid, and polar ecosystems. These crusts play essential roles in soil stabilization, nutrient cycling, water retention, and primary production in environments where vascular plant cover is sparse. Fungi are critical components, providing the physical scaffolding that holds mineral particles together and contributing to nutrient mobilization.

## Key Facts

### Composition
- States and Christensen (2001) identified 33 species of fungi associated with lichens, bryophytes, and graminoids of surface crusts in semidesert grassland ecosystems of Utah and Wyoming.
- Included new species of basidiomycetes, five new loculoascomycetes, and three mitosporic species previously unidentified from soil crust communities.
- Fungal component includes both free-living saprotrophs and fungal partners in lichen symbioses.

### Ecological Functions
- **Soil stabilization**: Fungal hyphae penetrate between mineral soil particles and act as a web to physically retain particles. Polysaccharide secretions from fungi and bacteria act as glue to bind particles.
- **Water management**: Hydrophobic nature of some fungal hyphae alters water flow, causing lateral surface movement rather than downward percolation, preventing erosion and retaining nutrients in upper horizons.
- **Nutrient cycling**: Crust organisms fix atmospheric nitrogen (cyanobacterial photobionts), trap dust particles, and contribute organic matter upon death and decomposition.
- **Primary production**: Crust lichens perform photosynthesis during brief pulses of water availability, fixing carbon in otherwise barren landscapes.

### Desert Carbon Fixation
- Lange et al. (1994) showed that soil crust lichens (Acarospora schleicheri, Caloplaca volkii, Lecidella crystallina) in the Namibian desert rapidly absorb nocturnal dew or fog, carry out photosynthesis, and shut down as daytime heat reduces water.
- Photosynthesis sustained for only ~3 hours per day after fog events.
- Net carbon gain approximately 126 mg C m⁻² day⁻¹; annual net primary production ~16 g C m⁻² y⁻¹ for 100% lichen cover.
- Similar rapid photosynthetic responses found in cyanobacterial soil crust lichens in Utah (Lange et al., 1998).

### Environmental Adaptations
- Crust organisms tolerate complete desiccation, extreme temperature fluctuations, and high UV radiation.
- Gauslaa and Solhaug (2001) showed that melanin content in fungal tissue within lichens reduces UVB and UVA penetration, acting as a natural sunscreen.
- Longevity of fungal biomass (compared to rapid bacterial turnover) provides permanency to soil-stabilizing function.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Cryptogamic crusts demonstrate how fungal-bacterial-algal consortia cooperate to create soil from bare rock — principles applicable to understanding substrate colonization in mushroom cultivation. The rapid-response strategy of crust lichens (exploiting brief windows of favorable conditions) mirrors the opportunistic colonization behavior of fungal mycelium encountering fresh substrate. The UV-protective melanin production documented in crust lichens is relevant to outdoor mushroom cultivation where UV exposure affects mycelial viability. Understanding biological soil crusts is important for cultivators using outdoor beds or log-based systems, where native crust communities may compete with or support cultivated species.

### Nitrogen Fixation by Crust Cyanobacteria
The cyanobacterial photobiont of Stereocaulon paschale within crust communities fixes approximately 20 kg ha⁻¹ y⁻¹ of atmospheric nitrogen, which could provide a large proportion of the 10–40 kg ha⁻¹ y⁻¹ plant nitrogen demand in upland boreal forests (Crittenden, 2000). This biological nitrogen input is critical in nitrogen-limited ecosystems where atmospheric deposition is low.

### Lichen Patch Effects on Nutrient Cycling
Lamontagne (1998) demonstrated in Alaskan taiga that net nitrogen mineralization was 7× higher and nitrate nitrogen 40× higher in lichen patches than adjacent forest islands. Higher mineralization rates are likely due to lower nitrogen immobilization into lichen tissue compared to tree tissue. Lichen-dominated areas of upper catchment slopes produce soluble mineral and dissolved organic nitrogen that runs off into lower-lying forest islands, where nutrients are utilized by trees. The landscape mosaic consists of forest islands (27% area), lichen patches (24%), and bedrock with crustose lichen (49%).

### Agricultural Implications
The shift from natural to agricultural systems disrupts cryptogamic crusts through physical disturbance. Tilling destroys fungal hyphal networks and reduces aggregate stability. Conversion of pasture to tilled crops reduces stable macroaggregates from 90% to 58%. However, minimal- or no-till practices can restore crust-like function by reducing physical disturbance and maintaining fungal-dominated decomposer communities. The decomposer community shifts from bacterial-dominated in tilled systems toward fungal-dominated in no-till systems, restoring greater variety of nutrient cycling controls including temporary immobilization and aggregate-mediated nutrient protection.

### Soil Crust Formation and Succession
Crust development follows a successional pattern: initial colonization by cyanobacteria creates a thin organic film stabilizing surface particles. Subsequent colonization by mosses and lichens adds structural complexity and organic matter input. Mature crusts can be several centimeters thick and persist for decades to centuries. Fungal species diversity increases as the crust develops and the underlying soil structure becomes more complex (Ascaso and Wierzchos, 1995).
