## Overview

Gentiana kurroo, commonly known as Himalayan gentian or Indian gentian, is a perennial herb in the family Gentianaceae. It is one of the most important medicinal gentians in the Ayurvedic tradition of the Indian subcontinent.

The species is native to the northwestern Himalaya, where it grows at elevations of 1,500–3,500 meters. It is prized for its intensely bitter roots, which contain high concentrations of secoiridoid glycosides.

Gentiana kurroo is classified as Endangered on the IUCN Red List due to overharvesting of wild roots for the herbal medicine trade. Unsustainable collection has depleted populations across much of its historical range.

The species was first described by John Forbes Royle in 1839 from specimens collected in the Kashmir Himalaya. It has since become one of the most heavily traded gentians in South Asian herbal markets.

Populations are fragmented across the species' range, with many historically known sites now severely depleted. Intensive root collection has eliminated several populations in the Kashmir Valley.

The species does not occur in cultivation at commercial scale, though experimental plots have been established at several Indian research stations. Transition from wild harvest to cultivated supply is a conservation priority.

Seed banking programs at the National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources (NBPGR) in India maintain germplasm accessions of Gentiana kurroo from multiple provenances. These collections are important for future breeding and reintroduction efforts.

## Cultivation

Gentiana kurroo requires cool, moist conditions and deep, organic-rich soil for successful cultivation. It is best grown at elevations above 1,200 meters where summer temperatures remain moderate.

Seed propagation requires cold stratification at 2–4°C for 10–12 weeks, followed by sowing on the surface of a sterile, peat-based medium. Germination occurs at 15–20°C over 4–8 weeks.

Pharmacological studies have demonstrated hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory activities. Gentian-based preparations stimulate bile flow and gastric secretion.

The species is a key ingredient in several commercial Ayurvedic formulations including Sudarshan churna and Mahasudarshan churna. Annual trade volume is estimated at 50–100 tonnes of dried root.
