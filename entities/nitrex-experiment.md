## Description

NITREX (NITrogen saturation EXperiments) is a pan-European experimental framework designed to investigate the effects of atmospheric nitrogen deposition on forest ecosystems. Established in the early 1990s, NITREX sites manipulated nitrogen inputs to forest plots by adding ammonium nitrate or excluding ambient deposition using roof structures, enabling researchers to study the ecological consequences of nitrogen saturation under controlled field conditions.

## Classification

- **Category:** Ecological research program / Experimental manipulation
- **Scope:** Pan-European, multiple forest types
- **Focus:** Nitrogen deposition, mycorrhizal communities, soil chemistry, forest health

## Key Facts

- NITREX experiments were conducted across multiple European countries including Sweden, the Netherlands, Denmark, and the United Kingdom.
- Experimental treatments included ambient nitrogen deposition (control), enhanced nitrogen addition (typically 35-65 kg N ha⁻¹ y⁻¹), and roofed exclusion plots that removed ambient nitrogen deposition.
- In Swedish Norway spruce forests, Brandrud (1995) used PCA analysis to show that ectomycorrhizal fungal communities shifted significantly under enhanced nitrogen, with Cortinarius and Russula declining while Paxillus involutus and Lactarius rufus increased.
- Jonsson (1998) demonstrated only 1-4% correspondence between fruit body observations and molecular identification of ectomycorrhizal root tips in NITREX plots, highlighting the unreliability of fruit body surveys.
- The experiments revealed that nitrogen addition had a greater effect on fungal fruiting than on actual mycorrhizal root colonization.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The NITREX experiments provide critical evidence that elevated nitrogen inputs — whether from atmospheric deposition or agricultural fertilization — fundamentally alter ectomycorrhizal fungal community structure. For mushroom cultivators and forest managers, this research demonstrates that:

1. Nitrogen enrichment reduces diversity of key ectomycorrhizal genera (Cortinarius, Russula) while favoring weedy or tolerant species (Paxillus, Lactarius rufus).
2. Fruit body production is a poor indicator of below-ground mycorrhizal status — mycorrhizae may persist on roots even when fruiting ceases.
3. The disconnect between above-ground fruiting and below-ground colonization means that apparent "disappearance" of species may not indicate true local extinction.
4. Forest management practices that increase nitrogen availability (fertilization, N-fixing companion plants) will shift mycorrhizal community composition, with implications for tree health, nutrient uptake, and ecosystem resilience.
