## Catchment Design (Permaculture)

## Description

Catchment design in permaculture refers to the application of natural catchment (watershed) patterns and processes as a template for integrated landscape design. [[david-holmgren]] identifies catchments as one of the fundamental organising patterns for permaculture, following the dendritic (tree-like) pattern of natural drainage systems. This approach integrates [[water-management]], nutrient cycling, vegetation planning, and settlement design at landscape scale.

## Classification

- **Category**: Design Methodology / Pattern Framework
- **Domain**: Landscape Design, Hydrology, Permaculture
- **Scale**: Catchment to bioregion
- **Key Proponents**: David Holmgren, P.A. Yeomans (Keyline)

## Key Facts

- Natural catchments follow a dendritic (tree-like) pattern: river is trunk, tributaries are branches, headwater forests are canopy
- This dendritic pattern serves as a revegetation metaphor giving priority locations for trees and forest within a catchment
- Keyline strategies slow flow of both water and nutrients down the landscape, cycling them through soil and plants multiple times
- Surplus run-off water stored in keyline dams irrigates pasture to rapidly build soil fertility and structure (which then stores more water)
- Urban catchment strategies catch water from buildings and hard surfaces for household use and garden irrigation
- Stream management strategies work with mixed exotic and native vegetation rather than destroying it
- Since the mid-1980s, the idea that catchment protection trees could be productive farm elements has taken root across Australia
- Agroforestry combinations include timber-pasture, timber-fodder, seed-honey production, and dryland tree crops (olives, carobs)

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Hydrological Design for Mushroom Production

Catchment-scale water management determines the moisture regime available for [[mushroom-cultivation]]. Keyline dams, swales, and managed waterways create the moist, shaded environments along drainage lines where woodland mushrooms thrive.

### Nutrient Cycling and Fungal Networks

The catchment principle of cycling nutrients through soil and plants as many times as possible depends on fungal decomposer networks. [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] extend the effective root zone of trees, increasing nutrient and water capture at catchment scale.

### Riparian Zone Cultivation

Stream-side vegetation management (mixed exotic and native) creates riparian zones with high humidity, shade, and woody substrate, ideal conditions for cultivating species like shiitake on logs, oyster mushrooms on stumps, and wine caps on wood chip beds.

### Soil Moisture Retention

Keyline design's emphasis on building soil that stores more water directly supports soil fungal communities, which in turn improve soil structure and water-holding capacity through glomalin production and organic matter decomposition.

### Integrated Tree-Mushroom Systems

The catchment priority for tree planting in headwaters, along drainage lines, and on ridges creates the woody infrastructure needed for forest mushroom cultivation integrated with timber, fodder, and honey production.
