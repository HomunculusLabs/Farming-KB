# Tilletia

## Description

*Tilletia* is a large and economically important genus of [[smut-fungi]]
(Ustilaginomycetes, [[basidiomycota]]) that primarily infects the ovaries of grass
hosts, replacing the grain with masses of dark, powdery teliospores. The genus
is the type for the Tilletiales, an order of smut fungi closely related to the
[[exobasidiales]] based on ultrastructural, biochemical, and nucleotide-sequence
analyses (Bauer et al. 2001). *Tilletia* species cause diseases known as bunts
or stinking smuts of cereals, which are among the most historically significant
plant diseases. The common bunt of wheat was one of the first plant diseases
for which effective chemical control was developed.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Ustilaginomycetes
- **Order:** Tilletiales
- **Family:** Tilletiaceae
- **Genus:** *Tilletia*

## Key Facts

- **Disease Name:** *Tilletia* species cause "bunt" or "stinking smut" of
  cereals. The disease is named for the characteristic fishy or decaying odor
  of infected grain, caused by trimethylamine produced by the fungus. Common
  bunt of wheat (*T. tritici* and *T. laevis*) and dwarf bunt (*T.
  controversa*) are the most economically important species.

- **Host Range:** Primarily infects grasses (Poaceae), with most species
  occurring on wheat, barley, oats, rye, and various wild grasses. Each
  *Tilletia* species generally has a limited host range, and host specificity
  is an important taxonomic character used to delimit species.

- **Infection and Symptoms:** Sori (spore masses) are produced in the ovaries
  of the host, replacing the grain with dark, powdery teliospore masses. The
  sorus may look like a somewhat enlarged seed, and infection can be difficult
  to detect in the field. Unlike some smut fungi that cause dramatic
  hypertrophy, *Tilletia* infections often preserve the general shape of the
  seed, making visual detection challenging.

- **Teliospore Morphology:** Teliospores are one-celled, thick-walled, and
  generally darkly pigmented, ranging from 3.5-50 micrometers in diameter
  depending on species. The spore wall may be smooth or ornamented with
  reticulate, cerebriform, echinulate, or tuberculate patterns. Some species
  have narrow, dark equatorial bands. Spore ornamentation is a key taxonomic
  character for species delimitation.

- **Life Cycle:** Teliospores germinate to produce promycelia (basidia) on
  which basidiospores (sporidia) are produced. Compatible sporidia fuse to form
  infective hyphae that penetrate seedling tissues and grow systemically within
  the host. The fungus then colonizes the developing inflorescence and replaces
  the grain with teliospore masses.

- **Economic Importance:** Before the advent of seed treatment fungicides,
  common bunt could cause devastating losses in wheat crops. While modern seed
  treatments have largely controlled the disease in developed agriculture,
  *Tilletia* species remain important quarantine pathogens and are of concern
  in organic and low-input farming systems.

- **Phylogenetic Relationships:** Modern molecular phylogenetic studies have
  shown the Tilletiales to be closely related to the Exobasidiales (Bauer et
  al. 2001). Conversely, smutlike phytopathogens in the genus *Microbotryum*
  are more closely related to the rust fungi (Urediniomycetes) than to the
  Ustilaginomycetes, illustrating the convergent evolution of smut morphology.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Cereal Pathology:** *Tilletia* is one of the most important genera of
  cereal pathogens. Understanding bunt epidemiology and control has been central
  to the development of plant pathology as a discipline since the 19th century.

- **Quarantine Regulations:** *T. controversa* (dwarf bunt) and *T. indica*
  (Karnal bunt) are regulated quarantine pathogens in many countries due to
  their potential economic impact on international grain trade. Even low levels
  of infection can trigger costly trade restrictions.

- **Seed Treatment History:** The development of chemical seed treatments to
  control bunt was one of the earliest successes in plant disease management.
  Seed treatment with copper-based compounds and later carboxin and other
  systemic fungicides effectively controlled common bunt.

- **Smut Fungi Diversity:** More than 1,300 species of smut fungi in 80 genera
  are known from 4,000 species of host plants representing more than 75 families
  of angiosperms worldwide. The number of known species may account for only
  about half of the extant species (Oberwinkler 1992).
