## Hericium ramosum

## Description

Hericium ramosum, commonly known as bear"s head tooth fungus or bear's head, is an edible mushroom in the Hericiaceae family. It is one of several Hericium species prized for both culinary and medicinal properties. The species produces large, heavily branched fruitbodies covered with cascading white spines or teeth, creating a dramatic coral-like appearance. Along with H. coralloides, it is commonly referred to as "bear"s head" to distinguish it from the more compact lion's mane ([[hericium-erinaceus]]). The flavor has been described as similar to crab or lobster. To enhance the lobster-like experience, pull the mushroom apart by hand into shreds and saute it in oil or butter with garlic. The species is primarily found on the eastern coastal plains of the United States.

## Key Facts

- One of four Hericium species: H. americanum, H. erinaceus (lion's mane, pom-pom), H. coralloides, and H. ramosum (bear's head)
- Produces large, heavily branched fruitbodies with cascading white spines, distinct from the more compact lion's mane
- Shares the genus-wide difficulty ratings: outdoor cultivation difficulty 2, indoor cultivation difficulty 2
- Edible with a flavor described as similar to crab or lobster
- Primarily found on the eastern coastal plains of the United States
- Fruits from wounds and crevices on hardwood trees and snags
- Wild specimens can reach impressive sizes, occasionally exceeding 15 pounds
- [[spore-print]] is white
- Fruitbody attaches laterally, preferring to grow off the sides of logs and trees, hanging downward
- The mushroom has a remarkable regenerative ability: tissue left at the attachment point can regrow into a new specimen

## Mycelium and Spawn

The [[mycelium]] is white and very irregular, forming small primordia directly on agar even before it reaches the edges of the plate. It is initially difficult to see on agar due to its extremely fine appearance. Using colored agar (blue dye with white mycelium) makes the faint mycelium more visible. The mycelium exudes a deep yellow metabolite on overcolonized spawn and fruiting substrates. Its smell can be sweet to slightly sour. Commercial spawn is typically available in the form of cereal grains, supplemented sawdusts, and wooden dowels for outdoor cultivation.

## Cultivation Notes

### Outdoor Cultivation

Outdoor cultivation uses fresh hardwood logs (primarily oak, maple, and cherry) inoculated with sawdust, plug, or cardboard spawn. This mushroom prefers to emerge from a small cavity, developing its primordia naturally inside shallow holes such as those made by woodpeckers. Once colonization nears completion, use a paddle-type boring bit to drill a few holes around the wood about 2 inches deep. Matching strain to preferred wood type is essential. Inoculation to fruiting: six to ten months. Expected outdoor yields are low; partially burying logs can improve yields. Expect one flush per year. Trim a mushroom when it has half developed and it will regenerate in about a week.

### Indoor Cultivation

Indoor cultivation uses supplemented hardwood sawdust sterilized in an autoclave for two hours, with grain or supplemented sawdust spawn. Commercial strains typically fruit within three weeks after a brief cold shock under refrigeration for one day. High levels of carbon dioxide and humidity are necessary for primordia formation. Once primordia form, they need light misting; allow slight drying between waterings to prevent rotting. For best results, deflate bags when colonization is complete (typically two weeks) by poking a small hole and pushing out all the air, then wrap plastic flat and tight, tape down, and poke two fruiting holes on one side. Expected yields are fair: approximately 1 pound per 5 pounds of supplemented sawdust substrate.

## Fruitbody Development and Identification

The fruitbody is a densely branched structure covered with elongating, cascading teeth that are soft and white when fresh, yellowing when mature or overmature. It differs from H. erinaceus (lion's mane) in its branching structure: while lion's mane forms a dense, compact ball with cascading spines, H. ramosum produces a more openly branched, coral-like form with distinct branches terminating in spine clusters. The mushroom prefers to grow laterally from the sides of logs and trees, hanging downward as it develops its [[spore]]-producing spines.

## Regenerative Fruiting

A remarkable characteristic of Hericium species is their ability to regenerate from residual tissue left at the attachment point. When a mature fruitbody is harvested by slicing it as close to the interior attachment as possible, even leaving a large chunk behind, the remaining tissue can regenerate into a new full-sized specimen within approximately two weeks. In one documented case, a 15-pound specimen was harvested, and two weeks later a second 15-pound specimen had regenerated from the tissue left behind in the tree cavity.

## Nutritional and Medicinal Properties

Cultivated Hericium have a protein content ranging from 28 to 30 percent (dry weight), making this one of the most protein-rich of all cultivated mushrooms. Studies have found that this genus is highly antioxidative, which can help in cases of nerve cell damage. It has been shown to reduce symptoms in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's patients, making it a species of significant interest in neuroprotective medicine.

## Storage and Marketing

A fresh Hericium mushroom will keep for two to three weeks at 38 to 42 F (3-6 C). Hericium species do not rehydrate well, becoming chewy when reconstituted. If drying is necessary, consider powdering. Cooking and freezing, canning, or brining are better options. Harvest when spines are approximately 1/2 inch long; as they approach full elongation, shelf life decreases and spines may yellow. With its wonderful seafood flavor, this mushroom sells itself and is often a favorite for adventurous beginners.

## Mycoremediation

This genus has shown activity against and inhibition of several bacterial species, most prominently Staphylococcus. Mycelium from this mushroom has been used for the treatment of paper bleaching and dye waste, indicating practical [[mycoremediation]] potential for industrial pollution cleanup.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Hericium ramosum expands the diversity of cultivated tooth fungi available to growers. Its striking branched appearance makes it visually appealing for farmers' markets and specialty mushroom sales. Wild spawn expansion is challenging because the mycelium is extremely weak and fragile; the best method is to slice dried specimens into thin sheets and layer them between wet sheets of cardboard. Laboratory cloning works best from mature specimens that have been air-dried for a day or two to reduce water content, preventing bacterial domination on transfers.
