## Overview

Oxyria digyna, commonly known as mountain sorrel, is a small perennial herb in the family Polygonaceae. It is one of the most widely distributed arctic-alpine plant species, occurring in circumpolar regions across the Northern Hemisphere.

The species typically grows 5–30 cm tall with distinctive kidney-shaped basal leaves on long petioles. Its tart, lemony flavor makes the leaves a well-known trail snack for hikers in mountainous regions.

Mountain sorrel is remarkably cold-tolerant, surviving in some of the harshest environments on Earth, including exposed ridge crests and scree slopes above 3,000 m elevation.

The genus name Oxyria derives from the Greek *oxys* meaning "sharp" or "sour," referring to the acidic taste of the leaves.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Order: Caryophyllales
- Family: Polygonaceae
- Genus: Oxyria (monotypic in genus)
- Species: O. digyna (L.) Hill

Oxyria digyna is the sole species in its genus. It is morphologically distinct from other Polygonaceae due to its kidney-shaped leaves and reduced paniculate inflorescence.

Basal leaves are reniform to cordate-orbicular, 1–4 cm broad, on slender petioles 3–15 cm long. The blade is fleshy and bright green, often with reddish margins.

Stem leaves are smaller and more lanceolate. The ochrea is short and membranous.

Flowers are small, greenish to reddish, arranged in loose terminal panicles. Plants are functionally dioecious, with male and female flowers on separate individuals.

The fruit is a small, winged achene, broadly ovate and approximately 4–5 mm, with conspicuous membranous wings that aid wind dispersal across alpine scree.

## Distribution and Habitat

Oxyria digyna has a circumpolar distribution encompassing arctic regions of North America, Europe, and Asia. It extends southward along mountain ranges including the Rockies, Alps, Scandinavian Mountains, and Himalayas.

In North America it ranges from Alaska across arctic Canada to Greenland, with disjunct populations in the Rocky Mountains of Colorado, Wyoming, and Montana. European populations occur in Iceland, Scandinavia, the Alps, and the Scottish Highlands.

The species occupies exposed, well-drained sites in arctic and alpine zones, including scree slopes, rock crevices, snowbed margins, and fellfield habitats. It favors neutral to basic substrates but tolerates a range of [[soil-ph]].

Populations occur from sea level in the High Arctic to above 4,000 m in the Himalayas. The species is well-adapted to short growing seasons, often flowering and setting seed within 6–8 weeks.

Mountain sorrel is classified as Least Concern by IUCN due to its vast circumpolar range. However, isolated southern populations are vulnerable to climate-driven habitat loss as alpine zones shrink.

In the Scottish Highlands, Oxyria digyna is restricted to a few high-altitude corries above 900 m. These populations represent the southernmost European outpost and are of high conservation concern.

Glacial refugia populations in the southern Rockies show distinct genetic lineages compared to arctic populations, suggesting long-term isolation. These genetically unique populations are priorities for conservation.

## Edible and Nutritional Value

The leaves of Oxyria digyna are edible raw or cooked, with a sharp, tangy flavor reminiscent of cultivated sorrel ([[rumex-acetosa]]). This acidity comes from oxalic acid and potassium binoxalate.

Hikers and mountaineers traditionally chew the leaves as a thirst-quenching trail snack. In larger quantities the leaves can be added to soups, salads, and sauces for a citrus-like tang.

## Cultivation

Oxyria digyna is occasionally cultivated in rock gardens and alpine troughs, where its compact habit and distinctive foliage are valued. It requires excellent drainage, cool conditions, and bright light.

Propagation is by seed, which benefits from cold stratification at 0–4°C for 4–6 weeks before sowing. Germination rates are moderate, typically 40–60% under [[psilocybe-cubensis-strain-potency-variability-controlled-conditions-bigwood-beug]].
