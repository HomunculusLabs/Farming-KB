## Overview

Picipes badius (syn. Polyporus badius, Royoporus badius) is a polyporoid basidiomycete fungus in the family Polyporaceae.

It produces tough, leathery basidiocarps on dead hardwood and occasionally on living trees.

The species was historically placed in Polyporus but was moved to Picipes following molecular phylogenetic studies in 2019 that demonstrated it belongs to a distinct clade separate from the Polyporus sensu stricto group.

Common names include the bay polypore and the mahogany polypore, referring to the dark reddish-brown color of mature specimens.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Picipes badius was originally described as Boletus badius by Christiaan Hendrik Persoon in 1797.

It was later transferred to Polyporus by Elias Fries and subsequently to Royoporus by A.B. De before its final placement in Picipes by Zmitrovich and Malysheva in 2019.

The authority is (Pers.) Zmitrovich & Malysheva.

Basidiocarps are stipitate, with a distinct central to eccentric stipe and a circular to fan-shaped pileus measuring 3–12 cm in diameter.

The upper surface is glabrous to finely velutinate, radially wrinkled, and colored dark bay to reddish-brown, often with concentric zonations.

The pore surface is white to cream when young, becoming pale brown in age, with circular to angular pores numbering 2–4 per mm.

The context is tough and corky, pale brown, with a distinct black line separating the tube layer from the context.

Stipe is cylindrical, dark brown to nearly black, typically 3–8 cm long and 0.5–1.5 cm thick, with a glabrous surface.

Basidiospores are cylindrical to slightly allantoid, hyaline, smooth, and measure 8–12 × 3–4 µm.

The hyphal system is dimitic, with generative hyphae bearing clamp connections and skeletal hyphae dominating the context.

## Distribution and Habitat

Picipes badius has a wide distribution across temperate and subtropical regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

It is commonly found throughout Europe, [[brown-rot-and-white-rot-fungi-in-mycoremediation]] of the heartwood.

Fruiting occurs from late spring through autumn, typically on fallen logs, branches, and stumps in deciduous forests.

It prefers moist, shaded environments and is commonly encountered along stream banks and in ravine forests.
