## Overview

**Sinapis arvensis**, commonly known as charlock, field mustard, or wild mustard, is an annual herbaceous plant in the family Brassicaceae. It is one of the most widespread and recognizable agricultural weeds in temperate regions worldwide.

The species is native to Europe and western Asia but has been introduced to every continent with temperate agriculture. It is a particularly troublesome weed in cereal, oilseed, and legume crops.

Despite its status as a weed, Sinapis arvensis has historical value as a source of mustard seed and oil. Its seeds were ground for mustard condiment before the widespread cultivation of white mustard (Sinapis alba).

The plant is a member of the glucosinolate-producing Brassicaceae, and its defense chemistry has been extensively studied as a model for understanding plant-herbivore interactions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Plantae — **Order:** Brassicales — **Family:** Brassicaceae

**Genus:** Sinapis — **Species:** S. arvensis L.

Sinapis arvensis was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The epithet *arvensis* means "of the field," referring to its common occurrence in cultivated land.

Taxonomic treatment has varied, with some authorities placing the species in the genus Brassica as Brassica kaber or Brassica sinapistrum. Molecular phylogenies support its placement in Sinapis.

The plant is an erect annual, typically 30–80 cm tall but occasionally reaching 1 meter. Stems are branched from the base, bristly-hairy (hispid), and green to slightly purplish.

Basal leaves form a rosette, with petiolate, lyrate-pinnatifid blades 8–20 cm long. The terminal lobe is the largest, with 2–4 pairs of smaller lateral lobes.

Upper stem leaves are smaller, sessile or short-petiolate, with dentate to entire margins and an auriculate (ear-shaped) base clasping the stem.

Flowers are bright yellow, approximately 15–20 mm in diameter, with four petals arranged in a cruciform pattern typical of Brassicaceae. They are borne in racemes that elongate as fruits develop.

The fruit is a silique (pod), 2–5 cm long, cylindrical to slightly quadrangular, and appressed to the rachis. The beak is stout and seedless, approximately 5–10 mm long.

Seeds are dark reddish-brown to black, spherical, 1.5–2 mm in diameter. A single plant can produce 2000–3500 seeds, with reports of up to 10,000 seeds on large individuals.

## Distribution and Habitat

Sinapis arvensis is native to Europe, the Mediterranean basin, and western Asia. It has been widely naturalized in North America, South America, southern Africa, Australia, and New Zealand.

The species occurs in a wide range of disturbed habitats including arable fields, roadsides, waste ground, railway embankments, and garden margins. It is most abundant in annually tilled cropland.

In North America, charlock is distributed across southern Canada and most of the contiguous United States. It is considered a noxious weed in several US states and Canadian provinces.

The species tolerates a broad climatic range, from Mediterranean to sub-boreal. It germinates in both spring and autumn, with spring-germinating cohorts being more common in cooler climates.

Soil preferences are broad, but the species performs best on fertile, moderately acidic to neutral loams. It is less competitive on very sandy or waterlogged soils.

## Cultivation and Uses

Sinapis arvensis has been used as a minor oilseed crop and mustard source in parts of Europe and Asia. The seeds contain 25–35% oil and 25–30% protein, making them nutritionally comparable to rapeseed.

Historically, charlock seed was ground and mixed with vinegar to produce a pungent mustard condiment. This use was largely superseded by white mustard (Sinapis alba) and Indian mustard (Brassica juncea) cultivation.

The seed oil is rich in erucic acid (C22:1), which limits its food value but makes it suitable for industrial applications including lubricants, biofuels, and erucamide production for plastics.

The species has been investigated as a potential cover crop and green manure. Its rapid growth and substantial biomass production can suppress weeds, scavenge residual soil nitrogen, and add organic matter.

In some regions, charlock is cultivated as a bee forage crop. The abundant yellow flowers produce both nectar and pollen, supporting honeybee populations during the late spring gap.

Young leaves can be eaten as a pungent salad green or cooked as a pot herb, though they become increasingly bitter with maturity. This use is primarily historical and local.

## Ecological Role

Sinapis arvensis is a host plant for several specialist insect herbivores, including the cabbage seedpod weevil (Ceutorhynchus obstrictus) and the pollen beetle (Meligethes aeneus). Both are significant pests of oilseed rape.

The large, accessible flowers provide important forage for pollinators, particularly bumblebees and hoverflies. The species is considered a valuable nectar plant in agricultural landscapes.

As a competitive agricultural weed, charlock can reduce crop yields by 10–30% at moderate to high densities. It competes for light, water, and nutrients, and its allelopathic root exudates may inhibit neighboring crop seedlings.

The species contributes to the maintenance of genetic diversity within Brassicaceae. Its wild populations serve as reservoirs of disease resistance and stress tolerance genes potentially useful in crop improvement.

Soil seed banks of Sinapis arvensis can persist for 10–20 years or longer. This prolonged dormancy makes the species exceptionally difficult to eradicate once established in a field.

## Bioactive Compounds

Sinapis arvensis produces glucosinolates as primary defense compounds. The dominant glucosinolate is sinigrin (allyl glucosinolate), which upon enzymatic hydrolysis by myrosinase yields allyl isothiocyanate.

Allyl isothiocyanate is responsible for the pungent, mustard-like flavor of crushed charlock seeds. This compound has demonstrated antimicrobial, antifungal, and insecticidal activities in laboratory studies.

Other glucosinolates present in smaller quantities include gluconasturtiin, glucobrassicin, and neoglucobrassicin. The total glucosinolate content of seeds ranges from 80–150 μmol/g dry weight.

The species also produces flavonoids (primarily kaempferol and quercetin glycosides), phenolic acids (sinapic acid derivatives), and tocopherols. These compounds contribute antioxidant capacity to the seed oil.

Glucosinolate hydrolysis products serve as oviposition stimulants for specialist herbivores such as the diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella). This specialization has been studied extensively in plant-insect coevolution research.
