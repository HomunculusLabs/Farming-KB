The species is native to Europe and western Asia but has been introduced to every continent with temperate agriculture. It is a particularly troublesome weed in cereal, oilseed, and legume crops.

Despite its status as a weed, Sinapis arvensis has historical value as a source of mustard seed and oil. Its seeds were ground for mustard condiment before the widespread cultivation of white mustard (Sinapis alba).

The plant is a member of the glucosinolate-producing Brassicaceae, and its [[brassica-juncea]]) cultivation.

The seed oil is rich in erucic acid (C22:1), which limits its food value but makes it suitable for industrial applications including lubricants, biofuels, and erucamide production for plastics.

The species has been investigated as a potential cover crop and green manure. Its rapid growth and substantial [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] within Brassicaceae. Its wild populations serve as reservoirs of disease resistance and stress tolerance genes potentially useful in crop improvement.

Soil seed banks of Sinapis arvensis can persist for 10–20 years or longer. This prolonged dormancy makes the species exceptionally difficult to eradicate once established in a field.
