## Description

The mesophilic composting phase is the first and third stage of the composting cycle, occurring when internal pile temperatures are between 68 and 104F (20-40C). [[mesophilic-organisms]] -- those that thrive at moderate temperatures -- dominate during these periods. In the initial mesophilic phase, decomposition begins as bacteria and fungi start breaking down readily available organic compounds. After the thermophilic phase peaks and subsides, mesophilic organisms return for a second mesophilic period during the maturation stage. Understanding the mesophilic phase is essential because it initiates the composting process and completes it, bookending the full composting cycle.

## Classification

- **Type:** Composting process phase
- **Temperature range:** 68-104F (20-40C)
- **Duration:** Variable -- days for initial phase; weeks for maturation phase
- **Position in cycle:** Phases 1 and 3 (flanking the thermophilic phase)
- **Key organisms:** Mesophilic bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods

## Key Facts

- **Bookend phases:** Mesophilic organisms are active in both the first stage of composting (initial decomposition) and the final stage (maturation). Their specially protected endospores survive the thermophilic phase, allowing them to reassert themselves when temperatures cool.

- **Initial decomposition:** During the first mesophilic phase, bacteria begin attacking straight chains of cellulose, breaking them into smaller chains of glucose through a process called depolymerization. [[brown-rot]] fungi (basidiomycetes) and specific bacteria (Bacillus spp., Heliospirillum spp.) also become active.

- **Endospore survival:** Many mesophilic organisms produce endospores -- spores resistant to chemicals and heat -- that enable them to survive the thermophilic phase. When temperatures drop back into the mesophilic range, these spores germinate and the organisms return to activity.

- **Heat generation:** The [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] of mesophilic organisms in the first phase generates heat that gradually raises the pile temperature from ambient to 104F (40C), at which point [[thermophilic-organisms]] take over. This heat production is entirely biological, not solar.

- **Maturation role:** During the final mesophilic phase (maturation), the most resistant plant component -- lignin -- is decomposed. Actinomycetes and basidiomycetes continue attacking difficult-to-digest plant remnants, and the earthy smell of good compost is produced.

## Role in the Full Composting Cycle

The three-phase composting cycle as described by Lowenfels and Lewis:

**Phase 1: Initial Mesophilic (68-104F / 20-40C)**
- Readily available sugars and simple proteins are rapidly consumed
- Cellulose depolymerization begins (bacteria breaking long chains into shorter glucose chains)
- Brown rot fungi (basidiomycetes) begin attacking woody materials
- Bacillus spp. and Heliospirillum spp. produce endospores to survive coming heat
- Larger soil organisms (springtails, mites, worms) join in breaking apart organic matter
- Metabolic heat builds, pushing temperatures toward 104F (40C)

**Phase 2: Thermophilic (104-150F / 40-65C)**
- Heat-adapted organisms dominate
- Complex carbohydrates fully broken down
- Hemicelluloses decayed
- Pathogens and weed seeds destroyed
- (See: [[thermophilic-composting]] Phase entity page)

**Phase 3: Maturation Mesophilic (cooling back to 68-104F / 20-40C)**
- Mesophilic organisms return from heat-protected endospores
- Lignin decomposition completed by basidiomycetes and actinomycetes
- Earthy smell of good compost produced by actinomycetes degrading cellulose, lignin, chitin, and protein
- Physical decomposers (nematodes, springtails, centipedes, worms) graze on fungi and bacteria
- Grazing causes microbial populations to increase, enhancing soil-binding activity
- Worms coat particles with mucus that binds them into aggregates
- Ants, snails, slugs, mites, spiders, rove beetles, and sow bugs open up organic matter

## Key Organisms Active During Mesophilic Phases

**Bacteria:**
- Bacillus spp. -- endospore-forming bacteria active in initial decomposition
- Heliospirillum spp. -- bacteria producing endospores for thermophilic survival
- Various mesophilic heterotrophs consuming simple organic compounds

**Fungi:**
- Basidiomycetes (brown rot fungi, "regular" mushrooms) -- attacking difficult-to-digest material
- Various mesophilic fungi contributing to cellulose and lignin breakdown

**Actinomycetes:**
- Streptomyces and related genera -- chain-like bacteria resembling fungi
- Produce the characteristic earthy smell of good compost and soil
- Attack the most resistant plant remnants during maturation

**Macroorganisms (maturation phase):**
- Nematodes -- graze on bacteria and fungi, releasing nutrients
- Springtails -- break apart organic matter, distribute microbes
- Earthworms -- pull material into burrows, create castings, bind particles
- Centipedes, rove beetles, sow bugs -- shred organic matter

## Temperature Management

- **If the pile drops below 104F (40C) before maturity:** Add green, high-nitrogen material to stimulate bacterial activity and heating
- **Optimal maturation range:** 104-131F (40-55C) after the initial thermophilic peak
- **Turning importance:** Ensures all material passes through the center (hottest zone) for complete decomposition
- **Finished indicator:** The pile is mature when you cannot recognize the original ingredients

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Compost quality:** The mesophilic maturation phase determines the final quality of compost. Incomplete maturation means lignin and other resistant compounds remain undegraded, producing inferior compost.

- **Earthy smell indicator:** The actinomycetes active during mesophilic maturation produce geosmin and other compounds that give good compost and soil their characteristic earthy smell. This is a practical quality indicator for cultivators.

- **[[soil-aggregate-formation]]:** The mesophilic maturation phase produces soil aggregates through worm mucus binding and microbial activity. These aggregates improve water retention, aeration, and structure in garden beds and [[kh2po4-phosphate-supplementation-mushroom-substrates]].

- **[[fungi-convention-on-biological-diversity]]:** The return of mesophilic organisms after thermophilic pasteurization creates a diverse microbial community in finished compost. This diversity is what makes compost an effective soil inoculant.
