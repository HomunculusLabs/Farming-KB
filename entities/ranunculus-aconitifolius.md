## Overview

**Ranunculus aconitifolius** L., commonly called aconite-leaved buttercup, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Ranunculaceae. It is native to the mountains of central and southern Europe, where it inhabits damp alpine meadows, stream banks, and tall-herb communities.

The species is valued as an ornamental garden plant for its white flowers and attractive divided foliage. It is sometimes confused with *Ranunculus platanifolius*, which is generally taller and has more deeply cut leaves.

Ranunculus aconitifolius was first described by Carl Linnaeus in his 1753 Species Plantarum. It has been cultivated in European gardens since at least the 16th century, prized for its elegant white blooms.

The plant is sometimes called "bower's buttercup" or "fair maids of France" in horticultural literature. The latter name refers specifically to the double-flowered form.

## Taxonomy

- **Authority:** Linnaeus (1753)

The species epithet *aconitifolius* means "with leaves like Aconitum" (monkshood), referring to the deeply divided, palmate foliage. No widely accepted subspecies are currently recognized.

It belongs to the *Ranunculus* sect. *Ranunculus* and is closely allied to *R. platanifolius* and *R. lanuginosus*. Some floras treat it within the *R. aconitifolius* aggregate.

Chromosome number is 2n = 16, consistent with most species in Ranunculus section Ranunculus. Hybridization with close relatives is rare in nature.

## Morphology

Plants grow 30–80 cm tall from a short, stout, erect rootstock. The stem is erect, branched above, and sparsely pilose to nearly glabrous.

Basal leaves are long-petiolate with broadly pentagonal to reniform blades, 4–10 cm across. The blade is palmately divided into 3–5 deeply incised segments with coarsely dentate margins.

The leaf venation is prominent beneath, with five primary veins radiating from the petiole attachment. Leaf texture is thin and herbaceous.

Stem leaves are smaller, sessile or short-petiolate, and similarly divided. Upper leaves become progressively reduced and bract-like.

Flowers are 2–3.5 cm in diameter with 5 white, obovate petals, 8–15 mm long. The sepals are 5, spreading, greenish to yellowish, and 4–7 mm long.

Stamens are numerous with yellow anthers. The receptacle is hairy, and the achenes are 2.5–3.5 mm, compressed, with a short, curved beak approximately 0.5 mm long.

The foliage is dark green and glossy on the upper surface. Leaves are sparsely hairy beneath, particularly along the veins.

Roots are fibrous and form a compact crown. The rootstock persists through winter, producing new shoots in early spring.

## Distribution and Habitat

Ranunculus aconitifolius is distributed across the mountain ranges of central and southern Europe. It occurs in the Alps, Pyrenees, Carpathians, Apennines, and the mountains of the Balkan Peninsula.

Its range extends from eastern Spain through France, Switzerland, Austria, Italy, and into Romania and the former Yugoslavia. Populations are scattered and typically localized to suitable montane habitats.

The species is absent from the Scandinavian mountains and the British Isles. Its absence from northern Europe distinguishes it from the more widespread *Ranunculus acris* and *R. repens*.

The species grows at elevations of 800–2,500 m. It prefers damp, nutrient-rich soils in semi-shaded to open situations.

Typical habitats include alpine tall-herb communities, wet meadows, stream margins, spring-fed flushes, and damp ledges on rock outcrops. It favours calcareous to slightly acidic substrates.

It frequently co-occurs with species such as *Caltha palustris*, *Geum rivale*, and *Cardamine pratensis* in wet montane meadows. These communities are among the most species-rich in alpine zones.

## Ecology

Flowering occurs from June to August. The white, open flowers attract a range of pollinators including flies, bees, and small beetles.

Like other buttercups, the plant contains ranunculin, which hydrolyzes to protoanemonin when tissues are damaged. This compound is toxic to livestock and deters herbivory.

The species plays a role in montane tall-herb communities, contributing to structural diversity and nectar resources. It is not considered invasive or aggressive in its native range.

Seed dispersal is primarily by gravity and wind. Achenes are relatively small and can be carried short distances by water along streams.

Ranunculus aconitifolius is classified as Least Concern across most of its range. However, some isolated populations at the margins of its distribution are vulnerable to habitat loss from alpine tourism and overgrazing.

Climate change poses a long-term threat by shifting suitable habitats to higher elevations. Populations confined to lower mountain ranges may face local extinction.

## Cultivation

Ranunculus aconitifolius is cultivated as a hardy ornamental perennial in temperate gardens. It is suitable for moist borders, rock gardens, and naturalistic planting schemes.

Plants prefer partial shade to full sun in cool-summer regions. They require consistently moist, humus-rich, well-drained soil.

In warm climates, afternoon shade is essential to prevent leaf scorch. The plant is unsuited to hot, humid summer conditions where it may decline rapidly.

Propagation is by seed sown fresh in autumn or by division in spring. Seeds require a period of cold stratification for reliable germination.

The species is hardy to USDA zones 4–7. It is relatively pest-free but may suffer from powdery mildew in dry, overcrowded conditions.

Slugs may feed on young shoots in spring. Deer and rabbits generally avoid the toxic foliage.

The double-flowered cultivar 'Flore Pleno' is the most commonly grown form in gardens. It has spherical white flower heads and is sometimes sold as "white bachelor's buttons."

In the wild, the species is protected in several European countries. Collection from natural populations is restricted in Austria, Switzerland, and parts of Italy.
