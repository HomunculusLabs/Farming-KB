The dried flower buds of this tree have been traded as a prized spice for over 2,000 years.

Cloves are among the most potent [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]], with Indonesia, Madagascar, India, and Sri Lanka as the leading producers. Global clove production exceeds 150,000 metric tons annually, serving both culinary and pharmaceutical markets.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Magnoliophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Myrtales
- **Family:** Myrtaceae
- **Genus:** Syzygium (alternative: Eugenia)
- **Species:** S. aromaticum (L.) Merr. & L.M. Perry (syn. E. caryophyllata)
- **Authority:** Originally described by Linnaeus as *Eugenia caryophyllata* (1753)

The clove tree is a medium-sized evergreen reaching 8–12 meters in height under cultivation, up to 20 meters in the wild. The trunk is straight with grayish-brown, fissured bark. Leaves are opposite, simple, leathery, 5–15 cm long, with a prominent midrib.

Flowers are borne in terminal clusters of 3–10. The calyx forms the commercially harvested "clove" bud — a 1.5–2 cm long, nail-shaped structure that is pink when immature, transitioning through red to deep brown at harvest. The petals are pale yellow and shed shortly after opening.

The fruit is a fleshy drupe (called "mother clove") containing a single seed. It is ovoid, 2–3 cm long, purple-red when ripe, and has a distinct clove aroma though milder than the dried buds.

## Distribution and Habitat

Clove is native to the Maluku Islands in eastern Indonesia, specifically Ternate, Tidore, Moti, Makian, and Bacan. The species requires a humid [[cannabis-harvest-timing-visual-cues]] is critical. Picking too early yields small, lightweight buds with low oil content; picking too late produces open flowers with reduced aromatic quality. Experienced harvesters can distinguish the ideal stage by color and firmness.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The essential oil of clove buds contains over 20 identified compounds. The principal constituents and their approximate concentrations are:

- **Eugenol:** 70–90% — primary active compound, a phenylpropanoid
- **Eugenyl acetate:** 5–15% — contributes to the sweet floral notes
- **β-Caryophyllene:** 2–8% — sesquiterpene with anti-inflammatory activity
- **α-Humulene:** 1–3% — sesquiterpene with anticancer properties
- **Methyl salicylate:** trace — mild analgesic

Eugenol exhibits broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, fungi, and viruses. It acts by disrupting microbial cell membranes and inhibiting ergosterol biosynthesis in fungi.

The anti-inflammatory activity of eugenol is mediated through inhibition of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and reduction of prostaglandin E2 production. Kuroda et al. (2012) demonstrated significant reduction of TNF-α and IL-6 cytokines in LPS-stimulated macrophages.

Eugenol also functions as a natural anesthetic and analgesic. It has been used in dentistry for over a century as a topical anesthetic for toothache and as an antiseptic in dental procedures. This application is supported by its ability to inhibit voltage-gated sodium channels.

Additional pharmacological activities documented in the literature include antioxidant activity (eugenol scavenges free radicals and upregulates endogenous antioxidant enzymes), antispasmodic effects on smooth muscle, and mild anticoagulant activity through inhibition of platelet aggregation.
