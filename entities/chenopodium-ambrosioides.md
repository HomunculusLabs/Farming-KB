## Overview

Chenopodium ambrosioides is a strongly aromatic annual or short-lived perennial herb in the family Amaranthaceae (formerly Chenopodiaceae), native to tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas.

It is best known as **epazote** in Mexican cuisine, where its pungent, resinous leaves are used to flavor beans, soups, and moles. The species has a long history of medicinal use, particularly as an anthelmintic (anti-parasitic) remedy.

The plant has become a widespread invasive weed in many tropical and subtropical regions worldwide, colonizing disturbed ground, agricultural fields, and urban areas with aggressive efficiency.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:** Kingdom Plantae → Amaranthaceae → subfamily Chenopodioideae → genus *Chenopodium*.

*Chenopodium ambrosioides* L. was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The specific epithet *ambrosioides* means "resembling ambrosia," referring to the strong aromatic fragrance.

The species has been moved between families as taxonomic understanding evolved — from Chenopodiaceae to Amaranthaceae under the APG system. Some authorities recognize *Dysphania ambrosioides* (Moq.) Mosyakin & Clemants as the accepted name.

The reclassification to *Dysphania* reflects molecular phylogenetic evidence showing that the strongly aromatic, glandular-pubescent species of *Chenopodium* s.l. form a distinct clade separate from the typical goosefoot species. This clade includes other aromatic species such as *Dysphania botrys* and *Dysphania graveolens*.

The plant is an erect herb reaching 0.4–1.2 m in height. Stems are branched, grooved, green to reddish-purple, and covered with glandular hairs that produce the characteristic sticky, aromatic exudate.

Leaves are alternate, lanceolate to ovate, 3–10 cm long and 1–4 cm wide, with irregularly toothed or lobed margins. The upper leaf surface is green and glandular-pubescent; the lower surface is paler with prominent venation.

Inflorescences are dense, spicate clusters in the leaf axils and at stem tips, composed of small greenish flowers lacking petals. Both male and female flowers are present (monoecious).

The fruit is a small lenticular utricle (achene), approximately 1 mm in diameter, containing a single glossy brown-black seed. Seeds are produced prolifically — a single plant can yield over 10,000 seeds.

Seed dispersal occurs through multiple mechanisms: wind, water, attachment to animal fur, and human transport of contaminated soil or plant material. The small seed size facilitates long-distance dispersal.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is native to Central and South America, with a natural range extending from Mexico through Central America, the Caribbean, and into northern South America.

Due to human introduction, it now has a pantropical distribution and is naturalized in southern Europe, Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Pacific islands. It is considered invasive in many of these regions.

The plant thrives in disturbed habitats — roadsides, vacant lots, cultivated fields, pastures, and waste ground. It tolerates a wide range of soil types and pH levels, from sandy coastal soils to heavy clays.

Epazote is remarkably drought-tolerant once established and can persist in semi-arid conditions. It is frost-sensitive and behaves as an annual in temperate climates but can persist as a perennial in frost-free zones.

The plant is tolerant of moderate salinity and has been observed growing in coastal environments. Its adaptability to marginal conditions contributes significantly to its success as a colonizing species across diverse ecosystems.

## Cultivation and Uses

Epazote is primarily gathered from the wild or volunteer populations, though it is also cultivated in traditional Mexican gardens and market gardens. Seeds are sown directly after the last frost in temperate areas.

The plant requires full sun to partial shade and tolerates poor soils. Germination occurs within 7–14 days. Leaves can be harvested beginning 45–60 days after sowing, with continuous harvest possible through the growing season.

For dried use, leaves should be harvested before flowering for optimal oil content. Drying preserves the aromatic compounds but alters the flavor profile compared to fresh material, producing a more muted but still recognizable epazote character.

In Mexican and Central American cuisine, fresh epazote is added to black beans, frijoles de la olla, quesadillas, tamales, and soups. The strong flavor — described as medicinal, minty, citrusy, and resinous — is an acquired taste for many.

The traditional use in beans is both culinary and practical — epazote is believed to reduce the flatulence-causing oligosaccharides in beans, though scientific evidence for this effect is limited.

Beyond beans, epazote is used in salsas verdes, enchiladas suizas, and the Oaxacan dish chilaquiles. In Guatemala, it flavors tamales de chipilín and various stews. The leaves are always used fresh and sparingly, as the flavor is overpowering in large quantities.

In Brazilian traditional medicine, the plant is known as *mastruz* or *erva-de-santa-maria* and is used for respiratory conditions including asthma and bronchitis. In the Caribbean, it is employed as an expectorant and febrifuge.

As an anthelmintic, the whole plant or seeds have been used across cultures to treat intestinal worms, particularly ascarids and hookworms. The essential oil, known as **chenopodium oil** or **American wormseed oil**, was commercially produced as an anthelmintic drug from the 19th through mid-20th centuries.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The essential oil of *C. ambrosioides* is dominated by **ascaridole**, a bicyclic monoterpene endoperoxide that is the primary anthelmintic compound. Ascaridole content ranges from 10–70% of the essential oil depending on chemotype and geographic origin.

Other major constituents include **p-cymene**, **limonene**, **α-terpinene**, and **trans-caryophyllene**. The oil also contains lesser amounts of camphor, myrcene, and carvacrol.

Ascaridole exerts anthelmintic activity by disrupting the metabolic pathways of intestinal parasites, particularly through oxidative damage to parasite tissues. At therapeutic doses, it is effective against *Ascaris lumbricoides*, hookworms, and whipworms.

**Toxicity:** The therapeutic window of ascaridole is narrow. Overdose can cause nausea, vomiting, dizziness, tinnitus, and in severe cases, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and death. Fatalities were reported in the early 20th century from improper dosing of chenopodium oil.

The essential oil also demonstrates antimicrobial activity against bacteria (*Staphylococcus aureus*, *E. coli*), fungi (*Candida albicans*), and protozoa (*Giardia lamblia*) in vitro, supporting broader ethnopharmacological uses.

Recent research has explored the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of leaf extracts. Flavonoids including quercetin and kaempferol derivatives have been isolated, contributing to free radical scavenging activity measured in DPPH and ABTS assays.

The species is also being investigated for potential larvicidal activity against mosquito vectors of dengue and malaria, with promising results in laboratory studies using the essential oil.

## Ecological Role

As a successful colonizer of disturbed habitats, *C. ambrosioides* plays a role in early succession. Its rapid growth, prolific seed production, and allelopathic properties give it a competitive advantage over native vegetation in many introduced ranges.

The glandular trichomes produce secondary metabolites that inhibit germination and growth of neighboring plants, contributing to its invasive success. This allelopathic activity has been documented against several crop species and native herbs.

In its native range, the plant is less aggressive and forms part of the natural weed flora. It provides some ecological value as a food source for generalist herbivores and as nectar/pollen source for small insects.

The species is classified as a noxious or invasive weed in numerous countries including Australia, South Africa, China, and several Pacific island nations. Control efforts typically combine mechanical removal with herbicide application.

Despite its invasive status, the plant has been used in phytoremediation studies for its ability to accumulate heavy metals from contaminated soils, particularly lead and cadmium. This dual nature — useful medicinal plant and aggressive weed — complicates management approaches.

Seeds persist in the soil seed bank for several years, making eradication challenging once established. Regular monitoring and early intervention are the most effective management strategies.
