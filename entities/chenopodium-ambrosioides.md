The plant thrives in disturbed habitats — roadsides, vacant lots, cultivated fields, pastures, and waste ground. It tolerates a wide range of soil types and pH levels, from sandy coastal soils to heavy clays.

Epazote is remarkably drought-tolerant once established and can persist in semi-arid conditions. It is frost-sensitive and behaves as an annual [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

For dried use, leaves should be harvested before flowering for optimal oil content. Drying preserves the aromatic compounds but alters the flavor profile compared to fresh material, producing a more muted but still recognizable epazote character.

In Mexican and Central American cuisine, fresh epazote is added to black beans, frijoles de la olla, quesadillas, tamales, and soups. The strong flavor — described as medicinal, minty, citrusy, and resinous — is an acquired taste for many.

The traditional use in beans is both culinary and practical — epazote is believed to reduce the flatulence-causing oligosaccharides in beans, though scientific evidence for this effect is limited.

Beyond beans, epazote is used in salsas verdes, enchiladas suizas, and the Oaxacan dish chilaquiles. In Guatemala, it flavors tamales de chipilín and various stews. The leaves are always used fresh and sparingly, as the flavor is overpowering in large quantities.

In Brazilian traditional medicine, the plant is known as *mastruz* or *erva-de-santa-maria* and is used for [[blesching-cannabis-safety-profile-subjective-therapeutic-window]] of ascaridole is narrow. Overdose can cause nausea, vomiting, dizziness, tinnitus, and in severe cases, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and death. Fatalities were reported in the early 20th century from improper dosing of chenopodium oil.

The essential oil also demonstrates antimicrobial activity against bacteria (*[[bloomfield-candida-albicans-yeast-hyphal-dimorphism]]*), and protozoa (*Giardia lamblia*) in vitro, supporting broader ethnopharmacological uses.

Recent research has explored the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of leaf extracts. Flavonoids including quercetin and kaempferol derivatives have been isolated, contributing to free radical scavenging activity measured in DPPH and ABTS assays.
