# Gymnopilus fulgens
Gymnopilus fulgens is a species of agaric fungus in the family
Hymenogastraceae (formerly Cortinariaceae), recognized as one of
approximately 14 psychoactive species within the genus Gymnopilus.
The mushroom is notable for its bright orange to golden-orange
coloration, bitter taste, and presence of the psychoactive compound
psilocybin. Unlike the well-known Psilocybe genus, Gymnopilus species
are primarily wood-inhabiting saprobes that fruit on dead hardwood
logs and stumps, often in conspicuous clusters. G. fulgens was
described by Petter Adolf Karsten in 1876 and has since been reported
from temperate and subtropical regions worldwide, though it remains
understudied compared to Psilocybe species.
## Taxonomy and Naming
- **Species:** G. fulgens (J. Schaeff.) P. Karst., 1876
- **Common Names:** Big laughing gym (applied to genus broadly),
  spectacular rustgill (sometimes applied to G. spectabilis,
  occasionally confused with G. fulgens)
- **Etymology:** "Gymno-" (naked) + "-pilus" (cap), referring to the
  exposed gills; "fulgens" from Latin for "shining" or "glittering,"
  describing the cap's bright appearance
## Macroscopic Description
### Cap (Pileus)
- **Shape:** Convex to broadly convex when young, becoming
  plano-convex to slightly depressed with age; margin often
  inrolled when young, becoming wavy and sometimes cracked
- **Surface:** Dry, finely fibrillose to scaly (especially near
  the center); hygrophanous to some degree; often cracking in
  dry weather
- **Color:** Bright orange to golden-orange when young and fresh;
  fading to yellow-orange or ochraceous with age; center often
  darker (deep orange to brown-orange)
- **Context:** Firm, yellowish-orange; does not change color on
  bruising (distinguishes from some blueing Gymnopilus species)
- **Taste:** Extremely bitter — this is a key diagnostic feature
  for the genus
- **Odor:** Faint to mild, sometimes described as slightly
  mealy or farinaceous
### Gills (Lamellae)
- **Attachment:** Adnate to adnexed, sometimes slightly sinuate
- **Color:** Bright yellow to orange-yellow when young, becoming
  rusty brown to cinnamon-rust with spore maturity
- **Edge:** Often paler (whiter) than gill face, especially
  when young
- **Lamellulae:** Present, of varying lengths (2–4 tiers)
### Stem (Stipe)
- **Shape:** Equal to slightly tapering upward, often curved
- **Surface:** Dry, fibrillose to slightly scaly below the
  ring zone; yellowish-orange, darker below the ring
- **Ring:** Present as a membranous partial veil remnant,
  forming a superior ring or annular zone; often evanescent
  or leaving only a fibrillose zone
  white to yellowish mycelial tomentum
- **Context:** Solid to slightly hollow with age; fibrous;
  yellowish-orange
### Spore Print
- **Color:** Rusty brown to ferruginous (characteristic of
  the genus Gymnopilus)
## Microscopic Features
### Spores
- **Shape:** Ellipsoid to amygdaliform (almond-shaped), sometimes
  slightly angular or rugose (wrinkled) in side view
- **Size:** 7.0–9.5 × 4.5–6.0 µm (average ~8.2 × 5.3 µm)
### Basidia
- **Clamp Connections:** Absent at basidial bases (important
  taxonomic character for Gymnopilus vs. Pholiota)
### Cheilocystidia and Pleurocystidia
- **Cheilocystidia:** Ventricose to lageniform (flask-shaped),
  18–35 × 5–10 µm; thin-walled; abundant, forming a sterile
  gill edge
- **Pleurocystidia:** Similar to cheilocystidia but less
  abundant; sometimes absent or sparse
- **Pileipellis:** Cutis of interwoven hyphae; hyphae 3–7 µm
  diameter, with yellowish-brown intracellular pigment
## Distribution and Habitat
- **Geographic Range:** Widely distributed in temperate and
  subtropical zones; reported from Europe (especially
  Scandinavia, Central Europe, British Isles), North America
  (eastern and Pacific Northwest), parts of Asia (Japan,
  Korea), and possibly South America
  (especially oaks, Quercus spp.; also beech, birch, alder,
  maple, and occasionally conifers)
- **Habitat:** Deciduous and mixed forests, woodlands, parkland;
  grows on fallen logs, stumps, standing dead trees, and
  buried wood
- **Fruiting Pattern:** Summer to autumn (July–November in
  Northern Hemisphere); occasionally in spring in warmer
  climates
- **Fruiting Habit:** Typically solitary to scattered, sometimes
  in small clusters or caespitose groups on the same substrate
  to similarity to non-psychoactive orange Gymnopilus species
## Chemistry and Active Compounds
Gymnopilus fulgens contains psilocybin and psilocin, though
concentrations are generally lower than in Psilocybe species.
The genus Gymnopilus is also notable for containing bis-noryangonin,
a precursor to the alpha-pyrone pigments characteristic of the genus.
### Reported Alkaloid Content
| Compound | Status | Notes |
|----------|--------|-------|
| Psilocybin | Present | Detected in several Gymnopilus species |
| Psilocin | Present (trace) | Product of psilocybin dephosphorylation |
| Baeocystin | Unconfirmed | Reported in some Gymnopilus species |
| Bis-noryangonin | Present | Characteristic pigment precursor |
| Gymnopilin | Present (genus) | Bitter principle; toxic to insects |

## Similar Species
| Species | Key Differences from G. fulgens |
|---------|-------------------------------|
| Gymnopilus spectabilis | Larger (cap to 20 cm); more robust; similar chemistry |
| Gymnopilus junonius | Larger; cap more scaly; found on conifers too |
| Gymnopilus penetrans | Smaller; brighter orange; grows on conifers |
| Gymnopilus sapineus | Smaller (cap to 5 cm); thinner flesh; conifer substrate |
| Gymnopilus luteofolius | Purple tones in cap and gills; smaller stature |
