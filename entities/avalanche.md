# Avalanche

## Description

An avalanche is a rapid flow of snow down a slope, representing one of the most dangerous
natural hazards in mountainous and cold-climate regions. Mollison provides detailed technical
analysis of avalanche mechanics, prediction, and design strategies for prevention and mitigation.
Understanding avalanche dynamics is essential for cold-climate [[permaculture]] settlement,
infrastructure design, and safe site selection.

Avalanches pick up additional material as they descend — snow, mud, boulders, clay, and
vegetation. Often the high-pressure air wave preceding the avalanche causes more destruction
than the snow mass itself, traveling at up to 330 m/sec and knocking down trees and buildings.

## Classification

- **Type:** Natural hazard / cryospheric mass movement
- **Category:** Cold-climate design constraint, safety consideration
- **Sub-types:** Systematic (annual), episodic (~35 years), periodic (rare events)
- **Trigger mechanisms:** Wind crust breakage, freeze-thaw, warm winds, spring thaw, cornice collapse
- **Snow movement modes:** Microcreep, snow boards, snow mounds, snow bodies, slides, full avalanches

## Key Facts

- Risk begins on slopes exceeding 6 degrees with snow accumulation of 15 cm or more
- Avalanche speeds reach 6.5 m/s on long slopes; compressed air blast travels at 330 m/sec
- Snow-dust avalanches produce waves of 10–100 m/sec and pressures of 35 atmospheres
- Impact forces range from 4–54 tonnes per square metre depending on mass and debris load
- 80% of avalanches occur where old compact snow is dislodged by moisture from air above 70% humidity
- Triggered by gunshot, cornice collapse, strong winds, skiers, or even animal movement
- Pioneer species like aspen delineate historical avalanche chutes on the landscape
- Acid rain damage to steep-slope forests creates new and expanding avalanche threat zones
- Meltwater lubrication typically triggers slides between midday and 3 pm in fine weather
- Sudden falls of 50–60 cm snow followed by clear weather create unstable conditions
- Solar radiation crust on snow preserves slope stability until broken by wind or thaw

## Snow Movement Modes (from Mollison)

- **Microcreep:** Freeze-thaw and gravity advance snow downslope a few centimetres per day
- **Snow boards (slip):** Thick snow masses covering metres per day
- **Snow mound:** Advances from rocky uplands as streamflow effect
- **Snow bodies:** Move en masse down short slopes
- **Snow slides:** Velocities of 4–5 m/sec, arrested by terraces
- **Avalanches:** Increase speed down long slopes up to 6.5 m/sec; episodic (every ~35 years)

## Avalanche Types (from Mollison)

- **Episodic:** Every approximately 35 years; great magnitude, largest destructive potential
- **Systematic:** Annual events; unusual but predictable, manageable with routine precautions
- **Periodic:** Rare events associated with exceptional snowfall years, highest surprise factor
- **Snow-dust:** Dry powder avalanches with extreme air blast effects and long runout distances

## Prevention and Mitigation

- Preservation of high-slope forests is the single best defense against avalanches
- Place essential services in tunnels to protect infrastructure from avalanche damage
- V-shaped barriers split avalanche front and reduce impact force on structures below
- Prevent settlement in high-risk zones to avoid catastrophic loss of life
- Snow barriers on plateaus above slopes hold snow above the danger zone effectively
- Cross-slope terraces and walls have generally proved ineffective as prevention
- Map avalanche chutes using botanical indicators: aspen strips, missing lower limbs, lean

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Site selection:** Avalanche zones must be avoided for all structures, gardens, and livestock areas
- **Botanical indicators:** Aspen strips, missing lower limbs, downslope lean reveal past avalanche paths
- **Forest management:** Maintaining healthy high-slope forests is the most effective prevention strategy
- **[[mushroom-cultivation]]:** Underground growing chambers must account for avalanche risk and snow loading
- **Mycorrhizal forests:** Healthy slope forests provide both avalanche protection and fungal habitat
- **Post-avalanche succession:** Creates unique habitat mosaics supporting diverse fungal communities
- **Design principle:** Map all [[permaculture-zones]] against avalanche risk before site development
