# Debaryomyces occidentalis

## Description

*Debaryomyces occidentalis* (also classified as *Schwanniomyces occidentalis*) is an ascomycetous yeast species isolated exclusively from soils. It is notable for its strong amylolytic activity — the ability to degrade starch — which distinguishes it from many other soil yeasts. This starch-degrading capability has attracted interest for industrial biotechnology applications, particularly in the production of single-cell protein and bioconversion of starchy waste materials.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Eukarya
- **[[kingdom-fungi]]
- **Phylum:** [[ascomycota]]
- **Class:** Saccharomycetes
- **Order:** Saccharomycetales
- **Genus:** *Debaryomyces* (syn. *Schwanniomyces*)
- **Species:** *D. occidentalis*

## Key Facts

- *D. occidentalis* is isolated exclusively from soils, making it one of the few yeast species with a primarily edaphic (soil-associated) ecology.
- The species produces powerful extracellular amylases capable of efficiently hydrolyzing starch to fermentable sugars.
- Its amylolytic activity exceeds that of *[[saccharomyces-cerevisiae]]*, making it a candidate for direct starch fermentation processes.
- Cell biomass has been evaluated as a source of single-cell protein (SCP) for animal feed applications.
- The species is osmotolerant, able to grow in environments with elevated solute concentrations, which is advantageous for processing concentrated starch slurries.
- *D. occidentalis* can utilize a wide range of carbon sources beyond starch, including various mono- and disaccharides.
- Molecular phylogenetic studies (rDNA sequencing) have clarified its placement within the Saccharomycetales and its relationship to other *Debaryomyces* species.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

*D. occidentalis* is primarily of interest in industrial microbiology and biotechnology:
- **Starch bioconversion:** Can directly convert starchy agricultural waste (cassava peel, potato waste, grain offal) into ethanol or protein-rich biomass.
- **Single-cell protein:** Fast growth on starch substrates makes it suitable for protein feed production.
- **Enzyme production:** Source of thermostable amylases for food and beverage processing.
- **Soil ecology:** As an exclusive soil isolate, it contributes to starch and organic matter cycling in terrestrial ecosystems.

For cultivators using grain-based substrates, understanding the native yeast communities that colonize grain — including starch-degrading species like *D. occidentalis* — can inform substrate preparation and sterilization protocols.
