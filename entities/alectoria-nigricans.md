# Alectoria nigricans (Black Witch's Hair Lichen)

## Overview

*Alectoria nigricans* is a distinctive fruticose lichen in the family Parmeliaceae, forming dark brown to blackish, hair-like tufts on rocks and tree bark in Arctic and high alpine environments. It is one of the most cold-tolerant lichen species in the Northern Hemisphere.

The species is characterized by its conspicuously dark thallus, which distinguishes it from the more common and lighter-colored *Alectoria sarmentosa*. The black pigmentation is partly due to dense concentrations of melanin-like compounds in the cortex.

*A. nigricans* has a circumpolar Arctic distribution, extending southward only at high elevations. It is a dominant epiphyte and saxicolous lichen on tundra landscapes and in alpine communities above the treeline.

The species plays an important ecological role in Arctic food webs, serving as winter forage for caribou and muskox. Its dark pigmentation may enhance heat absorption in cold environments.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Lecanoromycetes
- **Order:** Lecanorales
- **Family:** Parmeliaceae
- **Genus:** *Alectoria* Ach.
- **Species:** *A. nigricans* (Ach.) Nyl.

**Synonyms:**
- *Usnea nigricans* Ach. (basionym)
- *Bryoria nigricans* (Ach.) Brodo & D. Hawksw.
- *Alectoria jubata* var. *nigricans* (Ach.) Nyl.
- *Alectoria nigricans* f. *tomentella* (Lynge) Thomson

The thallus is fruticose, forming pendulous to suberect tufts 3–15 cm long. Branches are cylindrical, 0.1–0.3 mm in diameter, and dark brown to black throughout, lacking the paler bases seen in some congeners.

Branching is dichotomous to irregularly isotomic-dichotomous, producing a dense, tangled mass. The surface is matte to slightly glossy, with fine longitudinal striations visible under magnification.

The cortex is well-developed, approximately 15–25 μm thick, composed of densely packed, heavily pigmented hyphae. This pigmentation is responsible for the characteristically dark coloration.

Pseudocyphellae are absent or extremely rare, distinguishing *A. nigricans* from *A. sarmentosa* which has conspicuous white pseudocyphellae along branch surfaces.

Apothecia are rare, lateral, 1–2 mm in diameter, with a brown disc and thin thalline margin. Spores are broadly ellipsoid, colorless, 8 per ascus, approximately 8–12 × 5–7 μm.

Pycnidia are frequent, producing bacilliform conidia 5–8 × 1 μm. Vegetative dispersal occurs primarily through thallus fragmentation, which is effective in windy Arctic environments.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Alectoria nigricans* has a circumpolar Arctic and high alpine distribution. In North America, it occurs across the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, northern Alaska, Greenland, and southward in alpine zones of the Rocky Mountains and Coast Ranges.

In Eurasia, the species is found across Svalbard, Novaya Zemlya, northern Scandinavia, and the Kola Peninsula. Alpine populations extend through the Urals, Altai, and other Asian mountain ranges.

The species colonizes both lithic (rock) and corticolous (bark) substrates. On rock, it grows on granitic and basaltic outcrops, boulders, and stone surfaces in tundra landscapes. On bark, it prefers *Salix arctica*, *Betula nana*, and dead standing wood.

It is found from sea level in high Arctic regions to approximately 2,800 m in more southerly mountain ranges. The species tolerates extreme cold, prolonged darkness, and desiccation.

*A. nigricans* is characteristic of exposed, wind-swept tundra habitats and fell-field communities. It is less frequent in sheltered valleys or areas with persistent snow cover that limits light availability.

## Ecology and Symbiosis

*Alectoria nigricans* associates with a trebouxioid green algal photobiont, typical of Parmeliaceae. The symbiosis is adapted to extreme photophysiological conditions including prolonged [[darkness-low-light-facilitation-entheogenic-ceremonies]] and freezing temperatures.

The dark cortical pigmentation provides photoprotection against intense UV radiation during the Arctic summer. It may also increase absorption of solar radiation, raising thallus temperature and accelerating [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] during brief growing seasons.

The species is a significant component of caribou and muskox winter diets in the Canadian Arctic. Its high carbohydrate content provides critical energy during periods when other forage is unavailable.

*A. nigricans* contributes to soil formation on rocky Arctic substrates through slow weathering and [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] accumulation. Dead thallus material breaks down into humus that supports tundra vegetation.

The species hosts lichenicolous fungi including *Arthonia alectoriicola* and *Endococcus alectoriae*. These parasites are widespread but seldom cause significant thallus mortality.

## Chemistry and Secondary Metabolites

The primary secondary metabolite of *Alectoria nigricans* is alectoronic acid, a depsidone that is characteristic of the genus *Alectoria*. Some populations additionally contain α-collatolic acid.

Spot test reactions: K+ yellow, C−, KC+ yellow, Pd+ orange. The chemistry separates *A. nigricans* from *Bryoria* species that contain physodic or norstictic acid.

Usnic acid is absent from *A. nigricans*, which contrasts with *A. sarmentosa* where usnic acid is a major cortical compound. The absence of usnic acid contributes to the darker, less yellow coloration.

No toxic compounds have been identified in *A. nigricans*. The species is considered safe for consumption by wildlife, and its palatability to caribou is higher than some chemically defended *Bryoria* species.

## Traditional Uses and Conservation

Indigenous peoples of the Canadian Arctic, including the Inuit and Inuvialuit, have historically used *Alectoria* species as emergency food during starvation periods. The lichens were typically boiled or steamed to soften them.

*A. nigricans* was reportedly less preferred than *A. sarmentosa* or *Bryoria fremontii* as food due to its tougher texture and darker coloration. Its use was primarily restricted to dire circumstances.

In Greenlandic traditional medicine, *Alectoria* species were occasionally applied as wound dressings. The [[mycelium-running-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties-species-chart]] of lichen acids may have contributed some therapeutic benefit.

The species is not currently considered threatened due to its vast Arctic range. However, climate-driven shrub expansion in the tundra may reduce its habitat extent by increasing competition from vascular plants.

Warming temperatures and longer growing seasons favor shrub encroachment onto open tundra, which could displace lichen-dominated communities. Long-term monitoring studies have documented declines in fruticose lichen cover across parts of the Arctic.

## Similar Species

- ***Alectoria sarmentosa*** — Lighter grey-green color, conspicuous white pseudocyphellae, contains usnic acid. More southerly and oceanic distribution. Common on conifer bark in temperate forests.

- ***Bryoria fuscescens*** — Dark brown but not black, thinner branches, contains physodic acid. More abundant in [[fungal-diversity-boreal-forests-taiga]]. Frequently anastomosing branches.

- ***Bryoria trichodes*** — Pale brown to grey, contains norstictic acid. Less darkly pigmented cortex. More southerly distribution in boreal and subalpine forests.

- ***Bryoria nadvornikiana*** — Very similar dark coloration but contains thamnolic acid (K+ yellow → red). Alpine European species with limited Arctic distribution.
