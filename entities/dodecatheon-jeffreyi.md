The species is among the most conspicuous and widely distributed shooting stars in the western [[native-plant-gardening]] for its striking flowers and its role as a nectar source for [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] during the mid-nineteenth century.

Modern phylogenetic studies have shown that Dodecatheon is nested within Primula, and some taxonomic treatments now place the species as Primula jeffreyi. However, the traditional genus Dodecatheon remains in widespread use.

The plant produces a basal rosette of leaves and one or more erect, leafless scapes measuring 20–60 cm tall. The scapes are slender, slightly sticky near the inflorescence, and green to reddish in color.

The species is most abundant in the Cascade Range, Sierra Nevada, and Rocky Mountains, where it occurs in moist montane and subalpine habitats. Populations also extend to coastal ranges in the Pacific Northwest.

Elevation ranges from near sea level in coastal Alaska to approximately 3,500 meters in the southern Sierra Nevada and Rocky Mountains.

The plant grows in moist meadows, stream banks, seeps, marsh edges, open woods, and subalpine parklands. It prefers soils that are moist through the spring flowering period but may dry during summer dormancy.

Optimal habitats include montane meadows with snowmelt moisture, riparian zones along mountain streams, and damp openings in coniferous forests. The species is often locally abundant in favorable sites.

## Ecology and Pollination

The distinctive flower morphology of Dodecatheon jeffreyi is an adaptation to buzz pollination. The fused stamens form a tight cone that requires vibration by bumblebees to release pollen.

Buzz pollination is accomplished when a bumblebee grasps the stamen cone and vibrates its flight muscles, causing pollen grains to be ejected from pores at the anther tips. This specialized pollination mechanism is shared with other Dodecatheon species.

Bumblebees of the genus Bombus are the primary pollinators. The flowers provide only pollen as a reward, with no nectar produced. The bright color and reflexed petals serve as visual signals to attract pollinators.

The flowers are pendant, with the stamen cone pointing downward. This orientation is thought to protect the pollen from rain and to position the anthers for efficient pollen transfer during buzz pollination.

Flowering occurs from May through August depending on elevation and latitude. Higher elevation populations bloom later, often coinciding with snowmelt in subalpine meadows.

The plant enters dormancy by mid-summer in most locations, with above-ground foliage dying back completely. The dormant period may last 2–4 months depending on site conditions.

## Cultivation and Uses

Dodecatheon jeffreyi is cultivated in native plant gardens, rock gardens, and woodland gardens where its moisture requirements can be met. It is valued for its striking flowers and early summer bloom time.

The species prefers partial shade to full sun in consistently moist, well-drained soil. It performs best in cool summer climates and struggles in areas with hot, humid summers.

Propagation is by seed, which requires a period of cold stratification for reliable germination. Seeds should be sown in autumn or stratified at 4°C for 4–8 weeks before sowing in spring.

Seed-grown plants typically flower in their second or third year. The species can also be propagated by division of dormant tuberous roots in early autumn, though this is less commonly practiced.

Several [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] groups in the Pacific Northwest traditionally used Dodecatheon species for minor medicinal purposes. The roots were prepared as poultices for wounds or as mild remedies for various ailments.

No significant modern pharmaceutical or commercial applications exist for the species. Its primary value is ornamental and ecological as a component of native wildflower meadows.
