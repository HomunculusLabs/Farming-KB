## Stoddart

## Description

Stoddart is a range management researcher whose 1955 work provided critical data on rangeland degradation, soil compaction, and the relationship between poor range management and insect plagues. Mollison cites Stoddart's findings extensively in his treatment of rangelands, particularly regarding the quantitative impacts of livestock grazing on soil pore space, water infiltration, and the direct correlation between range depletion and grasshopper outbreaks. Stoddart's research established measurable benchmarks connecting grazing intensity to soil and vegetation degradation.

## Classification

- **Category**: Researcher / Range scientist
- **Field**: Range management and ecology
- **Period**: Active 1955 (cited publication date)
- **Contributions**: Soil compaction data, range-insect dynamics, runoff and erosion measurements

## Key Facts

- Published key range management research in 1955
- Found a direct correlation between range depletion and grasshopper outbreaks
- After only 9 years of stocking on range, soil pore space was reduced 44% in the top two inches and 60% in the lower 24-inch deep layer of soils
- Documented that runoff water from rangelands may carry 6-10% silt by volume
- Measured a flow of 17,500 tonnes of water per hour carrying 500 tonnes of organic matter and 1,750 tonnes of soil
- Established quantitative relationships between grazing intensity, soil pore space, and water infiltration rates
- Demonstrated that overgrazed rangelands absorb up to five times less water than properly managed range
- Provided data on vegetation cover thresholds: 65-70% total plant cover is needed to totally prevent erosion
- Documented the progression from good range climax (wheatgrass) through depletion stages to annual weed dominance with escalating runoff and soil loss

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Stoddart's range research has indirect but important connections to [[fungal-ecology]]:

1. **Soil compaction and fungi**: The 44-60% reduction in soil pore space from grazing directly reduces habitat for soil fungi and [[mycorrhizal-networks]] — compacted soils have less air space essential for fungal respiration
2. **Organic matter loss**: The measured loss of 500 tonnes of organic matter per hour in runoff represents massive loss of fungal substrate and habitat
3. **Vegetation-fungal connections**: Range depletion removes the plant hosts that support mycorrhizal partnerships, cascading into fungal community collapse
4. **Insect-fungal dynamics**: Grasshopper outbreaks on depleted range interact with entomopathogenic fungi (Beauveria, Metarhizium) that naturally regulate insect populations
5. **Soil food web disruption**: The compaction, organic matter loss, and vegetation change documented by Stoddart represent wholesale disruption of soil food webs including fungal networks
6. **Restoration [[mycology]]**: Stoddart's data provides benchmarks for understanding the degree of degradation that fungal inoculation and mycorrhizal restoration would need to address

## Quantitative Data (from Stoddart 1955)

Soil Pore Space and Infiltration:
- Slight/Undergrazed: 68.1% pore space, 4.14% infiltration per hour
- Overgrazed: 51.1% pore space, 2.16% infiltration per hour
- Depleted by Grazing: 46.5% pore space, 0.82% infiltration per hour

Vegetation Cover, Runoff, and Erosion:
- Wheatgrass (good climax): 0.4% runoff, 0.001 t/ha soil eroded — Sustainable
- Lupin and Needle grass: 4.99% runoff, 0.963 t/ha soil eroded — Exceeds soil creation
- Annual brome grass: 2.55% runoff, 0.425 t/ha soil eroded — Temporary stability
- Annual weed species: 60.8% runoff, 3.094 t/ha soil eroded — Near plough-culture losses
