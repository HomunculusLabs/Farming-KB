## Overview

Pachypodium geayi is a large, tree-forming caudiciform succulent endemic to southern Madagascar. It is one of the tallest species in the genus Pachypodium, reaching heights of 4–8 meters in cultivation and occasionally taller in habitat.

Common names include Madagascar palm (shared with other Pachypodium species) and Geay's pachypodium. The species was named after the French naturalist Raymond Geay, who collected in Madagascar in the early 20th century.

It is prized in cultivation for its dramatic silver-gray trunk, fierce paired spines, and clusters of white flowers with contrasting yellow centers. The plant's architectural form makes it a sought-after specimen in succulent collections worldwide.

Like all Pachypodium species, Pachypodium geayi is listed on CITES Appendix II, which regulates but does not prohibit international trade. All plant parts contain toxic cardiac glycosides characteristic of the Apocynaceae family.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Pachypodium geayi belongs to the family Apocynaceae, subfamily Apocynoideae. The species was formally described by Costantin and Bois in 1907 from type material collected near Fort Dauphin (now Taolanaro) in the Anosy region.

The genus Pachypodium contains approximately 25 species, all endemic to Madagascar and southern Africa. Pachypodium geayi falls within the Madagascar clade, closely allied with Pachypodium lamerei and Pachypodium baronii.

The plant develops a thick, bottle-shaped to cylindrical trunk covered in prominent paired spines up to 3 cm long. The bark is a distinctive silvery gray, which immediately distinguishes it from Pachypodium lamerei with its darker green trunk.

Branching begins once the trunk reaches 1–2 meters in height, producing a crown of stout ascending branches. Each branch is also armed with paired conical spines that persist for several years before bark overgrowth covers them.

The trunk can reach 30–50 cm in diameter at the base in mature specimens, tapering gradually upward. Internally, the trunk tissue is a water-storing parenchyma that allows the plant to survive months without rainfall.

Leaves are long, narrow, and dark green with a prominent pale midrib, reaching 20–40 cm in length and 2–4 cm in width. They cluster at the branch tips in pseudo-whorls and are fully deciduous during the dry winter dormancy.

New leaf growth emerges rapidly at the start of the rainy season, often before the first significant rainfall. This precocious leafing takes advantage of stored water reserves in the trunk to jumpstart photosynthesis.

Flowers appear in compact terminal cymes during the warm season, each measuring 4–6 cm across. The five petals are white to cream with a contrasting bright yellow corona at the center, producing a sweet fragrance at dusk.

The fruits are paired, narrowly cylindrical follicles 10–20 cm long containing numerous brown seeds. Each seed bears a tuft of silky hairs (coma) for wind dispersal across the arid landscape.

## Distribution and Habitat

Pachypodium geayi is endemic to the spiny forest and dry deciduous forest regions of southern and southwestern Madagascar. Its range extends from near Taolanaro westward to the Mandrare River valley.

It grows on well-drained sandy or rocky substrates, often on limestone outcrops, gneiss, and in xerophytic scrublands at 0–500 meters elevation. The species is a conspicuous structural element of the southern Madagascar spiny thicket ecoregion.

The region receives 400–800 mm of annual rainfall, predominantly during the austral summer (November through March). A pronounced dry season of 6–8 months follows, during which the plant sheds leaves entirely.

Temperatures remain warm year-round (15–38°C). The species tolerates extreme heat but has no frost tolerance — damage occurs below 10°C and death follows prolonged exposure near 0°C.

Soils in its native range are predominantly sandy, acidic to neutral (pH 5.5–7.0), and extremely low in organic matter. The species associates with other drought-adapted flora including Alluaudia procera, Didierea madagascariensis, and various Euphorbia species.

## Cultivation

Pachypodium geayi requires full sun to bright partial shade and a very well-draining soil mix. A blend of 50% mineral grit (pumice, perlite, coarse sand) and 50% organic potting mix provides ideal drainage.

Water thoroughly during the active growing season (spring through early autumn), allowing soil to dry completely between waterings. Overwatering is the most common cause of death, leading to rapid root rot in mature specimens.

Withhold all water during winter dormancy when the plant is leafless. A cool, dry resting period at 12–18°C promotes vigorous growth and flowering in the following season.

Propagation is primarily by seed, as stem cuttings rarely root and the wound frequently leads to fatal rot. Fresh seeds sown at 25–30°C typically germinate in 7–21 days.

Growth is moderate for a caudiciform — young plants form a distinct swollen caudex within 3–5 years. Specimens reach flowering size in 8–12 years from seed under optimal conditions.

In warm climates (USDA zones 10–11), Pachypodium geayi can be grown outdoors as a landscape specimen. It performs exceptionally well in rock gardens, Mediterranean-style plantings, and xeriscapes with excellent drainage.

Container-grown plants benefit from a deep pot that accommodates the substantial root system. Clay or terracotta pots are preferred over plastic because they allow better soil aeration and evaporation of excess moisture.

Repot infrequently, as the plant resents root disturbance and the spiny trunk makes handling difficult. Use thick gloves and support the heavy caudex during transplanting.

Pests include mealybugs, spider mites, and scale insects, which can hide in the dense spine clusters. Regular inspection and prompt treatment with neem oil or insecticidal soap keeps infestations manageable.

## Toxicity and Chemistry

Like other Apocynaceae, Pachypodium geayi produces cardiac glycosides throughout its tissues, particularly concentrated in the latex sap. These compounds include oleandrin-like cardenolides that affect cardiac muscle contraction.

Ingestion of any plant part can cause nausea, vomiting, cardiac arrhythmia, and in severe cases, death. The sap is also a skin irritant and should be washed off immediately after contact.

Despite toxicity concerns, some Malagasy traditional healers use minute quantities of Pachypodium bark preparations as purgatives and emetics. Such uses are extremely dangerous and not medically endorsed.

The toxic properties serve as an effective defense against herbivory by native lemurs and introduced grazing animals. Few natural predators can tolerate the bitter, cardenolide-laden tissues.

Research has identified geayin, a novel cardenolide compound, from the stem bark of Pachypodium geayi. This compound shows in vitro cytotoxic activity against certain cancer cell lines, though clinical applications remain unexplored.

## Ecology and Conservation

In its native habitat, Pachypodium geayi is a drought-adapted structural element of the spiny forest ecosystem. Its large, water-storing trunk provides a moisture reservoir supporting surrounding microhabitats during drought.

The fragrant white flowers attract hawk moths (Sphingidae) and other nocturnal pollinators. Fallen leaves contribute sparse but ecologically important organic matter to the nutrient-poor sandy soils.

The plant's spine architecture also provides shelter for small reptiles and invertebrates within the spiny forest. Lizard species in particular use the space between spine pairs as refuge from predators.

All Pachypodium species are listed on CITES Appendix II, controlling but not banning international trade. Export from Madagascar requires valid CITES permits, and enforcement has reduced but not eliminated illegal collection.

Primary conservation threats include habitat destruction for slash-and-burn agriculture (tavy), charcoal production, and illegal harvesting for the horticultural trade. The species' restricted range makes it vulnerable to localized habitat loss.

Several protected areas within its range, including Berenty Reserve and Andohahela National Park, provide habitat security. Ex situ conservation through seed-grown specimens in botanical gardens reduces pressure on wild populations.

Climate change projections suggest increased drought severity and altered rainfall patterns across southern Madagascar. These changes may compress suitable habitat for Pachypodium geayi, making ex situ collections increasingly important as genetic reservoirs.
