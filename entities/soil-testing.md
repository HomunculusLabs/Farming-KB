## Soil Testing

## Description

**Soil testing** is the laboratory analysis of soil samples to determine the levels of essential plant nutrients, pH, cation exchange capacity (CEC), organic matter content, soil texture, salt content, and biological activity. Soil testing is the foundational diagnostic practice for informed fertilization and soil management, replacing guesswork with quantitative data. Despite being universally recommended in gardening literature, it remains one of the most commonly skipped practices among home gardeners.

## Classification

- **Category:** Agricultural diagnostic practice / Laboratory procedure
- **Related Tests:** Nutrient analysis, pH testing, CEC measurement, organic matter content, biological assays, tissue testing
- **Domain:** Soil science, agronomy, horticulture, environmental science

## Key Facts

- Soil testing is the only reliable way to determine which nutrients are present, deficient, or in excess in a given soil.
- Tests can cost as little as ten dollars but provide information essential to avoiding wasted fertilizer, environmental runoff, and plant nutrient deficiencies.
- Essential tests include: nutrient levels (all 14 mineral nutrients), pH, CEC, organic matter percentage, and biological activity.
- **CEC testing** reveals the soil's capacity to hold cation nutrients (calcium, magnesium, potassium, ammonium nitrogen) — its "soil savings account."
- **pH testing** is critical because pH strongly influences nutrient availability, particularly phosphorus; the pH scale is logarithmic (each unit = 10× change).
- **Organic matter testing** (reported as percentage) is important for organic gardeners; most garden soils contain 4–8% organic matter. This number should trend upward over years.
- **Biological testing** assesses soil food web health, including mycorrhizal fungi populations and microbial diversity, though this may require a different laboratory.

## Sampling Protocol

- Use plastic containers and wooden or plastic tools only — brass, bronze, and galvanized tools can contaminate samples with copper or zinc.
- For biological tests, containers and tools must be sterilized; do not touch the soil with bare hands.
- Garden beds: sample to 6 inches (15 cm) depth.
- Trees and shrubs: sample 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) under the drip line.
- Lawns: sample to 4 inches (10 cm) depth.
- Take multiple samples per area and mix together for a single composite test.
- Cover problematic areas and high-performing areas separately.

## Finding a Laboratory

- Local laboratories have more experience with regional soil types and conditions.
- Government resources include Cooperative Extension Service, Soil and Water Conservation Districts, Natural Resources Conservation Service, and university soil labs.
- University laboratories provide straightforward results without product bias.
- Home testing kits are of limited value — they typically only test N-P-K and not the other eleven essential mineral nutrients.

## Frequency and Follow-Up

- Initial test establishes a baseline; second test at end of season or beginning of next season reveals trends.
- Vegetable/annual gardens: retest every 2–3 years.
- Lawns: retest every 5–7 years.
- Trees/shrubs/perennials: test only if problems develop.
- Test annually only if aggressively breaking the Law of Return or in competitive growing situations.
- Follow laboratory recommendations for amendments; request organic fertilizer recommendations if applicable.
- Oregon State University and Purdue University offer free fertilizer calculators for converting chemical to organic recommendations.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Biological testing can assess mycorrhizal fungi presence and population health — critical for understanding phosphorus and micronutrient delivery to plants.
- Soil food web testing reveals whether the biological cycling of nutrients is functioning, which is the primary mechanism by which organic nutrients become plant-available.
- Understanding soil test results helps cultivators make informed decisions about compost, mulch, and organic amendment applications rather than applying materials prophylactically.
- Tissue testing (in addition to soil testing) may be necessary when plants show deficiency symptoms, since visual symptoms of different deficiencies can be similar.

The soil testing exhibits distinct physical and chemical properties relevant to its use. These characteristics determine appropriate application rates and methods.

## Application Guidelines

For optimal results, soil testing should be applied according to recommended rates and timing specific to the target crop and soil conditions.

## Additional Information

Soil Testing is recognized for its contributions to its field of study. Research continues to explore its properties, applications, and relationships to related topics. Understanding its characteristics helps inform practical decisions and theoretical frameworks.
