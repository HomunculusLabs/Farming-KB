# Lavandula × intermedia

## Overview

Lavandula × intermedia, commonly known as lavandin, is a sterile hybrid between Lavandula angustifolia (English lavender) and Lavandula latifolia (spike lavender). The hybrid occurs naturally where the parent species overlap in range and has been extensively cultivated for essential oil production.

Lavandin is the most commercially important Lavandula taxon for essential oil yield, producing three to five times more oil per hectare than English lavender. The oil is widely used in perfumery, aromatherapy, cosmetics, and household cleaning products.

The species epithet intermedia reflects its intermediate morphology between the two parent species. Major commercial cultivars include Grosso, Abrialii, Super, and Provence, each with distinct oil composition profiles.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Lamiales
- **Family:** Lamiaceae
- **Genus:** Lavandula
- **Species:** Lavandula × intermedia Emeric ex Loisel.

The hybrid designation × indicates artificial or natural hybrid origin. Lavandin is sterile and reproduces exclusively through vegetative propagation. Chromosome number is 2n = 49 (allotetraploid), combining the diploid (2n = 30) L. angustifolia and the tetraploid (2n = 48) L. latifolia genomes.

### Morphology

Lavandula × intermedia is a bushy, aromatic shrub reaching 60–100 cm in height, larger than either parent species. The habit is rounded and densely branched, forming a mound of grey-green foliage.

Leaves are linear-lanceolate, 3–5 cm long, grey-green to silvery, and covered with stellate trichomes that give the foliage its characteristic dusty appearance. Leaves are evergreen in mild climates.

Inflorescences are terminal spikes 6–12 cm long bearing whorls of small flowers. The calyx is tubular and pubescent; the corolla is violet-blue to purple, approximately 8 mm long. Flowering occurs from July to September, later than English lavender.

The essential oil glands are concentrated in the calyx and flower buds. This distribution explains why harvest timing critically affects both oil yield and composition.

The root system is fibrous and relatively shallow, extending 30–50 cm laterally. This shallow rooting makes lavandin susceptible to drought stress during prolonged dry periods without irrigation.

## Distribution and Habitat

Lavandin is not known from the wild as a naturally established taxon, though spontaneous hybrids occur where L. angustifolia and L. latifolia ranges overlap in southern France, northern Italy, and eastern Spain.

Commercial cultivation is centered in the Provence region of France, which produces the majority of the world's lavandin oil. Significant plantings also exist in Bulgaria, Spain, Italy, Australia, and the United States.

The hybrid is more heat-tolerant and disease-resistant than English lavender, thriving in Mediterranean and temperate climates with hot, dry summers. It is less cold-hardy than L. angustifolia and may suffer winter damage below minus 15 degrees Celsius.

## Cultivation

Lavandin is propagated vegetatively by semi-hardwood cuttings taken in late summer or autumn. Cuttings root within 4–6 weeks under mist propagation and are overwintered in cold frames before spring planting.

Field planting is conducted in spring at spacings of 80–120 cm between plants and 150–200 cm between rows. Denser spacing increases yield per hectare but may reduce individual plant vigor.

The crop requires well-drained, slightly alkaline soils with a pH of 6.5–8.0. Heavy clay soils and waterlogged conditions promote root rot caused by Phytophthora species.

Mature plants begin commercial oil production in the second or third year after planting. Economic lifespan in well-managed fields is 10–15 years, after which yields decline and replanting becomes necessary.

Harvest occurs during full flower, typically July to August, using specialized combines that cut and collect the flowering stems. Fresh or slightly wilted material is distilled immediately for optimal oil recovery.

Yields range from 60 to 150 kg of essential oil per hectare, compared to 15–30 kg per hectare for English lavender. This yield advantage is the primary reason for lavandin's commercial dominance in the lavender oil industry.

Distillation is performed by steam distillation for 60–90 minutes. Oil quality is assessed by gas chromatography to verify compliance with ISO 3516 standards for lavandin oil.

## Essential Oil Composition

The essential oil of Lavandula × intermedia is distinguished from English lavender oil by higher camphor content and lower linalyl acetate proportion. Typical ranges for the major constituents include linalool (25–38 percent), linalyl acetate (20–35 percent), camphor (6–12 percent), and 1,8-cineole (3–8 percent).

Linalool and linalyl acetate together typically account for 50–70 percent of total oil composition. These compounds contribute the characteristic floral-herbaceous fragrance and possess documented antimicrobial and anxiolytic properties.

Camphor content is the primary quality differentiator between cultivars. Low-camphor cultivars such as Super and Provence (3–5 percent) command premium prices for aromatherapy use, while high-camphor types like Grosso (8–12 percent) are preferred for industrial applications.

Minor constituents include borneol, terpinen-4-ol, lavandulyl acetate, and coumarin. Trace amounts of sesquiterpenes contribute to the oil's fixative properties in perfumery compositions.

The cultivar Grosso dominates global production, accounting for approximately 80 percent of French lavandin oil output. Its consistent yield and broad-spectrum fragrance profile make it the industry standard.

## Uses and Applications

Lavandin essential oil is the most widely used lavender-type oil in commercial applications due to its high yield and relatively low production cost. It serves as a versatile base note in perfumery and functional fragrances.

In aromatherapy, lavandin oil provides calming and relaxing effects, though its higher camphor content makes it slightly more stimulating than English lavender oil. It is commonly diffused for stress reduction and sleep support.

Topical applications include use in massage oils, bath products, and skincare formulations. Anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects have been demonstrated in clinical studies comparing lavandin to ibuprofen for headache relief.

The dried flower spikes are used in sachets, potpourri, and culinary applications. Lavandin flowers impart a stronger flavor than English lavender and are used sparingly in baking and confectionery.

Industrial applications include use as a natural insect repellent, fabric freshener, and cleaning product fragrance. The oil's antimicrobial activity extends the shelf life of natural cosmetic formulations.

Post-harvest, the spent floral biomass (hydrolat and plant residue) is used as mulch or composted. Some distilleries extract additional minor compounds from the hydrosol fraction.
