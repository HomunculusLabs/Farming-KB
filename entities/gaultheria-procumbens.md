## Overview

**Gaultheria procumbens** L., commonly known as **eastern teaberry**, **checkerberry**, or **American wintergreen**, is a low-growing evergreen subshrub in the family Ericaceae. It is native to [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]].

The essential oil derived from this species served as the original commercial source of wintergreen oil before synthetic production became dominant. It remains a popular ornamental ground cover for shaded woodland gardens.

The specific epithet *procumbens* distinguishes it from the related western species G. shallon (salal), which is a much larger, upright shrub. G. procumbens is the type species of the genus Gaultheria and the most widely recognized member of the group in horticulture and commerce.

Other notable species in the genus include G. mucronata (prickly heath) of [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] Gaultheria species.

The root system consists of a shallow, fibrous network of rhizomes growing 2–5 cm below the soil surface. These rhizomes are densely branched and produce adventitious roots at nodes, enabling efficient colonization of suitable substrate. Root-associated mycorrhizal fungi, including [[dighton-ericoid-mycorrhizal-nutrient-acquisition]] associations.

Optimal growth occurs in partial to full shade with soil pH between 4.0 and 6.0. The plant is intolerant of alkaline soils and poorly drained conditions, which account for its restricted distribution to acidic substrates.

Gaultheria procumbens is remarkably cold-hardy, tolerating temperatures to −40 °C. Its evergreen foliage persists through severe winters, making it one of the few photosynthetically active understory plants during the cold season in its northern range.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The dominant bioactive compound in G. procumbens is **methyl salicylate**, which constitutes 98–99% of the essential oil. The compound is an ester of salicylic acid and methanol, responsible for the characteristic wintergreen aroma and flavor of all plant parts.

Methyl salicylate is absorbed through the skin and hydrolyzed to salicylic acid in the body, producing analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects. This pharmacology underpins the traditional use of wintergreen preparations for muscle and joint pain.

Topical application of methyl salicylate produces a sensation of warmth followed by localized analgesia, making it effective for temporary relief of minor arthritic and muscular pain. The compound acts through inhibition of cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, analogous to the mechanism of aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid), though with a slower onset and more prolonged duration of action.

Indigenous peoples applied poultices of bruised leaves to treat headaches, sore throats, and muscular aches. The oil was inhaled for respiratory congestion and applied topically as a counterirritant for rheumatic pain.

The Mohegan and other Algonquian peoples used a decoction of the leaves for treating kidney ailments and fever. The Penobscot chewed the leaves for cold symptoms, while the Iroquois used leaf infusions as an analgesic tea. These ethnobotanical uses directly informed early colonial-era herbal medicine in eastern North America.
