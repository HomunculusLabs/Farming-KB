# John Galvin

## Description

[[john-galvin]] served as the city forester of Boston, Massachusetts, during the 1870s. He is remembered through [[julius-auboineau-palmer]]'s writings for granting permission to collect mushroom specimens from city grounds during the period when Palmer was developing his mycological expertise.

Palmer's acknowledgment of Galvin provides a rare glimpse into the practical logistics of mycological fieldwork in 19th-century urban America, and the relationship between city officials and amateur naturalists.

## Classification

- **Nationality:** American
- **Location:** Boston, Massachusetts
- **Era:** 1870s
- **Field:** Urban forestry, municipal administration
- **Role:** City forester, public servant, facilitator of mycological research

## Key Facts

- Served as city forester of Boston during the 1870s
- Granted Palmer permission to collect mushroom specimens from city grounds when Palmer was unable to leave the city during the early period of his mycological study
- Described by Palmer as "popular and efficient" in his official capacity
- Praised specifically for maintaining gentlemanly conduct despite holding official power — Palmer noted that some officeholders "seem anxious to show their power by manners disagreeable to their fellow-citizens"
- Was a total stranger to Palmer with no personal or political relationship between them
- Palmer made his acknowledgment "more cheerfully" because of the absence of any personal connection, emphasizing the purely professional courtesy involved

## Palmer's Acknowledgment

Palmer's description of Galvin is notable for its warmth and specificity. He wrote:

> "Some men, holding office, seem anxious to show their power by manners disagreeable to their fellow-citizens; others do not forget that no official is less respected because he continues to be a perfect gentleman."

This praise was offered "more cheerfully" because Galvin was "a total stranger" to Palmer and they had "no personal or political relationship." The acknowledgment thus represents genuine appreciation for professional courtesy rather than obligation to a friend or political ally.

## Context of the Permission

During the early period of Palmer's mycological study, he was "impossible to be absent from the city." This constraint made city grounds — parks, public gardens, and other municipal lands managed by the forester's office — essential sources of fungal specimens.

Galvin's willingness to grant collection permission reflects an enlightened attitude toward public resources and scientific inquiry. At a time when [[mycology]] was a "[[neglected-science-of-mycology]]," Galvin's support for Palmer's research demonstrated unusual open-mindedness.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Galvin's role illustrates the importance of institutional access for mycological research. Modern foragers and mycologists continue to navigate questions of permission and land access when collecting specimens. Galvin's model — a public official facilitating [[roger-heim-scientific-study-psilocybin]] on public land — represents an ideal that modern park systems and municipal governments increasingly support through research permits and [[citizen-science-fungal-biodiversity-discovery]] programs.

For cultivators, the episode also demonstrates that valuable [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity]] can be found in [[query-how-do-green-roofs-and-living-walls-benefit-urban-environments]], including city parks and managed landscapes.
