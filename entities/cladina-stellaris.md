## Description

**Cladina stellaris** (also classified as *[[cladonia-stellaris]]*) is a fruticose (shrubby) lichen species in the family Cladoniaceae. It is a dominant component of northern boreal and arctic tundra lichen communities and serves as a critical winter forage species for reindeer and caribou. Following the Chernobyl nuclear disaster, C. stellaris was identified as a significant **accumulator of radiocesium (137Cs)**, contributing to the transfer of radionuclides into grazing animal populations and subsequently to human consumers of reindeer products.

## Key Facts

- **Radiocesium accumulation:** Following Chernobyl, C. stellaris collected in Norway in 1986 contained 16,572 Bq/kg of 137Cs (Gaare, 1990), reflecting its strong bioaccumulation capacity for radionuclides.
- **Critical reindeer forage:** Forms a major component of reindeer winter diet; reindeer densities of approximately four per hectare are common in parts of Norway, with lichens accounting for about 40% of their annual food intake and forming the greater part of winter feed.
- **Long biological half-life for radionuclides:** As a terricolous (ground-dwelling) lichen, it shows relatively long biological half-lives for radionuclides, extending the period of transfer to grazing animals. The related terricolous lichen [[cetraria-islandica]] had a biological half-life for 137Cs of approximately 2.5 years.
- **Boreal ecosystem biomonitoring:** Used as a biomonitor for radioactive fallout and long-range atmospheric deposition of pollutants in northern ecosystems.

## Ecological Role

C. stellaris is a keystone species in boreal and arctic ecosystems. It forms extensive mats on well-drained soils and rock surfaces in open habitats, often dominating the ground vegetation cover in northern boreal forests and tundra heaths. As a slow-growing lichen (typically 2–5 mm per year), it is highly sensitive to air pollution and environmental disturbance.

Its role as primary winter forage for Rangifer (reindeer and caribou) populations makes it ecologically critical for both wild and domesticated herd management. The lichen–reindeer–human food chain is one of the most significant pathways for radionuclide transfer from atmospheric fallout to human populations in arctic regions.

## Chernobyl Impact

The Chernobyl disaster highlighted the vulnerability of lichen-based food chains:

1. Lichens efficiently trapped atmospheric 137Cs deposition
2. The slow growth and long biological half-life meant contamination persisted for years
3. Reindeer accumulated 137Cs through lichen consumption, with mean activities of 6,700–24,000 Bq/kg across Norwegian sampling sites
4. This led to restrictions on reindeer meat consumption and significant cultural disruption for Sami herding communities

## Relevance to Mycology and Cultivation

- **Radionuclide transfer pathway:** C. stellaris represents a critical link in the lichen–reindeer–human food chain for radionuclide transfer, particularly following nuclear accidents.
- **Bioindicator for atmospheric deposition:** Its ability to accumulate radionuclides and heavy metals makes it valuable for monitoring environmental contamination in remote regions.
- **Sami pastoralism:** The contamination of C. stellaris after Chernobyl directly impacted indigenous Sami reindeer herding practices and cultural traditions in Scandinavia.

## Distinguishing Features

- Dense, shrubby, cushion-like growth form forming extensive yellow-gray mats
- Pale grayish-white to yellowish branching podetia
- Absence of apothecia (reproduces primarily vegetatively via thallus fragmentation)
- Chemical composition: contains usnic acid and other lichen substances
- Slow growth rate, forming large persistent colonies over decades

## Habitat and Distribution

C. stellaris is distributed across circumboreal and arctic regions, forming dominant lichen heaths on well-drained sandy and rocky soils. It is particularly abundant in northern Scandinavia, Siberia, Canada, and Alaska. The species thrives in open, exposed habitats with high light availability and low competition from vascular plants, often forming nearly pure stands covering extensive areas of tundra terrain.

## Growth and Longevity

As one of the slower-growing macrolichens, C. stellaris has growth rates of only 2–5 mm per year in length. Individual colonies can persist for decades to centuries, making them exceptionally long-lived organisms. This longevity, combined with continuous atmospheric deposition, leads to the progressive accumulation of airborne contaminants including heavy metals, radionuclides, and persistent organic pollutants.

## Conservation Concerns

C. stellaris is sensitive to air pollution, particularly sulfur dioxide, and has experienced declines in areas affected by industrial emissions. Overgrazing by excessive reindeer populations can also deplete lichen mats, reducing their capacity for regeneration. Climate change poses an additional threat as warming temperatures alter the competitive balance between lichens and vascular plants in tundra ecosystems.
