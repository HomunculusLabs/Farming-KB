# Hebeloma spoliatum

## Description

**Hebeloma spoliatum** is an ectomycorrhizal basidiomycete fungus that appears in successional sequences following nutrient enrichment of forest soils, particularly from decomposing animal carcasses. It is documented as a colonizer of nutrient-enriched sites in Japanese pine forests and is notable for its role in the fungal succession following organic matter deposition.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Basidiomycota
- **Class**: Agaricomycetes
- **Order**: Agaricales
- **Family**: Hymenogastraceae
- **Genus**: Hebeloma
- **Species**: H. spoliatum

## Key Facts

- Appears in early-to-mid successional stages on decomposing animal carcasses (e.g., dead cat) in pine forests
- Fruiting occurs approximately 100–200 days after nutrient deposition
- Can be outcompeted at specific microsites by [[hebeloma-radicosum]], which actively defends its territory
- Part of the ectomycorrhizal fungal succession on nutrient-enriched patches documented by Sagara (1995)
- Likely has affinity for ammonium-rich and protein-rich substrates typical of decomposing animal matter
- Co-occurs with [[saprotrophic-fungi]] such as Ascobolus denudatus and Tephrocybe tesquorum in early succession

## Ecological Role

H. spoliatum plays a role in:

- Exploiting nutrient-rich patches created by animal remains in forest ecosystems
- Participating in fungal succession from saprotrophic to mycorrhizal dominance on enriched substrates
- Facilitating nutrient transfer from decomposed animal matter to host tree roots
- Demonstrating the link between soil fauna activity and mycorrhizal community composition
- Competing with other Hebeloma species for access to localized nutrient resources

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Demonstrates the ecological importance of nutrient patch dynamics in forest soils
- Relevant to understanding how mycorrhizal communities respond to organic matter inputs
- Illustrates temporal succession patterns in ectomycorrhizal fruiting
- Important for forest management practices that affect soil organic matter distribution
- Contributes to understanding of belowground fungal competitive interactions
