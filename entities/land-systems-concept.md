# Land Systems Concept

## Description

The Land Systems concept is a method of land capability assessment that
identifies whole landscapes as integrated systems characterised by recurring
patterns of topography, soils, and vegetation. Developed by C.S. Christian and
G.A. Stewart in 1953 for CSIRO (Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research
Organisation), it provides a hierarchical geographical framework for
understanding landscape patterns from the bioregion down to individual site
level. The method remains one of the most powerful tools available to
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-designers-manual]] for reading and responding to landscape patterns.

## Classification

- **Category**: Landscape assessment methodology
- **Origin**: Australia (CSIRO, 1953)
- **Founders**: C.S. Christian and G.A. Stewart
- **Scale**: Bioregional to site-specific (nested hierarchical systems)
- **Related Disciplines**: Landscape ecology, pedology, geomorphology

## Key Facts

- A land system is defined as "an area or group of areas throughout which there
  is a recurring pattern of topography soils and vegetation" (Christian and
  Stewart, 1953)
- Land systems often cut across adjacent catchments in bands from headwaters to
  the sea, reflecting underlying climate, geology, and topography patterns
- Within land systems, characteristic patterns of repeating land "components"
  have more uniform microclimate, slope, aspect, soil type, and natural
  vegetation
- The method was applied to Kangaroo Island in South Australia in the 1950s,
  where soil scientist Northcote warned the government that salinity would
  follow clearing of specific land systems — advice that was ignored and proved
  devastatingly correct
- Land systems can be projected upward to encompass the bioregion and downward
  to include individual sites as nested geographic systems
- Published government studies are primarily at scales useful to planners and
  broadacre farmers but also applicable to consultants, real estate agents, and
  those searching for land to purchase
- The Australian standard reference for field methodology is R.C. McDonald et
  al, *Australian Soil and Land Survey: Field Handbook* (Inkata Press, 1984)

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

1. **Pattern Recognition**: The approach embodies the [[permaculture-principle-1-observe-interact]] of
   "[[design-from-patterns-to-details]]" by providing a top-down framework for
   landscape understanding that reveals underlying order in apparently complex
   terrain

2. **Predictive Land Management**: The Kangaroo Island case demonstrates how
   land systems analysis can predict long-term degradation (salinity) before it
   becomes visible, enabling preventive design rather than reactive remediation

3. **Overcoming Cultural Bias**: The method helps overcome biases about good and
   poor land — sloping land classified as problematic may actually offer
   permaculture advantages, while poorly drained wetlands may be among the most
   productive land uses for aquaculture

4. **Bioregional Design Foundation**: Land systems mapping provides the spatial
   foundation for whole farm planning, catchment master patterns, and
   bioregional-scale [[permaculture-design]] and implementation

5. **Holistic Assessment**: Unlike simple slope-and-soil methods, land systems
   incorporate ecosystem relationships and landscape evolution, making them more
   aligned with permaculture's integrated systems thinking

6. **Property Selection**: Land systems maps enable prospective land buyers to
   understand the diversity and capability of land within any bioregion, leading
   to more informed property selection decisions

## Limitations

- Little understood or used outside professional and scientific circles
- Patterns within one bioregion do not necessarily translate to another
- Standardisation attempts can ignore informal [[desert-landscape-reading-and-erosion-landforms]] skills
- Cultural loading of terms like "improvement" and "degradation" affects how
  results are interpreted and applied
- Training has traditionally been through apprenticeship within government
  agencies, limiting broader access to the methodology
- Geological-scale change from industrial culture means patterns may be novel
  and evolving, requiring adaptive rather than rigid application
