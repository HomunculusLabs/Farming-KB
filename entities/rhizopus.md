## Rhizopus (Bread Mold)

## Description

Rhizopus is a genus of common zygomycete fungi widely known as bread molds. In [[mushroom-cultivation]], Rhizopus species are frequent contaminants of [[grain-spawn]] and agar plates, among the most common mold contaminants encountered in the laboratory. They are easily recognized by their distinctive appearance: white or clear fuzzy growth with small black pinheads on the upper surface where spores discharge. Rhizopus grows extremely rapidly and can quickly overtake mushroom [[mycelium]] on nutritive media.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Zygomycota (Mucoromycota)
- **Class**: Mucoromycetes
- **Order**: Mucorales
- **Family**: Rhizopodaceae
- **Genus**: Rhizopus

## Key Facts

- Common bread mold, frequent contaminant in mushroom cultivation laboratories
- Appearance: white or clear fur with little black pinheads (sporangia) on the upper surface
- Extremely fast-growing, colonizing entire plates or jars within 24-48 hours
- Thrives in high-moisture environments; over-hydrated grain spawn is particularly susceptible
- Spores are ubiquitous in indoor and outdoor air
- Unlike mushroom mycelium (linear, cottony), Rhizopus grows as coarse, airy mycelium with visible sporangia

## Relevance to Cultivation

Rhizopus contamination is a persistent challenge in [[spawn-production]]. Most problematic in grain spawn that is too wet, where bacteria have weakened the substrate, or when sterile technique is compromised. Prevention requires proper grain hydration (moist but not wet externally), adequate sterilization times and pressures, and disciplined sterile technique. Contaminated containers must be removed from the lab immediately and sanitized with diluted bleach.

## Relevance to Mycology

Rhizopus species are important model organisms in [[fungal-genetics]] and metabolism, used industrially for organic acid production (fumaric acid, lactic acid), enzymes (amylases, lipases), and fermented foods (tempeh from R. oligosporus). Their rapid growth and simple morphology make them useful for studying fungal development and sporulation.

## Identification in Culture

- White or clear fuzzy aerial mycelium
- Black sporangia (pinhead-like structures) on upright sporangiophores
- Extremely fast radial growth
- Coarser texture than mushroom mycelium
- Does not form clamp connections
