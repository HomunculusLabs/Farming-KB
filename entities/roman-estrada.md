## Roman Estrada

## Description

Roman Estrada was a Mazatec shaman (curandero) from the town of Huautla de Jiménez in the Sierra Mazateca of Oaxaca, Mexico. He was one of two primary informants — alongside Irene Pineda de Figueroa — whose mushroom-induced shamanic discourses were recorded and analyzed by Henry Munn in his essay "The Mushrooms of Language." In his late fifties at the time of the study, Estrada had been eating the sacred mushrooms for approximately nine years, having begun his shamanic career after a dramatic cure from a traumatic illness. His recorded discourse, characterized by themes of fear and terror, stands in contrast to the female shamaness's themes of freshness and tenderness, representing what Munn called the "emotional poles" of the mushroom experience.

## Classification

- **Type**: Person — traditional healer / shaman
- **Ethnicity**: Mazatec Indian
- **Location**: Huautla de Jiménez, Sierra Mazateca, Oaxaca, Mexico
- **Vocation**: Medicine man, shaman, oracle, speaker of sacred words
- **Practice**: Mazatec mushroom ceremony (velada)
- **Years of practice**: Approximately 9 years at time of recording
- **Previous occupation**: Storekeeper (before shamanic vocation)

## Key Facts

- Began his shamanic career after being cured of a traumatic illness through mushrooms given to him by a 105-year-old medicine man from the village of San Lucas
- His illness began as appendicitis but developed into a traumatic neurosis (susto) after being operated on under ether anesthesia at the Latin American Hospital
- Conventional medical treatment failed — he visited hospitals in Córdoba, Mexico City, and Tehuacán without relief from his psychological symptoms
- The San Lucas shaman gave him mushrooms three times, after which his trauma was exorcised through a visionary reliving of the surgery
- Initially resisted the shamanic vocation, wanting only to be cured, but was told by the elder shaman: "it is no longer you who command"
- His vocation was confirmed when he heard the voice of his father, dead for 43 years, telling him to accept the work
- Learned the technique of "sucking through space" with a hollow tube of cane — drawing sickness out of patients from a distance without physical contact
- His shamanic discourse is characterized by themes of fear and terror, contrasting with the medicine woman's themes of freshness and tenderness
- Described the Mazatec concept of "fright" (susto) — a traumatic neurosis where the spirit is frightened from the body and must be retrieved
- The complete text of his discourse spans ninety-two pages in the original recording
- His niece, Eloina Estrada de Gonzalez, served as translator for the recorded materials
- His cure involved vividly reliving the operation in his imagination — the mushrooms "cut him open, arranged his insides, and sewed him up again"
- His case demonstrates the Mazatec belief that conventional medicine cannot truly cure because it is "divorced from all cooperation with the spirits"
- The old shaman from San Lucas showed him a table with ground tobacco and a cross — signs of the shaman's work — and offered him the vocation
- He described hearing an inner voice that he could not recognize as his own, which the elder shaman interpreted as his dead father's voice

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Roman Estrada represents the living tradition of Mazatec mushroom shamanism — the indigenous context in which *Psilocybe mexicana* and related species have been used as medicines for generations. His case illustrates the traditional understanding that the mushrooms serve as both diagnostic and therapeutic agents: they show the shaman the nature of the illness and provide the visionary experience through which healing occurs. His practice demonstrates that mushroom use in traditional Mazatec culture is inseparable from linguistic performance — the shaman's inspired speech during the ceremony is itself considered medicine.

## Ethnographic Significance

Estrada's account of his own cure is one of the most detailed first-person descriptions of a shamanic calling in the Mazatec ethnographic literature. His narrative demonstrates the traditional Mazatec understanding of illness as a spiritual event — the "fright" that causes the soul to leave the body — and the role of mushrooms in retrieving the lost spirit. His story also illustrates the process of vocation transmission from an elder shaman to a successor, a critical mechanism for the perpetuation of traditional mycological knowledge in an oral culture without written records.

## Psychological Analysis

From a psychological perspective, Roman Estrada's case illustrates several phenomena of interest to clinical researchers. His initial resistance to the shamanic vocation, followed by acquiescence upon hearing his dead father's voice, can be understood as the resolution of an intrapsychic conflict through the activation of the superego under psychedelic conditions. The elder shaman's technique of negating the patient's will in order to arouse it anticipates modern paradoxical intervention strategies in psychotherapy. The visionary reenactment of the surgery that cured his traumatic neurosis parallels modern exposure therapy techniques used in PTSD treatment.

## Physical Healing Techniques

In addition to his primary role as speaker and oracle, Roman Estrada employed physical healing techniques including massage and suction. His intoxicated condition was described as giving him "a strength to heal that he exercises by massage and suction." The suction technique involved using a hollow cane tube to draw illness from the patient's body from a distance, without direct physical contact. These physical techniques complemented the primary linguistic therapy and represent the multi-modal approach characteristic of Mazatec traditional medicine.

## Relationship to Other Mazatec Shamans

Roman Estrada's practice exists within a network of Mazatec shamans connected through vocation transmission. His own teacher was the 105-year-old shaman from San Lucas, and Estrada himself was in the process of transmitting knowledge to others at the time of Munn's study. This lineage-based transmission system ensures the continuity of mycological and therapeutic knowledge across generations, even in the absence of written records. The tension between traditional practice and modernization that Estrada navigated reflects broader challenges facing indigenous mushroom traditions.

## The Shamanic Table

The table prepared by the elder shaman from San Lucas for Roman Estrada contained symbolic objects representing the shamanic vocation: ground tobacco (San Pedro), a cross, and other items for the initiate to choose from. This table functioned as an altar at which to officiate, and the Mazatec refer to their mushroom sessions as "masses," indicating the sacerdotal dimension of the shaman's role. The table is a material representation of the shaman's authority and the sacred nature of the work, bridging the mundane and the supernatural through physical symbols.

## The Concept of Vocation in Mazatec Culture

In Mazatec tradition, the shamanic vocation is not chosen but imposed by supernatural forces through the mechanism of illness and cure. Roman Estrada initially refused the vocation, wanting only to be cured of his physical symptoms. The elder shaman recognized that the cure and the vocation were inseparable, telling him that his resistance was futile because the decision had already been made by forces beyond his control. This concept of involuntary vocation ensures that only those who have experienced the transformative power of the mushrooms firsthand become practitioners, maintaining the experiential basis of the tradition.
