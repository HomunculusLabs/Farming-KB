## Overview

**Tectona grandis**, commonly known as teak, is a large deciduous tree in the family Lamiaceae. It is one of the most valuable tropical hardwood species in the world, prized for its exceptional timber durability and resistance to decay.

Teak has been cultivated for over 2,000 years and remains the dominant plantation hardwood across tropical Asia, Africa, and [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-caribbean-central-america]]. Its natural range spans the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Tectona grandis was described by Carl Linnaeus the Younger in 1782. The genus Tectona contains three species, with T. grandis being the most commercially significant.

The family placement has shifted over time. Teak was long classified in Verbenaceae before molecular phylogenetics confirmed its position in Lamiaceae.

Teak is a tall tree reaching 30–45 meters in height with a straight, cylindrical trunk. The bole is typically clear of branches for 15–20 meters.

Leaves are large (15–45 cm), ovate to elliptic, and rough-textured with prominent venation. They are deciduous, shedding during the dry season in monsoon climates.

The flowers are small, white to purplish, arranged in large terminal panicles. They are fragrant and attract numerous pollinator species.

Fruit is a drupe approximately 1.5–2.0 cm in diameter, enclosed in a persistent calyx. Each fruit contains one to two seeds.

Seeds require scarification or soaking in water for 24–48 hours to improve germination rates. They remain viable for approximately one year under dry storage conditions.

## Distribution and Habitat

Teak occurs naturally in mixed deciduous forests across India, Myanmar, Thailand, and Laos. Natural populations have been heavily depleted by centuries of logging.

The species thrives in tropical monsoon climates with annual rainfall of 1,250–2,500 mm and a pronounced dry season of 3–5 months. It grows best on deep, well-drained alluvial soils.

Optimal elevation ranges from sea level to 800 meters. Frost and waterlogging are both poorly tolerated.

Soil pH requirements range from slightly acidic to neutral (5.5–7.5). Teak performs poorly on lateritic and highly leached soils with low calcium availability.

Teak has been widely introduced beyond its native range. Major plantations now exist in Indonesia, Costa Rica, Nigeria, Tanzania, and Panama.

Indonesia hosts the largest area of teak plantations globally, primarily on the island of Java. These plantations date to the Dutch colonial era beginning in the mid-19th century.

## Timber Properties and Uses

Teak heartwood is golden-brown to dark brown, highly durable, and naturally resistant to termites, marine borers, and fungal decay. This exceptional durability persists without chemical treatment.

The wood contains natural oils and silica, which contribute to its weather resistance and make it a preferred material for shipbuilding and outdoor furniture. Silica content (0.5–1.5%) can dull cutting tools.

Teak timber is moderately heavy (600–700 kg/m³ at 12% moisture content) with a straight grain and coarse, uneven texture. It seasons well with minimal warping or splitting.

Major uses include ship decking, luxury outdoor furniture, flooring, veneer, and carving. Teak is the standard reference hardwood for marine applications worldwide.

Teak oil extracted from heartwood waste has traditional applications as a wood finish and leather treatment. Sawdust has been investigated for activated charcoal production.

Teak wood is dimensionally stable, showing minimal shrinkage upon drying. This stability makes it particularly suited for precision joinery and marine cabinetry.

The wood works reasonably well despite its silica content, taking a smooth finish with proper tooling. Pre-drilling is recommended for nailing and screwing.

Heartwood color darkens with exposure to sunlight, developing an attractive patina. This aging process is considered desirable in high-end furniture markets.

## Cultivation and Silviculture

Teak is primarily propagated from seed, though tissue culture and [[hamilton-vegetative-propagation]] from superior genotypes are increasingly used in clonal plantations. Seed viability declines rapidly; fresh seeds germinate at 60–80%.

Nursery seedlings are raised in polybags for 6–8 months before outplanting at the onset of the rainy season. Initial spacing of 2 × 2 to 4 × 4 meters is common.

Rotation lengths range from 20 to 80 years depending on site quality and management objectives. Short-rotation plantations (20–30 years) [[neurotropic-fungi-in-central-america]] produce smaller logs for pulp and general construction.

Thinning is critical for producing high-quality timber. Early thinning at 5–8 years removes suppressed trees, with subsequent thinnings every 5–10 years.

[[cervantes-fertilizer-application-strain-feeding]] significantly improves growth rates on nutrient-poor sites. Recommended doses include 50–100 g N, 50 g P₂O₅, and 100 g K₂O per tree per year.

Growth rates of 10–25 m³/ha/year are achievable on good sites with intensive management. Plantation yields of 150–300 m³/ha over a 30-year rotation are typical.

Weed competition is a major constraint during the first two years after planting. Manual or chemical weed control significantly improves early survival and growth rates.

Pruning is practiced in high-value plantations to produce clear, knot-free boles. [[fukuoka-textdoc-orchard-management-weed-succession-formative-pruning]] at 2–3 years corrects early branching defects.

Mixed-species plantations with nitrogen-fixing trees have shown promise for improving soil fertility. However, teak is highly light-demanding and shade from companions can reduce growth.
