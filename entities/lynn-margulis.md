# Lynn Margulis

Lynn Margulis (1938–2011) was an American evolutionary biologist whose work on
symbiosis fundamentally transformed our understanding of cell evolution and the
history of life on Earth. She is best known for proposing the **endosymbiotic
theory**, which explains how eukaryotic cells originated through symbiotic
mergers between different prokaryotic organisms. Her career spanned five
decades and encompassed contributions to cell biology, microbiology, geosciences,
and evolutionary theory.

## Early Life and Education

Margulis was born Lynn Petra Alexander on March 5, 1938, in Chicago, Illinois.
She entered the University of Chicago at age 16 through its early admission
program and earned her A.B. in liberal arts in 1957 at age 19. She completed
a master’s degree in genetics and zoology at the University of Wisconsin–Madison
in 1960, studying chloroplast inheritance in Euglena under Hans Ris. She earned
her Ph.D. in genetics from the University of California, Berkeley in 1965, with
a dissertation on cytoplasmic inheritance in Chlamydomonas and Euglena that laid
the groundwork for her endosymbiotic theory.

## Endosymbiotic Theory

Margulis’s most celebrated contribution is the **serial endosymbiosis theory**
(SET), which proposes that eukaryotic cells arose through a series of symbiotic
mergers between prokaryotic organisms. Specifically, she argued that
**mitochondria** evolved from endosymbiotic alpha-proteobacteria, that
**chloroplasts** evolved from endosymbiotic cyanobacteria, and that eukaryotic
flagella (undulipodia) evolved from spirochete bacteria.

Her landmark 1967 paper, “On the Origin of Mitosing Cells,” was rejected by
approximately fifteen journals before being accepted by the *Journal of
Theoretical Biology*. The scientific establishment was deeply skeptical of the
idea that organelles were once free-living bacteria, with critics calling the
theory “untestable” and “speculative.” The turning point came in the late 1970s
and 1980s when molecular techniques — particularly rRNA sequencing by Carl Woese
and colleagues — provided strong evidence confirming the bacterial origin of
mitochondria and chloroplasts. By the 1990s, the endosymbiotic origin of these
organelles was considered established fact, representing one of the most
dramatic paradigm shifts in modern biology.

## Gaia Hypothesis

In the early 1970s, Margulis met James Lovelock and became a key scientific
collaborator in developing the **Gaia hypothesis** — the idea that Earth’s
biosphere, atmosphere, oceans, and soil function as a self-regulating system.
She provided critical microbiological support for the hypothesis, demonstrating
how microorganisms (especially cyanobacteria) regulate atmospheric composition,
carbon cycling, nitrogen cycling, and sulfur cycling. Her 1998 book *Symbiotic
Planet* articulated the deep connection between endosymbiosis and Gaia thinking,
framing Gaia as a scientifically testable hypothesis grounded in microbial
ecology rather than mysticism.

## Five Kingdoms Classification

With Karlene V. Schwartz, Margulis developed and popularized the **Five
Kingdoms** system of biological classification: Monera (prokaryotes), Protista
(protozoa and algae), Fungi, Plantae (plants), and Animalia (animals). Published
as *Five Kingdoms: An Illustrated Guide to the Phyla of Life on Earth* (1982)
with multiple subsequent editions, this system was a major improvement over the
traditional two-kingdom framework and became enormously influential in biology
education. While largely superseded by Carl Woese’s three-domain system, it was
the standard pedagogical framework for decades. Notably, Margulis gave **Fungi
their own kingdom**, recognizing their unique evolutionary significance — a
significant advance for [[mycology]].

## Symbiogenesis

Margulis argued that **symbiogenesis** — the creation of new species, organs,
and tissues through long-term integration of symbionts — is a major mechanism of
evolution that neo-Darwinism has systematically undervalued. In her controversial
2002 book *Acquiring Genomes* (with Dorion Sagan), she argued that new species
arise primarily through symbiotic merger of genomes, with natural selection
acting as a secondary editor. She maintained that mutation and selection alone
cannot explain the origin of major evolutionary innovations like new body plans
and cell types. This position placed her at odds with mainstream evolutionary
biologists including Richard Dawkins and John Maynard Smith, though it has
inspired the broader “extended evolutionary synthesis” movement.

## Connection to Mycology and Symbiosis Research

While not primarily a mycologist, Margulis’s work on symbiosis is deeply relevant
to mycology. She frequently cited **[[lichens]]** (fungus + algae/cyanobacteria
partnerships) as classic examples of stable symbiotic associations producing
organisms with properties different from either partner. She promoted the
importance of **[[mycorrhiza]]l** fungal associations with plant roots as
fundamental examples of how symbiosis shapes ecosystems. Her framework
encompassed fungal-bacterial interactions, [[endophytic-fungi]], and the broader
principle that all complex life is built from symbiotic mergers.

## Academic Career

Margulis held positions at Brandeis University (postdoctoral, 1965–1966),
Boston University (1966–1993, rising to Distinguished University Professor), and
the University of Massachusetts Amherst (1993–2011, as Distinguished Professor
of Geosciences). She directed the Lynn Margulis Laboratory at UMass and was
affiliated with the Graduate Program in Organismic and Evolutionary Biology.

## Key Publications and Honors

Major works include *Origin of Eukaryotic Cells* (1970), *Symbiosis in Cell
Evolution* (1981), *Five Kingdoms* (1982, with K.V. Schwartz), *What Is Life?*
(1995, with Dorion Sagan), *Symbiotic Planet* (1998), *Acquiring Genomes* (2002,
with Dorion Sagan), and *Kingdoms and Domains* (2009, with M.J. Chapman).

She received the National Medal of Science (1999), was elected to the National
Academy of Sciences (1983), won the William Procter Prize (Sigma Xi, 1999) and
the NASA Public Service Award, and was a fellow of the American Academy of Arts
and Sciences, the Russian Academy of Natural Sciences, and the Linnean Society.

## Personal Life

Margulis married astronomer Carl Sagan in 1957 (divorced 1963); their son
Dorion Sagan became a science writer and frequent collaborator. She had four
children total across three marriages, the last to Spanish microbiologist
Ricardo Guerrero (2000). Known for her passionate intellectual style, she was
a dedicated mentor throughout her career.

## Legacy

biology, representing one of the most significant paradigm shifts in 20th-century
biology. Modern genomics has confirmed her ideas through evidence of horizontal
