# Bacillus subtilis

## Taxonomy

Bacillus subtilis is a rod-shaped, Gram-positive, endospore-forming bacterium
belonging to the class Schizomycetes, order Eubacteriales, and family Bacillaceae.
The name Bacillus derives from the Latin "bacilliformis," meaning rod-like, in
reference to the characteristic cylindrical shape of these bacteria. The genus
Bacillus has been narrowed considerably over time and is now defined as comprising
Gram-positive, rod-like, aerobic bacteria that form spores. The variety mucoides,
formally Bacillus subtilis var. mucoides, is the specific form identified in The
Mushroom Cultivator as the bacterium responsible for spoiling grain spawn media.
While many bacteria are rod-shaped and have been loosely called "bacilli," the genus
concept is now more strictly defined based on Gram staining reaction, aerobic
metabolism, and endospore formation capability.

## Morphology

Bacillus subtilis cells are rod-like or cylindrical in shape, measuring 0.2-1.2
microns in diameter and 1-5 microns in length. When viewed through a microscope in
wet mount preparations, Bacilli exhibit excited wriggling movement back and forth.
Species in this genus move by the vibrating action of flagella ("hairs") that outline
each cell, though these flagella are difficult to observe microscopically without
using specific staining techniques. Bacilli are encapsulated by a thin but firm slime
layer, and conglomerations of cells give infected grain a slimy appearance. Under
adverse environmental conditions, particularly heat, a single hardened endospore
forms within each parent cell body. These endospores show extraordinary resistance to
heat, are low in water content, and are unaffected by drying. The macroscopic
appearance of contamination is a dull gray to mucus-like brownish slime characterized
by a strong, foul odor described as smelling like rotting apples, dirty socks, or
burnt bacon, giving rise to the common name "Wet Spot."

## Habitat and Ecology

Bacillus subtilis lives within a broad range of habitats and grows on almost anything
organic that is moist and surrounded by oxygen. The bacterium is particularly common
in soils, where it plays a role in organic matter decomposition. In the context of
mushroom cultivation, Bacillus is primarily encountered as a contaminant of grain
spawn media, where it finds ideal conditions for growth in the moist, nutrient-rich
grain environment. The organism is ubiquitous in nature and is found in soils,
composts, on plant surfaces, in water, and in the air. Its ability to form
endospores allows it to persist in environments that would otherwise be inhospitable,
including dry grain stored for extended periods. Endospores have been recovered from
the stomachs of mummies and proved viable after hundreds of years of dormancy.

## Role in Mushroom Cultivation

In The Mushroom Cultivator, Bacillus subtilis is identified as the primary bacterial
contaminant in mushroom cultivation and is described as the most difficult contaminant
to control. It is listed as "Wet Spot" or "Sour Rot" in the Contaminants of Mushroom
Culture chapter. Bacillus contamination is described as "pernicious and tenacious,"
posing a formidable threat to spawn makers due to its phenomenal reproductive
capability and the extreme resilience of its endospores. At room temperature, a
single Bacillus cell reproduces every 20 minutes, multiplying into nearly a million
daughter bacteria in only seven hours. In another seven hours, each of those million
bacteria can divide into a million more cells, meaning that one trillion bacteria can
evolve from a single parent cell in less than fourteen hours. This explosive growth
potential makes early detection and prevention critical.

## Contamination of Grain Spawn

Bacillus subtilis var. mucoides is specifically identified in The Mushroom Cultivator
as the common bacterium responsible for spoiling spawn media. If allowed to
proliferate, this contaminant wreaks havoc in a spawn laboratory, potentially
necessitating a complete shutdown of operations. A particularly insidious aspect of
Bacillus contamination is that spores and even strains of mushroom mycelium can
become hosts for Bacillus, carrying bacteria on their hyphae and then contaminating
any media onto which the mushroom mycelium is transferred. This means that seemingly
clean mushroom cultures can harbor hidden bacterial contamination that only manifests
when the mycelium is transferred to fresh media. The bacterium makes uncolonized
grain appear excessively wet, with pallid to whitish ridges along the margins of
individual grain kernels characterizing the contamination.

## Endospore Resistance and Sterilization

The endospores of Bacillus are the primary challenge in controlling this contaminant.
Parent cells are easily destroyed, but endospores are remarkably resilient. Under dry
conditions, endospores form in increasing numbers as temperatures rise to 130 degrees
F. In boiling water at 212 degrees F, endospore viability markedly decreases, with
ninety percent of Bacillus spores killed in only one minute. At autoclave
temperatures and pressures (250 degrees F at 15 psi), survivability falls well below
1%, but this residual 1% is significant given the rapid reproductive rate. In one
study (Shull and Ernst, 1962), the thermal death time of an exposed Bacillus
stearothermophilus population of 1,300,000 endospores was 250 degrees F for 13
minutes. Endospore endurance to heat is directly related to calcium content in the
host substrate. The most practical control method involves soaking grain at room
temperature for 24 hours prior to sterilization, allowing viable endospores to
germinate into heat-sensitive vegetative cells.

## Control Measures

The Mushroom Cultivator recommends several strategies for controlling Bacillus
contamination:

- **Air filtration** through high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters.
- **Thorough sterilization** of grain, with attention to steam penetration. Water in
  pressure cookers should be brought to a boil for 5 minutes before closing the vent
  valve to guarantee adequate steam penetration.
- **Proper storage and use** of relatively clean grains, replacing contaminated grain
  sources rather than double-sterilizing.
- **Pre-soaking grain** for 24 hours at room temperature prior to sterilization to
  allow endospores to germinate into vegetative cells that are easily killed.
- **Pre-cooking grain** for approximately 2 hours in water at a low boil before
  final sterilization.
- **Antibiotics** in agar media (gentamycin sulfate, penicillin, streptomycin,
  aureomycin) hinder or prevent growth of Bacillus contaminants.
- **Higher pressure sterilization** at 27 psi (270 degrees F) for heavily infested
  grain, with manufacturer consultation for safety.

## Interactions

While Bacillus subtilis is primarily known as a contaminant in mushroom cultivation,
The Mushroom Cultivator also notes potentially beneficial interactions involving
related Bacillus species. According to Park and Agnihotri (1969), Bacillus megaterium
stimulates primordia formation in certain strains of Agaricus brunnescens (bisporus).
Another species, Bacillus thermoufibricolous, if introduced at spawning, inhibits the
growth of competitor molds in rice bran and sawdust spawn prepared for shiitake
cultivation (Steineck, 1973). Additionally, B. subtilis itself is noted as being
developed to replace E. coli as a recombinant-DNA fermentor, highlighting its
importance in biotechnology beyond mushroom cultivation. The relationship between
Bacillus species and mushroom cultivation is therefore complex, encompassing both
harmful contamination and potentially beneficial stimulatory effects on fruiting and
competitor suppression.
