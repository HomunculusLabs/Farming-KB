# Fusarium culmorum

## Overview

fusarium culmorum is an important soil-borne fungal plant pathogen belonging to the phylum [[ascomycota-phylogeny-major-classes-lineages]]. It causes a range of diseases on cereal crops and other plants, including foot rot, root rot, ear blight, and seedling blight. The species is also a significant producer of mycotoxins that can contaminate grain and pose health risks to humans and animals. In addition to its pathogenic role, fusarium culmorum plays a part in [[soil-food-web]] dynamics as a food resource for soil fauna.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Sordariomycetes
- **Order:** Hypocreales
- **Family:** Nectriaceae
- **Genus:** Fusarium
- **Species:** F. culmorum

The genus Fusarium is one of the most economically important groups of filamentous fungi, containing numerous plant pathogens, mycotoxin producers, and opportunistic human pathogens. fusarium culmorum belongs to the Fusarium sambucinum species complex and is closely related to F. graminearum, with which it shares many ecological and pathological characteristics. The teleomorph of F. culmorum has been reported but is rarely observed in nature.

## Morphology and Identification

fusarium culmorum is a filamentous fungus with distinctive morphological features:

- **Colony:** On potato dextrose [[agar]] and other standard media, colonies grow rapidly, reaching 5 to 8 cm in diameter within 7 days at 25 degrees Celsius. Colonies are typically abundant, with a cottony to woolly aerial mycelium. The colony color ranges from pale yellow to reddish-brown or carmine-red on the reverse, with the surface showing white to pale pink aerial growth.
- **Hyphae:** Hyaline, septate, with regular branching. The mycelium is typically robust and produces abundant aerial hyphae.
- **Macroconidia:** The most distinctive feature is the production of robust, thick-walled, curved macroconidia. These are typically five- to seven-septate, with a characteristic foot-shaped (pedicellate) basal cell and a pointed or blunt apical cell. Macroconidia measure 25 to 50 micrometers in length and 4 to 7 micrometers in width.
- **Microconidia:** Produced sparingly or not at all in many isolates. When present, microconidia are one- to two-celled, ellipsoidal, and borne on short monophialides.
- **Chlamydospores:** Globose to subglobose, thick-walled, intercalary or terminal, smooth or rough-walled. They serve as survival structures in soil and are important for the long-term persistence of the species.
- **Sporodochia:** Cushion-shaped structures bearing clusters of conidiophores and conidia, which may be produced on infected plant tissue.

## Ecology and Habitat

fusarium culmorum is a cosmopolitan soil-borne fungus found in agricultural and natural ecosystems worldwide. Its primary habitats include:

- Agricultural soils, particularly those cropped to cereals (wheat, barley, oats, maize)
- Grassland soils and pastures
- Forest soils
- Plant debris and crop residues
- Stored grain and seed

The species is particularly prevalent in cool and temperate regions, where it is favored by moderate temperatures (15 to 25 degrees Celsius) and high moisture conditions. It persists in soil through chlamydospores and through colonization of crop residues.

## Role in Ecosystem Processes

### Plant Pathogenicity

fusarium culmorum is a significant pathogen of cereal crops and other plants. The diseases it causes include:

- **Foot and root rot:** Infection of the crown and roots of cereal seedlings and mature plants, leading to reduced tillering, stunting, and premature death
- **Ear blight (head blight):** Infection of cereal heads, causing bleaching of spikelets and shriveling of grain
- **Seedling blight:** Pre- and post-emergence damping-off of seedlings
- **Brown foot rot:** Darkening and rotting of the stem base

The pathogen infects plants through the roots or directly through the stem base, and can spread systemically within the plant. Infection is favored by cool, wet conditions during the growing season.

### Mycotoxin Production

fusarium culmorum is a significant producer of mycotoxins, including:

- **Deoxynivalenol (DON, vomitoxin):** A trichothecene mycotoxin that causes vomiting, feed refusal, and immunosuppression in animals
- **Nivalenol (NIV):** Another trichothecene with similar toxicological effects
- **Zearalenone:** An estrogenic mycotoxin that can cause reproductive problems in animals
- **Fumonisins:** Produced by some strains, associated with various animal health issues

These mycotoxins can contaminate grain at harvest and pose serious risks to food and feed safety. Regulatory limits for mycotoxins in grain are established in many countries.

### Soil Food Web Role

A particularly interesting ecological aspect of fusarium culmorum is its role as a food resource for soil fauna. As documented in Dighton (2003), Sabatini and Innocenti (2000) studied the feeding preferences of collembola (springtails) on soil-borne plant pathogenic fungi. Their research revealed that:

- All tested collembola species (Onychiurus amatus, O. tuberculatus, and folsomia candida) preferred fusarium culmorum mycelium over mycelia of other soil-borne pathogenic fungi
- Mycelia of [[blumeria-graminis]] and [[rhizoctonia-solani]] were grazed at lower intensities but were still capable of sustaining collembolan growth
- The fungus Bipolaris sorokinianaum was lethal to all collembola species tested

This selective grazing preference has important implications for the biological control of soil-borne pathogens. Collembola that preferentially consume pathogenic fungi like F. culmorum may reduce pathogen inoculum levels in soil, providing a natural form of disease suppression. The preference for F. culmorum may relate to its nutritional quality or the absence of toxic secondary metabolites that deter feeding.

## Interactions with Other Organisms

### Plant Hosts

fusarium culmorum attacks a wide range of plant hosts, with cereals being the most economically important. The pathogen uses a combination of enzymatic degradation of plant cell walls and production of phytotoxic metabolites to facilitate infection and colonization.

### Soil Fauna

The preference of collembola for fusarium culmorum mycelium, as documented by Sabatini and Innocenti (2000), represents an important top-down regulatory mechanism for this pathogen in soil. Grazing by soil fauna can reduce pathogen biomass, disrupt mycelial networks, and potentially decrease disease incidence in subsequent crops.

### Microbial Antagonists

Various soil bacteria and fungi exhibit antagonistic activity against fusarium culmorum, including Bacillus species, Trichoderma species, and [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]. These antagonists may suppress the pathogen through competition, antibiosis, or parasitism.

## Economic and Practical Significance

- **Crop losses:** fusarium culmorum causes significant yield losses in cereal crops worldwide, particularly in cooler climates where it replaces F. graminearum as the dominant cause of ear blight.
- **Mycotoxin contamination:** The mycotoxins produced by F. culmorum contaminate grain, reducing grain quality and marketability. Deoxynivalenol contamination can render grain unsuitable for human consumption or animal feed.
- **Food safety:** Mycotoxin exposure through contaminated grain poses risks to human and animal health, including acute toxicity, chronic health effects, and immune system suppression.
- **Biological control:** The feeding preference of collembola for F. culmorum suggests opportunities for enhancing natural biological control through conservation or augmentation of soil fauna populations.

## Management

Management strategies for Fusarium diseases caused by F. culmorum include:

- Crop rotation with non-host crops
- Tillage practices to bury infected crop residues
- Use of resistant or tolerant cereal cultivars
- Application of fungicides at flowering (for ear blight control)
- Biological control using microbial antagonists
- Conservation of soil fauna that contribute to natural pathogen regulation

## Research Notes

Dighton (2003) references fusarium culmorum in the context of collembola feeding preferences on soil-borne plant pathogenic fungi. The finding that all tested collembola species preferred F. culmorum mycelium highlights the importance of soil fauna in regulating plant pathogen populations and adds to the understanding of multitrophic interactions in soil food webs. This research has implications for designing farming systems that promote natural biological control through soil faunal conservation.
