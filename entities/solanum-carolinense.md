The plant is notable for its sharp spines, yellow berries, and high toxicity due to solanine glycoalkaloids. All parts of the plant are poisonous to livestock and humans if ingested.

The common name "horsenettle" is misleading, as the plant is not a true nettle (Urticaceae). The name likely refers to the stiff spines that resemble horse nettle prickles.

Carolina horsenettle is one of the most troublesome weeds in pastures, row crops, and disturbed sites throughout temperate North America.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Solanum carolinense was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The species epithet refers to the Carolinas, where the plant was first documented. It belongs to the subgenus Leptostemonum (the spiny solanums), a large group of approximately 350–450 species worldwide.

Several infraspecific taxa have been described, but most are not widely recognized. The species is closely related to Solanum rostratum and Solanum elaeagnifolium, with which it shares the spiny habit and glycoalkaloid chemistry.

The plant grows 30–100 cm tall from a deep, extensive rhizome system. Stems are covered with fine stellate hairs and numerous sharp yellowish spines up to 10 mm long.

Leaves are alternate, ovate to lanceolate, 5–13 cm long and 3–8 cm wide, with irregularly lobed or coarsely toothed margins. Both leaf surfaces bear stellate pubescence. The leaf margins are often armed with small prickles.

Flowers are borne in racemes or cymes. The corolla is 5-lobed, 15–25 mm across, pale violet to white, with prominent yellow anthers forming a conical cluster.

Flowering occurs from May through October, with peak blooming in midsummer. Individual flowers open in the morning and last approximately 2–3 days before senescing.

Fruits are globular berries 8–12 mm in diameter, initially green, ripening to yellow or yellowish-orange. Each berry contains 50–100 small flattened seeds. Seeds remain viable in the soil seed bank for up to 10 years.

Root systems can extend 1.5 meters deep and spread several meters laterally through rhizome networks. This extensive underground biomass is the primary mechanism for persistence and vegetative spread.

## Distribution and Habitat

Solanum carolinense is native to the southeastern and central United States, from Texas and Florida north to Nebraska and Pennsylvania. It has been introduced far beyond its native range, occurring across most of the continental United States and parts of southern Canada.

The species thrives in disturbed habitats including pastures, abandoned fields, roadsides, railway embankments, and cultivated cropland. It prefers well-drained sandy or loamy soils in full sun.

It is classified as a noxious weed in several U.S. states. Its deep rhizome system allows it to persist through drought and mechanical disturbance.

The plant shows moderate shade tolerance but achieves maximum growth and reproductive output in full sun. It is absent from areas with prolonged freezing or waterlogged soils.

Population densities can reach 20 or more plants per square meter in heavily infested pastures, significantly reducing available forage for livestock.

## Bioactive Compounds and Toxicity

All parts of Solanum carolinense contain glycoalkaloids, primarily solanine and solanidine. The highest concentrations are found in unripe berries and leaves.

Solasonine and solamargine are the principal steroidal glycoalkaloids identified. These compounds are bitter-tasting and act as feeding deterrents to herbivores.

Ingestion causes gastrointestinal distress, neurological symptoms, and in severe cases, death in livestock. Cattle and sheep are most commonly affected when grazing contaminated pasture.

Symptoms of poisoning include salivation, vomiting, diarrhea, ataxia, depression, and labored breathing. Fatalities are rare in adult livestock but more common in young animals.

The glycoalkaloid content varies seasonally, peaking during flowering and fruiting. Drought stress can also elevate glycoalkaloid concentrations in leaves and stems.

No specific antidote exists for solanine poisoning. Treatment is supportive, including activated charcoal administration, intravenous fluids, and symptomatic care.
