## Overview

Aconitum lycoctonum, commonly known as wolf's bane or northern wolfsbane, is a perennial herb in the family Ranunculaceae. It is native to the mountains and [[psilocybin-psilocin-variation-across-flushes]] its range.

The stem is erect, glabrous to sparsely pubescent, and typically unbranched below the inflorescence. Leaves are palmately divided into 5–7 deep, narrowly lanceolate segments with sharply toothed margins.

The flowers are borne in loose, often branched terminal racemes. Each flower has five sepals; the upper sepal forms a broad, rounded helmet (galea) 10–15 mm tall, and the lateral sepals are broadly ovate and slightly unequal.

The two upper petals (nectaries) are concealed within the helmet, each bearing a recurved nectar spur. Stamens are numerous, and the fruit consists of three to five follicles containing numerous winged seeds.

The flowers lack true petals; what appear to be petals are modified sepals adapted for pollinator attraction. This condition is common throughout the genus Aconitum and many other Ranunculaceae.

The root system consists of a cluster of tapering, fusiform roots that are dark brown externally and white internally. These roots contain the highest concentrations of alkaloids, making them the most toxic part of the plant.

## Distribution and Habitat

Aconitum lycoctonum occurs across a broad Eurasian range from the mountains of northern Spain through the Alps, Carpathians, and Balkans to Scandinavia and across northern Russia to Siberia. It is absent from the British Isles and the Mediterranean lowlands.

In mountainous regions, it grows at elevations from 800 to 2,500 m, preferring subalpine meadows, streambanks, and damp screes. In boreal latitudes, it descends to sea level and inhabits birch and conifer woodlands.

The species favors moist, nitrogen-rich, slightly acidic to neutral soils. It is frequently found near streams, in avalanche tracks, and in other sites with disturbed but nutrient-enriched substrates.

Common associates include [[caltha-palustris]], Geranium sylvaticum, and various tall herbs in subalpine tall-herb communities. It is classified as Least Concern across its broad range.

In the Scandinavian mountains, Aconitum lycoctonum is a characteristic species of the tall-herb belt above the birch forest line. It persists in snowbed communities where late-melting snow provides reliable moisture.

At the southern edge of its distribution in Spain and Italy, populations are small and isolated. These southern populations may represent relicts from colder climatic periods and are of conservation interest.

## Ecology

Aconitum lycoctonum flowers from July to September, later than most other subalpine Ranunculaceae. The flowers are adapted for pollination by long-tongued bumblebees (Bombus hortorum, B. pascuorum) that can reach the nectar within the helmet.

The nectar is alkaloid-free despite the toxic foliage, allowing safe feeding by pollinators. However, the pollen contains low levels of alkaloids that may discourage pollen-collecting bees and promote outcrossing.

[[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]] is prolific, with each follicle containing 20–40 winged seeds dispersed by wind. Seedling establishment requires bare, moist soil and is enhanced in recently disturbed microsites such as animal diggings or erosion scars.

Vegetative spread is limited; the plant forms a slowly expanding clump but does not produce stolons or spreading rhizomes. Longevity of individual plants is estimated at 15–25 years.

The potent alkaloid content provides near-complete protection from vertebrate herbivory. Cattle, sheep, and deer avoid the foliage entirely, and cases of livestock poisoning are rare in regions where the plant is native.

The species is a larval host plant for several moths in the genus Eupithecia, whose caterpillars feed on the flowers and developing seeds. These specialist herbivores have evolved resistance to the plant's alkaloid defenses.

Despite its toxicity to mammals, the nectar supports robust bumblebee populations in subalpine meadows. The plant contributes significantly to late-season pollinator resources in mountain habitats.

## Cultivation

Aconitum lycoctonum is cultivated as a tall, architectural perennial for moist, partially shaded borders and woodland gardens. It is hardy to USDA zones 3–7 and tolerates colder conditions than most other Aconitum species.

The plant prefers deep, moist, humus-rich soil in partial shade. It tolerates full sun in cool-summer climates provided the roots remain consistently moist.

Planting is best done in spring using container-grown plants, as the fleshy roots resent disturbance. Established clumps should not be divided frequently; allow 5–7 years between divisions.

The yellow flower color is unusual in the genus and combines well with blue and purple late-summer perennials such as [[aconitum-napellus]], Aster, and Gentiana. The tall stems may require staking in exposed sites.

Deadheading after flowering prevents unwanted self-seeding in the garden. Some gardeners leave a few seed heads for winter architectural interest.
