# Fairy Ring (Lawn Disease)

## Description

Fairy rings are circular or semi-circular patterns of dark green grass, dead grass, or mushrooms that appear in lawns and turf. They are caused by various species of basidiomycete fungi growing radially outward through soil as they decompose organic matter. In [[the-soil-food-web]] framework, fairy rings indicate a lack of fungal diversity — a monoculture of a single fungal species has become dominant. The solution is not chemical eradication but increasing overall fungal diversity through [[compost]] and compost tea applications.

## Classification

- **Causal organisms:** Various basidiomycete fungi (many species across multiple genera)
- **Type:** Fungal turfgrass disease / soil phenomenon
- **Visual pattern:** Circular or arc-shaped rings in turf
- **Three types:**
  - Type I: Dead zone with ring of dead grass
  - Type II: Dark green zone of stimulated growth
  - Type III: Ring of mushrooms (visible fruiting bodies)

## Key Facts

- Caused by "all sorts of fungi" — many different basidiomycete species can produce fairy ring symptoms
- Fungi grow radially outward through the soil, breaking down organic matter in a expanding circular pattern
- The resulting mycelial mat can create hydrophobic (water-repellent) soil zones
- Fairy rings are a sign of **fungal monoculture** — one species has dominated at the expense of diversity
- Management via [[soil-food-web]] principles: increase fungal diversity in the lawn by ensuring compost and teas contain a wide variety of fungal species
- With greater fungal diversity, the fairy ring organism gets outcompeted by other fungi
- Micro- and macroarthropods, mice, and shrews also consume fairy ring fungi, providing natural population control
- Gardeners should "rejoice" at seeing mushrooms in lawns generally — they indicate healthy fungal activity below ground
- Only problematic when a single species dominates (fairy ring) rather than a diverse fungal community

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Fairy rings offer key insights for mycologists and cultivators:

- **Monoculture vulnerability:** Even beneficial fungi become problematic when they form monocultures — diversity is the stabilizing force
- **[[ecological-succession]]:** Fairy rings represent a successional stage where one fungus temporarily dominates a resource
- **Competition as control:** The most effective control is competitive exclusion by a diverse fungal community, not chemical fungicides
- **Arthropod grazing:** [[soil-arthropods]] provide top-down control on fungal populations, preventing any single species from dominating
- **Practical management:** Compost teas with high fungal diversity inoculate soils with competing species that naturally suppress fairy ring fungi

This aligns with general mycological principles of competitive exclusion and the importance of community diversity in preventing pathogen dominance.
