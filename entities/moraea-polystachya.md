## Overview

*Moraea polystachya* (Thunb.) Ker Gawl., known as the blue peacock iris or bloutulp, is a cormous perennial in the family Iridaceae. It is one of the most widespread *Moraea* species in southern Africa.

The plant produces pale blue to violet flowers with yellow nectar guides on the outer tepals. Each bloom lasts only a single day but is produced in succession over several weeks.

It occurs across a broad range of habitats from the Cape Peninsula to the Eastern Cape. The species is adaptable and has become established in cultivation as a long-blooming ornamental.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Order: Asparagales
- Family: Iridaceae
- Subfamily: Iridaceae subfamily Iridoideae
- Tribe: Irideae
- Genus: *Moraea*
- Species: *M. polystachya*

The genus *Moraea* includes approximately 200 species, making it one of the largest genera of African Iridaceae. *M. polystachya* was first described by Carl Peter Thunberg and later transferred by Ker Gawler.

Plants grow 30–70 cm tall from a globose corm 10–20 mm in diameter. The corm is covered with dark brown, fibrous tunics that persist from previous seasons.

The plant produces a single, flattened, sword-shaped leaf that is 5–10 mm wide. The leaf is usually shorter than the flowering stem and is smooth along the margins.

Flowers are borne on branched stems, with each branch bearing 2–5 flowers opening in succession. The pale blue to violet tepals spread widely, 3–4 cm in diameter.

Each flower has three large outer tepals with conspicuous yellow nectar guides marked in darker blue. The three inner tepals are smaller, narrower, and often paler in color.

Flowers open in the morning and wilt by late afternoon, a characteristic shared with many *Moraea* species. Despite their ephemeral nature, the succession of blooms extends the display period significantly.

The style branches are expanded into petaloid crests that arch over the stamens. This is a defining feature of the genus and aids in pollinator guidance.

Capsules are oblong, 10–15 mm long, containing numerous small, dark brown seeds. Seeds are angular and lack specialized dispersal structures.

The chromosome number is 2n = 14, which is characteristic of the *Moraea* subgenus *Vieusseuxia*. Polyploid cytotypes have not been reported for this species.

*M. polystachya* is most closely related to *M. vegeta* and *M. spathulata*, which share the branched flowering stem and single-day flowers. It can be distinguished by its wider leaf and bluer flower color.

The species shows considerable variation in flower color across its range. Populations from the western Cape tend to have deeper blue flowers, while eastern populations are often paler violet.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Moraea polystachya* has one of the widest distributions of any *Moraea* species. It ranges from the Western Cape eastward through the Eastern Cape and into KwaZulu-Natal.

The species occurs in fynbos, renosterveld, grassland, and thicket vegetation types. It grows at elevations from sea level to 1,200 m, demonstrating considerable ecological breadth.

Unlike many Cape Iridaceae that are winter-growing, *M. polystachya* can grow in both winter- and summer-rainfall zones. This adaptability contributes to its wide distribution across different climatic regions.

Plants prefer well-drained, sandy or loamy soils but tolerate clay substrates. They are often found on road verges and disturbed sites where competition from shrubs is reduced.

Flowering occurs from August to November in the southern hemisphere. In winter-rainfall areas, peak flowering is in September–October.

The species is not considered threatened due to its wide distribution and ability to colonize disturbed habitats. It is assessed as Least Concern on the South African Red List.

Fire stimulates flowering in populations from fynbos habitats. Plants in frequently burned areas produce more flowers per stem than those in long-unburned sites.

The species co-occurs with many other Cape geophytes in fynbos and renosterveld. Common associates include *Watsonia borbonica*, *Gladiolus carneus*, and various *Protea* species.

## Cultivation

*Moraea polystachya* is cultivated as an ornamental bulb in suitable Mediterranean and subtropical climates. Its ease of cultivation and long flowering season make it attractive to bulb gardeners.

Corms are planted in autumn at 5–8 cm depth in well-drained soil. Full sun produces the most prolific flowering, though plants tolerate partial shade.

Water moderately during active growth and reduce watering as foliage matures. In summer-rainfall climates, the species grows during the warmer months and is dormant in winter.

Propagation is straightforward from fresh seed or corm offsets. Seeds germinate readily within 2–3 weeks at temperatures of 18–22°C.

Seed-grown plants typically flower in their second or third year. Corm offsets flower in the first season after separation from the parent corm.

The species is relatively disease-free when grown in well-drained conditions. Overwatering during dormancy is the most common cause of corm loss.

Flowering stems may require staking in exposed, windy sites. The branching stems can become top-heavy when carrying multiple open flowers.

## Ecology and Uses

The blue peacock iris is primarily valued as an ornamental garden plant. Its long bloom period and ease of cultivation make it suitable for rock gardens and mixed bulb borders.

In the wild, flowers are visited by a range of insects including bees and flies. The yellow nectar guides serve as visual cues directing pollinators to the nectar at the tepal bases.

The species is not known to have traditional medicinal or food uses. However, several *Moraea* species contain toxic compounds and *M. polystachya* should be treated as potentially toxic if ingested.

Some *Moraea* species are poisonous to livestock, particularly when corms are exposed by soil erosion. *M. polystachya* has been implicated in occasional stock poisoning in the Eastern Cape.

The genus *Moraea* is closely related to *Iris* and shares many morphological features including the petaloid style crests. This relationship is reflected in the common name "peacock iris."

The species was collected by early botanical explorers including Thunberg and Burchell. It was among the first *Moraea* species described from the Cape and has a well-documented taxonomic history.

In cultivation, the blue peacock iris can be naturalized in suitably mild gardens where it self-seeds freely. It has been reported as casually naturalized in parts of southern Australia.
