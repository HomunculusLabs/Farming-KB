## Overview

**Aldrovanda vesiculosa**, the waterwheel plant, is a free-floating aquatic
carnivorous plant and the sole extant species in its genus. It belongs to the
family Droseraceae, making it a close relative of the Venus flytrap
(*[[dionaea-muscipula]]*) and the sundews (*Drosera*).

The plant consists of a stem 5–20 cm long with whorls of 6–8 leaves at each
node. Each leaf terminates in a snap-trap mechanism that closely resembles a
miniature Venus flytrap, capturing small aquatic invertebrates in milliseconds.

*Aldrovanda* is one of the most widely distributed carnivorous plants
historically, occurring across Europe, Africa, Asia, and Australia. However,
populations have declined dramatically and the species is now classified as
Endangered on the IUCN Red List due to habitat loss and eutrophication.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Aldrovanda vesiculosa** L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The genus
name honors the Italian naturalist Ulisse Aldrovandi (1522–1605). The species
epithet *vesiculosa* refers to the small bladder-like traps.

The genus contains one extant species and several fossil taxa known from
Tertiary deposits across Europe. Molecular phylogenetic analyses place
*Aldrovanda* as sister to *Dionaea*, with both genera nested within
*Drosera*. This suggests the snap-trap mechanism evolved from a sundew-like
ancestor.

The plant body is a free-floating stem lacking roots. Whorls of 6–8 leaves
are produced at each node along the stem, giving the appearance of a small
aquatic wheel — hence the common name. The stem apex continuously produces
new whorls while older portions senesce and disintegrate.

Each trap is 3–6 mm long and consists of two bilobed halves. The trap margins
bear 12–20 trigger hairs and interlocking teeth. When a trigger hair is touched
twice within approximately 20 seconds, the trap snaps shut in 10–20
milliseconds, among the fastest movements in the plant kingdom.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Aldrovanda* has a remarkably broad but fragmented distribution. Historically
recorded from western Europe through Russia, Central Asia, India, Japan,
northern Australia, and eastern and southern Africa. Many historical
populations are now extirpated.

The species inhabits shallow, standing or slowly flowing freshwater bodies.
Preferred habitats include oligotrophic (nutrient-poor) lakes, ponds, ditches,
and marsh edges. Water pH is typically acidic to neutral (5.5–7.0).

The plant requires warm summer water temperatures (18–30°C) for active growth
and trap function. In [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]], it overwinters as dormant buds called
turions that sink to the bottom and resume growth in spring. Water clarity
is important — turbid conditions inhibit photosynthesis and trap function.

## Carnivory Mechanism

The snap-trap mechanism of *Aldrovanda* is convergent with that of *Dionaea*
muscipula. Each trap lobe contains sensory trigger hairs that detect mechanical
stimulation. A threshold of two hair deflections within approximately 20
seconds triggers the rapid closure.

Trap closure occurs through a rapid loss of [[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]] in motor cells
along the trap midrib. This hygroscopic mechanism does not require active
metabolic energy for the closing motion itself, though ATP is required to
subsequently reopen the trap.

Prey consists primarily of small aquatic invertebrates including water fleas
(*Daphnia*), copepods, mosquito larvae, and nematodes. The trap gradually
seals around captured prey and secretes digestive enzymes from glands on the
inner trap surface.
