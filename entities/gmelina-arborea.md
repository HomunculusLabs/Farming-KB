# Gmelina arborea

## Description

*Gmelina arborea* (white teak, gamhar) is a fast-growing deciduous tree in the family Lamiaceae (formerly Verbenaceae), native to South and Southeast Asia. It is widely cultivated as a pulpwood and timber species in tropical plantations. Research by Osonubi et al. (1990) demonstrated that inoculation with the saprotrophic fungus *[[chaetomium-bostrychoides]]* enhances both seed germination and drought tolerance in this economically important species.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Clade**: Tracheophytes
- **Clade**: Angiosperms

## Key Facts

- Used as a Nigerian pulpwood species in industrial forestry operations.
- Seed inoculation with the saprotrophic fungus *Chaetomium bostrychoides* increases seed germination rates.
- Fungal inoculation also increases tolerance to desiccating conditions, improving seedling survival during drought.
- Beneficial effects observed from inoculation at both the seed and seedling stages.
- Droughted inoculated seedlings showed significantly higher biomass than droughted uninoculated controls.
- Shoot biomass of droughted seed-inoculated plants reached 9.36 g dry weight vs. 5.89 g for uninoculated droughted controls.
- Root biomass of droughted seed-inoculated plants reached 4.47 g vs. 2.65 g for uninoculated droughted controls.
- The fungal benefit extends beyond mycorrhizal-type associations, showing that [[saprotrophic-fungi]] can directly enhance plant stress tolerance.

## Ecological Role

*G. arborea* is a pioneer species in tropical forests, colonizing disturbed sites and open areas. Its rapid growth rate and adaptability to diverse soil conditions make it ecologically important for early successional forest development. In managed ecosystems, it serves as a nurse tree providing shade and soil improvement for later-successional species.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Tropical forestry**: One of the most important plantation species in tropical Asia and Africa for pulp, paper, and timber production.
- **Bioinoculant potential**: The demonstrated benefit of *Chaetomium* inoculation suggests practical applications for improving nursery establishment and outplanting success.
- **Drought management**: Fungal inoculation provides a biological approach to drought mitigation in tropical reforestation.
- **Non-mycorrhizal fungal benefits**: Demonstrates that saprotrophic fungi, not just mycorrhizal fungi, can enhance plant establishment and stress tolerance.
- **Sustainable forestry**: Fungal inoculation could reduce irrigation needs and improve survival rates in tropical plantation forestry.
- **Agroforestry**: Fast growth and soil-improving properties make it suitable for agroforestry systems.

## Inoculation Results Detail

From Osonubi et al. (1990), complete biomass data for *Gmelina* seedlings:

**Before Drought (g dry weight)**:
- Seed inoculation: Shoot 1.35, Root 0.72 (droughted); Shoot 0.99, Root 0.48 (undroughted)
- Seedling inoculation: Shoot 0.83, Root 0.22 (droughted); Shoot 0.79, Root 0.35 (undroughted)

**After Drought (g dry weight)**:
- Seed inoculation: Shoot 9.36, Root 4.47 (droughted); Shoot 5.89, Root 2.65 (undroughted)
- Seedling inoculation: Shoot 6.92, Root 4.47 (droughted); Shoot 5.67, Root 2.65 (undroughted)

Key observation: Droughted inoculated plants outperformed undroughted uninoculated controls, demonstrating that fungal inoculation more than compensates for drought stress.

## Silvicultural Characteristics

- Fast-growing, reaching harvestable size in 10-15 years under optimal conditions.
- Produces lightweight, moderately durable timber used for furniture, plywood, and pulp.
- Coppices vigorously, making it suitable for short-rotation forestry.
- Tolerates a wide range of soil conditions including degraded and marginal sites.
- Nitrogen-fixing rhizobacteria in the rhizosphere may complement fungal associations.

## Mechanism of Fungal Drought Tolerance Enhancement

The mechanism by which *Chaetomium bostrychoides* enhances drought tolerance may involve:
- Production of osmoregulatory compounds that protect plant cell membranes
- Modification of root architecture promoting deeper or more extensive root systems
- Hormonal effects altering plant water-use efficiency
- Improved nutrient uptake enabling better osmotic adjustment
