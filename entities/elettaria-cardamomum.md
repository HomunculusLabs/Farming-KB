## Elettaria cardamomum (Cardamom)

## Overview

Elettaria cardamomum is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Zingiberaceae, native to the moist forests of southern India and Sri Lanka. It produces the world's third most expensive spice by weight, known as the "Queen of Spices," valued for its intensely aromatic seeds used in culinary, medicinal, and aromatic applications.

Cardamom has been cultivated for over 4,000 years and was traded along ancient spice routes from India to the Mediterranean. Today, Guatemala is the world's largest producer, followed by India and Sri Lanka. The spice consists of dried seed capsules harvested from the plant's aerial panicles.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Zingiberales
- **Family:** Zingiberaceae
- **Genus:** Elettaria
- **Species:** E. cardamomum (L.) Maton

The genus name derives from the Tamil *elakkāi* (எலக்காய்), meaning "green seed." Two botanical varieties are recognized: var. cardamomum (true cardamom, native to the Western Ghats) and var. major (Nepal cardamom, native to northeast India), with the former being the commercially dominant form.

Plants grow 2–5 m tall from a branching, subterranean rhizome system. The pseudostem is composed of overlapping leaf sheaths. Leaf blades are lanceolate, 30–60 cm long and 5–10 cm wide, dark green with a prominent midrib and silvery undersides.

The inflorescence is a panicle arising from the base of the pseudostem, 30–100 cm long, bearing 10–20 flowers at each node. The characteristic cardamom aroma results from a synergistic interaction between 1,8-cineole's cooling, camphoraceous notes and α-terpinyl acetate's sweet, fruity character. The balance of these two compounds determines perceived quality in commercial grading.

Geographical origin significantly influences essential oil composition. Indian cardamom typically has higher α-terpinyl acetate content (35–40%), while Guatemalan cardamom is richer in 1,8-cineole (45–55%). These chemical differences correspond to distinct flavor profiles valued in different culinary traditions.

## Culinary Uses

Cardamom is one of the most versatile spices in global cuisine. In Indian cooking, it is a key ingredient in *garam masala* and *chai* masala, and is used in both savory curries and sweet preparations such as *kheer* and *gulab jamun*.

In Middle Eastern and Scandinavian cuisines, cardamom is the dominant flavor in coffee (Arabic *qahwa*) and baked goods (Finnish *pulla*, Swedish *kardemummabullar*). The spice is used whole, bruised, or ground, with each form suited to different cooking applications.

Whole green capsules are preferred in Indian and Middle Eastern cooking for their visual appeal and gradual flavor release. Ground cardamom is used in baking and beverage preparations where rapid flavor extraction is needed. Decorticated seeds offer the most concentrated flavor.

In Nordic countries, cardamom consumption per capita is among the highest globally. The spice is used in traditional holiday baking, mulled wine (*glögg*), and breakfast pastries. Scandinavian bakers typically use a finer grind and higher proportion than Indian cuisine conventions.

The spice also features in savory North African dishes such as Moroccan *ras el hanout* and Ethiopian *berbere* spice blends. Cardamom pods are added to rice pilafs across Central Asian and Persian cuisines, where they infuse the cooking liquid with aroma.

## Pharmacology and Traditional Uses

Cardamom has been used in Ayurvedic, Unani, and Chinese medicine for millennia. In Ayurveda it is classified as a warming spice (*ushna virya*) used to balance *kapha* and *vata* doshas, prescribed for digestive weakness, respiratory congestion, and urinary disorders.

Modern pharmacological research has documented several bioactive properties:

- **Gastroprotective:** Cardamom seed extracts reduce gastric acid secretion and protect against ulcer formation in rodent models. The effect is attributed to the combined action of multiple terpenoids.
- **Anti-inflammatory:** 1,8-Cineole and α-terpinyl acetate inhibit NF-κB signaling and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine production in vitro. Oral administration reduces carrageenan-induced inflammation in animals.
- **Antimicrobial:** Essential oil demonstrates broad-spectrum activity against oral pathogens, foodborne bacteria, and Candida species. These properties support traditional use as a breath freshener and digestive aid.
- **Antioxidant:** Seed extracts scavenge free radicals and upregulate endogenous antioxidant enzymes including glutathione peroxidase and superoxide dismutase.
- **Antispasmodic:** Cardamom extracts relax intestinal smooth muscle in ex vivo preparations, supporting traditional use for digestive cramps and flatulence.

## Cultivation

Cardamom is propagated primarily by vegetative division of rhizome clumps. Suckers with 2–3 shoots and viable roots are transplanted at the onset of the monsoon season. Spacing is typically 2 × 2 m under natural forest shade or artificial shade structures.
