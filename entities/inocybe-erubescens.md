# Inocybe erubescens

## Overview

*Inocybe erubescens*, synonym *Inocybe patouillardii*, is a poisonous basidiomycete fungus in the family Inocybaceae. It is one of the most dangerous [[psilocybe-cubensis-cap-stem-alkaloid-distribution-strain-comparison]] that acts as a potent parasympathomimetic agent. Ingestion causes classic muscarinic poisoning within 30 minutes to 2 hours, [[comparison-pruning-fruit-trees-vs-grafting-fruit-trees]]s in parks, gardens, and woodland edges, often in proximity to human habitations, increasing the risk of accidental poisoning.

## Taxonomy

Basidiomycota → Agaricomycetes → Agaricales → **Inocybaceae → *Inocybe* → *I. erubescens***

- **Synonyms:** *Inocybe patouillardii* Bres. (1883), *Agaricus erubescens* (Bres.) Bres., *Inocybe erythrobotrya* Reumaux
- **Etymology:** *erubescens* means "becoming red," referring to the characteristic reddish discoloration that develops on bruised or cut flesh; *patouillardii* honors the French mycologist Narcisse Théophile Patouillard
- The taxonomic history of this species has been contentious; many European records previously attributed to *I. patouillardii* have been consolidated under *I. erubescens* following molecular phylogenetic studies

## Morphology

- **Cap:** 3–8 cm, conical to convex becoming broadly umbonate; surface silky-fibrillose with radial fibers, often splitting at the margin; color whitish to pale buff when young, developing reddish-brown stains on handling or with age; margin often irregular and split
- **Gills:** Adnexed to slightly sinuate, crowded, narrow; white when young, becoming greyish and eventually brownish with reddish tints; edges often fringed and uneven
- **Stem:** 4–8 cm × 0.8–1.5 cm, cylindrical, solid; surface white to pale buff with a silky sheen, staining distinctly reddish-brown when bruised or cut — this is a critical diagnostic feature; no ring or volva
- **Flesh:** White, firm; turns pinkish to reddish-brown slowly on exposure; odor mild to faintly spermatic (a characteristic Inocybe odor); taste mild or slightly mealy
- **[[cap-versus-stem-alkaloid-distribution-in-psilocybin-mushrooms]] and Habitat

- **Geographic range:** Widely distributed across temperate Europe, especially common in Western and Central Europe (France, Germany, Benelux, Britain, Italy, Spain); also reported from parts of North Africa and Asia
- **Habitat:** Terrestrial, ectomycorrhizal; found singly or in small groups on soil in deciduous and mixed woodlands, parks, gardens, orchards, and along hedgerows; frequently associated with oak, beech, hornbeam, and lime trees
- **Season:** Spring to early autumn (May–October in Europe); appears earlier in the year than many other Inocybe species, with a notable fruiting peak in late spring and early summer
- **Ecological role:** Obligate ectomycorrhizal partner of hardwood trees; forms mutualistic associations with tree roots, exchanging mineral nutrients for photosynthetic carbon

## Toxicity and Muscarine Poisoning

*I. erubescens* is considered the most dangerous muscarine-containing [[clitocybe-dealbata]]* or *Inocybe fastigiata*, making ingestion of even small quantities hazardous.

**Muscarine content:** Approximately 0.03–0.05% dry weight, among the highest recorded for any [[blue-light-phototropism-red-light-photoperiodism-dual-plant-vision-system]]
- Bradycardia (slow heart rate) and hypotension
- Bronchorrhea and bronchoconstriction, potentially leading to respiratory distress
- Nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and diarrhea
- In severe untreated cases: respiratory failure, cardiac arrest, and death

### Treatment

- [[fruit-tree-care-and-orchard-management]], and cardiac monitoring
- Hospitalization is recommended for all confirmed or suspected cases
- Activated charcoal may be administered if presentation is within 1 hour of ingestion
- **Important:** Muscarine is NOT dialyzable and is not metabolized by cholinesterase inhibitors — physostigmine is contraindicated

### Case Reports

Fatalities have been documented, particularly in cases involving children, elderly individuals, or delayed medical treatment. A notable outbreak in France in the 1980s involved multiple poisonings from *I. erubescens* collected in public parks. The species is responsible for a disproportionate number of serious mushroom poisonings in Europe relative to its actual abundance, likely because it fruits in accessible locations (parks, gardens) and can be confused with edible species by inexperienced foragers.
