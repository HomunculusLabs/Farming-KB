## Overview

**Bouteloua curtipendula** (Michx.) Torr., commonly known as sideoats grama, is a perennial warm-season bunchgrass native to North America. It is recognized as the state grass of Texas and is widely distributed across the Great Plains and southwestern United States.

The species is valued as a native forage grass, an ornamental landscape plant, and a key component of [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]].

The species is primarily a decumbent to erect bunchgrass, though some ecotypes produce short rhizomes that allow limited lateral spread. Under [[gadd-mycelial-network-resilience-grazing-pressure]], plants typically assume a more prostrate growth form.

Sideoats grama is a diploid species with 2n = 2x = 40 chromosomes, though some polyploid populations have been documented. The base chromosome number of x = 20 is shared with other members of the genus.

Flowering occurs from June through September, with the characteristic one-sided spikes developing a distinctive purplish-amber coloration at anthesis. Seed maturation follows approximately 4–6 weeks after flowering.

The inflorescence architecture is unique among North American grasses: the spike-bearing branches are arranged in a comb-like pattern along one side of the central axis, with each spike appearing to hang from a common stalk. This arrangement facilitates wind pollination.

Vegetative dormancy begins with the first hard frosts in autumn, with plants persisting as basal tufts through winter. Spring regrowth initiates from basal buds once soil temperatures consistently exceed 15°C.

## Distribution and Habitat

The native range extends from southern Canada (Ontario, Saskatchewan, Alberta) southward through the Great Plains to northern Mexico, and from the Rocky Mountains eastward to the Atlantic coast. It is one of the most widely distributed native grasses in North America.

Optimal habitats include mixed-grass prairies, open woodlands, rocky hillsides, and sandy plains. The species is tolerant of a wide range of soil types including clay, loam, sandy, and rocky substrates, but performs best on well-drained upland sites.

Elevation range spans from near sea level in the eastern portion of its range to over 2,400 meters in the southwestern mountains. It is less common in extremely arid desert environments and in continuously humid lowland regions.

Sideoats grama increases in relative abundance with moderate grazing pressure and is considered an increaser species in many rangeland condition assessments. It is often one of the last native grasses to decline under heavy grazing due to its low growth form and basal meristems.

The species shows strong preference for calcareous soils in portions of its range, particularly in the Edwards Plateau and Ozark regions where limestone-derived soils predominate. Soil pH tolerance ranges from mildly acidic to strongly alkaline conditions.

In the southwestern United States, sideoats grama is a common understory component of oak-juniper woodlands and pinyon-juniper savannas, persisting under partial canopy cover. It is also found in desert grassland transition zones alongside black grama and three-awns.

## Ecological Role

Sideoats grama is a principal component of mixed-grass prairie communities, often co-dominating with little bluestem, blue grama, and western wheatgrass. It occupies an intermediate position between the tallgrass and shortgrass prairie types.

The species provides forage for a wide range of herbivores including bison, elk, pronghorn, deer, and numerous small mammals. Its seeds are consumed by granivorous birds including mourning doves, wild turkeys, and various sparrows.

Fire adaptation is strong, with plants resprouting rapidly from basal buds after burning. Prescribed fire at 3–5 year intervals maintains sideoats grama productivity and reduces woody encroachment in mixed-grass prairie systems.
