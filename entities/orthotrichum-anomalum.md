## Overview

Orthotrichum anomalum, commonly known as the anomalous orthotrichum, is an acrocarpous moss in the family Orthotrichaceae. It is one of the most widespread and commonly encountered epiphytic mosses on tree bark and stone surfaces in temperate regions.

The species is notable for its distinctive capsule, which features eight deeply divided peristome teeth arranged in a single whorl. It bridges the [[ecological-niche]] between saxicolous and epiphytic growth habits, occurring on both rock and bark substrates.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Orthotrichum anomalum was described by Johann Hedwig in 1801. The genus Orthotrichum contains approximately 200 species distributed worldwide, with the greatest diversity in temperate mountainous regions. Molecular work has revealed that the genus as traditionally circumscribed is polyphyletic, leading to the segregation of several genera such as Nyholmiella and Lewinskya.

The gametophyte forms dark green to brownish-green cushions or mats, 1–3 cm tall. Stems are erect, densely tomentose at the base with reddish-brown rhizoids.

Leaves are lanceolate to ovate-lanceolate, 3–5 mm long, with a strong costa that extends to near the apex. Leaf margins are strongly recurved in the upper half and entire. The recurved margins create a distinctive channelled appearance visible under hand lens.

The lamina is multicostate, with 2–4 stereid bands in the costa cross-section. Upper laminal cells are short-rectangular with thickened walls; basal cells are elongate and thin-walled. Papillae are absent from the [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America. It is less common in the [[aquatic-fungi-spore-dispersal-ingold]] is primarily by wind, but the short seta places capsules close to the bark surface. Insects, particularly flies, have been observed visiting capsules and may serve as secondary dispersal agents.

The species contributes to canopy microhabitat complexity. Its cushions retain moisture and provide substrate for microarthropods, nematodes, and other cryptogams such as lichens and liverworts.

Seasonal growth patterns show peak gametophytic extension during spring and autumn when humidity is highest. Growth slows or ceases during summer drought periods and winter frost.

## Conservation

O. anomalum is generally common and secure throughout most of its range. However, loss of mature trees through urban development, agricultural intensification, and disease has reduced habitat availability.

The decline of elm trees due to Dutch elm disease has eliminated an important host species in many parts of Europe. Ash dieback is expected to have similar impacts on O. anomalum populations in affected regions.

Lichen interactions with O. anomalum are complex. Crustose lichens may compete for bark space, while foliose lichens such as Parmelia spp. sometimes grow intermingled with moss cushions in mutually stable associations.

[[cervantes-air-quality-ventilation]] improvement in many European cities since the 1980s has benefited epiphytic moss communities. Recolonization of previously denuded urban trees by O. anomalum has been documented in multiple cities.

Conservation efforts focus on preserving mature tree populations in hedgerows, parklands, and riparian corridors. These landscape features provide critical refugia for epiphytic bryophyte communities in intensively managed agricultural landscapes.

The species is protected under several national conservation frameworks. In the UK, it is listed as a Species of Principal Importance under the NERC Act.

## Research Significance

O. anomalum has been used as a bioindicator of epiphytic habitat quality in forest ecosystems. Its presence and abundance correlate with tree age, bark pH, and atmospheric moisture levels.
