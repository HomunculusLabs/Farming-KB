# Bougainvillea spectabilis (Great Bougainvillea)

## Overview

Bougainvillea spectabilis is a vigorous, woody, scrambling vine or shrub in the family Nyctaginaceae, native to South America.

It is one of the most widely planted ornamental plants in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide.

The species is renowned for its brilliant floral bracts, which range from magenta and purple to crimson and orange.

True flowers are small, white, and tubular, nearly hidden within the showy bracts.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Caryophyllales
- **Family:** Nyctaginaceae
- **Genus:** Bougainvillea
- **Species:** B. spectabilis
- **Authority:** Willd. (1799)
- **Synonyms:** Bougainvillea brasiliensis, B. peruviana (in part)

Stems are woody, thorned, and can reach 5–15 m when climbing or scrambling.

Thorns are stout, curved, 5–12 mm long, and arranged at nodes along the stems.

Leaves are alternate, ovate to broadly elliptic, 8–16 cm long, with entire margins.

The upper leaf surface is dark green and slightly rough; the lower surface is paler with prominent venation.

The inflorescence consists of three flowers surrounded by three large, papery bracts.

Bracts are 3–5 cm long, brightly colored, and persist for several weeks.

The actual flower is a slender, cream-white tube, 15–25 mm long, barely visible within the bracts.

Fruit is a small, narrow achene, 5–8 mm long, rarely produced in cultivation.

The bract color is not from petals but from modified leaves containing betacyanin pigments.

## Distribution and Habitat

B. spectabilis is native to Brazil, Peru, Ecuador, Colombia, and northern Argentina.

It occurs in dry forest margins, coastal scrub, and disturbed ground at low to mid elevations.

The species is drought-tolerant and thrives in full sun with minimal irrigation once established.

It prefers well-drained, slightly acidic soils and is intolerant of waterlogged conditions.

Bougainvillea has been introduced and naturalized across tropical and subtropical regions globally.

It is a defining feature of landscapes in the Mediterranean, southern California, India, and Southeast Asia.

## Cultivation

Bougainvillea is hardy to USDA zones 9–11 and requires warm conditions for continuous flowering.

Propagation is by semi-hardwood cuttings, which root readily under mist and bottom heat.

The plant flowers most prolifically when subjected to slight drought stress.

Regular pruning after flowering cycles maintains compact growth and encourages repeat blooming.

In cooler climates, bougainvillea is widely grown as a container plant and brought indoors in winter.

Standard forms (tree-trained bougainvillea) are popular in Mediterranean-style gardens.

The species and its hybrids are used extensively in street planting, highway landscaping, and public parks.

## Chemistry and Uses

Bougainvillea bracts contain betacyanins (bougainvillein), flavonoids, and phenolic compounds.

Leaf extracts have demonstrated anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and antidiabetic activities in vitro.

In traditional Brazilian medicine, leaf infusions are used to treat respiratory infections and diabetes.

Bougainvillein is the red-violet pigment responsible for the characteristic bract coloration.

The thorns have been used in traditional medicine as a styptic agent to stop bleeding.

No significant pharmacological studies have validated these traditional uses in humans.

## Ecological Role

In its native range, the flowers attract hummingbirds and long-tongued butterflies as pollinators.

The thorny, dense growth provides nesting habitat for small birds.

In naturalized areas, bougainvillea can form impenetrable thickets that exclude native species.

Its drought tolerance and vigorous growth give it competitive advantages in arid environments.

Seed production is rare in most cultivated settings, limiting its spread primarily to vegetative propagation.

## Cultural Significance

The genus is named after Louis Antoine de Bougainville, the French navigator who led the expedition that discovered the plant in Brazil in 1768.

Philibert Commerçon, the expedition's botanist, is believed to have been the first European to collect it.

Bougainvillea is the floral emblem of several cities including Guangzhou, China, and Campinas, Brazil.

The plant has become an iconic symbol of tropical and Mediterranean landscaping worldwide.

In India, bougainvillea is commonly planted along roadsides and railway embankments for erosion control.
