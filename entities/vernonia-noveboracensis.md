## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Asteraceae > Vernonieae > Vernonia > sect. Vernonia. Authority: (L.) Michx. (1803). Basionym: Serratula noveboracensis L. (1753).

Recent phylogenetic work has proposed transferring the species to the genus Vernoniastrum as Vernoniastrum noveboracense. This reclassification is not yet universally accepted.

Stems are erect, stout, longitudinally grooved, and often reddish-purple at the base. They are typically unbranched below the inflorescence, reaching 1.5–2.5 m tall.

The stem color intensifies to dark purple in cold autumn weather. Stems remain standing through winter, providing architectural interest.

Leaves are alternate, lanceolate to narrowly elliptic, 10–25 cm long and 1.5–4 cm wide. They are dark green above, paler beneath, with finely serrate margins and short petioles.

Leaf surfaces are rough-textured (scabrous) due to short, stiff hairs. The venation is prominent, with a conspicuous midrib and lateral veins.

Flower heads are arranged in loose, flat-topped corymbs at stem tips. Each head is 8–13 mm wide with 15–30 purple disk florets.

The involucral bracts are imbricate in 3–4 series, lanceolate, and often purple-tinged at the tips. The pappus consists of persistent, purplish-white bristles.

The fruit is a small cypsela (achene) 3–4 mm long with 10 ribs and tawny pappus bristles. Seeds are wind-dispersed and can travel considerable distances.

## Distribution and Habitat

Vernonia noveboracensis is native to eastern North America from Massachusetts to Florida, west to Ohio, Kentucky, and Tennessee. It is most common in the Atlantic coastal plain and Piedmont.

The species grows in moist meadows, alluvial floodplains, stream banks, damp roadsides, and wet pastures. It prefers full sun to light shade and moist to wet soils.

Typical habitats include low-lying areas that receive seasonal flooding. It tolerates clay soils and periodic inundation but not prolonged standing water.

Elevational range extends from sea level to about 600 m. Populations are most abundant in the coastal plain and lower Piedmont regions.

The species is classified as a facultative wetland plant (FACW+) in the USFWS wetland indicator system. It occurs in both wetland and non-wetland sites.

Vernonia noveboracensis can form large, dense colonies through root sprouting. Individual clumps may live for 15–20 years.

## Ecology

## Cultivation

Vernonia noveboracensis is hardy in USDA zones 5–9. It requires full sun and moist, fertile soil for best growth.

Plant in spring or fall, spacing plants 60–90 cm apart. The species is tolerant of clay soils and seasonal wetness, making it suitable for rain gardens.

Established plants develop deep taproots and are moderately drought-tolerant. However, consistent moisture produces the tallest, most vigorous growth.

Propagation is by seed (cold stratification improves germination) or division in spring. Division of mature clumps every 3–4 years maintains vigor.
