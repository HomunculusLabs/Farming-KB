## Lycium chinense

## Overview

Lycium chinense, commonly known as Chinese boxthorn or Chinese wolfberry, is a deciduous woody shrub in the family Solanaceae. The species is closely related to Lycium barbarum ([[iboga-root-bark-preparation]].

Chinese wolfberry produces small, bright red berries that are consumed fresh, dried, or processed into juice, tea, and traditional medicinal preparations. The fruit is rich in carotenoids, particularly zeaxanthin, and contains unique polysaccharide compounds associated with antioxidant and immunomodulatory activity.

Lycium chinense is distinguished from L. barbarum by its smaller fruit size, longer flowering period, and broader natural distribution across [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]].

The species occurs in a variety of habitats including forest margins, scrubland, riverbanks, roadsides, and disturbed ground. It tolerates a wider range of climatic conditions than Lycium barbarum, including warmer and more humid environments.

Chinese boxthorn grows from sea level to approximately 2000 m elevation. It prefers well-drained, sandy or loamy soils and full sun to partial shade.

The plant is commonly encountered as a semi-wild species in rural landscapes, where it persists around field edges and homesteads. Wild-harvested fruit commands higher prices than cultivated material in some regional markets.

Lycium chinense is tolerant of moderate soil salinity and has been planted for soil stabilization on marginal agricultural lands. Its deep root system helps prevent erosion on sloped terrain.

## Cultivation

Lycium chinense is propagated from seed or by hardwood cuttings. Seed-grown plants exhibit greater genetic variability, while cuttings produce clones with uniform fruit characteristics.

Cuttings 15–20 cm long are taken during dormancy and rooted in nursery beds with bottom heat. Transplanting to field positions occurs in spring at spacings of 1–2 m within rows and 2–3 m between rows.

The shrub begins bearing fruit in the second or third year. Maximum productivity is reached at 5–7 years, with well-maintained plants remaining productive for 20 or more years.

Fruit is harvested from early summer through autumn. Ripe berries are hand-picked or shaken onto catch frames, then dried in the shade or in mechanical dryers at temperatures below 45 degrees Celsius.

Yields of dried fruit range from 1000 to 2500 kg per hectare depending on cultivar, plant density, and management practices. Pruning is performed annually to maintain plant shape and maximize fruit-bearing surface area.

## Bioactive Compounds

Lycium chinense fruit contains a complex mixture of polysaccharides, carotenoids, flavonoids, and phenolic acids. Lycium barbarum polysaccharides (LBPs), structurally similar to those in L. barbarum, are the primary bioactive fraction.

Carotenoid content is dominated by zeaxanthin and its dipalmitate ester. Zeaxanthin concentration ranges from 50 to 200 mg per 100 g dry fruit weight, making Lycium fruit one of the richest dietary sources of this macular pigment.

Flavonoid compounds include quercetin, kaempferol, and rutin glycosides. These contribute to the antioxidant capacity measured by in vitro radical scavenging assays.

The root bark contains kukoamine A, a unique alkaloid with documented antihypertensive and antipyretic activity. Additional root constituents include betaine, atropine, and various phenolic amides.

Leaf material contains additional flavonoids, chlorogenic acid, and small amounts of tropane alkaloids. Leaves are consumed as a vegetable in some Chinese regional cuisines and are used in traditional medicine as a tonic.
