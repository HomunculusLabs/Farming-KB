## David L. Hawksworth

## Description

David Leslie Hawksworth is a prominent British mycologist and lichenologist known for his influential estimates of global fungal diversity. His 1991 and 2001 publications on the magnitude of [[fungal-biodiversity]] are among the most widely cited works in [[mycology]], establishing the commonly referenced figure of 1.5 to 3 million fungal species worldwide. Hawksworth's estimates fundamentally shaped how scientists and policymakers understand the scale of fungal diversity and the vast proportion of fungi that remain undiscovered.

## Classification

- **Profession**: Mycologist, lichenologist, systematic biologist
- **Affiliation**: International Mycological Institute (IMI), Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew; Natural History Museum, London
- **Key contributions**: Global fungal diversity estimation, [[fungal-taxonomy]], [[lichen]] systematics, biodiversity conservation
- **Notable publications**: Hawksworth (1991) Mycol. Res. 95:641–655; Hawksworth (2001) Mycol. Res. 105:1422–1432

## Key Facts

- In 1991, Hawksworth estimated that there may be as many as 3 million species of fungi on Earth, a figure derived by extrapolating from the known fungal flora of the British Isles and ratios of fungi to plants and insects.
- By 2001, he revised this to a more conservative estimate of 1.5 million species, though higher estimates remain plausible when considering unknown insect-associated fungi.
- Hawksworth calculated that only about 4.6% of the fungi that could exist have been described, highlighting the enormous gap in mycological knowledge.
- His estimation methodology used multiple independent approaches: (A) British Isles fungal diversity, (B) U.S. plants and plant products, (C) Biological flora of the British Isles, (D) alpine sedge community ratios, with the mean of these producing approximately 1.26 million species.
- Additional estimates incorporating unstudied substrates and the assumption of 30 million insect species yielded figures up to 3 million.
- His work directly inspired the "Where are the missing fungi?" research initiative championed by Hyde (2001a,b), triggering global surveys to discover undescribed fungal species.
- Hawksworth is also recognized for his contributions to lichen taxonomy and the development of fungal conservation policy.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Hawksworth's diversity estimates underscore the vast untapped potential of fungi for biotechnology, pharmaceuticals, agriculture, and cultivation.
- The fact that over 95% of fungal species remain undescribed suggests that many beneficial fungal species for cultivation, biocontrol, and soil management have yet to be discovered.
- His work provides the scientific basis for fungal biodiversity conservation, which directly impacts the preservation of mycorrhizal and [[saprotrophic-fungi]] essential for sustainable cultivation.
- Understanding the scale of fungal diversity motivates continued exploration of fungal communities in cultivation substrates, compost, and soil ecosystems.
- The gap between described and estimated fungal species highlights the need for molecular identification tools in applied mycology and cultivation research.
- Hawksworth's estimates justify investment in fungal culture collections and ex situ conservation of fungal genetic resources.
