# Pinus taeda

## Overview

**Pinus taeda**, the loblolly pine, is the most commercially important timber
species in the southeastern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]].

It is the most widely planted pine in the world, with over 13 million hectares
under cultivation across the American South.

Loblolly pine dominates the "pine belt" from east Texas to southern New Jersey
and is the principal species of the southern yellow pine group.

Its rapid growth, site adaptability, and compatibility with intensive
silviculture have made it the backbone of the southern U.S. forest products
industry.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Pinophyta
- **Class:** Pinopsida
- **Order:** Pinales
- **Family:** Pinaceae
- **Genus:** Pinus
- **Subgenus:** Pinus (Diploxylon / hard pines)
- **Species:** *Pinus taeda* L.

Described by Linnaeus in 1753, the epithet "taeda" refers to resinous wood
used for torches in antiquity.

The species is closely related to shortleaf and longleaf pine within subsection
*Australes*, and natural hybridization with shortleaf pine produces *Pinus ×
sondereggeri*.

## Morphology

Loblolly pine is a large evergreen conifer reaching 30–37 m in height and
0.6–1.2 m DBH at maturity.

Exceptional specimens on favorable sites can exceed 45 m in height.

The crown is broadly conical in youth, becoming rounded and open with age.

Bark is thick, dark reddish-brown, divided into broad irregular scaly plates
separated by shallow fissures.

Needles occur in fascicles of three, 15–25 cm long and 1.0–1.5 mm wide,
persisting 2–3 years before shedding.

They are slender, yellow-green to dark green, with finely serrulate margins
and two resin canals.

Cones are ovoid to cylindrical, 7–15 cm long and 3–5 cm wide when open,
with sharply pointed reflexed apophyses.

Seed cones mature in the second autumn after pollination; seeds are 5–6 mm
long with a 15–25 mm wing for wind dispersal.

The root system is moderately deep and widespreading, with well-developed
lateral roots contributing to windfirmness.

## Distribution and Habitat

Loblolly pine is native to the southeastern United States, ranging from
southern New Jersey and central Pennsylvania south to northern Florida and
west to eastern Texas.

It is most abundant on the Atlantic and Gulf Coastal Plains and in the
Piedmont, typically below 600 m elevation.

The species occupies a broad range of conditions, from poorly drained
flatwoods and bottomlands to well-drained upland ridges.

Optimal growth occurs on deep, moist, acidic Ultisol and Alfisol soils with
good drainage and moderate fertility.

Loblolly pine is moderately shade-intolerant, requiring full sunlight for
optimal growth and regeneration.

It is an early- to mid-successional species, commonly establishing after
agricultural abandonment or disturbance.

## Ecological Role

Loblolly pine provides critical habitat for numerous wildlife species
throughout its range.

Mature stands support the endangered red-cockaded woodpecker, which requires
living pines for cavity excavation.

Seeds are an important food source for bobwhite quail, wild turkey, fox
squirrels, and various songbirds.

The species provides significant cover and thermal refuge for white-tailed
deer and supports diverse avian communities including pine warblers and
brown-headed nuthatches.

Intensively managed stands accumulate 2–6 tonnes of carbon per hectare per
year, making loblolly pine a significant carbon sink in the southeastern U.S.

## Uses and Forestry

Loblolly pine is the single most important timber species in the United States
by volume harvested.

It provides southern yellow pine lumber used in construction framing, plywood,
oriented strand board, and pulpwood.

The wood is moderately heavy (specific gravity ~0.51), relatively strong, and
accepts pressure treatment readily.

Annual harvest volumes exceed 300 million cubic meters, making it the dominant
species for structural lumber production in the U.S.

Intensive silviculture includes site preparation, genetic improvement,
fertilization, competition control, and thinning.

Pulpwood rotations are 15–20 years; sawtimber rotations span 25–35 years.

Genetically improved varieties from breeding programs exhibit 15–25% volume
gains over unimproved stock.
