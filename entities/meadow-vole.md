# Meadow Vole

## Description

The meadow vole (*Microtus pennsylvanicus*) is a small rodent pest that poses significant challenges in winter greenhouse vegetable production. In *The Winter Harvest Handbook*, Coleman discusses meadow voles at pp. 175-178 as one of the most persistent and damaging pests in unheated and minimally heated [[mobile-greenhouse]] structures. Their presence in winter greenhouses represents a unique pest management challenge specific to protected winter cropping.

## Classification

- **Category**: Animal pest
- **Domain**: Greenhouse and field crop protection
- **Species**: *Microtus pennsylvanicus* (meadow vole / field mouse)
- **Order**: Rodentia
- **Family**: Cricetidae
- **Size**: 5-7 inches total length (including 1.5-2.5 inch tail)
- **Weight**: 1-2 ounces

## Key Facts

- Small, stocky rodents with short ears, short legs, and dense brown or gray-brown fur
- Active year-round, including under snow cover and inside greenhouses during winter
- Do not hibernate — remain active and feeding through the coldest months
- Primarily herbivorous — feed on roots, stems, seeds, bulbs, and above-ground vegetation
- Population cycles can cause dramatic damage spikes every 3-5 years
- Females can produce 5-10 litters per year with 3-6 young per litter
- In greenhouses, voles are attracted to the protected, warm environment and abundant food
- They feed on winter crops including lettuce, spinach, mache, claytonia, and other greens
- Vole damage in [[mobile-greenhouses]] is noted as a specific problem when greenhouses are moved to fresh ground (p. 176)

## Behavior in Greenhouse Environments

Meadow voles become especially problematic in winter greenhouse production for several reasons:

- **Shelter**: Greenhouses provide habitat free from predators and harsh winter weather
- **Food**: Dense, low-growing winter crops (mache, spinach, claytonia) provide ideal foraging
- **Cover**: Crop canopy creates protected runways and tunnels invisible to predators
- **Soil access**: Bare soil and loose potting media facilitate burrow construction
- **Concentration**: The enclosed space concentrates vole activity in a small area

Specific damaging behaviors include:

- Creating runways under crop canopy and tunneling through soil
- Girdling root crops (carrots, beets, turnips) by feeding on roots
- Destroying seedling stands by consuming seeds and young plants
- Contaminating harvest-ready crops with droppings and urine
- Undermining root systems of established plants through burrowing

## Organic Control Methods

In organic systems, vole control must avoid rodenticides and emphasizes prevention and physical methods:

### Prevention

- **Habitat management**: Remove crop debris and tall vegetation around greenhouse perimeters (pp. 139-140)
- **Mowing**: Keep grass short around greenhouse edges to eliminate cover
- **Gravel perimeter**: Create a 3-foot gravel border around greenhouses that voles avoid crossing
- **Crop rotation**: Moving mobile greenhouses disrupts established vole territories

### Active Control

- **Trapping**: Snap traps placed along vole runways and near burrow entrances, baited with peanut butter or apple slices
- **Encouraging predators**: Installing owl boxes, raptor perches, and habitat for snakes, foxes, and weasels
- **Exclusion**: Hardware cloth barriers (1/4 inch mesh) buried 6-12 inches at greenhouse edges
- **Repellents**: Castor oil-based repellents applied to soil around greenhouse perimeters
- **Cultural practices**: Prompt harvest and removal of spent crops to reduce food and cover

### Monitoring

- Regular inspection of crop rows for vole runways and damage patterns
- Use of apple slices or carrot pieces as monitoring baits to detect vole presence before damage occurs
- Tracking population trends to anticipate cyclical outbreaks

## Relevance to Cultivation

Meadow voles represent a unique challenge specific to winter greenhouse production that conventional field growers rarely face at the same intensity. The warm, protected greenhouse environment creates an artificial habitat that concentrates vole populations during the season when natural food sources are scarce and predators are less active. Understanding vole biology and behavior is essential for organic growers using unheated greenhouse systems, as failure to manage vole populations can result in significant crop losses during the high-value winter production season.
