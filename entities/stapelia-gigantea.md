# Stapelia gigantea

## Overview

Stapelia gigantea is a stem-succulent plant in the family Apocynaceae, subfamily Asclepiadoideae, native to southern Africa. It is renowned for producing the largest flowers of any succulent species, with blooms reaching thirty-five centimeters in diameter.

The species is commonly called the giant carrion flower or toad plant due to the strong odor of rotting meat emitted by its flowers to attract fly pollinators. Despite the unpleasant scent, Stapelia gigantea is widely cultivated as a curiosity ornamental for its extraordinary floral dimensions and starfish-shaped blooms.

Flower coloration is pale yellow with transverse maroon or reddish-brown bands and a densely pubescent corolla surface that mimics the texture of decaying flesh. The hairs trap heat and concentrate volatile scent compounds, enhancing the deception of visiting flies.

The genus Stapelia comprises approximately fifty species of stem succulents distributed across southern and eastern Africa. The genus name honors Johannes Bodaeus van Stapel, a seventeenth-century Dutch physician and botanist who studied exotic plants.

Several Stapelia species have been reassigned to other genera including Orbea and Huernia as taxonomic understanding has improved. Stapelia gigantea retains its original placement due to its distinct pollinarium morphology and molecular phylogenetic position.

## Taxonomy

Stapelia gigantea N.E.Br. was described by Nicholas Edward Brown in 1877 based on specimens collected in the northern Cape Province of South Africa.

Classification places it in the order Gentianales, family Apocynaceae, subfamily Asclepiadoideae, tribe Ceropegieae, subtribe Stapeliinae, genus Stapelia. Molecular phylogenetic studies by Meve in 2002 confirmed the monophyly of the genus and clarified relationships among Stapelia and closely related genera.

The species has no recognized subspecies or varieties, making it morphologically uniform throughout its range. It is closely related to Stapelia grandiflora and Stapelia kwebensis, with which it shares the large-flowered growth form.

Stapelia grandiflora produces slightly smaller flowers with more pronounced coloration and a stronger scent. The two species can be distinguished by corolla lobe shape: Stapelia gigantea has acuminate tips while Stapelia grandiflora has rounded or emarginate tips.

Hybridization with other Stapelia species occurs readily in cultivation. The hybrid Stapelia gigantea crossed with Stapelia leendertziae produces intermediate flower sizes with unusual color patterns sought by succulent collectors.

## Morphology

Stapelia gigantea forms clumps of four-angled, upright stems measuring fifteen to thirty centimeters tall and two to three centimeters in diameter. Stems are green, glaucous, and bear small, vestigial leaf rudiments along the angles that are quickly shed.

Stem surfaces are smooth with slightly tuberculate angles and marked by short, rigid hairs. The stems branch freely from the base, forming dense cushions that may cover fifty to one hundred centimeters in diameter after several years.

The root system is shallow and fibrous, adapted to absorb brief rainfall in [[jadam-ambient-temperature-principle]], increasing volatile dispersion.

The gynostegium is a complex structure typical of Asclepiadoideae, comprising fused stamens and stigma forming a pentagonal pollinarium apparatus. Pollinia are transferred as paired units (pollinia masses) by visiting flies.

## Distribution and Habitat

Stapelia gigantea is native to South Africa, Botswana, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Tanzania, and Malawi. It occurs in arid and semi-arid regions including the Kalahari Desert fringe, dry savanna, and rocky outcrops at elevations from three hundred to eighteen hundred meters.

The species has been introduced to cultivation worldwide and has occasionally naturalized [[c4-and-cam-photosynthesis]].

Frost tolerance is minimal, with tissue damage occurring below five degrees Celsius. Plants in cultivation require protection from freezing temperatures and prolonged exposure to cool, damp conditions that promote fungal stem rot.

In its native habitat, Stapelia gigantea frequently grows in the partial shade of xerophytic shrubs including Commiphora species and Boscia albitrunca. This microhabitat association provides insulation from extreme temperature fluctuations and reduces evaporative water loss from the stem surfaces.

## Cultivation

Stapelia gigantea is propagated from stem cuttings taken during the active growing period in spring and summer. Cuttings of five to ten centimeters are allowed to callus for three to five days before placement on dry sand to root.

Rooting typically occurs within two to four weeks. Established cuttings should not be watered until roots are visibly emerging, as premature watering causes rapid [[decomposition]] of the callus tissue.

Hormone rooting powders are generally unnecessary for Stapelia propagation and may in some cases promote fungal contamination of the cut surface. The natural wound response produces adequate auxin concentrations for root initiation.

Container cultivation requires a very porous substrate composed of coarse sand, perlite, and gravel in approximately equal proportions. Standard cactus or succulent mixes are often too water-retentive for stapeliads without additional amendments.

Watering follows a wet-dry cycle: thorough saturation followed by complete substrate drying. During winter dormancy, plants receive only occasional misting and are kept at temperatures above ten degrees Celsius.

The primary cultivation challenge is stem rot caused by the fungus Fusarium and various bacterial pathogens. Infected stems become dark and mushy at the base and must be removed immediately to prevent spread to adjacent stems. Well-ventilated growing conditions and minimal overhead watering reduce rot incidence.

Flowering occurs from late summer through autumn on two-to-three-year-old plants. Individual flowers open sequentially and last five to seven days before senescing.
