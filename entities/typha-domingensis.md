## Overview

**Typha domingensis**, commonly known as **southern cattail** or **cumbungi**, is a perennial aquatic plant in the family Typhaceae, widely distributed across tropical, subtropical, and warm [[mollison-permaculture-two-arid-water-harvesting-and-erosion-control]].

The species is closely related to and frequently hybridizes with *[[typha-latifolia]]* (broadleaf cattail), producing the introgressive hybrid *T. × glauca*.

In many regions, *T. domingensis* is considered an invasive species due to its aggressive clonal growth that can dominate and homogenize wetland plant communities.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Division Tracheophyta → Class Liliopsida → Order Poales → Family Typhaceae → Genus *Typha* → *T. domingensis*.

**Authority:** (Pers.) Poir. ex Steud. (1841).

**Basionym:** *Typha angustifolia* var. *domingensis* Pers. (1805).

**Stems (culms):** Erect, cylindrical, 1–3 m tall, 0.5–1.5 cm in diameter, pale green, pith-filled.

**Leaves:** Linear, flat to slightly V-shaped in cross-section, 0.8–1.5 cm wide, shorter than or equal to the stem height.

A key distinguishing feature from *T. latifolia* is the gap of 2–10 cm between the male and female inflorescence spikes.

**Male inflorescence:** Upper spike, 5–15 cm long, brown to reddish-brown, borne directly above the female spike with a visible bare internode.

**Female inflorescence:** Cylindrical, 10–25 cm long, 1–1.5 cm in diameter, dark chocolate-brown, densely packed with tiny pistillate flowers.

The familiar "cattail" appearance results from the female spike, which disintegrates into a cottony mass of wind-dispersed seeds in autumn.

**Rhizomes:** Robust, creeping, 2–5 cm in diameter, producing extensive clonal colonies that can cover hundreds of square meters.

**Roots:** Fibrous, arising from rhizome nodes, extending 30–60 cm into the substrate.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Typha domingensis* has a pantropical and subtropical distribution, occurring across the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Australia.

In North America, its range extends from the southern United States through Mexico, [[mollison-designers-wastewater-treatment-wetlands]].

The species is highly competitive in nutrient-enriched waters, often forming dense monospecific stands that exclude other wetland vegetation.

## Ecological Role

**Habitat provision:** Dense *T. domingensis* stands provide nesting habitat, cover, and foraging grounds for waterfowl, wading birds, amphibians, and fish.

The rhizome matrix stabilizes sediments and reduces shoreline erosion by absorbing wave energy.

**Water filtration:** The species is exceptionally effective at removing nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus), heavy metals, and organic pollutants from water.

Root-zone [[lichens-primary-production-soil-formation-dighton]]:** *T. domingensis* is one of the most productive wetland plants, with above-ground biomass yields of 2–6 tonnes per hectare per year.

**Invasive dynamics:** Monospecific *Typha* stands reduce plant diversity by 50–80% compared to mixed-species wetlands, altering invertebrate and avian [[edaphic-determinants-cannabis-microbiome-community-structure]].

The species expands rapidly via clonal rhizome growth, with lateral spread rates of 1–3 meters per year under favorable conditions.

## Ethnobotanical and Practical Uses

**Food:** Multiple plant parts are edible. Young shoots (0–30 cm tall) can be harvested in spring and eaten raw or cooked, with a flavor resembling asparagus or cucumber.
