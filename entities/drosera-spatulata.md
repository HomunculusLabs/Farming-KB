## Cultivation

D. spatulata is considered one of the easiest carnivorous plants to grow. It is widely recommended for beginners alongside D. capensis.

**Substrate:** A mix of sphagnum peat and perlite or silica sand (1:1 ratio) works well. Pure long-fiber sphagnum moss is also effective. The substrate must be kept consistently moist.

**Water:** Use distilled, rain, or reverse-osmosis water. Tap water contains minerals toxic to most carnivorous plants over time.

**Light:** Full sun to bright partial shade produces the widest laminae and best coloration. Under insufficient light, leaves become elongated and green without red pigmentation.

**Temperature:** The species tolerates a wide range from approximately 5°C to 35°C. Tropical lowland forms do not require dormancy, though temperate populations may slow growth in winter.

**Propagation:** Leaf cuttings, gemmae, and seed all work effectively. The species flowers prolifically and sets seed readily through self-pollination.

The Japanese form tends to produce broader laminae with more pronounced red coloration. Australian populations often display narrower petioles and a more [[fukuoka-ideal-rice-plant-morphology-compact-growth-high-yield]] habit.

Tropical Southeast Asian forms typically grow year-round without dormancy. Temperate populations from Japan and southern Australia may produce a winter resting bud (hibernaculum) in colder conditions.

The species hybridizes naturally with several congeners where ranges overlap. Hybrids with D. rotundifolia have been documented in Japan and southeastern Australia.

## Ecological Role

In its native habitats, D. spatulata contributes to local insect population control. It captures small flying insects including gnats, midges, and winged ants.

The species serves as a food source for specialized herbivores in some regions. Caterpillars of certain moths feed on the leaves and flowers despite the mucilage.
