The specific epithet *caerulea* means "blue," referring to the characteristic sepals. Common names include Colorado columbine, Rocky Mountain columbine, and blue columbine.

The species was first collected by Edwin P. James during the Stephen Long Expedition of 1820. It was later formally described and named in honor of its discoverer's contributions to western American botany.

The growing season is short, typically 10–14 weeks between snowmelt and first frost. Plants complete their reproductive cycle rapidly, producing seed by mid-August at higher elevations.

## Cultivation

*Aquilegia caerulea* is widely cultivated as an ornamental perennial in temperate gardens. It is hardy in USDA zones 3–7 and performs best in partial shade with consistent moisture.

Plants prefer rich, well-drained loam but tolerate clay if drainage is adequate. Propagation is primarily by seed, which requires cold stratification at 4°C for 3–4 weeks before germination.

Seeds should be surface-sown, as they require light to germinate reliably. Germination rates are typically 60–80% after stratification, with seedlings appearing in 3–4 weeks at 18–21°C.

Plants flower in late spring to early summer (May–July), producing blooms over 3–4 weeks. They are relatively short-lived perennials (3–5 years) but self-seed freely in favorable conditions.

Deadheading prolongs bloom but prevents desirable self-seeding in naturalized plantings. Division is difficult due to the plant's deep taproot and delicate fibrous roots.

Transplanting is best done in early spring before new growth emerges from the crown. Container-grown plants transplant more reliably than bare-root specimens.

Common pests include columbine sawfly (*Pristiphora rufipes*) and leaf miners that tunnel through foliage. [[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]] and crown rot can affect plants in humid conditions with poor air circulation.

## Ecological Role

The long nectar spurs of *Aquilegia caerulea* are adapted to pollination by hummingbirds and long-tongued hawkmoths (Sphingidae). In Colorado, broad-tailed hummingbirds (*Selasphorus platycercus*) are primary pollinators.

The genus *Aquilegia* is a model system for studying floral evolution and pollinator-driven speciation. Spur length variation within populations correlates with local pollinator assemblages and gene flow patterns.
