# Inocybe corydalina

**Inocybe corydalina** is a little-known species of mushroom in the family Inocybaceae that has been reported to contain [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]]. It is one of the rare examples of a psilocybin-producing species outside the traditional genera Psilocybe, Conocybe, Pluteus, and Panaeolus. The genus Inocybe is far better known for containing deadly poisonous species, making *I. corydalina* an especially dangerous mushroom for foragers who might confuse it with other Inocybe species.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Inocybaceae
- **Genus:** Inocybe
- **Section:** Inocybe

The genus Inocybe contains hundreds of species, the vast majority of which are mycorrhizal associates of trees and many of which contain dangerous levels of muscarine. The discovery that at least one species (*I. aeruginascens*) and possibly *I. corydalina* contain psilocybin is an exception rather than the rule within this genus. The taxonomy of *I. corydalina* and its close relatives has been complicated by morphological variability and the need for microscopic examination for reliable identification.

## Description

### Cap (Pileus)
The cap is small to medium, ranging from 15 to 40 mm in diameter. It is convex to bell-shaped when young, becoming broadly convex or nearly plane with age. The surface is dry and covered with fine radial fibrils or a silky-fibrillose texture, giving it a characteristic silky appearance. The color is variable, ranging from grayish-brown to olive-brown or dark brown. The cap margin is often incurved in young specimens and may become irregular with age.

### Gills (Lamellae)
The gills are adnexed to sinuate, moderately close, and initially pale gray before becoming grayish-brown with maturity. The gill edges are often finely fringed or crenulate, a characteristic feature of many Inocybe species. Reports of blue bruising on the gills are inconsistent, which is a significant concern for field identification.

### Stem (Stipe)
The stipe is cylindrical, solid or slightly stuffed, and measures 30 to 70 mm in length and 2 to 5 mm in thickness. It is whitish to pale brown, often with a slightly bulbous base. The surface may be pruinose near the apex. Blue bruising, when present, may appear at the base or on damaged areas, but this reaction is reportedly unreliable.

### Spores
Spores are smooth, bean-shaped (phaseoliform) to elliptical, a typical spore morphology for the genus Inocybe. They are brownish in deposit and measure approximately 8 to 11 µm by 4 to 6 µm. The presence of pleurocystidia and cheilocystidia with distinctive shapes is important for microscopic identification.

### Odor
The odor is often described as spermatic or unpleasant, a common trait among Inocybe species. This can serve as a warning characteristic, as many poisonous Inocybe species share this trait.

## Distribution and Habitat

inocybe corydalina is found in Europe and possibly in North America. It is a mycorrhizal species, forming associations with hardwood and coniferous trees. It grows singly or in small groups on the ground in forests, woodlands, and parks, particularly in association with oaks, beeches, and other hardwoods.

Fruiting occurs from summer through autumn. The species is considered uncommon to rare and is not frequently encountered even by experienced mycologists.

## Chemistry

The reported presence of [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]] in *I. corydalina* has been documented in the mycological literature, though quantitative analyses are limited. The alkaloid content is reported to be low compared to typical Psilocybe species. Importantly, the species may also contain muscarine, a toxic alkaloid found in many Inocybe species, which could cause cholinergic poisoning symptoms including sweating, salivation, lacrimation, and gastrointestinal distress.

## Safety Warning

This species should be considered **extremely dangerous to handle or consume**. The genus Inocybe contains numerous species that are lethally toxic due to muscarine content. Accurate identification requires microscopic examination by an expert mycologist. Even if *I. corydalina* contains psilocybin, the simultaneous presence of muscarine makes consumption hazardous. Confusion with deadly Inocybe species is a very real risk.

## Similar Species

- inocybe patouillardii — contains muscarine, potentially fatal
- [[inocybe-geophylla]] — common, contains muscarine, deadly
- Other Inocybe species — most are toxic, making the genus one of the most dangerous for amateur foragers

## Legal Status

As a species reported to contain psilocybin, it may be regulated in jurisdictions where psilocybin is controlled. However, the extreme rarity and danger of this species make it unlikely to be encountered or targeted.

## Seasonality

*I. corydalina* fruits from summer through autumn (July to October in the Northern Hemisphere), with peak fruiting during warm, wet periods. It is typically found during general forays for mycorrhizal mushrooms and is most likely to appear in mature forests with consistent soil moisture. Its fruiting pattern is similar to other mycorrhizal Inocybe species, which tend to produce single or small clusters of basidiocarps rather than large troops.
