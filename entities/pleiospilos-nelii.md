## Overview

**Pleiospilos nelii** Schwantes ex H.Jacobsen is a dwarf succulent native to the Eastern Cape of South Africa. It is commonly called the **split rock** or **mock gravel** for its remarkable resemblance to the stones among which it grows.

The species is a classic example of **mimicry in plants**, where the body coloration, texture, and shape closely match the surrounding quartzite gravel. This camouflage protects against herbivory by blending into the arid landscape.

Pleiospilos nelii produces large, **daisy-like yellow or orange flowers** up to 6 cm across — disproportionately large relative to the plant body, which typically measures only 5–8 cm in diameter. The flowers open in the afternoon and close at dusk over a period of several days.

The plant is popular in succulent collections worldwide and has received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit. It is one of the most commonly cultivated members of the Aizoaceae (ice plant family).

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Pleiospilos nelii belongs to the family **Aizoaceae** (ice plant family), subfamily Ruschioideae, tribe Ruschieae. The genus *Pleiospilos* contains approximately 35 species, all endemic to arid regions of South Africa.

The species was described by Gustav Schwantes and later validly published by Hermann Jacobsen. It is closely related to *Pleiospilos bolusii*, which has a more angular body shape and white to yellowish flowers.

The plant body consists of **one to three pairs of thick, hemispherical leaves** arranged oppositely. Each leaf pair is separated by a deep fissure from which the flower and new leaf pair emerge.

Leaf surfaces are **grey-green to brownish-green** with numerous dark green dots (idioblasts) that give a speckled, stone-like appearance. The dots are tannin-containing cells that may aid in UV protection.

A new leaf pair is produced **annually from the center fissure**, with the old pair gradually drying and shriveling as the new pair expands. This cycle typically occurs during the winter to spring transition.

The root system is **short and fibrous**, adapted to absorbing moisture from brief rainfall events in shallow soil pockets.

## Distribution and Habitat

Pleiospilos nelii is endemic to the **Eastern Cape Province** of South Africa, occurring in a limited area around Graaff-Reinet and the Camdeboo region. It is a karoo-desert specialist found at elevations of 600–1,200 m.

The habitat consists of **flat to gently sloping quartzite gravel plains** in the Nama-Karoo biome. Plants grow partially buried in the gravel, with only the top surfaces of the leaves exposed above ground level.

The climate is semi-arid with **summer rainfall** (200–350 mm annually) and very dry winters. Summer temperatures regularly exceed 35°C, while winter nights can drop below freezing.

The quartzite gravel substrate provides **high light reflectance** that moderates soil temperatures and creates the visual backdrop against which the plant's camouflage is effective.

Associated succulent species include *Lithops* species, *Conophytum* species, and various *Crassula* and *Stapelia* species in these rocky karoo communities.

The region is **seismically stable** and has been geologically unchanged for millions of years, allowing the evolution of the highly specialized mimicry strategies seen in Pleiospilos and related genera.

## Cultivation

Pleiospilos nelii is cultivated as a **greenhouse or windowsill specimen** in temperate regions, and as an outdoor rock garden plant in Mediterranean and desert climates (USDA Zone 10–11).

The most critical cultivation requirement is **excellent drainage**. A mineral-based substrate of pumice, grit, and a small proportion of loam (90:10 ratio) provides ideal conditions. Standard potting soil is fatal.

Watering must follow a **strict seasonal schedule**. The plant receives water primarily in late summer through autumn (its active growing period). Water should be withheld entirely during the leaf-changing period in spring, as the old leaves must dry completely before new ones emerge.

Full sun to **very bright indirect light** is essential for maintaining the compact, stone-like body shape. Etiolated (stretched) growth indicates insufficient light and is difficult to reverse.

Temperature requirements are **moderate** — the plant prefers 20–30°C during the growing season and can tolerate brief drops to 5°C in winter if kept completely dry.

Overwatering is the **primary cause of death** in cultivation, leading to rapid rotting. Many experienced growers water only 4–6 times per year. The plant's thick leaves store sufficient moisture to survive months between waterings.

## Ecology and Adaptations

The extraordinary **stone mimicry** of Pleiospilos nelii serves as primary defense against herbivory. Tortoises, ostriches, and small mammals that graze on succulent vegetation are unable to distinguish the plant from surrounding gravel.

The species possesses **CAM (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism)** photosynthesis, opening stomata at night to absorb carbon dioxide and minimizing water loss during hot daytime hours. This adaptation is shared with many desert succulents.

Flowers are **pollinated by small bees and flies** attracted to the bright yellow-orange petals and sweet nectar. The large flower size relative to the plant body represents a significant reproductive investment.

Seed capsules are **hygrochastic**, opening only when wetted by rain. This ensures seeds are released only when conditions are favorable for germination, a critical adaptation in the unpredictable karoo rainfall regime.

The plant's **subterranean growth habit** (partially buried in gravel) keeps the root zone cooler and reduces evaporative water loss from the leaf surfaces.

Unlike many succulents, Pleiospilos nelii produces **no spines, no toxic sap**, and no chemical deterrents. Its entire defense strategy relies on visual crypsis (mimicry) and its cryptic growth habit.

## Horticultural Significance

Pleiospilos nelii is a **staple of specialist succulent collections** and is frequently sold in garden centers as a novelty plant. Its compact size and unusual appearance make it popular for dish gardens and miniature landscapes.

The species is relatively **easy to grow** compared to other mimicry succulents such as *Lithops* and *Conophytum*, making it a good introduction to the "mesemb" group for beginners.

The large, showy flowers are the **primary ornamental feature**, appearing in late winter to early spring in cultivation (Northern Hemisphere). Flower color varies from deep golden-yellow to salmon-orange.

Specimens can live **10–15 years** in cultivation with proper care, gradually forming small clusters of multiple leaf pairs over time. The slow growth rate adds to their value as collector plants.

The species is not considered **threatened in the wild**, as its remote, arid habitat offers some protection from human disturbance. However, illegal collection by succulent poachers has been documented in the Graaff-Reinet area.

Pleiospilos nelii is sometimes confused with *Lithops* species, but is easily distinguished by its **ribbed leaf margins** and larger individual leaf size. True *Lithops* have smooth, flat-topped leaf pairs.

The species is also related to *Titanopsis* and *Aloinopsis*, which share similar karoo habitats but differ in leaf texture and flower structure.

Prices for nursery-grown specimens remain **affordable at $5–15 USD**, making this one of the most accessible mimicry succulents for beginning collectors.

The species has been featured in numerous botanical illustrations and succulent publications. Its distinctive appearance makes it one of the most photographed mesembs in cultivation.

Propagation is primarily by seed, as the plant does not produce offsets. Seeds germinate in 5-14 days at 20-25 C on damp sandy substrate and require careful handling.

Seedlings are tiny and grow slowly, typically reaching flowering size in 3-4 years under optimal conditions. Patience is essential for this species.
