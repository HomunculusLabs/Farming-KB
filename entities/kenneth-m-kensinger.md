## Description

Kenneth M. Kensinger (M.A.) is an American anthropologist and
linguist who conducted extensive ethnographic and linguistic
research among the Cashinahua people of southeastern Peru.
His work provides the most detailed account of Banisteriopsis
(ayahuasca/[[nixi-pae]]) use among the Cashinahua, documenting
both the communal hallucinogenic experience and its role in
Cashinahua epistemology and medical practice.

## Classification

- **Profession:** Anthropologist, linguist
- **Institutional affiliation:** Bennington College, Vermont
  (anthropology faculty); formerly Lecturer in Anthropology
  at Columbia University
- **Affiliated organization:** Summer Institute of Linguistics
  (SIL), Peru
- **Research area:** Cashinahua ethnography, linguistics,
  hallucinogenic plant use

## Key Facts

### Fieldwork

Kensinger conducted multiple field trips to the Cashinahua
between July 1955 and August 1968, spending a cumulative
number of years in linguistic and ethnographic research. His
long-term engagement provided exceptional depth of
understanding of Cashinahua culture and hallucinogen use.

### Background with SIL

Kensinger was formerly a member of the Summer Institute of
Linguistics (SIL) in Peru. SIL is a missionary-sponsoring
organization focused on linguistic research and Bible
translation among indigenous peoples. Kensinger"s dual role
as linguist and anthropologist gave him exceptional access
to and documentation of Cashinahua language and culture.

### Research on Cashinahua Hallucinogen Use

Kensinger's contribution to *[[hallucinogens-and-shamanism]]*
is the definitive account of *nixi pae* (ayahuasca) use
among the Cashinahua. His research documented:

- The communal nature of Cashinahua ayahuasca sessions
- Recurring vision themes (snakes, jaguars, spirits, trees,
  lakes, villages, traders, gardens)
- The relationship between hallucinatory information and
  practical decision-making
- The Cashinahua five-spirit cosmological system
- The division between herbalist and shaman medical
  specialists
- The role of *[[muka-dau]]* (bitter medicine) in shamanic
  healing
- The use of ayahuasca as a last-resort diagnostic tool

### Chemical Research Collaboration

Kensinger collaborated with Der Marderosian, Chao, and
Goldstein on chemical analysis of Cashinahua hallucinogenic
preparations. Their 1970 paper in Drug Dependence identified:
- Harmine and harmaline as active agents in the *Banisteriopsis*
- Dimethyltryptamine (DMT) as the active agent in the
  *Psychotria* (*nai kawa*) admixture

### Notable Documentation

Kensinger recorded striking instances of apparent paranormal
cognition during ayahuasca sessions:
- Informants who had never visited Pucallpa described the
  town in detail sufficient for Kensinger to recognize
  specific shops
- Six of nine men at one session independently reported
  seeing the death of Kensinger's grandfather—confirmed by
  radio two days later

### Conference Presentation

An earlier version of his Cashinahua paper was presented at
the symposium "Hallucinogens and Shamanism" at the annual
meeting of the American Anthropological Association in
Seattle, November 1968.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Kensinger"s work provides one of the most detailed
ethnographic accounts of traditional [[ayahuasca-preparation]]
and use, including precise botanical ingredients (*Banisteriopsis*
+ *[[psychotria-viridis]]*), preparation methods, dosage (1–2 pints),
timing, and communal setting. His documentation that the
Cashinahua find the experience deeply unpleasant yet endure
it for practical revelatory purposes is an important corrective
to romanticized views of traditional hallucinogen use.
