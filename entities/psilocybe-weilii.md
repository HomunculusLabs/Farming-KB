# Psilocybe weilii

**Psilocybe weilii** (nom. prov.) is a moderately potent psilocybin mushroom first reported from northern Georgia in the southeastern United States. It is a bluing species in the family Hymenogastraceae found fruiting in association with loblolly pine and sweetgum in red-clay soils. The species remains provisionally named and has not been formally validly published under the rules of fungal nomenclature.

The species is of interest to mycologists both for its psychoactive properties and for its ecological role in the piedmont forest ecosystem, where it contributes to the decomposition of organic matter and nutrient cycling within the pine-sweetgum association. Its restricted known range and specific habitat requirements make it one of the more geographically limited psilocybin mushrooms documented in North America. Its distinctive combination of habitat preference, microscopic characters, and geographic range set it apart from other members of the genus.

## Taxonomy

*Psilocybe weilii* was first reported by [[paul-stamets]] and colleagues following its discovery in northern Georgia after hurricane opal in 1995. The species epithet "weilii" honors Andrew Weil, the physician and advocate for integrative medicine who has written extensively on the cultural and therapeutic significance of psychoactive plants and fungi.

The designation *nomina provisoria* (nom. prov.) indicates that the taxon has not yet been formally and validly published under the [[mollison-designers-shelterbelt-economics-for-livestock-and-croplands]]. It therefore remains a provisional name awaiting formal typification and peer-reviewed publication. Until validly published, the name carries no standing in botanical or mycological nomenclature and should be regarded as provisional.

The species is placed within the genus [[psilocybe-cubensis]], section psilocybe-(section)|Psilocybe, which encompasses the bluing, psilocybin-containing species with dark spore prints and typically glutinous or viscid cap surfaces when moist. *P. weilii* represents the first confirmed report of a lignicolous, caerulescent (bluing) *Psilocybe* from the state of georgia-(u.s.-state)|Georgia, expanding the known diversity of psychoactive fungi in the American Southeast.

Molecular phylogenetic studies of the genus [[psilocybe-cubensis]] have reorganized many species in recent years, but *P. weilii* has not yet been included in published phylogenetic analyses due to its provisional status and limited availability of genetic material. Future work incorporating DNA sequencing may help clarify its relationships within the genus and determine whether it warrants formal recognition as a distinct species or may represent a previously described taxon.

## Description

### Cap (Pileus)

The cap measures 2 to 6 cm broad. When young it is campanulate to bluntly conic with an inrolled margin, soon expanding to broadly convex to nearly plane, becoming uplifted in age. The margin is often irregular and translucent-striate near the edge, which can split with age and become tough and opaque upon drying.

Coloration ranges from dark chestnut brown to deep olivaceous brown, typically with a distinctive blackish brown zone around the disc. The flesh is 3 to 4 mm thick at the center. The cap surface is viscid when moist, owing to a separable gelatinous pellicle that is a characteristic feature of the section *Psilocybe*. It is strongly hygrophanous, fading in drying to pallid brown or light brown. The flesh is whitish and bruises a conspicuous bluish color where damaged, an indicator of the presence of psilocybin and related compounds.

### Gills (Lamellae)

Gill attachment is adnate to sinuate, with two tiers of intermediate gills (lamellulae). They are close, even, and broad. When young they are light brown overall with pallid, floccose edges. At maturity the gills become dark chocolate brown as spores mature and deposit their pigment. The two-tiered lamellulae arrangement is a notable feature that aids in identification.

### Stem (Stipe)

The stem measures 25 to 70 mm long by 4 to 8 mm thick. It is equal or slightly swelling towards the base, which projects white rhizomorphs into the surrounding substrate. The stem surface is white, becoming dingy brown with age, and bruises bluish overall in age or from drying. It is covered with a well-developed sheath of whitish fibrillose patches below the partial veil zone and is pruinose (frosted with fine particles) above. The stem is cartilaginous and strigose, hollow and stuffed with a whitish pith.

### Partial Veil

The partial veil is cortinate in nature, forming a spider-web-like connection between the cap margin and the stem in young specimens. As the cap expands, the veil ruptures and leaves behind a fibrillose annular zone on the stem that is sometimes dusted with purplish violet brown spore deposits. This annular zone can serve as an aid in field identification.

### Microscopic Features

Spores are dark violet grayish black in deposit, a coloration typical of the genus [[psilocybe-cubensis]]. In side view they are subellipsoid, measuring 5.5 to 6.5 by 4 to 5 micrometers. In face view they appear subrhomboid to subellipsoid. The basidia are 4-spored, each bearing four sterigmata from which spores are produced.

Pleurocystidia are abundant on the gill faces, ranging from subfusoid to ventricose-rostrate with a short apex, or sublageniform, measuring 10.5 to 21.5 (up to 24) by 5 to 9.5 (up to 10.5) micrometers. Cheilocystidia are lageniform with short, single, or branched necks, measuring 20 to 37.5 by 5 to 6.5 micrometers. These cystidial features are important diagnostic characters for separating *P. weilii* from morphologically similar taxa within the genus. The combination of subellipsoid spores, abundant pleurocystidia, and lageniform cheilocystidia with branched necks provides a reliable microscopic profile for species-level identification.

## Habitat and Distribution

*Psilocybe weilii* grows gregariously to cespitose, and sometimes scattered, in red-clay soils topped with a thin layer of loblolly pine needles underneath sweetgum trees. This specific substrate association with pine needle litter over clay soils beneath sweetgum is a key ecological indicator for the species in the field and can help distinguish it from other southeastern Psilocybe species that favor different substrates or forest types.

The species appears to be closely associated with the piedmont region of the southeastern United States, where red-clay soils are prevalent and loblolly pine and sweetgum forests are common. The presence of a thin layer of decomposing pine needles over the clay appears to provide both the moisture retention and nutrient conditions necessary for fruiting.

It was first reported from southeastern cherokee-county,-georgia|Cherokee County in northern Georgia. The initial fruiting followed the passage of hurricane opal through the region in October 1995, suggesting that storm disturbance and heavy rainfall may play a significant role in triggering fruiting events for this species. The association with hurricane-driven rainfall patterns has been noted as a possible factor in its sporadic and sometimes abundant appearance following major autumn storms.

The species fruits from early September through November, corresponding to the autumn season in the southeastern United States. Fruiting occurs across a broad temperature range of 45 to 80 degrees Fahrenheit, with a preference for 60 to 75 degrees Fahrenheit. Its known range is restricted to the southeastern United States, primarily the piedmont and coastal plain regions of northern Georgia, though it may occur more broadly throughout the southeastern coastal plain where suitable habitat conditions of red-clay soil, pine needle litter, and sweetgum canopy overlap.

## Chemistry

*Psilocybe weilii* is described as moderately potent among the psilocybin mushrooms. Chemical analysis has detected the following psychoactive tryptamine alkaloids:

- [[psilocybin]] (the primary phosphorylated alkaloid and prodrug)
- [[psilocin]] (the dephosphorylated, pharmacologically active metabolite)
- [[baeocystin]] (a demethylated analog of psilocybin)

The presence of these compounds classifies *P. weilii* alongside other bluing [[psilocybe-cubensis]] species in containing the canonical tryptamine alkaloid profile. The bluing reaction upon bruising is a result of the oxidation of psilocin and related phenolic compounds, a reaction shared by most psilocybin-containing species in the genus. The specific concentrations of these alkaloids in *P. weilii* have not been extensively quantified in the published literature.

It is noted as the first report of a lignicolous, caerulescent *Psilocybe* from Georgia, expanding the known distribution of psilocybin-containing fungi in the American Southeast and highlighting the underexplored mycoflora of the region. Further analytical work using techniques such as high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) would help establish quantitative alkaloid profiles for the species.

## Legal Status

Like all psilocybin-containing mushrooms, *Psilocybe weilii* is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under the controlled substances act in the United States. This designation means that the species is considered to have a high potential for abuse and no currently accepted medical use. Possession, cultivation, and distribution of the species are illegal under federal law. The legal status extends to both fresh and dried specimens, as well as any material prepared from the mushroom containing its active alkaloids.

## Conservation

The conservation status of *Psilocybe weilii* has not been formally assessed by organizations such as the iucn red list due to its provisional taxonomic status. However, the species is potentially threatened by habitat loss in the rapidly developing piedmont region of northern Georgia. Urban and suburban expansion in the Atlanta metropolitan area has significantly reduced the acreage of mature pine and sweetgum forests growing on red-clay soils, which constitute the primary habitat for this species.

Additionally, the species' apparent dependence on storm-triggered fruiting events may make it particularly vulnerable to changes in precipitation patterns associated with climate change. Long-term monitoring of known populations and further survey work to document additional populations would be valuable for assessing the true range and conservation needs of this species.

## Discovery and Etymology

The species was discovered in northern Georgia in the autumn of 1995, shortly after hurricane opal made landfall and moved through the southeastern United States. The storm, a Category 4 hurricane at peak intensity, brought heavy rainfall and significant wind damage to northern Georgia. The storm's rains and wind disturbance appear to have stimulated the mushroom's fruiting, leading to its initial documentation by local foragers and mycologists.

The provisional epithet *weilii* honors Dr. [[andrew-weil]], a prominent physician, author, and proponent of integrative medicine. Dr. Weil has written extensively on the therapeutic and cultural significance of psychoactive plants and fungi, and several other bioactive species have been named in his honor. The species has not been formally published and therefore retains its provisional (*nomina provisoria*) status. Should a formal publication occur, it is possible that the name may be changed depending on the outcome of nomenclatural review and phylogenetic analysis.

## Similar Species

Several *Psilocybe* species may be confused with *P. weilii* due to overlapping morphological features and habitat preferences. Careful attention to macroscopic and microscopic characters is essential for accurate identification. Field foragers in the southeastern United States should exercise particular caution, as several toxic species of the genera Galerina and Inocybe may share similar forest-floor habitats and could be confused with small, brownish Psilocybe species.

- [[psilocybe-cubensis]]: A widely distributed subtropical species, but *P. cubensis* typically grows on bovine or equine dung and has a considerably larger stature with different habitat associations.
- psilocybe caerulipes entity the mushroom cultivator: Also found in eastern North America and known to fruit on decaying wood debris. *P. caerulipes* tends to be smaller in stature and fruits earlier in the season.
- psilocybe ovoideocystidiata: Found in the Ohio River Valley and eastern U.S.; it can be distinguished by its prominent ovoid cheilocystidia and different substrate preferences, often favoring riparian habitats with hardwood debris.
- [[psilocybe-cyanescens]]: A more robust species with a distinctly wavy cap margin at maturity, typically associated with woody debris in landscaped areas rather than pine needle litter over clay.

The combination of its northern Georgia distribution, association with loblolly pine needles over red-clay soil beneath sweetgum, and its specific microscopic features together distinguish *P. weilii* from its close relatives. Careful microscopic examination is recommended for definitive identification.
