## Rosmarinus officinalis

Rosmarinus officinalis (rosemary) is a woody, perennial evergreen herb in the family Lamiaceae, native to the Mediterranean basin. One of the most recognized and widely cultivated [[panax-ginseng]], and symbolic significance for over two millennia. The species is exceptionally drought-tolerant, long-lived (individual plants can survive 30+ years), and well-suited to permaculture systems as a permanent woody element providing year-round harvest, pollinator forage, and [[permaculture-designers-manual-essential-oil-plants]], dominated by 1,8-cineole, α-pinene, and camphor, has demonstrated antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, nootropic, and antioxidant activities. Recent research on rosmarinic acid and carnosic acid has generated significant interest in rosemary as a functional food and neuroprotective agent.

## Taxonomy and Naming
- **Order:** Lamiales
- **Family:** Lamiaceae (mint family)
- **Genus:** Rosmarinus
- **Species:** R. officinalis L.
- **Authority:** Carl Linnaeus (1753)
- **Synonyms:** Salvia rosmarinus Schleid. (2017 reclassification), R. latifolius Mill.
- **Common names:** Rosemary, old man, polar plant, compass weed, dew of the sea, incensier
- **Etymology:** From Latin "ros marinus" (dew of the sea), referring to its native coastal Mediterranean habitat. "Officinalis" indicates its historical use in apothecaries.
- **Note:** Molecular phylogenetics placed Rosmarinus within Salvia in 2017, though Rosmarinus officinalis remains the most widely used name.

## Description

### Growth Habit and Morphology
Dense, bushy evergreen shrub reaching 0.5–2.0 m in height with a similar spread. Growth habit varies from low and prostrate ('Prostratus', 30 cm tall × 1.5 m spread) to tall and upright ('Tuscan Blue', 2 m tall × 1 m spread). Young stems are green, square in cross-section, and densely covered in short white hairs, becoming woody, brown, and rounded with age. Mature bark is gray-brown and fissured. Individual specimens in Mediterranean gardens have been documented at over 50 years of age.

### Leaves
Evergreen, leathery, linear to needle-like, 1.5–4.0 cm long and 2–5 mm wide. Leaves are sessile, arranged in opposite pairs along the stems, with margins rolled under (revolute). The upper surface is dark green, glabrous, and gland-dotted; the lower surface is grayish-white due to dense tomentose hairs. The rolled margins create a channel on the lower surface, an adaptation to reduce water loss. When crushed, leaves release a strong, resinous, camphoraceous aroma.

### Flowers and Inflorescences
Flowers are borne in short, axillary or terminal racemes 2–6 cm long. Individual flowers are bilabiate, 10–14 mm long, with a tubular 5-toothed calyx and bilabiate corolla. The upper lip is hooded and two-lobed; the lower lip is three-lobed with the central lobe largest. Most common flower color is blue to violet-blue, but cultivars produce white, pink, and deep violet. Each flower has two long-exerted stamens. Blooming occurs primarily in spring (March–May) but can continue sporadically year-round in mild climates. Flowers are rich in nectar and highly attractive to bees.

## Cultivation Notes
- **Propagation:** Semi-hardwood cuttings (10–15 cm) in late summer root in 3–6 weeks under mist. Seed is slow and erratic (14–28 days, 30–50% germination). Cultivars must be vegetatively propagated.
- **Spacing:** 60–90 cm for hedging; 1–1.5 m for specimens.
- **Pruning:** Prune lightly after flowering. Can be sheared into formal hedges or topiary. Avoid cutting into old bare wood.
- **Harvest:** Leaves year-round. Highest essential oil content just before flowering.
- **Yield:** 1,000–3,000 kg fresh herb per hectare; 10–25 kg essential oil per hectare.
- **[[comparison-companion-planting-reference-vs-jadam-monoculture-critique]]:** Excellent for tomatoes (repels hornworm), sage, thyme, lavender, and brassicas. Deters cabbage moths, bean beetles, and carrot flies.
