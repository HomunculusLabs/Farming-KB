## Overview

**Saururus cernuus** is a herbaceous perennial in the family Saururaceae, native to wetlands of [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]]. It is commonly known as lizard's tail, a name referring to the arching, tail-like inflorescence that curves downward at its tip.

The species grows in shallow water and saturated soils along stream banks, marshes, swamps, and pond margins. It forms dense colonies through creeping rhizomes and can dominate wetland understories where conditions are favorable.

Lizard's tail produces white, fragrant flowers arranged on a distinctive curved spike from June through September. The flowers attract a variety of insect pollinators and the foliage provides habitat for aquatic invertebrates.

The plant has documented use in traditional medicine among [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] peoples and has shown phytoremediation potential for removing heavy metals from contaminated water. It is increasingly planted in [[comparison-constructed-wetlands-vs-bioswales]] for water treatment.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Saururus cernuus** L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The genus name derives from the Greek *sauros* (lizard) and *oura* (tail), referencing the distinctive inflorescence shape. The specific epithet *cernuus* means "nodding" or "drooping."

The family Saururaceae is a small family of [[permaculture-plants-herbaceous-perennials]] in the order Piperales, closely related to Piperaceae (peppers) and Aristolochiaceae (birthworts). The family contains only four genera, with *Saururus* being the only genus native to North America.

*S. cernuus* grows 30–90 cm tall from a creeping, fleshy rhizome. Stems are simple, erect, and unbranched, arising from nodes along the rhizome. Multiple stems form dense stands in favorable conditions.

Leaves are alternate, simple, cordate (heart-shaped), 5–12 cm long and 3–8 cm wide, with a repand (wavy) margin. The leaf surface is glabrous to pubescent, with 5–7 palmate veins radiating from the base.

The inflorescence is a terminal, curved spike 6–15 cm long, arching downward at maturity. Flowers are tiny, white, and lack petals and sepals; each flower has 3–6 white stamens on a short filament.

Flowering occurs from June to September. After pollination, small green fruits develop along the spike, each containing a single seed. The fruiting spike turns brown as seeds mature.

The root system is extensive and fibrous, with thick rhizomes that can extend 1–2 m horizontally from the parent plant. This rhizomatous spread allows rapid colonization of suitable habitat.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Saururus cernuus* ranges from southern Ontario and New York west to Michigan and Oklahoma, and south to Florida and eastern Texas. It is absent from New England north of Massachusetts and from most of the Gulf Coastal Plain.

The species is most abundant in the Mississippi River basin, the Ohio River Valley, and the southeastern Piedmont. Disjunct populations occur in the Ozarks and the Ouachita Mountains.

Habitat includes marshes, swamps, slow-moving streams, seepage areas, pond margins, and ditches. It grows in shallow standing water up to 15 cm deep or in saturated soils with fluctuating water levels.

Optimal conditions include full sun to partial shade and organic-rich, mucky soils. The species tolerates moderate shade but flowering is reduced under closed canopies.

It is an obligate wetland indicator (OBL) in most regions, meaning it almost always occurs in wetland habitats. Its presence is used in wetland delineation surveys.

The related Asian species *[[saururus-chinensis]]* (Chinese lizard's tail) is a similar wetland plant used in traditional Chinese medicine. The two species can be distinguished by inflorescence orientation and leaf pubescence.

## Cultivation

Lizard's tail is easily propagated by division of rhizomes in spring or early summer. Each division should include 2–3 nodes with attached roots. The divisions are planted directly into the substrate at a depth of 5–10 cm.

Seed propagation is possible but less reliable. Seeds require no cold stratification and germinate readily on moist soil surfaces. However, seedlings grow slowly compared to vegetative divisions.

In [[constructed-wetland-design]] stormwater treatment systems, *S. cernuus* is valued for its ability to tolerate fluctuating water levels and its capacity for [[plant-nutrient-uptake-mechanisms]]. It contributes to nitrogen and phosphorus removal.

The species is hardy to USDA zone 4 and requires no winter protection. Foliage dies back to the rhizome after frost, and new growth emerges in mid-spring.

No serious pests or diseases affect cultivated plants. It can become aggressive in garden settings if not contained, spreading beyond intended boundaries through its rhizomatous growth habit.

In water gardens, lizard's tail is best planted in containers or contained zones to prevent unwanted spread. It pairs well with other native wetland species like pickerelweed and blue flag iris.

## Ecological Role

The flowers of *S. cernuus* provide nectar and pollen for a variety of insects, including bees, flies, and small beetles. The white, fragrant spikes are particularly attractive to small native bee species.

The dense foliage and root masses provide habitat for aquatic macroinvertebrates, including damselfly and dragonfly nymphs. Amphibians use the stands for shelter and foraging.

Lizard's tail contributes to stream bank stabilization through its extensive rhizome network, which binds soil and reduces erosion. Colonies along stream margins help filter sediments and nutrients from surface runoff.

The species has demonstrated phytoremediation capacity, accumulating heavy metals including lead, copper, and zinc in its tissues. This makes it a candidate for use in constructed wetlands treating contaminated water.

It provides cover for fish fry and other small aquatic organisms along pond and stream margins. The arching flower spikes also serve as perches for damselflies and other odonates.

The species is moderately palatable to muskrats and nutria, which may limit its establishment in areas with high herbivore populations. Grazing typically stimulates regrowth from rhizomes.
