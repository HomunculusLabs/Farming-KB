# Fungal Compost

## Description

Fungal compost is compost that has been intentionally managed to promote fungal dominance, resulting in a material rich in fungal biomass, hyphae, and spores. It contrasts with bacterially dominated compost, which favors bacterial populations. Fungal compost is the primary soil amendment used when establishing or maintaining trees, shrubs, perennials, and other plants that prefer fungally dominated soils. It is one of the three foundational tools of [[soil-food-web]] gardening, alongside mulches and compost teas.

## Classification

- **Category**: Organic soil amendment
- **Type**: Fungally dominated compost
- **Role**: Inoculant and soil food web modifier

## Key Facts

- Produced by using high-carbon, brown (aged) organic materials as feedstock
- Woody materials, dry leaves, straw, and bark promote fungal growth during composting
- The composting process is allowed to proceed at a slower pace, giving fungi time to colonize
- [[fungal-hyphae]] are visible as white, thread-like networks throughout the finished compost
- Supports plants that prefer ammonium nitrogen (trees, shrubs, perennials)
- Contains spores of beneficial fungi that can establish mycorrhizal associations
- Rich in humic and fulvic acids produced by [[fungal-decomposition]]
- Provides habitat for fungal-feeding nematodes and microarthropods
- Typically has a more acidic pH than [[bacterial-compost]]

## Production Methods

To create fungally dominated compost:

1. Use a higher ratio of brown (carbon-rich) to green (nitrogen-rich) materials
2. Include woody chips, dry leaves, and straw in the feedstock
3. Allow the pile to compost more slowly with less frequent turning
4. Add fungal foods: kelp, humic acids, fulvic acids, phosphate rock dusts
5. Maintain moderate moisture levels—coarse, drier conditions favor fungi
6. Allow the compost to cure for a longer period to promote fungal colonization
7. Consider inoculating with forest soil or commercial fungal preparations

## Applications in the Garden

Fungal compost is applied specifically where fungal soil dominance is desired:

- **Under trees and shrubs**: Apply 1–2 inches to the drip line (not touching trunk/stem)
- **Around perennials**: Apply as a top-dressing in spring and autumn
- **Soil remediation**: Used to convert bacterially dominated soils toward fungal dominance
- **Compost tea production**: Used as the inoculum for brewing fungally dominated compost tea
- **Pathway restoration**: Applied to compacted paths to restore soil structure
- **New plantings**: Mixed into planting holes for trees, shrubs, and perennials

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Fungal compost is essential for mycology-oriented gardening because it directly introduces and supports fungal populations in the soil. For gardeners transitioning from chemical to biological management, fungal compost is the primary tool for re-establishing the fungal component of [[the-soil-food-web]] that has been destroyed by synthetic fertilizers and fungicides. It provides the fungal inoculum that forms the basis of mycorrhizal networks, decomposes woody organic matter, and competes with pathogenic fungi.

In the succession model, fungal compost represents the mid-to-late successional soil conditions that support woody plants and perennials. It is the biological opposite of the green, nitrogen-rich materials that promote bacterial dominance for vegetable gardens.

## Relationship to Other Entities

- **Bacterial compost**: The complementary compost type used for annuals and vegetables
- **Brown mulch**: Applied over fungal compost to maintain fungal habitat at the soil surface
- **Compost tea**: Fungal compost serves as the inoculum for brewing fungally dominated tea
- **Mycorrhizal fungi**: Fungal compost supports the soil conditions mycorrhizae need to establish
- **Trees, shrubs, and perennials**: The primary plant groups that benefit from fungal compost applications
- **Humic and fulvic acids**: Components of fungal compost that further promote fungal growth

## Practical Applications
Understanding these compost dynamics enables targeted amendment strategies
for specific crop needs and soil conditions. Different composting methods
produce distinct microbial communities that influence plant health outcomes.
Thermal composting favors bacterial dominance while fungal composting methods
promote mycorrhizal associations essential for perennial crop systems.
Application rates and timing depend on existing soil biology and crop stage.
