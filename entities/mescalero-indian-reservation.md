## Mescalero Indian Reservation

## Description

The Mescalero Indian Reservation is a Native American reservation in south-central New Mexico, established in 1873 for the [[mescalero-apache]]. The reservation subsequently became home to members of three Apache tribes: the Mescaleros (largest group), the Chiricahuas, and the Lipans.

## Classification

- **Type:** Geographic location / reservation
- **Location:** South-central New Mexico, United States
- **Established:** 1873
- **Tribal composition:** Mescalero Apache (majority), [[chiricahua-apache]], [[lipan-apache]]

## Tribal History

The reservation's population reflects a complex history of Apache consolidation:

- **Mescaleros:** The original tribe for whom the reservation was established in 1873
- **Chiricahuas:** Taken as prisoners of war in 1886 after Geronimo's capitulation; when freed in 1913, the majority chose to move to the Mescalero Reservation and merge with the Mescalero tribe
- **Lipans:** Destroyed as functioning groups during the latter half of the nineteenth century; their few remaining members joined the Mescaleros

## Shamanic Tradition

During 1959–60, there were thirteen accredited shamans on the reservation (Mescalero, Chiricahua, and Lipan) and approximately fifteen pseudoshamans who claimed supernatural power but were considered imposters. Key shamans included:

- **Ancient One:** The sole living person who had participated in the peyote camp that operated from approximately 1870 to post-1910
- **Black Eyes:** One of only two shamans at times judged to be witches (along with Ancient One)

## Peyote History

The reservation has a distinctive peyote history:

- **Period of use:** Approximately 1870 until some time after 1910
- **Source:** Mescaleros learned peyote rites from the Lipans (who obtained it from the Carrizo Indians) or from Tonkawas, Yaquis, or other northern Mexican groups
- **Outcome:** Peyote ceremonies led to disruption, bloodshed, and feuds due to the interaction between peyote's effects and Apache personality structure
- **Proscription:** Peyote was eventually defined as an evil substance and abandoned by the general populace
- **Residual use:** A few shamans and pseudoshamans were believed to continue using peyote for malevolent witchcraft and love magic

## Religious Framework

Apache religion on the reservation conceives the world as permeated by supernatural power (not intrinsically good or evil). Key features include:

- Power approaches people through plants, animals, or natural phenomena via dreams or hallucinatory experiences
- Any person can become a shaman — Opler (1936) described the Mescaleros as "a tribe of shamans, active or potentially active"
- Each shaman has private instructions and individually owned rites
- There has never been a chief shaman, consistent with native concepts of leadership and authority
- Peyote power is unique in being uniformly considered bad (most power is morally neutral)

## Anthropological Research

The reservation was the site of extensive interdisciplinary research by:

- **L. Bryce Boyer, M.D.:** Psychoanalyst studying personality organization and shamanism
- **Ruth M. Boyer, Ph.D.:** Anthropologist studying socialization practices
- **Harry W. Basehart, Ph.D.:** Professor of Anthropology at the University of New Mexico studying social structure

Research was supported by NIMH grants and ran from 1958 through at least 1971.

## Key Facts

- The reservation hosts three distinct Apache tribal traditions
- Aboriginal religio-medical philosophies are similar if not identical across the three tribes
- No one during the study period (1958–1971) was known to currently use peyote in ceremonies
- The reservation never adopted the Peyote Religion ([[native-american-church]])
- Child-rearing practices strongly resemble aboriginal tactics during the prelatency period

## Relevance to Ethnobotany

The Mescalero Reservation provides a crucial case study in how cultural factors determine the social outcome of hallucinogenic drug use. The same substance (peyote) that produced communal harmony in the Native American Church produced hostility and violence among the Mescaleros, demonstrating that pharmacological effects are mediated by personality structure, socialization practices, and cultural context.
