# Chiricahua Apache

## Description

The Chiricahua Apache are a Native American tribe historically known for their fierce resistance to United States and Mexican military forces, most famously under the leadership of [[geronimo]]. They were taken as prisoners of war in 1886 after Geronimo's capitulation, and when freed in 1913, the majority chose to join the [[mescalero-indian-reservation]] in New Mexico. The Chiricahuas used peyote in religious contexts as early as 1875, though remarkably no peyote tradition survived among the reservation community.

## Classification

- **Category**: Indigenous people / ethnic group
- **Language family**: Athabaskan (Apachean branch)
- **Historical territory**: Southwestern New Mexico, southeastern Arizona, northern Sonora and Chihuahua (Mexico)
- **Current location**: Mescalero Indian Reservation, New Mexico; Fort Sill, Oklahoma
- **Related groups**: [[mescalero-apache]], [[lipan-apache]], Western Apache
- **Population ranking on reservation**: Second-largest group after Mescaleros

## History

### Pre-Reservation Period

The Chiricahua Apache were among the most prominent and resistant of the Apache groups in the American Southwest and northern Mexico. Under leaders including Cochise, Victorio, and Geronimo, they conducted an extended campaign of raiding and resistance against both Mexican and American military forces throughout the 19th century. Their traditional territory spanned the rugged mountainous regions of what is now southwestern New Mexico, southeastern Arizona, and the Mexican states of Sonora and Chihuahua. Their military prowess and knowledge of the difficult terrain made them formidable opponents.

### Prisoner of War Period (1886-1913)

After the capitulation of Geronimo and his followers in 1886, the Chiricahua were taken as prisoners of war by the United States government. They were transported to various locations including Florida, Alabama, and finally Fort Sill, Oklahoma. This extended period of imprisonment — spanning 27 years and one of the longest for any tribal group in American history — had profound effects on their cultural practices, social organization, and spiritual life. Many traditional practices were disrupted or lost during this period.

### Reservation Period (1913-present)

When the Chiricahuas were freed in 1913, the majority chose to move to the Mescalero Indian Reservation in New Mexico and become part of the Mescalero tribe. Some elected to remain at Fort Sill, Oklahoma. On the reservation, the Chiricahuas maintain their distinct identity alongside Mescaleros and Lipans. The three groups share similar religio-medical philosophies, criteria for shamanic status, and shamanistic procedures.

## Religious and Shamanic Practices

The Chiricahua share fundamental religious concepts with the other Apache groups on the reservation:

1. **Supernatural power**: The world is permeated by power with no intrinsic good or evil attribute; its virtue resides in its potency alone
2. **Power acquisition**: Power approaches individuals through plants, animals, or natural phenomena via dreams or hallucinatory experiences
3. **Shamanic status**: Any person can be a power recipient, making shamanic practice potentially accessible to all community members
4. **Shared procedures**: Aboriginal religio-medical philosophies, shamanic criteria, and shamanistic procedures have been "similar if not identical" among the three Apache tribes throughout recorded times

### Personality and Cultural Factors

Research on the reservation Apache population identified a typical personality configuration characterized by impulse-driven behavior, fear of loss of control over repressed hostile urges, suggestibility, phobic tendencies, avoidance of introspection, suspiciousness, and unstable emotional attachments. Men are described as caught between passive and aggressive urges with insecure sexual identities. This configuration corresponds to the Western psychiatric diagnosis of character disorder with hysterical and impulsive attributes.

## Peyote Use

The Chiricahuas were documented using peyote in religious contexts in 1875 (Jones, 1899), making them one of the earlier Apache groups with confirmed peyote use. However, unlike the Mescaleros and Lipans who developed formal peyote camps and shamanistic peyote ceremonies, the Chiricahuas on the reservation have no confirmed peyote tradition. No one on the reservation has knowledge of peyote use by the Chiricahuas. The Chiricahuas were notably excluded from Shonle's 1925 map of peyote use distribution in the United States.

## Key Facts

- Geronimo led the last major Chiricahua resistance before the 1886 capitulation
- The Chiricahuas are the second-largest group on the Mescalero Reservation, after the Mescaleros
- Their prisoner-of-war period (1886-1913) was one of the longest in American history for any tribal group
- Despite early documented peyote use (1875), no peyote tradition survived among reservation Chiricahuas
- Their shamanic practices are essentially identical to those of the Mescalero and Lipan groups
- The Chiricahua experience illustrates how forced relocation and imprisonment can disrupt spiritual traditions involving psychoactive plants

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The Chiricahua Apache case illustrates how even closely related groups can have dramatically different relationships with hallucinogenic plants. While the Mescaleros adopted and then violently rejected peyote, and the Lipans used it in shamanistic contexts, the Chiricahuas have no surviving peyote tradition despite early documented use. This variation within a single cultural-linguistic group demonstrates the complexity of cultural factors — including forced relocation, imprisonment, and cultural disruption — that determine how psychoactive plant traditions are maintained, transmitted, or abandoned.

## Research Methodology

The research on Mescalero Reservation Apache shamanism was conducted by an interdisciplinary team: L. Bryce Boyer (a practicing psychoanalyst studying personality organization), Ruth M. Boyer (an anthropologist gathering socialization data), and Harry W. Basehart (Professor of Anthropology at the University of New Mexico studying social structure). L. Bryce Boyer used psychoanalytically oriented investigative interviews, conducting 1 to 145 interviews each with 60 different persons. The Rorschach test was employed as a supplementary research tool with all Apaches aged 50 and older.

## Legacy and Recognition

The Chiricahua Apache are recognized as one of the most historically significant Native American groups in the American Southwest. Their military resistance, the drama of Geronimo's capitulation, and their lengthy imprisonment have made them a prominent subject of historical and anthropological study, particularly regarding the impact of forced assimilation on spiritual practices.
