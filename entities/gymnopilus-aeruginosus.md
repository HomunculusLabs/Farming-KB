# Gymnopilus aeruginosus

**Gymnopilus aeruginosus**, commonly known as the **magic blue gym**, is a species of mushroom in the family Cortinariaceae (or Hymenogastraceae under some modern classifications). It is one of several *Gymnopilus* species confirmed to contain psilocybin and is recognized by its striking greenish-blue coloration, intensely bitter taste, and wood-inhabiting habit. The species represents an important example of the independent evolution of psilocybin biosynthesis outside the traditional psychoactive genera.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** [[agar]]icomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Cortinariaceae / Hymenogastraceae
- **Genus:** Gymnopilus
- **Species:** G. aeruginosus

The species epithet "aeruginosus" is derived from Latin, meaning "verdigris" or "copper-rust green," referring to the distinctive greenish coloration of the cap and other surfaces. The genus *Gymnopilus* is characterized by its rusty-brown spore print, partial veil that often forms a rudimentary annulus, and preference for wood substrates. Recent phylogenetic analyses have placed *Gymnopilus* within the family Hymenogastraceae, alongside Psilocybe and related genera.

## Description

### Cap
The pileus is medium-sized, measuring 30 to 100 mm in diameter. It is convex when young, becoming broadly convex to plane or slightly depressed at the center with age. The cap surface is smooth to slightly scaly, especially toward the center, and is hygrophanous. The color is distinctive: greenish-blue to olive-green when young and fresh, fading to yellowish-brown or rusty-brown as it dries. The margin is often slightly incurved when young and may develop fine radial splits in dry conditions.

### Gills
Gills are adnate to slightly sinuate (notched), close to subdistant, and relatively narrow. They are initially yellowish but develop the characteristic rusty-brown color of the genus as spores mature. The gill edges are even and may be slightly paler than the faces. The gills may develop a slightly greenish tint when young, matching the cap coloration.

### Stem
The stipe is 40 to 80 mm long and 5 to 15 mm thick, cylindrical or slightly clavate (club-shaped) at the base. The surface is smooth or finely fibrillose, often with a greenish to bluish tint when fresh. A partial veil may be present in young specimens, sometimes leaving a fragile annular zone or ring on the upper stem. The stem base often has whitish to yellowish mycelial threads attached to the wood substrate.

### Spores and Microscopic Features
The spore print is rusty-brown to orange-brown, a characteristic feature of the genus. Basidiospores are ellipsoid to amygdaliform, rough-walled (echinulate), measuring 8-11 × 5-7 µm. The rough spore ornamentation is an important microscopic diagnostic feature that distinguishes *Gymnopilus* from other genera with similar macroscopic appearance. Basidia are typically four-spored and clavate. Cheilocystidia are numerous, cylindrical to ventricose, and hyaline.

### Odor and Taste
Odor is mild or slightly mealy. The taste is distinctly bitter, often strongly so — this is a key field characteristic of psychoactive *Gymnopilus* species and helps distinguish them from non-psychoactive lookalikes. The bitter compounds persist even after cooking and make the mushroom entirely unsuitable for culinary use.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Gymnopilus aeruginosus* is found across North America, particularly in the Pacific Northwest and eastern deciduous forests. It has also been reported from parts of Europe and Asia. It is a saprotrophic wood-decomposing fungus, growing on dead and decaying hardwood logs and stumps, fallen branches of oak, maple, and other broadleaf trees, and occasionally on conifer wood or wood chips in landscaped areas.

Fruiting occurs from late summer through late autumn, typically after periods of cool, wet weather. It often grows in dense clusters or overlapping groups on large logs, sometimes forming extensive fruiting sheets on heavily decayed wood.

## Chemistry and Psychoactive Properties

*Gymnopilus aeruginosus* is one of the confirmed psychoactive species in the genus *Gymnopilus*. Chemical analyses have identified:

- **Psilocybin** — The primary psychoactive compound, though generally present in lower concentrations than in Psilocybe species
- **Psilocin** — The active dephosphorylated metabolite
- **[[baeocystin]]** — A related [[tryptamine]] alkaloid
- **Possible bis-noryangonin** — A bitter compound that may contribute to the intense bitter taste

The overall psychoactive potency is considered low to moderate. The bitter taste is a notable characteristic — it is so pronounced that the mushrooms are considered inedible for culinary purposes regardless of their psychoactive content. The intensity of bitterness may correlate with psilocybin content, though this relationship has not been formally quantified.

### Blue Staining
Some collections of *G. aeruginosus* exhibit blue or greenish-blue staining, particularly on the stem and cap when bruised or handled. This bluing reaction is associated with the presence of psilocin, which oxidizes upon exposure to air. However, the bluing in *Gymnopilus* species is often less intense and less consistent than in Psilocybe species, and its absence should not be taken as definitive evidence that psychoactive compounds are absent.

## Similar Species

Several *Gymnopilus* species resemble *G. aeruginosus* and vary in their psychoactive properties:

- **Gymnopilus luteofolius** — Similar greenish coloration, confirmed psychoactive, grows on wood
- **Gymnopilus purpuratus** — Purplish to reddish tones, confirmed psychoactive, South American species
- **Gymnopilus spectabilis** — Larger, orange to rusty-brown, confirmed psychoactive, Asian species
- **Gymnopilus junonius** — Very common, large, bitter, non-psychoactive or very weakly active
- **Gymnopilus validipes** — Another potentially psychoactive species in the genus

The greenish coloration and bitter taste help distinguish *G. aeruginosus* from the more common non-psychoactive *G. junonius*. Microscopic examination of spore ornamentation and cystidia is required for definitive identification.

## Cultural and Ethnobotanical Significance

There are no well-documented traditional or indigenous uses of *Gymnopilus aeruginosus* as an entheogen. The intense bitter taste would likely have discouraged any intentional consumption. Modern use is extremely rare due to the low potency and unpalatable flavor. The species is primarily of academic and scientific interest.

## Research Significance

*Gymnopilus aeruginosus* is of scientific interest for several reasons:

1. **Phylogenetic distribution of psilocybin** — The presence of psilocybin in *Gymnopilus* demonstrates that psilocybin biosynthesis has evolved independently across distantly related fungal lineages, supporting hypotheses of horizontal gene transfer or convergent evolution of the biosynthetic pathway
2. **Chemotaxonomy** — The correlation between bitter taste, greenish coloration, and psychoactive content in *Gymnopilus* species provides useful field indicators for screening potential psychoactive collections
3. **Wood-decay ecology** — As a saprotroph on hardwood, it contributes to forest decomposition processes and carbon cycling in temperate ecosystems

## Safety Notes

While *Gymnopilus aeruginosus* contains psilocybin, accurate field identification of *Gymnopilus* species is difficult. Some species in the genus are bitter and unpleasant but non-psychoactive. The intense bitter taste at psychoactive doses would make consumption extremely unpleasant. Proper identification should always be confirmed microscopically by examining spore morphology and ornamentation. The rusty-brown spore print and rough spore walls are key diagnostic features that distinguish *Gymnopilus* from other wood-inhabiting genera.

## Conservation Status

*Gymnopilus aeruginosus* has not been formally assessed for conservation status. Its association with decaying hardwood in temperate forests suggests that populations are tied to the availability of suitable woody substrates. Forest management practices that remove dead wood and fallen logs may impact local populations, though the species is not currently considered at risk.
