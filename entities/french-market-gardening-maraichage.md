## Description

French market gardening, known as "la culture maraichere," was an intensive system of year-round vegetable production practiced in and around Paris during the second half of the 19th century (approximately 1850-1900). The system represented the culmination of centuries of development in protected cultivation, beginning at the royal potager at Versailles under La Quintinie in the 1670s. Parisian maraichers achieved extraordinary productivity on very small land areas using hotbeds, cold frames, cloches, and composted horse manure, producing enough vegetables to feed Paris year-round and export surplus to England.

## Key Facts

- The cultivated land of Parisian growers covered approximately 600 hectares (about 6% of the total land within Paris city limits), feeding the city year-round with diverse fresh produce.
- Average garden size was 1-2 acres, yet growers averaged 4-8 harvests per year from the same ground.
- The system was powered by 100-400 tons of horse manure per acre annually, obtained from city stables as a by-product of horse-drawn transportation.
- Hotbeds heated with decomposing manure produced fresh salads in January and early cucumbers and melons in May-June.
- Up to 3,000 glass bell cloches per garden provided individual plant protection; each had to be hand-ventilated daily using notched sticks.
- Rye straw mats (1 inch thick) were rolled over frames and cloches for additional insulation on cold nights.
- Access paths were only 10 inches wide—too narrow for wheelbarrows—so manure was carried in woven willow basket backpacks called "hottes."
- The system was self-sustaining: manure provided both heat (fresh) and fertility (composted after use), with soil increasing in fertility year over year.
- The system influenced British gardening through William Robinson"s "Parks and Gardens of Paris" (1869), Kropotkin"s "Fields, Factories and Workshops" (1899), and a wave of English-language publications in the early 1900s.
- Joseph Fels (Fels-Naptha soap) bought 600 acres at Mayland, Essex, England to create French-style mini-farms; his foreman Joseph Smith wrote "French Gardening."
- The system declined after 1900 due to: automobile replacement of horses (losing manure supply), chemical fertilizers, urban expansion onto garden land, and the high skill/labor requirements.
- Eliot Coleman"s 1974 visit to Louis Savier's surviving maraicher garden in Banvilliers was formative in developing his Four Season Farm system.

## Relevance to Cultivation

The French maraicher system is the historical foundation of modern intensive, organic, four-season vegetable production. Its principles—maximum production from minimum land, compost-based fertility, protected cultivation, succession planting, and year-round harvest—directly inform contemporary organic market gardening worldwide. Eliot Coleman explicitly modeled his Four Season Farm on the maraicher tradition, adapting it with modern materials (plastic film, floating row covers) while preserving its core philosophy of simplicity, low external inputs, and high-quality outputs. For modern small-scale growers, the maraicher system demonstrates that intensive hand-labor agriculture can be both highly productive and ecologically sustainable.
