## Overview

Gentiana catesbaei, commonly called Catesby's gentian or Elliott's gentian, is a perennial herb endemic to the southeastern United States.

It is one of the later-flowering gentians of the Atlantic coastal plain, blooming from September through November.

The species produces showy blue-purple, funnel-shaped flowers that make it one of the more ornamental native gentians of the region.

Plants reach 30–60 cm tall with lanceolate leaves and unbranched flowering stems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Gentiana catesbaei belongs to family Gentianaceae, genus Gentiana, section Pneumonanthe.

The species was named in honor of Mark Catesby (1683–1749), the English naturalist who documented flora and fauna of colonial America.

It was first described by Johann Friedrich Wolff and later validated by Sereno Watson.

Gentiana catesbaei is closely allied to [[gentiana-saponaria]] and [[gentiana-andrewsii]], forming a complex of southeastern gentians.

Stems are erect, glabrous, typically unbranched, and arising from a short, stout rootstock.

Leaves are oppositely arranged, lanceolate to ovate-lanceolate, 4–10 cm long, sessile with clasping bases.

Flowers are terminal and axillary, funnel-shaped (infundibuliform), 3–5 cm long, deep blue-purple.

The corolla lobes are triangular, 6–10 mm long, with fringed plaits between the lobes.

The calyx is 15–25 mm long with five linear-lanceolate lobes, shorter than the corolla tube.

Capsules are ovoid-ellipsoid, 12–18 mm long, containing numerous small, winged brown seeds.

## Distribution and Habitat

Gentiana catesbaei occurs in the Atlantic coastal plain from southern Virginia southward through the Carolinas to northern Florida.

Its range extends westward into the piedmont regions of the Carolinas and Georgia.

The species inhabits wet pine flatwoods, savannas, seepage slopes, bogs, and moist pine barrens.

It requires moist to wet, acidic, sandy or peaty soils in open, sunny habitats.

Fire plays a critical role in maintaining the open habitats where G. catesbaei thrives, preventing woody encroachment.

Populations are often associated with pitcher plant bogs and other carnivorous plant communities.

Elevation range extends from near sea level to approximately 200 meters.

The species is considered rare throughout much of its range and is state-listed as threatened in several states.

## Ecology

Gentiana catesbaei is a long-lived perennial that persists through underground rootstocks during unfavorable conditions.

The showy blue-purple flowers are pollinated primarily by bumblebees (Bombus species) capable of navigating the deep corolla tube.

Flowering occurs late in the season, reducing competition for pollinators with sympatric gentian species.

[[cannabis-seed-germination]] requires cold stratification and may benefit from [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]].

The species is fire-adapted, surviving prescribed burns through its protected rootstock and benefiting from post-burn canopy opening.

Herbivory pressure is low due to the bitter secoiridoid glycosides present in all plant tissues.

## Conservation

Gentiana catesbaei faces ongoing threats from habitat loss due to drainage of wetlands, urban development, and fire suppression.

Without regular fire, woody species encroach on pine savannas, shading out the light-demanding gentian populations.

Several populations have been extirpated from historically documented sites in Virginia and North Carolina.
