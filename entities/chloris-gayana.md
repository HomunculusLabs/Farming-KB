## Chloris gayana (Rhodes Grass)

## Description

Chloris gayana, commonly known as Rhodes grass, is a stoloniferous perennial tropical grass widely cultivated for pasture, hay production, and soil conservation. In Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual*, it is listed among the important grass species as "Rhodes grass, Chloris gayana."

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Poaceae (Grass family)
- **Genus**: Chloris
- **Species**: C. gayana
- **Common names**: Rhodes grass, coral grass, Bambara grass

## Key Facts

- **Growth habit**: Stoloniferous perennial grass that spreads via above-ground runners (stolons), forming a dense, mat-like sward. Grows 60–150 cm tall at maturity, with flowering stems reaching up to 1.5 meters.
- **Native range**: Likely native to eastern and southern Africa (Rhodesia/Zimbabwe region, from which it takes its name). Now widely distributed across tropical and subtropical regions worldwide.
- **Climate adaptation**: Adapted to tropical and subtropical climates with annual rainfall of 600–1500 mm. Exhibits moderate drought tolerance and can survive in semi-arid conditions once established. Tolerates light frosts but is primarily a warm-season grower.
- **Soil requirements**: Highly adaptable to a wide range of soil types, including sandy, loamy, and clay soils. Prefers well-drained, moderately fertile soils with pH 5.5–8.0. Shows moderate salt tolerance, making it useful in mildly saline conditions.
- **Establishment**: Can be established from seed or vegetative stolons. Seeds are small and benefit from a firm, fine seedbed. Rapid establishment and ground coverage are key advantages of this species.
- **Persistence**: The stoloniferous growth habit enables Rhodes grass to recover rapidly from grazing, cutting, or drought, persisting for many years under appropriate management.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

### Pasture and Forage Production

Rhodes grass is one of the most widely planted tropical pasture grasses globally. It produces high-quality forage when young, with crude protein levels of 8–15% depending on growth stage and soil fertility. It is palatable to all classes of livestock and makes excellent hay when cut at the early heading stage. In permaculture livestock systems, it provides reliable warm-season grazing and can be stockpiled for deferred grazing during dry periods.

### Soil Conservation and Land Rehabilitation

The dense, mat-forming growth of Rhodes grass via stolons makes it an outstanding soil conservation species. It stabilizes soil on steep slopes, road embankments, and degraded land. The extensive stolon network binds surface soil particles while the fibrous root system penetrates the subsoil, reducing both surface erosion and mass wasting. It has been widely used in mine site rehabilitation across tropical regions.

### Saline and Sodic Soil Management

Rhodes grass exhibits moderate salt tolerance, making it a candidate species for permaculture systems dealing with saline soils or irrigation with moderately saline water. It can be used as a pioneer species to vegetate saline discharge areas, gradually improving soil structure and organic matter, which in turn helps leach salts from the root zone.

### Companion Planting with Legumes

Rhodes grass combines well with tropical legumes such as siratro (Macroptilium atropurpureum), greenleaf desmodium (Desmodium intortum), and stylo (Stylosanthes guianensis). These associations increase the protein content of the pasture, contribute nitrogen to the system, and extend the productive season. In permaculture designs, Rhodes grass-legume pastures can serve as productive ground layers in silvopastoral systems.

### Firebreak and Buffer Zone

The dense, low-growing sward of Rhodes grass can serve as an effective firebreak in permaculture designs in fire-prone tropical savanna environments. When maintained in a green, actively growing state through grazing or mowing, it can slow or stop the spread of low-intensity surface fires, protecting more vulnerable plantings.

### Hay and Silage Production

Rhodes grass is well-suited to hay production due to its relatively fine stems and good drying characteristics. Multiple cuts per year are possible under irrigation or in high-rainfall areas. It also makes acceptable silage when wilted to appropriate moisture content. This makes it valuable for on-farm feed storage in permaculture livestock systems.

## Management Considerations

Rhodes grass requires regular defoliation through grazing or mowing to maintain quality and prevent accumulation of rank, stemmy growth. It responds well to nitrogen fertilization but can persist on moderate fertility when grown with legumes. Some cultivars are susceptible to leaf blight and rust diseases in humid conditions. The species can become invasive in some native grassland ecosystems, so its use near conservation areas should be considered carefully.

## Cultivars

Important commercial cultivars include 'Callide' (late-flowering, higher quality), 'Katambora' (earlier flowering, nematode-resistant), 'Pioneer' (common type), and 'Finecut' (selected for hay production). Cultivar selection should match local rainfall patterns, soil conditions, and intended use.

### Integration with Permaculture Earthworks

Rhodes grass is well-suited to planting on and around permaculture earthworks including swales, contour banks, and dam spillways. Its stoloniferous spread provides rapid surface stabilization on disturbed soils following earthmoving. The species can be seeded directly onto freshly constructed earthworks and will establish quickly with minimal soil preparation, making it an ideal pioneer for the stabilization phase of site development.

### Use in Tropical Lawn and Amenity Plantings

In permaculture residential zones, Rhodes grass can serve as a durable, low-maintenance lawn species in tropical and subtropical areas. It tolerates foot traffic, recovers quickly from damage, and requires less water and fertilizer than many introduced lawn species. When mixed with low-growing legumes such as white clover (Trifolium repens), it creates a self-fertilizing turf that provides habitat for beneficial insects.

### Role in Integrated Pest Management

Dense Rhodes grass swards provide habitat for ground-dwelling predatory insects and spiders that contribute to natural pest control in adjacent crop areas. In permaculture zone designs, buffer strips of Rhodes grass between crop areas and native vegetation can support beneficial insect populations while reducing pest migration into production zones.
