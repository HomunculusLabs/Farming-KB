## Overview

**Peltigera aphthosa** (L.) Willd., commonly known as the **green dog lichen** or **freckle pelt lichen**, is a large foliose lichen in the family Peltigeraceae. It is one of the most distinctive members of the genus *Peltigera*, recognizable by the prominent cephalodia on its upper surface.

The species is a tripartite symbiotic association involving a fungal partner (mycobiont), a green algal photobiont (*Coccomyxa*), and a cyanobacterial partner (*Nostoc*) housed in specialized cephalodia. This triple symbiosis enables both photosynthesis and atmospheric nitrogen fixation.

*P. aphthosa* is circumpolar in distribution, found across boreal and arctic tundra, montane forests, and subalpine meadows of the Northern Hemisphere. It grows on mossy ground, rotting wood, and soil in moist, shaded habitats.

The species has a long history of use in traditional medicine and as a food source among indigenous Arctic peoples. In Scandinavia, it was historically used as a starvation food during famines, though raw consumption can cause digestive irritation.

Its nitrogen-fixing capacity makes it ecologically significant in nutrient-poor tundra and boreal ecosystems. Fixed nitrogen from *P. aphthosa* contributes measurably to soil nitrogen budgets in boreal forest floors and arctic tundra mats.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Peltigera aphthosa* was originally described by Linnaeus in 1753 as *Lichen aphthosus* and later transferred to the genus *Peltigera* by Willdenow in 1787. The specific epithet *aphthosa* refers to the small, blister-like cephalodia on the thallus surface.

The thallus is large, reaching 10–20 cm in diameter, with broad, rounded lobes 1–3 cm wide. The upper surface is grayish-green when dry and bright green when wet, with a finely tomentose margin.

The most diagnostic feature is the presence of dark green, roughly spherical **cephalodia** scattered across the upper surface. These structures, 1–5 mm in diameter, house the cyanobacterial partner *Nostoc* and are the site of nitrogen fixation.

The lower surface is pale with prominent, reticulate veins and simple to branched rhizines. Apothecia are common, arising from the thallus margin, with brown discs 2–6 mm in diameter and a thin, toothed exciple.

Spores are ellipsoid, 3-locular (septate into three chambers), hyaline, measuring 28–45 × 12–18 μm. Pycnidia produce bacilliform conidia approximately 5–7 × 1 μm.

The photobiont layer beneath the upper cortex contains *Coccomyxa* cells arranged in a dense palisade. This green algal partner gives the thallus its characteristic bright green coloration when hydrated.

Veins on the lower surface form a prominent reticulate network that aids in gas exchange and water transport. Rhizines arising from these veins anchor the thallus to the substrate and absorb moisture and nutrients.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Peltigera aphthosa* has a circumboreal distribution spanning northern Europe, Asia, and North America. In Europe it ranges from Scandinavia and the British Isles eastward through Siberia to the Russian Far East.

In North America it occurs across Alaska, northern Canada, and extends southward through the Rocky Mountains and the Pacific Northwest into the Cascade Range and Sierra Nevada at high elevations.

The species is characteristic of **boreal and subarctic habitats** including open tundra, subalpine meadows, and moist coniferous forests. It colonizes mossy substrates, rotting logs, damp soil, and humus-rich ground in shaded to semi-exposed positions.

Optimal habitats receive consistent moisture from fog, precipitation, or groundwater seepage. It is less tolerant of prolonged desiccation than some *Peltigera* species, restricting it to relatively humid microsites.

In Arctic tundra, *P. aphthosa* is one of the few large foliose lichens capable of persisting through the growing season. Its broad thallus captures maximum light during the short boreal summer, contributing significantly to annual carbon gain.

Elevation ranges from sea level in arctic coastal areas to above 2,500 m in alpine zones of temperate mountain ranges. It often grows intermixed with other *Peltigera* species, *Cladonia* lichens, and bryophytes.

In boreal forests, *P. aphthosa* shows a preference for late-successional stands with abundant coarse woody debris and deep moss layers. Forestry practices that remove coarse woody debris and simplify stand structure reduce available habitat.

The species is moderately shade-tolerant but requires sufficient light for photosynthesis. It is most vigorous under a partially open canopy or along forest edges where diffuse light penetrates to the ground layer.

## Symbiosis and Nitrogen Fixation

*Peltigera aphthosa* is a tripartite lichen, harboring both a green algal photobiont (*Coccomyxa* sp.) and a cyanobacterial partner (*Nostoc* sp.) simultaneously. The green alga occupies the main photosynthetic layer of the thallus.

The cyanobacterium is confined to specialized structures called **cephalodia**, which appear as dark green warts on the upper cortex. Each cephalodium is internally differentiated with a cortex, medulla, and *Nostoc* filaments embedded in a gelatinous matrix.

The *Nostoc* cephalodia are capable of **biological nitrogen fixation**, converting atmospheric dinitrogen (N₂) into ammonium (NH₄⁺) using the enzyme nitrogenase. Fixed nitrogen is released into the surrounding thallus and leached into the substrate, enriching the local soil.

Nitrogenase activity in *P. aphthosa* is temperature-dependent, with optimal rates between 15–25°C and strong inhibition above 35°C. Activity is also regulated by moisture availability, as desiccation halts metabolic processes.

The tripartite arrangement provides a nutritional division of labor. The green alga supplies carbon through photosynthesis, while the cyanobacterium provides fixed nitrogen. This dual-photobiont strategy may confer greater ecological flexibility than bipartite *Peltigera* species.

Cephalodia can develop de novo when free-living *Nostoc* colonies colonize the thallus surface, or they may be inherited vertically through thallus fragmentation. The establishment of new cephalodia represents an ongoing process of symbiont recruitment.

Rates of nitrogen fixation measured by acetylene reduction assay range from 1–5 μmol N₂ g⁻¹ dry weight h⁻¹ under field conditions. These rates are comparable to other cyanobacterial lichens and represent a significant input to boreal forest nitrogen cycles.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

*Peltigera aphthosa* produces several characteristic lichen secondary metabolites, primarily **triterpenoids** and **depsidones**. The major compound is peltigerine, a tetracyclic triterpenoid found across the *Peltigera* genus.

Additional compounds include tenuiorin, methyl gyrophorate, and various unidentified fatty acids. The chemistry of *P. aphthosa* differs from the closely related *P. leucophlebia* in the absence of leucophlebin.

Traditional use of *P. aphthosa* in Scandinavian and Siberian folk medicine includes treatment of coughs, wounds, and digestive ailments. The lichen was boiled into decoctions or applied topically as a poultice.

Limited pharmacological screening has revealed **moderate antimicrobial activity** against Gram-positive bacteria, attributed to tenuiorin and related depsides. Extracts have also shown anti-inflammatory activity in vitro.

Tenuiorin, a depsidone isolated from *P. aphthosa*, has demonstrated antioxidant activity in cell-free assays. Methyl gyrophorate exhibits weak antifungal properties against selected plant pathogenic fungi.

The cyanobacterial partner *Nostoc* produces microcystins and other bioactive peptides, though concentrations within intact cephalodia are generally low. Thorough toxicological evaluation of whole-thallus preparations has not been conducted.

Research interest in *Peltigera* metabolites has increased due to the discovery of novel antibacterial compounds from related species. *P. aphthosa* remains relatively under-investigated compared to its more accessible congeners.

## Ecology and Conservation

As a nitrogen-fixing lichen, *P. aphthosa* plays an important role in **nutrient cycling** within boreal and tundra ecosystems. Leached nitrogen from cephalodia enriches the substrate and benefits surrounding vascular plants and bryophytes.

The species serves as a substrate for invertebrates, providing shelter and food for mites, springtails, and insect larvae. Reindeer and caribou occasionally graze on *Peltigera* species during winter when other forage is scarce.

*P. aphthosa* is sensitive to air pollution, particularly sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides. It has declined in southern portions of its European range due to industrial emissions and is used as a bioindicator of forest air quality.

Climate warming poses a long-term threat by reducing suitable boreal habitat and increasing desiccation stress. Alpine populations at the southern edge of the range are particularly vulnerable to upward elevational shifts and habitat fragmentation.

The species is currently not listed as threatened, though local population declines have been documented in intensively managed boreal forests. Conservation recommendations include maintaining moist, old-growth forest microhabitats with abundant moss cover.

In fragmented boreal landscapes, *P. aphthosa* shows limited dispersal ability, restricting recolonization of restored habitats. Lichen translocation experiments have demonstrated that manual reintroduction to suitable sites can establish viable populations within 5–10 years.
