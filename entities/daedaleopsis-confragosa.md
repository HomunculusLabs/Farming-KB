## Daedaleopsis confragosa

## Overview

Daedaleopsis confragosa, commonly known as the thin-maze polypore or blushing bracket, is a wood-decay fungus in the family Polyporaceae. It is a widespread saprotrophic species found on dead hardwoods across [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of the Northern Hemisphere.

The species is notable for its daedaleoid (maze-like) pore surface that can transition to poroid with age, and its distinctive bruising reaction where the pore surface turns reddish-pink when damaged or rubbed. This blushing response is the origin of its common name.

### Morphology

The basidiocarp is a sessile to effused-reflexed bracket fungus, typically 5–15 cm wide and 1–3 cm thick at the base.

The upper surface is concentrically zoned with bands of brown, grey-brown, and reddish-brown, often with a radially wrinkled or furrowed texture. The margin is rounded and often pale in young specimens.

The pore surface is white to cream when fresh, developing the characteristic daedaleoid (labyrinthine) pore pattern that gives the genus its name. Pores are 1–3 per mm and angular to elongated. In older specimens, the pore surface may become more regularly poroid.

The context is corky and tough, with a double-layer structure. The tube layer is 2–8 mm deep.

Spores are cylindrical to allantoid, smooth, hyaline, measuring 7–10 × 2–3 μm. The hyphal system is trimitic.

The most distinctive field character is the blushing reaction: when the pore surface is rubbed or cut, it turns pinkish-red, then fades. This is especially pronounced in fresh material.

## Distribution and Habitat

Daedaleopsis confragosa has a broad distribution across temperate and boreal regions of the Northern Hemisphere, including Europe, [[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]] and Bjerkandera adusta.

It plays an important role in [[singh-fungal-biodegradation-of-polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbons]] hydrocarbons (PAHs), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), and synthetic dyes.

## Edibility and Uses

Daedaleopsis confragosa is not considered edible due to its tough, corky texture. It has no significant culinary applications.

The species has not been investigated for medicinal compounds to the extent of other polypores, though general white-rot fungi produce various [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]].

In forest management, the presence of D. confragosa on standing timber indicates advanced decay and structural compromise. It is not considered a serious pathogen of living trees, typically only colonizing already-weakened or dead wood.

## Bioactive Compounds

Like many polyporaceae, D. confragosa produces a range of [[biodiversity-fungal-secondary-metabolites]]. Research has identified several bioactive compounds including triterpenoids, sterols, and phenolic acids isolated from the fruiting body and mycelium.

Extracts of D. confragosa have demonstrated antioxidant activity in vitro, attributed primarily to [[aact-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds-wood-composting-ingham]] and terpenoid constituents.
