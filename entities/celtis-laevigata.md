Sugarberry typically reaches 15–25 meters in height with a trunk diameter of 40–80 cm. It develops a broad, rounded to vase-shaped crown with arching branches. In open positions, the crown spreads wider than it is tall.

Champion specimens have exceeded 30 meters in height with trunk diameters over 1 meter. The tree is relatively fast-growing, especially when young, with [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] and wind resistance. This deep root system also makes established trees difficult to transplant successfully.

The common name "sugarberry" refers to the sweet, date-like fruits that ripen in autumn and are consumed by numerous bird and mammal species. These fruits were historically eaten by Native Americans and early settlers.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Magnoliopsida → Rosales → Cannabaceae → *Celtis laevigata* Willd.

The species was described by Carl Ludwig Willdenow in 1806. It was previously placed in the family Ulmaceae but has since been moved to Cannabaceae based on molecular phylogenetic evidence. The genus *Celtis* contains approximately 60–70 species worldwide.

The genus has a [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] before or with the leaves. The species is monoecious, with male and female flowers on the same tree. Pollination is by wind.

The fruit is a small, round drupe, 6–10 mm in diameter, ripening from orange to dark purple or black in late summer to autumn. Each fruit contains a single hard seed. The flesh is thin but sweet, with a flavor reminiscent of dates.

Individual trees can produce large quantities of fruit, with heavy crops occurring in most years rather than the alternating pattern seen in some other fruit-bearing trees. The fruits persist on the tree for several weeks after ripening.

The seeds are dispersed primarily by birds, which consume the fruits and excrete the seeds at some distance from the parent tree. Mammalian dispersers include raccoons, foxes, and other frugivorous mammals that carry fruits to feeding sites.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Celtis laevigata* is distributed across the southeastern United States, from southeastern Virginia south to Florida and west through the Gulf Coast states to eastern Texas. It extends into northeastern Mexico and occurs as far north as southern Oklahoma and Arkansas.

The species is most abundant in bottomland hardwood forests, floodplains, and riparian corridors. It also occurs on upland sites, particularly on calcareous soils in the Edwards Plateau of Texas and the limestone hills of the southeastern Coastal Plain.

It tolerates a wide range of soil conditions, from acidic alluvial soils to alkaline limestone. Optimal growth occurs on deep, moist, fertile soils in bottomland positions. It is moderately tolerant of periodic flooding and seasonal waterlogging.

The species is notably drought-tolerant once established, a trait that makes it valuable for urban planting in the southeastern United States. It tolerates heat, urban pollution, and compacted soils better than many other native shade trees.

Elevational range extends from near sea level to approximately 600 meters. It is hardy to USDA Zone 6, though it is most commonly found in Zones 7–9. The species is more heat-tolerant than northern hackberry.

## Cultivation and Uses

Sugarberry is occasionally planted as an ornamental shade tree, valued for its rapid growth, drought tolerance, and attractive form. Its broad canopy provides dense shade, making it suitable for parks, large yards, and street plantings.

The wood is medium-weight, with a specific gravity of approximately 0.53. It has been used for fence posts, railroad ties, and general construction, though it has limited commercial value compared to other southern hardwoods. The wood is relatively difficult to season without warping.

The heartwood is pale yellow to light brown, with a fine, uniform texture. It works well with both hand and machine tools but is not considered durable in ground contact. Veneer production has been attempted commercially but has not achieved significant market adoption.

The sweet fruits are edible and can be eaten raw or used in preserves. Native American tribes, including the Cherokee and Comanche, collected sugarberry fruits for food. The fruits are small but plentiful, and mature trees can produce heavy crops.

In traditional medicine, preparations of hackberry bark were used as an astringent and for treating sore throats and menstrual disorders. The sap was used as a wash for wounds. These uses were shared with northern hackberry.

The species is relatively pest-free, though the hackberry nipple gall psyllid (*Pachypsylla celtidismamma*) commonly forms distinctive galls on the leaves. These galls are cosmetic and do not significantly affect tree health.

Several other gall-forming insects are associated with the species, including the hackberry bud gall maker and the hackberry petiole gall psyllid. These galls create sheltered habitats for the developing insects and are a distinctive feature of *Celtis* species in the landscape.

## Ecology

Sugarberry is a valuable wildlife food source throughout its range. The sweet fruits are consumed by numerous bird species, including mockingbirds, cedar waxwings, robins, bluebirds, and woodpeckers. Fall-migrating birds rely heavily on hackberry fruit.

Mammals that consume the fruit include foxes, raccoons, opossums, squirrels, and deer. The fruit persists into winter, providing a critical food resource during periods of scarcity. The tree's abundant and reliable fruit production makes it a keystone species in bottomland ecosystems.

The dense canopy provides nesting habitat for songbirds and roosting sites for raptors. The bark texture provides substrate for lichens and mosses. Cavities in mature trees serve as den sites for squirrels, bats, and cavity-nesting birds.
