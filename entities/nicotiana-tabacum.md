## Overview

**Nicotiana tabacum**, commonly known as cultivated tobacco, is an annual herbaceous plant in the family Solanaceae. It is the most widely cultivated species of the genus Nicotiana and the primary commercial source of tobacco products worldwide. The species is believed to be an allopolyploid hybrid originating from crosses between [[nicotiana-sylvestris]] and Nicotiana tomentosiformis.

Tobacco has been cultivated for centuries for its nicotine-rich leaves, which are processed for smoking, chewing, and snuff. The plant also serves as a [[canary-grass-phototropism-model-organism]] in plant molecular biology and genetics research.

All parts of the plant contain toxic alkaloids, primarily nicotine, which acts as a potent neurotoxin and [[mollison-designers-neem-tree-as-natural-insecticide]]. [[mckenna-food-gods-shamanic-tobacco-commercial-tobacco-redux]] production generates billions of dollars in annual revenue globally, though the crop carries significant public health implications.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Nicotiana tabacum L. belongs to the family Solanaceae, subfamily Nicotianoideae, tribe Nicotianeae. The species was formally described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum. It is an allotetraploid (2n = 48), resulting from natural hybridization events in [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]].

The maternal parent is thought to be Nicotiana sylvestris, a species native to the Andean foothills. The paternal genome was contributed by Nicotiana tomentosiformis or a closely related species. This hybrid origin accounts for the species' genetic variability and adaptability.

The plant grows 1 to 2 meters tall with a robust central stalk covered in glandular hairs. Leaves are large, simple, and alternate, ranging from 20 to 60 centimeters in length.

Leaf morphology varies considerably among cultivars, from broad and oval to narrow and pointed. Surface texture can be smooth or heavily pubescent, with trichome density influencing alkaloid and aroma compound production.

Flowers are tubular, approximately 5 centimeters long, with a pale pink to red corolla. The inflorescence is a terminal panicle that opens sequentially from the base upward over several weeks.

The fruit is a two-chambered capsule containing numerous small brown seeds, roughly 0.5 millimeters in diameter. A single plant can produce 100,000 to 300,000 seeds, which remain viable for several years under cool, dry storage.

The root system is extensive with a prominent taproot and dense lateral branching. Glandular trichomes on the leaf surface synthesize and secrete nicotine, which accumulates in vacuoles throughout the aerial tissues.

## Distribution and Habitat

Nicotiana tabacum is not known to exist in a truly wild state. Its center of origin lies in the Andean region of South America, likely modern-day Bolivia and Peru. Domestication and subsequent dispersal by indigenous peoples spread it throughout the Americas before European contact.

Following European colonization in the late 15th century, tobacco cultivation spread rapidly to Europe, Africa, and Asia. Today it is grown commercially in over 120 countries across tropical, subtropical, and [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]].

Major producers include China, India, Brazil, the United States, and Zimbabwe. China alone accounts for roughly 40 percent of global tobacco leaf production. The crop is cultivated from sea level to approximately 1500 meters elevation.

The plant thrives in well-drained, fertile soils with a pH of 5.5 to 6.5. Sandy loams with high [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] content produce the highest quality leaf for premium cigar and cigarette tobaccos.

It requires warm temperatures between 20 and 30 degrees Celsius and moderate rainfall of 500 to 1200 millimeters during [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]. Drought stress during the rapid growth phase can significantly reduce leaf quality and yield.

Tobacco is relatively intolerant of waterlogged soils and poorly aerated root zones. Raised beds and well-drained field sites are preferred in regions with heavy rainfall or high water tables.

Frosts are lethal at all growth stages, making the crop suitable only for frost-free [[jeavons-climate-adaptation-growing-seasons]] in temperate regions. In the tropics and subtropics, tobacco can be grown year-round with appropriate cultivar selection.

## Cultivation

Tobacco is typically grown as an annual crop from seed, with seedlings started in nursery beds or trays before transplanting. Transplanting occurs 6 to 10 weeks after sowing, when seedlings reach 15 to 20 centimeters in height.

Spacing varies from 40 to 60 centimeters within rows and 100 to 120 centimeters between rows. Plant density is adjusted based on cultivar, soil fertility, and the intended leaf quality grade. Lower densities generally produce larger, thicker leaves.

Two primary curing methods define tobacco product types: flue-curing and air-curing. Flue-curing uses heated barns to rapidly dry leaves over 5 to 7 days, producing a yellow-orange leaf high in sugar and mild in flavor.

Air-curing takes 4 to 8 weeks in ventilated barns, yielding a brown leaf with lower sugar content and deeper flavor. Burley tobacco, the principal air-cured type, is central to American blended cigarettes.

Other curing methods include sun-curing for Oriental tobacco and fire-curing for dark-fired varieties used in pipe tobaccos and snuff. Yields range from 1500 to 3000 kilograms of dried leaf per hectare depending on cultivar and conditions.

Fertilization demands are high, particularly for nitrogen and potassium. Excess nitrogen can produce excessively large leaves with poor burning qualities, while potassium improves leaf combustibility and sugar content.

Topping (removal of the flower head) and suckering (removal of axillary shoots) are standard practices. These operations redirect assimilates to leaf development and increase leaf size and alkaloid concentration.

Chemical suckercides, such as maleic hydrazide and fatty alcohols, are applied to reduce the labor cost of manual suckering. These compounds inhibit cell division in the axillary buds without significantly affecting leaf chemistry.

Harvesting is performed either by priming (removing individual leaves as they ripen from bottom to top) or by stalk-cutting (harvesting the entire plant at once). Priming produces more uniform leaf quality and is preferred for premium cigar and cigarette tobaccos.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The principal bioactive alkaloid in Nicotiana tabacum is nicotine, constituting 0.5 to 3 percent of the dry leaf weight. Nicotine (C10H14N2, molecular weight 162.23 g/mol) is a tertiary amine that acts as an agonist at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors.

Additional alkaloids include nornicotine, anabasine, anatabine, and myosmine, each present in trace amounts. Tobacco leaves also contain [[aact-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds-wood-composting-ingham]], flavonoids, carotenoids, and volatile terpenes that contribute to flavor and aroma.

Nicotine binds to nAChR subtypes with high affinity, particularly the alpha-4-beta-2 subtype in the ventral tegmental area. Activation produces dopaminergic stimulation in the mesolimbic pathway, which underlies the reinforcing and addictive properties of tobacco use.

The compound has both stimulant and relaxant effects depending on dose, [[mushroom-taste-route-of-administration-and-consumption-barriers]], and prior exposure. At low doses it enhances attention and cognition; at higher doses it produces nausea, vomiting, and potentially fatal respiratory paralysis.

Tobacco-specific nitrosamines (TSNAs), such as N-nitrosonornicotine (NNN) and 4-(methylnitrosamino)-1-(3-pyridyl)-1-butanone (NNK), form during curing and fermentation. These compounds are potent carcinogens implicated in cancers of the lung, oral cavity, esophagus, and pancreas.

[[singh-fungal-biodegradation-of-polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbons]] (PAHs) are generated during combustion of tobacco in smoking products and represent another major class of carcinogenic constituents.
