# Manure Tea (Anaerobic)

## Description

[[manure-tea]] is an anaerobic liquid amendment created by suspending a bag of manure in water for several weeks. It is one of the oldest liquid fertilizer preparations, employed by farmers and gardeners for centuries. However, manure tea carries significant pathogen risks — particularly *Escherichia coli* — and is considered unsafe and inadvisable for use in modern [[soil-food-web]] management. It should not be confused with [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT).

## Classification

- **Category:** Traditional liquid amendment (anaerobic)
- **Related terms:** barnyard tea, dung tea, manure steep
- **Contrasted with:** AACT (aerobic compost tea), compost extract, compost leachate

## Key Facts

- Created by placing manure (typically from cows, horses, chickens, or other livestock) in a porous bag suspended in water for several weeks
- The steeping process occurs **without aeration**, resulting in anaerobic conditions throughout the liquid
- Anaerobic decomposition produces alcohols, organic acids, and other compounds potentially toxic to plants
- Virtually guarantees the presence of **E. coli** and other fecal coliform bacteria due to the manure source and anaerobic conditions
- Has been used historically as a general-purpose liquid fertilizer, especially in pre-industrial agriculture
- Distinct from compost tea made from properly finished, aerobic compost

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Pathogen Risk

The primary concern with manure tea is the near-certainty of pathogenic contamination:

- **E. coli** bacteria thrive in the anaerobic, nutrient-rich environment of manure steeped in water
- Other potential pathogens include *Salmonella*, *Listeria*, and various parasitic organisms
- Anaerobic conditions favor the survival and proliferation of these organisms
- Application to food crops poses a direct human health risk
- Even application to ornamental plants can introduce pathogens to [[the-soil-food-web]]

### Why Anaerobic Conditions Are Problematic

- Aerobic microbes (the beneficial bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes that comprise a healthy soil food web) cannot survive in anaerobic conditions
- Anaerobic decomposition produces **alcohols** that are toxic to plant roots
- Anaerobic conditions select for entirely different microbial communities — ones dominated by putrefactive bacteria rather than beneficial decomposers
- The loss of aerobic microbial diversity eliminates the beneficial soil food web functions entirely

### Historical Context

Manure tea represents an early attempt at biological soil amendment, and its centuries of use attest to the intuitive understanding that manure contains something valuable for plant growth. However, modern soil microbiology has identified the problems with the anaerobic approach and developed the superior method of AACT to achieve the same goal safely.

## Safer Alternatives

- **Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT):** Uses finished, aerobic compost with active aeration to produce a microbially rich, pathogen-free liquid amendment
- **Vermicompost tea:** Made from worm castings (vermicompost), which are naturally rich in beneficial microbes and carry lower pathogen risk than raw manure
- **Compost extract (short-term):** While still not ideal, a brief extraction (hours, not weeks) from well-finished compost poses less risk than manure tea

## Practical Notes

- Do not use manure tea in modern soil food web management
- The risk of E. coli contamination makes it unsafe for any application involving food crops
- If manure must be used as a nutrient source, compost it fully (reaching thermophilic temperatures of 131°F+ for 15+ days) before brewing tea from the finished compost
- The key difference between manure tea and AACT is **aeration**: beneficial soil microbes require oxygen to thrive
## Practical Applications
Understanding these compost dynamics enables targeted amendment strategies
for specific crop needs and soil conditions. Different composting methods
produce distinct microbial communities that influence plant health outcomes.
Thermal composting favors bacterial dominance while fungal composting methods
promote mycorrhizal associations essential for perennial crop systems.
Application rates and timing depend on existing soil biology and crop stage.

## Key Considerations
Several factors influence the effectiveness of composting approaches:
feedstock quality, moisture content, aeration frequency, and temperature
management all play critical roles in determining final compost quality.
Monitoring microbial populations throughout the process helps ensure
consistent results across batches and seasonal variations.
Storage conditions after completion also affect biological activity.
