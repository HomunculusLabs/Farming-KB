# Lanai (Hawaii)

## Description

Lanai is the **sixth-largest of the Hawaiian Islands**, cited by Mollison as
an example of significant **fog precipitation**. According to Chang (1968),
Lanai receives approximately **127 cm (50 inches) of moisture from fog drip
alone** — a substantial water input that complements measured rainfall and
demonstrates the importance of fog harvesting in island and mountain
environments. Lanai is presented as a comparative case alongside Table
Mountain, South Africa, illustrating how fog precipitation varies with
topography and wind exposure.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Geography — Volcanic Island
- **Category:** Place / Fog precipitation exemplar
- **Location:** Hawaiian Islands, Pacific Ocean, USA
- **Area:** Approximately 364 km² (141 sq mi) — smallest inhabited Hawaiian island
- **Highest point:** Lana'ihale, 1,026 m (3,366 ft)
- **Climate type:** Tropical with significant orographic fog influence

## Geography and Climate

Lanai is a **single-shield volcano** island with a central mountain ridge
running north-south. The island's topography creates distinct windward and
leeward zones:

- The **Lana'ihale summit ridge** intercepts persistent northeast trade winds
  carrying Pacific moisture.
- **Upslope fog formation** occurs as trade winds are forced upward by the
  central ridge, cooling and condensing moisture.
- The windward (north and east) slopes receive substantially more fog
  precipitation than leeward areas.
- Much of the island was historically covered by native Hawaiian dryland
  forest, now largely converted to pineapple plantations and other agriculture.
- The island's relatively low maximum elevation (1,026 m) compared to
  neighboring Maui (Haleakalā at 3,055 m) means fog precipitation is moderate
  rather than extreme.

## Key Facts

- Receives approximately **127 cm (50 inches) of moisture from fog drip
  alone** (Chang, 1968), a significant portion of the island's total water
  budget.
- Fog precipitation at Lanai substantially supplements measured **rainfall**,
  indicating that standard precipitation data underestimates actual moisture
  inputs in fog-prone island environments.
- The island's central mountain ridge intercepting trade wind moisture creates
  ideal conditions for **upslope/orographic fog** condensation.
- In such fog-rich areas, **even field crops may thrive without irrigation**,
  making fog an agriculturally significant water source.
- Lanai's fog precipitation is notably less than Table Mountain, South Africa
  (329 cm), demonstrating how fog harvest varies with elevation, wind
  patterns, and ocean proximity.
- The island was historically known as the "Pineapple Isle" due to the
  Dole Company's pineapple plantation that covered much of its area.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Lanai's fog precipitation demonstrates key [[permaculture-principles]]:

- **Hidden water sources**: Designers must look beyond rain gauges when
  assessing water availability. Fog drip can be the dominant moisture input
  in certain topographies.
- **Trade wind harvesting**: In tropical and subtropical islands, consistent
  trade winds carrying oceanic moisture can be intercepted by vegetation or
  artificial structures such as mesh fog catchers.
- **Vegetation as infrastructure**: Natural and planted forests serve as
  passive fog condensers, converting atmospheric moisture into available
  ground water through drip — an irregular forest canopy is an excellent
  fog condenser.
- **Island [[permaculture]]**: On small islands with limited freshwater resources,
  fog harvesting may be the most viable strategy for expanding agricultural
  production without desalination.
- **Scale comparison**: The difference between Lanai and Table Mountain shows
  how fog precipitation varies with topography, wind exposure, and ocean
  proximity — designers must assess local conditions rather than generalizing.
