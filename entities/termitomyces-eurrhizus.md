## Overview

**Termitomyces eurrhizus** (Berk.) R. Heim is a highly prized [[bloomfield-boletus-satanus-edible-mushroom-hazards]] in the family Lyophyllaceae.

It is an obligate symbiont of fungus-growing termites (Macrotermitinae), cultivated within termite mounds across tropical Africa and South and [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]].

The species is among the most commercially valuable [[edible-wild-mushrooms-by-region]] in several African and Asian countries.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Lyophyllaceae
- **Genus:** Termitomyces R. Heim
- **Species:** T. eurrhizus (Berk.) R. Heim
- **Basionym:** Agaricus eurrhizus Berk. (1847)
- **Synonyms:** Podabrella eurrhiza (Berk.) Pegler, [[termitomyces-eurhizus]] (Berk.) R. Heim

The genus name *Termitomyces* references the termite association, while *eurrhizus* refers to the well-developed rooting structure.

### Morphology

The pileus is convex to plano-convex, 5–20 cm in diameter, smooth, and pale brown to grayish-brown. The cap surface often cracks with age, developing a scaly appearance.

A distinctive perforatorium — a sterile, root-like extension of the stipe — penetrates the termite comb from which the mushroom arises. This structure can extend 10–30 cm below ground and is diagnostic for the genus.

Gills are free to subdecurrent, white to cream, and crowded. The stipe is central, 5–20 cm long, 1–3 cm thick, with a conspicuous annulus that is often movable.

Spores are ellipsoidal, smooth, hyaline, non-amyloid, measuring 7–9 × 5–6 µm.

## Distribution and Habitat

*T. eurrhizus* is found across tropical Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia.

In Africa, it is widely distributed from West Africa through Central and East Africa. In Asia, it occurs in India, Thailand, Malaysia, and southern China.

The species is strictly associated with the fungus combs of Macrotermitinae termites, especially *Macrotermes* and *Odontotermes* species.

Fruiting is strongly seasonal, triggered by the onset of heavy rains. Mushrooms emerge rapidly from termite mounds, often appearing overnight.

## Symbiosis with Termites

The mutualism between *Termitomyces* and fungus-growing termites is one of the most sophisticated insect-fungus associations known.

Worker termites construct and maintain fungus combs — complex structures of chewed plant material colonized by the fungus.

The termites provide the substrate and optimal growing conditions. In return, the fungus partially degrades lignocellulose, making nutrients accessible to the termites.

Each termite colony maintains a single *Termitomyces* clone, transmitted vertically from parent to daughter colonies through alate reproductives.

Genetic studies show this vertical transmission has led to strict co-cladogenesis between termites and their fungal symbionts over millions of years.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

*T. eurrhizus* is one of the most commercially important wild [[palmer-edible-mushrooms-north-america]] [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]].

In West and Central Africa, it is sold in local markets at premium prices and forms an important source of income for rural collectors.

The flesh is firm, white, and has a mild to nutty flavor. It is used in soups, stews, and stir-fried dishes.

[[fukuoka-textdoc-fallacy-infinite-nutritional-analysis]] shows high protein content (20–30% dry weight), significant levels of essential amino acids, and minerals including potassium, phosphorus, and zinc.

The mushroom also contains bioactive polysaccharides with documented antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties.
