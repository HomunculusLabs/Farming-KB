# L-Ornithine

## Overview

L-ornithine is a non-proteinogenic alpha-amino acid.
It is a central recyclable carrier in the mammalian urea cycle.
The compound is not one of the twenty standard amino acids used by ribosomes.
Its main biochemical importance comes from nitrogen disposal and arginine metabolism.
Ornithine accepts a carbamoyl group to become citrulline.
Later in the same pathway, arginase regenerates ornithine from arginine.
That regeneration is why the pathway is also called the ornithine cycle.
Ornithine connects ammonia detoxification, amino acid catabolism, and liver function.
It is especially relevant to [[urea-cycle-biochemistry]].
The molecule also appears in polyamine biosynthesis.
Through ornithine decarboxylase, it can become putrescine.
That route links ornithine to cell growth and proliferation.
Its metabolic roles are broad, but its reference identity is the urea cycle carrier.

## Chemical Identity

Chemical formula: C5H12N2O2.
Molecular weight: 132.16 g/mol.
PubChem CID: 6262.
InChIKey: AHLPHDHHMVZTML-BYPYZUCNSA-N.
IUPAC name: (2S)-2,5-diaminopentanoic acid.
The biologically common form is L-ornithine.
The molecule has an alpha-amino group.
It has an alpha-carboxylate group.
It also has a terminal side-chain amino group.
That terminal amino group is the acceptor for carbamoyl phosphate in the urea cycle.
Ornithine is strongly polar and water soluble.
At physiological pH it is cationic or zwitterionic depending on protonation state.
It is one carbon shorter than lysine.
It is related to arginine by the addition of a guanidino group.
It is related to citrulline by addition of a carbamoyl group.

## Urea Cycle Role

Ornithine enters the mitochondrial matrix from the cytosol.
Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I first makes carbamoyl phosphate from ammonia and bicarbonate.
Ornithine transcarbamylase then transfers the carbamoyl group to ornithine.
The reaction produces citrulline and inorganic phosphate.
Citrulline leaves the mitochondrion for the cytosol.
After several cytosolic steps, arginine is formed.
Arginase hydrolyzes arginine into urea and ornithine.
The regenerated ornithine can return to the mitochondrion for another cycle.
Because it is regenerated, ornithine is not consumed stoichiometrically in urea formation.
It functions more like a carrier scaffold than a waste product.
The cycle depends on adequate ornithine transport across the mitochondrial membrane.
Transport defects can impair nitrogen disposal even when enzymes are intact.
Ornithine availability can therefore influence urea cycle flux.
This is most clinically important when ammonia is rising.

## Transport and Compartmentation

The urea cycle is split between mitochondria and cytosol.
Ornithine must cross the inner mitochondrial membrane to reach ornithine transcarbamylase.
Citrulline must leave the mitochondrion after it is produced.
A dedicated ornithine-citrulline antiporter coordinates that movement.
The transporter is encoded by SLC25A15 in humans.
Defects in this transporter cause hyperornithinemia-hyperammonemia-homocitrullinuria syndrome.
That condition illustrates that compartmentation is not a minor detail.
A metabolite can be abundant in the wrong compartment and still fail to support flux.
Ornithine is therefore an entity where location matters as much as concentration.
The mitochondrial step also ties ornithine use to energy state and ammonia supply.
High amino acid catabolism increases demand for mitochondrial ornithine cycling.

## Polyamine Biosynthesis

Ornithine is the substrate for ornithine decarboxylase.
That enzyme removes the carboxyl group to form putrescine.
Putrescine is a precursor of spermidine and spermine.
These polyamines support DNA packaging, translation, ion channel regulation, and cell growth.
Ornithine decarboxylase is one of the best-known regulated enzymes in growth biology.
Its activity can increase rapidly in proliferating cells.
This branch gives ornithine significance beyond hepatic nitrogen disposal.
The urea cycle and polyamine pathway compete for or share ornithine pools depending on tissue context.
In cancer biology, polyamine synthesis is often studied because growing cells require polyamines.
In microbiology, ornithine metabolism contributes to acid resistance and nutrient use.
The same small molecule therefore participates in both detoxification and biosynthesis.

## Medical Relevance

Ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency is the most common urea cycle disorder.
It is X-linked and can cause severe hyperammonemia.
In that disorder, ornithine cannot be efficiently converted to citrulline.
The block often leads to low citrulline and elevated orotic acid.
Free ornithine may accumulate in some contexts but diagnostic interpretation requires a full amino acid profile.
Ornithine transporter deficiency causes a distinct urea cycle-related syndrome.
Arginase deficiency can also alter ornithine handling because arginine is the source of regenerated ornithine.
Clinicians evaluate ammonia, plasma amino acids, urine orotic acid, and genetic results together.
Acute hyperammonemia is a medical emergency.
Metabolic therapies may include nitrogen scavengers, calories, dialysis, and pathway intermediates.
The right supplement depends on the exact enzymatic or transport defect.
Ornithine itself is marketed as a supplement, but therapeutic claims require careful evidence.

## History and Naming

The name ornithine derives from Greek roots associated with birds.
It was historically identified in studies of excretory nitrogen metabolism.
Its role became clearer as the urea cycle was worked out in the early twentieth century.
Hans Krebs and Kurt Henseleit described the urea-forming cycle in 1932.
Their work established ornithine as a regenerated participant rather than a simple precursor.
That insight made cyclic metabolic pathways easier to conceptualize.
Today ornithine is taught as a canonical example of a non-protein amino acid with essential metabolic function.
It is also a useful reminder that biological importance is not limited to proteinogenic amino acids.
