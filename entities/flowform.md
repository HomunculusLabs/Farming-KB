## Description

A **flowform** is a sculptural water feature designed to guide water through a series of vessel shapes that generate rhythmic vortices, figure-eight flow patterns, and aeration cascades. Flowforms exploit the natural phenomenon of vortex shedding from bluff bodies in flowing water — alternating vortices are released from each side of an obstacle, creating complex and aesthetically beautiful flow patterns. The concept was developed at the **Virbela Institute** and is referenced by Mollison as an application of natural flow dynamics to [[water-management]] and landscape design. Flowforms serve both functional purposes (water aeration, purification) and artistic purposes in permaculture and biodynamic design.

- **Domain**: Hydrology / Landscape Design

## Key Facts

- Flowforms are based on the principle that whirlpools (vortices) are shed alternately from a fixed bluff body in flow, each side generating its own vortex with different rotation.
- The alternating vortex pattern creates figure-eight or lemniscate flow paths.
- Beautiful and complex forms are generated by these alternating vortex sets.
- The Virbela Institute developed flowform technology as practical applications of natural flow dynamics.
- Flowforms aerate water through rhythmic cascading, improving dissolved oxygen levels.
- Related to the Overbeck jet phenomenon — the simple involuted mushroom shape formed when fluid is jetted into another medium.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Flowforms have practical applications in mycological irrigation. Aeration of irrigation water through flowforms increases dissolved oxygen, which benefits both plant roots and beneficial soil microorganisms including mycorrhizal fungi and bacteria that compete with pathogens. Oxygenated water promotes healthy microbial communities in [[mushroom-substrate-preparation]], particularly in liquid culture systems where dissolved oxygen is a limiting factor. The rhythmic mixing action of flowforms can be used in compost tea production — a technique increasingly used in organic mushroom cultivation to create biological inoculants. The vortex dynamics also model the flow patterns found in natural streams where many aquatic and riparian fungi thrive, informing the design of artificial waterways in integrated aquaculture-mushroom systems.

## Design Principles

- Vessel shapes are derived from natural flow forms found in streams, river bends, and coastal inlets.
- The cascade rhythm mimics natural water movement more closely than mechanical aeration.
- Materials include stone, concrete, ceramic, and metal — chosen for durability and aesthetic integration.
- Scale ranges from desktop models to large landscape installations.
- Can be gravity-fed, requiring no energy input beyond the water's own potential energy.
