## Overview

**Ranunculus occidentalis** (western buttercup) is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Ranunculaceae. It is native to western North America, ranging from southern Alaska to northern California and eastward to the Rocky Mountains.

The species is one of the most common buttercups in Pacific Northwest meadows and open woodlands. Its glossy yellow flowers appear from April through July, making it a familiar sight in regional grasslands and disturbed habitats.

Western buttercup plays an important ecological role as an early-season nectar source for native pollinators. It is often one of the first wildflowers to bloom in moist meadow communities after snowmelt.

The species is sometimes confused with the introduced creeping buttercup (Ranunculus repens), but can be distinguished by its erect, non-stoloniferous growth habit. It is also smaller-flowered than the tall buttercup (Ranunculus acris).

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Ranunculus occidentalis was described by Thomas Nuttall in 1838 from specimens collected during his western expedition. The species belongs to section Ranunculus within the genus, characterized by erect stems and deeply lobed basal leaves.

Plants grow 20–60 cm tall from a fibrous root system. Basal leaves are long-petiolate, broadly ovate in outline, and palmately divided into three primary lobes with further toothed margins.

Stem leaves are smaller and sessile, becoming increasingly linear toward the inflorescence. The stems are erect, sparingly branched, and covered with fine appressed hairs.

Flowers are solitary or in loose cymes of 2–5, each 1.5–2.5 cm in diameter. Sepals are reflexed, and the five glossy yellow petals measure 8–15 mm long.

Achenes are flattened, 2–3 mm long, with a short curved beak. They form an oblong head at maturity, typically containing 20–40 achenes per receptacle.

The glossy petal surface is caused by a thin layer of epidermal cells containing carotenoid pigments. This reflective quality enhances visibility to insect pollinators in the often-overcast Pacific Northwest spring.

Chromosome number is 2n = 14, consistent with most diploid members of section Ranunculus. Polyploid populations have not been documented in this species.

The species name "occidentalis" refers to its western North American distribution. Nuttall originally placed it in a broad concept that has since been split into several closely related taxa.

Hybridization with Ranunculus acris has been reported where the introduced species overlaps with native populations. These hybrids show intermediate morphology but are typically sterile.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species ranges from southern Alaska through British Columbia, Washington, Oregon, and into northern California. Isolated populations extend east to Idaho, Montana, and the western slopes of the Alberta Rockies.

It occupies moist to mesic grasslands, open oak woodlands, meadow margins, and disturbed sites from sea level to approximately 1800 m elevation. The plant tolerates a range of soil types but prefers loamy to clay-rich substrates with moderate moisture.

Three varieties are recognized: var. occidentalis (coastal), var. cultratus (interior), and var. nelsonii (Alaskan). These differ primarily in leaf dissection depth and achene beak length.

The coastal variety is found primarily west of the Cascade Range in maritime climates. Interior populations experience greater temperature extremes and typically produce more deeply divided leaves.

Western buttercup frequently co-occurs with other spring wildflowers including camas (Camassia quamash), fawn lily (Erythronium oreganum), and western bleeding heart (Dicentra formosa).

In the Willamette Valley, the species is associated with remnant native prairie fragments. These prairies have declined by over 99% since European settlement, making habitat conservation critical for long-term persistence.

## Ecology

Ranunculus occidentalis is a spring-blooming species that provides early pollen and nectar resources for native bees, syrphid flies, and small beetles. Its flowers are open-access, allowing generalist pollinators to visit freely.

The species contains ranunculin, a glycoside that hydrolyzes to protoanemonin upon tissue damage. This compound deters most herbivores, though some specialist insects feed on Ranunculus foliage despite the toxicity.

It functions as an early-successional species in disturbed habitats, colonizing roadsides and clearings. Plants decline as taller perennials and shrubs dominate, but the seed bank can persist for several years.

Mycorrhizal associations have been documented in related Ranunculus species, though Ranunculus occidentalis appears less dependent on fungal symbionts than many co-occurring forbs. The fibrous root system efficiently captures surface moisture during the wet spring season.

Seed dispersal occurs primarily through mechanical ejection as achenes dry and the receptacle shrinks. Water and animal fur can also transport achenes to new sites.

Foliage begins to senesce by mid-summer as soils dry out, with above-ground parts dying back entirely. The root crown persists and produces new shoots the following spring.

Slugs and snails occasionally feed on young shoots despite the ranunculin content. Damage is usually cosmetic and does not threaten established plants.

## Cultivation

Western buttercup is easily grown from seed sown in fall or early spring. Seeds require a period of cold stratification of 4–6 weeks to break dormancy.

Plants prefer full sun to partial shade and consistently moist, well-drained soil. They are hardy in USDA zones 4–8 and tolerate winter freezing without protection.

In garden settings the species can be aggressive, spreading by both seed and stolons. Deadheading after bloom reduces self-seeding and prevents unwanted colonization of adjacent plantings.

The plant has no major pest or disease problems. It is occasionally affected by powdery mildew in humid conditions but recovers without intervention.

Germination rates are typically 60–80% after proper stratification. Seedlings emerge in early spring and reach flowering size within one growing season under favorable conditions.

Division of established clumps can be performed in early spring or fall. Each division should include several growing points and a portion of the fibrous root mass.

Plants tolerate moderate foot traffic and can be used in casual lawn settings where a native wildflower component is desired. They naturalize readily without becoming invasive in mowed areas.

The species is not commonly available in the nursery trade. Seeds can be collected from wild populations with appropriate permits for restoration purposes.

## Ethnobotany and Uses

Several Coast Salish and Interior Salish groups used Ranunculus occidentalis as a topical counterirritant for joint and muscle pain. Crushed leaves were applied briefly to the skin, where protoanemonin produces a warming, rubefacient effect.

The plant was not consumed internally due to its toxicity. Protoanemonin can cause severe gastrointestinal irritation, and dried hay containing buttercup remains toxic to livestock.

Western buttercup has limited ornamental use due to its weedy habit. It is occasionally included in native wildflower mixes for meadow restoration projects in the Pacific Northwest.

Some indigenous communities used the plant as a dye source, producing a pale yellow color on wool fibers. The dye is not lightfast and fades within weeks of exposure to sunlight.

The species has been studied as a bioindicator for grassland health in the Willamette Valley. Its presence correlates with moderate disturbance regimes and native plant diversity.
