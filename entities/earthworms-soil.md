## Description

Earthworms are among the most important macrofauna in soil ecosystems, functioning as ecosystem engineers that dramatically alter soil structure, nutrient availability, and biological activity. In permaculture, earthworms are considered a keystone indicator of soil health and a primary agent of soil regeneration. Their biomass in healthy soils can be enormous, vastly exceeding the above-ground livestock carrying capacity of the same land. Mollison presents earthworms as central to the living soil paradigm that replaces the mineral-only view of conventional soil science.

## Classification

- **Phylum:** Annelida
- **Class:** Clitellata
- **Subclass:** Oligochaeta
- **Order:** Opisthopora / Crassiclitellata
- **Common families:** Lumbricidae (temperate), Megascolecidae (tropical/subtropical)
- **Soil classification tier:** Macrofauna (2-20 mm body width)

## Key Facts

### Biomass and Abundance
- On semi-arid poor pasture supporting only 3-6 sheep per hectare, the same land may carry up to 65 tonnes of earthworms per hectare.
- Even lower estimates of 2-5 tonnes of pasture grubs per hectare greatly exceed visible livestock biomass.
- Soil recycled by earthworms varies from 2 to 150 tonnes per hectare per year (equivalent to 0.5-25 cm depth of soil turned over annually).

### Nutrient Cycling
- Turnover of nitrogen by earthworms exceeds that of the litter fall of plants.
- Worm casts concentrate nutrients, particularly nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, in plant-available forms.
- Worm casts bring base minerals (calcium, magnesium) to the soil surface through capillary action.
- Worm-processed soil has higher pH, greater organic matter content, and improved crumb structure compared to unprocessed soil.

### Soil Structure
- Earthworm burrowing creates macropores that allow water infiltration, gas exchange, and root penetration.
- Burrows act as "pump pistons" (like a train in a tunnel), drawing in and exhausting both waste gases and atmospheric gases.
- Worm casts form stable soil aggregates that resist erosion.
- Casts can bring up minerals from deep soil profiles, enriching surface layers.

### Population Variability
- Populations vary dramatically based on soil conditions.
- Acid soils of coniferous and oak forests yield few earthworms.
- Humic peats support very few worms.
- Rich loams with adequate organic matter support the highest populations.
- Earthworms are rare in compost piles but plentiful in mulch.

### Vulnerability
- Earthworm populations decline sharply under tillage, which destroys their burrow networks and exposes them to predation.
- Pesticides, herbicides, and synthetic fertilisers reduce worm populations.
- Waterlogging for extended periods kills earthworms.
- Heavy vehicle compaction destroys their habitat.
- Fire and burning eliminate surface organic matter that worms depend on.

### Earthworm Casts and Contamination
- Worm casts from contaminated pig, sheep, or domestic wastes can contain excess zinc.
- Animals penned in galvanized areas produce excess zinc in pen wastes.
- Kevin Handreck recommends a limit of 10% worm casts in potting mixes.
- This is particularly relevant for urban gardeners using worm farms fed with household waste.

### Interaction with Other Soil Organisms
- Earthworm burrows provide channels for root growth and movement of mesofauna.
- Worm activity creates favourable conditions for bacteria and fungi.
- Casts are colonised by microorganisms that further process organic matter.
- Earthworms are part of the food web: they are prey for birds, moles, and some ground beetles.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Earthworms are perhaps the single best indicator of soil health in permaculture systems. Their presence signals adequate organic matter, good soil structure, appropriate moisture, and absence of toxic contamination. Mollison's data showing that pasture can support 65 tonnes of earthworms per hectare alongside only 3-6 sheep demonstrates that the real productivity of land is underground. Every permaculture practice — mulching, composting, minimal tillage, diverse plantings, green manures — supports earthworm populations. In return, earthworms provide free soil cultivation, nutrient concentration, aeration, drainage, and organic matter processing. A permaculture garden without earthworms is a clear signal that something is fundamentally wrong with the soil management approach.

## Earthworm Species

Three ecological groups dominate temperate soils: epigeic (surface-dwelling,
compost-feeders like Eisenia fetida), endogeic (topsoil-dwelling, soil-feeders
like Aporrectodea caliginosa), and anecic (deep-burrowing, surface-feeders
like Lumbricus terrestris). Each group processes organic matter differently.
