## Description

Indigo carmine (also known as Acid Blue 9 or FD&C Blue No. 2) is a synthetic blue dye widely used in the [[staycare-fungal-treatment-of-textile-industry-wastewater]] for dyeing denim and other fabrics. It is one of the most common industrial dyes discharged into waterways from textile mills, subject to EPA daily allowable release standards. Tradd Cotter investigated the use of mushroom mycelium for mycoremediation of indigo carmine-contaminated wastewater from textile mills near his farm in South Carolina, discovering that while oyster mushrooms could colonize and fruit on denim fabric, they were not the most efficient species for decolorization.

## Classification

- **Category**: Industrial chemical / Synthetic dye / Environmental pollutant
- **Chemical name**: 5,5′-Indigodisulfonic acid sodium salt
- **Also known as**: Acid Blue 9, FD&C Blue No. 2, E132, indigotine
- **CAS number**: 860-22-0
- **Molecular formula**: C₁₆H₈N₂Na₂O₈S₂
- **Appearance**: Blue powder or granules

## Key Facts

- The primary dye responsible for the blue color in denim jeans and many other textiles
- Textile mills are permitted by the EPA to release limited quantities into waterways under daily allowable standards
- Persistent environmental pollutant that resists natural degradation
- Cotter's initial experiments grew oyster mushrooms on old jeans to test decolorization
- Oyster mushrooms (*[[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-pleurotus-ostreatus]]*) colonized and fruited well on cotton denim but were not effective at decolorizing the indigo carmine
- Turkey tail mushrooms (*[[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]]*) and several other species are more efficient at the decolorization process
- The discovery that old cotton clothing can support fruiting oyster mushrooms led to the broader technique of cultivating mushrooms on natural fiber textiles

## Mycoremediation Research

Cotter's work with indigo carmine illustrates a broader principle in mycoremediation: different fungal species have different [[fungal-enzymatic-capabilities]] for breaking down specific pollutants. While oyster mushrooms excel at colonizing cellulosic substrates (cotton, cardboard, straw), their lignin-modifying enzyme systems are less effective at degrading certain synthetic dyes compared to white-rot fungi like turkey tail, which produce more aggressive peroxidase and [[singh-fungal-laccase-enzymes-bioremediation]].

## Applications in Cultivation

The indigo carmine research led indirectly to the development of cultivation techniques using old cotton clothing, denim, hemp fabric, sisal rope, and bamboo textiles as [[mushroom-growing-substrates]]. This finding has implications for disaster relief (debris as substrate), waste reduction (textile waste as cultivation media), and survival foraging (clothing as food source in emergencies).

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Indigo carmine represents the intersection of mushroom cultivation, mycoremediation, and industrial pollution management. The research demonstrated that mushroom mycelium can colonize dyed textiles for food production even when the primary goal (decolorization) requires different species. It also highlighted the importance of species selection for specific mycoremediation targets — a key principle for practitioners designing bioremediation systems.
