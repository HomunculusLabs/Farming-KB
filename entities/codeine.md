## Overview

**Codeine** (methylmorphine) is an opiate alkaloid found naturally in the opium poppy (*[[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] (when combined with non-opioid analgesics) and Schedule V for certain low-dose preparations. International scheduling varies, with many countries restricting its availability due to abuse potential.

## Chemical Properties

Codeine has the molecular formula **C₁₈H₂₁NO₃** and a molar mass of 299.37 g/mol.

Its systematic IUPAC name is (5α,6α)-7,8-didehydro-4,5-epoxy-3-methoxy-17-methylmorphinan-6-ol.

The compound differs from morphine by a single methyl group substitution at the phenolic hydroxyl at position 3, which significantly reduces its affinity for the mu-opioid receptor.

Codeine phosphate is the most commonly used pharmaceutical salt form, appearing as a white crystalline powder soluble in water.

It has a melting point of approximately 154–156 °C for the free base form.

## Pharmacology

Codeine acts primarily as a prodrug, with approximately 5–15% of an administered dose being metabolized to morphine by CYP2D6 via O-demethylation.

This metabolic conversion is responsible for the majority of its analgesic effect, making codeine's efficacy highly dependent on individual CYP2D6 phenotype.

Approximately 5–10% of the population are CYP2D6 ultra-rapid metabolizers, producing substantially more morphine from codeine and experiencing enhanced effects with increased risk of toxicity.

Conversely, 5–10% of individuals are poor metabolizers with negligible CYP2D6 activity, experiencing little to no analgesic benefit from codeine.

Codeine itself has weak direct agonist activity at mu-opioid receptors, estimated at approximately 0.1 times the potency of morphine.

It also exhibits weak activity at kappa and delta opioid receptors, though these contributions to its clinical effects are considered minimal.

Additional metabolic pathways include [[glucuronidation]] to codeine-6-glucuronide (an active metabolite with weak analgesic properties) and N-demethylation to norcodeine.

The prodrug nature of codeine distinguishes it from most other clinically used opioids, which act directly on opioid receptors without requiring metabolic activation.

## Effects and Dosage

At standard analgesic doses (30–60 mg orally), codeine produces mild analgesia, slight sedation, and a sense of well-being.

The onset of action occurs within 30–45 minutes following oral administration, with peak plasma concentrations reached at approximately 1–2 hours and a duration of 4–6 hours.

As an antitussive, codeine acts on the medullary cough center at doses of 10–20 mg, effectively suppressing cough reflex without the higher doses needed for analgesia.

Common side effects include constipation, nausea, drowsiness, dizziness, and pruritus, consistent with the general opioid side effect profile.

Respiratory depression is significantly less pronounced than with morphine at equianalgesic doses, contributing to codeine's historically favorable safety reputation.

In overdose, codeine produces opioid toxidrome symptoms: pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression, decreased consciousness, and hypotension.

Ultra-rapid metabolizers face elevated risk of respiratory depression even at standard doses due to rapid morphine accumulation.

Fatal interactions with alcohol and other central nervous system depressants are a significant clinical concern.

## Medical Use and Legal Status

Codeine is indicated for the management of mild to moderately severe pain where the use of an opioid analgesic is appropriate.

It is frequently formulated in combination products with acetaminophen (paracetamol), aspirin, or ibuprofen, which enhance analgesia through complementary mechanisms.

As an antitussive, codeine remains one of the most effective prescription cough suppressants, though its use has declined in many countries due to safety concerns.
