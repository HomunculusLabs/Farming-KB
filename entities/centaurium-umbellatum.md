## Overview

Centaurium umbellatum, commonly called common centaury or feverwort, is a biennial or annual herb widely distributed across Europe and western Asia.

It is one of the most familiar European gentian family members, recognized by its pink to rose-purple flowers arranged in flat-topped cymes.

The plant has a centuries-long history as a bitter tonic and febrifuge in European folk medicine.

It typically grows 10–30 cm tall, forming a basal rosette in the first year and flowering stems in the second.

The species is sometimes confused with [[what-a-plant-knows-photoperiodism-flowering-time-measurement]].

Stem leaves are ovate-lanceolate, sessile, and clasping at the base.

Flowers are 8–12 mm across with five pink to rose-purple corolla lobes, each with a small basal scale.

The calyx tube is 5–8 mm long with five narrow, acute lobes.

Capsules are cylindrical, 8–12 mm long, dehiscing along two valves to release numerous tiny brown seeds.

## Distribution and Habitat

Centaurium umbellatum occurs throughout most of Europe, from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean and from the British Isles eastward to western Asia.

It has been introduced and naturalized in parts [[fungal-salt-tolerance-and-ion-homeostasis]], enabling colonization of coastal grassland habitats.

Elevation range extends from sea level to approximately 1,800 meters in southern European mountains.

## Chemistry and Traditional Uses

The bitter principles of Centaurium umbellatum are dominated by secoiridoid glycosides, particularly gentiopicroside, centapicrin, and swertiamarin.

Centapicrin is considered the primary bitter compound, contributing to the plant"s characteristic intensely bitter taste.

Additional constituents include xanthones (eustomin and demethyleustomin), triterpenes, and phenolic acids.

In traditional European herbalism, the flowering herb was infused as a digestive bitter, appetite stimulant, and treatment for fevers.

The common name "feverwort" reflects its historical use as a febrifuge across rural European communities.

Nicholas Culpeper recommended centaury for cleansing the blood and treating wounds in his 17th-century herbal.

Modern herbal practice uses Centaurium preparations primarily as digestive tonics, often in combination with other bitter herbs.

The aerial parts are harvested during flowering when glycoside concentrations peak.

## Ecology

Centaurium umbellatum is a facultative biennial that can complete its [[agaricus-campestris-biology-and-life-cycle]] as an annual under favorable conditions.

Flowers are visited by a wide range of generalist pollinators including hoverflies, bees, and small butterflies.

The species exhibits protandry, with anthers maturing before stigmas, promoting cross-pollination.
