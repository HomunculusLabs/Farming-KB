# Ring Garden (Atoll Technique)

## Description

The ring garden is a circular garden design developed for coral atoll and calcareous island conditions, where the impenetrable platen layer makes conventional gardening impossible. The technique involves breaking through the platen in a circular pattern, filling the resulting pit with organic mulch, and irrigating from a single central drip point. Mollison describes it as combining maximum linear planting footage with minimum water delivery infrastructure in one of the most space-efficient garden designs for arid island conditions.

## Classification

- **Type**: Garden design technique — specialized for coral atolls
- **Shape**: Circular (ring)
- **Diameter**: 4-5 feet effective (limited by drip water lateral spread of 2-2.5 feet)
- **Water delivery**: Single central drip point with wick distribution
- **Soil replacement**: Imported [[humus]] and mulch replacing sand over broken platen
- **Climate suitability**: Tropical arid islands, atolls, calcareous cays

## Key Facts

- Provides approximately 12 running feet of row crop within a 4-foot diameter circle, all within reach of a single drip point.
- The circular geometry allows drip water to extend laterally approximately 2-2.5 feet in all directions from the center, achieving complete soil moisture coverage.
- The bottom of a properly dug ring garden touches the water table, creating a passive "wick watering" system in addition to the [[drip-irrigation]].
- Inner edges are planted with cabbages, peas, and beans; a reinforcing wire cylinder in the center supports climbing cucumbers and beans.
- Sprinkler systems must NOT be used on atolls — they lose water to hot winds and surface evaporation while accelerating platen reformation beneath.
- The drip system feeds into a fibrous organic wick that distributes moisture evenly without surface evaporation.
- Platen reforms within 12 months if the area is not maintained with growing plants — the living root systems of established plants keep the platen cracked and permeable.
- The center of the ring garden is described as "a very pleasant place to sit" — Mollison recommends leaving gaps for the gardener.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

The ring garden demonstrates multiple [[permaculture-design-principles]] in miniature:

1. **Edge maximization**: The circular form maximizes the planting edge (12 linear feet) within the minimum area (4-foot diameter), achieving extraordinary space efficiency.

2. **Single-point resource delivery**: One drip point serves the entire garden through geometric optimization, minimizing infrastructure.

3. **Stacked functions**: The garden provides food production, water table management, platen disruption, and human amenity simultaneously.

4. **Passive systems integration**: The wick watering and water table contact create a largely self-regulating moisture system.

5. **Modular scalability**: Multiple ring gardens can be arranged in patterns — circles, lines, or other configurations — to scale production while maintaining the efficiency of individual units.

## Design Variations

- **Circle patterns**: Multiple rings arranged for maximum linear footage and hose efficiency
- **Linear patterns**: Possible alternative where geometry of the site favors rows
- **Keyhole integration**: Rings could incorporate keyhole access paths for maintenance
- **Tiered rings**: Varying depth to access different moisture zones

## Construction Process

1. Shovel away surface sand to expose the platen layer
2. Break up the platen in a circular pattern using picks or bars
3. Heap excavated sand around the perimeter to form a raised ring
4. Fill the circle with imported mulch, compost, and organic matter
5. Install a central drip emitter connected to water supply
6. Create a fibrous organic wick at the drip point for moisture distribution
7. Plant vegetables around the ring edge and climbing crops on central support
8. The base of the mulch layer should reach the water table for wick irrigation

## Limitations

- Requires imported organic matter, which is scarce on atolls
- Must be maintained actively — abandonment leads to platen reformation
- Maximum effective diameter is limited to approximately 5 feet by water physics
- Not suitable for all root crops that need greater depth
- Dependent on consistent drip water supply
