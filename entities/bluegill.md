## Bluegill (Lepomis macrochirus)

## Description

The bluegill is a freshwater sunfish native to North America and one of the most extensively studied species in pond aquaculture. An insectivore that also consumes plankton and small invertebrates, it serves as both a primary food fish and a forage species in permaculture polyculture systems. Its role in pond dynamics makes it central to understanding fish yield optimisation.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Animalia
- **Phylum**: Chordata
- **Class**: Actinopterygii
- **Order**: Perciformes
- **Family**: Centrarchidae (sunfishes)
- **Genus**: Lepomis
- **Species**: L. macrochirus

## Key Facts

- Native to eastern and central North America, widely introduced elsewhere
- Insectivorous feeding strategy -- consumes aquatic insects, zooplankton, and small crustaceans
- Typical stocking rate of approximately 3,900 fish per hectare in monoculture
- Prolific breeder -- will overpopulate ponds unless predators are present to cull young
- Widely used as forage fish for [[largemouth-bass]] in recreational fishing ponds
- Market size typically around 200 g

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Mollison uses bluegill as a primary case study in fish pond polyculture dynamics. The species demonstrates several critical [[permaculture-principles]]:

### Trophic Level and Yield
As an insectivore, bluegill occupies a mid-level trophic position. In fertilised ponds:
- Bluegill alone (3,900/ha): 186.4 kg/ha/year
- Bluegill with Gambusia: 470.6 kg/ha (an 18-fold increase!)
- Bluegill with bass and fathead minnows: 282.6 kg/ha (66% increase over bass-bluegill alone)

### Predator-Prey Dynamics
- Bluegill alone: 316.6 kg/ha
- Bluegill plus bass: 484.4 kg/ha (53% increase through controlled predation)
- Without predators, bluegill overbreed and stunt, producing large numbers of unmarketable small fish

### Food Type Reversal
In fertilised ponds without supplementary food, bluegill can outproduce catfish. However, when pelleted food is provided, catfish dramatically outperform bluegill. This demonstrates the importance of matching species selection to the intended food management strategy.

### Polyculture Principles
Bluegill exemplify Mollison's principle of judicious admixture of species. Yield increases of 40-200% are achievable through appropriate polyculture combinations. Bluegill serve functions of:
- Forage conversion (insects and plankton to fish protein)
- Population regulation (when paired with predators like bass)
- Waste cycling (consuming decomposer organisms)

## Cultivation Notes

- Requires predator species (bass, pike, snakehead, or soft-shelled turtles) to prevent overpopulation
- Optimal stocking requires careful balance between forage availability and breeding control
- Stunted populations develop rapidly in overcrowded conditions
- Compatible with mussel and plant additions for water quality improvement

## Stocking Rate Dynamics

Bluegill demonstrate a critical stocking rate threshold beyond which the proportion of unmarketable fish increases dramatically. At 5,000/ha, bluegill yield 316.2 kg with 97.5% marketable (above 200 g). At 10,000/ha, yield increases to 903.2 kg but marketability drops to 50.4%. Fish below market size took an additional 7 months to reach the 200 g that fast growers achieved in just 6 months (Swingle, 1966).

This data highlights the selection opportunity: fast growers from any cohort can be identified and retained as future brood stock, progressively improving the population's growth characteristics over successive generations.

## Shallow Refuge Design

Mollison hypothesises that devoting 50% of a pond to cover or special habitat for small forage species would not reduce total pan fish yield, drawing an analogy to the 30% tree shelter rule for livestock in fields. This has direct implications for bluegill pond design, where shallow water refuges and vegetation zones should be planned as integral components rather than afterthoughts.
