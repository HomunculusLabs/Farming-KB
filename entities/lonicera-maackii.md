## Overview

Lonicera maackii, commonly called Amur honeysuckle or bush honeysuckle, is a
deciduous shrub native to East Asia. It has become one of the most widespread
and ecologically damaging invasive woody plants in [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]].

Amur honeysuckle forms dense thickets that shade out native understory plants,
alter soil chemistry, and reduce overall forest biodiversity. It leafs out
earlier in spring than most native species, giving it a competitive advantage.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Lonicera maackii was described by August Heinrich Rudolf Grisebach in 1844 and
is placed in the family Caprifoliaceae. It is named after the Russian botanist
and explorer Richard Maack, who collected specimens in the Amur River region.

The shrub is multistemmed and typically grows 3 to 6 meters tall, occasionally
reaching 8 meters under favorable conditions. Stems are hollow and pithy when
young, with bark that becomes shaggy and peeling on older branches.

Leaves are opposite, ovate to elliptic, and 3 to 8 centimeters long. They are
semievergreen, often retaining green foliage well into December in southern
parts of its range.

Flowers are paired in the leaf axils and are tubular, 1.5 to 2 centimeters long.
They are white to pale yellow, becoming cream or yellowish with age, and are
fragrant. Blooming occurs from April through June.

Fruit is a paired bright red berry approximately 6 to 8 millimeters in
diameter, each containing several small seeds. Berries persist on the plant
through late fall and into winter.

## Distribution and Habitat

In its native range, Lonicera maackii occurs in northeastern China, Korea,
Japan, and the Russian Far East. It typically grows in forest margins,
thickets, and riparian corridors.

In [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], it has naturalized across a vast area from the Great Plains
to the Atlantic Coast, from Wisconsin and Ontario south to Texas and northern
Florida. It is particularly problematic in the Ohio River Valley and the
Mid-Atlantic states.

The species tolerates a wide range of conditions including full sun to deep
shade, and from dry upland soils to seasonally flooded bottomlands. This broad
ecological amplitude contributes significantly to its invasiveness.

It is commonly found along forest edges, roadways, fence rows, abandoned
fields, and in disturbed urban woodlands. It spreads rapidly into intact
forest interiors via bird-dispersed seeds.
