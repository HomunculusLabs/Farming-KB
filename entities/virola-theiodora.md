# Virola theiodora

**Virola theiodora** (Spr.) Warb., commonly known as the **Cumala Tree**, is a large tree native to the western Amazon basin. It is probably the most important species of Virola used as a psychoactive snuff by indigenous peoples of the Amazon, containing both DMT and 5-MeO-DMT in its resin.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Family:** Myristicaceae (Nutmeg Family)
- **Genus:** Virola
- **Species:** V. theiodora
- **Authority:** (Spr.) Warb.
- **Common names:** Cumala Tree, Epena (snuff), Parica (snuff), Yakawana

The genus Virola comprises approximately 60 species of [[mollison-designers-moringa-and-multipurpose-tropical-trees]] distributed throughout Central and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]]. Several Virola species are used as psychoactive snuffs, but V. theiodora is generally considered the most important. The genus belongs to the Myristicaceae, the same family that includes nutmeg ([[myristica-fragrans]]), which also contains psychoactive compounds. The species epithet "theiodora" is derived from Greek roots meaning "gift of the gods," reflecting its significance to indigenous Amazonian peoples.

## Morphology

Virola theiodora is a slender tree 25 to 75 feet (7.5 to 23 meters) in height, native to the forests of the western Amazon basin.

The cylindrical trunk, approximately 1.5 feet (46 cm) in diameter, has a characteristic smooth bark that is brown mottled with gray patches. The leaves have a tea-like fragrance when dried and are oblong or broadly ovate, 3.5 to 13 inches (9 to 33 cm) long and 1.75 to 5 inches (4 to 11 cm) wide. The leaf arrangement is spiraled with prominent lateral veins.

The male inflorescences are many-flowered, usually brown- or gold-hairy, shorter than the leaves. The very small flowers are borne singly or in clusters of 2 to 10 and are strongly pungent. The fruit is subglobose, 0.4 to 0.8 inches (1 to 2 cm) by 0.2 to 0.6 inches (0.5 to 1.5 cm). The seed is covered for half its length by a membranaceous, orange-red aril. When the trunk or branches are wounded, a blood-red resin exudes from the inner bark, and this resin is the primary psychoactive material.

## Distribution

V. theiodora occurs in the tropical zones of [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]] and South America, with its primary use documented among indigenous peoples of the western Amazon basin. The species is found in Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru, growing in lowland [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]]. It is particularly abundant in the interfluvial regions between the Rio Negro and the Rio Solimoes.

## Ethnobotanical Uses

### Psychoactive Snuff (Epena/Parica)

The resin of the inner bark of V. theiodora is the primary ingredient in several Amazonian psychoactive snuffs, known by various indigenous names including **Epena** and **Parica**. The preparation involves scraping the inner bark, collecting the exuded resin, and sometimes toasting or fermenting it before grinding it into a fine snuff powder.

### Waika Use

The Waika (Yanomami) people are among the best-documented users of Virola snuffs. They employ the snuff in healing ceremonies, divination, and recreational contexts. Among the Waika, the snuff is blown into the nostrils of the user through long tubes by an assistant, allowing for the delivery of large doses of the psychoactive powder.

### Other Indigenous Users

Multiple Amazonian indigenous groups use Virola snuffs, including:

- **Yanomami** (Waika) — use Epena for [[metzner-shamanic-healing-and-divination-paradigm]]
- **Bora** and **Miraña** — use Parica snuff in ceremonial contexts
- **Maku** — use Virola snuff for hunting magic and spiritual purposes
- **Barasana** — include Virola resin in their psychoactive snuff preparations

### Preparation Methods

The resin is typically prepared by:

1. Scraping the inner bark of the tree
2. Collecting the blood-red resin that exudes
3. Sometimes boiling or toasting the resin to concentrate it
4. Grinding with alkaline ashes (often from Elizabetha princeps bark)
5. Drying the mixture into a fine powder for nasal insufflation

The addition of alkaline plant ashes serves to free the alkaloids from their salt forms (converting them to freebase), which enhances absorption through the nasal mucosa. This technique — converting water-soluble alkaloid salts to lipid-soluble freebase forms for nasal or buccal absorption — demonstrates sophisticated indigenous understanding of pharmacological principles.

### Oral Use

Some Amazonian groups also consume Virola resin orally, usually in the form of pellets or as an ingredient in other preparations. Oral consumption requires the co-administration of MAO inhibitors (typically from [[ayahuasca-and-banisteriopsis-caapi]] or other beta-carboline-containing plants) to make the DMT orally active, similar to the principle underlying Ayahuasca.

## Chemistry

The resin of Virola theiodora contains two major [[tryptamine]]:

- **N,N-Dimethyltryptamine (DMT)** — the primary visionary compound
- **5-Methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (5-MeO-DMT)** — a more potent but less visual tryptamine

The addition of alkaline plant ashes to the snuff preparation may serve to free the alkaloids from their salt forms, enhancing absorption through the nasal mucosa. The ratio of DMT to 5-MeO-DMT varies between individual trees and populations, which may contribute to the variability in subjective effects reported by different indigenous groups.

Other minor constituents may include:

- **N-Methyltryptamine (NMT)** — a tryptamine precursor
- **Bufotenine (5-HO-DMT)** — present in some Virola species
