## Description

Ethylene (C2H4) is a simple hydrocarbon gas manufactured in anaerobic soil microsites
through the action of ferrous iron as a catalyst during the decay of organic matter.
It is a critical regulator of soil microbial activity and nutrient availability, forming
half of the oxygen-ethylene cycle that governs soil health in undisturbed ecosystems.
Present at 1–2 ppm in natural soils (Smith, 1981), ethylene is also a well-known plant
hormone regulating fruit ripening, flower senescence, and root growth.

## Classification

- **Category:** Soil gas and phytohormone
- **Chemical formula:** C2H4
- **Production site:** Anaerobic soil microsites
- **Catalyst:** Ferrous iron (Fe2+), not ferric iron (Fe3+)
- **Natural concentration:** 1–2 ppm in undisturbed soil

## The Oxygen-Ethylene Cycle

In natural, undisturbed soil, a continuous cycle operates at the microsite level:

1. Organic matter decays in an oxygen-depleted microsite.
2. Ferrous iron acts as catalyst, producing ethylene gas.
3. Ethylene inhibits (suspends) microbial activity at the site.
4. Ethylene diffuses outward from the anaerobic zone.
5. Oxygen floods back into the site, re-activating biological processes.
6. Nutrients bound during the anaerobic phase become available to plant roots.
7. The cycle repeats continuously under forests and grasslands.

## Key Facts

- Under balanced oxygen-ethylene conditions, most soil nitrogen exists as ammonia —
  directly useful to plants and plant roots.
- The cycle is continuous in natural soils, maintained by the mosaic of aerobic and
  anaerobic microsites in well-structured crumb.
- Ethylene production requires ferrous iron — the process goes from decaying leaf to
  ethylene via a ferrous iron catalyst.

## What Disrupts Ethylene Production

- **Cultivation and aeration:** Converts soil to fully aerobic conditions.
- **Ploughing/digging:** Oxidises ferrous iron to ferric iron, blocking the catalyst.
- **Nitrate formation:** Aerated conditions convert nitrogen to nitrate and nitrite,
  which actively inhibit ethylene production.
- **Nutrient lock-up:** In aerobic conditions, plant nutrients bind tightly to ferric
  iron and become unavailable for root uptake.
- **Humus loss:** Aeration oxidises humus to CO2, removing the ethylene precursor material.

## Consequences of Disruption

- Plant nutrients become unavailable, locked in ferric iron compounds.
- Humus oxidises and is lost as atmospheric CO2.
- Root pathogens (mostly aerobic) flourish in ploughed soil.
- Nitrate nitrogen leaches from bare soil into waterways.
- Net nutrient loss occurs via atmospheric pollution, stream pollution, and soil depletion.
- Soil shifts from fungal-dominated to bacterial-dominated communities.

## Smith's Recommendations for Restoration

1. Minimise all soil disturbance.
2. Apply surface mulch (not incorporated) — old leaves are the best ethylene precursors.
3. Use very small but frequent ammonia fertiliser until balances recover.
4. Maintain permanent pasture or forests.
5. Keep orchards with permanent green crop as mulch.
6. Practice no-dig or mulched gardening exclusively.
7. Use low- or no-cultivation field cropping with forest strips providing leaves.
8. Include legumes in proportion to natural plant associations at all successional stages.

## Related Soil Gases

- **Methane (CH4):** Produced in anaerobic soils/waters; soil supplies at least 16% of
  atmospheric methane.
- **Ammonia (NH3):** Released from actively nodulating legumes; used as plant nutrient.
  Also manufactured in sands via titanium/rutile (TiFe) in sunlight.
- **Carbon dioxide (CO2):** Product of humus decay and respiration; much of atmospheric
  CO2 and ammonia comes from soil.
- **Sulphuretted hydrogen (H2S):** From anaerobic decay and thiobacillus (sulphur bacteria).
- **Sulphur dioxide (SO2):** From anaerobic decomposition processes.

## Gaseous Exchange Mechanisms

- Earth tides (lunar) affect groundwater and soil air pressure — about 25 cm rise and fall
  across the continental USA.
- Pressure differentials from wind and weather systems draw air through soil crevices.
- Animal burrows act as Pitot tubes, creating pressure differentials for ventilation.
- Termites erect permeable surface structures for gas diffusion.
- A single broadleaf tree can increase transpiration area by 40x compared to bare soil.

## Relevance to Cultivation

- The oxygen-ethylene cycle is the strongest scientific argument for no-till agriculture.
- Ploughing disrupts the cycle, locks up nutrients, promotes pathogens, and loses humus.
- Mulching with old leaves is the simplest restoration method.
- Permanent vegetation maintains the aerobic-anaerobic mosaic naturally.

## Relevance to Mycology

- Fungal decomposition is linked to anaerobic microsites where ethylene forms.
- Mycorrhizal associations benefit from balanced nutrient availability the cycle provides.
- Ploughing shifts soil from fungal-dominated to bacterial-dominated communities.
- Fungi transport materials including nitrogen into decaying wood via hyphal networks.
