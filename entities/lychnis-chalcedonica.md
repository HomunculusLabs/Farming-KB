## Taxonomy and Morphology

Lychnis chalcedonica was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. Some modern taxonomic treatments place it within the genus Silene as Silene chalcedonica, though the name Lychnis chalcedonica remains widely used in horticulture.

The genus Lychnis is closely related to Silene and Agrostemma within the tribe Sileneae. Molecular phylogenetic studies suggest that Lychnis may be paraphyletic with respect to Silene.

Stems are erect, sturdy, and covered with short, stiff hairs. Leaves are lanceolate to ovate-lanceolate, 5–12 cm long, with entire margins.

The foliage is medium green and slightly rough to the touch due to the presence of short pubescence. Lower leaves are larger and may reach 15 cm, while upper leaves are progressively smaller toward the inflorescence.

Flowers are borne in dense terminal cymes of 10–50 individual blooms. Each flower is 2–3 cm in diameter with five deeply forked petals, creating the characteristic cross or star shape.

The calyx is tubular and prominently veined with ten longitudinal ridges. Stamens are ten in number with bright red filaments that complement the petal color.

The fruit is an ovoid capsule containing numerous small, dark brown to black seeds. Seeds are approximately 1 mm in diameter with a minutely tuberculate surface texture.

Seed viability is generally high, with germination rates of 70–85% under optimal conditions. Seeds do not require cold stratification and can be sown immediately after collection.

## Distribution and Habitat

Lychnis chalcedonica is native to a broad region of central and eastern Russia. Its range extends from the Volga River basin eastward through the Urals into western Siberia and northern Central Asia.

The species grows in moist meadows, along riverbanks, and in forest clearings at moderate elevations. It prefers full sun to light shade and fertile, moisture-retentive soils.

In its native habitat, it is found in tall-herb meadow communities alongside species such as valerian, meadowsweet, and various grasses. These habitats experience cold winters and warm, humid summers.

The [[continental-climate]] of its native range produces temperature extremes from -35 °C in winter to +35 °C in summer. This climatic adaptability explains the species' exceptional hardiness in cultivation.

It has naturalized sporadically in parts of Europe and North America. In cultivation, it is hardy to USDA zones 3–9, making it suitable for gardens across most [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] or directly outdoors after frost danger passes. Seeds germinate in 14–21 days at 18–21 °C.

Basal cuttings can be taken in spring from emerging shoots. These root readily in moist sand or perlite within three to four weeks.

Division of mature clumps can be performed every three to four years in spring or autumn. Established plants are long-lived and require minimal maintenance beyond staking in exposed sites.

Deadheading spent blooms extends the flowering period and prevents unwanted self-seeding. Some gardeners leave late-season seedheads for winter interest and to provide food for seed-eating birds.

The vivid scarlet flowers are highly attractive to hummingbirds and butterflies. This makes the plant a valuable addition to wildlife gardens and pollinator borders.

Named cultivars include 'Carnea' with salmon-pink flowers, 'Alba' with white flowers, and 'Dusky Salmon' with deeper orange-pink tones. The species form remains the most commonly grown in gardens.

The double-flowered form 'Flore Pleno' produces dense clusters of petal-packed blooms that resemble small carnations. It is slightly less vigorous than the single form but offers longer-lasting floral display.

## Ecology

In its native Russian meadow habitats, Lychnis chalcedonica is pollinated by a variety of insects including butterflies, moths, and bees. The bright red flower color is particularly effective at attracting diurnal lepidoptera.

The species shows moderate [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

In garden settings, Lychnis chalcedonica serves as a host plant for several species of moths in the Hadena genus. These insects lay eggs in the flowers, and larvae develop within the seed capsules.

Foliage is occasionally browsed by deer but is not a preferred food source. The plant contains saponins and trace amounts of toxic glycosides that provide moderate chemical defense against herbivory.
