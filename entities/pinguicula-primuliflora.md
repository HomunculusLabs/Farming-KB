## Overview

**Pinguicula primuliflora**, commonly known as the **southern butterwort** or **primrose butterwort**, is a carnivorous plant native to the southeastern [[bloomfield-rust-germ-tube-tactile-sensing-leaf-surface-navigation]] is densely covered with two types of glandular trichomes. Pedunculate glands produce the sticky mucilage that traps prey, while sessile glands secrete digestive enzymes.

The [[dmt-maoi-plants-united-states]], primarily the Gulf Coastal Plain from southeastern Mississippi through southern Alabama to the Florida Panhandle and northern Florida.

The species inhabits wet, sandy soils in bogs, seepage slopes, wet pine savannas, and margins of streams and ponds. It requires permanently moist to saturated substrates.

Elevation ranges from near sea level to approximately 100 meters. The species is most common in areas with acidic, nutrient-poor sandy or peaty soils.

Climate is warm temperate to subtropical, with hot, humid summers and mild winters. The species tolerates brief freezes but is not cold-hardy.

Habitat loss due to urban development, agriculture, and fire suppression has reduced wild populations. The species persists in protected areas including state parks and national forests.

Prescribed fire is important for maintaining the open, sunny conditions the species requires. Fire suppression allows woody vegetation to encroach and shade out butterwort populations.

## Carnivory and Prey Capture

The leaves of *P. primuliflora* function as passive flypaper traps. Insects that land on the mucilage-coated leaf surface become stuck and are gradually digested.

The mucilage is produced continuously by pedunculate glands across the leaf surface. The adhesive properties increase in humid conditions when prey activity is highest.

Unlike sundews, butterwort leaves do not exhibit thigmonastic movement. The leaf does not curl or fold around captured prey. Digestion occurs in situ on the leaf surface.

Prey consists primarily of small flying insects including [[query-how-to-control-fungus-gnats-organically]], fruit flies, midges, and small mosquitoes. The species is especially effective at capturing insects in humid, sheltered environments.

Sessile glands secrete proteases and other digestive enzymes onto the trapped insect. Nutrient absorption occurs through the leaf surface over a period of several days.

The leaf edge rolls inward slightly after prey capture, forming a shallow cup that helps retain digestive fluids. This marginal rolling is the only movement the leaf exhibits.

## Cultivation

*P. primuliflora* is one of the easiest butterworts to cultivate and is widely available in the horticultural trade. It thrives in a peat-sand or peat-perlite mix kept consistently moist.

The species prefers bright, indirect light but tolerates partial shade. Under strong light, leaves may develop pinkish-red margins and more pronounced glandular mucilage production.

Temperatures between 18–28 °C are ideal, with high humidity preferred. The species does not require a pronounced dormancy period, though growth may slow in winter.

Propagation is readily achieved through leaf cuttings placed on moist peat. Plantlets emerge from the leaf margins within 3–6 weeks. The species also produces plantlets from the leaf tips in terrarium conditions.

The species can self-pollinate, producing abundant seed. Seeds are small and dust-like, requiring light for germination. Sowing on the surface of moist peat yields good results.
