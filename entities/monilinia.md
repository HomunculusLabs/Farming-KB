## Monilinia

## Description

*Monilinia* is a genus of filamentous ascomycete fungi in the family
Sclerotiniaceae that causes **[[brown-rot]]** of stone fruits and pome
fruits, as well as **blossom blight** and **twig canker** diseases. The
genus is one of the most important groups of fruit pathogens worldwide,
responsible for significant postharvest losses in peaches, cherries,
plums, apples, and pears.

*Monilinia* species are notable for producing **mummified fruits** —
dried, shriveled, often dark brown to blackened fruits that remain
attached to the tree or fall to the ground, serving as overwintering
structures and sources of primary inoculum in the following season.

## Classification

- **[[kingdom-fungi]]
- **Phylum:** [[ascomycota]]
- **Class:** Leotiomycetes
- **Order:** Helotiales
- **Family:** Sclerotiniaceae
- **Genus:** *Monilinia*

The Sclerotiniaceae includes several important plant-pathogenic genera
characterized by the production of **apothecia** (cup-shaped fruiting
bodies) from sclerotia or mummified fruit. Related genera include
*Sclerotinia* and *Botryotinia*.

## Key Facts

### Important Species

- **Monilinia fructicola** — the most common cause of brown rot on
  **peaches, nectarines, cherries, and plums** in North America and
  many other regions; the most economically significant species
- **Monilinia laxa** — causes blossom blight and twig canker of **stone
  fruits**, especially almond and cherry; more common in Europe and
  the Mediterranean
- **Monilinia fructigena** — causes brown rot primarily of **pome
  fruits** (apples, pears) in Europe and Asia
- **Monilinia vaccinii-corymbosi** — causes **mummy berry** disease of
  blueberries, a significant disease in commercial blueberry production

### Disease Cycle and Symptoms

**Blossom Blight:**
- Infection of flowers in spring produces **blighted blossoms** that
  turn brown, wither, and may produce gray-tan conidial tufts
- Blossom blight serves as a bridge to **twig canker** development

**Fruit Brown Rot:**
- Fruit infections begin as **small, circular brown spots** that rapidly
  expand
- Infected fruit becomes **soft, brown, and rotten**, often covered
  with concentric rings of buff-colored conidial pads (spore-producing
  cushions)
- Severely infected fruit **desiccate and shrivel** into "mummies"
  — dried, leathery structures that may remain attached to the tree

**Mummies and Overwintering:**
- Mummified fruits serve as **primary inoculum sources** for the
  following season
- In spring, mummies on the ground may produce **apothecia** (cup-
  shaped sexual fruiting bodies) that discharge **ascospores** to
  initiate blossom infections
- Mummies remaining on the tree produce **conidia** (asexual spores)
  that infect flowers and fruit directly

### Environmental Conditions

- **Warm, wet conditions** during bloom favor blossom blight
- Fruit infection is favored by **high humidity and temperatures of
  20–25°C**
- Wounds (insect damage, hail, mechanical injury) greatly increase
  susceptibility to fruit infection
- Postharvest rot can develop rapidly at room temperature; **cold
  storage** slows but does not eliminate decay

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Fruit Cultivation Impact

- Brown rot is among the **top three most destructive diseases** of
  stone fruits worldwide, causing losses of 50–100% in untreated
  orchards during wet seasons
- Postharvest losses can be substantial even with fungicide programs,
  as latent (quiescent) infections may not be visible at harvest but
  develop during storage and marketing
- Integrated disease management combines **cultural practices** (pruning,
  sanitation, removing mummies), **chemical control** (fungicides during
  bloom and preharvest), and **postharvest treatments** (cold storage,
  controlled atmosphere)

### Mushroom Cultivation Relevance

- Fruit orchards affected by *Monilinia* brown rot may provide **compost
  substrate material** (prunings, fallen fruit) that should be thoroughly
  composted before use in [[mushroom-cultivation]]
- Mummified fruit on the ground can harbor viable *Monilinia* inoculum;
  proper composting temperatures (60°C+) will destroy the pathogen
- The Sclerotiniaceae family, which includes *Monilinia*, represents an
  important group of **sclerotium-producing fungi** — a survival
  structure also produced by cultivated mushrooms like *Morchella*
  (morels)

### Blueberry Mummy Berry

- *Monilinia vaccinii-corymbosi* has a particularly fascinating disease
  cycle on blueberries:
  - Mummified berries on the ground produce **small mushroom-like
    apothecia** in spring
  - Ascospores infect emerging **leaves and flower buds**
  - Infected leaves and flowers produce **conidia** that are
    **attractive to pollinators**
  - Pollinators carry conidia to **stigmas**, where the fungus
    germinates along with pollen and grows into the developing fruit
  - The infected berry fills with fungal **pseudosclerotia** and
    eventually mummifies
- This pathogen has evolved to **hijack pollinator behavior** for
  dispersal — one of the most remarkable examples of pathogen
  manipulation of insect vectors

- *Monilinia* species serve as models for studying **fruit-pathogen

- The mummy berry system (*M. vaccinii-corymbosi*) is studied for
