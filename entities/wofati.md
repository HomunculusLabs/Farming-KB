# Wofati

## Overview

A wofati is an earth-sheltered building design developed by permaculture
practitioner Paul Wheaton at the Wheaton Labs compound in Montana. The
name is a coined term with no etymology beyond being Wheaton's creation.

The design combines principles of earth-sheltered architecture, passive
solar heating, and natural building materials to create structures that
require minimal energy for heating and cooling. Wofatis are intended to
be low-cost, low-impact alternatives to conventional housing.

Unlike fully underground structures, wofatis use earth berms on three
sides with an open south-facing wall, typically glazed, to capture solar
gain. This semi-submerged approach provides thermal mass benefits while
maintaining natural light and ventilation.

## Design and Construction

A wofati is built into an earth berm on a hillside, with the building
partially set into the ground. The design follows a set of rules codified
by Wheaton, known informally as the "Wofati Code."

The core structural elements are round wood poles (typically un-milled
timber harvested on-site) arranged in an A-frame or modified post-and-
beam configuration. No concrete foundation is required; posts are set
directly on or into the earth.

The roof is the defining feature: a thick layer of living earth (minimum
18 inches / 46 cm) supported by a waterproof membrane and wood framing.
The earth roof provides insulation, thermal mass, and integrates the
structure into the landscape.

The south-facing wall is largely open, usually as large windows or a
glass wall, oriented to maximize winter solar gain. This glazing is the
primary heat source during cold months.

Earth is bermed against the north, east, and west walls to below the
roofline, insulating those walls with soil. The south face remains open
to sunlight and views.

## Materials and Techniques

Wofatis emphasize locally sourced, natural, and often salvaged materials.
The primary construction materials include round wood poles, earth, straw
bales (for insulation), and a waterproof membrane for the roof.

The roof waterproofing typically uses heavy-duty polyethylene sheeting
(EPDM or similar) sandwiched between the wood framing and the earth
layer. This membrane must be carefully installed to prevent water
infiltration, the most common failure point in earth-sheltered buildings.

Straw bales may be used as insulation within the walls, placed between
the structural posts and an interior plaster finish. Cob, light clay
straw, or wood chip-clay slip are alternative infill options.

The interior finish typically uses earthen plaster (cob plaster) over
the insulating layer. Floors are often poured earth, flagstone, or
salvaged materials.

No mechanical heating or cooling system is required in the design.
Thermal performance depends on proper orientation, adequate earth berm
depth, and quality of the roof insulation layer.

## Thermal Performance

The thermal strategy relies on three principles: earth berm insulation
(minimizing heat loss on three sides), passive solar gain through the
south glazing (providing winter heat), and thermal mass from the earth
roof and berm walls (buffering temperature fluctuations).

Interior temperatures are reported to remain between 10–21°C (50–70°F)
year-round without mechanical heating, even in the Montana climate where
winter temperatures regularly drop below −18°C (0°F). The earth roof
and berm create a significant thermal lag, preventing rapid temperature
changes.

Summer cooling is achieved through the earth mass, natural ventilation,
and shading of the south glazing. The earth berm maintains the interior
at roughly ground temperature, which stays relatively stable throughout
the year.

The passive solar aspect requires careful calculation of overhang angles
to permit full winter sun access while blocking high-angle summer sun.

## Advantages and Limitations

Advantages include extremely low construction cost (often under $10,000
using salvaged and on-site materials), minimal energy consumption for
climate control, small ecological footprint, integration with the
landscape, and use of natural, non-toxic materials.

Limitations include potential moisture problems if the roof membrane
fails, limited natural light compared to above-ground structures,
regulatory hurdles (earth-sheltered buildings often do not meet
conventional building codes), and the need for careful site selection
with proper drainage.

## Ecology and Integration

Wofatis are designed to integrate into permaculture food-production
systems. The earth roof can support low-growing vegetation, and the
disturbed soil from excavation is typically used for hugelkultur beds
or earthworks elsewhere on the site.

Wheaton Labs serves as the primary demonstration site, with multiple
wofati structures in varying stages of completion serving as living
proof-of-concept experiments.
