# Tagasaste

Tagasaste (Chamaecytisus proliferus), commonly known as tree lucerne,
is a fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing leguminous shrub or small tree
native to the Canary Islands. Widely planted in permaculture,
agroforestry, and pastoral farming systems across temperate regions,
tagasaste is valued for its high-protein fodder, rapid biomass
production, soil improvement capabilities, and ornamental qualities.
It has become naturalized in parts of Australia, New Zealand, and
the western United States. The species is remarkably drought-tolerant
once established and can produce palatable, nutritious browse for
livestock year-round in mild climates.

## Taxonomy and Naming

- **Order:** Fabales
- **Family:** Fabaceae (Leguminosae)
- **Subfamily:** Faboideae
- **Genus:** Chamaecytisus
- **Species:** C. proliferus
- **Authority:** (L.f.) Link
- **Synonyms:** Cytisus proliferus L.f., Genista proliferus (L.f.)
  Lam., Spartium proliferum (L.f.) Kuntze
- **Common names:** Tree lucerne, tagasaste, Canarian broom, Lucerne
  tree
- **Etymology:** "Tagasaste" is derived from the Guanche (aboriginal
  Canary Island) name for the plant. "Tree lucerne" refers to its
  similarity in forage quality to alfalfa (lucerne in British
  English).

The taxonomic history is complex, with the species having been
placed in Cytisus, Genista, and Spartium before its current
placement in Chamaecytisus. Some authorities still list it under
Cytisus proliferus.

## Description

### Growth Habit and Size
Tagasaste is an evergreen shrub or small tree typically growing 3-6
meters tall, though specimens under ideal conditions can reach 8-10
meters. It has a spreading, multi-stemmed growth habit with a
rounded to irregular crown. The growth rate is extremely rapid —
young plants can grow 1-2 meters per year under favorable conditions.
Lifespan is typically 10-20 years in cultivation, with productivity
declining after 8-12 years.

### Leaves
Leaves are trifoliate (three leaflets per leaf), with each leaflet
being obovate to elliptic, 10-25 mm long and 5-12 mm wide. The upper
leaf surface is dark green and glossy, while the lower surface is
paler and slightly hairy. Leaf margins are entire. The compound
leaves are arranged alternately along the stems, giving a dense,
leafy canopy.

### Flowers
Flowers are borne in dense terminal and axillary racemes from late
winter through spring (August-November in the Southern Hemisphere).
Individual flowers are typical papilionoid (pea-like), 12-18 mm long,
white with a yellowish keel and sometimes a faint pink or lavender
tinge. The flowers are highly attractive to bees and other pollinators,
making tagasaste an excellent nectar source.

### Pods and Seeds
Seed pods are linear-oblong, 20-40 mm long and 6-8 mm wide, covered
in short hairs, and black at maturity. Each pod contains 4-10 kidney-
shaped seeds that are olive-green to brown with a dark hilum. Seed
weight is approximately 5-7 mg per seed. The pods dehisce explosively
when dry, dispersing seeds several meters.

### Root System
The root system is extensive and deep, with a prominent taproot that
can penetrate 2-3 meters or more. This deep rooting contributes to
drought tolerance and the ability to access nutrients from subsoil
layers. Nitrogen-fixing root nodules form in association with
Rhizobium bacteria, contributing significant amounts of nitrogen to
the soil.

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Native range:** Canary Islands (Tenerife, La Palma, La Gomera,
  Gran Canaria), where it grows in montane and submontane zones at
  600-2000 meters elevation on volcanic soils.
- **Introduced range:** Widely naturalized in southeastern Australia
  (especially Tasmania and Victoria), New Zealand, Hawaii, California,
  Oregon, Chile, South Africa, and parts of Europe.
- **Climate requirements:** Hardy to approximately -8°C once
  established. Prefers Mediterranean climates with cool, wet winters
  and warm, dry summers. Tolerates annual rainfall from 350-1500 mm.
- **Soil tolerance:** Adaptable to a wide range of soil types
  including volcanic, sandy, loamy, and clay soils. Prefers well-
  drained, slightly acidic to neutral soils (pH 5.5-7.0). Intolerant
  of waterlogging and heavy, poorly drained clays.
- **Salt tolerance:** Moderate; can tolerate light coastal exposure
  but not heavy salt spray or saline water tables.
## Uses in Permaculture and Agriculture
- **Fodder:** The primary use of tagasaste is as high-protein browse
  for livestock. Leaf protein content ranges from 18-28% (dry
  matter basis), comparable to alfalfa. Palatability is generally
  good for cattle, sheep, goats, and deer. Annual dry matter yields
  of 8-15 tonnes/hectare are achievable with appropriate management.
- **Nitrogen fixation:** Fixes 100-200 kg N/ha/year through root
  nodules, significantly improving soil fertility for interplanted
  crops or pasture.
- **Shelter and windbreak:** Rapid growth and dense foliage make it
  an excellent windbreak species for exposed farms.
- **Bee forage:** Prolific winter-spring flowering provides critical
  nectar and pollen when few other species are in bloom.
- **Erosion control:** Deep root system and rapid establishment make
  it effective for stabilizing steep slopes and degraded land.
- **Soil improvement:** Leaf litter adds organic matter, and the
  nitrogen-fixing root system improves soil structure and fertility.
- **Ornamental:** Attractive white flowers and glossy foliage make it
  a useful landscape plant in suitable climates.
- **Wood fuel:** Wood burns well with moderate calorific value,
  suitable for firewood in areas where other fuel sources are limited.
## Cultivation
- **Propagation:** Primarily from seed. Seeds require scarification
  (hot water treatment or mechanical abrasion) to break physical
  dormancy. Pre-soaking in 80-90°C water for 1-2 minutes followed
  by 24 hours in cool water gives reliable germination. Cuttings
  from semi-hardwood can also be used but with lower success rates.
- **Establishment:** Direct seeding or transplanting seedlings.
  Seedlings establish quickly if planted into moist, weed-free soil.
  Weed competition is the main challenge in the first year.
- **Spacing:** In fodder plantations, spacing of 1-2 meters within
  rows and 3-5 meters between rows is typical, giving densities of
  1,000-3,300 plants per hectare.
- **Management:** Coppicing or pollarding at 0.5-1.5 meters
  encourages bushy regrowth and maintains accessibility for
  livestock browsing. Cutting frequency of 2-4 times per year
  maintains productive, leafy regrowth.
- **Pests and diseases:** Generally pest-free. Root rot can occur in
  poorly drained soils. Some insect browsing on young plants may
  require protection during establishment.
