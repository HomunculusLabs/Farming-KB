# Nepeta cataria (Catnip)

## Overview

Nepeta cataria, commonly known as catnip or catmint, is a hardy perennial herb in the
Lamiaceae (mint) family. Native to Europe, Asia, and parts of Africa, it has naturalized
widely across [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]]. The plant is famous for its profound behavioral effects on
domestic cats, caused by the iridoid terpenoid nepetalactone found in its essential oil.
Beyond its feline appeal, catnip has a long history of human use as a medicinal tea,
culinary herb, and garden companion plant. It is drought-tolerant, cold-hardy, and
excellent for pollinator gardens.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae → **Clade:** Tracheophytes → **Clade:** Angiosperms → **Clade:**
  Eudicots → **Clade:** Asterids → **Order:** Lamiales → **Family:** Lamiaceae →

## Botanical Description

- **Growth habit:** Upright to spreading perennial, 50–100 cm tall
- **Stem:** Square (typical of Lamiaceae), pubescent, branching from the base
- **Leaves:** Opposite, heart-shaped to triangular, 3–8 cm long, coarsely toothed,
  gray-green, covered in fine hairs
- **Flowers:** Small, two-lipped, white to pale lavender, arranged in dense whorled
  cymes; blooms June–September
- **Root:** Extensive fibrous root system with shallow rhizomes; can spread
  aggressively in favorable conditions
- **Aroma:** Strong minty, pungent scent from essential oils concentrated in leaves
  and stems

## Key Chemical Constituents

- **Nepetalactone** (4aα,7α,7aα-nepetalactone): The primary active compound, an
  iridoid monoterpene; constitutes 70–99% of the essential oil; responsible for
  feline attraction and insect-repellent properties
- **Nepetalic acid:** Degradation product of nepetalactone
- **Thymol:** Minor constituent with antimicrobial activity
- **Carvacrol:** Phenolic monoterpene with antioxidant properties
- **Caryophyllene:** Sesquiterpene with anti-inflammatory activity
- **Citronellol, geraniol, limonene:** Minor [[query-how-do-fungal-volatile-compounds-affect-plant-growth-and-other-microorganisms]] contributing to aroma
- **Rosalin:** Phenolic glycoside with reported mild sedative effects
- **Flavonoids:** Including luteolin, apigenin, and quercetin derivatives

## Feline Behavioral Effects

- Approximately 70–80% of domestic cats exhibit a behavioral response to catnip
- Effects include rolling, rubbing, pawing, licking, hyperactive play, and
  vocalization lasting 5–15 minutes
- The response is mediated through olfactory receptors, specifically binding to the
  feline vomeronasal organ
- Response is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait; kittens under 3–6 months
  typically do not respond
- Cats cannot overdose on catnip; effects self-limit and tolerance builds with
  frequent exposure, requiring a refractory period of approximately 2 hours

## Traditional and Modern Medicinal Uses

- **Nervous system:** Mild sedative and anxiolytic; traditionally used as a tea for
  insomnia, nervous tension, and headaches
- **Digestive:** Carminative for colic, flatulence, indigestion, and menstrual cramps;
  the tea is gentle enough for children
- **Respiratory:** Diaphoretic tea used for colds, fevers, and bronchial congestion
- **Topical:** Poultice of bruised leaves applied to cuts, abrasions, and insect
  bites; traditional remedy for toothache
- **Menstrual support:** Emmenagogue properties; used to promote menstruation and
  relieve associated discomfort
- **Modern research:** Preliminary studies suggest anxiolytic and antimicrobial
  activity; nepetalactone shows promise as a natural insect repellent comparable to
  DEET in some assays

## Preparation and Dosage

- **Tea (infusion):** 1–2 teaspoons dried herb in 250 mL boiling water, steeped
  10–15 minutes; drink 1–3 cups daily
- **Tincture:** 1:5 in 40% ethanol, 2–4 mL three times daily
- **Dried herb:** May be stuffed into pillows or sachets for mild sleep aid
- **Cat enrichment:** 1–2 teaspoons dried leaf offered loose or in toys; fresh
  stems and leaves also effective

## Insect Repellent Properties

- Nepetalactone is a potent repellent of mosquitoes, flies, cockroaches, and
  other insects; comparable efficacy to DEET in laboratory studies
- Essential oil can be diluted in carrier oil for topical application
- Dried catnip scattered in gardens or stored with grain deters pest insects
- [[comparison-companion-planting-reference-vs-jadam-monoculture-critique]] around vegetables reduces aphid and flea beetle pressure

## Cultivation and Permaculture Applications

- **Hardiness:** USDA zones 3–9; highly cold-tolerant and drought-resistant once
  established
- **Soil:** Prefers well-drained sandy loam, pH 6.1–7.8; tolerates poor and rocky
  soils
- **Light:** Full sun to partial shade; more aromatic in full sun
- **Propagation:** Easily grown from seed (stratify 1–2 weeks for best germination),
  cuttings, or division; seeds require light for germination
- **Companion planting:** Attracts [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] including parasitic wasps,
  tachinid flies, and pollinators; deters flea beetles, aphids, and squash bugs
- **Permaculture role:** Ground cover, [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]], [[nutrient-dynamic-accumulator-reference]],
  pollinator corridor plant; integrates well in herb spirals and food forest
  understories
- **Maintenance:** Cut back after flowering to encourage rebloom and prevent
  self-seeding; remove invasive runners as needed

## Safety and Contraindications

- Generally recognized as safe (GRAS) for human consumption in moderate amounts
- Avoid large medicinal doses during pregnancy (emmenagogue properties)
- May cause drowsiness when combined with sedative medications
- No known toxicity; excessive consumption may cause mild gastrointestinal upset
