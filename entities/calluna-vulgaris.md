## Overview

Calluna vulgaris, the common heather or ling, is a low-growing evergreen shrub in the family Ericaceae and the sole species in the genus Calluna. It is the dominant plant of European heathland and moorland ecosystems, forming extensive purple-flowered carpets across millions of hectares.

Heather has been economically and culturally significant for centuries, used for thatching, bedding, broom-making, dye production, and honey. Its dominance in upland landscapes has made it an iconic symbol of moorland across the British Isles and Scandinavia.

The plant supports a specialized invertebrate fauna and provides critical habitat for ground-nesting birds including red grouse, golden plover, and curlew.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Ericaceae → Ericoideae → Calluna → C. vulgaris.

Authority: (Linnaeus) Hull, British Flora (1808). Basionym: Erica vulgaris L.

The genus name derives from the Greek "kallunein" (to cleanse), referencing historical use of heather twigs as brooms.

C. vulgaris is a multistemmed evergreen shrub typically 20–50 cm tall, forming dense mats that can exceed 1 m in depth over decades.

**Stems** are slender, wiry, and densely clothed with overlapping scale-like leaves in four rows.

**Leaves** are minute, 1–3 mm long, opposite and decussate, borne in overlapping pairs that give shoots a characteristic columnar appearance.

Leaf margins are rolled inward, forming a groove that minimizes water loss in exposed windy habitats.

**Flowers** are borne in terminal racemes, each flower subtended by a conspicuous colored bract longer than the calyx.

The corolla is a short urn-shaped tube, 3–4 mm, with four small lobes. Color ranges from deep pink-purple to white.

Flowering occurs from July through September, with peak bloom typically in mid-August.

**Fruit** is a small loculicidal capsule, 2–3 mm, containing numerous minute dust-like seeds dispersed by wind.

## Distribution and Habitat

C. vulgaris has a wide Eurasian distribution, from Iceland and Norway southward to the Iberian Peninsula, and eastward through central Europe to the Ural Mountains.

It also occurs in parts of North Africa (Morocco, Algeria) and has been naturalized in eastern Canada, New Zealand, and Tasmania.

The species is the dominant component of Atlantic and sub-Atlantic heathlands, occupying acidic, nutrient-poor soils derived from sandstone, granite, and peat.

Optimal habitats include lowland heaths, upland moors, blanket bogs, and montane slopes up to 2,000 m elevation.

Heather requires full sun and cannot tolerate dense shade; canopy closure from invading birch or pine leads to its gradual elimination.

Fire is a key ecological process in heathland management, as mature heather becomes leggy and unproductive after 10–15 years.

Controlled burning creates a mosaic of age classes that maximizes both structural diversity and grouse food availability.

## Bioactive Compounds

Heather flowers contain flavonoids ([[quercetin]], myricetin, [[kaempferol]] derivatives), tannins, and phenolic acids.

The leaves produce arbutin and [[honey-based-herbal-preparations]] use flowering shoots in infusions for urinary complaints, rheumatism, and gout.

Heather honey, produced from the nectar of C. vulgaris, is distinguished by its amber color, thixotropic gel-like texture, and high antioxidant content.

The heather beetle (Lochmaea suturalis) is the primary herbivore, with outbreaks causing extensive defoliation cycles.

Heather provides nesting cover and food for red grouse (Lagopus lagopus scotica), whose diet is approximately 90% heather shoots in winter.
