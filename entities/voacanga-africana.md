# Voacanga africana

**Voacanga africana** Stapf is a tropical tree of West Africa belonging to the family Apocynaceae (dogbane family). The bark and seeds of this tree contain significant quantities of indole alkaloids of the iboga type, including ibogaine, making it a plant of both ethnobotanical and pharmacological interest.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Family:** Apocynaceae (Dogbane Family)
- **Genus:** Voacanga
- **Species:** V. africana
- **Authority:** Stapf
- **[[australian-magic-mushroom-common-names]]:** Voacanga, African Voacanga, Gaboon wood

The genus Voacanga comprises approximately 10 species of tropical [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]] distributed across Africa and Southeast Asia. V. africana was first described by the British botanist Otto Stapf in the late 19th century. The Apocynaceae family is well known for containing psychoactive species, including [[tabernanthe-iboga]] and the Asian rauwolfia-serpentina, both of which share the ibogaine-type indole alkaloid profile with Voacanga.

## Morphology

Voacanga africana is a tropical tree reaching considerable height in its native habitat, typically growing to 30 to 50 feet (10 to 15 meters) in height. As a member of the Apocynaceae, it produces characteristic milky latex when cut or wounded, a feature common to many members of this family.

The tree has simple, opposite leaves that are elliptic to oblong, with a glossy dark green upper surface and a lighter green underside. The leaves are typically 4 to 8 inches (10 to 20 cm) long. The flowers are white to pale yellow, fragrant, and arranged in clusters. The fruit is a follicle (a dry capsule that splits along one side) containing numerous seeds embedded in a pulp. The seeds are the primary plant part used for psychoactive purposes.

## Distribution

V. africana is native to tropical West Africa, where it grows in forested habitats. Its range extends from Ghana and Ivory Coast through Nigeria, Cameroon, Gabon, and the Democratic Republic of Congo. The tree is found in both primary and secondary rainforest, as well as in forest margins and disturbed areas.

Several related Voacanga species are also found in tropical Africa:

- **V. bracteata** Stapf — found in West and Central Africa
- **V. dregei** E. Mey. — found in southern Africa
- **V. grandiflora** (Miq.) Rolfe — found in West Africa
- **V. thouarsii** Roem. et Schult. — found in Madagascar and East Africa

## Ethnobotanical Uses

### Hunting Poison and Stimulant

In West Africa, the bark of V. africana is used as a hunting poison, stimulant, and potent aphrodisiac. The multiple applications of the bark reflect the complex pharmacology of its alkaloid content. As a hunting poison, the bark may be added to arrow tips or to pit traps to immobilize game animals.

### Visionary Use

Supposedly, the seeds of V. africana are used by African magicians in order to produce visions. The seeds are employed within a context of magical and spiritual practice, where the knowledge of their preparation and use is a closely guarded secret. The visionary experience produced by Voacanga seeds is described as similar to but milder than that of [[tabernanthe-iboga]], with iboga-like visual phenomena and dream recall.

### V. grandiflora

The seeds of the related **V. grandiflora** (Miq.) Rolfe are also used by magicians in West Africa for visionary purposes. Unfortunately, the details of these practices are not yet uncovered, as the knowledge of the magicians remains a closely guarded secret. The secrecy surrounding Voacanga use reflects the broader pattern of concealment that characterizes African traditional spiritual practices, where knowledge is transmitted through initiation and apprenticeship rather than through open documentation.

### Traditional Medicine

Beyond its psychoactive uses, V. africana has applications in traditional African medicine:

- **Fever treatment** — the bark is used as an antipyretic
- **Gastrointestinal complaints** — bark preparations for diarrhea and dysentery
- **Cardiovascular conditions** — used as a cardiotonic in some traditions
- **Pain relief** — the bark has analgesic properties attributed to its alkaloids

## Chemistry

The bark and seeds of Voacanga africana contain up to 10% indole alkaloids of the iboga type:

- **Voccamine** — the primary alkaloid, a complex iboga-type indole
- **Ibogaine** — a potent psychedelic with anti-addictive properties
- **Voacangine** — another significant alkaloid
- **Ibogamine** — an iboga-type alkaloid also found in [[plants-of-the-gods-tabernanthe-iboga]]
- **Tabersonine** — a precursor alkaloid in the iboga biosynthetic pathway
- **Vobasine** — a related indole alkaloid

The presence of ibogaine and related alkaloids connects V. africana chemically to [[tabernanthe-iboga]], the West African shrub from which ibogaine was first isolated. Both plants belong to the Apocynaceae family and share a similar indole alkaloid profile. However, the total alkaloid content and the specific ratios of individual alkaloids differ between the two specieserotoninarmacology

The primary psychoactive alkaloids in V. africana are ibogaine and voacangine, both of which act on multiple receptor systems:

- **Ibogaine** — acts as a NMDA receptor antagonist, kappa-opioid receptor agonist, and serotonin reuptake inhibitor; produces a complex psychedelic experience with oneirophrenic (dream-like) qualities
- **Voacangine** — a precursor to ibogaine with similar but milder effects
- **Voccamine** — has demonstrated antiarrhythmic and cardiotonic properties

The anti-addictive properties of ibogaine have received considerable scientific attention, with research suggesting that a single dose can significantly reduce opioid withdrawal symptoms and drug craving. These properties have led to the development of ibogaine-assisted therapy for addiction treatment, though legal [[palmer-about-mushrooms-foraging-basics-and-safety]] concerns have limited its clinical adoption.

## Safety Considerations

V. africana and its alkaloids carry significant risks:

- **Cardiotoxicity** — ibogaine can cause dangerous cardiac arrhythmias, including QT interval prolongation
- **Neurotoxicity** — high doses may cause cerebellar damage
- **Severe nausea** — ibogaine and related alkaloids are profoundly emetic
- **Drug interactions** — dangerous interactions with serotonergic and cardiac medications
- **Fatalities** — several deaths have been attributed to ibogaine use, primarily from cardiac causes

The therapeutic window between effective and dangerous doses of ibogaine is narrow, and medical screening and monitoring are considered essential for safe administration.

## Cultural Significance

Voacanga africana represents an important but poorly documented element of African ethnopharmacology. Its use by traditional magicians and healers in West Africa places it within a rich tradition of psychoactive [[mckenna-inquisition-suppression-of-plant-knowledge]] that has received far less scientific attention than its New World and Asian counterparts. ## Historical Documentation

The psychoactive properties of V. africana were first brought to wider attention through the work of Richard Evans Schultes and others who documented the ethnobotany of West Africa in the mid-20th century. The chemical characterization of Voacanga alkaloids followed, with ibogaine being first isolated from Voacanga species before its more famous identification in Tabernanthe iboga. Modern interest in V. africana has been driven primarily by the search for sustainable sources of ibogaine for addiction treatment research.

## Comparison with Other Iboga-Type Plants

V. africana can be compared with other plants containing iboga-type alkaloids:

- [[tabernanthe-iboga]] — the primary African source of ibogaine, used in Bwiti ceremonies
- tabernanthe-alboflorida — a related species with similar alkaloid profile
- eroscephalum-oblongifolium — another African plant with iboga-type alkaloids

V. africana differs from T. iboga in its growth habit (a tree versus a shrub), its total alkaloid content, and the specific ratios of individual alkaloids. The bark of V. africana contains a higher proportion of voccamine and voacangine relative to ibogaine compared to T. iboga root bark.

## Legal Status and Research

V. africana is not internationally controlled, though its ibogaine content may subject it to regulation in some jurisdictions. The plant has attracted interest as a potential sustainable source of ibogaine for addiction treatment research, as it grows faster and produces more biomass than T. iboga. Several research groups have investigated the extraction and purification of ibogaine from V. africana bark and seeds.
