# Salvia divinorum

**Salvia divinorum** is a psychoactive herb in the mint family (Lamiaceae), native to the cloud forests of the Sierra Mazateca in Oaxaca, Mexico. It is the only known natural source of salvinorin A, a potent neoclerodane diterpene and kappa-opioid receptor agonist. The Mazatec people have used the plant for centuries in divinatory rituals and healing ceremonies, referring to it as "ska María Pastora" (leaves of Mary the Shepherdess).

## Taxonomy

- **Family:** Lamiaceae (mint family)
- **Genus:** Salvia
- **Species:** S. divinorum
- **Authority:** Epling & Játiva, 1962

The species was first described scientifically by Carl Epling and Carlos Játiva based on specimens collected by Sterling Bunnell in 1962. It is one of approximately 900 species in the genus Salvia, but the only one known to produce psychoactive effects in humans.

## Botany

Salvia divinorum is a perennial herb that grows to over 1 meter in height. It produces large, oval, slightly hairy leaves (up to 20 cm long) on hollow, square stems characteristic of the Lamiaceae. The plant rarely produces viable seed and propagates almost exclusively through vegetative cloning — a rooted stem section placed in water or moist soil. Flowers are white with purple calyces, borne in whorls on tall racemes, but flowering is uncommon under cultivation.

The plant's native habitat is restricted to a narrow band of cloud forest between 300 and 1500 meters elevation in the Sierra Mazateca. It prefers partial shade, high humidity, and rich, well-draining soil.

## Chemistry

The primary psychoactive constituent is **salvinorin A** (C₂₃H₂₈O₈), a non-nitrogenous neoclerodane diterpene. Unlike most classical psychedelics, salvinorin A is not an alkaloid — it contains no nitrogen and does not act on serotonin receptors. Instead, it is a highly selective kappa-opioid receptor (KOR) agonist, making it pharmacologically distinct from other hallucinogenic plants.

Key properties of salvinorin A:
- **Potency:** Active at doses of 200–500 μg when vaporized (smoked), making it one of the most potent naturally occurring psychoactive substances known
- **Duration:** Effects last 5–15 minutes when smoked; 30–60 minutes when chewed as a quid
- **Mechanism:** Selective KOR agonism with no significant affinity for other receptor systems
- **Related compounds:** Salvinorin B through F are present in the plant but are inactive or significantly less potent

## Traditional Use

The Mazatec shamans of Oaxaca use Salvia divinorum primarily in ritual contexts:
- **Divination:** To diagnose illness, locate lost objects, or receive guidance from spirits
- **Healing ceremonies:** Often used alongside psilocybin mushrooms (see [[psilocybe-cubensis]]) and morning glory seeds ([[ipomoea-violacea]])
- **Method:** Fresh leaves are chewed as a quid or crushed and infused in water; the bitter juice is absorbed through the oral mucosa

Mazatec use is characterized by a dark, quiet setting, with the shaman guiding the experience through prayer and song. Recreational use is culturally disapproved of by traditional Mazatec practitioners.

## Pharmacology

Salvinorin A's selectivity for the KOR distinguishes it from all other natural hallucinogens:

| Property | Salvinorin A | LSD | Psilocybin |
|----------|-------------|-----|------------|
| Primary target | KOR | 5-HT2A | 5-HT2A |
| Nitrogenous | No | Yes | Yes |
| Active dose (smoked) | 200–500 μg | 25–100 μg | 10–20 mg |
| Duration | 5–15 min | 8–12 hr | 4–6 hr |
| Legal status (US) | Unscheduled (varies) | Schedule I | Schedule I |

The KOR pathway is associated with dissociation, introspection, and perceptual distortion, but typically not the euphoria or visual richness characteristic of serotonergic psychedelics.

## Legal Status

Salvia divinorum's legal status varies significantly worldwide:
- **United States:** Not federally scheduled as of 2026, but regulated or banned in many states
- **Australia:** Schedule 9 (prohibited substance)
- **Many European countries:** Banned or restricted
- **Mexico:** Unregulated but culturally protected

## Cultivation

The plant is relatively easy to cultivate from cuttings but sensitive to environmental conditions. Key requirements:
- **Light:** Dappled shade or indirect sunlight; direct sun causes wilting
- **Humidity:** High (60–80%); low humidity causes leaf curling and browning
- **Temperature:** 15–27°C; not frost-tolerant
- **Propagation:** Stem cuttings rooted in water or moist medium; seeds are extremely rare and often non-viable

## Research and Therapeutic Potential

Salvinorin A has attracted significant research interest due to its unique pharmacological profile:

- **KOR agonism research:** The kappa-opioid receptor system is implicated in mood regulation, stress response, and addiction. KOR antagonists have been investigated for depression and anxiety treatment
- **Neuroprotection:** Some preclinical evidence suggests KOR agonists may have neuroprotective properties
- **Substance use disorders:** Preliminary research has explored whether the intense but brief dissociative experience produced by salvinorin A might have therapeutic potential for conditions such as treatment-resistant depression and addiction
- **Structure-activity relationships:** The salvinorin scaffold has been extensively modified in medicinal chemistry research, producing analogs with varying KOR affinity, selectivity, and duration of action
- **Pharmacokinetics:** Salvinorin A has poor oral bioavailability due to extensive first-pass metabolism; it is effectively inactive when swallowed, requiring sublingual absorption, smoking, or vaporization

The unique non-alkaloid, non-serotonergic mechanism of salvinorin A makes it an important tool compound in psychopharmacology, distinct from the classical psychedelic pharmacophore shared by psilocybin, LSD, and DMT.

## History of Western Discovery

Salvia divinorum was largely unknown outside Oaxaca until the mid-20th century:
- **1938:** Jean B. Johnson, a anthropologist working with the Mazatec, first reported the plant's psychoactive use
- **1950s:** R. Gordon Wasson and Albert Hofmann visited Mazatec healers and observed salvia use ceremonies; Wasson brought specimens back for identification
- **1962:** Epling and Játiva formally described the species based on Wasson's collections
- **1982:** Alfredo Ortega and colleagues isolated salvinorin A and determined its structure
- **1990s–2000s:** The plant gained popularity as a recreational psychoactive, particularly after Daniel Siebert's research and advocacy brought it to wider attention via the internet
- **2002:** First published clinical human study of salvinorin A by Thomas Prisinzano and colleagues

## Varieties and Cultivars

Several named cultivars exist in cultivation:
- **Blosser ("Palatable"):** The most widely circulated clone, selected for relatively milder flavor and consistent growth
- **Wasson-Hofmann:** The original clone collected by Wasson and Hofmann; reportedly more potent but slower-growing
- **Appaloosa:** A variegated cultivar with distinctive leaf patterns
- **Luna:** Another widely circulated clone with large leaves

Because the plant rarely sets seed, the vast majority of cultivated specimens are vegetatively propagated clones of a small number of original collections, resulting in very low genetic diversity among commercially available plants.
