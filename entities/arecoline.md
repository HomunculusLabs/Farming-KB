# Arecoline

## Overview

Arecoline (1,2,5,6-tetrahydro-1-methyl-3-pyridinecarboxylic acid methyl ester) is a pyridine-class alkaloid and the primary psychoactive constituent of the areca nut (*Areca catechu*), the fourth most widely used psychoactive substance in the world after caffeine, nicotine, and alcohol.

It functions primarily as a muscarinic acetylcholine receptor agonist, producing parasympathomimetic effects including increased salivation, bronchoconstriction, and gastrointestinal stimulation, alongside central nervous system stimulation.

Despite its stimulant and mild euphorigenic properties that have sustained its use for millennia across South and Southeast Asia, arecoline is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the IARC due to its strong association with oral submucous fibrosis and oral squamous cell carcinoma.

## Chemical Properties

- **IUPAC name:** Methyl 1-methyl-1,2,5,6-tetrahydropyridine-3-carboxylate
- **Molecular formula:** C₈H₁₃NO₂
- **Molecular weight:** 155.19 g/mol
- **CAS number:** 63-75-2
- **Structure type:** Tetrahydropyridine ester; bicyclic nitrogen-containing heterocycle
- **Physical state:** Oily liquid at room temperature
- **Boiling point:** 209–210°C at 0.5 mmHg
- **Solubility:** Freely soluble in water, ethanol, chloroform, and ether
- **pKa:** 7.41 (conjugate acid), existing predominantly in protonated form at physiological pH
- **Optical activity:** Chiral molecule; naturally occurring arecoline is the (−)-enantiomer

## Pharmacology

### Receptor Activity

- **Muscarinic acetylcholine receptors:** Full agonist at M₁, M₂, M₃, M₄, and M₅ subtypes
  - EC₅₀ at M₁: ~3 µM
  - EC₅₀ at M₃: ~5 µM
- **Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors:** Partial agonist activity, contributing to stimulant effects
- **GABA-A receptors:** Weak positive allosteric modulation at high concentrations
- **Monoamine transporters:** Inhibits dopamine and serotonin reuptake at micromolar concentrations, contributing to its rewarding properties
- **TAAR1 (Trace amine-associated receptor 1):** Weak agonist activity detected in vitro

### Mechanism of Action

The parasympathomimetic action is primarily mediated through M₁ and M₃ receptor activation in the autonomic nervous system.

Central stimulant effects arise from a combination of muscarinic receptor activation in the CNS and monoamine reuptake inhibition, creating a profile that users describe as a combination of nicotine-like stimulation with mild MDMA-like warmth and increased sociability.

### Pharmacokinetics

- **Absorption:** Rapidly absorbed through oral mucosa; peak plasma levels within 15–30 minutes of chewing
- **Bioavailability:** ~25–40% (first-pass metabolism reduces systemic availability)
- **Distribution:** Widely distributed; crosses the blood-brain barrier readily
- **Metabolism:** Hepatic; primarily via esterase hydrolysis to arecaidine, then N-methylation and conjugation
- **Half-life:** Approximately 1 hour
- **Excretion:** Renal, primarily as arecaidine and glucuronide conjugates

## Effects

### Desired Effects

- **Stimulation:** Increased alertness, elevated heart rate, heightened energy (10–30 mg oral dose)
- **Euphoria:** Mild to moderate, described as warm and sociable rather than intense
- **Gastrointestinal:** Increased salivation, mild warming sensation in the abdomen
- **Sweating:** Profuse perspiration, especially facial and upper body
- **Duration:** 30–90 minutes depending on dose, preparation, and individual metabolism

### Adverse Effects

- **Acute:**
  - Nausea, dizziness, vertigo at higher doses
  - Tachycardia and palpitations
  - Hypersalivation followed by dry mouth (paradoxical effect)
  - Pupil constriction (miosis)
  - Tremor and restlessness
- **Chronic:**
  - ⚠️ **Oral submucous fibrosis:** Progressive stiffening of oral mucosa, precancerous condition
  - ⚠️ **Oral squamous cell carcinoma:** Strong dose-dependent association; IARC Group 1 carcinogen
  - ⚠️ **Oral leukoplakia:** White patches on oral mucosa, premalignant lesion
  - Gum recession, tooth staining, periodontal disease
  - Cardiovascular disease (increased risk of MI and stroke with chronic use)
  - Reproductive toxicity and developmental effects documented in animal models

## Synthesis and Biosynthesis

### Biosynthesis

Arecoline is synthesized in the areca nut (*Areca catechu*) from lysine via the cadaverine pathway:

1. Lysine decarboxylation → cadaverine
2. Cadaverine oxidation and cyclization → N-methylputrescine
3. Further oxidation and ring closure → arecoline precursor
4. Esterification of the carboxylic acid group → arecoline

The alkaloid content of dried areca nuts ranges from 0.1–0.5% fresh weight, with variation by cultivar, ripeness, and processing method.

### Chemical Synthesis

Laboratory synthesis proceeds via condensation of N-methyl-4-piperidone with ethyl chloroformate, followed by reduction and esterification. Several total syntheses have been reported but are of academic rather than commercial interest, as natural extraction from *Areca catechu* remains the primary source.

## Legal Status

- **International:** Not scheduled under the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances
- **United States:** Unscheduled; arecoline is not a controlled substance at the federal level, though some states restrict areca nut sales to minors
- **European Union:** Areca nut classified as a carcinogen under EU regulations; sales restrictions vary by member state
- **India:** Areca nut is a regulated agricultural commodity; gutka (areca nut + tobacco preparation) is banned in several states
- **Taiwan, Thailand, Malaysia:** Various restrictions on areca nut sales and chewing in public spaces

## History and Cultural Significance

Areca nut chewing dates back at least 2,000 years, with evidence from archaeological sites across the Pacific and South Asia.

The practice is deeply embedded in social customs across India, Taiwan, Papua New Guinea, and Southeast Asia, where areca nut (often wrapped in betel leaf with slaked lime — the "betel quid") serves as a social lubricant, ceremonial offering, and traditional digestive aid.

The alkaloid was first isolated and characterized by Jahns in 1888. Its pharmacological properties were investigated throughout the 20th century, with increasing attention to its carcinogenic potential from the 1980s onward.
