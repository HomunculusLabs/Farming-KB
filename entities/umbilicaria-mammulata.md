## Overview

Umbilicaria mammulata, commonly known as the smooth rock tripe or elephant hide lichen, is one of the largest foliose lichens in [[palmer-early-american-mycology-nineteenth-century]] history.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Umbilicaria mammulata was described by Erik Acharius in 1803 and has undergone several taxonomic revisions. It belongs to the order Umbilicariales within the class Lecanoromycetes, and the genus Umbilicaria contains approximately 50 species worldwide with centers of diversity in arctic, alpine, and montane regions.

The thallus is large, circular to irregularly shaped, and attached to the substrate by a single central holdfast or umbilicus. The upper surface is gray to brownish-gray, smooth to slightly wrinkled, and may develop a glossy sheen when wet. The thallus margin is entire to slightly undulate and often lighter in color than the center.

Growth rates are relatively slow, with annual radial extension estimated at 2 to 5 millimeters per year under optimal conditions. The largest specimens, which can exceed 30 centimeters in diameter, likely represent individuals that are several decades to over a century old.

The upper surface is relatively smooth compared to other Umbilicaria species, which is reflected in the common name "smooth rock tripe." However, older thalli may develop a network of fine cracks and a more rugose texture.

The lower surface is black to dark brown with a smooth to finely papillose texture. The central umbilicus is well-developed and provides the sole attachment point, allowing the thallus to be easily peeled from the rock surface. This feature distinguishes Umbilicaria from most other saxicolous lichens.

Thallus thickness ranges from 0.5 to 1.5 millimeters, and the internal anatomy includes a well-developed upper cortex, a loosely interwoven medulla, and a lower cortex. The medulla is white and can constitute up to 70 percent of the thallus thickness. This thick, cottony medulla is responsible for the lichen's water-holding capacity and provides the bulk of the material used as food.

The photobiont is a species of Trebouxia, embedded in a layer just below the upper cortex. The algal cells are arranged in a relatively loose network, with the hyphae of the mycobiont surrounding individual algal cells or small groups.

Apothecia are common, appearing as dark brown to black, lecanorine discs on the upper surface. They are 1 to 5 millimeters in diameter and may be abundant in well-developed thalli. Spores are simple, ellipsoid, colorless, and measure 10 to 18 by 5 to 9 micrometers.

Vegetative reproduction occurs through thallus fragmentation, which is likely the primary mode of dispersal. Fragments detached by wind, rain, or animal activity can colonize new rock surfaces if conditions are suitable for establishment.

## Distribution and Habitat

Umbilicaria mammulata is widely distributed in eastern North America, ranging from Newfoundland and Labrador southward through the Appalachian Mountains to northern Georgia. It also occurs in the Great Lakes region and the Ozark and Ouachita Mountains, though it is less abundant in these areas.

The species reaches its greatest abundance in the southern and central Appalachian Mountains, where suitable rock outcrops are extensive and moisture levels are adequate. Isolated populations in the Ozarks and Ouachitas represent disjunct occurrences at the western edge of the species' range.

The species is saxicolous, growing on exposed acidic rock surfaces, particularly granite, gneiss, and sandstone. It is most commonly found on vertical to overhanging rock faces and large boulders in open, well-lit situations. It avoids limestone and other calcareous substrates.

The species shows a strong preference for rock surfaces with moderate to steep inclination, which facilitates water drainage and prevents prolonged waterlogging. Horizontal surfaces are generally colonized by other lichen species that tolerate wetter conditions.

Umbilicaria mammulata is most abundant at elevations above 1,000 meters in the southern Appalachians, where it forms extensive colonies on rock outcrops, cliffs, and balds. At lower elevations and more northern latitudes, it occurs at near sea level on exposed rock surfaces.

In the Great Smoky Mountains National Park, Umbilicaria mammulata is among the most conspicuous lichens on high-elevation rock outcrops and is a defining element of the exposed rock community. Biomass estimates from Appalachian rock faces suggest that this species can contribute 100 to 500 grams per square meter of lichen cover.

The species requires good air quality and is sensitive to sulfur dioxide pollution. Historical declines were documented in areas near industrial centers, though populations have recovered in many regions following air quality improvements since the mid-twentieth century.

Recovery rates following pollution abatement have been documented in several eastern [[fungal-community-profiling-rock-mineral-surfaces-gadd]], contributing to soil formation.

The rate of biological weathering by Umbilicaria species has been estimated at 0.01 to 0.1 millimeters of rock surface per century. While individually modest, the cumulative effect across extensive rock surfaces over geological time scales contributes significantly to pedogenesis in montane landscapes.

The species serves as an important food source for terrestrial snails and slugs in moist forest habitats. Several species of land snails are specialized grazers of Umbilicaria, feeding on both the algal layer and the fungal tissue.
