# Boletus sub-tomentosus

## Description

Boletus sub-tomentosus, known as the suede bolete or olive-red bolete, is a medium-to-large
pored mushroom with a dry, velvety cap surface in shades of olive-brown to reddish-brown. The
pore surface on the underside is yellowish, often bruising blue-green when pressed or cut. The
stem is stout, sometimes curved, and typically reticulated near the apex. The flesh is pale,
soft, and may show a blue reaction on exposure to air.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Boletales
- **Family:** Boletaceae
- **Genus:** Boletus
- **Species:** Boletus sub-tomentosus

## Key Facts

- Palmer referred to this species as the "sud-tomentosus" in his plates and as the "olive-red
  Boletus" in Peck's catalogue nomenclature.
- Under litmus paper, this mushroom shows a marked acid reaction.
- When heavily charged with water from dew or rain, the spongy substance enters a state of
  acidulous fermentation, at which point it exhibits the most brilliant blue when pressed, cut,
  or broken.
- Palmer was made ill by specimens that were old and waterlogged, despite having previously eaten
  bushels of the same species from the same location.
- The key lesson: only young specimens with flesh too solid to admit of fermentation should ever
  be consumed.
- Mr. Casamajor found the blue matter to be soluble but could not explain its appearance upon
  exposure to air.

## Edibility

Edible when young and fresh. Becomes problematic when over-mature or waterlogged, as chemical
changes in the tissue produce acidulous fermentation. Palmer documented cases of illness from
consuming specimens that had absorbed excessive moisture. The illness manifested as purging and
vomiting but was not fatal. The species illustrates the principle that even known edible fungi
can become harmful through [[decomposition]] or chemical change.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Important case study in fungal chemistry: the acid reaction and blue bruising response are
  tied to water content and fermentation state.
- Demonstrates that edibility is not binary — condition, age, and handling matter critically.
- The blue oxidation reaction has been studied as a model for enzymatic browning in boletes.
- Palmer's experience with this species was pivotal in forming his theory that chemical change
  in the mushroom's substance, not inherent toxicity, was the cause of illness.
- Relevant to modern foragers as an example of why over-mature specimens of any species should
  be rejected.

## Identification Notes

- Cap: 8-15 cm, dry, velvety to suedelike; olive-brown to reddish-brown.
- Pore surface: yellow, bruising blue-green; tubes moderately long.
- Stem: 6-12 cm, solid, often curved, slightly reticulate near apex.
- Flesh: white to pale yellow, sometimes slowly bluing on cutting.
- [[spore-print]]: olive-brown.
- Smell: pleasant, faintly fungal.

## Chemical Properties

The species shows a marked acid reaction under litmus paper testing. The blue coloration that
appears on bruising is caused by oxidation of variegatic acid and related pulvinic acid
derivatives. Water-saturated specimens undergo acidulous fermentation, intensifying the blue
reaction and presumably altering the [[chemical-composition-of-hallucinogens]] in ways that affect edibility.

## Historical Notes

Palmer's account is notable for its precision: he identified the exact specimens that made him
ill, having been plucked from the same matrix from which he had previously consumed large
quantities safely. This observation — that a normally edible species became harmful through
environmental conditions — was ahead of its time and remains relevant to food safety in
mycophagy.
