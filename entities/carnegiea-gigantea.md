The saguaro is a keystone species of the Sonoran Desert ecosystem, providing critical nesting habitat, food, and shelter for dozens of animal species. Its striking silhouette has become emblematic of the American Southwest in art, film, and popular culture.

Saguaros grow extremely slowly, typically adding only 2.5 to 7.5 centimeters in height during their first eight years of life. [[king-stropharia-ecological-interactions-permaculture]]. Its flowers, fruits, and structural cavities provide resources utilized by numerous desert organisms.

Saguaro flowers bloom from late April through June, opening at night and remaining open until the following afternoon. The flowers are pollinated primarily by lesser long-nosed bats, which migrate northward through the saguaro range during the flowering season.

White-winged doves, Gila woodpeckers, and a variety of bee species also serve as important pollinators. Each flower produces a bright red, fleshy fruit containing approximately 2,000 small black seeds.

Gila woodpeckers and gilded flickers excavate nesting cavities in saguaro trunks, creating homes subsequently used by elf owls, [[gottlieb-peyote-cultivation-from-seed]] is straightforward but extremely slow. Seeds germinate readily in well-drained sandy media, though seedlings require protection from direct sun and desiccation for their first several years.

In the nursery trade, saguaros are often harvested from wild populations under strict permitting regulations. Legal harvest requires permits from the Arizona Department of Agriculture, and transport of wild saguaros is heavily regulated.

Indigenous peoples of the Sonoran Desert, including the Tohono O'odham, have harvested saguaro fruit for millennia. The fruit is traditionally boiled into a syrup used to make ceremonial wine and preserves.

The woody rib skeleton of dead saguaros has been used extensively for construction, fencing, and tool-making by indigenous communities. The ribs are lightweight, durable, and readily split into useful lengths.

Saguaro cactus boots, the callus structures that form around woodpecker nest cavities, are harvested as decorative items and were historically used as water containers by indigenous peoples.
