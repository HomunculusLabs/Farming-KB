**Leaves** are evergreen, broadly elliptic to oblanceolate, 10–20 cm long and 3–6 cm wide. The upper surface is dark green and glossy; the lower surface is pale green with a dense indumentum of scales.

Leaf margins are entire and slightly revolute. Petioles are 10–20 mm, with scattered scales.

**Flowers** are borne in large terminal corymbose inflorescences of 10–25 flowers. Individual flowers are funnel-shaped, 40–55 mm long, with 5 lobes.

Flower color ranges from lilac-purple to violet-blue, rarely white or pink. The throat often has darker spotting.

**Fruit** is a woody, dehiscent capsule 15–25 mm long, containing numerous small winged seeds. Capsules persist on the bush for over a year.

The shrub can reach 5–8 m in height and forms extensive multi-stemmed thickets. Stems are erect when young, becoming spreading with age.

## Distribution and Habitat

The native range of *R. ponticum* extends around the Black Sea coast from Bulgaria through Turkey, Georgia, and the Caucasus to the Lebanon-Syria border region.

Disjunct native populations also occur in the Iberian Peninsula (ssp. *baeticum*), the Rif Mountains of Morocco, and possibly southern Portugal.

The species was introduced to Britain in the late 18th century as an ornamental plant. It was widely planted on country estates, particularly in the 19th century, and naturalized rapidly in the mild, wet climate of western Britain and Ireland.

Invasive populations now cover an estimated 90,000 hectares in Britain alone, with significant infestations in Wales, western Scotland, and Ireland.

Outside its native range, the species has naturalized in parts of France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Germany, and New Zealand.

**Habitat** in invasive range includes acid oak-woodland, coniferous plantations, heathland, peat bog margins, and riparian corridors. It tolerates deep shade and can establish beneath closed forest canopy.

## Toxicity and Chemistry

All parts of *R. ponticum* contain grayanotoxins (andromedotoxins), a group of polyhydroxylated cyclic diterpenes that act as neurotoxins by binding to voltage-gated sodium channels.

The primary toxins include grayanotoxin I (andromedotoxin), grayanotoxin II (rhodotoxin), and grayanotoxin III. Concentrations are highest in nectar, young leaves, and flower buds.

Grayanotoxin poisoning in humans causes nausea, vomiting, dizziness, hypotension, bradycardia, and in severe cases cardiac arrhythmia and respiratory depression. Symptoms typically onset within 1–6 hours of ingestion.

"Mad honey disease" is caused by consuming honey produced from *R. ponticum* nectar. This is a well-documented public health issue in the Black Sea region of Turkey, where deliberate production of toxic honey has been used historically.
