## Overview

**Adansonia digitata**, the African baobab, is a iconic tree of sub-Saharan Africa belonging to the family Malvaceae.

It is the most widespread of the eight baobab species and reaches enormous size, with trunk diameters exceeding 10 meters.

Every part of the tree is used by local communities — fruit pulp, seeds, leaves, bark, and roots all provide food, medicine, or raw materials.

Its distinctive silhouette, with a massive trunk and sparse crown of branches resembling roots, has made it a symbol of the African savanna landscape.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Malvales
- **Family:** Malvaceae
- **Subfamily:** Bombacoideae
- **Genus:** Adansonia
- **Species:** A. digitata L.

The species was described by Linnaeus in 1768, with the genus named after the French naturalist Michel Adanson who documented it in Senegal.

It is the only baobab species native to mainland Africa, though it has been introduced to other [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]], the Caribbean, and parts of Australia.

The species thrives in hot, [[medicinal-mushroom-dietary-supplements]].

**Leaves** are eaten as a fresh or dried vegetable, providing protein, calcium, and iron.

Fresh leaves contain approximately 4% protein, 80 mg calcium, and 3.6 mg iron per 100 g.

Dried leaf powder is a traditional ingredient in soups and stews across West Africa.

**Seeds** are edible after roasting and contain roughly 30–40% oil, rich in linoleic and oleic [[stable-carbon-isotope-signature-fatty-acids-mycorrhizal-carbon-tracking]].

The seed oil is used in cooking and increasingly in cosmetics for its moisturizing properties.
