## Overview

**Aloe striata** Haw. is a stemless succulent in the family Asphodelaceae, native to the semi-arid regions of the Western and Eastern Cape, South Africa. It is distinguished by its pale blue-green leaves with striking pink to coral margins that lack the teeth typical of most Aloe species.

The species is widely cultivated as an ornamental landscape plant in Mediterranean and arid-climate gardens worldwide. Its drought tolerance, compact form, and showy coral-red flower spikes make it a favorite among landscape designers.

The species is commonly called "coral aloe" in reference to the coral-pink leaf margins and the salmon-colored flower racemes. The specific epithet "striata" refers to the longitudinal lines or striations visible on the leaf surfaces, especially on dry specimens.

Three subspecies are currently recognized. Subsp. *striata* is the most widespread and commonly cultivated form.

Subsp. *karasbergensis* occurs in the Karas Mountains of southern Namibia and has more robust leaves. Subsp. *komaggasensis* is restricted to the Namaqualand coast and has narrower leaves with less prominent striations.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

The genus *Aloe* comprises over 600 species distributed across Africa, Madagascar, and the Arabian Peninsula. Recent phylogenetic studies have reclassified the genus into several segregate genera, though *Aloe striata* remains in the core *Aloe* clade.

Aloe striata is a stemless or very short-stemmed species forming a solitary rosette. The rosette reaches 40–60 cm in diameter with 20–30 leaves arranged in a dense spiral.

Leaves are broad, lanceolate, 30–50 cm long and 8–12 cm wide at the base. The surface is smooth, pale blue-green to grey-green, often with faint longitudinal striations.

Leaf margins are the most distinctive feature — smooth, unarmed, and edged with a conspicuous pink to coral-red band. This colorful margin contrasts sharply with the pale leaf surface and intensifies in full sun.

Unlike most Aloe species, the leaf margins lack marginal teeth entirely. This smooth margin is a diagnostic character that distinguishes Aloe striata from similar species.

Inflorescences are branched panicles 60–90 cm tall, bearing 3–5 spreading racemes. Each raceme carries 20–30 tubular flowers that are coral-red to salmon-pink, 30–35 mm long.

Flowering occurs in late winter to early spring (July–September in the southern hemisphere). The nectar-rich flowers attract sunbirds and bees in the native habitat.

The species does not produce offsets or suckers. Reproduction in the wild is entirely by seed, which contributes to its solitary growth habit.

Fruits are oblong capsules 15–20 mm long that split open at maturity. Each capsule contains numerous flat, winged seeds adapted for wind dispersal.

## Distribution and Habitat

Aloe striata is endemic to the Western and Eastern Cape provinces of South Africa. Its range extends from the Little Karoo through the Eastern Cape valleys to the border of KwaZulu-Natal.

Plants grow on stony slopes, flats, and rocky outcrops in semi-arid vegetation. Elevations range from 200 to 1,200 m, primarily in rain-shadow valleys.

The habitat receives 200–400 mm of annual rainfall, predominantly in summer in the eastern part of the range. Winter rainfall supplements moisture in the western portion of its distribution.

Associated vegetation types include Valley Thicket, Spekboomveld, and Eastern Cape Succulent Thicket. Companion plants include *Portulacaria afra*, *Crassula ovata*, and various *Euphorbia* species.

The species is not currently considered threatened. However, illegal collection and habitat loss from agricultural expansion have been noted as emerging concerns.

Subsp. *karasbergensis* has a more restricted range and may warrant closer conservation monitoring. Its populations in southern Namibia are small and geographically isolated.

## Cultivation

Aloe striata is one of the easiest aloes to cultivate and is highly adaptable to garden conditions. It prefers well-drained soil in full sun to light shade.

Established plants are remarkably drought-tolerant, surviving months without supplemental irrigation. However, regular watering during the growing season produces more vigorous growth and better flowering.

The species tolerates temperatures to about −4 °C for brief periods. In colder climates, it performs well as a container plant that can be moved indoors during winter.

Propagation is primarily by seed, as the species does not produce offsets. Seeds germinate readily in 7–14 days at 20–25 °C when sown on moist, sandy medium.

Hybridization with other Aloe species is common in cultivation. Notable hybrids include crosses with *Aloe arborescens* and *Aloe ferox*, producing intermediate forms with varied flower colors.

The smooth leaf margins make this species safer for gardens with children and pets compared to toothed aloes. This practical advantage contributes to its popularity in residential landscaping.

## Ecology

Aloe striata is pollinated primarily by sunbirds (Nectarinidae) in its native habitat. The tubular, nectar-rich red flowers are perfectly adapted to bird pollination.

The species follows the CAM photosynthetic pathway. This allows efficient water use by opening stomata at night for carbon fixation.

Seeds are small, winged, and wind-dispersed. Each capsule contains numerous seeds that are released when the fruit dries and splits open.

The smooth, unarmed leaves lack the physical defense of toothed Aloe species. However, the bitter aloin compounds in the leaf tissue provide chemical deterrence against most herbivores.

During extreme drought, plants may close their rosettes and suspend growth. This drought avoidance strategy allows rapid recovery when rains return.

Root systems are shallow but extensive, adapted to capturing brief rainfall events. The fleshy roots store water and carbohydrates to sustain the plant during dry periods.

The species is relatively long-lived, with individual plants persisting for 20–30 years. Older plants may develop a short decumbent stem as lower leaves are shed.

## Horticultural Significance

The coral aloe is among the most widely planted ornamental aloes in Mediterranean-climate gardens worldwide. It is particularly popular in California, southern Europe, and Australia.

The species has received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit. This award recognizes plants of outstanding excellence for ordinary garden use.

It is widely used in xeriscaping and water-wise garden design. The architectural rosette form provides year-round structure with minimal maintenance requirements.

Mass plantings of Aloe striata create dramatic displays when in flower. The coral flower spikes rise above the foliage in late winter, providing color during a season when few other plants bloom.

The species is also suitable for large container cultivation. Specimens in terra cotta pots are a common feature in Mediterranean-style courtyard gardens and rooftop terraces.

In Southern California, Aloe striata is a staple of public garden design. It appears prominently at the Huntington Botanical Gardens and the San Diego Zoo Safari Park.

The species has no serious pest or disease problems when grown in appropriate conditions. Aloe mite occasionally deforms flower stalks but does not threaten plant health.
