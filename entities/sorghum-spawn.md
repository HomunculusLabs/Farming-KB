# Sorghum Spawn (Grain Spawn)

## Description

Sorghum spawn is mushroom mycelium grown on sorghum ([[sorghum-bicolor]]) seeds, serving as the inoculant for [[mushroom-substrate-bags]]. Sorghum grain is widely used in tropical and subtropical regions as a spawn carrier because of its availability, nutritional suitability, and physical properties. The sorghum-based grain spawn represents the critical intermediate stage between laboratory tissue culture on PDA and the final fruiting substrate [[contamination-prevention-in-mushroom-cultivation]].

## Classification

- **Category**: Grain spawn / mushroom inoculant
- **Base Material**: Sorghum bicolor (sorghum) seeds
- **Function**: Vehicle for transferring mycelium to bulk substrate
- **Stage**: Second stage in spawn production (after tissue culture on PDA)

## Production Process

### Preparation of Sorghum Seeds
1. **Selection**: Use clean, whole sorghum grains free from mold and debris
2. **Soaking**: Soak grains in water for 8-12 hours to hydrate fully
3. **Boiling**: Briefly boil the soaked grains (15-20 minutes) until expanded but not burst
4. **Draining**: Drain excess water thoroughly — moisture content is critical
5. **Drying surface**: Allow surface moisture to evaporate to prevent clumping

### Filling and Sterilization
1. Fill prepared sorghum into autoclavable bags or bottles (typically 500g-1kg per container)
2. Plug with cotton or filter material for gas exchange
3. Sterilize by autoclaving at 121°C for 1-2 hours
4. Allow to cool to room temperature in a clean environment

### Inoculation
1. Transfer under sterile conditions (laminar flow hood or glove box)
2. Cut small pieces of colonized PDA culture using sterile instruments
3. Place PDA pieces into the sorghum containers
4. Seal and shake gently to distribute the inoculum

### Incubation
1. Incubate at appropriate temperature (24-28°C depending on species)
2. Shake containers every 3-5 days to distribute mycelium evenly
3. Full colonization takes 10-21 days depending on species and conditions
4. Colonized spawn should appear completely white with no visible uncolonized grain

## Key Facts

- Sorghum is preferred in Thailand and Southeast Asia due to its local availability and low cost
- Each kilogram of sorghum spawn can inoculate 20-50 substrate bags depending on the system
- Proper moisture content of sorghum grain is critical: too dry and mycelium grows slowly; too wet and contamination risk increases
- Thailand spawn is noted as among the least expensive globally
- Spawn can be stored at cool temperatures (4-8°C) for several weeks after full colonization
- Quality spawn should be pure white, smell mushroom-fresh, and show no colored contamination spots

## Substrate Inoculation

Once sorghum spawn is fully colonized, it is used to inoculate substrate bags:
1. Substrate bags are pasteurized and cooled
2. Under clean conditions, sorghum spawn is mixed into the substrate
3. Typical inoculation rate: 1-3 tablespoons of spawn per substrate bag
4. Spawn is distributed evenly throughout the substrate for uniform colonization
5. Bags are sealed and moved to the incubation house

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Sorghum spawn production is the critical bridge between laboratory culture work and field-scale mushroom production. In the FAO disability [[mushroom-cultivation-disabilities-nine-week-training-program]], the spawn multiplication step on sorghum represents a skill that can be mastered by trainees with various disabilities. The manual emphasizes that understanding the full spawn production cycle — from tissue culture through sorghum spawn to substrate inoculation — gives mushroom farmers greater self-sufficiency and control over their operation quality.

Self-produced spawn significantly reduces costs compared to purchasing commercial spawn, improving the profitability of small-scale mushroom enterprises. The ability to produce quality spawn is a key differentiator between subsistence and profitable mushroom farming operations.

## Quality Control

Good sorghum spawn should colonize evenly, break apart without becoming wet paste, and recover quickly after shaking.
Any sour smell, green mold, black spots, bacterial slime, or uncolonized wet grain is a warning sign.
Because spawn multiplies into many substrate bags, a small contamination problem at this stage can become a large crop failure.
Careful labeling by strain, date, and batch helps growers compare performance and avoid using old or suspect spawn.
For training programs, these checks are practical skills as important as the laboratory steps themselves.
