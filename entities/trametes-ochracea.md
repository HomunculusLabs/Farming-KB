## Overview

**Trametes ochracea** (Pers.) Gilb. is a poroid basidiomycete fungus in the family Polyporaceae.

It is a white-rot saprotroph found on dead hardwood logs and branches across temperate and boreal regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

The species is closely related to the turkey tail (*Trametes versicolor*) and shares many ecological roles but is less commonly collected and studied.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Polyporales
- **Family:** Polyporaceae
- **Genus:** Trametes Fr.
- **Species:** T. ochracea (Pers.) Gilb.
- **Basionym:** Boletus ochraceus Pers. (1797)
- **Synonyms:** Polyporus ochraceus (Pers.) Fr., Coriolopsis ochracea (Pers.) Murrill, Coriolus ochraceus (Pers.) Pat.

The species epithet *ochracea* refers to the ochre-yellow coloration typical of fresh basidiocarps.

### Morphology

Basidiocarps are sessile to effused-reflexed, forming semicircular brackets 2–8 cm wide and 1–3 cm thick.

The upper surface is zonate with concentric zones of ochre, tan, and pale brown, often becoming darker and more furrowed with age. A fine tomentum covers the margin of actively growing specimens.

The pore surface is white to cream when fresh, staining brownish on handling. Pores are round to angular, 3–5 per mm. The context is tough and corky, white to cream-colored.

Spores are cylindrical to slightly allantoid, hyaline, smooth, and inamyloid, measuring 5–7 × 1.5–2.5 µm.

The hyphal system is trimitic, with generative hyphae bearing clamp connections.

## Distribution and Habitat

*T. ochracea* is widely distributed across Europe, North America, and temperate Asia.

It is particularly common in Scandinavia, the British Isles, and the Pacific Northwest of North America.

The species fruits almost exclusively on dead hardwood, especially birch (*Betula* spp.), oak (*Quercus* spp.), and alder (*Alnus* spp.).

Fruiting occurs from late summer through autumn, with basidiocarps persisting through winter in milder climates. It is occasionally found on standing dead trees but prefers fallen logs in advanced stages of decomposition.

## Ecological Role

As a white-rot fungus, *T. ochracea* secretes lignin-modifying enzymes including lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, and laccase.

These enzymes break down lignin in wood cell walls, enabling the fungus to access cellulose and hemicellulose as carbon sources.

This decomposition process is essential for nutrient cycling in forest ecosystems, returning carbon and minerals to the soil from dead woody material.

The species contributes to deadwood biodiversity by creating substrate for invertebrates and microorganisms.

## Chemistry and Bioactivity

Like other *Trametes* species, *T. ochracea* produces a range of secondary metabolites with biological activity.

Laccase enzymes from this species have demonstrated potential in bioremediation applications, particularly in the degradation of synthetic dyes and phenolic pollutants.

Polysaccharide fractions isolated from the fruiting body show immunomodulatory properties in vitro, similar to the better-studied polysaccharopeptide (PSP) from *T. versicolor*.

Triterpenoids and sterols have also been detected, though the pharmacological profile of this species remains less characterized than its close relatives.

## Edibility and Uses

*T. ochracea* is considered inedible due to its tough, corky texture. It is too woody for culinary use and is not known to be toxic.

The primary interest in this species lies in its ecological role and its potential for applied mycology research.

Laccase production makes it a candidate for industrial biotechnology applications including wastewater treatment and biobleaching in paper production.

### Bioremediation Potential

Laboratory studies have demonstrated that *T. ochracea* laccase can decolorize azo dyes and degrade phenolic compounds at rates comparable to those of *T. versicolor*.

The species tolerates a wider pH range in culture than some other white-rot fungi, which may prove advantageous for industrial scale-up.

Heavy metal tolerance has been observed in strains isolated from contaminated sites, suggesting a role in mycoremediation of polluted environments.

## Distinguishing Similar Species

*T. ochracea* is most easily confused with *T. versicolor*, from which it can be separated by its more uniformly ochre upper surface and less strongly zonate patterning.

*T. hirsuta* has a more prominently hairy upper surface and larger pores (2–3 per mm).

*Coriolopsis gallica* is larger and more robust, with a darker brown to gray upper surface that lacks the ochre tones characteristic of *T. ochracea*.

Microscopically, spore dimensions and hyphal features provide definitive identification.
