## Cortinarius praestans

## Description

*Cortinarius praestans* (commonly known as the surprising cortinarius or the magnificent cort) is one of the largest species in the genus *Cortinarius*, family Cortinariaceae, order Agaricales. It produces impressive, robust fruiting bodies with caps reaching 10–20 cm in diameter, coloured dark brown to violet-brown, often with a lilac tinge. The gills are initially violet, turning rusty-brown with age. The stem is thick, bulbous at the base, and shows remnants of the cortina. This ectomycorrhizal species associates primarily with broadleaf trees, especially oak and beech, in calcareous soils.

## Key Facts

- Recorded as having the **lowest radiocesium activity** (0.5 kBq/kg dry weight) among Slovenian fungi tested after the Chernobyl disaster (Byrne, 1988), despite being a member of the Cortinariaceae — a family known for cesium accumulation.
- This is a striking contrast to *[[cortinarius-armillatus]]* from the same study, which accumulated 43–44 kBq/kg — nearly 100 times more radiocesium.
- Demonstrates the extreme **within-family variation** in radiocesium accumulation, even among congeneric species.
- Considered one of the finest edible *Cortinarius* species, though extreme caution is required as many *Cortinarius* species are deadly poisonous and difficult to distinguish.
- Found in deciduous forests on calcareous (alkaline) soils, a more restricted habitat than many *Cortinarius* species.
- Rare and locally distributed across Europe, considered threatened in some regions due to habitat loss.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

*Cortinarius praestans* provides important comparative data in radioecology:

- **Species-specific accumulation**: The enormous range of radiocesium accumulation within *Cortinarius* (0.5 kBq/kg for *C. praestans* vs. 43–44 kBq/kg for *C. armillatus*) demonstrates that [[dighton-fungal-radionuclide-uptake-mechanisms]] is highly species-specific and cannot be generalized even within a genus.
- **Edibility concerns**: As a potentially edible species in a genus containing deadly toxic species (*C. rubellus*, *C. orellanus*), it illustrates the critical importance of accurate identification in mushroom foraging.
- **Conservation**: Its rarity and association with old-growth calcareous forests make it an indicator species for forest habitat quality.
- **Mycorrhizal specificity**: Association with broadleaf trees on alkaline soils contrasts with many radiocesium-accumulating *Cortinarius* species that associate with conifers on acidic soils, suggesting substrate chemistry influences radionuclide uptake.
- **Risk assessment**: Understanding within-genus variation is essential for accurate risk assessment of radiation exposure from wild mushroom consumption.

This species highlights the complexity of predicting radiocesium accumulation based on taxonomy alone and underscores the need for species-specific data in radioecological assessments.
