# Arctostaphylos uva-ursi

## Overview

*Arctostaphylos uva-ursi* (L.) Spreng., commonly known as bearberry or
kinnikinnick, is a low-growing evergreen subshrub in the family
Ericaceae. Native to circumboreal regions of the Northern Hemisphere,
it is one of the most widely documented medicinal plants in both
Western herbalism and Indigenous North American medicine.

The species is valued for urinary tract antiseptic properties attributed
to the glycoside arbutin. Its dense, mat-forming growth habit also
makes it an important groundcover for erosion control and a functional
element in permaculture and restoration landscapes.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Tracheophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Ericales
- **Family:** Ericaceae
- **Genus:** *Arctostaphylos*
- **Species:** *A. uva-ursi* (L.) Spreng. (1825)

**Basionym:** *Arbutus uva-ursi* L. (1753)

**Common names:** bearberry, kinnikinnick, pinemat manzanita, hog
cranberry, sandberry, rockberry. "Bearberry" derives from bears
heavily consuming the fruit. "Kinnikinnick" is Algonquian for "that
which is mixed," referring to the traditional smoking mixture.

## Morphology

**Growth form:** Evergreen subshrub forming dense mats 5–15 cm tall,
spreading 1–3+ meters via rooting stems.
**Leaves:** Alternate, obovate, 1–4 cm, dark green above, pale
beneath. Leathery, persisting 1–3 years.
**Flowers:** Small, urn-shaped, 4–6 mm, white to pale pink, in
short racemes of 5–15. Blooms April–June.
**Fruit:** Red drupe 6–9 mm, mealy when fresh, sweeter after frost.
Ripe August–October, persists through winter.

**Roots:** Shallow, fibrous, with ericoid mycorrhizal associations
for nutrient uptake in poor soils.

## Distribution and Habitat

**Range:** Circumboreal — Canada, northern US, Greenland, Iceland,
Europe (Scandinavia to Mediterranean mountains), northern Asia.

**Habitat:** Dry, rocky, sandy, or gravelly soils in open woods,
barrens, coastal dunes, alpine tundra. Sea level to 3,000+ m.
Tolerates acidic soils (pH 4.0–6.0) and nutrient-poor substrates.
Drought- and fire-adapted; resprouts from rhizomes.

## Bioactive Compounds

Primary medicinal constituents are concentrated in the leaves:

- **Arbutin** (6–9% dry weight) — hydroquinone β-D-glucoside;
  hydrolyzed in the urinary tract to hydroquinone, a urinary
  antiseptic
- **Hydroquinone** — active metabolite; antibacterial against *E.
  coli*, *Proteus*, *Pseudomonas*, *Staphylococcus*
- **Tannins** (15–20%) — galloyl and ellagitannins with astringent
  and anti-inflammatory effects
- **Ursolic acid** — triterpene with anti-inflammatory and
  hepatoprotective activity
- **Allantoin** — cell-proliferant and wound-healing compound
- **Flavonoids** — quercetin, kaempferol, myricetin glycosides

## Pharmacology and Medical Use

**Urinary tract infections:** The primary approved use in European
herbal medicine (EMA-approved traditional herbal medicinal product).
Arbutin is absorbed in the GI tract, transported to the kidneys, and
hydrolyzed to hydroquinone in alkaline urine, where it exerts
bacteriostatic activity against *E. coli* (causative agent in 80–90%
of uncomplicated UTIs).

**Dosage:** 400–800 mg arbutin daily (divided doses) for 7–14 days.
Longer use contraindicated (hydroquinone hepatotoxicity risk).
Urine alkalinization enhances conversion.

**Other uses:** Diuretic (tannin-mediated), wound healing (allantoin),
postpartum recovery (Native American traditions).

**Contraindications:** Pregnancy, renal disease, hepatic impairment,
children under 12. Extended use (>2 weeks) risks hepatotoxicity
and retinal damage.

## Ethnobotanical Use

Among the most historically significant medicinal plants in North
America. Indigenous peoples used leaves for urinary complaints,
gonorrhea, and kidney ailments. Dried leaves were mixed with tobacco
and other herbs as "kinnikinnick" — a ceremonial and recreational
smoking blend used by Algonquian, Cheyenne, Lakota, and many other
nations.

## Permaculture and Ecological Applications

- **Erosion control:** Dense mat stabilizes slopes, roadcuts, and
  coastal dunes; roots bind loose sandy or gravelly soils
- **Groundcover:** Living mulch under trees in food forests, suppressing
  weeds while tolerating overhead shade
- **Pioneer species:** Thrives on degraded soils, useful for mine
  reclamation and disturbed-land restoration
- **Wildlife value:** Winter food for birds (grouse, waxwings)
  and mammals (bears, foxes, deer); flowers provide nectar for
  native bees
- **Fire adaptation:** Resprouts vigorously after fire

## Cultivation

Hardy to USDA zones 2–7. Propagate by seed (stratify 60–90 days),
softwood cuttings, or division. Requires well-drained acidic soil,
full sun to partial shade. Drought-tolerant once established.
Virtually pest-free.

## Similar Species
