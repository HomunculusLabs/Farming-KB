# Bendigo Home Builder's Club

## Description

The Bendigo Home Builder's Club is a community labour exchange organisation described by [[bill-mollison]] in *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* as a practical example of [[informal-economy-and-community-currencies]] in action. Located in Victoria, Australia, the club operates as a mutual aid network where 35 families cooperate to build individual homes through shared labour. Instead of using cash payments, the system uses a time-based credit and debit system where one hour of labour equals one credit, regardless of the type of work performed. Mollison presents this as a model that any community can replicate for cooperative infrastructure projects of any kind, from housing to farm buildings to community facilities.

## Classification

- **Category:** Community organisation / Labour exchange
- **Domain:** [[permaculture-community-design]] economics, housing construction, cooperative labour
- **Location:** Bendigo, Victoria, Australia
- **Scale:** 35 member families
- **Related systems:** Community Barter Club, [[let-system]]

## Key Facts

- **Membership:** 35 people (families) participating in cooperative house building
- **Annual fee:** $5 per family per year, covering printing and distribution costs of the Club's newsletter
- **Unit of exchange:** Hours of labour with all labour considered equal regardless of skill type or complexity
- **Legal mechanism:** Uses a standard labour exchange form that is legally binding between members
- **Accounting:** For every hour of work performed, the recipient is debited and the donor is credited in the ledger
- **Labour Organiser:** A designated person manages the balance of payments and dispatches labourers to recipients
- **Credit requirement:** A recipient must have at least 60 hours in credit before receiving labour from others on their own project
- **Mutual roles:** Each member can be either a recipient or donor of labour at different times in the building cycle
- **Governance:** Minimal bureaucracy — newsletter communication and a single organiser role keep overhead low
- **Philosophy:** Embodies the principle that all labour has equal value when measured in time, not skill premium

## Operational Model

The Bendigo Home Builder's Club follows a round-robin pattern of mutual aid that could be described as sequential reciprocity:

1. Members join by paying a nominal annual fee ($5 per family)
2. Members contribute labour hours to other members' house-building projects on scheduled work days
3. Labour hours are tracked through legally binding exchange forms signed by both parties after each work session
4. A Labour Organiser coordinates dispatch of workers and tracks cumulative credit balances for all members
5. Members accumulate credit hours before becoming eligible to receive labour on their own building projects
6. The system continues cyclically until all present housing needs among members are met, then may disband or redirect

## Comparison with Other Labour Exchange Models

The Bendigo model is distinctive because it applies labour exchange to a single focused project type (housing) rather than a broad range of goods and services. The LET System and Community Barter Club cover general exchanges, while the Bendigo model achieves deep specialisation. This focus allows members to develop construction skills together, share specialised tools efficiently, and build institutional knowledge about local building codes, materials, and techniques.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

While the Bendigo model focuses on housing construction, its principles directly transfer to cultivation and mycology:

- Infrastructure such as [[mushroom-fruiting-chambers]], greenhouse construction, and composting facilities can be built cooperatively using the same labour exchange model
- Groups can pool labour for seasonal tasks like [[substrate-preparation]], mass inoculation days, or harvest events
- Small-scale cultivators can exchange labour hours during peak seasons without cash outlay
- The equal valuation of labour means all contributions from substrate mixing to marketing are valued as equivalent units of time
- Community-scale permaculture installations such as food forests, swales, and earthworks can be built using this mutual aid approach
- The model scales well to any infrastructure-building need a cultivation community might have, from raised beds to [[irrigation-systems]]

## Design Principles Embodied

1. **Use small and slow solutions** — builds houses one at a time through gradual cooperation rather than all at once
2. **[[permaculture-integrate-rather-than-segregate]] segregate** — combines housing needs with community bonding and skill sharing
3. **Use [[permaculture-principle-use-and-value-renewable-resources]] resources and services** — human labour as the primary renewable resource
4. **Design from [[permaculture-principle-design-from-patterns-to-details]]** — the round-robin pattern applies universally to cooperative building efforts

## Implementation Considerations

- The legally binding exchange form is essential for building trust and accountability among members
- A minimum credit threshold of 60 hours prevents free-riding and ensures reciprocity before receiving help
- The Labour Organiser role is critical and must be filled by someone who is organised and trusted by all members
- The model scales well to 35-50 participants but may need subdivision into smaller circles beyond that size
- Newsletter communication keeps all members informed of needs, availability, and upcoming project schedules
- Annual fees should be kept minimal to avoid creating financial barriers to participation
