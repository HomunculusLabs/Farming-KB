# Inocybe aeruginascens

A psychoactive Inocybe species notable for containing the rare compound aeruginascin, unique among known [[psilocybin-mushrooms]]. First described by [[jochen-gartz]] and romagnesi.

## Taxonomy

- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Inocybaceae
- **Genus:** Inocybe
- **Authority:** Originally described by Gartz and Romagnesi
- **Etymology:** "aeruginascens" refers to the greenish (aeruginous) coloration that develops with age

## Description

- **Cap (pileus):** 1-3 cm diameter, conical to convex, becoming broadly umbonate with age. Surface silky-fibrillose, radially striate toward margin. Color: pale ochre to brownish, often with a distinctive greenish tinge (hence the name). Margin frequently irregular.
- **Gills (lamellae):** Adnexed to sinuate, moderately crowded. Color: clay-brown to olivaceous brown, becoming darker with spore maturity. Edge often whitish and fringed.
- **Stem (stipe):** 3-6 cm long, 2-4 mm thick. Equal or slightly thicker at base. Surface pruinose (frosted) near apex, fibrillose below. Pale to brownish. No ring. Cortina (web-like veil) present in young specimens.
- **Flesh:** Thin, pale brownish. Odor: mild or slightly spermatic (characteristic of Inocybe genus). Taste: mild.
- **[[spore-print]]:** Dark brown to tobacco brown.
- **Spores:** Smooth, elliptical to bean-shaped (amygdaliform), 8-11 x 5-6 µm. Apex with prominent apical germ pore.
- **Chemical reaction:** Moderate blueing reaction on bruising, especially at stem base and gill edges.

## Distribution

- **Type locality:** Europe, first documented from France by Romagnesi
- **Germany:** Widespread in central and southern Germany. Gartz documented extensive collections from parks, gardens, and disturbed urban areas.
- **France:** Original collections from the Paris region
- **Habitat:** Mycorrhizal with broadleaf trees (particularly oak, beech, birch) and occasionally conifers. Found in parks, gardens, forest edges, along paths, and in disturbed ground. Often associated with planted trees in urban settings.
- **Season:** June to October in Central Europe
- **Frequency:** Locally common in suitable habitats; often overlooked due to small size and Inocybe genus reputation for toxicity

## Chemistry

Inocybe aeruginascens is chemically notable for three reasons:

### Aeruginascin (Unique Compound)

Discovered by [[jochen-gartz]] in this species, aeruginascin (4-phosphoryloxy-N,N,N-trimethyltryptamine) is a quaternary ammonium tryptamine not found in any other known mushroom species. This compound distinguishes I. aeruginascens from all other psychoactive mushrooms.

### Alkaloid Profile

| Compound | Concentration (% dry weight) |
|---|---|
| Psilocybin | 0.02-0.08% |
| Baeocystin | 0.01-0.04% |
| Aeruginascin | 0.01-0.03% |
| Norbaeocystin | Trace |

### Gartz's Aeruginascin Hypothesis

Gartz made a significant observation: users who consumed Inocybe aeruginascens reported predominantly positive experiences with very low incidence of anxiety, panic, or "bad trips" compared to users of other psilocybin-containing species. Gartz hypothesized that aeruginascin may act as a euphoriant or anxiolytic, modulating the psychedelic experience to produce a more consistently pleasant subjective state. This hypothesis has not been formally tested in clinical settings but represents one of the most intriguing observations in ethnopharmacology. See [[psilocybin-mushroom-chemistry]] for further discussion.

## Cultural and Recreational Use

### German "Fasanenstrasse" Phenomenon

Gartz documented one of the most remarkable cases of urban mushroom use in Europe: the Fasanenstrasse in Berlin, where I. aeruginascens grew abundantly along a tree-lined avenue. Local residents collected and consumed these mushrooms regularly, reportedly with consistently positive experiences. This case is notable because:
- It represents the only documented case of regular Inocybe consumption (most Inocybe species are dangerously toxic)
- The positive user reports align with Gartz's aeruginascin hypothesis
- It demonstrates psychoactive mushroom use in a major urban center

### Identification Risks

Consuming Inocybe species is EXTREMELY DANGEROUS without expert identification:
- Many Inocybe species contain dangerous levels of muscarine
- Some Inocybe species cause delayed kidney failure (orellanine-type toxins)
- The greenish coloration of I. aeruginascens is subtle and variable
- Misidentification with toxic Inocybe species could be fatal
- Only experts should attempt to identify Inocybe to species level

## Ecology

- Mycorrhizal association with trees (ectomycorrhizal)
- Prefers disturbed or semi-natural habitats: parks, gardens, roadsides, forest edges
- Often found with planted trees in urban settings, suggesting tolerance of anthropogenic environments
- Fruits solitary or in small scattered groups, never in large clusters

## Similar Species

| Species | Key Differences | Danger Level |
|---|---|---|
| I. geophylla (earthy inocybe) | Silvery-white to lilac cap, no green tinge, no bluing | Contains muscarine |
| I. patouillardii | Darker cap, no green tinge, no bluing | Contains muscarine |
| I. fastigiata | Conical cap, no bluing | Contains muscarine |
| I. rimosa (split inocybe) | Radially cracked cap surface, no bluing | Contains muscarine |
| I. lacera | Strongly fibrillose cap, larger, no bluing | Contains muscarine |

**Critical warning:** Most Inocybe species that resemble I. aeruginascens contain muscarine and/or other dangerous toxins. Do NOT consume any Inocybe without expert verification.
