# Eucalyptus wandoo

## Description

Eucalyptus wandoo, commonly known as **wandoo** or **white gum**, is a medium-sized tree endemic to Western Australia. It is a key species of the wheatbelt and goldfields regions, producing dense, durable timber and thriving in semi-arid conditions. The pale, smooth bark gives it the common name white gum. It is closely related to the powderbark wandoo (E. wandoo subsp. pulverea).

## Classification

- **Family:** Myrtaceae
- **Genus:** Eucalyptus
- **Species:** E. wandoo
- **Common names:** Wandoo, white gum

## Key Facts

- Medium-sized tree typically 10–25 m tall
- Smooth pale grey to white bark, sometimes with rough stocking at base
- Native to the wheatbelt and adjacent areas of southwestern Western Australia
- Produces hard, dense, pale-coloured timber of excellent durability
- Adapted to semi-arid conditions with 300–600 mm annual rainfall
- Prefers clay loams and heavy soils, often on valley floors and lower slopes
- Important component of wandoo woodland ecosystems
- Good honey producer with light-coloured honey

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Timber:** Dense, durable timber used for fence posts, sleepers, flooring, and mining timbers (pages 94, 96, 117)
- **Firewood:** Excellent firewood with high density and calorific value (pages 94, 96, 117)
- **Honey:** Produces good quality, light-coloured honey valued by beekeepers (pages 94, 96)
- **Shelter and shade:** Well-suited to semi-arid shelterbelt plantings
- **Soil conservation:** Deep root system helps manage dryland salinity and soil erosion

## Woodland Ecology

- Wandoo is both a timber tree and a woodland-forming species.
- In southwestern Australia it contributes to open eucalypt woodland structure.
- The canopy is generally lighter than dense forest species.
- This can allow grasses, herbs, and shrubs to persist below.
- The tree's adaptation to dry summers makes it useful in Mediterranean climates.
- Its site preferences are almost the opposite of moisture-loving [[eucalyptus-saligna]].
- Wandoo tolerates lower rainfall and heavier soils.
- It is therefore relevant to dryland farm forestry and restoration.
- Its flowers can support beekeeping when seasonal conditions are favorable.
- That use connects the tree with [[faires-beekeeping-and-honey-production]].

## Timber Properties

- Wandoo timber is dense, hard, and durable.
- These traits made it valuable for fence posts, sleepers, and mining timbers.
- The same density makes it excellent firewood.
- High density also means the timber can be difficult to work without suitable tools.
- Slow to moderate growth may be worthwhile where long-lived durable wood is needed.
- Farm plantings should distinguish between short-term shelter and long-term timber goals.
- Trees grown for posts can be spaced differently from trees grown for habitat.
- Durable heartwood usually requires age and appropriate growing conditions.
- The source's repeated page references suggest a broad practical value in permaculture lists.

## Site Selection

- Wandoo often grows on clay loams, gravelly soils, valley floors, and lower slopes.
- It is useful where sandy or acidic-site species may not thrive.
- The note's existing link to [[plants-tolerant-of-alkaline-soils]] is important.
- Alkaline tolerance makes it relevant to many inland and wheatbelt plantings.
- Deep roots may help stabilize soil and manage water tables.
- In dryland salinity contexts, trees can contribute to broader hydrological repair.
- They are not a single cure, but they are useful components of landscape design.
- Planting should follow local provenance guidance whenever possible.

## Habitat Value

- Mature wandoo provides hollows and perches for wildlife.
- Bark, flowers, and canopy structure support insects and birds.
- The page's link to [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]] is therefore fitting.
- In a farm system, wandoo shelterbelts can serve production and biodiversity goals together.
- Nectar flows can support pollinators.
- Leaf litter and woody debris create habitat at ground level.
- Retaining old trees is especially important because hollows develop slowly.
- New plantings should be understood as future habitat, not immediate replacement for old woodland.

## Management Notes

- Establishment is easiest when seedlings are planted into moist soil before dry weather.
- Early browsing protection may be necessary.
- Mulch and weed reduction help young trees through the first summers.
- Once established, the species is notably drought hardy.
- Overwatering or planting in waterlogged sites may be counterproductive.
- Fire planning is important in any eucalypt planting.
- Spacing should allow access for maintenance and future thinning.
- Mixed plantings with shrubs and grasses can better imitate woodland structure.
- Wandoo's best role is in dry, open, durable, habitat-rich plantings.
