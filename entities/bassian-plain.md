# Bassian Plain

## Description

The Bassian Plain is a paleogeographic feature — a now-submerged land bridge that connected mainland Australia with Tasmania during periods of lower sea level, most recently during the last ice age (approximately 18,000–12,000 years ago). It takes its name from the Bass Strait, the body of water that now occupies the area between southeastern Australia and Tasmania. The flooding of the Bassian Plain at the end of the last ice age is cited by Holmgren as a dramatic example of rapid, large-scale environmental change.

## Classification

- **Category**: Geographic feature / paleogeographic region
- **Location**: Between southeastern Australia (Victoria) and Tasmania
- **Current status**: Submerged beneath Bass Strait

## Key Facts

- Connected the Australian mainland to Tasmania during glacial periods when sea levels were 120–130 meters lower than present
- Covered thousands of square kilometers of what is now ocean floor
- Was inhabited by ancestors of Aboriginal Tasmanians who would have witnessed its progressive flooding
- As sea levels rose at the end of the last ice age (12,000–8,000 years ago), winter storms breached coastal dunes, flooding areas that would never return to dry land
- The flooding would have been perceptible on human timescales — a slow but relentless encroachment year after year
- Named after George Bass, the British explorer who first charted the strait in 1798
- Contains archaeological sites from the Pleistocene era, now underwater

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Temporal edge effect**: Holmgren uses the Bassian Plain as an example of a "temporal edge between two stable systems" — the transition between ice age and interglacial — analogous to ecological edges where biodiversity and productivity are highest
- **Rapid change precedent**: The relatively rapid submergence of an entire inhabited plain demonstrates that far-reaching environmental change can occur with great rapidity, a principle relevant to climate change adaptation
- **Long-term thinking**: Understanding that the landscape we see today is transient on geological timescales encourages permaculture designers to think across multiple timescales and design for change rather than stability
- **Indigenous knowledge**: The experience of Aboriginal peoples witnessing this transformation underscores the importance of observational skills and pattern recognition across long timescales — core permaculture competencies
