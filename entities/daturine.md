## Description

Daturine is an alkaloid derived from plants of the genus *Datura* (family Solanaceae), closely related to [[atropine]] and [[hyoscyamine]]. In the context of 19th-century mushroom poisoning treatment, daturine was one of several Solanaceae-derived alkaloids recommended as potential antidotes to Amanita (Amanitine) poisoning. It is now generally considered synonymous with or closely related to atropine/hyoscyamine in modern pharmacology. The Solanaceae (nightshade) family was identified by Palmer as providing the "most trustworthy antidotes" to fungal alkaloid poisoning.

## Classification

- **Type:** Tropane alkaloid
- **Source plants:** *[[datura-stramonium]]* (jimsonweed/thorn apple), other *Datura* species
- **Family:** Solanaceae (nightshade family)
- **Related compounds:** Atropine, hyoscyamine, scopolamine (hyoscine)
- **Pharmacological class:** Anticholinergic / Parasympatholytic
- **Chemical relationship:** Daturine is now understood to be primarily hyoscyamine or a mixture of hyoscyamine and related tropane alkaloids
- **Target receptors:** Muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (competitive antagonist)

## Key Facts

- Palmer listed daturine alongside nicotine and atropine as the "most trustworthy antidotes" for Amanitine poisoning, all derived from the nightshade family (Solanaceae).
- The term "daturine" was used in 19th-century pharmacology for the alkaloidal extract from *Datura* plants. Modern chemistry has clarified that this extract contains primarily hyoscyamine (with atropine being the racemic form) and scopolamine.
- Daturine/atropine works as a competitive antagonist at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, directly counteracting the effects of muscarine-type poisoning. This is the physiological basis for its antidotal action.
- Palmer reported that subcutaneous injection of atropine (essentially the same pharmacological agent as daturine) showed promise in treating Amanita poisoning cases, with one patient appearing to improve under its influence.
- The Solanaceae family provided multiple related alkaloids for antidote research because of their shared anticholinergic mechanism of action.
- Palmer specifically named three Solanaceae alkaloids in order of efficacy: daturine, nicotine, and "above all, atropine" -- suggesting atropine was considered the most effective of the group.

## Source Plants

The *Datura* genus includes several species known for their tropane alkaloid content:
- **Datura stramonium** (jimsonweed): The most common source, widespread globally
- **Datura inoxia** (moonflower): Higher alkaloid content, used medicinally
- **[[datura-metel]]** (Hindu datura): Used in traditional medicine across Asia
- All parts of the plant contain tropane alkaloids, with highest concentrations in seeds and leaves

## Pharmacological Mechanism

The antidotal action of daturine operates through several interconnected mechanisms:
- **Competitive receptor blockade:** Daturine occupies muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, preventing muscarine from binding and activating them
- **Parasympathetic inhibition:** Blocks the parasympathetic nervous system effects that muscarine overstimulates, including excessive salivation, lacrimation, and bronchial secretions
- **Physiological antagonism:** Counters the life-threatening bradycardia (slow heart rate), bronchorrhea (excessive bronchial fluid), and other cholinergic effects
- **Reversible action:** The antagonism is competitive and dose-dependent, meaning the effect can be titrated based on symptoms

## Historical Treatment Protocol

In Palmer's era, the recommended approach to Amanita poisoning involved several steps:
1. **Subcutaneous injection of atropine** (or daturine/belladonna preparations) as the primary treatment
2. **Avoidance of emetics and cathartics** -- considered "worse than useless" for Amanitine poisoning because they may accelerate absorption
3. **Enemata of warm water** with soap, possibly mixed with oil of sweet almonds or oil of olives to relieve tenesmus
4. **Sweet-oil internally** as a soothing and potentially protective agent
5. **Avoidance of vinegar** -- while vinegar could neutralize the alkaloid before ingestion, administering it after absorption could accelerate toxin uptake

## Case Evidence

Palmer documented several treatment observations relevant to daturine and its analogues:
- A consulting physician suggested belladonna (source of atropine/daturine) after observing the patient's symptoms and comparing them with known belladonna effects
- The patient seemed to improve under belladonna/atropine treatment, though it was administered late (fourth day) and the case still ended fatally
- The treatment prolonged life by twelve hours, suggesting some therapeutic effect
- A dog poisoned with Amanitine and given vinegar as "antidote" had all symptoms "fearfully aggravated" -- demonstrating the danger of incorrect treatment

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Daturine's historical significance in mycology centers on mushroom poisoning treatment:

- **Emergency medicine:** Atropine (the modern equivalent of daturine) remains a key drug in the treatment of certain types of mushroom poisoning, particularly those involving muscarinic symptoms (SLUDGE syndrome: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, gastrointestinal distress, emesis).
- **Mechanistic understanding:** The antagonistic relationship between muscarine (from *A. muscaria*) and atropine/daturine was one of the earliest documented examples of receptor-level drug antagonism, foundational to modern pharmacology.
- **Limitations:** Modern medicine recognizes that atropine is effective against muscarine poisoning but has limited efficacy against the more deadly amatoxins ([[alpha-amanitin]]) found in *A. phalloides* and related species.
- **Plant-fungus pharmacology:** The relationship between Solanaceae alkaloids and fungal toxins remains a fascinating intersection of botany, mycology, and pharmacology.
