# Pinus palustris

## Overview

**Pinus palustris**, the longleaf pine, is the keystone species of the
longleaf pine ecosystem in the southeastern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]].

Once dominating over 37 million hectares, longleaf pine has been reduced to
approximately 2% of its original range by logging, turpentining, and fire
suppression.

The species is renowned for its grass stage, a prolonged juvenile period in
which the tree invests in root development while remaining fire-resistant.

Longleaf pine ecosystems are among the most biologically diverse temperate
forests in [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], supporting hundreds of endemic species.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Pinophyta
- **Class:** Pinopsida
- **Order:** Pinales
- **Family:** Pinaceae
- **Genus:** Pinus
- **Subgenus:** Pinus (Diploxylon / hard pines)
- **Species:** *Pinus palustris* P.Mill.

Described by Philip Miller in 1768, the epithet "palustris" means "of the
marshes," referring to some native lowland habitats.

Longleaf pine is closely related to [[pinus-taeda]], slash pine (*P. elliottii*),
and shortleaf pine within subsection *Australes*.

## Morphology

Longleaf pine is the tallest of the southern pines, commonly reaching 25–30 m
with recorded heights exceeding 35 m.

Mature DBH ranges from 40 to 80 cm; exceptional specimens exceed 1 m.

The crown is open and irregular with a characteristic "limby" form in
open-grown trees.

Bark is thick (2–5 cm), orange-brown to reddish-brown, forming large
irregular plates with deep fissures.

Needles are the longest of any [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] American pine, 20–45 cm long,
in fascicles of three.

They are dark green, stout, and flexible, persisting 2–3 years.

Cones are large, 15–25 cm long and 5–8 cm wide, with sharply keeled,
spiny-tipped apophyses.

Seeds are large (8–10 mm) with a 20–30 mm wing.

The distinctive grass stage lasts 3–15 years: seedlings develop a thick root
collar and taproot while producing only a dense tuft of needles.

The terminal bud is protected by dense needle bases, making seedlings
extremely fire-resistant.

## Distribution and Habitat

Longleaf pine ranges from southeastern Virginia south to central Florida and
west to eastern Texas.

Historically, it dominated the Atlantic and Gulf Coastal Plains from sea
level to approximately 300 m elevation.

The species thrives on well-drained to moderately drained sandy Entisol and
Ultisol soils.

Longleaf pine is strongly fire-adapted and requires frequent low-intensity
fires at 1–5 year intervals for regeneration.

Without fire, longleaf is outcompeted by hardwoods and more shade-tolerant
pines.

## Ecological Role

The longleaf pine ecosystem supports over 900 endemic plant species, making
it among the most species-rich temperate communities outside the tropics.

Frequent fire maintains an open canopy and diverse herbaceous understory
rich in wiregrass, legumes, and asters.

The ecosystem supports the endangered red-cockaded woodpecker, gopher tortoise,
eastern indigo snake, and frosted flatwoods salamander.

The gopher tortoise, a keystone species, excavates burrows used by over 360
other species.

Longleaf pine restoration is a major conservation priority, with the
America's Longleaf Restoration Initiative targeting 3.2 million hectares.

## Uses and Forestry

Longleaf pine produces exceptionally strong, dense, decay-resistant wood
(specific gravity 0.54–0.59).

Historically the primary source of naval stores (turpentine, rosin, pitch),
the species was heavily exploited for this industry.

The heartwood is extremely durable, valued for heavy construction, bridge
timbers, utility poles, and flooring.

Longleaf sawtimber commands premium prices over other southern pines.

Natural rotation lengths are 80–120 years; managed rotations of 30–50 years
produce pulpwood and small sawtimber.

The species is less amenable to intensive plantation silviculture than
[[pinus-taeda]] but outperforms it on droughty, nutrient-poor sites.
