## Laetiporus gilbertsonii

## Description

Laetiporus gilbertsonii is a species of [[chicken-of-the-woods]] mushroom found primarily in western North America. It is one of several closely related Laetiporus species distinguished by cap color, pore color, and host tree preference. Like other members of the genus, it produces large, fleshy, bracket-shaped fruiting bodies with brilliant orange to yellow coloring that are prized as edible mushrooms when harvested young and tender.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Polyporales
- **Family:** Fomitopsidaceae
- **Genus:** Laetiporus
- **Species:** L. gilbertsonii

## Key Facts

- Western North American species of chicken of the woods
- Produces large, colorful, bracket-shaped fruiting bodies
- [[mycelium]] is orange and powdery, with a distinctive sulfur or rotten egg odor
- [[spore-print]] is white
- Shares the common name "chicken of the woods" with congeners
- Difficulty level: Outdoor cultivation 3/5, Indoor cultivation 5/5
- Fruitbody forms overlapping layers or large soft brackets on wood
- Primordia start as small whitish bumps that expand into full brackets

## Cultivation Notes

### Substrate Preferences

L. gilbertsonii is a hardwood-loving species. Strains should be isolated from wild fruitings and matched with their preferred wood type. When purchasing spawn, confirm the original wood type the strain was isolated from, as each strain prefers unique wood chemistry and density and generally cannot grow on other wood types.

### Outdoor Cultivation

- Best suited for outdoor stump or round cultivation
- Use larger-diameter stumps or cut rounds
- Layer supplemented sawdust spawn between rounds
- Fresh wood preferred (cut no more than one month before inoculation)
- Inoculation to fruiting time: one to two years
- Ground contact enhances moisture movement to developing fruiting bodies
- Expected yields: Low — not a high-yielding or predictable fruiter
- Typically produces one flush per year

### Indoor Cultivation

Indoor cultivation is not recommended. No successful fruiting has been achieved under sterile conditions or on supplemented sawdust blocks. The species may require microbial symbionts to complete its reproductive cycle and produce fruiting bodies.

### Laboratory Culture

- Best cloned from older, drier specimens rather than fresh ones
- Store specimens in paper bag in refrigerator for a few days before cloning
- Crack open flesh looking for dry, powdery texture with no moisture wicking
- Take multiple samples of fluffiest tissue with brightest color for best isolation results
- Grain and sawdust formulas can produce antibiotic extracts without fruiting

### Spawn Types

- Sawdust spawn
- Plug spawn
- Cardboard spawn

## Harvesting

- Use a sharp knife to trim outer silky tips back toward the attachment point
- Flesh near the wood becomes tough and fibrous — texture more like cardboard than chicken
- Bending tips causes them to break at the "magic spot" separating edible flesh from [[compost]]
- Only harvest young, tender specimens

## Storage

- Fresh: two to three weeks at 38–42°F (3–6°C)
- Longer shelf life than fleshier mushrooms like shiitakes and oysters
- Cooking and freezing works well for long-term preservation
- Does not reconstitute well when dried — powder instead

## Marketing

- Market under the common name "chicken of the woods"
- Best prepared by parboiling sliced fruitbodies, then cooking as chicken substitute
- Popular with vegetarians as a meat substitute

## Nutritional and Medicinal Properties

- Protein content: 12–14 percent (dry weight)
- May help regulate blood pressure and sugar levels
- High levels of anti-inflammatory compounds
- Incredibly high activity against Staphylococcus — promising for staph infection research

## Mycoremediation Potential

- Strong enzymatic activity degrades a wide range of chemical dyes
- Good candidate for mycofiltration units in textile operations
- Useful for solid-state decolorization of clothing scraps
- Antibiotic metabolites can be extracted from colonized grain and sawdust blocks

## Identification

- Distinguished by cap color, pore color, and host tree preference
- Brilliant orange to yellow upper cap surface
- White, orange, or yellow pore surface beneath
- Overlapping bracket or rosette growth form
- White [[spore]] print

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

L. gilbertsonii is valued as a choice edible in western North America. Its cultivation remains challenging, with no reliable indoor fruiting methods. The species' strong enzymatic capabilities against chemical dyes make it a candidate for mycoremediation in textile waste treatment. Cultivators should focus on outdoor stump cultivation and be prepared for low but rewarding yields of this prized mushroom.
