## Overview

**Ulva lactuca** is a species of green macroalgae (sea lettuce) in the family
Ulvaceae, widely distributed in marine and estuarine environments worldwide.
It is one of the most commonly encountered intertidal seaweeds on temperate
coastlines.

The species is edible and has been consumed as food for centuries in coastal
communities across Europe, Asia, and the Pacific Islands. It is rich in
protein, dietary fiber, vitamins, and essential minerals.

*Ulva lactuca* is ecologically significant as a primary producer in coastal
ecosystems and as a bioindicator of water quality. Excessive growth can cause
nuisance "green tides" in eutrophic waters.

The common name "sea lettuce" derives from the thallus's translucent green
appearance and leaf-like morphology, which closely resembles garden lettuce.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- Kingdom: Chromista
- Division: Chlorophyta
- Class: Ulvophyceae
- Order: Ulvales
- Family: Ulvaceae
- Genus: *Ulva*
- Species: *U. lactuca*

Authority: L. (1753).

The thallus is a flat, leaf-like blade, 10–30 cm long and up to 20 cm
wide, though specimens can exceed 60 cm under favorable conditions. It
is attached to the substrate by a small discoid holdfast.

The thallus is composed of only two cell layers (distromatic), making it
translucent when held against light. The edge may be smooth or deeply lobed
depending on environmental conditions.

Cell dimensions are approximately 10–30 µm in length. The two cell layers
are separated by a thin layer of extracellular matrix. Chloroplasts are
cup-shaped and parietal, characteristic of the Ulvophyceae.

The genus *Ulva* has undergone significant taxonomic revision with
molecular phylogenetics. Some previously described species have been
synonymized, and *U. lactuca* is now considered a species complex.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Ulva lactuca* has a cosmopolitan distribution in temperate and tropical
marine waters. It occurs on all continents except Antarctica and is found
in the Atlantic, Pacific, Indian, and Arctic Oceans.

The species inhabits the intertidal and shallow subtidal zones, typically
growing on rocks, shells, artificial structures, and other hard substrates
from the upper intertidal to depths of approximately 10 meters.

It is highly tolerant of variable salinity and is common in estuaries,
harbors, and tidal pools. The alga thrives in nutrient-rich waters and
frequently forms extensive blooms in eutrophic coastal environments.

Growth rates are rapid, with *Ulva* capable of doubling its biomass every
2–3 days under optimal conditions of light, temperature, and nutrient
availability.

## Nutritional and Chemical Composition

*Ulva lactuca* is approximately 15–25% protein (dry weight), 40–50%
carbohydrate, 1–3% lipid, and 10–15% ash (mineral content). The protein
is relatively complete, containing most essential amino acids.

Dietary fiber content ranges from 30–45% of dry weight, primarily as
soluble ulvan polysaccharides. Ulvan has demonstrated prebiotic and
immunomodulatory properties in laboratory studies.

The species is rich in minerals, particularly iodine (up to 300 mg/kg dry
weight), iron, magnesium, calcium, and potassium. Vitamin content includes
significant levels of vitamins A, C, and B12.

Key bioactive compounds include ulvan sulfated polysaccharides and phenolic
compounds. Ulvan extracts have shown antiviral and anti-tumor activities.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

Sea lettuce is widely consumed in East Asian cuisines, particularly in
Japan (as *aosa* or *hitoegusa*), Korea, and China. It is eaten raw in
salads, cooked in soups, or dried and used as a seasoning.

In northern Europe, particularly Scotland, Ireland, and Scandinavia, sea
lettuce has traditionally been gathered for food and animal fodder. It is
increasingly used in Western fine dining and health food products.

The flavor is mild and slightly marine, with a crisp texture when fresh.
Dried sea lettuce reconstitutes readily and is used as a garnish or soup
ingredient.

Harvesting should be avoided from polluted or eutrophic waters due to the
alga's capacity to accumulate [[fungal-biosorption-of-heavy-metals-by-filamentous-fungi]] and contaminants. Certified
commercial harvesting from clean waters is the recommended source.

## Ecological Significance

*Ulva lactuca* plays a central role in coastal [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]]. Rapid
uptake of nitrogen and phosphorus makes it an effective biofilter in
integrated multi-trophic aquaculture systems.

The species provides habitat and food for marine invertebrates, including
gastropods, amphipods, and small crustaceans. Decomposing *Ulva* biomass
enriches sediments and supports detritivore communities.

Excessive growth of *Ulva* in response to anthropogenic nutrient loading
causes "green tides" that can deplete [[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]], smother benthic
communities, and produce hydrogen sulfide upon decomposition.

These bloom events are increasing worldwide, particularly in enclosed bays
receiving agricultural and municipal runoff. Management strategies include
nutrient source reduction and biomass harvesting for commercial use.
