## Eleagnus umbellata

Eleagnus umbellata, commonly known as autumn olive, autumn berry, or spreading oleaster, is a deciduous shrub in the family Elaeagnaceae native to eastern Asia. Introduced to [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]], it has become one of the most widespread and controversial invasive woody plants in the eastern United States. Despite its invasive status, E. umbellata possesses a remarkable combination of ecological traits
that make it one of the most functionally useful shrubs [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]], and establishes rapidly on degraded land. This tension between its aggressive invasiveness and its exceptional permaculture utility makes it one of the most debated species in [[phosphate-accumulators-sustainable-agriculture]]. Managing its spread while harnessing its nitrogen-fixing and food-producing capacities represents a nuanced challenge for land stewards.

## Taxonomy and Naming

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Magnoliophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Rosales
- **Family:** Elaeagnaceae
- **Genus:** Elaeagnus
- **Species:** E. umbellata Thunb. (1784)
- **Common names:** Autumn olive, autumn berry, spreading oleaster, Japanese silverberry, umbellata oleaster
- **Synonyms:** Elaeagnus parvifolia Royle, Elaeagnus crispa Thunb.
- **Not to be confused with:** Russian olive ([[elaeagnus-umbellata]] forms a symbiotic association with actinorhizal bacteria
of the genus Frankia, housed in root nodules. This differs from legume-rhizobia symbiosis in several important ways:

- **Nitrogenase enzyme:** Frankia fixes atmospheric N₂ into ammonium using the oxygen-sensitive nitrogenase complex, protected within the nodule structure
- **Fixation rates:** Estimated 15–60 kg N/ha/year depending on stand age and soil conditions — comparable to many leguminous shrubs
- **Nodule structure:** Coralloid root nodules visible as small, branched outgrowths on fine roots, especially in spring and early summer
- **Non-legume advantage:** Frankia associations are less specific than rhizobia-legume partnerships, allowing E. umbellata to nodulate with diverse Frankia strains across varied soil conditions
- **Soil improvement:** Leaf litter from E. umbellata is nitrogen-rich (C:N ratio ~20:1), contributing significantly to soil nitrogen pools as it decomposes
