# Eleagnus umbellata

Eleagnus umbellata, commonly known as autumn olive, autumn berry, or spreading oleaster, is a deciduous shrub in the family Elaeagnaceae native to eastern Asia. Introduced to North America in the 1830s as an ornamental and for erosion control, it has become one of the most widespread and controversial invasive woody plants in the eastern United States. Despite its invasive status, E. umbellata possesses a remarkable combination of ecological traits
that make it one of the most functionally useful shrubs in permaculture design:
it fixes atmospheric nitrogen through actinorhizal root symbiosis, produces abundant nutritious fruit exceptionally high in the antioxidant lycopene, tolerates extremely poor soils, provides excellent wildlife habitat, and establishes rapidly on degraded land. This tension between its aggressive invasiveness and its exceptional permaculture utility makes it one of the most debated species in ecological restoration and sustainable agriculture. Managing its spread while harnessing its nitrogen-fixing and food-producing capacities represents a nuanced challenge for land stewards.

## Taxonomy and Naming

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Magnoliophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Rosales
- **Family:** Elaeagnaceae
- **Genus:** Elaeagnus
- **Species:** E. umbellata Thunb. (1784)
- **Common names:** Autumn olive, autumn berry, spreading oleaster, Japanese silverberry, umbellata oleaster
- **Synonyms:** Elaeagnus parvifolia Royle, Elaeagnus crispa Thunb.
- **Not to be confused with:** Russian olive (Elaeagnus angustifolia), a related but distinct species with narrower leaves and silvery-yellow fruit

The genus name derives from Greek elaia (olive) + agnos (chaste tree), a misleading reference as the plant is unrelated to olives. The specific epithet umbellata refers to the umbel-like arrangement of flowers.

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Native range:** Eastern Asia: China, Japan, Korea, and the Himalayan region; found in open woodlands, forest margins, and mountain slopes from sea level to 3,000 meters
- **Introduced range:** Widely naturalized in eastern and central North America from Maine to Virginia, west to Wisconsin and Iowa; also introduced in parts of Europe, New Zealand, and the Caribbean
- **Introduction history:** First introduced to North America in 1830; widely planted by the USDA Soil Conservation Service from the 1950s–1970s for wildlife habitat, erosion control, and windbreaks
- **Habitat preference:** Disturbed sites, old fields, roadsides, forest edges, riparian zones, and degraded land; highly tolerant of drought, infertile soils, salt, and pH extremes (4.5–8.0)
- **USDA hardiness:** Zones 3–8
- **Light requirements:** Full sun to partial shade; most vigorous and fruit-abundant in full sun

## Nitrogen Fixation Biology

Elaeagnus umbellata forms a symbiotic association with actinorhizal bacteria
of the genus Frankia, housed in root nodules. This differs from legume-rhizobia symbiosis in several important ways:

- **Nitrogenase enzyme:** Frankia fixes atmospheric N₂ into ammonium using the oxygen-sensitive nitrogenase complex, protected within the nodule structure
- **Fixation rates:** Estimated 15–60 kg N/ha/year depending on stand age and soil conditions — comparable to many leguminous shrubs
- **Nodule structure:** Coralloid root nodules visible as small, branched outgrowths on fine roots, especially in spring and early summer
- **Non-legume advantage:** Frankia associations are less specific than rhizobia-legume partnerships, allowing E. umbellata to nodulate with diverse Frankia strains across varied soil conditions
- **Soil improvement:** Leaf litter from E. umbellata is nitrogen-rich (C:N ratio ~20:1), contributing significantly to soil nitrogen pools as it decomposes

## Nutritional Profile of Fruit

The fruit of E. umbellata has gained attention for its exceptional lycopene
content, which exceeds that of tomatoes by 5–17 times:

| Nutrient | Value (per 100g fresh weight) | Comparison |
|----------|------------------------------|------------|
| Lycopene | 15–54 mg | 5–17× more than raw tomato |
| Lycopene (per g dry weight) | 0.4–1.7 mg | Highest known botanical source |
| Vitamin C | 12–30 mg | Moderate; comparable to orange |
| Vitamin E | 3.5–5.5 mg | Good source of tocopherols |
| Flavonoids | 70–130 mg | High; includes quercetin, kaempferol |
| Carotenoids | 20–60 mg | Lycopene, β-carotene, lutein |
| Sugars | 5–8 g | Primarily glucose and fructose |
| Organic acids | 1–2 g | Malic and ascorbic acids |

The unusually high lycopene content is of particular nutritional interest, as lycopene is a potent antioxidant associated with reduced cardiovascular disease risk and prostate cancer prevention. The fruit's tart flavor and silvery skin have limited its commercial adoption despite its nutritional value, though autumn berry products (jams, sauces, fruit leather) are gaining niche market traction in the eastern United States.

## Invasiveness and Ecological Impact

E. umbellata is classified as invasive in at least 20 U.S. states and is
listed as a noxious weed in several Mid-Atlantic and Midwestern states. Its invasive characteristics include:

- **Dispersal:** Birds consume the abundant fruit and disperse seeds widely; each shrub can produce 20,000–80,000 fruits per year
- **Germination:** Seeds have a hard seed coat requiring cold stratification; soil seed banks can persist for 3+ years
- **Growth rate:** Rapid growth of 0.5–1 meter per year; begins fruiting at 3–5 years of age
- **Competitive advantage:** Nitrogen fixation allows establishment on nitrogen-poor sites where native species cannot compete
- **Allelopathy:** Leaf litter may contain allelopathic compounds that inhibit germination of some native plant species
- **Ecological impact:** Forms dense thickets that shade out native understory plants; alters soil nitrogen cycling; reduces plant species diversity in invaded communities

## Permaculture Applications

When managed responsibly, E. umbellata provides exceptional function stacking in permaculture systems:

- **Nitrogen-fixing nurse plant:** Improves soil nitrogen for interplanted fruit trees and berry bushes; commonly used as a chop-and-drop mulch source in food forests
- **Windbreak and hedgerow:** Dense, thorny growth provides effective wind protection and livestock barriers
- **Food production:** Abundant, nutritious fruit for preserves, wine, and fresh eating; autumn berry harvest festivals are emerging in the northeastern U.S.
- **Wildlife habitat:** Dense cover provides nesting for songbirds; fruit is a critical winter food source for many bird species
- **Erosion control:** Deep root system and rapid growth stabilize slopes and degraded land
- **Pioneer for restoration:** Establishes on severely degraded sites, building soil organic matter and nitrogen capital for subsequent succession planting

## Management and Control

For land managers seeking to balance utility with containment:

- **Mechanical control:** Cutting stimulates resprouting; repeated cutting over 3–5 years can exhaust root reserves. Mowing before seed set reduces spread
- **Chemical control:** Foliar application of glyphosate or triclopyr in late summer/early fall is most effective; cut-stump treatment with concentrated herbicide prevents resprouting
- **Grazing:** Goats will browse E. umbellate and can provide effective control in managed rotational grazing systems
- **Containment strategy:** In permaculture settings, regular harvesting of fruit and periodic coppicing for mulch helps control spread while capturing the plant's productive output
