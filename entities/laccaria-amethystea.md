## Overview

*Laccaria amethystea* (syn. *Laccaria amethystina*) is a small, brightly colored woodland mushroom found across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere. It is named for its vivid amethyst-purple coloration, which fades as the mushroom ages — giving rise to its common name "Amethyst Deceiver."

The species is an ectomycorrhizal associate of broadleaf and coniferous trees and is one of the most commonly encountered purple fungi in temperate forests. While edible and widely consumed in some cultures, it is thin-fleshed and generally considered of limited culinary value.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hydnangiaceae
- **Genus:** *Laccaria*
- **Species:** *L. amethystea*

The species was originally described as *Agaricus amethysteus* by Jean Baptiste François Pierre Bulliard in 1784 and later transferred to the genus *Laccaria* by Mordecai Cubitt Cooke in 1884. The epithet *amethystea* refers to the purple gemstone amethyst.

Many authorities continue to use the name *Laccaria amethystina*, which remains common in European mycological literature. Molecular studies have confirmed the species as distinct from other *Laccaria* species in the genus.

### Synonyms

- *Laccaria amethystina* (Bull.) Cooke
- *Agaricus amethysteus* Bull.
- *Clitocybe amethystina* (Bull.) Quél.

## Morphology

The cap is 1–4 cm in diameter, convex when young and becoming broadly convex to depressed with age. When fresh, the cap is a deep amethyst-purple that fades to pale lilac, buff, or nearly white as the mushroom matures — the source of the common name "Deceiver."

The gills are adnate to sinuate, thick and widely spaced, with the same purple coloration as the cap when young, fading similarly with age. A distinctive feature of the genus *Laccaria* is the warty or irregular spores, visible under magnification.

The stipe is 3–8 cm long and 2–5 mm thick, fibrous, and tough. It is solid when young, becoming hollow with age. Color matches the cap but may remain somewhat purple longer than the cap surface.

Flesh is thin, purple when cut in fresh specimens, and has a mild, indistinct taste and odor. Spore print is white. Spores are spherical to broadly ellipsoid, 7–10 µm in diameter, ornamented with conspicuous spines or warts — a key diagnostic feature for the genus.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Laccaria amethystea* has a wide distribution across Europe, Asia, and North America. In Europe, it is one of the most common woodland mushrooms, found from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean. In North America, it occurs primarily in the East and Pacific Northwest.

The species forms ectomycorrhizal associations with a broad range of tree hosts, including oaks (*Quercus* spp.), beech (*Fagus* spp.), pines (*Pinus* spp.), spruces (*Picea* spp.), and birches (*Betula* spp.). This broad host range contributes to its wide geographic distribution.

It fruits from late summer through autumn, typically August to November, often in large scattered groups or troops on forest floors with thick leaf litter. The fruiting habit is gregarious, and productive years can produce abundant flushes.

## Ecological Role

As an ectomycorrhizal fungus, *L. amethystea* plays an important role in forest nutrient cycling. The mycelial network connects to tree root systems, facilitating the exchange of mineral nutrients (particularly phosphorus) extracted from soil organic matter for photosynthetic carbon from host trees.

*Laccaria* species are among the most common ectomycorrhizal genera in disturbed and regenerating forests, making them pioneer mycorrhizal associates. They are often among the first fungi to colonize the root systems of young trees in reforestation sites.

The species is being studied for potential use in forest restoration and reforestation programs, as *Laccaria* inoculants can improve seedling survival and growth rates in degraded sites. This application is particularly relevant in tropical and subtropical reforestation using *Laccaria laccata* and related species.

## Edibility

*Laccaria amethystea* is technically edible and is consumed in some European countries, particularly in Eastern Europe and Russia. However, the thin flesh and small size limit its culinary appeal.

When collected in quantity, the mushrooms can be used in soups, stews, or as a garnish. They are not generally considered worth collecting for the table in Western European and North American foraging traditions, where larger and more flavorful species are available.

There are no known toxic lookalikes in the *Laccaria* genus. The combination of purple coloration, widely spaced thick gills, and warty spores makes identification relatively straightforward. As always, positive identification should be confirmed before consumption.

## Similar Species

- ***Laccaria laccata*** — Deceiver; similar size and shape but tan to orange-brown, not purple
- ***Laccaria bicolor*** — Two-colored laccaria; larger, with bicolored stipe and purplish tones only at the base
- ***Cortinarius traganus*** — Larger, has a rusty-brown spore print and cobweb-like partial veil
- ***Inocybe geophylla*** var. *violacea* — Purple form of a toxic Inocybe; has adnexed gills and a mealy odor

## Cultivation and Mycological Research

*Laccaria amethystea* has not been successfully cultivated commercially, though *Laccaria* species have been grown experimentally in symbiotic culture with tree seedlings. The genus is of particular interest to forest mycology researchers studying ectomycorrhizal networks.

Genomic studies of *Laccaria* species have revealed mechanisms of mycorrhizal symbiosis, including gene families involved in nutrient exchange and communication with host roots. These findings have broader implications for understanding forest ecosystem function.

The species has been investigated as a bioindicator of forest health and soil quality, as its presence and abundance correlate with specific soil conditions and forest maturity levels.
