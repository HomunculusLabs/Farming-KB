## Australian Dingo

## Description

The Australian dingo (*Canis lupus dingo*) is a free-ranging dog found primarily
in Australia, with closely related populations in Southeast Asia, particularly
Thailand. Dingoes are medium-sized canids with a lean, agile build, typically
weighing 15-20 kg, with coat colours ranging from sandy yellow to red-ginger,
with occasional black-and-tan and white individuals. They are recognised as one
of the oldest dog lineages in the world, representing an early stage in the
domestication process.

The dingo arrived in Australia approximately 4,000 years ago, likely brought by
Austronesian seafarers. Since its arrival, the dingo has played a complex role
in Australian ecosystems as an apex predator and as a companion to Aboriginal
Australians. Holmgren cites the dingo and its Thai relatives as the ancestors of
domesticated dogs, presenting the relationship between dingoes and Aboriginal
people as evidence for a co-evolutionary model of domestication.

This co-evolutionary perspective challenges the traditional "capture and tame"
model of animal domestication, suggesting instead that mutual benefits led to
incrementally closer relationships between humans and wild animals over time.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Animalia
- **Family**: Canidae
- **Genus**: Canis
- **Species**: C. lupus
- **Subspecies**: C. l. dingo
- **Common Names**: Dingo, warrigal, Australian wild dog
- **Origin**: Southeast Asia (arrived in Australia ~4,000 years ago)
- **[[fungal-biodiversity-conservation-status]]**: Vulnerable (due to hybridisation with domestic dogs)

## Key Facts

- Dingoes are now recognised, along with their relatives in Thailand, as the
  ancestors of all domesticated dogs worldwide.
- They arrived in Australia approximately 4,000 years ago via Austronesian
  seafarers.
- The dingo"s relationship with Aboriginal Australians suggests domestication
  was a co-evolutionary process: mutual benefits resulted in incrementally closer
  relationships, rather than a "capture and tame" model.
- As apex predators, dingoes play a crucial role in regulating ecosystems by
  controlling populations of herbivores (kangaroos, rabbits) and mesopredators
  (foxes, cats).
- Pure dingoes are threatened by hybridisation with domestic and feral dogs,
  with some estimates suggesting very few truly pure populations remain.
- Dingoes are highly intelligent, social animals that live in packs with complex
  hierarchies and communication systems.
- They are generalist predators with a diet including mammals, birds, reptiles,
  and insects.
- In agricultural contexts, dingoes are both valued for ecosystem regulation and
  persecuted as livestock predators.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Ecosystem Regulation**: As apex predators, dingoes help maintain balanced
  ecosystems where herbivore pressure on vegetation is controlled, indirectly
  supporting healthy plant and fungal communities.
- **Soil Health via [[keystone-species-and-trophic-cascades]]**: By controlling herbivore populations,
  dingoes reduce overgrazing, allowing vegetation cover that protects soil from
  erosion and maintains conditions favourable for [[mycorrhizal-networks]].
- **Co-evolutionary Model**: The dingo's domestication story provides insights
  into how humans can develop working relationships with animals in agricultural
  systems, including [[integrated-pest-management-in-tropical-polycultures]] cultivation contexts.
- **Landscape Management**: In [[permaculture-design]], understanding the role of
  apex predators helps inform decisions about [[integrated-pest-management]] and
  [[shelterbelt-benefits-and-livestock-protection]] in broadacre systems.
- **Wild Systems Integration**: The dingo example illustrates how working with
  natural systems and their inherent dynamics can be more effective than attempting
  to exclude or eliminate wild species.
- **Biodiversity Maintenance**: Healthy dingo populations are associated with
  greater native biodiversity, including soil organisms and fungal communities
  that benefit from complex, undisturbed ecosystems.
