# Sterilization

Sterilization is the process of completely eliminating all viable microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi (yeasts and molds), [[cotter-compost-substrate-preparation-manure-mushroom-cultivation]].

## Methods

### Autoclaving (Steam Sterilization)

Autoclaving is the gold standard for sterilizing culture media, substrates, and equipment in mycology and microbiology. It uses pressurized saturated steam at 121 degrees C (250 degrees F) and 15 psi (approximately 1 atmosphere above standard atmospheric pressure, or ~103 kPa gauge pressure) for a sustained period.

The underlying principle is that saturated steam under pressure reaches temperatures above the normal boiling point of water. At 121 degrees C, steam penetrates materials rapidly and transfers heat efficiently through condensation on cooler surfaces. The combination of heat and moisture causes irreversible denaturation of proteins, nucleic acids, and other essential biomolecules in microorganisms.

- **Ethylene oxide gas**: used in industrial and laboratory settings for sterilizing heat-sensitive equipment; highly effective but toxic and requires specialized aeration chambers
- **Ozone**: gaseous sterilant used in sealed chambers for equipment decontamination; leaves no chemical residues but requires careful handling due to its toxicity

### Filtration

For heat-sensitive liquids that cannot be autoclaved (such as some enzyme solutions, heat-labile growth factors, or antibiotic solutions), sterilization is achieved by passage through membrane filters with pore sizes of 0.22 micrometers. This physically removes bacteria and fungi but does not remove viruses or some small mycoplasma species. Filtration is commonly used for sterilizing nutrient solutions that are added to autoclaved media after it has cooled below 60 degrees C.

Sterilization of materials is necessary but not sufficient by itself. It must be combined with rigorous aseptic (sterile) technique during all handling procedures to maintain sterility and prevent contamination. The fundamental principles of aseptic technique in mycology include:

### Common Contaminants

- **Bacteria** (Bacillus species, Pseudomonas species): appear as slimy, discolored patches, cloudy zones, or sour-smelling areas on agar. Bacillus endospores are particularly heat-resistant and may survive inadequate sterilization. Bacterial wet spot (caused by Bacillus subtilis) is a common problem in grain spawn, causing kernels to become slimy and stuck together
- **Trichoderma** (green mold): the most common and destructive mold contaminant in mushroom cultivation. It grows rapidly as a dense white mat that quickly turns green as spores are produced. Trichoderma can outcompete and overgrow most mushroom mycelium in a matter of days
- **Aspergillus** and **Penicillium**: produce characteristic green, blue-green, or yellow-green colonies. Some species produce airborne spores in enormous quantities and can contaminate entire cultivation areas
- **Neurospora** (red bread mold): produces fast-growing orange to pink mycelium with abundant red spores. Particularly problematic in warm conditions
- **Mucor** and **Rhizopus** (pin molds): produce rapidly growing, coarse, cottony mycelium that can quickly overwhelm mushroom cultures
- **Cobweb mold** (Hypomyces): produces fine, grayish mycelium that resembles spider webbing over mushroom surfaces and substrates
- **Yeasts**: produce smooth, creamy, opaque colonies on agar media. While less destructive than molds, yeast contamination indicates a breach in [[eclss-environmental-control-life-support]], and early detection:

- Ensure substrates and media are fully sterilized with adequate time and temperature
- Maintain a clean cultivation environment with controlled airflow
- Work quickly and efficiently to minimize exposure of sterile materials to ambient air
- Monitor cultures regularly for early signs of contamination (discoloration, unusual odors, atypical growth patterns)
- Discard contaminated materials promptly and decontaminate the area to prevent spore spread
- Use higher spore loads or more aggressive mycelial inoculation to give the desired species a competitive advantage over contaminants
