## Description

The ethylene-oxygen cycle is a continuous alternation between aerobic (oxygen-dominated) and anaerobic (ethylene-dominated) conditions at soil microsites. First described in detail by A. Smith (1981, "The Living Soil", *Permaculture Journal* #7), this cycle is fundamental to nutrient availability and plant health. In natural, uncultivated soils, the cycle runs continuously: oxygen is consumed by microbial activity at a microsite, ethylene is produced under the resulting anaerobic conditions, ethylene diffuses outward and suspends microbial activity, then oxygen floods back to reactivate the site. This "dance" keeps nutrients cycling and available to plant roots.

## Classification

- **Category:** Soil biochemical process
- **Domain:** Soil microbiology / soil chemistry
- **Key gases:** Oxygen (O2) and Ethylene (C2H4)
- **Catalyst:** Ferrous iron (Fe2+)
- **Spatial scale:** Microsites within soil [[crumb-structure]]

## Key Facts

### The Cycle Mechanism
1. At an aerobic microsite, soil organisms consume oxygen through respiration.
2. As oxygen is depleted, the microsite becomes anaerobic.
3. Under anaerobic conditions, and in the presence of ferrous iron (Fe2+), ethylene (C2H4) is manufactured from decaying leaf material.
4. Ethylene inhibits (suspends) microbial activity at the site.
5. Ethylene diffuses outward from the anaerobic site.
6. As ethylene diffuses away, oxygen floods back into the microsite.
7. Microbial activity resumes and the cycle repeats.

### Typical Concentrations
- Ethylene is present at 1-2 ppm in natural soils (Smith, 1981).
- Carbon dioxide is also present at similar low concentrations.
- Both gases are produced continuously in undisturbed soil.

### Role of Ferrous Iron
- The process from decaying leaf material to ethylene production requires a ferrous iron (Fe2+) catalyst.
- When soils are cultivated and aerated, ferric iron (Fe3+) forms instead of ferrous iron.
- Ferric iron cannot catalyse ethylene formation, breaking the cycle.
- Plant nutrients become tightly bound to ferric iron and unavailable for root uptake.

### What Happens When Cultivation Disrupts the Cycle
- Ploughing and digging create fully aerobic conditions.
- Nitrogen converts to nitrate or nitrite forms, which inhibit ethylene production.
- Ferric rather than ferrous iron forms, preventing ethylene formation.
- Nutrients become locked in unavailable forms bound to ferric iron.
- Humus is oxidised to carbon dioxide and lost to the atmosphere.
- Most plant root pathogens require aerobic conditions, so cultivation favours pathogens.
- Nitrate nitrogen is highly mobile and leaches from bare soil.
- Net result: atmospheric pollution, stream pollution, and low soil nutrient states.

### Gases Manufactured in Soils
- **Ammonia:** Manufactured in sands in the presence of sunlight via titanium or rutile (TiFe) catalysts.
- **Ethylene:** Produced in anaerobic microsites via ferrous iron catalysis.
- **Methane, CO2, sulphuretted hydrogen, sulphur dioxide:** Formed in anaerobic soils and waters, escape as biogas or marsh gas.
- **Ammonia from legumes:** Released from actively nodulating legume roots (trees and herbs).

### Catalyst Metals for Nitrogen Fixation
- Molybdenum, vanadium, and zinc assist root bacteria in creating available soil nitrogen.

### Smith"s Recommendations for Restoring the Cycle
- Least soil disturbance possible.
- Use of surface mulch (not incorporated into soil) as an ethylene precursor; old leaves are best.
- Very small but frequent ammonia fertiliser until soil balances recover.
- Permanent pasture, forests, orchards with permanent green crop as mulch.
- No-dig or mulched gardens.
- No- or low-cultivation of field crops, preferably between strips of forest.
- Use of legumes in proportion similar to natural plant associations in the area.

## Relevance to Cultivation

The ethylene-oxygen cycle is one of the most compelling scientific arguments for no-till and permaculture approaches. It demonstrates that the conventional practice of ploughing, which appears to "open up" and aerate the soil, actually destroys a sophisticated natural nutrient-cycling mechanism. When left undisturbed under mulch and permanent vegetation, soils maintain a continuous alternation between aerobic and anaerobic microsites that keeps nutrients available, suppresses root pathogens, and preserves humus. This cycle cannot be replicated by any chemical input — it requires the complex, undisturbed architecture of living soil with its crumb structure, organic matter, ferrous iron, and diverse microbial community.
