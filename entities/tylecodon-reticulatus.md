## Overview

**Tylecodon reticulatus** (L.f.) Tölken is a succulent shrublet in the family Crassulaceae, native to the Western Cape and Northern Cape of South Africa. It is distinguished by its thick, swollen stems and reticulate (net-like) bark pattern.

The species is one of the larger Tylecodon species, forming shrublets up to 60 cm tall. It is a winter-growing, summer-dormant succulent that sheds its leaves during the hot season.

In spring, it produces tall inflorescences bearing nodding, tubular flowers in shades of greenish-yellow to brownish-red. These unusual flowers make it a sought-after specimen for collectors of caudiciform plants.

The plant contains cardiac glycosides and is toxic to livestock in its native range. Despite its toxicity, it is prized in cultivation for its sculptural stem form and seasonal flowers.

Tylecodon species were formerly classified within the genus *Cotyledon* until Tölken's 1978 revision separated them based on leaf arrangement and dormancy patterns.

It has a relatively slow growth rate, with mature specimens taking 10–15 years to develop their characteristic thick stems. This slow growth contributes to its value among succulent enthusiasts.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Tylecodon reticulatus was originally described as *Cotyledon reticulata* by Carl Linnaeus the Younger in 1781. Helmut Tölken transferred it to the genus *Tylecodon* in 1978 during his revision of the Crassulaceae.

The genus *Tylecodon* comprises approximately 50 species, all endemic to southern Africa. It is distinguished from the closely related *Cotyledon* by its spirally arranged (rather than opposite) leaves and summer-deciduous habit.

Stems are erect, thick, and succulent, reaching 40–60 cm in height and 2–4 cm in diameter. The bark is distinctive: gray-brown with a prominent net-like (reticulate) pattern of fissures.

Leaves are crowded at stem tips during the growing season, ovate to obovate, 3–7 cm long and 1.5–3 cm wide. Leaf surfaces are glabrous, green to blue-green, sometimes with a glaucous bloom.

The specific epithet "reticulatus" refers to the net-like pattern on the older stems. This reticulate bark is diagnostic and distinguishes it from most other Tylecodon species.

Inflorescences are tall, slender, glandular-pubescent peduncles 15–30 cm long. Each peduncle bears 3–8 nodding, tubular flowers approximately 1.5 cm long.

Flowers are greenish-yellow to reddish-brown, with recurving petal tips. Flowering occurs in spring (September–October in South Africa), before new leaves emerge.

The calyx is small and green, while the corolla tube is conspicuously swollen at the base. Nectar accumulates in the swollen base, attracting pollinators.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is endemic to South Africa, occurring in the Western Cape and Northern Cape provinces. Its range extends from the Tanqua Karoo through the Roggeveld Escarpment.

It grows on rocky hillsides, quartzite slopes, and in arid shrubland at elevations of 400–1200 m. Plants are most commonly found growing in crevices among shale and quartzite rocks.

The native climate is semi-arid to arid, with winter rainfall of 100–250 mm annually. Summer temperatures regularly exceed 35 °C, while winter temperatures may drop to −2 °C.

Plants are adapted to extreme drought, surviving on moisture stored in their thick stems. The summer-deciduous habit reduces water loss during the harshest months.

Associated vegetation includes sparse Karoo shrubland with *Euphorbia* species, *Aloe* species, and other succulents. The species co-occurs with several other Tylecodon species in the Roggeveld.

Population density is typically low, with individual plants spaced several meters apart. This sparse distribution reflects competition for limited water resources.

The species shows some ability to regenerate after fire from surviving rootstock. However, frequent fires may eliminate populations in heavily grazed areas.

## Cultivation

Tylecodon reticulatus requires very well-draining soil, ideally a gritty mix with minimal organic matter. A combination of coarse sand, gravel, and a small proportion of loam works well.

Water during the winter growing season, allowing the soil to dry between waterings. Withhold water entirely during summer dormancy when leaves have been shed.

The species prefers full sun to partial shade, with bright light promoting compact growth and better flowering. Insufficient light leads to etiolated, weak stems.

It is frost-tender and should be protected below 5 °C. In cold climates, grow in containers that can be moved indoors or into a greenhouse.

Propagation is primarily by seed, as stem cuttings are slow to root. Seed should be sown in autumn on the surface of sandy medium and kept barely moist.

Germination typically occurs within 2–3 weeks at temperatures of 15–20 °C. Seedlings grow slowly and should not be transplanted for at least one year.

All parts of the plant are toxic due to cardiac glycoside content. Handle with care and keep away from children and pets.

## Ecological Role

In its native Karoo habitat, Tylecodon reticulatus is a component of the succulent shrubland community. It provides structural habitat for small invertebrates in its bark fissures.

Flowers are pollinated by sunbirds and specialized long-tongued flies. The tubular, nodding flower shape is adapted for bird pollination.

The cardiac glycosides in its tissues provide effective defense against most herbivores. However, some specialist insects have evolved tolerance to these compounds.

The reticulate bark provides microhabitat for lichens and small mosses in moist microsites. This epiphytic community contributes additional biodiversity to the plant's surface.

Seed dispersal is primarily by wind. The small, lightweight seeds are produced in large quantities to compensate for low germination rates in the arid environment.

The species uses CAM photosynthesis, opening stomata at night to minimize water loss. This adaptation is essential for survival in the water-limited Karoo environment.

Population dynamics are slow, with recruitment events tied to unusually wet years. Drought years may result in complete reproductive failure.

## Uses and Ethnobotany

Tylecodon reticulatus has no significant traditional medicinal use due to its high toxicity. Ingestion causes nausea, cardiac arrhythmia, and potentially death in livestock.

The species is occasionally implicated in livestock poisoning in the Karoo. Farmers manage populations in rangelands to reduce losses during drought years when other forage is scarce.

Its primary value is as a collector's specimen in specialist succulent collections. The sculptural stems and unusual reticulate bark make it a distinctive ornamental subject.

It is displayed in botanical gardens specializing in southern African flora. Several Karoo botanical gardens feature mature specimens in their succulent collections.
