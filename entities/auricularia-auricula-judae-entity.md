# Auricularia auricula-judae

Auricularia auricula-judae, commonly known as wood ear, jelly ear, or Judas's ear, is a species of edible jelly fungus in the family Auriculariaceae. Recognized by its distinctive ear-shaped, gelatinous fruiting bodies, this saprotrophic basidiomycete colonizes dead and dying hardwood, particularly elder (Sambucus nigra), from which its specific epithet derives — a reference to the legend that Judas Iscariot hanged himself from an elder tree. The fungus has been cultivated for centuries in East Asia, where it is a staple ingredient in soups and stir-fries, valued for its unique crunchy texture and mild flavor. Beyond culinary use, A. auricula-judae has attracted significant pharmacological interest for its polysaccharide content, immunomodulatory properties, and potential cardiovascular benefits. Global production exceeds 4 million metric tons annually, with China accounting for over 90% of commercial supply.

## Taxonomy and Naming

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Auriculariales
- **Family:** Auriculariaceae
- **Genus:** Auricularia
- **Species:** A. auricula-judae (Bull.) J. Schröt. (1888)
- **Basionym:** Tremella auricula-judae Bull. (1789)
- **Synonyms:** Hirneola auricula-judae (Bull.) Berk., Auricularia auricula (older usage, now a separate Asian species complex)
- **Common names:** Wood ear, jelly ear, Judas's ear, tree ear, black fungus (Chinese: 木耳, mù'ěr), kikurage (Japanese)
The taxonomy of the Auricularia auricula complex has been revised significantly. Molecular phylogenetic studies distinguish the European A. auricula-judae from the Asian A. heimuer and A. cornea, which are the primary species cultivated commercially in China. Many older references conflated these taxa under the single name A. auricula.

## Description
### Macroscopic Morphology
- **Fruiting body:** Gelatinous, ear-shaped to cup-shaped, 2–12 cm across and 1–4 mm thick
- **Upper surface (hymenial):** Smooth to slightly wrinkled, reddish-brown to dark brown, translucent when fresh
- **Lower surface (sterile):** Finely hairy (hirsute), grayish to brown, covered with a dense tomentum
- **Texture:** Extremely rubbery and gelatinous when fresh; tough and shriveled when dried, reconstituting fully with water
- **Margin:** Often wavy and undulating, sometimes lobed
- **Flesh:** Gelatinous, cartilaginous, lacking any distinct odor or flavor
- **Spore print:** White to cream

### Microscopic Features

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Basidia | Cylindrical to clavate, 50–60 × 3–4 µm, transversely septate (phragmobasidia) |
| Basidiospores | Allantoid (sausage-shaped), 14–17 × 5–6 µm, smooth, hyaline, thin-walled |
| Hymenium | Located on upper (adaxial) surface, directly on the gelatinous context |
| Hyphae | Generative hyphae 2–3 µm wide, lacking clamp connections |
| Hairs | On sterile surface: cylindrical, septate, 50–100 × 3–5 µm, thick-walled |

## Distribution and Habitat
- **Geographic range:** Cosmopolitan in temperate and subtropical regions worldwide; especially common in Europe, Asia, and North America
- **Substrate:** Saprotrophic on dead hardwood, most frequently on elder (Sambucus nigra), but also on beech, oak, ash, walnut, and other broadleaf trees
- **Ecology:** Primary decomposer of hardwood; contributes to nutrient cycling in forest ecosystems
- **Season:** Year-round in mild climates; most commonly observed in autumn through spring in temperate zones
- **Habitat:** Broadleaf and mixed woodlands, hedgerows, parkland, and urban environments on dead standing wood or fallen branches
- **Growth form:** Typically solitary to scattered, occasionally gregarious in favorable conditions

## Cultivation
A. auricula-judae and its close Asian relatives (A. heimuer) are among the most commercially cultivated mushrooms worldwide, particularly in China, which produces over 90% of global supply. Cultivation methods include:
- **Log cultivation:** Submerged hardwood logs inoculated with spawn; traditional small-scale method requiring 6–12 months colonization before fruiting
- **Substrate bags:** Sawdust-based supplemented substrates (oak or beech sawdust with wheat bran) in sterile polyethylene bags — the dominant modern method enabling 60–90 day production cycles
- **Optimal conditions:** Temperature 20–25°C for mycelial growth, 15–20°C for fruiting; high humidity (85–95%); indirect light; adequate ventilation
- **Yield:** Biological efficiency typically 80–120% on supplemented sawdust substrates
- **Drying:** Most commercial product sold dried, as reconstitution fully restores texture; dried product stores for years with minimal quality loss

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds
- **Polysaccharides:** Water-soluble β-glucans (primarily 1,3-β-glucan with 1,6-β-branches) constitute 30–40% of dry weight; the primary bioactive fraction responsible for immunomodulatory and antitumor activities
- **Melanin:** Dark pigments in the fruiting body with antioxidant and radioprotective properties
- **Adenosine:** Present at notable concentrations, contributing to antiplatelet and anticoagulant effects
- **Triterpenoids:** Minor constituents with reported anti-inflammatory activity
- **Dietary fiber:** High content of insoluble fiber; low in fat and protein relative to other cultivated mushrooms
- **Vitamins and minerals:** Contains B vitamins (B1, B2), iron, calcium, and phosphorus; notable for relatively high iron content among edible fungi
- **Polyphenols:** Phenolic compounds including protocatechuic acid and related antioxidants
- **Sterols:** Ergosterol and ergosterol peroxide with reported bioactivity

## Pharmacological Research
- **Anticoagulant/antiplatelet activity:** Polysaccharide fractions inhibit platelet aggregation and reduce blood viscosity, supporting traditional use for cardiovascular health and stroke prevention
- **Immunomodulation:** β-glucans stimulate macrophage activity, enhance NK cell function, and modulate cytokine production (TNF-α, IL-2, IFN-γ); polysaccharide extracts demonstrate enhanced immune surveillance in animal models
- **Antitumor effects:** Polysaccharide extracts show tumor-inhibitory activity in murine models of sarcoma and hepatoma; mechanisms include immune activation, direct apoptosis induction, and anti-angiogenesis
- **Hypolipidemic effects:** Reduces serum cholesterol and triglyceride levels in animal studies, possibly through bile acid binding and hepatic lipid metabolism modulation
- **Hypoglycemic activity:** Polysaccharide fractions demonstrate blood glucose-lowering effects in diabetic rodent models via improved insulin sensitivity
- **Antioxidant activity:** Melanin and polyphenol fractions scavenge free radicals (DPPH, ABTS assays) and reduce lipid peroxidation
- **Gut health:** Soluble dietary fiber acts as a prebiotic, promoting beneficial Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium populations; supports intestinal barrier integrity

## Culinary Significance
Wood ear is a foundational ingredient in East Asian cuisine, prized for its crisp, slightly crunchy texture that contrasts with softer ingredients. Key applications include hot and sour soup (suanla tang), moo shu pork, Buddhist vegetarian dishes, and numerous stir-fried preparations. The dried mushroom rehydrates in 20–30 minutes, expanding 5–8 times in volume. Its mild, earthy flavor absorbs seasonings readily, making it a versatile textural component rather than a primary flavor driver. In traditional Chinese medicine, wood ear is classified as neutral in thermal nature and is prescribed to nourish the blood, lubricate the intestines, and support cardiovascular health.

## Historical Context
The earliest documented use of Auricularia species dates back over 1,500 years in Chinese medicinal texts, where wood ear was prescribed for ailments of the throat, digestive system, and circulation. The species was introduced to European mycology in the 18th century, though it remained primarily a curiosity of botanical collectors rather than a culinary item in Western cuisine until the globalization of Asian food culture in the late 20th century. The association with Judas Iscariot and the elder tree reflects medieval European folk taxonomy, which frequently linked plant characteristics to religious narratives. Modern commercial cultivation began in earnest in China during the 1970s and has since expanded dramatically, with annual production now measured in millions of metric tons.

## Field Identification Tips
- The ear-shaped, gelatinous fruiting body growing directly from dead hardwood is highly distinctive and unlikely to be confused with toxic species
- The hirsute (hairy) underside is a key diagnostic feature distinguishing it from smoother jelly fungi
- Texture is rubbery and flexible when fresh, not truly gelatinous-liquid like some Tremella species
- Color ranges from tan to dark brown; the darker specimens are typically more mature
- Best collected in cool, wet weather; specimens dry up and become inconspicuous during dry periods
- Elder trees (Sambucus nigra) are the classic host substrate, though the fungus is not limited to this host

## Conservation Status
Not threatened; common and widespread. Rated as Least Concern by most regional assessments. Commercial cultivation eliminates any pressure from wild harvesting.
