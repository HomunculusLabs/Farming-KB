# Allium porrum

## Overview

**Allium porrum** (syn. *Allium ampeloprasum* var. *porrum*), the leek, is a
cultivated vegetable in the family Amaryllidaceae, subfamily Allioideae.

It is closely related to garlic, onion, shallot, and chive. Leeks are
distinguished by their elongated cylindrical white shaft of tightly wrapped
leaf bases and broad flat green leaves.

The species has been cultivated for over 4,000 years and remains a staple
vegetable across Europe, the Mediterranean, and parts of Asia. It serves as
both a culinary ingredient and a cultural symbol, most famously as the
national emblem of Wales.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

The species was described by Linnaeus in 1753. Some authorities treat the
cultivated leek as a variety of *Allium ampeloprasum*, while others
maintain *A. porrum* as a distinct species.

The taxonomic relationship between leek, elephant garlic (*A. ampeloprasum*
var. *ampeloprasum*), and kurrat (*A. kurrat*) remains debated. All three
share a common wild ancestor in the *A. ampeloprasum* complex.

Leeks are biennial herbs grown as annuals. The edible pseudostem is
typically 15–30 cm long and 3–8 cm in diameter at maturity.

Leaves are broad, flat, and lanceolate, 30–60 cm long, with a distinctive
blue-grey to dark green coloration. Flowers are borne on a stout spherical
umbel up to 10 cm diameter, with pale lilac to white bell-shaped florets.

The root system is fibrous and relatively shallow, extending 30–50 cm
laterally. This makes leeks moderately drought-sensitive compared to other
*Allium* species.

## Distribution and Habitat

The wild progenitor (*Allium ampeloprasum*) is native to the Mediterranean
basin, from the Iberian Peninsula to Turkey and North Africa.

Wild populations occur in rocky coastal habitats, dry hillsides, and
disturbed ground at elevations up to 1,500 m. The species tolerates poor,
sandy soils and moderate drought.

Cultivated leeks are grown worldwide in temperate regions. Major producers
include France, Belgium, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Turkey.

Leeks prefer cool, moist conditions at 15–20°C. They are frost-tolerant and
can overwinter in mild climates, extending fresh vegetable availability
through autumn and winter.

## Cultivation

Leeks are propagated by seed, sown in early spring for autumn harvest or
midsummer for winter harvest. Germination occurs in 10–14 days at optimal
soil temperatures of 16–20°C.

Transplanting is standard commercial practice. Seedlings are grown for 8–10
weeks before transplanting at the 3–4 leaf stage, spaced 15–20 cm apart in
rows 30–45 cm apart.

Mounding soil around the developing pseudostem is essential for producing
long, well-blanched white shafts. This is repeated 2–3 times during the
growing season.

Maturity is reached 90–120 days after transplanting. Most commercial
cultivars are harvested at 2.5–5 cm diameter.

Common pests include onion fly (*Delia antiqua*), leek moth
(*Acrolepiopsis assectella*), and allium leaf miner (*Phytomyza gymnostoma*).
Fungal diseases include rust (*Puccinia allii*), white rot (*Sclerotium
cepivorum*), and downy mildew (*Peronospora destructor*).

Cultivar groups include summer leeks (fast, small), autumn leeks
(mid-season), and winter leeks (slow, frost-hardy, large). Notable
cultivars include 'Muscat', 'Bandit', and 'Tadorna'.

## Culinary and Nutritional Use

Leeks have a milder, sweeter flavor than onions, with a subtle earthy
quality. The white and light-green parts are preferred for cooking.

The vegetable is central to classic European dishes including vichyssoise
(French chilled leek-potato soup), cock-a-leekie (Scottish chicken-leek
soup), and quiche aux poireaux.

Leeks can be braised, sautéed, grilled, roasted, or served raw. They pair
well with potatoes, cream, cheese, eggs, mustard, and white wine.

Leeks provide approximately 61 kcal per 100 g raw, with moderate vitamin K,
vitamin C, vitamin B6, manganese, and folate. They contain flavonoids
(primarily kaempferol) and organosulfur compounds including allicin
derivatives.

## History and Cultural Significance

Leeks were cultivated in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia by approximately
2000 BCE. They appear in Egyptian tomb paintings and were reportedly favored
by the Roman Emperor Nero for their perceived benefits to the voice.

The leek has been a dietary staple in Britain since Roman times. According
to legend, the 7th-century Welsh King Cadwaladr ordered soldiers to wear
leeks on their helmets to distinguish themselves from Saxon enemies.

The Welsh tradition of wearing leeks on Saint David's Day (March 1)
continues today. Leeks appear on the reverse of the 1985 and 1990 British
one-pound coins.

France alone produces approximately 160,000 tonnes of leeks annually, and
the vegetable maintains strong commercial importance across Western Europe.
