## Mescalero Apache

## Description

The Mescalero Apache are a Native American tribe currently residing on the [[mescalero-indian-reservation]] in south-central New Mexico. They are significant in the study of [[hallucinogens-and-shamanism]] for their historically unique experience with peyote ([[lophophora-williamsii]]): they adopted peyote for shamanistic ceremonies around 1870 but subsequently abandoned it due to the violent conflicts it produced. The Mescalero case illustrates how cultural personality structure can determine the social impact of hallucinogenic drug use.

## Classification

- **Category**: Indigenous people / ethnic group
- **Language family**: Athabaskan (Apachean branch)
- **Location**: Mescalero Indian Reservation, south-central New Mexico
- **Related groups**: Chiricahua Apache, Lipan Apache (co-resident on reservation)
- **Significance**: Unique case of peyote adoption and rejection in shamanistic context

## History with Peyote

### Adoption (ca. 1870-1870s)

The Mescaleros used peyote in religious rites as early as 1867, with the practice becoming more formalized by 1870:
- The Lipans obtained peyote from the Carrizo Indians
- The Mescaleros are said to have learned peyote rites from the Lipans or from the Tonkawas, Lipans, Yaquis, or other non-Apachean groups of northern Mexico
- Various Mescalero shamans acquired "peyote power" and became leaders of peyote camps
- Peyote was ingested only during Mescalero and Lipan shamanistic ceremonies, not for social purposes

### Rejection (post-1910)

The Mescaleros ultimately abandoned peyote use due to severe social disruption:

1. **Fundamental incongruity**: In ordinary shamanistic practice, a single shaman is the principal figure. Peyote made its effects common to all participants, destroying the uniqueness of the shaman's experience
2. **Shamanistic rivalries**: The peyote meetings became places where shamanistic rivalries and witchcraft flourished
3. **Violence**: Hostilities became "so open and bloody" that the peyote gatherings were abandoned
4. **Demonization**: Peyote was defined as an evil substance — uniquely, "peyote power is unique among the Mescaleros in that it is uniformly considered to be bad"

### Contrast with Other Tribes

The Mescalero experience contrasts sharply with peyote use in other tribes:
- Most North American tribes use peyote harmoniously in communal settings (e.g., [[native-american-church]])
- Similarly harmonious communal use of ayahuasca occurs among the Cashinahua, Sharanahua, and Campa
- The Mescalero case illustrates how personality structure and culture determine the impact of hallucinogen use

## Religious Concepts

### Power
Mescalero religion centers on supernatural power that permeates the world:
- Power has no intrinsic attribute of good or evil — its virtue resides in its potency
- Power approaches people through a plant, animal, or natural phenomenon via dreams or hallucinatory experience
- Any person is a potential power recipient, leading Opler to describe the Mescaleros as "a tribe of shamans, active or potentially active"
- An individual might own any number of powers

### Witchcraft vs. Shamanism
- Those who use power for the common good are shamans
- Those who use power selfishly are witches
- Yet shamans are implicitly witches because a shaman who saves a life must sacrifice his own or a loved person's life
- This creates endemic jealousies, enmities, and suspicion

## Personality Structure

Research by L. Bryce Boyer identified a typical Apache personality configuration:
- Impulse-ridden, with fear of losing control of repressed hostile urges
- Suggestible and phobic
- Tendency to avoid introspection, seeking outer controls for behavior
- Suspicious, dependent, with unstable libidinal attachments
- Men caught between passive and aggressive urges with insecure sexual identities
- Corresponds to Western psychiatric diagnosis of character disorder with hysterical and impulsive attributes

## Key Facts

- The reservation was established in 1873 for the Mescaleros
- Chiricahuas joined after being freed from prisoner-of-war status in 1913
- Lipans were destroyed as functioning groups in the late 19th century, with survivors joining the Mescaleros
- During 1959-60, there were 13 accredited shamans and approximately 15 pseudoshamans on the reservation
- Peyote use has not been confirmed since the early 20th century
- No knowledge of peyote use by reservation Chiricahuas exists

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The Mescalero Apache case demonstrates that the cultural context of hallucinogenic plant use is as important as the pharmacology of the plants themselves. The same peyote cactus that produces harmonious communal experiences in the Native American Church generated violence and social disruption among the Mescalero. This case is essential for understanding the [[set-and-setting]] principle in ethnopharmacology and the cultural factors that shape the experience of psychoactive plants.
