# Elaeagnus umbellata

**Common names:** Autumn olive, autumn berry, Japanese silverberry, spreading oleaster

## Overview

**Elaeagnus umbellata** is a deciduous shrub in the family Elaeagnaceae, native to eastern Asia from the Himalayas through Japan.
Widely planted for erosion control, wildlife habitat, and ornamental purposes, it has become naturalized across eastern North America and parts of Europe.
In permaculture, it is valued as a nitrogen-fixing pioneer species producing edible, nutrient-dense fruit exceptionally high in lycopene.

## Botanical Description

Autumn olive is a multi-stemmed shrub reaching 3 to 5 meters in height, occasionally forming a small tree up to 7 meters.
The bark is smooth and gray-brown on young stems, becoming fissured with age.
Branches are often armed with sharp thorns up to 5 cm long.
Leaves are elliptic to lanceolate, 4 to 8 cm long, with a distinctive silvery scaly coating on the undersurface.
The upper leaf surface is dark green with scattered silvery dots.
Fragrant, pale yellow to creamy-white flowers appear in clusters of 1 to 3 from the leaf axils in April to June.
The fruit is a small drupe 6 to 9 mm in diameter, ripening from silvery-green to bright red or occasionally yellow-orange in September through November.
Each fruit contains a single seed.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to China, Korea, and Japan.
Introduced to North America in 1830 for ornamental planting and widely distributed by soil conservation agencies from the 1950s through 1970s for erosion control and wildlife food plots.
Now naturalized across much of eastern North America from southern Canada to the southeastern United States.
Highly adaptable to poor, disturbed soils including sandy loams, gravelly substrates, and mine spoils.
Tolerates drought, saline conditions, and pH from 4.5 to 8.0. Hardy to USDA Zone 3.

## Nitrogen Fixation

Like all Elaeagnaceae, autumn olive forms a symbiotic relationship with actinorhizal bacteria of the genus Frankia, enabling atmospheric nitrogen fixation.
Root nodules are visible as coral-pink to orange clusters on fine roots.
Nitrogen fixation rates reach 10 to 30 kg N per hectare per year under favorable conditions.
This capacity allows autumn olive to thrive on nitrogen-poor degraded soils and to improve soil fertility for interplanted species, making it an excellent nurse plant in ecological restoration and food forest establishment.

## Fruit and Nutrition

The fruit is edible raw or cooked, with a tart, astringent flavor when underripe that sweetens significantly after frost.
The fruit is remarkably high in lycopene, containing 15 to 54 mg per 100 g fresh weight, which is 3 to 18 times the concentration found in raw tomatoes.
This makes autumn olive one of the richest plant sources of lycopene known.
The fruit also contains significant levels of vitamin C, vitamin E, flavonoids, and other carotenoids including beta-carotene and lutein.
Fruit yields average 5 to 15 kg per mature shrub annually.

## Culinary Uses

Autumn olive fruit is used in jams, jellies, fruit leathers, sauces, pies, and beverages.
The tart flavor pairs well with sweeteners and complements autumn dishes.
Processing removes the astringency of underripe fruit.
The fruit can be dried and ground into a nutritional supplement powder.
Seed removal is optional for most processed products as the single small seed is soft enough to eat.

## Cultivation

Autumn olive is easily grown from seed after cold stratification for 60 to 90 days at 1 to 5 degrees Celsius.
Semi-hardwood cuttings taken in late summer root readily under mist.
Plants establish quickly and begin fruiting within 3 to 5 years from seed, 2 to 3 years from cutting.
Spacing of 2 to 4 meters is recommended. No fertilizer is required once the nitrogen-fixing symbiosis is established.
Plants tolerate severe pruning and can be managed as a hedge or windbreak at 1.5 to 2 meters height.

## Permaculture Functions

### Pioneer and Nurse Plant
Autumn olive rapidly colonizes degraded, nutrient-poor sites and builds soil through leaf litter decomposition and nitrogen fixation.
It provides shade and wind protection for establishing fruit and nut trees.
In food forest systems, it serves as a productive canopy middle layer.

### Windbreak and Hedgerow
The dense, thorny growth habit makes autumn olive an effective windbreak and livestock barrier.
It tolerates shearing and forms an impenetrable hedge.

### Wildlife Habitat
The fruit is consumed by over 50 species of birds and mammals including songbirds, game birds, foxes, raccoons, and bears.
The thorny branches provide nesting sites and cover for small birds and mammals.
Flowers provide nectar for bees and other pollinators in early spring.

### Erosion Control
The extensive root system stabilizes slopes, streambanks, and disturbed soils.
It is widely used in mine reclamation and riparian buffer plantings.

## Invasive Potential

Autumn olive is classified as invasive in many parts of eastern North America due to its aggressive seed dispersal by birds and ability to outcompete native vegetation in open habitats.
In permaculture systems, this tendency can be managed by regular pruning for fruit harvest, which reduces seed production.
Sterile or reduced-fertility cultivars are under development.

## Management in Permaculture Systems

In food forests, autumn olive is typically managed as a nitrogen-fixing support species in the middle canopy layer.
Regular pruning for fruit harvest simultaneously controls spread and produces mulch material.
Coppicing at 1 to 1.5 meters encourages bushy regrowth and extends the productive lifespan.
Plants can be removed entirely once the overstory trees are established and nitrogen fixation is no longer needed, though the productive fruit yield argues for retention.

## Propagation Details

Seed propagation requires removal of the fleshy fruit pulp followed by cold stratification at 1 to 5 degrees Celsius for 60 to 90 days.
Germination rates of 60 to 80 percent are typical.
Semi-hardwood cuttings 10 to 15 cm long, taken from current season growth in late summer, root under intermittent mist in 4 to 8 weeks with hormone treatment.
Root cuttings and division of established clumps are also effective.
Transplant survival exceeds 85 percent when adequate moisture is maintained during establishment.

## Companion Plants

Autumn olive pairs effectively with fruit trees including apple, pear, plum, and persimmon as a nitrogen-fixing understory.
It provides early-season wind protection and soil improvement for establishing nut trees such as chestnut, hazelnut, and black walnut.
In polyculture hedgerows, it combines well with rugosa rose, elderberry, and hazelnut for multi-function barriers providing fruit, nitrogen fixation, and wildlife habitat.

## Nutrient Analysis

Detailed nutrient analysis of autumn olive fruit per 100 g fresh weight shows approximately 40 to 80 kcal, 1.2 to 2.5 g protein, 0.5 to 1.5 g fat, 8 to 15 g carbohydrate, 3 to 5 g fiber, and significant amounts of vitamin A as beta-carotene, vitamin C, vitamin E, and the exceptionally high lycopene content.
The fatty acid profile of the seed includes oleic acid, linoleic acid, and alpha-linolenic acid.
