## Overview

**Elaeagnus umbellata** Thunb., commonly known as **autumn olive**, is a deciduous shrub in the family Elaeagnaceae. Native to East Asia (China, Korea, Japan), it was widely introduced to [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]] plant beginning in the 1830s.

The species has become one of the most widespread invasive shrubs in [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]]. Intercropping with fruit trees or timber species can reduce or eliminate the need for synthetic nitrogen inputs.

The nitrogen contribution extends beyond the root zone through leaf [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] to surface soils, gradually improving fertility of degraded sites.

## Nutritional Value

Autumn olive berries are exceptionally rich in **lycopene**, containing 15–54 mg lycopene per 100 g fresh weight — 3–17 times the concentration found in raw tomatoes. Lycopene is a carotenoid antioxidant associated with reduced cardiovascular disease and prostate cancer risk.

The fruit also contains significant levels of vitamin C (12–30 mg/100 g), vitamin E (tocopherols), and flavonoids including quercetin and kaempferol derivatives. Total antioxidant capacity (ORAC) is among the highest of temperate fruits.

The flavor is tart to sweet-tart, depending on ripeness and cultivar. Fruit can be eaten fresh, processed into jams, jellies, sauces, and fruit leather, or pressed for juice. The seeds are edible but fibrous.

## Invasive Potential

Autumn olive is listed as a noxious or invasive weed in multiple U.S. states and is prohibited for sale in several jurisdictions. The species spreads rapidly via bird-dispersed seed, forming dense thickets that displace native vegetation.

Once established, the shrub alters soil chemistry through nitrogen enrichment, favoring nitrophilic species over native plants adapted to low-nitrogen soils. This "nitrogen shadow" effect can persist for decades after removal.
