# Chelation in Plant Physiology

## Description

Chelation is a chemical process in which organic molecules bind to metal ions,
forming stable, water-soluble complexes that keep minerals available for plant
and microbial uptake. [[elaine-ingham]] discusses chelation extensively in her
field guide, covering chelated minerals, chelating agents, and the role of
chelation in plant physiology and nutrient cycling. Understanding chelation is
central to Ingham's approach because [[soil-food-web]] naturally produces
chelating compounds that make minerals available to plants without synthetic
fertilizers.

## Classification

- **Field**: Biochemistry / Soil Chemistry
- **Process**: Formation of coordination complexes between organic ligands and
  metal ions
- **Natural chelators**: Humic acids, fulvic acids, amino acids, organic
  acids, siderophores

## Key Facts

- The word "chelate" comes from the Greek "chele" meaning claw — the organic
  molecule wraps around the metal ion like a claw.
- Chelation prevents mineral ions from reacting with other soil components
  (precipitating as insoluble forms) and keeps them in plant-available
  solution.
- Natural chelating agents include humic acids, fulvic acids, citric acid,
  amino acids, and microbial siderophores.
- Synthetic chelators (EDTA, DTPA, EDDHA) are used in conventional
  agriculture but differ from natural chelation in their environmental
  behavior.
- Mycorrhizal fungi produce glomalin, a potent chelating compound that binds
  soil particles and holds minerals.
- Bacterial siderophores are among the strongest natural chelating agents
  known, specifically binding iron.
- Chelated minerals are taken up by plants through normal nutrient absorption
  pathways.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Compost Tea and Nutrient Cycling

Ingham's discussion of chelation connects directly to compost tea biology:

1. **Microbial chelation**: Beneficial bacteria and fungi in compost tea
   produce organic acids and siderophores that chelate minerals, making them
  available to plants.
2. **Humic acid as chelator**: Humic acids added to compost tea serve as both
  food for beneficial organisms and powerful natural chelating agents.
3. **Fungal chelation**: Mycorrhizal fungi and saprophytic fungi produce
  oxalic acid and other chelating compounds that dissolve mineral particles
  and release nutrients.
4. **Natural vs. synthetic**: Ingham emphasizes that natural biological
  chelation is superior to synthetic chelation because it operates within
  the soil food web and supports overall ecosystem health.

### Plant Nutrition

- Chelated minerals are the primary form in which plants absorb micronutrients
  (iron, zinc, manganese, copper).
- Without chelation, many mineral ions would precipitate as insoluble
  hydroxides or phosphates in the soil.
- The biological nutrient cycling that Ingham advocates is fundamentally a
  chelation-driven process.

## Management Notes

- Support natural chelation by maintaining diverse microbial communities.
- Include humic acids in compost tea recipes for their chelating properties.
- Avoid over-reliance on synthetic chelators that can bypass biological
  nutrient cycling.
- Ensure adequate fungal populations, as fungi are the primary chelating
  organisms in most soil systems.
- Test soil mineral availability to confirm that biological chelation is
  functioning effectively.
