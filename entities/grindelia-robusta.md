The species is distinguished by its bright yellow composite flower heads and copious sticky, resinous exudate (gum) that coats the flower buds, giving the plant its common name.

Gumweed has a long history of use in traditional medicine by Indigenous peoples of western [[cannabis-respiratory-conditions]].

Modern herbalism continues to employ grindelia preparations as expectorants, antispasmodics, and anti-inflammatory agents, particularly for asthma, bronchitis, and other conditions involving bronchial constriction and excess mucus.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Grindelia robusta* was described by Thomas Nuttall in 1841. The taxonomic relationship between *G. robusta*, *G. squarrosa*, and *G. hirsutula* has been debated, with some authorities treating them as conspecific.

Classification: Plantae → Tracheophyta → Magnoliopsida → Asterales → Asteraceae → Asteroideae → Astereae → **Grindelia** → ***G. robusta***.

The plant grows 30–120 cm tall with an erect, branching stem. Leaves are alternate, lanceolate to ovate, 3–10 cm long, with irregularly toothed margins that are often recurved (the "curlycup" character).

The stem and leaf surfaces are glandular, covered in sticky amber resin that has a distinctive balsamic, camphoraceous aroma. The resin content is highest in the flower buds.

Flower heads are 1.5–3 cm in diameter, composed of yellow ray florets surrounding golden disk florets. Involucral bracts are distinctive: spreading, recurved, and heavily resin-coated.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Grindelia robusta* occurs primarily in coastal and near-coastal regions of western North America, from southern British Columbia through Washington, Oregon, and California into northern Baja California.

It is commonly found in grasslands, coastal bluffs, roadsides, disturbed areas, and open woodlands at elevations from sea level to approximately 1,500 meters.

The species tolerates a wide range of soil conditions, from sandy coastal soils to heavy clays, and exhibits moderate [[comparison-essential-oils-vs-neem-oil-vs-insecticidal-soap]] rich in monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes.

### Grindelic Acid

Grindelic acid is the signature compound of the genus. It is a labdane-type diterpene acid with demonstrated anti-inflammatory, bronchodilatory, and antispasmodic properties.

### Pharmacological Actions

Extracts of *G. robusta* demonstrate several pharmacological activities relevant to respiratory health. The resin acts as a topical counterirritant and expectorant, promoting mucous secretion and clearance from the bronchial passages.

Spasmolytic effects on smooth muscle have been documented in vitro, supporting traditional use for asthma and bronchospasm. Anti-inflammatory activity has been demonstrated in carrageenan-induced edema models.

The flavonoid fraction contributes antioxidant activity, scavenging free radicals and inhibiting lipid peroxidation. This may protect respiratory epithelial tissue from oxidative damage during inflammatory responses.

## Traditional and Modern Uses

Indigenous peoples of California and the Pacific Northwest used grindelia resin topically for skin wounds, burns, and insect bites, and internally for respiratory ailments, stomach complaints, and fever.

The Eclectic physicians of the 19th century formalized grindelia as a treatment for asthma, whooping cough, bronchitis, and cardiac conditions involving palpitation. It was listed in the US Pharmacopoeia from 1882 to 1926.

Contemporary herbalists use grindelia primarily as an expectorant and antispasmodic for acute and chronic respiratory conditions. Typical preparations include tinctures (1:2–1:5 in 60% ethanol), [[dried-herb-storage]] infusions, and fluid extracts.

The dried flowering tops and buds are the primary plant parts used medicinally, harvested just before full flower opening when resin content peaks.
