## Description

Fungal melanins are dark brown to black pigments produced by many fungal species, particularly those inhabiting exposed environments such as soil surfaces, rock surfaces, and lichen thalli. Melanins serve multiple protective functions including absorption of ultraviolet (UV) radiation, protection against desiccation, metal chelation, and defense against enzymatic attack. In the context of lichen biology, melanins produced by the fungal symbiont play a critical role in shielding the photosynthetic algal partner from damaging UV radiation.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Chemistry → Fungal Secondary Metabolites → Pigments
- **Type**: Heteropolymer derived from phenolic compounds (dihydroxynaphthalene or dihydroxyphenylalanine pathways)
- **Color**: Dark brown to black
- **Location**: Fungal cell walls, cytoplasm, extracellular secretions

## Key Facts

### UV Protection in Lichens
- Gauslaa and Solhaug (2001) demonstrated that melanin content in fungal tissue within lichens reduces penetration of UVB (280–315 nm) and UVA (315–400 nm) radiation.
- Melanins act as a natural sunscreen, shifting the wavelength of light entering the lichen to higher wavelengths that cause less structural damage.
- This protection is critical for lichens growing on exposed soil surfaces and rock faces where UV intensity is high.

### Relationship to Other Pigments
- Solhaug and Gauslaa (1996) demonstrated that removal of the orange pigment parietin in the lichen Xanthoria parietina increased damage from excessive light penetration.
- Multiple pigment systems (melanins, parietin, usnic acid, etc.) work together to regulate light exposure in lichens.
- The evolution of melanin-containing fungal symbionts in surface-colonizing lichens is likely an adaptation for protecting the algal photobiont's photosynthetic function.

### Broader Functions of Fungal Melanin
- **Metal binding**: Melanins chelate heavy metals (Fe, Cu, Zn), contributing to metal tolerance in fungi.
- **Enzymatic resistance**: Melanized cell walls are more resistant to lytic enzymes, enhancing fungal survival.
- **Thermoregulation**: Dark pigments absorb heat, potentially aiding thermophilic species.
- **Virulence factor**: In pathogenic fungi (e.g., Cryptococcus neoformans, Wangiella dermatitidis), melanin contributes to immune evasion.
- **Structural reinforcement**: Melanins cross-link with cell wall components, increasing rigidity.

### Ecological Distribution
- Particularly abundant in fungi from high-UV environments: deserts, alpine zones, polar regions, and exposed rock surfaces.
- Common in stress-tolerant (S-strategist) fungi.
- Found in many ectomycorrhizal fungi with dark mycelium (e.g., Cenococcum geophilum).

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Melanin production has direct implications for mushroom cultivation. Dark-pigmented (melanized) mushroom species such as shiitake (Lentinula edodes) may have greater UV tolerance than light-colored species. Understanding melanin's role helps explain why some contaminant molds (particularly dematiaceous fungi with dark mycelium) are more resistant to UV sterilization. Melanin content also influences the nutritional profile of mushrooms, as melanins have antioxidant properties. For outdoor cultivation, selecting melanin-rich species may reduce UV damage to fruiting bodies. The metal-chelating properties of melanin are relevant to understanding heavy metal bioaccumulation in cultivated mushrooms.

### Biochemistry of Fungal Melanin
Fungal melanins are synthesized through two main pathways: the 1,8-dihydroxynaphthalene (DHN) pathway, common in ascomycetes and their relatives, and the dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA) pathway, found in some basidiomycetes. The DHN pathway involves polyketide synthases producing 1,3,6,8-tetrahydroxynaphthalene, which is further modified through reduction and dehydration reactions. The resulting polymer is an amorphous, insoluble material incorporated into cell walls.

### Melanin in Lichen Biology
Lichens growing on exposed surfaces face intense UV radiation that can damage both the fungal and algal partners. The fungal symbiont in many lichens produces melanins concentrated in the upper cortical layer, creating a protective filter above the photosynthetic algal layer. This arrangement is analogous to the epidermis in plant leaves, where UV-absorbing compounds protect underlying photosynthetic tissue. Gauslaa and Solhaug (2001) quantified this effect, showing measurable reductions in UVB and UVA transmission through melanized cortical tissue compared to non-melanized tissue.

### Interaction with Other Photoprotective Pigments
Melanins work in concert with other fungal and lichen pigments. The orange pigment parietin (in Xanthoria parietina) and usnic acid (in many lichen species) provide additional photoprotection. Solhaug and Gauslaa (1996) showed that experimental removal of parietin increased light damage to lichen tissue. Multiple pigment layers create a graduated filter system: outer melanins absorb the most damaging short-wavelength UV, while inner pigments regulate visible light reaching the photobiont.

### Melanin in Free-Living Fungi
Beyond lichens, melanin is produced by many free-living fungi, particularly those inhabiting exposed environments. Rock-inhabiting fungi (RIF) in desert and polar environments are often heavily melanized. Aspergillus niger (literally "black mold") derives its color from melanin, contributing to its resilience in diverse environments including rock surfaces where it participates in mineral weathering. The ubiquitous soil fungus Alternaria is another heavily melanized genus found in endolithic communities.

### Role in Stress Tolerance Strategy
Melanin production is characteristic of stress-tolerant (S-strategist) fungi as defined by Grime's R-C-S framework. The energetic cost of melanin synthesis represents the investment in defense that distinguishes S-strategists from ruderal (R-strategist) fungi. This investment pays off in environments with sustained stress (UV exposure, desiccation, metal toxicity) but makes melanized fungi less competitive against faster-growing, non-melanized species when stress is removed.
