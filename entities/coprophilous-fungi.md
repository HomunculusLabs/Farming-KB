## Description

Coprophilous fungi are a specialized group of [[saprotrophic-fungi]] that colonize and decompose animal dung (feces). They represent a diverse assemblage of species from multiple taxonomic groups, including ascomycetes, zygomycetes, and [[basidiomycetes]]. These fungi play a critical ecological role in nutrient recycling by breaking down the complex organic matter in herbivore dung and returning nutrients to the soil. They exhibit remarkable adaptations for dispersal, often producing spores that are inadvertently ingested by herbivores along with vegetation and then pass through the digestive tract to germinate on fresh dung.

## Classification

Coprophilous fungi are not a monophyletic group but a functional guild spanning multiple fungal taxa:

- **Ascomycota:** genera such as Sordaria, Podospora, Ascobolus, Chaetomium
- **Zygomycota (Mucoromycota):** genera such as Pilobolus, Phycomyces, Mucor
- **Basidiomycota:** genera such as Coprinus, Panaeolus, Stropharia

## Key Facts

- Richardson (2001) documented the diversity and occurrence of coprophilous fungi, recording hundreds of species across different dung types and geographic regions.
- Many coprophilous fungi exhibit host specificity, with different fungal communities developing on the dung of different herbivore species.
- Pilobolus species are famous for their explosive spore-discharge mechanism, shooting sporangia up to 2 meters toward light to maximize ingestion by grazing animals.
- Dung fungi are among the first colonizers of fresh feces, initiating a predictable succession of fungal species over time.
- The fungal succession on dung progresses from fast-growing sugar fungi (Mucorales) through ascomycetes to slower-growing basidiomycetes that degrade cellulose and lignin.
- Dung fungal communities are used in paleoecological studies to reconstruct past herbivore distributions and grassland ecosystems.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Coprophilous fungi are essential for nutrient cycling in pastoral and grassland ecosystems, directly relevant to agricultural soil fertility.
- Some species (e.g., certain Coprinus and Stropharia) are edible and cultivated on composted manure substrates.
- Understanding dung-[[fungal-ecology]] is relevant for managing grazing systems and pasture health in agricultural and permaculture contexts.
- The enzymatic capabilities of coprophilous basidiomycetes, particularly cellulases and ligninases, have potential biotechnological applications in biofuel production and waste management.
- In [[mushroom-cultivation]], composted animal manure is a traditional substrate ingredient, and coprophilous fungi play a role in substrate conditioning.

## Ecological Role

Coprophilous fungi are keystone decomposers in grassland and pastoral ecosystems. By rapidly colonizing herbivore dung, they release locked nutrients (N, P, K) back into the soil, maintaining the fertility of grazing lands. Their [[spore-dispersal]] strategies, evolved in concert with herbivore behavior, represent one of the most elegant examples of fungal-animal coevolution. The predictable succession of fungal species on dung serves as a model system for studying community ecology and microbial succession.
