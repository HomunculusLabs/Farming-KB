## Cultivation and Uses

Common privet has been a standard hedging plant in European and [[childrens-herbal-medicine]], the leaves and bark were used as a tonic, diuretic, and treatment for coughs. The fruit was used in folk medicine despite its mild toxicity, which can cause gastrointestinal distress in humans.

The hard, white wood has limited commercial use but has been employed for turnery, tool handles, and engraving blocks. Its flexibility when green made it useful for basket weaving in rural traditions.

Privet hedges require regular maintenance, typically 2–3 shearings per [[livestock-fencing-guide]] due to its thornless, dense growth. Living hedges of L. vulgare were common in European mixed farming systems before the advent of wire fencing.

## Ecological Role

In its native range, common privet provides cover and food for birds and small mammals. The berries are an important autumn food source for species including thrushes, blackbirds, and waxwings.

Compared to the closely related L. sinense (Chinese privet), L. vulgare is considered somewhat less aggressive as an invader. However, the two species frequently hybridize in areas where they co-occur, producing intermediates with combined invasive traits.

The dense foliage creates valuable nesting habitat for songbirds. In hedgerow systems, privet contributes to the structural diversity that supports high invertebrate biodiversity.

The flowers attract a range of pollinators, particularly bees and hoverflies. The long flowering period and abundant nectar make it a useful component of pollinator-supportive plantings in its native range.

Privet foliage serves as a larval food plant for several moth species, including the privet hawk-moth (Sphinx ligustri). However, the ornamental value of the shrub sometimes conflicts with the defoliation caused by heavy caterpillar infestations.

Fall foliage color is generally unremarkable compared to native shrubs, with leaves turning dark purple or brown before dropping. This has contributed to declining popularity in regions where showier alternatives are available.

## Invasive Potential

Ligustrum vulgare is listed as invasive or noxious in multiple U.S. states and Canadian provinces. It forms dense thickets that shade out native understory vegetation and alter forest regeneration patterns.
