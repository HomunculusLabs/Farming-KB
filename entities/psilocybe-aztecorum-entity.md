# Psilocybe aztecorum

**Psilocybe aztecorum**, known as **teonanácatl** (flesh of the gods) in Nahuatl, is a psychedelic mushroom species in the family Hymenogastraceae. It is native to the high-elevation pine forests of central Mexico and has a long history of ritual use by Aztec and contemporary indigenous Mazatec communities.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae
- **Genus:** *Psilocybe*
- **Species:** *P. aztecorum*

First described by Roger Heim in 1956 from specimens collected on Mount Pelado near Mexico City at elevations above 3,000 meters. The specific epithet *aztecorum* honors the Aztec civilization that used this mushroom ceremonially.

## Description

### Macroscopic Features

- **Cap:** 1.5–4 cm diameter, conic to campanulate, expanding to convex with age, umbonate; surface smooth, viscid when moist, silvery-blue to chestnut-brown with a greenish tinge, fading to straw-yellow when dry; margin often with hanging veil remnants
- **Gills:** Adnate to subdecurrent, crowded, grayish with whitish edges, darkening to purplish-brown with spore maturity
- **Stipe:** 4–10 cm long, 2–4 mm thick, equal to slightly enlarged at base, hollow; white to cream, bruising prominently blue to blue-black; partial veil leaving a persistent fibrillose annulus zone
- **Flesh:** Thin, white, bruising intensely blue; odor farinaceous (mealy), taste mild to slightly bitter
- **Spore print:** Dark purplish-brown
- **Spores:** Ellipsoid, 10–13 × 6.5–8 µm, thick-walled, with a broad germ pore

### Microscopic Features

- **Cheilocystidia:** Ventricose with long necks, 18–30 × 4–7 µm
- **Pleurocystidia:** Rare to absent
- **Pileipellis:** Cutis with embedded gelatinized hyphae
- **Basidia:** 4-spored, clavate, 14–18 × 4–5 µm
- **Pigmentation:** Intracellular pigment granules in pileipellis hyphae, giving the characteristic blue-green coloration

### Key Identification Features

The combination of high-altitude pine forest habitat, small to medium size, conic cap with silvery-blue tones when young, prominent blue bruising on all parts, and the persistent annulus zone on the stipe distinguish *P. aztecorum* from other *Psilocybe* species in its range. The farinaceous odor and absence of pleurocystidia are additional diagnostic characters.

## Distribution and Habitat

*P. aztecorum* is found exclusively in the high-altitude (>2,500 m, typically 3,000–4,000 m) coniferous forests of central Mexico, particularly on the volcanic mountains surrounding the Valley of Mexico (Popocatépetl, Iztaccíhuatl, Mount Pelado, Nevado de Toluca). It grows gregariously on decaying wood debris, needle mats, and sometimes directly on buried wood of **Montezuma pine** (*Pinus montezumae*) and other high-elevation pines. Fruiting occurs from June through October during the rainy season, with peak fruiting in August and September. The species has also been reported from the state of Puebla on Cerro Pico de Orizaba, the highest peak in Mexico.

## Ethnomycological Significance

*P. aztecorum* is one of the most historically significant psychoactive mushrooms. The Aztecs used it in religious ceremonies alongside *Psilocybe caerulescens* (teotlaquilnanácatl) and possibly *P. mexicana*. The Nahuatl term **teonanácatl** ("divine mushroom" or "flesh of the gods") was documented by 16th-century Spanish chroniclers including Bernardino de Sahagún, who described its use in coronation ceremonies and divinatory rituals.

Contemporary Mazatec and Nahua communities in the mountains of Oaxaca and Puebla continue ceremonial use of *Psilocybe* species, though *P. mexicana* and *P. caerulescens* are more commonly employed than *P. aztecorum* in current practice.

## Chemistry

The principal psychoactive compounds found in *P. aztecorum* are:

- **Psilocybin:** 0.2–0.8% dry weight (bimolecular phosphate ester of 4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine)
- **Psilocin:** 0.05–0.2% dry weight (dephosphorylated active metabolite)
- **Baeocystin:** Trace amounts (4-phosphoryloxy-N-methyltryptamine)
- **Norbaeocystin:** Trace amounts (4-phosphoryloxytryptamine)

Total tryptamine content is moderate compared to other *Psilocybe* species. The blueing reaction upon bruising is attributed to oxidation of psilocin and related phenolic compounds. Analytical studies by Bigwood and Beug (1977–1982) and Gartz (1994) confirmed the presence and approximate concentrations of these alkaloids across multiple collections.

## Pharmacology

Psilocybin is a serotonin 5-HT₂A receptor agonist. After ingestion, it is rapidly dephosphorylated by alkaline phosphatase to psilocin, the pharmacologically active compound. Effects include:
- Visual and auditory hallucinations
- Altered perception of time and space
- Synesthesia
- Ego dissolution and mystical-type experiences
- Enhanced emotional sensitivity and introspection

Onset occurs within 20–60 minutes, peaking at 2–3 hours, with total duration of 4–7 hours.

## Cultivation Notes

*P. aztecorum* has been successfully cultivated in vitro on various substrates including rye grain, horse manure, and hardwood sawdust. Mycelial growth occurs at 18–25°C with optimal fruiting temperatures of 10–18°C. However, commercial cultivation is extremely rare due to the species' specialized habitat requirements and limited demand compared to more easily grown species like *P. cubensis* and *P. mexicana*. Most available material comes from wild collection or specialized mycological collections.

## Legal Status

*P. aztecorum* is illegal to possess, cultivate, or distribute in most jurisdictions due to its psilocybin content. In Mexico, the species received partial legal protection in 2006 when the Mexican government decriminalized the use of psilocybin mushrooms for indigenous ceremonial purposes, though commercial sale remains prohibited. Under the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971), psilocybin is a Schedule I substance.

## Conservation Status

*P. aztecorum* is considered rare and potentially threatened due to its restricted high-altitude habitat. Climate change, deforestation of montane pine forests, and overcollection by recreational foragers pose conservation concerns. Its limited distribution on a few volcanic peaks makes it particularly vulnerable to habitat loss. The species has been proposed for inclusion on the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species.

## Similar Species

- *Psilocybe cubensis* — larger, lower altitude, tropical/subtropical
- *Psilocybe caerulescens* — grows at lower elevations, on pasture soil
- *Psilocybe mexicana* — smaller, prefers grasslands and disturbed soils
- *Psilocybe hoogshagenii* — similar habitat, more robust stature
- *Psilocybe weldenii* — close relative, overlapping range
