# Psilocybe aztecorum

**Type:** Species (Fungi, Basidiomycota, Agaricales)
**Category:** Psilocybin-containing mushroom
**Also known as:** ninos, apipitzin ("little children" in Nahuatl); historically
designated *teonanacatl* ("flesh of the gods") by the Aztecs

## Overview
Psilocybe aztecorum is a psychedelic mushroom species native to the high
mountains of central Mexico. Described by Roger Heim and later emended by
Guzman, it is one of two probable candidates for the teonanacatl ("flesh of
the gods") reported by the 16th century chronicler Bernardino de Sahagun as
being used by the Aztecs in religious ceremonies.

## Key Details

### Taxonomy
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae
- **Genus:** Psilocybe
- **Authority:** Heim emend. Guzman
- **Varieties:** P. aztecorum var. aztecorum, P. aztecorum var. bonetii

### Morphology
- **Cap:** 1.5-3.5 cm broad, obtusely conic to campanulate, expanding to
  broadly convex. Surface viscid when moist, dark chestnut brown, strongly
  hygrophanous, fading to straw yellow when dry. Often bruises bluish.
- **Gills:** Adnate to adnexed, light purplish gray to dark purplish brown
  with whitish edges.
- **Stem:** 25-95 mm long by 3-4 mm thick, whitish to grayish, easily
  bruising bluish. White rhizomorphs at base. Partial veil cortinate,
  occasionally leaving an annular zone.
- **Spores:** Violet black in deposit, 10.5-14 by 7-9 um, ellipsoid.
- **Basidia:** 1-, 2-, 3-, or 4-spored.
- **Cheilocystidia:** 35-45 by 5-8 um, fusoid with long thin neck.

### Habitat and Distribution
- Grows gregarious on soil rich in wood debris, rarely on pinecones, in
  open woods of Pinus hartwegii mixed with grasses, and in alpine meadows
  and grasslands above 2,000 meters.
- Elevation: 2,000-4,000 meters above sea level. Var. bonetii prefers
  lower altitudes (2000-3300 m) and associates with Pinus montezumae and
  Abies religiosa.
- Fruiting season: June through October (rainy season; cool temperatures at
  high elevation may trigger fruiting).
- Range: High mountains of central Mexico, including the volcanic peaks
  Popocatepetl and Iztaccihuatl around Mexico City, restricted to the
  central Mexican altiplano. Probably more widely distributed than reported.
- These high-altitude habitats distinguish it from lower-elevation Mexican
  species such as Psilocybe cubensis and Psilocybe caerulescens.

### Chemistry
- Hofmann (1958) detected 0.02% psilocybin and no psilocin in dried
  specimens analyzed two years after collection (likely degraded).
- Fresh specimens are strongly bluing and considered moderately to highly
  active, indicating significantly higher potency when fresh.
- Contains psilocybin and psilocin as primary active compounds, consistent
  with the classic indole alkaloid profile found in Mexican Psilocybe species.
- The discrepancy between fresh potency (strongly bluing) and dried
  analysis results highlights the degradation of psilocybin and psilocin
  over time, especially in improperly stored specimens.

## Relevance
P. aztecorum holds immense cultural significance as one of the most likely
species behind the Aztec teonanacatl ceremonies documented by the 16th
century chronicler Bernardino de Sahagun. Its discovery and
documentation by Roger Heim and Albert Hofmann in the 1950s was
foundational to the modern understanding of psychedelic mushrooms and
led directly to the isolation of psilocybin. The species shares
characteristics with temperate Psilocybes like P. baeocystis and
P. quebecensis, suggesting evolutionary connections between New World
tropical and temperate psychedelic fungi. Its high-altitude habitat in
pine forests above 3000 meters distinguishes it from the lowland
dung-inhabiting species more commonly encountered in the region.

Gartz documents that when Guzman studied the species in 1978, he found
that several active curanderas (indigenous healers) had been the focal
point of sacred mushroom worship just twenty years earlier. However, by
the time of Guzman's investigation, the next generation no longer
ingested the mushrooms, considering the hallucinogenic species to be
nothing more than a popular trading commodity for Western tourists. This
cultural shift illustrates the impact of tourism and modernization on
indigenous mushroom traditions in Mexico.
