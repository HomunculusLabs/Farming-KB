## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Ceratophyllum demersum* L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The genus *Ceratophyllum* contains 6–10 species depending on taxonomic treatment, with *C. demersum* being the most widespread and morphologically variable.

Key morphological features:

- **Growth form:** Free-floating or loosely rooted, completely submerged, with no true roots.
- **Stems:** Central axis, 30–200+ cm long, branched, brittle, capable of forming dense mats.
- **Leaves:** Whorled, 6–12 per node, each leaf dichotomously branched 1–3 times into narrow segments.
- **Leaf margins:** Serrated (toothed), particularly on the upper leaf segments, giving a rough texture.
- **Flowers:** Minute, unisexual, solitary in leaf axils, lacking sepals and petals; male flowers with 8–12 stamens.
- **Fruit:** A spiny achene, 4–6 mm, with two basal spines and surface spines.
- **Color:** Dark green to olive-green, often with a reddish tinge in high-light conditions.

The chromosome number is 2n = 24. The species exhibits phenotypic plasticity in leaf segment number and branching pattern depending on water depth, [[guzman-allen-gartz-psilocybe-semilanceata-temperate-cosmopolitan-distribution]] in freshwater systems worldwide. It occurs in ponds, lakes, slow rivers, canals, ditches, and reservoirs from tropical to cold-temperate regions. It is absent only from Antarctica and some remote oceanic islands.

The species tolerates a wide range of ecological conditions. It grows in water from 0.3 to 10+ meters deep, though dense stands typically form at 1–3 meters. It tolerates pH from 5.5 to 9.0, moderate hardness, and conductivity up to 1,500 μS/cm.

Hornwort overwinters as dormant bud-like structures called turions in cold-temperate regions. These dense, brittle, leaf-packed structures sink to the substrate in autumn and regenerate in spring when water temperatures exceed 10°C. In warmer climates, the species grows year-round without a dormant phase.

Hornwort is shade-tolerant and can persist under a dense canopy of floating-leaved plants such as *Nuphar* and *Nymphaea*. It also thrives in open water with high light availability. The species is particularly successful in eutrophic waters where nutrient availability supports rapid [[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]] concentrations in the water column through photosynthesis. However, nighttime respiration in very dense stands can deplete oxygen in poorly mixed water bodies.

In lake restoration projects, *C. demersum* is often among the first submerged macrophytes to recolonize after nutrient load reduction. Its presence indicates improving water quality and can initiate a positive feedback loop where macrophyte beds stabilize sediments and reduce internal phosphorus loading.

Waterfowl including mallards, teal, and coots feed on hornwort foliage and the invertebrates associated with it. The plant also serves as a substrate for fish spawning, particularly for species that attach eggs to submerged vegetation. Submerged macrophyte beds including *C. demersum* support higher invertebrate diversity than unvegetated substrates.

The allelopathic potential of *C. demersum* has been documented in several studies. The plant releases polyphenols and other [[basidiomycete-mating-systems-sexual-reproduction]] occurs in late summer through autumn. Male flowers release pollen that floats to the water surface and drifts to female flowers. Seed production is variable and less important than vegetative propagation for population maintenance.

Growth rates are extremely rapid under favorable conditions. Biomass can double in 7–14 days during peak summer growth. Annual above-ground productivity ranges from 200 to 800 g dry weight per square meter depending on nutrient availability, light, and water temperature.

The species achieves maximum growth at water temperatures of 20–28°C. Growth ceases below approximately 10°C. Nutrient limitation (particularly nitrogen) triggers turion formation in temperate populations, redirecting resources from vegetative growth to dormant bud production.

## Phytoremediation

*C. demersum* is widely used in [[mollison-designers-wastewater-treatment-wetlands]] systems for nutrient removal and pollutant remediation. The species effectively absorbs nitrogen and phosphorus from contaminated water, with removal rates comparable to rooted emergent species.

The plant accumulates heavy metals including lead, cadmium, zinc, and arsenic in its tissues. Bioconcentration factors for some metals exceed 1,000 in rootless tissue, making it one of the most efficient aquatic accumulators among freshwater macrophytes. The absence of true roots means all nutrient and metal uptake occurs through leaf surfaces.

Hornwort has been investigated for removal of organic micropollutants including pharmaceuticals, pesticides, and endocrine-disrupting compounds from wastewater. Laboratory studies demonstrate removal efficiencies of 50–90% for selected compounds within 7-day exposure periods.

The species shows particular promise for arsenic remediation in contaminated groundwater. Studies have demonstrated arsenic accumulation concentrations exceeding 1,000 mg/kg dry weight in plant tissue. Harvested contaminated biomass requires appropriate disposal as hazardous waste to prevent arsenic re-entry into the food chain.

## Invasive Potential

*C. demersum* is listed as an invasive species in several countries including New Zealand, parts of Australia, and some US states. Dense mats can obstruct waterways, impede recreational activities, and alter aquatic ecosystem structure.
