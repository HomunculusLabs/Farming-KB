# Streak Test

## Description
The streak test is a diagnostic technique used to screen liquid cultures and spawn slurries for contamination by dragging a sample across a fresh agar plate and observing the resulting growth patterns.

## Classification
- **Category**: Diagnostic technique / Quality control
- **Domain**: [[mycology]], laboratory technique
- **Function**: Contamination detection and culture purity verification

## Key Facts

### Standard Protocol
1. Sterilize an inoculation loop by flaming until red-hot
2. Dip the cooled loop into the [[liquid-culture]] or slurry to be tested
3. Drag the loop across a fresh agar plate in a large Z pattern
4. Use an agar formula with no added antibiotics
5. Avoid potato-based agar (many bacteria do not propagate well on it)
6. [[malt-extract-agar]] is recommended to ensure all bacteria species present become visible
7. Seal the plate with Parafilm or tape
8. Incubate for several days and observe

### Interpreting Results
- **Clean culture**: A solid, continuous line of mycelium forms along the Z streak
- **Bacterial contamination**: Irregularly shaped growths, cloudy or clear, smooth and shiny
- **Mold contamination**: Colored (green, black, yellow, brown) circular powdery colonies

### Troubleshooting Contamination
- If positive for contaminants, repeat using every preceding expansion generation
- Work backwards from the contaminated culture to the original isolate
- Identify which expansion generation introduced the contamination
- Revert to the last uncontaminated generation to maintain culture purity

### Applications
- Screening liquid cultures before [[grain-spawn]] inoculation
- Testing purity of cultures from other laboratories
- Verifying HEPA filtration systems are functioning correctly
- Quality control at every stage of the [[spawn-production]] pipeline

## Relevance to Cultivation
The streak test is an indispensable quality control tool that prevents the cascade of losses when contaminated cultures are unknowingly expanded through multiple generations. By catching contamination early, cultivators save weeks of work and significant material costs.
