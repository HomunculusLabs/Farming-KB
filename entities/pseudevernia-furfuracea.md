## Overview

Pseudevernia furfuracea, commonly known as tree moss or felted lichen, is a widespread foliose lichen in the family Parmeliaceae. It grows abundantly on the bark and branches of coniferous and deciduous trees across temperate and boreal regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

The species is easily recognized by its gray-green upper surface and dark brown to black lower surface with a dense tomentum. It is commercially harvested in parts of Europe as a fixative and base material for perfumery and cosmetic products.

Pseudevernia furfuracea produces several characteristic lichen acids, including physodic acid and chloroatranorin, which contribute to its antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties.

The specific epithet furfuracea refers to the bran-like, mealy texture of the thallus surface when viewed under magnification. This characteristic distinguishes it from the morphologically similar genus Evernia.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Pseudevernia furfuracea was first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 as Lichen furfuraceus. It was later transferred to the genus Pseudevernia by Friedrich Wilhelm Zopf in 1903.

The lichen forms large, irregularly lobed thalli up to 8 centimeters across. Lobes are linear to elongated, branching dichotomously, with edges that curl upward in dry conditions.

The upper cortex is gray-green to gray when dry, becoming greener when wet. The lower surface is dark brown to black with a dense mat of rhizines that anchor it to the substrate.

Apothecia are rare, appearing as brown to dark brown discs up to 5 millimeters in diameter on short stalks. Pycnidia are common, producing colorless conidia.

Soredia are absent in the typical morph, though sorediate forms have been reported. Propagation occurs primarily through thallus fragmentation.

The photobiont is a green alga of the genus Trebouxia, consistent with most members of Parmeliaceae.

Spore morphology includes eight-spored asci with hyaline, ellipsoid ascospores measuring 10 to 16 micrometers in length. Ascus structure places the species within the Lecanoromycete lineage.

## Distribution and Habitat

Pseudevernia furfuracea has a broad circumpolar distribution across Europe, Asia, and North America. In Europe, it ranges from the Mediterranean to Scandinavia and extends eastward through Siberia.

In North America, it occurs from Alaska across Canada to the northeastern United States and southward along the Appalachian Mountains. Disjunct populations are found in the Rocky Mountains.

The species is absent from tropical and arid regions, reflecting its requirement for consistent atmospheric moisture. It reaches its greatest abundance in oceanic climates with moderate year-round rainfall.

The species is most abundant in montane and boreal coniferous forests, where it colonizes the trunks and branches of fir, spruce, pine, and larch. It also grows on deciduous trees, particularly oak and birch, in mixed forests.

Pseudevernia furfuracea shows moderate tolerance to air pollution and can survive in suburban and urban park settings. It is less pollution-sensitive than Evernia prunastri but more sensitive than Hypogymnia physodes.

Optimal growth occurs in well-lit, humid microhabitats with moderate air quality. It is frequently found at mid-canopy heights on older trees with rough bark.

Elevation range extends from sea level to approximately 2,000 meters in mountainous regions. At higher elevations, it transitions to rock surfaces and becomes more abundant on exposed substrates.

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds

The chemistry of Pseudevernia furfuracea is dominated by depsidones and depsides. The major secondary metabolites are physodic acid and physodalic acid, both depsidones with documented bioactivity.

Chloroatranorin, a depside, contributes to the distinctive yellow-green medullary reaction observed in spot tests. The medulla turns yellow with potassium hydroxide and red upon the addition of sodium hypochlorite.

Thin-layer chromatography is the standard method for confirming the chemical profile of Pseudevernia furfuracea. Physodic acid produces a characteristic blue fluorescence under longwave UV light after chromatographic separation.

Protocetraric acid is present in trace amounts and contributes to the white medullary reaction in some chemical races. At least two chemotypes are recognized based on varying proportions of these compounds.

Extracts of Pseudevernia furfuracea demonstrate antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria, including Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus subtilis. Antifungal activity has been documented against Candida albicans and dermatophyte species.

Anti-inflammatory properties have been attributed to physodic acid, which inhibits cyclooxygenase-2 expression in vitro. The species also shows moderate antioxidant capacity in DPPH radical scavenging assays.

Cytotoxic effects against certain cancer cell lines have been reported for physodic acid extracts, though these findings remain preliminary. No clinical applications have been developed from these bioactivity studies.

## Uses and Ethnobotany

Pseudevernia furfuracea has a long history of use in European perfumery and cosmetic industries. Dried thalli are harvested commercially, particularly in France and eastern Europe, as a fixative base for potpourri and perfumes.

In traditional European folk medicine, infusions of the lichen were applied externally to treat wounds, skin infections, and respiratory ailments. The antimicrobial properties of its lichen acids provided some empirical basis for these practices.

The species has been used as a dye lichen, producing yellow to brown colors depending on the mordant used. Iron mordants yield olive-green shades, while alum mordants produce warm yellows.

In Nordic countries, Pseudevernia furfuracea was traditionally stuffed into pillows and mattresses as an insulating and aromatic material. This practice was most common in rural communities where the lichen was abundant.

In ecological monitoring, Pseudevernia furfuracea serves as a bioindicator of heavy metal deposition and atmospheric pollution. Its thallus accumulates lead, cadmium, and radioactive isotopes proportionally to environmental concentrations.

Beekeepers in some alpine regions report that honey bees occasionally collect material from Pseudevernia furfuracea, though this behavior is uncommon and poorly understood.

The commercial harvest of wild Pseudevernia furfuracea has raised sustainability concerns in some European countries. Regulations have been proposed to limit collection to managed forests with documented lichen abundance.

## Ecology

Pseudevernia furfuracea is an epiphytic lichen that plays a modest role in forest nutrient cycling. As thalli decompose, they release bound nitrogen and carbon back into the canopy ecosystem.

The species provides microhabitat for various invertebrates, including mites, springtails, and small insects that shelter among its lobes. Some lichen-associated fungi are known to colonize senescent portions of the thallus.

Grazing by lichen-feeding snails and slugs can significantly reduce local biomass in damp forest habitats. The lichen acids that deter most herbivores are only partially effective against specialized lichenivores.

Pseudevernia furfuracea competes with other epiphytic lichens for trunk and branch space. It commonly co-occurs with Hypogymnia physodes, Evernia prunastri, and Parmelia sulcata in mixed epiphyte communities.

Mortality is primarily caused by bark shedding, branch fall, and desiccation during drought periods. The species can persist in a desiccated state for months and resume photosynthesis rapidly after rehydration.

Successional dynamics favor Pseudevernia furfuracea on intermediate-aged bark surfaces, where it occupies niches between early-colonizing crustose lichens and late-successional macrolichens.

Climate warming may affect the species distribution in coming decades, as it is adapted to cool, humid conditions. Range contraction at southern latitudes has been predicted by several lichen climate models.
