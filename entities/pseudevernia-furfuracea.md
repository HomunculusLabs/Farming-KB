## Overview

Pseudevernia furfuracea, commonly known as tree moss or felted lichen, is a widespread foliose lichen in the family Parmeliaceae. It grows abundantly on the bark and branches of coniferous and deciduous trees across temperate and boreal regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

The species is easily recognized by its gray-green upper surface and dark brown to black lower surface with a dense tomentum. It is commercially harvested in parts of Europe as a fixative and base material for perfumery and cosmetic products.

Pseudevernia furfuracea produces several characteristic lichen acids, including physodic acid and chloroatranorin, which contribute to its antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties.

The specific epithet furfuracea refers to the bran-like, mealy texture of the thallus surface when viewed under magnification. This characteristic distinguishes it from the morphologically similar genus Evernia.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Pseudevernia furfuracea was first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 as Lichen furfuraceus. It was later transferred to the genus Pseudevernia by Friedrich Wilhelm Zopf in 1903.

The lichen forms large, irregularly lobed thalli up to 8 centimeters across. Lobes are linear to elongated, branching dichotomously, with edges that curl upward in dry conditions.

The upper cortex is gray-green to gray when dry, becoming greener when wet. The lower surface is dark brown to black with a dense mat of rhizines that anchor it to the substrate.

Apothecia are rare, appearing as brown to dark brown discs up to 5 millimeters in diameter on short stalks. Pycnidia are common, producing colorless conidia.

Soredia are absent in the typical morph, though sorediate forms have been reported. Propagation occurs primarily through thallus fragmentation.

The photobiont is a green alga of the genus Trebouxia, consistent with most members of Parmeliaceae.

Spore morphology includes eight-spored asci with hyaline, ellipsoid ascospores measuring 10 to 16 micrometers in length. Ascus structure places the species within the Lecanoromycete lineage.

## Distribution and Habitat

Pseudevernia furfuracea has a broad circumpolar distribution across Europe, Asia, and North America. In Europe, it ranges from the Mediterranean to Scandinavia and extends eastward through Siberia.

In North America, it occurs from Alaska across Canada to the northeastern United States and southward along the Appalachian Mountains. Disjunct populations are found in the Rocky Mountains.

The species is absent from tropical and [[air-pollution-fungal-community-responses]] and can survive in suburban and urban park settings. It is less pollution-sensitive than [[hypogymnia-physodes]].

Optimal growth occurs in well-lit, humid microhabitats with moderate air quality. It is frequently found at mid-canopy heights on older trees with rough bark.

Elevation range extends from sea level to approximately 2,000 meters in mountainous regions. At higher elevations, it transitions to rock surfaces and becomes more abundant on exposed substrates.

## Uses and Ethnobotany

Pseudevernia furfuracea has a long history of use in European perfumery and cosmetic industries. Dried thalli are harvested commercially, particularly in France and eastern Europe, as a fixative base for potpourri and perfumes.

In traditional European folk medicine, infusions of the lichen were applied externally to treat wounds, skin infections, and respiratory ailments. The [[medicinal-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties]] of its lichen acids provided some empirical basis for these practices.

The species has been used as a dye lichen, producing yellow to brown colors depending on the mordant used. Iron mordants yield olive-green shades, while alum mordants produce warm yellows.

In Nordic countries, Pseudevernia furfuracea was traditionally stuffed into pillows and mattresses as an insulating and aromatic material. This practice was most common in rural communities where the lichen was abundant.

In ecological monitoring, Pseudevernia furfuracea serves as a bioindicator of heavy metal deposition and atmospheric pollution. Its thallus accumulates lead, cadmium, and radioactive isotopes proportionally to environmental concentrations.

Beekeepers in some alpine regions report that honey bees occasionally collect material from Pseudevernia furfuracea, though this behavior is uncommon and poorly understood.
