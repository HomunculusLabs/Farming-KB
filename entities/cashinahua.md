## Description

The Cashinahua (also spelled Kashinawa) are a small indigenous
American Indian tribe of approximately 500 persons (at time of
study) living in the tropical rain forest of southeastern Peru
along the Curanja River. They are classified as members of the
Panoan language family and the Jurua-Purus Culture Area.

Additional Cashinahua communities live along the Jurua and Embira
rivers and their tributaries in the state of Acre in western
Brazil. The Peruvian and Brazilian groups maintain kinship ties
and periodic contact.

## Classification

- **Language family:** Panoan
- **Culture area:** Jurua-Purus (Upper Amazon)
- **Location:** Curanja River, southeastern Peru; Acre, western Brazil
- **Population:** ~500 (Peruvian group, at time of Kensinger's fieldwork 1955–1968)

## Key Facts

### Ayahuasca Use (Nixi Pae)

The Cashinahua call [[ayahuasca]] *[[nixi-pae]]*, meaning "the vine
drunkenness." It is prepared from stalks of several *Banisteriopsis*
lianas combined with leaves of *Psychotria* (called *nai kawa*).
Active constituents include [[harmine]] and harmaline from the vine,
and dimethyltryptamine (DMT) from the *Psychotria* additive.

Any initiated Cashinahua male may drink nixi pae. Usage varies
from men who never partake to those who drink every time a brew
is prepared. Sessions occur no more frequently than every other
week, always after dark (roughly 8 PM to 2–3 AM).

### Group Hallucinogenic Experience

The Cashinahua are notable for their communal approach to
ayahuasca. Unlike the individualistic Jivaro, Cashinahua men
participate together and share revelatory content considered
pertinent to the common good. Men maintain physical contact
during sessions—lining up on logs, wrapping arms and legs
around one another—as an anchor against the terrors of the
spirit world. Ayahuasca is never taken alone.

### Recurring Vision Themes

Common hallucinatory themes across Cashinahua experiences:
1. Brightly colored, large snakes
2. Jaguars and ocelots
3. Spirits (ayahuasca spirits and others)
4. Large or falling trees
5. Lakes filled with anacondas and alligators
6. Cashinahua and other Indian villages
7. Traders and their goods
8. Gardens (manioc, plantains, corn, peanuts)

### Purpose of Use

The Cashinahua drink nixi pae to obtain information unavailable
through normal channels—learning about distant or future events
that could affect the society or individuals. Visions are treated
as portents guiding practical decisions (enlarging gardens when
famine is foreseen, leaving the village when disease approaches).

### Medical System

Two types of medical specialists exist:
- **Herbalist** (*huni bata dauya* / *huni dauya*): treats disease
  with plant remedies ("sweet medicine") based on symptoms
- **Shaman** (*huni mukaya*): treats diseases caused by spirit
  beings using *[[muka-dau]]* ("bitter medicine"), a spiritual power
  received from spirit familiars

Ayahuasca is a last resort for the shaman when normal methods
fail, used to consult spirits outside his usual sphere of influence.

### Spirit Beliefs

Each person is believed to possess five spirits:
- *Bedu yuxin* — "eye spirit" (true personality)
- *Yuda bake yuxin* — "body child spirit" (shadow)
- *Nama yuxin* — "dream spirit" (causes dreams/hallucinations)
- *Pui yuxin* — "spirit of defecation"
- *Isun yuxin* — "spirit of urination"

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The Cashinahua represent one of the best-documented examples of
communal hallucinogen use in an essentially aboriginal setting.
Their preparation of *Banisteriopsis* + *Psychotria* brews
demonstrates sophisticated indigenous phytochemical knowledge—
combining a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (harmine/harmaline)
with DMT to achieve oral activity. Their culture illustrates
how hallucinogenic plants can be deeply integrated into social
structure, medical practice, and epistemology without Western
influence.
