# Sooty Mold

## Description
Sooty mold refers to a dark, often black, fungal growth that appears on the surfaces of leaves, stems, and fruit of plants. It is not caused by a single fungal species but rather by a complex of mostly saprophytic (non-parasitic) fungi in the order Capnodiales, including genera such as Capnodium, Cladosporium, and Alternaria. The fungi do not directly infect plant tissue — instead, they grow on honeydew, the sugary excrement produced by sap-feeding insects such as aphids, scale insects, mealybugs, and whiteflies. Sooty mold is an indicator of insect infestation and can reduce plant vigor by blocking photosynthesis.

## Classification
- **Type**: Complex of multiple fungal genera
- **Primary genera**: Capnodium, Cladosporium, Alternaria, Aureobasidium, Antennariella
- **Order**: Capnodiales (primarily)
- **Growth habit**: Epiphytic/saprophytic — grows on surface, does not penetrate plant tissue
- **Relationship**: Secondary colonizer of honeydew deposits

## Key Facts

### Formation and Appearance
- Appears as a black, soot-like coating on leaf surfaces, stems, and fruit
- Can be wiped off relatively easily by rubbing (it sits on the surface)
- Thrives wherever honeydew-producing insects are active
- Common on citrus, camellia, gardenia, holly, and many ornamental and fruit plants
- Can cover large areas of leaf surface, reducing photosynthetic capacity significantly

### Relationship with Insect Pests
- Sooty mold is an indicator of sap-feeding insect infestation
- Honeydew-producing insects include:
  - Scale insects (soft scale and armored scale)
  - Aphids
  - Mealybugs
  - Whiteflies
  - Psyllids
- The mold fungi use honeydew as their sole nutrient source
- Heavy sooty mold growth indicates a significant insect population feeding on the plant

### Impact on Plants
- Reduces photosynthesis by blocking light from reaching chloroplasts
- Can reduce plant vigor, growth rate, and yield when coverage is extensive
- Aesthetic damage on ornamental plants and fruit
- Does not directly parasitize plant tissue
- Heavy infestations can lead to premature leaf drop

### Management via Compost Tea (AACT)
- Elaine Ingham reports that sooty mold can be managed using actively aerated compost tea
- The beneficial organisms in AACT consume the honeydew before sooty mold fungi can colonize it
- Key mechanism: beneficial bacteria and fungi in the tea outcompete sooty mold organisms for the honeydew food source
- Associated with managing scale insect populations — as scale is controlled, honeydew production decreases
- Ingham notes: "We typically do not see the sooty mold that typically develops after scale gets going, because the beneficial organisms consume the honeydew produced by the scale so the sooty mold does not have a food to grow on"
- Requires maintaining beneficial organism populations through regular foliar application

### Integrated Management
- Control the honeydew-producing insects to eliminate the food source for sooty mold
- Use AACT foliar sprays to establish beneficial organisms on leaf surfaces
- Encourage beneficial insects that prey on aphids and scale
- Improve air circulation and light penetration through pruning
- Wash off honeydew and mold with water or mild soap solution for immediate relief

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology
- **Indicator organism**: Presence of sooty mold is a diagnostic sign of sap-feeding insect activity
- **Citrus and ornamental production**: Common problem requiring management in many crop systems
- **Compost tea efficacy**: Demonstrates how AACT organisms consume food resources on leaf surfaces, preventing secondary fungal colonization
- **Ecological understanding**: Understanding the three-way relationship between plant, insect, and fungus is key to effective management
- **Biological control integration**: Combining AACT with beneficial insect management provides comprehensive control
