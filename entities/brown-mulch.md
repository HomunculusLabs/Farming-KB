# Brown Mulch

## Description

Brown mulch refers to aged, carbon-rich organic materials applied to the soil surface to promote fungal activity in [[the-soil-food-web]]. Unlike green mulches (fresh, nitrogen-rich materials that promote bacteria), brown mulches feed fungal populations, making them the preferred mulch type for trees, shrubs, perennials, and other plants that thrive in fungally dominated soils. Brown mulch is one of the three primary [[soil-food-web]] management tools and is central to maintaining woody plant health.

## Classification

- **Category**: Organic surface amendment
- **Type**: Fungi-supporting mulch
- **Role**: Fungal food source, weed suppression, moisture retention, soil structure builder

## Key Facts

- Made from aged, dry, carbon-rich plant materials: fallen leaves, bark chips, wood chips, twigs, and straw
- The "brown" designation refers to the carbon-rich, aged nature of the material, not just its color
- Coarse, drier mulches support more fungal activity than fine, wet mulches
- Bark chips and wood chips are particularly effective at promoting fungal colonization
- Fallen leaves from the plant itself are the ideal brown mulch—nature's own recycling system
- Leaves should be left in place under trees and shrubs rather than removed
- Running leaves over with a lawn mower opens them up for faster bacterial and fungal colonization
- Brown mulch should extend at least to the [[drip-line]] of trees and shrubs
- Mulch depth of a few inches is sufficient; too deep can create problems
- Brown mulch suppresses weeds by blocking light and tying up nitrogen at the soil-mulch interface
- The mulch also insulates soil, moderating temperature fluctuations

## Application for Trees, Shrubs, and Perennials

1. Start with a 1–2 inch layer of [[fungal-compost]] under the plant (not touching the stem/trunk)
2. Cover with brown mulch to the drip line
3. Include the plant's own fallen leaves as the foundation of the mulch layer
4. Add twigs and sticks dropped by the plant; mow over them to speed decomposition
5. Refresh annually in spring and autumn
6. Keep mulch a few inches away from stems and trunks to prevent bark rot
7. Never remove fallen leaves from under trees and shrubs—they are nature's mulch

## Why Brown Mulch Works for Woody Plants

Trees, shrubs, and perennials evolved in mid-to-late successional environments with fungally dominated soils. Brown mulch recreates these conditions by:

- Providing carbon-rich organic matter that fungi specialize in decomposing
- Promoting the fungal decomposition pathway that produces ammonium nitrogen
- Supporting mycorrhizal fungi that associate with woody plant roots
- Maintaining the coarse, airy surface conditions that fungal hyphae prefer
- Creating the habitat structure for fungal-feeding nematodes and microarthropods

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Brown mulch is arguably the most important management tool for mycology-oriented gardening. It directly creates the fungal habitat at the soil surface that supports both saprotrophic and mycorrhizal fungi. For mushroom cultivators, understanding brown mulch principles translates directly to understanding the woody substrate requirements of lignicolous (wood-decay) fungi. The same principles—carbon-rich materials, moderate moisture, coarse texture, and time—apply whether the goal is healthy trees or productive mushroom beds.

In the soil food web calendar, autumn is the critical time for brown mulch application. The massive influx of fallen leaves provides the year's primary fungal food source, and properly managed, this material will be substantially decomposed by the following spring through microbial action.

## Relationship to Other Entities

- **Green mulch**: The bacterial counterpart; both are applied based on the target plant community
- **Fungal compost**: Applied under brown mulch as a soil inoculant for fungal biology
- **Fungal compost tea**: Soil drenches complement brown mulch applications
- **Drip line**: The target zone for brown mulch application under trees and shrubs
- **Mycorrhizal fungi**: Brown mulch creates the surface conditions that support mycorrhizal networks
- **Soil compaction**: Brown mulch protects soil from compaction by reducing impact and supporting structure-building biology
