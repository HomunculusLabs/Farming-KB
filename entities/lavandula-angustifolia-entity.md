# Lavandula angustifolia

Lavandula angustifolia (English lavender) is an evergreen perennial shrub in the family Lamiaceae, native to the Mediterranean region. Widely cultivated for its fragrant flowers and essential oil, it is one of the most economically important aromatic plants in the world. The species is a cornerstone of permaculture designs for its dual role as a pollinator attractant and a versatile medicinal and culinary herb. Its drought tolerance and low maintenance requirements make it a staple in sustainable landscaping, herb gardens, and commercial essential oil production across temperate zones.

## Taxonomy and Naming
- **Order:** Lamiales
- **Family:** Lamiaceae (mint family)
- **Genus:** Lavandula
- **Species:** L. angustifolia Mill.
- **Authority:** Philip Miller (1768)
- **Synonyms:** L. officinalis Chaix, L. vera DC., L. spica L.
- **Common names:** English lavender, true lavender, narrow-leaved lavender
- **Etymology:** From Latin "lavare" (to wash), referring to its traditional use in bathing and laundry. The specific epithet "angustifolia" means "narrow-leaved."
- **Related species:** L. × intermedia (lavandin, hybrid), L. latifolia (spike lavender), L. stoechas (Spanish lavender)

## Description

### Growth Habit and Morphology
Compact woody shrub reaching 30–60 cm in height and 40–50 cm in spread. Forms a dense, rounded mound with multiple stems arising from a woody base. The growth habit is bushy and upright when young, becoming more open and slightly sprawling with age. Mature plants develop attractive silver-gray foliage that provides year-round structural interest. The root system is relatively shallow but extensive, with a woody taproot and numerous lateral roots that help stabilize soil on slopes.

### Leaves
Narrowly linear to lanceolate, 2–6 cm long and 4–6 mm wide. Gray-green to silver-gray in color due to a dense covering of stellate trichomes. Leaves are entire with revolute margins that curl under, an adaptation to reduce water loss in drought conditions. Leaf arrangement is opposite and decussate. When crushed, leaves release the characteristic lavender fragrance from volatile oil glands on the leaf surface.

### Flowers and Inflorescences
Inflorescences are terminal spikes (racemes) 2–8 cm long, borne on unbranched peduncles rising 10–30 cm above the foliage. Each spike consists of whorls of flowers with 6–10 flowers per whorl. Individual flowers are small (8–12 mm), tubular, and bilabiate. The calyx is tubular, 5-toothed, and covered in dense gray hairs. Flower color ranges from pale lilac to deep violet-blue. Peak bloom occurs from June to August in temperate regions.

## Distribution and Habitat
- **Native range:** Western Mediterranean — southern France, Spain, Italy, Balkans. Naturalized across Europe, North America, and Australia.
- **Altitude:** 300–1,800 m in native range.
- **Climate:** Hardy to USDA zones 5–9. Prefers Mediterranean climates with warm, dry summers and cool, wet winters. Does not tolerate prolonged humidity or tropical conditions.
- **Soil:** Well-drained, sandy or gravelly loam with pH 6.5–8.0 (slightly alkaline preferred). Extremely sensitive to waterlogging.
- **Light:** Full sun, requiring 6–8 hours of direct sunlight daily.
- **Drought tolerance:** High. Established plants survive extended dry periods, making them ideal for xeriscaping and water-wise gardens.

## Ethnobotanical and Cultural Significance
Lavender has been used for over 2,500 years across multiple civilizations. Ancient Egyptians used lavender in mummification. Romans added lavender to their bathwater (hence "lavare") and used it for cooking and scenting laundry. Medieval monasteries cultivated lavender in their physic gardens. During the Great Plague of London (1665), lavender was worn in posies and burned as fumigant. The modern lavender industry in Provence produces over 1,200 metric tons of essential oil annually. Lavender is a key element in the perfume industry, featured in iconic formulations such as Fougère Royale (1882).

## Cultivation Notes
- **Propagation:** Softwood cuttings (4–6 cm) in late summer root in 3–4 weeks under mist. Seed is slow and erratic; cultivars must be vegetatively propagated.
- **Spacing:** 30–45 cm apart for hedging, 60–90 cm for specimen plants.
- **Pruning:** Essential after flowering — cut back to 2–3 cm above previous year's wood. Do not cut into old bare wood as it rarely resprouts.
- **Harvest:** Flowers at 50–75% bloom, harvested in the morning after dew dries for peak oil content.
- **Yield:** 500–1,500 kg fresh flowers per hectare; 8–15 kg essential oil per hectare.
- **Companion planting:** Attracts pollinators (bees, butterflies); repels cabbage moths, aphids, and certain flea beetles. Good companion for roses, sage, and thyme. Avoid planting near mint.
- **Pests:** Generally pest-resistant. Susceptible to root rot (Phytophthora), leaf spot (Septoria), and alfalfa mosaic virus. Rabbit and deer resistant.

## Permaculture Functions
- **Pollinator support:** Rich nectar source for bees, butterflies, and hoverflies; blooms during the mid-summer pollinator gap.
- **Pest deterrence:** Aromatic compounds repel aphids, whiteflies, and certain moths when planted as a border or intercrop.
- **Soil stabilization:** Shallow but extensive root system helps prevent erosion on slopes and banks.
- **Income generation:** Essential oil, dried flowers, sachets, culinary products, and value-added goods (soaps, candles, cosmetics).
- **Edge species:** Ideal for zone 2–3 placement in permaculture designs, functioning as both a productive and aesthetic boundary plant.
- **Microclimate:** Low hedging provides wind protection for more sensitive crops in permaculture guilds.

## Conservation Status
- **IUCN status:** Not evaluated (widespread cultivation).
- **Wild populations:** Declining in parts of native range due to habitat loss and over-harvesting for essential oil production.
- **Genetic diversity:** Broad in cultivated germplasm but narrowing as few high-yielding cultivars dominate commercial production. Gene banks maintain over 400 accessions worldwide.
