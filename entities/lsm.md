## Overview

**Lysergic acid morpholide** (LSM) is a hallucinogenic lysergamide and a close structural
analogue of LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide). It is one of several lysergamide
derivatives explored during the mid-20th century as part of systematic research into
structure-activity relationships of ergoline-based psychedelics.

LSM replaces the diethylamide group of LSD with a morpholine ring, a six-membered
heterocycle containing both nitrogen and oxygen. This substitution modifies the
molecule's pharmacokinetic properties while retaining significant 5-HT₂A receptor
affinity and psychedelic activity.

The compound was first synthesized and tested by researchers at Sandoz Laboratories
and is catalogued in Alexander Shulgin's *TiHKAL* (Tryptamines I Have Known and Loved)
under entry #26. It is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance in the United
States under the Federal Analogue Act.

## Chemical Properties

- **Molecular formula:** C₂₀H₂₄N₂O₃
- **Molecular weight:** 340.42 g/mol
- **IUPAC name:** (6aR,9R)-7-methyl-4,6,6a,7,8,9-hexahydroindolo[4,3-fg]quinoline-9-carboxylic acid morpholine amide
- **SMILES:** CC1CN(C2CC3=CNC4=CC=CC(=C34)C2=C1)C(=O)N5CCOCC5
- **Appearance:** White crystalline solid

The morpholine ring substitution represents the key structural difference from LSD.
In LSD, the amide nitrogen bears two ethyl groups (diethylamide), while in LSM the
nitrogen is incorporated into a morpholine ring system.

This cyclization constrains the conformational flexibility of the amide group and
introduces a polar oxygen atom. The effects on [[tihkal-receptor-binding-serotonin]] and metabolic
stability are discussed in the pharmacology section.

LSM is soluble in organic solvents including ethanol, methanol, and DMSO, and insoluble
in water. Like other lysergamides, it is sensitive to light, heat, and oxidation.

## Pharmacology

LSM acts primarily as a partial agonist at the serotonin 5-HT₂A receptor, the same
receptor primarily responsible for the psychedelic effects of LSD and psilocin.

The morpholine ring modification reduces receptor affinity compared to LSD but does not
eliminate psychedelic activity. In animal models and human reports, LSM produces
effects qualitatively similar to LSD but with reduced potency and altered duration.

The compound also shows affinity for 5-HT₁A, 5-HT₂C, and dopamine D₂ receptors,
consistent with the broader lysergamide pharmacological profile. The exact binding
constants for LSM have not been as thoroughly characterized as those for LSD.

Metabolism likely proceeds via N-dealkylation and aromatic hydroxylation pathways
common to ergoline derivatives. The morpholine ring may confer greater metabolic
stability than the more labile diethylamide group of LSD, potentially contributing
to differences in duration and onset.

## Effects

- **Onset:** 60–120 minutes (slower than LSD)
- **Duration:** 8–12 hours
- **Visual threshold:** Approximately 100–200 µg (estimated; less potent than LSD)
- **Common dose range:** 150–400 µg

The subjective effects of LSM closely parallel those of LSD and other classical
psychedelics, including visual hallucinations, enhanced pattern recognition,
synesthesia, altered time perception, and emotional amplification.

Users and researchers consistently note that LSM is less potent than LSD on a
weight-for-weight basis. Estimates suggest it requires approximately 2–3 times the
dose of LSD to achieve comparable subjective intensity.

The qualitative character of the experience is described as slightly "flatter" or
less "sharp" than LSD, with some reports suggesting a reduction in the characteristic
stimulant component. The come-up phase is reported as more gradual.

Comedown and aftereffects are similar to other lysergamides, including potential
fatigue and mild emotional sensitivity in the 24–48 hours following the experience.

## Legal Status

LSM is controlled as a Schedule I substance under the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] Controlled
[[controlled-substances-act-and-psychedelic-research]] via the Federal Analogue Act, as it is substantially similar to LSD.
Possession, sale, and synthesis are prohibited without DEA registration.

In the United Kingdom, LSM is controlled as a Class A drug under the Misuse of Drugs
Act 1971, as it is an ester or amide of lysergic acid substituted at the nitrogen
atom. The UK Psychoactive Substances Act 2016 provides additional coverage.

International scheduling varies. Many nations control LSM under analogue provisions
or broad lysergamide bans, though it is not specifically listed in the UN Convention
on Psychotropic Substances.

## History

LSM was first synthesized during the 1950s–1960s period of intensive ergoline research
at Sandoz Laboratories. Researchers systematically explored amide substitutions on the
lysergic acid scaffold to map structure-activity relationships.

It appeared in the academic literature in the late 1960s, with publications documenting
its psychotomimetic properties in animal models and limited human trials.

Alexander Shulgin documented LSM in *TiHKAL* (1997), providing synthesis details and a
brief qualitative account. Shulgin noted it was "less interesting" than LSD but
represented an important data point in the lysergamide series.

Modern interest has resurfaced alongside the broader research chemical movement, though
it remains far less common than LSD, 1P-LSD, ALD-52, or ETH-LAD.
## Related Compounds

- **ETH-LAD:** Ethyl-substituted lysergamide with reported increased potency
