## Description

Fungal culture preservation methods encompass the range of techniques used to maintain the viability, morphological, physiological, and genetic integrity of fungal cultures over time. Methods range from simple, low-cost short-term approaches (serial transfer, distilled water storage, oil overlay) to permanent long-term methods (liquid nitrogen cryopreservation, lyophilization). The choice of method depends on the species, available resources, and the intended duration of preservation.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Mycological technique
- **Category**: Culture collection management / Strain preservation
- **Time scales**: Short-term (<1 year), Medium-term (1–10 years), Long-term/Permanent (>10 years)
- **Primary methods**: Continuous growth, drying, freezing

## Key Facts

### Short-Term Preservation (<1 Year)

#### Serial Transfer
- Most common short-term method; simple and inexpensive
- Cultures transferred periodically to fresh agar media
- Stored at 5–20°C or frozen to extend intervals
- Disadvantages: contamination risk (mites, microorganisms), drying, morphological/physiological changes over time
- Risk of losing sporulation ability or pathogenicity after repeated transfers
- Alternating nutrient-rich and nutrient-poor media at each transfer helps maintain healthy cultures
- Inappropriate for long-term preservation (>1 year)

### Medium-Term Preservation (1–10 Years)

#### Oil Overlay
- Low-cost, low-maintenance method for agar slant cultures
- High-quality mineral oil or liquid paraffin sterilized by autoclaving (15 lb pressure, 2 hours)
- Entrapped moisture removed by heating to 170°C for 1–2 hours
- Agar slants covered with ~10 mm of oil; entire surface submerged
- Stored upright at room temperature (15–20°C); 12°C for *Pythium* and *Phytophthora*
- Can preserve cultures for several years, exceptionally up to 32 years
- Suitable for mycelial/nonsporulating cultures not amenable to freezing or freeze-drying
- Oil reduces mite infestations
- Disadvantage: fungi continue to grow slowly; selection for mutants that grow under oil conditions possible
- Many basidiomycetes can be maintained but growth rates slow with increased storage time

#### Distilled Water Storage
- Inexpensive, low-maintenance method
- Water suppresses morphological changes in most fungi
- Successfully used for oomycetes, basidiomycetes, ectomycorrhizal fungi, ascomycetes, hyphomycetes, plant pathogens, actinomycetes, and human pathogens/yeasts
- Most basidiomycetes survive at least 2 years at 5°C; viability decreases after 5–10 years
- Ascomycetes and mitosporic forms survived up to 10 years at 20°C
- Sterile straws or Pasteur pipettes used to cut disks from growing colony edge
- Disks transferred to sterile tubes/vials with distilled water
- Room temperature storage (loosely capped) or 4°C (tightly capped)

#### Organic Substrata

- **Wood chips**: Wood-inhabiting fungi stored on beech wood chips in malt-extract broth; some survive up to 10 years; requires vigorous colonization
- **Cereal grains**: *Sclerotinia*, *Magnaporthe*, *Leptosphaeria*, *Rhizoctonia* stored on oats, barley, wheat, rye, millet, sorghum for up to 10 years; grains soaked, autoclaved, inoculated, incubated, dried, and frozen at -25°C
- **Agar strips**: Vacuum-dried strips from colony growing edge; *Pythium*, *Rhizoctonia*, some basidiomycetes survive 18 months; ascomycetes 3–5 years
- **Insect/plant tissue**: Host tissue used for pathogenic fungi; infected roots dried and frozen; *Neozygites* conidia preserved on frozen aphid mummies

#### Soil or Sand Storage
- Low-maintenance, cost-effective for *Rhizoctonia*, *Septoria*, *Pseudocercosporella*
- Glass bottles filled with sand or loam soil, autoclaved
- Spore/mycelial suspension added; 2–14 days growth at room temperature
- Stored at 4°C

#### Silica Gel Method
- Developed by Perkins (1962) for *Neurospora* species
- Preserves sporulating fungi for 4–11 years
- Prevents all fungal growth and metabolism
- Skim milk used as protectant; spores suspended and added to chilled silica gel
- After drying, tubes stored at 4°C in moisture-proof containers
- *Pythium* and *Phytophthora* do not survive this process
- Fungal Genetic Stock Center has used this method since 1962 for *Aspergillus nidulans* and *Neurospora crassa*
- Glass beads can substitute for silica gel

### Long-Term/Permanent Preservation

#### Freezing (Mechanical)
- Most fungi survive at -20°C to -80°C
- Failure rate: 5.1% for mitosporic ascomycetes, zygomycetes, yeasts after 5 years at -20°C
- Failure rate: 2.3% for medically important fungi after 6 months–13 years at -70°C
- 10% glycerol as cryoprotectant; nonsporulating basidiomycetes and ascomycetes preserved up to 5 years at -80°C
- Vigorously growing/sporulating cultures survive better
- Repeated freeze-thaw cycles significantly reduce viability

#### Liquid Nitrogen Cryopreservation
- Most effective permanent preservation method
- Storage at or below -139°C (ice crystals stop growing; biophysical processes too slow to affect survival)
- Recommended for dictyostelids, amoebae, zygomycetes (Entomophthorales), oomycetes, phytopathogenic fungi, yeasts, poorly sporulating ascomycetes, higher basidiomycetes growing only as mycelia
- Prevents increased genetic variability of distributed stocks
- ATCC and major collections store seed stock in liquid nitrogen for genetic fidelity

**Cryoprotectants**:
- Penetrating agents: glycerol, DMSO (pass through cell membrane; protect intracellularly and extracellularly)
- Nonpenetrating agents: sucrose, lactose, glucose, mannitol, sorbitol, dextran, polyvinyl-pyrrolidone, hydroxyethyl starch (protect externally)
- Glycerol and DMSO most effective for fungi; polyethylene glycol also usable

#### Lyophilization (Freeze-Drying)
- Fungal cultures frozen then dried under vacuum
- Highly successful with mitospore-producing cultures
- Excellent for permanent preservation (recommended alongside cryopreservation)
- Requires specialized, expensive equipment

### Sclerotization
- Some fungi produce sclerotia or other long-term survival propagules in culture
- Stored at 3–5°C; viable for 1–5 years
- *Magnaporthe*, *Phymatotrichum*, *Cylindrocladium* produce sclerotia/microsclerotia viable 2–5 years
- Myxomycete spherules germinated after 1–3 years
- *Physarum* sclerotia induced on sterile cellophane over 1% water agar

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Culture preservation is fundamental to mushroom cultivation for maintaining production strains, breeding stock, and reference collections. Commercial operations must preserve vigorous, high-yielding strains without genetic drift. The distilled water and oil overlay methods are accessible to small-scale cultivators, while liquid nitrogen is the gold standard for commercial strain libraries. Understanding which methods work for specific groups (e.g., basidiomycetes generally harder to preserve than ascomycetes) directly impacts strain management decisions. The risk of losing sporulation or fruiting ability through repeated serial transfer is a critical consideration for cultivators who must decide between convenience and genetic stability.
