## Soil Block

## Description
A soil block is a compressed cube of growing medium used as a container-free transplant production system. Instead of plastic pots or trays, seedlings are grown in self-standing blocks of compressed potting mix, which are then transplanted directly into the field or greenhouse bed. Eliot Coleman uses soil blocks extensively for producing transplants of overwintered crops like spinach and lettuce that are later set out under quick hoops. The technique eliminates transplant shock because roots air-prune at the block surface rather than circling inside a container.

## Classification
- **Category**: Propagation technique / Transplant production system
- **Type**: Container-free [[coleman-sowing-seedling-production]]
- **Materials**: Compressed growing medium (potting mix)
- **Tools**: Soil block maker (hand press or mechanical)

## Key Facts

### How It Works
- A specialized metal form (soil block maker) is pressed into a moistened potting mix
- The form creates compressed cubes, typically in standard sizes: 3/4-inch (mini), 1.5-inch, and 2-inch
- Seeds are placed in small indentations on top of each block
- Blocks are placed on open trays or propagation benches — no containers needed
- Root tips reach the edge of the block and stop growing (air pruning)
- When transplanted, roots immediately begin growing into surrounding soil

### Advantages Over Container Transplants
- **No transplant shock**: Roots are not disturbed or circling at transplant time
- **No plastic waste**: Eliminates the need for pots, trays, and cell packs
- **Better root architecture**: Air pruning creates a dense, fibrous root system
- **Lower cost**: No ongoing container purchase or storage
- **Faster field establishment**: Roots grow immediately into surrounding soil
- **Space efficient**: Blocks can be packed tightly on benches

### Coleman's Usage
- Used for [[coleman-overwintered-spinach-cold-house-production]] and lettuce crops under quick hoops
- Spinach and lettuce seeded in soil blocks on October 1
- Transplanted out two weeks later (mid-October)
- Direct seeding an alternative but outdoor germination in fall can be spotty
- Soil block transplants provide more reliable establishment for late-fall plantings
- Described in detail in *The New Organic Grower*

### Soil Block Sizes
- **Micro blocks (3/4-inch)**: For tiny seeds, germination stage
- **Standard blocks (1.5-inch)**: Most common for general transplanting
- **Large blocks (2-inch)**: For larger-seeded crops or longer growing periods
- Blocks can be nested: a micro block inserted into a standard block for potting-on without disturbance

### Potting Mix Requirements
- Mix must have sufficient fibrous material to hold together when compressed
- Typically includes peat moss, compost, perlite/vermiculite, and lime
- Must be moist enough to bind but not waterlogged
- Coleman's mix formulations detailed in *The New Organic Grower*

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Mushroom Substrate Analog
- The compressed block concept parallels mushroom substrate block production
- [[enterprise-development-mushroom-growers]] already use compressed sawdust/bran blocks (e.g., for shiitake, oyster mushrooms)
- The principle of a self-contained, containerless growing medium is identical
- Air pruning concept applies: mycelium at the block surface benefits from gas exchange

### Spawn Production
- Soil block techniques could be adapted for creating mushroom spawn blocks
- The compression provides structural integrity without containers
- Moisture retention in compressed blocks is relevant to maintaining substrate hydration

### Transplant Establishment
- The elimination of transplant shock is directly relevant to [[growing-gourmet-outdoor-mushroom-bed-establishment]] inoculation
- Spawn blocks or plug spawn placed in beds establish faster when the surrounding medium is compatible
- The principle of matching the block medium to the target environment reduces establishment failure

## Historical Context
- Soil block technology was developed and popularized in European market gardening
- Particularly associated with Dutch and Belgian intensive [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]]
- Coleman learned and adapted the technique for American [[coleman-deep-organic-farming]]
- The method aligns with organic principles of waste reduction and working with biological systems
