# Psilocybe cubensis

*Psilocybe cubensis* is the most widely known and cultivated psilocybin-containing mushroom in the world. A tropical and subtropical coprophilous species, it has served as the primary subject of mycological research into psychedelic fungi and remains the most accessible species for both study and cultivation due to its robust growth and forgiving fruiting requirements.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae
- **Genus:** *Psilocybe* (section *Cubensae*)
- **Species:** *P. cubensis* (Earle) Singer, 1948
- **Synonyms:** *Stropharia cubensis* Earle (1906), *Naematoloma caerulescens* Murrill

The species was first described by Franklin Sumner Earle in 1906 from material collected in Cuba. Rolf Singer transferred it to *Psilocybe* in 1948. Gastón Guzmán's 1983 monograph *The Genus Psilocybe* established the modern taxonomic framework, placing it in section *Cubensae* alongside *P. subcubensis* and *P. cambodginiensis*.

## Description

### Macroscopic Features
- **Cap (Pileus):** 1.5–8 cm diameter, convex to plane with age, often with a broad umbo. Surface smooth to slightly viscid when moist. Color varies from golden-brown to pale straw-yellow, bruising blue-green where damaged.
- **Gills (Lamellae):** Adnate to adnexed, gray when young, darkening to deep purple-brown with spore maturity. Gill edges remain whitish (appendiculate).
- **Stipe (Stem):** 4–15 cm long, 0.4–1.4 cm thick, hollow, equal to slightly clavate. White to yellowish, bruising prominently blue. Partial veil leaving a persistent membranous annulus.
- **Veil:** Well-developed partial veil that ruptures during maturation, leaving a membranous annulus on the upper stipe. Universal veil absent or rudimentary.
- **Flesh:** White, bruising distinctly blue to blue-green on exposure to air due to psilocin oxidation.
- **Spore print:** Dark purple-brown to nearly black.
- **Odor:** Farinaceous (mealy or flour-like), mild.
- **Taste:** Farinaceous, mild.

### Microscopic Features
- **Basidiospores:** 11.5–17.5 × 7.5–9.5 µm, subellipsoid to rhomboid in face view, thick-walled, with a distinct germ pore.
- **Basidia:** 4-spored, occasionally 2-spored (which tends to produce larger sporocarps).
- **Cheilocystidia:** Ventricose to lageniform, 15–30 × 4–8 µm.
- **Pleurocystidia:** Scarce to absent.

## Distribution and Habitat

*P. cubensis* is pantropical and subtropical, found naturally in:
- Southern United States (Gulf Coast states, particularly Florida and Texas)
- Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean
- South America (Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Brazil, Bolivia)
- Sub-Saharan Africa
- Southeast Asia (Thailand, Cambodia, Vietnam, Indonesia)
- Northern Australia
- Parts of South Asia (Goa, Tamil Nadu)

The species is coprophilous, fruiting primarily on the dung of herbivorous mammals, especially cattle (*Bos taurus*) and water buffalo (*Bubalus bubalis*). It rarely grows on enriched soils or decaying plant matter. Fruiting occurs during warm, humid conditions, typically after heavy rainfall, with peak seasons varying by latitude.

## Chemistry

### Active Compounds

| Compound | Typical Range (% dry weight) |
|----------|------------------------------|
| Psilocybin | 0.5–1.2% (up to 1.8% in select varieties) |
| Psilocin | 0.1–0.4% |
| Baeocystin | 0.01–0.3% |
| Norbaeocystin | Trace |

Psilocybin (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) is a prodrug that is dephosphorylated to the pharmacologically active psilocin (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) by alkaline phosphatase enzymes in the body. Psilocin acts primarily as a partial agonist at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, with additional activity at 5-HT1A, 5-HT2C, and 5-HT2B receptors.

### Varietal Chemistry Variation
Significant variation in psilocybin content exists between geographic populations and cultivated varieties. Factors affecting potency include:
- Genetics (strain/variety selection)
- Substrate composition and nutritional profile
- Fruiting temperature and humidity
- Harvest timing (before or after veil rupture)
- Post-harvest handling and storage conditions

## Named Varieties

Cultivated varieties (often called "strains" in popular usage, though mycologically these are not distinct strains but rather clonal lineages or regional populations) include:

- **Golden Teacher:** One of the most widely circulated varieties. Moderate potency, reliable fruiting, large golden caps. Popular with novice cultivators.
- **B+:** Large-fruiting variety with caramel-colored caps. Tolerant of a wider range of environmental conditions. Good beginner variety.
- **Ecuador:** Collected from highland Ecuadorian specimens. Produces dense, medium-sized fruits with above-average potency.
- **Cambodian:** Originating from specimens found near Angkor Wat. Fast colonizer, moderate potency, smaller fruiting bodies.
- **Mazatapec:** One of the original Mexican varieties. Slower colonizer but known for a distinctive visual experience profile.
- **Thai (Koh Samui):** Collected from Koh Samui, Thailand. Rapid colonizer, moderate potency, shorter, stockier fruits.
- **Penis Envy:** A distinctive mutation with unusually thick stems and underdeveloped caps. Consistently among the most potent cultivated varieties, with reported psilocybin content 1.5–3× that of typical *P. cubensis*.
- **APE (Albino Penis Envy):** Leucistic mutation of Penis Envy. Reduced pigmentation, high potency.
- **Lizard King:** Found growing on wood chips and bovine dung in the American Pacific Northwest. Unusual for *P. cubensis* in its occasional lignicolous habit.
- **Menace:** A recent selection known for consistently high potency and dense cluster formation.

## Cultivation

*P. cubensis* is the most commonly cultivated psychedelic mushroom due to its:
- Rapid colonization speed (7–14 days on grain spawn)
- Tolerance of a wide range of substrates and environmental conditions
- Reliable fruiting on simplified substrates
- Dense yields (50–200+ grams fresh per 4-liter substrate block)

### Common Substrates
- **Grain spawn:** Rye grain, brown rice, millet, or wheat, sterilized by pressure cooking
- **Bulk substrates:** Coco coir and vermiculite (CVG), pasteurized straw, composted horse manure
- **All-in-one methods:** Brown rice flour and vermiculite in half-pint jars (PF Tek), the method that introduced widespread home cultivation

### Fruiting Parameters
- **Temperature:** 23–27°C (73–80°F) optimal for fruiting
- **Humidity:** 90–95% relative humidity for primordia formation, 85–92% during fruit maturation
- **Fresh air exchange (FAE):** Critical during all stages to prevent CO₂ accumulation and promote normal development
- **Light:** Ambient indirect light (12 hours on/off cycle) is a secondary but necessary trigger for fruiting direction and metabolism

## Ethnobotanical and Historical Significance

*P. cubensis* was used ceremonially by several Mesoamerican cultures. Stone "mushroom stones" dating to 300–500 BCE found in Guatemala and Mexico may depict *Psilocybe* species. The species gained worldwide attention after R. Gordon Wasson's 1957 *Life* magazine article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom," which documented Mazatec mushroom ceremonies in Oaxaca, Mexico, where Maria Sabina used *P. cubensis* (called *nti-ši-tho*, "the little ones that spring forth") alongside *P. mexicana*.

Albert Hofmann isolated and identified psilocybin and psilocin from *P. cubensis* specimens collected by Wasson in 1958, publishing the results in *Experientia*.

## Legal Status

*P. cubensis* is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance in the United States under the Controlled Substances Act. It is similarly restricted in most countries, though legal frameworks are evolving:
- **Decriminalized:** Several US municipalities including Denver, Oakland, Santa Cruz, Ann Arbor, and Washington D.C. (possession and personal cultivation deprioritized)
- **Legal/Regulated:** Oregon (Ballot Measure 109, 2020) permits supervised therapeutic use of psilocybin-containing mushrooms
- **Netherlands:** Fresh psilocybin mushrooms (truffles) were sold openly until a 2008 ban; *Psilocybe tampanensis* and *P. mexicana* sclerotia (truffles) remain available
- **Brazil:** Cultivation and use are not explicitly criminalized
- **Jamaica:** Legal and home to several licensed retreat centers
