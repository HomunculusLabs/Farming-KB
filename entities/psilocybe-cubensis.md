# Psilocybe cubensis

**Psilocybe cubensis** is a species of psychedelic mushroom whose principal active compounds are psilocybin and psilocin. It is the most widely known and cultivated psilocybin-containing mushroom in the world, belonging to the family Hymenogastraceae.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi → **Division:** Basidiomycota → **Class:** Agaricomycetes → **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae → **Genus:** *Psilocybe* (section *Cubensae*) → **Species:** *P. cubensis* (Earle) Singer, 1949

Originally described as *Stropharia cubensis* by Franklin Sumner Earle in 1906 from Cuban specimens, the species was reclassified by Rolf Singer into *Psilocybe* in 1949. Several synonyms exist including *Naematoloma caerulescens*.

## Morphology

*P. cubensis* is a medium-to-large agaric with the following features:

- **Cap (Pileus):** 1.5–8 cm diameter, conic to convex when young, becoming broadly convex to plane with age. Surface is hygrophanous — reddish-cinnamon when moist, fading to pale yellowish or white when dry. Often develops a distinct umbo.
- **Gills (Lamellae):** Adnate to adnexed, grayish in youth, darkening to deep purple-brown to nearly black at maturity, with white edges.
- **Stipe (Stem):** 4–15 cm tall, 0.4–1.5 cm thick, hollow, white to yellowish. Bruises prominently blue when damaged — a key identification feature from psilocin oxidation.
- **Veil:** Persistent membranous annulus (ring) on the upper stipe from the partial veil. Universal veil remnants may form a volva at the base.
- **Spore Print:** Dark purplish-brown. **Spores:** Ellipsoid, 11.5–17.5 × 8–11.5 µm, thick-walled, with a distinct germ pore.

## Lifecycle

Spores germinate on suitable substrate to form haploid monokaryotic mycelium. Two compatible monokaryons fuse into dikaryotic mycelium, which colonizes the substrate. Under high humidity, fresh air exchange, and temperatures of 24–27°C, primordia (pins) form and develop into mature fruiting bodies within 5–15 days. Mature caps release billions of spores. The full cycle from inoculation to harvest typically spans 4–8 weeks.

## Natural Habitat

*P. cubensis* is a coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) species native to subtropical and tropical regions worldwide. It grows on dung of herbivores including cattle, water buffalo, horses, and elephants. Geographic distribution spans:

- **Americas:** Mexico, Central America, Colombia, Ecuador, Brazil, Peru, and the U.S. Gulf Coast states (Florida, Texas, Louisiana)
- **Caribbean:** Cuba, Puerto Rico, Jamaica, Trinidad
- **Southeast Asia:** Thailand, Vietnam, Cambodia, Indonesia, Philippines
- **Africa:** Nigeria, Tanzania, Kenya, Madagascar
- **Oceania:** Northeastern Australia

It fruits predominantly during warm, humid rainy seasons, from sea level to approximately 1,800 meters elevation.

## Chemical Profile

| Compound | Concentration (dry weight) | Notes |
|----------|---------------------------|-------|
| **Psilocybin** | 0.44–1.35% | Prodrug; dephosphorylated to psilocin in vivo |
| **Psilocin** | 0.10–0.60% | Primary psychoactive; 5-HT2A receptor agonist |
| **Baeocystin** | 0.01–0.30% | N-methyl-4-hydroxytryptamine; role not fully characterized |
| **Norbaeocystin** | Trace | 4-hydroxytryptamine; minor constituent |

Total tryptamine content varies significantly by strain, substrate, growing conditions, genetics, and harvest timing. Potency is highest just before veil tearing and degrades with drying, heat, light, and oxygen exposure.

## Common Strains

Hundreds of cultivated strains exist. Notable varieties include:

- **Golden Teacher:** Most popular strain; moderate potency, large fruits, golden caps, reliable yields. Favored by novices.
- **B+ (Be Positive):** Robust, wide-ranging tolerance; large caps, moderate potency, excellent colonizer.
- **Ecuador:** Highland Ecuador origin; tall thick stipes, moderate-to-high potency, prefers cooler fruiting.
- **Cambodian:** Near Angkor Wat; fast colonizer, small-to-medium fruits, contamination-resistant.
- **Thai (Koh Samui):** Fast fruiting, moderate potency, slender stature.
- **Penis Envy:** Mutation-derived; unusually high potency (1.5–3× average), thick dense fruits, slow colonizer.
- **Albino A+:** Leucistic variant with white caps; moderate potency, distinct appearance.
- **McKennaii:** Named for Terence McKenna; high potency, large fruits.

Strain differences in potency, morphology, and growth are attributed to genetic and epigenetic variation, not species-level differences.

## Cultivation Methods

*P. cubensis* is widely cultivated for its fast growth and forgiving nature:

### Substrate & Spawn
- **Spawn grains:** Rye berries, brown rice, whole oats, or millet; sterilized at 15 PSI for 90–120 minutes.
- **Bulk substrates:** Pasteurized straw, composted manure, coco coir + vermiculite (CVG), or coco coir + vermiculite + gypsum.
- **All-in-one bags:** Pre-sterilized grain-to-bulk mixtures for simplicity.

### Techniques
1. **PF Tek:** Brown rice flour + vermiculite in half-pint jars, steam sterilized; beginner-friendly, small yields.
2. **Monotub:** Spawn inoculated into bulk substrate in modified plastic tubs; high yields, industry standard.
3. **Spawn Bags:** Grain bags inoculated with liquid culture or spore syringe; expanded into monotubs.
4. **Uncle Ben's Tek:** Pre-cooked rice bags as simplified spawn medium; popular with beginners.

### Environmental Parameters
- **Colonization:** 24–27°C; **Fruiting:** 21–24°C; **Humidity:** 85–95%; **FAE:** Essential during fruiting; **Light:** Ambient indirect, 12h on/off cycle.

## Potency Variation

Potency varies by a factor of 2–10× between samples, influenced by genetics (Penis Envy highest; Golden Teacher moderate), harvest timing (pre-veil-tear most potent), substrate nutrition, drying method (freeze-drying best), and storage conditions. Typical dried dosages: 1.0–2.5 g (mild) to 3.0–5.0 g (strong).

## Identification and Safety

The primary dangerous look-alike is **Galerina marginata** (deadly galerina), which contains lethal amatoxins. Key differences: *P. cubensis* grows on herbivore dung with a dark purple-brown spore print and distinct blue bruising, while *G. marginata* grows on wood debris with a rusty brown print and no bruising. Other look-alikes include inactive *Psathyrella* and low-potency *Panaeolus* species.

*P. cubensis* is not physiologically toxic (LD₅₀ in mice ~280 mg/kg psilocybin, ~1,000× typical human dose). No fatalities from ingestion alone are documented. Risks include psychological distress at high doses, nausea during onset, rare HPPD, and fatal misidentification of *Galeria* species.

## Legal Status

- **United States:** Schedule I federally. Oregon, Colorado, and several municipalities have decriminalized possession.
- **UN:** Psilocybin is Schedule I under the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances.
- **Netherlands:** Fresh mushrooms banned in 2008; psilocybin truffles (*sclerotia*) remain legally sold.
- **Bahamas, Jamaica, Samoa:** No specific prohibition; retreat centers operate openly.
- **Canada:** Schedule III; medical exemptions granted case-by-case.
- **Portugal:** Decriminalized for personal use since 2001.

## Historical and Cultural Use

Psilocybin mushroom use dates back at least 6,000–9,000 years in Mesoamerica. Stone "mushroom stones" from Guatemala (~1000 BCE) and murals at Tepantitla, Teotihuacan (300–500 CE) depict ritual mushroom use. Known in Nahuatl as **teonanácatl** ("flesh of the gods"), mushrooms were used in healing and divination by Aztec, Mazatec, Mixtec, and Zapotec peoples — traditions continuing today.

R. Gordon Wasson participated in a Mazatec velada in 1956, publishing his account in *Life* magazine (1957) and bringing *P. cubensis* to global attention. Albert Hofmann isolated psilocybin and psilocin from the species in 1958 at Sandoz Laboratories. The McKenna brothers pioneered cultivation in the 1970s, and the PF Tek (1990s) democratized home cultivation.

## Current Research

Renewed scientific interest has produced significant findings:

- **Treatment-resistant depression:** Carhart-Harris et al. (2016) demonstrated sustained antidepressant effects from a single psilocybin dose. COMPASS Pathways and Usona Institute are conducting Phase II/III trials.
- **End-of-life anxiety:** Studies at Johns Hopkins (Ross 2016) and NYU (Griffiths 2016) showed significant reductions in depression and anxiety in cancer patients.
- **Neuroplasticity:** Psilocybin promotes structural and functional neural plasticity through 5-HT2A receptor-mediated pathways.
- **Default Mode Network:** fMRI studies show decreased DMN connectivity correlating with ego dissolution and mystical experiences.
- **Microdosing:** Widely practiced anecdotally for cognitive enhancement; rigorous clinical evidence remains limited and mixed.

As of 2026, psilocybin-assisted therapy holds FDA breakthrough therapy designations and is in advanced clinical trials across multiple countries, with therapeutic approval anticipated in the near term.
