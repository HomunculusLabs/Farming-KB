## Overview

**Psilocybe cubensis** is a coprophilous basidiomycete mushroom and the most widely known psychoactive fungus in the world. It is the most commonly cultivated psilocybin-containing mushroom due to its relatively straightforward growth requirements and substantial fruiting body size.

The species was first described in 1906 by Franklin Sumner Earle in Cuba, from specimens found growing on bovine dung. Common names include "golden teacher," "cubes," and "San Isidro." It has become the foundational species for both recreational and therapeutic research into classical psychedelics.

P. cubensis contains the psychoactive alkaloids psilocybin and psilocin, which are serotonergic 5-HT2A receptor agonists. It has been used for millennia in Mesoamerican indigenous ceremonies and is now the subject of renewed clinical interest for treating depression, anxiety, and substance use disorders.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:**

- Kingdom: Fungi
- Division: Basidiomycota
- Class: Agaricomycetes
- Order: Agaricales
- Family: Hymenogastraceae
- Genus: *Psilocybe*
- Species: *P. cubensis* (Earle) Singer

The species was originally placed in the genus *Stropharia* by Rolf Singer in 1949 and has undergone several taxonomic revisions. Genetic studies place it within a clade of coprophilous *Psilocybe* species primarily distributed in subtropical and tropical regions.

**Morphology:**

The cap is 1.5–8 cm in diameter, convex to plano-convex, often with a broad umbo. Color ranges from pale golden-brown when young to lighter tan or white with age. The cap surface is hygrophanous, drying to a markedly lighter color. A removable gelatinous pellicle covers the cap surface, a key diagnostic feature.

Gills are adnate to adnexed, gray when immature, darkening to purplish-brown with spore maturity. The stipe is 4–15 cm tall, 0.4–1.4 cm thick, hollow, and whitish, often bruising bluish where damaged. Spore prints are dark purplish-brown. Spores measure 11.5–17.5 × 8–12 µm, ellipsoid to subrhomboid in shape, with a thick wall and a broad germ pore.

## Distribution and Habitat

P. cubensis is pantropical and subtropical in distribution. It occurs naturally throughout the Gulf Coast states of the US, Central and South America, the Caribbean, Southeast Asia, and subtropical regions of Australia and Africa. It has been introduced to parts of Europe and elsewhere through human activity.

The species is fundamentally coprophilous, fruiting primarily from the dung of large herbivores — especially cattle (*Bos taurus*), but also horses, elephants, and water buffalo. It colonizes dung in pastures, grasslands, and tropical lowland forests. Substrate preference is fresh to aged bovine dung in warm, humid environments with regular rainfall.

Fruiting occurs most abundantly during warm rainy seasons when temperatures remain consistently above 18°C (65°F) with high ambient humidity. In tropical climates, fruiting can occur nearly year-round.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

The principal psychoactive constituents are:

- **Psilocybin** (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine): The prodrug form, comprising approximately 0.5–1.3% of dried weight. It is dephosphorylated to psilocin by alkaline phosphatase enzymes in vivo.
- **Psilocin** (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine): The active metabolite, present at 0.1–0.6% of dried weight. Primary agonist at 5-HT2A receptors.
- **Baeocystin** (4-phosphoryloxy-N-methyltryptamine): A minor alkaloid present at 0.01–0.3%, with weak psychoactive activity.
- **Norbaeocystin** (4-phosphoryloxytryptamine): Trace constituent of uncertain pharmacological significance.

Psilocybin and psilocin act primarily as agonists at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors in the prefrontal cortex. This receptor activation produces altered perception, synesthesia, ego dissolution, and changes in emotional processing. Therapeutic effects are hypothesized to involve increased neural plasticity, reduced default mode network activity, and enhanced emotional processing through amygdala modulation.

Dried material typically contains 0.5–1.0% total psilocybin, though this varies significantly by strain, growing conditions, and harvest timing.

## Cultivation

P. cubensis is the easiest psilocybin-containing mushroom to cultivate and serves as the standard species for both amateur and research cultivation. It fruits readily on a wide range of cellulosic substrates.

**Substrates:** Brown rice flour/vermiculite (PF tek), grain spawn (rye, millet, wheat) transferred to bulk substrates such as coco coir, manure, or straw. Supplemented substrates with gypsum and vermiculite improve yields.

**Environmental parameters:**

- Colonization temperature: 24–27°C (75–80°F)
- Fruiting temperature: 20–25°C (68–77°F)
- Relative humidity: 85–95% during fruiting
- Fresh air exchange: Essential for proper fruiting body development
- Light: Ambient indirect light sufficient; not photosynthetic

- Fruiting typically begins 7–14 days after introducing fruiting conditions.
- Biological efficiency (dry weight of mushrooms per dry weight of substrate): 50–200% depending on technique.

The species is notable for its robust colonization speed, resistance to contamination on properly prepared substrates, and willingness to fruit in simple fruiting chambers without elaborate environmental controls.

## Ecological Role

As a primary decomposer of herbivore dung, P. cubensis plays a significant role in nutrient cycling in tropical and subtropical grassland ecosystems. It breaks down complex plant material that has already been partially digested by herbivores, accelerating the return of nitrogen and minerals to the soil.

The bluing reaction upon bruising is caused by the oxidation of psilocin to quinoid compounds. This reaction may serve a defensive function, as quinone oxidations produce reactive compounds toxic to insect larvae and other potential grazers. Several studies suggest psilocybin in coprophilous fungi evolved as an anti-insectan defense, deterring dung-feeding invertebrates from consuming the fruiting body.

P. cubensis contributes to the fungal diversity of pasture ecosystems and interacts with a range of soil microorganisms during its saprotrophic phase. Its mycelial networks help aggregate dung particles and facilitate microbial succession in the decomposition process.

## Varieties and Strains

Numerous cultivated varieties of P. cubensis have been selected or isolated by mycologists and cultivators. These strains differ in growth speed, potency, fruiting body morphology, and colonization vigor, though all belong to the same species genetically.

Notable cultivated strains include:

- **Golden Teacher**: The most widely distributed strain, known for golden caps, moderate potency, and reliable fruiting. Origin uncertain; likely collected in the southeastern US or Gulf Coast region.
- **B+**: A robust strain producing large fruiting bodies, tolerant of a wide range of environmental conditions. Often recommended for beginners.
- **Penis Envy**: A distinctive strain with thick, dense stems and reduced cap size. Reports suggest higher-than-average potency. A mutation derived from Amazonian stock.
- **Amazonian**: Originally collected in the Amazon basin. Produces large flushes of medium-sized mushrooms with moderate to high potency.
- **Cambodian**: Isolated near Angkor Wat, Cambodia. Known for fast colonization and slender fruiting bodies.
- **Thai Koh Samui**: Collected on the island of Koh Samui, Thailand. Small to medium fruits with a reputation for rapid, intense effects.

Genetic analyses reveal that despite morphological differences, cultivated strains show relatively low genetic diversity compared to wild populations. This bottleneck reflects decades of clonal propagation from limited founder collections. Wild populations from different geographic regions show greater genetic divergence than any cultivated strains.

## Legal Status and Research

Psilocybin and psilocybin-containing mushrooms are scheduled as controlled substances in many jurisdictions worldwide. In the US, psilocybin is classified as Schedule I under the Controlled Substances Act, though several cities and states have deprioritized or decriminalized possession of entheogenic fungi.

Clinical research into P. cubensis-derived psilocybin has experienced a significant resurgence since the 2010s. Notable trials include:

- COMPASS Pathways Phase IIb trial for treatment-resistant depression, using synthetic psilocybin at 25 mg doses.
- Johns Hopkins studies demonstrating sustained antidepressant and anxiolytic effects in cancer patients and healthy volunteers.
- Ongoing trials at Imperial College London examining psilocybin for depression using fMRI-based neuroimaging.

The FDA granted Breakthrough Therapy designation to psilocybin-assisted therapy for treatment-resistant depression in 2018 and again in 2024, accelerating clinical development timelines. Several biotech companies are now pursuing psilocybin-based therapeutics derived from synthetic or fungal sources.
