# Psilocybe cubensis

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae
- **Genus:** *Psilocybe*
- **Species:** *P. cubensis*

*Psilocybe cubensis* was first described by Franklin Sumner Earle in 1906 from specimens collected in Cuba. It is the most widely known and cultivated psilocybin-containing mushroom. The species epithet *cubensis* refers to its type locality. Over 60 published strains or varieties exist, though most lack formal taxonomic recognition and are better described as cultivars or genetic lineages rather than true taxonomic subspecies.

## Morphology

- **Cap:** 1.5–8 cm diameter; convex when young, expanding to plane or slightly uplifted with age. Surface smooth, hygrophanous — golden-brown to ochraceous when moist, fading to pale straw or nearly white when dry. Often develops a distinct umbo (central bump).
- **Gills:** Adnate to adnexed, close, initially pale gray, darkening to deep purple-black with spore maturity. Spore print is dark purple-brown.
- **Stipe:** 4–15 cm tall, 0.4–1.5 cm thick; cylindrical, hollow, fibrous. White to yellowish. Surface bruises prominently blue when damaged, a key identification feature.
- **Veil:** Partial veil present in young specimens, leaving a persistent annulus (ring) on the upper stipe after rupture. Annulus membranous, often darkening with spore deposition.
- **Flesh:** Thin, whitish to pale yellow. Bruises blue to blue-black where injured due to oxidative degradation of psilocin.
- **Microscopic:** Spores ellipsoid, 11.5–17.5 × 8–11.5 µm, thick-walled, with a large germ pore. Basidia 4-spored. Cheilocystidia variable, fusiform to ventricose.

## Life Cycle

*P. cubensis* follows the typical agaric life cycle. Haploid basidiospores germinate on suitable substrate, forming monokaryotic mycelium. Compatible monokaryons fuse to form dikaryotic mycelium, which colonizes the substrate extensively. Under appropriate environmental conditions (high humidity, 23–27°C, fresh air exchange, and light), the mycelium forms hyphal knots that develop into primordia (pins). These mature into full fruiting bodies within 5–14 days. Mature basidia on the gill surfaces undergo karyogamy and meiosis, producing haploid spores that are forcibly discharged into the air to complete the cycle.

## Chemical Profile

The principal psychoactive compounds are psilocybin and psilocin, both tryptamine alkaloids:

- **Psilocybin:** Typically 0.5–1.3% dry weight; can reach 1.8% in potent strains. A prodrug — dephosphorylated to psilocin by alkaline phosphatase enzymes in vivo.
- **Psilocin:** Typically 0.1–0.6% dry weight; the pharmacologically active metabolite responsible for subjective effects.
- **Minor alkaloids:** Baeocystin (0.01–0.3%), norbaeocystin, and aeruginascin have been detected but are present in trace amounts and their individual pharmacological contributions remain poorly characterized.

Potency varies significantly based on genetics, substrate composition, growing conditions, harvest timing, and storage/drying method.

## Pharmacology

Psilocybin is a prodrug for psilocin, which acts primarily as a **5-HT2A serotonin receptor agonist** with additional affinity for 5-HT1A, 5-HT2B, 5-HT2C, and a range of other serotonergic and dopaminergic receptors. The 5-HT2A agonism in cortical layer V pyramidal neurons is the primary mechanism driving the characteristic psychedelic effects. Functional MRI studies demonstrate that psilocin decreases default mode network activity and increases global brain connectivity, correlating with subjective experiences of ego dissolution and altered perception.

The effective oral dose of psilocybin is approximately 6–20 mg (equivalent to roughly 1–3.5 g dried *P. cubensis* material, depending on potency). Onset occurs within 20–60 minutes, peak effects at 60–120 minutes, with total duration of 4–6 hours. Psilocybin is metabolized primarily by hepatic monoamine oxidase (MAO-A), with a plasma elimination half-life of approximately 2.5 hours.

## Effects

- **Perceptual:** Visual distortions, enhanced color perception, geometric patterns (often eyes-closed), synesthesia, altered perception of time and space.
- **Cognitive:** Altered thought patterns, enhanced introspection, ego dissolution at higher doses, increased cognitive flexibility.
- **Emotional:** Heightened emotional range, mystical or transcendental experiences, anxiety (dose-dependent, influenced by set and setting).
- **Somatic:** Nausea (common in early phase), pupil dilation, mild tachycardia, changes in body temperature perception.

## Cultivation Methods

*P. cubensis* is the most commonly cultivated psilocybin mushroom due to its relatively simple requirements and robust growth. Standard methods include:

- **Spore syringe inoculation** onto sterilized grain spawn (rye, brown rice, millet) in mason jars.
- **Spawn-to-bulk transfer** into pasteurized or sterilized bulk substrates: coco coir/vermiculite, manure-based compost, or straw.
- **Monotub fruiting** — the most popular home cultivation method, using modified plastic storage containers with passive and active air exchange.
- **Optimal conditions:** Substrate temperature 24–27°C during colonization; fruiting temperature 22–25°C with relative humidity >90%, fresh air exchange, and indirect light (12 hours on/off cycle).

Yields of 100–300 g fresh weight per 1 kg dry substrate are typical. PF Tek (brown rice flour and vermiculite cakes) is the standard beginner method.

## Notable Strains and Varieties

Strains are domesticated lineages selected for specific traits. Nomenclature is informal and not taxonomically standardized:

- **Golden Teacher:** One of the most widely distributed strains; moderate potency, reliable fruiting, distinctive golden caps. Popular with beginners.
- **B+:** Large fruiting bodies, resilient to suboptimal conditions, moderate potency. One of the oldest domesticated strains.
- **Ecuador:** Dense, medium-to-large fruits with thick stems; reported moderate-to-high potency.
- **Penis Envy:** Distinctive morphology with thick, underdeveloped caps and dense stems. Among the most potent domesticated strains, with psilocybin content reportedly 1.5–2× typical *P. cubensis* levels.
- **Cambodian:** Fast colonizer, moderate size, moderate potency. Originally collected near Angkor Wat.
- **Thai (Koh Samui):** Fast-growing, moderate potency, smaller fruits adapted to tropical conditions.
- **African Transkei:** Originating from the Transkei region of South Africa; moderate potency, prolific fruiting.
- **Mazatapec:** Mexican origin; slow colonizer but reliable, moderate potency, favored by traditionalist cultivators.

## Historical Use

Archaeological evidence (Mesoamerican mushroom stones, murals at Tepantitla) suggests ritual use of psilocybin mushrooms dates back at least 3,000 years among the Mazatec, Nahua, and other indigenous peoples of Central America. *P. cubensis* specifically grows across subtropical and tropical regions of the Americas, from the southern United States through Central America to northern South America, and was almost certainly among the species used ceremonially.

Albert Hofmann first isolated and identified psilocybin and psilocin from *Psilocybe mexicana* in 1958. R. Gordon Wasson published his famous 1957 *LIFE* magazine article describing his experiences with Mazatec curandera María Sabina, which introduced psilocybin mushrooms to Western culture. The species gained further notoriety during the 1960s counterculture movement.

## Legal Status

*P. cubensis* is regulated under international drug control law via the **1971 UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances** (Schedule I). Psilocybin and psilocin are listed, making cultivation and possession illegal in most jurisdictions. Notable exceptions as of 2026:

- **Netherlands:** Fresh psilocybin mushrooms (truffles) remain technically legal; dried mushrooms have been banned since 2008.
- **Brazil:** The mushrooms themselves are not specifically listed in federal drug scheduling, though psilocybin extraction is prohibited.
- **Jamaica:** No specific legislation against psilocybin mushrooms; retreat centers operate openly.
- **Portugal:** Decriminalized for personal use (along with all drugs) since 2001.
- **United States:** Schedule I under the Controlled Substances Act. Several cities and states (Oregon, Colorado) have deprioritized enforcement or legalized supervised therapeutic use. FDA has granted Breakthrough Therapy designation for psilocybin in treatment-resistant depression and major depressive disorder.

## Risk Profile

*P. cubensis* has low physiological toxicity. No deaths have been directly attributed to psilocybin mushroom overdose — the estimated lethal dose (LD50) in rats exceeds 280 mg/kg, translating to roughly 1.7 kg of dried mushroom for a 70 kg human, an amount virtually impossible to consume. Primary risks include:

- **Psychological distress:** Anxiety, paranoia, or panic reactions, particularly at high doses, in unprepared users, or in unsupportive settings ("bad trips"). Usually self-limiting.
- **Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder (HPPD):** Rare; involves persistent visual disturbances. Estimated prevalence <1% among regular users.
- **Precipitation of psychiatric conditions:** Psilocybin can trigger latent psychotic disorders in predisposed individuals. Contraindicated for individuals with personal or family history of schizophrenia or bipolar disorder.
- **Accidental injury:** Impaired judgment during intoxication poses real physical risk.
- **Misidentification:** Wild foraging carries risk of confusion with toxic species (e.g., *Galerina marginata*, which contains amatoxins).

## Ecological Role

*P. cubensis* is a **coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) saprotroph**. It colonizes the dung of herbivorous mammals, particularly ruminants (cattle, buffalo, horses). The fungus plays a role in nutrient cycling by breaking down complex plant material that has passed through the herbivore digestive tract, contributing to decomposition and soil nutrient redistribution. It is found throughout tropical and subtropical regions worldwide — the Americas, Southeast Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, and Australia — wherever suitable substrates and warm, humid conditions exist. Fruiting typically occurs during or after rainy periods when humidity is elevated. The species is not known to be parasitic or pathogenic to any living host organism.
