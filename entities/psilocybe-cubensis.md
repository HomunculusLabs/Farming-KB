# Psilocybe cubensis

**Psilocybe cubensis** is a species of psychedelic mushroom in the family Hymenogastraceae. It is the best-known and most widely distributed psilocybin-containing mushroom, native to subtropical and tropical regions worldwide. It is commonly referred to as the "golden teacher," "cubes," or simply "cubensis."

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae
- **Genus:** *Psilocybe*
- **Species:** *P. cubensis*

The species was first described in 1906 by Franklin Sumner Earle as *Stropharia cubensis*, based on specimens collected in Cuba. It was later reclassified into the genus *Psilocybe* by Rolf Singer in 1949. Molecular phylogenetic studies have confirmed its placement within *Psilocybe* sensu stricto, though some contemporary taxonomists have proposed reclassifying it under the genus *Hymenogastranae* based on cladistic analysis. The specific epithet *cubensis* refers to its type locality in Cuba.

## Morphology

### Cap (Pileus)
The cap ranges from 1.5–8 cm in diameter, convex when young, expanding to broadly convex or plane with age. The surface is smooth and hygrophanous, ranging from reddish-cinnamon brown when moist to pale golden or nearly white when dry. A distinct umbo is often present. The cap margin is sometimes adorned with transient veil remnants in young specimens.

### Gills (Lamellae)
Gills are adnate to adnexed, grayish when young, darkening to purplish-brown or nearly black with spore maturity. This darkening results from the dense deposit of purple-brown basidiospores.

### Stem (Stipe)
The stipe measures 4–15 cm in length and 0.4–1.4 cm in thickness. It is hollow, equal or slightly thickened at the base, and whitish to yellowish. The partial veil leaves a persistent membranous annulus (ring) on the upper stem, which may darken with spore fall. Bruising of any tissue typically produces a conspicuous blue to blue-black discoloration, indicative of psilocybin oxidation.

### Spores
Basidiospores are ellipsoidal, smooth, thick-walled, 11.5–17.3 × 8–11.5 µm. The spore print is dark purplish-brown.

## Lifecycle

*Psilocybe cubensis* is a saprotrophic basidiomycete that reproduces sexually via basidiospores. Its lifecycle follows the typical agaric pattern: spore germination produces haploid hyphae, which form a monokaryotic mycelium. Compatible monokaryons fuse (plasmogamy) to produce a dikaryotic mycelium, which is the dominant vegetative stage. Under appropriate environmental conditions—particularly changes in temperature, humidity, and light—the dikaryotic mycelium forms primordia that develop into mature fruiting bodies (mushrooms). Basidia on the gill surfaces undergo karyogamy and meiosis, producing haploid basidiospores that are forcibly discharged into the air to complete the cycle. The entire fruiting cycle from pin formation to spore release typically takes 5–14 days.

## Habitat and Distribution

*P. cubensis* is a dung-inhabiting species that grows on the feces of herbivorous mammals, particularly cattle (*Bos taurus*), in subtropical and tropical climates. It is found across the globe between approximately 25°N and 35°S latitude. Major regions of occurrence include:

- Gulf Coast and southeastern United States
- Mexico and Central America
- Northern South America (Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru)
- Caribbean islands (Cuba, Jamaica, Puerto Rico)
- Subtropical regions of Southeast Asia (Thailand, Cambodia, Vietnam)
- Sub-Saharan Africa
- Northeastern Australia

Fruiting occurs primarily during warm, rainy seasons when temperatures range from 24–30 °C (75–86 °F) and relative humidity is high.

## Cultivation

*P. cubensis* is the most commonly cultivated psilocybin-containing mushroom due to its relatively fast colonization speed, moderate difficulty, and substantial fruiting body size. Cultivation methods include:

- **PF Tek (Psilocybe Fanaticus Technique):** A beginner-friendly method using brown rice flour and vermiculite in half-pint mason jars, sterilized by pressure cooking and inoculated with spore syringes.
- **Monotub / Bulk Cultivation:** Colonized grain spawn (typically rye, oats, or millet) is mixed with a bulk substrate, usually a blend of coconut coir and vermiculite, in a large polypropylene container. This method yields significantly larger harvests.
- **Straw and Wood-Based Substrates:** Less common for cubensis but viable, particularly with pasteurized straw or hardwood sawdust amendments.

Optimal cultivation parameters include substrate temperatures of 24–27 °C for colonization and 22–25 °C for fruiting, with relative humidity above 90% and adequate fresh air exchange. Numerous cultivated strains exist (e.g., B+, Golden Teacher, Amazonian, Cambodian, Penis Envy), though these represent genetic lineages of the same species rather than distinct taxonomic varieties.

## Pharmacology

### Active Compounds

*Psilocybe cubensis* contains two principal psychoactive alkaloids:

- **Psilocybin** (4-PO-DMT): A prodrug that is dephosphorylated by alkaline phosphatase enzymes in vivo to yield the pharmacologically active psilocin. It typically constitutes 0.5–1.2% of the dry weight of the mushroom.
- **Psilocin** (4-HO-DMT): The active metabolite and primary psychoactive compound, typically present at 0.1–0.5% of dry weight. Concentrations vary significantly depending on genetics, growing conditions, and harvest timing.

Minor alkaloids may include baeocystin (0.01–0.3%) and norbaeocystin, though their pharmacological contribution is considered negligible.

### Mechanism of Action

Psilocin acts primarily as a partial agonist at the 5-HT₂A serotonin receptor, with additional affinity for 5-HT₁A, 5-HT₂C, and 5-HT₂B receptors. The psychedelic effects are largely mediated through cortical 5-HT₂A receptor activation, which modulates thalamocortical connectivity, the default mode network, and executive function regions.

### Effects

Onset occurs within 20–60 minutes, with peak effects at 90–120 minutes and a total duration of 4–6 hours. Dose-dependent effects range from mild perceptual alterations at sub-gram doses to profound ego dissolution, synesthesia, and mystical-type experiences at doses exceeding 3–5 g of dried mushroom. Physical effects include mydriasis, mild tachycardia, increased body temperature, and occasional nausea.

## History and Ethnobotany

*P. cubensis* has been used for at least 2,000–3,000 years in Mesoamerican indigenous ceremonial contexts. Stone mushroom effigies ("mushroom stones") dating to approximately 1000 BCE have been found in Guatemala and Mexico. The species was among those identified in María Sabina's Mazatec velada ceremonies in Huautla de Jiménez, Oaxaca, Mexico. R. Gordon Wasson's 1957 *Life* magazine article describing his participation in a Mazatec ceremony is credited with introducing psilocybin mushrooms to Western audiences. Albert Hofmann subsequently isolated and identified psilocybin and psilocin from *P. cubensis* specimens collected in Mexico, publishing his findings in 1958.

## Legal Status

*Psilocybe cubensis* and its active constituents are regulated internationally:

- **United States:** Listed as Schedule I under the Controlled Substances Act since 1968. Both the mushrooms themselves and purified psilocybin are illegal to possess, cultivate, or distribute at the federal level. Several states and municipalities have enacted deprioritization or decriminalization measures (e.g., Oregon, Colorado, Denver, Oakland, Washington, D.C.).
- **United Nations:** Psilocybin and psilocin are listed in Schedule I of the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances, requiring signatory nations to regulate their manufacture, distribution, and possession.
- **Netherlands:** While psilocybin-containing mushrooms were banned in 2008, fresh *P. cubensis* truffles (sclerotia) remain legally sold in licensed "smart shops."
- **Elsewhere:** Legal status varies. Several countries, including the Bahamas, Brazil, Jamaica, Nepal, and Samoa, have no specific legal prohibition on psilocybin mushrooms. Many European nations, Canada, Australia, and most of Asia classify them as controlled substances.

## Safety Profile

### Toxicity

Psilocybin has a high therapeutic index. The LD₅₀ in mice is approximately 280 mg/kg (intravenous) and 12.5 mg/kg (intraperitoneal). A lethal dose in humans has not been established and is estimated to exceed 1,000 times a typical active dose. No confirmed human deaths from psilocybin overdose alone have been documented.

### Psychological Risks

The primary risks are psychological rather than physiological:

- **Acute Anxiety / "Bad Trip":** Can occur, particularly at high doses, in unprepared users, or in unsupportive settings. Effects may include severe anxiety, paranoia, and transient psychosis-like symptoms.
- **Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder (HPPD):** A rare condition involving persistent visual disturbances resembling those experienced during intoxication. Prevalence is estimated at 1–4% among regular psychedelic users.
- **Precipitation of Psychiatric Conditions:** Individuals with personal or family histories of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or other psychotic disorders are at elevated risk for adverse psychological reactions.

### Physiological Risks

- **Species Misidentification:** The most significant physical danger associated with foraging is confusion with toxic species, particularly members of the genus *Galerina* (e.g., *Galerina marginata*), which contains lethal amatoxins.
- **Serotonin Syndrome:** Theoretical risk when combined with serotonergic medications (SSRIs, MAOIs, triptans), though documented cases are exceedingly rare.

### Contraindications

Use is contraindicated in individuals taking monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs), lithium, or serotonergic psychiatric medications, as well as those with a history of psychotic disorders or severe cardiovascular disease.
