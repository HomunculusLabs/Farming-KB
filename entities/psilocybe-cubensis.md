# Psilocybe cubensis

## Overview

*Psilocybe cubensis* is a species of psychedelic mushroom whose principal active compounds are psilocybin and psilocin. It is the most widely known and cultivated psilocybin mushroom, belonging to the family Hymenogastraceae within the order Agaricales. The species is a coprophilous fungus — it colonizes the dung of herbivores, particularly cattle, in subtropical and tropical environments worldwide.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae
- **Genus:** *Psilocybe*
- **Species:** *P. cubensis*

The species was first described in 1906 by Franklin Sumner Earle in Cuba. Its type locality is Cuba, which is reflected in the specific epithet *cubensis*. Rolf Singer transferred it to the genus *Stropharia* in 1949, but modern phylogenetic studies have reaffirmed its placement in *Psilocybe*.

## Morphology

### Cap (Pileus)
- Diameter: 1.5–8 cm
- Convex to broadly convex, expanding to plane with age
- Color: Cinnamon-brown to golden when young, fading to pale yellow or nearly white with maturity
- Surface: Hygrophanous; smooth to slightly viscid when moist
- Margin: Often decorated with remnants of the partial veil

### Gills (Lamellae)
- Attachment: Adnate to adnexed, sometimes seceding with age
- Color: Grayish initially, darkening to deep purple-brown to nearly black as spores mature
- Spore print: Dark purple-brown

### Stem (Stipe)
- Height: 4–15 cm
- Thickness: 0.4–1.5 cm
- Color: Whitish to yellowish, often bruising blue where handled or damaged
- Hollow, with a persistent partial veil forming an annular ring

## Chemistry

The primary psychoactive compounds in *P. cubensis* are:

| Compound | Class | Approx. % (dry weight) |
|----------|-------|----------------------|
| Psilocybin | Tryptamine alkaloid | 0.5–1.3% |
| Psilocin | Tryptamine alkaloid | 0.1–0.6% |
| Baeocystin | Tryptamine alkaloid | Trace |
| Norbaeocystin | Tryptamine alkaloid | Trace |

Psilocybin (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) is a prodrug — it is dephosphorylated by alkaline phosphatase enzymes in the body to produce psilocin (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine), which is the pharmacologically active form. Psilocin is a partial agonist at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, primarily in the prefrontal cortex.

## Distribution and Habitat

*P. cubensis* has a pantropical and subtropical distribution. It is found naturally in:

- Gulf Coast states of the United States (Texas, Florida, Louisiana)
- Mexico, Central America, and northern South America
- Southeast Asia (Thailand, Vietnam, Cambodia)
- Sub-Saharan Africa
- Australia (northeastern regions)
- Parts of the Caribbean

It grows predominantly on bovine and equine dung in pastures and grasslands, fruiting from late spring through autumn in temperate regions and year-round in tropical zones.

## Cultivation

*P. cubensis* is the most commonly cultivated psilocybin mushroom due to its relatively fast colonization speed, moderate potency, and tolerance of a range of environmental conditions. Cultivation methods include:

1. **Spore syringe inoculation** onto sterilized grain spawn (rye, millet, or brown rice)
2. **Monotub / bulk substrate** colonization using coco coir and vermiculite
3. **PF Tek** (Psilocybe Fanaticus Technique) using brown rice flour cakes in half-pint mason jars

Optimal fruiting conditions: 23–27°C (73–81°F), 85–95% relative humidity, fresh air exchange, and indirect light on a 12/12 cycle.

## Pharmacology and Clinical Research

### Mechanism of Action
Psilocin acts primarily as a 5-HT2A receptor agonist. The subjective psychedelic effects — visual distortions, synesthesia, altered sense of time, ego dissolution — are mediated through cortical excitation and disruption of the default mode network (DMN).

### Clinical Trials
Modern clinical research has investigated psilocybin for:

- **Treatment-resistant depression:** Double-blind studies at Imperial College London and Johns Hopkins have shown rapid and sustained antidepressant effects following a single or dual dose.
- **End-of-life anxiety:** Terminal cancer patients receiving psilocybin-assisted therapy reported significant reductions in death-related anxiety and depression.
- **Obsessive-compulsive disorder:** Open-label studies suggest dose-dependent reduction in OCD symptoms.
- **Alcohol and tobacco dependence:** Preliminary trials indicate psilocybin may increase abstinence rates when combined with psychotherapy.

### Legal Status
The legal status of *P. cubensis* varies globally. Psilocybin and psilocin are classified as Schedule I substances under the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971). However, several jurisdictions have decriminalized or deprioritized enforcement:

- Netherlands (fresh mushrooms were legal until 2008; truffles remain legal)
- Portugal (decriminalized for personal use)
- Oregon, Colorado (regulated therapeutic use)
- Brazil (non-prohibited in fresh form)
- Jamaica, Bahamas (no specific legislation)

## Ethnobotanical History

The use of psilocybin mushrooms dates back at least 6,000 years based on rock art found in the Sierra Mazateca region of Oaxaca, Mexico. The Mazatec people refer to *Psilocybe* species as *nti xi tho* ("little ones that spring forth") and have used them ceremonially for divination and healing. María Sabina, a Mazatec curandera, introduced *P. cubensis* and related species to the Western world in the 1950s through R. Gordon Wasson, who published an account of a velada ceremony in *Life* magazine (1957).

## Varieties and Strains

Numerous cultivated varieties exist, many originating from wild collections:

- **Golden Teacher** — Widely available; moderate potency; reliable colonizer
- **B+** — Large fruiting bodies; tolerant of suboptimal conditions
- **Penis Envy** — Characteristic thick stems and bulbous caps; higher potency
- **Ecuador** — Dense, meaty fruits; originally collected in the Ecuadorian Andes
- **Cambodian** — Fast colonizer; discovered near Angkor Wat
- **Thai Koh Samui** — Compact fruits; originally from Koh Samui, Thailand
- **Mazatapec** — Slow colonizer; traditional Mexican variety

## Safety Profile

*P. cubensis* mushrooms are non-toxic in the classical sense — the LD50 of psilocybin in mice is approximately 280 mg/kg, making a lethal overdose from mushroom consumption practically impossible. The primary risks are psychological:

- Acute anxiety or panic ("bad trip")
- HPPD (Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder) — rare, long-lasting visual disturbances
- Precipitation of psychotic episodes in individuals with a personal or family history of schizophrenia or bipolar disorder
- Accidental ingestion of toxic lookalikes (e.g., *Galerina marginata*, which contains amatoxins)

Contra-indications include cardiovascular conditions, pregnancy, and concurrent use of MAOIs, lithium, or stimulants.
