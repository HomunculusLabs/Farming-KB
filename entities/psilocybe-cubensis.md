# Psilocybe cubensis

**Psilocybe cubensis** is the most widely known and cultivated psilocybin-containing mushroom in the world. A coprophilous (dung-dwelling) species in the family Hymenogastraceae, it grows naturally on the dung of herbivorous mammals across tropical and subtropical regions globally. It is commonly referred to as the "golden teacher," "gold top," or simply "cubes." The species has become the foundational organism for both amateur and research-oriented mycological cultivation, and its global distribution is deeply intertwined with the history of cattle domestication and human migration.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae (formerly Strophariaceae)
- **Genus:** Psilocybe
- **Species:** P. cubensis

The species was first described by Franklin Sumner Earle in 1906 from specimens collected in Cuba, with the specific epithet *cubensis* referring to the type locality. Earle originally placed it in the genus *Stropharia* as *Stropharia cubensis*. The species was later transferred to *Psilocybe* by Rolf Singer in 1949. Some mycological authorities have at times treated it under the generic name *Stropharia*, and the combination *Psilocybe (Stropharia) cubensis* is sometimes used to reflect this taxonomic history. Gastón Guzmán, the foremost authority on Psilocybe taxonomy, confirmed its placement in *Psilocybe* sensu stricto.

Molecular phylogenetic studies have placed *P. cubensis* within a clade that includes other coprophilous Psilocybe species, supporting the hypothesis that the dung habitat represents a strong selective pressure shaping the evolution of this group. Several taxa previously described as distinct species (such as *Psilocybe subcubensis* Guzmán) have been proposed as conspecific with *P. cubensis*, though the full resolution of species boundaries within this complex remains an active area of systematic research.

## Morphology

The fruiting body of *Psilocybe cubensis* is medium to large for a Psilocybe species. The cap (pileus) ranges from 1.5 to 8 cm in diameter, beginning convex or campanulate (bell-shaped) in young specimens and expanding to broadly convex or plane with age, sometimes developing a slight umbo (central bump). The cap surface is smooth and hygrophanous — it is a distinctive golden-brown to caramel color when moist, fading to pale yellowish or nearly white as it dries. The cap margin is often decorated with remnants of the partial veil in young specimens, appearing as a white, fibrillose ring zone.

The gills (lamellae) are adnate to adnexed (broadly attached to the stem), crowded, and grayish in youth, darkening to purplish-black or nearly black at maturity as spores mature. This dark spore coloration is a key identifying feature. The stipe (stem) is 4–15 cm tall and 0.4–1.5 cm thick, hollow, and equal to slightly bulbous at the base. It is white to pale yellow, often bruising bluish where damaged — a reaction caused by the oxidation of psilocin and related indole compounds. The partial veil leaves a persistent membranous annulus (ring) on the upper stem that may be whitish or take on purplish tones from deposited spores.

The spore print is dark purplish-brown to nearly black. Spores are approximately 11–17 × 7–12 μm, elliptical to rhomboid in shape, thick-walled, and feature a distinct germ pore. The presence of a germ pore is a microscopic feature that helps distinguish *P. cubensis* from superficially similar non-psychoactive dung fungi.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Psilocybe cubensis* has a pantropical and subtropical distribution, found throughout Central and South America, the Caribbean, Southeast Asia, India, sub-Saharan Africa, and northern Australia. It is one of the most widely distributed psychoactive fungi on Earth. Stamets and others have suggested that the species was originally native to the Old World tropics — specifically Africa — and was introduced to the Americas during the Columbian Exchange, traveling with Brahman cattle brought by Spanish colonizers and slave traders. The mushroom's close association with bovine dung would have facilitated this transcontinental dispersal.

The species is primarily coprophilous, fruiting directly from the dung of herbivorous animals, especially cattle (*Bos taurus*, *Bos indicus*) but also horses, elephants, and other large herbivores. It can also occasionally be found in enriched soils, particularly those with high organic content from decomposing manure or compost. Fruiting occurs during warm, humid conditions, typically after rainfall, and is most prolific during the wet season in tropical climates.

In the modern era, human cultivation has dramatically expanded the effective range of *P. cubensis* beyond its natural tropical distribution. Cultivated specimens are found indoors worldwide, and spores from indoor cultivation operations have been released into the environment, leading to naturalized populations in some temperate areas where the species would not otherwise persist year-round.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The primary psychoactive compounds in *Psilocybe cubensis* are **psilocybin** (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-*N*,*N*-dimethyltryptamine) and **psilocin** (4-hydroxy-*N*,*N*-dimethyltryptamine), along with smaller amounts of **baeocystin** and **norbaeocystin**. Psilocybin serves as a prodrug that is dephosphorylated to psilocin by alkaline phosphatase enzymes in the body. Psilocin is the primary active compound, acting as a partial agonist at serotonin 5-HT₂A receptors in the brain.

The typical psilocybin content of dried *P. cubensis* ranges from 0.5% to 1.2% by dry weight, though this varies considerably depending on genetics, growing conditions, substrate, harvest timing, and drying method. The Bigwood and Beug (1990) study of variation in psilocybin and psilocin levels found significant intra-species variation, with some specimens containing substantially more or less of the active compounds than the species average. Wild specimens from certain tropical populations (particularly those from Southeast Asia) have been reported with higher alkaloid content than typical cultivated strains.

Gartz (1989) demonstrated that supplementing the substrate with tryptamine precursors can substantially increase the psilocybin content of cultivated *P. cubensis*, a finding that has influenced both amateur cultivation practices and research into the biosynthetic pathway of psilocybin production. The metabolic pathway proceeds from tryptophan through tryptamine, N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), and 4-hydroxy-DMT (psilocin), with the final phosphorylation step producing psilocybin.

## Cultivation

*Psilocybe cubensis* is by far the easiest psilocybin mushroom to cultivate, which accounts for its status as the most commonly grown species worldwide. The species fruits readily on a wide range of substrates, including cereal grains (rye, wheat, brown rice), straw, composted manure, and supplemented wood-based substrates. Several landmark developments in cultivation methodology are associated with this species:

- **PF Tek (Psilocybe Fanaticus Technique):** Developed by Robert "Psilocybe Fanaticus" McPherson in the early 1990s, this simplified cultivation method used brown rice flour and vermiculite in half-pint canning jars. The PF Tek democratized home cultivation and made *P. cubensis* accessible to hobbyists without specialized laboratory equipment.
- **The Mushroom Cultivator (Stamets & Chilton, 1983):** Provided the first comprehensive, scientifically grounded guide to cultivating *P. cubensis* and other gourmet and medicinal mushrooms on grain spawn and bulk substrates.
- **Multi-spore vs. isolated culture:** Most home cultivation uses multi-spore inoculation, which produces genetic variability. Commercial and research-oriented growers often use isolated monocultures derived from single-sector agar isolates for consistency and predictable fruiting characteristics.

The species is particularly well-suited to cultivation because it is a fast colonizer, has relatively low contamination susceptibility compared to other species, fruits abundantly, and tolerates a range of environmental conditions. Typical indoor cultivation involves inoculation of sterilized grain spawn, colonization at approximately 24–27°C, and fruiting initiation through exposure to fresh air exchange, high humidity (90–95%), and light (though the species does not require light for fruiting, light exposure improves cap formation and directs growth).

## Notable Cultivated Strains

Numerous cultivated strains of *P. cubensis* have been developed, many with distinctive names reflecting their origin, appearance, or purported characteristics. Some of the most widely known include:

- **Golden Teacher:** One of the most popular strains, known for its golden caps and reputation as a beginner-friendly variety with moderate potency.
- **B+ (Big Fruit):** A large-fruiting strain that tolerates a wider range of growing conditions than most, making it resilient for novice cultivators.
- **Ecuador:** A strain originally collected from highland Ecuador, known for producing dense clusters of medium-sized mushrooms.
- **Cambodian:** Collected near the Angkor Wat temple complex in Cambodia, known for fast colonization and relatively small but abundant fruiting bodies.
- ** Penis Envy:** A distinctive strain with unusually thick stems and underdeveloped caps, reputed to have above-average potency.
- **Thai Koh Samui:** A strain collected from the Thai island of Koh Samui, known for rapid colonization and prolific fruiting.

It is important to note that most of these "strains" have not been formally characterized genetically, and the phenotypic differences between them may reflect growing conditions as much as genetic differences. The species' natural genetic variability is vast, and naming conventions in the amateur cultivation community do not correspond to formal botanical taxonomic categories.

## Historical and Cultural Significance

*Psilocybe cubensis* was the species used by R. Gordon Wasson and his wife Valentina Wasson during their famous 1955 expedition to Huautla de Jiménez, Oaxaca, Mexico, where they participated in a velada (healing ceremony) guided by the Mazatec curandera María Sabina. While the Mazatec people traditionally use several species of *Psilocybe* (which they call *nti xi tho* or "little things that spring forth"), *P. cubensis* is among the species found in the region.

The Wassons' account, published in *Life* magazine in 1957, introduced psilocybin mushrooms to Western consciousness and catalyzed a wave of scientific and cultural interest. Albert Hofmann subsequently isolated and identified psilocybin and psilocin from cultivated specimens of *P. cubensis* at Sandoz Laboratories in 1958, synthesizing both compounds and making them available for clinical research.

The species became a central figure in the 1960s counterculture, with figures like Timothy Leary and Richard Alpert (Ram Dass) conducting the Harvard Psilocybin Project using extracts derived from *P. cubensis*. The species' ease of cultivation made it the primary vehicle through which psychedelic experiences became accessible outside of indigenous ceremonial contexts.

## Potency Variation

The Bigwood and Beug (1990) study, one of the most comprehensive analyses of psilocybin mushroom potency, demonstrated significant variation in alkaloid content both within and between populations of *P. cubensis*. Factors influencing potency include:

- **Genetics:** Different geographic populations and cultivated strains vary substantially in their baseline alkaloid production capacity.
- **Substrate composition:** The nutritional composition of the growing medium affects both total biomass and the concentration of psychoactive alkaloids. Substrates rich in nitrogen sources tend to produce higher alkaloid levels.
- **Fruiting conditions:** Temperature, humidity, light exposure, and fresh air exchange during the fruiting phase all influence alkaloid content. Some evidence suggests that lower temperatures during fruiting may increase potency.
- **Harvest timing:** Alkaloid content changes throughout the fruiting cycle. Caps generally contain higher concentrations than stems, and the ratio of psilocybin to psilocin shifts as the mushroom matures. Harvesting just before or just after the veil tears (partial veil separation) is a common practice, though the optimal harvest time for maximum potency is debated.
- **Drying and storage:** Psilocybin is relatively stable when dried and stored in cool, dark, dry conditions. Psilocin is less stable and degrades more rapidly with exposure to heat, light, and oxygen. Improperly stored dried mushrooms can lose significant potency over months.

## Confusion with Toxic Species

Several non-psychoactive or toxic mushroom species grow in similar habitats and can be confused with *P. cubensis* by inexperienced foragers. The most significant lookalikes include:

- **Panaeolus species (e.g., *P. sphinctrinus*):** Share the coprophilous habitat but have black spore prints and do not contain psilocybin. They are non-toxic but also non-psychoactive.
- **Chlorophyllum molybdites (green-spored parasol):** Grows in similar enriched soils and grassy areas. This species causes significant gastrointestinal distress and is one of the most commonly consumed toxic mushrooms in North America. It can be distinguished by its green spore print (vs. the dark purplish-brown of *P. cubensis*).
- **Stropharia species:** Some *Stropharia* species resemble *P. cubensis* but are generally larger and lack the blueing reaction.

Proper identification requires careful examination of the spore print color, the presence of blueing, the habitat, and microscopic features. Beginners should never consume wild mushrooms without expert verification.

## Legal Status

*Psilocybe cubensis* is illegal to possess, cultivate, or distribute in most countries worldwide due to its psilocybin content. In the United States, both the mushrooms themselves and the psilocybin they contain are Schedule I controlled substances under the Controlled Substances Act. However, psilocybin mushroom spores do not contain psilocybin or psilocin and exist in a legal gray area in many jurisdictions, including most US states (with exceptions including California, Georgia, and Idaho).

A growing decriminalization movement has led to the deprioritization of psilocybin mushroom enforcement in several US cities, including Denver, Oakland, Santa Cruz, Ann Arbor, Washington D.C., and the state of Oregon (which legalized supervised therapeutic use in 2020). Similar policy shifts are occurring in other countries, including the Netherlands (where fresh "paddos" were sold legally in smart shops until 2008) and Brazil (where the species is not specifically scheduled).

## Ecological Role

As a coprophilous saprotroph, *Psilocybe cubensis* plays a role in nutrient cycling in pasture ecosystems. The mushroom's mycelium colonizes herbivore dung, breaking down complex organic materials and returning nutrients to the soil. The fruiting bodies are consumed by various invertebrates, and the spores are adapted for dispersal through animal digestive tracts — the thick-walled spores with germ pores can survive passage through an herbivore's gut, facilitating colonization of fresh dung deposits.
