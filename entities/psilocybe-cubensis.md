# Psilocybe cubensis

**Psilocybe cubensis** is a species of psychedelic mushroom whose principal active compounds are psilocybin and psilocin. It is the most widely known and cultivated psilocybin mushroom, belonging to the family Hymenogastraceae in the order Agaricales.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae
- **Genus:** Psilocybe
- **Species:** P. cubensis

Originally described in 1906 as *Stropharia cubensis* by Franklin Sumner Earle in Cuba, the species was later moved to the genus *Psilocybe* by Rolf Singer in 1949. More recent molecular phylogenetics has prompted some taxonomists to reclassify it under *Hymenogastraceae* rather than the traditional placement in *Strophariaceae* or *Bolbitiaceae*.

## Description

The cap (pileus) ranges from 1.6–8 cm in diameter, conic to convex when young, becoming broadly convex to plane with age. Color varies from pale brown to golden yellow, often with a darker center that fades with drying. The cap surface is hygrophanous — it changes color as it loses or absorbs moisture.

The gills are adnate to adnexed, gray to nearly black with maturity, with whitish edges. The stipe (stem) is 4–15 cm tall and 0.4–1.4 cm thick, hollow, and equal to slightly enlarged at the base. A distinctive partial veil leaves a persistent membranous annulus (ring) on the upper stipe. The stem bruises bluish to bluish-green when handled, a reaction caused by the oxidation of psilocin.

## Distribution and Habitat

*P. cubensis* is a pantropical and subtropical species found throughout the Americas, Southeast Asia, Australia, and parts of Africa. It grows in a wide range of substrates but is most commonly associated with:

- Dung of herbivorous mammals (especially cattle and horses)
- Enriched pastures and meadows
- Subtropical grasslands

The species thrives in humid environments with temperatures between 23–27°C (75–80°F). Fruiting is triggered by rain and high humidity following a dry period.

## Chemistry

The primary psychoactive compounds are:

| Compound | Concentration (% dry weight) | Effect |
|----------|---------------------------|--------|
| Psilocybin | 0.5–1.2% | Prodrug; converted to psilocin in the body |
| Psilocin | 0.1–0.6% | Primary psychoactive agent (5-HT2A agonist) |
| Baeocystin | 0.01–0.3% | Minor alkaloid, less well characterized |
| Norbaeocystin | Trace | Structurally related minor compound |

Potency varies significantly between strains, growing conditions, and developmental stage. Young specimens and mature caps generally contain higher concentrations than stems.

## Pharmacology

Psilocybin is a prodrug that is dephosphorylated by alkaline phosphatase in the liver and intestinal mucosa to produce psilocin. Psilocin acts primarily as a partial agonist at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, with additional affinity for 5-HT1A, 5-HT2C, and 5-HT2B receptors. The subjective effects include:

- Altered perception of time and space
- Visual hallucinations and synesthesia
- Ego dissolution and mystical-type experiences
- Enhanced emotional introspection

Onset occurs within 20–60 minutes of ingestion, peaking at 2–3 hours, with effects lasting 4–6 hours. The compound is metabolized by monoamine oxidase (MAO) and excreted renally.

## Cultivation

*P. cubensis* is one of the easiest psilocybin-containing mushrooms to cultivate, which has made it the species of choice for both research and recreational growers. Common methods include:

- **PF Tek:** A beginner-friendly method using brown rice flour and vermiculite in half-pint mason jars
- **Monotub:** Bulk cultivation using grain spawn inoculated into a substrate of coco coir and vermiculite
- **Straw/pasteurized substrate:** Traditional method using pasteurized wheat straw or horse manure

Optimal colonization temperature is 24–27°C. Fruiting requires high humidity (90%+), fresh air exchange, indirect light, and a temperature drop of 3–5°C from colonization.

## Legal Status

The legal status of *P. cubensis* varies widely by jurisdiction:

- **United States:** Schedule I under the Controlled Substances Act (federal). However, several states and municipalities have decriminalized possession and cultivation of psilocybin-containing mushrooms.
- **Netherlands:** Fresh mushrooms were banned in 2008, but truffles (sclerotia) of *Psilocybe* species remain legally sold in "smart shops."
- **Brazil:** Not specifically regulated; cultivation and personal use occupy a legal gray area.
- **Canada:** Illegal under the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act, though exemptions exist for clinical trials and some indigenous ceremonial use.

## Notable Strains

Several cultivated varieties have become widely recognized:

- **Golden Teacher:** One of the most popular strains, known for moderate potency and reliable growth
- **B+:** Large-capped strain with moderate potency, tolerant of varied conditions
- **Penis Envy:** Distinctive morphology with above-average potency; slower colonizer
- **Cambodian:** Fast colonizer originating from specimens found near Angkor Wat
- **Ecuador:** Known for producing consistently large flushes in humid conditions

## Research and Therapeutic Potential

Clinical research into psilocybin-assisted therapy has accelerated since the 2010s. Notable areas of investigation include:

- **Treatment-resistant depression:** Phase II trials have shown rapid and sustained antidepressant effects from a single dose
- **End-of-life anxiety:** Studies at Johns Hopkins and NYU demonstrated significant reductions in anxiety and depression in terminal cancer patients
- **Substance use disorders:** Preliminary evidence suggests psilocybin may reduce alcohol and tobacco dependence
- **Cluster headaches:** Anecdotal reports and early studies suggest potential benefit

In 2018, the U.S. FDA granted Breakthrough Therapy Designation to psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression, accelerating the regulatory pathway for clinical development.

## Ecology

*P. cubensis* plays a role in nutrient cycling in pasture ecosystems. As a saprotrophic fungus, it decomposes organic matter in herbivore dung, releasing nutrients back into the soil. The species is often among the first colonizers of fresh dung, competing with other coprophilous fungi.

## Safety and Toxicity

The LD50 of psilocybin in mice is approximately 280 mg/kg (intravenous) and 12.5 mg/kg (intraperitoneal), making it remarkably safe in terms of acute toxicity. There are no confirmed human deaths from psilocybin overdose alone. However, psychological risks include:

- Acute anxiety or panic reactions ("bad trips")
- Triggering of latent psychiatric conditions in predisposed individuals
- HPPD (Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder) in rare cases

Harm reduction practices include set and setting preparation, the presence of a trusted guide or sitter, and starting with low doses (0.5–1 g dried for beginners).
