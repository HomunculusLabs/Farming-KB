# Soya Bean (*Glycine max*)

## Description

The soya bean is discussed by [[bill-mollison]] in *[[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual* not as a crop to celebrate, but as a cautionary example of industrial agricultural dysfunction. Mollison critiques the soya bean's role in global deforestation, monoculture expansion, and corporate food control, while acknowledging its nutritional significance in vegetarian diets.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Fabaceae (legume family)
- **Genus**: *Glycine*
- **Species**: *G. max*
- **Type**: Annual grain legume

## Key Facts

- Mollison's critique centres on multiple problems with the global soya industry:
  - **Corporate control**: "Soya beans are one of the foods owned (100% of patent rights) by a few multinationals."
  - **Deforestation**: "In 1980-82 caused more deforestation in the USA and Brazil than any other crop."
  - **Misallocation**: "About 70% of the beans were either fed to pigs, or used in industry as a base for paint used on motor vehicles."
  - **Soil erosion**: "Grains and grain legumes account for most of the erosion of soils in every agricultural region."
  - **Exploitation**: Western vegetarian diets relying on soya often source from "areas where real famine threatens" — mung beans from India, chickpeas from Ethiopia, soya from Africa and India.
- Despite these critiques, soya beans are nutritionally significant as one of the few plant sources of complete protein.
- The crop is nitrogen-fixing through rhizobial symbiosis, contributing to soil fertility when properly managed.
- Global production exceeds 350 million tonnes annually (as of recent decades), with Brazil and the USA as dominant producers.

## Ecological Concerns

- Large-scale soya monocultures in the Amazon and Cerrado biomes drive massive habitat loss
- Annual cultivation leaves soil bare between crops, contributing to erosion
- Heavy herbicide use (especially with herbicide-tolerant GMO varieties) impacts soil biology
- Long-distance transport of soya products generates significant carbon emissions
- Corporate seed patents reduce farmer autonomy and genetic diversity

## Relevance to Permaculture and Mycology

Mollison's critique of soya illustrates the permaculture distinction between industrial monoculture and integrated polyculture. While soya beans themselves are valuable — particularly for their nitrogen-fixing rhizobial associations — their industrial-scale production damages the very systems permaculture seeks to build.

The rhizobial nodules on soya roots support nitrogen-fixing bacteria (*Bradyrhizobium japonicum*), a process facilitated by healthy soil fungal networks. Mycorrhizal associations improve soya's phosphorus uptake and drought resistance. However, industrial soya cultivation typically relies on chemical inputs that damage these fungal communities. In permaculture systems, legumes like soya would be integrated into diverse polycultures where their nitrogen fixation benefits surrounding plants, and soil fungal networks remain intact. Small-scale, locally-consumed soya production represents a responsible alternative to the global commodity system Mollison criticises.
