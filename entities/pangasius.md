# Pangasius (Pangas Catfish)

## Description

Pangasius is a genus of large freshwater catfish native to [[guzman-thailand-southeast-asia-psychoactive-fungi-distribution]], widely cultivated in pond and cage aquaculture systems throughout the Mekong River basin. In [[permaculture-pond-and-water-garden]] systems, they are valued as additive polyculture species that increase total yield without competing with other cultured fish.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Animalia
- **Phylum**: Chordata
- **Class**: Actinopterygii
- **Order**: Siluriformes (catfishes)
- **Family**: Pangasiidae (shark catfishes)
- **Genus**: Pangasius
- **Notable species**: P. hypophthalmus (tra/swai), P. bocourti (basa)

## Key Facts

- Native to the Mekong and Chao Phraya river systems of Southeast Asia
- Can grow very rapidly -- reaching 1-2 kg in 6-12 months under culture conditions
- Adults may reach 10-30+ kg in the wild
- Omnivorous with a preference for molluscs, crustaceans, and plant detritus
- One of the most important aquaculture species globally by production volume
- Marketed internationally as "swai", "basa", "tra", or simply "pangasius"

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Mollison specifically mentions pangas as an example of non-competing polyculture addition:

### Non-Competitive Polyculture
- The addition of pangas (Pangasia) to ponds rich in molluscs does not affect yields of carp or Tilapia
- Pangas feeding efficiency is unaffected by the presence of other species
- Total yield increases additively -- pangas production is essentially a bonus on top of existing polyculture output
- This demonstrates the ideal [[permaculture-polyculture-fish-pond]] outcome: each additional species adds to total productivity without diminishing others

### Niche Partitioning
- Pangasius occupies a distinct feeding niche as a mollusc specialist
- In ponds with abundant snail and bivalve populations, pangas convert this otherwise underutilised food source directly to fish protein
- Their bottom-feeding habits complement mid-water and surface-feeding species like Tilapia

### Design Implications
Mollison's reference to pangas illustrates the [[permaculture-principle-1-observe-interact]] of [[gaias-garden-stacking-functions]]: by adding a species that feeds on an unused resource (molluscs), the system's total efficiency increases without additional inputs. This is analogous to adding mussels to fish ponds for water quality, or adding plants for oxygen production.

## Cultivation Notes

- Requires warm tropical or subtropical water temperatures (25-32 degrees C optimal)
- Can be grown at high densities in intensive systems
- Tolerant of turbid water conditions
- Major commercial aquaculture species in Vietnam, Bangladesh, and throughout Southeast Asia
- Susceptible to cold -- water below 15 degrees C can be lethal

## Modern Aquaculture Scale

Pangasius has become one of the most significant aquaculture species globally, with Vietnam alone producing over 1.5 million tonnes annually. The tra/swai catfish industry demonstrates that Mollison's polyculture principles can scale from backyard ponds to industrial operations:

- Grown in ponds, cages, and pens throughout the Mekong Delta
- Feed conversion ratios as low as 1.2-1.6:1 on commercial diets
- Can tolerate crowding densities that would stress most other species
- Processing into fillets for international export provides significant rural employment

However, intensive monoculture systems have experienced disease outbreaks and water quality issues that validate Mollison's advocacy for polyculture diversity as a risk management strategy.

## Permaculture Design Lessons

The pangasius example teaches a fundamental permaculture lesson: when adding a new species to a system, the ideal candidate occupies an unused niche. Pangasius converts molluscs -- which most other cultured fish ignore -- into high-value protein. This principle should guide all species selection in permaculture pond design, prioritising species that utilise underexploited resources rather than competing with existing components.

## Nutritional Profile

Pangasius fillets are lean and white-fleshed, with a mild flavour that appeals to broad consumer markets. Nutritional characteristics include:
- High protein content (15-20% by weight)
- Low fat content in fillets (2-5%), though whole fish are fattier
- Good source of omega-3 fatty acids (though lower than marine fish)
- Rich in B vitamins, particularly B12, and minerals including selenium and phosphorus

The fish's efficient feed conversion means that each kilogram of feed produces more edible protein than many competing aquaculture species, making it an economically and ecologically efficient choice for permaculture systems focused on [[fungal-single-cell-protein-production]].
