# Fusarium graminearum

**Fusarium graminearum** is a major [[fungal-plant-pathogen-interactions]] and the primary causal agent of Fusarium head blimycotoxins also known as scab or tombstone disease, in cereal crops worldwide. It is one of the most economically destructive plant pathogens in global agriculture, causing billions of dollars in annual losses through reduced grain yield and quality, and through contamination of grain with mycotoxins that pose serious risks to human and animal health. The species is also known by its teleomorph name, *Gibberella zeae*.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Sordariomycetes
- **Order:** Hypocreales
- **Family:** Nectriaceae
- **Genus:** Fusarium
- **Species Complex:** *Fusarium graminearum* species complex (FGSC), comprising at least 15 phylogenetically distinct species

The teleomorph (sexual) stage is *Gibberella zeae*, which produces perithecia on crop residues. Molecular phylogenetic studies have revealed that what was historically considered a single species actually comprises a species complex with distinct geogrmyceliumstributions and biological properties. The most commonly studied lineage is *F. graminearum* sensu stricto, predominant in North America and Europe.

## Morphology

### Anamorph (Fusarium Phase)
- **Colony Growth:** Rapid growth on potato dextrose [[agar]], producing fluffy, aerial mycelium with a carmine red to reddish-brown pigmentation on the reverse side
- **Macroconidia:** Slender, curved (falcate), typically 5-septate (occasionally 3 to 7 septate), measuring 25 to 65 by 3 to 6 μm, with a distinctive foot-shaped basal cell and a pointed apical cell
- **Microconidia:** Rarely produced, unicellular, ovoid to ellipsoidal, 5 to 10 by 2 to 3 μm, produced on monophialides or polyphialides
- **Chlamydospores:** Thick-walled survival structures, globose to subglobose, produced singly or in pairs, important for long-term survival in soil

### Teleomorph (Gibberella Phase)
- **Perithecia:** Dark blue to black, flask-shaped sexual fruiting bodies, 200 to 350 μm in diameter, produced on colonized crop residues (particularly wheat and maize stalks)
- **Asci:** Cylindrical, containing 8 ascospores each
- **Ascospores:** Fusiform to ellipsoidal, 1 to 3-septate, approximately 18 to 28 by 4 to 6 μm, hyaline, and forcibly discharged into the air to initiate new infections

## Disease Cycle

The disease cycle of *F. graminearum* involves both sexual and asexual reproduction:

1. **Overwintering:** The fungus survives the winter as mycelium and perithecia on infected crop residues (stubble, straw, maize stalks) on or near the soil surface
2. **Sporulation:** In spring, perithecia mature and forcibly discharge ascospores into the air. Macroconidia may also be produced through asexual reproduction during warm, humid conditions
3. **Primary Infection:** Ascospores are wind-dispersed and land on susceptible cereal heads during anthesis (flowering). Infection requires free moisture on the head surface for 48 to 72 hours, typically provided by rain or heavy dew
4. **Colonization:** The fungus invades through the anthers or directly through glume tissues, spreading through the spikelet and into the rachis
5. **Secondary Spread:** Under favorable conditions, the fungus produces macroconidia on infected spikelets that are splash-dispersed to neighboring spikelets or heads
6. **Grain Infection:** The fungus colonizes developing kernels, leading to shriveled, discolored "tombstone" kernels and mycotoxin accumulation

## Host Range and Geographic Distribution

- **Primary Hosts:** Wheat (*Triticum aestivum*), barley (*Hordeum vulgare*), maize (*Zea mays*), and triticale
- **Secondary Hosts:** Oats, rye, rice, and various grass species
- **Geographic Range:** Nearly worldwide in temperate and subtropical cereal-growing regions. Particularly prevalent in humid and semi-humid areas, including the Midwestern United States, eastern Canada, the Pampas of Argentina, the Po Valley of Italy, and the lower Yangtze River Valley of China

## Mycotoxins

*Fusarium graminearum* is one of the most significant mycotoxin-producing fungi, producing several classes of toxic [[biodiversity-fungal-secondary-metabolites]]:

### Trichothecenes
- **Deoxynivalenol (DON / Vomitoxin):** The most prevalent trichothecene in contaminated grain, causing feed refusal, vomiting, and immunosuppression in livestock. Regulatory limits for DON in food and feed exist in most countries
- **3-Acetyl-DON and 15-Acetyl-DON:** Acetylated derivatives of DON that can be deacetylated to DON during mammalian metabolism
- **Nivalenol (NIV):** Another type B trichothecene, more toxic than DON on a molar basis, though generally produced at lower levels
- **T-2 Toxin and HT-2 Toxin:** Type A trichothecenes occasionally produced by some *F. graminearum* strains

### Zearalenone
- A non-steroidal estrogenic mycotoxin that can cause hyperestrogenism and reproductive problems in livestock, particularly swine. Zearalenone contamination often co-occurs with DON in affected grain

### Fumonisins
- While more commonly associated with *[[fusarium-verticillioides]]*, some *F. graminearum* strains can produce low levels of fumonisins

## Genomics

The genome of *F. graminearum* PH-1 was one of the first Fusarium genomes to be sequenced:

- **Genome Size:** Approximately 36 Mb, containing approximately 13,300 predicted protein-coding genes
- **Chromosome Number:** 4 chromosomes
- **Gene Clusters:** Contains numerous secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters, including the TRI (trichothecene) gene cluster spanning approximately 26 kb and containing 15 core genes
- **Transposable Elements:** A significant portion of the genome consists of transposable elements, which contribute to genome plasticity and the evolution of virulence and toxin profiles
- **Comparative Genomics:** Comparison with other *Fusarium* species has revealed both shared and unique virulence factors, providing insights into the evolution of plant pathogenicity

## Management and Control

Management of Fusarium head blight requires an [[solomon-insects-and-diseases-integrated-approach]]:

- **Resistant Varieties:** The most effective long-term strategy, though resistance is quantitative (controlled by multiple genes) and no commercially available varieties are fully resistant. Type II resistance (resistance to spread within the spike) is particularly important
- **Fungicide Application:** Triazole fungicides (propiconazole, tebuconazole, metconazole, prothioconazole) applied at anthesis can reduce FHB severity and mycotoxin accumulation, though control is often incomplete
- **Cultural Practices:** Crop rotation with non-host crops, tillage to bury infected residue, and adjustment of planting dates to avoid anthesis during periods of high moisture
- **Biological Control:** Application of antagonistic microorganisms, particularly *Cryptococcus flavescens* and *Bacillus* species, has shown promise in reducing FHB
- **Monitoring and Forecasting:** Weather-based risk assessment models are widely used to time fungicide applications

## Research Significance

*F. graminearum* serves as a model organism for studying:

- **Fungal-Plant Interactions:** The molecular dialogue between pathogen and host during infection
- **Secondary Metabolism:** Regulation and biosynthesis of trichothecene mycotoxins
- **[[fungal-sexual-reproduction]]:** The role of sexual recombination in generating genetic diversity and adapting to host resistance
- **Population Genetics:** Using [[gadd-fungal-multilocus-sequence-typing]] (MLST) and other molecular tools to track the spread and evolution of *F. graminearum* populations worldwide

## Economic Impact

The financial toll of *F. graminearum* on global agriculture is enormous:

- **Direct Yield Losses:** Fusarium head blight causes estimated annual losses of $1 to $3 billion USD in the United States alone, with similar magnitudes reported in China, Canada, and Europe. In epidemic years, losses can exceed 50% of expected yield in severely affected fields
- **Mycotoxin Rejection:** Grain exceeding regulatory limits for DON (typically 1 to 2 ppm for human food, 5 to 10 ppm for animal feed in most jurisdictions) may be rejected entirely from food supply chains, compounding yield losses with quality penalties
- **Downstream Industry Costs:** Contaminated grain requires additional testing, segregation, and diversion to lower-value uses such as ethanol production, where mycotoxins may concentrate in distillers dried grains with solubles (DDGS)
- **Fungicide Expenditure:** Foliar fungicide applications specifically targeting FHB cost growers an estimated $20 to $40 per hectare per application, often requiring one to two applications per season in high-risk areas

## Research History

Key milestones in *F. graminearum* research include:

- **1884:** The teleomorph *Gibberella zeae* is first described by Schweinitz from corn stalks in North America
- **1930s:** DON (vomitoxin) is first associated with *Fusarium*-contaminated grain after outbreaks of feed refusal in livestock
- **1980s-1990s:** Epidemics of FHB in the US Midwest and Canada drive increased research funding. Molecular markers for population genetics are developed
- **2003:** The genome of *F. graminearum* strain PH-1 is completed, one of the first *Fusarium* genomes, revealing the TRI gene cluster and numerous secondary metabolite biosynthetic pathways
- **2000s:** Development of quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping for FHB resistance in wheat, identification of the Fhb1 resistance locus
- **2010s:** CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing is applied to *F. graminearum* for functional characterization of virulence and toxin biosynthesis genes
- **2020s:** Integrated genomic and epidemiological surveillance networks track the global spread of DON and NIV chemotypes and emerging fungicide resistance
