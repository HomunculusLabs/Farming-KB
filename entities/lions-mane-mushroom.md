# Lion's Mane Mushroom (*Hericium erinaceus*)

## Overview

Lion's Mane is a medicinal and culinary mushroom in the family Hericiaceae, recognizable by its cascading white spines that resemble a lion's mane. Native to temperate forests of North America, Europe, and Asia, it has been used for over 2,000 years in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) as a spleen and stomach tonic. Modern research has focused on its nootropic and neuroprotective properties, particularly its ability to stimulate nerve growth factor (NGF) synthesis through unique compounds called hericenones and erinacines.

**Common names:** Monkey's head mushroom, bearded tooth fungus, hedgehog mushroom, 猴头菇 (hóu tóu gū, Chinese), yamabushitake (山伏茸, Japanese).

## Description

The fruiting body forms a white, spherical to irregularly lobed mass of soft, cascading spines 1–5 cm long, hanging from a single branched base. Mature specimens can reach 5–25 cm across. Young fruiting bodies are tender with a mild, slightly sweet, seafood-like flavor often compared to lobster or crab. The spore print is white; spores are roughly spherical to broadly ellipsoid, ~5–7 µm. The flesh is white and does not change color when cut. Ecologically, Lion's Mane is saprotrophic and weakly parasitic, causing white heart rot on hardwoods.

## Habitat & Distribution

Found on dead or dying hardwood trees — especially oak (*Quercus* spp.), walnut, beech, maple, and sycamore. Prefers temperate and boreal forests; fruits from late summer through autumn (August–November). Native to North America, Europe, and Asia. Now cultivated commercially worldwide and one of the most widely grown [[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-hericium-erinaceus|medicinal mushroom]]s.

## Cultivation

Primary method involves inoculating sterilized hardwood sawdust substrate (oak, beech, or mixed hardwood) supplemented with wheat bran or rice bran (10–20%). Colonization occurs at 20–25°C; fruiting is triggered by dropping temperature to 15–20°C with high humidity (85–95% RH). Low indirect light and fresh air exchange are critical during fruiting. Biological efficiency typically reaches 80–120% on supplemented sawdust, with first harvest at ~4–6 weeks. Log cultivation is also practiced, similar to shiitake production.

## Active Compounds

### Hericenones (Fruiting Body)
Fatty acid derivatives (phenolic compounds) isolated from the fruiting body. Major types include hericenones C through J. Hericenones C and D have been shown to stimulate NGF synthesis in vitro. These compounds are not present in significant quantities in [[mycelium-running-remediating-toxic-waste|mycelium]].

### Erinacines (Mycelium)
Diterpenoid compounds isolated primarily from mycelial culture, with erinacine A being the most studied. Erinacine A is a potent stimulator of NGF synthesis, demonstrated in both in vitro and in vivo models. It crosses the blood-brain barrier more effectively than hericenones in animal models.

### Other Bioactives
- **β-glucans** (20–30% dry weight): Immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory
- **Hericium polysaccharides:** Antitumor activity in animal studies
- **Terpenoids, sterols, and phenolic acids:** Antioxidant properties

## Neuroprotective Mechanisms

1. **NGF stimulation:** Hericenones and erinacines induce NGF synthesis in astrocytes, promoting neurite outgrowth and neuronal survival
2. **BDNF upregulation:** Increased brain-derived neurotrophic factor expression in the hippocampus (animal studies)
3. **Anti-inflammatory:** Inhibition of NF-κB pathway, reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β)
4. **Antioxidant:** Scavenging of reactive oxygen species; reduced lipid peroxidation in brain tissue
5. **Anti-amyloid:** Inhibition of Aβ plaque formation and potential disaggregation of existing plaques in vitro and in animal models

Evidence is growing but limited by small sample sizes and heterogeneous preparations. Larger Phase II/III trials are needed.

## Traditional Use (TCM)

Known in Chinese as 猴头菇 ("monkey head mushroom"), first described in medicinal literature ~2,000 years ago and documented in the *Bencao Gangmu*. Classified as sweet (味甘) and mild (性平), entering the Spleen and Stomach meridians. Primary indications: strengthening the spleen, nourishing the stomach, promoting digestion, and supporting vitality. Traditionally decocted in soups with chicken or pork bone broth as both food and medicine (药食同源).

## Culinary Uses

Flavor profile is mild and slightly sweet, developing a remarkable resemblance to lobster, crab, or scallops when cooked. Common preparations include sautéing in butter, breading and frying as "crab cakes," and adding to soups, risottos, and pasta. Widely used as a seafood substitute in vegetarian and vegan cooking. Fresh specimens are preferred; older ones become tough and bitter.

## Dosage

| Form | Typical Dose |
|------|-------------|
| Dried fruiting body powder | 1–3 g/day |
| Standardized extract (8:1–10:1) | 250–500 mg/day |
| Dual extract (water + ethanol) | 500–1,000 mg/day |
| Tincture (1:4) | 2–4 mL/day |
| Culinary (fresh) | No specific limit |

Cognitive benefits typically require 4+ weeks of consistent use and may diminish upon discontinuation. No officially established RDA exists.

## Safety Profile

GRAS status (FDA) as a food ingredient. Rare side effects include gastrointestinal discomfort, skin rash, and headache. Caution advised with anticoagulant therapy (theoretical antiplatelet activity), immunosuppressants, and autoimmune conditions. Insufficient safety data for pregnancy and lactation. No acute toxicity observed in animal studies at doses up to 5,000 mg/kg. Discontinue 2 weeks before scheduled surgery.

## Related Species

*Hericium coralloides* (coral hedgehog), *H. americanum* (bear's head tooth), and *H. abietis* (bear's head) are often confused in foraging — all are edible. Legal worldwide and available as a dietary supplement.

## Etymology & Discovery

First formally described by Christian Hendrik Persoon in 1797 as *Hydnum erinaceus*, later transferred to genus *Hericium*. The genus name derives from Latin *hircus* (goat), referring to the beard-like spines; the species epithet *erinaceus* from Latin for hedgehog, also referencing the spiny appearance. The Japanese name yamabushitake (山伏茸) refers to the yamabushi — mountain ascetic monks whose flowing robes the fruiting body resembles.
