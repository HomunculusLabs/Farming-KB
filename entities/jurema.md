## Overview

**[[iboga-root-bark-preparation]] is the primary psychoactive plant part
- **Leaves:** Bipinnately compound, 5–10 cm long, with 2–4 pairs of pinnae, each bearing 10–20 pairs of tiny linear leaflets 3–6 mm long. The sensitive leaves fold when disturbed (seismonastic movement), a characteristic of the genus
- **Flowers:** Spikes of fragrant white to pale yellow staminate flowers, 2–4 cm long, appearing after rainfall events
- **Fruit:** Flat, brittle pods 3–6 cm × 6–10 mm, containing 3–5 brown oval seeds 2–3 mm long
- **Roots:** Extensive lateral root system with nitrogen-fixing nodules; root bark is thick, reddish, and rich in [[hplc-analysis-of-tryptamine-alkaloids]]

## Distribution and Ecology

- **Native range:** Northeastern Brazil (Caatinga biome), extending through [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]].

## Phytochemistry

### Tryptamine Alkaloids

The root bark is the primary plant part harvested for its psychoactive alkaloids:

| Compound | Concentration (root bark, dry weight) | Notes |
|----------|--------------------------------------|-------|
| N,N-DMT | 0.5–1.1% | Primary psychoactive compound |
| N-Methyltryptamine (NMT) | 0.05–0.2% | Minor contribution |
| β-Carboline trace alkaloids | Trace | Not pharmacologically significant in isolation |

The inner root bark (cambium layer) contains the highest alkaloid concentrations. Stem bark contains only trace amounts and is not used for psychoactive purposes.

### Non-Psychoactive Compounds

- **Tannins:** Proanthocyanidins and flavonoid tannins (concentrated in stem bark)
- **Saponins:** Triterpenoid saponins with hemolytic activity
- **Flavonoids:** Including kaempferol and quercetin derivatives
- **Phenolic acids:** Caffeic, ferulic, and gallic acids
- **Mucilaginous polysaccharides:** Contributing to wound-healing properties of the bark

## Traditional Uses

### Brazilian Jurema Traditions

The psychoactive use of M. tenuiflora root bark has been documented in northeastern Brazil since at least the 18th century, and likely predates European contact. [[kava-traditional-preparation-ceremonial-context]] methods include:

- **Jurema wine (vinho de jurema):** Root bark is macerated or decocted, often in combination with other plants, to produce a fermented or unfermented beverage consumed in communal ceremonial contexts. Indigenous groups including the Kariri-Xocó, Fulniô, and Atikum continue these practices
- **Traditional ceremonies:** Often involve singing, dancing, and contact with ancestral spirits. The jurema ceremony (toré) was suppressed during colonial and post-colonial periods but has experienced revival since the 1980s
- **Historical accounts:** 18th-century Portuguese chroniclers documented the use of jurema among indigenous peoples of Pernambuco, describing its effects as producing visions and spiritual experiences

### Mexican Traditional Medicine (Tepezcohuite)

In Mexico, M. tenuiflora is used primarily for dermatological purposes, with no known psychoactive tradition:

- **Burn treatment:** The inner bark powder is applied topically to burns, promoting rapid re-epithelialization and reducing scarring. This use became internationally known after the 1984 San Juan Ixhuatepec gas explosion in Mexico City, where burn victims were treated with tepezcohuite
