# Mollisonian Permaculture Principles

## Description

The Mollisonian [[permaculture-principles]] are a set of five core design principles articulated by [[bill-mollison]] in *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* (Chapter 2.14). These principles form the operational guidance for [[permaculture-design]], complementing the ethical foundations (Care of Earth, Care of People, Setting Limits) with practical directives for working with natural systems. Together with the ethical basis and the rules of resource use, they constitute the complete philosophical and practical framework of permaculture.

## Key Facts — The Five Principles

1. **Work with nature** — assist rather than impede natural elements, forces, pressures, processes, agencies, and evolutions. Design should align with natural patterns rather than opposing them. This means observing what nature does on a site and designing to enhance those processes rather than replacing them.

2. **The problem is the solution** — everything works both ways. Advantage or disadvantage depends on perception. If the wind blows cold, use both its strength and its coolness to advantage. A corollary: everything is a positive resource; the designer's task is to discover how each element can serve the system.

3. **Make the least change for the greatest possible effect** — small, well-placed interventions can cascade through systems. Leverage points are preferred over wholesale transformation. A single swale, a carefully placed windbreak, or a strategically located pond can transform the productivity of an entire landscape.

4. **The yield of a system is theoretically unlimited** — the only limit on uses of a resource is the information and imagination of the designer. This principle rejects the idea of fixed carrying capacity and instead frames yield as a function of design intelligence, creativity, and the number of beneficial connections made.

5. **Everything gardens, or has an effect on its environment** — every organism actively modifies its surroundings. Designers can harness these effects rather than treating organisms as passive resources. Chickens scratch, bees pollinate, worms aerate soil, beavers create wetlands, fungi decompose.

## Supporting Principles and Directives

Mollison supplements the five core principles with additional directives:

- **Principle of Stability**: Stability comes from beneficial connections between components, not from the number of diverse components
- **Principle of Stress and Harmony**: Stress is prevention of natural function or forced function; harmony is the integration of chosen and natural functions
- **Principle of Disorder**: Any resource input beyond what a system can use productively throws it into disorder; oversupply is chronic pollution
- **Principle of Cyclic Opportunity**: Every cyclic event increases the opportunity for yield; to increase cycling is to increase yield
- **Categories of Resources**: Five types — those that increase by modest use, those unaffected by use, those that degrade if not used, those reduced by use, and those that pollute if used
- **Policy of Resource Management**: Ban resources that permanently reduce yields of sustainable resources

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

These five principles are the operational backbone of all permaculture design:

1. **Working with nature** underpins the entire methodology — from choosing locally adapted species to designing water harvesting systems that follow natural flow patterns
2. **Problem as solution** transforms obstacles into resources: weeds become mulch, slope enables water harvesting, shade creates microclimates for shade-loving crops, "weeds" indicate soil conditions
3. **Least change, greatest effect** is the principle of strategic intervention — a single swale, keypoint, or windbreak can transform the productivity of an entire site without wholesale clearing
4. **Unlimited yield** drives innovation — polycultures, stacking functions, and multi-layer food forests exist because designers imagined more connections than monoculture allows
5. **Everything gardens** expands the design toolkit to include the natural behaviours of all organisms — the designer becomes a conductor orchestrating the activities of many species

- Form the operational counterpart to the three ethics of permaculture (Care of Earth, Care of People, Setting Limits)
- Related to Birch's Six Principles of Natural Systems
- Elaborated in Chapter 2.14 of *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*

- Foundation for all subsequent permaculture design methodologies and teaching
