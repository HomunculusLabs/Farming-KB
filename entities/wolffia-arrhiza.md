## Overview

Wolffia arrhiza is a species of free-floating aquatic plant in the family Araceae (subfamily Lemnoideae, formerly Lemnaceae), commonly known as rootless duckweed or spotted duckweed. It is widely considered one of the smallest flowering plants on Earth.

Individual plants are tiny, lens-shaped fronds measuring only 0.5–1.5 mm in length and 0.3–0.8 mm in width. Despite their minuscule size, these fronds are complete plants capable of photosynthesis, growth, and reproduction.

Wolffia arrhiza has a nearly cosmopolitan distribution in tropical and subtropical regions. It is found across Africa, southern Europe, South and Southeast Asia, and parts of the Americas.

The species has attracted scientific and commercial interest as a potential source of high-quality protein for human and animal nutrition. Its extremely rapid growth rate makes it one of the fastest-growing plants known.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Angiosperms → Monocots → Alismatales → Araceae → Lemnoideae → Wolffia → Wolffia arrhiza (L.) Horkel ex Wimm.

The species was originally described as Lemma arrhiza by Linnaeus in 1753. It was transferred to the genus Wolffia by Horkel in 1832 and validated by Wimmer in 1933.

Wolffia is the most reduced genus in the duckweed family. Plants lack true roots, stems, and leaves, consisting instead of a single oval to spherical frond called a thallus.

**Fronds** are ovoid to nearly spherical, green, and measure 0.5–1.5 mm long and 0.3–0.8 mm wide. The dorsal surface is convex and green; the ventral surface is nearly flat and slightly paler.

A single stomatal pore is present on the dorsal surface near the center of the frond. No true roots are produced, though a small rootless papilla may be present on the ventral surface.

**Flowers** are extremely rare in nature and consist of a single stamen and a single pistil embedded in a dorsal cavity of the frond. Flowering has been observed primarily under stress conditions.

The fruit is a microscopic utricle containing a single smooth seed approximately 0.3 mm in diameter. Seed production is uncommon as vegetative reproduction dominates.

**Reproduction** occurs almost exclusively by vegetative budding. A new daughter frond emerges from a pocket on one end of the parent frond and separates once it reaches roughly the parent's size.

Under optimal conditions, Wolffia arrhiza can double its biomass in 1.5–2.5 days. This makes it one of the fastest-growing angiosperms known, surpassing even many microalgae in biomass accumulation rate.

## Distribution and Habitat

Wolffia arrhiza has a pantropical and subtropical distribution. It is native to Africa, southern Europe (Mediterranean basin), South Asia, Southeast Asia, and parts of Australia and the Americas.

In Europe, the species reaches its northern limit around 50°N latitude. It is established in southern France, Italy, the Balkans, and the Iberian Peninsula but rarely persists through cold winters in northern Europe.

The species grows in still or very slow-moving freshwater bodies. Typical habitats include ponds, ditches, marshes, rice paddies, lake margins, and slow streams with minimal current.

Wolffia arrhiza prefers nutrient-rich (eutrophic) waters with high levels of nitrogen and phosphorus. It thrives in warm conditions with water temperatures between 20–35 °C.

The plant forms dense green mats on the water surface that can completely cover small water bodies. These mats reduce light penetration and can deplete dissolved oxygen in underlying water.

Wolffia arrhiza is frequently found in mixed-species duckweed communities alongside Lemna minor, Lemna gibba, Spirodela polyrrhiza, and other Lemnaceae species.

## Nutritional and Culinary Value

Wolffia arrhiza has emerged as a promising alternative protein source due to its exceptional protein content and rapid biomass production. Dry biomass contains approximately 35–45% crude protein by weight.

The protein quality is high, with a favorable amino acid profile comparable to soy protein. It contains adequate levels of all essential amino acids, particularly lysine and methionine.

In addition to protein, Wolffia biomass contains 20–30% carbohydrate, 5–8% lipid, and significant amounts of dietary fiber, vitamins, and minerals. It is notably rich in omega-3 fatty acids and carotenoids.

Wolffia is consumed as a human food in several Asian countries. In Myanmar (Burma), it is harvested from natural water bodies and consumed as a vegetable called kye-yan.

In Thailand and Laos, Wolffia species are collected from ponds and markets are sold as a fresh vegetable for soups and salads. The taste is mild and slightly green, similar to young spinach.

Commercially, Wolffia biomass has been approved as a novel food ingredient in the European Union and several other jurisdictions. It is marketed as a sustainable protein ingredient for food products and supplements.

Animal feed trials have demonstrated Wolffia as an effective replacement for soybean meal in poultry, swine, and fish diets. Feed conversion ratios are comparable or superior to conventional protein sources.

Research on large-scale Wolffia cultivation for protein production is ongoing, with several companies developing commercial production systems using closed bioreactors and open pond facilities.

## Ecological Role

Wolffia arrhiza plays a significant role in freshwater ecosystems as both a primary producer and a food source. Dense surface mats provide habitat for microinvertebrates and feeding grounds for waterfowl.

Ducks, geese, and other waterfowl consume Wolffia as a significant food source during migration and breeding seasons. The high protein and mineral content makes it particularly valuable for growing ducklings.

The dense mats reduce light penetration to submerged vegetation, potentially suppressing macrophyte growth beneath the surface. This shading effect can alter the composition of aquatic plant communities.

Rapid nutrient uptake by Wolffia mats can reduce eutrophication symptoms including algal blooms. However, the shading and oxygen depletion beneath thick mats can create unfavorable conditions for fish and submerged organisms.

Wolffia contributes to nutrient cycling by rapidly assimilating dissolved nitrogen and phosphorus. Harvesting Wolffia mats from nutrient-rich water bodies is an effective strategy for nutrient removal.

## Phytoremediation

Wolffia arrhiza is highly effective at removing nutrients and contaminants from wastewater. Its rapid growth rate and high nutrient uptake capacity make it a valuable tool for phytoremediation applications.

The species removes nitrogen primarily through direct uptake of ammonium and nitrate ions. Phosphorus removal occurs through uptake and sequestration in plant biomass.

Wolffia has demonstrated the ability to accumulate heavy metals including lead, cadmium, arsenic, and chromium from contaminated water. Metal accumulation occurs primarily in the frond tissue.

Pharmaceutical contaminants including antibiotics and hormones are also degraded or bioaccumulated by Wolffia species. This makes the plant useful for tertiary treatment of municipal and agricultural wastewater.

Constructed wetland systems incorporating Wolffia have achieved removal rates of 80–95% for total nitrogen and 70–90% for total phosphorus. These systems are particularly effective in tropical and subtropical climates.

Regular harvesting of Wolffia biomass is essential for sustained phytoremediation performance. Harvested biomass can be processed for animal feed or biogas production, closing the nutrient recycling loop.
