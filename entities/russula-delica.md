## Russula delica

## Overview

Russula delica is a distinctive member of the Russulaceae family, commonly known as the milk-white russula or white brittlegill.

It is a large, predominantly white mushroom found in association with coniferous trees across Europe, North Africa, and parts of Asia.

The species is noted for its robust, compact basidiocarp and its tendency to discolor brownish-ochre with age or handling.

First described by Elias Magnus Fries in 1838, it has undergone significant taxonomic revision and is part of a complex of white Russula species.

Despite its imposing appearance, it is considered only mediocre as an edible, with a hot and acrid taste in many specimens that limits culinary appeal.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi

### Synonyms

- Agaricus delicus Fr. (1838)
- Russula delica var. alba Rea (1922)

The epithet *delica* means "delicate" in Latin, which is somewhat ironic given the mushroom's substantial stature and robust construction.

Recent molecular work has suggested that the name R. delica has been widely misapplied in European collections, with many specimens actually belonging to R. chloroides.

True R. delica sensu stricto may be restricted to Mediterranean and North African populations associated with Pinus halepensis.

## Morphology

The cap is 5–15 cm in diameter, convex when young, developing a central depression with age to become deeply infundibuliform.

Surface is smooth, matt, white when young but soon developing brownish to ochraceous stains, especially toward the center and margin.

The cap margin is initially inrolled and remains slightly incurved for a considerable time, a useful distinguishing feature in the field.

Unlike many Russula species, the cap cuticle does not peel readily, peeling at most one-quarter of the radius.

Gills are adnexed to slightly decurrent, crowded, white to cream-colored, sometimes developing a pale buff tint with age.

Fruiting occurs from late summer through late autumn, typically August to November, in coniferous forests on acidic to neutral soils.

It may also occur under spruce and fir in montane environments, though pine associations are by far the most common.

## Edibility

Russula delica is generally classified as an [[bloomfield-boletus-satanus-edible-mushroom-hazards]] of limited culinary value due to its frequently acrid taste.

Specimens with mild flavor can be eaten after thorough cooking, and are used in some regional Italian and Spanish cuisines.

The flesh does not have the crisp texture prized in better Russula species like R. vesca or R. cyanoxantha, tending to be somewhat granular.
