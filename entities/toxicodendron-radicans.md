## Taxonomy and Morphology

Toxicodendron radicans was originally described by Carl Linnaeus as Rhus radicans in 1753 and transferred to Toxicodendron by Otto Kuntze in 1891. The genus Toxicodendron was long subsumed within Rhus but molecular phylogenetic studies in the 1990s and 2000s supported its recognition as a distinct genus within Anacardiaceae.

Several infraspecific taxa have been described. The most widely recognized varieties include var. radicans (climbing vine form), var. rydbergii (erect shrub form of the western interior), and var. negundo (found primarily in the southwestern United States and northern Mexico).

The compound leaves are alternate and trifoliolate, a characteristic captured by the widely known mnemonic "leaves of three, let it be." Each leaflet is 3-12 cm long with serrate or entire margins and pointed tips.

Leaflet shape varies considerably across the species range. Northern populations tend toward more lobed leaflets, while southern and western populations frequently bear entire or slightly crenulate margins.

Seasonal color change in poison ivy foliage is dramatic and often overlooked as an identification hazard. Autumn leaves turn brilliant shades of red, orange, and yellow, and can remain allergenic on the forest floor well into winter.

The fruit is a grayish-white drupe approximately 3-5 mm in diameter, borne in loose clusters. Each drupe contains a single seed and persists through winter, providing a critical food resource for birds.

Stem morphology depends on growth form. Ground-creeping forms produce slender stolons, while climbing vines develop aerial rootlets that firmly anchor the plant to supporting structures. These rootlets give the bark a characteristic hairy appearance, which itself can cause dermatitis upon contact.
