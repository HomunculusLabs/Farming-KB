## Description

A cloche (from the French word for "bell") is a transparent glass or plastic cover placed over individual plants or small groups of plants to protect them from cold, wind, and pests while creating a warm microclimate for accelerated growth. In historical French market gardening, 18-inch-diameter glass bell jars called cloches were a primary season-extension tool, used by the thousands in the [[parisian-maraicher]] system to produce vegetables year-round. The cloche is one of the earliest forms of protected cultivation in European horticulture.

## Classification

- **Category**: Protected cultivation tool / season extension device
- **Type**: Individual plant cover (glass bell jar)
- **Materials**: Glass (historical), plastic/polycarbonate (modern)
- **Historical period**: Used extensively 1850–1920 by Parisian maraichers; precursors from 17th century

## Key Facts

- Traditional French cloches were 18-inch-diameter bell-shaped glass jars placed directly over plants in the field.
- Parisian maraichers used up to 3,000 individual cloches per garden, requiring enormous hand labor for daily management.
- Each cloche had to be individually ventilated on sunny days using a small notched stick to prop up one edge, preventing overheating.
- Cloches were used for every stage of plant growth: starting transplant seedlings, protecting young plants, and sheltering mature heads of lettuce up to harvest.
- They could be covered with rye straw mats for additional insulation during very cold periods.
- Cloches were typically arranged in tidy rows in the field, set close together to maximize covered growing area.
- The system enabled French growers to export fresh salads to England throughout the winter despite being in the same climate zone.
- Modern equivalents include plastic cloches, row covers, low tunnels, and individual plant protection domes.
- The cloche system was documented by [[william-robinson]] in "Parks and Gardens of Paris" (1869) and by numerous French horticultural authors.
- Cloches declined with the rise of larger greenhouse structures and the disappearance of cheap manual labor.

## Advantages

- Creates a warm, sheltered microclimate (typically 5-15°F warmer than ambient).
- Protects from wind, frost, and desiccating conditions.
- Allows early spring planting and late fall harvest extension.
- Portable and reusable for many seasons when made of glass.
- No energy input required beyond solar radiation.

## Disadvantages

- Extremely labor-intensive for daily ventilation of hundreds or thousands of units.
- Glass cloches are fragile and expensive to replace.
- Risk of overheating on sunny days if not vented promptly.
- Limited coverage area per unit compared to continuous row covers or greenhouses.

## Relevance to Cultivation

The cloche represents a fundamental innovation in protected cultivation that enabled the development of intensive, year-round market gardening in northern climates. The daily management of thousands of cloches by the Parisian maraichers demonstrates the extraordinary level of skilled hand labor that made their system productive. Modern growers can use lightweight plastic cloches or row covers as low-cost, low-tech season extension tools following the same principles. The cloche concept—individual plant protection using transparent covers—lives on in products like Wall-O-Water plant protectors, polytunnel low covers, and frost blankets used by contemporary organic growers.
