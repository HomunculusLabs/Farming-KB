## Overview

**Morus nigra**, the black mulberry, is a deciduous tree in the family Moraceae native to
Southwestern Asia. It is cultivated worldwide for its dark, flavorful fruit, which is richer
in anthocyanins and flavor compounds than the white mulberry (*M. alba*) or red mulberry
(*M. rubra*). Black mulberry fruit has a long history of use in traditional Persian, Greek,
and Roman medicine, where it was valued for throat ailments, digestive complaints, and as a
general tonic.

The species is slow-growing but long-lived, with specimens exceeding 300 years recorded in
Europe. Its fruit is commercially important for jams, syrups, liqueurs, and traditional
desserts across the Mediterranean, Middle East, and Central Asia.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:**

- Kingdom: Plantae — Division: Magnoliophyta — Class: Magnoliopsida
- Order: Rosales — Family: Moraceae — Genus: *Morus*
- Species: *M. nigra* L. (1753)

The species was described by Linnaeus in 1753. No significant synonyms are in current use,
though early botanical literature sometimes confused it with *M. rubra*.

**Morphology:**

Black mulberry is a medium-sized deciduous tree reaching 6–12 m in height. The trunk is
short and gnarled, developing a spreading, rounded crown with age. Bark is dark grey-brown
and deeply fissured on older specimens.

Leaves are broadly ovate to cordate, 10–20 cm long, with a coarsely serrate margin and a
rough upper surface. Leaf shape is highly variable, with some leaves simple and others
deeply lobed on the same tree — more so than in *M. alba*.

The fruit is a compound sorosis (aggregate fruit), 2–3 cm long, ripening from red to deep
purple-black. Black mulberry fruit is notably larger, juicier, and more intensely flavored
than other *Morus* species, with a balanced sweet-tart profile.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Morus nigra* is native to a region stretching from the Iranian Plateau through Southwest
Asia to the Levant. It has been naturalized across Southern Europe, the Mediterranean
Basin, and parts of East Asia following centuries of cultivation.

The tree prefers warm-temperate to subtropical climates (USDA zones 5–9). It is more cold-
sensitive than *M. alba*, suffering significant twig dieback below −20°C. It thrives in
deep, well-drained loamy soils with a pH of 5.5–7.0 but tolerates clay and sandy substrates
with adequate moisture.

Black mulberry is moderately drought-tolerant once established, requiring roughly 600–1000 mm
annual rainfall. It performs best in full sun but tolerates partial shade, particularly in
hotter regions where afternoon protection is beneficial.

## Cultivation

Propagation is most commonly by hardwood cuttings (30–45 cm, taken in winter), which root
readily within 6–8 weeks under mist. Softwood cuttings and grafting onto *M. alba* rootstock
are also practiced. [[jeavons-seed-propagation-techniques]] produces variable offspring and is generally avoided for
named cultivars.

Trees begin bearing fruit 3–5 years after planting from cuttings. Mature trees can produce
25–50 kg of fruit annually. Peak productivity occurs between 10–30 years, though trees
remain productive for over a century.

Pruning is essential for managing the spreading habit and maintaining harvestable height.
Summer pruning after fruiting is recommended, as black mulberry bleeds heavily if pruned
during dormancy. A modified open-center form works well for home orchards.

Key cultivars include 'Hicks Fancy' (Australia), 'King James' (England), and 'Noir de
Spain' (Mediterranean region), each selected for fruit size, flavor, or [[jeavons-climate-adaptation-growing-seasons]].

## Edibility and Culinary Use

Black mulberry fruit is eaten fresh, dried, or processed into preserves, syrups, and
beverages. The flavor is considered superior to other mulberry species — richer, more
complex, and with better sugar-acid balance.

The fruit is high in anthocyanins (primarily cyanidin-3-glucoside), vitamin C, vitamin K,
and iron. Fresh fruit contains approximately 9–10% sugars (glucose and fructose) and 1.5%
organic acids (primarily citric and malic acid).

In Turkish cuisine, the fruit is used to make *dut pestili* (fruit leather) and *dut
şerbeti* (mulberry sherbet). In British traditional cooking, black mulberry jam was prized
as a luxury preserve. The fruit is also fermented into wine and used as a base for
liqueurs.

## Bioactive Compounds and Traditional Medicine

Black mulberry fruit and leaves are rich in bioactive compounds. The fruit contains
anthocyanins (10–20× higher than *M. alba*), resveratrol, quercetin, kaempferol, and
chlorogenic acid. Leaves contain 1-deoxynojirimycin (DNJ), an alpha-glucosidase inhibitor
relevant to blood sugar regulation.

In Persian traditional medicine (*Teb-e Sonati*), black mulberry fruit was prescribed for
sore throat, mouth ulcers, and constipation. Leaf preparations were used as a hypoglycemic
agent. Roman naturalists including Pliny the Elder recommended mulberry syrup for
inflammatory conditions of the mouth and throat.

Modern pharmacological studies have demonstrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective,
and antidiabetic activities in vitro and in animal models. Human clinical data remains limited,
though preliminary trials suggest potential benefits for glycemic control.

## Permaculture and Agroforestry Applications

Black mulberry serves multiple functions in permaculture and food forest designs. As a
productive fruit tree, it provides high-value yields with relatively low maintenance once
established. Its deep taproot system improves soil structure and accesses subsoil nutrients.

The tree provides excellent [[free-range-poultry-forage-design-mollison]] — chickens readily consume fallen fruit, reducing
waste and contributing to egg yolk pigmentation. The dense canopy offers summer shade for
livestock and understory crops, and the [[dighton-fungal-decomposition-leaf-litter-dynamics]] contributes to soil [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]].

In agroforestry systems, black mulberry is compatible with nut trees (walnut, chestnut),
berries, and nitrogen-fixing understory species. It can serve as a windbreak component in
mixed hedgerows and functions well as a boundary tree in zone 2–3 designs.
