# Cation Exchange Capacity

## Description

Cation exchange capacity (CEC) is a fundamental soil property that measures the total capacity of soil particles to hold and exchange positively charged ions (cations). It is a critical determinant of soil fertility, nutrient retention, and plant availability of essential minerals. CEC is expressed in units of centimoles of charge per kilogram of soil (cmolc/kg) or historically as milliequivalents per 100 grams (meq/100g). The higher the CEC, the more nutrients a soil can retain against leaching.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil science concept
- **Related properties**: Anion exchange capacity (AEC), base saturation, pH buffering capacity
- **Units**: cmolc/kg (SI) or meq/100g (traditional)

## Key Facts

- Soil CEC arises primarily from negatively charged sites on clay particles and organic matter (humus).
- Humus has the highest CEC of any soil component (150–300 cmolc/kg), far exceeding most clay minerals (kaolinite: 3–15 cmolc/kg, montmorillonite: 80–120 cmolc/kg).
- In tropical soils, the charge on clay particles is pH-dependent: as pH increases, negative charge increases, enhancing CEC. This is particularly important for oxidic and kaolinitic soils.
- Essential nutrient cations held by CEC include calcium (Ca²⁺), magnesium (Mg²⁺), potassium (K⁺), ammonium (NH₄⁺), and sodium (Na⁺).
- Low CEC soils lose nutrients rapidly through leaching, especially under high rainfall conditions typical of the humid tropics.
- CEC can be increased by adding organic matter (compost, green manure, mulch), certain clays (bentonite), or liming materials that raise pH.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Mollison devotes significant attention to CEC in the humid tropics chapter, where it is identified as the central challenge of tropical soil management:

- **Tropical soil fertility crisis**: In deeply weathered tropical soils, silica and calcium are in low supply. Aluminium ions substitute for silica in clays, creating the oxidic kaolinitic soils common in weathered volcanic regions. These soils have inherently low CEC, making them nutrient-poor once cleared of vegetation.
- **pH-dependent CEC management**: Mollison diagrams how CEC and ionic bonds on soil particles change with pH in tropical clays. Raising pH through additions of lime, crushed basalt, or cement powder increases the negative charge on soil particles, improving their ability to hold calcium, sodium, and phosphates against leaching.
- **Humus as CEC enhancer**: Restoring humus through green manure crops (especially Leucaena and tree legumes) is the primary strategy for increasing CEC sustainably. Mollison notes that once cleared, humic particles leach out to about 30% of prior levels, and infertility becomes apparent in crops such as banana and sugar cane.
- **Small, frequent fertilization**: In low-CEC soils, large fertilizer applications are wasteful because nutrients leach before plants can absorb them. Mollison recommends light spreads at 6-week intervals, with a gradual transition from annual to perennial crops.

## Practical Applications

- **Soil amendment strategy**: Add basalt, cement powder, coral sand, and bamboo mulch to supply essential nutrients and raise pH, thereby increasing CEC.
- **Organic matter management**: Prioritize mulch, compost, and green manure as the most sustainable way to build CEC in tropical soils.
- **Perennial over annual crops**: Since any cultivation loses humus as carbon dioxide, perennial systems maintain higher CEC than annual cropping.
- **Fertilizer strategy**: In low-CEC soils, apply small amounts frequently rather than large doses infrequently to minimize leaching losses.
