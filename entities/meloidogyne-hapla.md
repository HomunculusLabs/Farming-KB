# Meloidogyne hapla

## Description

Meloidogyne hapla, commonly known as the northern root-knot nematode, is a
sedentary endoparasitic nematode that causes characteristic galls (knots) on the
roots of infected plants. It has an extremely wide host range encompassing
hundreds of plant species and is one of the most economically important plant-
parasitic nematodes in temperate agriculture. Viaene and Abawi (1998) documented
significant levels of fungal parasitism on M. hapla egg masses and juveniles in
organic soils, revealing natural biocontrol potential.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Eukaryota
- **Kingdom:** Animalia
- **Phylum:** Nematoda
- **Class:** Secernentea
- **Order:** Tylenchida
- **Family:** Meloidogynidae
- **Genus:** Meloidogyne
- **Species:** M. hapla

## Key Facts

- **Root-knot formation:** M. hapla induces the formation of root galls by
  modifying plant root cells into specialized feeding cells (giant cells) that
  supply nutrients to the developing nematode.
- **Wide host range:** Attacks hundreds of plant species including vegetables
  (carrot, tomato, lettuce), legumes, cereals, and ornamental plants. Unlike
  some Meloidogyne species, M. hapla can reproduce on many dicotyledonous crops
  in temperate regions.
- **Fungal parasitism of eggs:** Viaene and Abawi (1998) found 13-30% of
  M. hapla egg masses in organic soils were infected with parasitic fungi,
  including Fusarium, Verticillium, and Alternaria species.
- **Juvenile trapping:** The same study found 5-24% of J2 stage juveniles were
  attacked by [[nematophagous-fungi]] (Arthrobotrys, Monacrosporium), indicating
  multi-stage natural control.
- **Northern distribution:** M. hapla is the most cold-tolerant root-knot
  nematode species, capable of overwintering in soil in temperate regions where
  other Meloidogyne species cannot survive.
- **Reproductive capacity:** A single female can produce 500-1000 eggs in a
  gelatinous egg mass, enabling rapid population buildup under favorable
  conditions.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Vegetable crop damage:** M. hapla is a major pest of high-value vegetable
  crops in temperate regions, causing significant yield losses through root
  damage that impairs water and nutrient uptake.
- **Natural biocontrol:** The finding that 13-30% of egg masses are naturally
  parasitized by fungi suggests that enhancing these fungal populations could
  provide effective biological control.
- **Organic farming relevance:** The parasitism levels were documented in
  organic soils, suggesting that organic management practices may foster higher
  populations of naturally occurring biocontrol fungi.
- **Integrated management:** Combining resistant cultivars, cultural practices
  (crop rotation, soil solarization), and fungal biocontrol agents offers
  sustainable M. hapla management without relying solely on chemical nematicides.

## Ecological Interactions

- Egg masses parasitized by Fusarium, Verticillium, and Alternaria fungal
  species in natural soils.
- J2 juveniles trapped by Arthrobotrys and Monacrosporium nematophagous fungi.
- Root galls alter rhizosphere chemistry, affecting colonization by beneficial
  microbes including [[mycorrhizal-fungi]].
- Population dynamics influenced by soil type, organic matter, temperature, and
  moisture.
