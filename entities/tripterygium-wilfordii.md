## Overview

Tripterygium wilfordii is a deciduous perennial vine in the family Celastraceae, commonly known as thunder god vine or lei gong teng. It is one of the most potent immunosuppressive plants in traditional Chinese medicine.

The species has been used for over 2,000 years in China for treating inflammatory and autoimmune conditions. Its diterpenoid triepoxide constituent, triptolide, is among the most studied natural immunosuppressants.

Clinical trials have demonstrated efficacy in rheumatoid arthritis, lupus nephritis, and other autoimmune disorders. The plant's therapeutic window is narrow, with significant toxicity at higher doses.

Modern drug development programs have produced semisynthetic derivatives such as (5R)-5-hydroxytriptolide (LLDT-8) with improved safety profiles. Tripterygium wilfordii exemplifies the challenges of translating potent botanical medicines into mainstream therapeutics.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Angiosperms — Eudicots — Rosids
- **Order:** Celastrales
- **Family:** Celastraceae
- **Genus:** Tripterygium
- **Species:** T. wilfordii Hook.f.

A woody deciduous vine reaching 3–8 m in length with brown, longitudinally fissured bark. Young stems are slender, greenish-brown, and densely pubescent.

Leaves are alternate, broadly ovate to elliptic, 6–12 cm long, with serrate margins and acute tips. The upper leaf surface is dark green and glabrous; the lower surface is paler with prominent venation.

Flowers are small, greenish-white, and arranged in dense terminal panicles 8–15 cm long. Blooming occurs from May to June in native habitats.

The fruit is a three-winged samara-like capsule, 1.5–2 cm across, turning reddish-brown at maturity. Seeds are cylindrical, dark brown, and enclosed within the winged pericarp.

Root bark is the primary medicinal part, with a thin yellow-brown exterior and white inner tissue. The root system is extensive, with lateral roots penetrating 1–2 m into the soil.

## Distribution and Habitat

Endemic to southern and central China, including Fujian, Zhejiang, Jiangxi, Hunan, and Guangdong provinces. Also occurs in Taiwan, Korea, and northern Vietnam.

The species inhabits mountain slopes, forest margins, and scrubland at elevations of 200–2,000 m. It prefers moist, well-drained, acidic soils in partially shaded positions.

Thunder god vine grows as an understory scrambler, climbing through shrubs and small trees. It is cold-hardy to approximately -10°C, with winter dormancy from November to March.

Wild populations have declined due to overharvesting of roots for pharmaceutical production. Cultivation efforts in Fujian and Zhejiang provinces are expanding to meet commercial demand.

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds

Triptolide (a diterpenoid triepoxide) is the most significant bioactive compound and primary active marker. It constitutes approximately 0.001–0.01% of root dry weight.

Additional diterpenoids include tripdiolide, triptonide, and 16-hydroxytriptolide. These share the characteristic triepoxide ring system that confers biological activity.

Triterpenoids in the root include wilfordic acid, tripterygic acid, and celastrol (also found in other Celastraceae). Celastrol has attracted attention as a potent NF-kB inhibitor.

Alkaloids present include wilfortrine, euonine, and wilfordine. Quinone-methide triterpenes such as pristimerin and tingenone contribute to cytotoxic activity.

The root also contains polysaccharides with immunomodulatory properties. Glycoside constituents include tripterygium glycosides, which form the basis of commercial pharmaceutical preparations.

## Pharmacology and Uses

Immunosuppressive activity is the hallmark pharmacological property, mediated through inhibition of NF-kB signaling and T-cell proliferation. Triptolide binds to XPB subunit of TFIIH, blocking transcription.

In rheumatoid arthritis, standardized extracts reduce joint swelling, morning stiffness, and inflammatory markers. Clinical efficacy is comparable to methotrexate in several Chinese randomized trials.

Anti-inflammatory effects involve suppression of COX-2, iNOS, TNF-alpha, IL-1 beta, and IL-6 production. These cytokine-suppressive effects are observed at nanomolar concentrations of triptolide.

Anticancer activity has been demonstrated against multiple tumor cell lines including pancreatic, breast, and prostate cancer. Triptolide induces apoptosis via mitochondrial pathways and caspase activation.

Male antifertility effects are a well-documented side effect, with reversible sperm suppression at therapeutic doses. This has prompted research into male contraceptive applications.

Toxicity is dose-limiting, with gastrointestinal, hepatic, renal, and reproductive adverse effects. The therapeutic index is narrow, requiring careful monitoring during clinical use.

## Cultivation

Propagation is primarily by seed, though germination rates are low (20–30%) without stratification. Cold stratification at 4°C for 60–90 days improves germination to approximately 50%.

Root cuttings provide a more reliable propagation method, with 60–80% success rate. Cuttings of 8–12 cm from mature roots are planted in spring at 5 cm depth.

Cultivation requires acidic, organic-rich soils (pH 5.0–6.5) with consistent moisture. The species is shade-tolerant and performs best under 40–60% shade cloth.

Root harvest occurs 3–5 years after establishment, with yields of 1–3 kg dried root per plant. Processing involves bark removal, slicing, and drying at low temperature to preserve triptolide content.
