## Description

Rust fungi are a large and economically devastating group of obligate biotrophic plant pathogens that cause diseases on an enormous range of hosts, including wheat, oats, rye, fruits, and pines. Named for the rusty-orange color of the spore masses they produce on infected plant surfaces, rust fungi have shaped human history through their impact on food production. In *[[teaming-with-microbes-actinomycetes]]*, Lowenfels and Lewis cite rust fungi alongside [[smut-fungi]] as examples of pathogenic fungi that impact agricultural and horticultural crops.

Rust diseases are among the most destructive plant diseases worldwide. Stem rust of wheat, caused by *Puccinia graminis* f. sp. *tritici*, has caused famines and altered the course of civilizations. The development of resistant wheat varieties by Norman Borlaug was recognized with the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970, underscoring the global significance of rust fungi.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Pucciniomycetes
- **Order:** Pucciniales (formerly Uredinales)
- **Notable Genera:** *Puccinia*, *Uromyces*, *Gymnosporangium*, *Cronartium*, *Melampsora*, *Phragmidium*

The order Pucciniales contains more than 5,000 species, making it one of the largest orders of plant-pathogenic fungi. All members are obligate parasites that require living host tissue to complete their life cycles.

## Key Characteristics

- Obligate biotrophs — cannot be cultured on artificial media without host tissue
- Produce up to five distinct spore stages in a complete life cycle (spermatial, aecial, uredinial, telial, and basidial)
- Many species are heteroecious, requiring two unrelated host plants to complete their full life cycle
- Urediniospores (repeating spores) are responsible for rapid epidemic spread during [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]
- Teliospores are thick-walled survival spores that overwinter [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]]
- Produce haustoria — specialized feeding structures that penetrate host cells without killing them

## Life Cycle

Rust fungi possess some of the most complex life cycles in the fungal kingdom. A complete (macrocylic) rust life cycle involves five spore stages:

1. **Spermatia (pycniospores)** — haploid spores produced on the primary host, functioning in sexual reproduction
2. **Aeciospores** — dikaryotic spores produced on the primary host that infect the alternate host
3. **Urediniospores** — the "repeating" stage; dikaryotic spores that reinfect the same host species repeatedly during the growing season, driving epidemics
4. **Teliospores** — the overwintering stage; thick-walled diploid spores produced at the end of the growing season
5. **Basidiospores** — haploid spores produced from germinating teliospores that infect the primary (alternate) host

Not all rust species complete all five stages. Some are autoecious (completing their life cycle on a single host) and some are microcyclic (having reduced life cycles with fewer spore stages).

## Major Diseases

- **Stem rust of wheat** (*Puccinia graminis* f. sp. *tritici*) — one of the most destructive wheat diseases; the Ug99 race variant threatens global wheat production
- **Leaf rust of wheat** (*Puccinia triticina*) — the most common and widely distributed wheat rust
- **Stripe/yellow rust** (*Puccinia striiformis*) — favored by cooler temperatures; expanding range with climate change
- **Crown rust of oats** (*Puccinia coronata*) — limits oat production in many regions
- **Cedar-apple rust** (*Gymnosporangium juniperi-virginianae*) — a heteroecious rust alternating between eastern red cedar and apple
- **White pine blister rust** (*Cronartium ribicola*) — five-needle pines alternate with currants/gooseberries (*Ribes* spp.)

## Agricultural Impact

Rust fungi have enormous economic impact on global agriculture:

- Wheat rusts alone cause losses estimated at billions of dollars annually worldwide
- Stem rust epidemics in the mid-20th century destroyed up to 70% of wheat crops in some regions
- The emergence of Ug99 (a virulent stem rust race first detected in Uganda in 1999) threatens wheat production across Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia
- Coffee leaf rust (*Hemileia vastatrix*) devastated coffee production in Sri Lanka in the 19th century and continues to affect coffee-growing regions globally

## Role in the Soil Food Web

While rust fungi are primarily above-ground pathogens, they have connections to the soil ecosystem discussed in *Teaming with Microbes*:

- Infected plant tissues fall to the soil and become part of the organic matter processed by decomposer fungi and bacteria
- Severely infected plants have reduced root exudate production, which can diminish [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]]
- Rust-infected plants may allocate more resources to defense, altering the composition of [[root-exudates]]

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Understanding rust fungi is critical for several reasons:

- Rust monitoring and forecasting systems guide fungicide application timing in [[night-break-photoperiodism-manipulation-commercial-agriculture]]
- Breeding for rust resistance is a major focus of cereal crop improvement programs
- The gene-for-gene relationship between rust avirulence genes and plant resistance genes is a foundational concept in plant pathology
- Rust fungi demonstrate the importance of [[fungi-convention-on-biological-diversity]] — removing alternate hosts can break the disease cycle

## Interesting Facts

- Roman philosopher Pliny the Elder described cereal rusts and erroneously attributed them to divine punishment
- The Romans held an annual festival, the Robigalia, sacrificing dogs to Robigus, the god of rust, to protect their crops
- Wheat stem rust spores have been documented traveling over 1,000 miles on wind currents
- *Puccinia graminis* has been explored as a potential biological weapon targeting food crops
- Some rust fungi produce hormones that trick their host plants into forming specialized structures (aecia) that house spore production
