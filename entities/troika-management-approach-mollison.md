# Troika Management Approach

## Description

The Troika Management Approach is a decentralised organisational governance model described by [[bill-mollison]] in *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, Chapter 14.10, as a practical alternative to conventional board-based, consensus-based, or hierarchical decision-making. Named after the Russian word for a three-horse sleigh team where no single horse dominates, the approach assigns each area of activity to a small autonomous team of one to three people who manage independently without group meetings, votes, or consensus processes. The system eliminates the time-wasting, frustrating, and divisive dynamics of large-group decision-making that Mollison identified as the primary cause of community failure.

## Classification

- **Category:** Organisational governance model
- **Domain:** [[permaculture-community-design]], group management, cooperative organisation
- **Attribution:** Bill Mollison, Permaculture: A Designers' Manual, Chapter 14.10 ([[effective-working-groups]] and Right Livelihood)
- **Origin:** Community organisation principles developed through observation of [[holmgren-community-design-and-intentional-communities]], cooperatives, and village systems worldwide

## Core Principles

### Abolish Group Decision Meetings
Mollison advocates abolishing formal decision meetings entirely:
- Hold one meeting to vote to abolish decision meetings (usually carried unanimously)
- Hold one meeting to reach consensus to abolish consensus (should not take long)

### Task-Based Work Allocation
All work in a community falls into two categories:
1. **Creative/productive/constructive tasks** — volunteers are sought and tasks are allotted to them with agreed timetables and completion stages
2. **Maintenance tasks** (domestic, office, garden) — people are rostered on a weekly or monthly schedule

Where no volunteers appear for any task, the group contributes a tithe to pay a contractor.

### The Troika Unit (1-3 People)
- Each area of action or task is managed by 1-3 individuals working as a unit
- They manage independently but may work to a general plan and schedule
- Completion dates are set and notified to all people in the community
- Reports, diaries, or plans are made public or minuted for transparency
- Occasional understudies are attached to ensure fail-safe continuity

### Accountability Without Control
- No person or group has rights of decision over other functions or groups
- Non-involved people have no say in tasks they are not familiar with
- Groups that persistently fail to deliver are replaced by others willing to try
- Power seekers are nullified by the structure itself

## Group Size Dynamics

Mollison provides specific guidance on optimal group sizes for different functions:

| Size | Optimal Function |
|------|-----------------|
| 1-3 people | Executive decision, least meeting time, fast changes possible, fast replacement of key people |
| 4-6 people | Good volunteer or cooperative work group; ideal for work exchange systems |
| 7-20 people | Function well only in social conditions; at 7+, decisions become slow and frustrating |
| 30-40 people | Minimal group to cover most human functions and cope with almost any type of problem |
| 200-300 people | Minimum for genetic variability; hamlet-size settlement with careful breeding |
| 300-600 people | Upper limit where people know every other person by name; limit of identity and real participation |
| 1,000-5,000 | Upper limit of federations of tribes; bioregional group or village size limit |

## Key Advantages

1. **Speed:** Small groups of 1-3 reach fast agreements and know each other's movements
2. **Autonomy:** Each troika controls its own area without interference from others
3. **Social meetings:** All group meetings become convivial, social, and informational rather than frustrating decision events
4. **Scalability:** Cells of 1-3 people can run very large networks through occasional affinity meetings
5. **Portfolio occupations:** Any person can belong to two or three teams, creating a diverse working life
6. **Natural replacement:** Understudies and duplication ensure continuity without bureaucracy

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

Mollison identifies these as primary errors causing constant conflict in communities:
- Group consensus on all decisions (tyranny of the dissenter)
- Group leadership by one or a few (tyranny of the leader)
- Rules about lifestyle (tyranny of proscription)
- Attempts to live in large group households (lack of privacy)
- Overscheduling of meetings
- Neglect of income-producing activities
- Poor financial accounting leading to poor maintenance of facilities and equipment

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The Troika approach has direct applications for [[mushroom-cultivation]] and permaculture operations:

- **[[spawn-production]] team (1-3 people):** Manage culture maintenance, grain preparation, and inoculation independently
- **Fruiting operations team (1-3 people):** Manage environmental controls, harvesting, [[mushroom-spawn-vitality-testing-and-quality-assessment]] on their own schedule
- **[[bulk-substrate-preparation]] team (1-3 people):** Manage composting, pasteurisation, and [[chen-maitake-substrate-formulation]] autonomously
- **Marketing and distribution team (1-3 people):** Manage sales, delivery, and customer relationships without oversight
- Each team operates autonomously within a shared general plan with coordination through published reports
- New cultivators can serve as understudies before taking on full troika roles, building capacity naturally

## Work-Netting Extension

Mollison extends the troika concept to "work-netting" — a regional-scale coordination where autonomous cells across a wider area cooperate through occasional meetings (every 2 years) in affinity or work integration groups. No person can force another group to cooperate but must offer reasonable, rewarding, and fair cooperation. This enables large-scale coordination without hierarchical control.
