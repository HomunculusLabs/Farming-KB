# Intensive Cropping

## Description

Intensive cropping is a production philosophy and set of practices aimed at maximizing food production per unit of land area through careful management of soil fertility, crop spacing, [[succession-planting]], and rotation. Rooted in the French *maraicher* tradition and refined by modern organic growers, intensive cropping achieves high yields without synthetic chemical inputs by treating soil biological health as the foundation of productivity.

## Classification

- **Category**: Agricultural system — production philosophy
- **Origin**: French *maraicher* tradition (Paris, 18th–19th century)
- **Modern exemplars**: [[eliot-coleman]], [[john-jeavons]], French intensive / biointensive methods
- **Scale**: Market garden (1–5 acres typical)
- **System**: Organic, [[pest-philosophy-biological-agriculture]]

## Key Facts

- The Parisian *maraichers* fed a city of 1 million people using approximately 6,000 acres of intensively managed gardens in the 19th century
- Key principles: close spacing, deep soil preparation, continuous cropping, generous composting, and crop rotation
- Intensive spacing typically uses 2–4x the plant density of conventional field spacing
- Succession planting ensures beds are never empty — as soon as one crop is harvested, another is planted
- [[cover-crops]] are integrated between cash crops to maintain soil fertility
- Deep soil preparation (double-digging or broadfork aeration) creates optimal root zone conditions
- The system relies on biological soil fertility rather than soluble fertilizers
- Compost applications of 1/4–1/2 inch per year maintain organic matter levels
- Mobile greenhouses enable crop rotation across the entire farm area, preventing soil depletion

## Historical Development

**Parisian market gardens (17th–19th century)**:
- Intensive cultivation on small plots (typically 1–2 acres per grower)
- Horse manure from city stables provided both fertility and heat (hotbeds)
- Multiple crops per bed per year through careful succession planning
- Efficient use of cloches and cold frames for season extension

**[[french-garden-system-british-adaptation]] (19th–20th century)**:
- British market gardeners adapted French methods to local conditions
- Emphasis on standardization and systematization of intensive techniques
- Development of specialized [[winter-harvest-tools-and-equipment]]

**Modern organic intensive (20th–21st century)**:
- Coleman and others combined European intensive methods with organic principles
- Mobile greenhouses solved the soil depletion problem inherent in permanent structures
- Modern materials (polytunnels, row covers) replaced glass and manure hotbeds
- Mechanical tools (seeders, greens harvesters) improved labor efficiency

## Relevance to Cultivation

Intensive cropping is the foundational production system for profitable small-scale market gardening:

- **Land efficiency**: Achieving high yields on small acreage reduces land costs and brings marginal land into productive use
- **[[economic-viability-water-retention-landscapes]]**: High gross revenue per acre makes small-scale farming economically sustainable
- **Winter production**: Intensive methods are especially valuable in protected winter cultivation where every square foot of greenhouse space must be productive
- **Soil health focus**: The system's reliance on biological fertility creates resilient soils that improve over time
- **Reduced external inputs**: Generous composting and green manures replace purchased fertility
- **Environmental benefits**: High productivity per acre means less total land needed for food production, preserving natural habitats

## Spacing Guidelines

- **Close spacing**: Plants are spaced at the minimum distance that allows full development
- **Bed system**: Permanent or semi-permanent beds (typically 30 inches wide) with narrow paths
- **Block planting**: Plants arranged in offset grids rather than rows to maximize coverage
- **Intercropping**: Compatible crops grown in the same bed simultaneously

## Fertility Management

- **Compost**: Applied annually at 1/4–1/2 inch depth
- **Green manures**: [[cover-crops]] integrated between cash crops
- **Rock minerals**: Greensand, phosphate rock, and limestone as needed based on soil tests
- **Legume rotations**: Nitrogen-fixing crops in rotation to supply nitrogen naturally
