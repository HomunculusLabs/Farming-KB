## Polyporus squamosus

## Description

*Polyporus squamosus* (commonly known as **Dryad's Saddle**) is a large bracket fungus noted for its bitter taste. It is mentioned by [[julius-auboineau-palmer]] as an example of a fungus that is bitter and tastes of "leaves and grass," rendering it unfit for culinary use despite not being dangerously poisonous. It belongs to the [[polyporei]] group of fungi, which are characterized by a sponge-like layer of tubes under the cap rather than gills.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Polyporales
- **Family:** Polyporaceae
- **Genus:** *Polyporus* (historical classification)
- **Species:** *P. squamosus*
- **Common name:** Dryad's Saddle

## Key Facts

- Large bracket fungus found on dead or dying hardwood trees, especially elm, beech, and maple
- Cap features conspicuous brown scales on a cream-yellow background
- Produces a sponge-like layer of pores on the underside rather than gills
- Described by Palmer as bitter in taste with notes of leaves and grass
- Not considered dangerously poisonous but unpalatable due to its bitterness
- Young specimens are sometimes edible if thoroughly cooked, though quality is poor
- Belongs to the Polyporei group -- fungi with a spongy tube layer under the cap
- Common in temperate regions across North America and Europe
- Often fruits in the same location year after year
- Can grow quite large, with caps reaching 60 cm or more in diameter
- One of the most commonly encountered bracket fungi in temperate forests

## Morphology

The fruiting body is fan-shaped or semicircular, typically 10-60 cm across. The upper surface is cream to yellowish with concentric zones of dark brown scales (hence squamosus, meaning scaly). The pore surface underneath is white to cream, with angular pores that are relatively large and visible to the naked eye. The flesh is thick and tough, becoming corky and inedible with age. The stem, when present, is short, thick, and lateral, often darkening toward the base. The overall appearance resembles a large shelf or fan projecting from the side of a tree trunk or fallen log.

## Habitat and Ecology

Found as a saprophyte on dead or dying hardwood trees, *P. squamosus* plays an important ecological role in decomposing dead wood and recycling nutrients back into the forest ecosystem. It causes a white rot in the heartwood of its host trees, breaking down lignin and leaving cellulose behind. The fungus is particularly common on elm, beech, maple, and other broadleaf species. It typically fruits in spring and autumn, often appearing in overlapping clusters on standing dead wood or fallen logs. As a saprophyte, it does not typically attack healthy living trees.

## Position in Palmer's Classification System

Palmer divides fungi into three broad groups based on their spore-bearing structure:

1. **Agaricini** -- gilled mushrooms with thin lines radiating from center like wheel spokes
2. **Polyporei** -- spongy tube layer under cap; includes Boleti when stem and pores are well-defined
3. **Puff-balls** -- globular, no stem or cap

*P. squamosus* falls in the Polyporei group. Palmer notes that many in this group are too slimy, bitter, or tough for food, though the mild-tasting, modest-colored Boleti are considered delicious eating. He specifically mentions *P. squamosus* as an example of the bitter varieties within this group.

## Edibility Notes

While very young specimens can be eaten when thoroughly cooked, the mature fruiting bodies are tough, corky, and distinctly bitter. Palmer includes *P. squamosus* among those fungi that are "bitter" and taste of "leaves and grass," placing it in the second of his three categories of mushroom poisoning: slimy, acrid, or nauseous species (as opposed to indigestible fungi or those containing subtle alkaloids like the Amanitas).

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

*P. squamosus* is one of the most commonly encountered bracket fungi, making it important for foragers to recognize and identify correctly. Its distinctive scaly cap and large size make it relatively easy to identify even for beginners. It serves as a useful representative of the Polyporei group in any classification system for field identification of fungi.
