## Description

[[arthur-koestler]] (1905–1983) was a Hungarian-British author, journalist, and philosopher whose work bridged science, philosophy, and literature. Mollison quotes extensively from Koestler's 1967 book *The Ghost in the Machine* as an epigraph to Chapter 2 of *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual*, using Koestler's description of living organisms as "open systems" to frame the theoretical foundations of [[permaculture-design]].

The quoted passage describes how living organisms maintain their complex forms and functions through continuous exchanges of energies and materials with their environment — constantly building up more complex substances, energies, and patterns of information rather than running down like mechanical clocks. This concept of living systems as self-organizing open systems is fundamental to permaculture theory.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Philosophy of science, systems theory
- **Era**: Mid 20th century
- **Tradition**: European intellectual tradition, holism

## Key Facts

- Born in Budapest, Hungary; became a British citizen
- *The Ghost in the Machine* (1967) argues against behaviorist and reductionist approaches to understanding mind and life
- Coined the term "holon" to describe entities that are simultaneously wholes and parts of larger wholes
- Proposed the concept of "holarchy" — a hierarchical system of holons — as an alternative to traditional reductionist hierarchies
- His theory of holons and holarchies influenced systems thinking, organizational theory, and ecology
- Also known for *Darkness at Noon* (1940), a novel about the Moscow show trials

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Koestler's open-systems framework is relevant to cultivation in several ways:

1. **Open-system design**: Living cultivation systems are open systems that exchange energy and materials with their environment. Understanding this prevents the error of treating growing systems as closed, controllable machines.
2. **Self-organization**: The concept that living systems build up complexity rather than running down (as mechanical systems do) is key to designing cultivation systems that improve over time rather than degrading.
3. **Holonic structure**: Each component in a cultivation system (a fungal mycelial network, a compost pile, a garden bed) is both a whole system in itself and a part of a larger system. Design must address both levels simultaneously.
4. **Complexity and information**: Koestler's emphasis on living systems building "more complex patterns of information" parallels the way mycelial networks process and distribute information about their environment.
