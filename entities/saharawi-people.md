# Saharawi People

## Description

The Saharawi (also Sahrawi) people are the Indigenous inhabitants of the Western Sahara region of North Africa. Mollison references the Saharawi specifically as refugees in Algeria who developed two methods of heroic soil rehabilitation under irrigation that successfully leach salt from severely degraded soils. Their techniques demonstrate that even the most hostile, salt-contaminated land can be made productive through appropriate earthworks and soil replacement strategies.

## Classification

- **Type:** Indigenous people / cultural group
- **Region:** Western Sahara, refugee camps in southwestern Algeria
- **Language:** Hassaniya Arabic
- **Population:** Approximately 500,000-600,000

## Key Facts

- Indigenous to the Western Sahara, a former Spanish colony disputed between Morocco and the Polisario Front
- Since 1975, large populations have lived in refugee camps near Tindouf, Algeria
- The camps are located in the Hamada, one of the harshest desert environments on Earth
- Temperatures regularly exceed 50C in summer; rainfall is negligible
- Despite extreme conditions, Saharawi refugees have developed sophisticated garden and soil rehabilitation techniques
- Mollison cites their methods from International Agricultural Development (Jan-Feb 1987)

## Soil Rehabilitation Methods

The Saharawi developed two distinct approaches to reclaiming salt-contaminated desert soil:

### Method 1 (Shingle Available)

- Salt soil is excavated and removed to a depth of 1-2 meters
- A layer of shingle or pebbles is laid down at the base as drainage
- Non-salted soil is carted in and placed above the shingle layer
- The shingle layer facilitates salt leaching as irrigation water passes through
- Beds are periodically flushed with low-salt water to maintain leaching

### Method 2 (No Stones Available)

- Topsoil is removed if clayey, to a depth of 20 cm
- Salt-free sand is brought in as a replacement surface
- Deep drainage canals are cut to carry away surplus salt with irrigation water
- Continuous flushing through the canal system gradually reduces soil salinity

## Significance in Permaculture

Mollison presents the Saharawi methods as proof that total soil rehabilitation is possible even under the most extreme desert conditions:

- Demonstrates that human ingenuity can overcome seemingly impossible soil problems
- Shows the value of appropriate earthworks (drainage, soil replacement) in [[gypsum]] application, [[bentonite]] amendment, raised beds, and mulching
- Validates the [[soil-rehabilitation-methods]] described by Mollison represent the most intensive intervention on the spectrum of desert soil treatments. Where gypsum, bentonite, raised beds, and mulch represent lighter approaches, the Saharawi methods involve total soil reconstruction. Their success validates the fundamental [[permaculture]] premise that no soil is beyond rehabilitation if appropriate techniques are applied. For practitioners working in severely salted or degraded arid environments, the Saharawi methods provide a proven template for heroic soil restoration.

## Related Entities

- Gypsum - lighter soil amendment for clay penetration in salted soils
- Bentonite - volcanic clay used to improve water retention in sandy desert soils
- Swale - water harvesting earthwork that also aids salt leaching
- [[corridor-farming]] - broader desert agriculture strategy

## Broader Applications of Saharawi Methods

The principles demonstrated by the Saharawi can be adapted to other severely degraded sites:

- Mine tailings reclamation: similar soil replacement on contaminated substrates
- Coastal salt-affected land rehabilitation following tsunami or storm surge inundation
- Urban brownfield sites where industrial contamination requires total soil reconstruction
- Agricultural land degraded by decades of improper irrigation and salinization
- The key insight is that no soil condition is beyond rehabilitation if drainage and replacement are feasible

## Lessons for Permaculture Designers

Mollison draws several design principles from the Saharawi example:

- Always assess the full spectrum of soil treatment options before abandoning a site
- Drainage is the foundation of salt management; without it, surface treatments will fail
- Soil importation may be more cost-effective than decades of failed conventional amendments
- Community-scale mobilization can achieve earthworks that individuals cannot
- The most constrained contexts often produce the most innovative solutions
