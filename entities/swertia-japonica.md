## Overview

Swertia japonica, known in Japan as "senburi" (千振), is an annual or biennial herb in the family Gentianaceae. It is one of the most important medicinal plants in traditional Japanese Kampo medicine.

The species produces erect stems bearing clusters of small, white to pale pink flowers with distinctive purple nectar guides. It is native to Japan, Korea, and parts of eastern China.

Swertia japonica is the primary commercial source of swertiamarin and amarogentin, two intensely bitter secoiridoid glycosides. These compounds are used in pharmaceutical bitter tonics and digestive aids.

The Japanese name "senburi" translates roughly as "thousand times extraction," referring to the herb's extreme bitterness that persists through many infusions.

Swertia japonica has been used in Japanese medicine for over 1,000 years. It appears in the earliest Kampo pharmacopeias as a remedy for [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]] of the Old World.

Plants are erect annuals or biennials, 20–60 cm tall. The root system consists of a slender taproot, yellowish-white when fresh, with a characteristically intense bitter taste.

Stems are solitary or sparingly branched from the base, quadrangular, and glabrous. Leaves are opposite, lanceolate to ovate-lanceolate, 2–5 cm long.

Basal leaves form a rosette that withers early in [[berry-growing]]. These introductions likely originated from medicinal garden escapes.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

Swertia japonica contains a complex mixture of secoiridoid glycosides, xanthones, and triterpenoids. The primary bitter compounds are swertiamarin, amarogentin, and gentiopicroside.

Swertiamarin is the major constituent, comprising 1–3 percent of dry weight in aerial parts. Amarogentin is present in smaller quantities but is far more bitter, detectable at dilutions of 1:58,000.

Xanthones including bellidifolin and methylbellidifolin contribute anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective activities. These compounds are concentrated in the leaves and stems.

Pharmacological studies demonstrate cholagogue, hepatoprotective, and gastroprotective effects. The bitter principles stimulate gastric acid secretion and bile flow.

Traditional preparations include decoctions, powders, and tinctures. Standardized extracts are available commercially in Japanese pharmacies.

Animal studies show protective effects against carbon tetrachloride-induced liver damage. The hepatoprotective mechanism involves antioxidant and anti-inflammatory pathways.

The bitter threshold of amarogentin makes it one of the most bitter naturally occurring compounds known. This property is exploited in bitterness assays for pharmaceutical quality control.

## Cultivation

Swertia japonica is cultivated commercially in Japan, particularly in Hokkaido and Nagano Prefecture. Cultivation has increased as wild populations have declined.

Seeds require light for germination and should be surface-sown in spring. Germination rates are moderate, typically 50–70 percent under optimal conditions.

Plants prefer cool, moist conditions with well-drained, humus-rich soil. Partial shade improves growth in warmer regions.

The crop is harvested in autumn at the end of the flowering period. Whole plants are dried and processed for medicinal use.

Yield averages 200–400 kg of dried herb per hectare under good management. Quality is assessed by swertiamarin content, which varies with [[mushroom-desiccant-drying-methods]].
