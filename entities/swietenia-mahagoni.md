West Indian mahogany was the first New World timber species to be commercially exploited by European colonists, beginning in the early 16th century. The species became the benchmark for fine cabinetry and shipbuilding wood, giving rise to the term "mahogany" as a generic descriptor for reddish-brown tropical hardwoods.

The species is listed as **Endangered** on the IUCN Red List due to centuries of overexploitation and habitat loss. It is protected under CITES Appendix II, which regulates international trade.

Mature trees typically reach 20–30 meters in height with trunk diameters of 60–100 cm, though historical records describe specimens exceeding 35 meters. The crown is dense, rounded, and symmetrical, making the species popular as an ornamental shade tree.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Optimal conditions include annual rainfall of 1,000–2,500 mm, mean annual temperatures of 24–30°C, and well-drained soils. The species is notably drought-tolerant and salt-spray tolerant, allowing it to thrive in coastal environments.

Natural populations have been severely depleted throughout most of the native range. Remaining significant stands are found primarily in protected areas in Cuba and on isolated Caribbean islands.

Swietenia mahagoni has been cultivated for timber since the colonial era, with extensive plantations established across the tropics. However, the shoot borer moth (*Hypsipyla grandella*) causes significant damage to young trees in plantations, limiting commercial viability.

## Ecology

Swietenia mahagoni contributes to tropical forest canopy structure as a large-emergent to canopy tree. Its dense crown provides significant shade and microhabitat diversity for understory organisms.

The winged seeds are adapted for wind dispersal, capable of traveling considerable distances from parent trees. Seed dispersal patterns facilitate colonization of disturbed areas and forest gaps.

As a native canopy tree in Caribbean dry forests, the species provides critical habitat for birds, including parrots, pigeons, and migratory warblers. The large crown supports epiphytic communities including bromeliads and orchids.

In its introduced range, S. mahagoni can form dense monospecific stands that suppress native vegetation. The species is considered invasive in parts of Fiji, the Philippines, and northeastern Australia.

The shoot borer moth (*Hypsipyla grandella*) is the primary natural herbivore and represents the most significant biological constraint on the species' silviculture. The borer tunnels into terminal shoots, causing multiple forked leaders and deformed growth.

Management of shoot borer damage in plantations remains one of the most persistent challenges in tropical forestry research. Mixed-species plantings with non-host companion species have shown the most promising results, reducing borer incidence by 40–60%.
