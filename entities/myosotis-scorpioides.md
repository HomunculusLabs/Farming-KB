## Overview

Myosotis scorpioides, commonly known as true forget-me-not or water forget-me-not, is a rhizomatous perennial herb in the family Boraginaceae. It is native to Europe and western Asia but has become widely naturalized in North America and other temperate regions.

The plant typically grows 15–50 cm tall, forming loose mats along stream margins, pond edges, and other wet habitats. Its small, bright blue flowers with yellow eyes are among the most recognizable wildflowers of European waterways.

True forget-me-not is valued as an ornamental marginal aquatic plant and provides important habitat for riparian invertebrates. Its common name derives from the German legend "Vergissmeinnicht," associated with medieval courtly love.

It differs from the garden forget-me-not (Myosotis sylvatica) in its preference for wet habitats, longer flowering period, and more spreading growth habit.

The specific epithet "scorpioides" refers to the scorpion-tail curve of the developing flower cyme, a characteristic inflorescence form in the Boraginaceae family.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Clade: Tracheophytes
- Clade: Angiosperms
- Clade: Eudicots
- Clade: Asterids
- Order: Boraginales
- Family: Boraginaceae
- Genus: Myosotis
- Species: M. scorpioides

Stems are decumbent to ascending, rooting at the lower nodes where they contact moist soil. Leaves are oblong-lanceolate, 2–6 cm long, sparsely hairy, and sessile or with short petioles.

Flowers are 5–10 mm in diameter with five sky-blue corolla lobes surrounding a yellow or white central eye (the corona). They are arranged in scorpioid cymes that uncurl as they mature, a feature shared with many Boraginaceae.

Each flower produces five stamens that are attached to the corolla tube. The style is short and the stigma is capitate. Flowers open sequentially along the cyme, with 2–4 flowers open at any given time.

The fruit consists of four small, shiny nutlets (1.0–1.5 mm) that are dark brown to black at maturity. Nutlets are dispersed by water, animals, and occasionally ants.

The root system consists of fibrous roots arising from a short rhizome and from stem nodes in contact with soil or water.

Plants growing in shallow standing water develop more elongated stems and slightly broader leaves than terrestrial forms, displaying modest phenotypic plasticity in response to submersion.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to Europe from the British Isles and Scandinavia eastward to the Caucasus and northern Turkey. Also native to parts of North Africa (Algeria, Morocco). In its native range, it is widespread and locally abundant.

Introduced to North America by the 19th century, now naturalized across much of the northeastern United States, eastern Canada, the Pacific Northwest, and scattered western localities. Listed as invasive in some wetland habitats.

The earliest North American records date from the 1880s in New England. It likely arrived as a garden plant that escaped cultivation and spread via waterways.

The plant grows in shallow water (to 10 cm depth), along stream and pond margins, in marshes, ditches, and damp meadows. It prefers circumneutral to slightly acidic soils (pH 5.5–7.5) and full sun to light shade.

Hardiness zones 4–9. It tolerates seasonal flooding and brief periods of drought, though persistent dry conditions cause dieback.

In the southern parts of its introduced North American range (Virginia, North Carolina), populations are more localized and tend to occur at higher elevations or in shaded riparian corridors where moisture is reliable year-round.

## Cultivation and Uses

**Ornamental use:** Widely planted as a marginal aquatic in water gardens, pond edges, and stream-side plantings. The bright blue flowers provide color from May through September.

**Propagation:** By division of established clumps in spring or autumn. Also from seed sown on moist soil surface in spring. Germination is typically good without stratification.

Self-seeding occurs in favorable conditions, though seedlings may not come true to type if other Myosotis species are nearby. Vegetative spread via stem rooting is the primary natural colonization mechanism.

**Wildlife gardens:** The extended flowering period makes it valuable for pollinator gardens near water features. Flowers attract small bees, hoverflies, and butterflies.

**Aquarium and terrarium use:** Submerged growth forms are occasionally sold for coldwater aquariums. The terrestrial form is more commonly cultivated.

The plant requires consistently moist to wet soil. It tolerates shallow standing water and is an excellent choice for bog gardens and rain gardens.

No serious pests or diseases are commonly reported. Powdery mildew may affect plants in overly dry conditions. Slug damage is minimal due to the hairy foliage.

## Ecological Role

In its native range, Myosotis scorpioides contributes to riparian zone biodiversity, providing nectar and pollen for a range of insects. The dense mats offer shelter for amphibians and aquatic invertebrates along pond margins.

In North America, where it is naturalized, it can form dense monocultures that displace native wetland flora. Control measures include manual removal and shading with taller native species.

Despite its invasive potential in North America, true forget-me-not does not appear on most state noxious weed lists. Its impacts are considered moderate compared to aggressive wetland invaders like purple loosestrife (Lythrum salicaria).

The species hybridizes readily with other Myosotis taxa where ranges overlap, complicating identification in the field. Hybrids may show intermediate flower color and leaf morphology.

As a marginal aquatic, it plays a role in bank stabilization. The rooting stem nodes bind soil particles and reduce erosion along stream edges.

## Chemistry and Traditional Use

Myosotis scorpioides contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids at low concentrations, a chemical trait shared across the Boraginaceae. The specific PA profile includes lycopsamine and intermedine, though at lower levels than in Symphytum or Cynoglossum species.

In European folk medicine, the plant was occasionally used as a mild astringent and wound-healing herb. Poultices of crushed leaves were applied to minor cuts and insect bites. These uses are largely historical and not supported by modern pharmacological study.

The flowers contain anthocyanin pigments responsible for the blue coloration. The pH-dependent color change from pink (in bud) to blue (at anthesis) is driven by vacuolar pH shifts during flower development.

No significant modern pharmacological or clinical studies have been conducted on this species. It is not used in contemporary herbal medicine.

The genus name Myosotis derives from the Greek "mouse ear," referring to the shape of the leaves. This etymology is shared across the genus and applies to all 50+ Myosotis species.
