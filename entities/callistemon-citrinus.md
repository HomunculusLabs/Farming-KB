## Overview

**Callistemon citrinus**, commonly known as the **crimson bottlebrush**, is an evergreen shrub or small tree in the family Myrtaceae, native to eastern Australia. It is one of the most widely cultivated Australian native plants, grown for its striking red bottlebrush flower spikes.

The species typically grows 2–5 meters in height as a shrub, occasionally reaching 8–10 meters as a small tree. Its dense, bushy habit and showy flowers make it a popular garden subject in temperate [[query-how-to-grow-rare-tropical-fruits-in-marginal-and-subtropical-climates]] worldwide.

In cultivation, compact forms are widely available, with dwarf cultivars reaching only 0.5–1.0 meters. These smaller selections are popular for [[cervantes-container-gardening-pot-size]] and urban landscaping where space is limited.

C. citrinus produces [[permaculture-designers-manual-essential-oil-plants]] rich in 1,8-cineole and citral, which has commercial value in perfumery and traditional medicine. The species is also important as a nectar source for birds and insects.

The genus was introduced to European horticulture in the late 18th century and has been in continuous cultivation since. Its adaptability to a range of climates has made it one of the most recognizable Australian native plants internationally.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Callistemon citrinus** was described by William Jackson Hooker in Curtis's Botanical Magazine in 1828. The genus name derives from Greek *kallistos* (most beautiful) and *stemon* (stamen), referring to the prominent stamens.

Recent phylogenetic work has proposed merging Callistemon into Melaleuca, as the two genera are not monophyletic with respect to each other. However, the name Callistemon remains widely used in horticulture.

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Myrtales
- **Family:** Myrtaceae
- **Tribe:** Myrteae
- **Genus:** Callistemon
- **Species:** C. citrinus

Leaves are lanceolate to narrowly elliptic, 3–7 cm long and 5–15 mm wide, with a prominent midvein. They are stiff, leathery, and aromatic when crushed, releasing a lemon-scented odor from which the specific epithet derives.

New growth is often coppery-pink or bronze-tinted, providing ornamental interest even when the plant is not in flower. Mature leaves are dark green on the adaxial surface and paler beneath.

The characteristic inflorescence is a dense cylindrical spike 5–12 cm long and 3–5 cm in diameter, composed of numerous flowers with long, bright red filaments. The combined effect resembles a traditional bottle brush.

Each individual flower is small, with a minute calyx and no visible petals — the showy display is composed entirely of the extended stamens. The anthers are dark-colored, contrasting with the red filaments.

Fruit is a small, woody, cup-shaped capsule 5–7 mm in diameter, clustered along the branches. Capsules persist for several years and release fine seeds upon fire-induced or natural dehiscence.

The persistent woody capsules give branches a characteristic bumpy appearance. In the wild, fire stimulates mass seed release and subsequent germination from the soil [[query-how-do-i-create-a-seed-bank-and-start-seed-saving]].

## Distribution and Habitat

C. citrinus is native to the eastern coast of Australia, from southeastern Queensland through eastern New South Wales to the border region with Victoria. Its range extends approximately 100 km inland from the coast.

The species grows naturally along creek banks, swamp margins, and in moist gullies within eucalypt forest and woodland. It is commonly found in sandy or gravelly alluvial soils along watercourses.

It also occurs on roadside verges and in disturbed areas where adequate moisture is available. The species is a component of riparian scrub communities alongside Casuarina and Melaleuca species.

Climate in its native range is warm temperate to subtropical, with annual rainfall of 600–1,500 mm distributed relatively evenly throughout the year. It tolerates light frosts but is damaged by temperatures below -5°C.

The species has naturalized in parts of southern Africa, New Zealand, and the western [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]]. In some areas it has become weedy along waterways, though it is less aggressively invasive than some Myrtaceae relatives.

## Cultivation and Horticulture

C. citrinus is one of the most widely grown Australian native ornamentals, cultivated in gardens across temperate, Mediterranean, and subtropical regions. Numerous cultivars have been selected for flower color, size, and [[coleman-hardy-winter-vegetables-cold-tolerance-mechanisms]].

Propagation is primarily from seed, which germinates readily within 2–4 weeks without pretreatment. Named cultivars are propagated vegetatively by semi-hardwood cuttings to maintain flowering characteristics.

Cuttings taken in late summer from semi-hardened [[gadd-hyphal-tip-growth-and-branching-mechanisms]] root readily under mist. Grafting onto Melaleuca rootstock is practiced for cultivars with poor root systems in heavy soils.

The species adapts to a wide range of soils, from sandy loams to heavy clays, provided drainage is adequate. It tolerates periodic waterlogging and moderate drought once established, making it a versatile landscape plant.

Pruning after flowering promotes bushy regrowth and maintains a compact form. Neglected specimens can become leggy and sparse, but respond well to hard pruning into older wood.

The species is moderately long-lived, with cultivated specimens surviving 20–40 years. It is relatively pest-free, though scale insects, psyllids, and myrtle rust (Austropuccinia psidii) can affect plant health.

Well-known cultivars include 'Burgundy' (deep red flowers), 'Splendens' (large flower spikes), and 'Endeavour' (compact, bright red). These selections dominate the nursery trade in Australia and are widely exported.

## Ethnobotany and Ecology

Aboriginal Australians used C. citrinus bark and leaves for medicinal purposes. Infusions of the aromatic leaves were used to treat respiratory complaints and skin infections, leveraging the [[medicinal-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties]] of the essential oil.

The leaves produce 0.5–1.0% essential oil by [[comparison-steam-distillation-vs-co2-extraction-for-essential-oils]]. The oil is rich in 1,8-cineole (30–45%) and citral (10–20%), giving it both antiseptic and lemon-fragrance properties.

C. citrinus is a magnet for nectar-feeding birds, particularly honeyeaters (Meliphagidae) in its native range. The flowers also attract butterflies, [[query-how-to-attract-native-bees-and-beneficial-insects]], and European honeybees, making it a valuable pollinator-supporting garden plant.
