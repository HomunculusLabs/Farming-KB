## Pirozynski and Malloch Hypothesis (1975)

## Description

The Pirozynski and Malloch hypothesis, published in 1975, proposes that land plants only evolved in conjunction with a mycorrhizal fungal partner. Based on their reinterpretation of the fungal structures documented by [[kidston-and-lang]] (1921) in the fossil plants [[rhynia]] and Asteroxylon from the Rhynie Chert, they argued that these ancient plant-fungal associations were primitive mycorrhizae. This hypothesis suggests that the [[mycorrhizal-symbiosis]] was not a later evolutionary development but a fundamental prerequisite for the colonization of land by plants.

## Classification

- **Concept type**: Evolutionary hypothesis
- **Authors**: K. A. Pirozynski and D. W. Malloch
- **Publication**: "The origin of land plants: A matter of mycotropism." Biosystems 6:153–164 (1975)
- **Subject**: Evolutionary biology, paleomycology, plant-fungal coevolution
- **Status**: Influential but debated

## Key Facts

- Pirozynski and Malloch (1975) reinterpreted fungal structures in Rhynie Chert fossils as evidence of mycorrhizal associations in the earliest land plants.
- Their hypothesis states that the evolution of land plants was dependent on (or greatly facilitated by) a symbiotic fungal partner that provided access to soil nutrients.
- The hypothesis was built on the observation that mycorrhizal associations are nearly universal among modern land plants, suggesting an ancient and fundamental partnership.
- They proposed that the fungal partner acted as an extension of the primitive plant root system, absorbing water and mineral nutrients from the substrate.
- Dighton (2003) notes that the detail and descriptions in the original Kidston and Lang (1921) publication leave significant doubt about the actual function of the fungal associations seen in the fossils.
- Alternative interpretations include: the fungi were pathogens, non-mycorrhizal endophytes, or saprotrophs colonizing already-dead plant material.
- The hypothesis remains influential in shaping how scientists think about the evolution of mycorrhizal symbiosis, even as the debate about the specific Rhynie Chert evidence continues.
- Modern molecular evidence supports the ancient origin of mycorrhizal associations, lending indirect support to the general principle of the hypothesis.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- The hypothesis underscores that mycorrhizal associations are not optional enhancements but ancient, fundamental partnerships that plants have relied on for over 400 million years.
- This evolutionary perspective supports the practice of [[mycorrhizal-inoculation-guide]] in cultivation: if plants evolved with fungal partners, they may not perform optimally without them.
- The hypothesis explains why most crop plants form mycorrhizal associations and why suppressing these relationships through excessive fertilization or fungicide use can reduce plant health.
- Understanding the evolutionary depth of plant-fungal partnerships motivates sustainable cultivation practices that preserve and enhance mycorrhizal communities.
- The debate about the original Rhynie Chert fungi highlights that fungal ecological roles (mutualist, pathogen, saprotroph) are context-dependent — a principle directly applicable to cultivation management.
- The hypothesis is a foundational concept for anyone studying mycorrhizal biology, [[fungal-ecology]], or sustainable agriculture.
