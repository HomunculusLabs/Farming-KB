## Overview

Gladiolus tristis, commonly called the marsh butterfly lily or ever-flowering gladiolus, is a cormous perennial in the Iridaceae family. Native to the winter-rainfall regions of the Western Cape of South Africa, it is one of the most fragrant species in the genus.

The species is unusual among gladioli for its nocturnal fragrance, which intensifies at dusk and persists through the night. The spicy-sweet scent, described as resembling cloves or carnations, attracts night-flying moth pollinators.

Plants reach 40–80 cm in height with slender, erect stems bearing two to four funnel-shaped flowers. Flower color ranges from pale yellow-green to creamy white, often with darker markings on the lower tepals.

Gladiolus tristis has naturalized in parts of California, Australia, and the Mediterranean region. It is valued by gardeners for its fragrance and adaptability to Mediterranean-climate gardens.

The species epithet "tristis" means "sad" or "dull," likely referring to the subdued flower color compared to showier Gladiolus species. Despite the name, the plant is prized for its unusual evening fragrance.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Angiosperms — Monocots — Asparagales
- **Family:** Iridaceae
- **Genus:** Gladiolus
- **Species:** Gladiolus tristis L.
- **Synonyms:** Gladiolus tristis var. concolor, Gladiolus tristis var. ovatus

The corm is globose, approximately 1.5–2 cm in diameter, covered with brown fibrous tunics. Corms produce offsets freely, forming small colonies over time.

Leaves are narrow, linear, and usually three to four in number, reaching 20–40 cm long. They are gray-green with a prominent midrib and arise from the base of the plant.

Flowers are 3–4 cm long, funnel-shaped, with six tepals that are subequal in length. The lower three tepals often bear a diffuse yellow or greenish-yellow marking.

Flowering occurs in late winter to early spring (August–October in the Southern Hemisphere). Each inflorescence produces two to four flowers that open sequentially over two to three weeks.

Chromosome number is 2n = 30, consistent with most species in Gladiolus section Ophiolychnus. The species crosses readily with close relatives in this section.

## Distribution and Habitat

Gladiolus tristis is endemic to the Western Cape Province of South Africa. Its range extends from the Cape Peninsula eastward to Caledon and Bredasdorp in the Overberg region.

The species grows in seasonally damp habitats including marshy flats, seeps, stream margins, and winter-wet depressions. It thrives in areas that flood during the winter rainy season and dry out completely in summer.

Soils are typically acidic, sandy loams derived from Table Mountain Sandstone or granite-derived clays. Elevational range extends from near sea level to approximately 600 meters.

The winter-rainfall Mediterranean climate of the Western Cape provides ideal growing conditions. Plants are adapted to mild, wet winters and hot, dry summers with complete summer dormancy.

Naturalized populations exist in coastal California, southwestern Australia, and parts of the Mediterranean basin. These introductions likely resulted from ornamental plantings that escaped cultivation.

In the fynbos biome, Gladiolus tristis occurs in renosterveld vegetation types. This critically endangered habitat has been reduced to less than 5% of its original extent through agricultural conversion.

## Cultivation

Gladiolus tristis requires a Mediterranean-climate growing cycle with winter moisture and summer dryness. Plant corms in autumn, 5–8 cm deep, in well-drained soil enriched with compost.

Full sun to light shade produces the best flowering. In hot inland climates, afternoon shade extends the bloom period and prevents flower scorching.

Water moderately during the winter growing season, allowing the soil to dry between waterings. Cease all irrigation after foliage yellows in late spring to enforce summer dormancy.

Container culture works well using a free-draining mix of sand, loam, and compost. Pots should be kept dry during summer dormancy and moved to a sheltered location to prevent corm rot.

Fertilize lightly with a low-nitrogen formula in early winter as growth resumes. Excessive nitrogen produces lush foliage at the expense of flowers.

Corms can be left in the ground year-round in zones 8–10. In colder regions, lift corms after foliage dies back and store in a cool, dry location until autumn replanting.

The species is relatively pest-free compared to large-flowered hybrid gladioli. Thrips and spider mites occasionally affect foliage but rarely cause serious damage to healthy plants.

Propagation by corm offsets is straightforward and produces flowering plants within one to two years. Seed propagation requires patience but maintains greater genetic diversity in collections.

## Conservation

Gladiolus tristis is not currently listed as threatened, but its renosterveld habitat is critically endangered. Less than 5% of original renosterveld vegetation remains in the Western Cape.

Urban expansion around Cape Town continues to pressure remaining wild populations. Several documented populations have been lost to housing development in the Cape Flats area.

The species benefits from occurring in some protected areas including Table Mountain National Park. However, many populations exist on private land with no formal protection status.

Ex situ conservation through bulb collections and seed banks provides an important safety net. Kirstenbosch National Botanical Garden maintains representative accessions of Western Cape Gladiolus species.

## Ecology

Nocturnal fragrance production is an adaptation to moth pollination (sphingophily). The pale flower color and intense evening scent specifically attract hawkmoths (Sphingidae) and owlet moths (Noctuidae).

The species is a component of the Cape Floristic Region's remarkable bulb diversity. This biodiversity hotspot contains over 1,500 geophyte species, many of them restricted to specialized habitats.

Seed capsules are three-valved, containing numerous winged seeds adapted for wind dispersal. Germination occurs in autumn with the onset of winter rains, producing flowering-size corms in two to three years.

Corm dormancy is an adaptation to the Western Cape's severe summer drought. The fibrous tunic protects the dormant corm from desiccation and soil-borne pathogens during the dry season.

Gladiolus tristis has been used in hybridization programs to introduce fragrance into garden gladioli. The species crosses readily with other Section Ophiolychnus members, producing fragrant offspring with varied flower colors.

The species is occasionally confused with Gladiolus carneus, which shares similar pale flower coloration. However, Gladiolus carneus lacks the intense nocturnal fragrance and has broader, more overlapping tepals.

Unlike many Western Cape bulbs, Gladiolus tristis tolerates slightly alkaline soils. This adaptability contributes to its success as a naturalized species in limestone regions of the Mediterranean.
