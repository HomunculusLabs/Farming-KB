## Description

Zizania lacustris (commonly known as wild rice or northern wild rice) is a tall annual aquatic grass growing to 2.5 m in height. It is one of the most recently domesticated grain crops, having been brought into cultivation only within the last 30 years (as of Mollison's writing in the 1980s). Several perennial species exist within the same genus. The grain has been gathered for centuries by Amerindian tribes of Canada and the United States, from Minnesota to Florida, and remains both a wild-harvested and commercially cultivated crop.

The plant is uniquely suited to cold swamps and shallow waters that experience intense summer heat alongside freezing winters. This combination of climatic extremes is essential for the grain's development and the seed stratification process. Wild rice grows in flooded fields at 16-20 cm water depth and produces tall, elegant seed heads that ripen gradually over a period of 3-4 weeks.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Poaceae (grass family)
- **Genus**: Zizania
- **Species**: Z. lacustris
- **Common names**: Wild rice, northern wild rice, Canadian wild rice, Indian rice
- **Growth habit**: Tall annual aquatic grass, up to 2.5 m
- **Related species**: Zizania palustris, Zizania aquatica, Zizania latifolia
- **Native range**: Northern North America, Great Lakes region, Minnesota to Florida

## Key Facts

### Cultivation Requirements
- Suited to cold swamps and shallows with intense summer heat
- Requires cold winters and hot summers for optimal production
- Grows in flooded fields at 16-20 cm water depth
- Fields dried out 2-3 weeks before machine harvesting
- Seed stratified over winter in cold water (bags under icy lake water or at 4C in refrigerators)
- Sown in spring, harvested from mid-autumn
- Ideal spacing: 20 x 35 cm
- Home plot yields: 200-500 g per head with selected stock
- Fields must be rested or sown to another crop when introducing new cultivars

### Production Statistics
- Approximately 3 million kg total production (mostly Minnesota region)
- About 50% still wild-gathered by Amerindians using traditional canoe harvesting
- Commercial yields are low: 50-100 kg/ha by machine
- Prices historically high: approximately $10/kg (1984 figures)
- Fields of 100 hectares can yield up to $100,000 annually in rice alone
- Low machine yields (less than 10%) due to gradual seed ripening over 3-4 weeks
- Home garden plots: 200-800 plants sufficient for family needs
- Mechanical harvesting equipment costs $300,000 or more

### Ecological Associates and Polyculture
- Wild ducks harvest approximately 70% of total production from flyway fields
- Self-generated polyculture includes crayfish (Homarus), mink, beaver, and thousands of waterfowl
- Coyote range the bunds; packrats store significant quantities of seed
- Duck potato (Sagittaria) can become a competitive weed but is also valuable waterfowl food
- Potential for coldwater fish-rearing (trout, bullheads) in canals and ditches, not yet developed
- Azolla manuring is not traditionally practiced but has potential

### Traditional and Innovative Harvesting
- Amerindian method: beating grain into canoes over a 3-4 week harvest season
- Seed bundling strategy: 10-20 heads tied together to prevent predation and shattering
- Periodic untying for threshing into canoes
- Packrats may be the most efficient harvesters: they store large quantities of clean grain in artificial shelters insulated with Typha (cattail) seed silk
- Bill Mackently uses grey squirrels to collect hickory nuts and acorns using a similar strategy, leaving them about 15% of the harvest

### Supplementary Products
- Duck fattening and crayfish harvest from the canal systems
- Mussel meats and fish production as accessory yields
- Vine crops of hops or silverberry (Actinidia arguta) on trellises
- Restricted fur production (mink from wild rice fields)
- Special forestry and recreational fishing or tour potential

## Relevance to Cultivation

Zizania lacustris represents one of the most recently domesticated grain crops and is uniquely suited to cold-climate aquaculture systems. Its integration into permaculture designs offers multiple stacked yields: nutritious grain, waterfowl habitat, crayfish, mink, and potential fish production. The traditional Amerindian approach of sequential harvesting over 3-4 weeks is far more productive per unit area than mechanical single-pass harvesting, making small-scale home plots surprisingly efficient and economically viable.

The wild rice system demonstrates how a single crop naturally generates a rich polyculture. For permaculture designers, wild rice culture provides a model for cold-climate water-based food production with integrated livestock (fish, crayfish, waterfowl) and supplementary crops. The chinampa configuration is well-suited to wild rice, combining canal culture with raised-bed production. The system described by pioneer growers Hubert and Leonard Jacobson of Aitken, Minnesota shows how complex multi-yield systems develop even from initially simple monoculture plantings.

For wildlife refuges, there is no better autumn fodder for waterfowl. A small terrace or pond of wild rice should be the aim of every home gardener who can obtain seed and who enjoys a nutritious grain.
