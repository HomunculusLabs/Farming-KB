## Overview

Anemone hepatica (syn. Hepatica nobilis), commonly known as hepatica or liverleaf, is a low-growing perennial in the family Ranunculaceae. It is one of the earliest spring wildflowers in temperate deciduous woodlands.

The species is native to Europe, Asia, and [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]]. It typically grows 5–15 cm tall and forms slowly spreading clumps over many years.

The common name "liverleaf" refers to the three-lobed leaves, which resemble the shape of a human liver. In the medieval Doctrine of Signatures, this resemblance suggested the plant could treat liver ailments.

This folk belief persisted into the 18th century despite a lack of pharmacological evidence. The genus name Hepatica itself is derived from the Latin *hepaticus* (of the liver).

The flowers appear in early spring, often before the new foliage has fully expanded. Bloom color ranges from deep blue-violet through pink and white.

In Japan, Anemone hepatica is celebrated as one of the first signs of spring. It is known as "Mimizuku-so" (owl flower) due to the furry flower buds.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Eudicots
- **Order:** Ranunculales
- **Family:** Ranunculaceae
- **Genus:** Anemone
- **Species:** Anemone hepatica L.

The basionym is Hepatica hepatica (L.) Stell. The genus Hepatica was historically maintained as separate from Anemone but molecular studies support its inclusion within Anemone.

In North America, the species is treated as Anemone americana (Hepatica americana) or Anemone acutiloba (Hepatica acutiloba) depending on leaf shape. European plants are typically rounded-lobed.

The chromosome number is 2n = 14. The genus Anemone in the broad sense contains approximately 150 species worldwide.

## Morphology

Anemone hepatica is a stemless perennial growing from a short, vertical rhizome. The rhizome produces new roots annually and slowly branches with age.

Leaves are basal, long-petioled, and palmately 3-lobed, 3–8 cm across. They are leathery in texture, dark green above, and often purplish beneath.

The leaves are evergreen, persisting through winter and senescing only after the new leaves expand in spring. This winter-green habit allows photosynthesis during mild spells.

Flowers are solitary, 2–4 cm in diameter, with 6–10 petal-like sepals (true petals are absent). Beneath each flower are three small, green, ovate involucral bracts.

Flowering occurs from February to May depending on latitude and elevation. Individual flowers last 5–10 days and are followed by small, hairy achenes.

The achenes are 3–4 mm long with a short, curved style that persists as a beak. Each flower produces 10–30 achenes arranged in a globose head.

The plant typically forms clumps 15–25 cm in diameter over 10–20 years. Growth is slow but very long-lived, with individual plants persisting for decades in stable woodland sites.

## Distribution and Habitat

Anemone hepatica occurs across Europe from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean and eastward through Russia to Siberia, China, and Japan. In North America, related taxa occupy eastern deciduous forests from Nova Scotia to Minnesota.

The species grows in deciduous and mixed woodlands, particularly on calcareous soils. It prefers dappled shade, leaf litter, and moist, well-drained humus-rich substrates.

It is locally abundant in beech and oak-hornbeam forests of central Europe. In northern Europe it is more scattered and confined to favorably warm microsites.

Populations on acid soils are smaller and less vigorous than those on limestone. The species benefits from the calcium-rich environments provided by woodland over chalk.

The species is classified as Least Concern globally. However, it is protected in several European countries where wild collection for gardens has depleted local populations.

## Ecology

The [[equatorial-photoperiod-early-flowering]] strategy allows Anemone hepatica to capture sunlight before the tree canopy closes. It stores energy in its rhizome during the brief spring growing period.

Flowers are pollinated by early-emerging insects, particularly bees and flies. The flowers close at night and during cold weather, protecting pollen from moisture loss.

Seeds bear an elaiosome and are dispersed by ants through myrmecochory. This dispersal mechanism is common among woodland spring ephemerals.

The leaves contain ranunculin and protoanemonin, making them toxic to most herbivores. The leathery texture also provides physical defense against slugs and other invertebrate grazers.

Anemone hepatica forms [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] that enhance [[aact-microbial-foliar-nutrient-uptake-co2-stomata-ingham]] from the nutrient-poor leaf litter layer. These fungal partnerships are critical for seedling establishment.
