## Overview

**Alpinia officinarum** Hance, commonly known as **lesser galangal**, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Zingiberaceae. Native to southern China and Southeast Asia, it has been used for centuries in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), Ayurveda, and Southeast Asian culinary traditions.

The dried rhizome is the primary plant part used medicinally and as a spice. It contains a rich profile of bioactive flavonoids, diarylheptanoids, and volatile oils responsible for its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and gastrointestinal activities.

Lesser galangal should not be confused with greater galangal (*[[alpinia-galanga]]*), which is a larger, more pungent species with overlapping but distinct uses.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Order Zingiberales → Family Zingiberaceae → Subfamily Alpinioideae → Genus *Alpinia* → Species *A. officinarum*.

**Synonyms:** *Languas officinarum* (Hance) Farw., *Alpinia chinensis* Roscoe (nom. illeg.), *Amomum galanga* L. (misapplied).

**Rhizome:** Horizontal, knobby, cylindrical, 1–2 cm in diameter. Externally reddish-brown with prominent ring-like nodes. Internally pale yellow to reddish-brown with a fibrous texture. The rhizome has a spicy, aromatic, slightly camphoraceous odor and a pungent, warming taste.

**Stems:** Pseudostems up to 1–1.5 m tall, formed by overlapping leaf sheaths. Leaves are lanceolate, 20–40 cm long and 3–6 cm wide, with a pointed tip and smooth margins.

**Flowers:** Terminal racemes on separate flowering shoots. Flowers are white with a yellow labellum marked with red veins, approximately 2–3 cm long. The flowering period is typically spring to early summer.

**Fruit:** Capsular, approximately 1 cm diameter, containing numerous small dark seeds.

## Distribution and Habitat

*A. officinarum* is native to the humid subtropical forests of southern China, particularly Guangxi, Guangdong, Hainan, and Yunnan provinces. It also occurs naturally in northern Vietnam and Laos.

The plant is cultivated extensively in southern China for both [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]]. Optimal growth occurs at temperatures of 20–30°C with consistent moisture.

## Bioactive Compounds

The rhizome of *A. officinarum* contains a diverse array of [[blesching-cannabis-oxidative-stress-and-antioxidant-defense]].

Gastroprotective effects include reduction of gastric acid secretion, enhancement of gastric mucosal defense, and inhibition of *Helicobacter pylori* growth. These activities support the traditional use for digestive complaints and stomach disorders.

Anticancer research has identified galangin as a promising candidate, with in vitro studies showing induction of apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in breast, liver, and colon cancer cell lines. [[mushroom-cancer-clinical-trials-overview]] are limited but preclinical data are encouraging.

## Traditional and Culinary Uses

In TCM, lesser galangal rhizome (known as 高良姜, *gao liang jiang*) is classified as acrid and hot, targeting the spleen and stomach meridians. It is prescribed for cold-type abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhea, and poor appetite.

In Southeast Asian cuisines, particularly Thai and Indonesian cooking, the fresh rhizome is used as a spice in curries, soups, and pickles. Its flavor is more intense and medicinal than greater galangal, so it is used in smaller quantities.

The dried rhizome is an ingredient in Chinese five-spice powder blends and various herbal formulations. It is also used in traditional liqueurs and bitters in European [[herbal-medicine]] traditions.

## Cultivation

*A. officinarum* is propagated primarily by division of rhizome pieces in spring. Each division should contain 2–3 viable buds. Plant spacing is typically 30–45 cm apart in rows 60–90 cm apart.

Harvesting occurs 3–4 years after planting when the rhizomes have developed sufficient size and bioactive compound concentration. The rhizomes are dug, washed, sliced, and dried in the sun or at low temperatures.

Post-harvest, the dried rhizomes are typically stored in a cool, dry location. Quality grading is based on color intensity, aroma strength, and absence of mold or insect damage. Premium-grade material commands significantly higher prices in Chinese herbal markets.

## Distinction from Greater Galangal

Lesser galangal (*A. officinarum*) and greater galangal (*A. galanga*) are frequently confused in commercial and herbal contexts, but they are botanically and chemically distinct species.
