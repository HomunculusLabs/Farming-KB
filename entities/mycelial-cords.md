## Description

Mycelial cords (also called rhizomorphs in some species) are specialized, aggregated hyphal structures formed by many basidiomycete fungi that function as high-conductivity transport channels within [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]]. These cord-like structures develop when individual hyphae align, aggregate, and differentiate into organized bundles capable of efficiently transporting water, nutrients, and carbon compounds over distances ranging from centimeters to many meters. Mycelial cords are central to the foraging and nutrient redistribution strategies of both saprotrophic and [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] in forest ecosystems.

## Classification

- **Type**: Specialized fungal structure (aggregated hyphal organ)
- **Forming organisms**: Primarily basidiomycete fungi (both saprotrophic and ectomycorrhizal)
- **Taxonomic distribution**: Common in Agaricales, Polyporales, and other wood-decay and soil-dwelling basidiomycetes
- **Function**: Long-distance nutrient and water transport

## Key Facts

- Mycelial cords form when individual hyphae aggregate into organized bundles, creating specialized high-conductivity channels that dramatically increase transport efficiency compared to individual hyphae.
- Cords can extend for meters or even hectares in natural environments, connecting widely separated resource patches in a continuous network.
- The architecture of corded networks is not static but is continuously reconfigured in response to nutritional cues, environmental conditions, damage, and predation through growth, branching, fusion, or regression.
- Cord formation occurs in both saprotrophic basidiomycetes (which decompose dead organic matter) and ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes (which form mutualistic associations with plant roots).
- Key functions include scavenging nutrients from soil, concentrating nutrients from decomposing organic matter, relocating nutrients between different organic resources, and making nutrients available to plants.
- The internal anatomy of cords includes differentiated vessel hyphae (wide, often empty hyphae for bulk transport) and fiber hyphae (thick-walled for structural support).
- In "Fungi in the Environment" (Chapter 1 by Bebber et al. and Chapter 6 by Boddy & Jones), cords are described as central to understanding fungal foraging behavior and nutrient economy in heterogeneous environments.
- Network-level behavior includes sophisticated resource allocation: when a new resource is discovered, the network can redirect nutrients both toward the new find and back to the original base.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Mycelial cord formation is an indicator of healthy, vigorous mycelial growth in mushroom cultivation — the presence of visible cords (often called "rhizomorphic growth") typically signals strong colonization and readiness to fruit.
- Understanding cord formation helps cultivators optimize substrate composition and moisture levels, as cords develop most readily in substrates with appropriate physical structure and water content.
- In outdoor cultivation (mushroom beds, log cultivation, buried substrates), corded networks enable fungi to forage beyond their original inoculation point and colonize new substrate material.
- The transport capacity of mycelial cords directly influences the ability of cultivated fungi to move nutrients to developing fruiting bodies, impacting mushroom size and yield.
- Cord formation is used as a visual selection criterion when choosing vigorous strains for commercial cultivation — rhizomorphic strains are often preferred over cottony (tomentose) growth types.
- Understanding the dynamic nature of cord networks (growth, regression, reconfiguration) informs cultivation practices around substrate supplementation, watering, and environmental management.
