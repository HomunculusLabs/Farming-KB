## Varieties

Three major varieties are commonly recognized. *P. menziesii* var. *menziesii* is the coast Douglas-fir
of the Pacific slope. It is the fastest-growing and tallest variety. *P. menziesii* var. *glauca* is the
Rocky Mountain or interior Douglas-fir. It is generally smaller, hardier, and more [[query-which-grain-is-best-for-drought-tolerant-small-scale-farming]]. *P. menziesii* var. *lindleyana* is the Mexican Douglas-fir. The varieties intergrade in some
regions, especially where coastal and interior influences meet.

## Identification

Mature coast trees may reach 60-100 m tall under favorable conditions. Interior trees are usually shorter
but still substantial forest dominants. Needles are single, soft, flat, and arranged around the twig
rather than in bundles. Crushed needles often smell resinous, citrusy, or sweet. Young bark is thin,
smooth, gray, and marked with resin blisters. Old bark becomes very thick, corky, furrowed, and fire-
[[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] into Mexico. Coast
Douglas-fir dominates moist lowland and montane forests west of the Cascade and Sierra Nevada crests.
Interior Douglas-fir occupies drier mountains, valleys, and mixed-conifer forests across the Rockies and
Intermountain West. It grows from near sea level to high elevations depending on latitude and variety.
Best growth occurs on deep, well-drained soils with moderate moisture. Seedlings tolerate some shade but
usually need canopy openings to become dominant. The tree is strongly associated with fire-shaped
landscapes.

## Longevity and Size

Coast Douglas-fir can live more than 500 years. Exceptional old-growth individuals may exceed 1,000
years. Historical records describe trees over 100 m tall, and the species remains one of the tallest
living conifers. Large trunks can exceed 2 m in diameter, with rare old trees much larger. Interior
Douglas-fir is typically shorter-lived, commonly under 400 years. Its slower growth and smaller stature
reflect harsher continental conditions.
