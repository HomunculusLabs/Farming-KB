# Pluteus salicinus

**Pluteus salicinus** is a small to medium saprotrophic mushroom in the family Pluteaceae, notable among mycologists for its documented psilocybin content. It is one of the few psychoactive species outside the genus *Psilocybe*.

Commonly called the "willow shield" or "psychoactive pluteus," this species grows on dead hardwood in temperate forests across Europe and North America. It is visually inconspicuous and easily overlooked by casual foragers.

The specific epithet *salicinus* refers to its frequent association with willow trees (*Salix*), though it is not restricted to this host. Its drab greyish cap provides excellent camouflage against the bark and debris of its typical substrate.

First described by Christian Hendrik Persoon in 1798, *P. salicinus* gained wider attention after Jochen Gartz confirmed the presence of psilocybin and psilocin in European collections during the 1990s. It remains a rarely collected species with limited distribution data.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Pluteaceae
- **Genus:** Pluteus
- **Species:** P. salicinus (Pers.) P. Kumm. (1871)
- **Basionym:** Agaricus salicinus Pers. (1798)

The cap is 20–60 mm in diameter, convex to plano-convex, often with a low umbo. Surface is hygrophanous, smooth to slightly silky, greyish-brown when moist fading to pale grey or buff on drying. Radial fibrils may be present on the cap surface, especially toward the margin.

The margin is initially incurved, sometimes slightly striate in age. Gills are free from the stipe, crowded, initially white turning pink with spore maturity — a key diagnostic character of *Pluteus*. Cheilocystidia are thin-walled, flask-shaped to utriform, often with a narrow neck.

The stipe is 30–70 mm tall, 2–5 mm thick, central, cylindrical, white to greyish, smooth or slightly fibrillose. Context is thin and white. Odor is mild or slightly mealy. Taste is not distinctive.

Spore print is pink. Spores are ellipsoid, smooth, 6–8 × 5–6.5 µm. Basidia are 4-spored, clavate. Pleurocystidia are absent or rare.

## Distribution and Habitat

*P. salicinus* is found across temperate regions of Europe (Britain, France, Germany, Scandinavia) and North America (Pacific Northwest, eastern Canada). Scattered reports exist from Japan.

It is a saprotroph growing exclusively on dead hardwood, particularly willow (*Salix* spp.), alder (*Alnus* spp.), ash (*Fraxinus* spp.), and occasionally beech. Fruitings occur singly or in small groups on logs, branches, and stumps in damp forests.

The species shows a preference for well-decomposed wood in advanced stages of decay. It is most commonly found on fallen branches and small-diameter woody debris in the forest understory rather than on large stumps or standing dead wood.

The fruiting season extends from late summer through autumn (August to November in the Northern Hemisphere). It favors shaded, moist riparian habitats and is considered uncommon to rare throughout its range.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

*P. salicinus* contains the indole alkaloids psilocybin and psilocin, confirmed by Gartz (1996) through TLC and HPLC analysis of European specimens. Baeocystin has also been detected in some collections, though at lower concentrations than the primary alkaloids.

Reported alkaloid concentrations are variable and generally lower than those of *Psilocybe cubensis* or *P. semilanceata*. Gartz (1996) reported approximately 0.03% psilocybin and 0.01% psilocin in dried material from German collections.

North American specimens analyzed by Allen and Gartz (2001) showed slightly higher variability in psilocybin content, ranging from 0.01% to 0.3% of dry weight. This geographic variation may reflect genetic differences between European and North American populations, or differences in substrate and environmental conditions.

The biosynthetic pathway for psilocybin in *Pluteus* is presumed identical to that in *Psilocybe*, involving the enzymes PsiD, PsiK, PsiM, and PsiH, though this has not been experimentally confirmed in *Pluteus* species.

The bluing reaction characteristic of psilocybin-containing fungi is often weak or absent in *P. salicinus*, making field identification of psychoactivity unreliable. [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]] is required to confirm the presence of tryptamine alkaloids in any given collection.

Pharmacologically, the effects are consistent with 5-HT2A receptor agonism by psilocin, the active metabolite. Subjective effects at typical doses are mild to moderate compared to other psilocybin mushrooms, likely reflecting the lower alkaloid content.

### Dosage and Effects

Due to variable alkaloid content, dosage ranges for *P. salicinus* are poorly established. Estimated dried material doses based on Gartz's analytical data suggest 5–15 g for threshold effects, 15–30 g for moderate effects.

Onset occurs within 30–60 minutes after oral ingestion, with effects lasting 3–5 hours. The experience profile is typical of psilocybin: visual enhancement, altered perception, euphoria, and introspective thought at moderate doses.

## Identification Notes

Field identification of *P. salicinus* is challenging due to its small size, drab coloration, and similarity to non-psychoactive *Pluteus* species. The free pink gills distinguish *Pluteus* from most other genera, but separating *P. salicinus* from *P. cervinus* requires close attention to cap color and habitat.

Unlike many psilocybin mushrooms, *P. salicinus* does not reliably exhibit blue bruising on the stipe or cap when handled. This absence of bluing should not be used to rule out psychoactivity. [[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] of cheilocystidia and spore morphology provides definitive identification.

Collectors should be aware that several *Pluteus* species grow in similar habitats. Positive identification requires mature specimens with developed spore color and microscopic confirmation.

## Related Species

Several *Pluteus* species are morphologically similar and lack psychoactive properties:

- *P. cervinus* (deer shield) — larger, brown cap, no bluing, most common Pluteus
- *P. atromarginatus* — dark gill edges, no known alkaloids
- *P. umbrosus* — darker cap, found on oak, associated with old-growth forests
- *P. salicinus* var. *atrius* — darker variant with reportedly higher alkaloid content
- *P. leoninus* — yellowish cap, grows on beech and oak, no psychoactivity

## Cultivation

*P. salicinus* is not commercially cultivated. No standardized cultivation parameters exist in the published literature. Its saprotrophic habit on hardwood suggests potential for wood-based substrate cultivation similar to oyster mushrooms.

Hobby cultivators have reported successful fruiting on pasteurized hardwood sawdust supplemented with bran, with incubation at 22–25°C and fruiting triggered by reduced temperature and increased fresh air exchange. Yields are low compared to *P. cubensis*.

The mycelium is white to greyish, relatively slow-growing on agar media. Optimal spawn substrates include rye grain or millet. Colonization of [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]] blocks typically takes 3–4 weeks at 24°C.

Fruiting requires high humidity (90–95%) and moderate temperatures (16–20°C). Light is necessary for normal fruiting body development. [[stamets-biological-efficiency-yield]] is estimated at 20–40% based on limited hobby grow data.

No named cultivars or commercial strains exist. Spore collections are maintained by a few specialty mycology suppliers and private collectors.
