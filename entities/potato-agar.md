## Potato Agar

## Description

Potato agar is a simple, inexpensive agar growth medium used in [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] for germinating spores and culturing mycelial tissue. Made by boiling potato pieces in water to create a nutrient broth and then adding powdered agar as a gelling agent, it is one of the most accessible and beginner-friendly agar formulas. Potato agar is particularly valued because it is difficult for bacteria to grow on, making it an excellent starter medium for those new to tissue culturing.

## Classification

- **Category:** Growth medium
- **Field:** Mycology / Tissue culture
- **Type:** Basic agar formula
- **Alternatives:** [[malt-extract-agar]], dog food agar, cornmeal agar, tomato juice agar, brewer"s malt agar

## Key Facts

- Potato agar is prepared by slicing a small potato into chunks (no peeling required), boiling for one hour in 3-4 cups of water to create "potato tea" broth.
- After straining the potato solids, agar powder is added at a ratio of approximately 1 teaspoon (5 grams) per 2 cups of hot potato broth.
- Agar powder must be added to hot (not cold) liquid to prevent clumping. Professional labs use hot plate stirrers to homogenize agar before sterilizing.
- The agar solution is sterilized in a pressure cooker at 15 PSI for 20 minutes using an Erlenmeyer flask or wide-bottomed glass container.
- The flask top is plugged with cotton or polyester fill and covered with aluminum foil before sterilization.
- Pouring temperature is approximately 100°F (38°C). The agar gels as it cools to room temperature.
- Potato agar is difficult for bacteria to grow on, making it an ideal starter medium for beginners.
- For picky mushroom species that need a more complete food source, other bases can be substituted: powdered brewer's malt, dog and rabbit food, tomato juice, cattle feed, cornmeal, beet pulp, and others.
- Different ecotypes of the same mushroom species can differ in their enzymatic ability to break down and use compounds, so customizing agar formulas can improve culture performance.
- The more agar is supplemented, the greater the risk of attracting and cultivating contaminants, so the goal is finding the supplementation range that yields the best results while minimizing mold and bacterial competition.
- Agar plates can be stored sealed in ziplock bags in the refrigerator for one to two months.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Potato agar is the foundational growth medium recommended for beginning mushroom cultivators. Its simplicity and low cost make it accessible, while its resistance to bacterial contamination gives beginners a higher success rate when first attempting [[spore-germination-and-tissue-culturing]]. As cultivators advance, they can experiment with alternative agar bases to optimize growth for specific mushroom species and strains. Keeping detailed records of which species perform best on which agar formulas allows cultivators to build a personalized reference library that improves culture performance over time. The potato agar preparation process also teaches essential sterile technique skills—pressure cooker sterilization, aseptic pouring, and contamination prevention—that transfer to all subsequent laboratory work.

## Preparation Summary

1. Slice a small potato into chunks (do not peel). Boil in 3-4 cups water for 1 hour.
2. Strain out solids. Measure the hot broth.
3. Add 1 teaspoon agar powder per 2 cups of hot broth. Swirl to dissolve.
4. Pour into Erlenmeyer flask. Plug with cotton/polyester fill. Cover with foil.
5. Sterilize in pressure cooker at 15 PSI for 20 minutes.
6. Cool to 100°F (38°C). Add antibiotics if desired (at 105-110°F / 41-43°C).
7. Pour into sterile petri plates in front of [[laminar-flow-hood]].
8. Allow to gel at room temperature (approximately 1 hour).
9. Label with date, formula, and additives.
10. Store unused plates sealed in ziplock bags in the refrigerator.
