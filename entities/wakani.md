## Wakani

## Description

In [[jivaro]] [[shamanism]], wakani refers to a category of supernatural spirit birds that serve as auxiliary agents in bewitching operations. The term translates roughly as "soul" or "spirit" bird. Unlike the [[pasuk]] (a personal spirit helper bound to a single shaman), wakani birds are supernatural servants available to anyone who wishes to use them for bewitching purposes. They function as airborne delivery systems for [[tsentsak]] (magical darts) in the ongoing supernatural warfare between bewitching and curing shamans.

## Classification

- **Category**: Supernatural entity / spirit bird
- **Cultural context**: Jivaro people (Shuar/Achuara), Upper Amazon, Ecuador
- **Function**: Auxiliary bewitching agent and tsentsak delivery system
- **Etymology**: Wakani = "soul" or "spirit" in Jivaro
- **Availability**: Unlike pasuk, available to any practitioner

## Characteristics and Function

### Summoning and Deployment

Shamans possess the power to call wakani birds and deploy them as spirit helpers against victims. The process involves:

1. The bewitching shaman calls the wakani birds
2. The shaman blows on the birds, activating them
3. The birds are sent to the house of the intended victim
4. They fly around and around the victim, causing supernatural terror

### Effects on Victims

The wakani birds cause specific supernatural afflictions:
- **Fever**: The frightening presence of the circling spirit birds induces fever in the victim
- **Insanity**: Continued exposure to the wakani birds" supernatural presence causes mental disturbance
- **Death**: If unchecked, the affliction progresses to death "shortly thereafter"

### Resupply Function

The wakani birds serve as supernatural delivery vehicles for tsentsak (magical darts):

1. After a bewitching shaman attacks a victim, he may station a wakani bird near the victim"s house
2. If a curing shaman sucks out the intruding tsentsak, the bewitcher sends the wakani bird more tsentsak
3. The bird throws the new tsentsak from its beak into the victim
4. By continually resupplying the wakani bird, the bewitcher makes it impossible for the curer to permanently rid the patient of magical darts

## Countermeasures

Curing shamans must deal with wakani birds to successfully treat bewitched patients:

1. If tsentsak continue entering the patient after the pasuk is killed, the curer suspects a wakani bird
2. The curer drinks maikua (Datura arborea or D. suaveolens) — a hallucinogen described as even more powerful than natemd
3. The curer also drinks tobacco juice for additional spiritual power
4. Sneaking silently into the forest, the curer attempts to kill the wakani bird with tsentsak
5. After killing the bird, the curer returns to blow away the residual "atmosphere" of tsentsak from the house

## Key Facts

- Wakani birds are "supernatural servants available to anyone" — not bound to a specific practitioner
- They function as mobile delivery systems for tsentsak, continuously re-attacking victims
- Counteracting wakani birds requires the use of maikua (Datura), a more powerful hallucinogen than natemd
- The concept illustrates the layered nature of Jivaro supernatural warfare: pasuk guards on the ground, wakani birds attack from the air
- The word wakani connects to broader Amazonian concepts of spiritual power and soul-flight

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The wakani concept highlights the layered pharmacological system of Jivaro shamanism, where different plants (natemd, tobacco, piripiri, maikua) serve different spiritual functions. The need for maikua (Datura) to combat wakani birds specifically illustrates how Jivaro shamans deploy different plant medicines for different supernatural challenges, reflecting sophisticated ethnobotanical knowledge of plant properties and applications.

## Comparative Amazonian Bird Spirits

Bird spirits play important roles across multiple Amazonian shamanic traditions. The Jivaro wakani birds serve as hostile bewitching agents, while the Campa hawk spirit Kodkiti serves as a benevolent messenger bringing tobacco from "River"s Beginning." This contrast reflects the broader difference between Jivaro shamanism (characterized by supernatural warfare) and Campa shamanism (characterized by communal harmony and spirit worship).

## Etymological Note

The term wakani connects to broader Amazonian and Andean concepts of spiritual power and soul-flight. Related concepts appear in Quechua (huaca/huakani) and other indigenous languages of the region, suggesting a shared cultural understanding of birds as intermediaries between the material and spiritual worlds.
