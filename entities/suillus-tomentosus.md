# Suillus tomentosus

## Overview

Suillus tomentosus, commonly known as the false slippery jack or woolly
slippery jack, is an ectomycorrhizal bolete fungus in the family Suillaceae.
It is a obligate symbiont of coniferous trees, particularly hard pines in
western North America.

The species is notable for its distinctive woolly-fibrillose cap surface and
its importance as a mycorrhizal partner in forest regeneration. It plays a
significant role in nitrogen cycling and phosphorus uptake in pine forest
ecosystems.

Unlike many Suillus species that are considered edible but mediocre, *S.
tomentosus* is widely regarded as one of the better edible members of the
genus, though the slimy cuticle must be removed before cooking.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Boletales
- **Family:** Suillaceae
- **Genus:** Suillus
- **Species:** S. tomentosus (Kauffm.) A.H. Sm. & Thiers

The species was originally described as *Boletus tomentosus* by Calvin
Kauffman in 1926 and transferred to *Suillus* by Alexander Smith and Harry
Thiers in 1964. Some authorities have treated it as a variety of *S.
variegatus*, but molecular data support its status as a distinct species.

### Physical Description

The cap is 40–120 mm in diameter, broadly convex to nearly plane with age.
The cap surface is dry and covered with a dense tomentum of woolly fibrils,
giving it a characteristic velvety or felty texture. Color ranges from
yellowish-brown to olive-brown or reddish-brown.

The pore surface is initially whitish, becoming dull yellow to ochraceous
with age. Pores are angular, 1–2 per mm, and adnate to slightly decurrent.
Tubes are 5–10 mm deep.

The stipe is 40–80 mm tall and 8–18 mm thick, cylindrical or slightly
clavate, solid. It bears a well-developed annulus (ring) that is superior
to median, often with glandular dots below the ring. The stipe surface is
pale yellow to brownish above the ring and more fibrillose below.

Flesh is pale yellow, soft, and does not stain when cut. Spore print is
olive-brown to cinnamon-brown. Basidiospores are 7–10 × 3–4.5 µm,
elliptical-fusiform, smooth, and inamyloid.

## Distribution and Habitat

*S. tomentosus* is widely distributed across western North America, from
Alaska through British Columbia and the Pacific Northwest, extending south
through the Rocky Mountains into Colorado and New Mexico. It also occurs
in the Great Lakes region and northeastern North America.

The species is an obligate ectomycorrhizal associate of conifers, most
commonly found with lodgepole pine (*Pinus contorta*), jack pine (*Pinus
banksiana*), and ponderosa pine (*Pinus ponderosa*). It also associates
with spruce (*Picea* spp.) and larch (*Larix* spp.).

Fruiting occurs from late summer through autumn (July–October), typically
in young to mature coniferous forests. It is frequently abundant in
disturbed or regenerating forest sites, including recently burned areas
and clearcuts where pines are re-establishing.

## Ecology and Mycorrhizal Associations

As an ectomycorrhizal fungus, *S. tomentosus* forms mutualistic
associations with the fine roots of coniferous host trees. The fungal
mantle surrounds root tips, and a Hartig net penetrates between cortical
cells, facilitating bidirectional nutrient exchange.

The fungus receives photosynthate (carbon compounds) from the host tree and
provides enhanced access to soil nitrogen and phosphorus in return. Suillus
species are particularly efficient at accessing organic nitrogen sources
through extracellular protease activity.

*S. tomentosus* is considered an early-successional ectomycorrhizal
fungus, colonizing young pine seedlings readily. It plays an important role
in pine forest regeneration after disturbance, and has been investigated
for use in commercial forest nursery inoculation programs.

Research has shown that *Suillus* species, including *S. tomentosus*, can
access phosphorus from mineral sources (apatite) through organic acid
exudation, a capability that benefits host trees growing on nutrient-poor
soils.

## Edibility

*S. tomentosus* is generally considered edible and is among the better-
regarded Suillus species for the table. However, several preparation
considerations apply.

The slimy, glutinous cuticle beneath the woolly tomentum should be fully
removed before cooking, as it can cause gastrointestinal upset in some
individuals and has an unpleasant texture. The tube layer (pore surface)
should also be removed in mature specimens.

Young specimens with firm flesh are preferred. The mushroom is suitable for
sautéing, drying, or pickling. Its flavor is mild and slightly nutty,
without the strong resinous taste common to some Suillus species.

As with all wild mushrooms, positive identification is essential. *S.
tomentosus* can be confused with other Suillus species, though its
distinctive woolly cap surface and association with hard pines aid
identification.
