# Australorp

## Description

The [[australorp]] is an Australian-developed chicken breed mentioned by [[bill-mollison]] in *[[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual* as a specific example of a heavy breed. Mollison notes that heavy breeds like the Australorp "cannot fly over a 1.2 m fence" — a practical characteristic with significant implications for permaculture system design, fencing costs, and component placement. As a breed developed specifically for Australian conditions, it represents the permaculture principle of using locally-adapted species.

## Classification

- **Category**: Chicken Breed — Dual Purpose (Eggs and Meat)
- **Origin**: Australia (developed from Black Orpingtons imported from England)
- **Type**: Heavy Breed
- **Context**: Permaculture Livestock Integration
- **Primary Use**: Eggs (brown) and meat

## Key Facts

- Developed in Australia in the early 20th century from Black Orpington stock brought from England
- Name derived from "Australian Orpington" — originally called "Australorp" from the 1920s
- Classified as a heavy breed — too heavy to fly over a 1.2 m fence (cocks 3.9 kg, hens 2.9 kg)
- Black plumage with a characteristic green sheen — dark colouration provides cold hardiness but less heat tolerance than white breeds
- Dual-purpose breed valued for both prolific egg-laying and meat production
- Holds world record for egg laying: 364 eggs in 365 days (set by an Australorp hen in the 1920s)
- Known for calm, docile temperament — easier to manage in confined systems than flighty light breeds
- Well-adapted to Australian conditions including heat, drought, and variable forage quality
- Good foragers that work well in free-range and rotational systems

## Breed Characteristics in Design Context

Mollison uses the Australorp to demonstrate how intrinsic characteristics affect system design:

- **Flight inability**: contained by standard 1.2 m fencing — low infrastructure cost for rotational systems
- **Black plumage**: absorbs heat — advantage in cold climates, liability in extreme heat without shade
- **Heavy build**: good meat yield but higher feed requirements than light breeds
- **Calm temperament**: easy to handle, suitable for intensive rotational systems and small-scale operations
- **Dual-purpose**: one breed fills both egg and meat functions, reducing system complexity
- **Australian origin**: pre-adapted to local conditions, diseases, and forage types

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

The Australorp is a particularly appropriate breed for Australian permaculture systems:

1. **Low containment cost** — as a heavy breed unable to clear standard fencing, Australorps can be managed with lower fencing infrastructure, reducing costs for orchard and pasture integration
2. **Climate adaptation** — developed for Australian conditions, well-suited to the range of climates found across the continent, from temperate to semi-arid
3. **Dual-purpose efficiency** — excellent egg production combined with good meat yield means fewer separate components needed in the system to meet household needs
4. **Temperament for integration** — calm temperament makes Australorps suitable for intensive rotational systems, orchard integration, and backyard food production where frequent human contact is expected
5. **Black plumage trade-off** — Mollison notes that "thickly-feathered large dark chickens survive extreme cold" but may struggle in extreme heat. In hot Australian summers, shade provision becomes critical — this is itself a design opportunity (integration with trees)
6. **Self-reliance** — their foraging ability and hardiness mean they can supplement feed rations with insects, seeds, and vegetation, reducing external inputs

Mollison's design methodology (Chapter 3) uses chicken breeds to demonstrate that intrinsic characteristics must be catalogued and matched to system needs. The Australorp represents a breed well-suited to many permaculture applications due to its flightlessness, dual-purpose productivity, and Australian origin.
