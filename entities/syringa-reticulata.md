## Overview

Syringa reticulata, the Japanese tree lilac, is a deciduous small tree or large shrub in the family Oleaceae. It is the largest species in the genus Syringa, typically reaching 6–12 m in height with a spreading crown and distinctive cherry-like bark.

The species is native to eastern Asia, including Japan, Korea, northeastern China, and the Russian Far East. It is valued in temperate horticulture for its showy creamy-white panicles, which appear later than those of common lilac (S. vulgaris), extending the lilac flowering season into early summer.

S. reticulata is notable for its ornamental bark, which exfoliates in thin plates on mature trunks to reveal a polished, copper-brown surface. This characteristic, combined with its reliable urban tolerance, has made it a popular street tree and specimen planting in cold-climate cities.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Magnoliopsida → Lamiales → Oleaceae → Oleeae → Syringa → S. reticulata (Blume) H.Hara. The species was originally described as Ligustrum reticulatum by Carl Ludwig Blume in 1850 and transferred to Syringa by Hiroshi Hara in 1947.

The specific epithet reticulata refers to the prominent reticulate (net-like) leaf venation, which is more pronounced than in other Syringa species. The species complex includes the subspecies S. reticulata subsp. reticulata (Japan) and S. reticulata subsp. amurensis (continental Asia). The continental subspecies typically has larger leaves and inflorescences than the Japanese type.

Recent taxonomic treatments recognize S. pekinensis as a synonym of S. reticulata rather than a distinct species. The cultivar 'Pekinensis' in the nursery trade derives from this continental form and is characterized by narrower, more pointed leaves.

The tree typically reaches 6–12 m tall with a broadly ovate to rounded crown. Trunk diameter at maturity is 20–40 cm. The growth habit is multi-stemmed when young, developing a single [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], particularly in the northeastern United States and the Great Lakes region. The species is not considered invasive in these areas, though self-seeding can occur near cultivated specimens.

In its native Japan, S. reticulata is a common component of secondary forests and forest edges in mountainous regions. It frequently co-occurs with Acer palmatum, Stewartia pseudocamellia, and Enkianthus perulatus in cool-temperate montane communities.

The species tolerates urban conditions including air pollution, compacted soils, deicing salts, and restricted root space. These qualities have led to its selection as a preferred street tree in cities such as Helsinki, Oslo, and Minneapolis.

Propagation is by seed, which requires 2–3 months of cold stratification at 1–5 °C for reliable germination. Softwood cuttings under intermittent mist root at approximately 60–80% with IBA treatment. Grafted and budded nursery stock is commercially available on S. vulgaris rootstock.

Pruning requirements are minimal compared to shrub lilacs. The species develops a strong central leader naturally and requires only removal of dead, damaged, or crossing branches. Heavy pruning is not recommended as it compromises the natural form and delays flowering.

Transplant success is high when young trees are moved balled-and-burlapped or container-grown. Larger field-grown specimens are more difficult to establish and may require several years to recover root systems after transplanting.

Cultivar 'Ivory Silk' is the most widely planted selection, noted for its compact habit (6–8 m), profuse flowering, and resistance to [[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]]. Other named selections include 'Chanticleer', 'Summer Snow', and the dwarf 'Cameo Jewel'.

The dwarf cultivar 'Cameo Jewel' reaches only 2–3 m and is suitable for smaller gardens where a standard tree lilac would overwhelm the space. It retains the characteristic exfoliating bark and late flowering of the species in a more compact form.

## Uses

The primary use of S. reticulata is ornamental, valued for its late flowering, attractive bark, and durable form. It serves as an effective specimen tree, street tree, and large-scale landscape planting in cold-climate regions.

In traditional Korean and Chinese medicine, the bark and leaves have been used as antipyretic and anti-inflammatory agents. Phytochemical studies have identified oleuropein, verbascoside, and several iridoid glycosides in leaf and bark extracts.

The roots and bark have also been used in folk medicine as a treatment for rheumatic complaints and digestive disorders. These traditional applications lack rigorous clinical validation but have stimulated modern investigation into the species' bioactive constituents.
