## Myrica pensylvanica

## Overview

**Myrica pensylvanica** (northern bayberry) is an upright, semi-evergreen shrub native to the eastern coast of [[north-carolina-mycodiversity]], commonly found on coastal dunes, sandy barrens, and rocky headlands. The species is valued in landscaping for salt tolerance, nitrogen-fixing root symbiosis, and aromatic waxy fruit.

The plant typically reaches 1.5–3 m in height with a spreading, colony-forming habit. It is dioecious, with separate male and female plants. Female plants produce clusters of grayish-white, waxy drupes that persist through winter and are historically used for candle-making. Northern bayberry is one of the most cold-hardy members of the Myricaceae, making it a valuable subject for studying [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]].

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Fagales
- **Family:** Myricaceae
- **Genus:** Myrica
- **Species:** M. pensylvanica
- **Authority:** Loisel.
- **Synonyms:** Myrica carolinensis Mill., Myrica cerifera var. pensylvanica (Loisel.) Fern.

Some modern treatments place this species within *Morella* rather than *Myrica*, as *M. pensylvanica* (Loisel.) Small. However, *Myrica* remains widely used in horticultural and ecological literature.

The Myricaceae is a small family of approximately 50 species distributed across five genera. Molecular phylogenetic studies have confirmed the family's placement within Fagales as sister to the Juglandaceae. The family is distinguished by its actinorhizal root nodules, aromatic foliage, and waxy fruit coatings. Fossil Myricaceae pollen extends back to the Late Cretaceous, suggesting an ancient lineage.

Chromosome number for *M. pensylvanica* is 2n = 32, consistent with other diploid Myricaceae species. Polyploid forms have not been documented. The species shows moderate [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]].

Flowers appear in April to May before or with leaf emergence. Male catkins are yellowish, 1–2.5 cm long, borne in clusters at leaf axils. Female flowers are inconspicuous, borne in short spikes. The species is wind-pollinated and dioecious. Successful fruit set requires proximity of male and female plants, typically within 3–6 m of each other. In landscape plantings, a ratio of approximately one male to six females ensures adequate pollination.

Fruit is a drupe, 4–6.5 mm in diameter, covered in a thick, grayish-white waxy coating (bayberry wax). Fruits persist on female plants through autumn and winter, providing late-season food for birds. Each fruit contains a single seed. The wax layer, composed primarily of triglycerides of palmitic and myristic acids, melts at approximately 45–48°C and was historically the most commercially valuable product of the plant.

Seed dispersal is primarily by birds, which consume the waxy fruit and excrete the seed intact. Water dispersal also occurs along coastal waterways. Seeds remain viable in the soil seed bank for 2–4 years, allowing the species to persist through periods of unfavorable establishment conditions. Germination rates are highest after passage through bird digestive tracts, which removes the waxy seed coat.

## Distribution and Habitat

Northern bayberry occurs along the Atlantic coast from Newfoundland and Nova Scotia south to North Carolina. Inland populations extend into the Great Lakes region and parts of the Appalachian plateau. It is most abundant on sandy coastal soils but also occurs on rocky slopes and in pine barrens.

Disjunct populations have been recorded in Arkansas and Oklahoma, likely representing Pleistocene relicts. These inland populations are of conservation interest due to their isolation and distinct genetic composition.

The species is intolerant of deep shade and competes poorly under closed forest canopy. It is a classic early-successional species, colonizing disturbed sandy sites and gradually being replaced by taller woody species as soil develops. Optimal pH ranges from 4.5 to 6.5, reflecting its association with acidic coastal and pine barren soils.

Northern bayberry occurs in USDA hardiness zones 3–7. It is most vigorous in zones 4–6 where cold winters and moderate precipitation favor its growth. Along the southern edge of its range in the Carolinas, it is largely restricted to maritime forests and dune systems where cool ocean breezes moderate summer temperatures.

## Cultivation

Northern bayberry is widely planted as an [[permaculture-difficult-sites-plant-guide]]. It tolerates full sun to partial shade, salt spray, drought, and poor sandy soils. Once established, it requires virtually no supplemental irrigation or fertilization.

Propagation is primarily by seed, which requires cold stratification for 60–90 days. Seeds have a hard, waxy coating that benefits from a brief sulfuric acid scarification or hot water soak before stratification. Softwood cuttings taken in summer root at moderate rates (30–50%) under mist. Semi-hardwood cuttings are less reliable.

The shrub responds well to renewal pruning and can be cut back hard in early spring to rejuvenate old, leggy specimens. Suckering habit can be managed by removing unwanted basal shoots annually. Spacing of 1.5–2.5 m is recommended for hedge plantings. Growth rate is moderate, averaging 30–45 cm per year under favorable conditions. Established plants can live 30–50 years.

Northern bayberry performs best in full sun but tolerates light shade, particularly in hotter southern portions of its range. It is highly drought-tolerant once established, owing to its deep taproot and waxy, moisture-retentive foliage. The species is also notably resistant to most insect pests and foliar diseases, making it a low-maintenance landscape choice. Root rot can occur on poorly drained sites, particularly in container production.

## Ecological Role

Like other Myricaceae, northern bayberry forms root nodules containing *Frankia* actinorhizal symbionts. These nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert atmospheric N₂ into ammonium, enriching nutrient-poor sandy soils. This capacity makes bayberry an important facilitator species in coastal dune succession.

The waxy fruit is consumed by at least 20 bird species, including northern flicker, yellow-rumped warbler, tree sparrow, and eastern bluebird. Fruit persistence into winter makes it a critical late-season food resource when other berries are depleted. The dense branching structure provides nesting cover for songbirds and shelter for small mammals.

Bayberry foliage supports caterpillars of several specialist Lepidoptera. The imperial moth (*Eacles imperialis*) and laurel sphinx (*Sphinx kalmiae*) both feed on Myricaceae foliage. Deer browsing is generally light due to the aromatic, resinous foliage.

In coastal dune ecosystems, northern bayberry serves as a primary sand-stabilizing shrub. Its extensive lateral root system, supplemented by [[juniperus-virginiana]]*). This nurse-plant function has been documented extensively along the mid-Atlantic barrier island systems.

Actinorhizal nodules on bayberry roots can fix an estimated 5–15 kg N/ha/year. This nitrogen input is significant in nutrient-limited dune and barrens ecosystems, where total soil nitrogen may be below 0.05%. The Frankia strains associated with Myricaceae are cross-infective to some degree with other actinorhizal hosts including Alnus and Hippophae, though specificity patterns vary geographically.
