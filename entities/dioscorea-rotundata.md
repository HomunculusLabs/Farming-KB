## Overview

**Dioscorea rotundata**, known as white yam or white Guinea yam, is a tropical vine species cultivated primarily for its large underground tubers. It is the most economically important yam species in West and Central Africa.

White yam accounts for approximately 70–75% of total yam production worldwide, with Nigeria alone producing over 50 million tonnes annually. The species is a dietary staple for over 300 million people across the yam belt of West Africa.

The tubers are rich in complex carbohydrates and serve as a critical food security crop in regions where cereals and other staples are unreliable. White yam also holds deep [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. It requires well-drained, sandy loam to loamy soils with pH 5.5–6.5 and moderate to high fertility.

Optimal growth temperatures range from 25–30°C, with growth ceasing below 15°C. White yam is sensitive to waterlogging and heavy clay soils, though it tolerates seasonal drought once established through its deep root system.

The species is well adapted to the alternating wet-dry seasons of the West African savanna zone. During the dry season, the above-ground vine dies back and the tuber enters dormancy until the next rains.

## Cultivation

White yam is propagated using small whole tubers (seed yams) or tuber pieces (setts) weighing 100–300 g. Setts are cut from the head, middle, or tail of the parent tuber, with head setts preferred for their superior sprouting ability.

Planting occurs at the start of the rainy season with mounds or ridges spaced 1 m apart within rows and 1 m between rows. The crop requires 8–12 months from planting to harvest, depending on variety and environmental conditions.

Yields range from 10 to 25 tonnes per hectare under traditional cultivation, with improved varieties and management achieving 30–40 tonnes per hectare. The crop requires staking for optimal yield, though unstaked production is common in resource-limited systems.

Post-harvest losses are a major constraint, with tuber deterioration beginning within 2–4 weeks of harvest. Storage methods include barns, pits, and improved ventilated structures, though losses of 10–30% remain typical.

## Nutritional Value and Culinary Use

White yam tubers contain approximately 60–75% moisture, 20–30% carbohydrate (primarily starch), 1.5–3% protein, and 0.1–0.3% fat. They are a significant source of dietary energy in West African diets.

The starch has a high amylose content, giving cooked white yam a firm, mealy texture preferred for pounded yam (*iyan*), a traditional dish where boiled tubers are pounded into a smooth elastic paste.

Yam is also boiled, fried (*dundun*), roasted, or processed into flour for amala and other dishes. Yam flour has a longer shelf life than fresh tubers and is an important commercial product.

White yam provides moderate levels of vitamin C, thiamine, and riboflavin. It is richer in potassium and dietary fiber than cassava, though lower in beta-carotene compared to orange-fleshed sweet potato varieties.

The tubers contain dioscorine, a mildly toxic alkaloid that is destroyed by cooking. Proper heat processing is essential before consumption, as raw yam can cause gastrointestinal distress.

## Ecology and Applications

In traditional farming systems, white yam is intercropped with maize, cassava, cowpea, and vegetables in complex polycultures. The climbing habit allows yam to exploit vertical space above shorter companion crops.

Yam contributes to soil health through the incorporation of crop residues and the [[blesching-cannabis-fungal-infections-candida]].

Anthracnose caused by *Colletotrichum gloeosporioides* is the most important foliar disease, producing dark lesions on leaves and stems. Severe infections can defoliate plants and reduce yields by 30–50%.

[[cervantes-viral-diseases-cannabis]] including yam mosaic virus (YMV) and yam mild mosaic virus (YMMV) are widespread in West Africa. They are transmitted by aphids and through vegetative propagation, leading to progressive yield decline over successive generations.
