## Commonwork

## Description

Commonwork is a land-use system described by [[bill-mollison]] in *Permaculture: A Designer"s
Manual* in which a trust, tribe, or individual farmer allows multiple land uses by multiple
self-employed people on a single property. The system is akin to the African "mahisa" or
livestock loan system of Botswana. In a commonwork, the land is closely assessed for its
total productive potential, then divided into enterprises that individual lessees operate
independently while contributing a percentage of gross income to a shared capital fund.
It represents one of the most comprehensive models for intensive, multi-use land management
in [[permaculture-design]].

## Classification

- **Category:** Land use system / Multiple enterprise framework
- **Domain:** Permaculture -- Land access, community design, bioregional development
- **Origin:** Tribal and traditional models (African mahisa, Botswana livestock systems)
- **Scale:** Applicable from single farms (40+ families on 100 ha) to bioregional networks

## How It Works

1. **Land Assessment:** The land area is closely assessed for earth resources, wildlife
   potential, forest and aquaculture capacity, small and large livestock suitability,
   arable land, mixed orchard potential, and socio-educational possibilities
2. **Enterprise Design:** The trust or owners design and advertise specific enterprises
   ranging from hobbies (butterfly forage systems) to full-time occupations (trout farms)
3. **Legal Framework:** Lease documents specify rights of use, lease length, responsibilities
   of trust and lessee, and payment terms (typically 10% of gross income)
4. **Capital Fund:** The 10% contributions from all lessees form a shared capital fund for
   research, development, and maintaining services (roads, fences, power, land taxes)
5. **Self-Employment:** All lessees are self-employed; land costs are minimal and access
   is secure
6. **Interdependence:** Enterprises are designed so that each one helps supply others
   and regional needs

## Core Principles

- **Total Ecology:** The system designs for a complete human ecology, not just single crops
  or enterprises
- **Self-Employment:** No employees or employers; all participants are independent operators
- **Modular Enterprise:** Each "living" is a complete ecological totality (e.g., the beekeeper,
  the bees, their water, flowers, pollen, propolis, hives, forage access, and market rights)
- **Fair Income Differential:** Inspired by the Mondragon 1:4.5 ratio -- a sweeper should
  not receive less than 25% of the total wage of a doctor
- **Enterprise Synergy:** Each enterprise should supply others as well as regional needs
- **Open Access:** Occupations are available to all, with a proportion reserved for
  disadvantaged groups

## Design Priorities for a Commonwork Land Trust

Mollison specifies a clear sequence of development priorities:

1. **Priority 1 -- Water Storage:** Maximize water storage using [[keyline-design]], swales,
   soil conditioning, ridge forestry, and broad canals/dams for diversion and irrigation
2. **Priority 2 -- Forest Systems:** Specify forest sites and types, including forage
   forests for locally acceptable or endemic animal species
3. **Priority 3 -- Crop Areas:** Specify orchard and perennial crop areas based on best
   soils, low slopes, windbreaks, and water access
4. **Priority 4 -- Access and Edge:** Specify complex access routes, trackways, critical
   edges for flowers, bee fodders, trellis systems, and wayside crops
5. **Other:** Caretaker house sites, earth material stockpiles, archaeological checks,
   development journal, and open planning

## Probable Livelihoods for 100 ha (40+ Families)

Mollison lists specific enterprises suitable for a commonwork:

- Bees and bee products
- Poultry
- Ducks
- Fish species (aquaculture)
- Forage systems
- Fuel forestry
- Structural forestry
- Cut flowers
- Plant nursery
- Methane and wind energy systems, solar systems (manufacture and power sales)
- Accommodation and tours
- Workshops and field days
- Training programmes
- Cooperative store
- Credit union
- Insurance services
- Equipment leasing
- Processing of raw materials (pottery, bricks, dried/pickled/smoked foods)

## Key Facts

- Lessees pay approximately 10% of gross income to the trust as a capital contribution
- More than one farm or trust can join a commonwork system
- The trust sets broad conditions of sustainable use; contributors to the capital fund
  vote in their own cash management group
- Members can leave, sell out to new lessees, and be refunded their 10% contribution
  (less base maintenance and service costs, plus any interest)
- Commonworks need to be close to towns or on public transport routes for accessibility
- The system can absorb much of the unemployment in a district
- A proportion of occupations can be assigned to disadvantaged groups

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **[[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] as Enterprise:** [[duggar-mushroom-growing-history-and-methods]] (outdoor log cultivation,
  indoor [[fruiting-chambers-and-environmental-control]], or truffle orchards) fits naturally as a commonwork enterprise,
  using forest by-products and waste substrates from other enterprises
- **Substrate Synergy:** Fuel forestry waste becomes [[fao-spent-mushroom-substrate-composting-recycling]]; spent mushroom
  substrate becomes compost for orchards and crop areas -- creating closed-loop cycles
  between enterprises
- **Spawn Production:** A dedicated micro-enterprise can produce mushroom spawn for the
  commonwork"s own use and for regional sale
- **[[integrated-pest-management]]:** Poultry and duck enterprises provide pest control for
  crop areas while also producing manure for composting
- **Forest Mushroom Systems:** Structural and fuel forestry areas can double as mushroom
  cultivation zones (shiitake on oak logs, oyster on straw from crop areas)
- **Aquaculture Integration:** Fish ponds receive nutrient-rich water from composting
  operations; duckweed from ponds feeds poultry; fish waste fertilizes crops
- **Processing and Value-Add:** Drying, pickling, and smoking enterprises process the
  commonwork's own mushroom, fish, [[latitude-effects-on-photosynthesis-and-crop-production]] for higher-value products
- **Nursery Enterprise:** A plant nursery can produce both garden plants and mushroom
  spawn as complementary product lines
- **Educational Tourism:** Mushroom cultivation workshops, mycology field days, and
  permaculture training programmes generate income while spreading knowledge

## Comparison with Other Land Access Models

| Feature | Commonwork | Single Farm | Subdivision | Cooperative |
|---------|-----------|------------|-------------|-------------|
| Number of enterprises | Many | 1-3 | 1 per lot | 1 shared |
| Self-employment | All | Owner only | Individual | Collective |
| Land cost per person | Low (shared) | High | High | Medium |
| Diversity of production | Very high | Low | Low | Medium |
| Risk distribution | Across enterprises | Concentrated | Individual | Shared |
| Capital requirement | Low per enterprise | High | High (purchase) | Medium |

## The "Living" Concept

Mollison defines a "living" as a complete human ecological totality. For a beekeeper, a
living includes not just the bees, but their water, flowers, pollen, propolis, the means
to make beehives, clear legal access to forage, a registered bee site, and the right to
sell or [[coleman-stir-fry-pak-winter-market-product]] to pay for other life needs. This holistic view ensures each
enterprise is self-sustaining rather than dependent on external inputs.
