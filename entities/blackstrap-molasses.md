# Blackstrap Molasses

## Description

Blackstrap molasses is the dark, viscous byproduct of the third boiling of sugar cane or sugar beet juice during sugar refining. It contains concentrated minerals (iron, calcium, magnesium, potassium), trace elements, and residual sugars. In [[compost-tea-brewing]], molasses serves as a primary microbial food source, sticker-spreader, and fungal growth promoter. [[elaine-ingham]] discusses molasses extensively as one of the most important compost tea ingredients.

## Classification

- **Category**: Microbial food / compost tea ingredient / sticker-spreader
- **Source**: Third extraction from sugar cane or sugar beet refining
- **Types**: Blackstrap (third boil, most mineral-dense), unsulfured (preferred), dried/molasses powder
- **Nutrient profile**: ~55% total sugars, high in Ca, Mg, K, Fe, and trace minerals
- **Microbial role**: Food for bacteria and fungi; promotes fungal dominance at proper ratios

## Key Facts

1. **Unsulfured Required**: Only unsulfured molasses should be used in compost tea. Sulfured molasses contains sulfur dioxide as a preservative, which kills beneficial microorganisms. Always check labels for "unsulfured" or "unsulphured."

2. **Fungal Promoter**: Contrary to common assumption that molasses only feeds bacteria, Ingham demonstrates that molasses promotes fungal growth over bacterial growth at proper concentrations. Adding molasses to compost piles favors fungi, which speeds up the whole decomposition process dramatically.

3. **Actinomycetes Stimulation**: Molasses stimulates actinomycete (Actinobacteria) growth. These filamentous bacteria are important decomposers that bridge bacterial and fungal functions in soil. They are visible as white thread-like growth in healthy compost.

4. **Concentration Matters**: The amount of molasses added determines which organisms dominate. Too much molasses can drive the tea anaerobic by feeding rapid bacterial growth that consumes all available oxygen. Ingham recommends specific concentration ranges to maintain aerobic conditions.

5. **Sticker-Spreader Function**: Unsulphured molasses acts as a sticker-spreader in foliar applications, helping organisms adhere to leaf surfaces. This is critical for disease prevention, as organisms must physically occupy leaf surface space to outcompete pathogens.

6. **Cane or Beet**: Both cane and beet molasses work for compost tea. Cane molasses is more commonly available and typically has a higher mineral content. Either type provides the sugars and trace minerals needed by microorganisms.

7. **Dry vs Wet Forms**: Molasses is available in liquid and dried forms. Dried molasses (also called dried cane sugar) can be convenient for compost applications, while liquid molasses is standard for tea brewing. The liquid form dissolves more readily in tea water.

8. **Compost Pile Application**: Adding 500ml per 4kg of dry waste (approximately 2 pints per 10 pounds) to compost piles favors fungal growth and dramatically speeds decomposition. This is a practical technique for converting ligno-cellulosic waste without high temperatures.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Compost Tea Brewing
Molasses is a foundational ingredient in both bacterial and fungal compost tea recipes:
- **Bacterial tea**: Molasses as primary food source
- **Fungal tea**: Molasses combined with [[humic-acid]], fish hydrolysate, and kelp
- **Rate**: Typically 1-2 tablespoons per 5 gallons of tea

### Compost Production
Adding molasses to compost feedstocks accelerates decomposition by feeding the organisms that break down organic matter. This is particularly useful for woody, high-carbon materials that decompose slowly.

### Soil Drench
Molasses applied as a soil drench feeds soil microorganisms directly, stimulating biological activity and nutrient cycling. The minerals in molasses (especially calcium and iron) become available to plants through microbial processing.

### Mushroom Substrate Connection
The principles of molasses as a fungal food apply to [[mushroom-substrate-preparation]]. Molasses added to composting substrates promotes the fungal communities that precondition substrate for mushroom mycelium colonization.

### Practical Guidelines
- Always use **unsulfured** molasses
- Start with small amounts and increase gradually
- Monitor dissolved oxygen — if it drops below 5.5 mg/L, reduce molasses
- Store in sealed container to prevent fermentation
- Can be used as a compost accelerator at 500ml per 4kg dry waste
