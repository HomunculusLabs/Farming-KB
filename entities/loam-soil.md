## Description

Loam is the ideal garden soil texture, consisting of relatively equal parts sand, silt, and clay. It combines the water-holding and nutrient-retention properties of fine particles with the drainage and aeration benefits of coarser particles. Loam is widely regarded as the gold standard for agricultural and horticultural growing media.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Soil science
- **Category**: Soil texture class
- **Composition**: ~30–50% sand, ~30–50% silt, ~20–30% clay, with 5–10% organic matter
- **Textural class**: Medium-textured soil on the USDA soil triangle

## Key Facts

- Loam balances the surface area of silt and clay (which hold nutrients and water) with the pore space of sand (which aids drainage and pulls in air).
- The sand component provides gravitational drainage; the silt and clay components retain capillary water and adsorb nutrients via [[cation-exchange-and-nonessential-elements]].
- Organic matter (humus) in loam further increases surface area and water-holding capacity while hosting soil food web organisms.
- A teaspoon of good loam contains approximately 1 billion bacteria, several yards of fungal hyphae, several thousand protozoa, and a few dozen nematodes.
- Good garden loam is coffee-colored, rich in organic matter, holds water yet still drains when saturated, and has a pleasant earthy smell.

## Texture Components

### Sand
- Particle diameter: 0.0625–2 mm
- Visible to the naked eye
- Large pore spaces; mostly gravitational water
- Gritty texture between fingers
- Settles quickly in water (within minutes)

### Silt
- Particle diameter: 0.004–0.0625 mm
- Requires a microscope to see individual particles
- Smaller pores hold more capillary water than sand
- Flour-like texture when rubbed between fingers
- Influenced by gravity; settles out in water

### Clay
- Particle diameter: <0.004 mm
- Requires an [[bloomfield-ascospore-extreme-resilience-electron-microscope]] to resolve
- Enormous surface area relative to volume
- Plastic and slippery when wet; absorbs and holds water
- Contains hydrous aluminum silicates with magnesium and iron
- Greatest [[cation-exchange-capacity]] of all particle types

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Loam supports the most diverse and active soil food web of any mineral soil texture, providing optimal habitat for bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and arthropods.
- The balanced pore structure ensures adequate oxygen for aerobic organisms (including [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]) while maintaining sufficient moisture for microbial metabolism.
- An acre of good loam soil contains roughly 2,000 lbs of bacteria, 2,400 lbs of fungi, 900 lbs each of earthworms and arthropods, 133 lbs of protozoa, and several pounds of small mammals.
- Soil texture directly influences fungal-to-bacterial biomass ratios: loam can support either community depending on management practices (tillage reduces fungi; mulch and compost promote them).
- [[grass-seed-substrate-for-mushroom-cultivation]], loamy soils with higher organic matter provide ideal outdoor bed substrates, supporting both [[decomposer-fungi-succession]] and [[the-soil-food-web]] organisms that cycle nutrients.
- A simple jar test (soil + water + dispersant like Calgon, shaken and allowed to settle) allows gardeners to determine their soil's sand/silt/clay proportions and how close it is to loam.
- Adding [[coleman-compost-and-organic-fertility-management]] matter to sandy or clay-heavy soils progressively improves their texture toward loam, simultaneously building the soil food web.

## Practical Test

The jar sedimentation test: fill a quart jar with two cups of water, a tablespoon of dispersant, and soil from the top 6 inches. Shake vigorously, then let settle. Sand settles in minutes, silt in hours, clay may take days. Measure each layer's proportion to classify your soil on the texture triangle.

## Practical Applications
These soil principles directly inform management decisions in agriculture
and horticulture. Understanding soil structure dynamics helps growers
optimize tillage practices, amendment applications, and planting schedules.
Biological soil management focuses on feeding soil organisms rather than
directly supplying nutrients to plants, creating self-sustaining fertility.
Regular soil food web assessments guide adaptive management strategies.
