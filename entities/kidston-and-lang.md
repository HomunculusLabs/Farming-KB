# Kidston and Lang (1921) — Rhynie Chert Fossil Plants

## Description

Robert Kidston and William Henry Lang published a landmark series of papers between 1917 and 1921 documenting the fossil plants of the Rhynie Chert in Aberdeenshire, Scotland. Their 1921 publication (Part V) is particularly significant for [[mycology]] because it documented fungal structures within the tissues of primitive land plants [[rhynia]] and [[asteroxylon]]. These observations provided the earliest fossil evidence of fungal-plant associations and sparked a century of debate about the nature and significance of these ancient interactions.

## Classification

- **Authors**: Robert Kidston (1852–1921), William Henry Lang (1874–1960)
- **Publication**: "On old red sandstone plants showing structure, from the Rhynie Chert bed, Aberdeenshire. Part V." Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh 52:855–912
- **Year**: 1921
- **Subject**: Paleobotany, paleomycology, early land plant-fungal associations
- **Fossil site**: Rhynie Chert, Aberdeenshire, Scotland

## Key Facts

- The Kidston and Lang (1921) publication is Part V of a series documenting the anatomy of early Devonian land plants preserved in the Rhynie Chert.
- The Rhynie Chert is a silica-rich hot-spring deposit that preserved organisms in exceptional cellular detail, creating one of the world's most important fossil sites.
- Their documentation of fungal structures within Rhynia and Asteroxylon tissues represents the earliest known evidence of fungal associations with land plants.
- The quality of preservation was remarkable: hyphae, spores, and other fungal structures could be identified at the cellular level within the plant fossils.
- [[pirozynski-and-malloch-hypothesis]] (1975) later reinterpreted these fungal structures as evidence of a primitive mycorrhizal association, proposing that land plants evolved in conjunction with mycorrhizal partners.
- Dighton (2003) notes that the detail and descriptions in the original publication leave much doubt about the actual function of the fungal-plant association — the fungi could represent pathogens, non-mycorrhizal endophytes, or saprotrophs colonizing dead plant material.
- Kidston was a renowned Scottish paleobotanist; Lang was a professor of botany at the University of Manchester.
- Their work laid the foundation for the entire field of paleomycology.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- The Kidston and Lang fossils provide the deep-time context for understanding why [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] are so universal and ecologically important in modern cultivation systems.
- Their documentation of fungal structures in the earliest land plants supports the hypothesis that [[mycorrhizal-symbiosis]] was a prerequisite for plant terrestrialization.
- The ongoing debate about the nature of these fossil fungi parallels modern challenges in classifying fungal functional roles — a given fungus may be mutualist, pathogen, or saprotroph depending on context.
- The Rhynie Chert evidence reinforces the importance of maintaining fungal partnerships in modern agriculture and cultivation, as these relationships have been essential for over 400 million years.
- Understanding the history of fungal-plant associations helps researchers appreciate the evolutionary depth of mycorrhizal technologies used in sustainable cultivation.
