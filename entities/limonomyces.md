## Limonomyces

## Description

*Limonomyces* is a small genus of fungi in the family Corticiaceae (order Corticiales) known primarily as pathogens of turfgrass. Species within this genus cause diseases such as **pink patch** on lawn and turfgrass species. In *Teaming with Microbes*, *Limonomyces* is listed on page 146 among the fungal pathogens that can affect turfgrass and that are amenable to biological management through soil food web practices.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Eukarya
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Corticiales
- **Family:** Corticiaceae
- **Genus:** *Limonomyces*

## Key Facts

- Listed on page 146 of *Teaming with Microbes* among turfgrass diseases that can be suppressed through proper soil food web management.
- **Pink patch disease:** Caused primarily by *Limonomyces roseipellis*, this disease produces pinkish patches on the leaves of infected turfgrass.
- **Cool-season pathogen:** Most active during cool (15-22 C), moist conditions in spring and autumn. It preferentially attacks slow-growing turf that is deficient in nitrogen.
- **Superficial infection:** Unlike root-rot pathogens, *Limonomyces* primarily colonizes leaf surfaces and sheaths, causing damage that is often more cosmetic than lethal. Severely infected patches may thin but rarely kill the entire turf plant.
- **Corticioid affinities:** As members of the Corticiales, *Limonomyces* species are related to the crust fungi (corticioid basidiomycetes) commonly found decomposing wood. This evolutionary background in decomposition has been repurposed toward a parasitic lifestyle on grass tissue.
- Spread through **infected clippings, equipment, water splash, and wind**. The fungal structures (sclerotia and mycelial fragments) can survive in thatch and debris between outbreaks.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Turfgrass management:** Pink patch caused by *Limonomyces* is common on residential lawns, golf courses, and sports fields, particularly on perennial ryegrass, fine fescue, and Kentucky bluegrass.
- **Nitrogen deficiency indicator:** This disease often appears in under-fertilized turf. Adequate nitrogen fertilization (preferably from organic sources) promotes vigorous growth that outpaces the slow, superficial infection.
- **Biological suppression:** Applications of compost and compost tea introduce diverse microbial communities that compete with *Limonomyces* on the leaf surface and in the thatch layer.
- **Cultural controls:** Improving drainage, reducing thatch buildup, increasing air circulation, maintaining appropriate mowing heights, and avoiding evening irrigation all reduce the moisture conditions that favor *Limonomyces* infection.
- **Diagnostic value:** The presence of pink patch often indicates broader soil health issues — particularly nitrogen deficiency and poor soil biology. It serves as a visual cue that [[the-soil-food-web]] needs attention.
- **Mycological interest:** The evolutionary transition of *Limonomyces* from wood-decomposing ancestors to turf parasites illustrates the ecological flexibility of corticioid basidiomycetes and provides insights into how decomposer fungi can evolve pathogenic lifestyles.

## Ecological Role

In natural grassland ecosystems, *Limonomyces* and related genera function as minor parasites that primarily affect stressed or senescing grass plants. Their impact is generally limited by the diversity and health of the plant community. In monoculture turf systems, however, the uniform host population and artificial growing conditions can allow these fungi to reach economically significant levels. Understanding their biology reinforces the principle that diverse, well-nourished plant and soil communities are inherently more resistant to disease.
