## Overview

**Pyropia yezoensis** (Ueda) M.S. Hwang & H.G. Choi is a red marine macroalga in the family Bangiaceae. It is the primary species used for nori production in Japan, Korea, and China, making it the most economically valuable seaweed species globally.

The species has a heteromorphic life cycle alternating between a microscopic filamentous conchocelis phase and a macroscopic leaf-like gametophytic phase. Only the gametophyte is harvested for food production.

Annual nori production exceeds 2.5 billion sheets, with a market value of several billion US dollars. P. yezoensis cultivation represents one of the most sophisticated and productive forms of marine aquaculture.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Rhodophyta, Bangiophyceae, Bangiales, Bangiaceae, *Pyropia*. The authority is (Ueda) M.S. Hwang & H.G. Choi (2011). The basionym is *Porphyra yezoensis* Ueda (1932).

The genus *Pyropia* was erected in 2011 based on molecular phylogenetic evidence separating it from the traditional *Porphyra* sensu lato. This reclassification has been widely adopted in the scientific literature.

The gametophytic thallus is a single layer of cells, forming a thin, membranous sheet 15–35 cm long and 5–15 cm wide. The blade is 20–40 μm thick, translucent, and reddish-purple to dark brownish-red in color.

Cell structure reveals a single central stellate chloroplast per cell. The thallus attaches to substrates via a small basal holdfast cell and reproduces both sexually through spermatangia and carpogonia and asexually via archeospores.

## Distribution and Habitat

P. yezoensis is native to the northwest Pacific, occurring from the Kuril Islands through Hokkaido, Honshu, and Shikoku in Japan, along the Korean Peninsula, and in eastern China. It is most abundant in sheltered bays and estuaries.

The species grows in the upper intertidal zone, where it is periodically exposed to air during low tides. This intertidal habit is unusual among cultivated seaweeds and reflects its adaptation to fluctuating environmental conditions.

Optimal growth occurs at 10–20°C with moderate light intensity and salinities above 25 ppt. The species is winter-growing in most of its range, with peak biomass from November to April.

## Chemistry and Nutrition

Dried P. yezoensis (nori) is exceptionally nutritious, containing 25–50% protein by dry weight. It is one of the richest plant sources of vitamin B12, containing 77.6 μg per 100 g dry weight, though bioavailability in humans remains debated.

The species is rich in taurine (1.0–1.2% dry weight), an amino acid important for cardiovascular health. It also contains significant amounts of vitamins A, C, E, and K, as well as the carotenoids β-carotene and lutein.

Mineral content includes high levels of iodine (4.5–6.5 mg per 100 g dry weight), iron, zinc, calcium, and phosphorus. The mineral profile contributes to its traditional use as a dietary supplement.

Phycobiliproteins, including phycoerythrin and phycocyanin, constitute up to 10% of soluble protein. These pigments have antioxidant properties and are commercially extracted for use as natural colorants and fluorescent markers.

The lipid fraction is rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids, particularly eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, 20:5n-3), which comprises 30–50% of total fatty acids. This EPA content rivals that of many marine fish oils.

## Cultivation

Nori cultivation in Japan dates to the 17th century, when the vertical net method was developed. Modern cultivation uses horizontal or vertical nets deployed in estuaries and bays from September to April.

The conchocelis phase is maintained in indoor laboratory conditions on shell substrates throughout summer. In autumn, cool water temperatures trigger conchospore release, which settle on cultivation nets deployed at sea.

Harvesting involves mechanical cutting of the growing blade tips every 10–15 days, allowing regrowth for multiple harvests per season. Each net typically yields 3–5 harvest cycles before senescence.

After harvest, the thalli are rinsed, shredded, and processed into dried sheets using a paper-making technique. The resulting nori sheets are inspected, graded, and packaged for commercial distribution.

Yield ranges from 30–60 tonnes fresh weight per hectare per season. Japan produces approximately 300,000 tonnes annually, with China and Korea producing similar quantities.

## Culinary Use

Nori is the most widely consumed seaweed in the world, primarily as the wrapping for sushi rolls and onigiri (rice balls). It is also used as a condiment, garnish, and flavoring ingredient in various East Asian dishes.

The umami flavor of nori derives from its high glutamate content (1,000–1,500 mg per 100 g dry weight), comparable to kombu. Dried nori sheets are typically 18 × 20 cm and are roasted before consumption to enhance crispness.

Beyond sushi, nori is incorporated into ramen, noodle soups, rice dishes, and snack foods. A recent global trend has expanded nori use to Western cuisines, including nori chips, popcorn seasoning, and salad toppings.

## Related Species

Several Pyropia species are cultivated alongside P. yezoensis for nori production. *Pyropia tenera* was historically the dominant nori species in Japan and is still preferred for its superior flavor and texture.

*Pyropia haitanensis* is the primary nori species cultivated in southern China and has greater heat tolerance than P. yezoensis. It accounts for the majority of Chinese nori production.

*Pyropia pseudolinearis* is cultivated in Korea and produces thinner, more delicate sheets than P. yezoensis. The species is valued for its bright coloration and sweet flavor profile.

Natural hybridization occurs between cultivated Pyropia species, creating strains with combined agronomic traits. Selective breeding programs in Japan and Korea have developed cultivars with enhanced disease resistance and higher protein content.

## Life Cycle and Reproduction

*Pyropia yezoensis* exhibits a heteromorphic biphasic life cycle, one of the most distinctive among commercially cultivated seaweeds.

The macroscopic gametophyte blade is haploid, producing spermatangia (male) or carpogonia (female) along the thallus margins. Fertilization produces diploid zygotes that develop into carpospores without a visible carposporophyte phase.

Carpospores settle on calcareous substrates such as mollusk shells and develop into the microscopic diploid conchocelis phase. This filamentous endolithic stage was unknown until 1949, when Kathleen Drew-Baker linked it to the seasonal disappearance of Porphyra blades.

After several months of growth within the shell matrix, the conchocelis phase releases conchospores under autumn conditions of shorter day length and cooler temperatures. These conchospores germinate into new gametophyte blades, completing the cycle.

## Ecological and Economic Significance

Nori from *P. yezoensis* represents a multi-billion dollar global industry, with Japan, Korea, and China as the primary producing nations. The nori market in Japan alone exceeds ¥100 billion annually.

Aquaculture of *P. yezoensis* supports coastal communities throughout East Asia, providing employment in farming, processing, distribution, and related industries.

Climate change and ocean acidification pose emerging threats to cultivation, motivating research into heat-tolerant and disease-resistant strains through selective breeding and genomic approaches.

In natural settings, *P. yezoensis* provides habitat and foraging substrate for intertidal invertebrates including limpets, small crustaceans, and juvenile fish. It is susceptible to oomycete pathogens such as *Olpidiopsis* spp., which can devastate aquaculture crops.
