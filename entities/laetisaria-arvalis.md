# Laetisaria arvalis

Laetisaria arvalis is a soil-inhabiting basidiomycete fungus recognized as a potential biological control agent against soil-borne plant pathogenic fungi, particularly [[rhizoctonia-solani]] and Pythium species. It was first described and investigated as a biocontrol organism by Burdsall and colleagues (1980) and represents an early example of systematic screening for mycoparasitic fungi as alternatives to chemical pesticides in agriculture.

## Taxonomy and Classification

L. arvalis belongs to the Aphyllophorales (Corticiaceae) within the Basidiomycota. Its classification among the corticioid fungi reflects its resupinate, crust-like basidiocarp morphology, typical of fungi that grow flat on their substrates. Despite its basidiomycete affinities and relationship to wood-decay fungi, L. arvalis is primarily studied for its antagonistic interactions with plant pathogenic fungi in soil environments rather than for its wood-decaying capabilities.

## Discovery and Biocontrol Potential

L. arvalis was investigated as a biological control agent for [[rhizoctonia-solani]], one of the most important soil-borne plant pathogens worldwide. R. solani causes damping-off, root rot, and stem canker on a wide range of crop plants, and its control has been a major focus of plant pathology research for decades. The fungus is among approximately 30 species of mycoparasites recorded for R. solani (Butler 1957; Chand and Logan 1984; Jeffries 1995).

### Efficacy Against Rhizoctonia solani

Key findings from biocontrol studies include:

- L. arvalis demonstrates antagonistic activity against R. solani in controlled conditions
- Nonpathogenic conspecific isolates and binucleate isolates of Rhizoctonia species (now reclassified as Ceratorhiza) can control R. solani even better in some systems, such as sugar beet (Ichielevich-Auster et al. 1985; Herr 1988; Lewis and Papavizas 1992)
- The mechanism of antagonism is not fully understood, although it may involve competition for nutrients and space rather than direct mycoparasitism

### Comparison with Other Biocontrol Agents

While L. arvalis showed early promise as a biocontrol agent, subsequent research has focused on other mycoparasites as more practical agents for controlling R. solani:

- **[[trichoderma-harzianum]]** and other Trichoderma species are both more common and more efficient mycoparasites of R. solani, using complex methods of action involving chitinase and beta-1,3-glucanase (Elad et al. 1984)
- **[[trichoderma-virens]]** affects host fungi mainly with its toxic metabolites gliotoxin and gliovirin (Tu 1980; Howell and Stipanovic 1983)
- **[[verticillium-biguttatum]]** has been used successfully on a large scale against R. solani, particularly with the development of "green-crop harvesting" techniques (Mulder et al. 1992)
- **Binucleate Rhizoctonia** isolates (Ceratorhiza) have shown superior control efficacy in certain crop systems

## Isolation and Selective Culture

L. arvalis was isolated selectively by placing soil pellets or table beet seeds that had been buried in soil on a selective medium containing an ethanol solution of phenol, dichloran, benomyl, and thiabendazol with antibacterial antibiotics (Papavizas et al. 1983). This technique allowed detection of concentrations as low as seven propagules per gram of soil, making it a valuable method for quantifying the biocontrol agent in soil ecosystems.

The development of selective media for L. arvalis was an important methodological advance, allowing researchers to:

- Monitor populations of the biocontrol agent in field soils
- Study the survival and spread of introduced L. arvalis inoculum
- Compare different formulations and application strategies
- Assess the impact of agricultural practices on biocontrol agent populations

## Interaction with Pythium Species

In addition to its activity against R. solani, L. arvalis has demonstrated antagonistic potential against Pythium species, broadening its applicability as a biocontrol agent for damping-off diseases caused by multiple soil-borne pathogens. The ability to target both Rhizoctonia and Pythium makes L. arvalis particularly interesting for integrated disease management in crops susceptible to multiple soil-borne pathogens.

## Ecological Role in Soil

L. arvalis is a naturally occurring soil fungus, found in agricultural soils where it may contribute to the suppressiveness of certain soils to plant diseases. Its presence in soil is influenced by:

- Agricultural management practices including tillage and crop rotation
- Soil organic matter content and composition
- Soil moisture and temperature conditions
- The native soil microbial community composition

The fungus can be enriched in soil by the repeated addition of the host fungus, a strategy that has been applied to [[verticillium-biguttatum]] using R. solani as bait (van den Boogert and Jager 1983). A similar approach could potentially be used to establish and maintain L. arvalis populations in agricultural soils.

## Mechanism of Antagonism

The mechanism by which L. arvalis antagonizes R. solani is not fully understood. Unlike [[trichoderma-harzianum]], which exhibits directed hyphal growth toward R. solani, mostly coiling around the host, sometimes forming appressoria, and penetrating the host hyphae, L. arvalis may act primarily through competition. The involvement of antibiotic production or enzyme-mediated lysis has not been conclusively demonstrated for L. arvalis, though these mechanisms are common among mycoparasitic fungi.

## Research Significance

L. arvalis was among the first basidiomycete fungi to be systematically evaluated as a biocontrol agent for soil-borne plant pathogens. The research conducted on this species contributed to the broader understanding of mycoparasitism as a mechanism for biological control and helped establish methodological approaches for screening and evaluating potential biocontrol organisms.

## Morphological Characteristics

L. arvalis produces a resupinate (crust-like) basidiocarp that grows flat on its substrate, typically soil particles, decaying organic matter, or the surface of culture media. The basidiocarp is thin, membranous, and white to cream-colored, often extending as a flat mycelial mat that may be difficult to detect without careful examination. The hyphal system is monomitic, consisting of generative hyphae without clamp connections. Basidia are cylindrical to clavate and produce four sterigmata, bearing basidiospores that are ellipsoidal to oblong, thin-walled, and hyaline. The inconspicuous nature of its fruiting structures means that L. arvalis is more commonly detected through selective culture methods than through direct observation in the field.

## Geographical Distribution

L. arvalis has been documented primarily from temperate agricultural soils, with records from North America and Europe. Its distribution is likely wider than currently documented, as the inconspicuous nature of its fruiting structures and the need for selective culture methods mean that it is probably under-reported in soil mycological surveys. The fungus is most likely to be encountered in agricultural fields where crops susceptible to [[rhizoctonia-solani]] are grown, as the presence of the host fungus may enrich for L. arvalis populations through competitive interactions. Further systematic surveys using the selective medium developed by Papavizas and colleagues would likely expand the known distribution of this species.

## Soil Ecology and Survival Strategies

As a soil-inhabiting basidiomycete, L. arvalis faces the challenge of surviving in a complex and competitive environment. Soil fungi must contend with fluctuating moisture conditions, competition from bacteria and other fungi for limited nutrients, and the presence of antagonistic organisms. L. arvalis likely persists in soil through the production of resistant structures such as thick-walled hyphal segments or sclerotium-like aggregations, though detailed studies of its survival structures are limited. Its ability to antagonize soil-borne plant pathogens may confer an ecological advantage by reducing competition from rapidly growing pathogenic fungi that would otherwise dominate the soil microbial community.

## Agricultural Application Strategies

The potential use of L. arvalis in agricultural systems involves several approaches to introducing and maintaining the biocontrol agent in soil:

- **Soil incorporation**: Direct addition of L. arvalis inoculum to soil at planting time, either as a culture preparation or as colonized organic substrate
- **Seed coating**: Application of L. arvalis to seeds before planting, ensuring proximity between the biocontrol agent and the emerging seedling roots
- **Crop rotation**: Management of cropping sequences to maintain or enhance L. arvalis populations in soil between susceptible crops
- **Organic amendments**: Use of compost or other organic amendments that support the growth and activity of L. arvalis and other beneficial soil microorganisms

These strategies reflect the broader principles of conservation biological control, where agricultural practices are managed to support naturally occurring or introduced biocontrol agents rather than relying solely on inundative applications of microbial preparations.

## Comparative Analysis with Trichoderma-Based Biocontrol

The comparison between L. arvalis and Trichoderma-based biocontrol systems highlights important differences in biocontrol agent selection. [[trichoderma-harzianum]] and [[trichoderma-virens]] are among the most commercially successful fungal biocontrol agents, with well-characterized mechanisms of action including mycoparasitism, antibiosis, and competition. L. arvalis, as a basidiomycete, represents a fundamentally different taxonomic group with potentially different ecological capabilities. While Trichoderma species are primarily soil saprotrophs that secondarily attack other fungi, L. arvalis may have evolved more specialized antagonistic capabilities against soil-borne basidiomycete pathogens. The exploration of basidiomycete biocontrol agents beyond L. arvalis remains an underdeveloped area of research with significant potential for discovering new modes of action.

## Legacy and Influence on Biocontrol Research

Although L. arvalis has not been developed into a commercial biocontrol product, the research conducted on this species established important precedents for the systematic evaluation of mycoparasitic fungi as plant disease control agents. The work of Burdsall and colleagues (1980) and Papavizas and colleagues (1983) on L. arvalis demonstrated that soil basidiomycetes could be isolated selectively, enumerated quantitatively, and evaluated for biocontrol potential using standardized methods. These methodological contributions have been adopted and adapted by subsequent researchers working with a wide range of fungal biocontrol agents, extending the influence of L. arvalis research well beyond the species itself.

## Interaction with Soil Fauna

L. arvalis, like many soil fungi, likely interacts with soil fauna including nematodes, microarthropods, and earthworms. These interactions may influence the dispersal, survival, and biocontrol efficacy of the fungus in soil. Soil fauna can transport fungal propagules through the soil profile, create channels that facilitate hyphal growth, and modify the soil environment in ways that affect fungal activity. The potential for combining fungal biocontrol agents like L. arvalis with soil fauna management represents an area of integrated pest management that warrants further investigation.
