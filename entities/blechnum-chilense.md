## Overview

Blechnum chilense (Kaulf.) C. Chr., commonly known as the Chilean hard fern or costilla de vaca, is a large evergreen fern in the family Blechnaceae. It is native to southern Chile and Argentina, where it is a dominant understory species in temperate rainforests.

The species is one of the largest Blechnum ferns, producing fronds up to 1.5 m tall that form imposing, trunkless clumps. Its common name "costilla de vaca" (cow's rib) refers to the prominent, rib-like fertile fronds that stand erect above the sterile foliage.

Blechnum chilense is a dimorphic fern, producing separate sterile and fertile fronds with distinctly different morphology. The sterile fronds are broad and spreading, while the fertile fronds are narrow and upright.

The species has gained popularity in temperate horticulture for its dramatic architectural form and lush, tropical appearance despite being fully hardy in mild temperate climates. It is increasingly planted in woodland gardens worldwide.

In its native Chile, B. chilense is one of the most conspicuous and recognizable fern species, forming vast stands along roadsides and forest margins throughout the southern half of the country.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Division: Polypodiophyta
- Class: Polypodiopsida
- Order: Polypodiales
- Family: Blechnaceae
- Genus: Blechnum
- Species: *B. chilense* (Kaulf.) C. Chr.

**Basionym:** *Lomaria chilensis* Kaulf. (1824). The species has also been placed in the genus *Spicantopsis* and *Lomaria* by various authors. The current placement in Blechnum follows the Pteridophyte Phylogeny Group classification.

Sterile fronds are 60–150 cm long and 20–35 cm broad, pinnate with 30–50 pairs of pinnae. The pinnae are sessile, linear-lanceolate, 10–18 cm long, with serrate margins and a coriaceous texture.

Fertile fronds are erect, 80–150 cm tall but only 3–5 cm broad, with strongly reduced pinnae that bear continuous sori along the margins. These narrow, spike-like fronds are dramatically different from the sterile foliage.

The stipe is stout, 20–40 cm long, covered at the base with dark brown, entire scales. The rachis is firm and dark-colored, providing structural support for the large fronds.

Sori are continuous along the pinna margins of fertile fronds, protected by a linear indusium that opens inward.

Spores are pale yellow, bilateral, and measure 35–45 µm in diameter. Spore release occurs over an extended period from November to March in the southern hemisphere, coinciding with the warmest and driest season.

## Distribution and Habitat

Blechnum chilense is endemic to southern South America, occurring from approximately 35°S in central Chile to 55°S in Tierra del Fuego. It also occurs in adjacent areas of western Argentina in the Andean foothills.

The species is a characteristic component of the Valdivian temperate rainforest, one of the world's most biodiverse temperate forest ecosystems. It forms extensive stands in forest clearings, along streams, and on disturbed ground.

It grows from sea level to approximately 1200 m in the Andes, preferring moist, humus-rich soils with good drainage. The species tolerates a wide range of light conditions from deep shade to open, disturbed sites.

Annual rainfall throughout much of its range exceeds 2000 mm, and the species is poorly adapted to seasonal drought. This moisture dependency limits its cultivation potential in Mediterranean and continental climates.

In the fjord region of southern Chile, B. chilense is often the first vascular plant to colonize landslide scars and recently deglaciated terrain. Its pioneering ability makes it ecologically important in primary succession.

The species shows remarkable tolerance of volcanic ash deposition, quickly resprouting from its rhizome after burial. This resilience was dramatically demonstrated following the 2008 eruption of Chaitén volcano in southern Chile.

## Ecology

Blechnum chilense plays a dominant role in the understory of Nothofagus (southern beech) forests throughout its range. Its dense frond canopy shades the ground, suppressing herbaceous competitors and creating a distinctive microclimate.

The species forms extensive, clonal colonies through a creeping rhizome system that can spread several meters from the parent plant. These colonies persist for decades, often outlasting the canopy trees that initially provided shade.

As a pioneer species, B. chilense stabilizes disturbed soils and accumulates organic matter that facilitates later-successional species establishment. Its role in primary succession after volcanic eruptions has been documented in southern Chile.

The fronds provide important habitat for forest-floor invertebrates and amphibians. In the Valdivian forest, the ground-level humidity retained by Blechnum clumps is critical for the survival of endemic frog species.

Decaying fronds contribute significantly to forest floor organic matter, forming a thick humus layer that supports diverse fungal and microbial communities.

The species is classified as Least Concern by IUCN standards due to its extensive range and abundant populations. However, habitat loss from commercial forestry plantations of exotic pine and eucalyptus threatens some northern populations.

## Cultivation

Blechnum chilense is cultivated as a dramatic foliage plant for woodland gardens, damp borders, and shaded architectural plantings. It is hardy to USDA zones 8–10, tolerating brief freezes to approximately −8°C.

The species requires consistently moist, humus-rich soil in partial to full shade. It tolerates sun in cool, maritime climates but suffers in hot, dry conditions. Shelter from cold winds is essential in exposed sites.

Established plants spread slowly via creeping rhizomes, eventually forming impressive clumps 2–3 m across. This spreading habit should be considered when siting the plant, as it can overwhelm smaller companions.

Propagation is by spores or rhizome division. Spores germinate readily on sterile media at 15–20°C. Division of established clumps is best performed in early spring, ensuring each section includes active growing points.

The species is increasingly available from specialty fern nurseries in Europe, North America, and Australasia. Its dramatic form has made it popular in contemporary garden design.

In the Pacific Northwest of North America, B. chilense has naturalized in some coastal areas, where its spreading habit and shade tolerance allow it to persist in disturbed woodland habitats.

## Ethnobotany

The indigenous Mapuche people of southern Chile use Blechnum chilense fronds in traditional medicine for treating skin conditions and wounds. The fronds are applied as poultices, leveraging their antimicrobial properties.

The species has been used as emergency livestock fodder during severe winters in rural Chile. The tough, leathery fronds provide roughage when other forage is unavailable beneath snow cover.

In Mapuche basketry, the strong stipes of B. chilense are woven into utilitarian containers. The durable fiber resists decay, making it suitable for storage baskets and fishing creels.

Modern research has confirmed antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity in B. chilense extracts. Several caffeic acid derivatives have been isolated that show promising activity in cell-based assays.

The species is also being investigated as a source of natural antioxidants for food preservation applications. Extracts from B. chilense fronds have shown efficacy in preventing lipid oxidation in meat products.
