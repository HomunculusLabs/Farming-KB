## Overview

**Cyathea interrupta** (Hook.) Copel., formerly known as *Cibotium chamissonis* in some treatments, is a large arborescent fern endemic to the Hawaiian Islands. It is one of the dominant tree fern species in Hawaiian wet forests.

The species is known locally as hāpuʻu iʻi or simply hāpuʻu, names shared with other Hawaiian tree ferns in the Cibotium and Cyathea genera. It reaches heights of 4–8 meters with a stout trunk and spreading frond crown.

Hawaiian tree ferns hold deep cultural significance in Native Hawaiian tradition. The woolly trunk fibers (pulu) were used as a wound dressing, stuffing material, and embalming agent in pre-contact Hawai'i.

Cyathea interrupta is distinguished from co-occurring Cibotium species by its darker trunk scales and differences in sorus morphology. Reclassification from Cibotium has been supported by molecular phylogenetics.

Along with Cibotium glaucum and Cibotium chamissoi, Cyathea interrupta forms the characteristic tree fern layer of Hawaiian montane wet forests. The three species often co-occur but partition the understory by elevation and moisture preference.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Polypodiophyta
- **Class:** Polypodiopsida
- **Order:** Cyatheales
- **Family:** Cyatheaceae
- **Genus:** Cyathea
- **Species:** Cyathea interrupta (Hook.) Copel.

The species was originally described as *Cibotium chamissonis* by Kaulfuss and later transferred to Cyathea by Copeland. The taxonomic history reflects ongoing revision of Hawaiian tree ferns.

Molecular studies using chloroplast markers have confirmed its placement in Cyatheaceae rather than Dicksoniaceae, where Cibotium resides. The reclassification was formalized in recent floras of Hawai'i.

Key synonyms include *Cibotium chamissonis* Kaulf. and *Alsophila interrupta* (Hook.) R.M.Tryon. The specific epithet "interrupta" refers to the interrupted pattern of sori on the fertile fronds.

Phylogenetic analysis places Cyathea interrupta within a Pacific clade of Cyathea species, distinct from the Caribbean and Australasian lineages. Its closest relatives are likely other Pacific island endemics with similar dispersal histories.

## Morphology

The trunk is erect and robust, typically 10–20 cm in diameter, reaching 4–8 meters tall. The surface is densely covered with persistent stipe bases and a thick layer of adventitious roots.

Fronds are bipinnate to tripinnate, 2–4 meters long, forming a broad crown. Stipes are dark brown, densely scaly, and armed with small prickles along the lower surface.

The scales on the stipe base are glossy dark brown to blackish, lanceolate, and entire. This dark coloration distinguishes Cyathea interrupta from the lighter brown scales of Cibotium glaucum.

Pinnae are broadly lanceolate with deeply divided pinnules. Fertile pinnules bear sori on the undersides, positioned near the margin with a reduced indusium. Spores are pale and trilete.

The croziers (emerging fronds) are densely covered in dark brown scales and pale woolly fibers. This pulu was the material traditionally harvested by Native Hawaiians for various practical uses.

Individual plants may produce 15–30 fronds per year, with older fronds dying back and hanging pendant from the trunk for months. The crown typically holds 8–15 active fronds at any given time, creating a dense canopy overhead.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cyathea interrupta is endemic to the main Hawaiian Islands, occurring on Kaua'i, O'ahu, Moloka'i, Maui, and Hawai'i Island. It is absent from the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands and smaller islets.

The species inhabits wet and mesic forests at elevations of 300–1,800 meters. It is most abundant in the windward montane forests of each island, particularly on slopes receiving orographic rainfall.

On younger lava substrates, Cyathea interrupta is among the first tree fern species to establish, often appearing within 20–30 years of a flow cooling. It acts as a pioneer in primary succession on volcanic surfaces.

Prime habitat includes the Alaka'i Swamp on Kaua'i, the Ko'olau Mountains of O'ahu, and the windward slopes of Haleakalā on Maui. On Hawai'i Island, it occurs in the Hilo and Puna districts.

Cyathea interrupta prefers sites with annual rainfall exceeding 2,500 mm and frequent cloud immersion. It grows on volcanic soils, both 'a'ā and pāhoehoe substrates, where organic matter has accumulated.

Populations are healthiest in intact native forest with an overstory of 'ōhi'a lehua (*Metrosideros polymorpha*) and koa (*Acacia koa*). Disturbed sites with invasive species show reduced recruitment.

The species shows moderate shade tolerance but grows fastest in light gaps created by canopy treefall. In deep shade, fronds become elongated and sparser as the plant directs energy toward reaching higher light levels.

## Ecology and Conservation

As a dominant understory species, Cyathea interrupta creates microhabitat structure essential for native epiphytes and invertebrates. Its trunk provides substrate for filmy ferns, mosses, and endemic Hawaiian orchids.

The endemic Hawaiian happy-face spider (Theridion grallator) is frequently found on the undersides of Cyathea interrupta fronds. The frond architecture provides ideal hunting grounds for these small, cryptic spiders.

The species regenerates readily after disturbance, colonizing lava flows and landslide scars where moisture is sufficient. Frond production continues year-round, with peaks during the wet season from November to March.

Habitat degradation from feral pigs, goats, and invasive plants poses the primary conservation threat. Pigs uproot tree fern seedlings and create disturbed soil favorable for invasive species establishment.

Climate change threatens Hawaiian montane forests through reduced rainfall and increased temperature. The "cloud-lifting" effect reduces cloud forest area, pushing suitable habitat upslope toward island summits.

The species is not currently listed as threatened by IUCN, but Hawaiian conservation agencies consider it a species of concern due to ongoing habitat loss. Fencing and invasive species removal benefit populations.

Cyathea interrupta provides critical watershed protection in Hawaiian montane forests. The trunk's root mantle acts as a sponge, capturing fog drip and slow-releasing moisture into the watershed throughout dry periods.

## Cultivation and Uses

Cyathea interrupta is occasionally cultivated in Hawaiian gardens and botanical collections. It requires consistent moisture, partial shade, and protection from wind to maintain healthy fronds.

The species is sensitive to transplanting; established specimens resent root disturbance. Container-grown plants should be moved to their permanent location while young, before the trunk has formed.

Outside of Hawai'i, Cyathea interrupta is rare in cultivation due to collection restrictions and difficulty matching its exacting humidity requirements. A few specimens thrive in conservatories at botanical gardens in Florida and Southeast Asia.

Traditional Hawaiian uses of hāpuʻu were extensive. The pulu (trunk fiber) served as wound dressing, fire-starting tinder, and stuffing for pillows and mattresses. Trunk cores were used as famine food.

In modern horticulture, the species is propagated from spores collected from wild populations. Germination requires sterile conditions and takes 6–12 weeks. Juvenile plants grow slowly, forming a visible trunk after 4–6 years.

The starchy trunk core was baked in imu (underground ovens) during times of food scarcity. While not palatable by modern standards, it provided essential carbohydrates when other crops failed after storms or volcanic events.
