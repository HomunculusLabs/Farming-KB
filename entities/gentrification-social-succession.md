## Gentrification (Social Succession)

## Description

Gentrification is a process of neighbourhood transformation in which rundown inner-city areas are progressively occupied by wealthier residents, displacing the original working-class and low-income inhabitants. David Holmgren analyses gentrification through the lens of ecological succession, treating it as a social analogue to the ecological processes of pioneer species colonisation, climax development, and system renewal. He observes that gentrification follows a multi-phase successional pattern that may itself be a form of pulsing or four-phase system cycle, with implications for understanding how communities form, transform, and regenerate.

## The Successional Pattern of Gentrification

Holmgren describes gentrification as proceeding through recognisable phases that parallel ecological succession:

### Phase 1: Pioneer (Bohemian)
Students, artists, and other trendsetters are attracted to physically rundown but socially dynamic working-class inner-city areas. These "pioneers" create cultural capital through galleries, cafes, studios, and alternative lifestyles. They tolerate poor housing conditions in exchange for affordable rent and vibrant community life.

### Phase 2: Renovator
Owner-renovators are attracted by the cultural vibrancy created by the pioneers and by affordable housing prices. They invest in property improvements, raising the visual appeal and amenity of the area, but also raising rental and resale values.

### Phase 3: Displacement
Rising property values push out many of the original working-class occupants and the pioneer settlers who can no longer afford to live in the area they helped create. This parallels how early successional species are shaded out by later arrivals.

### Phase 4: Professional / Climax
Investor owners rent out renovated townhouses to upwardly mobile young professionals. By this stage, the dynamic community that attracted the original bohemians has often declined or disappeared entirely.

### Phase 5: Decline and Renewal
Absent and disinterested owners, increasing crime, and eventually declining values create a new opportunity for the poor to obtain shelter and create the possibility of community renewal — beginning the cycle again.

## Key Facts

- Over the last 30 years (from Holmgren's writing), gentrification transformed the old inner suburbs of most cities of the Western world.
- The process is analogous to ecological succession in that pioneer "species" (bohemians, artists) create conditions suitable for other "species" (renovators, professionals).
- The pioneers rarely view the arrival of new migrants and tourists with equanimity, seeing newcomers as unappreciative consumers of the cultural resources they created.
- There is evidence of further stages beyond simple gentrification, potentially forming a pulsing or four-phase system of urban social change.
- A similar social succession process occurs in rural areas when back-to-the-land migrants revitalise depressed rural communities.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Social succession model:** Understanding social succession helps permaculture designers recognise that community development follows ecological patterns, with predictable phases and dynamics that can be anticipated and worked with.

- **Rural analogue:** The same successional process operates in rural areas, where alternative-lifestyle pioneers create the social infrastructure that later attracts mainstream settlers and tourists — as happened across rural Australia from the 1970s.

- **Design implications:** If community development follows successional patterns, permaculture designers can anticipate and work with these processes rather than being surprised or disappointed by them.

- **Resilience thinking:** The cyclical nature of gentrification and decline suggests that stable climax communities are not permanent endpoints but phases in a larger pulsing cycle.

- **Bioregional culture:** Understanding social succession helps in designing bioregional cultures that can maintain identity and resilience through cycles of change.

## Connections

- Analogous to ecological succession and the four-phase ecosystem model (Holling's cycle)
- Related to the back-to-the-land movement as a form of social pioneering in rural areas
- Links to the concept of "nouveau riche succession" in family wealth patterns across generations
- Illustrates the permaculture principle of creatively using and responding to change in a social context
- Demonstrates the same dynamics described in Jane Jacobs' urban neighbourhood analysis
