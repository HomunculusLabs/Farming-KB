## Hans Tscherter

## Description

Hans Tscherter was Albert Hofmann"s capable laboratory assistant at Sandoz Pharmaceuticals in Basel, Switzerland, who participated in two of Hofmann's most important discoveries: the isolation of the active principles of the sacred mushroom teonanacatl (psilocybin and psilocin) and the isolation of the active principles of the Mexican magic morning glory ololiuhqui (lysergic acid amides). Tscherter's skilled laboratory work was essential to the success of both investigations, and Hofmann specifically acknowledged his contribution in LSD: My Problem Child.

## Classification

- **Type**: Person, laboratory technician / chemist
- **Employer**: Sandoz Pharmaceuticals, Basel, Switzerland
- **Role**: Laboratory assistant to Albert Hofmann
- **Key contributions**: Isolation of psilocybin, psilocin, and ololiuhqui active principles

## Key Facts

- Described by Hofmann as his "capable laboratory assistant"
- Participated in the chemical investigation of the Mexican magic mushrooms (teonanacatl), contributing to the isolation of psilocybin and psilocin
- Participated in the chemical investigation of ololiuhqui seeds, contributing to the isolation of lysergic acid amide, lysergic acid hydroxyethylamide, and ergobasine
- Advanced the working hypothesis that the active principles of ololiuhqui could be indole compounds (the same class as LSD, psilocybin, and psilocin), which proved correct
- Used colorimetric tests to confirm the presence of indole compounds in ololiuhqui extracts, which gave the characteristic blue coloration
- His skilled laboratory technique enabled the rapid isolation and purification of active compounds from challenging plant materials
- Worked alongside Hofmann during the period of Sandoz's most significant contributions to psychopharmacology

## The Ololiuhqui Discovery

The ololiuhqui investigation demonstrated Tscherter's contributions to psychopharmacological research. He and Hofmann hypothesized that the active principles might be indole compounds, an extremely improbable assumption given the vast number of other possible chemical classes. Using colorimetric tests for indoles, they quickly confirmed their hypothesis and succeeded in isolating the active principles in pure form. The identification revealed that the ancient Mexican magic drug contained lysergic acid amides identical to compounds already present in Hofmann's laboratory from decades of ergot research.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Tscherter's laboratory work contributed directly to the isolation of psilocybin and psilocin from Psilocybe mexicana, the active compounds that define the genus Psilocybe and are the basis of modern psilocybin research. Without skilled laboratory technicians like Tscherter, the transition from ethnographic knowledge of psychoactive mushrooms to precise chemical understanding and pharmaceutical development would not have been possible. His contribution illustrates the often unheralded but essential role of laboratory technicians in advancing scientific knowledge.

## Scientific Collaboration

The working relationship between Hofmann and Tscherter exemplified the traditional model of chemist-assistant collaboration that produced many of the most important pharmaceutical discoveries of the twentieth century. Hofmann formulated the hypotheses and directed the research program, while Tscherter executed the laboratory work with skill and precision. This division of labor was standard in pharmaceutical research of the era, though modern science increasingly recognizes the contributions of laboratory technicians as co-equal partners in the research enterprise. Tscherter's willingness to pursue Hofmann's seemingly improbable indole hypothesis for ololiuhqui, and his technical ability to perform the necessary extractions and tests, were essential to the success of the investigation.

## The Doubt at Sydney

When Hofmann presented the ololiuhqui findings at the IUPAC Natural Products Congress in Sydney in 1960, the audience of chemists received the results with deep skepticism. The notion that lysergic acid compounds, previously known only from the lower fungus ergot (Claviceps purpurea), could occur in higher plants of the Convolvulaceae family seemed too improbable to be believed. The fact that these compounds had been isolated through Tscherter's careful laboratory work, using standard analytical methods with reproducible results, eventually overcame the initial resistance. Subsequent confirmation by laboratories in the United States, Germany, and Holland established the finding as one of the most surprising discoveries in phytochemistry.

## The Significance of the Ololiuhqui Discovery

The discovery of lysergic acid derivatives in ololiuhqui seeds by Tscherter and Hofmann had implications far beyond the identification of a new psychoactive plant. It demonstrated that ergoline alkaloids, previously thought to be restricted to the fungal genus Claviceps (ergot), also occurred naturally in higher plants of the family Convolvulaceae. This finding expanded the known biosynthetic capabilities of higher plants and opened new avenues for research into the evolution and distribution of psychoactive compounds in nature. The chemical kinship between the ergot alkaloids from which LSD was derived and the morning glory compounds confirmed the deep structural relationships between naturally occurring psychoactive substances that span fungi, plants, and the human nervous system.
