## Description

Agaricus bulbosus is an obsolete binomial name used in 19th-century mycological literature to refer to a poisonous member of the [[amanita-genus]].

[[julius-auboineau-palmer]], writing in the 1870s-1880s, cited this name from the work of French mycologist Dr. F. S. Cordier. The name reflects the historical taxonomic practice of placing many gilled mushrooms under the broad genus Agaricus before modern reclassification.

Based on Palmer's and Cordier's descriptions, this species corresponds to a bulbous-based Amanita. The "bulbosus" epithet refers to the swollen, bulbous base of the stem, a characteristic feature of many dangerous Amanita species.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Amanitaceae
- **Genus:** Amanita (historically placed in Agaricus)

## Key Facts

- **Toxicity:** Extremely poisonous, responsible for fatal mushroom poisonings
- **Folk test failure:** Cordier tested this species with silver and gold during cooking; neither metal blackened
- **Visual appeal:** Despite its lethal nature, the [[bloomfield-fungal-fruiting-body-morphology-ecological-function]] is attractive and not obviously dangerous
- **Historical significance:** Used as a key example by Cordier to debunk folk [[forensic-identification-methods-psilocybin-mushrooms-tlc-gc-hplc]]
- **Treatment (19th century):** Only hypodermic injection of atropia offered any hope, and even this was experimental

## Identification Features

Palmer described the identifying characteristics of Amanita species including A. bulbosus:

- **Volva (wrapper):** A white membranous cup around the base of the stem, close to the soil
- **Annulus (ring):** A veil-like membrane encircling the stem approximately two-thirds up from the ground
- **Cap warts:** Scurfy, irregular warts on the cap surface, well-defined in young specimens
- **Gills and stem:** Always pure white
- **Cap color:** Variable — white, drab, golden, red, or greenish
- **Texture:** Soft and sticky when moist

Palmer emphasized that all three marks — volva, annulus, and cap warts — must be present together to identify the sub-genus Amanita with confidence.

## Cordier's Testing

Dr. Cordier specifically used A. bulbosus to test the widely-believed [[silver-spoon-test]] method for detecting mushroom poison.

The folk belief held that if silver or gold turned black during cooking, the mushrooms were poisonous. If the metal remained bright, they were safe.

Cordier's findings were definitive:

1. The mushroom was cooked with silver implements
2. Neither silver nor gold blackened upon contact
3. This proved the folk test was completely unreliable
4. A lethal mushroom passed the "safety test" without any visible reaction

Cordier stated: "Whether silver or gold grow black or remain brilliant in contact with mushrooms, the conclusion is equally of no value."

## Toxicity and Treatment

Palmer described the poison as "a subtle alkaloid, not to be detected by smell or taste, yet surer than arsenic in its fatal consequences to human life."

The only known treatment mentioned was hypodermic injection of atropia or some other alkaloid from the nightshade family. Palmer described this as "an antidotal measure yet in its infancy" and noted that physicians were "absolutely powerless to save life, however prompt and energetic the treatment."

## Nomenclature Notes

The name "Agaricus bulbosus" reflects pre-modern taxonomic conventions. As mycology developed, the genus Amanita was separated from Agaricus to accommodate species with volva and annulus characteristics.

Palmer's reference to "Agaricus bulbosus" as "a variety of the Amanita family" shows the transitional state of [[fungal-taxonomy]] in the late 19th century.

Modern equivalents of the name may refer to several Amanita species, including those in the [[amanita-phalloides]] group ([[amanita-phalloides-death-cap]] relatives).

## Relevance to Mycology

1. **Taxonomic history:** Illustrates the reclassification from Agaricus to Amanita as mycological taxonomy evolved
2. **Toxicology reference:** Represents the class of mushrooms containing amatoxins responsible for most fatal poisonings
3. **Folk test debunking:** Cordier's experiments with this species provided empirical evidence against dangerous identification shortcuts
4. **Forager education:** Palmer used this species as a primary example when teaching the public about mushroom dangers

## Historical Context

This species was central to Palmer's argument that no general rule can detect [[identifying-poisonous-mushrooms]]. Its ability to pass every folk safety test while remaining lethal made it the perfect counterexample to popular but dangerous beliefs about [[bloomfield-angel-of-death-poisonous-mushroom-identification-toxinology]].
