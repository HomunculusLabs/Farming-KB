# Gymnopilus luteofolius

A psilocybin-containing mushroom of the genus Gymnopilus, notable for its distinctive purple-red to orangish scaly cap and its bluing reaction that may be temperature-dependent.

Originally described as Pholiota luteofolia (Peck) Saccardo, this species was later transferred to the genus Gymnopilus by Singer.

While its analysis has not yet been formally reported in the scientific literature, Stamets considers it likely active based on its pronounced bluing reaction, particularly in cold weather.

It is one of at least 11 Gymnopilus species worldwide confirmed or suspected to contain psilocybin.

## Morphology

Cap: 2-6 (8) cm broad, convex at first, expanding with maturity to broadly convex to nearly plane.

Surface is dry, covered with dense, often fibrillose and appressed purple-red to orangish scales that disappear in age.

Color ranges from dark red to reddish brown, fading in drying to pinkish red or yellowish red, and eventually yellow.

The cap sometimes bruises bluish-green, especially in cold weather.

Margin is even, inrolled to incurved when young, soon straightening, adorned with fibrillose veil remnants.

Flesh is thick, reddish to purplish, then fading to yellowish in drying.

Gills are adnate to sinuate to slightly decurrent, close to subdistant and broad.

Gills are initially yellow, then become rusty orange, with serrated edges.

Stem: 30-80 mm long by 3-10 mm thick.

Stem is equal to enlarged downwards when forming singly, and narrow at the base.

Color is yellowish or stained rust-colored in age.

Base of the stem sometimes bruises bluish.

Partial veil is densely cortinate to nearly membranous, leaving a superior membranous annulus or fibrillose annular zone soon dusted with rusty orange spores.

Microscopic features: Spores are rusty orange in deposit, ellipsoid and roughened, measuring 5.5-8.5 by 3.5-4.5 um.

Basidia are 4-spored.

Pleurocystidia are 30-38 by 5-10 um, fusoid to subventricose.

Cheilocystidia are 23-28 by 4-7 um, ventricose to flask-shaped with or without a swollen head.

## Habitat and Distribution

Common on woody debris and on wood chips used in nurseries and landscaping.

Found from California to British Columbia, fruiting from June through November.

[[david-arora]] has found it along the California coastal region on wood chips of Eucalyptus and pine.

Hesler (1969) reported it widely scattered across the United States including Texas, Florida, New York, Tennessee, Michigan, and New Mexico.

Primarily found on conifer wood, less commonly on cottonwoods and other hardwoods.

Also occurs on stumps and logs, growing when forming singly and often curving when growing in clusters.

## Cultivation

Clones of Stamets' collection on Cortes Island, British Columbia, have successfully produced mushrooms in culture.

The mushroom can be easily cultivated on hardwood logs and wood chips using standard techniques.

## Chemistry and Potency

Considered mildly to moderately active according to anecdotal reports.

The bluing phenomenon may be temperature-driven, with colder temperatures encouraging a stronger reaction.

Formal analysis of this species has not yet been reported in the scientific literature.

## Safety Warning

As with all Gymnopilus species, collectors must be able to distinguish this mushroom from deadly Galerinas.

Both Gymnopilus and Galerina produce rusty spores and can have rings, making confusion potentially fatal.

The bluing reaction of G. luteofolius could potentially be confused with the blackening reaction of some Galerinas.

Only skilled mycologists should attempt identification of rusty-spored psilocybin mushrooms.
