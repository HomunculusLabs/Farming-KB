Hesler (1969) reported it widely scattered across the United States including Texas, Florida, New York, Tennessee, Michigan, and New Mexico.

Primarily found on conifer wood, less commonly on cottonwoods and other hardwoods.

Also occurs on stumps and logs, growing when forming singly and often curving when growing in clusters.

## Cultivation

Clones of Stamets' collection on Cortes Island, British Columbia, have successfully produced mushrooms in culture.

The mushroom can be easily cultivated on hardwood logs and wood chips using standard techniques.

## Chemistry and Potency

Considered mildly to moderately active according to anecdotal reports.

The bluing phenomenon may be temperature-driven, with colder temperatures encouraging a stronger reaction.

Formal analysis of this species has not yet been reported in the scientific literature.

## Safety Warning

As with all Gymnopilus species, collectors must be able to distinguish this mushroom from deadly Galerinas.

Both Gymnopilus and Galerina produce rusty spores and can have rings, making confusion potentially fatal.

The bluing reaction of G. luteofolius could potentially be confused with the blackening reaction of some Galerinas.

Only skilled mycologists should attempt identification of rusty-spored psilocybin mushrooms.
