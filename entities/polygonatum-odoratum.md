## Polygonatum odoratum

**Polygonatum odoratum** (fragrant Solomon's seal, scented Solomon's seal) is a rhizomatous perennial herb in the family Asparagaceae. It is native to [[hamilton-shade-garden-plants]] plant with a long history of cultivation.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Polygonatum odoratum was described by Jonas C. Dillenius and later validated by Philip Miller. The genus Polygonatum contains approximately 60–70 species distributed across the Northern Hemisphere.

The species was formerly placed in Convallariaceae or Liliaceae but is now classified within Asparagaceae subfamily Nolinoideae based on molecular systematics.

Plants grow 30–60 cm tall from creeping, tuberous rhizomes. The unbranched stems are gracefully arching, glabrous, and slightly angled.

Leaves are alternate, sessile, and ovate-lanceolate, 5–12 cm long and 2–5 cm wide. They are arranged in two ranks along the stem, giving a distinctive herringbone pattern.

The tubular flowers are pendulous, 14–18 mm long, borne in pairs or clusters from the leaf axils. They are greenish-white with a pronounced sweet fragrance, the source of the specific epithet "odoratum."

Flowering occurs from May to June. The fruit is a dark blue-black berry, 6–10 mm in diameter, ripening in late summer to early autumn.

## Distribution and Habitat

P. odoratum is broadly distributed across temperate Eurasia. In Europe, it ranges from Ireland and Portugal eastward to the Urals and the Caucasus.

In East Asia, the species occurs in China, Korea, Japan, and eastern Siberia. The Chinese populations, which supply most of the commercial "yu zhu" material, are concentrated in the northeastern and central provinces.

The species grows in deciduous woodlands, forest margins, shaded hillsides, and rocky ravines. It prefers [[humus]]-rich, well-drained loams with a slightly acidic pH.

P. odoratum is tolerant of deep shade and is often found as an understory component in mixed deciduous forests. It also colonizes hedgerows and old garden sites where it can persist for decades.

The plant is cold-hardy to USDA Zone 3 and tolerates prolonged freezing. In continental climates, the aerial stems die back to the rhizome each winter and re-emerge in spring.

## Cultivation and Uses

P. odoratum is easily cultivated in shade to partial shade gardens. It requires moist, organic-rich soil and is intolerant of waterlogged conditions or prolonged drought.

Propagation is achieved through rhizome division in [[jadam-pesticide-for-slugs-and-snails]] may damage young shoots. Deer generally avoid browsing Polygonatum due to mild toxicity of the foliage.

In traditional Chinese medicine, the rhizome is used to nourish yin, moisten the lungs, and relieve thirst. It is prescribed for dry cough, diabetes symptoms, and fatigue.

European herbal traditions employ P. odoratum as a wound herb and mild astringent. The rhizome was historically applied to bruises, sprains, and skin inflammations.

The species was included in the official pharmacopoeias of several European countries through the early 20th century. It has since been largely replaced by synthetic anti-inflammatory agents.

In East Asian culinary traditions, the rhizomes are sometimes candied or used in sweet soups. The processed rhizome has a starchy texture and mild, slightly sweet flavor.

The young shoots are edible when cooked and have been consumed as a spring vegetable in parts of Europe and East Asia. The berries are mildly toxic and should not be eaten.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

The rhizome of P. odoratum contains a diverse array of bioactive constituents. The principal compounds include steroidal saponins, flavonoids, polysaccharides, and lectins.

Steroidal saponins based on diosgenin and pennogenin aglycones are the most studied constituents. Polygonatosides A through K have been isolated and characterized from the rhizome.

Polysaccharides from P. odoratum demonstrate significant immunomodulatory activity. They enhance macrophage phagocytosis and stimulate lymphocyte proliferation in vitro.

Anti-diabetic effects have been documented in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rat models. Rhizome extracts reduce blood glucose levels and improve insulin sensitivity.

Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities are attributed to flavonoid constituents including luteolin, apigenin, and their glycosides. These compounds inhibit NF-κB signaling and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine production.
