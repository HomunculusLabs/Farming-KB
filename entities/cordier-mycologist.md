# Cordier (Mycologist)

## Description

Cordier refers to a French mycological authority cited by Julius A. Palmer in *About Mushrooms*. Palmer references "Cordier of France" as one of the standard foreign text-book authors whose theories about [[mushroom-edibility-testing-safety-protocols]] were contradicted by Palmer's own experimental evidence. Palmer specifically noted that his personal tasting methodology — progressively increasing the amount of an unknown mushroom consumed over several days — "controverts theories stated in such authorities as Berkeley of England or Cordier of France." Cordier's work represented the conservative European mycological establishment that Palmer challenged through direct empirical testing.

## Key Facts

- Referenced by Palmer alongside Berkeley of England as one of the two major European authorities whose edibility testing theories were contradicted by Palmer's personal experimentation.
- Palmer's specific criticism was that these authorities held overly conservative or incorrect theories about how to determine mushroom edibility, which Palmer's decade of direct tasting experiments disproved.
- The Cordier reference likely pertains to a French mycological text or manual that was among the standard references available in Palmer's personal library.
- Palmer's critique of Cordier and similar authorities was part of his broader argument that general ignorance about mushrooms was "nothing less than a disgrace" and that existing scientific literature provided inadequate practical guidance.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Cordier represents the European mycological establishment of the 19th century that Palmer challenged. Understanding the historical tension between conservative authority-based mycology and empirical testing approaches helps contextualize [[telluride-mushroom-festival-modern-mycological-culture]] methodology, which combines [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]], [[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]], and controlled tasting to establish edibility. Modern cultivators benefit from both traditions: the systematic classification of European mycology and the practical testing ethos advocated by Palmer.
