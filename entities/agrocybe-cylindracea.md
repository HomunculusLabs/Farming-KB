# Agrocybe cylindracea (Chestnut Mushroom)

## Overview

**Agrocybe cylindracea**, commonly known as the **chestnut mushroom**, **cotton stag mushroom**, or **poplar mushroom**, is an edible agaric in the family Strophariaceae (sometimes placed in Tubariaceae). It is one of the most commercially cultivated mushroom species in East and Southeast Asia, particularly in China, Japan, and Korea, where it is prized for its nutty flavor, firm texture, and long shelf life. The species has been commercially cultivated since the 1980s and is the fourth most cultivated mushroom in China by production volume.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Strophariaceae
- **Genus:** *Agrocybe*
- **Species:** *A. cylindracea* (DC.) Maire

### Synonyms

- *Agrocybe aegerita* (V. Brig.) Singer — widely used synonym in older literature
- *Pholiota aegerita* (V. Brig.) Quél.
- *Naematoloma aegerita* (V. Brig.) P.D. Orton
- Note: *Agrocybe aegerita* was reclassified as a synonym of *A. cylindracea* following phylogenetic studies in the 2000s

## Morphology

Fruiting bodies are medium to large. Caps measure 3–10 cm in diameter, starting convex and expanding to plane with a broad umbo. The cap surface is smooth, hygrophanous, ranging from pale tan to cinnamon-brown when moist, fading to pale buff when dry. The margin often retains fragments of the partial veil in young specimens. Gills are adnexed to adnate, initially pale gray, darkening to rusty brown as spores mature. The stipe is 4–12 cm long, 0.5–1.5 cm thick, cylindrical, and features a prominent membranous annulus (ring) that can persist or disappear with age. The flesh is white, firm, and has a mild, slightly nutty aroma. Spore print is rusty brown. Spores are ellipsoid, 9–13 × 5.5–7.5 µm, with a germ pore. Basidia are clavate, 4-spored. Cheilocystidia are numerous, ventricose, with narrow necks.

## Distribution and Habitat

*A. cylindracea* is a saprotrophic species found naturally across temperate and subtropical regions of Europe, Asia, and North Africa. It fruits primarily on dead or dying wood of broadleaf trees, especially **poplar** (*Populus* spp.), **elm** (*Ulmus* spp.), and **oak** (*Quercus* spp.), typically from spring through autumn. In the wild, it can be found growing in clusters on stumps, fallen logs, and buried roots, often at the base of living trees where heartwood rot has created suitable substrate. The species has been widely introduced through cultivation and can occasionally be found naturalized outside its native range, particularly in North America and Australia where it persists near former cultivation sites.

## Cultivation

Chestnut mushrooms are commercially cultivated on supplemented sawdust substrates, typically using hardwood chips or sawdust from oak, poplar, or beech supplemented with wheat bran or rice bran at 10–20%. Key cultivation parameters:

- **Spawn run temperature:** 22–28°C
- **Fruiting temperature:** 15–22°C
- **Relative humidity:** 85–95%
- **Fresh air exchange:** Moderate to high
- **Light:** Indirect ambient light sufficient for fruiting
- **Crop cycle:** First flush 25–35 days after inoculation; total 3–5 flushes over 60–90 days
- **Biological efficiency:** 60–120% depending on substrate and strain

The species is also grown on **natural logs** using the traditional inoculation method, particularly on poplar and oak bolts 10–20 cm in diameter and 40–100 cm long. Log cultivation produces fruiting bodies over 2–3 years with seasonal flushes in spring and autumn. Spawn run takes 6–12 months depending on log species, diameter, and environmental conditions. Submerged fermentation techniques have been developed for producing mycelial biomass and intracellular polysaccharides at industrial scale, with optimized media containing glucose, yeast extract, and mineral salts achieving biomass yields of 15–25 g/L dry weight.

## Nutritional Profile

Per 100 g fresh weight: approximately 90% water, 3.5 g protein, 0.5 g fat, 4.5 g carbohydrates, 1.5 g dietary fiber. The mushroom is a good source of B vitamins (thiamine, riboflavin, niacin), ergothioneine, and minerals including potassium, phosphorus, and selenium. Dried specimens contain 15–25% protein by weight with a favorable amino acid profile including all essential amino acids. The species is notably rich in **ergothioneine** (1.5–2.5 mg/g dry weight), a thiourea derivative with potent antioxidant properties that is bioaccumulated from the substrate and concentrated in the fruiting body. Compared to other commercially cultivated mushrooms, *A. cylindracea* ranks among the highest in ergothioneine content, exceeded only by porcini (*Boletus edulis*) and shiitake (*Lentinula edodes*).

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

*A. cylindracea* has attracted significant research interest for its bioactive metabolites. Studies from Chinese, Japanese, and European laboratories have demonstrated multiple pharmacological activities:
- **Polysaccharides:** β-glucan fractions isolated from fruiting bodies and mycelium demonstrate immunomodulatory activity, stimulating macrophage activation, NK cell cytotoxicity, and cytokine production (IL-2, TNF-α, IFN-γ). Water-soluble polysaccharides (ACP) show antitumor activity in murine sarcoma-180 and Lewis lung carcinoma models, with tumor inhibition rates of 30–45% at doses of 50–200 mg/kg.
- **Agrocybenine:** A bioactive sesquiterpene isolated from the fruiting body with reported anti-inflammatory properties, inhibiting COX-2 and iNOS expression in LPS-stimulated macrophages.
- **Ergosterol and ergosterol peroxide:** Display antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities in vitro, with ergosterol peroxide showing cytotoxicity against HepG2 and MCF-7 cancer cell lines (IC50 15–25 µM).
- **Lectins:** Show antiproliferative effects against certain cancer cell lines; a galactose-binding lectin (ACL) from the mushroom induces apoptosis in HL-60 leukemia cells through mitochondrial pathways.
- **Phenolic compounds:** Contribute to the species' antioxidant capacity (DPPH IC50 0.8–1.2 mg/mL), comparable to other commercially cultivated mushrooms. Total phenolic content ranges from 3–6 mg GAE/g dry weight.
- **Hypolipidemic effects:** Animal studies demonstrate that dietary supplementation with *A. cylindracea* powder (5–10% of diet) reduces serum total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and triglycerides in hyperlipidemic rats, while increasing HDL cholesterol.

## Culinary Use

The chestnut mushroom is highly regarded in East Asian cuisine. The caps and stipes are firm enough to hold their shape during cooking, making them suitable for stir-frying, grilling, braising, and soups. The flavor intensifies with cooking and develops a characteristic nutty, slightly peppery taste that some compare to cashews or chestnuts. In China, the mushroom is sold fresh, dried, and canned in all major markets. It pairs well with soy sauce, garlic, ginger, and sesame oil. The species is gaining popularity in Western markets as a specialty cultivated mushroom, often featured in upscale restaurant dishes and farmers' markets. The dried mushroom rehydrates well and can be stored for over a year without significant loss of flavor or nutritional quality.

## Comparison with Related Species

*A. cylindracea* is frequently confused with other cultivated Agrocybe species and members of the Strophariaceae. Accurate identification is important for both foragers and cultivators:

- **Agrocybe praecox** (Early Agaric): Fruits in spring; cap is lighter colored with a less persistent ring; prefers grassy areas rather than wood. Less commercially cultivated but more commonly encountered in European grasslands.
- **Pholiota nameko** (Nameko): Also has a prominent annulus and grows on wood; distinguished by its viscid (slimy) cap surface and gelatinous coating. *A. cylindracea* has a dry cap. Nameko is the more commercially important species in Japan.
- **Stropharia rugosoannulata** (Wine Cap): Much larger, with a distinctive burgundy cap when young. Both species grow on wood chips but occupy different ecological niches. Wine Cap is more cold-tolerant.
- **Kuehneromyces mutabilis** (Velvet Shank): Similar substrate preference; distinguished by its two-toned, scaly cap and preference for cooler temperatures. Contains toxins when raw and must be thoroughly cooked.

## Seasonality and Fruiting Patterns

In the wild, *A. cylindracea* fruits predominantly in spring (April–June) and again in autumn (September–November) in temperate regions, corresponding to periods of moderate temperature and high humidity. In subtropical and tropical cultivation, the species can produce year-round with controlled environments. Wild fruiting is triggered by rain events following warm periods, with mushrooms appearing 5–10 days after significant rainfall. The species is less prolific during hot, dry summers and cold winters, though mild winters may still produce occasional flushes.

## Pests and Diseases in Cultivation

- **Trichoderma spp.:** The most common competitor mold in indoor cultivation, colonizing exposed substrate surfaces. Maintaining proper hygiene during inoculation and ensuring adequate spawn run conditions minimize contamination.
- **Sciarid flies (fungus gnats):** Larvae damage mycelium and introduce bacterial contamination. Yellow sticky traps and biological controls (*Steinernema feltiae* nematodes) are effective management strategies.
- **Bacterial blotch (*Pseudomonas tolaasii*):** Causes brown, sunken lesions on caps. Controlled by reducing humidity during fruiting and improving air circulation.
- **Mites:** Can vector mold spores between cultivation blocks. Maintaining clean grow areas and regular monitoring helps prevent infestations.

## Historical and Cultural Significance

The species has been collected from the wild for centuries in China, where it was known as "cha shu gu" (tea tree mushroom) due to its association with tea plantations. Systematic cultivation began in the 1980s in Fujian and Zhejiang provinces, and by the 2000s China was producing over 300,000 metric tons annually. In Japan, the mushroom is valued in nabemono (hot pot) dishes and is one of the few commercially cultivated species that maintains market demand alongside shiitake and shimeji. In Korea, dried *A. cylindracea* is a traditional ingredient in samgyetang (ginseng chicken soup).

## Permaculture and Agroforestry Applications

*A. cylindracea* is well-suited to agroforestry systems due to its ability to fruit on a wide range of hardwood substrates. In food forest designs, it can be inoculated into poplar, elm, or oak logs in the understory layer. The species decomposes lignin and cellulose efficiently, contributing to nutrient cycling in woody systems. Its compatibility with outdoor log cultivation makes it accessible for small-scale and homestead growers without specialized equipment. The spent substrate from indoor cultivation can be composted as a high-quality soil amendment, rich in fungal biomass and partially degraded organic matter. In integrated mushroom-vegetable systems, spent blocks can be used as mulch for garden beds, providing slow-release nutrients and moisture retention. The species also shows promise in mycoforestry applications, where inoculated logs are placed in reforestation sites to accelerate decomposition of woody debris and improve soil structure for subsequent tree plantings.

## Notable Cultivars and Strains

Several commercial strains of *A. cylindracea* have been developed for cultivation, varying in yield, fruiting temperature, and substrate preference:

- **AC-01:** The most widely used commercial strain in China; high yield (BE >100%) on supplemented oak sawdust; optimal fruiting at 18–22°C; large, uniform caps
- **AC-03:** Selected for warm-weather cultivation; fruits well at 22–28°C; popular in Southeast Asian production
- **AC-05:** Developed for outdoor log cultivation; faster spawn run on poplar bolts; produces more flushes per year than other strains
- **Wild-type isolates** from European populations show genetic and morphological differences from Asian commercial strains, with generally smaller fruiting bodies but reportedly more intense flavor
