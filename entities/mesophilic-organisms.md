## Description

Mesophilic organisms are microorganisms that thrive at **moderate temperatures**, specifically between 68°F and 104°F (20°C and 40°C). They are the first and last microbial communities active in the composting process — dominating the initial mesophilic phase before thermophilic temperatures are reached, surviving the thermophilic phase as heat-resistant spores, and then reasserting dominance during the final maturation stage. Mesophiles include bacteria, fungi, actinomycetes, protozoa, and nematodes that perform essential decomposition functions in both compost and soil ecosystems.

## Classification

- **Type**: Temperature-based ecological classification
- **Temperature range**: 68–104°F (20–40°C)
- **Includes**: Bacteria, fungi, actinomycetes, protozoa, nematodes, and microarthropods
- **Opposite classification**: Thermophilic organisms (104–150°F+), Psychrophilic organisms (below 68°F)

## Key Facts

### Role in the Composting Cycle

#### Phase 1: Initial Mesophilic Stage
- **First organisms active** when a compost pile is assembled.
- Begin breaking down **cellulose** — straight, difficult-to-digest chains broken into smaller glucose chains through **depolymerization**.
- Brown rot fungi (basidiomycetes) and certain bacteria (*Bacillus* spp., *Heliospirillum* spp.) actively decompose recalcitrant materials.
- Microbes produce **endospores** — heat-resistant spores that ensure survival through the upcoming thermophilic phase.
- Larger soil organisms join in, physically breaking apart organic matter while searching for food.
- Metabolic activity generates heat, progressively raising pile temperature toward 104°F (40°C).

#### Phase 3: Maturation Stage (Return of Mesophiles)
- As complex proteins and carbohydrates diminish, metabolic activity decreases and pile temperature drops below 104°F (40°C).
- Mesophilic organisms **reassert themselves** from their protected endospores, replacing thermophilic organisms.
- Complete the decay of the most resistant plant component — **lignin**.
- Actinomycetes (chain-like bacteria resembling fungi) attack remaining difficult-to-digest plant remnants.
- **Basidiomycetes** (fungal participants) continue their work during maturation.
- Physical decomposers (nematodes, springtails, centipedes, worms) graze on bacteria and fungi, stimulating population increases.
- Worms coat particles with mucus, binding them into aggregates — improving soil structure.
- Ants, snails, slugs, mites, spiders, rove beetles, and sow bugs further shred material for microbial attack.

### Key Mesophilic Compost Organisms

| Organism | Role |
|----------|------|
| *Bacillus* spp. | Cellulose depolymerization, endospore production |
| *Heliospirillum* spp. | Breakdown of complex organic matter |
| Basidiomycetes | Lignin and cellulose decomposition |
| Actinomycetes | Final-stage decay, earthy smell production |
| Nematodes | Bacterial/fungal grazing, nutrient mineralization |
| Springtails | Physical decomposition, fungal spore dispersal |
| Earthworms | Material processing, aggregate formation, castings production |

### Soil Ecosystem Function
- The vast majority of soil biological activity occurs at mesophilic temperatures under normal field conditions.
- Most beneficial soil food web interactions (nutrient cycling, disease suppression, aggregate formation) are driven by mesophilic organisms.
- Soil temperatures in temperate growing regions typically remain within the mesophilic range during the growing season.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

1. **Compost production**: Mesophilic organisms perform the essential first and last stages of composting — initiating decomposition and completing the maturation that produces stable, plant-safe humus.
2. **Soil food web management**: Since most soil biological activity is mesophilic, maintaining soil temperatures and moisture levels that support mesophilic communities is fundamental to soil health.
3. **Cold composting**: "Cool" or "cold" composting relies entirely on mesophilic organisms (never reaching thermophilic temperatures), resulting in slower decomposition but higher macro-organism diversity (worms, beetles, millipedes, arthropods).
4. **Mushroom substrate colonization**: Most cultivated mushroom species grow optimally at mesophilic temperatures (20–30°C), and mesophilic microorganisms in substrates compete with or support mycelial growth.
5. **Compost maturity**: The return of mesophilic organisms signals compost maturity — when mesophiles dominate and the pile no longer heats up after turning, compost is finished.
6. **Cool compost benefits**: Maintaining a cool compost pile alongside hot compost provides greater organism diversity — more worms, beetles, millipedes, and arthropods that enhance the soil food web when applied.

## Temperature Management in Compost

- Keep pile at **104–131°F (40–55°C)** after initial thermophilic run-up to 150°F (65°C).
- If pile drops below 104°F (40°C) before maturity: add more green, high-nitrogen material.
- Compost is finished when it no longer heats up after turning — mesophiles have fully reasserted dominance.
