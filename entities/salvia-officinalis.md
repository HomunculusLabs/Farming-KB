# Salvia officinalis

## Overview
Salvia officinalis, commonly known as common sage or garden sage, is a perennial evergreen subshrub in the mint family (Lamiaceae).
Native to the Mediterranean region, sage has been cultivated for millennia for its culinary, medicinal, and aromatic properties.
The species name "officinalis" indicates its historical use in apothecary and official pharmacopoeia.
Sage is rich in essential oils and polyphenolic compounds that confer antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cognitive-enhancing properties.
Unlike its psychoactive relative Salvia divinorum, S. officinalis is non-psychoactive and is primarily valued as a culinary herb and mild medicinal plant.
The plant has been naturalized in many temperate regions worldwide and is a staple herb in European and Mediterranean cuisines.
Dried sage leaves are widely used as a seasoning for poultry, pork, sausage, and stuffing, as well as in herbal teas and infusions.

## Taxonomy and Classification
- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes, Angiosperms, Eudicots, Asterids
- **Order:** Lamiales
- **Family:** Lamiaceae
- **Subfamily:** Nepetoideae
- **Tribe:** Mentheae
- **Genus:** Salvia
- **Species:** S. officinalis L.
The genus Salvia contains over 900 species, making it the largest genus in the Lamiaceae.
S. officinalis is the type species of the genus, first formally described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753.
Several cultivars have been developed for ornamental and culinary use.
Notable varieties include 'Tricolor' (variegated leaves with white, pink, and green), 'Purpurea' (purple-tinged leaves), and 'Berggarten' (broad, rounded leaves).
'Extrakta' is a cultivar selected for exceptionally high essential oil content, often exceeding 2.5% dry weight.
Natural hybrids with S. lavandulifolia and S. fruticosa occur in Mediterranean regions where their ranges overlap.

## Morphology
- **Growth habit:** Woody-based perennial subshrub, 30–80 cm tall and wide. Forms a rounded, bushy mound with age.
- **Stems:** Woody at the base, herbaceous toward the tips. Square in cross-section (characteristic of Lamiaceae). Gray-green and pubescent.
- **Leaves:** Oblong-lanceolate, 3–10 cm long and 1–4 cm wide. Gray-green to silvery-green, covered in fine woolly hairs (tomentose).
  Margins entire or finely toothed. Strongly aromatic when crushed, with a warm, camphoraceous, slightly bitter scent.
- **Flowers:** Bilabiate (two-lipped), 20–30 mm long, arranged in whorls of 4–8 along terminal spikes.
  Color ranges from violet-blue to lavender, pink, or white. Calyx is bell-shaped and persistent. Flowers attract pollinators.
- **Roots:** Extensive woody root system with lateral and deep taproot components. Plants become increasingly woody after 3–5 years.
- **Seeds:** Small nutlets (schizocarps), dark brown to black, approximately 2–3 mm. Germination rate declines rapidly after 1–2 years.

## Chemical Composition
The pharmacological properties of S. officinalis are primarily attributed to its essential oil and polyphenolic compounds.
Concentrations vary significantly with genotype, growing conditions, harvest time, and geographic origin.
European Pharmacopoeia standards require minimum 15 mL/kg essential oil content for dried sage leaves.

### Essential Oil (0.5–2.5% of dry weight)
- **1,8-Cineole (eucalyptol):** 15–40% — expectorant, antimicrobial, contributes characteristic aroma
- **Thujone (α- and β-thujone):** 10–50% — GABA receptor antagonist, neurotoxic at high doses; primary safety concern
- **Camphor:** 5–20% — counter-irritant, analgesic, aromatic
- **Borneol:** 1–5% — antimicrobial, cooling sensation
- **Linalool:** 1–3% — anxiolytic, sedative at low concentrations
- **Bornyl acetate, α-pinene, β-pinene, limonene:** Minor constituents contributing to the complex aroma
- **Viridiflorol and manool:** Triterpenoids with anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activity

### Phenolic Compounds
- **Rosmarinic acid:** Major phenolic acid; potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory. 1–3% of dry weight.
- **Carnosic acid and carnosol:** Diterpenoid phenols; antioxidant, neuroprotective. Relatively heat-stable.
- **Caffeic acid:** Antioxidant, anti-inflammatory
- **Flavonoids:** Including luteolin, apigenin, and their glycosides
- **Sagecoumarin:** A unique coumarin derivative with reported antifungal and antibacterial activity

## Traditional and Modern Medicinal Uses
Sage has an extensive history of medicinal use spanning over 2,000 years, documented in texts from Dioscorides, Pliny the Elder, and medieval herbals.
- **Cognitive enhancement:** "Why should a person die, when sage grows in his garden?" — medieval proverb.
  Clinical trials support memory-enhancing effects, particularly in older adults with mild cognitive impairment.
- **Menopausal symptoms:** Sage extracts show efficacy in reducing hot flashes and night sweats.
  A meta-analysis confirmed significant reductions in menopausal symptom scores compared to placebo.
- **Oral health:** Antimicrobial activity against Streptococcus mutans, Candida albicans, and periodontal pathogens.
  Sage mouthwashes reduce gingival inflammation and oral bacterial load in clinical settings.
- **Gastrointestinal:** Antispasmodic and carminative properties. Traditionally used for indigestion, bloating, and appetite stimulation.
- **Anti-inflammatory:** Topical application for sore throats (as a gargle), minor wounds, and inflammatory skin conditions.
- **Antimicrobial:** Broad-spectrum activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, fungi, and some enveloped viruses.
- **Antidiabetic:** Preliminary evidence suggests blood glucose-lowering effects through inhibition of intestinal glucose absorption.
- **Hyperlipidemia:** Animal studies indicate sage leaf extracts may reduce serum cholesterol and triglyceride levels.

## Cultivation
- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 4–10. Hardy and drought-tolerant once established.
- **Soil:** Well-drained, sandy to loamy soil. Prefers alkaline to neutral pH (6.0–8.0). Poor drainage leads to root rot.
- **Light:** Full sun (6+ hours). Tolerates partial shade but becomes leggy with reduced essential oil production.
- **Propagation:** Softwood cuttings (most reliable), semi-hardwood cuttings, layering, or seed (stratification improves germination).
- **Spacing:** 45–60 cm between plants.
- **Watering:** Drought-tolerant once established. Water deeply but infrequently. Overwatering is the most common cause of plant failure.
- **Fertilization:** Minimal requirements. Excessive nitrogen reduces essential oil content and aromatic intensity.
- **Pruning:** Cut back by one-third after flowering. Hard pruning in early spring rejuvenates woody plants.
- **Harvest:** Leaves throughout the growing season. Optimal essential oil content just before flowering.
  Dry in bundles in a warm, well-ventilated area out of direct sunlight.
- **Lifespan:** Plants become woody and less productive after 3–5 years. Replacement through cuttings is recommended.

## Permaculture and Ecological Functions
- **Pollinator plant:** Flowers attract bees, butterflies, and beneficial insects. Extended bloom period from late spring to mid-summer.
- **Companion planting:** Repels cabbage moths, carrot flies, and flea beetles. Good companion for rosemary, cabbage, beans, and carrots.
- **Ground cover:** Low, spreading habit provides living mulch in herb spirals and Mediterranean-style plantings.
- **Erosion control:** Extensive root system stabilizes soil on slopes and banks.
- **Dynamic accumulator:** Deep roots mine minerals from subsoil, bringing calcium, potassium, and trace minerals to the surface.
- **Drought resilience:** Once established, sage requires minimal irrigation, making it highly suitable for xeriscaping.
- **Wildlife habitat:** Provides shelter for beneficial predatory insects such as ground beetles and predatory mites.

## Safety and Contraindications
- **Thujone content:** Primary safety concern. Thujone is a GABA_A receptor antagonist. Culinary use is safe; therapeutic doses require caution.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** Avoid therapeutic doses due to thujone and emmenagogue properties. Culinary amounts are generally considered safe.
- **Epilepsy:** Avoid concentrated preparations (essential oil, tinctures) due to thujone's potential to lower seizure threshold.
- **Drug interactions:** May enhance sedative effects of benzodiazepines and barbiturates. May interact with antidiabetic medications.
- **Essential oil toxicity:** Should not be ingested internally. Topical use requires dilution (maximum 1–2% in carrier oil).
- **Allergic reactions:** Rare but possible, particularly in individuals sensitized to other Lamiaceae species.
