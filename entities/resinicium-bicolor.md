## Description

Resinicium bicolor is a cord-forming basidiomycete fungus that decomposes wood and forms distinctive [[mycelial-cord]] networks in soil. It is used as a model organism in studies of fungal foraging behavior, mycelial network dynamics, and interactions with soil invertebrates.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Basidiomycota
- **Class**: Agaricomycetes
- **Order**: Trechisporales
- **Family**: Trechisporaceae
- **Genus**: Resinicium
- **Species**: R. bicolor

## Key Facts

- Cord-forming basidiomycete that produces visible mycelial cords (rhizomorphs) for long-distance nutrient transport through soil
- Studied alongside Hypholoma fasciculare and Phanerochaete velutina as one of three model cord-forming species for comparative mycelial ecology research
- Exhibits differential mycelial morphology responses to grazing by soil invertebrates, particularly the collembolan Folsomia candida
- Forms mycelial networks that can be analyzed using graph theory and network analysis tools developed for spatial ecological networks
- Wood-decay fungus that plays a role in carbon and nitrogen cycling in forest floor ecosystems
- Produces mycelial cords that serve as major transport pathways through soil, connecting wood resources
- Demonstrates species-specific responses to soil microfaunal grazing, with different morphological changes compared to Hypholoma fasciculare and Phanerochaete velutina
- Used in microcosm experiments with wood-block inocula and compressed soil or sand substrata to study network development over time

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Resinicium bicolor serves as an important model organism for understanding cord-forming behavior and mycelial network dynamics in wood-decay basidiomycetes. The species demonstrates how fungi build, maintain, and remodel transport networks in response to environmental conditions and biotic interactions. Understanding these dynamics contributes to broader knowledge of how cultivated wood-decay fungi (such as shiitake, oyster, and lion's mane) colonize substrates and establish efficient nutrient transport networks. The species is also relevant to forest ecology and the decomposition of woody substrates in mushroom cultivation systems.
