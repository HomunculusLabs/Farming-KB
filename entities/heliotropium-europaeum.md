## Overview

Heliotropium europaeum, commonly known as common heliotrope or potato weed, is an annual herb in the family Boraginaceae. Native to the Mediterranean region and Central Asia, it has become widely naturalized in Australia, North America, and southern Africa.

The plant is notorious for its high concentration of hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids. All parts contain these compounds, which cause cumulative liver damage in livestock and humans.

Despite its toxicity, Heliotropium europaeum has a long history of use in traditional Mediterranean medicine. It has been applied topically for skin conditions and used internally in very small doses for fever and inflammatory disorders.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Plantae — **Family:** Boraginaceae — **Genus:** Heliotropium — **Species:** H. europaeum L.

The genus name derives from the Greek "helios" (sun) and "trope" (turn), referring to the inflorescence's tendency to track the sun across the sky. Synonyms include Heliotropium abortuivum and Heliotropium bracteatum.

Stems are erect to spreading, 15–50 cm tall, densely covered with appressed white pubescence. The plant branches from the base, forming a bushy habit in open conditions.

Leaves are alternate, ovate to elliptic, 1–4 cm long, with undulate margins. Both surfaces are gray-green and densely pubescent, giving the foliage a silvery appearance.

Flowers are white, sessile, and arranged in dense scorpioid cymes that uncoil progressively as they mature. Each corolla is funnel-shaped, approximately 2–3 mm in diameter, with five small lobes.

The fruit is a four-lobed schizocarp that separates at maturity into 1–4 one-seeded nutlets. Nutlets are ovoid, approximately 1.5–2 mm long, with a wrinkled surface texture.

## Distribution and Habitat

Heliotropium europaeum is native to southern Europe, North Africa, the Middle East, and Central Asia. It has been introduced to Australia, the Americas, and southern Africa through agricultural trade and contaminated seed stock.

The species thrives in disturbed habitats including cultivated fields, roadsides, waste ground, and overgrazed pastures. It prefers dry, open sites with well-drained soils and full sun exposure.

In Australia, common heliotrope is one of the most significant summer-growing weeds in cropping systems. It is widespread across New South Wales, Victoria, South Australia, and southern Western Australia.

Populations in North America are concentrated in California and the southwestern states. It is less common in eastern North America, where it occurs sporadically in agricultural areas.

## Toxicology

The primary toxic principles are pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs), including heliotrine, supinine, europine, and their N-oxides. These compounds are present in all plant parts, with highest concentrations in young leaves and flowers.

Pyrrolizidine alkaloids are metabolized in the liver by cytochrome P450 enzymes to reactive dehydropyrrolizine intermediates. These metabolites form DNA cross-links and protein adducts, leading to cellular damage and necrosis.

Chronic ingestion causes hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD), characterized by obliteration of central veins and centrilobular hepatocyte necrosis. Clinical signs in livestock include progressive weight loss, jaundice, photosensitization, and behavioral changes.

The alkaloids persist in dried plant material, making contaminated hay equally dangerous to fresh forage. There is no specific antidote for PA poisoning — treatment consists of removing the source and providing supportive care.

Pyrrolizidine alkaloids from Heliotropium species have been detected in honey at concentrations up to several micrograms per gram. Regulatory agencies in the EU, Australia, and New Zealand monitor PA levels in honey products due to public health concerns.

## Ecology and Ethnobotany

Heliotropium europaeum is a C3 summer-growing annual that germinates after warm-season rainfall. Flowering occurs from June through October in the Northern Hemisphere, with seed set completing before the first frost.

The plant serves as a host for various Lepidoptera larvae, including some specialist borers in its native range. Its small white flowers attract generalist pollinators, particularly small bees, hoverflies, and other dipterans.

Seed production is prolific, with individual plants producing several hundred nutlets that remain viable in the soil seed bank for 3–5 years. The species can form dense stands that suppress native vegetation in disturbed habitats.

In traditional Mediterranean herbalism, Heliotropium europaeum was used externally as a poultice for wounds and inflammatory skin conditions. Internal use was rare and carefully dosed due to well-known toxicity.
