## Overview

**Campsis radicans**, commonly known as trumpet vine or trumpet creeper, is a vigorous deciduous climbing vine in the family Bignoniaceae. It is native to the eastern United States and southeastern Canada.

The species is renowned for its large, trumpet-shaped, reddish-orange flowers that attract hummingbirds and long-tongued bees. It is one of the most cold-hardy members of the Bignoniaceae.

Trumpet vine grows rapidly and can become invasive in garden settings, spreading by both seeds and root suckers. It is widely planted for its spectacular floral display and wildlife value.

The genus name *Campsis* comes from the Greek word for "curving," referring to the curved flower stamens. The species epithet *radicans* means "rooting," describing its tendency to produce roots along the stems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Campsis radicans** was first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 as *Bignonia radicans*. It was later transferred to the genus *Campsis* by Édouard Spach in 1844.

The specific epithet *radicans* refers to the plant's tendency to produce rooting stems. A closely related species, *Campsis grandiflora*, is native to China and is frequently crossed with *C. radicans* to produce the hybrid *Campsis × tagliabuana*.

Trumpet vine is a woody deciduous vine that climbs by aerial rootlets, reaching heights of 10–15 meters. The stems are woody, brown, and produce numerous adventitious roots that grip supporting structures.

Leaves are pinnately compound, 20–30 cm long, with 7–11 ovate leaflets. Each leaflet is 3–8 cm long, serrate along the margins, and dark green above with a paler underside.

The flowers are borne in terminal corymbose panicles from mid-summer through early autumn. Each flower is tubular-trumpet-shaped, 5–8 cm long, with five rounded lobes and a prominently flared mouth.

Flower color ranges from bright red-orange to salmon-pink, with yellow-throated interiors. The blooms are highly attractive to ruby-throated hummingbirds, which are the primary pollinators.

Fruit is an elongated, linear capsule, 8–15 cm long, that matures from green to brown. It splits open at maturity to release numerous flat, winged seeds that are dispersed by wind.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Campsis radicans* is native to the eastern and central United States, from Connecticut to Florida and west to Nebraska and Texas. Its range extends into southeastern Ontario, Canada.

It grows naturally along forest edges, riverbanks, thickets, and disturbed sites. It prefers moist, well-drained soils in full sun but tolerates a wide range of conditions including drought and poor soils.

The vine has been widely planted as an ornamental and has naturalized beyond its native range in parts of the western United States and Europe. It is hardy to USDA zones 4–9.

## Cultivation

Trumpet vine is propagated from seed, softwood cuttings, root cuttings, or layering. Root cuttings are particularly effective and can produce new plants within a single [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].

It requires a sturdy support structure such as a trellis, pergola, or strong fence. The weight of mature vines can be substantial, and unsupported plants may damage lightweight structures.

Pruning should be done in late winter or early spring before new growth begins. Heavy pruning controls size and encourages vigorous flowering on new wood.

No serious pest or disease problems affect established plants, though [[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]] and leaf spot can occur in humid conditions. Deer generally avoid browsing the foliage.

## Ecological Role

The tubular red flowers of *Campsis radicans* are specifically adapted for pollination by hummingbirds. Ruby-throated hummingbirds (*Archilochus colubris*) are the primary pollinator in [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America.

Long-tongued bees, including bumblebees and carpenter bees, also visit the flowers. Some bees "rob" nectar by chewing holes near the base of the corolla, bypassing the pollination mechanism entirely.

The vine provides nesting habitat and cover for small birds. The dense foliage creates sheltered spaces that are used by warblers, wrens, and other songbirds.

Despite its wildlife value, trumpet vine can become ecologically problematic in disturbed habitats. It spreads aggressively by root suckers and can form dense thickets that exclude native vegetation.

## Traditional and Medicinal Uses

The plant has limited ethnobotanical use. [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] tribes reportedly used the roots for medicinal purposes, though detailed records are scarce and pharmacological data is minimal.

Some traditional sources mention preparations of the leaves and bark for treating [[cannabis-for-skin-conditions-detailed]] and digestive complaints. These uses have not been substantiated by modern [[hofmann-lsd-clinical-research-early-studies]].

All parts of the plant contain irritant compounds, and contact with the foliage can cause dermatitis in sensitive individuals. The sap may also cause skin irritation upon prolonged exposure.

## Invasive Potential and Management

Despite its wildlife value, trumpet vine can become ecologically problematic in disturbed habitats. It spreads aggressively by root suckers and can form dense thickets that exclude native vegetation.

Control of established trumpet vine requires persistent effort. Mechanical removal of above-ground growth must be combined with excavation of the extensive root system to prevent regrowth from root fragments.

Herbicide application using triclopyr or glyphosate is effective when applied to cut stems or foliage. Follow-up treatments are typically necessary, as the root system can persist and resprout for several years.

Gardeners should consider planting trumpet vine in contained areas with root barriers to prevent unwanted spread. Regular monitoring and prompt removal of suckers help maintain control in cultivated settings.
