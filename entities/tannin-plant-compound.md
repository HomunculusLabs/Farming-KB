## Description

Tannins are a class of astringent, polyphenolic compounds found in many plants, particularly concentrated in bark, leaves, wood, and some fruits. In the context of composting and soil food web management, tannins are identified as one of the key components of brown organic materials alongside lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose. These compounds are among the difficult-to-digest substances that give brown materials their characteristic resistance to rapid decomposition and that selectively support fungal dominance over bacterial dominance in compost and mulch. Tannins affect decomposition rates, microbial community composition, and nutrient cycling in soils and compost piles.

## Classification

- **Type:** Polyphenolic secondary metabolites
- **Chemical class:** Hydrolyzable tannins and condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins)
- **Molecular weight:** 500 to over 20,000 Da
- **Solubility:** Water-soluble (hydrolyzable) to poorly soluble (condensed)
- **Occurrence:** Widespread in vascular plants, especially in bark, leaves, unripe fruits, seeds, and heartwood

## Key Facts

- **Brown material component:** Tannin is listed alongside lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose as one of the primary components of brown composting materials (autumn leaves, bark, wood chips, twigs, branches). These are the materials that support fungal dominance in compost and mulch.

- **Resistance to decomposition:** Tannins are difficult for bacteria to digest, which is why brown materials tend to favor fungal over bacterial growth. Fungi possess the enzymatic machinery (polyphenol oxidases, laccases) to break down tannins and other phenolic compounds, while most bacteria cannot.

- **Anti-microbial properties:** Tannins have natural antimicrobial properties. They can bind to proteins, including microbial enzymes, inhibiting bacterial growth. This protein-binding capacity is what makes unripe fruits astringent and what makes some tree barks resistant to decay.

- **Nitrogen binding:** Tannins can bind to nitrogen-containing compounds, temporarily making nitrogen unavailable to plants and microbes. This contributes to the slow decomposition rate of tannin-rich materials like bark and certain leaves.

- **Fungal food selection pressure:** The presence of tannins in organic materials acts as a selection pressure favoring fungi over bacteria. Only organisms with the appropriate enzymes can access the carbon and nutrients locked in tannin-rich materials.

## Types of Tannins

**Hydrolyzable tannins:**
- Composed of gallic acid or ellagic acid units esterified to a sugar core
- Found primarily in oak, chestnut, and sumac
- More easily broken down by microbial enzymes
- Can be hydrolyzed to release gallic acid and sugar

**Condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins):**
- Polymers of flavan-3-ol units (catechin, epicatechin)
- Found in conifer bark, many tree species, and some leaves
- More resistant to microbial decomposition than hydrolyzable tannins
- Contribute to the long-lasting nature of bark chip mulch

## Role in Composting and Mulching

In the composting system described by Lowenfels and Lewis:

1. **Brown materials** (bark, wood chips, autumn leaves) contain tannins, lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose
2. **Fungi** have the enzymes to break down these difficult-to-digest compounds
3. **Bacteria** cannot efficiently attack tannin-rich materials until fungi have partially processed them
4. This dynamic creates **fungal dominance** in piles with high tannin content

**In mulch:**
- Bark chips high in tannins last 3-4 years as mulch
- Tannins contribute to the weed-suppressing properties of bark mulch
- The slow release of tannin degradation products can affect soil chemistry

## Tannin Content in Common Composting Materials

| Material | Relative Tannin Content | Decomposition Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Oak leaves | High | Moderate (6-12 months) |
| Pine bark | High | Slow (2-4 years) |
| Cedar chips | Very high (plus terpenes) | Very slow; avoid as mulch |
| Maple leaves | Moderate | Moderate (3-6 months) |
| Grass clippings | Low | Rapid (1-3 months) |
| Fruit waste | Low to moderate | Rapid |
| Straw | Low | Moderate |

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Mushroom substrate chemistry:** Tannin content in mushroom substrates affects mycelial growth rates. Some mushroom species (e.g., oyster mushrooms) produce laccases and other enzymes that can degrade tannins, while others may be inhibited by high tannin concentrations.

- **Outdoor mushroom bed preparation:** When preparing outdoor mushroom beds, tannin-rich materials like oak leaves and bark can be used as long-term, slow-release substrate components. However, very high tannin levels may initially inhibit mycelial growth.

- **Fungal dominance in soil:** Tannin-rich mulches help establish fungal-dominated soil conditions beneficial for trees, shrubs, perennials, and mycorrhizal associations. This is the basis of the Rule #6 (brown materials support fungi).

- **Allelopathic effects:** Some tannin-rich materials (especially fresh oak leaves and cedar) can have allelopathic effects -- inhibiting the growth of other plants or fungi nearby. Aging or composting reduces these effects.

- **Compost tea quality:** The tannin content of compost used for making compost tea affects the microbial communities in the tea. Fungal-dominant compost (high tannin starting materials) produces teas rich in fungal spores and hyphal fragments.

## Environmental Effects

- **Soil pH:** Tannins can contribute to soil acidification as they decompose, which may benefit acid-loving plants and fungi
- **Organic matter stabilization:** Tannin-protein complexes are relatively stable in soil, contributing to long-term organic matter accumulation
- **Water quality:** Leached tannins can color water (brownish tint) but are not harmful; in fact, they contribute to dissolved organic carbon in aquatic ecosystems
