## Polypodium cambricum

## Overview

Polypodium cambricum, commonly known as southern polypody or Welsh polypody, is an evergreen fern in the family Polypodiaceae.

It is native to western and southern Europe, the Mediterranean basin, and the Macaronesian islands, where it grows on rocks, walls, and tree bases.

The species is distinguished from the common polypody (Polypodium vulgare) by its broader pinnae and preference for alkaline substrates.

Polypodium cambricum is prized in horticulture for its numerous cultivated forms with intricately divided fronds.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Polypodium cambricum was described by Linnaeus in 1753, with the specific epithet referring to Cambria, the Latin name for Wales.

The species belongs to the Polypodium vulgare aggregate, a complex of closely related European polypodies that were long treated as varieties of a single species.

Fronds are 15–40 cm long, pinnate with broadly ovate to oblong pinnae that are markedly wider than those of Polypodium vulgare.

The rachis is green and glabrous, with pinnae attached alternately along its length.

Sori are large, round, and conspicuous on the frond undersides, golden-orange at maturity, lacking an indusium (a defining character of the Polypodiaceae).

The rhizome is creeping, densely scaly, and has a sweet licorice-like taste when chewed.

Spores are bilateral, yellowish, and smooth, measuring approximately 50–60 µm.

## Distribution and Habitat

Polypodium cambricum has a western and southern European distribution, from the British Isles and Iberian Peninsula east to Italy and the Balkans.

It is particularly abundant in Wales, western England, and along the Atlantic coasts of France, Spain, and Portugal.

Macaronesian populations occur on the Azores, Madeira, and the Canary Islands, often at higher elevations.

The species is calcicolous, growing on limestone pavements, mortared walls, cliff faces, and the bases of old trees on alkaline soils.

It tolerates Mediterranean summer drought by becoming seasonally dormant, reviving with autumn rains.

Elevation ranges from sea level to approximately 1800 m in southern mountain ranges.

The species reaches its northern limit in Scotland and southern Scandinavia, where it is restricted to coastal sites with mild winters.

In the Mediterranean region, it grows on ancient stone walls and ruins alongside other rupicolous ferns such as Asplenium ruta-muraria.

## Ecology and Cultivation

Polypodium cambricum is an obligate calcicole in the northern parts of its range, though southern populations can tolerate mildly acidic substrates.

It is less cold-hardy than Polypodium vulgare, generally rated to USDA Zone 7 or 8, requiring protection in colder gardens.

The species grows as an epiphyte, lithophyte, or terrestrial plant, showing remarkable substrate flexibility within its preferred pH range.

Spore propagation produces variable offspring, while division of the creeping rhizome yields uniform clones.

Numerous cultivars have been selected for frond division, including 'Cambricum' (the original crested form), 'Richard Kayse', and 'Whilharris'.

The fern is low-maintenance in cultivation, requiring only well-drained alkaline soil and shade to partial shade.

It is resistant to most pests and diseases, though excessive winter wet can cause rhizome rot in poorly drained sites.

The cultivar 'Richard Kayse' was discovered on a Welsh cliff in 1668 and is one of the oldest documented fern cultivars still in cultivation.

## Uses and Ethnobotany

The rhizome of Polypodium cambricum, like other European polypodies, was traditionally used as a mild sweetener and herbal remedy.

Historical texts describe "polypody of the oak" being used to treat coughs, digestive complaints, and melancholy, though this likely referred to several Polypodium species.

The licorice-flavored rhizome was candied and eaten as a confection in medieval England and France.

In modern horticulture, the cultivated forms are highly sought by fern enthusiasts, with over 50 named cultivars in circulation.

The species has no current pharmacological significance, though phytochemical studies have identified osladin (a natural sweetener) in the rhizome.

Osladin from Polypodium species is approximately 500 times sweeter than sucrose by weight, making it one of the most potent natural sweeteners known in the plant kingdom.

## Related Species

Polypodium vulgare (common polypody) is the most widespread European species, differing in its narrower pinnae and acidic substrate preference.

Polypodium interjectum is a hexaploid species arising from ancient hybridization between Polypodium cambricum and Polypodium vulgare, with intermediate morphology.

The three European polypodies form a polyploid complex that has been the subject of extensive biosystematic study.

Polypodium cambricum hybridizes with Polypodium vulgare to produce the sterile triploid Polypodium × mantoniae.

The genus Polypodium is cosmopolitan, with approximately 150 species distributed across temperate and tropical regions worldwide.

Polypodium cambricum is unique among European polypodies for producing viable apogamous spores in some populations, a form of asexual reproduction through unreduced spores.
