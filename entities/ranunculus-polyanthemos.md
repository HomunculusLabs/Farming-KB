# Ranunculus polyanthemos

## Overview

Ranunculus polyanthemos (spreading buttercup) is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family [[ranunculus-acris|buttercup family]], native to Europe and western Asia. It is closely related to the common meadow buttercup but distinguished by its more spreading growth habit and smaller flowers.

The species is a common component of neutral grasslands, meadows, and pastures across central and northern Europe. It has been introduced to parts of North America where it occurs as a minor weed of agricultural land.

First described by the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus in his 1753 Species Plantarum, R. polyanthemos has a complex nomenclatural history due to its close relationship with the R. acris aggregate. The specific epithet means "many-flowered," reflecting the numerous small blooms produced on branched stems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Ranunculus polyanthemos belongs to the Ranunculus acris aggregate, a group of closely related taxa that have historically been treated as subspecies or varieties of [[ranunculus-acris]]. It is now generally accepted as a distinct species by most European floras, though the boundary between the two remains debated.

Within the genus Ranunculus, R. polyanthemos falls in section Ranunculus, a group characterized by erect stems, palmately divided basal leaves, and yellow flowers with reflexed sepals. Molecular phylogenetic studies confirm its placement as sister to R. acris in a clade of European mesic grassland species.

The plant grows 30–70 cm tall from a fibrous rootstock without bulbous basal swellings. Stems are erect, branched above the middle, and covered with appressed hairs that distinguish the species from the glabrous-stemmed [[ranunculus-bulbosus]].

Basal leaves are palmately divided into three to five primary lobes, each further divided into narrow, acute segments. The leaves are markedly hairy on both surfaces, giving them a greyish-green appearance compared to the darker foliage of [[ranunculus-acris]].

Flowers are 12–20 mm in diameter with five glossy yellow petals, typically smaller and more numerous than those of meadow buttercup. The calyx is reflexed against the pedicel at anthesis, and the achenes are sparsely pilose with a short hooked beak.

The nectary at the base of each petal forms a distinctive scale-like pocket, a feature shared with other members of the Ranunculus acris group. The receptacle is conspicuously hairy, which aids in distinguishing the species from some related taxa when fruits are immature.

Chromosome number is 2n = 14, consistent with most European species in the R. acris aggregate. Polyploid populations have been reported from the eastern part of the range but are uncommon.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is widespread across Europe from southern Scandinavia to the Mediterranean, extending eastward through the Balkans to western Siberia and the Caucasus. It is most abundant in central Europe, particularly in Germany, Poland, and the Czech Republic.

R. polyanthemos occupies neutral to slightly calcareous grasslands, hay meadows, road verges, and lightly grazed pastures. It is characteristic of the Arrhenatheretum elatioris plant community, which defines species-rich European hay meadows.

The species avoids both very acidic and very wet sites, preferring free-draining loam or clay-loam soils. It is moderately tolerant of drought and can persist in south-facing slopes where other buttercup species may struggle.

Soil pH preference ranges from 5.5 to 7.5, with optimum growth at near-neutral conditions. The species shows little tolerance of waterlogging, distinguishing it ecologically from wetland buttercups such as [[ranunculus-lingua]].

Population density is highest in traditionally managed meadows with late summer hay cuts. It declines under intensive grazing or frequent fertilizer application, which favors competitive grasses over forb species.

In the British Isles, the species is largely replaced by R. acris subsp. acris, though it occurs in scattered localities in eastern England. Continental populations are more robust and widespread, particularly in the Baltic states and central European lowlands.

## Ecology

Flowering occurs from May to July, with peak bloom in mid-June across most of its range. The flowers are insect-pollinated, with hoverflies (Syrphidae) and small beetles being the most frequent visitors.

Like other buttercups, R. polyanthemos contains ranunculin, a glycoside that hydrolyzes to protoanemonin when plant tissues are damaged. This compound causes blistering in the mouths of grazing animals, providing effective herbivory deterrence.

The species is a poor competitor in tall, rank swards but thrives where management maintains intermediate vegetation height. Traditional hay meadow management, with a single annual cut and no fertilizer, provides ideal conditions.

Seed production is prolific, with individual plants producing 200–500 achenes per season. Achenes require a period of cold stratification to germinate, with optimal germination occurring at 10–15°C after 4–8 weeks of chilling.

The species forms a persistent soil seed bank, with viable seeds remaining dormant for up to 5 years. This seed bank allows rapid recolonization after disturbance and contributes to the species' resilience in managed grasslands.

Interactions with mycorrhizal fungi are well developed, with arbuscular mycorrhizal associations reported from grassland root surveys. These associations improve phosphorus uptake in the nutrient-poor soils of traditionally managed meadows.

## Uses and Cultivation

Ranunculus polyanthemos has no significant horticultural use and is rarely cultivated. Its weedy tendency and the availability of more ornamental buttercups such as [[ranunculus-asiaticus]] limit its appeal for garden settings.

The species is classified as an injurious weed under agricultural weed legislation in several European countries, though it is less problematic than [[ranunculus-repens]] in pasture settings. Control measures typically involve improved drainage and liming to reduce soil acidity.

In traditional European folk medicine, the plant was occasionally used as a counter-irritant for joint pain, though this use is discouraged due to the blistering properties of protoanemonin. Modern herbalism does not employ the species.

Dried specimens are frequently encountered in European herbarium collections, where they have been used to study phenological shifts in response to climate warming. Flowering dates have advanced by 5–10 days over the past century in central European collections.

The plant has some ecological value in species-rich meadow seed mixes for restoration projects, where it contributes to the diversity of forb species. It supports a range of generalist pollinators and serves as a food plant for the larval stages of several micro-moth species.

Its conservation significance lies primarily as an indicator species for species-rich, low-intensity grassland. Declines in R. polyanthemos populations across western Europe parallel the broader loss of traditionally managed hay meadows, making it a useful bioindicator for grassland quality assessments.

## Similar Species

Ranunculus polyanthemos is most easily confused with [[ranunculus-acris]], which shares its habitat and general morphology. The spreading habit, smaller flowers, and hairy leaves of R. polyanthemos separate it from the taller, larger-flowered, and less hairy meadow buttercup.
