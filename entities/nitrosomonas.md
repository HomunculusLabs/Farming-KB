# Nitrosomonas

## Description

*Nitrosomonas* is a genus of Gram-negative, chemoautotrophic bacteria that plays a foundational role in the nitrogen cycle by performing the first step of nitrification: the oxidation of ammonia (NH3/NH4+) to nitrite (NO2-). These bacteria are among the most important microorganisms in soil ecology, wastewater treatment, and aquaculture, as they initiate the conversion of reduced nitrogen compounds into forms that can be further processed or taken up by plants.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Bacteria
- **Phylum:** Pseudomonadota (Proteobacteria)
- **Class:** Betaproteobacteria
- **Order:** Burkholderiales
- **Family:** Nitrosomonadaceae
- **Genus:** *Nitrosomonas*

## Key Facts

- Mentioned on page 49 of *Teaming with Microbes* as a key nitrifying bacterium in the soil nitrogen cycle.
- **Chemoautotrophs:** *Nitrosomonas* derives energy not from sunlight or organic matter, but from the chemical oxidation of ammonia to nitrite. It uses this energy to fix carbon dioxide into organic compounds, making it a primary producer in the dark subterranean ecosystem.
- **First step of nitrification:** *Nitrosomonas* converts ammonium (NH4+) to nitrite (NO2-). This is the rate-limiting step in the overall nitrification process, making *Nitrosomonas* populations a bottleneck in nitrogen availability for plants.
- **Slow-growing:** Due to the low energy yield of ammonia oxidation, *Nitrosomonas* grows very slowly compared to heterotrophic bacteria. Population recovery after soil disturbance (tillage, chemical application) can take weeks to months.
- **Sensitive to environmental conditions:** Optimal activity occurs at neutral to slightly alkaline pH (7.0-8.0), moderate temperatures (25-30 C), adequate moisture, and good aeration. Acidic conditions, waterlogging, and certain agricultural chemicals can inhibit their activity.
- Found predominantly in **aerobic zones** of soil — the top layers and around root zones where oxygen is available and ammonium is produced by mineralization of organic matter.
- Forms **microcolonies** in soil, often in association with *Nitrobacter* (which performs the second nitrification step), creating efficient nitrogen-processing consortia.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Nitrogen availability:** *Nitrosomonas* directly controls the rate at which ammonium (retained in soil by cation exchange) is converted to nitrite, which is then rapidly converted to nitrate by *Nitrobacter*. Nitrate is the preferred nitrogen form for most annual plants but is highly mobile and prone to leaching.
- **Soil food web management:** Practices that harm *Nitrosomonas* populations — such as excessive tillage, broad-spectrum fumigation, or heavy chemical fertilizer use — can impair nitrification and reduce plant-available nitrogen.
- **Compost quality:** In [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] and finished compost, robust *Nitrosomonas* populations indicate healthy nitrogen cycling. Their absence or suppression suggests anaerobic conditions, chemical contamination, or immature compost.
- **Fungal-bacterial balance:** In soils dominated by fungal biomass (such as forest soils and perennial polycultures), nitrogen tends to remain in ammonium form, which favors woody perennial plants. In bacterially dominated soils (annual vegetable beds, lawns), active *Nitrosomonas* drives nitrification, producing nitrate preferred by annuals.
- **Mushroom cultivation:** Excess nitrate from rapid nitrification can be detrimental to some fungal species. Understanding nitrification rates helps substrate formulators balance nitrogen sources appropriately.

## Ecological Role

*Nitrosomonas* is a keystone chemolithoautotroph in [[the-soil-food-web]]. By converting ammonia to nitrite, it links the mineralization of organic nitrogen (performed by decomposer bacteria and fungi) with the production of plant-available nitrate (completed by *Nitrobacter*). This nitrification pathway is essential for productive agricultural soils and natural ecosystems alike. The energy that *Nitrosomonas* harvests from this reaction makes it a unique primary producer — an organism at the base of a food chain that does not depend on sunlight.
