## Description

**Maitake cultivation** refers to the commercial and hobbyist practice of growing *Grifola frondosa* (Dick.: Fr.) S. F. Gray, commonly known as maitake or hen-of-the-woods. This prized edible and medicinal mushroom requires specialized techniques due to its intricate structure, larger-than-usual size, high oxygen demands, and specific environmental requirements at each growth stage. Synthetic-log cultivation on supplemented [[hardwood-sawdust-substrate]] is the primary commercial method. The species is closely related to other polypore mushrooms studied in fungal ecology and environmental biology.

## Classification

- **Category:** Cultivation technique
- **Species:** *Grifola frondosa* (Dick.: Fr.) S. F. Gray
- **Common names:** Maitake, hen-of-the-woods, dancing mushroom
- **Primary method:** Synthetic-log cultivation (supplemented sawdust in bags)
- **Climate preference:** Temperate; fruits naturally in fall

## Key Facts

- Maitake is highly aerobic and has an enormous appetite for oxygen relative to other cultivated mushrooms, requiring dedicated growing rooms with excellent ventilation.
- The growing room should NOT be shared with other mushroom species due to maitake's exacting requirements.
- Major morphogenetic transitions include: (1) mycelial-coat formation on substrate surface, (2) primordia initiation, and (3) fruiting-body differentiation and development.
- In China and Japan, spawn run and fruiting body development are carried out in two separate rooms for environmental control.
- Substrate is primarily hardwood sawdust (75-80%) supplemented with [[wheat-bran]] (18-23%), sucrose (1%), and calcium compounds (1%).
- Cotton plugs or microfilter windows on polypropylene bags allow gas exchange during spawn run; bags are opened or slit for fresh air during fruiting.
- [[soil-casing]] (humus-rich hardwood forest soil, 0.5-0.8 cm particles) significantly increases yield (300-500 g/bag vs. 150-300 g/bag without casing).
- Total mushroom production takes approximately 3 months from inoculation to harvest.

## Cultivation Stages

### Spawn Run Phase

The spawn run is the initial colonization phase where [[mycelium]] grows through the supplemented sawdust substrate. For maitake, this phase requires temperatures of 20-25°C with controlled humidity (65-70% relative). Bags are kept sealed with filter patches or cotton plugs to maintain a high CO₂ environment that favors vegetative growth. Full colonization typically takes 30-45 days depending on strain vigor and substrate composition. [[sawdust-spawn]] is the preferred inoculum type for maitake, though [[liquid-spawn]] can also be used for faster colonization.

### Primordia Initiation

Once the mycelial coat fully covers the substrate surface, conditions are shifted to trigger primordia formation. Temperature is lowered to 18-22°C, CO₂ levels are reduced by introducing fresh air, and light exposure is increased. This stage requires precise environmental control and is where many growers encounter difficulties, as maitake is particularly sensitive to CO₂ accumulation, which causes abnormal morphology (elongated stems, small caps). See mushroom fruiting biology light tropisms and environmental triggers for more on fruiting triggers.

### Fruiting Body Development

During fruiting, temperatures are further reduced to 15-20°C with high humidity (85-95%). The characteristic rosette-shaped fruiting bodies develop over 15-21 days. Proper ventilation is critical — maitake's large biomass and high metabolic rate produce significant CO₂ that must be continuously removed. Condensation from temperature fluctuations can damage developing fruiting bodies, making climate stability essential.

## Substrate Formulations

Three standard substrate formulas are documented for maitake cultivation:
1. **Formula I:** Hardwood sawdust (78%) + wheat bran (20%) + sucrose (1%) + calcium (1%)
2. **Formula II:** Sawdust + bran + forest soil addition
3. **Formula III:** Sawdust + recycled spent substrate (20%) + bran (10%)

[[cottonseed-hulls]] can substitute up to 25% of the hardwood sawdust basal ingredient, providing improved aeration and supplementary nutrition. Substrate moisture should be 60-63% with pH 5.5-6.5 before sterilization.

## Strain Selection

Unlike [[pleurotus-ostreatus]] or [[ganoderma-lucidum]], a high percentage of *G. frondosa* strains do not fruit reliably under cultivation conditions. This makes systematic strain testing essential before committing to commercial production. Commercial spawn suppliers typically offer tested fruiting strains, but growers should validate performance on their specific substrate and in their facilities. See mushroom spawn production and laboratory techniques for spawn production details.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Medicinal importance:** Maitake contains bioactive polysaccharides, particularly D-fraction (beta-glucan), with documented immune-modulating, anti-tumor, and anti-diabetic properties (Nanba, 1995, 1997; Stamets, 1993, 1998).
- **Economic value:** As both a premium culinary mushroom and medicinal species, maitake commands high market prices, making commercial cultivation economically attractive.
- **Environmental parameters:** Precise control of temperature, humidity, light, and CO₂/O₂ at each stage is critical. Spawn run: 20-25°C; Primordia: 18-22°C; Fruiting: 15-20°C.
- **Strain selection challenge:** Unlike oyster or reishi mushrooms, a high percentage of *G. frondosa* strains do not fruit well, making strain testing essential before production.
- **Problem-solving:** Common challenges include contamination, abnormal fruiting body morphology from CO₂ excess, and condensation damage from temperature fluctuation. Consult mushroom contamination identification and troubleshooting for detailed troubleshooting guidance.

The species is classified as a white-rot fungus, meaning it preferentially degrades lignin over cellulose in wood substrates. This physiological characteristic has important implications for substrate formulation and preparation, as maitake mycelium colonizes hardwood sawdust most efficiently when the lignin-to-cellulose ratio mimics natural oak wood.

Commercial-scale maitake production in Asia typically achieves biological efficiency of 50-100% on supplemented hardwood sawdust blocks. Yield depends heavily on proper strain selection and precise control of the fruiting environment.
