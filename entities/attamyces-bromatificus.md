## Description

**Attamyces bromatificus** is a fungal species that has never been found outside the nests of leaf-cutting ants. It represents one of the most obligate examples of fungal-insect mutualism, forming the basis of the attine ant fungus-garden symbiosis.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: [[ascomycota]] (historically classified; phylogenetic placement debated)
- **Genus**: Attamyces
- **Species**: A. bromatificus

## Key Facts

- Has never been found outside the nests of leaf-cutting ants, indicating obligate symbiosis
- Ants carry fungal inoculum to new nests in an infrabuccal pocket, a small cavity at the esophageal opening
- The fungus is cultivated on leaf material collected and prepared by worker ants
- Produces staphylae (food bodies) that serve as the primary food reward for the ant colony
- Abundance of staphylae increases in smaller passages accessible only to minima (small) worker ants
- Described by Cherrett et al. (1989) as part of the mutualistic leaf-cutting ant association

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Attamyces bromatificus represents the evolutionary pinnacle of fungal cultivation by insects. The leaf-cutting ant system parallels human [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]]: [[substrate-preparation]] (leaf cutting and processing), inoculation (carried in infrabuccal pockets), environmental management (temperature and humidity in nests), and competitor exclusion (weeding of undesirable molds). Understanding this natural system provides insights into the principles of controlled fungal cultivation, particularly regarding sterile technique and substrate management.

## Ecological Role

This fungus is a keystone species in neotropical forest ecosystems, as leaf-cutting ants are dominant herbivores. The fungal gardens process enormous quantities of leaf material, making A. bromatificus a major driver of nutrient cycling. The obligate mutualism between the fungus and its ant cultivators demonstrates coevolutionary specialization.
