## Overview

**Coccinia grandis** (L.) Voigt, commonly known as **ivy gourd**, is a perennial climbing vine in the family **Cucurbitaceae** widely grown across tropical Asia and Africa for its edible immature fruits and tender leaves.

The species is valued as a productive [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]].

Ivy gourd is also recognized as an aggressive invasive species in several [[query-how-do-i-prune-brambles-for-maximum-fruit-production]], and nutritional value have made it both a valued food plant and a problematic weed depending on context.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Coccinia grandis* was originally described as *Bryonia grandis* by Linnaeus in 1753 and later transferred to *Coccinia* by Johann Gottfried Voigt.

The authority citation (L.) Voigt reflects this reclassification within the cucumber family Cucurbitaceae, tribe Cucurbiteae.

It is a vigorous, dioecious climber with tendrils, capable of covering large areas of vegetation and structures within a single [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]], and northern Australia.

The species occupies a wide ecological range, growing from sea level to approximately 1500 m elevation in areas with annual rainfall of 800–2500 mm.

It thrives in disturbed habitats including forest edges, roadsides, abandoned fields, and urban waste areas, where it can rapidly smother native vegetation.

The vine is particularly problematic on Pacific islands such as Hawaii, Guam, and Saipan, where it is listed as a noxious weed.

## Cultivation

Ivy gourd is typically propagated by stem cuttings (15–20 cm segments) or by seed, with cuttings preferred for maintaining desired fruit characteristics.

Plants are established on trellises or fences at 1–2 m spacing, with male and female plants required for fruit production in dioecious populations.

The vine begins flowering 2–3 months after planting, with harvestable immature green fruits appearing approximately 45–60 days after flower opening.

Immature fruits are harvested at 3–5 cm length when still firm and green — mature red fruits become fibrous and are generally not consumed as vegetables.

Regular harvesting every 2–3 days during the fruiting season promotes continued flower production and extends the harvest period to 6–8 months.

The plant responds well to organic fertilization and irrigation, though it is remarkably drought-tolerant once established.

## Nutritional Value

Immature fruits of *Coccinia grandis* contain approximately 1.5–2.0% protein, 0.2% fat, and 4–6% carbohydrate on a fresh-weight basis.

The fruits are a good source of vitamin C, provitamin A carotenoids, and dietary fiber, with mineral content including calcium, iron, and phosphorus.

Young leaves are also edible and provide higher protein content per unit weight than the fruits, though they are consumed in smaller quantities.

Traditional medicinal systems across South Asia attribute blood sugar-lowering properties to ivy gourd, and some pharmacological studies have identified hypoglycemic compounds in leaf and fruit extracts.

Antioxidant activity in both fruits and leaves has been documented, attributed to flavonoids, tannins, and beta-carotene content.
