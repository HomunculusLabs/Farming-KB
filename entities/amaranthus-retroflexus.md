# Amaranthus retroflexus

## Overview

*Amaranthus retroflexus*, commonly known as redroot [[amaranthus-palmeri]], rough pigweed, or green amaranth, is an annual herbaceous plant in the family Amaranthaceae. Despite being classified as one of the world's most widespread agricultural weeds, it is also a highly nutritious edible plant with deep roots in hu [[mollison-town-permaculture-urban-food-systems]] tems across multiple continents. The species exemplifies the blurred boundary between "weed" and "crop" and is increasingly recogni [[water-harvesting-and-conservation-in-permaculture]] ture [[faires-mushroom-growing-and-foraging]] communities as a valuable resource.

Native to [[palmer-edible-mushrooms-north-america]], *A. retroflexus* has become a cosmopolitan species found on every inhabited continent. It is a vigorous pioneer species capable of rapidly colonizing disturbed soils, making it both a challenge for conventional agriculture and an ally in ecological restoration.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Eudicots
- **Order:** Caryophyllales
- **Family:** Amaranthaceae
- **Genus:** *Amaranthus*
- **Species:** *A. retroflexus*

The genus name *Amaranthus* derives from the Greek *amarantos* (ἀμάραντος), meaning "unfading" or "immortal," referring to the long-lasting flower heads. The species epithet *retroflexus* describes the backward-bending (reflexed) flower bracts, a key identification feature.

## Morphology

### Growth Habit
*A. retroflexus* is an erect, bushy annual growing 0.3–2.5 meters tall, though most specimens reach 0.5–1.5 meters. It produces a strong central taproot that can extend 1 meter or more into the soil, giving it exceptional drought tolerance and nutrient-scavenging ability.

### Stem
The stem is stout, upright, and often reddish or pinkish at the base (hence "redroot pigweed"), with branching in the upper portions. Stems are typically hairless or sparsely hairy with short, appressed trichomes.

### Leaves
Leaves are alternate, ovate to rhombic-ovate, 3–15 cm long and 2–8 cm wide, with entire margins and a distinct petiole. The leaf surface is rough to the touch due to short, stiff hairs on the underside, which distinguishes it from the smoother-leaved *Amaranthus hybridus*. A characteristic V- or U-shaped watermark is often visible on the upper leaf surface near the petiole.

### Flowers and Inflorescence
The species is monoecious, with separate male (staminate) and female (pistillate) flowers on the same plant. Flowers are borne in dense, terminal and axillary spike-like inflorescences that are thick, bristly, and green. Female flowers have five conspicuous tepals (bracts) that are longer than the flower and sharply reflexed backward at the tips — the diagnostic feature noted in the species name.

### Seeds
Each plant can produce between 100,000 and 500,000 seeds, making it one of the most prolific seed producers in the plant kingdom. Seeds are small (1–1.5 mm diameter), lenticular (lens-shaped), glossy black to dark brown, and extremely persistent in the soil seed bank. Seeds can remain viable for 5–40+ years depending on burial depth and soil conditions.

## Nutritional Profile

*A. retroflexus* is remarkably nutritious, often exceeding cultivated vegetables in mineral and protein content:

| Nutrient | Value (per 100g fresh weight) |
|----------|-------------------------------|
| Protein | 2.5–4.0 g |
| Vitamin A | 6,000–10,000 IU |
| Vitamin C | 60–80 mg |
| Calcium | 250–400 mg |
| Iron | 3.0–5.0 mg |
| Phosphorus | 60–80 mg |
| Potassium | 300–500 mg |
| Magnesium | 50–70 mg |

The leaves are rich in betalains (the same pigments found in beets), which have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. The seeds contain approximately 14–16% protein with a favorable amino acid profile, including lysine — an amino acid often deficient in cereal grains.

## Culinary Uses

### Young Leaves and Shoots
Young leaves and tender stems (under 30 cm tall) can be eaten raw in salads or cooked as a potherb, similar to spinach. Cooking reduces oxalate and nitrate content. The flavor is mild and pleasant, becoming earthier with maturity.

### Seeds
The tiny seeds can be harvested by threshing dried flower heads and winnowing. They can be ground into flour, popped like miniature popcorn, or added to granola and baked goods. Historically, several *Amaranthus* species were staple crops of the Aztec, Inca, and other pre-Columbian civilizations.

### Preparation Notes
- Always positively identify before consumption; distinguish from toxic look-alikes like *Datura* seedlings.
- Cook leaves to reduce oxalates and potential nitrate accumulation (more of a concern in nitrogen-fertilized soils).
- Avoid harvesting from roadsides or contaminated sites due to heavy metal uptake potential.

## Ecological Role in Permaculture

### Pioneer Species Function
*A. retroflexus* excels as a pioneer species in ecological succession. Its deep taproot breaks compacted soil, cycles nutrients from deep subsoil layers to the surface, and its dense canopy suppresses less desirable weeds. In permaculture design, it can serve as a dynamic accumulator — a plant that mines minerals from deep soil horizons and makes them available to other plants when its biomass decomposes.

### Soil Remediation
Research has demonstrated that *A. retroflexus* has significant phytoremediation potential:
- **Heavy metal accumulation:** Can hyperaccumulate cadmium, lead, and zinc from contaminated soils, though this means it should NOT be eaten from contaminated sites.
- **Nitrate scavenging:** Efficiently captures excess nitrates from agricultural runoff, preventing leaching into waterways.
- **Soil organic matter:** When terminated and incorporated as green manure, it contributes significant biomass and nutrients.

### Companion Planting Considerations
In permaculture contexts, *A. retroflexus* can be managed rather than eradicated:
- Allow it to grow as a living mulch between rows, then chop-and-drop before seed set.
- Use it as a trap crop for certain pest insects (e.g., tarnished plant bug).
- Its pollen is a food source for beneficial insects, though it is also wind-pollinated and can cause allergic reactions in sensitive individuals.

### Biomass Production
Under favorable conditions, a single plant can produce 1–3 kg of fresh biomass. As a green manure crop, it can add significant organic matter and nitrogen when incorporated into the soil, though it is not a nitrogen fixer (it accumulates, rather than fixes, nitrogen).

## Agricultural Impact

*A. retroflexus* is classified as one of the world's worst agricultural weeds by the USDA and international weed science organizations. It causes yield reductions in corn, soybeans, cotton, and vegetable crops through competition for water, nutrients, and light.

Key characteristics that make it a problematic weed:
- Extremely high seed production (100,000–500,000 seeds per plant)
- Prolonged seed bank viability (5–40+ years)
- Rapid germination and growth (can emerge in waves throughout the growing season)
- Herbicide resistance: Populations resistant to triazine, ALS-inhibitor, and EPSPS-inhibitor (glyphosate) herbicides have been documented.

Integrated weed management (IWM) approaches that combine cultural, mechanical, and chemical methods are recommended for control, as reliance on any single strategy (particularly herbicides) has driven the evolution of resistant populations.

## Distribution

Native to North America, now found worldwide in temperate and tropical regions. Particularly abundant in agricultural areas, disturbed sites, gardens, and waste places. Prefers rich, disturbed soils but tolerates a wide range of conditions including drought and moderate salinity.

## Ethnobotanical History

Indigenous peoples of North America utilized *A. retroflexus* extensively:
- The Hopi, Navajo, and Pueblo peoples ground the seeds into flour for bread and porridge.
- Young leaves were cooked as greens.
- The plant was used medicinally for digestive complaints, diarrhea, and as a poultice for wounds and insect bites.
- Red dye was extracted from the roots for use in basketry and textiles.

European settlers initially adopted the plant as a food source during hard times, a practice documented in the literature of the American colonial period and the Great Depression era.
