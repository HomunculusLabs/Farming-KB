# Taro (*Colocasia esculenta*)

## Description

Taro is a tropical root crop mentioned by [[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual* as one of the fail-safe subsistence crops that "hold constant value" in diverse agricultural systems. It is a starchy corm crop that has been cultivated for thousands of years across the tropics and subtropics, serving as a dietary staple for millions of people worldwide.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Araceae (aroid family)
- **Genus**: *Colocasia*
- **Species**: *C. esculenta*
- **Common names**: Taro, dasheen, eddoe, kalo (Hawaiian), arvi (Hindi)

## Key Facts

- Mollison lists taro alongside potato and arrowroot as crops that provide reliable subsistence value regardless of fluctuating climatic and market conditions.
- One of the oldest cultivated crops in the world, with evidence of cultivation dating back over 9,000 years.
- The corms are rich in starch but must be cooked before eating to destroy calcium oxalate crystals (raphides) that cause severe irritation.
- Taro thrives in wet, flooded conditions (paddy culture) but also grows in dryland cultivation.
- Hawaii's traditional ahupua'a system centered on taro (kalo) cultivation in terraced lo'i (pondfields).
- The plant is highly productive per unit area, yielding 15-30 tonnes per hectare under good conditions.
- Leaves are also edible when properly cooked and provide significant vitamins A and C.

## Cultivation Requirements

- **Climate**: Tropical to subtropical; frost-sensitive
- **Water**: Abundant — can be grown in flooded paddies or with consistent irrigation
- **Soil**: Prefers rich, water-retentive soils with high organic matter
- **Propagation**: By cormels or setts (sections of the main corm)
- **Growth period**: 6-12 months depending on variety and conditions

## Relevance to Permaculture and Mycology

Taro is an exemplary permaculture crop for several reasons. It functions in multiple zones — from wetland edges to flood-irrigated paddies. Its broad leaves create shade that suppresses weeds and maintains soil moisture. The flooded cultivation systems support diverse aquatic ecosystems including beneficial microorganisms and fungi. Taro's role in traditional Polynesian agriculture demonstrates integrated land management where a single crop supports entire communities while maintaining ecological health.

In mycological terms, taro's root zone supports diverse communities of beneficial fungi and microorganisms, particularly in flooded systems where anaerobic and aerobic zones create niches for varied fungal species. The organic matter from taro cultivation contributes to soil-building processes mediated by [[tagari-publications]]. Chapter 2, section on Maximum Product Yield Concept.
