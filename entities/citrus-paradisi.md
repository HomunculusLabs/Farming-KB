## Overview

Citrus paradisi, the grapefruit, is an evergreen [[mollison-designers-avocado-mango-and-subtropical-tree-crops]] in the family Rutaceae. It is believed to be a hybrid of the sweet orange (*C. sinensis*) and the pomelo (*C. maxima*), originating in Barbados during the seventeenth century.

The fruit is large, typically 10 to 15 centimeters in diameter, with a yellow-orange rind and segmented, juicy pulp. Flavor ranges from sweet to distinctly bitter and acidic depending on cultivar and growing conditions.

Grapefruit is a commercially significant citrus crop grown extensively in the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], South Africa, and Mediterranean countries. Global production exceeds 7 million metric tons annually.

The species is notable for its pharmacological interactions, as grapefruit juice inhibits cytochrome P450 enzymes in the liver and intestines. This interaction affects the metabolism of numerous medications and has been extensively studied since the 1990s.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Citrus paradisi was first described by the Scottish botanist James Macfadyen in 1830. The genus Citrus belongs to the subfamily Aurantioideae within the Rutaceae, a family of approximately 160 genera.

Molecular phylogenetic studies confirm that grapefruit is an interspecific hybrid, with pomelo (*C. maxima*) as the maternal parent and sweet orange (*C. sinensis*) as the paternal contributor. Sweet orange itself is a hybrid of mandarin and pomelo.

The tree is medium-sized, reaching 5 to 6 meters in height under cultivation, with a rounded to spreading crown. Young branches are often thorned, though thorniness decreases with age in most commercial cultivars.

Leaves are simple, alternate, and glossy dark green, measuring 7 to 15 centimeters long with crenulate margins. Leaf petioles are distinctly winged, a characteristic feature of the genus.

Flowers are white, fragrant, and borne in axillary cymes. Each flower has four to five petals approximately 1.5 centimeters long, with numerous yellow stamens surrounding a superior ovary.

The fruit is a modified berry known as a hesperidium, with a leathery rind (flavedo and albedo) enclosing segmented pulp vesicles. The rind ranges from pale yellow to pink-red depending on cultivar.

Grapefruit cultivars are broadly classified by flesh color: white (such as 'Duncan' and 'Marsh'), pink ('Thompson'), and red ('Ruby' and 'Star Ruby'). Red-fleshed cultivars contain lycopene as the primary pigment.

Seeds are polyembryonic in many cultivars, producing nucellar seedlings that are genetically identical to the parent tree. This characteristic is exploited in citrus breeding and propagation programs.

The flavedo (outer rind) contains numerous oil glands that release aromatic [[comparison-essential-oils-vs-neem-oil-vs-insecticidal-soap]] when crushed. These glands are visible as small translucent dots on the fruit surface.

Grapefruit trees can live and bear fruit for 50 to 75 years under favorable conditions. Productivity typically declines after 25 to 30 years, at which point orchards are often replanted.

The rootstock-scion interaction significantly influences fruit quality characteristics including juice content, soluble solids concentration, and acidity. Unsuitable rootstock combinations can produce fruit with off-flavors or excessive bitterness.

Unlike many citrus species, grapefruit retains its fruit on the tree for extended periods without significant quality loss. This "store-on-tree" characteristic allows flexible harvest scheduling over several months.

[[tree-canopy-fungal-sampling-techniques]] density affects both yield and fruit quality. Pruning to maintain open canopy structure improves light penetration, reduces pest pressure, and enhances internal fruit color development.

## Distribution and Habitat

Grapefruit is cultivated in subtropical and warm [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] worldwide, typically between 25° and 40° latitude. Major production areas include Florida and Texas in the United States, South Africa, and Israel.

The species requires warm temperatures for optimal growth, with a minimum of 1,200 to 1,500 hours above 13°C annually. It is more cold-sensitive than sweet orange, with significant damage occurring at temperatures below -3°C.

Optimal soils are well-drained sandy loams with a pH of 5.5 to 6.5, though the tree tolerates a range of soil types. Good drainage is essential, as waterlogging promotes root rot caused by Phytophthora species.

The tree thrives in full sun and requires consistent irrigation during the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. Mature trees are moderately drought-tolerant once established but produce best with regular water availability.

## Cultivation

Commercial grapefruit orchards are typically established at densities of 200 to 400 trees per hectare. Trees begin bearing fruit at three to five years of age and reach full production by eight to ten years.

Propagation is primarily by budding onto rootstock, with 'Carrizo' citrange and 'Swingle' citrumelo being widely used rootstocks. Rootstock selection influences tree vigor, disease resistance, and fruit quality.

[[soil-moisture-monitoring-irrigation-management]] is critical for fruit quality, with regulated deficit irrigation used to concentrate soluble solids. Excessive irrigation during the later stages of fruit development dilutes juice solids and increases peel thickness.

Harvest occurs from October through May in the Northern Hemisphere, depending on cultivar and region. Fruit does not ripen further after harvest, so picking at proper maturity is essential for quality.

Major pests include citrus rust mite, citrus leafminer, and [[comparison-olive-vs-fig-vs-pomegranate-mediterranean-fruit-trees]] fly. Huanglongbing (citrus greening disease), caused by Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus, poses a serious threat to grapefruit production globally.

Post-harvest treatments include degreening with ethylene for early-season fruit and application of imazalil or thiabendazole fungicides to control stem-end rot and [[growing-gourmet-trichoderma-green-mold-management]] during storage and transport.

## Nutritional Value and Chemistry

Grapefruit is an excellent source of vitamin C, providing approximately 31 milligrams per 100 grams of fresh fruit. It also contains significant amounts of potassium, folate, and dietary fiber.

The characteristic bitter flavor is primarily attributed to naringin, a flavanone glycoside concentrated in the membranes between pulp segments. Naringin content varies between cultivars, with white grapefruit generally containing more than red varieties.

Red grapefruit cultivars are rich in lycopene, a carotenoid antioxidant associated with reduced risk of cardiovascular disease and certain cancers. Lycopene content in red grapefruit can exceed that of raw tomatoes on a fresh weight basis.

Furanocoumarins, particularly bergamottin and 6',7'-dihydroxybergamottin, are the compounds responsible for grapefruit's drug interactions. These compounds irreversibly inhibit intestinal CYP3A4, reducing first-pass metabolism of affected drugs.
