## Overview

**Salix pentandra** L., commonly known as **bay willow**, is a medium-sized deciduous shrub or small tree in the family Salicaceae. It is native to northern and central Europe, extending across northern Asia to Siberia, and is naturalized in parts [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].

Leaves are alternate, simple, lanceolate to narrowly elliptic, 5–12 cm long and 2–4 cm wide, with finely serrate margins. The upper surface is dark green and glossy, while the lower surface is paler and glaucous. When crushed, the leaves emit a strong, pleasant fragrance reminiscent of bay laurel ([[mollison-designers-charcoal-production-biochar-soil-amendment]].

## Distribution and Habitat

Salix pentandra is widely distributed across northern and central Europe, from Scandinavia and the British Isles east through the Baltic states, Poland, and Russia to western Siberia. In the south, its range extends to the Alps and the Carpathian Mountains.

The species has been naturalized in eastern Canada, particularly in Newfoundland, Quebec, and Ontario, where it was likely introduced for [[salix-fragilis]] and S. alba.

Bay willow is characteristic of riparian and wetland habitats, including stream banks, lake margins, fens, marshes, and damp woodland. It prefers moist, mineral-rich soils and is tolerant of periodic flooding, though it is less dependent on permanently saturated conditions than some other willow species.

The species extends to higher elevations than most European willows, occurring up to 1,500 meters in the Alps and Carpathians. Seeds germinate readily on moist, bare soil, particularly in freshly disturbed riparian areas. Established plants also spread vegetatively through branch fragmentation, as broken branches that fall into water or moist ground readily root and establish new individuals.

The species provides habitat and food for numerous bird species. The dense branching structure offers nesting sites for warblers and thrushes, while the catkins are visited by siskins and other finches for seed during autumn and winter. It is hardy to USDA Zone 3 and tolerates a wide range of soil conditions, including heavy clay and periodically waterlogged sites.

Propagation is straightforward from hardwood cuttings taken in winter, which root readily in moist soil. The species grows rapidly, typically 60–100 cm per year in favorable conditions, and can be managed by coppicing or pollarding to maintain a compact form or harvest flexible stems for basketry.
