## Overview

**Vigna angularis**, commonly known as the adzuki bean, azuki bean, or red mung bean, is an annual legume in the family Fabaceae. It is native to East Asia and has been cultivated in Japan, Korea, and China for over 2,000 years.

The crop is valued for its small, reddish-brown seeds that are sweet-tasting and rich in protein, fiber, and micronutrients. Adzuki beans are a staple ingredient in East Asian desserts, pastries, and traditional confections.

As a nitrogen-fixing legume, adzuki bean contributes to soil fertility in rotation systems. Its relatively short growing season and adaptability to diverse climates have facilitated its spread to over 30 countries.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Fabales
- **Family:** Fabaceae
- **Subfamily:** Faboideae
- **Genus:** Vigna
- **Species:** V. angularis (Willd.) Ohwi & H. Ohashi

**Synonyms:** Phaseolus angularis (Willd.) W.F. Wight, Dolichos angularis Willd., Azukia angularis (Willd.) Ohwi.

Adzuki bean is an erect, bushy annual herb typically 30–60 cm tall, though some climbing varieties can reach 90 cm. The stems are slender, green to purplish, and sparsely pubescent.

Leaves are trifoliate with ovate to rhomboid leaflets 5–12 cm long. The terminal leaflet is larger than the lateral pair. Petioles are 5–15 cm long with stipels at the base.

Flowers are papilionaceous, yellow to pale orange, borne in axillary racemes of 5–20 flowers. Flowering is self-pollinating, which favors genetic uniformity in cultivated varieties.

Pods are cylindrical, 5–13 cm long, and contain 5–12 seeds. Mature pods are black or dark brown and may shatter at maturity in wild types.

Seeds are small (4–6 mm long), typically red but ranging from white, black, mottled, to green in different cultivars. The hilum is white and conspicuous.

## Cultivation

Adzuki bean is primarily grown in East Asia, with China accounting for approximately 70% of global production. Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and Thailand are other major producers.

The crop grows well in temperate to subtropical climates with temperatures between 18–30°C. It is relatively cold-tolerant compared to other Vigna species and can be cultivated at higher latitudes.

Optimal soils are well-drained sandy loams with pH 5.5–7.0. The crop is moderately drought-tolerant but performs best with consistent moisture during flowering and pod-filling stages.

Sowing occurs in spring (April–May) in temperate regions and during the monsoon season in tropical areas. Seed rate is 20–30 kg/ha with row spacing of 30–45 cm.

Maturity occurs in 90–120 days depending on variety and growing conditions. Average yields range from 800–1,200 kg/ha, with improved Japanese varieties reaching 2,000 kg/ha under optimal management.

Inoculation with Bradyrhizobium spp. enhances nodulation and nitrogen fixation. The crop fits well in rotation with rice, wheat, or barley.

## Nutritional Value

Adzuki beans contain approximately 19–25% protein on a dry weight basis. They are notably lower in fat than many other legumes, with only 0.5–2% lipid content.

The seeds are rich in dietary fiber (15–17%), particularly soluble fiber including resistant starch. This contributes to their low glycemic index and prebiotic properties.

**Key nutrients per 100 g dry seeds:**

- Protein: 19–25 g
- Dietary fiber: 15–17 g
- Carbohydrates: 55–60 g
- Fat: 0.5–2 g
- Iron: 4–5 mg
- Potassium: 1,250–1,400 mg
- Folate: 220–250 µg

Polyphenols, including proanthocyanidins and catechins, are concentrated in the seed coat.

Red varieties show significantly higher antioxidant capacity than white-seeded types.

Sprouting adzuki beans increases vitamin content and reduces trypsin inhibitor activity by 50–70%.

## Ecology and Applications

Adzuki bean forms effective nitrogen-fixing symbioses with Bradyrhizobium, contributing 40–80 kg N/ha to the soil. This makes it a valuable break crop in cereal-based farming systems.

The crop provides good ground cover during growth, helping suppress weeds and reduce soil erosion. Its dense canopy shades out competing weeds effectively.

In intercropping systems, adzuki bean pairs well with maize, sorghum, or millet. The legume supplies nitrogen to the companion cereal while the cereal provides structural support.

Crop residues are nutritious fodder for ruminants, containing 7–9% crude protein. The straw is palatable and can be fed fresh or as hay.

The crop has shown potential as a green manure crop in organic farming systems due to its rapid biomass accumulation and nitrogen contribution.

## Culinary and Cultural Significance

Adzuki bean is one of the most culturally significant legumes in East Asia. In Japan, it is the primary ingredient in **anko** (sweet red bean paste), used in wagashi, manju, dorayaki, and taiyaki.

In Chinese cuisine, adzuki beans are used in sweet soups, mooncakes, and steamed buns. Korean cuisine features them in **patbingsu** (shaved ice dessert) and **patjuk** (red bean porridge).

The beans are typically boiled, sweetened, and mashed into paste for confectionery applications. Their naturally sweet flavor reduces the need for added sugar compared to other bean pastes. Processing methods include steaming, pressure-cooking, or sprouting.

In traditional Chinese medicine, adzuki beans are considered a "cooling" food that supports kidney function and reduces edema. They are often prescribed for damp-heat conditions. Modern research has investigated their potential anti-diabetic and anti-obesity effects.
