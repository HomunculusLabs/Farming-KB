# Mycorrhizae

Mycorrhizae (singular: mycorrhiza) are symbiotic associations between fungi and plant roots, found in over 80% of terrestrial plant species. The term comes from the Greek *mykes* (fungus) and *rhiza* (root). These mutualistic relationships are among the most widespread and ecologically important on Earth, forming a [[fungal-hyphae]] explore soil volumes far beyond the root depletion zone, accessing phosphate from organic matter and mineral surfaces. Phosphorus is transported to the plant as phosphate ions.
- **Nitrogen**: AMF access ammonium and amino acids; ECM fungi are particularly efficient at mining organic nitrogen from soil organic matter via protease secretion.
- **Carbon**: The host plant supplies 10–20% of its photosynthetically fixed carbon (up to 30% in some ECM associations) to the fungal partner as glucose and fructose.
- **Micronutrients**: Copper, zinc, and other trace elements are also transported to plants via fungal hyphae.
- **Water**: Mycorrhizal hyphae improve plant water uptake, especially under drought conditions, by extending the effective absorptive surface area by 100–1000×.

## Common Mycorrhizal Network (CMN)

Individual mycorrhizal networks can interconnect multiple plants of the same or different species, forming a **common mycorrhizal network** (CMN). Through CMNs, plants can:

- Share carbon and nutrients (especially from older "mother trees" to shaded seedlings)
- Transmit chemical alarm signals in response to herbivore attack
- Transfer defensive compounds and allelochemicals
- Facilitate seedling establishment in competitive environments

Suzanne Simard's landmark research on Douglas-fir and paper birch forests demonstrated that carbon moves through CMNs from canopy-dominant trees to understory seedlings, a phenomenon dubbed the "mother tree effect."

## Ecological Significance

Mycorrhizae are keystone organisms in terrestrial ecosystems:

- **Carbon cycling**: Mycorrhizal fungi receive 5–20 Gt of carbon annually from their plant partners, representing a significant global carbon sink
- **Nutrient cycling**: They control the rate-limiting step of decomposition — the mobilization of nutrients from organic matter into plant-available forms
- **Plant community structure**: Mycorrhizal type (AM vs. ECM) influences forest composition, succession dynamics, and competitive outcomes between tree species
- **Ecosystem resilience**: Diverse mycorrhizal communities buffer ecosystems against drought, pathogen outbreaks, and nutrient perturbation

## Evolution and Fossil Record

The earliest evidence of mycorrhizal associations comes from the Ordovician (~460 Ma), with *Aglaophyton major* fossils showing arbuscule-like structures in the tissues of early land plants. The AMF-plant symbiosis likely facilitated the colonization of land by plants. Ectomycorrhizae evolved independently multiple times, with molecular evidence suggesting ECM associations in Pinaceae date to the Jurassic (~180 Ma).

## Commercial Inoculants

The global mycorrhizal inoculant market has grown substantially, with products targeting agriculture, horticulture, and ecological restoration. Key considerations:

- **Species selection**: *Rhizophagus irregularis* (formerly *Glomus intraradices*) is the most commonly used AMF species in commercial products due to ease of cultivation and broad host compatibility
