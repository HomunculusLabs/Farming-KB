# Psilocybe subfimetaria

**Psilocybe subfimetaria** is a psilocybin-containing mushroom species originally described from Australia. As the specific epithet suggests, it is closely related to and morphologically similar to *Psilocybe fimetaria*, a species first described from Europe. The species is part of a group of coprophilous and coprophilous-associated *Psilocybe* mushrooms found in temperate and subtropical regions of the [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]].

## Taxonomy

*Psilocybe subfimetaria* was described by mycologist Gastón Guzmán, the leading authority on the genus *Psilocybe*. The epithet *subfimetaria* means "somewhat like *fimetaria*," acknowledging the close morphological resemblance to *Psilocybe fimetaria*. The two species are distinguished primarily by differences in spore morphology, microscopic features, and geographic distribution.

Molecular phylogenetic studies have suggested that the *Psilocybe fimetaria* complex may encompass several geographically distinct but morphologically similar taxa. The relationship between *P. subfimetaria* and *P. fimetaria* sensu stricto requires further investigation using modern molecular methods.

## Description

- **Cap**: Small, 1 to 2.5 cm in diameter. Convex to broadly convex, sometimes with a slight depression at the center in age. Hygrophanous, reddish brown to cinnamon brown when moist, drying to a pale buff or ochraceous. Surface is smooth, slightly viscid when wet.
- **Gills**: Adnate to sinuate, close to subdistant. Initially grayish, becoming dark purplish brown as spores mature.
- **Stipe**: 3 to 6 cm long, 1 to 2 mm thick. Equal to slightly enlarged at the base. Pallid to brownish, often with blue-green bruising, especially at the base. Surface is smooth with a fine white pruina near the apex. Partial veil may form a fibrillose annular zone.
- **Spore print**: Dark purplish brown.
- **Spores**: Ellipsoidal to broadly ellipsoidal, 9 to 13 by 6 to 8 µm, with a distinct germ pore. Spore wall is moderately thick.
- **Taste**: Farinaceous.
- **Odor**: Slightly farinaceous or indistinct.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Psilocybe subfimetaria* is primarily known from Australia, where it has been collected in temperate and subtropical regions. It has also been reported from New Zealand and possibly other parts of Australasia.

The species is coprophilous, growing on the dung of herbivorous animals, particularly cattle and horses. It may also be found on dung-enriched soils and grasses in pastures. Fruiting occurs during the cooler, wetter months, typically from autumn through spring in southern Australia.

## Psychoactive Compounds

*Psilocybe subfimetaria* contains [[psilocin]] as its principal psychoactive alkaloids. The blue bruising reaction, while sometimes modest, confirms the presence of these compounds. Analytical data on exact alkaloid concentrations are limited due to the relative rarity of collections, but the species is considered to be moderately psychoactive.

## Ecological Role

As a coprophilous fungus, *P. subfimetaria* plays a role in nutrient cycling by decomposing herbivore dung. The spores are adapted to survive passage through the digestive tracts of grazing animals, facilitating dispersal to new substrate. This ecological strategy is shared with many other *Psilocybe* species and is a key factor in the [[guzman-global-distribution-patterns-neurotropic-fungi]] of coprophilous [[forensic-identification-methods-psilocybin-mushrooms-tlc-gc-hplc]] worldwide.

## Similar Species

- **Psilocybe fimetaria**: The European counterpart, very similar in macroscopic features. Distinguished primarily by geographic range and subtle microscopic differences.
- **Psilocybe cubensis**: Much larger, more robust species found in similar pasture habitats. Far more potent and easily identified by its larger size and persistent annulus.
- **[[gartz-panaeolus-subbalteatus-taxonomy-and-intoxication-history]]**: Found in similar habitats but belongs to a different genus with black spores and mottled gills.
- **Psilocybe coprophila**: A small coprophilous species with less pronounced blue bruising.

## Microscopic Features

[[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] is essential for distinguishing *P. subfimetaria* from closely related taxa. Basidia are four-spored, clavate, measuring 16–22 × 4–5.5 µm. Cheilocystidia are ventricose to lageniform, 14–24 × 4–7 µm, forming a conspicuous sterile band on the gill edges. Pleurocystidia are absent or rare. The pileipellis is a cutis of narrow (2–3 µm), hyaline to pale brown, gelatinized hyphae. Clamp connections are absent at the basidial bases. The spore morphology — particularly the combination of large, broadly ellipsoidal spores with a distinct germ pore — is the most reliable microscopic character for separating *P. subfimetaria* from *P. fimetaria* sensu stricto.

## Molecular Phylogeny

The phylogenetic placement of *P. subfimetaria* has not been extensively studied using modern molecular methods. It is presumed to belong to the same broad clade as *P. fimetaria* and other coprophilous Psilocybe species. Molecular phylogenetic analyses of the genus have revealed that many traditionally defined species complexes contain multiple cryptic taxa, and the *P. fimetaria* complex is likely no exception. DNA barcoding of additional Australasian collections would help resolve whether *P. subfimetaria* represents a genuinely distinct lineage or falls within the morphological variation of *P. fimetaria* sensu lato. The geographic disjunction between Australian and European populations is suggestive of allopatric speciation, but molecular confirmation is needed.
## Taxonomic History

*Psilocybe subfimetaria* was described by [[psilocybe-cubensis]]. However, the species' rarity and the availability of more easily cultivated alternatives have limited interest in developing dedicated cultivation techniques. If spore prints or tissue cultures were obtainable, standard Psilocybe cultivation methods on enriched horse or cow manure substrates would be a reasonable starting point.
## Conservation Status

*Psilocybe subfimetaria* has not been formally assessed for conservation status. Its dependence on pastoral habitats with herbivore dung means that changes in agricultural practices — including reduced grazing, vermiculture treatments, and the use of anti-helminthic drugs in livestock — could potentially affect its populations. However, the species appears to be naturally uncommon even in suitable habitat, and the extent to which it is threatened by agricultural changes is unknown.
## Seasonality and Fruiting

*Psilocybe subfimetaria* fruits during the cooler, wetter months in southern Australia, typically from autumn through spring (April to October). Peak fruiting generally occurs during the winter months of June through August, when consistent rainfall and moderate temperatures create optimal conditions. The species fruits directly on or near herbivore dung, appearing 3–10 days after significant rainfall events. Individual fruiting bodies are relatively short-lived, typically persisting for 5–10 days before deteriorating. The species may produce multiple flushes from the same dung pat over a period of several weeks during extended wet periods. Its preference for cooler temperatures distinguishes it from some tropical coprophilous species that require warmer conditions for fruiting.
## Identification Notes

Field identification of *Psilocybe subfimetaria* requires attention to several characters that distinguish it from other coprophilous species:
- Small cap (1–2.5 cm) with hygrophanous coloration: reddish brown to cinnamon when moist, fading to pale buff when dry
- Slightly viscid cap surface when wet, becoming dry and dull as it dries
- Blue-green bruising on the stipe, especially at the base, though this reaction can be modest
- Dark purplish brown spore print (distinct from the black spore print of Panaeolus species)
- Growth on or near herbivore dung in temperate pastures

The modest bluing reaction means that the psychoactive content is not always immediately apparent from handling. Spore print color is a critical diagnostic character — the dark purplish brown spore print distinguishes *P. subfimetaria* from the many dung-inhabiting species with black (Panaeolus) or brown (Conocybe) spore prints. The farinaceous (mealy) taste and odor provide supporting characters. Definitive identification requires microscopic examination, particularly of spore dimensions (9–13 × 6–8 µm), which are larger than those of many similar species.
