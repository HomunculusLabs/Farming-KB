## Description

Dano (Spanish for "harm" or "damage") is a folk illness recognized in the Peruvian Amazon, particularly among residents of Belen (Iquitos), caused by the intentional willing of evil by another person through envy (envidia) or vengeance (despecho). It represents a category of magical illness in which physical symptoms are attributed to supernatural attack rather than natural causes. What is crucial in understanding dano is the perceived cause of the syndrome, not the actual manifestation of the disease.

## Classification

- **Type:** Folk illness / culture-bound syndrome
- **Geographic distribution:** Peruvian Amazon, especially Iquitos and Belen
- **Causation model:** Magical -- caused by malevolent human agency via witchcraft
- **Related conditions:** Susto, Pulsario, Mal de Ojo
- **Key triggers:** Envidia (envy), despecho (vengeance)

## Key Facts

- Dano is attributed to envy (envidia) or feelings of vengeance (despecho) from others
- Belefinos recognize the important role that interpersonal strife and stress play in generating such infirmities
- Associated physical symptoms include hemorrhaging, muscular pain, loss of consciousness, suffocation, tumors, and consistent bad luck (called saladera)
- The cause of the syndrome is considered more important than the actual physical manifestation of the disease
- Dano is believed caused either by means of a powerful potion which is slipped into a drink or else thrown across a doorstep late at night
- A witch (brujo) may be paid to cause magical harm to someone, by taking ayahuasca for such purposes
- Some witches as well as ayahuasqueros are believed to control special spirits or familiars whom they can send to do evil or retribute evil magic
- People believe that a chonta or thorn that carries disease-producing substances can be shot through the air by such witches at their enemies
- Treatment requires a sick person suffering from dano to seek a healer to neutralize the harm before it is fatal
- The condition is considered psychosomatic by outside observers but very real within the cultural framework
- Most ayahuasqueros will acknowledge that they are visited at one time or another by possible clients who wish not only to be healed, but to cause harm to others
- Some ayahuasqueros refuse this trade, but others are willing and specialize in the use of psychedelic drugs for socially defined evil purposes

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Dano illustrates the dual-use nature of plant hallucinogens in Amazonian culture: ayahuasca can be employed both for healing and for causing magical harm. Some ayahuasqueros specialize in using psychedelic drugs for socially defined evil purposes, claiming they can leave their bodies while intoxicated and inflict harm and even incurable disease upon their clients' enemies. This dual role of plant medicines -- as tools for both curing and sorcery -- is essential for cultivators and researchers to understand. The belief system surrounding dano directly structures how, when, and why ayahuasca is used in therapeutic contexts. Healers treat dano by using ayahuasca visions to identify the person responsible for the bewitchment and to determine the appropriate counter-measure.

## Treatment Methods

- The healer uses ayahuasca visions to identify the agent responsible for the magical harm
- Visionary content is analyzed collaboratively between healer and patient to determine causality
- Traditional techniques include sucking out the magical object, blowing tobacco smoke, and reciting orations
- Herbal remedies and store-bought medicines may supplement ritual treatment
- The healer's symbolic presentation of omnipotence is crucial for successful treatment
- The healer must bring to light the cause of his patient's disease without the benefit of merely focusing upon a simple set of external symptoms

## Witches and Sorcery

The belief in dano is inseparable from the broader system of witchcraft and sorcery in the Peruvian Amazon. Most ayahuasqueros will acknowledge to the anthropologist that they are visited at one time or another by possible clients who may wish not only to be healed, but indeed, to cause harm to others. Some ayahuasqueros refuse this trade, but others are willing and specialize in the use of psychedelic drugs for socially defined evil purposes. They maintain that while intoxicated by the drug, they can leave their bodies and inflict harm and even incurable disease upon their clients' enemies.

The chonta thorn is a key element of dano-related sorcery. People believe that a chonta or thorn that carries disease-producing substances can be shot through the air by witches at their enemies. This magical projectile represents a culturally specific explanation for sudden-onset localized pain or illness.

## Diagnostic Process

The healer's diagnostic process for dano is central to ayahuasca therapy:
1. The healer must determine the origin of the illness before any treatment can begin
2. Ayahuasca visions reveal the agent responsible for the bewitchment -- patients frequently report seeing the person responsible for their magical illness
3. The healer and patient collaboratively analyze the visionary content to identify the specific type of magical harm (potion in drink, doorstep attack, spirit familiar, chonta thorn)
4. The healer performs appropriate counter-measures based on the identified cause
5. The healer's omnipotent stance reinforces patient confidence in the cure

## Evil Magic and Healing Magic

The dual nature of ayahuasca use -- for healing and for harm -- reflects a broader pattern in Amazonian cosmology where the same plants and powers can be directed toward either constructive or destructive ends. The healer who cures dano may use techniques that mirror those of the witch who caused it, but directed toward neutralization rather than harm. This mirrors the concept of power in Apache shamanism, where power itself has no intrinsic moral quality but is shaped by the intentions of its wielder.

## Ayahuasca Visions and Dano Diagnosis

One major pattern of informants' visions during ayahuasca sessions is the report of the person responsible for bewitching them. Others report a panorama of activity in which some man or woman expresses innermost thoughts and feelings toward the patient, such as sexual desire, vengeance, or hate. At times, some potion might be manufactured in a vision, which would later be thrown across a doorstep late at night. The healer uses these visionary reports to identify the specific type of dano and the agent responsible.

The generalized, immobilizing anxiety present in the sociocultural milieu of Belen is changed into solid fear placed squarely on the shoulders of some evil-doer by a healer whose presentation of self includes an omnipotent stance. The drug serves mainly diagnostic and revelatory purposes, enabling the healer to identify the magical cause of illness and then to deflect or neutralize the evil magic deemed responsible.

## Doctrinal Compliance

Ehrenwald (1966) traced the continuity between present-day scientific therapy and primitive healing, coining the phrase "doctrinal compliance." This concept describes the phenomenon where a patient ends up complying with the therapist's unconscious wishes and expectations. In the ayahuasca healing context, patients who have been exorcised by the healer prior to the session are more likely to experience visions consistent with the healer's diagnostic framework. This unconscious process operates in both magical and modern therapy procedures, and is particularly pertinent to the treatment of dano in Iquitos.
