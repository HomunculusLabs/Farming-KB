## Overview

Anchusa azurea, commonly called Italian bugloss or garden anchusa, is a herbaceous perennial in the family Boraginaceae. It is prized for its intense gentian-blue flowers and coarse, hairy foliage that forms an upright clump reaching 90–150 cm in height.

The species is native to the Mediterranean region and southeastern Europe, where it grows in dry meadows and rocky slopes. It has been cultivated as an ornamental since at least the 16th century and has naturalized in parts of western Europe and [[childrens-herbal-medicine]] as an anti-inflammatory and demulcent.

The species is sometimes confused with [[anchusa-officinalis]] (common bugloss), which has smaller flowers and a more sprawling habit. Anchusa azurea can be distinguished by its taller stature and larger, more intensely blue corollas.

Anchusa azurea is closely related to Anchusa officinalis and Anchusa undulata, all sharing the characteristic bright blue corollas and bristly indumentum. The species was described by Philip Miller in 1768.

Several synonyms exist in the literature, including Anchusa italica Retz. and Anchusa paniculata Aiton. The taxonomic circumscription of the genus Anchusa has been revised multiple times, and some authorities place species from this group in the segregate genus Hormuzakia.

The plant produces erect, branching stems clothed in coarse white bristles. Basal leaves are lanceolate to oblong, 15–30 cm long, with a rough, hispid surface. Stem leaves are smaller and sessile, clasping the stem at their base.

Flowers are borne in scorpioid cymes (the typical Boraginaceae inflorescence that uncurls like a scorpion's tail). Each flower has a five-lobed corolla, 12–18 mm across, of vivid gentian-blue with a white or cream eye.

The fruit is a four-lobed schizocarp that splits into four one-seeded nutlets (mericarps). Nutlets are ovoid, brown at maturity, and roughly textured.

The chromosome number is 2n = 16, consistent with the base number x = 8 found across most of the Anchusa genus. Polyploid cytotypes have been reported in some populations from the eastern Mediterranean.

## Distribution and Habitat

Anchusa azurea is native to the northern Mediterranean basin, with a range extending from Spain and southern France through Italy and the Balkans to western Turkey. It occurs at elevations from sea level to approximately 1,200 m.

The species favors dry, well-drained calcareous soils in full sun. Typical habitats include rocky hillsides, open woodland margins, roadsides, and disturbed ground. It tolerates drought well once established but dislikes waterlogged conditions.

As a garden escape, Anchusa azurea has naturalized in parts of the United Kingdom, the [[berry-growing]], and southern Australia. It is not considered invasive in any jurisdiction but can persist in waste areas and along fence lines.

Within its native range, Anchusa azurea populations are generally stable, though habitat loss from agricultural intensification has reduced some lowland occurrences. The species benefits from traditional grazing regimes that maintain open grassland habitat.

Populations on the Iberian Peninsula tend to have narrower leaves and slightly smaller flowers than those from the Balkans, reflecting adaptation to drier conditions in the west of the range.

## Cultivation

Anchusa azurea is cultivated primarily for its vivid blue flowers, which are among the most intense blues available in temperate perennial gardens. The species is hardy in USDA zones 3–8 and grows best in full sun with well-drained, moderately fertile soil.

Plants can be propagated by seed sown in spring or by root cuttings taken in late autumn. Division is possible but difficult due to the plant's deep taproot. Seed requires no pretreatment and germinates in 14–21 days at 18–21°C.

The main cultivar is 'Loddon Royalist', which won the RHS Award of Garden Merit. It produces deeper violet-blue flowers on compact plants reaching 90 cm. Other selections include 'Dropmore' and 'Opal', offering shades from sky-blue to violet.

Plants are relatively short-lived perennials, often declining after 3–4 years. They benefit from being allowed to self-seed, as volunteer seedlings tend to be vigorous. Anchusa azurea is deer-resistant due to its bristly foliage, making it a useful choice for rural gardens. It is also reported to be rabbit-resistant, though very hungry animals may sample young shoots in spring.

## Traditional and Medicinal Uses

Anchusa azurea has been used in Mediterranean [[magical-illness-peruvian-amazon-folk-medicine]] as a demulcent, expectorant, and anti-inflammatory agent. Preparations of the root and leaves were traditionally applied to wounds and skin inflammations.

The genus name Anchusa derives from the Greek "anchousa" (αγχουσα), meaning "paint" or "cosmetic," referring to the use of Anchusa root as a red dye. The roots contain anchusin, a red-brown pigment used historically in cosmetics and fabric dyeing.

Anchusa root was traded as a cheaper substitute for alkanet (Alkanna tinctoria) dye. The dye is fat-soluble and was used to color oils, ointments, and pharmaceutical preparations before [[singh-fungal-degradation-of-synthetic-dyes-and-textile-waste]] became available.

Modern phytochemical studies have identified pyrrolizidine alkaloids in Anchusa azurea, including intermedine and lycopsamine. These compounds are hepatotoxic and limit internal use of the plant. External applications in traditional medicine are considered lower risk.
