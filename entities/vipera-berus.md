## Vipera berus

## Overview

Vipera berus, the common European adder, is the most widely distributed venomous snake in Europe and the only venomous snake native to the United Kingdom. Its range extends from western Europe across Scandinavia and Russia to the Pacific coast of Asia.

The adder is a relatively small and unaggressive species that rarely poses a serious threat to humans. Envenomations are typically mild and fatalities are exceedingly rare, making it one of the least dangerous venomous snakes in its range.

Despite its modest medical significance, Vipera berus is ecologically important as a mesopredator in temperate ecosystems. It has been the subject of extensive research in [[query-best-kiwifruit-varieties-for-cold-climates]].

## Taxonomy [[trichome-development-and-morphology]]

Vipera berus belongs to the family Viperidae, subfamily Viperinae. It was first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1758 as Coluber berus. The specific epithet berus is of uncertain etymology, possibly derived from an older vernacular name.

Two subspecies are generally recognized: the nominate Vipera berus berus and Vipera berus bosniensis, the Bosnian adder. Some authorities have elevated certain populations to full species status, though this taxonomy remains debated.

Adult adders are relatively small, measuring 50 to 70 cm in total length with a maximum of approximately 90 cm. Females are typically larger and more robust than males. Sexual dimorphism in coloration is pronounced and consistent.

Males are typically grey, pale brown, or silvery with a dark zigzag dorsal stripe. Females are usually brown, copper, or reddish-brown with a darker zigzag pattern. Melanistic (all-black) individuals occur in both sexes, though they are more common in cooler regions.

The head is distinct from the neck, triangular in shape, and covered with small irregular scales. The snout is slightly upturned and the pupils are vertically elliptical. A prominent dark stripe runs from the eye to the angle of the mouth.

The body is stout with keeled dorsal scales arranged in 21 to 23 rows at midbody. The ventral scales number 130 to 160 in males and 132 to 164 in females. The subcaudal scales are paired in males and single in females.

## [[seasonal-distribution-and-habitat-of-wild-mushrooms]]

Vipera berus has the most extensive [[distribution-of-neurotropic-fungi]] any snake species in the world. Its range spans from western Europe (Portugal, Spain, France, United Kingdom) eastward across Scandinavia, central Europe, and Russia to Sakhalin Island and the Korean peninsula.

The species also occurs in northern Italy, the Balkans, Turkey, and northern Mongolia. It is absent from Ireland, Mediterranean islands, and most of southeastern Europe. Isolated populations exist at high elevations in the Alps and Carpathians.

Habitat preferences include heathlands, moorlands, bogs, open woodland, forest edges, and rocky hillsides. The adder favors areas with a mix of open ground for basking and dense vegetation for cover.

In the United Kingdom, adders are strongly associated with heather moorland and coastal dunes. In Scandinavia, they occur above the Arctic Circle, making them one of the few reptiles adapted to subarctic conditions.

Elevation ranges from sea level to approximately 3,000 meters in the Alps. The species requires well-drained soils for hibernation sites and areas of exposed ground for thermoregulation.

[[dighton-fungal-responses-climate-change]] may affect Vipera berus populations at the southern margins of its range. Rising temperatures could force northern retreat in countries such as Spain and France, while potentially expanding suitable habitat in Scandinavia.

## Venom Composition

Vipera berus venom is primarily hemotoxic, though it is considerably less potent than that of many other viperids. The LD50 in mice is approximately 0.55 to 1.45 mg/kg by intravenous injection.

Major venom components include snake venom metalloproteinases (SVMP), serine proteases, and phospholipase A2 enzymes. The venom also contains smaller quantities of C-type lectins, disintegrins, and L-amino acid oxidases.

Snake venom metalloproteinases are the primary agents of local tissue damage. They degrade basement membranes and capillary endothelium, producing pain, swelling, ecchymosis, and occasionally blistering or necrosis at the bite site.

Serine proteases affect the coagulation cascade, potentially causing thrombocytopenia and mild coagulopathy. Unlike some viper species, Vipera berus venom does not typically produce severe defibrinogenation or spontaneous hemorrhage.

Phospholipase A2 enzymes contribute to myotoxicity and may cause transient elevation of creatine kinase. Severe rhabdomyolysis is uncommon with adder envenomation.

The relatively mild venom reflects the species' small size and typical prey of small mammals, lizards, and amphibians. Venom yield is low at approximately 10 to 18 mg per bite in adults.

## Clinical Significance

Vipera berus is the only venomous snake native to much of northern and central Europe, making it the primary cause of snakebite in countries such as the United Kingdom, Sweden, Norway, and Finland. An estimated 50 to 200 significant envenomations occur annually across its range.

Most bites result in only local effects including pain, swelling, erythema, and lymphangitis. Systemic symptoms develop in approximately 5 to 10 percent of cases and may include nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and hypotension.

Severe envenomation can produce angioedema, particularly affecting the face and lips. Anaphylactic reactions to the venom itself occur rarely but have been documented in previously sensitized individuals.

Fatalities are extremely uncommon, with fewer than 10 deaths reported in the United Kingdom over the past century. Most deaths have involved very young children, elderly individuals, or those with preexisting medical conditions.

Antivenom therapy is available in many European countries, with Zagreb antivenom being the most widely used product. However, antivenom is reserved for moderate to severe envenomations due to the risk of serum sickness and anaphylaxis.

In the United Kingdom, the National Health Service maintains standardized protocols for adder bite management. Supportive care including analgesia, elevation of the affected limb, and monitoring for systemic progression is the mainstay of treatment for most cases.

Pediatric patients may experience more severe reactions relative to body size. Pregnant women bitten by Vipera berus have been reported to experience fetal distress [[wild-fermentation-and-spontaneous-cultures]] abortion, though successful pregnancies following envenomation have also been documented.
