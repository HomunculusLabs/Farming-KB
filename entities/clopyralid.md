# Clopyralid

## Description

Clopyralid is a [[aact-clopyralid-persistent-herbicide-compost-contamination-ingham]] in the pyridine carboxylic acid family, widely used for broadleaf [[coleman-preemergence-flaming-weed-control]] in agricultural and turf settings. It is notorious for its extreme persistence in [[compost]] and manure streams, where it can remain active for four to six years after the treated forage or hay has been digested and decomposed. This longevity makes clopyralid a significant contaminant threat to [[teaming-with-microbes-the-organic-gardeners-guide-to-the-soil-food-web]] and mushroom cultivators who rely on composted manures.

## Classification

- **Category**: Synthetic herbicide (pyridine carboxylic acid)
- **Chemical class**: Chloropyridine
- **Common trade names**: Stinger, Transline, Curtail, Reclaim
- **Regulatory status**: Restricted or monitored in many countries due to persistence concerns

## Key Facts

- **Extreme persistence**: Clopyralid can persist in compost and manure for four to six years, surviving the digestive process of livestock and the [[composting]] process itself.
- **Low degradation threshold**: Even at very low concentrations (parts per billion), clopyralid can cause significant damage to sensitive plants including beans, tomatoes, and other broadleaf crops.
- **Compost contamination pathway**: The primary route of contamination is through livestock fed treated hay or forage. The herbicide passes through the animal's digestive tract intact and remains active in the manure.
- **Symptoms of exposure**: Plants grown in contaminated soil or compost exhibit twisted or burnt leaves, stunted growth, abnormal tissue development, and epinasty (downward curling of leaves).
- **Detection method**: The bean test — planting herbicide-sensitive seeds like beans, corn, and tomatoes into the soil or compost and observing for stunting or foliage damage.
- **Broad-spectrum damage**: Affects solanaceous crops (tomatoes, peppers, potatoes), legumes (beans, peas), and composites (sunflowers) particularly severely.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Threat to Mushroom Substrates

Clopyralid contamination in composted manure is a critical concern for mushroom cultivators who use manure-based substrates, particularly for Agaricus species and secondary decomposers. Since many cultivators source compost from agricultural operations, the risk of introducing clopyralid into [[green-cannabis-rockwool-hydroponic-growing-media]] is real and documented.

### Organic Growing Risk

Organic gardeners and cultivators relying on manures for fertilization have experienced total crop loss due to persistent herbicides in composted manures. Cotter specifically warns that clopyralid can persist for four to six years and notes that many countries are attempting to ban or inform the public on the use of these herbicides.

### Testing Protocol

The bean test described by Cotter provides a low-cost, accessible method for detecting clopyralid contamination:
1. Plant herbicide-sensitive seeds (beans, corn, tomatoes) in the suspect compost or soil
2. Water and observe for signs of herbicide damage
3. Compare growth to control plants in known-clean soil
4. Look for twisted leaves, stunted growth, and abnormal tissue

### Mycoremediation Potential

Given its persistence and widespread contamination, clopyralid is a candidate target for [[advanced-mycoremediation-research-techniques]]. Fungi capable of degrading pyridine ring structures could potentially be deployed to remediate contaminated compost or soil, though specific protocols for [[fungal-degradation-of-pcbs-by-white-rot-fungi]] clopyralid are not yet well established.

## Safety and Handling

- Always test bulk composted manure before purchasing for cultivation use
- Wear gloves and eye protection when handling potentially contaminated materials
- Many countries are pursuing bans or public education campaigns regarding clopyralid use
- When in doubt, perform the bean test before committing to large-scale [[substrate-preparation]]
