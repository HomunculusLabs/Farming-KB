# Mimosa tenuiflora
**Jurema Preta / Tepezcohuite / Carbonal**
- **Division:** Magnoliophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Fabales
- **Family:** Fabaceae (subfam. Caesalpinioideae, formerly Mimosoideae)
- **Genus:** Mimosa
- **Authority:** (Willd.) Poir. (1810)
- **Synonyms:** Mimosa hostilis (Mart.) Benth. (taxonomically incorrect
  but widely used)

## Overview
Mimosa tenuiflora is a perennial tree native to northeastern Brazil and
extending into Central America and southern Mexico. It is one of the most
significant entheogenic plants in the Americas, renowned as the primary
source of DMT in traditional Brazilian jurema ceremonies and as a sacred
plant in indigenous and Afro-Brazilian religious practices. Separately,
the bark (marketed as "tepezcohuite" in Mexico) has been used for
centuries for wound healing, burns, and skin conditions, with modern
research validating potent antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and
regenerative properties.

## Morphology
- **Habit:** Perennial tree or shrub, 4–8 m (to 12 m optimal)
- **Bark:** Dark gray-brown, fissured; inner bark rich reddish-pink —
  the primary harvested material for both entheogenic and medicinal use
- **Leaves:** Bipinnate, 5–10 cm, 2–4 pairs of pinnae with 15–30 pairs
  of 3–5 mm leaflets; nyctinastic (fold at night)
- **Flowers:** Spicate, white to pale pink stamens in dense cylindrical
  heads, 3–6 cm, fragrant
- **Fruits:** Flat pods, 4–7 × 0.8–1.2 cm, 3–5 seeds
- **Roots:** Shallow lateral system; nitrogen-fixing via rhizobial
  symbiosis

## Habitat and Distribution
- **Brazil:** Caatinga biome (Bahia, Ceará, Paraíba, Pernambuco, Piauí)
- **Mexico:** Chiapas, Oaxaca, Morelos, Puebla, Guerrero
- **Central America:** Guatemala to Panama
- **Northern South America:** Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru
- **Habitat:** Tropical dry forest, thorn-scrub, secondary growth;
  0–1000 m altitude; tolerant of poor, acidic, drought-prone soils
- **Ecology:** Pioneer species; colonizes degraded land rapidly.
  Coppices vigorously after cutting

## Chemistry
### Psychoactive Compounds (Root Bark)
- **N,N-DMT:** 0.5–1.7% in dried root bark; primary entheogenic
  alkaloid
- **N-Methyltryptamine (NMT):** 0.01–0.06%
- **β-Carbolines:** Trace harmine/harmaline (insufficient for MAO
  inhibition — traditional preparations require separate MAOI source)
- Trunk/branch bark contains <0.05% DMT

### Medicinal Compounds (Inner Bark)
- **Tannins:** 10–15% condensed proanthocyanidins — astringent and
  antimicrobial
- **Flavonoids:** Kumatakenin, isoflavones — anti-inflammatory
- **Saponins:** Triterpenoid saponins — surfactant properties
- **Phenolic acids:** Gallic, chlorogenic, ferulic acid — antioxidant
- **Polysaccharides:** Arabinogalactans — stimulate fibroblast
  proliferation and collagen deposition for wound repair

## Ethnobotanical Use
### Jurema Ceremony (Northeastern Brazil)
Jurema preparations ("vinho de jurema") involve soaking or boiling
shaved root bark. Because M. tenuiflora lacks sufficient β-carbolines
for oral activity, traditional preparations incorporate Banisteriopsis
caapi bark or Peganum harmala seeds as MAOI sources. Ceremonial use
continues among Tupi, Pataxó, and Fulni-ô indigenous groups and in
the Jurema Sagrada (Toré) tradition of Afro-Brazilian syncretic
religions. Archaeological evidence suggests the practice may extend
back millennia.

### Tepezcohuite (Mexico)
Pre-Columbian and continuing use for burns (1st–3rd degree), wounds,
ulcers, skin infections, and dermatological conditions. After the 1984
San Juan Ixhuatepec gas explosion in Mexico City, tepezcohuite was
widely used in burn clinics with notable results, sparking
international research. Mexican pharmaceutical companies now produce
tepezcohuite-based creams, soaps, and capsules.

## Cultivation and Sustainability
- **Propagation:** Seeds germinate after scarification; also from
  cuttings and root suckers
- **Growth:** Fast-growing pioneer; 3–5 m in 3–5 years
- **Drought tolerance:** High; adapted to Caatinga's 6–9 month dry
  season
- **Sustainability concern:** Root bark harvesting kills the tree.
  Increasing entheogenic and pharmaceutical demand threatens wild
  populations. Non-lethal trunk bark harvest yields lower DMT but
  is recommended for sustainability

## Pharmacological Notes
Modern pharmacological studies on M. tenuiflora bark extracts have
demonstrated:
- **Antimicrobial activity** against Staphylococcus aureus,
  Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida albicans (attributed to tannins
  and saponins)
- **Anti-inflammatory effects** via inhibition of COX-2 and reduction
  of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 pro-inflammatory cytokines
- **Wound healing acceleration** in rat models — tepezcohuite-treated
  wounds showed 40–60% faster closure rates than untreated controls,
  with increased collagen density and organized granulation tissue
- **Antioxidant capacity** (DPPH and ORAC assays) comparable to vitamin
  C at equivalent concentrations, primarily from phenolic acids and
  flavonoids
