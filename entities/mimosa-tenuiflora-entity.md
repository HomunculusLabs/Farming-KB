# Mimosa tenuiflora

Mimosa tenuiflora (syn. Mimosa hostilis), commonly known as jurema preta, tepezcohuite, or carbonal, is a perennial tree in the family Fabaceae native to northeastern Brazil and extending into Central America and southern Mexico. It is one of the most significant ethnobotanical plants in the Americas, valued for millennia by indigenous peoples for its bark's exceptionally high concentration of N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT) — typically 0.5–1.5% in the inner root bark. The bark has been used traditionally in psychoactive brews, topical wound treatments, and cosmetic preparations. In modern times, M. tenuiflora has gained global recognition as the primary botanical source of DMT for ayahuasca analogs and changa preparations, while simultaneously being investigated for its remarkable wound-healing and antimicrobial properties. Its nitrogen-fixing capability, drought tolerance, and rapid growth also make it a valuable species for permaculture reforestation and soil rehabilitation in degraded tropical and subtropical landscapes.

## Taxonomy and Naming

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Magnoliophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Fabales
- **Family:** Fabaceae (subfamily: Mimosoideae)
- **Genus:** Mimosa
- **Species:** M. tenuiflora (Willd.) Poir. (1806)
- **Synonyms:** Mimosa hostilis (Mart.) Benth. (1840), Acacia hostilis Mart.
- **Common names:** Jurema preta (Brazil), tepezcohuite (Mexico), carbonal (Mexico), jurema (Brazil), calumbi (Brazil), tepescohuite
- **Etymology:** "Tenuiflora" from Latin tenuis (slender) + flos (flower), referring to the delicate flowers; "tepezcohuite" from Nahuatl tepētzohuītl, meaning "tree of the skin"

The taxonomic history is complex. The plant was first described as Acacia hostilis by Martius in 1837, transferred to Mimosa by Bentham in 1840, and later determined to be conspecific with Mimosa tenuiflora described by Willdenow in 1806. "Mimosa hostilis" remains the most widely used name in popular literature and online trade, though M. tenuiflora is the accepted botanical name.

## Description

### Growth Habit and Vegetative Morphology

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Growth form | Perennial deciduous tree or large shrub |
| Height | 4–8 meters, occasionally to 10 m |
| Trunk diameter | 10–25 cm, often gnarled and multi-stemmed |
| Bark | Dark gray to brown, rough and fissured; inner bark pinkish-red to reddish-brown |
| Branches | Armored with recurved prickles (stipular thorns), 3–6 mm long |
| Leaves | Bipinnately compound, 5–10 cm long; 2–4 pairs of pinnae, each with 15–30 pairs of small leaflets |
| Leaflets | Tiny, 3–6 mm × 0.5–1 mm, oblong, sensitive to touch (nyctinastic movement) |
| Root system | Deep taproot; lateral roots extensive; inner root bark is the primary DMT-containing tissue |

### Flower, Fruit, and Seed

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Inflorescence | Cylindrical spikes, 2–5 cm long, axillary, white to pale pink |
| Flower structure | Small, fragrant, 5-merous; prominent white stamens giving fluffy appearance |
| Flowering period | November to July (Southern Hemisphere; primarily December–March) |
| Fruit | Flat, linear legume pod, 4–7 cm × 0.8–1.2 cm, brown at maturity |
| Seeds | 3–5 per pod, oblong, brown, 3–4 mm, hard-coated |
| Germination | Seed coat scarification required; boiling water soak effective |
| Seed viability | Moderate; 2–3 years under cool dry storage |

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Native range:** Northeastern Brazil (Caatinga biome), extending from Ceará to Minas Gerais; also found in southern Mexico (Chiapas, Oaxaca), Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, Venezuela, and Colombia
- **Introduced/cultivated:** Widely planted in tropical and subtropical regions for agroforestry, erosion control, and ornamental purposes
- **Elevation:** 0–1,000 meters above sea level
- **Habitat:** Tropical dry forests, savannas, scrublands, and degraded lands; a pioneer species that colonizes disturbed areas rapidly
- **Climate:** Adapted to semi-arid conditions; tolerates annual rainfall of 250–1,000 mm; tolerates brief frost but not sustained freezing
- **Soil:** Tolerates poor, sandy, and rocky soils; prefers well-drained substrates; performs well in degraded and lateritic soils
- **USDA hardiness:** Zones 9b–12

## Chemistry and Active Compounds

The phytochemistry of M. tenuiflora has been extensively studied. The inner
root bark is one of the richest natural sources of DMT:

### Psychoactive Compounds

| Compound | Tissue | Concentration | Notes |
|----------|--------|--------------|-------|
| N,N-DMT | Inner root bark | 0.31–1.53% (dry weight) | Primary tryptamine alkaloid |
| N-methyltryptamine (NMT) | Inner root bark | 0.02–0.15% | Minor tryptamine |
| 5-MeO-DMT | Inner root bark | Trace to 0.02% | Present at negligible levels |
| β-carbolines (harmine, harmaline) | Root bark | Trace | Insufficient for oral activity alone |

### Wound-Healing Compounds

| Compound | Function | Notes |
|----------|----------|-------|
| Tannins (proanthocyanidins) | Astringent, antimicrobial, protein-precipitating | 10–16% in bark |
| Flavonoids (quercetin, myricetin) | Antioxidant, anti-inflammatory | Multiple glycosides present |
| Saponins | Surfactant, antimicrobial | Moderate concentrations |
| Mucilaginous polysaccharides | Wound-dressing, moisture retention | Present in bark extracts |

The bark's wound-healing properties are primarily attributed to its high tannin content, which precipitates proteins to form a protective barrier over wounds, combined with antimicrobial flavonoids that reduce infection risk.

## Traditional and Ethnobotanical Use

### Jurema Cult (Northeastern Brazil)

The use of M. tenuiflora as a psychoactive sacrament is one of the oldest
continuous entheogenic traditions in the Americas:

- **Vinho de Jurema:** A traditional brew made from the inner root bark of M. tenuiflora, consumed in indigenous and Afro-Brazilian ceremonies in the states of Pernambuco and Paraíba. Unlike ayahuasca, the traditional jurema brew is activated by the addition of β-carboline-rich plants (typically Banisteriopsis caapi or admixture plants containing MAO inhibitors)
- **Indigenous use:** The Atikum, Truká, Pankararu, and Tuxá peoples of northeastern Brazil have used jurema in spiritual ceremonies for centuries, possibly millennia. Archaeological evidence of Mimosa use dates to pre-Columbian ceramic pipe residues
- **Cultural significance:** Jurema is central to the Toré ritual complex, a syncretic ceremony combining indigenous, African, and Catholic elements
- **Legal context:** The use of jurema in traditional ceremonies is recognized under Brazil's 2004 regulatory framework for ayahuasca religious use (CONAD Resolution 01/2004)

### Tepezcohuite (Mexico)

In Mexico, the focus of M. tenuiflora use is predominantly dermatological rather than psychoactive:

- **Traditional wound care:** Mayan and other indigenous groups used bark poultices and decoctions for burns, wounds, and skin infections
- **Modern commercial use:** Tepezcohuite bark extract is widely marketed in Mexico and internationally in creams, soaps, and cosmetic products for skin regeneration, anti-aging, and burn treatment
- **Pharmaceutical validation:** Clinical studies have demonstrated that tepezcohuite bark extract accelerates wound healing comparable to silver sulfadiazine, with lower rates of adverse reactions

## Permaculture and Agroforestry Applications

Mimosa tenuiflora is an exceptionally valuable species for permaculture systems in tropical and subtropical drylands:

- **Nitrogen fixation:** Forms symbiotic associations with rhizobia (Bradyrhizobium spp.), fixing atmospheric nitrogen and improving soil fertility for companion plantings
- **Pioneer species:** Rapidly colonizes degraded, eroded, and compacted soils, making it ideal for ecological restoration and mine reclamation
- **Erosion control:** Deep root system stabilizes soil on slopes and degraded hillsides
- **Drought tolerance:** Once established, survives prolonged dry periods; deciduous habit reduces water demand during drought
- **Windbreak:** Dense, thorny canopy provides effective wind protection for more sensitive crops
- **Mulch and green manure:** Nitrogen-rich foliage provides excellent mulch material; leaves decompose rapidly due to low C:N ratio
- **Fuelwood:** Dense wood burns hot and is valued as firewood and charcoal
- **Living fence:** Thorny branches make effective livestock barriers when planted as a hedgerow
- **Bee forage:** Flowers provide nectar and pollen for honey bees during dry season when few other plants are in bloom
