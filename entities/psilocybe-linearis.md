# Psilocybe linearis

*Psilocybe linearis* is a psilocybin-containing mushroom species known from South America.
It is one of the less frequently collected and studied members of the genus Psilocybe, with a distribution centered in Andean and sub-Andean regions of the continent.
The species epithet refers to its characteristically elongated, slender fruiting bodies.

## Taxonomy

The species was described from South American collections and has been included in the taxonomic frameworks established by gaston-guzman|Gastón Guzmán and other mycologists who have surveyed psilocybin mushrooms across the Neotropics.
The epithet *linearis* refers to the notably elongated and slender stem, which is proportionally longer relative to cap diameter than many other species in the genus.

In Guzmán's infrageneric classification, *P. linearis* was placed within Section Psilocybe based on its bluing reaction, spore morphology, and confirmed psychoactive chemistry.
This section encompasses the majority of known psilocybin-containing taxa and is characterized by the development of a blue staining reaction when the fruiting body is injured.

The taxonomic status of some South American Psilocybe species remains under investigation, as molecular phylogenetic studies have revealed that morphological species concepts in the genus sometimes encompass multiple genetically distinct lineages.
Additional collections and DNA sequencing of *P. linearis* specimens from across its range would help clarify its relationships within the genus and assess whether it represents a single widespread species or a species complex.

## Morphology

### Macroscopic Features

- **Cap**: Small, ranging from 5 to 20 mm in diameter. Conic to broadly conic or campanulate when young, becoming convex with age, occasionally developing a slight umbo (central bump). Surface is hygrophanous, dark brown to reddish brown when moist, fading to pale brown or tan as it dries. The cap surface is smooth to slightly wrinkled, and the margin may show slight striations when wet.
- **Gills**: Adnate to sinuate attachment, moderately spaced (subdistant). Grayish when young, darkening to deep purplish brown with spore maturity. Gills stain blue-green when bruised or injured, with the color change typically developing within 30 seconds to two minutes. The gill edges may remain paler than the faces.
- **Stem**: 30 to 70 mm long, 1 to 2.5 mm thick, cylindrical and equal. Surface is white to pale buff, often with a silky sheen from longitudinal fibrils. Exhibits characteristic blue bruising, especially near the base and where handled. The stem is typically hollow and fragile, with walls that may be compressed or flattened when dried.
- **Partial veil**: Ephemeral, often not observed in mature specimens. When present in young buttons, it is fibrillose and cortinate (cobwebby), leaving at most faint fibrillose traces on the stem but no persistent annulus.
- **Flesh**: Very thin in the cap, somewhat more substantial in the stem. Whitish to pale brownish, bruising slowly to distinctly blue when cut or damaged. The blue color typically begins developing within a minute of injury and deepens over several minutes.

### Microscopic Features

- **Spores**: Elliptical to rhomboidal, approximately 10 to 14 by 6 to 8 micrometers, with a well-defined apical germ pore. Spore wall is smooth and moderately thickened, approximately 0.5 to 1 micrometer thick. Spore print is dark purple-brown to nearly black, similar to other species in the genus.
- **Basidia**: 4-spored (rarely 2-spored), clavate, measuring approximately 15 to 20 by 4 to 6 micrometers. Clamp connections are absent at basidial bases, a characteristic feature of the genus Psilocybe.
- **Cheilocystidia**: Present on gill edges, ventricose to lageniform (flask-shaped), often with a long narrow neck. Hyaline, thin-walled, and sometimes encrusted with pigment granules at the apex. These cells measure approximately 15 to 30 by 4 to 8 micrometers.
- **Pleurocystidia**: May be present or scarce, when found they are similar in morphology to cheilocystidia but generally less numerous. Their abundance can vary significantly between specimens and may be influenced by maturity and environmental conditions.
- **Pileipellis**: Composed of a cutis of interwoven cylindrical hyphae, 2 to 4 micrometers in diameter. Some hyphae may be slightly gelatinized, particularly near the cap surface, contributing to the slightly viscid texture when the cap is moist.

### Bluing Reaction

The blue staining reaction serves as the primary field diagnostic feature for distinguishing *P. linearis* from non-psychoactive look-alikes.
Bruising develops on the stem (especially the base), gills, and cap flesh.
The intensity of the reaction varies among specimens and may correlate with alkaloid content, though the relationship is not strictly linear.

The biochemical basis of the bluing involves oxidation of psilocin by endogenous enzymes that are released upon tissue damage.
The resulting blue-colored quinone polymers are similar to those formed in other psilocybin-containing species and represent one of the most characteristic field-identifiable features of the genus.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Psilocybe linearis* has been collected from montane and sub-montane regions of South America, including Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia.
It inhabits humid tropical and subtropical forests, particularly cloud forests and mossy areas along streams and ravines in the Andean cordillera.

The species typically fruits on decaying plant material including leaf litter, twigs, and woody debris in forested environments.
It has been found growing both terrestrially and on partially buried wood, often in association with bryophytes (mosses) and other moisture-retaining substrates.
Collections have been made at elevations ranging from approximately 1,500 to 3,000 meters above sea level.

Fruiting typically occurs during the rainy season when humidity is consistently high and temperatures are moderate, ranging from 10 to 20 degrees Celsius at the elevations where it occurs.
In montane cloud forests, suitable conditions may persist for much of the year due to persistent fog and precipitation from orographic lifting.

### Ecological Role

As a saprotrophic fungus, *P. linearis* plays a role in decomposing plant litter and recycling nutrients in montane forest ecosystems.
Its preference for mossy, high-humidity microhabitats suggests sensitivity to environmental moisture and temperature, which may make it vulnerable to habitat disturbance and climate change.

The species contributes to the decomposition of woody debris and leaf litter in cloud forest ecosystems, processes that are essential for nutrient cycling and soil formation in these biodiversity-rich but fragile environments.
Changes in cloud forest moisture regimes due to climate change could potentially impact fruiting patterns and population viability for this and other moisture-sensitive fungal species.

*P. linearis* is often found in association with bryophyte communities, which maintain high local humidity through water retention and evapotranspiration.
This association suggests that the presence of healthy moss populations may be an important factor in supporting viable *P. linearis* populations in montane habitats.

## Chemistry

The species is psychoactive, containing [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]] as its primary active alkaloids, confirmed through both chemical extraction and the presence of the characteristic blue bruising reaction.
Analytical data from researchers including [[bigwood]] and [[beug]] have placed it within the known range of potency for Neotropical Psilocybe species, likely in the low to moderate range.

[[baeocystin]] and [[norbaeocystin]] have been identified in related South American species and are presumed to be present in *P. linearis* as well, though specific quantitative analysis remains limited.
The pharmacological significance of these minor alkaloids is not fully understood, but they may contribute to the overall psychoactive profile.

### Biosynthetic Pathway

The biosynthesis of psilocybin in *P. linearis* follows the conserved tryptophan-derived pathway characteristic of all psilocybin-producing fungi.
L-tryptophan serves as the initial substrate and is decarboxylated by the enzyme PsiD to yield tryptamine.

The enzyme PsiM then catalyzes two successive N-methylations, converting tryptamine to N-methyltryptamine (MMT) and then to N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT).
A subsequent hydroxylation at the 4-position of the indole ring, catalyzed by the cytochrome P450 enzyme PsiK, converts DMT to 4-hydroxy-DMT (psilocin).

The final step involves phosphorylation of psilocin by a 4-hydroxytryptamine kinase to produce the stable phosphate ester psilocybin (4-PO-DMT), which accumulates in the fungal tissue as the primary storage form of the alkaloid.
This gene cluster (*PsiD*, *PsiK*, *PsiM*) is conserved across psilocybin-producing species and is expected to be present in *P. linearis* as well.

## Cultivation

No well-established cultivation methods exist for *P. linearis*.
Its natural growth on decaying plant material in forest environments suggests it could potentially be grown on sterilized lignocellulosic substrates such as hardwood sawdust or straw, but specific nutritional requirements and environmental preferences have not been systematically investigated.

Based on the cultivation parameters of related montane species, a substrate of supplemented hardwood sawdust with wheat bran or rice bran would likely support mycelial colonization.
Incubation at 20 to 24 degrees Celsius with high humidity would be expected to promote robust mycelial growth.

Fruiting induction would likely require elevated humidity (95% or above), fresh air exchange, and moderate temperatures (15 to 20 degrees Celsius), approximating the cool, misty conditions of its natural Andean cloud forest habitat.
A cold shock may or may not be necessary depending on the species' specific requirements, which remain undocumented.

The primary barrier to cultivation is the scarcity of available spore prints or living culture material.
Field collection from Andean cloud forests, followed by tissue culture isolation, would be necessary to establish any domesticated strains for cultivation research.

## Conservation Status

The conservation status of *P. linearis* has not been formally assessed.
However, its restriction to montane cloud forest habitats, which are among the most threatened ecosystems in the Andes due to deforestation, climate change, and agricultural expansion, suggests potential vulnerability.
Additional field surveys are needed to assess population sizes and distribution across its range.

## Similar Species

*Psilocybe linearis* may be confused with other small, brown Neotropical psilocybin mushrooms:

- [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]] — much larger, coprophilous habitat on dung
- [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-cyanescens-species-guide]] — undulating cap margin, temperate distribution
- [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] — different habitat (grasslands), distinctive conic cap with a pronounced papilla
- Non-psychoactive Deconica species — lack blue staining reaction entirely
- [[psilocybe-fascicata]] — Central American distribution, different growth habit in clusters

The most reliable field character for distinguishing *P. linearis* from look-alikes is its elongated, slender stem (30–70 mm) relative to the small cap (5–20 mm), combined with the Andean montane distribution and moss-associated habitat.
The blue staining reaction further distinguishes it from non-psychoactive Deconica species that may occupy similar ecological niches.
