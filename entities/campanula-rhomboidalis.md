## Taxonomy and Morphology

Campanula rhomboidalis was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The specific epithet refers to the rhombic shape of the stem leaves, which are widest at or below the middle and taper to both ends.

The species belongs to the Campanula rotundifolia aggregate, a taxonomically complex group of closely related species and subspecies. It is distinguished from Campanula rotundifolia by its more slender stems, smaller flowers, and narrower stem leaves.

Chromosome counts reveal Campanula rhomboidalis as a diploid (2n = 34), while many populations of Campanula rotundifolia are tetraploid. This ploidy difference supports the recognition of Campanula rhomboidalis as a distinct species rather than a subspecies.

Hybridization with other members of the rotundifolia group has been reported where species co-occur, particularly in the Alps. However, intermediate forms are uncommon and the species maintains its morphological integrity across most of its range.

The plant grows 15–40 cm tall with slender, wiry stems that are often curving or nodding at the tip. The stems arise from a compact fibrous rootstock and are typically unbranched.

Basal leaves are round to kidney-shaped, long-petiolate, and usually wither by [[trifolium-pratense]], Anthoxanthum odoratum, and Leontodon hispidus in traditionally managed hay meadow communities. These communities depend on late-summer mowing to maintain their species diversity.
