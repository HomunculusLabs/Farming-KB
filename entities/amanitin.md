## Description

Amanitin (also referred to as Amanita-toxin or α-amanitin) is a bicyclic octapeptide toxin and the principal lethal compound found in deadly *Amanita* species. It has a complicated and as-yet-undefined chemical structure that resists the action of heat, drying, and digestive juices. This extraordinary stability means cooking, drying, or preserving the fungus does not neutralise the poison.

## Classification

- **Type:** Mycotoxin (amatoxin class)
- **Chemical family:** Bicyclic octapeptides
- **Source organisms:** *Amanita phalloides*, *Amanita verna*, *Amanita virosa*, and some *Lepiota* and *Galerina* species
- **Mechanism:** Inhibits RNA polymerase II, halting protein synthesis in eukaryotic cells

## Key Facts

- Amanitin is responsible for over 90% of recorded deaths from mushroom poisoning worldwide.
- It is heat-stable, meaning cooking does not denature the molecule — a critical point for foragers.
- The toxin resists drying and the action of digestive juices, allowing it to survive passage through the gastrointestinal tract intact.
- Death from ingestion is apparently due to this heat-resistant amanita-toxin, which in experimental animals produces the majority of the lesions described in human post-mortems.
- The incubation period before symptoms appear is typically 10–12 hours, by which time cellular damage is already extensive.
- Symptoms progress from severe abdominal pain, vomiting, and diarrhoea to liver degeneration, kidney failure, delirium, collapse, and death.
- More than 50% of poisoning cases are fatal, with some estimates placing fatality as high as 90%.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Understanding amanitin is essential for anyone involved in mushroom identification, foraging, or cultivation safety education. The toxin's stability makes it impossible to render dangerous species safe through any culinary preparation method. For mycologists, amanitin-producing species serve as critical identification challenges — their superficial resemblance to edible species like the Field Mushroom (*Agaricus campestris*) makes them the most dangerous fungi in temperate regions. Antidotes include antiphalloidian serum (produced by immunising sheep at the Institut Pasteur) and intravenous glucose therapy to counter the hypoglycaemia caused by liver damage.

## Related Toxins

- **Phalloidin:** A related polypeptide toxin from the same species; quickly active but destroyed by heat.
- **Phallin:** A glucoside that attacks red blood corpuscles; readily destroyed by heat and digestive juices, and therefore not the primary cause of death.
- **Muscarine:** Found in *Amanita muscaria* but in small amounts; acts on the parasympathetic nervous system.
