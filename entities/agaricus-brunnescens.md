# Agaricus brunnescens

## Introduction

agaricus brunnescens, commonly known as the Button Mushroom, White Button Mushroom,
Champignon, or Table Mushroom, is the most widely cultivated mushroom in the world by a
significant margin. It forms the foundation of the global commercial mushroom industry,
a multi-billion dollar enterprise spanning every inhabited continent.

Note that A. brunnescens is the older taxonomic name for what is now widely known as
[[psilocybe-cubensis]],
[[hypholoma-capnoides]] and [[hypholoma-sublateritium]]), and
as a marketing comparison point for agrocybe-aegerita|Pioppino.

## Description

The cap of agaricus brunnescens measures 3 to 10 cm in diameter. At the button stage,
the cap is hemispherical and tightly closed against the stem, giving the mushroom its
common name. As it matures, the cap expands from convex to nearly plane, eventually
developing a slightly uplifted margin in fully opened specimens.

Color varies by strain, from pure white in the common commercial White variety to light
brown in the Cremini and Portobello strains. The cap surface is smooth and may become
slightly scaled or cracked in drier conditions. When bruised or cut, the flesh turns a
distinct pinkish to reddish-brown color, a key diagnostic feature.

The gills are initially pink when young, progressing through a grayish-pink stage before
reaching their mature chocolate brown color. This color progression is an important
indicator of maturity for harvest timing. The gills are free from the stem and are
crowded and narrow.

The stem measures 3 to 8 cm in length and is white, cylindrical, and solid. A
well-developed membranous annulus (ring) is present on the upper stem, a remnant of
the partial veil that protects the gills during development. The spore print is dark
chocolate brown, consistent across all varieties of the species.

## Distribution

agaricus brunnescens was originally described from the grasslands of Europe and
North America, where it occurs naturally in meadows, pastures, and open fields. Today,
it is cultivated worldwide on every continent, making it one of the most globally
distributed fungal species in existence.

Wild populations continue to exist in temperate grasslands, though the vast majority
of specimens encountered in commerce are products of controlled cultivation. The
species has been transported widely through agricultural and horticultural activities,
and escaped cultivated strains can sometimes be found in non-native environments.

## Natural Habitat

In nature, agaricus brunnescens is a saprophyte that colonizes grasslands, pastures,
compost heaps, and rich soils. It is commonly found in fields after rain, particularly
in areas with well-manured soils or accumulations of organic matter. The species plays
an important ecological role in decomposing organic material in grassland ecosystems.

Wild specimens often appear in rings or arcs (fairy rings) as the mycelium expands
outward from a central point over successive fruiting seasons. This growth pattern
reflects the underground [[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]]'s radial expansion through the soil substrate.

## Cultivation

agaricus brunnescens is grown on composted substrates, traditionally based on horse
manure but increasingly using synthetic composts formulated from straw, hay, poultry
manure, gypsum, and various [[cervantes-organic-amendments-chart]]. The composting process itself is carefully
managed over 7 to 21 days to create the specific chemical and biological conditions
required by the species.

The cultivation process involves several distinct phases: [[cotter-compost-substrate-preparation-manure-mushroom-cultivation]] and
composting, pasteurization to eliminate competitors, spawn running, casing layer
application, and carefully managed fruiting conditions. The casing layer, typically a
mixture of peat moss and limestone, is essential for triggering fruitbody formation.

Cultivation takes place in specialized growing rooms, converted buildings, caves, and
underground facilities where temperature, humidity, and fresh air exchange can be
precisely controlled. The industry has developed highly automated systems for large-scale
production, particularly in the Netherlands, the United States, China, and India.

The species employs a two-allele heterothallic mating system, though most cultivated
strains are secondary homothallic (self-fertile), simplifying the breeding and
maintenance of commercial strains. Varieties include the White Button (most common),
Cremini or Brown (mature button stage), and Portobello (fully mature with a large,
open cap).

## Medicinal Properties

While agaricus brunnescens is primarily valued as a food crop, research has
identified several [[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]] in the species. It contains beta-glucans,
ergothioneine, and various antioxidants that may contribute to general health benefits
including immune system support and anti-inflammatory effects.

The mushroom is a good source of B vitamins, selenium, copper, and potassium. Some
studies have investigated potential anti-cancer properties of Agaricus polysaccharides,
though the commercial Button Mushroom is less studied in this regard than the related
agaricus-blazei|Himematsutake.

## Flavor and Preparation

agaricus brunnescens has a mild, versatile flavor that has made it the most
commonly consumed mushroom in Western cuisine. The White Button variety has a subtle,
earthy taste that pairs well with virtually any dish. The Cremini offers a deeper,
richer flavor, while the Portobello provides a robust, meaty taste and substantial
texture.

Preparation methods are diverse: raw in salads, sauteed, grilled, stuffed, baked,
and added to soups, sauces, casseroles, and stir-fries. The Portobello is particularly
popular as a vegetarian burger substitute when grilled or broiled. All varieties should
be cleaned gently and stored refrigerated for optimal freshness and shelf life.
