## Overview

**Cotyledon orbiculata** L., commonly called pig's ear or round-leaf navel-wort, is a succulent shrublet in the family Crassulaceae. It is widespread across southern Africa, from the Western Cape through to the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal, and Namibia.

The species is one of the most variable and widely distributed Cotyledon species, with several recognized varieties differing in leaf shape, size, and flower color. It is a robust, easy-to-grow plant commonly found in cultivation worldwide.

Plants form erect or spreading shrublets 60–120 cm tall with thick, fleshy stems. The leaves are large, rounded, and coated with a powdery white bloom that rubs off easily.

In cultivation it is valued for its architectural form and low maintenance requirements. It is one of the most commonly encountered Cotyledon species in Mediterranean and subtropical gardens.

The species has ethnobotanical significance in southern Africa, where it has been used traditionally for treating ear infections, wounds, and skin conditions.

It is one of the best-known succulent species in South African traditional medicine. Multiple ethnic groups across its range have documented uses for the plant.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Cotyledon orbiculata was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in *Species Plantarum*. It is the type species of the genus *Cotyledon*.

Several varieties are recognized, including var. *orbiculata*, var. *dactylopsis*, var. *flanaganii*, var. *mucronata*, and var. *engleri*. These vary in leaf shape from nearly circular to narrowly lanceolate.

Stems are succulent, gray-brown, reaching 60–120 cm tall. Young stems are green and smooth, becoming woody and peeling with age.

Leaves are opposite, decussate, thick and fleshy, 5–15 cm long and 3–8 cm wide. The leaf shape varies from broadly ovate to almost circular depending on the variety.

Leaf margins often develop a reddish tinge under strong light or drought stress. The leaf surface is covered with a powdery white epicuticular wax (pruinose).

Flowering stems bear terminal cymes of 3–12 pendulous, tubular flowers. Flowers are orange-red to yellow, 2–3 cm long, with a swollen base and recurving petal tips.

Flowering occurs mainly in winter to spring (June–September in the southern hemisphere). Each flower produces a dry follicle containing numerous small seeds.

Roots are fibrous and spreading, adapted to anchoring in rocky substrates. Plants can regenerate from root fragments if the above-ground portions are destroyed.

The species hybridizes readily with other Cotyledon species where ranges overlap. Natural hybrids show intermediate leaf and flower characteristics.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species has the widest distribution of any Cotyledon, occurring across southern Africa. It ranges from Namibia through the Northern Cape, Western Cape, Eastern Cape, and into KwaZulu-Natal.

It grows in diverse habitats including rocky outcrops, cliff faces, dry thicket, and coastal scrub. The species is remarkably adaptable, occurring from sea level to 1500 m elevation.

Rainfall in its range varies from 100 mm in arid karoo to 800 mm in eastern thicket. It tolerates both winter and summer rainfall regimes, a trait uncommon among southern African succulents.

Populations are generally stable, and the species is not considered threatened. Its broad ecological tolerance contributes to its wide distribution.

It frequently colonizes road cuts and disturbed rocky ground, taking advantage of open, well-drained microsites.

The species shows remarkable phenotypic plasticity across its range. Coastal populations have broader, fleshier leaves than inland desert populations.

## Cultivation

Cotyledon orbiculata is one of the easiest succulents to grow. It tolerates a wide range of conditions from full sun to partial shade.

Use well-draining soil with added grit or sand. Standard cactus potting mix amended with perlite is suitable for container culture.

Water moderately during the growing season, allowing soil to dry between waterings. Established plants are drought-tolerant and can survive extended dry periods.

The species tolerates light frost to approximately −3 °C if kept dry. In colder climates, grow in containers that can be moved to a frost-free location.

Propagation is by stem cuttings, leaf cuttings, or seed. Stem cuttings root within 2–3 weeks in warm, dry conditions.

Plants may become leggy with insufficient light, producing elongated stems with widely spaced leaves. Regular pruning maintains a compact shape.

## Bioactive Compounds and Toxicity

Cotyledon orbiculata contains cardiac glycosides, notably cotyledoside and orbicuside. These compounds have potent effects on cardiac muscle contraction.

The plant is toxic to livestock, particularly sheep and cattle, when consumed in quantity. Poisoning incidents increase during drought when other forage is unavailable.

Traditional healers in South Africa use diluted leaf preparations to treat ear infections, boils, and skin ulcers. The antimicrobial properties have been partially validated in laboratory studies.

Despite traditional use, the cardiac glycoside content makes internal consumption dangerous. Even topical applications should be used with caution.

Research has demonstrated anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties in leaf extracts. Several studies have investigated its potential as a source of novel cardiac drugs.

The species is classified as a Schedule 2 poison under South African law due to its cardiac glycoside content. Livestock losses have been documented across the Karoo region.

## Ecological Role

The tubular, pendulous flowers are pollinated by sunbirds (Nectarinia species) in the wild. The flower shape and color are classic examples of bird-pollination syndrome.

The species provides perching and nesting sites for small birds in its native thicket habitat. The dense growth form offers protection from predators.

CAM photosynthesis allows efficient water use in arid environments. Stomata open at night, reducing transpirational water loss by up to 90% compared to daytime gas exchange.

The powdery leaf coating reflects excess solar radiation and reduces herbivore feeding. This epicuticular wax is a key adaptation for survival on exposed rock faces.

The wax layer also inhibits fungal pathogen establishment on the leaf surface. Plants in humid cultivation environments may lose this coating, becoming more susceptible to disease.

Older plants develop a dense woody framework that persists for decades. Some documented specimens in habitat exceed 50 years of age.
