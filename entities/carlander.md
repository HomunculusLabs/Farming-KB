# Carlander (Ecologist)

## Description

Carlander is a researcher cited by Bill Mollison in *[[permaculture]]: A Designer's Manual* (Chapter 2, Section 2.13) for work demonstrating the relationship between species diversity and ecosystem productivity in freshwater systems. Carlander showed that the standing crop of fish in reservoirs is an increasing function of the number of species present — a foundational finding that connects biodiversity directly to yield. This empirical finding provides one of the strongest scientific justifications for polyculture design in permaculture systems.

## Classification

- **Type:** Person — Fisheries Ecologist, Researcher
- **Domain:** Fisheries ecology, reservoir management, species-diversity-productivity relationships
- **Research Focus:** Freshwater fish populations in reservoir ecosystems
- **Key Finding:** Species diversity drives total biomass productivity in aquatic systems

## Key Findings

### Species Diversity and Fish Yield

Carlander's core finding, as cited by Mollison:

> The standing crop of fish in different reservoirs is an **increasing function of the number of species present**.

This means that reservoirs supporting more fish species consistently produce greater total fish biomass than those with fewer species. The relationship is not merely correlational — species diversity appears to actively drive productivity through more complete resource utilization.

### Mechanisms Behind the Pattern

Several ecological mechanisms explain why more species produce more total biomass:

1. **Niche complementarity:** Different fish species occupy different ecological niches — feeding at different depths, on different prey, at different times of day. Together they harvest more of the available energy than any single species could.
2. **Resource partitioning:** Multiple species divide the available food resources more completely, reducing waste and unused ecological space.
3. **Trophic complexity:** A diverse fish community includes species at multiple trophic levels (herbivores, insectivores, piscivores), creating more efficient energy transfer through the food web.
4. **Stability through diversity:** Multiple species provide redundancy — if one species declines, others can fill its ecological role, maintaining total productivity.

### Implications

- **Complexity produces yield:** More species create more niches, more efficient resource partitioning, and more complete utilization of available energy and nutrients.
- **Simple systems underperform:** Reservoirs with few species, like simplified agricultural systems, cannot match the total productivity of species-rich systems.
- **Parallel to terrestrial systems:** Mollison notes this pattern holds in "most wild systems" — complexity and standing crop both exceed what is found in simplified cultivated ecologies that replace them.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Polyculture justification:** Carlander's finding provides rigorous empirical support for polyculture over monoculture — more species in a system leads to greater total yield. This is a cornerstone principle of [[permaculture-design]] and directly contradicts the monoculture paradigm of industrial agriculture.
- **Aquaculture design:** For permaculture systems incorporating ponds and aquaculture, Carlander's work suggests stocking multiple compatible fish species rather than single-species monocultures to maximize protein yield per unit area. Include surface feeders, mid-water fish, and bottom feeders.
- **Against simplification:** The finding validates the permaculture critique of replacing complex natural systems (African veld, Australian savannah) with simplified pasture or monoculture plantation — such replacement "grossly decreases total yields" while requiring enormous energy inputs.
- **Energy accounting:** The energy cost of simplifying complex ecosystems far exceeds the value of the simplified replacement system when measured by total energy return rather than monetary return.
- **Pond design principle:** Design ponds with multiple niches (deep water, shallows, margins, submerged vegetation, rock piles) to support diverse fish communities and maximize protein production per unit area.

## Connection to Permaculture Principles

Carlander's work directly supports several Mollison [[permaculture-principles]]:

1. **Diversity of beneficial connections** — not raw diversity alone, but functional complexity that increases total yield through beneficial interactions
2. **System yield is theoretically unlimited** — limited only by designer information, imagination, and the functional connections between components
3. **Everything gardens** — each species occupies a niche and processes resources that benefit the whole system
4. **Cyclic opportunity** — more species create more cycling pathways, increasing opportunities for yield at each trophic transfer

## Practical Aquaculture Applications

For permaculture designers building aquaculture systems, Carlander's finding translates into specific stocking strategies:

- **Multi-species fish ponds:** Stock combinations like tilapia (algae feeders), catfish (bottom feeders), and bass (predators) to utilize all pond niches.
- **Integrated systems:** Combine fish with ducks (manure input), aquatic plants (water filtration), and rice or water vegetables (nutrient uptake) in paddy-fish systems.
- **Avoid monoculture ponds:** Single-species fish farming leaves ecological niches unfilled, wasting available food resources and reducing total protein yield.
- **Habitat complexity:** Add submerged structures, varying depths, and aquatic vegetation to create the niche diversity that supports multiple fish species.
