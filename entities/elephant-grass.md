# Elephant Grass

## Description

Elephant grass (*Pennisetum purpureum*), also known as Napier grass or Uganda grass, is a tall, robust perennial bunchgrass native to tropical Africa. It is one of the highest-yielding forage grasses in the world and a critical component of tropical livestock systems, erosion control programs, and bioenergy research. The grass forms large clumps 2–5 meters tall with thick stems 1–3 cm in diameter and broad leaves 60–120 cm long.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Poaceae (Gramineae)
- **Subfamily**: Panicoideae
- **Genus**: Pennisetum
- **Species**: *P. purpureum*
- **Hybrid**: *P. purpureum × P. glaucum* (pearl millet Napier or "king grass")

## Key Facts

- Among the highest biomass-producing terrestrial plants, yielding 30–80 tonnes of dry matter per hectare per year under optimal management.
- Grows 2–5 meters tall, with some improved varieties reaching 7 meters.
- Propagates primarily by stem cuttings and root splits; does not produce abundant viable seed.
- Crude protein content ranges from 8–16% depending on fertilization, age at harvest, and variety.
- Excellent silage crop due to high soluble carbohydrate content in stems.
- Used extensively in push-pull pest management systems in East Africa, where it attracts and traps stemborer moths while repelling them from adjacent maize crops.
- Important bioenergy crop candidate; ethanol yields per hectare exceed those of sugarcane in some trials.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Mollison includes elephant grass in his tropical mulch comparison trials (Table 10.1), demonstrating its effectiveness as a mulch material:

- **Mulch production**: Elephant grass mulch produced strong yields across multiple crops in Mollison's referenced trials: maize 3.3 t/ha, cowpea 0.9 t/ha, soybean 1.3 t/ha, and cassava 16.6 t/ha. These yields significantly exceeded bare-ground controls for most crops.
- **Biomass engine**: The extraordinary productivity of elephant grass makes it a key "biomass engine" in permaculture systems, converting sunlight and soil nutrients into large volumes of organic matter for mulch, compost, or animal feed.
- **Erosion control**: Dense root systems and rapid above-ground growth make it the standard species for contour hedge planting on tropical slopes, forming living terraces that trap sediment and slow runoff.
- **Integrated livestock systems**: Central to cut-and-carry zero-grazing systems across East Africa, where smallholder dairy farmers harvest and carry fresh grass to stalled animals, converting sunlight into milk protein efficiently.

## Ecological Considerations

- Can become weedy in humid tropics; requires active management to prevent spread into unwanted areas.
- Does not tolerate waterlogging or extended flooding; best on well-drained sites.
- King grass (Napier-pearl millet hybrid) offers improved palatability and regrowth in some environments.

## Cultivation Notes

- Plant stem cuttings (2–3 nodes) at 45° angle with one node buried; spacing 0.5–1.0 m between plants, 1.0 m between rows.
- First cut 3–4 months after planting; subsequent cuts every 6–8 weeks during growing season.
- Cut at 10–15 cm height to promote vigorous regrowth; avoid cutting too low as it damages growing points.
- Responds dramatically to nitrogen fertilization; application of 100–200 kg N/ha/year is typical for intensive forage systems.
- Intercrop with legumes (Leucaena, Desmodium, Stylosanthes) to improve protein content and provide nitrogen.
