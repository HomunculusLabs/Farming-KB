## Overview

**Thermopsis rhodanthoides** (J.Howell) J.F.Macbr., commonly known as the rose-flowered thermopsis or rose buckbean, is a perennial herbaceous legume native to the western United States. It belongs to the Fabaceae family, subfamily Faboideae, and is one of approximately 28 species in the genus Thermopsis, commonly known as false lupines or buckbeans.

The species is endemic to a relatively restricted range in the interior Pacific Northwest and northern Rocky Mountains. Thermopsis rhodanthoides is distinguished from related species by its rose-pink to purplish flowers, a coloration unusual within the predominantly yellow-flowered genus. This distinctive floral pigmentation makes it one of the most recognizable members of the genus.

The genus name Thermopsis is derived from the Greek "therme" (heat) and "opsis" (appearance), though the etymological reference is unclear. The common name "buckbean" is shared with several Thermopsis species and reflects the superficial resemblance of their trifoliolate leaves to those of Menyanthes trifoliata, the true buckbean.

Thermopsis rhodanthoides typically grows 30–60 centimeters tall and produces erect racemes of showy pea-like flowers from late spring through mid-summer. The species is a component of montane and subalpine plant communities, often occurring on rocky slopes, talus fields, and open woodland margins at elevations of 1,200–2,800 meters.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Thermopsis rhodanthoides was originally described as Piptanthus rhodanthoides by John Thomas Howell in 1945 and later transferred to the genus Thermopsis by James Francis Macbride in 1943. The specific epithet rhodanthoides means "resembling a rose flower," referring to the distinctive pink to rose-purple corolla color that distinguishes this species from its predominantly yellow-flowered congeners.

The genus Thermopsis is closely related to Baptisia and Lupinus within the tribe Genisteae. Molecular phylogenetic analyses place Thermopsis within a clade that also includes Anagyris and Piptanthus, genera of Mediterranean and Asian distribution. This biogeographic pattern suggests an ancient Tertiary origin with subsequent divergence driven by continental drift and climatic fluctuations.

Thermopsis rhodanthoides is an erect, rhizomatous perennial herb arising from a thickened, branching caudex. Stems are 30–60 centimeters tall, typically unbranched or sparingly branched above the base, and covered with a mixture of spreading and appressed pubescence. The foliage is predominantly basal, with stem leaves reduced in size and number toward the inflorescence.

Leaves are trifoliolate with petioles 3–8 centimeters long. Leaflets are broadly elliptic to obovate, 3–7 centimeters long and 2–4 centimeters wide, with entire margins and a distinctive silvery-green coloration. The upper leaf surface is sparsely pubescent while the lower surface is more densely hairy, particularly along the midrib and vein axils.

The inflorescence is a terminal, erect raceme 10–25 centimeters long, bearing 10–30 flowers. Each flower is papilionaceous, 15–22 millimeters long, with a rose-pink to purplish standard petal, wings of similar coloration, and a keel that is often slightly darker. The calyx is campanulate, 6–10 millimeters long, with five shallow teeth and a dense villous indumentum.

The fruit is an oblong to linear legume (pod), 3–6 centimeters long and 8–12 millimeters wide, covered with dense spreading hairs. Pods are dehiscent at maturity, splitting along both sutures to release 5–15 seeds. Seeds are ovoid, approximately 4–5 millimeters long, olive-brown to dark brown, with a smooth seed coat and a small white hilum.

## Distribution and Habitat

Thermopsis rhodanthoides is native to the interior Pacific Northwest and northern Rocky Mountain regions of the western United States. Its documented range extends through central and eastern Oregon, eastern Washington, northern Idaho, and western Montana. The species reaches its greatest abundance in the Blue Mountains of northeastern Oregon and the Wallowa Mountains.

Populations are typically found at elevations of 1,200–2,800 meters, with a concentration of records between 1,500 and 2,200 meters. The species favors montane and subalpine habitats including rocky slopes, talus fields, open Ponderosa pine and Douglas-fir woodlands, subalpine meadows, and riparian corridors. It shows a strong affinity for well-drained, gravelly to rocky substrates derived from volcanic and metamorphic parent materials.

Thermopsis rhodanthoides prefers full sun to partial shade and well-drained, slightly acidic to neutral soils (pH 5.5–7.0). Annual precipitation in its range varies from 40 to 100 centimeters, much of which falls as winter snowpack. The species is adapted to a pronounced winter dormancy period and initiates growth shortly after snowmelt in late spring.

The species is classified as Vulnerable (G3) by NatureServe, reflecting its restricted geographic range and susceptibility to habitat disturbance. Population sizes at individual sites are generally small, typically fewer than 100 plants, with larger populations occurring on protected public lands.

## Ecology and Interactions

Thermopsis rhodanthoides forms nitrogen-fixing root nodules in association with rhizobial bacteria, contributing to soil nitrogen enrichment in the nutrient-poor montane and subalpine communities it inhabits. This capability is particularly significant on talus and scree slopes where organic matter accumulation is limited.

The rose-pink flowers of Thermopsis rhodanthoides are pollinated primarily by bumblebees (Bombus spp.) and other long-tongued bees. The unusual flower color within the genus may represent a specialization for a specific pollinator fauna, as most Thermopsis species with yellow flowers are visited by a broader range of generalist pollinators. The flowers produce modest quantities of nectar and exhibit no obvious floral scent.

The foliage contains quinolizidine alkaloids, a class of compounds common in the tribe Genisteae that deter herbivory by mammals and insects. These alkaloids, including thermopsine and cytisine derivatives, render the foliage unpalatable to livestock and most wild ungulates. However, the alkaloid content also limits the species' value as a forage plant for managed grazing systems.

Seed dispersal in Thermopsis rhodanthoides is primarily ballistic, with the dehiscent pods forcibly ejecting seeds when they dry and split. This autochorous mechanism disperses seeds 1–3 meters from the parent plant. Secondary dispersal may occur through surface water runoff on steep slopes and transport by granivorous rodents that cache or consume the seeds.

The rhizomatous root system enables vegetative reproduction, allowing established clones to persist for decades and expand gradually. Individual genets may cover areas of 1–3 square meters, with interconnected ramets sharing a common root system. This clonal growth habit contributes to population persistence even when seedling recruitment is limited by unfavorable conditions.

## Conservation Status

Thermopsis rhodanthoides faces several conservation challenges related to its restricted range and specific habitat requirements. The primary threats include habitat degradation from recreational activities, invasive species encroachment, and altered fire regimes in montane forest ecosystems.

Off-highway vehicle use on Forest Service and BLM lands has been documented to directly damage Thermopsis rhodanthoides populations through soil compaction and physical destruction of plants. Road maintenance and trail construction activities can also impact populations located along transportation corridors in montane areas.

Invasive plant species, particularly Centaurea stoebe (spotted knapweed) and Bromus tectorum (cheatgrass), compete with Thermopsis rhodanthoides for limited resources on disturbed sites. These invasive species alter fire frequency and intensity, creating conditions that favor further invasion and reduce habitat suitability for native forbs.

Climate change represents a significant long-term threat to montane and subalpine species such as Thermopsis rhodanthoides. Warming temperatures may shift suitable habitat upward in elevation, potentially reducing the total available habitat area on mountain peaks. Altered precipitation patterns, particularly reduced winter snowpack, could affect the timing of growth initiation and flowering.

Conservation priorities for the species include population monitoring on public lands, protection of high-quality montane habitats, and control of invasive species in occupied sites. The species receives some protection through its occurrence on National Forest and BLM lands, but specific management plans for Thermopsis rhodanthoides are limited. Ex-situ conservation through seed banking has been initiated by several regional native plant programs.
