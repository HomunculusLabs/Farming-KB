# Drosera aliciae

## Overview

Drosera aliciae, commonly called Alice's sundew, is a small rosette-forming perennial carnivorous plant in the family Droseraceae. It is endemic to the southwestern Cape region of South Africa.

The species produces flat, circular rosettes of spoon-shaped leaves covered in glistening tentacles. Each tentacle secretes a sticky mucilage that traps insects, which the plant digests to supplement nutrient uptake from its impoverished native soils.

Drosera aliciae is one of the most widely cultivated sundew species due to its compact size, attractive appearance, and ease of growth. It thrives on sunny windowsills and in terrariums, making it accessible to beginning carnivorous plant growers.

The species was named in honor of Alice Marguerite Pegler (1861–1929), a South African botanist and plant collector. It was formally described by R. Hamet in 1905.

In the horticultural trade, Drosera aliciae is sometimes sold under the outdated synonym Drosera aliciae forma major. This name refers to a slightly larger-growing form that may represent a local variant rather than a distinct taxon.

The species hybridizes readily with other South African rosette sundews, particularly Drosera capensis and Drosera spatulata. These hybrids are occasionally available from specialist nurseries.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Phylum Magnoliophyta → Class Magnoliopsida → Order Caryophyllales → Family Droseraceae → Genus Drosera → Species D. aliciae.

The genus Drosera comprises over 250 species of carnivorous herbs distributed across all continents except Antarctica. Drosera aliciae belongs to a subgroup of South African rosette sundews that includes several other horticulturally important species.

Plants form flat rosettes 3–8 cm in diameter with 15–30 leaves. Each leaf is spoon-shaped (spatulate), 1–3 cm long, with a broad lamina and a narrow petiole.

Mature plants may produce multiple rosettes from the base, forming small clumps over time. Older leaves at the center of the rosette gradually die back while new leaves emerge from the growing point.

The leaf surfaces are densely covered with glandular tentacles up to 4 mm long. These tentacles produce a clear, sticky mucilage that glistens in sunlight, giving the plant its characteristic dewdrop appearance.

In bright light, the tentacles and leaf margins develop a deep red to crimson pigmentation. In lower light, the foliage remains predominantly green.

Flower scapes rise 10–20 cm above the rosette, bearing 2–6 small, five-petaled flowers. Petals are pink to light purple, 8–12 mm long, and self-pollinating in most cultivated forms.

The flowers open sequentially over several days, with each bloom lasting only a single day. Seeds are tiny, dark brown, elongated, approximately 0.5 mm long, and produced in large quantities per capsule.

The root system is fibrous and shallow, serving primarily as an anchor rather than for nutrient absorption. Most nutrient uptake occurs through the carnivorous leaves rather than the roots.

## Distribution and Habitat

Drosera aliciae is endemic to the Western Cape Province of South Africa, where it occurs in a narrow coastal band. Its range extends from approximately Stilbaai to Knysna along the southern Cape coast.

The species grows in seasonally wet, sandy or peaty soils on coastal flats and in seepage areas. It is found at low elevations, typically below 300 m, in areas receiving winter rainfall.

The native habitat experiences a Mediterranean climate with cool, wet winters and warm, dry summers. Plants may enter a brief summer dormancy in response to drying conditions, though cultivated specimens often remain evergreen.

Populations are threatened by habitat loss due to coastal development and agricultural expansion. The species is not currently listed as endangered but has a restricted natural range.

Several disjunct populations show minor morphological variation, particularly in leaf width and tentacle density. These differences are insufficient for taxonomic recognition but may reflect local adaptation to microhabitat conditions.

## Carnivorous Adaptations

The glandular tentacles on the leaf surface secrete a viscous mucilage containing digestive enzymes. When an insect lands on the leaf, it becomes trapped in the sticky secretion.

Adjacent tentacles slowly bend inward over a period of minutes to hours, pressing the prey against the leaf surface. This tentacle movement increases contact between the digestive secretions and the captured prey.

The speed and extent of tentacle movement in Drosera aliciae are less dramatic than in some other species, such as Drosera burmannii. However, the mechanism is functionally identical and equally effective at subduing small prey.

Digestive enzymes including proteases and phosphatases break down the insect's soft tissues over 1–2 days. The dissolved nutrients are absorbed through the leaf surface, supplementing the plant's nitrogen and phosphorus intake.

The carnivorous habit is an adaptation to the nutrient-poor, acidic soils of the species' native habitat. Insects provide essential minerals that are unavailable from the sandy substrates.

Research has shown that fed Drosera plants produce significantly more flowers and seeds than unfed controls. This confirms the direct reproductive benefit of carnivory in nutrient-limited environments.

## Cultivation

Drosera aliciae is among the easiest sundews to grow and is recommended for beginners. It thrives in bright indirect light to partial direct sun, producing the best coloration with at least 4 hours of direct sun daily.

The growing medium should be a mix of sphagnum peat and silica sand or perlite in a 1:1 ratio. Standard potting soil and fertilizer are fatal to carnivorous plants, as their roots are adapted to very low nutrient conditions.

Terracotta and concrete containers should be avoided, as they leach minerals into the growing medium over time. Plastic or glazed ceramic pots are the preferred containers for all carnivorous plant species.

Plants should be maintained in a water tray, keeping the soil consistently moist with distilled, rain, or reverse-osmosis water. Tap water contains dissolved minerals that accumulate and harm the plants over time.

Ideal temperatures range from 15–28°C during the growing season. The species tolerates brief periods near freezing but should be protected from prolonged frost.

Humidity levels of 50–70% are optimal, though the species adapts to lower humidity in well-ventilated indoor environments. Terrarium culture provides ideal humidity but requires attention to air circulation to prevent fungal issues.

Plants benefit from a winter rest period with slightly cooler temperatures (10–15°C) and reduced photoperiod. Growth slows during this period but does not cease entirely in most indoor conditions.

Propagation is straightforward from leaf cuttings, root cuttings, or seed. Leaf cuttings placed on moist sphagnum produce plantlets within 3–4 weeks.

Seed requires no special stratification and germinates in 2–4 weeks at temperatures of 20–25°C. Fresh seed germinates at higher rates than stored seed, which declines in viability after 1–2 years.

## Ecological Role

In its native seepage habitats, Drosera aliciae grows alongside other carnivorous plants including Drosera capensis and Utricularia species. These carnivorous plant communities are characteristic of the nutrient-poor wetlands of the southern Cape.

The species contributes to insect population regulation in its microhabitat, capturing primarily small flies, gnats, and springtails. The captured insects are mostly aerial plankton rather than ground-dwelling species.

Botanical surveys of the southern Cape wetlands have documented Drosera aliciae as a component of the Fynbos vegetation type. This biodiverse ecosystem is one of the world's most threatened biomes, with many endemic species facing conservation challenges.
