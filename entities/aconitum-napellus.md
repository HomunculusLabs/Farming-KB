## Monkshood (*Aconitum napellus*)

## Overview

*Aconitum napellus* is a herbaceous perennial in the buttercup family (Ranunculaceae), native to mountainous regions of Europe. It produces striking hooded blue-purple flowers and contains aconitine, one of the most potent plant neurotoxins known — capable of causing lethal cardiac arrhythmias from dermal absorption alone.

Despite its extreme toxicity, monkshood has a 2,000-year history in European and Asian traditional medicine and remains a source of modern pharmacological research. The genus was historically used in arrow poisons across Eurasia.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Ranunculales
- **Family:** Ranunculaceae
- **Genus:** *Aconitum*
- **Species:** *A. napellus* L.

**Etymology:** Greek *akóniton* (dart poison), reflecting its use on weapon tips.
Common name "monkshood" refers to the helmet-shaped upper sepal.

**Notable subspecies:**
- *A. n.* subsp. *napellus* — Western and central Europe (type subspecies)
- *A. n.* subsp. *vulparia* — Central and southern Europe
- *A. n.* subsp. *neomontanum* — Eastern Alps and Carpathians

## Morphology

- **Habit:** Upright perennial, 0.5–1.5 m tall; stems unbranched or sparsely
  branched above
- **Root:** Tuberous, consisting of a parent tuber with daughter tubers attached;
  roots are the most toxic plant part (1–3% alkaloids by dry weight)
- **Leaves:** Palmately 5–7 lobed, deeply divided, dark green and glossy,
  5–12 cm diameter; cauline leaves alternate, reduced upward
- **Flowers:** Racemose inflorescence, 10–40 flowers per spike; sepals form a
  distinctive helmet (galea) 12–22 mm high, blue-violet to deep indigo;
  two modified nectar-spurred petals enclosed within the hood
- **Fruit:** Follicle, 15–22 mm, containing numerous small black seeds

Flowering occurs July–September. The hooded flower morphology is unique among
European temperate flora and immediately diagnostic.

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Native range:** Mountainous regions of western, central, and southern Europe;
  from the British Isles and northern Spain east to the Carpathians
- **Habitat:** Damp mountain meadows, subalpine pastures, stream banks, forest
  clearings, and rocky slopes at 300–2,600 m elevation
- **Naturalized:** Parts of hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management; tolerates partial shade

## Chemistry: Aconite Alkaloids

The root contains a complex mixture of diterpenoid alkaloids:

- **Aconitine (C₃₄H₄₇NO₁₂):** The principal toxin; binds voltage-gated sodium
  channels (Nav1.7) in their open state, preventing inactivation. Causes
  persistent sodium influx, membrane depolarization, and fatal cardiac
  arrhythmias (ventricular fibrillation, asystole)
- **Mesaconitine and hypaconitine:** Structural analogues with similar potency
- **Pseudaconitine:** Present in some subspecies; slightly less potent
- **Additional constituents:** Benzoylaconine (hydrolysis product, less toxic),
  aconine, and minor alkaloids (songorine, karacoline)

⚠️ **Lethality:** Estimated lethal oral dose is 1–2 mg aconitine (approximately
1–5 g of fresh root, or even less of dried root). Dermal absorption of
aconitine from handling fresh plant material has caused fatalities. The
toxin is heat-stable — cooking does not destroy it.
