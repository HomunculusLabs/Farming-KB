# Hygrophorus coccineus

## Overview

Hygrophorus coccineus, commonly known as the scarlet waxy cap or rosy waxy cap, is a strikingly colored [[coleman-hardy-winter-vegetables-cold-tolerance-mechanisms]] and late-season fruiting distinguish it from most other *Hygrophorus* species.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Kingdom: Fungi — Division: Basidiomycota — Class: Agaricomycetes — Order: Agaricales — Family: Hygrophoraceae — Genus: Hygrophorus — Species: H. coccineus (Schaeff.) Fr.

The species was originally described as *Agaricus coccineus* by Jacob Christian Schaeffer in 1774. Elias Magnus Fries transferred it to *Hygrophorus* in 1838. The epithet *coccineus* (Latin for "scarlet") refers to the vivid red cap color.

Molecular phylogenetic studies place *H. coccineus* in the *Hygrophorus* clade associated with coniferous forests, closely related to *H. puniceus* and *H. hypothejus*. Some authorities have treated these taxa as conspecific, though most contemporary European mycologists maintain them as separate species.

**Cap** measures 3–7 cm diameter, initially hemispherical, becoming convex to broadly umbonate with age. Surface is smooth and characteristically viscid to glutinous when wet, with a shiny appearance when dry.

Cap color ranges from bright scarlet to orange-red or vermilion, sometimes fading to salmon-pink at the margin with age. The viscid cuticle can be peeled easily, revealing the paler flesh beneath.

**Gills** are adnexed to subdecurrent, moderately spaced, thick, and distinctly waxy in texture — the defining characteristic of the genus. Color is white to pale cream, sometimes developing a pinkish tint near the stipe base.

**Stipe** measures 4–8 cm long and 6–15 mm thick, cylindrical and often slightly tapering upward. Surface is white and covered in fine white fibrils or a mealy pruina, particularly near the apex.

The stipe is solid and firm, with white flesh that has a mild, slightly mealy or radish-like odor and taste. The base may develop yellowish staining with age.

**Spore print** is white. **Spores** measure 7–9 × 4–5 µm, ellipsoid, smooth, and inamyloid.

## Distribution and Habitat

H. coccineus is found across temperate and boreal Europe, from Scandinavia and the British Isles south to the Mediterranean region. Its distribution extends eastward through central Europe into parts of western Russia.

The species is ectomycorrhizal, forming obligate associations with coniferous trees, particularly Scots pine (*[[dighton-pine-needle-litter-decomposition-fungal-succession]].

The species shows some preference for north-facing slopes and shaded positions within forests, where moisture levels remain higher during autumn. Fruitings are often more abundant in years with above-average autumn rainfall.

Elevation range extends from sea level to approximately 1,500 meters in mountainous regions, though it becomes increasingly uncommon above 1,000 meters.

## Edibility

H. coccineus is generally considered edible, though it is not highly regarded for culinary purposes. The flesh is mild with a faint radish-like flavor that some foragers find pleasant but others consider insipid.

The viscid cap cuticle should be removed before cooking, as it can be unpleasantly slimy. The waxy gills become more palatable when thoroughly cooked. The stipe is typically firmer than the cap and may require longer cooking times.

⚠️ While *H. coccineus* itself is not toxic, it occurs in the same habitats and season as several other red-capped fungi that require careful differentiation. Collectors should verify the waxy gill texture, white spore print, and ectomycorrhizal habit before consuming any red-capped mushroom.

The species is relatively uncommon and local in most of its range, so collection should be conservative to avoid impacting wild populations. Many mycologists recommend photographing rather than collecting this species.

## Bioactive Compounds

Limited pharmacological investigation has been conducted on *H. coccineus*. Extracts from related *Hygrophorus* species have demonstrated antimicrobial and antioxidant properties, suggesting similar potential in this species.

The vivid red pigmentation of the cap is due to carotenoid compounds, primarily beta-carotene and related xanthophylls. These pigments have documented antioxidant activity and may contribute to the mushroom's [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]] against competing microorganisms. Similar compounds may be present in *H. coccineus* but have not been specifically characterized.

## Ecological Role

As an ectomycorrhizal species, *H. coccineus* forms mutualistic associations with the fine root systems of coniferous trees. The [[fungal-mycelium-hyphae-and-the-feeding-phase]] extends the effective absorptive surface area of host roots, facilitating enhanced uptake of water, phosphorus, and micronutrients from the soil.
