# Description

Dilution to extinction is a serial dilution technique used in [[mushroom-cultivation]] to
isolate the specific microbial communities that trigger fruitbody formation in species
that require or benefit from microbial interaction with [[casing-soil]]. The method involves
progressively diluting a soil sample from a mushroom stem base across twelve sequential
tubes, each containing 9 ml of sterilized water, transferring 1 ml (10 percent) from one
tube to the next.

The result is a series of samples with theoretically decreasing microbial populations,
down to a single individual cell in the final tube. By applying each dilution to identical
containers of colonized substrate with sterilized casing soil, cultivators can identify
the precise dilution level that triggers fruiting and the adjacent dilution that does not,
thereby isolating the minimum viable microbial community needed for fruitbody formation.

The identified dilution sample can then be expanded into a [[microbial-slurry]] for use in
production-scale casing operations, providing a practical method for inducing fruiting in
species that have stubbornly resisted cultivation due to unknown microbial requirements.

# Key Facts

Equipment required includes 250 ml sterilized water (distilled preferred), a sterile
dispensing tool such as a pipette or baster, twelve sterilized jars or test tubes with
caps, one large quart jar, and fresh sterile pipette tips for each transfer.

The serial dilution procedure begins by collecting soil from the stem base of a fresh wild
or cultivated mushroom, including any binding rhizomorphs and adhering mycelium. This soil
sample is added to 250 ml cooled sterilized water in a large jar labeled 0, shaken
vigorously, and allowed to settle for 10 minutes.

Twelve smaller sterilized tubes are filled with 9 ml sterilized distilled water each and
labeled 1 through 12. Using a sterile pipette, 1 ml is transferred from jar 0 to tube 1,
which is then shaken vigorously. A fresh pipette tip is used to transfer 1 ml from tube 1
to tube 2, and the process continues through tube 12.

# Casing Dilution Assay

Prepare 42 colonized containers of identical weight and species, such as quart jars with
pure mycelium culture. Apply sterilized casing soil to 39 containers, leaving 3 without
casing as a no-casing control group. Number 36 jars as dilutions D1-1 through D12-3 with
three replicates per dilution.

Label 6 control jars: C1-1 through C1-3 (casing with sterile water only, no dilution) and
C2-1 through C2-3 (no casing at all). This eliminates the possibility that an abiotic
factor such as a chemical or physical trigger in the casing soil itself is responsible for
fruiting.

Apply 1 ml of each dilution to its group of three jars using a sterile pipette, uncovering
and recovering the cultures as quickly as possible. Seal with breathable filters and
incubate in darkness at the species-appropriate temperature. After casing colonization
over a few weeks, transfer to [[fruiting-conditions]] with diffuse light and cooler
temperature, and observe which dilution triggers fruiting.

# Relevance to Cultivation

Many commercially important mushroom species require microbial partnerships to initiate
fruiting: Agaricus (button, portabella), Stropharia ([[king-stropharia]]), Morchella (morels),
Lepiota (parasol), and Clitocybe (blewits). Dilution to extinction provides a systematic,
low-technology method for identifying these microbial triggers without requiring DNA
sequencing or advanced laboratory equipment.

Laboratories with more advanced capabilities can use polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or
denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE) on successful dilution samples to identify
the microbial communities present, advancing the understanding of fungal-microbial
symbiosis in fruitbody formation.

## Practical Applications

Dilution extinction has several important applications in sustainable agriculture and ecological design. Practitioners integrate these methods into holistic management systems that enhance productivity while maintaining ecological balance.

## Historical Significance

The development and understanding of dilution extinction has evolved through decades of observation, experimentation, and knowledge transfer across cultures and scientific disciplines.
