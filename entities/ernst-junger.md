# Ernst Junger

## Description

Ernst Junger (1895-1998) was a German writer, philosopher, and cultural critic whose literary work and personality had a profound influence on Albert Hofmann. Hofmann described Junger's influence as a "radiance" that gave the world "a new, translucent splendor" through his stereoscopic perspective on surfaces and depths. The two men conducted one of the first non-clinical LSD experiments together in February 1951 at Hofmann's home in Bottmingen, along with the pharmacologist Heribert Konzett, marking a significant moment in the cultural history of psychedelics.

- **Key works**: Das Abenteuerliche Herz, Auf den Marmorklippen, Heliopolis, Besuch auf Godenholm

## Key Facts

- Hofmann's enchantment with Junger began with "Das Abenteuerliche Herz" (The Adventurous Heart), which he read repeatedly over forty years
- Their first correspondence began in 1947 when Hofmann sent birthday greetings as a "thankful reader"
- Junger incorporated drug themes into his novel Heliopolis (1949), featuring the character Antonio Peri, a drug researcher who "captured dreams like others chase butterflies"
- In Heliopolis, Junger described Peri's library containing works by De Quincey, E.T.A. Hoffmann, Poe, Baudelaire, and a "paper on the phantastica of ergot by Hofmann-Bottmingen"
- The first LSD experiment together took place in February 1951 with a low dose of 0.05 mg
- Junger initially compared LSD to "a house cat" next to the "tiger" mescaline, but later revised this assessment after higher-dose experiments
- Hofmann gave Junger the rare 1855 monograph by Ernst Freiherrn von Bibra, "Die Narkotischen Genussmittel und der Mensch"
- Junger's story "Besuch auf Godenholm" incorporated the incense stick experience from their LSD session
- Junger argued that drugs should only be tried in "small circles" and disagreed with Huxley's notion that transcendence could be given to the masses
- Their correspondence addressed fundamental philosophical questions about whether hallucinogenic drugs represent a "forbidden transgression of limits"

## The 1951 LSD Experiment

The experiment took place at Hofmann's home in Bottmingen at 10:00 AM, with pharmacologist Heribert Konzett present as medical backup. At the low dose of 0.05 mg, the experience was characterized by intensification of aesthetic experience: roses of unknown luminosity, Mozart's flute and harp concerto perceived as "heavenly music," and the contemplation of incense smoke. Junger enjoyed oriental color displays while Hofmann traveled among Berber tribes in North Africa. Konzett experienced "a breath of timelessness." The return to normal consciousness involved strong sensitivity to cold, celebrated with dinner and Burgundy wine.

## Philosophical Correspondence

Hofmann and Junger maintained a rich correspondence about the fundamental questions raised by hallucinogenic drugs. Hofmann asked whether substances that so deeply affect the mind represent a forbidden transgression, whether the innermost core of being can be damaged by material substances, and what implications psychoactive drugs have for free will. Junger responded that the risk exists in every aspect of existence and that the new substances represent a crossing over into a new world age, comparing wine to the new psychedelic drugs as classical physics is to modern physics.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Junger's intellectual engagement with LSD and hallucinogenic substances represents the intersection of psychedelic chemistry with European high culture and philosophical tradition. His literary treatment of drug experiences, from Das Abenteuerliche Herz through Heliopolis and Besuch auf Godenholm, created a cultural framework for understanding psychedelics as tools for exploring consciousness rather than merely recreational drugs. This intellectual framework influenced how subsequent generations of researchers and practitioners understood the significance of psychoactive substances including psilocybin mushrooms.

## Von Bibra and the History of Drugs

Hofmann gave Junger the rare and valuable 1855 monograph by Ernst Freiherrn von Bibra, "Die Narkotischen Genussmittel und der Mensch" (Narcotic Pleasure Drugs and Man), a pioneering standard work in drug literature and a primary source for the history of drugs. Von Bibra's definition of "narkotischen Genussmittel" encompassed substances far beyond modern narcotics, including coffee, tobacco, coca, fly agaric, and hashish. His observation that the individual who abuses a drug "sinks into insignificance beside the numbers of those who pass calm and happy hours with a moderate dose" remains as relevant today as when it was written over a century ago. This exchange of drug literature between Hofmann and Junger enriched both men's understanding of the deep historical roots of humanity's relationship with psychoactive substances.

## Philosophical Significance

The Hofmann-Junger correspondence and shared experiments represent a unique intersection of chemistry, literature, and philosophy in the history of psychedelics. Hofmann brought the perspective of the natural scientist who had created the most potent psychedelic known to humanity, while Junger brought the perspective of one of Europe's greatest living writers and cultural critics. Their exchange on whether psychedelic drugs represent a "forbidden transgression of limits" — with Hofmann expressing concern about the implications for free will and the integrity of the self — remains one of the most profound philosophical discussions of the psychedelic experience ever recorded. Junger's response, that "compared to the effects of radiation, those of the magical drugs are more genuine and much less rough," positioned psychedelics as a more refined form of humanity's evolving relationship with transformative substances and technologies.
