# Charles Arden-Clarke and David Hodges

## Description

Charles Arden-Clarke and David Hodges are researchers cited by Mollison from their article in New Scientist (12 February 1987) on the subject of soil erosion in the United Kingdom. Their work provided scientific evidence linking soil erosion to declining organic matter levels, lending formal research support to what organic farmers and permaculturalists had long observed. Their findings are used by Mollison to argue for the urgent adoption of organic farming methods.

## Classification

- **Type**: People / researchers

## Key Facts

- Published findings in New Scientist, 12 February 1987, on soil erosion in the UK.
- Their central finding: "many of the recent outbreaks of severe erosion are clearly linked to falling levels of organic matter in the soil."
- They demonstrated that "the more organic matter there is in the soil, the more stable it is" — soil stability results from good soil structure and water infiltration.
- Inorganic soils (lacking organic matter) break down under rain impact, leading to surface sealing, increased overland flow, and subsequent erosion as rills or gullies.
- Without organic matter to bond soil particles, the destroyed surface can "powder and blow away."
- Their research focused particularly on delicate soils over chalk substrates, where they recommended replacing crops with pasture or forests.
- They identified intensive arable use and winter cropping as practices that create the most erosion.

## Relevance to Cultivation

- **Evidence for organic methods**: Their research provided peer-reviewed, mainstream scientific backing for organic farming and [[green-manure]], grass leys on rotation, hedgerows, and minimal cultivation are not merely beneficial but imperative for soil preservation.
- **Diagnostic value**: Organic matter levels can serve as a key indicator of soil health and erosion vulnerability — the lower the organic matter, the higher the erosion risk.
