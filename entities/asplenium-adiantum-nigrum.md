## Overview

Asplenium adiantum-nigrum L., commonly known as the black spleenwort, is an evergreen perennial fern in the family Aspleniaceae. It is native to Europe, western Asia, and parts of Africa, where it grows on rocks, walls, and in rocky crevices.

The species name refers to the dark, almost black rachis that contrasts sharply with the bright green lamina. This distinctive coloration makes it one of the easiest ferns to identify in the field.

Asplenium adiantum-nigrum is polymorphic, with several recognized varieties that differ in frond division and habitat preference. The species complex has been the subject of extensive taxonomic study due to its variable morphology and widespread distribution.

The fern is valued in cultivation for rock gardens and wall plantings, where its compact size and evergreen habit provide year-round interest. It is one of the most adaptable Asplenium species in cultivation.

The species shows considerable phenotypic plasticity, with frond size and division varying dramatically depending on moisture availability and exposure. Shaded, moist plants produce large, luxuriant fronds while exposed individuals remain compact.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Division: Polypodiophyta
- Class: Polypodiopsida
- Order: Polypodiales
- Family: Aspleniaceae
- Genus: Asplenium
- Species: *A. adiantum-nigrum* L.

The species was described by Linnaeus in 1753. **Synonyms** include *Amesium adiantum-nigrum* (L.) Newman and *Tarachia adiantum-nigrum* (L.) C. Presl.

Fronds are evergreen, 10–35 cm long and 3–8 cm broad, deltoid to lanceolate in outline. The lamina is 2–3-pinnate at the base, becoming simpler toward the apex, with a characteristically dark, glossy rachis.

Pinnae are ovate to oblong, with the basal pair the largest and often slightly overlapping. Pinnules are serrate with sharp teeth, and the ultimate segments are oblong to linear with a single vein.

The stipe is 3–10 cm long, dark brown to black at the base, becoming greenish toward the lamina. Scales at the stipe base are narrow, dark brown, and slightly clathrate (lattice-like).

Sori are linear, arranged along the veins on the undersurface of the pinnules. Each sorus is covered by a membranous indusium that opens toward the midvein.

Spores are brown, bilateral, and measure 28–35 µm. Spore output per frond is modest compared to larger fern species, reflecting the small frond size and limited sorus-bearing surface area.

## Distribution and Habitat

Asplenium adiantum-nigrum occurs throughout Europe from the Mediterranean to southern Scandinavia, extending east to the Caucasus and the Himalayas. Disjunct populations occur in the mountains of East Africa and the Canary Islands.

The species is lithophytic, growing on rocks, stone walls, cliffs, and in rocky crevices. It prefers calcareous substrates but tolerates acidic rocks where moisture is available. It is common on mortared walls in rural areas.

In the British Isles, it is widespread and locally frequent, occurring from sea level to approximately 600 m. It is particularly abundant in limestone districts, on drystone walls, and on rocky outcrops in western regions.

The species is notably tolerant of urban environments, frequently colonizing old brick walls, bridge abutments, and railway cuttings in cities and towns across Europe.

Mediterranean populations often grow in more exposed, sun-baked positions than northern plants. These southern forms tend to have smaller, more leathery fronds adapted to drought stress.

In North Africa, the species occurs in the Atlas Mountains of Morocco and Algeria, where it grows in shaded cliff crevices above 1000 m. These populations show some genetic differentiation from European material.

## Ecology

The species occupies a niche as a lithophytic pioneer, colonizing bare rock surfaces and wall crevices where competition from vascular plants is minimal. Its roots penetrate narrow fissures, accessing trapped moisture and organic debris.

Asplenium adiantum-nigrum provides microhabitat for small invertebrates and mosses in its root zone. The humus accumulated at the base of established clumps supports a miniature ecosystem of decomposers.

The dark rachis may serve a thermoregulatory function, absorbing solar radiation and warming the frond tissue in cool, exposed habitats. This adaptation is shared with other rock-dwelling Asplenium species.

Spore dispersal is wind-mediated, with the linear sori positioned to release spores into air currents flowing over the rock surface. Spores can remain viable for several years in the spore bank within rock crevices.

The species hybridizes with other Asplenium species where they co-occur, including A. trichomanes and A. scolopendrium. Hybrids are typically sterile but may persist vegetatively for extended periods.

Asplenium × alternifolium (A. adiantum-nigrum × A. septentrionale) is one of the most frequently encountered hybrids in the genus. It displays intermediate morphology and is found on acidic rock faces in western Europe.

## Cultivation

Asplenium adiantum-nigrum is cultivated for rock gardens, wall crevices, and alpine troughs. It is hardy to USDA zones 6–8 and tolerates a wider range of conditions than many Asplenium species.

It prefers partial shade to full sun in cooler climates, with well-drained alkaline or neutral soil. The species is remarkably drought-tolerant, surviving in wall crevices that receive only occasional rainfall.

Planting in limestone crevices or tucking into drystone walls mimics its natural habitat. Established plants require virtually no maintenance and will self-seed into suitable crevices nearby.

Propagation is by spores or careful division. Spores germinate readily on sterile media at 15–20°C. The species also produces bulbils at the frond base in some varieties, which can be separated and rooted.

No widely available cultivars exist, though selected forms with particularly dark rachises or compact growth have been distributed among fern enthusiasts.

The species has received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit, confirming its reliability and ornamental value in British garden conditions.

## Ethnobotany

Asplenium adiantum-nigrum has been used in traditional European folk medicine as a pectoral remedy. Fronds were prepared as infusions for treating coughs and chest complaints, reflecting the broader medicinal use of spleenworts.

The genus name Asplenium derives from the Greek "a-" (without) and "splen" (spleen), referencing the historical belief that these ferns could treat spleen disorders. This use was documented by Dioscorides and persisted into the medieval period.

In the Canary Islands, the endemic variety A. adiantum-nigrum var. *corunnense* has been used in traditional medicine by the indigenous Guanche people. The fronds were applied as poultices for skin ailments.

Modern phytochemical analysis has identified flavonoids, phenolic acids, and terpenoids in A. adiantum-nigrum. Several of these compounds show antioxidant and mild antimicrobial activity in vitro.

The species is classified as Least Concern throughout its European range. However, local declines have been noted where traditional drystone walls are replaced by concrete or metal fencing, eliminating its primary habitat in agricultural landscapes.
