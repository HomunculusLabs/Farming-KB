# Mycena

## Description

Mycena is a large genus of small basidiomycete fungi in the family Mycenaceae, commonly found in forest ecosystems worldwide. These fungi are important saprotrophic decomposers of leaf litter, woody debris, and other plant residues on the forest floor. Some species are notable for bioluminescence. Mycena species play a critical role in nutrient cycling and litter binding in both temperate and tropical ecosystems.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Division**: Basidiomycota
- **Class**: Agaricomycetes
- **Order**: Agaricales
- **Family**: Mycenaceae
- **Genus**: Mycena

## Key Facts

- Mycena is one of four genera (alongside Collybia, Marasmiellus, and Marasmius) primarily responsible for binding leaf litter on forest floors, preventing downslope erosion and organic matter loss.
- In spruce forest floors, [[mycena-galopus]] competes with Marasmius androdaceous for resources. Collembola grazing preferentially targets Marasmius, restricting it to lower soil depths and allowing Mycena to dominate in the A soil horizon (Newell, 1984a,b).
- Mycena species are secondary and tertiary colonizers of decomposing leaf litter, appearing after initial colonization by sugar fungi and ascomycetes. They possess greater enzymatic competence to break down cellulose and lignin.
- Tropical Mycena species contribute to canopy-level decomposition by forming hyphae and cords that trap and decompose leaf litter above the forest floor.
- Some Mycena species produce bioluminescent fruit bodies (e.g., [[mycena-chlorophos]]), one of the most visually striking phenomena in mycology.

## Ecological Role

- Functions as a late-stage decomposer in fungal succession on leaf litter, particularly during stages when recalcitrant organic compounds dominate
- Contributes to nutrient immobilization in fungal biomass, temporarily sequestering nitrogen and phosphorus during decomposition
- Helps bind forest floor leaf litter, reducing erosion and downslope nutrient loss, particularly important in tropical wet forests
- Serves as a food source for fungivorous [[soil-arthropods]] (collembola, acari), linking decomposition to soil food webs
- Fruit bodies release nutrients in pulses during spring and fall, potentially coinciding with peak tree root growth and nutrient demand

## Decomposition and Enzyme Activity

- Produces cellulolytic and [[ligninolytic-enzymes]] as a basidiomycete decomposer
- Colonizes resources after initial ascomycete and mitosporic species have depleted simple carbohydrate sources
- Part of the successional transition from sugar fungi to basidiomycetes as described by Swift et al. (1979)

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Mycena species are not commercially cultivated for food but are important model organisms for studying fungal succession and decomposition dynamics
- Their competitive interactions with other decomposer fungi (e.g., Marasmius) demonstrate how grazing pressure can shape fungal community structure
- Understanding Mycena's role in litter binding has applications for erosion control in forestry and land management
- Bioluminescent species (M. chlorophos) are of interest for aesthetic and educational cultivation
- The genus illustrates the importance of fungal diversity in maintaining efficient decomposition and nutrient cycling

## Habitat and Distribution

- Cosmopolitan genus found in virtually all forest types worldwide
- Particularly abundant in temperate and boreal coniferous and deciduous forests
- Tropical species contribute to both forest floor and canopy decomposition
- Often among the most frequently encountered mushroom genera on forest floors during autumn

## Bioluminescence

Several Mycena species produce bioluminescent fruit bodies and mycelia:
- Mycena chlorophos: The most famous bioluminescent species, found in subtropical and tropical Asia
- Bioluminescence is thought to attract insects for [[spore-dispersal]]
- The light-producing reaction involves luciferin-luciferase chemistry
- Bioluminescence has also been observed in mycelia of some species, suggesting additional ecological functions beyond spore dispersal

## Competitive Interactions

The Mycena-Marasmius system in spruce forests illustrates key principles of fungal community dynamics:
- Direct competitive interactions between basidiomycete species for the same resources
- Third-party effects (collembola grazing) can shift competitive outcomes
- Preferential grazing on one species releases the competitor from competitive pressure
- This demonstrates how soil fauna can structure fungal community composition
- The resulting vertical stratification of species reduces direct competition
