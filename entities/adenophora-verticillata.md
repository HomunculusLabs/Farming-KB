## Overview

Adenophora verticillata is a tall herbaceous perennial in the family Campanulaceae, commonly known as the lady bell or whirl-flower adenophora. It produces graceful spikes of nodding, bell-shaped, pale blue flowers in late summer and autumn.

The species is native to East Asia, where it ranges from eastern Siberia through northeastern China, Korea, and Japan. It grows in grasslands, forest margins, and mountain meadows.

Adenophora verticillata is one of several Adenophora species used in traditional Chinese medicine under the name "nan sha shen." The root is harvested for its yin-nourishing and lung-moistening properties.

The species is distinguished from [[adenophora-triophylla]] by its taller stature, larger flowers, and whorled cauline leaves. It is the most robust of the East Asian adenophoras.

The plant is cultivated both as an ornamental and as a medicinal crop in Korea and northeastern China. It is valued for its late-season flowers when most other perennials have finished blooming.

The species is sometimes called "lady bell" in English horticultural literature, though this name is also applied to other Adenophora species. The Korean name is "jeol-gol-deung-gol-pul."

In Korea, the young shoots are occasionally gathered as a wild vegetable, known as "doraji-namul" when prepared with other bellflower relatives.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Adenophora verticillata belongs to the genus Adenophora, section Adenophora, which is characterized by a disc-like nectary at the base of the style. The genus comprises approximately 60 species distributed across Eurasia.

The species was described by Friedrich Ernst Ludwig von Fischer. A synonym is Adenophora latifolia Herder, which appears in some older Russian floras.

Plants grow 60–120 cm tall from a thick, fleshy, carrot-like taproot. The root is white internally and can reach 30 cm in length.

Stems are erect, sturdy, unbranched, and usually glabrous or sparsely hairy. The stem base may be reddish-tinged.

Leaves are whorled or subverticillate at the middle nodes, a distinctive character reflected in the species epithet "verticillata." Leaves are lanceolate to ovate-lanceolate, 5–12 cm long, with serrate margins.

Inflorescences are narrow, elongated panicles or racemes bearing 10–30 flowers. The flowers nod on slender pedicels.

Individual flowers are campanulate, 1.5–2.5 cm long, pale blue to white. The corolla is divided to about one-third into five short, triangular lobes.

The style is long-exserted, and a prominent disc gland surrounds the base. This disc distinguishes Adenophora from Campanula.

Capsules are ovoid-globular, 5–8 mm in diameter, and dehisce by three apical pores. Seeds are small, brown, and narrowly winged.

Chromosome number is 2n = 34. The species is diploid.

## Distribution and Habitat

Adenophora verticillata is distributed across eastern Siberia, northeastern China (Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning), Mongolia, Korea, and northern Japan (Hokkaido, Honshu). It is primarily a continental species.

The species grows at elevations of 100–1,500 m in grasslands, forest margins, scrub, and mountain meadows. It prefers moist, fertile soils but tolerates periods of drought.

In its native range, it is a component of species-rich meadow communities. Associated species include various grasses, asters, and other tall forbs.

The species prefers slightly acidic to neutral soils. It is hardy to USDA zones 4–8 and tolerates severe winter cold.

Populations in Korea tend to be shorter and more compact than Chinese populations. Japanese plants from Hokkaido are the most robust.

The species is not considered threatened across most of its range. However, wild populations near urban areas in Korea and China have declined due to habitat loss and overharvesting.

Conservation efforts focus on establishing protected meadow reserves and promoting cultivated sources for medicinal use.

## Medicinal Use

In traditional Chinese medicine, the root of Adenophora verticillata is classified as "nan sha shen" (南沙参), meaning "southern sand root." It is used to nourish yin, moisten the lungs, and generate fluids.

The primary indications include dry cough, sore throat, and thirst due to yin deficiency. It is commonly combined with Ophiopogon root and Scrophularia root in formulations.

Pharmacological studies have identified triterpenoid saponins, polysaccharides, and flavonoids as the main active constituents. The saponins exhibit expectorant and immunomodulatory activity.

Modern research suggests potential anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Water extracts have shown protective effects on gastric mucosa in animal studies.

The species is cultivated commercially for medicinal use in Jilin and Heilongjiang provinces of China. Roots are harvested in autumn after 2–3 years of growth.

Quality assessment is based on root size, color, and saponin content. Market demand is steady, with annual production estimated at several hundred tonnes of dried root.

Concerns about overharvesting from wild populations have prompted cultivation programs in several Chinese provinces. Cultivated roots are considered equivalent to wild-harvested material in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia.

The distinction between "nan sha shen" (Adenophora) and "bei sha shen" (Glehnia littoralis) is clinically important in TCM practice. The two have overlapping but distinct therapeutic applications.

## Cultivation

Adenophora verticillata grows best in fertile, well-drained loam soils in full sun to light shade. It requires consistent moisture during the growing season.

Propagation is by seed, which should be cold-stratified for 4–6 weeks before spring sowing. Germination rates are generally high under proper conditions.

Seedlings develop slowly in their first year and may not flower until the second or third season. Transplanting is best done in early spring.

The species is relatively pest-free. Root rot can occur in poorly drained soils, and aphids may attack young shoots.

For medicinal root production, plants are spaced 20–30 cm apart in rows 40 cm apart. Roots are harvested in the second or third autumn.

In ornamental gardens, Adenophora verticillata combines well with other late-summer perennials such as asters, Japanese anemones, and boltonia. Its pale blue flowers provide a cooling contrast to warmer autumn colors.

The species is not self-fertile and requires cross-pollination to set seed. Planting at least two genetically distinct individuals ensures good seed production.

Deadheading prevents unwanted self-seeding, which can be prolific in favorable conditions. Alternatively, seed can be collected and shared through plant societies.

The species is relatively short-lived for a perennial, typically persisting 5–8 years in cultivation. Regular renewal from seed maintains vigor.
