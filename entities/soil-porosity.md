## Soil Porosity

## Description

Soil porosity refers to the network of voids (pore spaces) between solid mineral and organic particles in soil. These pore spaces are filled with either air or water (or both) and are essential for root respiration, microbial metabolism, water movement, and nutrient transport. In good garden soil, approximately half the pore spaces are filled with water and the other half with air. Porosity is determined primarily by soil texture, organic matter content, and biological activity.

## Key Facts

- Pore spaces are the voids between individual mineral and organic particles; they are filled by air or water.
- **Three types of soil water occupy pore spaces:**
  - **Gravitational water:** Moves through large pores driven by gravity. Drains quickly through soil like water poured into gravel. Displaces air as it flows through, then draws fresh air in behind it.
  - **Capillary water:** Held in smaller pore spaces by cohesion (water molecules attracted to each other) and adhesion (water attracted to soil particle surfaces). Not influenced by gravity. Can move uphill. This is the primary water source for plants between rainfall events.
  - **Hygroscopic water:** A film only a few molecules thick, bound to extremely small soil particles by electrical forces. Bonds are so strong that plant roots cannot absorb it, but it is critical for microbial survival and movement. Cannot be removed without applying extreme heat.
- When water flows through soil, it pushes stale air out and draws fresh air in from the surface — a vital exchange that delivers oxygen to roots and soil organisms.
- Soil food web organisms use oxygen and produce carbon dioxide through metabolic activity; the presence of CO2 indicates living soil that needs fresh air exchange.
- **Poor porosity** (compacted soils lacking adequate pore space) leads to anaerobic conditions where oxygen is depleted. Anaerobic microbes produce alcohols and other compounds toxic to plant root cells.
- Biological activity directly improves porosity: earthworms, insect larvae, moles, and burrowing animals create channels for air and water. Fungal hyphae and bacterial slime bind particles into aggregates with pore space between them.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Porosity is the reason compaction is one of the most damaging conditions for both plant growth and fungal cultivation. Mycelium requires oxygen to grow aerobically; compacted, anaerobic substrates favor contaminant organisms and suppress beneficial fungi. In mushroom cultivation, substrate preparation is essentially a porosity management exercise: achieving the right balance of water-holding capacity (capillary pores) and gas exchange (larger pores). In garden soil, maintaining porosity through minimal tillage, organic matter additions, and biological activity is essential for healthy root development and robust soil food web function.
