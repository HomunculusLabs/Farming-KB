## Polypore

## Description
Polypores (also known as bracket fungi or shelf fungi) are a morphological group of wood-decomposing fungi that produce fruiting bodies with a pore surface on the underside rather than gills. They are ecologically important as primary decomposers of dead wood in forest ecosystems and include many species of medicinal and commercial interest. Polypores present unique challenges for tissue culturing compared to gilled mushrooms.

## Classification
- **Category**: Morphological group (not a single taxonomic group)
- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Primarily Basidiomycota
- **Major families**: Polyporaceae, Ganodermataceae, Fomitopsidaceae, Meripilaceae

## Key Facts

### Morphology
- Bracket or shelf-shaped fruiting bodies (conks) growing from wood
- Pore surface on the underside instead of gills — tubes release spores
- Often tough, woody, or leathery texture
- Can be perennial, adding new layers each year
- Range from small shelves to massive conks

### Culturing Challenges
- Generally do not have good viable interior tissue that readily colonizes agar
- Tissue sampling from base attachment point may be the only viable option
- Brackets and conks should be soaked in 3% hydrogen peroxide for several hours before culturing
- Lower success rate than gilled mushroom cloning

### Special Cloning Techniques
- Submerge entire mushroom cap and stem underwater with attachment point exposed
- Wedge mushroom in container to keep it in upside-down position
- Lack of oxygen forces mycelium up and out of attachment point seeking air
- Once mycelium fuzzes up at attachment point, snip and use for cloning
- Alternatively: chop colonized deadwood into bits, cover with 3% hydrogen peroxide, refrigerate overnight, transfer to agar

### Notable Genera
- Ganoderma: Reishi/lingzhi [[medicinal-mushrooms]] (G. lucidum, G. tsugae)
- Trametes: Turkey tail (T. versicolor) — medicinal
- Laetiporus: [[chicken-of-the-woods]] — edible
- Fomes: Brown rot and white rot decomposers

### Ecological Role
- Primary decomposers of dead and dying wood
- White rot polypores decompose lignin (brownish-white residue)
- Brown rot polypores decompose cellulose (brownish cubical residue)
- Critical for nutrient cycling in forest ecosystems
- Some species are parasitic on living trees

## Relevance to Cultivation
Polypores include some of the most medicinally valuable fungi in cultivation (reishi, turkey tail). Their unique tissue characteristics demand specialized culturing techniques that differ significantly from standard gilled mushroom cloning. Understanding these techniques is essential for cultivators working with medicinal mushrooms and researchers isolating wild polypore species.
