## Distribution and Habitat

Scarlet oak ranges from southern Maine west to southern Wisconsin, south to northern Alabama and Georgia, and along the Appalachian Mountains to the Ohio River Valley. It is absent from the coastal plain.

The species is most abundant on dry, acidic, sandy or gravelly upland soils. It is characteristic of ridge tops, south-facing slopes, and dry oak-heath forests in the Appalachian and Piedmont regions.

The species is intolerant of shade and poorly drained soils. It is outcompeted by more shade-tolerant species on mesic sites but dominates on xeric ridges where competition is reduced.

Scarlet oak sprouts vigorously from the root collar after disturbance, including fire, logging, or windthrow. This sprouting capacity allows it to persist in frequently disturbed landscapes and recover from damage that would kill non-sprouting species.

Growth rates on favorable sites average 30–50 cm per year in height during the first 20 years. Maximum age typically ranges from 80–120 years, though exceptional specimens may exceed 150 years.

Fire is important for maintaining scarlet oak in forest communities.

## Cultivation and Uses

Scarlet oak is primarily managed as a component of mixed oak forests rather than in pure stands. Natural regeneration depends on adequate light at the forest floor and protection from acorn predation.

Trees begin producing acorns at 20–25 years of age, with reliable crops every 3–5 years. Individual trees can produce 5,000–15,000 acorns per year during mast years.

The wood is heavy, hard, and strong with a specific gravity of 0.63. Heartwood is reddish-brown and marketed as "red oak lumber" alongside other *Lobatae* species. Uses include flooring, furniture, cabinetry, and railroad ties.

Scarlet oak is a popular ornamental tree for parks and large landscapes. Its compact root system, compared to other oaks, makes it somewhat easier to transplant. The spectacular fall color is its primary ornamental asset.

Several cultivars are available, including 'Splendens' with particularly vivid fall color and 'Fastigiata' with a narrow, columnar form. The species is hardy to USDA Zone 4 and tolerates urban conditions including compacted soils and [[north-american-dmt-containing-plants-phalaris-desmanthus-peganum]] oak bark was a major export commodity in the 18th and 19th centuries.

Quercetin and kaempferol glycosides have been isolated from scarlet oak leaves. These flavonoids exhibit antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities in vitro. The leaves also contain triterpenoids including betulinic acid.

Acorn extracts demonstrate antimicrobial activity against gram-positive bacteria and certain fungi. The astringent properties of oak bark have been used in traditional medicine for wound treatment, diarrhea, and as a gargle for sore throats.

## Ecological Role

Scarlet oak acorns are among the most palatable of the red oak group for wildlife. They are consumed by white-tailed deer, wild turkey, black bear, squirrels, blue jays, woodpeckers, and numerous small mammals.

The species provides important nesting habitat for woodland birds. Its open canopy structure allows light penetration to the forest floor, supporting diverse understory communities that benefit ground-nesting birds and [[mollison-designers-winter-forest-foraging-wildlife-cooperation]].
