# Ecosynthesis

## Description

Ecosynthesis is the evolution of new ecosystems composed of both native and exotic species responding to novel environmental conditions. The concept, developed and championed by David Holmgren, challenges the orthodox conservation biology view that ecosystems are fixed and ancient systems. Instead, ecosynthesis recognizes that the relationships between species and their physical environment are remarkably plastic, leading to rapid ecosystem evolution when conditions change.

The term describes a process by which novel combinations of indigenous, naturalised, and exotic species self-organise into functional ecosystems that may bear little resemblance to pre-disturbance communities. Holmgren argues that ecosynthesis has had, and continues to have, beneficial effects in moderating and repairing the environmental impact of human expansion, as well as providing new resources as plants and animals naturalise.

## Key Facts

- Ecosynthesis challenges the conservation biology orthodoxy that treats ecosystems as static, ancient assemblies of co-evolved species.
- Palaeobotanical evidence shows many of today's "natural" ecosystems are actually novel, contingent combinations of species that came together during the current interglacial period (last 10,000 years), not surviving intact from before the ice ages.
- Australia's current fire-prone, eucalypt-dominated vegetation is atypical of other interglacial periods when rainforest dominated, suggesting even "indigenous" systems are not climatically optimal.
- European wooden ships seeded coastal waters worldwide with European coastal lifeforms, creating radically changed marine ecosystems that scientists now record as baseline conditions.
- Mutualism (cooperative relationships between species) is a major factor in assisting the spread of exotic species, a finding from thoughtful conservation biology research.

## Case Studies

### Riparian Weedscapes of Melbourne (Yarra River)

Holmgren and colleague P. Morgan studied the Yarra River floodplain in metropolitan Melbourne in 1982. Despite diverse land use impacts, they found successional patterns emerging toward a forest dominated by indigenous river red gums (*Eucalyptus camaldulensis*) with a closed canopy understorey of tall shrubs including indigenous, Australian native, and exotic species. The ground layer was dominated by wandering jew (*Tradescantia albiflora*). These novel forests emerged from diverse starting points — grazed woodland, cultivated fields, and even freshly bulldozed areas — demonstrating systemic, not random, successional patterns.

### Riparian Weedscapes of Hepburn Springs

After moving to Hepburn Springs in 1985, Holmgren observed succession from primary weedscape of blackberry (*Rubus fruticosus*), gorse (*Ulex europaeus*), and cape broom (*Genista monspessulana*) to deciduous forest of willow (*Salix alba x fragilis*), hawthorn (*Crataegus monogyna*), and native blackwood (*Acacia melanoxylon*). University research confirmed willows captured 40 times more sediment and 10 times more phosphorus than eucalypts, providing near-optimal water filtering in the steep catchment.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Ecosynthesis validates the Permaculture approach of designing agricultural systems modelled on natural processes. Rather than using vague analogy, permaculture designers make direct application of self-organisational processes continuously at work in nature. Understanding ecosynthesis helps permaculturalists:

1. **Recognise functional value in "weedy" successional communities** that may outperform managed native revegetation.
2. **Work with emerging ecosystem dynamics** rather than imposing rigid, high-energy control.
3. **Predict successional trajectories** using pattern recognition skills to guide low-input management.
4. **Appreciate the role of exotic species** in building functional ecosystems, especially in urban and degraded landscapes.

The concept directly supports the permaculture principle that "the problem is the solution" — what appears as invasive chaos may be nature's process of building new functional ecosystems better adapted to current conditions.
