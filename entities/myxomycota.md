## Description

Myxomycota (plasmodial [[slime-molds]], also known as Myxogastria or Myxomycetes) are a group of amoeba-like organisms that inhabit damp, rotting wood, leaves, manure, lawn thatch, rotting mushrooms, and other organic material. In *[[teaming-with-microbes-actinomycetes]]*, Lowenfels and Lewis describe them as unusual-looking organisms that spend most of their lives pursuing bacteria and yeast in the soil, differentiated from true fungi primarily by their feeding mechanism — while fungi digest food externally, slime molds engulf food and digest it internally.

The most distinctive feature of plasmodial slime molds is their plasmodium stage — a multinucleated mass of cytoplasm that can grow to visible sizes, slowly creeping over surfaces at about 1 millimeter per hour while engulfing bacteria, fungal spores, and organic debris. The common genus *Physarum* forms masses about 1 inch thick and can grow to 1 foot or more wide.

## Taxonomy

- **Domain:** Eukarya
- **Supergroup:** Amoebozoa
- **Phylum:** Amoebozoa (various classifications)
- **Class:** Myxogastria (syn. Myxomycetes)
- **Orders:** Physarales, Stemonitales, Trichiales, Liceales, Echinosteliales
- **Common Genera:** *Physarum*, *Stemonitis*, *Trichia*, *Arcyria*, *Fuligo*
- Approximately 500-1000 species described worldwide

## Key Characteristics

- **Plasmodium stage** — multinucleated, coenocytic mass of cytoplasm without cell walls
- **Shuttle streaming** — rhythmic back-and-forth flow of cytoplasm visible under magnification
- **Movement speed** — approximately 1 millimeter per hour
- **Coloration** — plasmodia in shades of tan, yellow, pink, or red; often quite attractive
- **Regeneration** — if cut in half or quarters, the pieces will rejoin
- **Sporangia** — fruiting structures in yellow, blue, red, brown, and white forming beautiful color nets
- **Size range** — *Physarum* species typically ~1 inch thick, up to 1+ foot wide
- **Chemotaxis** — can detect and move toward organic food sources

## Life Cycle

The myxomycete life cycle involves several distinct stages:

1. **Spores** — thick-walled [[fungal-sclerotia-and-resting-structures]] dispersed by wind, water, or animals
2. **Germination** — spores release haploid myxamoebae or flagellated swarm cells
3. **Feeding stage** — myxamoebae crawl through soil ingesting bacteria, fungal spores, and small protozoa; they lock up nutrients and prevent leaching
4. **Plasmogamy** — compatible myxamoebae or swarm cells fuse to form a zygote
5. **Plasmodium growth** — the zygote grows through repeated nuclear division without cell division, forming the multinucleated plasmodium
6. **Migration** — plasmodium emerges from soil and moves over surfaces, engulfing food
7. **Sporulation** — plasmodium finds an appropriate location and forms fruiting structures (sporangia)
8. **Spore formation** — meiosis occurs within sporangia, producing haploid spores

The entire life cycle is driven by moisture availability. When conditions become unfavorable (drying, temperature extremes), plasmodia either form resistant sclerotia or proceed directly to sporulation.

## Movement and Feeding

The plasmodial stage is the most remarkable aspect of myxomycete biology:

- **Cytoplasmic streaming** — the cytoplasm flows rhythmically in visible waves, distributing nutrients and organelles throughout the mass
- **Chemotaxis** — plasmodia can detect chemical gradients from food sources and navigate toward them
- **Problem-solving** — *[[physarum-polycephalum]]* has been shown to solve mazes, find efficient networks between food sources, and even approximate solutions to complex mathematical problems
- **Regeneration** — plasmodia can be cut into pieces that will either rejoin or form separate individuals
- **Engulfment feeding** — unlike fungi, which secrete enzymes externally, plasmodia physically surround and internalize their food

## Role in Soil Food Web

Myxomycota play several roles in [[the-soil-food-web]] described in *Teaming with Microbes*:

- **Bacterial population control** — myxamoebae consume large quantities of bacteria
- **[[dighton-fungal-nutrient-immobilization-turnover]]** — ingested nutrients are held within cells, preventing leaching
- **Nutrient cycling** — nutrients are released when slime molds die and decompose
- **Soil particle binding** — slime secretions help bind soil particles, improving soil structure
- **Food source** — myxamoebae are prey for insect larvae, worms, and specialized beetles with mandibles adapted for scooping soft organisms

## Ecological Significance

Plasmodial slime molds contribute to ecosystem function in several ways:

- **Decomposition** — they process organic matter, particularly in moist microhabitats
- **Microbial regulation** — by consuming bacteria and fungi, they help maintain [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]] balance
- **Nutrient retention** — their ability to immobilize nutrients prevents losses from the soil system
- **[[coprophilous-fungi-biodiversity-indicators]]** — the diversity of slime mold species in an area reflects habitat quality and moisture availability
- **Tropical forest ecology** — particularly diverse and abundant in tropical forests with high humidity and abundant dead wood

## Relevance to Cultivation

While slime molds are not directly managed in cultivation, understanding their role has practical implications:

- **Soil health** — their presence indicates adequate moisture and organic matter, both important for cultivation
- **Compost systems** — slime molds participate in composting, helping process organic material
- **Minimal impact** — despite their sometimes alarming appearance (looking like "dog vomit"), slime molds rarely damage garden plants
- **Biological research** — *Physarum polycephalum* is used in research on biological computation and [[biological-network-optimization]]

## Interesting Facts

- *[[fuligo-septica]]* is commonly called "dog vomit slime mold" due to its appearance
- *Physarum polycephalum* solved a maze in a famous 2000 experiment by Nakagaki et al.
- The same *Physarum* species recreated the Tokyo rail network layout when oat flakes were placed to represent cities
- Slime mold swarm masses are sometimes mistaken for fungal growths or even animal excrement
- Their sporangia come in beautiful colors and intricate shapes, described by Lowenfels as "as pretty as anything you can grow in your gardens"
- When conditions become unfavorable, plasmodia dry up and turn to powdery dust, leaving only spores to regenerate
