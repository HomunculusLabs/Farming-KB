# Arabidopsis thaliana

**Arabidopsis thaliana** is a small flowering plant in the mustard family Brassicaceae.
It is commonly called thale cress, mouse-ear cress, or simply Arabidopsis.
Although it is an unremarkable roadside weed in the field, it is one of the most important model organisms in plant biology.
Its short [[growing-gourmet-black-morel-sclerotia-life-cycle]], compact genome, self-fertilization, and easy transformation made it the reference plant for genetics, development, and molecular physiology.

## Taxonomic position

**Domain:** Eukaryota.
**Kingdom:** Plantae.
**Clade:** Angiosperms.
**Order:** Brassicales.
**Family:** Brassicaceae.
**Genus:** *Arabidopsis*.
**Species:** *Arabidopsis thaliana*.
The species was first described from central Europe and was named in honor of the German botanist Johannes Thal.
Carl Linnaeus placed it in *Arabis* in 1753, and Gustav Heynhold later established the modern combination *Arabidopsis thaliana*.
It is related to crop and laboratory-relevant brassicas, but it is not itself a major food crop.

## Description

*A. thaliana* is usually a winter annual, though some accessions behave as spring annuals or rare biennials.
Mature plants are small, often about 20 to 25 centimeters tall under ordinary conditions.
Leaves form a basal rosette, and the flowering stem carries smaller cauline leaves.
Flowers are small, white, and typical of Brassicaceae, with four petals and six stamens.
The fruit is a slender silique containing many small seeds.
The plant normally self-pollinates, which makes inbred laboratory lines easy to maintain.
Its full generation time can be about six weeks under favorable laboratory growth conditions.

## Distribution and habitat

The native range includes much of Europe, western and central Asia, and parts of North Africa.
The species has spread widely as a naturalized weed in temperate regions beyond its native range.
It often grows on roadsides, railway margins, walls, disturbed soil, rocky ground, sandy substrates, and agricultural edges.
Natural populations include both winter-annual and rapid-cycling strategies depending on climate and local adaptation.
Thousands of accessions have been collected from different regions and are used to study natural variation.
This geographic variation is valuable because many laboratory traits depend on accession background.

## Why it became a model organism

Arabidopsis became dominant because it combines a multicellular plant body with experimental convenience.
The plant is small enough to grow by the hundreds or thousands in growth rooms.
It produces many seeds, allowing genetic screens with large sample sizes.
It self-fertilizes, enabling stable mutant lines and recombinant inbred populations.
Its genome is small compared with many crop plants and contains five chromosomes.
The nuclear genome was the first plant genome to be fully sequenced, providing a reference for plant genomics.
Transformation with *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* can be done by floral dip without tissue culture in many protocols.
Large T-DNA insertion collections, mutant libraries, mapping populations, and reporter lines make gene function work unusually tractable.

## Laboratory accessions

The Columbia accession, usually written Col-0, is the most widely used reference background.
Landsberg erecta, abbreviated Ler, is another historically important laboratory background.
Wassilewskija, C24, Cape Verde Islands, and many wild accessions appear in natural variation studies.
Different accessions vary in flowering time, dormancy, trichomes, pathogen response, root architecture, temperature tolerance, and hormone sensitivity.
Because background effects can be large, Arabidopsis papers usually specify the accession used.

## Genome and genetics

The nuclear genome is roughly 135 to 157 megabase pairs depending on measurement and annotation conventions.
It contains five chromosome pairs and tens of thousands of protein-coding and non-coding genes.
Arabidopsis genetics supports forward screens, reverse genetics, transgenics, genome editing, reporter imaging, and quantitative trait mapping.
The Arabidopsis Information Resource, or TAIR, is the central community database for genes, annotations, literature, and stock information.
Its standardized gene identifiers and mutant resources make it a backbone organism for comparative plant biology.
Many crop gene functions are initially inferred by comparison with Arabidopsis homologs.

## Developmental biology

Arabidopsis is a reference organism for flower development, root patterning, leaf initiation, meristem maintenance, and embryogenesis.
The ABC model of floral organ identity was clarified through Arabidopsis and snapdragon genetics.
Genes such as *AGAMOUS*, *APETALA*, and *PISTILLATA* became classic examples of developmental regulators.
Root development studies use the transparent, simple root tip to track cell files, stem cells, and hormone gradients.
Trichome studies use Arabidopsis leaf hairs as a model for epidermal cell fate and patterning.
The wiki's [[trichome-development-and-morphology]] page references Arabidopsis because its GLABROUS and MYB-bHLH-WD40 pathways are foundational comparisons.

## Light and hormone biology

Arabidopsis has been central to research on phytochromes, cryptochromes, phototropins, and shade avoidance.
In the wiki's [[phytochrome-photoreception]] page, Arabidopsis provides the PHYA through PHYE gene family example.
PHYB is especially important for red and far-red light responses and shade detection.
PHYA is important for very-low-fluence and far-red responses.
The species is also a standard system for auxin transport, cytokinin signaling, abscisic acid responses, jasmonate, salicylic acid, and ethylene biology.
Fungal and bacterial plant-growth assays often use Arabidopsis seedlings because root architecture responds visibly to hormones and symbiont signals.
The wiki's [[auxin-production-by-symbiotic-fungi]] page uses Arabidopsis root branching as a bioassay for fungal indole-3-acetic-acid effects.

## Plant-microbe interactions

Arabidopsis is not a classic mycorrhizal host, which makes it unusual compared with many land plants.
Nevertheless, it is widely used to study innate immunity, bacterial pathogenesis, endophytes, root exudates, and microbiome assembly.
The species interacts experimentally with *Pseudomonas syringae*, *Botrytis cinerea*, [[foliar-epiphytic-fungi-sooty-mildews-powdery-mildews]], oomycetes, and many beneficial microbes.
Its immune signaling pathways include salicylic acid, jasmonate, ethylene, pattern-triggered immunity, and effector-triggered immunity.
Root microbiome studies use Arabidopsis because sterile seedlings can be inoculated with defined microbial communities.
Its limitations must be remembered when generalizing to crops, trees, or mycorrhizal plants.
## Practical cultivation
Arabidopsis can be grown [[singh-fungal-interactions-with-heavy-metals-in-soil]], agar plates, [[comparison-aeroponics-guide-vs-cannabis-hydroponic-systems]], or controlled-environment chambers.
Seeds are tiny and often stratified at low temperature to synchronize germination.
Plants are commonly grown under long-day conditions when rapid flowering is desired.
Short-day or vernalization treatments are used when studying flowering regulation and natural life-history variation.
Good experiments randomize positions, track accession background, and separate developmental age from chronological age.
## Relevance to this wiki
Arabidopsis appears across concept pages whenever plant molecular biology needs a reference organism.
It anchors discussions of phytochrome genes, trichome development, auxin assays, plant stress signaling, and root architecture.
It is useful for explaining mechanisms but should not be treated as a direct substitute for every crop or perennial plant.
[[query-how-does-foliar-feeding-work-for-cannabis]] trichome pages, Arabidopsis supplies a regulatory comparison rather than a resin-gland equivalent.
For soil symbiosis pages, Arabidopsis seedling assays are experimentally convenient but ecologically narrower than mycorrhizal host systems.
## Key takeaways
*Arabidopsis thaliana* is the reference plant for modern genetics and molecular plant biology.
Its advantages are small size, fast generation time, selfing, abundant seed, easy transformation, and a compact sequenced genome.
Its most common laboratory background is Col-0, but accession choice can change experimental outcomes.
It is central for studying light perception, flowering time, root development, immunity, hormones, and epidermal patterning.
It is a powerful model organism, not a universal ecological proxy for all plants.
Use it in wiki pages as a mechanistic reference point while checking whether the concept also requires crop, tree, fungal-symbiosis, or field-ecology evidence.
