# Abies magnifica

## Overview

**Abies magnifica** A. Murray bis, commonly known as the red fir or silvertip fir, is a large evergreen conifer in the family Pinaceae. It is native to the high mountains of California and western Nevada, forming extensive forests on the western slopes of the Sierra Nevada and in the southern Cascade Range.

Red fir is a dominant component of the upper montane and subalpine forest belt in the Sierra Nevada. It is distinguished by its massive size, purplish cones, and the silvery appearance of new growth, which gives rise to the alternative common name "silvertip fir."

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Pinophyta
- **Class:** Pinopsida
- **Order:** Pinales
- **Family:** Pinaceae
- **Genus:** Abies
- **Species:** A. magnifica A. Murray bis

**Authority:** Described by Andrew Dickson Murray (1863). The epithet *magnifica* means "magnificent" in Latin, referring to the tree's imposing stature and appearance in its mountain habitat.

Two varieties are generally recognized. **Var. magnifica** (typical red fir) occurs throughout the main range of the species. **Var. shastensis** Lemmon (Shasta red fir) occupies the northern portion of the range in the southern Cascades and Klamath Mountains and is sometimes treated as a separate species, *A. shastensis*.

**Synonyms:** Abies amabilis var. magnifica (A. Murray bis) Franco, Abies shastensis Lemmon.

## Morphology

Red fir is a very large tree, reaching 40–60 m in height with a straight trunk 90–180 cm in diameter. The largest specimens approach 75 m in height with trunks exceeding 250 cm DBH, making it one of the largest true firs.

The crown is narrow and conical in young trees, becoming broadly columnar in maturity. Branches are arranged in regular whorls, descending to within a few meters of the ground on open-grown trees. Lower branches on forest-grown individuals are often self-pruned.

Bark on young trees is smooth, gray, and marked with resin blisters. Mature bark is thick (5–10 cm), deeply furrowed, and reddish-brown to cinnamon-colored, with broad, scaly ridges. The reddish inner bark distinguishes it from the white inner bark of [[pinus-contorta]] and [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], with basal areas exceeding 100 m²/ha. These forests provide critical winter habitat for Sierra Nevada wildlife including mule deer, black bears, fishers, and the endangered Sierra Nevada red fox.

Red fir seeds are consumed by numerous birds and mammals, including chickarees (*Tamiasciurus douglasii*), Steller's jays (*Cyanocitta stelleri*), and crossbills (*Loxia* spp.). The dense canopy provides thermal cover and snow interception that moderates subnivean conditions.

Ectomycorrhizal associations are extensive, with partners including *Cenococcum geophilum*, *Laccaria laccata*, *Rhizopogon* spp., and numerous *Cortinarius* species. These associations are critical for [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] in the cold, nutrient-poor soils of upper montane forests.

Fire return intervals in red fir forests historically ranged from 30 to over 200 years, depending on elevation and fuel loads. Mixed-severity fire regimes maintained a mosaic of age classes. Modern fire suppression has altered these patterns, increasing stand density and susceptibility to bark beetles.

The western pine beetle (*Dendroctonus brevicomis*) and fir engraver beetle (*Scolytus ventralis*) attack weakened and stressed red fir trees. Drought-induced mortality events have increased in frequency and severity since the early 2000s, particularly during multi-year dry periods.

The California spotted owl (*Strix occidentalis occidentalis*) relies on red fir forests for nesting and foraging habitat. These forests also support populations of the Pacific fisher (*Pekania pennanti*) and northern goshawk (*Accipiter gentilis*).
