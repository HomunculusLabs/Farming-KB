## Overview

Cucumis sativus, the cucumber, is a widely cultivated creeping vine in the
family Cucurbitaceae. It produces cylindrical fruits used primarily as a
fresh vegetable, pickle, or garnish in cuisines worldwide.

The species likely originated in the foothills of the Himalayas in India,
where wild relatives still grow. Archaeological evidence suggests cultivation
dates back over 3,000 years.

Cucumbers are grown on every inhabited continent and represent one of the
most important vegetable crops globally by production volume. China alone
accounts for roughly 75% of world production.

The fruit is botanically a pepo, a type of berry with a hard outer rind.
Three main market classes exist: fresh-slicing, pickling, and specialty
types including English, Persian, and Armenian cucumbers.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Cucumis sativus L. is the type species of the genus Cucumis, family
Cucurbitaceae, order Cucurbitales. The genus includes approximately 50
species, with melon (C. melo) as the most important relative.

The plant is a tender annual vine with trailing or climbing stems 1–3 meters
long. Stems are angular, grooved, and covered with bristly hairs.

Leaves are simple, alternate, and broadly cordate with 3–5 pointed lobes.
Leaf size ranges from 10 to 25 cm across with a rough, hairy surface.

Tendrils are branched and coiling, arising from leaf axils opposite the
leaves. They provide the climbing and scrambling mechanism.

Root systems are relatively shallow for a cucurbit, with most feeder roots
in the top 30 cm of soil. This makes the crop sensitive to drought stress
but responsive to frequent irrigation.

Flowers are monoecious (separate male and female on same plant) in most
cultivars. Some modern gynoecious cultivars produce predominantly female
flowers to increase yield.

Male flowers appear in clusters on slender peduncles, while female flowers
are solitary with an inferior ovary visible as a small fruit at the base.

Fruits are cylindrical pepos typically 15–30 cm long and 3–8 cm in diameter
for slicing types. Pickling types are smaller, 5–15 cm, and more blocky.

The skin color ranges from dark green to light green, with some cultivars
showing white or yellow striping. The rind may be smooth or prickly with
trichomes.

The flesh is pale green to white, crisp, and watery, with small seeds in a
central cavity. Seedlessness (parthenocarpy) is a common trait in greenhouse
and greenhouse-forced cultivars.

## Cultivation

Cucumbers require warm conditions with optimal growth at 25–30°C. They are
sensitive to frost and perform poorly below 15°C. A frost-free period of
50–70 days is required for most cultivars.

The crop prefers well-drained, fertile sandy loam soils with pH 5.5–7.0 and
high organic matter. Raised beds with plastic mulch and drip irrigation are
standard in commercial production.

Spacing varies by system: 0.3–0.6 meters between plants in rows 1–2 meters
apart for bush types, or wider for vining types trained on trellises.

Trellis culture improves fruit quality, reduces disease pressure, and
increases planting density. Trellised cucumbers produce straighter fruits
than ground culture.

Key pests include cucumber beetles (Acalymma vittatum and Diabrotica
undecimpunctata), aphids (Aphis gossypii), spider mites (Tetranychus
urticae), and thrips.

Yields range from 10 to 50 tonnes per hectare depending on production system,
cultivar, and growing season. Greenhouse production can achieve significantly
higher yields per unit area than field production.

## Nutritional Value

Cucumber flesh is approximately 95% water, making it one of the lowest-calorie
vegetables. A 100 g serving provides roughly 15 kcal with minimal protein,
fat, or carbohydrate.

Despite low macronutrient density, cucumbers contain useful amounts of vitamin
K (16 µg per 100 g) and potassium (147 mg per 100 g). Vitamin C content is
moderate at approximately 2.8 mg per 100 g.

The skin contains small amounts of β-carotene and lutein. Cucurbitacins,
the bitter triterpenoid compounds that give wild cucumbers their bitterness,
are present at very low levels in cultivated types.

Cucurbitacins have documented anti-inflammatory and cytotoxic properties in
laboratory studies. Commercial cultivars are bred to minimize cucurbitacin
content for palatability.

## Culinary Use

Fresh cucumbers are consumed raw in salads, sandwiches, and as snacks. They
are a key ingredient in Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, and Southeast Asian
cuisines.

Pickled cucumbers are one of the most widely consumed preserved vegetables
globally. Dill pickles, bread-and-butter pickles, and sweet gherkins represent
major commercial categories.

In Japanese cuisine, cucumbers appear in sunomono (vinegared salad), sushi
rolls, and as a crisp garnish. Chinese cuisine uses them in stir-fries, cold
dressed salads, and pickled preparations.

Tzatziki, a yogurt-based sauce made with grated cucumber, garlic, and dill,
is a staple of Greek and Turkish cuisines. Similar preparations exist across
the Balkans and Middle East.

Indian cuisine uses cucumber in raita (yogurt relish), kachumber salad, and
various chutneys. The fruit is also juiced for cooling beverages in summer.
