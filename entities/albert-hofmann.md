# Albert Hofmann

Albert Hofmann (1906-2008) was a Swiss chemist best known for discovering LSD-25 (lysergic acid diethylamide) in 1943 and isolating psilocybin from Psilocybe mushrooms in 1958. He worked at Sandoz Pharmaceuticals in Basel for his entire career, producing over 100 scientific publications. His discovery of both LSD and psilocybin makes him arguably the most influential figure in modern psychedelic chemistry.

## Discovery of LSD (1943)

Hofmann's discovery was not entirely accidental, as he later emphasized: "LSD came into being within a systematic research program, and the 'accident' did not occur until much later." While researching ergot alkaloids for Sandoz — seeking compounds that might stimulate circulation and respiration — he first synthesized LSD-25 in 1938. Five years later, on April 16, 1943, he accidentally absorbed a small amount through his fingertips and experienced the first LSD trip. Three days later, on April 19 — now known as "Bicycle Day" — he took the first intentional dose of 250 micrograms, cycling home from the laboratory through a rapidly transforming Basel.

LSD was initially marketed as Delysid for psychiatric research, and Sandoz distributed it to researchers worldwide. Hofmann called LSD his "problem child" but remained a lifelong advocate for its therapeutic and spiritual potential, arguing that "if people would learn to use LSD's vision-inducing capability more wisely, under suitable conditions, in medical practice and in conjunction with meditation, then in the future this problem child could become a wonder child."

## Isolation of Psilocybin (1958)

After [[r-gordon-wasson]] brought specimens of Psilocybe mexicana from his 1957 expedition to Mexico (documenting the Mazatec mushroom ceremony of Maria Sabina), Hofmann identified and synthesized the active compounds psilocybin and psilocin at Sandoz. This chemical identification of the mushroom's active principles transformed ethnomycology from a purely observational field into one with a biochemical foundation.

## Eleusinian Mysteries Research

Co-authored "The Road to Eleusis" (1978) with [[r-gordon-wasson]] and Carl Ruck, proposing that Claviceps purpurea (ergot) was the kykeon — the sacred potion consumed during the ancient Greek Eleusinian Mysteries for nearly 2,000 years. This hypothesis, while debated, exemplified Hofmann's broader interest in the role of psychoactive substances in religious and cultural history.

## "LSD: My Problem Child" (1980)

Hofmann's autobiographical account details the history of his discovery, its consequences, and his philosophical views on LSD's potential. The book reveals a scientist with a deeply mystical worldview: "Because I wanted to gain insight into the structure and essence of matter, I became a research chemist. Intrigued by the plant world since early childhood, I chose to specialize in research on the constituents of medicinal plants." His childhood experiences of spontaneous visionary states on forest walks above Baden, Switzerland, shaped his conviction that hallucinogens could provide access to "a miraculous, powerful, unfathomable reality that was hidden from everyday sight."

## Philosophy

Hofmann believed that "the spiritual crisis pervading all spheres of Western industrial society can be remedied only by a change in our world view" — shifting from materialistic dualism toward "a new consciousness of an all-encompassing reality." He saw LSD and related compounds as potential tools for this transformation, though he warned consistently against careless use. He was photographed with William Burroughs (1978) and John Lilly (1981), reflecting his engagement with the broader countercultural interest in consciousness exploration.

## Biography

- Born January 11, 1906, in Baden, Switzerland
- PhD in chemistry from University of Zurich (1929)
- Joined Sandoz in 1929; got his own lab in 1935
- Remained at Sandoz until retirement
- Four children: Dieter, Gaby, Beatrice, and Andreas
- Lived to age 102, dying in 2008

## Legacy

Hofmann's discoveries fundamentally shaped the 20th century's understanding of consciousness, brain chemistry, and the relationship between humans and psychoactive plants. His work enabled the scientific study of serotonin receptors, advanced understanding of mental illness, and launched both the medical and countercultural exploration of psychedelics.
