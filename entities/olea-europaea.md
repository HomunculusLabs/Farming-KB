## Overview

*Olea europaea* L., the olive, is an evergreen tree in the family Oleaceae native to the Mediterranean basin, parts of Asia, and Africa.

It is one of the oldest cultivated fruit trees, with archaeological evidence of olive oil production dating back approximately 6,000 years.

The species is renowned for its fruit, which yields olive oil — a cornerstone of Mediterranean cuisine and one of the most widely consumed culinary oils globally.

Olive trees are exceptionally long-lived, with individual specimens documented exceeding 2,000 years of age.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Lamiales → Oleaceae → Oleeae → *Olea* L.

* Olea europaea* L. (1753). Subspecies include *O. e. subsp. europaea* (cultivated olive) and *O. e. subsp. cuspidata* (wild olive, distributed across Africa and Asia).

The cultivated olive (*subsp. europaea*) is further divided into *var. europaea* (cultivated) and *var. sylvestris* (oleaster, the wild form).

**Morphology:** Trees grow 3–12 m tall with a gnarled trunk that can reach 1–2 m in diameter in ancient specimens.

Leaves are opposite, lanceolate, 4–10 cm long and 1–3 cm wide, with a dark green adaxial surface and silvery-scaled abaxial surface.

Flowers are small, white, and arranged in axillary panicles, with four petals fused into a short tube.

The fruit is a drupe (olive) 1–2.5 cm long, green when unripe and black-purple when fully mature, containing a single hard endocarp (pit).

Root systems are extensive, with lateral roots spreading 1.5–2 times the canopy radius and a deep taproot in well-drained soils.

## History and Domestication

The olive was first domesticated in the Levant approximately 5,000–6,000 years ago, with early cultivation centered in modern-day Syria, Palestine, and Crete.

Olive oil production spread throughout the Mediterranean during the Bronze Age, becoming a major commodity in Minoan, Greek, and Roman economies.

The olive branch was adopted as a symbol of peace and victory in ancient Greek culture, a symbolism that persists to the present day.

Spanish missionaries introduced olives to the Americas in the 16th century, and the tree is now cultivated in California, South America, Australia, and South Africa.

Global olive oil production exceeds 3 million tonnes annually, with Spain, Italy, Greece, Tunisia, and Turkey accounting for approximately 80% of world output.

The International Olive Council (IOC) regulates olive oil grading standards, defining categories including extra virgin, virgin, refined, and pomace oil.

High-density planting systems (super-high-density, 1,500–2,500 trees/ha) using hedgerow configurations have transformed modern olive cultivation in Spain and Australia.

## Cultivation

Olives thrive in Mediterranean climates with mild, wet winters and hot, dry summers, at temperatures ranging from 5–35°C.

Trees tolerate drought exceptionally well once established, surviving on 300–600 mm annual rainfall, though irrigation increases yields significantly.

Optimal soil pH ranges from 5.5 to 8.5, with calcareous, well-drained soils being preferred.

Major cultivars include 'Picual', 'Arbequina', 'Koroneiki', 'Frantoio', 'Leccino', and 'Manzanilla', each suited to specific regions and end uses.

Trees typically begin bearing fruit 3–5 years after planting, with full production reached at 20–30 years.

Yields range from 2–12 tonnes of fruit per hectare depending on cultivar, tree density, irrigation, and management intensity.

Key pests include the olive fruit fly (*Bactrocera oleae*), olive moth (*Prays oleae*), and black scale (*Saissetia oleae*).

Verticillium wilt (*Verticillium dahliae*) is the most serious soil-borne disease, capable of killing mature trees.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

Olive oil is composed primarily of triacylglycerols (97–99%), with oleic acid (C18:1, omega-9) constituting 55–83% of total fatty acids.

Minor bioactive constituents include squalene (0.2–0.7%), tocopherols (100–300 mg/kg), phytosterols (1000–2500 mg/kg), and phenolic compounds (50–1000 mg/kg).

Oleuropein, the predominant phenolic glycoside in olives, is hydrolyzed during oil processing to hydroxytyrosol and tyrosol.

Hydroxytyrosol exhibits potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cardioprotective activities in clinical and preclinical studies.

Extra virgin olive oil (EVOO) retains the highest phenolic content and is associated with reduced cardiovascular disease risk in Mediterranean diet epidemiological studies.

Oleocanthal, a phenolic compound in EVOO, has been shown to inhibit cyclooxygenase-1 and -2 with an ibuprofen-like mechanism.

## Ecology and Applications

Olive groves form characteristic Mediterranean agroecosystems that support significant biodiversity, including birds, insects, and understory flora.

The trees are used in agroforestry systems, intercropped with cereals, legumes, or grazing livestock in traditional Mediterranean farming.

Olive wood is dense, fine-grained, and highly valued for woodworking, carving, and decorative items.

Olive leaves contain oleuropein at concentrations of 60–90 mg/g dry weight and are used in traditional medicine and dietary supplements.

Waste from olive oil processing (olive mill wastewater and pomace) poses environmental challenges but can be composted or used for bioenergy production.

The species is increasingly planted in arid-region restoration projects due to its drought tolerance, soil stabilization capacity, and economic value.
