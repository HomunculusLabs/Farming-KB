## Description

Ascomycotina (equivalent to the modern phylum Ascomycota in current taxonomy) is the largest phylum of fungi, commonly known as "sac fungi." Along with Basidiomycotina, they constitute the "higher fungi" (Dikarya) and are characterized by the production of sexual spores (ascospores) within a sac-like structure called an ascus. Dighton references Ascomycotina as one of the two groups of higher fungi that produce large, visible fruiting bodies.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Ascomycota (formerly subdivision Ascomycotina)
- **Subphyla**: Pezizomycotina (filamentous ascomycetes), Saccharomycotina (true yeasts), Taphrinomycotina
- **Key feature**: Production of asci — sac-like cells within which sexual spores (ascospores) are formed (typically 8 per ascus)
- **Spore production**: Ascospores formed endogenously (inside the ascus)

## Key Facts

- Largest fungal phylum with approximately 64,000 described species, though estimates suggest many more remain undiscovered
- Includes an enormous diversity of forms: cup fungi, truffles, morels, flask fungi, mildews, many yeasts, and the vast majority of lichen-forming fungi
- The fungal partner (mycobiont) in approximately 98% of all lichen species is an ascomycete — making Ascomycotina the dominant phylum in lichen symbioses
- Includes many critical plant pathogens: powdery mildews (Erysiphales), Dutch elm disease (*Ophiostoma*), chestnut blight (*Cryphonectria parasitica*), ergot (*Claviceps purpurea*), and apple scab (*Venturia inaequalis*)
- The edible morel (*Morchella* spp.) and truffle (*Tuber* spp.) are among the most prized ascomycete fruiting bodies, with truffles commanding some of the highest prices of any food commodity
- Many ascomycetes are important industrial organisms: *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (baker's/brewer's yeast), *Aspergillus* species (fermentation, enzyme production), *Penicillium* species (cheese, antibiotics)
- Ascomycete fruiting bodies (ascocarps) range from microscopic perithecia and cleistothecia to macroscopic apothecia (cup fungi, morels) and hypogeous structures (truffles)
- Dighton notes that both Ascomycotina and Basidiomycotina produce large visible fruiting bodies that serve as sinks for translocated carbon and nutrients, making them important food sources in ecosystems

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Several ascomycetes are commercially cultivated: morels (*Morchella* — increasingly cultivated indoors), truffles (*Tuber* — through orchard establishment with inoculated host trees), and various edible cup fungi
- The fermentation industry relies heavily on ascomycete yeasts (*Saccharomyces*) and filamentous fungi (*Aspergillus*, *Monascus*) for food and beverage production
- Many ascomycetes are significant contaminants in mushroom cultivation: *Trichoderma* (green mold), *Aspergillus* (yellow/brown molds), *Penicillium* (blue-green molds) — understanding their biology is essential for contamination management
- Lichen-forming ascomycetes (which produce lichenic acids for rock weathering) illustrate the extreme chemical capabilities of this phylum, relevant to understanding the enzymatic and metabolic diversity available for biotechnological applications
- The production of ascospores within protective asci (often enclosed within fruiting structures) represents a different dispersal strategy from basidiomycete spores, influencing how these fungi colonize substrates and respond to environmental conditions
- Ascomycete yeasts are relevant to mushroom substrate preparation, particularly in the fermentation of compost and the development of microbial communities in supplemented substrates
- Ergot (*Claviceps purpurea*) is an ascomycete of significant pharmaceutical importance, producing alkaloids used in medicine (ergotamine, ergometrine)

## Ecological Significance

Ascomycotina is arguably the most ecologically diverse fungal phylum, occupying roles as decomposers, mutualists (lichens, endophytes), pathogens (of plants, animals, and insects), and food sources. The lichen-forming ascomycetes in particular are responsible for primary succession on bare rock surfaces, initiating soil formation processes that ultimately support entire terrestrial ecosystems.
