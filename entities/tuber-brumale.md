Its aroma is distinctively pungent, often described as musky or reminiscent of fermented turnips and cured meats, setting it apart from the more delicate Tuber melanosporum.

The species is commercially significant in France, Italy, and Spain, though it commands a lower market price than Périgord black truffle (T. melanosporum) due to its stronger, less refined aroma.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Pezizomycetes
- **Order:** Pezizales
- **Family:** Tuberaceae
- **Genus:** Tuber
- **Species:** T. brumale Vittad. (1831)

Two named varieties are recognized: T. brumale var. brumale and T. brumale var. moschatum De Ferry, the latter noted for a more pronounced muscat-like fragrance.

The species was first described by Carlo Vittadini in 1831 based on Italian collections from Lombardy.

## Culinary Use

T. brumale is used similarly to other black truffles — shaved over pasta, risotto, eggs, and cream-based dishes. Its stronger flavor means it is used more sparingly than T. melanosporum.

In Italian cuisine, it is particularly valued in Umbria and Piedmont for winter dishes. In France, it is considered a secondary truffle species but is widely sold in local markets.

Its lower market value (typically 10–25% of T. melanosporum prices) makes it accessible for everyday cooking. Some chefs prefer its bold character for long-cooked dishes where subtler truffles would be lost.

## Cultivation

T. brumale can be cultivated by inoculating host seedlings (oak, hazel) with [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-spore-extraction-methods]] slurry, though commercial plantations are far less common than those for T. melanosporum.
