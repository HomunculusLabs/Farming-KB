## Overview

Tetrapterys mucronata is a woody liana in the family Malpighiaceae, distributed across tropical South America. It is known by several common names including yajé de brujos (sorcerer's yajé) and ayahuasca del monte in parts of Colombia and Ecuador.

The species belongs to a genus of approximately 100 tropical vines and shrubs, few of which have been thoroughly investigated for alkaloid content. Tetrapterys mucronata is the best-documented psychoactive member of the genus.

Tetrapterys mucronata is notable for containing both tryptamines and beta-carbolines in its leaf and stem tissue. This dual alkaloid profile means the plant alone can potentially produce oral psychoactivity without additional MAOI admixture plants.

Indigenous peoples of the Colombian and Ecuadorian Amazon have used this species as a standalone entheogen and as an [[comparison-native-bee-pollinators-vs-honeybees]].

Fruit is a distinctive four-winged samara approximately 2–3 cm across, facilitating wind dispersal through the forest canopy. The winged fruit is the defining characteristic of the genus Tetrapterys.

The bark is thin and smooth on young stems, developing shallow fissures on older growth. When cut, the bark exudes a watery sap that oxidizes to a reddish-brown color on exposure to air.

## Distribution and Habitat

Tetrapterys mucronata is native to the northwestern Amazon basin, with documented populations in Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and western Brazil. It grows in lowland [[king-stropharia-ecological-interactions-permaculture]] include relationships with native bee species that serve as primary pollinators. The plant also hosts several species of Lepidoptera larvae that feed on its foliage without apparent ill effects from the alkaloid content.

Climate change and deforestation pose potential threats to wild populations throughout the northwestern Amazon. The species' reliance on intact forest canopy for support structures makes it vulnerable to habitat fragmentation.
