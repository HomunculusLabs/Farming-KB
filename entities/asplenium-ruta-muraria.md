## Asplenium ruta-muraria

## Overview

Asplenium ruta-muraria, commonly known as wall rue spleenwort or wall rue, is a small evergreen fern in the family Aspleniaceae.

It is one of the most distinctive small ferns of temperate regions, readily recognized by its fan-shaped pinnae that resemble the leaves of common rue (Ruta graveolens).

The species is notable for its ability to colonize vertical rock faces and mortared walls, growing in crevices where few other vascular plants survive.

It has a widespread distribution across Europe, Asia, and eastern North America, with scattered populations in northern Africa.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Asplenium ruta-muraria was described by Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum.

Two subspecies are widely recognized: subsp. ruta-muraria (European and North American) and subsp. dolomiticum (southern European calcicole).

The fronds are small, 3–12 cm long, arising from a short creeping rhizome clothed in dark brown scales.

Pinnae are fan-shaped (flabellate) to cuneate, with rounded lobes and a distinctive wedge-shaped base.

The stipe is greenish-brown to dark brown, approximately half the total frond length, and brittle at the base.

Sori are linear along the veins on the underside of the pinnae, with membranous indusia that open toward the midrib.

Spores are bilateral, brown, and produced abundantly even on very small plants.

## Distribution and Habitat

Asplenium ruta-muraria is native to Europe, where it is widespread from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean and from the British Isles to the Ural Mountains.

In North America it occurs in eastern Canada and the northeastern United States, with disjunct populations in the Ozark and Ouachita Mountains.

Asian populations extend from Turkey through the Caucasus to the Himalayas and montane China.

The species is strictly calcicolous, requiring alkaline substrates such as limestone, chalk, mortar, or basic volcanic rock.

It colonizes vertical or near-vertical surfaces including cliff faces, stone walls, bridge abutments, and old building ruins.

Elevation ranges from sea level to 2400 m in southern European mountain ranges.

The species has naturalized on historic stone buildings across Europe, where some colonies on medieval churches and castles have persisted for centuries.

Urban populations demonstrate remarkable tolerance to air pollution, making wall rue one of the few ferns that thrives in city centers.

## Ecology and Cultivation

Asplenium ruta-muraria is a true rupicolous (rock-dwelling) specialist, with roots that penetrate deep into hairline crevices to access moisture and nutrients.

It tolerates extreme desiccation, surviving prolonged dry periods by curling its fronds inward and reducing metabolic activity.

The fern prefers shaded to partially shaded aspects, though it can tolerate full sun on cool, north-facing walls.

Spore dispersal is highly effective, with wind carrying spores into wall crevices and rock joints where they germinate readily.

Cultivation requires alkaline conditions; adding crushed limestone or oyster shells to the growing medium is essential.

The species is hardy to USDA Zone 4, withstanding both extreme cold and summer heat provided the root zone stays cool.

In garden settings it is ideal for trough gardens, crevice gardens, and shaded stone walls.

The fern is notably slow-growing, with individual clumps expanding only 1–2 cm per year, but colonies can persist for decades on stable wall surfaces.

## Uses and Ethnobotany

Wall rue has a long history in European folk medicine, where it was used to treat coughs, digestive complaints, and skin conditions.

The name "spleenwort" derives from the ancient Doctrine of Signatures, where the spleen-shaped sori were believed to indicate efficacy against spleen disorders.

In traditional English herbalism, the fern was prepared as an infusion or syrup for treating pulmonary complaints.

The species has no significant modern pharmacological applications, though preliminary phytochemical screening has identified common fern constituents.

It is occasionally grown in specialist fern collections and alpine gardens for its distinctive form and wall-colonizing habit.

Conservation status is secure across most of its range, though some populations on old mortar walls face threats from building renovation.

## Related Species

Asplenium trichomanes (maidenhair spleenwort) shares the wall habitat but has linear pinnae rather than fan-shaped ones.

Asplenium scolopendrium (hart's tongue fern) is a much larger relative with entire, strap-shaped fronds that grows in similar limestone habitats.

Asplenium adiantum-nigrum (black spleenwort) has darker, more triangular fronds and occurs in similar rocky sites but is less strictly calcicolous.

Hybridization between Asplenium ruta-muraria and other Asplenium species is rare but documented, producing sterile intermediates with irregular pinnae morphology.

The genus Asplenium is the largest fern genus globally, with over 700 species exhibiting remarkable ecological diversity from epiphytes to lithophytes.

Asplenium ruta-muraria is sometimes placed in the segregate genus Ceterach based on molecular phylogenetics, though this classification remains debated among pteridologists.
