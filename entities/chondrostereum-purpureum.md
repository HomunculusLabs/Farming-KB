Its white-rot decay mechanism and ability to colonize freshly cut stumps make it a valuable tool in vegetation management and forest regeneration.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Polyporales
- **Family:** Chondrostereaceae
- **Genus:** Chondrostereum
- **Species:** *C. purpureum* (Pers.) Pouzar (1959)
- **Synonyms:** *Thelephora purpurea* Pers. (1798), *Stereum purpureum* (Pers.) Fr. (1838)
- **Etymology:** *Chondro-* (cartilage) + *stereum* (firm); *purpureum* (purple), referring to the color of the fruiting body.

### Fruiting Body

- **Type:** Effused-reflexed to pileate (shelf-like), semicircular
- **Size:** 2–8 cm across, 1–4 cm radial extension
- **Upper surface:** Concentrically zoned, greyish-brown with fine hairs, margin often purple-tinted
- **Hymenium (underside):** Smooth to slightly wrinkled, distinctly lilac to purple
- **Flesh:** Tough, leathery, corky when dry, 1–2 mm thick
- **[[malt-extract-agar]] are white, cottony, becoming cream-colored with age.
- Aerial mycelium is moderately dense; [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of Europe, [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]].

It occurs as a saprotroph on dead hardwood branches, trunks, and stumps.

Primary hosts include *Prunus* species (cherry, plum, peach, apricot), apple (*Malus domestica*), poplar (*Populus* spp.), birch (*Betula* spp.), willow (*Salix* spp.), and numerous other hardwoods.

The fungus enters living trees through pruning wounds, frost cracks, or other bark injuries, establishing a systemic infection that spreads through the xylem.

Infected branches develop a characteristic silver sheen in the leaves due to separation of the palisade mesophyll from the underlying tissue — the diagnostic symptom of silver leaf disease.

## Pathogenicity and Silver Leaf Disease

Silver leaf disease progresses in three phases.

In the **invasive phase**, the fungus colonizes the xylem vessels of a living host, producing toxins that cause leaf discoloration and silvering.

During the **stress phase**, the host's defenses slow fungal spread, and symptoms may partially recede if the tree is vigorous.

In the **degradation phase**, the fungus transitions to a white-rot saprotroph, decomposing the lignin and cellulose of dead wood.

ⓘ In stone fruit orchards, silver leaf can cause significant economic losses.

Infected branches should be pruned at least 15 cm below visible symptoms, and wounds treated with fungicidal wound paint.
