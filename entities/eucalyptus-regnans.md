# Eucalyptus regnans

## Description

Eucalyptus regnans, commonly known as **mountain ash** or **Victorian ash**, is the tallest flowering plant in the world and one of the tallest tree species on Earth, capable of exceeding 100 metres in height. Native to cool, mountainous regions of southeastern Australia (Victoria and Tasmania), it is a fast-growing, straight-trunked tree that produces high-quality pale timber.

## Classification

- **Family:** Myrtaceae
- **Genus:** Eucalyptus
- **Species:** E. regnans
- **Common names:** Mountain ash, Victorian ash, swamp gum, stringy gum

## Key Facts

- The tallest flowering plant species, with recorded heights exceeding 100 m
- Native to the cool, moist mountain forests of Victoria and Tasmania
- Fast-growing — can reach 60 m in 50 years under optimal conditions
- Bark is smooth, greyish-white to light brown, shedding in long ribbons
- Timber is pale cream to light brown, straight-grained, and relatively easy to work
- Short-lived for a eucalypt (typically 200–400 years), killed by intense wildfire
- Prefers deep, fertile, well-drained soils in high-rainfall areas (over 1000 mm)
- Ecologically important as a keystone species in montane ash forests

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Timber:** Major commercial species for sawlogs, pulpwood, and woodchips; pale timber prized for flooring, furniture, and joinery (pages 18, 20)
- **Firewood:** Moderate-quality firewood; fast growth compensates for lower density (page 20)
- **[[soil-temperature-plant-growth-rate-doubling-rule]] makes it one of the most effective species for carbon capture
- **[[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]]:** Provides essential nesting hollows for threatened species like Leadbeater's possum

## Cultivation Notes

- Requires cool, moist mountain conditions with high rainfall
- Prefers deep, fertile, well-drained loamy soils
- Extremely fast growth in suitable sites
- Intolerant of drought and extended dry periods
- Sensitive to repeated wildfire (obligate seeder — does not resprout from lignotuber)
- Plantations established for timber on 30–40 year rotations
- Frost-tolerant once established, but seedlings need protection

## Notable Uses from Source

- [[gadd-fungal-carbon-sequestration]] with [[mycofiltration-watershed-protection]]. Its value is ecological as well as commercial: old trees create hollows, coarse woody debris, and fungal habitat that cannot be replaced by short rotations alone.

## Habitat Structure

As Eucalyptus regnans ages, it contributes more than living timber volume. Broken limbs, hollows, bark ribbons, fallen logs, and decaying stumps create habitat layers for mammals, birds, insects, mosses, and fungi. These structures take time to develop, which means old-growth mountain ash forest cannot be recreated simply by planting fast-growing seedlings.

The species is therefore central to debates about plantation rotation length and native forest conservation. Short rotations may produce wood fiber, but they do not reproduce the full habitat architecture of mature wet forest. A permaculture or restoration reading should distinguish between production stands and ecological forests.

## Water and Landscape Effects

Mountain ash forests are strongly tied to catchment hydrology. Young regrowth can use large amounts of water during rapid establishment, while mature forests regulate shade, soil cover, and stream conditions. In watershed planning, the tree's productivity must be weighed against the timing of water yield, fire history, and the need for continuous canopy cover.

These dynamics make the species a case study in scale. It is rarely a backyard tree; it belongs to mountainsides, catchments, reserves, and forestry landscapes where its height and ecological influence can be accommodated.

## Propagation and Establishment

Seed propagation is straightforward when fresh seed and suitable nursery conditions are available. The harder task is establishing seedlings in the correct landscape: they need moisture, light, weed control, and protection during the vulnerable early period. Once established on an appropriate site, growth can be extremely rapid.

Because mature trees become enormous, planting decisions should anticipate future windthrow risk, road access, firebreaks, and the consequences of eventual harvest or natural collapse. The species is best planned at forest scale rather than as an isolated amenity planting.
