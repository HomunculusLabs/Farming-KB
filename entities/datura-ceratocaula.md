# Datura ceratocaula

## Overview

**Datura ceratocaula** is an annual or short-lived perennial herbaceous plant in the family Solanaceae, notable for being the only Datura species that grows in aquatic or semi-aquatic habitats. It is commonly known as the sacred datura, toloache, or water trumpet.

The species is native to Mexico and Central America, where it grows in shallow water, marshes, and seasonally flooded areas. It is distinguished from other Datura species by its strongly toothed calyx, erect fruiting capsule, and preference for wetland environments.

Like all Datura species, Datura ceratocaula contains potent tropane alkaloids including scopolamine and hyoscyamine. It has been used in traditional Mesoamerican medicine and ritual practices for centuries, and all parts of the plant are dangerously toxic if ingested.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Datura ceratocaula L. was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum. The specific epithet "ceratocaula" means "horn-stemmed," referring to the thickened, angular stem characteristic of the species.

It is placed in the genus Datura within the tribe Datureae of Solanaceae. The genus includes approximately 10–12 species of herbaceous plants, distributed primarily in the Americas. Datura ceratocaula is sometimes considered part of the Datura stramonium species complex.

The plant is an annual or short-lived perennial herb reaching 60–120 cm in height. The stem is thick, fleshy, and prominently angular or winged, adaptations that may aid in buoyancy or structural support in aquatic habitats. The root system is fibrous and relatively shallow, anchoring the plant in muddy substrates.

Young plants develop a rosette of leaves before elongating the flowering stem. The growth habit is more robust and succulent than that of Datura stramonium, reflecting its adaptation to consistently moist environments.

Leaves are large (10–25 cm), ovate to broadly triangular, with coarsely dentate to lobed margins. The leaf surface is glabrous to sparsely pubescent, and leaves often have a slightly bluish-green coloration compared to other Datura species. Leaf texture is thinner and more membranous than in terrestrial relatives.

Flowers are borne singly in leaf axils. The corolla is white to pale lavender, 10–15 cm long, with a funnel-shaped tube. The calyx is 4–6 cm long, prominently toothed, and encloses the developing fruit, splitting irregularly at anthesis.

The fruit is an erect, nodding capsule 3–5 cm long, covered with numerous short, blunt spines. Unlike Datura stramonium, the capsule does not dehisce explosively. Seeds are numerous, brown to black, reniform, approximately 3–4 mm long.

## Distribution and Habitat

Datura ceratocaula is native to Mexico, ranging from the central highlands to the southern states of Oaxaca and Chiapas. Its distribution extends southward through Guatemala, Honduras, and into Nicaragua.

The species is unique among Datura in its strong association with aquatic and semi-aquatic habitats. It grows in shallow ponds, marshes, ditches, seasonally flooded fields, and along the margins of slow-moving streams and lakes.

Elevation ranges from near sea level to approximately 2,000 meters. It is most common in the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt, where volcanic crater lakes and seasonal wetlands provide ideal growing conditions. The plant can also colonize rice paddies and irrigation canals.

Growth occurs primarily during the rainy season, when standing water is available. The species tolerates periodic drying but requires saturated soils for optimal development and flowering. It is considered a weed in some agricultural settings.

Seeds are dispersed by water movement and may remain viable in pond sediments for several years. This seed bank strategy allows the species to persist through dry periods and rapidly colonize newly flooded areas. The seeds can float, facilitating spread between isolated water bodies.

## Cultivation and Uses

Datura ceratocaula is rarely cultivated intentionally, unlike several other Datura species that are grown as ornamentals. When grown, it requires consistently moist to waterlogged soil conditions that make it impractical for most garden settings.

In traditional Mesoamerican medicine, the plant has been used to treat various ailments including asthma, rheumatism, and skin conditions. Preparations typically involve external application as poultices or baths, though some traditional practices include oral administration at carefully controlled doses.

Among the Nahua and other indigenous peoples of central Mexico, Datura ceratocaula has ritual significance as an entheogen. It has been used in divination ceremonies and healing rituals, often in combination with other psychoactive plants.

All medicinal and ritual use carries significant risk of poisoning. Symptoms of tropane alkaloid toxicity include dry mouth, blurred vision, tachycardia, hallucinations, and potentially fatal respiratory depression. Modern medical authorities strongly discourage any internal use of the plant.

Cases of accidental poisoning have been documented when the plant is confused with edible greens or when seeds are ingested by children attracted to the spiny capsules. Livestock poisoning is rare due to the aquatic habitat, but can occur during drought when animals access dried plants along receding water margins.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

Datura ceratocaula contains the tropane alkaloids scopolamine (hyoscine), hyoscyamine, and atropine as its primary bioactive constituents. These compounds are concentrated in the seeds and leaves, with lower levels in stems and roots.

Scopolamine acts as a competitive antagonist at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors throughout the autonomic nervous system and central nervous system. It produces mydriasis, anhidrosis, tachycardia, urinary retention, and at higher doses, profound cognitive disturbances including hallucinations and amnesia.

Hyoscyamine, the precursor to atropine, has similar antimuscarinic properties with somewhat greater activity at gastrointestinal smooth muscle receptors. Atropine is produced by racemization of hyoscyamine during drying or processing of plant material.

Alkaloid content in Datura ceratocaula is comparable to other Datura species, with total tropane alkaloid concentrations of 0.2–0.6% in dried leaf tissue. Seed alkaloid content can reach 0.4–0.8%, making seeds the most dangerous plant part for accidental ingestion.

Treatment of poisoning follows standard anticholinergic antidote protocols. Physostigmine (also known as eserine) reverses central and peripheral anticholinergic effects by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase. Benzodiazepines manage agitation, and supportive care addresses hyperthermia and cardiovascular instability.

Recovery typically requires 24–48 hours of monitored medical care. Prolonged confusion and visual disturbances may persist for several days after acute symptoms resolve. Psychiatric evaluation is recommended following intentional ingestion due to the risk of persistent perceptual changes.

## Cultural Significance

Datura ceratocaula occupies a notable place in Aztec and Nahua ethnobotany. It was known as "toloatzin" or "tlapatl" in Nahuatl, names shared with other Datura species used in traditional practice.

The Florentine Codex, compiled by Bernardino de Sahagún in the 16th century, describes Datura species used by Aztec physicians and priests. The text references preparations applied externally for joint pain and gout, as well as ritual use in divination and prophecy ceremonies.

Sahagún documented that Aztec healers classified Datura preparations according to dosage, recognizing that excessive amounts caused dangerous delirium while carefully measured doses produced therapeutic effects. This detailed pharmacological knowledge reflects centuries of empirical observation by Mesoamerican peoples.

In modern Mexican folk medicine, the species is sometimes referred to as "toloache de agua" to distinguish it from terrestrial Datura species. It is considered particularly potent by traditional healers, who recommend it only for external applications.

The species has also attracted interest from ethnopharmacologists studying Mesoamerican psychoactive plant complexes. Its aquatic habitat is unusual among psychoactive Solanaceae and may represent an ecological specialization that limits its distribution and traditional use compared to more widespread species.

Chemical analyses comparing Datura ceratocaula to other Datura species have shown broadly similar tropane alkaloid profiles, though some studies suggest a higher proportion of scopolamine relative to hyoscyamine. This ratio may contribute to differences in subjective effects reported by traditional practitioners.

The species is of conservation interest due to habitat loss from wetland drainage and agricultural conversion in central Mexico. While not currently listed as threatened, its specialized aquatic habitat makes populations vulnerable to water management decisions and urban development.
