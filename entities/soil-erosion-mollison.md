# Soil Erosion

## Description

Soil erosion is the loss of topsoil through the action of wind, water, and chemical degradation. Mollison identifies soil erosion as one of the central threats to permanent agriculture, noting that "soil creation is one of the central themes of [[permaculture]]" and that soil degradation represents "the loss of production and hence of dependent plants and animals." This section covers the mechanisms, causes, and permaculture approaches to preventing and reversing soil erosion.

## Classification

- **Type**: Environmental process / land degradation
- **Category**: Soil science, landscape management
- **Subtypes**: Wind erosion, sheet erosion, gully erosion, tunnel erosion, soil collapse/deflocculation

## Key Facts

### Mechanisms of Erosion
- **Wind erosion**: Dust storms, blow-outs of dunes and foreshores.
- **Sheet erosion**: Generalised surface water flow off bare areas and croplands.
- **Gully erosion**: Concentrated water flow over deep but unstable sediment.
- **Tunnel erosion**: Sub-surface scouring of soils below the surface.
- **Soil collapse/deflocculation**: Increased salt concentrations in clay-fraction soils causing structural breakdown.

### Causes of Erosion
- Deforestation, soil compaction, disturbed soil-water balance (increased overland flow, rising water tables, salt seepages).
- Overgrazing, broad-scale plough agriculture, episodes of high winds or rains in drought periods.
- Severe disturbance from animal tracks, roading, and ill-advised earthworks.
- Economic and political pressures forcing inappropriate land use, over-production on fragile soils.
- Falling levels of organic matter in soil — the key factor identified by Arden-Clarke and Hodges (New Scientist, 1987).

### Key Research
- Charles Arden-Clarke and David Hodges (New Scientist, 12 Feb 1987) demonstrated that "many of the recent outbreaks of severe erosion are clearly linked to falling levels of organic matter in the soil."
- Soils with higher organic content are more stable due to better structure and water infiltration; inorganic soils break down under rain.
- [[erik-van-der-werf]] (Agomeda Agricultural Project, Ghana, 1985-86) showed that permanently mulched and cropped soils achieved bulk density of 0.92 g/cc versus 1.35 for annually burnt bush — even with active cropping.

## Relevance to Cultivation

- **Prevention strategies**: Windbreaks, tree crops, fast-spreading grasses for wind erosion; permanent crop, terracing, forestry for water erosion.
- **Gully repair**: Diversion and spreader drains plus gabions to heal scoured areas.
- **Tunnel erosion**: De-stocking, contour drainage, establishment of deep-rooted plants.
- **Salinity**: Reafforestation to lower groundwater tables, deep interceptor drains to cut off salt seepages.
- **Organic matter management**: Mulching, [[green-manure]], grass leys on rotation, hedgerows, and minimal cultivation are described as "not only urgent but imperative."
- **Economic context**: Mollison argues that third world debt and western over-production are primary factors in soil collapse, requiring systemic economic and political change alongside technical solutions.

## Permaculture Principles

- Soil erosion repair is the design priority wherever erosion occurs.
- The basis of land use planning should encompass permitted or restricted use of soils, carefully plotted following analyses of slope, soil stability, minimal forest clearing, and maximum permitted crop/livestock levels.
- "The time to examine the organic farming approach has passed, the time to adopt it has arrived."

## Practical Applications

Soil Erosion Mollison plays a critical role in sustainable land management and regenerative agriculture.
Understanding these processes enables practitioners to design systems that work with natural
soil biology rather than against it, reducing inputs while improving long-term productivity.

## Management Considerations

Effective management requires monitoring soil indicators and responding to changes in
soil structure, organic matter content, and biological activity. Key strategies include
minimizing soil disturbance, maintaining ground cover, and incorporating diverse plantings.
