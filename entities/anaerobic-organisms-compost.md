## Anaerobic Organisms (Compost Context)

## Description

Anaerobic organisms are microorganisms that thrive in the absence of oxygen,
carrying out metabolism without relying on aerobic respiration. In composting
and compost tea production, [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]] and the organisms that
proliferate under them are a major concern. [[elaine-ingham]] devotes significant
attention to anaerobic organisms in her field guide, explaining why they are
generally detrimental in compost tea systems and how to prevent anaerobic
conditions from developing during the brewing process.

## Classification

- **Category**: Microbial physiology group
- **Key types**: Facultative anaerobes, obligate anaerobes
- **Common genera in compost context**: *Clostridium*, *Bacillus* (some
  species), sulfate-reducing bacteria, methanogens
- **Metabolic modes**: Fermentation, anaerobic respiration (using nitrate,
  sulfate, iron, or carbonate as electron acceptors)

## Key Facts

- Anaerobic conditions develop when dissolved oxygen drops below approximately
  5-6 ppm in compost tea, or when compost piles become waterlogged or
  compacted.
- Anaerobic decomposition produces metabolic byproducts that are toxic to
  plants: alcohol, organic acids (acetic, butyric, propionic), hydrogen
  sulfide, ammonia, and methane.
- Many anaerobic bacteria are known plant pathogens or produce phytotoxic
  compounds that damage root tissue.
- Alcohol produced by [[jadam-anaerobic-fermentation]] in compost tea can kill plant
  roots at concentrations as low as 1 ppm.
- Anaerobic conditions favor *E. coli* and other enteric bacteria that are
  human health concerns.
- Anaerobic compost (putrefied rather than decomposed) has a foul odor
  (rotten egg, ammonia, vomit-like) that indicates the presence of harmful
  organisms and compounds.
- The shift from aerobic to anaerobic conditions can happen rapidly — in as
  little as 15-20 minutes in a compost tea brewer if aeration fails.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Compost Tea Production

Ingham emphasizes anaerobic organisms as the primary threat to compost tea
quality:

1. **Aeration is critical**: The entire purpose of "[[actively-aerated-compost-tea]]" compost
   tea is to maintain oxygen levels that suppress anaerobic organisms while
   promoting beneficial aerobic biology.
2. **Dissolved oxygen monitoring**: Ingham recommends monitoring dissolved
   oxygen levels throughout the brewing process to ensure aerobic conditions
   are maintained.
3. **Food [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]]**: Over-adding food resources (molasses, sugars)
   can cause microbial populations to bloom so rapidly that they consume all
   available oxygen, triggering anaerobic conditions.
4. **Smell test**: Anaerobic compost tea has a distinctive bad odor (putrid,
   sulfur, ammonia), while properly made aerobic tea smells earthy and sweet.

### Compost Production

- [[thermal-composting-vs-aact]] must maintain adequate oxygen throughout the pile to
  prevent anaerobic pockets.
- Turning compost introduces oxygen and prevents anaerobic conditions from
  developing.
- Vermicomposting naturally maintains aerobic conditions through worm activity
  and the open structure of worm bedding.

## Management Notes

- Maintain continuous aeration during [[compost-tea-brewing]] — never let the
  pump stop.
- Use appropriate food resource quantities — more is not better.
- Monitor dissolved oxygen if possible; aim for >6 ppm throughout brewing.
- Trust your nose — if compost tea smells bad, it IS bad; do not apply it
  to plants.
- If anaerobic conditions develop, discard the tea and start over.
- Ensure compost used as inoculum is fully aerobic and properly made.
