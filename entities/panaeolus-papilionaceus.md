# Panaeolus papilionaceus

**Panaeolus papilionaceus** is a common species of mushroom in the family Bolbitiaceae, section Coprinariae. It is a coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) fungus found worldwide in temperate and subtropical regions. The species is notable for its distinctive mottled gill pattern, which is characteristic of the entire genus Panaeolus, and for its historical implication in cases of unintentional psilocybin|psilocybin ingestion due to its close resemblance to psychoactive species in the same genus.

## Taxonomy and Synonymy

The species was originally described by Jean-Baptiste Bory de Saint-Vincent in 1789 as Agaricus papilionaceus. The specific epithet *papilionaceus* is derived from the Latin *papilio* (butterfly), likely referring to the cap's bell shape and the delicate, wing-like appearance of the gills when viewed from below, or perhaps to the way the cap opens like butterfly wings as the mushroom matures.

The taxonomy of P. papilionaceus has been the subject of considerable debate for over a century. Some authorities consider it conspecific with [[panaeolus-campanulatus]] (originally described by Bulliard in 1789) and Panaeolus retirugis (described by Fries in 1821), treating all three as a single, highly variable species. Under this interpretation, the name P. papilionaceus has priority.

Other authorities maintain them as separate taxa based on differences in cap surface ornamentation (smooth versus wrinkled versus prominently rugose) and spore morphology. Molecular phylogenetic studies have not yet fully resolved this species complex, and different databases and field guides may use different names for what may or may not be the same biological entity. Under the broadest interpretation, the name P. papilionaceus may apply to the entire species aggregate.

## Description

### Cap (Pileus)
The cap measures 1.5 to 4 cm in diameter, broadly campanulate (bell-shaped) when young, becoming convex to broadly convex or nearly plane with age, often retaining a slight umbo or papilla. The surface is smooth to slightly wrinkled or plicate, and distinctly hygrophanous. When moist, the cap is dark brown to grayish-brown, often with a paler disc (center) and a distinctly darker margin. As it dries, it fades dramatically to a grayish, grayish-buff, or pale tan color. The margin is typically translucently striate when moist (the striations visible as lines radiating from near the center) and may become slightly upturned and ragged with age. The flesh is thin, fragile, and whitish.

### Gills
The gills are adnexed to adnate (narrowly attached), moderately close, and are the most distinctive feature of this species and the genus as a whole. They are characteristically mottled or spotted — a hallmark feature of the genus Panaeolus that gives rise to the informal group name "mottlegills." This mottling results from the uneven, asynchronous maturation of spores on the gill faces, creating a distinctive pattern of dark patches (where spores are mature and dark brown) and pale patches (where basidia have not yet produced mature spores). The gills are initially whitish to pale gray, developing the mottled pattern progressively as the mushroom ages.

### Stipe (Stem)
The stipe is 5 to 12 cm long and 2 to 4 mm thick, cylindrical, hollow, and equal or slightly thickened toward the base. It is smooth, white to pale gray or grayish, and often covered with a fine pruinose (frosty or powdery) coating, especially near the apex. The surface is somewhat shiny when dry. The base may have whitish mycelial strands attached, anchoring it to the substrate. There is no ring or annulus present at any stage of development. The stipe is brittle and easily broken.

### Spores and Microscopic Features
Spores are broadly elliptical to lemon-shaped (ellipsoid with a flattened or concave apiculus, technically termed "sublimoniform"), smooth, with a notably thick wall, and dark brown to blackish-brown in deposit. They measure approximately 11 to 14 µm by 7 to 9 µm, making them relatively large for the genus. The thick spore wall is a characteristic feature of many Panaeolus species and is thought to aid in survival during passage through animal digestive tracts. Basidia are typically two-spored (occasionally four-spored), which is a useful microscopic character for distinguishing it from some close relatives. Cheilocystidia are abundant at the gill edges, cylindrical to ventricose or broadly clavate, and thin-walled.

## Distribution and Habitat

P. papilionaceus has a cosmopolitan distribution, found across Europe, North America, Central and South America, Asia, Africa, and Australia. It is one of the most widespread dung-inhabiting mushrooms in the world and can be found on every continent except Antarctica.

As a coprophilous (dung-loving) species, it grows almost exclusively on the dung of herbivorous animals, particularly that of horses, cattle, sheep, goats, and other grazing mammals. It may also occasionally appear on heavily enriched soil, compost heaps, or well-rotted straw, but dung is its primary and preferred substrate. It is most commonly found in pastures, meadows, agricultural fields, and along trails and roadsides where animals graze or defecate. Fruiting occurs from spring through autumn in temperate regions, with peak abundance during warm, humid conditions following rainfall. In tropical and subtropical regions, it may fruit year-round whenever moisture is sufficient. The mushrooms often appear in scattered groups or small clusters on individual dung pats.

## Psychoactive Properties

The psychoactive status of P. papilionaceus is a subject of ongoing uncertainty and debate in the mycological and mycopharmacological literature.

### Historical Reports
Several older references and field guides have listed P. papilionaceus as a psychoactive species containing psilocybin|psilocybin and/or psilocin|psilocin. Some recreational mushroom foragers have historically collected it along with more reliably psychoactive Panaeolus species, particularly in regions where truly psychoactive species are less common. Anecdotal reports of mild psychoactive effects have appeared in the underground mycophilic literature since at least the 1970s.

### Modern Analysis
Modern chemical analyses using more sophisticated techniques have yielded inconsistent results. Some collections have shown only trace or undetectable levels of psilocybin|psilocybin and psilocin|psilocin, while others have reported measurable but low quantities. The variability may reflect genuine biochemical differences between geographically separated populations, differences in growth conditions and substrate, or — most likely — confusion with truly psychoactive species in the field. The species complex may also contain both psychoactive and non-psychoactive populations that have not yet been resolved taxonomically.

The most reliably psychoactive species in the genus include [[panaeolus-cyanescens-cultivation-guide]], [[panaeolus-cinctulus]] (also known historically as [[panaeolus-subbalteatus-profile]]), and [[panaeolus-tropicalis-cultivation-differences]]. P. papilionaceus should **not** be relied upon for psychoactive effects.

## Similar Species

- **[[panaeolus-campanulatus]]** — Very similar; some authorities treat them as synonymous. If separated, [[panaeolus-campanulatus]] typically has a more uniformly smooth cap without the slight wrinkling or plicate surface seen in P. papilionaceus.
- **Panaeolus retirugis** — Another close relative in the complex, with a more prominently wrinkled or rugose cap surface that develops a network of ridges as the cap dries.
- **[[panaeolus-cinctulus]]** — A well-documented psychoactive species with a more robust stature and a distinct dark band (zonate pattern) on the cap when moist, making it easier to distinguish in the field.
- **[[panaeolus-sphinctrinus]]** — Similar in size and habitat but typically smaller and more fragile, with a strongly umbonate cap and often found growing directly on dung rather than on nearby soil.
- **[[panaeolina-foenisecii]]** — The haymaker's mushroom, found in similar grassland habitats but with distinctly brown (not black) spores and gills that are never mottled — the gills mature synchronously, producing a uniform brown color.

## Ecological Role

As a coprophilous saprotroph, P. papilionaceus plays an important role in nutrient cycling in pastoral and agricultural ecosystems. It is among the first colonizers of fresh dung, breaking down complex organic matter including undigested cellulose and other plant fibers. This decomposition process releases nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients in forms available to plants and soil microorganisms. The lifecycle of coprophilous fungi is tightly linked to the grazing patterns of herbivores, and these fungi contribute significantly to the decomposition of animal waste in natural grasslands, preventing the accumulation of dung and maintaining soil fertility.

## Cultural Significance

P. papilionaceus and its close relatives have been known since antiquity as mushrooms associated with animal dung. References to dark-spored mushrooms growing on manure appear in some of the earliest mycological texts. In the context of psychedelic mushroom literature, the species has been a persistent source of taxonomic confusion, with some authors including it in lists of psychoactive fungi and others firmly excluding it. This ambiguity has contributed to its reputation as an unreliable or borderline psychoactive species, and modern foragers are generally advised to seek more definitively psychoactive species instead.

## Notes

The entire genus Panaeolus is characterized by the distinctive mottled gill pattern that gives the group its informal common name "mottlegills." This pattern results from asynchronous spore maturation, where patches of basidia develop and release spores at different rates across the gill surface, creating a speckled appearance of dark and light areas. While this pattern is a useful field identification feature for the genus as a whole, it does not distinguish between psychoactive and non-psychoactive species within the genus. Accurate identification to species level in Panaeolus always requires microscopic examination of spore morphology and basidial characters.

## Seasonality

P. papilionaceus fruits from spring through autumn in temperate regions (April to October in the Northern Hemisphere), with peak abundance during warm, humid periods following rainfall. In tropical and subtropical regions, fruiting can occur year-round whenever sufficient moisture is available. Individual fruitings are triggered by warm temperatures (above 15°C) combined with sustained humidity, typically appearing 3–7 days after rainfall on fresh or aging dung deposits.
