## New Zealand Flax (*Phormium tenax*)

## Description

New Zealand flax is a perennial rush-like plant native to New Zealand that produces high-quality fibre suitable for fabric production. In [[david-holmgren]]'s *[[permaculture]]: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability*, it is highlighted as a prime example of a slow-growing perennial crop that can substitute for environmentally damaging annual fibre crops, particularly cotton. It was grown in Tasmania during World War II and is widely adaptable to seasonally wet bottomland in high-rainfall southern Australian regions.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Asphodelaceae (formerly Hemerocallidaceae)
- **Genus**: *Phormium*
- **Species**: *P. tenax*
- **Growth habit**: Perennial, evergreen, clump-forming
- **Native range**: New Zealand

## Key Facts

- Produces high-quality fabric fibre that can substitute for several annual fibre crops, including cotton
- Cotton is described as the most environmentally damaging of all broadacre crops due to high nutrient and water demand and pest susceptibility
- Substituting cotton with New Zealand flax would result in significant savings by preventing soil degradation, conserving water, and reducing chemical and fertiliser use
- Grown in Tasmania during World War II (confirmed by [[bill-mollison]])
- Widely adaptable to seasonally wet bottomland in high-rainfall areas of southern Australia
- As a perennial, it does not require replanting each year, saving energy and reducing soil disturbance
- Embodies the permaculture principle of using slow-growing, long-lived perennial species to create sustainable systems

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

New Zealand flax is a textbook example of Holmgren's permaculture Principle 10 ("Use [[small-and-slow-solutions]]"). It demonstrates how perennial plants can replace annual crops with superior sustainability outcomes:

- **Soil conservation**: No annual cultivation means no soil disturbance or erosion from replanting
- **Water efficiency**: Lower water requirements compared to cotton
- **Chemical reduction**: Reduced need for pesticides and synthetic fertilisers
- **Energy savings**: Eliminates the energy cycle of annual sowing, tending, and harvesting
- **Multi-functional**: Provides fibre, ornamental value, and habitat

Holmgren uses New Zealand flax as evidence that making greater use of perennial plants for human needs is "perhaps the most fundamental demonstration of the principle that slow is sane."

## Cultivation Notes

- Prefers seasonally wet, bottomland positions in high-rainfall areas
- Suited to southern Australian climate zones
- Hardy perennial requiring minimal ongoing management once established
- Harvesting fibre is a skilled process requiring traditional or adapted techniques
