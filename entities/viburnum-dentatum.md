## Overview

**Viburnum dentatum**, commonly known as arrowwood viburnum or southern arrowwood, is a deciduous shrub in the family Adoxaceae native to [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of the Northern Hemisphere. [[cervantes-selfing-single-seed-descent]].

Fruit ripening occurs August–October, with peak palatability after the first frost reduces residual astringency.

## Distribution and Habitat

Arrowwood viburnum is widespread in eastern North America from southern New Brunswick and Maine through the Appalachian Mountains to northern Florida. It extends west to eastern Iowa, eastern Kansas, and central Texas.

The species is absent from the Gulf Coastal Plain and most of the lower Mississippi Valley. Several closely related taxa were historically segregated as varieties but are now often treated as part of a variable species complex.

Arrowwood grows in a broad range of habitats including open woodlands, forest edges, thickets, streambanks, meadows, and disturbed sites. It is notably shade-tolerant as a sapling but fruits best in partial to full sun.

It thrives on moist, well-drained loamy soils with pH 5.0–7.0 but tolerates clay, sandy soils, and periodic flooding. [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]].

## Ecological Role

Arrowwood is among the most important wildlife food shrubs in eastern deciduous forests. The fruits are consumed by numerous bird species including cedar waxwings, northern flickers, gray catbirds, American robins, eastern bluebirds, and ruffed grouse.

White-tailed deer browse the twigs and foliage moderately, while cottontail rabbits feed on bark and lower stems during winter. The dense thicket-forming habit provides cover for nesting songbirds and small mammals.

The flowers are highly attractive to pollinators, particularly [[mollison-designers-cloudburst-and-stormwater-management]] plantings due to its tolerance of fluctuating soil moisture.
