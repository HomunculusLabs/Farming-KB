# Periderm

## Description

The periderm is the outer protective tissue layer that replaces the epidermis in older stems and roots of woody plants. Commonly known as bark, the periderm forms from lateral meristematic cells as plants age and provides both protection and regulated airflow to the underlying tissues. It is a key component of the dermal tissue system in mature woody plants.

## Classification

- **Tissue Type:** Dermal tissue (secondary protective tissue)
- **Origin:** Lateral meristematic cells (phellogen / cork cambium)
- **Occurrence:** Only in woody plants (absent in herbaceous species)
- **Replaces:** Epidermis in mature stems and roots

## Structure

The periderm is composed of multiple cell layers:

- **Phellogen (cork cambium):** The lateral meristem that produces periderm cells
- **Phellem (cork cells):** Dead cells filled with suberin, providing waterproofing
- **Phelloderm:** Inner layer of living cells produced by the cork cambium

Cork cells are dead at maturity and filled with suberin, making them impermeable to water and gases. The periderm also contains lenticels — small raised pores that allow gas exchange through the otherwise waterproof barrier.

## Key Facts

- Forms from lateral meristematic cells as woody plants age
- Replaces the epidermis in stems and roots of woody species
- More commonly called bark
- Provides protection against physical damage, pathogens, and desiccation
- Allows regulated airflow into the plant through lenticels
- Cork cells are dead cells filled with suberin (the same waxy substance found in the Casparian strip)
- Only woody plants produce a periderm; herbaceous plants retain their epidermis throughout their life cycle

## Functions

### Protection
The periderm serves as a physical and chemical barrier against pathogen entry, physical damage from animals and environmental factors, water loss through desiccation, and temperature extremes.

### Gas Exchange
While suberin-filled cork cells are impermeable, lenticels in the periderm allow controlled gas exchange, enabling oxygen supply to living tissues beneath the bark.

### Water Regulation
Suberin in cork cells creates a waterproof layer preventing water loss from the stem or root surface, complementing the cuticle's role on leaves.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Grafting:** Understanding periderm structure is essential for grafting woody plants, as cambium layers must be aligned
- **Fungal pathogens:** Many canker diseases infect through wounds in the periderm; healthy bark is a primary defense
- **Mycorrhizal roots:** In woody perennials, periderm covers older root surfaces while active mycorrhizal associations occur on younger root tips
- **Bark mulch:** Periderm-rich bark is widely used as mulch, contributing to soil organic matter and fungal habitat
- **Cork harvesting:** The cork oak (Quercus suber) produces commercially valuable periderm

## Seasonal Development

Periderm development follows seasonal patterns:

- **Active growth:** Cork cambium is most active during the growing season
- **Winter dormancy:** Periderm thickens and provides insulation against freezing
- **Annual rings:** In some species, annual growth rings are visible in bark
- **Bark shedding:** Some species (e.g., eucalyptus, birch) shed periderm in strips or sheets

## Commercial Uses

- **Cork products:** Cork from Quercus suber is harvested every 9-12 years for wine stoppers, flooring, and insulation
- **Bark mulch:** Periderm-rich bark is a major commercial mulch product
- **Medicinal compounds:** Some bark-derived compounds (e.g., willow bark salicin, cinchona bark quinine) have pharmaceutical significance
- **Tannins:** Bark tannins have been used for leather tanning for millennia
