# Lophophora diffusa

**Lophophora diffusa** is a species of small, spineless cactus endemic to a restricted range in the Mexican state of Querétaro. It is closely related to [[mescaline-entity]] and instead containing pellotine as its primary alkaloid.

## Taxonomy and Description

Lophophora diffusa was originally classified as a variety of L. williamsii (L. williamsii var. diffusa) but was elevated to species status based on consistent morphological and chemical differences. It is one of two widely recognized species within the genus Lophophora.

Additional taxa have been proposed by some researchers including L. fricii and L. jourdaniana, though the taxonomic validity of these remains debated. Some authorities recognize only two species (L. williamsii and L. diffusa), while others accept up to four species in the genus.

The plant is similar in general form to [[mescaline-entity]] in L. diffusa. This distinction was a key factor in the elevation of L. diffusa to species status.

Instead of mescaline, the primary psychoactive alkaloid in L. diffusa is:

- **Pellotine** (anhalidine) — the predominant alkaloid, present at concentrations of 0.1-1% dry weight

Additional alkaloids found in L. diffusa include:

- **Anhalonidine** — a tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloid
- **Lophophorine** — a biogenic amine also found in L. williamsii
- **N-Methylpellotine** — a methylated derivative of pellotine

Pellotine (N-methyl-3,4-dimethoxyphenethylamine) is a phenethylamine alkaloid with sedative and mild psychoactive properties. It produces effects that are qualitatively different from mescaline, typically described as more sedating, dreamy, and less visually intense.

The experience has been compared to a mild dream state rather than the vivid psychedelic experience produced by mescaline-containing cacti. Some users report a sense of physical relaxation, mild euphoria, and dreamlike imagery without the pronounced visual phenomena characteristic of mescaline.

Some researchers have reported detecting trace amounts of mescaline in certain L. diffusa specimens, though these findings may reflect misidentification of specimens or natural hybridization zones where the two species ranges approach each other.

The chemical distinction between the two species is generally reliable and consistent, supporting their status as separate species. Chemical analysis is considered the most reliable method for distinguishing L. diffusa from L. williamsii when morphological characteristics are ambiguous.

## Traditional Use

L. diffusa is known to have been used traditionally by indigenous peoples in the Querétaro region, though its ceremonial use was less widespread and less well documented than that of L. williamsii.

The species is sometimes referred to as false peyote due to its visual similarity to L. williamsii and the different character of its psychoactive effects. The Otomi people of central Mexico have been reported to use L. diffusa in traditional medicine and ceremonial contexts.

Otomi healers may use the plant for ailments of the digestive and nervous systems, reflecting the sedative properties of its primary alkaloid pellotine. The distinction between L. diffusa and L. williamsii was likely recognized by indigenous peoples long before Western science formalized the taxonomic split.

The markedly different psychoactive effects would have made the distinction practically significant for ceremonial use, with each species serving different purposes in indigenous pharmacopeia.

## Cultivation

L. diffusa is cultivated similarly to L. williamsii, though it is somewhat less commonly encountered in cultivation. The species requires well-draining mineral soil, warm temperatures, and protection from prolonged direct sun in hotter climates.

Growth rate is slow, comparable to L. williamsii. Grafting onto faster rootstock such as [[trichocereus-bridgesii]] significantly accelerates growth.

The species is generally considered somewhat more sensitive to overwatering and rot than L. williamsii, requiring careful watering practices. A mineral-based soil mix with added limestone chips replicates its native habitat conditions well.

Seed germination follows standard procedures for Lophophora species, requiring warm, humid conditions. L. diffusa is self-sterile, meaning cross-pollination between different plants is required for viable seed production.

This self-sterility is an important consideration for cultivators, as isolated plants will not produce viable seeds. Growers must maintain at least two genetically distinct plants for successful seed production.

## Identification and Distinguishing Features

For cultivators and collectors, distinguishing L. diffusa from L. williamsii is an important practical skill. The most reliable distinguishing features are:

1. **Rib count**: L. diffusa typically has 8-13 ribs; L. williamsii typically has 5-9
2. **Color**: L. diffusa is yellowish-green; L. williamsii is blue-green
3. **Rib shape**: L. diffusa ribs are more irregular and tuberculate
4. **Wool density**: L. diffusa has sparser, lighter wool
5. **Geography**: L. diffusa is found only in Querétaro; L. williamsii ranges across northern Mexico and south Texas

When morphological identification is uncertain, chemical analysis provides definitive identification. The presence or absence of mescaline is the key distinguishing characteristic, with L. diffusa containing only trace amounts at most.

Hybridization between the two species is possible in cultivation, and hybrids may exhibit intermediate characteristics in both morphology and chemistry. Such hybrids complicate identification and are generally referred to by informal names.

## Commercial Availability

L. diffusa is available from specialist cactus nurseries and seed suppliers, though it is less commonly offered than L. williamsii. Seeds are the most common form of commercial trade, as international shipment of live plants is restricted by CITES regulations.

Prices for L. diffusa seeds and plants tend to be higher than for L. williamsii due to the species restricted natural range, slower propagation rate, and lower commercial availability. Established multi-headed specimens are particularly valued by collectors.

## Conservation Status

L. diffusa faces significant conservation pressures in its restricted natural range. The species is listed on CITES Appendix II, regulating international trade.

Habitat destruction through agricultural expansion, urban development, and road construction continues to reduce available habitat. Illegal collection by both commercial and private collectors further threatens wild populations.

The species extremely limited range makes it particularly vulnerable to habitat loss and climate change. Conservation organizations have called for strengthened protections and habitat restoration efforts in the Sierra Gorda region.
