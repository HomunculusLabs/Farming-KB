## Overview

Mastocarpus stellatus, commonly known as carrageen or Irish moss, is a red alga in the family Phyllophoraceae found on rocky shores of the north Atlantic. It is closely related to and frequently confused with [[palmer-mushroom-hunting-field-identification]] character.

Tetrasporangial sori appear as pale, raised patches on branch surfaces. The medulla contains large, thick-walled cells, while the cortex is composed of small, densely pigmented cells.

## Distribution and Habitat

M. stellatus occurs on both sides of the north Atlantic. In Europe, it ranges from Norway and Iceland south to Portugal and the Mediterranean coast of Spain. On the western Atlantic, it extends from Newfoundland to Long Island, New York.

The species occupies the lower intertidal and shallow subtidal zones, typically from 0 to 5 m depth. It is most abundant on semi-exposed rocky shores with moderate wave action.

Wave exposure is a key factor determining abundance and morphology. Specimens from exposed sites tend to be shorter and more compact.

It frequently co-occurs with Chondrus crispus, with M. stellatus tending to dominate slightly higher on the shore. Both species form mixed beds in the mid-to-lower intertidal.

The species tolerates a range of salinities but is less tolerant of reduced salinity than C. crispus. It is absent from strongly estuarine environments.

## Chemistry and Nutrition

Pigments include R-phycoerythrin, which gives the species its characteristic red coloration. Extracted phycobiliproteins have commercial potential as natural colorants and fluorescent markers.

## Ecology

M. stellatus provides important habitat structure in intertidal communities. Its fronds shelter a diverse assemblage of small invertebrates, including amphipods, polychaetes, and juvenile mollusks.

The complex three-dimensional structure of dense Mastocarpus beds supports higher invertebrate diversity than adjacent bare rock.

The species is relatively slow-growing compared to opportunistic seaweeds, with annual productivity of 1–3 kg fresh weight per square meter in natural beds. Turnover occurs primarily through [[gadd-mycelial-network-resilience-grazing-pressure]] from patellid limpets and Littorina snails can be significant, particularly on juvenile plants. The cartilaginous texture of mature thalli provides some resistance to herbivory.

M. stellatus competes with fucoid algae and Chondrus crispus for primary substrate space. Disturbance from wave action and ice scour creates colonization opportunities that the species can exploit.

## Harvesting and Economic Use

Mastocarpus stellatus has been harvested for centuries along the coasts of Ireland, Scotland, and Brittany. Traditional harvesting involves hand-picking at low tide during summer months.

Harvesters typically select larger, more mature thalli and leave holdfasts attached to the rock to allow regeneration.

Commercial carrageenan extraction from M. stellatus peaked in the mid-20th century but has since declined with the rise of tropical Kappaphycus cultivation. Wild-harvested material from Europe now primarily serves specialty markets.

In Ireland, the species is still collected for traditional use in desserts, soups, and as a thickening agent. It is sold dried or as a purified carrageenan powder at local markets.

The species is occasionally used in cosmetics and pharmaceutical formulations where high-gel-strength carrageenan is desired. Small-scale aquaculture trials have been conducted but have not progressed to commercial production.
