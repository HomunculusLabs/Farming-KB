## Overview

Curcuma kwangsiensis is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Zingiberaceae, native to the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region of southern China and northern Vietnam. Its dried rhizome is an important crude drug in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), where it is known as *Gui E Zhu* (桂莪术). The species is closely related to *C. phaeocaulis* and *C. wenyujin*, all three being official sources of the TCM drug *E Zhu* (莪术).

The rhizomes contain significant concentrations of curcuminoids and sesquiterpenes, which contribute to their documented anti-inflammatory, antitumor, and hepatoprotective activities. *C. kwangsiensis* is distinguished from related species by its higher essential oil content and distinctive volatile composition. The plant is cultivated commercially in Guangxi and neighboring provinces.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Curcuma kwangsiensis S.G. Lee & C.F. Liang belongs to the order Zingiberales, family Zingiberaceae, subfamily Zingiberoideae, tribe Hedychieae. The species was first formally described in 1977 from specimens collected in Guangxi province. It is placed in the *Curcuma* subgenus *Curcuma*, section *Curcuma*.

The plant grows 50–120 cm tall with broad, lanceolate leaves 25–50 cm long and 8–15 cm wide. Leaves are glabrous to sparsely pubescent with a distinct central leaf vein. The inflorescence is a terminal spike 15–25 cm long with green bracts tipped in pink or purple. Flowers are yellow with a labellum marked in darker yellow or orange.

Rhizomes are fleshy, ovoid to cylindrical, 3–8 cm in diameter, with a pale yellow to orange interior. They produce both fleshy mother rhizomes and numerous fibrous secondary roots bearing small ellipsoid tubers. The rhizome aroma is camphoraceous and spicy, characteristic of the *E Zhu* drug group.

## Distribution and Habitat

Curcuma kwangsiensis is endemic to the limestone karst regions of southern China, primarily Guangxi province, with disjunct populations in southern Yunnan and Guizhou. It also occurs in northern Vietnam along the Chinese border. The species grows in subtropical monsoon forests at elevations of 200–800 meters.

The plant prefers well-drained, slightly acidic soils derived from limestone parent material. Natural populations are found in forest understories, along stream banks, and in disturbed secondary growth areas. Wild populations have declined due to overharvesting and habitat loss, though the species is maintained through commercial cultivation.

## Cultivation

Commercial cultivation of *C. kwangsiensis* is centered in Guangxi, particularly the counties of Yulin, Guiping, and Tengxian. Planting material consists of rhizome sections (seed pieces) with 2–3 buds, planted in prepared beds at 30–40 cm spacing during early spring (March–April).

The crop requires a 9–10 month growing period before harvest in December or January. Optimal growth occurs at 22–30°C with 1,200–1,800 mm annual rainfall and partial shade. Excessive sun exposure reduces rhizome quality. Yields of fresh rhizome range from 15–25 tonnes per hectare under commercial conditions.

Post-harvest processing involves washing, slicing, and drying the rhizomes. Traditional sun-drying takes 7–10 days; modern facilities use forced-air dryers at 50–60°C. The dried drug is graded by size, color, and essential oil content. The essential oil is extracted by steam distillation for pharmaceutical use.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The rhizome essential oil (1.5–4.0% dry weight) is dominated by **curdione** (20–35%), **curcumol** (5–15%), and **germacrone** (10–20%). These sesquiterpenes are characteristic markers for *C. kwangsiensis* and distinguish it from *C. phaeocaulis* (higher furanodiene) and *C. wenyujin* (higher germacrone). Other notable volatiles include **curzerene**, **neocurdione**, and **isocurcumenol**.

Curcuminoid content is moderate at 0.5–1.5% dry weight, lower than *C. longa* (turmeric) but pharmacologically significant. The three major curcuminoids — **curcumin**, **demethoxycurcumin**, and **bisdemethoxycurcumin** — contribute anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity.

Pharmacological studies demonstrate that *C. kwangsiensis* extracts exhibit antitumor activity through multiple mechanisms: inducing apoptosis in cancer cell lines, inhibiting angiogenesis, and modulating immune function. Curdione has shown specific activity against liver cancer cells (HepG2) and gastric cancer cells (SGC-7901) in vitro.

In TCM, *E Zhu* (the dried rhizome of *C. kwangsiensis* and related species) is classified as acrid, bitter, and warm. It is prescribed to promote blood circulation, remove blood stasis, alleviate pain, and stimulate appetite. Clinical applications include treatment of dysmenorrhea, abdominal masses, and post-surgical recovery.

## Traditional Use and Ethnobotany

In Guangxi folk medicine, fresh *C. kwangsiensis* rhizome is pounded and applied externally as a poultice for wounds, sprains, and inflammatory skin conditions. Internal preparations include decoctions for digestive complaints and powdered rhizome mixed with honey for respiratory ailments.

The species has been used in TCM for over 400 years, first recorded in the *Bencao Gangmu* supplementary texts of the Ming dynasty. Modern TCM pharmacopoeias distinguish it from other *E Zhu* sources based on its higher volatile oil content and different therapeutic emphasis on blood-activating properties.
