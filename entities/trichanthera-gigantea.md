The species is valued in tropical agroforestry and permaculture systems for its rapid growth, high leaf protein content, palatability to ruminants and monogastrics, and ability to produce biomass year-round.

It has become one of the most important multi-purpose fodder trees in smallholder farming systems across Latin America, [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-caribbean-central-america]] to Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia.

It occurs naturally in moist forest margins, riparian zones, and disturbed areas at elevations from 0 to 1,200 meters. It has been widely introduced beyond its native range throughout the tropics.

## Nutritional Value

*T. gigantea* foliage is notably high in crude protein, typically ranging from 18–24% on a dry matter basis in young leaves, comparable to many commercial concentrate feeds.

This protein content is maintained across seasons, unlike many tropical grasses that decline in quality during dry periods, making it an especially valuable dry-season supplement.

The leaves contain adequate levels of essential minerals including calcium (1.5–2.0% DM), phosphorus (0.2–0.3% DM), and magnesium (0.3–0.4% DM).

The fiber fraction is moderate, with neutral detergent fiber (NDF) of 35–45% and acid detergent fiber (ADF) of 25–30%, supporting good intake in ruminants.

In vitro dry matter digestibility ranges from 65–75%, and in vivo trials with cattle, goats, pigs, and rabbits have shown favorable intake and nutrient utilization.

The foliage contains low levels of anti-nutritional factors compared to many tropical leguminous fodder trees, though mild condensed tannin concentrations of 1–2% have been reported.

## Cultivation and Management

Propagation is primarily through stem cuttings of 20–40 cm length, which root readily in moist soil within 2–3 weeks. [[green-cannabis-grow-bible-cloning-and-seed-propagation]] is possible but rarely used due to low viability and slow germination.

Establishment spacing of 1.0–1.5 m × 1.0–1.5 m is recommended for dedicated fodder banks, yielding approximately 30,000–40,000 plants per hectare.

First harvest occurs 4–6 months after planting, with subsequent cutting intervals of 60–90 days depending on rainfall and management intensity.

Annual dry matter yields range from 10–25 tonnes per hectare depending on soil fertility, rainfall, and cutting regime. The plant coppices vigorously and maintains productivity for 8–15 years.

Fertilization with organic manure or moderate NPK application significantly improves yield, particularly on degraded soils where the species is often planted for rehabilitation.
