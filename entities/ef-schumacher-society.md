# E. F. Schumacher Society

## Description

The E. F. Schumacher Society, based in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, USA, is a research
and advocacy organization dedicated to promoting decentralized, human-scale economic systems.
Named after economist E. F. Schumacher (author of *Small Is Beautiful*), the society maintains
data on local currency systems and operates its own community currency. [[bill-mollison]] references
the society in *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual* as a key resource for communities seeking
to establish local money systems and take control of their economic destiny.

## Classification

- **Category:** Research and advocacy organization
- **Domain:** Permaculture -- [[informal-and-formal-community-economics]], local currency systems
- **Location:** Great Barrington, Massachusetts, USA
- **Namesake:** E. F. (Ernst Friedrich) Schumacher (1911-1977), German-British economist
- **Function:** Research, data collection, advocacy, and operation of local currency systems

## Core Activities

1. **Local Currency Data:** Maintains comprehensive data on community currency systems,
  their design, implementation, and outcomes
2. **Currency Operations:** Runs its own local currency, providing a live demonstration
  model for other communities
3. **Research:** Studies the [[microdosing-theory-and-practice]] of decentralized economics, bioregional
  finance, and community self-reliance
4. **Advocacy:** Promotes the adoption of local currency and community banking systems
5. **Education:** Provides resources, publications, and guidance to communities
   establishing their own economic systems
6. **Networking:** Connects communities and practitioners working on local economic systems

## Connection to E. F. Schumacher

The society is named for Ernst Friedrich Schumacher, whose influential 1973 book *Small Is
Beautiful: Economics as if People Mattered* argued that:
- Economics should serve human needs, not the reverse
- Small-scale, [[appropriate-technology]] outperforms megascale systems
- Local self-reliance builds more resilient economies than globalization
- Natural resources are finite and must be treated as capital, not income
- The health of an economy should be measured by the health of its communities

## Key Contributions

- Demonstrates that local currency systems are practical, not just theoretical
- Provides empirical data on what works and what does not in community currency design
- Serves as a living laboratory for bioregional economic principles
- Connects [[permaculture-principles]] of local self-reliance with concrete financial tools
- Bridges the gap between economic theory and community practice

## Philosophy

The society's work aligns closely with [[permaculture-ethics]]:

- **Care for the Earth:** Local economies reduce transport, pollution, and resource depletion
- **Care for People:** Human-scale economics prioritizes community wellbeing over profit
- **Fair Share:** Local currency prevents wealth concentration by keeping money circulating
  within the community

  through economic crises, ensuring cultivators always have a market
- **Knowledge Commons:** The society's research model supports open sharing of cultivation
  knowledge, mycological research, and growing techniques as common resources

## Currency Design Principles (from Schumacher Society research)

For a local currency to be valid and functional:

1. **Backed by Real Resources:** Must be redeemable against a tangible local asset
2. **Community Confidence:** People must trust the currency, backed by transparent reserves
3. **Demand:** There must be genuine need for local goods and services purchasable with it
4. **Exchange Mechanism:** Must be convertible to national currency at published rates
5. **Anti-Hoarding Design:** Notes can be dated with periodic reissue to prevent accumulation
6. **Conservative Issuance:** Currency issued should not exceed reserve value by more than 3x
7. **Living Reserves:** Where reserves are living things (forests, biological stocks), wealth
   naturally increases over time

## Implementation Guidance

- Start with a real, pledged resource as reserve (timber, water, stored grain)
- Print currency on local material, difficult to forge, numbered and dated
- Educate local businesses and banks in accepting and exchanging the currency
- Lend to builders and producers who create real community value
- Use currency for community needs: food, shelter, trade
- Producers of local goods accept only local currency, creating demand
