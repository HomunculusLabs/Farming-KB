## Description

Nematode-trapping fungi (also called nematophagous or predaceous fungi) are a polyphyletic group of fungi that have evolved specialized structures to capture, kill, and consume nematodes (roundworms). These fungi play important ecological roles as predators in soil food webs and have attracted significant attention as potential biocontrol agents against plant-parasitic nematodes that cause major agricultural losses worldwide. They employ diverse trapping mechanisms ranging from adhesive nets and constricting rings to specialized adhesive spores.

## Classification

- **Type**: Polyphyletic functional group (not a single taxonomic clade)
- **Major genera**: Arthrobotrys, Dactylellina, Drechslerella, Monacrosporium, Duddingtonia, Stylopage, Nematoctonus
- **Taxonomic distribution**: Primarily Ascomycota (Orbiliales) and some Basidiomycota (Pleurotaceae)
- **[[ecological-niche]]**: Soil-dwelling predatory fungi

## Key Facts

- Nematode-trapping fungi have evolved at least five distinct types of trapping structures: adhesive networks, adhesive knobs, constricting rings (non-constricting rings), adhesive branches, and stephanocysts.
- The constricting ring is the most sophisticated trapping device: when a nematode enters the three-celled ring, the cells rapidly inflate (within 0.1 seconds) to constrict and capture the prey.
- Trapping structures are typically induced by the presence of nematodes or nematode-derived compounds (e.g., nematode pheromones such as ascarosides), demonstrating a predator-prey signaling system.
- After capture, the fungus penetrates the nematode cuticle using a combination of mechanical pressure and enzymatic degradation, then forms trophic hyphae inside the body to digest and absorb nutrients.
- In "Fungi in the Environment" (Chapter 12 by Anders Tunlid), DNA microarray technology is used to explore the molecular basis of the interaction between nematode-trapping fungi and their prey, revealing genes involved in trap formation, adhesion, and virulence.
- These fungi are found in virtually all soil types, with higher abundance in organic-rich soils and environments with high nematode populations.
- Some species (e.g., Duddingtonia flagrans) produce thick-walled chlamydospores that survive passage through the digestive tracts of grazing animals, making them ideal for controlling parasitic nematodes in livestock.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Nematode-trapping fungi are among the most promising biocontrol agents for managing plant-parasitic nematodes (Meloidogyne, Globodera, Heterodera) that devastate crops including tomatoes, potatoes, and carrots.
- Duddingtonia flagrans has been commercialized for biological control of gastrointestinal nematodes in livestock, with applications in pasture management.
- In mushroom cultivation, certain nematode species (e.g., Aphelenchoides composticola) can damage mycelium and reduce yields; nemode-trapping fungi offer a potential biological management strategy.
- Understanding the ecology of these fungi in compost and casing layers could inform cultivation practices that naturally suppress nematode pests.
- Research on nematode-trapping fungi contributes to broader knowledge of fungal morphogenesis, as trap formation involves rapid cellular differentiation in response to environmental cues.
- The predatory habit demonstrates the remarkable ecological versatility of fungi and their importance in [[soil-food-web]] regulation.
