# Scutellospora pellucida

## Description

*Scutellospora pellucida* is a species of arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungus in the genus *Scutellospora*, suborder Gigasporineae, order Glomales. It is known from sand dune habitats where it can exhibit highly aggregated spatial distributions—occurring abundantly in single samples while being absent from others at the same site. This patchy distribution has made it a reference species in discussions of statistical methods for comparing AM fungal species abundances.

## Classification

- **[[kingdom-fungi]]
- **Phylum:** Zygomycota / Glomeromycota
- **Order:** Glomales
- **Suborder:** Gigasporineae
- **Family:** Gigasporaceae
- **Genus:** *Scutellospora*
- **Species:** *S. pellucida*

## Key Facts

- In a study of 11 sand dune soil samples (Koske, unpublished data), spores of *S. pellucida* were found in only one sample but were abundant there
- In the same study, *S. calospora* occurred in much lower numbers but in five of the 11 samples
- Despite the apparent dominance of *S. pellucida* based on average spore abundance, statistical tests revealed no significant difference in abundance between the two species
- This example demonstrates that frequency of occurrence may be more informative than abundance alone when dealing with rare or highly aggregated species
- Member of a genus with 28 described species characterized by bilayered spore walls
- The species name "pellucida" (translucent) refers to the relatively clear appearance of the spore wall

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

*Scutellospora pellucida* is significant for methodological and statistical aspects of AM fungal ecology:

- Serves as a textbook example of why both abundance and frequency of occurrence must be combined when calculating importance values (IV) for AM fungal species
- Illustrates the highly aggregated distribution of AM fungal spores in soil, a fundamental challenge in sampling design
- Demonstrates that rare species can be numerically dominant in individual samples while being absent from most of the habitat
- Relevant to discussions of sampling adequacy—large numbers of samples may be needed to detect rare but locally abundant species
- Highlights the limitations of standard diversity indices borrowed from plant ecology when applied to spore count data
