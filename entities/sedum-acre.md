# Sedum acre

**Sedum acre** (biting stonecrop, goldmoss stonecrop, wallpepper) is a low-growing, mat-forming succulent in the family Crassulaceae. It is native to Europe and parts of northern Africa and western Asia.

The species is widely recognized for its bright yellow star-shaped flowers and its acrid, peppery-tasting foliage. It has been used in traditional European folk medicine as a counter-irritant and topical remedy.

Despite its ornamental appeal, S. acre contains toxic piperidine alkaloids that cause skin irritation and gastrointestinal distress if ingested. It is classified as an invasive species in parts of North America and Australia.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Sedum acre was described by Carl Linnaeus in Species Plantarum (1753). The specific epithet "acre" means bitter or pungent, referring to the acrid taste of the leaves.

The species belongs to the subfamily Sedoideae within Crassulaceae.

Molecular phylogenetic studies place it in a clade with S. sexangulare and S. album, all sharing similar floral morphology and alkaloid chemistry. No significant infraspecific taxa are currently recognized, though several cultivars exist in the horticultural trade.

Plants form dense mats of creeping stems reaching 5–10 cm in height. Leaves are fleshy, cylindrical to ovoid, 4–8 mm long, arranged in alternate or whorled clusters along the stems.

The bright yellow flowers are 5-merous, 10–12 mm in diameter, with acute petals. They are borne in terminal cymes containing 3–6 flowers each.

Flowering occurs from June through August. The fruit is a follicle containing numerous small seeds adapted to wind dispersal.

## Distribution and Habitat

S. acre is native to most of Europe, extending eastward to western Siberia and southward into northern Africa. It has been widely introduced in North America, where it is naturalized across much of the northeastern United States and Canada.

The species colonizes rocky outcrops, thin sandy soils, walls, and gravelly banks. It is highly tolerant of drought, poor soils, and full sun exposure.

It thrives on calcareous substrates and is frequently associated with limestone pavements. However, it is equally adaptable to acidic siliceous rocks and anthropogenic habitats such as brick walls and concrete.

It is commonly found in coastal cliff communities, inland limestone pavements, and old stone walls. In urban settings, it frequently appears on rooftops, between paving stones, and along railway embankments.

In parts of the Great Lakes region and the Pacific Northwest, S. acre is considered invasive. It can outcompete native rock-outcrop vegetation and alter soil chemistry through its persistent leaf litter.

## Cultivation

Sedum acre is among the easiest succulents to grow and is widely used in rock gardens, green roofs, and groundcover plantings. It is hardy to USDA Zone 3.

Propagation is readily achieved through stem cuttings, division, or seed. Cuttings root within 2–3 weeks when placed on moist, well-drained substrate in bright indirect light.

Seeds are tiny and require light for germination. They are typically surface-sown on a sandy medium and kept at 15–20°C. Germination occurs within 2–4 weeks under optimal conditions.

Division of established mats is the most reliable propagation method for garden use. Each division should include roots and several rooted stems to ensure rapid reestablishment.

The species requires excellent drainage and tolerates nutrient-poor substrates. Overwatering or heavy clay soils promote crown rot and fungal pathogens.

It performs best in full sun but tolerates partial shade. Shade conditions produce elongated, less compact growth and reduced flowering.

In green roof applications, S. acre is valued for its extreme drought tolerance and shallow root system. It requires virtually no maintenance once established.

## Bioactive Compounds

S. acre contains piperidine alkaloids as its primary bioactive constituents. The most notable compounds include sedridine, sedamine, and sedinone.

Total alkaloid content ranges from 0.03–0.15% of dry weight depending on growing conditions and harvest timing. Concentrations peak during flowering and decline after seed set.

The alkaloid profile of S. acre is distinct from other Sedum species, which typically accumulate flavonoids and phenolic acids rather than piperidine derivatives. This chemical signature aids in taxonomic identification.

These alkaloids are concentrated in the leaves and stems. They are responsible for the characteristic acrid, peppery taste and the irritating properties of the plant.

Contact with the fresh plant sap can cause dermatitis, erythema, and blistering in sensitive individuals. This has led to its historical use as a rubefacient and vesicant in European folk medicine.

Internal ingestion causes nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and in severe cases, cardiac arrhythmias. The toxic dose in humans is relatively high due to low alkaloid concentrations.

Animal poisoning has been documented in livestock, particularly sheep and cattle grazing on overgrazed pastures where S. acre dominates. Symptoms include salivation, bloat, and diarrhea.

Poultry are relatively resistant due to their limited intake of the plant. Rabbits and guinea pigs, however, are highly susceptible and should be kept away from enclosures containing S. acre.

Traditional applications included topical treatment of warts, corns, and skin ulcers. These uses are not supported by modern clinical evidence and carry significant risk of chemical burns.

## Ecological Role

The flowers of S. acre attract a range of pollinators, including bees, flies, and small beetles. The prolonged bloom period provides a reliable nectar source in rocky, exposed habitats.

Honey bees (Apis mellifera) are frequent visitors, though the nectar yield per flower is low due to the small floral size. The species is not considered a significant honey source.

The dense mat-forming growth stabilizes thin soils on rocky substrates and steep slopes. This makes it ecologically significant in primary succession on gravel and scree slopes.

Several species of aphids and mealybugs feed on Sedum species. In return, predatory insects including ladybirds and lacewings are attracted to these colonies.

The persistent leaf litter contributes organic matter to nutrient-poor substrates. Decomposition rates are slow due to the succulent tissues, creating a gradual soil-building process.

In its introduced range, S. acre can form monocultures that exclude native pioneer species. This is most problematic on rock outcrops and alvar communities with limited native flora.

Several cultivars are available in the nursery trade, selected for flower size or growth habit. Notable selections include 'Aureum', with brighter yellow flowers, and 'Minus', a compact dwarf form.

The species has been investigated for potential phytoremediation applications. Preliminary studies suggest limited capacity for heavy metal accumulation in contaminated soils, though results are inconsistent.

In traditional European herbalism, the plant was associated with the doctrine of signatures. Its yellow flowers were believed to indicate efficacy for jaundice and liver complaints, an association without pharmacological basis.
