## Overview

**Drosera adelae**, commonly known as the **lance-leaved sundew**, is a perennial carnivorous plant endemic to the Rockingham Bay region of northeastern Queensland, Australia. It is one of three closely related Australian Drosera species informally called the "Three Sisters."

The species is distinctive for its elongated, lance-shaped leaves covered in glandular tentacles. Unlike many sundews, it grows as a rosette and does not produce an erect stem.

*D. adelae* is considered one of the easiest sundews to cultivate indoors. Its rapid growth and prolific vegetative reproduction make it popular among beginning carnivorous plant growers.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Caryophyllales → Droseraceae → *Drosera* → *D. adelae*.

**Authority:** F.Muell. (1864).

The species belongs to *Drosera* section *Lanceolatae*, alongside *D. schizandra* and *D. prolifera*. These three species share a Queensland distribution and similar ecological niches.

Leaves are linear-lanceolate, 10–25 cm long and 0.5–1 cm wide. They arise from a central rosette and may be erect, ascending, or decumbent depending on light conditions.

The root system is relatively shallow and fibrous. In cultivation, the species frequently produces adventitious roots at nodes along creeping stems.

The species requires high humidity and consistent moisture year-round. It is often found growing on steep embankments above flowing water, where humidity remains elevated.

Climate in its native range is tropical, with warm temperatures (22–30 °C) and high annual rainfall exceeding 3,000 mm. The species does not experience a significant dry season in situ.

The species is listed as vulnerable under Queensland legislation due to its restricted natural range. Habitat clearing for agriculture and urban development have reduced available habitat.

Conservation efforts focus on protecting remaining populations within national parks and reserves. The species remains secure in several protected areas within the Wet Tropics World Heritage Area.

## Carnivory and Prey Capture

Like all Drosera species, *D. adelae* captures prey using sticky mucilage secreted by glandular tentacles on its leaf surface. Insects are trapped upon contact with the adhesive droplets.

The tentacles exhibit thigmonastic movement, slowly bending toward the captured prey within minutes. This motion brings additional glands into contact with the prey, improving adhesion and digestion.

The bending response is triggered by the release of auxin-like hormones from stimulated glands. This mechanism is shared across the genus but is particularly pronounced in species with long, mobile tentacles.

Digestive enzymes are secreted directly onto the trapped insect. The leaf may also fold inward along its long axis to further envelop larger prey items, though this response is slower than in some other sundews.

Common prey includes fungus gnats, small flies, and ants. The species is particularly effective at capturing small flying insects in humid, sheltered environments.

The tentacle mucilage contains polysaccharides and proteolytic enzymes. Research indicates the adhesive properties are primarily due to a complex mixture of arabinogalactan proteins.

Nutrient absorption studies show that *D. adelae* derives a significant portion of its nitrogen from captured prey. Leaves without prey access show reduced growth rates and chlorosis over time.

Temperatures between 18–30 °C are suitable, with higher humidity preferred. The species does not require a dormancy period and grows year-round under favorable conditions.
