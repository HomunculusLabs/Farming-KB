## Description

The Ifugao rice terraces are an ancient wet-terrace agricultural system located in the mountainous Cordillera region of Luzon, Philippines. Maintained by the Ifugao people for at least 3,000 years, these terraces represent one of the oldest continuously operated agricultural systems on Earth. Harold Conklin, in his ethnographic study published in Scientific American (February 1982), calculated that approximately 1,000 years of collective labor created the terrace infrastructure that exists today.

The system demonstrates extraordinary sustainability through self-governance, operating without any external authority. The Ifugao manage complex shared irrigation and canal systems through community organization and mutual obligation, providing a model that Mollison cites as superior to any centralized governance system ever developed.

## Classification

- **Type**: Traditional agricultural terrace system
- **Location**: Cordillera Mountains, Luzon, Philippines
- **Elevation**: Mountainous terrain with extreme rainfall
- **Age**: 3,000+ years of continuous use
- **Cultural group**: Ifugao people (indigenous to the Cordillera)
- **Primary crops**: Wet rice (padi), sweet potato
- **Associated researcher**: Harold Conklin (ethnographer, Yale University)

## Key Facts

### Physical System Design
- Wet terraces with approximately 2 cm of productive soil layer
- Half the mass of bunds (terrace walls) consists of stone
- Annual rainfall: 3 to 6 meters per year (extreme, necessitating constant maintenance)
- Complex canal and ditch systems require annual earth and stone repairs
- Labor exchange value: one day of rice for one hour of work
- Family of 5 spends 400 person-days of productive labor per year (80 days per able individual)

### Land Allocation Per Family
- 0.5 hectares of padi rice (primary staple grain)
- 0.25 hectares of sweet potato (supplementary staple)
- 1 hectare of woodlot and grove crop (fuel, construction materials, animal forage)
- Domestic pigs and chickens fed entirely from crop gleanings and residues
- Integrated fish culture in terrace ponds (traditional practice)

### Self-Governance and Social Organization
- The Ifugao recognize no external political authority
- Construction and maintenance of common canal systems and shared irrigation infrastructure necessitates community organization and self-control
- Mollison notes this parallels Fox's (1977) observations on Indonesian palm cultures
- Demonstrates that self-sustained systems develop self-governance never achieved by centralized authority
- Water is regarded as a public resource in all democratic, self-organized societies
- Hydraulic despotism, as found in colonized areas, predisposes societies to exploitation

### Sustainability Achievements
- Continuous production for over 3,000 years without soil degradation
- Integrated livestock (pigs, chickens) convert crop residues into manure for terrace fertility
- No reliance on external inputs, synthetic fertilizers, or biocides
- Community-based water management adapted to extreme rainfall conditions
- Stone and earth construction engineered specifically for 3-6 m annual rainfall
- Complex polyculture including fish in terrace ponds and diverse margin plantings

### Comparison With Other Ancient Water Systems
- **Nile flood culture**: Approximately 17,000 years old (sadly but perhaps temporarily ended by the Aswan Dam)
- **Chinampa system (Mexico)**: 2,000+ years old, similar land-water integration
- **Asian flood-plain and lakeside cultures**: Comparable antiquity and sustainability
- **Swiss trout canals (Huet)**: Modern canal culture with 40-60% yield advantage over ponds

## Relevance to Cultivation

The Ifugao terraces are a masterclass in sustainable terrace agriculture that directly informs modern permaculture design. Key principles demonstrated include:

1. **Edge maximization**: Terraces create maximum productive edge between land and water, increasing biological productivity
2. **Closed nutrient cycling**: Pigs and chickens convert crop residues into manure, maintaining terrace fertility without external inputs
3. **Community-scale infrastructure**: Shared canals and ditches require cooperative management, demonstrating how common resources can be sustainably managed
4. **Climate-appropriate design**: Stone bunds and drainage systems engineered specifically for 3-6 m annual rainfall
5. **Minimal soil, maximum production**: Only 2 cm of soil yet sustained productivity for millennia through careful management
6. **Labor efficiency**: 80 days per person per year achieves complete food self-sufficiency

The system's longevity proves that properly designed water-management agriculture can be permanently sustainable. This stands in stark contrast to modern industrial agriculture, which degrades soils within decades. The labor economics compare favorably with industrial systems while producing zero pollution, zero soil erosion, and zero ecological degradation.

For mycology and specialty cultivation, the Ifugao model of humid terraced growing with integrated livestock and aquatic production provides a template for incorporating mushroom cultivation zones within terrace systems. The traditional practice of inoculating logs in upper terraces for fungal production (documented by Conklin) demonstrates that mycological production has long been part of these integrated systems.
