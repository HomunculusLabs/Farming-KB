## Overview

Echeveria agavoides is a compact stemless succulent in the family Crassulaceae, native to the semi-arid highlands of central Mexico. It is widely known as the "lipstick echeveria" or "Molded Wax Plant" for its sharply pointed, glossy green leaves tipped with red.

The species is among the most popular ornamental Echeveria in global horticulture, prized for its symmetrical rosette form and minimal maintenance needs. It has earned the Royal Horticultural Society"s Award of Garden Merit for reliable garden performance.

Echeveria agavoides was introduced to European horticulture in the mid-nineteenth century and has remained in continuous cultivation since. Its durability and ease of propagation have made it one of the most widely grown succulents worldwide.

The species is valued by breeders as a parent in Echeveria hybridization programs. Its compact habit and stress-responsive coloration are traits commonly sought in hybrid offspring.

In its native Mexico, the plant is sometimes called "cona" or "siempreverde" in local communities. These common names reflect its evergreen, persistent rosette that remains attractive year-round.

## Taxonomy and Description

Echeveria agavoides was described by Charles Lemaire in 1862 and belongs to series Urbiniae within the genus. The specific epithet "agavoides" refers to its resemblance to plants in the genus Agave, particularly the sharply acute leaf tips.

The plant forms dense, stemless rosettes typically 10–15 cm in diameter but reaching 20 cm at maturity. Leaves are triangular-ovate, glabrous, and highly succulent, colored pale to mid-green with prominent red tips and margins under strong light.

Each rosette contains 25–35 leaves arranged in a tight spiral. The leaves measure 3–7 cm long and 2–4 cm wide, with a smooth, waxy cuticle that gives them a glossy appearance.

The leaf tips terminate in a small, sharp mucro that can be prickly to the touch. This terminal spine is the primary distinguishing feature separating the species from rounder-leaved Echeveria in the same series.

Inflorescences emerge as arching cymes 20–30 cm tall, bearing urn-shaped flowers with pinkish-red petals tipped in yellow. Each inflorescence produces 5–12 flowers opening sequentially over two to three weeks.

Flowering occurs primarily in late spring through early summer, triggered by lengthening days and warm temperatures. The flowers are hummingbird-pollinated in habitat.

Chromosome number is 2n = 54, consistent with many polyploid members of series Urbiniae. This high ploidy level contributes to the species' genetic stability and vigor in cultivation.

The roots are fibrous and shallow, adapted to exploiting brief moisture in thin soil pockets. Plants produce abundant fine root hairs that dry and regenerate with each wetting cycle.

Rosettes that reach maturity may produce offsets at the base, forming small clumps over several years. In cultivation, these offsets can be separated and rooted independently to produce genetically identical plants.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is endemic to the Mexican states of México, Puebla, and Oaxaca, growing at elevations between 1,200 and 2,400 meters. It inhabits rocky outcrops, volcanic cliffs, and well-drained slopes in oak-pine woodlands.

Plants grow lithophytically in thin soil pockets on exposed rock faces, often in partial shade of scattered shrubs. The substrate is typically volcanic in origin, providing excellent drainage and mineral-rich conditions.

Annual rainfall in its native range averages 500–700 mm, concentrated in a summer monsoon season from June through September. The dry season lasts six to eight months, during which the plant survives on stored leaf moisture.

Winter temperatures in its habitat can drop to near freezing, giving the species greater cold tolerance than many tropical Echeveria. This hardiness makes it suitable for outdoor cultivation in USDA zones 9b–11.

The species is not considered threatened, though habitat degradation from agricultural expansion and over-collection has reduced some wild populations. Several populations occur within protected areas in the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt.

Populations at higher elevations tend to exhibit more compact growth and deeper red coloration than those at lower, warmer sites. This altitudinal variation has contributed to confusion in herbarium collections and the description of several now-synonymized forms.

## Cultivation

Echeveria agavoides requires well-drained, gritty soil with a pH of 6.0–7.0 and tolerates standard cactus potting mixes without issue. A mix of one part potting soil to two parts perlite or pumice provides ideal drainage.

Overwatering is the primary cause of loss; the plant prefers to dry completely between waterings. During active growth from spring through autumn, water every 7–10 days, reducing to monthly in winter dormancy.

Full sun to bright partial shade produces the best leaf coloration, with red tip intensity increasing under stronger light. Plants grown in insufficient light lose their compact form and develop elongated, pale green foliage.

Propagation is straightforward by leaf cuttings, stem offsets, or seed. Leaf cuttings root within two to three weeks at temperatures of 18–24°C, making the species exceptionally easy to reproduce in large quantities.

Seed propagation is less common but produces genetically variable offspring. Fresh seed germinates readily at 20–25°C on the soil surface without covering, typically within 7–14 days.

Mealybugs and root rot from overwatering are the most common problems. Aphids may attack flower buds in spring, and fungus gnat larvae can damage roots in persistently damp media.

Outdoor plantings in Mediterranean climates are largely pest-free. Snails and slugs may feed on young growth in damp conditions but rarely threaten established plants.

The species benefits from a winter rest period with reduced watering and temperatures of 5–10°C, which promotes flowering the following spring. Plants grown year-round in warm conditions may flower sporadically or not at all.

## Varieties and Similar Species

Several cultivars have been selected for enhanced coloration and form. 'Lipstick' features vivid red leaf margins and tips, while 'Ebony' displays dark red to nearly black marginal coloration under stress.

'Frank Reinelt' is a named selection with particularly compact growth and deep pinkish-red tips. 'Corderoyi' is an older cultivar with broader, shorter leaves than the typical form.

The cultivar 'Miranda' is notable for its large rosettes reaching 25 cm across with pronounced red leaf margins. 'Romeo' produces deep burgundy foliage under full sun exposure.

Variegated forms exist in cultivation but are rare and command high prices among collectors. These chimeric mutants display creamy white or yellow striping but are generally less vigorous than non-variegated plants.

Echeveria agavoides is sometimes confused with Echeveria elegans, which has narrower, more pointed leaves without red tips. It also resembles compact Agave species but differs in its herbaceous, non-fibrous leaf texture.

## Uses

The primary use of Echeveria agavoides is ornamental, both as a container specimen and in rock garden plantings. Its symmetrical form and stress coloration make it a staple in succulent dish gardens and living wall arrangements.

The species is used extensively in xeriscaping in Mediterranean and arid-climate landscapes worldwide. It tolerates prolonged drought once established and requires no supplemental irrigation in suitable climates.

Container-grown specimens benefit from terracotta pots, which allow faster soil drying than glazed ceramic or plastic containers. Unglazed clay also provides root aeration and reduces the risk of basal rot.

Echeveria agavoides is popular in bridal bouquets and floral arrangements for its rosette shape and long-lasting cut quality. The leaves remain turgid and attractive for several days without water.

In Mexico, Echeveria rosettes are occasionally used in traditional decorative arrangements. The species has no significant ethnobotanical or medicinal use, though some Crassulaceae relatives have documented folk-medicine applications.

Like many Crassulaceae, Echeveria agavoides employs CAM (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism) photosynthesis. Stomata open at night to absorb carbon dioxide, minimizing water loss during hot daylight hours.

This photosynthetic adaptation allows the plant to thrive in environments with extreme daytime temperatures and limited water availability. Understanding CAM physiology is important for proper cultivation timing of watering and fertilization.
