## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Cucurbitales
- **Family:** Cucurbitaceae
- **Genus:** *Benincasa* Savi
- **Species:** *B. hispida* (Thunb.) Cogn.

*Benincasa* is a monotypic genus, making *B. hispida* its sole species.

The species has no widely recognized synonyms in current use, though historical taxonomic literature occasionally refers to it as *Cucurbita hispida* Thunb. or *Benincasa cerifera* Savi.

## Morphology

The plant is a trailing or climbing annual vine reaching 3–5 meters in length, with angular stems covered in coarse hairs.

Leaves are large (15–25 cm), broadly ovate to cordate, with 5–7 shallow lobes, a hairy upper surface, and a rough texture.

Flowers are yellow, monoecious, with male flowers in racemes and solitary female flowers. The calyx is densely hispid, giving the species its epithet.

The fruit is an oblong to cylindrical pepo, 20–60 cm long and 10–30 cm in diameter, weighing 2–20 kg. Immature fruit is densely hairy; mature fruit develops a white waxy bloom.

Seeds are oval, flat, white, approximately 1 cm long, embedded in white spongy flesh.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Benincasa hispida* is believed to have originated in tropical Asia, with wild progenitors in the region spanning India to Japan.

Cultivation extends across South Asia (India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka), [[query-how-to-grow-rare-tropical-fruits-in-marginal-and-subtropical-climates]] at elevations up to 1500 meters, requiring mean temperatures of 20–30°C for optimal growth.

It performs best in well-drained sandy loam to loam soils with pH 6.0–7.0 and moderate fertility, though it tolerates poorer soils better than many cucurbits.

## Nutritional Value

The flesh of mature *B. hispida* is approximately 96% water, making it one of the lowest-calorie vegetables available, with approximately 13 kcal per 100 g of raw flesh.

It provides modest amounts of vitamin C (8–10 mg/100 g), potassium (140–180 mg/100 g), and small quantities of calcium, phosphorus, and B vitamins.

The seeds are more nutrient-dense, containing approximately 30% protein, 45% fat (rich in linoleic acid), and significant amounts of vitamin E and minerals including iron and zinc.

Dietary fiber content is moderate in flesh (2–3 g/100 g) and higher in the rind and seeds.

## Culinary and Medicinal Use

In Chinese cuisine, winter melon is used in soups (notably winter melon soup with pork or dried shrimp), stir-fries, and in the preparation of candied strips known as "donggua tiao" and the traditional sweet "winter melon cake."

In Indian cuisine, the immature fruit is prepared as curries (petha in North India), stews, and in the sweet confection "petha" from Agra.

In Ayurvedic medicine, the fruit is classified as cooling, diuretic, and digestive, prescribed for urinary disorders, digestive complaints, and as a general tonic for convalescence.

Pharmacological studies have demonstrated hypoglycemic activity in diabetic rat models, with the fruit extract reducing blood glucose levels through enhanced insulin secretion and peripheral glucose uptake.

Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties have been documented in the fruit pulp and seeds, attributed to triterpenoids, flavonoids, and [[fukuoka-citrus-orchard-ground-cover-cultivation]] in tropical food forests, suppressing weeds and reducing soil moisture loss through evaporation.
