Common frogs are among the first amphibians to breed in spring, often beginning while ice still partially covers breeding ponds. This early breeding strategy gives their tadpoles a head start before newt and dragonfly predators become active.

Winter hibernation typically occurs on land, under logs, stones, or in [[hamilton-garden-ponds-and-aquatic-plants]].

Common frogs can tolerate moderately acidic conditions, with successful breeding documented at pH levels as low as 4.0. This tolerance allows them to exploit habitats avoided by more sensitive amphibian species.

## Ecology and Life Cycle

Common frogs emerge from hibernation in late winter or [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]], often when temperatures exceed 5°C. Males arrive at breeding sites before females and begin calling to attract mates.

The male's advertisement call is a low, growling croak, quieter than that of many other frog species. Breeding activity peaks between March and May depending on latitude and elevation.

Females lay 1,000–4,000 eggs in gelatinous clumps (frogspawn) in shallow water. Each egg is 2–3 mm in diameter, surrounded by a protective jelly coat that swells on contact with water.

Tadpoles hatch after 2–4 weeks and undergo metamorphosis over approximately 12–16 weeks. Metamorphs leave the water as miniature froglets, measuring roughly 10–15 mm in length.

Sexual maturity is reached at 2–4 years of age. Adults feed primarily on invertebrates, including insects, spiders, slugs, and earthworms, which they capture with a sticky tongue.

In captivity, common frogs have been documented living up to 12 years, though wild individuals typically survive 5–8 years. Annual mortality is highest during the tadpole stage, where predation and pond desiccation are major causes of death.

Common frogs are preyed upon by herons, owls, grass snakes, and various mammalian predators. Their primary defense is rapid leaping, supplemented by skin secretions that are mildly distasteful.

Tadpoles are primarily herbivorous, feeding on algae and aquatic vegetation. As development progresses, they become increasingly omnivorous, consuming small invertebrates and detritus.
