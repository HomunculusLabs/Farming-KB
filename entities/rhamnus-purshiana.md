# Rhamnus purshiana

## Overview

*Rhamnus purshiana* (cascara sagrada, sacred bark) is a deciduous shrub or small tree in the family Rhamnaceae, native to the Pacific Northwest of North America.

It is the botanical source of cascara, one of the most widely used herbal laxatives in Western pharmacopeias for over a century. The dried, aged bark contains anthraquinone glycosides that stimulate colonic motility.

The species was named in honor of Frederick Traugott Pursh, the German-American botanist who first described it in 1813 from specimens collected in the Pacific Northwest.

## Taxonomy

**Kingdom:** Plantae

**Division:** Magnoliophyta

**Class:** Magnoliopsida

**Order:** Rhamnales

**Family:** Rhamnaceae

**Genus:** Rhamnus

**Species:** R. purshiana

**Authority:** DC. (1805)

**Synonyms:** *Frangula purshiana* (DC.) Cooper, *Rhamnus purshianus* DC.

Modern taxonomic treatments often place this species in *Frangula*, though the name *Rhamnus purshiana* remains prevalent in pharmacopeial and herbal literature.

## Morphology

**Habit:** Deciduous shrub to small tree, 4–10 m tall, with a slender trunk and spreading crown. Bark is thin, smooth, and silvery-gray on young branches, becoming darker and fissured with age.

**Leaves:** Alternate, simple, elliptic to oblong-elliptic, 5–15 cm long, 2–5 cm wide, finely serrate margins, prominent parallel lateral veins, dark green and glossy above, paler beneath.

**Flowers:** Small, greenish-white, dioecious, borne in axillary umbel-like clusters of 2–5. Petals 5, reflexed. Staminate flowers have 5 stamens; pistillate flowers have a superior 3-4 chambered ovary.

**Fruit:** Drupaceous, 6–8 mm diameter, initially red, ripening to deep blue-black or purplish-black. Each fruit contains 2–3 seeds. Fruits are mildly laxative but less potent than the bark.

**Bark:** The medicinal bark is collected from the trunk and larger branches. Fresh bark is strongly emetic — it must be aged for at least one year before use to allow oxidative degradation of the irritating anthrone constituents.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to western North America, from British Columbia south through Washington, Oregon, and California, with populations extending into Idaho and western Montana.

It grows in moist to dry forests, riparian zones, and mixed conifer-hardwood stands, typically at elevations from sea level to 1500 m.

The species is shade-tolerant in its youth but requires increasing light as it matures. It is commonly found as an understory component of Douglas-fir, western redcedar, and western hemlock forests.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The primary active constituents are hydroxyanthracene derivatives, predominantly cascara glycosides A and B (aloe-emodin-8-O-β-D-glucoside derivatives).

Other anthraquinone glycosides include barbaloin (aloin), chrysophanol, and emodin in varying concentrations. These compounds are present primarily as C-glycosides in fresh bark and are converted to the more active O-glycoside forms during the aging process.

**Mechanism of action:** Anthraquinone glycosides are hydrolyzed by intestinal bacteria to aglycones (rhein, aloe-emodin), which stimulate peristalsis in the colon via direct action on the enteric nervous system and increased prostaglandin secretion.

Onset of action occurs 6–12 hours after oral administration, consistent with colon-delivery pharmacokinetics. Prolonged use leads to melanosis coli and potential tolerance development.

## Medicinal Use

Cascara sagrada bark is approved by the German Commission E and ESCOP as a treatment for constipation, particularly in cases of atonic or slow-transit constipation.

It was listed in the U.S. Pharmacopeia from 1890 until removed in 2002 due to insufficient safety data submission, though it remains widely available as a dietary supplement.

Standardized preparations deliver 20–30 mg of hydroxyanthracene derivatives per dose, taken as tablets, liquid extracts, or bark tea.

Contraindications include intestinal obstruction, appendicitis, inflammatory bowel disease, and pregnancy. Long-term use beyond 1–2 weeks is not recommended.

## Harvesting and Processing

Bark is typically harvested in spring or early summer when the sap is flowing, which facilitates easier removal from the trunk and branches.

Only bark from branches at least 2–3 cm in diameter is collected; trunk bark is preferred for its higher glycoside concentration. The bark is stripped in longitudinal strips and dried in the shade or at temperatures below 40°C to preserve anthraquinone integrity.

**Critical aging requirement:** Fresh bark contains free anthrones that cause severe cramping, nausea, and vomiting. Commercial cascara must be aged for a minimum of one year, though many processors age for 2–3 years.

The aged bark turns from reddish-brown to a darker brown or nearly black color, indicating the necessary oxidative conversion of emodin anthrones to the less irritating glycosidic forms.

## Conservation Status

*Rhamnus purshiana* is not currently listed as threatened or endangered, but wild populations have declined in parts of its range due to historical overharvesting for the pharmaceutical trade.

Commercial supplies are now largely sourced from cultivated stands and managed forests in the Pacific Northwest. Sustainable harvesting protocols require leaving at least 50% of the bark on each tree to prevent mortality.

The species plays an ecological role as a food source for birds and small mammals, which disperse its seeds. Its riparian habitat associations make it important for stream bank stabilization.
