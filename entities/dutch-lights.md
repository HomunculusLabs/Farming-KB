# Dutch Lights

## Description

Dutch lights are a traditional cold-frame design consisting of approximately 30-by-60-inch glass panes set in wooden frames. They are a simplified, time-tested approach to cold-frame [[coleman-french-historical-winter-vegetable-production]] originating in the Netherlands. Eliot Coleman adopted the Dutch light design for his [[winter-harvest-system]] after observing their use during trips to European farms in the 1970s.

## Classification

- **Category**: Agricultural infrastructure / season-extension technology
- **Type**: Cold-frame glazing system
- **Origin**: Netherlands (Holland)
- **Period of use**: Historical to present day

## Key Facts

### Design and Construction

Dutch lights consist of large glass panes (approximately 30 x 60 inches) mounted in wooden frames. The design is simpler than the French cold-frame models observed at Louis Savier's market garden. Comparative investigations of different cold-frame designs have shown that the standard old-time model — a bottomless box made of 2-inch-thick planks, 8 to 12 inches high at the back and 6 to 8 inches high at the front, covered with glass panels — remains the best performing design.

### Climate Moderation Effect

A cold frame built with Dutch lights provides the equivalent of moving plants to a climate one and one-half USDA zones warmer, or approximately 500 miles to the south on the U.S. East Coast. A standard cold frame will usually prevent crops inside from freezing until outdoor temperatures drop below 25F (-4C).

### Historical References

Coleman cited three key references for Dutch light design:
- Carter, A. R. *Dutch Lights for Growers and Gardeners*. London: Vinton & Co., 1956.
- Quarrell, C. P. *Intensive Salad Production*. London: Crosby Lockwood, 1938.
- Willmott, P. K. *Dutch Lights and Frames*. London: Ernest Benn Ltd, 1958.

### The Double-Layer Breakthrough

In the winter of 1980, Coleman constructed an [[cold-house-unheated-greenhouse]] in a traditional Dutch style — a structural frame of 2x4 lumber covered with Dutch lights — and erected cold frames inside over winter crops. This double-layer approach moved the effective growing climate an additional 500 miles south to USDA Zone 8, with the added benefit of a snow-shedding roof overhead.

## Relevance to Cultivation

### Winter Harvest Systems

Dutch lights are foundational to Coleman's winter harvest methodology. They represent the first layer of protection that enables cold-hardy vegetable production through winter months without supplemental heating. The simplicity and time-tested nature of the design makes them accessible to growers with limited resources.

### Philosophy of Simplicity

Coleman deliberately chose Dutch lights over more technological solutions (Styrofoam insulation, reflective covers, space-age materials) based on the conviction that simpler is better, especially where simpler has been time-tested. This philosophy guides the entire winter harvest approach.

### Transition to Modern Materials

While Dutch lights served as the original model, commercial winter production eventually required a more economical and labor-efficient inner layer. Floating row covers held a foot above the soil on flat-topped wire wickets replaced the glass cold frames for commercial operations, though the principles of double coverage established with Dutch lights remain central to the system.
