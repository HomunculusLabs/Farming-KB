# Desfontainia spinosa

**Desfontainia spinosa** R. et P., commonly known as **Taique** (in Chile) or **Borrachero** (in Colombia, meaning "intoxicator"), is one of the least-known yet culturally significant hallucinogenic plants of the Andes. It is a beautiful shrub with glossy holly-like leaves and striking tubular red flowers.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Family:** Desfontainiaceae (sometimes placed in Loganiaceae or Potaliaceae)
- **Genus:** Desfontainia
- **Species:** D. spinosa
- **Authority:** R. et P.
- **Common names:** Taique, Borrachero, Palo Santo

Botanists are not in agreement as to the number of species in the genus Desfontainia. The family placement has been debated, with some authorities assigning it to Loganiaceae or Potaliaceae rather than its own family Desfontainiaceae.

Modern molecular phylogenetic studies have generally supported the recognition of Desfontainiaceae as a distinct family within the order Gentianales. The genus is monotypic or nearly so, with D. spinosa being the most widely recognized species. Some taxonomists have described additional species from various Andean locations, but these are not universally accepted.

The genus name honors the French botanist Rene Louiche Desfontaines, who was a prominent figure in late 18th-century botany.

## Morphology

Desfontainia spinosa is a beautiful shrub ranging from 1 to 6 feet (30 cm to 1.8 m) in height. The leaves are glossy green, resembling those of Christmas holly (Ilex aquifolium), with spiny margins that give the foliage a striking ornamental appearance.

The leaves are opposite, leathery in texture, and persist throughout the year. Each leaf measures approximately 1 to 3 inches (2.5 to 7.5 cm) in length. The leaf margins are dentate with small spines, creating a holly-like appearance that makes the plant easily recognizable even when not in flower.

The flowers are tubular and red with a distinctive yellow tip, making them visually reminiscent of certain Fuchsia species. The combination of holly-like leaves and fuchsia-like flowers makes D. spinosa one of the most ornamental psychoactive plants in existence. The flowers are pollinated by hummingbirds, which are attracted to the red tubular blossoms.

The berry is white or greenish yellow, globose, approximately 0.5 inches (1.2 cm) in diameter, containing many lustrous seeds. The fruit is eaten by birds, which may contribute to seed dispersal.

## Distribution

The species occurs in the highlands of Central America and South America. It has been specifically reported as a hallucinogen from Chile and southern Colombia, suggesting a distribution along the Andean cordillera.

The plant is found at elevations between 1,500 and 3,500 meters, growing in cloud forests and montane habitats. Its range extends from Colombia through Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia into southern Chile and Argentina. It prefers humid, shaded environments and is often found growing as an understory shrub in montane forest ecosystems.

## Ethnobotanical Uses

### Chilean Use (Taique)

In Chile, Desfontainia spinosa is known as **Taique** and has been used for shamanic purposes similar to [[latua-pubiflora]] (Latue). In southern Chile, the Mapuche people traditionally employed Taique in their shamanic practices. The leaves are prepared as a tea or infusion, and the resulting intoxication is described as profoundly hallucinogenic.

The Mapuche shamans (known as machis) use Taique in ceremonies designed for divination, healing, and communication with the spirit world. The plant is considered a powerful ally that requires respect and proper ritual preparation. The machi's relationship with Taique is described as a personal spiritual contract that requires ongoing commitment and respect.

### Colombian Use (Borrachero)

In Colombia, the plant is called **Borrachero**, meaning "intoxicator," a name it shares with Brugmansia species in the same region. Colombian shamans of the **Kamsa** tribe take a tea of the leaves for their psychoactive effects.

The Kamsa use Borrachero in ceremonies that involve healing, prophecy, and spiritual warfare. The Kamsa shamans report that the plant allows them to see the hidden causes of illness and to communicate with spirit beings who can assist in curing disease.

### Comparison with Other Borrachero Plants

The name "Borrachero" is shared among several psychoactive plants in the Colombian Andes, including species of Brugmansia and Iochroma. This naming pattern reflects the shared cultural understanding of these plants as powerful intoxicants used in shamanic contexts.

The tropane-containing Brugmansia species produce a deliriant intoxication, while D. spinosa is reported to produce a more classically hallucinogenic experience, suggesting different pharmacological profiles. This distinction is important for understanding the specific role of D. spinosa within the broader Andean pharmacopoeia of psychoactive plants.

## Chemistry

The specific psychoactive constituents of Desfontainia spinosa have not been fully characterized. Early phytochemical investigations have suggested the presence of several alkaloid classes, though definitive identification of the active principles remains incomplete.

Some researchers have speculated that D. spinosa may contain unique alkaloid compounds not yet identified in other psychoactive plants. These unknown constituents could explain its distinctive subjective effects compared to the deliriant Brugmansia species with which it shares the name Borrachero.

Preliminary phytochemical screening has detected trace amounts of various alkaloid classes, but none have been definitively linked to the psychoactive effects. The total alkaloid content appears to be relatively low, which may explain why the plant requires specific preparation methods to produce its effects.

## Pharmacology

Without definitive identification of the active compounds, the pharmacology of D. spinosa remains speculative. However, ethnobotanical reports consistently describe hallucinogenic effects that are more visionary and less deliriant than those of tropane-containing Solanaceae.

This suggests that the active constituents may act on serotonin receptors (5-HT2A) rather than muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, which are the primary targets of tropane alkaloids like scopolamine and hyoscyamine. The absence of typical anticholinergic symptoms such as dry mouth, dilated pupils, and confusion further supports the hypothesis of a non-tropane mechanism of action.

## Safety Considerations

The safety profile of Desfontainia spinosa has not been systematically studied. Traditional users emphasize the importance of proper preparation and ceremonial context. Given the plant's potency as reported by indigenous users, caution is advised.

The possibility of tropane or other toxic alkaloids in the plant means that improper use could lead to dangerous anticholinergic effects including tachycardia, hyperthermia, and delirium. Anyone considering working with this plant should exercise extreme caution and seek guidance from experienced practitioners.

## Historical Documentation

Early references to Taique use among the Mapuche appear in the ethnographic literature of the 19th and early 20th centuries. The plant was noted by several European naturalists who traveled through the Chilean Andes, though it was often confused with other locally used psychoactive species.

Richard Evans Schultes brought renewed attention to D. spinosa in the mid-20th century as part of his broader work on South American psychoactive plants. Schultes documented its use by both the Mapuche of Chile and the Kamsa of Colombia, establishing its importance as a cross-cultural Andean hallucinogen.

## Cultural Significance

Desfontainia spinosa represents one of the more enigmatic psychoactive plants of South America. Despite its recognized hallucinogenic properties and traditional use by multiple indigenous groups, it has received far less scientific attention than more famous Andean hallucinogens like Ayahuasca or San Pedro.

Its use in southern Chile parallels that of Latua pubiflora, suggesting a shared cultural tradition of hallucinogenic plant use among the Mapuche and related peoples of southern South America. The fact that D. spinosa is used by cultures separated by thousands of kilometers of Andean terrain indicates that knowledge of its psychoactive properties arose independently in multiple cultural contexts.

## Comparison with Related Species

D. spinosa can be compared with several other psychoactive plants of the Andes:

- [[brugmansia-sanguinea]] — produces deliriant effects via tropane alkaloids
- [[latua-pubiflora]] — a Solanaceae hallucinogen used by Mapuche in Chile
- [[datura-stramonium]] — contains scopolamine and hyoscyamine
- cestrum-parqui — another Solanaceae with reported toxic properties

The key distinction of D. spinosa from these tropane-containing plants is the apparent quality of its hallucinogenic effects, which are described as more visionary and less deliriant. This pharmacological difference may reflect a fundamentally different class of active compounds.

## Conservation and Research Needs

As an understudied psychoactive plant, D. spinosa presents significant opportunities for pharmacological research. Priority areas include isolation and identification of the active alkaloids, characterization of receptor binding profiles, and documentation of remaining indigenous knowledge about its preparation and use. Conservation of both the plant and the cultural traditions surrounding its use should be considered an ethnobotanical priority.
