# Desfontainia spinosa

**Desfontainia spinosa** R. et P., commonly known as **Taique** (in Chile) or **Borrachero** (in Colombia, meaning "intoxicator"), is one of the least-known yet culturally significant hallucinogenic plants of the Andes. It is a beautiful shrub with glossy holly-like leaves and striking tubular red flowers.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Family:** Desfontainiaceae (sometimes placed in Loganiaceae or Potaliaceae)
- **Genus:** Desfontainia
- **Species:** D. spinosa
- **Authority:** R. et P.
- **Common names:** Taique, Borrachero, Palo Santo

Botanists are not in agreement as to the number of species in the genus Desfontainia. The family placement has been debated, with some authorities assigning it to Loganiaceae or Potaliaceae rather than its own family Desfontainiaceae.

Modern molecular phylogenetic studies have generally supported the recognition of Desfontainiaceae as a distinct family within the order Gentianales. The genus is monotypic or nearly so, with D. spinosa being the most widely recognized species. Some taxonomists have described additional species from various Andean locations, but these are not universally accepted.

The genus name honors the French botanist Rene Louiche Desfontaines, who was a prominent figure in late 18th-century botany.

## Morphology

Desfontainia spinosa is a beautiful shrub ranging from 1 to 6 feet (30 cm to 1.8 m) in height. The leaves are glossy green, resembling those of Christmas holly (Ilex aquifolium), with spiny margins that give the foliage a striking ornamental appearance.

The leaves are opposite, leathery in texture, and persist throughout the year. Each leaf measures approximately 1 to 3 inches (2.5 to 7.5 cm) in length. The leaf margins are dentate with small spines, creating a holly-like appearance that makes the plant easily recognizable even when not in flower.

The flowers are tubular and red with a distinctive yellow tip, making them visually reminiscent of certain Fuchsia species. The combination of holly-like leaves and fuchsia-like flowers makes D. spinosa one of the most ornamental psychoactive plants in existence. The flowers are pollinated by hummingbirds, which are attracted to the red tubular blossoms.

The berry is white or greenish yellow, globose, approximately 0.5 inches (1.2 cm) in diameter, containing many lustrous seeds. The fruit is eaten by birds, which may contribute to seed dispersal.

## Distribution

The species occurs in the highlands of Central America and South America. It has been specifically reported as a hallucinogen from Chile and southern Colombia, suggesting a distribution along the Andean cordillera.

The plant is found at elevations between 1,500 and 3,500 meters, growing in cloud forests and montane habitats. Its range extends from Colombia through Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia into southern Chile and Argentina. It prefers humid, shaded environments and is often found growing as an understory shrub in montane forest ecosystems.

## Ethnobotanical Uses

### Chilean Use (Taique)

In Chile, Desfontainia spinosa is known as **Taique** and has been used for shamanic purposes similar to [[brugmansia-sanguinea]] — produces deliriant effects via tropane alkaloids
- [[datura-stramonium]] — contains scopolamine and hyoscyamine
- cestrum-parqui — another Solanaceae with reported toxic properties

The key distinction of D. spinosa from these tropane-containing plants is the apparent quality of its hallucinogenic effects, which are described as more visionary and less deliriant. This pharmacological difference may reflect a fundamentally different class of active compounds.

## Conservation and Research Needs

As an understudied psychoactive plant, D. spinosa presents significant opportunities for pharmacological research. Priority areas include isolation and identification of the active alkaloids, characterization of receptor binding profiles, and documentation of remaining indigenous knowledge about its preparation and use. Conservation of both the plant and the cultural traditions surrounding its use should be considered an ethnobotanical priority.
