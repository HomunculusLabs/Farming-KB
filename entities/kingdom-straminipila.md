## Description

Kingdom Straminipila (also called Stramenopila or Heterokonta) is a kingdom of eukaryotic organisms that includes several groups of fungus-like organisms traditionally studied by mycologists. These organisms—oomycetes (water molds), hyphochytriomycetes, labyrinthulids, and thraustochytrids—are phylogenetically unrelated to true fungi ([[kingdom-fungi]]) but share convergent morphological features. They are characterized by heterokont flagella (one smooth, one with tubular mastigonemes) and mitochondrial cristae of the tubular type.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Eukaryota
- **Kingdom**: Straminipila (Stramenopila)
- **Relationship**: Phylogenetically distant from [[kingdom-fungi]]; convergent morphology
- **Flagellar type**: Heterokont (anterior tinsel + posterior whiplash)
- **Mitochondrial cristae**: Tubular
- **Phyla/Groups treated**: Oomycota, Hyphochytriomycota, Labyrinthulomycota (Labyrinthulales, Thraustochytriales)

## Key Facts

### Distinguishing from True Fungi

- Straminipilans are not fungi—they belong to a separate kingdom
- Convergent evolution produced fungus-like morphologies (filamentous growth, spore production) independently
- Key differences: cell walls contain cellulose (not chitin); flagellated cells have heterokont flagellation
- Tubular mitochondrial cristae (vs. flattened cristae in true fungi of [[kingdom-fungi]])
- Molecular phylogenies (Baldauf et al. 2000) clearly separate Straminipila from [[kingdom-fungi]]
- These distinctions are critical for accurate [[biodiversity-oomycetes-straminipiles]] assessments

### Major Groups

#### Oomycota (Water Molds)

- The most economically important group within Straminipila
- Includes serious plant pathogens: *Phytophthora* (potato late blight, sudden oak death), *Pythium* (damping-off diseases), *Plasmopara* (downy mildews)
- Produce zoospores with two flagella (one tinsel, one whiplash)
- Cell walls composed of cellulose and β-glucans
- Historically classified as fungi due to filamentous growth and absorptive nutrition

#### Hyphochytriomycota (Hyphochytriomycetes)

- Small group of zoosporic organisms
- Produce zoospores with a single anterior tinsel flagellum
- Mostly saprobic or weak parasites on fungi, algae, and invertebrate eggs
- Found in aquatic and soil habitats

#### Labyrinthulomycota

- Includes Labyrinthulales (net slime molds) and Thraustochytriales
- Marine and estuarine organisms
- Produce a network of membranous tubes (bothrosome-derived) for glide motility
- Some species commercially important as sources of omega-3 fatty acids (DHA)
- Aplanochytrium is a related genus sometimes treated separately

### Historical Classification

- All Straminipilan groups were historically classified as fungi
- Studied by mycologists due to ecological and morphological similarity
- Retained in mycological curricula and reference works (including *Biodiversity of Fungi*) for practical reasons
- Cavalier-Smith (2001) proposed formal classification systems; these continue to be refined
- Protocols for sampling these organisms are included alongside true fungi in biodiversity surveys

### Phylogenetic Context

- Baldauf et al. (2000) used multiple protein-coding genes (α-tubulin, β-tubulin, actin, EF-1α) plus rDNA
- Confirmed Straminipila as a distinct lineage from [[kingdom-fungi]]
- Correspondence of non-molecular traits (flagellum type, mitochondrial cristae) supports molecular phylogeny
- Straminipila also includes brown algae and diatoms, though these are not treated in mycological works
- Research by [[joseph-spatafora]] and [[meredith-blackwell]] contributed to clarifying these relationships within the broader [[biodiversity-of-fungi-fungal-phylogeny-kingdom-classification]]

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

While not true fungi, Straminipilan organisms—especially oomycetes—are critically important to cultivation. *Pythium* and *Phytophthora* species are among the most devastating contaminants and pathogens in mushroom cultivation facilities. Understanding that these organisms are not fungi is essential for effective control: many fungicides targeting chitin synthesis or ergosterol are ineffective against cellulose-walled oomycetes. Proper identification of contaminants as oomycetes vs. true fungi determines the appropriate treatment strategy in cultivation operations. This distinction is covered in practical detail in [[straminipiles-oomycetes-fungus-like-chromists]].

For cultivators using [[synthetic-log-cultivation]] methods, oomycete contamination can be particularly problematic during the high-humidity fruiting phase. Knowing that these organisms respond to different control agents than true fungal contaminants allows growers to select appropriate treatment strategies.
