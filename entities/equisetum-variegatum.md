## Overview

**Equisetum variegatum**, commonly known as the **variegated horsetail**, is a perennial fern ally in the family Equisetaceae. It is distinguished from other horsetails by its dark green, whorled branches that remain appressed against the main stem rather than spreading outward.

The species is circumpolar in distribution, favoring cold climates including arctic tundra, alpine meadows, and subarctic wetlands across the Northern Hemisphere. It is one of the smaller horsetail species, typically reaching only 10–30 cm in height.

Unlike many Equisetum species that produce distinct sterile and fertile shoots, E. variegatum bears both vegetative and reproductive structures on the same stem. This homosporous condition simplifies its life cycle relative to heterosporous fern allies.

The genus Equisetum is the sole surviving member of the order Equisetales, which was far more diverse during the Carboniferous Period. Modern horsetails represent a relict lineage with no close living relatives. Molecular phylogenetic studies place Equisetum as sister to all other extant ferns, highlighting its deep evolutionary divergence.

Equisetum variegatum is a silica accumulator, drawing substantial quantities of silicic acid from soil and depositing it in epidermal cell walls. This trait is shared across the genus but is especially concentrated in species of cold environments where herbivore pressure from grazing mammals is a significant selective factor.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Equisetopsida → Equisetales → Equisetaceae → *Equisetum variegatum* Schleich. ex F.Weber & D.Mohr.

The specific epithet *variegatum* refers to the banded appearance created by the alternating dark green stem nodes and lighter internodes. It was first described by Schleichendorf and later validly published by Weber and Mohr in the early 19th century.

Stems are annual, unbranched or sparingly branched, with 6–10 ridges. The central cavity occupies roughly one-third to one-half of the stem diameter. Branches, when present, are borne in whorls at the nodes but remain tightly appressed to the stem.

This appressed branching habit is the most reliable field character distinguishing E. variegatum from the closely related Equisetum scirpoides, which has spreading branches. The two species frequently co-occur in arctic habitats.

Sheaths at the nodes are dark with 3–4 conspicuous dark teeth. The teeth are connate at the base, forming a low membranous collar. The sheath margins are sharply defined and often bear a dark band at the tip, contributing to the variegated appearance that gives the species its name.

Stem cross-sections reveal a characteristic arrangement of vallecular canals beneath each ridge and a smaller central carinal canal associated with the vascular bundle. This internal anatomy is diagnostic for the genus and useful for species identification when external characters overlap.

Cones are terminal, cylindrical, and blunt-tipped, appearing in mid-summer.

Spores are green, spherical, and ornamented with elaters that facilitate dispersal by wind. The elaters are hygroscopic, coiling and uncoiling in response to humidity changes to propel spores away from the parent strobilus. The spore-bearing strobili are 8–15 mm long and arise at the tips of fertile shoots.

## Distribution and Habitat

Equisetum variegatum has a **circumpolar distribution** spanning northern Europe, northern Asia, and northern North America. In Europe, it occurs from Scandinavia and the British Isles eastward through Siberia.

In North America, it ranges from Alaska across northern Canada to Greenland, with disjunct populations extending southward in alpine zones of the Rocky Mountains and the Appalachians. It reaches its southern limit at approximately 45°N latitude in montane habitats.

The species is notably absent from most of the contiguous [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] fungi, which enhance [[diffusion-osmosis-and-active-transport-in-plants]] mechanism allows the plants to accumulate silicon at concentrations far exceeding those of surrounding soil solution.

Historically, indigenous peoples of northern regions used the rough, silica-rich stems of Equisetum species for polishing wooden tools, bone implements, and metal surfaces. The specific use of E. variegatum is less well-documented than E. arvense due to its smaller stature and more remote distribution.

In Scandinavian folk traditions, horsetails were sometimes used as a abrasive for scouring wooden vessels and as a fining agent in [[comparison-beer-brewing-vs-cider-making]]. The high silica content of the stems made them effective for removing residue from cooking implements.
