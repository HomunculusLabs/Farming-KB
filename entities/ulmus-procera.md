## Ecological Role

Ulmus procera supports a diverse community of associated organisms. Over 80 species of invertebrate are recorded feeding exclusively on elms in Britain.

The elm leaf beetle (Xanthogaleruca luteola) and the white-letter hairstreak butterfly (Satyrium w-album) are among the most notable elm specialists. The butterfly declined severely as elm populations collapsed.

Dead elm wood provides habitat for a range of saproxylic beetles and fungi. The rarity of mature standing dead elm has made these species increasingly dependent on alternative hosts.

Bird species including treecreepers, nuthatches, and woodpeckers utilize elm bark and branches for foraging and nesting. The dense canopy offers roosting sites for colonial bats.

Elm seeds are an important food source for finches and other granivorous birds during the brief period of samara production in early summer.

## Timber and Uses

English elm wood is interlocked-grained, making it highly resistant to splitting under mechanical stress. This property made it the preferred timber for wheel hubs, boat keels, and chair seats.

The heartwood is reddish-brown and moderately durable, while the sapwood is paler and more permeable. Wood density ranges from 540–610 kg/m³ at 12% moisture content.

Elm was historically valued for water-resistant applications including pipe-making, lock gates, and submerged pilings. Roman writers documented its use for vine-training stakes across Gaul and Britain.

The bark contains significant quantities of mucilaginous compounds, traditionally used in herbal medicine for wound dressings and gastrointestinal complaints. Inner bark was chewed for its demulcent properties.

Ornamental planting of English elm declined sharply after the Dutch elm disease pandemic. However, disease-resistant cultivars and species are now widely available for landscape use.

The wood burns poorly when green due to high water content but produces moderate heat when seasoned. Elm charcoal was historically preferred by gunpowder manufacturers for its consistent burn rate.

Hedgerow elms served as living fence posts in traditional agricultural systems. Their ability to regenerate from pollarding made them invaluable for sustainable wood production in lowland farming.
