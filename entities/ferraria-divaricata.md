## Overview

Ferraria divaricata is a cormous perennial in the family Iridaceae, native to the winter-rainfall regions of the Western and Northern Cape provinces of South Africa.

It is commonly known as the spreading spider iris, referring to the intricately branched, star-shaped flowers that resemble spider legs. The blooms display a complex pattern of frilled edges and contrasting color markings.

The intricate flowers are among the most complex in the iris family, combining frilled tepal margins with dramatic color patterns that have fascinated botanists since the species' early description.

The genus Ferraria comprises approximately 14 species, all endemic to sub-Saharan Africa. Ferraria divaricata is one of the more widely distributed members of the genus, occurring across several vegetation types.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Monocots
- **Order:** Asparagales
- **Family:** Iridaceae
- **Genus:** Ferraria
- **Species:** F. divaricata

The genus is named after Giovanni Ferraria, an Italian botanist of the 17th century. The specific epithet "divaricata" describes the widely spreading, divaricate branching of the flower tepals.

Chromosome studies report a base number of x = 9 for the genus, consistent with the broader pattern in Crocoideae. Polyploidy has not been reported in Ferraria, unlike some related genera.

Ferraria belongs to the subfamily Crocoideae and is most closely related to the genera Lapeirousia and Codonorhiza. Molecular phylogenetic studies support its placement within the tribe Ixieae.

The species was first described by Carl Friedrich von Gaertner in 1807 from material collected in the Cape region. Several synonyms exist including Ferraria angulosa and Ferraria densepunctata.

## Morphology

The corm is small, globose, approximately 10–15 mm in diameter, covered with dark brown, fibrous tunics that persist as a net-like mesh. Contractile roots pull the corm deeper into the soil annually.

Leaves are narrow, sword-shaped, and lightly ribbed, reaching 20–40 cm in length and 5–10 mm in width. They are produced in a basal fan and are usually prostrate or spreading.

The flowering stem is branched, 15–35 cm tall, bearing 2–5 flowers on short pedicels. The stem is slender and often zigzag in form, characteristic of the genus.

Each flower opens in the morning and closes by late afternoon, a pattern common among fly-pollinated species that synchronizes with peak diurnal fly activity.

Flowers are 30–50 mm in diameter, with six tepals arranged in two whorls. The outer tepals are spreading and deeply frilled at the margins, colored yellow-green to brownish with dark purple or maroon spots.

The inner tepals are smaller, erect to spreading, with crisped margins and similar spotting patterns. The overall appearance is of a small, intricate star with ornate frilled edges.

The flowers produce a faint to moderately strong odor described as fetid or carrion-like. This scent is a key adaptation for attracting fly pollinators and distinguishes Ferraria from most other Iridaceae.

The capsule is ovoid, 10–15 mm long, containing numerous small, winged seeds adapted for wind dispersal. Flowering occurs from August to October.

## Distribution and Habitat

Ferraria divaricata occurs from the Cape Peninsula northward through the West Coast and into Namaqualand in the Northern Cape. Its range extends across a variety of winter-rainfall vegetation types.

The species exhibits some phenotypic plasticity across its range, with northern populations in Namaqualand producing smaller flowers on shorter stems than southern populations near Cape Town.

The species grows in sandy and gravelly soils on coastal flats, hillside slopes, and rocky outcrops. It occurs in fynbos, strandveld, and succulent karoo vegetation communities.

Unlike some Ferraria species restricted to specific soil types, F. divaricata shows broad edaphic tolerance. It occurs on acidic sandstone-derived sands as well as alkaline limestone gravels.

Some populations show a preference for disturbed ground, including road cuttings and quarries, suggesting a degree of ruderal tendency uncommon in the genus.

The species is found from near sea level to approximately 600 meters elevation. Populations are generally small and scattered but collectively cover a wide geographic range.

## Cultivation

Ferraria divaricata requires a Mediterranean climate cycle with cool, wet winters and hot, dry summers. Plant corms in autumn in well-drained, sandy soil with minimal organic matter.

Full sun exposure promotes the best flower production and compact growth. The species is suitable for rock gardens, bulb frames, and containers in temperate regions.

In containers, a mix of equal parts sand, loam, and grit provides suitable growing media. Pots should be deep enough to accommodate the contractile roots, at least 15 cm.

Summer dormancy is critical — corms must remain completely dry from November through March. In summer-rainfall climates, lift corms after foliage dies back and store in a cool, dry location.

The unusual flowers are short-lived, each lasting only one to two days. However, plants produce multiple flowers in succession over a two-to-three-week blooming period.

Despite its unusual beauty, F. divaricata remains rare in cultivation outside of specialist collections. The short-lived flowers and specific growing requirements limit its commercial appeal.

Propagation is by seed sown fresh in autumn. Seedlings flower in their second or third year. Corm division is possible but slow, as offset production is limited.

## Ecology and Significance

The foul-smelling flowers of Ferraria divaricata are adapted for fly pollination (myophily). The dark, spotted tepals and frilly margins mimic decaying organic matter, attracting carrion flies and dung flies.

Pollen is sticky and adheres to the fly's body during feeding visits.

Nectar is produced at the base of the tepals, and flies transfer pollen while feeding. This pollination strategy is unusual among Iridaceae, most of which are bee-pollinated.

The species plays a role in the diverse geophyte communities of the Cape Floristic Region. It co-occurs with Babiana, Lachenalia, and Oxalis species in the winter-growing bulb flora.

The Cape Floristic Region contains over 1,500 geophyte species, making it the richest bulb flora on Earth. Ferraria divaricata contributes to this remarkable diversity with its distinctive floral morphology.

Conservation status has not been formally evaluated by the IUCN, but the species is not considered threatened due to its broad distribution and occurrence in several protected areas including the West Coast National Park.

Ferraria species are occasionally targeted by corm-feeding mammals such as porcupines and baboons. The fibrous corm tunics may provide some protection against herbivory.
