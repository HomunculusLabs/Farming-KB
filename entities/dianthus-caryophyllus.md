## Overview

**Dianthus caryophyllus**, commonly known as the carnation or clove pink, is a herbaceous perennial plant in the family Caryophyllaceae. It is native to the Mediterranean region and has been cultivated for over 2,000 years for its fragrant, showy flowers.

The species is one of the world's most important cut flowers, with global production exceeding 6 billion stems annually. Carnations are valued in the floral industry for their exceptional vase life, fragrance, and wide color range.

The genus name *Dianthus* derives from Greek words meaning "flower of the gods" (*dios* + *anthos*). The specific epithet *caryophyllus* refers to the clove-like scent of the flowers.

Modern commercial cultivars have been developed primarily from a complex hybrid swarm involving *D. caryophyllus* and several other *Dianthus* species, particularly *D. knappii* and *D. deltoides*. This genetic complexity contributes to the remarkable diversity of flower forms and colors available today.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Dianthus caryophyllus* L. belongs to the family Caryophyllaceae, tribe Caryophylleae, subfamily Caryophylloideae. It is the type species of the genus *Dianthus*, which contains approximately 300 species worldwide.

The plant grows 30–80 cm tall with slender, glaucous, linear leaves 5–15 cm long and 1–2 mm wide. Leaves are opposite, sessile, and joined at the base forming a distinctive sheath around the stem.

Flowers are 3–5 cm in diameter, produced singly or in small cymes of 2–5. The calyx is cylindrical, 2–3 cm long, with five bracts at the base. Petals are typically serrated or fringed at the margins, though cultivated forms vary widely.

Wild-type flowers are pink with a darker ring near the center. Cultivated varieties span white, yellow, orange, red, purple, and striped or bicolor patterns. Each flower has five petals and ten stamens.

The fruit is a cylindrical capsule 1–2 cm long, opening at the apex with four teeth. Seeds are small, black, and reniform, approximately 2–3 mm in diameter.

Chromosomal studies confirm a diploid number of 2n = 30 for most wild populations. Polyploid cultivars exist in commercial collections, though diploid forms predominate in breeding programs.

The floral anatomy includes a distinctive hypogynous ovary with a central placenta. The calyx tube is characteristic of the Caryophylloideae subfamily and serves as a key taxonomic character for species delimitation within the genus.

## Distribution and Habitat

The wild form of *D. caryophyllus* is native to the Mediterranean Basin, particularly the coastal regions of southern Europe. Populations have been recorded in Spain, Italy, Greece, and the Balkan Peninsula.

In the Middle Ages, carnations were widely grown in European monastery gardens and were valued for their clove-like fragrance, which was used to flavor wines and cordials. The French name "œillet" (little eye) refers to the flower's shape.

Spanish monks cultivated carnations extensively in monastery gardens, and the species spread throughout Europe via religious institutions. By the 16th century, carnations were established garden plants across the continent.

The modern carnation industry began in the late 19th century with the development of new cultivars in France and England. The Malmaison carnation, a large-flowered type, became enormously popular in Victorian England.

The introduction of perpetual-flowering carnations in the early 20th century revolutionized the industry. These cultivars flowered year-round under greenhouse conditions, enabling consistent commercial supply regardless of season.

Advances in post-harvest technology, particularly the development of floral preservatives containing sugars and antimicrobial agents, extended vase life from 5–7 days to 14–21 days. This improvement was critical to establishing carnations as a mass-market commodity flower.

Carnations hold cultural significance worldwide. In Korea, they are a traditional graduation flower. In Portugal, red carnations symbolize the 1974 Carnation Revolution. In the United States, they are the official flower of Mother's Day.

Different colors carry specific meanings in the language of flowers: red for love, white for purity, pink for gratitude, and yellow for disappointment. Green carnations have been associated with Oscar Wilde and LGBTQ+ identity since the late 19th century.
