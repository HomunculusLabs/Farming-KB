## Description

Dryland salting (also known as dryland salinity) is a form of soil degradation that occurs in non-irrigated agricultural and pastoral lands, where the removal of deep-rooted perennial vegetation (primarily trees) causes saline groundwater to rise to the soil surface. Unlike wetland salting, which results from over-irrigation, dryland salting is driven by vegetation clearing, soil compaction, and altered hydrology. It represents one of the most serious and widespread forms of land degradation in semi-arid and arid regions worldwide, particularly in Australia.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil degradation process
- **Type**: Non-irrigation salinity
- **Related processes**: Desertification, deflocculation, soil collapse, groundwater rise
- **Contrasted with**: Wetland salting (irrigation-induced salinity)

## Key Facts

- Dryland salting is an induced problem that did not exist before clearing, cropping, and grazing of native landscapes. It is therefore theoretically preventable and reversible.
- Salting is usually confined to lowlands and does not occur in sloping upland, forested, or ungrazed regions.
- Australia loses 30,000-40,000 hectares of arable land to salt and desertification each year.
- In the West Australian wheat belt, 10,000 hectares of farmland is lost to salt annually, with approximately 260,000 hectares of salted soils documented in croplands.
- The process can extend into sub-humid areas receiving up to 100 cm average annual rainfall -- essentially any region where evaporation equals or exceeds precipitation.
- Paradoxically, salted areas increase rapidly after two or more wet seasons with flooding. Flooded and waterlogged soil patches are those that later develop salt scalds or salt pans.

## Mechanism

Two principal models explain dryland salting:

### Groundwater Rising Theory

The earlier and widely accepted model holds that removal of deep-rooted trees allows rainfall to infiltrate beyond the root zone, causing the saline groundwater table to rise. As the water table approaches the surface, [[capillary-action-in-soils]] draws salt upward, where it concentrates upon evaporation. This model was promoted by the S.A.L.T. (Salinity Liaison Team) initiative founded by Bill Mollison and Terry White in 1978.

### Soil Collapse Theory (Whittington)

Developed by Harry Whittington of Brookton, West Australia, this model argues that deforestation is not the sole cause. Primary soil collapse results from:

1. **Clearing and cultivation**: Destroys soil structure [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]].
2. **Hoofed animal compaction**: Physical crushing of soil aggregates by livestock.
3. **Surface water swamping**: Compacted soils cannot absorb rainfall, leading to overland flow that carries small quantities of cyclic salt.

Sodium ions from dissolved salt bind to clay particles, displacing calcium and causing **deflocculation** -- the disintegration of clay crumb structure when sodium exceeds 15% of exchangeable cations. This seals the soil surface and cements the subsoil into a water-repellent block, further reducing infiltration in a cascade effect.

## Salt Sources

- **Cyclic salt**: Carried inland by sea winds as rain nuclei.
- **Connate salt**: Remnant salts in soils and sediments from ancient marine periods.
- **Rock-derived salt**: Leached from rock minerals by groundwater movement.
- **Forest-derived salt**: Many desert trees exude or store salt in leaves, releasing it to surface soils during rainfall.

## Prevention and Remediation

- **Absolute ban on clearing**: In any area subject to salting, particularly arid to semi-arid lands.
- **Reforestation**: Permanent deep-rooted trees pump down groundwater and salt below damage levels.
- **Interceptor banks**: Carefully engineered earthworks (as developed by W.A.S.A.L.T.) that redirect surface water flows and prevent waterlogging.
- **Gypsum application**: Displaces sodium on clay particles, restoring soil structure and improving infiltration.
- **Tile or deep drainage**: Physical removal of saline water from the root zone.
- **Drip irrigation at root level**: Minimises excess water application in irrigated areas.

## Historical Context

W. C. Lowdermilk of the United States Department of Agriculture surveyed archaeological evidence of ancient civilisations in the Middle East, Israel, and dryland China in 1928-1929 (U.S.D.A. Bulletin #99, 1929). He documented the pattern of hillside agricultural settlement abandonment and concentration of populations on valley floors, wadis, and oases. Excavations in the Saharan and Thar deserts showed catastrophic sand flows had buried irrigation canals and then the settlements themselves -- a 7,000-year pattern of civilisational collapse driven by soil salinisation.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Dryland salting is central to permaculture design in arid and semi-arid zones:

- **Tree-based farming systems**: Permanent tree cover prevents groundwater rise and maintains the freshwater-saltwater balance.
- **[[desert-landscape-reading-and-erosion-landforms]]**: Understanding salting processes enables designers to place elements correctly -- forests on recharge areas, salt-tolerant species in discharge zones.
- **Soil management**: Avoiding compaction, [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]], and preventing surface sealing are foundational permaculture practices that directly prevent salting.
- **Water harvesting**: Designed swales and infiltration systems that direct water into the soil profile rather than allowing overland flow reduce the cascade effect.
