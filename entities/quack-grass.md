# Quack Grass (Weed Indicator)

## Description

Quack grass (*Elymus repens*, formerly *[[agropyron]] repens*) is a perennial rhizomatous grass weed common in lawns and agricultural fields. In [[the-soil-food-web]] framework, quack grass serves as a biological indicator: its presence signals that the [[soil-food-web]] is not cycling sufficient nitrogen. Addressing the root cause involves adding protozoa and beneficial nematodes via [[compost]], compost tea, or [[protozoa-soup]] to increase nitrogen cycling, rather than attempting chemical eradication.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Family:** Poaceae (grass family)
- **Genus:** *Elymus*
- **Species:** *E. repens*
- **Growth habit:** Perennial, rhizomatous, aggressive spreader
- **Indicator status:** Insufficient nitrogen cycling in soil food web

## Key Facts

- Aggressive perennial weed that spreads via an extensive underground rhizome network
- Presence in lawns indicates the soil food web is not cycling enough nitrogen to support desired grass species
- Shares the indicator role with clover (*Trifolium* spp.) — both signal nitrogen cycling deficiency
- Soil food web management approach: add protozoa and beneficial nematodes to increase nitrogen cycling
- Can be delivered via:
  - Finished compost containing active protozoa and nematode populations
  - Compost tea brewed to include these organisms
  - Homemade protozoa soup (grass clippings soaked in aerated water)
- As nitrogen cycling improves, competitive grasses outcompete quack grass naturally
- Traditional control with herbicides is counterproductive because it further damages the soil food web that would naturally suppress the weed
- Rhizomes can persist in soil for years, making complete eradication difficult — soil food web restoration provides long-term suppression

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Quack grass management through soil food web biology demonstrates the indicator weed concept:

- **Nitrogen cycling deficiency:** Without adequate protozoa and nematode populations, nitrogen remains locked in bacterial and fungal biomass, unavailable to plants
- **The grazing food chain:** Protozoa consume bacteria → release ammonium → plants absorb nitrogen → competitive grasses outcompete weeds
- **Biological approach vs. chemical approach:** Herbicides kill the weed temporarily but worsen the underlying soil food web deficiency
- **Patience required:** Restoring nitrogen cycling takes time as protozoan and nematode populations establish and multiply
- **Compost tea as delivery vehicle:** Compost tea can introduce thousands of protozoa and nematodes per milliliter, accelerating the restoration of nitrogen cycling
