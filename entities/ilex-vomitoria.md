## Overview

**Ilex vomitoria**, commonly known as yaupon, is an evergreen shrub or small tree in the family Aquifoliaceae native to the southeastern [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] plant species native to the continent that contains significant concentrations of caffeine.

The species ranges from coastal Virginia south to central Florida and west to eastern Texas and southeastern Oklahoma. Yaupon is remarkably adaptable, thriving in coastal dunes, pine flatwoods, hardwood hammocks, and inland sandy soils.

Despite its specific epithet *vomitoria*, the plant does not cause vomiting at normal consumption levels. The name originated from a misinterpretation of Indigenous purification rituals. Yaupon tea is experiencing a modern revival as a locally sourced caffeinated beverage.

The genus *Ilex* contains approximately 400–600 species distributed worldwide, with the greatest diversity in tropical and subtropical regions of Asia and the Americas.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Order Aquifoliales → Family Aquifoliaceae → Genus *Ilex* → Species *I. vomitoria* Aiton.

**Authority:** William Aiton, *Hortus Kewensis* (1789).

The species epithet refers to the erroneous belief that the plant induced vomiting, a misconception perpetuated from early colonial accounts of Indigenous ceremonial use.

**Growth form:** Broadleaf evergreen shrub, typically 1–3 m tall, occasionally reaching 8 m as a small tree in favorable conditions. Forms dense, multi-stemmed thickets from root suckers.

**Bark:** Gray to grayish-white, smooth on young branches, becoming slightly rough and fissured with age.

**Leaves:** Alternate, simple, elliptic to obovate, 1–3 cm long and 0.8–1.5 cm wide. Margins are crenate to serrate, usually with 1–4 teeth per side. Dark glossy green above, lighter green below.

**Flowers:** Dioecious, with male and female flowers on separate plants. Flowers are small, white, 4–6 mm across, produced in axillary clusters. Blooming occurs March–May.

**Fruit:** Red drupe 5–6 mm in diameter, containing 3–4 nutlets. Fruits persist through winter and are consumed by numerous bird species. Female plants produce abundant fruit when pollinized.

## Distribution and Habitat

Yaupon is native to the southeastern [[comparison-caffeine-plants-yerba-mate-vs-guayusa-vs-yaupon-vs-tea]] (*[[ilex-guayusa]]*).

Caffeine content ranges from 0.8–1.5% dry weight, comparable to commercial tea (*[[cannabis-harvest-timing-visual-cues]], leaf age, and processing method. Young leaves and apical shoots contain the highest concentrations.

The leaves also contain polyphenolic compounds including chlorogenic acid, rutin, and various flavonoids. These contribute antioxidant activity and are responsible for the tea's astringent flavor.

Unlike many caffeine-containing plants, yaupon contains no detectable theacrine or other novel purine alkaloids. Its alkaloid profile is among the simplest of the caffeinated hollies.

The specific epithet *vomitoria* was assigned by William Aiton based on accounts of Indigenous ritual use. Modern analysis confirms the plant contains no emetic compounds; vomiting in ceremonial contexts resulted from consuming extremely large volumes of hot yaupon tea, not from any pharmacological property of the plant itself.

## Ethnobotanical Use

Yaupon has the longest documented history of caffeine use in [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]]. Indigenous peoples of the Southeast, including the Cherokee, Choctaw, Creek, and Timucua, prepared a caffeinated beverage called "asi" or "black drink" from roasted yaupon leaves.

The black drink was consumed in large quantities during purification rituals, council meetings, and ball game ceremonies. Its ceremonial importance is documented in 16th-century Spanish accounts and continues in some communities today.

Leaves were harvested, parched over fire to develop flavor, and boiled to produce a dark, caffeinated infusion. The beverage was consumed both hot and cold, sometimes sweetened with honey.

European settlers adopted yaupon tea during the American Revolution as a substitute for imported Chinese tea. It was commercially harvested and exported to Europe in the 18th and 19th centuries but declined in use after tea imports resumed.

Modern yaupon tea is produced by several small-scale companies in the southeastern United States, marketing it as a sustainable, locally grown alternative to imported tea and coffee.

Research interest in yaupon has increased since 2020, with studies examining its antioxidant capacity, caffeine pharmacokinetics, and potential as a commercial crop in the southeastern United States.
