## Description

Amy Y. Rossman is an American mycologist who served as director of the Systematic Mycology Laboratory at the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) in Beltsville, Maryland. She is recognized as the originator and driving vision behind the landmark reference *Biodiversity of Fungi: Inventory and Monitoring Methods*, and for developing protocols for fungal biodiversity inventory through her work on the All Taxa Biodiversity Inventory (ATBI) in Costa Rica.

## Classification

- **Profession**: Mycologist, systematic biologist
- **Affiliation**: USDA Agricultural Research Service, Systematic Mycology Laboratory, Beltsville, Maryland, USA
- **Specialty**: Fungal systematics, biodiversity inventory methodology, [[ascomycota|Ascomycota]]
- **Era**: Late 20th century–present

## Key Facts

### Role in *Biodiversity of Fungi*

- Originated the concept of a comprehensive fungal protocols book
- Approached Mercedes Foster about publishing the much-needed reference, which Foster enthusiastically endorsed
- Recruited Hal Burdsall, [[gerald-bills|Gerald Bills]], and [[gregory-mueller|Gregory Mueller]] to help carry out the project
- Planning began early in 1994 under her leadership
- Conceived the book organized by sampling methodology rather than taxonomic lines for maximum utility and minimal repetition
- Identified primary authors for each chapter

### All Taxa Biodiversity Inventory (ATBI)

- Developed protocols for sampling fungi as part of the planned ATBI of the Guanacaste Conservation Area in Costa Rica (Rossman et al. 1998)
- Provided outline-form recommendations for fungal inventory
- Coordinated with colleagues to produce lists of potential economic, educational, and scientific products from fungal surveys

### Workshop Organization

- Organized a workshop held October 15–19, 1995, at the Systematic Mycology Laboratory in Beltsville, Maryland
- Over 65 workshop participants reviewed draft chapter overviews
- Discussions served as preliminary reviews and helped authors reach consensus on main points
- Ensured uniformity across the volume and focus on primary goals of standardized methods

### Legacy

- Without Rossman's vision, the *Biodiversity of Fungi* project would not have become reality
- Her work on ATBI protocols in Costa Rica established a foundation for standardized fungal inventory globally
- The reference methods she championed remain the most comprehensive attempt to standardize fungal survey methodology

## Research on Ascomycota Systematics

Rossman's systematic research has focused extensively on the [[ascomycota|Ascomycota]], one of the largest phyla of fungi:

1. **Nectriaceous fungi:** Her work on the Nectriaceae (Hypocreales) contributed to understanding of plant-pathogenic and mycoparasitic fungi, including genera important in biological control.
2. **Monographic treatments:** Rossman produced comprehensive monographic works that revised and clarified species boundaries in morphologically complex groups.
3. **Culture-based taxonomy:** She emphasized the importance of cultivating fungi in the laboratory for accurate identification, connecting culture morphology with field characters.
4. **Specimen curation:** As director of a major herbarium and culture collection, Rossman advanced standards for preserving fungal specimens and living cultures.

## Impact on Mycological Infrastructure

Rossman's contributions extend beyond individual research projects to the institutional infrastructure of mycology:

1. **USDA Systematic Mycology Laboratory:** Under her leadership, the laboratory maintained and expanded its role as a central resource for fungal identification and systematics in North America.
2. **ATBI methodology:** The protocols developed for the Costa Rica ATBI became a template for biodiversity inventory projects worldwide, establishing fungi as a critical component of all-taxa surveys.
3. **Interdisciplinary collaboration:** The *Biodiversity of Fungi* project brought together mycologists, ecologists, botanists, and zoologists in a collaborative framework that elevated the visibility of fungal science.
4. **Training and mentorship:** Through the 1995 workshop and subsequent projects, Rossman helped train a generation of mycologists in standardized biodiversity assessment methods.

## Connection to Cultivation and Applied Mycology

Rossman's systematic expertise has indirect but important connections to mushroom cultivation:

1. **Accurate identification of pathogens:** Proper systematic identification of fungal contaminants and pathogens is essential for developing targeted control strategies in cultivation facilities.
2. **Biodiversity prospecting:** Standardized inventory methods enable discovery of new species with potential for cultivation or novel bioactive compound production.
3. **Conservation through cultivation:** As wild populations of some fungal species decline due to habitat loss and over-harvesting, systematic documentation supports conservation efforts and development of cultivated alternatives.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Rossman's work on fungal biodiversity inventory methodology directly supports cultivation science by establishing standardized approaches for documenting fungal diversity at any site. Cultivars and wild strains must be properly identified and documented; the protocols Rossman developed ensure consistency in sampling and identification that benefits both academic mycology and applied cultivation research. Her emphasis on organizing by ecological group rather than taxonomy aligns with how cultivators approach different substrate and habitat-based growing systems.
