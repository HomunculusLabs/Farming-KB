## Overview

**Daphne mezereum**, commonly known as **mezereon** or **February daphne**, is a deciduous shrub in the family Thymelaeaceae, native to Europe and western Asia.

It is renowned for its intensely fragrant, lilac-pink flowers that appear in late winter to [[cervantes-selfing-single-seed-descent]]. The bright color and sweetish taste make the berries attractive and dangerous to children.

## Distribution and Habitat

*D. mezereum* is native to most of Europe, from Scandinavia and the British Isles south to the Mediterranean, extending eastward through the Caucasus to Siberia and northern China.

The species was historically introduced to Britain and parts of northern Europe, where it is now naturalized in some areas.

It typically grows in open woodland, forest margins, scrubland, and rocky hillsides on calcareous or neutral soils.

Elevation range extends from sea level to approximately 2,000 meters in mountainous regions of southern Europe.

The plant prefers partial shade but tolerates full sun in cooler climates, and is relatively drought-tolerant once established.

It is found most commonly on well-drained loamy or sandy soils with moderate fertility and a pH near neutral.

## Cultivation

Mezereon is a popular garden shrub valued for its winter-to-early-spring bloom period and intense fragrance.

It is hardy to USDA Zone 4 (−34 °C), making it suitable for cold-climate gardens where few other shrubs flower so early.

The plant prefers a sheltered position with dappled shade and well-drained soil enriched with [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]].

Propagation is typically by seed, which requires cold stratification for 2–3 months and may take 12–18 months to germinate.

Softwood cuttings taken in early summer and treated with rooting hormone can also produce new plants, though success rates are variable.

The shrub resents transplanting due to its deep taproot, so permanent placement should be selected at planting time.

Pruning is minimal and should be limited to removing dead or damaged wood immediately after flowering.

Contact with the sap can cause dermatitis in sensitive individuals, and gloves are recommended when handling the plant.

## Chemistry and Toxicity

The primary toxic principles of *D. mezereum* are daphnane-type diterpene esters, particularly mezerein and daphnetoxin.

These compounds are concentrated in the bark, roots, and especially the berries, with toxin levels highest during fruiting.

Ingestion of as few as 5–10 berries can cause severe poisoning in children, while 15–20 berries may be fatal to adults.
