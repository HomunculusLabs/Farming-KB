## Overview

Saussurea costus is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Asteraceae, native to the high Himalayas of northern India, Nepal, Pakistan, and western China. It is the source of costus root (kuth), one of the most important medicinal plants in Ayurvedic, Unani, and traditional Chinese medicine.

The dried roots of S. costus have been traded along Silk Road routes for over two millennia, valued for their aromatic, anti-inflammatory, and carminative properties. The species was so commercially significant that it was depicted on ancient Greek and Roman trade ceramics.

Costus root was among the most expensive commodities in the ancient spice trade, with prices rivalling those of cinnamon and frankincense. Theophrastus and Dioscorides both describe costus as a medicament of great value imported from India.

Wild populations of S. costus have declined dramatically due to overharvesting for the medicinal trade and habitat degradation. The species is classified as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List and is listed in CITES Appendix I, prohibiting international commercial trade in wild-harvested material.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Saussurea costus (Falc.) Lipsch. was originally described as Aplotaxis lappa D.Don and later transferred to the genus Saussurea by Lipschitz in 1964. The specific epithet "costus" derives from the Sanskrit "kustha," meaning "that which stands in the earth," referring to the deep-rooting habit.

The species belongs to the tribe Cardueae within Asteraceae and is one of approximately 300 species in the genus Saussurea. It is closely related to S. lappa (a synonym under some authorities) and S. medusa, the latter also used medicinally in Himalayan traditional medicine.

Plants grow 100–200 cm tall, with thick, erect stems arising from a large, fleshy taproot. The root is the primary economic organ, weighing 1–3 kg in mature plants and exuding a characteristic aromatic odor when cut.

The root tissue is creamy-white when fresh, darkening to brown upon drying. Fresh roots contain higher concentrations of volatile oils, while dried roots have a more concentrated sesquiterpene lactone profile.

### Leaf morphology

Leaves are large, broadly ovate to cordate, 30–60 cm long, with dentate to irregularly lobed margins. The upper surface is green and rough with scattered hairs, while the lower surface is tomentose with a dense white-gray indumentum.

Basal leaves form a large rosette, while cauline leaves are progressively smaller and more deeply lobed toward the inflorescence. Leaf petioles are stout, winged, and partially clasping at the stem base.

Leaves exude a bitter, aromatic latex when damaged, reflecting the presence of sesquiterpene lactones. The latex deters herbivory and may contribute to the plant's antimicrobial properties.

### Floral morphology

The inflorescence is a terminal, globose capitulum 3–5 cm in diameter, surrounded by large, purple, leaf-like bracts. These involucral bracts are the most visually striking feature, giving the flower head an artichoke-like appearance.

Florets are purple, tubular, and bisexual, numbering 20–40 per head. Flowering occurs from July to September at high elevations. The achenes are small, cylindrical, and bear a pappus of feathery bristles.

## Distribution and Habitat

S. costus is native to the Himalayas, occurring at elevations of 2,500–4,000 meters in the states of Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, and Jammu and Kashmir in India, as well as in Nepal, Bhutan, and southwestern China (Xizang/Tibet and Yunnan).

The species inhabits alpine meadows, rocky slopes, moraines, and open forest margins in the subalpine and alpine zones. It prefers north-facing slopes with well-drained, sandy-loam soils derived from glacial deposits, where seasonal snowmelt provides consistent moisture.

Climate at the species' range includes cold winters with heavy snowpack, cool summers with mean temperatures of 8–15°C, and annual precipitation of 800–2,000 mm, largely as monsoon rain during the growing season.

The species requires a cold stratification period for seed germination and is adapted to the extreme diurnal temperature fluctuations of high-altitude environments. These environmental requirements make cultivation at low elevations technically challenging.

## Phytochemistry

The roots of S. costus contain a complex mixture of sesquiterpene lactones, sesquiterpene alcohols, and essential oil components. The principal bioactive sesquiterpene lactones include costunolide, dehydrocostus lactone, and alantolactone.

Costunolide and dehydrocostus lactone exhibit documented anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and hepatoprotective activities. Dehydrocostus lactone inhibits NF-κB signaling, a key pathway in inflammatory and neoplastic processes.

The essential oil (1–3% of dry root weight) is dominated by α-costol, β-costol, and costunolide. The oil has demonstrated antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and several fungal pathogens.

Additional constituents include inulin-type fructans (up to 30% of root dry weight), which serve as the primary carbohydrate storage compound. These fructans have prebiotic properties that support gut microbiome health.

Phenolic compounds including chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, and various flavonoid glycosides contribute to the antioxidant capacity of root extracts. Trace amounts of alkaloids have also been detected in some provenances.

## Traditional Medicine

In Ayurveda, costus root (known as kuth) is classified as a bitter tonic and is used to treat respiratory disorders, digestive complaints, and skin diseases. It is a component of several classical formulations, including Chyawanprash and Dashamoola.

In traditional Chinese medicine, S. costus is known as mu xiang and is used as a qi-regulating herb for abdominal pain, diarrhea, and vomiting. It is considered one of the 50 fundamental herbs in the Chinese pharmacopoeia.

In Unani medicine (Greco-Arabic tradition), the root is called qust and is used for respiratory ailments, joint pain, and as a general tonic. Avicenna's Canon of Medicine lists costus root among the most valued aromatic medicaments.

The root is also used in Tibetan medicine (known as tsu-ku) for digestive disorders and as a component of Precious Pills (rinchen rilbu). This cross-cultural usage across multiple medical traditions underscores the species' enduring pharmacological importance.

Clinical studies have shown benefit in bronchial asthma, ulcerative colitis, and rheumatoid arthritis. Costunolide derivatives are being investigated as potential anticancer agents, with several preclinical studies demonstrating activity against breast, colon, and lung cancer cell lines.

Dehydrocostus lactone has shown promise as a neuroprotective agent in animal models of Alzheimer's disease, reducing amyloid-beta accumulation and neuroinflammation. These findings have stimulated renewed pharmaceutical interest in the species.

## Conservation

S. costus is listed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List due to population declines exceeding 80% over the past three generations. The primary threats are overharvesting of wild roots for the medicinal trade and habitat loss from grazing pressure and infrastructure development.

CITES Appendix I listing (since 1975) prohibits international commercial trade in wild-harvested costus root. Despite this, illegal harvesting continues in remote Himalayan areas where enforcement is limited.

India's Wildlife Protection Act (1972) includes S. costus in Schedule VI, affording it additional domestic protection. Nepal has designated the species as a priority for in-situ conservation within its protected area network.

Cultivation programs have been established in India, Nepal, and China to reduce pressure on wild populations. The species can be propagated from seed or root cuttings, though cultivation at lower elevations produces roots with altered phytochemical profiles compared to wild-harvested material.

Agronomic trials in the Kashmir Valley and Uttarakhand have demonstrated that cultivated roots can achieve marketable size within 3–4 years. However, the sesquiterpene lactone content of cultivated roots is typically 30–50% lower than wild-harvested material.

Community-based conservation initiatives in Uttarakhand and Nepal involve local harvesters in sustainable collection protocols and cultivation training. These programs aim to balance the economic needs of mountain communities with species recovery.

In vitro propagation through tissue culture has been developed as an alternative to wild collection. Somatic embryogenesis and organogenesis protocols allow rapid multiplication of elite germplasm for conservation plantings and commercial cultivation.
