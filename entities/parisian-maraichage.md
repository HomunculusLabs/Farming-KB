## Overview

Parisian maraichage was the intensive system of market gardening practiced in and around Paris, France, from approximately 1850 to 1900. It represented the apex of pre-industrial vegetable production, achieving remarkable levels of productivity, sustainability, and year-round output on very small land areas using hand labor, horse manure hotbeds, and glass cloches. The system is a direct historical ancestor of modern intensive and four-season growing methods.

## Scale and Productivity

- Total Area: Approximately 600 hectares (~1,500 acres) within Paris city limits
- Individual Garden Size: 1-2 acres average
- Harvests Per Year: 4-8 from the same piece of ground
- Surplus: Paris exported vegetables to England
- Year-Round Production: Fresh salads in January, early cucumbers and melons in May-June

## Core Techniques

### 1. Horse Manure Hotbeds
- 100-400 tons of horse manure per acre annually
- Fresh manure placed under glass-covered frames for bottom heat
- Manure also filled into walkways between frames for extra warmth
- Spent manure composted and returned to beds as soil amendment
- Delivered daily by wagons returning from vegetable market deliveries

### 2. Glass Cloches
- 18-inch-diameter glass bell jars over individual plants
- Up to 3,000 cloches per grower, individually vented with notched sticks
- Covered with straw mats for additional insulation on cold nights
- Used from seedling establishment through mature crop protection

### 3. Intensive Interplanting and Succession
Multiple crops occupied the same ground simultaneously and sequentially.
Example: Radish and carrot broadcast, then lettuce transplanted among them.
Radishes harvested first, then lettuces, then cauliflower transplanted among
carrots, then carrots pulled, then cauliflowers finished, and ground prepared
for the next cycle.

### 4. Straw Mat Insulation
- 1-inch-thick mats woven from rye straw
- Rolled out over glass covers on cold nights
- Lightweight for quick deployment and removal

### 5. Willow Basket Backpacks (Hottes)
- Woven willow baskets worn as backpacks
- Extension arched over the head for depositing loads by bending forward
- Needed because garden paths were only 10 inches wide

## Sustainability

The system was remarkably sustainable:
- Energy powered entirely by decomposing horse manure (urban transportation by-product)
- Composted manure maintained and increased soil fertility despite intensive production
- Transportation waste fully recycled through the food system
- Vincent Gressent observed in 1870 that pest problems were absent wherever copious
  compost use and rational crop rotations were practiced
- Food produced within the city it fed (zero food miles)

## Influence on Modern Growing

The Parisian system directly influenced Eliot Coleman's [[four-season-farm]],
British intensive gardening (via Robinson, Kropotkin, and the 1905 delegation),
Joseph Fels' experimental farms at Mayland Essex, modern SPIN farming concepts,
and bio-intensive gardening methods.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The principles translate directly to modern intensive mushroom and vegetable systems.
Decomposing substrate generates heat (applicable to mushroom grow rooms). Intensive
spacing maximizes production per area. [[spent-mushroom-substrate]] parallels spent
hotbed manure. Staggered crop cycles mirror vegetable succession. The layered
protection approach prefigures modern [[mushroom-house-design]].
