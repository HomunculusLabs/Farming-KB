The plant has been cultivated for over 5,000 years, making it one of humanity's oldest crop species. Its global economic importance is immense, with tea ranking as the second most consumed beverage worldwide after water.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:**

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Ericales
- **Family:** Theaceae
- **Genus:** Camellia
- **Species:** Camellia sinensis (L.) Kuntze
- **Varieties:** C. sinensis var. sinensis (Chinese type, smaller leaves), C. sinensis var. assamica (Assam type, larger leaves)

Camellia sinensis was first described by Carl Linnaeus as Thea sinensis in 1753. It was reclassified into the genus Camellia by Otto Kuntze in 1887.

Two major varieties are recognized in cultivation. Camellia sinensis var. sinensis is a smaller-leafed shrub adapted to cooler, high-altitude climates of eastern China and Japan. Camellia sinensis var. assamica produces larger leaves and thrives in the tropical lowlands of northeast India, Sri Lanka, and Southeast Asia.

**Morphology:**

The plant is an evergreen shrub reaching 1–9 meters in height when unpruned. Under cultivation, it is maintained at waist height through regular pruning to encourage lateral branching and tender leaf growth.

Leaves are alternate, elliptic to ovate, 4–15 cm long and 2–5 cm wide, with serrated margins and a glossy dark green adaxial surface. Young leaves and buds are covered in fine silvery hairs, which are particularly prized in white tea production.

Flowers are white to pale pink, 2.5–4 cm in diameter, with 5–7 petals and numerous yellow stamens. They bloom in autumn and winter, typically October through February in the Northern Hemisphere.

Fruits are three-seeded capsules, roughly 1.5 cm in diameter, that ripen the following year.

## Distribution and Habitat

Camellia sinensis is native to the region spanning from the eastern Himalayas through southern China to northern Myanmar and northern Thailand. The species likely originated at the intersection of the Assam region of India and Yunnan province of China.

It is cultivated across a broad geographic belt between approximately 42°N and 30°S latitude. Major producing countries include China, India, Kenya, Sri Lanka, Turkey, Indonesia, Vietnam, and Japan.

Plants are typically spaced 1.0–1.5 meters apart in rows. New plantations require 3–5 years to reach productive maturity, though harvesting can begin in the second or third year at reduced yields.

Harvesting is done by hand-plucking or mechanical shearing. The highest-grade teas require selective plucking of the terminal bud and the top one or two leaves. Standard commercial grades may include three or four leaves per flush.

Tea bushes produce several growth flushes per year depending on climate. Subtropical regions yield 3–5 flushes, while tropical conditions may support year-round harvesting.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The pharmacological activity of tea is primarily attributed to four classes of compounds: methylxanthines, polyphenolic catechins, theanine, and volatile aroma compounds.

**Methylxanthines:**

Caffeine content in dry tea leaves ranges from 2–5%, comparable to coffee. Tea also contains theobromine (0.1–0.5%) and trace amounts of theophylline. These compounds contribute to the stimulant effects of tea.

**Polyphenols:**

Green tea contains 30–40% polyphenols by dry weight, primarily catechins. The major catechins are epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG, 50–60% of total catechins), epigallocatechin (EGC), epicatechin gallate (ECG), and epicatechin (EC).

Black tea processing oxidizes catechins into theaflavins (1–3% of dry weight) and thearubigins (10–20%). These oxidation products are responsible for the darker color and altered flavor profile of black tea.

**[[l-theanine]]:**

This unique amino acid constitutes 1–2% of dry tea leaf weight. L-theanine readily crosses the blood-brain barrier and modulates neurotransmitter activity, promoting alpha-wave production without causing drowsiness.
