## Bernardino de Sahagun

## Description

Bernardino de Sahagun (c. 1499-1590) was a Franciscan friar and pioneering ethnographer who compiled the most comprehensive early record of Aztec civilization, culture, and natural history. His monumental work "Historia General de las Cosas de Nueva Espana" (General History of the Things of New Spain) contains some of the earliest written descriptions of psychoactive plants used by the Aztecs, including ololiuhqui, peyotl, and teonanacatl. His chronicles serve as a primary historical source referenced by both Hofmann and Schultes in their investigations of Mexican magic drugs.

## Key Facts

- Arrived in New Spain (Mexico) in 1529, shortly after the Spanish conquest
- Spent over sixty years documenting Aztec culture, language, and natural history
- Developed innovative ethnographic methods, training Nahuatl-speaking informants to write accounts of their own culture in their language
- His chronicle describes ololiuhqui ("coatl xoxouhqui" or "green snake") as an herb "that stupefies and deprives one of reason"
- Documented the use of teonanacatl (sacred mushrooms) and peyotl (peyote) in Aztec religious practice
- Often called the "father of modern ethnography" for his systematic approach to documenting indigenous culture
- His work preserved knowledge of Aztec civilization that would otherwise have been lost in the destruction following the conquest
- Cited by both Richard Evans Schultes and Albert Hofmann as a primary source on the historical use of Mexican psychoactive plants

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Sahagun's chronicles are among the earliest and most important historical sources documenting the pre-Columbian use of psychoactive mushrooms (teonanacatl) and other plant medicines in Mesoamerica. Without his careful documentation, knowledge of the Aztec mushroom cult might have been completely lost, and the 20th-century rediscovery of psychoactive mushroom use by Wasson, Hofmann, and others would have lacked the historical foundation that guided their research. His work demonstrates the critical importance of preserving ethnographic knowledge of indigenous plant use for future generations of researchers.

## The Challenge of Translation

Sahagun's work faced the fundamental challenge of translating indigenous concepts into European categories. The Nahuatl term "ololiuhqui" refers not just to the plant and its seeds but to a complex of religious, medical, and magical practices that have no direct European equivalent. Similarly, "teonanacatl" ("flesh of the gods") conveys a theological understanding of the sacred mushrooms that resists reduction to pharmacological terms. Sahagun's bilingual approach, preserving the Nahuatl text alongside the Spanish, allowed later scholars to recover meanings that were lost or distorted in translation. This methodological innovation has become standard practice in modern ethnography.

## Institutional Context

Sahagun worked within the institutional framework of the Franciscan order and the Spanish colonial administration, a context that both enabled and constrained his research. The Franciscans were generally more sympathetic to indigenous culture than other religious orders, and some supported the preservation of native knowledge as part of their evangelizing mission. However, the broader colonial project of cultural suppression meant that Sahagun's work was constantly at risk of censorship and destruction. The fact that his manuscript survived at all, eventually reaching the Medici Library in Florence (hence the name Codex Florentinus), is a testament to the value that he and his collaborators placed on preserving indigenous knowledge against the destructive forces of colonization.

## Documentation of Teonanacatl

Sahagun's documentation of teonanacatl ("flesh of the gods"), the sacred mushrooms of Aztec religion, provided the historical basis for the twentieth-century rediscovery of psychoactive mushroom use in Mexico. His description of how the mushrooms were consumed during religious ceremonies, the visions they produced, and their role in divination and healing practices guided Wasson, Hofmann, and others in their search for surviving mushroom cults among contemporary indigenous peoples. Without Sahagun's account, the idea that psychoactive mushrooms were used in Mesoamerican religion might have been dismissed as a Spanish colonial fantasy, and the rich tradition of mushroom use that survives among the Mazatec and other indigenous groups might never have been recognized by Western science.
