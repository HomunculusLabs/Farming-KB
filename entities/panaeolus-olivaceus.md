# Panaeolus olivaceus
**Panaeolus olivaceus**, commonly known as the **olive mottlegill**, is a small,
dung-inhabiting mushroom found worldwide in temperate and subtropical regions. It is one
of several species in the genus panaeolus known to contain psychoactive tryptamine
compounds, though its potency is generally considered low to moderate compared to more
prominent species like [[panaeolus-cyanescens]].
## Taxonomy and Naming
Panaeolus olivaceus was first described scientifically by Fries. The specific epithet
*olivaceus* refers to the olive-brown coloration that develops on the cap as the
mushroom matures. The genus name Panaeolus derives from Greek words meaning "all
variegated," referring to the mottled appearance of the gills as spores mature at
different rates. Some authorities place this genus within the family Bolbitiaceae,
while others assign it to Psathyrellaceae following modern molecular phylogenetic
studies.
## Morphology
### Cap
The cap measures 1.5 to 4 cm in diameter, ovate to campanulate when young, expanding to
broadly convex or nearly plane with age. The surface is smooth and hygrophanous,
appearing pale grayish or silvery when moist and drying to a dull brownish or
olive-brown color. The margin is typically lighter than the center and may show faint
radial striations when moist. The cap often retains a small umbo or papilla at the
center.
### Gills
The gills are adnexed to adnate, moderately close, with a characteristic mottled
appearance that is typical of the genus Panaeolus. This mottling results from the
uneven maturation of spores across the gill surface, creating a pattern of dark and
light patches. The gill color ranges from grayish to dark brown or blackish as spores
mature.
### Stipe
The stipe is 4 to 10 cm long and 2 to 4 mm thick, cylindrical, equal, and hollow.
The surface is smooth, often with a whitish or silvery sheen when young, becoming
brownish with age. The stipe is fragile and easily broken, a characteristic shared with
many Panaeolus species. No annulus or partial veil is present.
### Spores and Microscopic Features
Spores are ellipsoid to lemon-shaped (limoniform), smooth, thick-walled, with a
distinct germ pore at the apical end, measuring approximately 11 to 14 µm by 7 to 9
µm. Spore print is jet black. The spore size is relatively large for the genus, which
aids in distinguishing P. olivaceus from smaller-spored relatives such as P. fimicola
(8 to 11 by 6 to 7 µm) and P. campanulatus (10 to 13 by 6 to 8 µm). Basidia are two-
and four-spored. Cheilocystidia are abundant, ventricose to cylindrical, measuring 20
to 40 by 8 to 14 micrometers, forming a conspicuous sterile band on the gill edges.
Pleurocystidia are absent. The pileipellis is a cutis of interwoven hyphae. Clamp
connections are absent. The combination of lemon-shaped spores with thick walls and a
germ pore, abundant ventricose cheilocystidia, and the absence of pleurocystidia
provides reliable microscopic identification of this species.
## Distribution and Habitat
Panaeolus olivaceus has a broad cosmopolitan distribution, reported from North America,
South America, Europe, Asia, Africa, and Australia. It is most commonly found in warm
temperate to subtropical climates but can appear in temperate regions during the summer
months.
As a coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) species, P. olivaceus grows almost exclusively on
herbivore dung, particularly that of horses, cattle, and sheep. It may also
occasionally appear on enriched soil or compost. Fruiting occurs from spring through
autumn, typically from April through October in temperate regions, often appearing in
large numbers following warm rains. The mushroom is frequently encountered in pastures,
fields, and meadows where livestock graze. In warmer climates, fruiting can extend
through the winter months. Peak fruiting occurs during periods of warm, humid weather,
typically 2 to 5 days after significant rainfall when temperatures exceed 15 degrees
Celsius. The species shows a preference for older, partially decomposed dung patties
over very fresh deposits, and may also colonize dung that has been partially dried and
then re-wetted by rain.
## Psychoactive Properties
Chemical analysis has confirmed the presence of psilocybin and psilocin in Panaeolus
olivaceus, though in relatively low to moderate concentrations. Reported psilocybin
content is approximately 0.01% to 0.10% per dry weight, with psilocin present at lower
levels. Some analyses have also detected small amounts of serotonin and related
tryptamine compounds including urea and baeocystin. The total psychoactive alkaloid
content is significantly lower than that of [[panaeolus-cyanescens]] (1.5% to 3.5%
total tryptamines) or [[panaeolus-tropicalis]] (0.3% to 1.0%), meaning that
substantially larger quantities of fresh material would be required to produce
noticeable psychoactive effects. The bluing reaction in P. olivaceus is typically weak
to moderate, consistent with its relatively low psilocin content. Analytical data for
this species remains limited compared to more prominent psychoactive Panaeolus species.
The low potency and small size of P. olivaceus make it an unlikely candidate for
intentional recreational use. Most reports of psychoactivity come from accidental or
exploratory consumption rather than deliberate harvesting. The effects, when
experienced, are consistent with other psilocybin-containing mushrooms: visual
alterations, changes in thought patterns, and mild euphoria.
## Identification Notes
Panaeolus olivaceus can be difficult to distinguish from other small, dark-spored,
dung-inhabiting mushrooms:
- [[panaeolus-fimicola]] is very similar but typically has a more reddish-brown cap
  coloration
- [[panaeolus-campanulatus]] tends to be smaller with a more pronounced bell shape
- [[panaeolus-sphinctrinus]] has a more pointed umbo and different spore morphology
- [[panaeolus-subbalteatus]] is generally larger and has a more robust stature with a
  distinct band on the cap margin
The olive-brown mature cap coloration, large spores, and habitat on horse dung are the
most reliable field characteristics. Microscopic examination of spore size and shape
is recommended for confident identification.
## Ecological Role
Panaeolus olivaceus is a primary decomposer of herbivore dung, playing an important
role in nutrient cycling in pasture ecosystems. As a coprophilous fungus, it colonizes
fresh dung and breaks down the complex organic matter, facilitating the release of
nutrients back into the soil. This ecological function makes it a significant component
of the microbial communities in grazed landscapes.
The lifecycle of P. olivaceus is closely tied to its herbivore hosts. Spores are
ingested by grazing animals, survive passage through the digestive tract, and are
deposited in fresh dung where they germinate and colonize the substrate. This strategy
ensures that the fungus encounters freshly available organic matter for growth and
reproduction.
## Cultural Significance
Panaeolus olivaceus is not a species of major cultural or ethnobotanical significance.
Unlike [[panaeolus-cyanescens]], which has documented use in traditional Hawaiian and
Pacific Islander cultures, P. olivaceus is primarily of interest to mycologists and
amateur mushroom enthusiasts. Its presence as a minor psychoactive species is noted in
field guides and mycological literature but it has not been the subject of extensive
chemical or pharmacological investigation.
