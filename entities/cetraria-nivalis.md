## Cetraria nivalis

## Overview

**Cetraria nivalis**, commonly known as the **snow lichen** or **pale Iceland lichen**, is a fruticose lichen in the family Parmeliaceae. It forms dense, pale gray to white cushion-like mats on Arctic and alpine tundra substrates.

The species is one of the most widespread lichens in Arctic regions, where it plays a significant role in caribou and reindeer diets. It is closely related to the better-known *[[berry-growing]], Greenland, Iceland, Scandinavia, and northern Russia.

In North America, the species extends from northern Alaska across the Canadian Arctic Archipelago to Labrador and Newfoundland. It also occurs in alpine habitats southward through the Rocky Mountains and the Appalachians.

The lichen grows on well-drained, sandy or gravelly soils in tundra and alpine environments. It frequently colonizes moraines, eskers, and exposed mineral soils where competition from vascular plants is limited.

It is also found on rock outcrops, hummocks, and stable sand dunes in coastal Arctic areas. The species tolerates a wide range of substrate pH but prefers slightly acidic to neutral conditions.

Elevation ranges from near sea level in the Arctic to approximately 3,000 meters in alpine zones of more southerly mountain ranges. It is one of the few lichen species that can dominate exposed, wind-scoured ridge tops.

## Ecology and Symbiosis

*Cetraria nivalis* is a major component of caribou and reindeer winter diets across the Arctic. During winter, when vascular plants are snow-covered, lichens may constitute up to 70–90% of caribou food intake.

The lichen's high carbohydrate content provides essential energy during the harsh Arctic winter. However, its nutritional value is limited by low protein content and the presence of lichen acids that can be toxic in large quantities.

Caribou have evolved specialized rumen microflora to detoxify lichen acids, particularly usnic acid and protolichesterinic acid. Other mammals lacking these adaptations may experience kidney damage from consuming large amounts of *Cetraria*.

The species contributes to soil formation and stabilization in Arctic environments. Its mat-like growth traps wind-blown sediment and [[fungal-community-profiling-rock-mineral-surfaces-gadd]].

## Chemistry and Secondary Metabolites

*Cetraria nivalis* produces protolichesterinic acid as its primary secondary metabolite, along with usnic acid and traces of fumarprotocetraric acid. These compounds can be identified using standard TLC methods.

Protolichesterinic acid is a fatty acid derivative with demonstrated antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties. It is the most abundant lichen acid in the medulla of *C. nivalis*.

Usnic acid is concentrated in the cortical layer and provides UV protection for the photobiont. It also has broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and has been investigated as a potential antibiotic agent.

The species is C+ red in medullary spot tests due to the presence of protolichesterinic acid. This reaction distinguishes it from *C. islandica*, which contains different depsidone compounds.

## Similar Species and Identification

*Cetraria nivalis* is most commonly confused with *Cetraria islandica*, which has a similar growth form and habitat. However, *C. islandica* is typically darker and more brownish, with broader, more flattened branches.
