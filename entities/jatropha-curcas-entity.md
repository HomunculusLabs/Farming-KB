# Jatropha curcas
## Physic Nut
Semi-evergreen shrub or small tree in the Euphorbiaceae family, native to
Central America and now pantropical in distribution. Jatropha curcas gained
global attention in the 2000s as a drought-tolerant biofuel feedstock, with
seeds containing 30–40% inedible oil suitable for biodiesel production. While
the biofuel hype has subsided due to yield disappointments, the species
remains valuable in permaculture as a living fence, erosion control plant,
and medicinal species. All parts of the plant contain toxic lectins and
phorbol esters, making it inedible and necessitating careful handling.
## Taxonomy and Naming
- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Malpighiales
- **Family:** Euphorbiaceae
- **Genus:** Jatropha
- **Species:** J. curcas L.
- **Authority:** Carl Linnaeus, 1753
- **Common names:** Physic nut, Barbados nut, purging nut, pignon d'Inde
- **Etymology:** Greek "iatros" (physician) + "trophe" (nourishment);
  "curcas" believed derived from "curcas" (a Malayalam word for the plant)
  or from the Carib word "casvi" (physic)
- **Related species:** J. integerrima (rose-flowered jatropha),
  J. multifida (coral plant), J. podagrica (bellyache bush) — all ornamental
## Description
### Growth Habit and Morphology
- **Habit:** Deciduous to semi-evergreen shrub or small tree, 2–5 m tall;
  occasionally reaching 8 m in favorable conditions
- **Bark:** Smooth, gray to brown, exuding white watery latex when cut
- **Leaves:** Alternate, palmately lobed (3–5 lobes), 7–15 cm long and
  broadly ovate; bright green, glabrous above, slightly pubescent beneath;
  long petioles (5–15 cm)
- **Flowers:** Dichogamous (male and female on same inflorescence);
  yellow-green, small (1–1.5 cm diameter), borne in terminal cymes;
  male flowers more numerous than female (approximately 10:1 ratio)
- **Fruit:** Trilocular capsule, 2–3 cm diameter, initially green turning
  yellow then brown-black at maturity; each capsule contains 2–3 seeds
- **Seeds:** Ovoid, 1.5–2 cm long, black-brown with white caruncle;
  30–40% oil by weight; ~1,500–2,000 seeds per kg
- **Roots:** Deep taproot (up to 5 m) with extensive lateral root system;
  contributes to drought tolerance and soil stabilization
### Latex Production
All vegetative parts produce copious white latex when damaged, containing
alkaloids, lectins, and proteolytic enzymes. The latex has traditional
medicinal uses but is a significant skin and eye irritant.
## Distribution and Habitat
- **Native range:** Mexico and Central America; possibly also Caribbean
- **Current distribution:** Pantropical; widely naturalized in Africa,
  South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Pacific Islands; introduced as biofuel
  crop in India, Brazil, Indonesia, Philippines, and Mozambique
- **Habitat preference:** Tropical and subtropical lowlands, 0–1,500 m
  elevation; grows on marginal, degraded, and eroded soils; tolerates
  annual rainfall from 250–1,500 mm; thrives in well-drained sandy or
  gravelly soils; intolerant of waterlogging and frost
- **Invasiveness:** Listed as invasive in parts of Australia, Pacific
  Islands, and some African countries due to prolific seed production
  and unpalatability to livestock
## Chemistry and Active Compounds
### Seed Oil (Jatropha Oil)
- **Oil content:** 30–40% of seed weight; 50–60% of kernel weight
- **Fatty acid composition:** Oleic acid (37–63%), linoleic acid
  (19–41%), palmitic acid (14–17%), stearic acid (3–10%)
- **Properties:** Yellowish, semi-drying oil; iodine value 95–105;
  saponification value 188–196; unsuitable for food use due to toxic
  constituents in the press cake
- **Biodiesel quality:** Fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) profile meets
  ASTM D6751 and EN 14214 standards; high oxidative stability due to
  relatively low polyunsaturated fatty acid content
### Toxic Compounds
- **Phorbol esters (tumor promoters):** Six phorbol esters identified in
  J. curcas seed oil and kernel, primarily 12-O-tetradecanoylphorbol-
  13-acetate (TPA)-type compounds; responsible for the purgative,
  cytotoxic, and tumor-promoting effects; absent in some Mexican
  non-toxic genotypes
- **Curcin:** Type I ribosome-inactivating protein (RIP) similar to
  ricin; present in seeds and latex; inhibits protein synthesis by
  depurinating adenine residue in 28S rRNA; LD50 in mice ~3.1 mg/kg
  (intraperitoneal)
- **Lectins:** Sugar-binding proteins with hemagglutinating and
  cytotoxic activities; contribute to oral toxicity of seeds
- **Trypsin inhibitors:** Reduce protein digestibility of press cake,
  limiting its value as animal feed despite high protein content
### Medicinal Constituents
- **Flavonoids:** Apigenin, vitexin, isovitexin in leaves and roots
- **Terpenoids:** Beta-sitosterol, stigmasterol, betulinic acid
- **Alkaloids:** Jatrophine, jatropholone in roots and bark
## Medicinal and Ethnobotanical Uses
Despite its toxicity, J. curcas has a long history of traditional
medicinal use across its range. Key applications include:
- **Laxative/purgative:** Seeds administered in small doses (1–2 seeds)
  as a strong purgative; overdose causes severe gastroenteritis
- **Wound healing:** Latex applied topically to wounds, cuts, and skin
  infections; antimicrobial activity documented against Staphylococcus
  aureus and Candida albicans
- **Anti-inflammatory:** Leaf extracts and bark decoctions used for
  rheumatic pain and inflammation; supported by in vivo studies
  showing COX-2 inhibition and TNF-alpha reduction
- **Antimalarial:** Leaf and stem extracts show in vitro activity
  against Plasmodium falciparum; used traditionally in West Africa
- **Anticancer:** Phorbol esters from toxic genotypes show tumor-promoting
  activity, but isolated non-phorbol constituents (curcin, betulinic
  acid) exhibit selective cytotoxicity against cancer cell lines
## Permaculture Applications
### Living Fence and Boundary
Jatropha's primary permaculture value is as a living fence. Planted at
15–30 cm spacing in single or double rows, it forms an impenetrable,
long-lived hedge within 1–2 years. The plants are unpalatable to
livestock and wildlife (due to toxicity), drought-tolerant, and require
minimal maintenance once established.
### Erosion Control
The deep taproot and extensive lateral root system make J. curcas
effective for stabilizing slopes, gullies, and degraded lands. In
tropical rehabilitation projects, it is often the first species
established on severely eroded sites.
### Phytoremediation
J. curcas accumulates heavy metals (lead, cadmium, zinc) from
contaminated soils, making it a candidate for phytoremediation of
mine tailings and industrial sites. Growth is reduced under heavy metal
stress but the species survives where few others can.
### Agroforestry Integration
- **Intercropping:** Compatible with food crops in alley farming systems
  once established (2+ years); root competition minimal after canopy
  management
  substantially below early optimistic projections (5–12 tonnes/ha);
  irrigation and fertilization improve yields but reduce the marginal-
  land advantage
## Cultivation Notes
- **Propagation:** Primarily by seed (direct or nursery-raised seedlings);
  vegetative propagation by stem cuttings (20–30 cm, 1–2 year old wood)
  gives faster establishment but lower yields and shorter lifespan
- **Planting:** At onset of rains; seedlings planted in pits 30×30×30 cm;
  spacing 2×2 m to 3×3 m depending on use (closer for hedges)
- **Time to first harvest:** 12–18 months from seed; 6–12 months from
  cutting
- **Yield:** 0.5–2.0 tonnes seed/ha/year; 5–8 tonnes fruit/ha/year
- **Lifespan:** 30–50 years; peak production years 3–15
