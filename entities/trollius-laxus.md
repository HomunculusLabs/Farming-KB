## Overview

**Trollius laxus** Salisbury, commonly known as the **spreading globeflower**, is a rare perennial herb in the family Ranunculaceae. It is one of only two *Trollius* species native to North America.

The plant is distinguished from its Eurasian relatives by its sprawling growth habit and pale yellow to cream-colored flowers. It is a spring-blooming wetland specialist that forms attractive clumps of deeply lobed foliage.

This species is of significant conservation concern throughout its range in northeastern North America. It occupies calcareous wetlands and is considered threatened or endangered in several U.S. states.

The genus name *Trollius* derives from the German word "Trollblume" (globular flower), referring to the nearly spherical flower shape common in the genus. The specific epithet *laxus* means loose or spreading, describing the plant's growth habit.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Trollius laxus** was described by James Edward Salisbury in 1796. It belongs to the tribe Adonideae within Ranunculaceae.

Two subspecies are recognized: subsp. *laxus* (eastern North America) and subsp. *albiflorus* (western Rocky Mountains). Some taxonomists treat the western subspecies as a distinct species, *Trollius albiflorus*.

The plant reaches 20–60 cm in height with a sprawling to ascending growth habit. Unlike the erect stems of *Trollius europaeus*, the stems of *T. laxus* often lean or spread outward.

Leaves are palmately lobed with 3–5 deep segments, each further divided into coarsely toothed lobes. Basal leaves are long-petioled (5–20 cm) while cauline leaves are sessile or short-petioled.

The foliage is bright green and glossy, maintaining its appearance through the growing season. In autumn, leaves may develop yellow to bronze tones before senescing.

Flowers are 2–4 cm in diameter with 5–8 pale yellow to cream-white sepals that slightly overlap. The sepals serve as the primary attractive organ since the true petals are highly reduced.

The true petals are modified into nectaries that are shorter than the stamens. This characteristic distinguishes *T. laxus* from Eurasian *Trollius* species which have longer nectary petals.

Flowers typically number 1–3 per stem, occasionally up to 5 on vigorous plants. Each flower lasts approximately 7–10 days depending on temperature and pollination success.

The fruit is a cluster of 5–12 follicles, each follicle 8–12 mm long with a short beak. Seeds are dark brown to black, ovoid, and approximately 1.5 mm long.

## Distribution and Habitat

Subspecies *laxus* occurs in scattered populations from Connecticut and New York westward to Ohio and Michigan. Historical records also exist from Pennsylvania and New Jersey.

The species has been extirpated from several counties within this historic range. Many remaining populations are small and isolated from one another by significant distances.

It inhabits calcareous fens, marl wetlands, spring seeps, and wet meadows over limestone or dolomite bedrock. These habitats are typically groundwater-fed and remain saturated throughout the growing season.

Soils are circumneutral to slightly alkaline (pH 6.8–7.8), often with a marl or organic peat surface layer. Populations occur at elevations of 100–600 m in the eastern part of the range.

Subspecies *albiflorus* occurs in the northern Rocky Mountains from Montana to Wyoming. It inhabits subalpine wet meadows and stream margins at elevations of 1500–2500 m.

The two subspecies are separated by approximately 2,000 km of Great Plains habitat. This geographic disjunction likely dates to Pleistocene glacial episodes when boreal wetlands were more continuous.

## Ecology

Flowering occurs from late April through June, with peak bloom in mid-May across most eastern populations. The flowers open during the day and close at night.

The species is primarily bee-pollinated. Small halictid bees and queen bumblebees (*Bombus* spp.) serve as the principal pollinators during the spring flowering period.

Flowers produce both nectar and pollen as rewards. The nectary petals secrete small amounts of concentrated nectar accessible only to insects with sufficiently long mouthparts.

Reproduction occurs both sexually through seed and vegetatively through short rhizomes. Clonal patches can persist for decades in stable wetland habitats.

Seed production varies considerably between populations. Some colonies produce few viable seeds due to pollinator limitation or small population size effects.

Seeds require cold stratification for germination and typically germinate the second spring after dispersal. This delayed germination strategy is common in Ranunculaceae wetland species.

The foliage contains ranunculin, a glycoside that converts to protoanemonin when plant tissue is damaged. This compound makes the foliage toxic to most herbivores and likely provides defense against grazing.

Associated wetland species include *Parnassia glauca*, *Lobelia kalmii*, *Carex sterilis*, and various calcicolous sedges. *Trollius laxus* functions as an indicator species for high-quality calcareous fen communities.

## Conservation

*Trollius laxus* subsp. *laxus* is listed as endangered or threatened in Connecticut, New York, Ohio, Michigan, and New Jersey. It is considered globally vulnerable (G3) by NatureServe.

The primary threats include wetland drainage, limestone quarrying, agricultural runoff, and natural succession. As open fen habitats fill in with woody vegetation, *T. laxus* populations decline due to increased shading.

The Nature Conservancy and multiple state natural heritage programs actively monitor known populations. Several sites are protected as nature preserves or conservation easements.

Management techniques include prescribed burning, woody species removal, and hydrological restoration. These interventions aim to maintain the open, sunny conditions the species requires.

Total population estimates suggest fewer than 100 extant occurrences across the eastern range. Many populations consist of fewer than 50 flowering individuals, which raises concerns about genetic bottlenecks and long-term viability.

Ex situ conservation efforts include seed banking at several regional botanical gardens. However, the seeds are short-lived in storage and require periodic regeneration to maintain viability.

## Cultivation

In cultivation, *Trollius laxus* requires consistently moist to wet, lime-rich soil in partial shade to full sun. It performs well in bog gardens, rain gardens, and along pond margins.

Soil pH should remain above 6.5 for best growth. The species does not tolerate acidic conditions or extended periods of drought.

Propagation is achieved through division in early spring or fall. Plants can also be grown from seed sown fresh in fall for natural cold stratification.

Division every 3–4 years maintains plant vigor and prevents the center of the clump from dying out. The species is reliably hardy to USDA Zone 3.

Despite its horticultural merit, *Trollius laxus* remains uncommon in the nursery trade. Gardeners should ensure any purchased plants are nursery-propagated, not wild-collected.

The species has received attention from native plant societies as a candidate for rare species garden programs. Its attractive flowers and manageable size make it a good subject for conservation-oriented horticulture.
