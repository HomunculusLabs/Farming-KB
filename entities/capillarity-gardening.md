## Capillarity (Soil Water Movement)

## Description

Capillarity (capillary action) is the movement of water through small spaces in soil against the force of gravity. In garden soils, capillary action draws moisture upward from wetter soil layers below to drier layers near the surface. Understanding capillarity is essential for seed germination success, irrigation efficiency, and drought management [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]]. [[steve-solomon]] identifies it as a key concept for growing food without irrigation.

## Classification

- **Category:** Soil physics / water dynamics
- **Type:** Physical phenomenon ([[bloomfield-buller-drop-surface-tension-spore-catapult-basidiospore-discharge]] and adhesion)
- **Domain:** Soil science, agronomy, horticulture
- **Practical importance:** Critical for seed germination and water management

## Key Facts

- **Mechanism:** Water moves through tiny soil pore spaces via surface tension and adhesive forces between water molecules and soil particles
- **Direction:** Moves water from wetter zones to drier zones — typically upward from deeper, moister soil layers toward the surface
- **Observation method:** After tilling soil, footprints compress soil particles firmly enough to allow capillary rise; the next morning, footprint depressions remain damp while the surrounding tilled (loose) surface is dry
- **Seed germination connection:** Capillary moisture is what keeps seed beds moist enough for germination; firming soil over seeds with a rake or board establishes capillary contact with deeper moisture
- **Tillage interaction:** Tilling breaks capillary channels near the surface, creating a dry mulch layer that reduces evaporation — this is both a challenge (for germination) and a benefit (for [[cervantes-mulch-outdoor-moisture-conservation]])
- **Soil type dependence:** Clay soils have strong capillary action through tiny pores; sandy soils have weaker capillarity due to larger pore spaces
- **Dry farming:** Understanding and managing capillarity enables growing vegetables without irrigation by maintaining connection to subsoil moisture reserves

## Practical Observation

After tilling, check footprints the next morning:
- **Footprints damp, surrounding soil dry** = Capillarity is working correctly
- **Everything dry** = Subsoil may be too dry for capillary rise, or soil type lacks fine particles
- **Everything wet** = Recent rain or irrigation; check again after drying period

## Applications in Gardening

| Practice | How Capillarity Is Used |
|---|---|
| Seed sowing | Firm soil over seeds to maintain capillary moisture contact |
| Dry farming | Maintain capillary connection to subsoil moisture by avoiding deep surface disturbance |
| Irrigation efficiency | Water deeply and infrequently; capillarity redistributes moisture through soil profile |
| Mulch management | Loose surface mulch breaks capillary channels, reducing evaporative water loss |
| Raised beds | Properly constructed beds maintain capillary connection to native soil below |

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Mycelial moisture:** Capillary action maintains the consistent moisture levels in substrate that mycelium requires for growth
- **Mushroom bed management:** Outdoor mushroom beds rely on capillary moisture from underlying soil to maintain proper humidity at the casing or substrate layer
- **Compost moisture:** Capillary action distributes moisture throughout compost piles, preventing dry pockets where decomposition stalls
- **Casing layer:** [[contamination-prevention-in-mushroom-cultivation]], the casing layer relies on capillary action to deliver moisture from the substrate to the surface where pinning occurs
- **[[bulk-substrate-preparation]]:** Properly moistened substrates maintain even moisture distribution through capillary action rather than requiring surface watering
- **[[soil-food-web]]:** Soil microorganisms depend on water films maintained by capillary action on soil particle surfaces
