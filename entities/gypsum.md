# Gypsum

## Description

Gypsum (calcium sulfate dihydrate) is a soft sulfate mineral widely used in agriculture as a soil amendment to improve soil structure, supply calcium and sulfur, and treat specific soil problems without affecting pH. It is one of the evaporite minerals that precipitates from seawater after calcite and dolomite but before rock salt. In permaculture, gypsum is valued as a natural soil conditioner particularly useful for dispersive and high-sodium soils.

## Classification

- **Category**: Mineral / Sulfate
- **Chemical formula**: CaSO4.2H2O
- **Crystal system**: Monoclinic
- **Mohs hardness**: 2 (can be scratched with a fingernail)
- **Specific gravity**: 2.31-2.33

## Formation and Occurrence

Gypsum forms through evaporation of saline waters and is one of the most common evaporite minerals. It precipitates from seawater after calcite and dolomite have crystallized. Major deposits occur in ancient evaporite basins worldwide. It also forms through the reaction of sulfuric acid (produced from oxidizing pyrites) with calcium minerals in soil. Mollison notes the evaporite sequence: calcite and dolomite first, then gypsum and anhydrite, then rock salts.

## Key Facts

- Unlike lime (calcium carbonate), gypsum does not raise [[crumb-structure]], only partly relieved by gypsum application
- Supplies sulfur, an essential secondary macronutrient for plant growth
- Application rates typically 1-5 tonnes per hectare for soil structure improvement
- When heated, gypsum loses water to become anhydrite (CaSO4)

## Properties

- **Color**: White, colorless, gray, yellow, red, or brown
- **Luster**: Vitreous to silky, pearly on cleavage surfaces
- **Transparency**: Transparent to translucent
- **Solubility**: Moderately soluble in water (2.0-2.5 g/L at 25 degrees C)
- **Anhydrite**: When heated or in very dry conditions, gypsum loses water to become anhydrite

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

### Soil Structure Improvement
- Primary use in agriculture is improving crumb structure of clay soils
- Calcium ions displace sodium on clay particles, allowing aggregation
- Improved aggregation increases water infiltration and reduces surface crusting
- Reduces soil compaction and improves root penetration
- Particularly important in arid and semi-arid regions with saline or sodic soils
- Only partly relieves crumb structure loss in high-salt dryland soils

### Sodic Soil Reclamation
- Displaces sodium from clay exchange sites through ion exchange
- The displaced sodium is then leached below the root zone with irrigation
- Essential amendment for bringing sodic soils back into agricultural productivity
- Often the most cost-effective treatment for severely sodium-affected land

### Nutrient Supply
- Supplies calcium (approximately 23% by weight) without affecting pH
- Supplies sulfur (approximately 19% by weight), needed for protein synthesis
- Sulfur deficiency is increasingly common in agricultural soils due to reduced atmospheric deposition
- Particularly useful for brassica crops and other sulfur-demanding plants

### Permaculture Applications
- Natural and widely available mineral that aligns with permaculture preference for local inputs
- Can be sourced from quarry byproducts and industrial waste streams
- Used in greywater treatment systems and constructed wetlands as a filtration medium
- Helps establish productive soils on degraded or salt-affected sites
- Part of the evaporite sequence connecting marine and terrestrial mineral cycles

## Agricultural Applications

Gypsum (calcium sulfate dihydrate, CaSO4·2H2O) serves multiple roles in soil
management. It provides calcium without raising pH, making it ideal for
acid-sensitive crops in already-alkaline soils. As a sulfur source, it supports
protein synthesis in legumes and brassicas.

## Soil Structure Benefits

Gypsum displaces sodium on clay exchange sites, improving soil structure in
sodic soils. This flocculation effect enhances water infiltration and root
penetration. Apply at 1-5 tons per acre depending on sodium levels.

## Compatibility Notes

Do not apply gypsum where soil sulfur levels are already high. Excess sulfur
can acidify soils and inhibit molybdenum availability. Always conduct soil tests
before gypsum application.
