# Plumeria rubra

## Overview

Plumeria rubra is a deciduous shrub or small tree in the family Apocynaceae, native to Mexico, Central America, and Colombia. It is widely cultivated throughout tropical and subtropical regions for its showy, fragrant flowers commonly known as frangipani.

The species produces large, terminal inflorescences of salverform flowers with overlapping petals that range from white and yellow to deep pink and red. Each cultivar displays a distinct color gradient, often with a contrasting yellow center.

Plumeria rubra contains iridoid glycosides, triterpenoids, and flavonoids with documented anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and cytotoxic activities. Traditional medicinal use spans wound healing, skin conditions, and respiratory complaints across Mesoamerican and Southeast Asian healing systems.

The genus name honors Charles Plumier, a seventeenth-century French botanist who documented Caribbean flora. The common name frangipani derives from the Italian nobleman Marquis Frangipani, who created a perfume resembling the flower's scent in the sixteenth century.

## Taxonomy

Plumeria rubra L. was first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum.

The full classification places it in the order Gentianales, family Apocynaceae, subfamily Rauvolfioideae, tribe Plumerieae, genus Plumeria. The genus comprises approximately eight species of neotropical trees and shrubs.

Synonyms include Plumeria acuminata Aiton, Plumeria acutifolia Poir., and Plumeria rubra var. acutifolia (Poir.) Woodson. Several formerly recognized species have been reduced to varietal or cultivar status within Plumeria rubra based on overlapping morphological characters and inter-fertility.

Major varieties include var. acutifolia (white flowers with yellow center, the most common ornamental form), var. rubra (red to pink flowers), and var. tricolor (multi-colored petals). The taxonomy remains somewhat unsettled due to extensive hybridization in cultivation.

Natural hybrids between Plumeria rubra and Plumeria obtusa occur in areas where ranges overlap in the Caribbean. These intermediates exhibit combinations of parental traits including the evergreen foliage of Plumeria obtusa with the larger flowers of Plumeria rubra.

## Morphology

Plumeria rubra typically reaches heights of five to eight meters as a small tree with a gnarled, branching crown. The trunk and branches are thick, succulent, and produce copious white latex when cut or broken, a characteristic feature of the Apocynaceae.

Leaves are large, elliptic to oblong, twenty to forty-five centimeters long and five to fifteen centimeters wide, arranged in terminal clusters. They are leathery, glossy dark green above, and paler beneath with prominent venation.

Leaf anatomy shows a thick cuticle and sunken stomata on the abaxial surface, adaptations consistent with drought tolerance during the dry season. The mesophyll contains idioblasts with calcium oxalate raphide crystals.

Leaf abscission occurs during the dry season or in response to cool temperatures, leaving the tree briefly bare before new growth emerges. The leaf scar pattern on the branches creates distinctive raised rings.

Flowers are borne in terminal panicles, each inflorescence carrying dozens of buds that open sequentially over several weeks. Individual flowers measure five to eight centimeters in diameter with five broadly overlapping petals, a narrow corolla tube, and a yellowish throat.

Fragrance intensity varies considerably among cultivars, with white and yellow forms typically producing the strongest scent. The volatile profile is dominated by linalool, benzyl benzoate, and various phenylpropanoids.

The fruit is a dry, paired follicle fifteen to thirty centimeters long containing numerous winged seeds. Mature follicles dehisce to release seeds dispersed by wind.

## Distribution and Habitat

Plumeria rubra is native to the Pacific coast of Mexico, extending through Central America to Colombia and Venezuela. It grows in tropical dry forests, thorn scrub, and seasonally deciduous forests at elevations from sea level to approximately one thousand meters.

The species is naturalized throughout the Caribbean, South and Southeast Asia, Pacific Islands, and parts of Africa. It thrives in warm, well-drained sites with full sun exposure and tolerates brief drought once established.

In Hawaii, plumeria has become an iconic ornamental tree central to the lei-making tradition. The flowers are strung into fragrant garlands worn for ceremonies, graduations, and informal greeting.

Optimal growth occurs with annual rainfall of one thousand to two thousand millimeters and mean temperatures between twenty-five and thirty degrees Celsius. The species is intolerant of prolonged frost and sustained temperatures below five degrees Celsius.

Plumeria rubra is frequently planted in cemeteries and temple grounds across Southeast Asia. In Balinese Hindu tradition, the flower is associated with devotion and is placed on shrines during daily offerings.

## Cultivation

Plumeria rubra is propagated primarily from hardwood cuttings taken during the dormant season. Cuttings of thirty to sixty centimeters are allowed to dry for one to two weeks before planting in well-drained sandy loam to prevent rot.

Softwood cuttings taken during active growth show higher rooting percentages but are more susceptible to fungal contamination. Bottom heat at twenty-five degrees Celsius significantly improves rooting speed and uniformity.

Rooting typically occurs within four to eight weeks without supplemental rooting hormones, though auxin application accelerates the process. Seed propagation is also viable but produces variable offspring due to heterozygosity.

Established trees require minimal irrigation and respond well to balanced fertilizer applications during the growing season. Pruning is limited to shaping and removal of dead wood, as heavy pruning reduces flowering.

A balanced NPK ratio of ten to thirty to ten applied monthly during spring and summer promotes vigorous flowering. Phosphorus supplementation is particularly important as deficiency manifests as reduced bud formation and pale petal coloration.

Major pests include spider mites (Tetranychus urticae), whiteflies, and the plumeria rust fungus Coleosporium plumeriae, which causes orange pustules on leaf undersides. Cultural controls and fungicidal sprays manage rust in humid climates.

The plumeria stem borer (Lagocheirus undatus) attacks the succulent branches, creating galleries that weaken structural integrity. Infested branches should be pruned and destroyed immediately to prevent larval spread.

Container cultivation is common in temperate regions where plants are overwintered indoors or in greenhouses. Dwarf cultivars selected for bonsai and patio culture reach only one to two meters at maturity.

Grafting onto seedling rootstock is practiced to accelerate flowering and improve disease resistance in commercial nursery production. The most widely used rootstock is Plumeria rubra var. acutifolia due to its vigorous growth and compatibility with all cultivated varieties.

## Traditional Uses and Pharmacology

In Mexican traditional medicine, the latex of Plumeria rubra is applied externally to treat skin infections, wounds, and dental caries. A decoction of the bark serves as a purgative and diuretic.

The Nahuatl name "cacaloxochitl" means "crow flower" and references the dark bark from which the white flowers emerge. Sixteenth-century Spanish chroniclers documented its use among Aztec healers for treating fever and dysentery.

In Indian Ayurvedic practice, plumeria flowers and leaves are used in formulations for cough, fever, and dysentery. The flowers are offered in Hindu and Buddhist religious ceremonies throughout South and Southeast Asia.

In Thai traditional medicine, plumeria bark tea is consumed as an antipyretic and the crushed leaves are applied as a poultice for swollen joints. Vietnamese herbalists use the root decoction to treat venereal disease and as a diuretic.

Phytochemical analysis has identified iridoid glycosides including plumieride and fulvoplumierin, the latter showing antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria. Triterpenoids such as ursolic acid and oleanolic acid contribute anti-inflammatory effects.

Extracts demonstrate in vitro cytotoxicity against several cancer cell lines, including HeLa and MCF-7, at concentrations of fifty to two hundred micrograms per milliliter. Flavonoids including quercetin and kaempferol glycosides have been isolated from the flowers.

The methanolic leaf extract showed significant wound-healing activity in rat excision wound models, achieving ninety-two percent closure by day fourteen compared to seventy-eight percent in controls. Histological analysis confirmed increased collagen deposition and angiogenesis at wound sites.

The latex contains irritant compounds similar to those found in other Apocynaceae species. Contact dermatitis has been reported in sensitive individuals handling the plant material without protection.

Plumeria rubra bark extracts have demonstrated antipyretic activity in animal models at doses of two hundred to four hundred milligrams per kilogram body weight. The iridoid glycosides are believed to act through inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis pathways.

A 2019 study identified eleven volatile organic compounds from the flower essential oil, with linalool comprising thirty-one percent of total composition. The essential oil showed dose-dependent inhibition of acetylcholinesterase, suggesting potential nootropic applications.
