# Cangagua

## Description

Cangagua is the local Ecuadorian term for an iron-silica pan (silcrete/ferricrete composite) that lies approximately 3 metres below the surface of some tropical rainforests. When the overlying forest is cleared and topsoil is lost, this glassy, impermeable surface is exposed — creating what Mollison describes as "a daunting sight." Cangagua exemplifies the irreversible damage that can result from deforestation over hidden soil concretions in tropical regions.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil concretion / geological formation
- **Composition**: Iron-silica cemented pan
- **Regional name**: Cangagua (Ecuador)
- **General equivalents**: Silcrete, ferricrete, duripan
- **Geographic occurrence**: Tropical rainforest regions, notably Ecuador
- **Depth**: Approximately 3 metres below forest floor, following hill contours

## Key Facts

- Lies 3 m below the surface following hill contours in Ecuadorian tropical rainforest
- Composed of an iron-silica composite pan (both silcrete and ferricrete characteristics)
- Glassy and impermeable when exposed
- Topsoil loss after deforestation exposes the pan permanently
- Loss of forest cover to loss of topsoil to exposure of cangagua equals **irreversible land degradation**
- The glassy, impermeable nature prevents root penetration and water infiltration

## Management in Permaculture

- **Never clear forest** where cangagua is known to exist beneath
- The only sustainable land use is perpetual forest managed for non-timber products:
  - Honey, fruits, medicines, and other non-destructive harvests
- No rehabilitation method exists once cangagua is exposed
- Pre-development soil investigation is mandatory in tropical regions

## Relevance to Cultivation

Cangagua is Mollison's primary case study for why subsurface soil investigation is essential before any tropical land clearing. It demonstrates that the visible forest may be the only thing making the land productive — and once removed, the underlying geology may render the land permanently barren. This has profound implications for permaculture design in tropical zones: site assessment must go beyond surface observation to include subsurface probing, especially on hillsides in volcanic or tropical regions.
