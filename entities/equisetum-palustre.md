Unlike the common field horsetail (*E. arvense*), marsh horsetail is considered toxic to livestock due to its alkaloid content. It is one of the few horsetail species known to cause significant poisoning in grazing animals.

The species is a spore-bearing vascular plant that reproduces via wind-dispersed spores rather than seeds. It belongs to an ancient lineage of fern allies that has persisted since the Paleozoic era.

Marsh horsetail contains notable concentrations of silica in its stems, a characteristic shared across the Equisetaceae. This silica content historically made it useful as a polishing material and abrasives.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Equisetum palustre was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum. The genus name derives from the Latin *equus* (horse) and *seta* (bristle), referring to the horsetail-like appearance of the stems.

It belongs to subgenus Equisetum, the horsetail clade characterized by branched aerial stems. The specific epithet *palustre* means "of marshes," reflecting its preferred wetland habitat.

The sterile stems are green, erect, and reach 20 to 60 centimeters in height. They are prominently branched, with 6 to 14 branches per node arranged in whorls around the central stem.

Branches are slender, ascending, and themselves may bear secondary whorls of smaller branches. The central stem has 6 to 12 shallow ridges with broad grooves between them.

The sheath at each node is 3 to 7 millimeters long with dark teeth at the apex. These teeth are persistent and connate at the base, forming a low membrane between them.

Fertile stems are unbranched and shorter than sterile stems, reaching only 15 to 25 centimeters. They bear a terminal strobilus containing sporangiophores that release green spores in early summer.

Spores are spherical, approximately 40 to 50 micrometers in diameter, and bear four elaters. It thrives in carex-dominated sedge meadows and at the margins of ponds and ditches.

## Bioactive Compounds and Toxicity

Equisetum palustre is the most toxic horsetail species commonly encountered in European pastures. The primary toxic principle is palustrine, an alkaloid unique to this species within the genus.

Palustrine acts as a neurotoxin, causing muscle weakness, tremors, ataxia, and in severe cases, death in livestock. Horses are particularly susceptible, though cattle and sheep are also affected.

The alkaloid concentration varies seasonally, peaking during active growth in spring and early summer. Dried hay containing marsh horsetail retains toxicity, making contaminated forage dangerous year-round.
