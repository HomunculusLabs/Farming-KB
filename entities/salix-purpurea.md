## Overview

**Salix purpurea**, commonly known as purple willow or purple osier, is a deciduous shrub in the family Salicaceae native to Europe and parts of western Asia. It is widely cultivated for its colorful stems and valued as a basketry willow second only to *[[salix-viminalis]]*.

The species typically reaches 1–4 meters in height with slender, flexible rods that turn distinctive purple, red, or violet in winter. This winter stem color makes it a popular ornamental plant alongside its practical uses.

Purple willow is one of the most cold-hardy willows, thriving in climates where other basketry species fail. Its combination of ornamental value, cold tolerance, and practical utility makes it one of the most widely planted willows in [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]].

The species has a long history of cultivation, with archaeological evidence of willow basketry dating back over 8,000 years in Europe. Purple willow was among the first species managed by early agricultural communities for craft purposes.

Purple willow is increasingly used in short-rotation coppice bioenergy systems and phytoremediation projects. Its tolerance of waterlogged soils and heavy metal contamination suits it for environmental restoration applications.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Salix purpurea** L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The specific epithet *purpurea* refers to the purple or violet coloration of the young stems, which is the species' most distinctive field character.

The species belongs to *Salix* section *Vetrix* (subgenus *Vetrix*), closely related to *S. viminalis* and *S. daphnoides*. Natural hybrids with *S. viminalis* (*S. × smithiana*) and *S. caprea* (*S. × multinervis*) occur in the wild.

Purple willow forms a bushy, multi-stemmed shrub 1–4 m tall, occasionally reaching 5 m. The bark on older wood is gray and rough, while current-year stems are smooth, glossy, and vividly colored in shades of purple, red, or violet-green.

The root system is vigorous and spreading, producing dense networks of fine roots in the upper 30 cm of soil. This shallow, extensive root mat makes the species particularly effective at binding loose soil and stabilizing slopes.

Branching is typically ascending to spreading, creating a rounded to vase-shaped crown. The crown density varies with management, ranging from open and airy in neglected plants to dense and bushy in regularly coppiced stools.

Leaves are narrowly elliptic to lanceolate, 4–10 cm long and 0.8–2 cm wide, with entire or very finely toothed margins. The upper surface is dark green and glabrous; the lower surface is glaucous and may be slightly hairy along the midrib.

Flowers appear in early spring before or with the leaves. Both male and female catkins are relatively small, 1–3 cm long, appearing sessile or on short leafy shoots. Male flowers have 2 stamens with fused filaments — a key diagnostic feature.

The two-stamen condition is relatively uncommon among willows and distinguishes *S. purpurea* from most related species in section *Vetrix*, which typically have more stamens. This character is used in taxonomic keys.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Salix purpurea* is native to much of Europe, from the British Isles and Iberian Peninsula eastward to the Baltic states, Ukraine, and western Turkey. It also occurs in North Africa (Morocco, Algeria) and the Caucasus.

The species is found along riverbanks, stream margins, and in wet meadows and fens. It tolerates a wider range of soil moisture conditions than many willows, growing from permanently saturated to moderately dry sites.

Naturalized populations exist in North America, particularly in the northeastern United States and eastern Canada, where it has escaped from cultivation. It is also planted in Australia and New Zealand for soil conservation and ornamental purposes.

The species is cold-hardy to USDA zone 3 (−40°C), making it one of the most cold-tolerant willow species. This extreme cold tolerance extends its cultivation range well beyond most other basketry willows.

Unlike many *Salix* species, purple willow tolerates moderately dry soils once established, making it suitable for rain gardens and bioswales. It also withstands occasional salt spray, allowing coastal plantings in sheltered locations.

Propagation is primarily from hardwood cuttings, which root readily in moist soil. The species can also be grown from seed, though seed viability is brief and germination requires saturated conditions.

## Cultivation and Uses

Purple willow is a premier basketry species, prized for rods that are both colorful and highly flexible when soaked. The fine rods are especially valued for intricate weaving work, basket borders, and colored basket elements.

Rods are harvested annually in late winter before bud break, sorted by color and thickness, and stored damp until needed. The purple-red coloration is most intense on one-year-old rods and fades on older wood.

In bioenergy plantings, *S. purpurea* performs well on marginal land with poor drainage. Dry matter yields of 8–12 tonnes per hectare per year are achievable under short-rotation coppice management with 3-year cutting cycles.

The species has demonstrated significant phytoremediation potential, particularly for cadmium and zinc removal from [[fungal-competition-survival-contaminated-soils]]. It has been used successfully in riparian buffer zones to filter agricultural runoff containing nutrients and pesticides.

Ornamental cultivars including 'Nana' (dwarf form) and 'Pendula' (weeping form) are widely planted in gardens. The winter stem color provides landscape interest when most other shrubs are dormant.

In living willow structures, purple willow rods add color contrast alongside green-stemmed species. Garden designers frequently use it in mixed willow hedges and woven garden features.

The wood has a density of approximately 450–500 kg/m³ and is used for small turned items, tool handles, and [[mollison-designers-charcoal-production-biochar-soil-amendment]]. The flexible rods have historically been used for making coracles, fish traps, and cricket bats.

## Bioactive Compounds

The bark of *Salix purpurea* contains salicin and related phenolic glycosides, though at lower concentrations than *S. alba* or *S. purpurea*. Salicin content in bark averages 0.5–1.5% by dry weight.

Tannin content is relatively high at 6–12% by weight, making the bark a potential commercial source of vegetable tannins. The tannins are predominantly condensed catechin-type polymers.

Recent research has identified novel [[aact-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds-wood-composting-ingham]] in *S. purpurea* bark with antioxidant and [[mycelium-running-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties-species-chart]]. These include specific flavonoid glycosides and phenolic acid derivatives not found in other *Salix* species.

The distinctive purple stem coloration results from anthocyanin pigments, primarily cyanidin-based compounds. Anthocyanin concentration increases with exposure to ultraviolet radiation and cold temperatures.

Willow leaf rust (*Melampsora* spp.) can affect purple willow, though it shows moderate resistance compared with *S. viminalis*. [[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]] and bacterial crown gall are occasional problems in cultivation.
