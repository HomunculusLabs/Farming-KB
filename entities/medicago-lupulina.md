## Overview

Medicago lupulina, commonly known as black medick, is an annual or short-lived perennial legume in the family Fabaceae.

It is native to Europe, North Africa, and temperate Asia, and has become widely naturalized across North America, South America, Australia, and New Zealand.

The species is one of the most common and widely distributed Medicago species globally, occurring in lawns, pastures, roadsides, and disturbed ground.

Its small black ripe seed pods and resemblance to hop flowers contribute to both its common and scientific names.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Medicago lupulina was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 and is placed within the section Lupulinae of the genus Medicago.

The specific epithet lupulina refers to the hop-like appearance of the flower heads, drawing a parallel with [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of the world and is naturalized across North America, South America, southern Africa, Australia, and New Zealand.

The species occupies a remarkably wide ecological amplitude, occurring from sea level to approximately 2,500 meters elevation.

It is characteristic of disturbed habitats including lawns, pastures, meadows, agricultural fields, roadsides, riverbanks, and waste areas.

Black medick tolerates a broad range of soil conditions from acidic to alkaline and sandy to clay textures, though it prefers moderately fertile, well-drained soils.

It is particularly abundant in temperate regions with moderate to high rainfall and is a common weed component of maintained turfgrass.

## Ecology and Interactions

Medicago lupulina is a valuable resource for pollinators, providing nectar and pollen to honeybees, bumblebees, and various solitary bee species.

Its long flowering period from spring through autumn makes it one of the more consistently available floral resources in temperate landscapes.

The species is a host plant for the larvae of several butterfly species, most notably the common blue butterfly and clouded yellow butterfly.

Nitrogen fixation rates in black medick can reach 80 to 150 kilograms per hectare annually, making it a significant contributor to soil fertility in mixed communities.

Seeds possess hardseededness, a physical dormancy mechanism that allows persistence in soil seed banks for five to ten years or longer.

This hardseeded trait facilitates survival through unfavorable conditions and contributes to the species' success as a colonizer of disturbed sites.

Black medick forms [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]], which enhance phosphorus uptake and [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]] is moderate to good, with crude protein content of 18 to 24 percent in leaf tissue during vegetative growth stages.

Digestibility compares favorably with white clover in early growth stages but declines more rapidly as stems mature and lignify.

The species has been investigated as a potential ground cover in orchards and vineyards, where its low growth habit and shade tolerance provide soil protection.

Its drought tolerance is lower than that of some other annual medics, limiting its use in arid and semi-arid environments.
