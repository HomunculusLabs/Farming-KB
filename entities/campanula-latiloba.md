## Overview

Campanula latiloba is a robust herbaceous perennial in the family Campanulaceae, commonly known as the great bellflower. It produces tall spikes of large, open, violet-blue to white bell-shaped flowers in mid to late summer.

The species is valued in horticulture for its imposing stature and long bloom period. It is native to southeastern Europe and western Asia, where it grows in woodland margins and meadow clearings.

Campanula latiloba is sometimes confused with Campanula lactiflora but differs in its broader corolla lobes and denser inflorescence. It is one of the tallest bellflower species in cultivation.

The species has received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit, confirming its reliability and ornamental value in temperate gardens.

It was introduced to cultivation in Europe in the early nineteenth century from seeds collected in the Caucasus. Since then it has become a staple of cottage gardens and perennial borders across the temperate world.

The plant dies back completely to ground level in winter, re-emerging from the crown in mid-spring. New growth is bronze-tinted before maturing to green.

In the language of flowers, bellflowers traditionally symbolize gratitude and humility. Campanula latiloba's large blooms make it particularly suitable for cut-flower arrangements.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Campanula latiloba belongs to section Campanula (syn. section Medium) within the genus Campanula. The species was described by Alphonse de Candolle and is closely allied to Campanula lactiflora and Campanula alliariifolia.

A synonym is Campanula macrostachya W.Koch ex Trautv., which appears in some older floras. The species epithet "latiloba" refers to the broad corolla lobes that distinguish it from close relatives.

Plants reach 90–150 cm in height with erect, unbranched or sparsely branched flowering stems. The stem base is typically reddish and sparsely pubescent.

Basal leaves are large, cordate to ovate, coarsely toothed, and long-petiolate, forming substantial rosettes up to 40 cm across. They are mid-green and softly pubescent on both surfaces.

Cauline leaves are smaller, lanceolate to ovate, sessile or short-petiolate, and semi-clasping. The foliage is bright green and softly hairy on both surfaces.

Inflorescences are long, loose terminal racemes bearing 15–40 flowers. Each flower is subtended by a small linear bract that is shorter than the pedicel.

Individual flowers are campanulate, 3–5 cm long, usually violet-blue but occasionally white. The corolla is divided to about one-third of its length into five broadly triangular, reflexed lobes.

The calyx lobes are linear-lanceolate, acute, and about one-third the length of the corolla. They spread outward as the fruit matures.

The style is exserted beyond the corolla and curves downward at the tip. The stamens are attached near the base of the corolla tube, and the anthers are narrowly linear.

Capsules are nodding, ovoid, 8–12 mm long, and dehisce by three basal pores. Seeds are small, light brown, and elliptic, approximately 1 mm in length.

Chromosome number is 2n = 34, consistent with other members of section Campanula.

The species is diploid and shows regular meiosis, which contributes to its fertility in cultivation. Hybridization with other tall campanulas produces fertile offspring.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is native to the Caucasus region, northeastern Turkey, and northern Iran. It grows at elevations of 600–2,200 m in subalpine meadows, forest clearings, and along stream banks.

Campanula latiloba prefers moist, humus-rich soils in partial shade to full sun. It is hardy to USDA zones 5–8 and tolerates cold winters with reliable snow cover.

In its native habitat, it often colonizes recently disturbed ground such as avalanche chutes and stream margins. The species thrives in soils with pH ranging from neutral to slightly alkaline.

Populations in the Greater Caucasus tend to be more robust, with taller stems and larger flower heads. Turkish populations are somewhat smaller but more floriferous.

In cultivation it has naturalized sparingly in parts of western and central Europe, particularly in the United Kingdom. It is not considered invasive in any region of its introduced or native range.

The species is not considered threatened in its native range due to its wide distribution and ability to colonize disturbed habitats.

However, some isolated populations in northern Iran are restricted to small areas and could be vulnerable to habitat loss. No formal conservation assessment has been published by IUCN.

## Cultivation

Campanula latiloba thrives in fertile, moisture-retentive but well-drained soils. It performs best in full sun to partial shade with protection from hot afternoon sun in warmer climates.

Propagation is by seed sown in spring or by basal cuttings taken in early spring. Seeds require light for germination and should be surface-sown at 18–21 °C.

Germination typically occurs within 14–21 days under optimal conditions. Seed-grown plants may flower in their second year.

Division of established clumps is possible but difficult due to deep taproots. It is best attempted in early spring before new growth begins or in early autumn after flowering.

When transplanting, take care to dig deeply to preserve the taproot. Plants resent being moved once established and may sulk for a season.

The species is largely pest-free but can be affected by slugs on new growth in spring. Powdery mildew may occur on stressed plants in humid conditions with poor air circulation.

Deer and rabbits typically avoid Campanula latiloba due to its milky sap. This makes it a good choice for gardens in areas with heavy browsing pressure.

Notable cultivars include 'Gloaming' with soft lavender-pink flowers and 'White Crown' with pure white blooms. Both selections maintain the tall, stately habit of the species.

Plants benefit from staking in exposed sites due to their considerable height. Cutting stems back after flowering encourages a second, smaller flush of blooms in early autumn.

## Ecological Role

The large, open flowers attract a wide range of pollinators including bumblebees, honeybees, and solitary bees. The extended bloom period from July to September provides late-season nectar sources.

In its native range, Campanula latiloba grows in species-rich subalpine plant communities alongside geraniums, aconites, and other tall forbs. It serves as a larval host for several moth species in the Noctuidae family.

The deep taproot system helps stabilize soil on steep subalpine slopes. Plants persist for many years once established and gradually form impressive clumps.

The copious seed production supports granivorous birds and small mammals in autumn. Dead flower stems provide overwintering habitat for beneficial insects.

In garden settings, Campanula latiloba pairs well with other tall perennials such as delphiniums, verbascums, and aconites. Its vertical form provides architectural contrast to rounded and mounding plants.

The species has been used in breeding programs to introduce height and large flower size into hybrid bellflowers. Several inter-sectional crosses with Campanula persicifolia have been attempted.
