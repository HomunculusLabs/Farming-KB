# Andreaea petrophylla

## Overview

Andreaea petrophylla is a species of granite moss in the family Andreaeaceae, one of
the most ancient and morphologically distinctive lineages of mosses. The genus
Andreaea contains approximately 100 species that colonize exposed rock surfaces in
cold, mountainous, and polar environments.

Granite mosses are named for their characteristic growth on hard siliceous rocks
such as granite, quartzite, and gneiss. Andreaea petrophylla forms dark brown to
blackish, cushion-like colonies tightly adpressed to rock surfaces.

The species is notable for its primitive morphology, which lacks many features
common in more derived moss groups. It has no peristome teeth on its capsule and
the capsule dehisces by irregular longitudinal slits rather than an operculum.

Andreaea petrophylla and its relatives are among the earliest-diverging lineages
of extant mosses, providing important insights into the early evolution of
bryophyte morphology and ecology.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Andreaea petrophylla Ehrh. ex Hedw. is placed in the family Andreaeaceae, order
Andreaeales, class Andreaeopsida. This class is sister to all other mosses, making
it one of the most basal extant lineages within Bryophyta.

Plants form dense, dark brown to nearly black cushions 1 to 3 centimeters tall,
closely adpressed to the substrate. The cushions are typically compact and crustose
in appearance, firmly attached to the rock surface by rhizoids.

Stems are erect, sparingly branched, with a central strand. The leaves are small,
0.8 to 1.5 millimeters long, ovate to lanceolate, with a short or indistinct nerve.

The leaves are crisped and contorted when dry, appressed to the stem. When moist,
they spread outward but remain relatively stiff and incurved at the tips.

Leaf cells are small, quadrate to short-rectangular, with thickened cell walls.
The lamina is unistratose throughout, with no differentiated alar cells.

The capsule is sessile or nearly so, elevated on a very short pseudopodium derived
from gametophytic tissue rather than a true sporophytic seta. This is a distinctive
feature of Andreaeaceae, as most other mosses have capsules borne on a true seta.

The capsule is globose to ovoid, with a rudimentary operculum. Dehiscence occurs
by four to eight irregular longitudinal slits that split the capsule wall, a
mechanism unique to Andreaeaceae among mosses.

Spores are spherical, 10 to 16 micrometers in diameter, finely papillose.
Spore dispersal occurs gradually as the capsule walls split progressively.

## Distribution and Habitat

Andreaea petrophylla has a wide distribution in cold-temperate and boreal regions
of the Northern Hemisphere. It occurs throughout montane and arctic Europe, Asia,
and North America.

In Europe, the species is found in Scandinavia, the British Isles, the Alps,
Carpathians, and other mountain ranges. It extends into arctic regions including
Svalbard, Iceland, and northern Russia.

In North America, it occurs across Canada and Alaska, southward through the Rocky
Mountains and Appalachian Mountains. It is particularly common in the arctic
tundra zone.

The species is an obligate lithophyte, growing almost exclusively on hard
siliceous rocks including granite, quartzite, gneiss, and sandstone. It is rarely
found on calcareous substrates.

Andreaea petrophylla colonizes exposed rock surfaces, ledges, boulders, and cliff
faces. It prefers open, well-drained sites with high humidity but is tolerant of
full sun and extreme desiccation in high-elevation and high-latitude environments.

It is found from near sea level in arctic regions to over 3000 meters in alpine
zones. The species is often a dominant component of the biological soil crust
community on exposed rock in cold environments.

## Physiology and Adaptations

Andreaea petrophylla possesses several physiological adaptations suited to its
exposed, high-stress habitat on rock surfaces. Its dark pigmentation, caused by
anthocyanin and other flavonoid compounds, provides protection against ultraviolet
radiation at high elevations.

The moss exhibits considerable desiccation tolerance, surviving prolonged dry
periods on exposed rock faces. Recovery upon rehydration is slower than in
specialized desiccation-tolerant species like Tortula ruralis, requiring hours
rather than minutes for full photosynthetic recovery.

The sessile capsule on a pseudopodium is an adaptation to exposed habitats. By
minimizing the length of the fertile stalk, the capsule is less vulnerable to
damage from wind and ice on exposed rock surfaces.

## Ecology

Andreaea petrophylla is a pioneer species on exposed rock surfaces in cold
environments. It plays a key role in the initial stages of lithosere succession,
contributing to rock weathering and soil formation.

The moss contributes to biological weathering through the production of organic
acids that slowly dissolve mineral surfaces. Over time, accumulated organic matter
from dead moss tissue creates thin soil pockets that support colonization by other
organisms.

In arctic and alpine communities, Andreaea petrophylla is often found growing
alongside lichens, particularly crustose species of Rhizocarpon and
Acarospora. These communities form important biological soil crusts that stabilize
surfaces and influence nutrient cycling.

The species supports limited microfaunal communities, including tardigrades,
nematodes, and mites that inhabit the moss cushions. These invertebrate
communities are more diverse in Andreaea cushions than on bare rock surfaces.

Andreaea petrophylla is sensitive to air pollution and is absent from areas with
significant atmospheric contamination. Its presence in mountain environments
can serve as a bioindicator of clean air conditions.
