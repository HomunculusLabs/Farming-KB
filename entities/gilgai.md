## Description

Gilgais are natural surface depressions or micro-relief features formed in soils with high contents of expansive clays (particularly smectites). They are sometimes called "crabholes," "melonholes," or "self-mulching soils." The term comes from the Kamilaroi Aboriginal language of Australia. Gilgais consist of alternating mounds and depressions that create a natural pattern of water collection and soil variation across the landscape.

## Formation Process

- Gilgais form through the repeated wetting and drying (shrink-swell) cycles of expansive clay soils.
- During wet periods, clay absorbs water and expands; during dry periods, it shrinks and cracks.
- Over many cycles, the surface develops a regular pattern of elevated mounds (microknolls) and depressions (microlows).
- The depressions collect water, organic matter, and fine particles, while the mounds shed material.
- This process can continue for thousands of years, producing pronounced microtopography.

## Classification

- **Geomorphic feature**: A type of patterned ground or microrelief.
- **Associated soils**: Vertisols (USDA) or Vertosols (Australian classification) — cracking clay soils.
- **Distribution**: Widespread in arid and semi-arid regions worldwide, particularly Australia, India, Africa, and parts of the Americas.

## Key Facts

- Gilgais function as natural swales, collecting and infiltrating run-off water in arid landscapes.
- The depression areas typically have deeper, more fertile, and less saline topsoil than the mounds.
- The alternating wet-dry microenvironments support different plant communities within a small area.
- Mollison describes gilgais as "natural swales developed from pockets of expanding clays" that form part of the arid-zone water harvesting potential.

## Ecological Role

- **Water concentration**: In arid regions, gilgai depressions are among the few locations where water persists after rain, supporting vegetation that cannot survive on surrounding flats.
- **Species diversity**: The moisture and nutrient gradients between mound and depression create microhabitat diversity that supports a wider range of plant and animal species than uniform terrain.
- **Salt distribution**: In saline landscapes, salt tends to concentrate on the mounds while the depressions remain relatively less saline, creating refugia for salt-sensitive species.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Natural [[permaculture]] emulates through constructed swales.
- **Soil moisture management**: Understanding gilgai formation helps land managers work with, rather than against, natural soil behaviour in clay-rich regions.
- **Dryland aquaculture**: Mollison notes that gilgais and claypans host aquatic and swamp plant species adapted to "very hot and somewhat brackish or salty water," making them potential sites for small-scale aquaculture in deserts.
- **Species selection**: Plants suited to gilgai depressions (waterlogging tolerant) differ from those on gilgai mounds (drought tolerant), informing mosaic planting strategies.
- **Indicator of soil type**: The presence of gilgais signals expansive clay soils, which have specific management requirements (timing of cultivation, [[tagari-publications]], pp. 433–434.
