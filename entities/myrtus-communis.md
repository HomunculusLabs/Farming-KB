# Myrtus communis

## Overview

Myrtus communis, commonly known as true myrtle or common myrtle, is an evergreen shrub in the family Myrtaceae native to the Mediterranean basin. It has been revered since antiquity as a sacred plant of Aphrodite and Venus, symbolizing love, beauty, and fertility in classical mythology.

The species is valued for its aromatic leaves and berries, both of which are used in culinary, medicinal, and perfumery applications. Myrtle essential oil, distilled from the leaves and twigs, is an important ingredient in traditional Mediterranean herbal medicine and modern aromatherapy.

Myrtle plays a significant ecological role in Mediterranean maquis and garrigue ecosystems, providing food and habitat for numerous bird and insect species. It is also widely cultivated as an [[hamilton-ornamental-shrub-care]] in gardens worldwide, appreciated for its glossy foliage, fragrant white flowers, and adaptability to pruning.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Myrtus communis L. is the sole species in the genus Myrtus, family Myrtaceae, order Myrtales. Two subspecies are generally recognized: subsp. communis (the typical form) and subsp. tarentina, a more compact dwarf form with smaller leaves native to southern Italy.

The plant is an evergreen shrub typically growing 1-5 meters tall with a dense, bushy habit. Bark is smooth and grayish-brown on young branches, becoming rough and furrowed with age. The wood is hard and close-grained, historically used for turnery and tool handles.

Leaves are opposite, ovate to lanceolate, 2-5 cm long and 1-2 cm wide, with a leathery texture and glossy dark green upper surface. The leaf margins are entire and slightly revolute. When crushed, the leaves release a characteristic aromatic fragrance due to their essential oil content.

Flowers are solitary, axillary, hermaphroditic, with five white petals and numerous prominent stamens extending 1-2 cm beyond the petals. The flowers are intensely fragrant and bloom from May to July across the Mediterranean range. The distinctive brush-like stamens are a characteristic feature of the Myrtaceae family.

Fruits are globose berries 1-1.5 cm in diameter, initially green ripening to dark blue-black or purplish-black. Each berry contains 3-15 small kidney-shaped seeds embedded in a juicy pulp. The berries are edible with a slightly aromatic, sweet-tart flavor.

## Distribution and Habitat

Myrtus communis is native to the Mediterranean region, extending from southern Portugal and Morocco eastward to Turkey, Syria, and Iran. It is particularly abundant in the western Mediterranean, where it forms a dominant component of the maquis vegetation.

The species occurs from sea level to 800 meters elevation, typically on dry, rocky hillsides, limestone outcrops, and in scrubland communities. It thrives in Mediterranean climates with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters, tolerating temperatures from -5 to 40 degrees Celsius.

Myrtle is adaptable to various soil types including calcareous, sandy, and volcanic substrates, though it prefers well-drained soils. It demonstrates moderate [[fungal-salt-tolerance-and-ion-homeostasis]], enabling growth in coastal environments. The species resprouts vigorously after fire, a key survival trait in fire-prone [[fungal-diversity-mediterranean-ecosystems]].

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

The leaves of Myrtus communis contain 0.5-1.5% essential oil composed primarily of alpha-pinene, 1,8-cineole, limonene, linalool, myrtenyl acetate, and myrtenol. The oil composition varies significantly with geographic origin, harvest time, and plant part, with myrtenyl acetate being the dominant compound in some chemotypes.

[[aact-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds-wood-composting-ingham]] in the leaves include myricetin, quercetin, kaempferol, and their glycoside derivatives, along with galloyl derivatives and hydrolyzable tannins. The berries are rich in anthocyanins, primarily delphinidin-3-glucoside and malvidin-3-glucoside, responsible for their dark pigmentation.

Antimicrobial activity of myrtle essential oil has been demonstrated against Gram-positive bacteria including [[staphylococcus-aureus]] and Streptococcus pyogenes, as well as [[bloomfield-candida-albicans-yeast-hyphal-dimorphism]] and [[aspergillus-niger]]. This supports the traditional use of myrtle preparations for treating respiratory and skin infections.

Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties are attributed to the combined action of essential oil terpenes and phenolic compounds. In vitro studies show inhibition of cyclooxygenase-2 and nitric oxide production in macrophage cell lines, with IC50 values comparable to standard anti-inflammatory drugs.

Hypoglycemic effects have been documented in animal studies, where aqueous leaf extracts reduced blood glucose levels in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats. The mechanism may involve enhanced insulin secretion and peripheral glucose uptake, though clinical validation in humans remains limited.

Antispasmodic activity of myrtle extracts has been demonstrated on isolated intestinal smooth muscle preparations, providing pharmacological support for the traditional use in treating gastrointestinal cramps and colic. Myrtenol, a major constituent of the essential oil, has been identified as the primary spasmolytic compound.

## Culinary and Traditional Use

Myrtle berries are used as a spice in Mediterranean and Middle Eastern cuisines, particularly in Sardinia, Corsica, and Lebanon. In Sardinia, they flavor the traditional liqueur mirto rosso (red) and mirto bianco (white), made by macerating berries or leaves in alcohol.

The leaves are used to wrap cheese and meat during grilling, imparting a subtle aromatic flavor. Dried myrtle leaves can be substituted for bay leaves in some recipes, offering a more delicate, sweeter fragrance. The berries are also used in meat marinades, stuffing mixtures, and fruit preserves.

In Corsican cuisine, myrtle berries are a key ingredient in fiadone cheesecake and are used to flavor cured meats. Turkish cuisine incorporates myrtle in the preparation of pickle relishes and fruit desserts. The berries yield a dark purple food coloring used in confectionery and beverage production.

In traditional Mediterranean medicine, myrtle preparations have been used to treat respiratory ailments including bronchitis, sinusitis, and whooping cough. The leaves were brewed as a tea for digestive complaints, and the berries consumed as a remedy for diarrhea and dysentery.

Myrtle extract has been used topically as an astringent for wound healing and as a mouthwash for gum inflammation. The essential oil is applied in aromatherapy for its calming and antiseptic properties, and as a component of perfumes since ancient Egyptian and Roman times.

## Cultivation

Myrtus communis is widely cultivated as an ornamental shrub in Mediterranean, subtropical, and [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]]. The species tolerates pruning well and is commonly shaped into formal hedges, topiary specimens, and bonsai. Cultivars include variegated forms, compact dwarf varieties, and selections with particularly abundant flowering.

Propagation is achieved through seed, semi-hardwood cuttings, and layering. Seeds require cold stratification for optimal germination. Cuttings taken in late summer root readily under mist propagation with bottom heat. The plant prefers full sun to partial shade and well-drained soil.

Established plants are drought-tolerant and require minimal irrigation in Mediterranean climates. Fertilization needs are low, though a balanced application in spring supports vigorous growth. Few serious pests or diseases affect cultivated myrtle, though scale insects and red [[cervantes-spider-mites-identification-control]] may occur under glasshouse conditions.

Hardiness varies among provenances, with western Mediterranean populations tolerating temperatures to -10 degrees Celsius. In colder climates, myrtle can be grown in containers and overwintered indoors. The plant responds well to renovation pruning and can regenerate from old wood after severe cutting back.

## Cultural and Historical Significance

Myrtle has been a symbol of love, beauty, and immortality across Mediterranean cultures for over three millennia. [[psychedelic-use-in-ancient-greece]], myrtle wreaths were awarded to victors in athletic games and worn by brides at weddings. The plant was sacred to Aphrodite, who was said to have emerged from a myrtle grove.

Roman emperors used myrtle branches as symbols of supreme authority, and Roman brides carried myrtle sprigs in their bouquets. The Roman naturalist Pliny the Elder documented numerous medicinal applications of myrtle in his Natural History, reflecting its central role in classical herbalism.
