## Description

Organic mulch consists of natural materials derived from once-living organisms that are placed on top of soil to reduce erosion, prevent weed growth, insulate plants, and -- critically in the soil food web approach -- establish or supplement specific members of the soil food web. Unlike inorganic mulches (plastic sheeting, gravel), organic mulches decompose over time, feeding soil organisms and cycling nutrients back into the soil. In the soil food web gardening system described by Lowenfels and Lewis, organic mulch is one of the three primary tools (alongside compost and compost tea) for restoring and maintaining healthy soil biology. By selecting the right type of mulch -- green or brown, coarse or fine, wet or dry -- gardeners can establish either fungal or bacterial dominance to match the needs of their plants.

## Classification

- **Type:** Soil management technique / soil food web tool
- **Category:** Organic ground cover
- **Materials:** Leaves, leaf mold, aged pine needles, grass clippings, aged bark, wood chips, straw, well-rotted manure, seaweed, plant remnants, paper
- **Function:** Erosion control, weed suppression, insulation, moisture retention, soil food web management

## Key Facts

- **Soil food web gardening tool:** Organic mulch is one of three primary tools (with compost and compost tea) for soil food web management. It provides nutrients and habitat for soil organisms, supporting a living soil ecosystem.

- **Fungal vs. bacterial control:** The type of mulch determines which soil organisms dominate:
  - **Brown mulch** (aged leaves, bark, wood chips) supports fungal dominance
  - **Green mulch** (fresh grass clippings) supports bacterial dominance
  - This follows Rule #6: brown materials support fungi, green materials support bacteria

- **Cold compost:** Mulch is essentially a form of cold compost. It does not heat up like a compost pile, but it decays over a longer period. By providing different kinds of organic matter as mulch, gardeners can establish or supplement different members of the soil food web.

- **Worm and arthropod habitat:** Worms pull mulch material into underground dens for shredding, producing nutrient-rich castings, worm tunnels, better water retention, and improved aeration. Micro- and macroarthropods live in mulches, speeding decay and attracting other food web members.

- **Nutrient immobilization:** Mulches can cause a feeding frenzy by bacteria and fungi that ties up nitrogen, sulfur, phosphate, and other nutrients at the soil surface. This actually helps control shallow-rooted weeds, while deeper-rooted crop plants access nutrients below the mulch-soil interface.

## Types of Organic Mulch

**Brown (Fungal-Supporting) Mulches:**
- **Autumn leaves:** The preferred brown mulch; support fungal dominance unless ground too finely (which opens them to bacteria). Leaf mulches grow fungi faster than wood chips. Decompose in approximately 6 months.
- **Wood chips and shredded bark:** Aged chips are excellent brown mulches. Bark chips last 3-4 years due to high lignin and hemicellulose content. Should be mixed with nitrogen source to prevent nitrogen robbing.
- **Aged pine needles:** Good mulch but must be aged first to allow terpenes (toxic to many plants) to dissipate.
- **Sawdust:** Can be used but has very high C:N ratio (500:1); must be supplemented with nitrogen.
- **Peat moss:** Biologically sterile; should be mixed with other materials to introduce microbiology.

**Green (Bacterial-Supporting) Mulches:**
- **Fresh grass clippings:** The most readily available green mulch, containing necessary sugars to attract and feed bacteria. Must avoid clippings from lawns treated with pesticides or herbicides, or from yards with dogs. Should not be piled too thickly to avoid anaerobic composting.
- **Fresh-picked weeds and green plant material:** High in easy-to-digest sugars and nitrogen.

**Other Organic Mulches:**
- **Straw:** Moderate C:N ratio, good general-purpose mulch
- **Seaweed:** Rich in micronutrients and minerals
- **Paper and cardboard:** High carbon content, good for sheet mulching

## Rules for Mulch Application (from Lowenfels and Lewis)

- **Rule #6:** Aged, brown organic materials support fungi; fresh, green organic materials support bacteria.
- **Rule #7:** Mulch laid on the surface tends to support fungi, while mulch worked into the soil tends to support bacteria.
- **Rule #8:** Wet, ground-up mulch supports bacterial colonization (bacteria need moisture; increased surface area from grinding aids colonization). Some bacteria produce antibiotics that suppress fungal growth.
- **Rule #9:** Coarse, dry mulches support fungal activity. Mulches with less than 35% moisture are considered "dry mulches."

## Application Guidelines

- **Thickness:** 2-3 inches (5-7.5 cm) maximum. Thicker layers can block moisture and air, and smother mycorrhizal fungi.
- **Placement:** Do not place mulch snug against stems or trunks; this causes microbial decay of the plant itself. Back off several inches from the base.
- **Wood chip size:** Use chips 3/8 inch or larger to minimize bacterial nitrogen immobilization at the soil-mulch interface.
- **Compost combination:** Mulches work best when used in conjunction with compost -- apply compost first, then mulch on top.

## Mulch Duration by Material

| Material | Approximate Duration |
|---|---|
| Bark chips | 3-4 years |
| Wood chips | 1-3 years |
| Leaves | 6 months (complete decomposition) |
| Grass clippings | 1-3 months |
| Straw | 6-12 months |
| Pine needles | 1-2 years |

## Nitrogen Management with Mulch

The nitrogen robbing concern is real but often overstated:
- Primarily occurs at the thin interface of soil and mulch
- Rarely affects the rhizosphere or organisms below the surface
- Minimized by using wood chips 3/8 inch or larger (less bacterial colonization)
- Can be prevented by mixing a nitrogen source (green grass, alfalfa meal) with brown mulches

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Fungal habitat creation:** Brown mulches create surface conditions ideal for fungal growth and mycelial networks. This is directly relevant for outdoor mushroom cultivation and for establishing mycorrhizal associations with plants.

- **Moisture management:** Mulch maintains consistent soil moisture, which is critical for mycelial growth in outdoor beds and for maintaining active soil food webs that support plant health.

- **Weed suppression in mushroom beds:** Thick organic mulch suppresses competing vegetation around outdoor mushroom patches and in forest garden settings.

- **Terpene awareness:** Pine needles and cedar chips contain terpenes toxic to many plants and fungi. These must be aged before use around sensitive crops or mushroom beds.

- **Soil food web synergy:** Mulch combined with compost and compost tea creates the most robust soil food web. The compost inoculates with biology, the mulch provides habitat and food, and the compost tea supplements microbial populations.

## Cautions

- **Avoid herbicide-contaminated materials:** Grass clippings from treated lawns can carry persistent herbicides (clopyralid, aminopyralid) that damage vegetables and fungi.
- **Avoid dog-contaminated materials:** Grass from yards with dogs should not be used as mulch.
- **Avoid cedar chips:** High terpene content is toxic to many plants.
- **Avoid anaerobic conditions:** Grass clippings piled too thickly can go anaerobic, producing alcohols that kill plant cells at concentrations as low as 1 ppm.
