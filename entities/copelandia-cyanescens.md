# Copelandia cyanescens

**Blue Meanies** | *Copelandia cyanescens* (Berk. & Broome) Singer

Copelandia cyanescens is a potent psilocybin-containing mushroom native to tropical and subtropical regions worldwide. It is one of the most psychoactive fungi known, consistently containing high concentrations of [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]] relative to other psilocybian species. The species is widely known by the common name "blue meanies," a reference to the intense blue bruising reaction that occurs when the mushroom is handled or damaged. Along with [[psilocybe-cubensis]], C. cyanescens is one of the two most commonly encountered psychoactive mushrooms in tropical regions.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** bolbitiaceae
- **Genus:** Copelandia

The species was originally described as Agaricus cyanescens by Miles Joseph Berkeley and Christopher Edmund Broome in 1871, based on specimens collected in Sri Lanka (then Ceylon). Rolf Singer transferred it to the genus Panaeolus as Panaeolus cyanescens, and later proposed the genus Copelandia to accommodate it and related species characterized by their coprophilous habit, mottled gills, and strong blueing reaction. The taxonomic status of Copelandia remains debated — some modern authorities treat it as a section within Panaeolus (Panaeolus sect. Copelandia), while others maintain it as a separate genus.

Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (1998) listed 12 species in the genus Copelandia in their worldwide distribution of neurotropic fungi, making it the second most species-rich genus of psilocybin-containing fungi after Psilocybe.

## Morphology

### Cap
The pileus measures 1.5 to 4 cm in diameter, hemispherical to campanulate (bell-shaped) when young, expanding to convex or broadly convex with age, often retaining a small umbo. The cap surface is smooth and hygrophanous — when moist it is dark brown to chestnut-brown, sometimes with a bluish tint, becoming pale grayish-brown to nearly white when dry. The margin is slightly translucent-striate when moist and often slightly wavy or undulating. The surface is remarkably fragile and easily damaged, readily bruising an intense blue or blue-black where touched.

### Gills
The gills are adnexed to free, close, and broad, with a characteristic mottled (variegated) pattern as spores mature asynchronously. The gills are initially gray but become black as spores mature, with the mottled appearance created by the mixture of dark, mature basidiospores and pale, immature ones. The gill edges are white and fimbriate (fringed). They bruise blue when touched.

### Stem
The stipe is 4 to 12 cm long and 2 to 4 mm thick, cylindrical, equal or slightly thickened at the base, and hollow. It is white to pale grayish, smooth, and pruinose (covered with a fine frosty powder) near the apex. The entire stipe bruises intensely blue to blue-black when handled — this reaction is one of the most pronounced among all psilocybin-containing mushrooms and is a key identification feature. There is no annulus (ring).

### Flesh and Spores
The flesh is thin, whitish to pale grayish, and stains blue almost immediately upon exposure. The odor is farinaceous (mealy or flour-like) to slightly fruity. The taste is farinaceous. The spore print is black. Basidiospores are ellipsoid to lemon-shaped, 11 to 17 by 7 to 12 µm, smooth (unlike the rough spores of Panaeolina), thick-walled, with a conspicuous germ pore.

## Distribution and Habitat

Copelandia cyanescens has a pantropical and subtropical distribution, found throughout the Caribbean, Central and South America, tropical Africa, South and Southeast Asia, Australia, and the Pacific Islands. In the United States, it is found in Hawaii, Florida, and along the Gulf Coast. The species has been introduced to some temperate regions through the horticultural trade, particularly in association with imported livestock.

The species is strictly coprophilous (dung-inhabiting), growing primarily on the dung of herbivorous mammals, especially cattle, horses, and water buffalo. It may also be found on enriched soil in pastures and fields where dung has decomposed. Fruiting occurs throughout the year in tropical climates, triggered by rainfall and high humidity. In subtropical areas, it fruits during the warm and wet seasons.

The natural substrate preference for herbivore dung has made C. cyanescens a common find in cattle pastures throughout the tropics, where it often grows in abundance after heavy rains.

## Chemistry and Potency

Copelandia cyanescens is among the most potent of all psilocybin-containing mushrooms. Chemical analyses have consistently reported high concentrations of [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]]:

- **Psilocybin:** 0.3% to 1.3% of dry weight (reported range varies by study)
- **Psilocin:** 0.1% to 0.6% of dry weight
- **Baeocystin:** present in detectable quantities in most analyses

These concentrations are significantly higher than those typically found in [[psilocybe-cubensis]] (0.3% to 1.0% psilocybin) and comparable to or exceeding those of [[psilocybe-azurescens]]. The high potency, combined with the relatively small size of individual mushrooms, means that a relatively small number of specimens can produce a strong psychoactive experience.

The intense blue bruising reaction is caused by the oxidation of psilocin (and possibly other phenolic compounds) when the tissue is damaged, forming blue-colored quinone polymers. The degree of blueing is not necessarily proportional to psilocybin content but is generally more pronounced in species with higher total indole alkaloid concentrations.

## Ethnopharmacology

Copelandia cyanescens has a long history of use among indigenous peoples in tropical regions:

- **Southeast Asia:** Used traditionally in parts of Thailand, Cambodia, and Indonesia, where it grows abundantly in water buffalo pastures
- **Hawaii:** Historically used and continues to be collected for recreational and spiritual purposes
- **Caribbean:** Found throughout the islands, where it has been used both traditionally and in modern contexts
- **Australia:** Found in subtropical eastern Australia, particularly in Queensland and northern New South Wales

In modern contexts, C. cyanescens is one of the most commonly used natural psilocybin sources in tropical regions. The "blue meanies" name has become widely recognized in recreational drug culture, referring to the intense blueing and the strong effects produced.

## Cultivation

Copelandia cyanescens can be cultivated but is generally considered more challenging to grow than [[psilocybe-cubensis]]. The primary methods include:

- **Spawning to pasteurized dung or composted manure** — the natural substrate, providing the most consistent results
- **Straw-based substrates** — pasteurized wheat or rice straw can serve as an alternative
- **Grain spawn** — used to inoculate bulk substrates

Key challenges in cultivation include the species' sensitivity to contamination, its requirement for warm tropical temperatures (25 to 30 degrees C for optimal growth), and its relatively long colonization times compared to P. cubensis. The thin flesh and small size of the mushrooms mean that yields per unit of substrate are lower than for P. cubensis, though the higher potency partially compensates for this.

## Similar Species

- **[[copelandia-cambodginiensis]]**: Very similar but smaller, with a more pronounced campanulate cap; also highly potent
- **copelandia tropicalis**: Found in similar habitats, distinguished by subtle morphological differences
- **panaeolus cinctulus**: Also mottled-gilled but does not blue as intensely and has lower potency
- **[[psilocybe-cubensis]]**: Much larger, grows on dung but has a different cap shape and gill structure; lower potency
- **Panaeolus antillarum**: A common lookalike in tropical pastures that does not contain psilocybin and does not bruise blue

The combination of the intense blue bruising, mottled gills, coprophilous habit, and tropical distribution makes C. cyanescens relatively straightforward to identify in the field, though confusion with inactive Panaeolus species is possible and caution should be exercised.

## Seasonality and Collection Tips

Copelandia cyanescens fruits throughout the year in truly tropical climates, with peak fruiting correlated with the rainy season. In subtropical areas such as Florida and Hawaii, it fruits primarily from late spring through autumn. The mushrooms typically appear 2 to 5 days after significant rainfall and can persist for several days if conditions remain humid. Key collection considerations include:

- Search cattle pastures and horse paddocks 1 to 3 days after heavy rain
- Look on well-rotted dung pats, not fresh ones
- The intense blue bruising upon handling is the most reliable field indicator
- Be prepared for rapid deterioration — specimens should be processed quickly after collection
- Always check for the mottled (variegated) gill pattern and black spore print

## Conservation Status

Copelandia cyanescens is not considered threatened. Its pantropical distribution, association with domestic livestock, and ability to grow in human-modified environments ensure abundant habitat. However, changes in agricultural practices, particularly the increased use of anti-helminthic drugs in livestock that pass through dung and may affect fungal growth, could potentially impact populations in the long term.

## Historical and Etymological Notes

The specific epithet "cyanescens" means "becoming blue" in Latin, referring to the species' most distinctive characteristic — the intense blue bruising reaction. The common name "blue meanies" became widely known through the recreational psychedelic community in Australia and Hawaii during the 1970s and 1980s, when the species was one of the most commonly used natural psilocybin sources in those regions. The name is a cultural reference unrelated to the Beatles' animated film "Yellow Submarine," though the coincidence of the blue color theme is often noted.

## Legal Status

Copelandia cyanescens is illegal in many countries under laws that prohibit psilocybin and psilocybin-containing fungi. In the United States, it is treated the same as other psilocybin mushrooms under federal and most state laws. In the Netherlands, following the 2008 ban on psilocybin mushrooms, Copelandia species were specifically included in the prohibition. In some tropical countries where the species is endemic, enforcement of laws against possession is often minimal, and the mushrooms continue to be used traditionally.
