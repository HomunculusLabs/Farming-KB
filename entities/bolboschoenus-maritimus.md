## Overview

**Bolboschoenus maritimus**, commonly known as sea clubrush or saltmarsh clubrush, is a perennial sedge with a [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], it ranges from coastal Alaska to California and across the continent to the Atlantic seaboard.

It is common in coastal salt marshes, brackish ditches, and estuarine margins.

Inland populations occupy alkaline and saline wetlands, including playas and prairie potholes.

The species also occurs in freshwater margins of lakes, rivers, and irrigation canals.

It tolerates a wide range of salinities, from fresh to hypersaline conditions.

Elevation ranges from sea level to approximately 2,000 m in interior basins of western North America.

## Ecology

Bolboschoenus maritimus is a clonal species that spreads aggressively through rhizomatous growth and tuber production.

Tubers serve as both storage organs and vegetative propagules, enabling rapid colonization of disturbed sites.

The species forms dense monospecific stands that can exclude other wetland vegetation.

It provides habitat and food for waterfowl, especially during autumn migration when tubers are an important food source.

Muskrats and other semi-aquatic mammals consume both tubers and culm bases.

The dense root network stabilizes sediments and reduces erosion in coastal and riparian zones.

Bolboschoenus maritimus is an indicator species for moderately saline wetland conditions.

## Ethnobotany and Uses

The starchy tubers of Bolboschoenus maritimus have been consumed by Indigenous peoples in several regions.

[[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] groups in the Great Basin and California harvested and roasted tubers as a carbohydrate source.

In parts of Asia, the tubers are collected as a famine food and occasionally sold in local markets.
