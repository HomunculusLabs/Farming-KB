## Overview

Deparia acrostichoides, commonly known as the silvery glade fern or silvery spleenwort, is a deciduous terrestrial fern native to eastern North America and East Asia. It is recognized by its broadly triangular fronds and the distinctive silvery-white scales on the rachis and costa beneath.

The species was historically placed in the genus Athyrium and is still frequently encountered under the name Athyrium acrostichoides in older literature. Molecular phylogenetic studies supported its transfer to the genus Deparia in the late 20th century.

Gardeners and horticultural references still widely use the Athyrium name, which can cause confusion when selecting plants from nurseries. Both names refer to the same species, and plants sold under either name are identical.

Silvery glade fern typically grows 40 to 100 centimeters tall and forms loose colonies through its short-creeping rhizomes. It is most commonly found in rich moist woods, ravine bottoms, and along stream banks in forested regions.

The combination of large size, attractive silvery indument, and tolerance of cultivation has made this species increasingly popular in shade gardens. It provides bold texture in woodland plantings and pairs well with finer-textured ferns and shade perennials.

The common name silvery glade fern refers both to the silvery scales on the frond undersurface and the species' preference for forest glades and openings. The alternative name silvery spleenwort reflects its former classification in the spleenwort group.

Mature plants typically produce 10 to 30 fronds per season, forming an open vase-shaped clump. The overall effect is lighter and airier than that of more densely packed fern species such as Dryopteris or Polystichum.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Deparia acrostichoides was first described as Aspidium acrostichoides by André Michaux in 1803. It has been shuffled through several genera including Nephrodium, Diplazium, and Athyrium before its current placement in Deparia by Masahiro Kato in 1984.

The species is tetraploid with a chromosome number of 2n = 160. This polyploid origin likely contributes to its morphological variability across its geographic range and its adaptability to diverse forest habitats.

Fronds are 40 to 100 centimeters long and 20 to 50 centimeters wide, broadly triangular to ovate in outline. The stipe is 15 to 40 centimeters long, green to straw-colored, and bears scattered brown scales at the base.

The most distinctive feature is the dense covering of silvery-white, membranous scales on the rachis and costae beneath the pinnae. These scales give the undersurface of the frond a characteristic silvery sheen that is visible when fronds are viewed from below.

The scales are lanceolate and 2 to 4 millimeters long, with a clathrate cell pattern visible under magnification. This type of scale structure is shared with other Deparia species and helps distinguish the genus from morphologically similar Athyrium taxa.

The lamina is bipinnate to bipinnate-pinnatifid, with 10 to 20 pairs of pinnae that are lanceolate and taper to a point. Pinnae are deeply cut, with the lower pinnae being the largest and directed somewhat forward.

Sori are elongate and curved, following the vein tips along the undersides of the pinnae. The indusium is attached along one side and is initially whitish, becoming brown as spores mature.

Spores are brown, monolete, and produced from June through September. Germination occurs on moist soil surfaces within forest interiors, with gametophyte development requiring 4 to 8 weeks under favorable humidity.

## Distribution and Habitat

Deparia acrostichoides occurs across eastern North America from Ontario and Quebec south to Georgia and Alabama, and west to Minnesota and Arkansas. Disjunct Asian populations are found in Japan, Korea, and northeastern China.

The species is most abundant in the Appalachian region, the Great Lakes states, and the Ozark and Ouachita highlands. It becomes less common in the Atlantic coastal plain and is absent from the Gulf Coast states.

Silvery glade ferns are plants of mesic to moist forests, favoring rich woods with deep organic soils. Typical habitats include coves, ravines, stream terraces, and north-facing slopes in deciduous or mixed forests.

The species shows a strong association with calcareous substrates in parts of its range, particularly in the Appalachian Valley and Ridge province. On these limestone-derived soils it can form extensive colonies on talus slopes and cliff bases.

Elevation ranges from near sea level in the northern part of its range to approximately 1,500 meters in the southern Appalachians. At higher elevations it is restricted to sheltered coves and mesic slopes where moisture is abundant.

The species is absent from the Coastal Plain of the southeastern United States, where summer heat and periodic drought limit its distribution. This elevational and latitudinal restriction makes it primarily a species of the Interior Highlands and Appalachian provinces.

## Ecology

Deparia acrostichoides contributes to the structural diversity of forest understory communities through its large, bold-textured fronds. The dense colonies provide cover for ground-dwelling amphibians and small mammals.

The species is relatively shade-tolerant but produces the largest fronds in canopy gaps or along forest edges where diffuse light penetrates. This intermediate shade tolerance allows it to persist in both closed-canopy forests and recently disturbed sites.

White-tailed deer browsing can significantly impact Deparia populations in areas with high deer density. The large, succulent fiddleheads are among the first fern growth consumed in spring, and repeated browsing can suppress frond production.

Silvery glade fern is a host for several species of gall midges and fern aphids that feed on the frond tissue. These herbivores typically cause only minor cosmetic damage and do not significantly affect plant health.

The creeping rhizome system contributes to soil stabilization on steep slopes and stream banks. Root networks help bind loose forest litter and organic material, reducing surface erosion during heavy rainfall events.

Spore production is abundant, with each fertile frond producing thousands of spores. Wind dispersal facilitates colonization of suitable habitat patches across the forest landscape. The lightweight spores can travel considerable distances from parent plants, enabling gene flow between geographically separated populations.

### Spore Germination

Gametophytes of Deparia acrostichoides are cordate and relatively long-lived compared to those of many other ferns. This extended gametophyte longevity may enhance the species' ability to colonize temporally variable habitats where conditions for sporophyte establishment fluctuate seasonally.

## Cultivation

Deparia acrostichoides is increasingly available in the nursery trade and is valued for its bold texture and silvery rachis character. It is recommended for large shade gardens where its size can be accommodated.

The species is hardy in USDA zones 3 through 8, tolerating severe winter cold when established. It performs best in regions with consistent summer rainfall and moderate temperatures.

Planting should be in partial to full shade with moist, humus-rich soil that is slightly acidic to neutral. The species tolerates heavier clay soils better than many other woodland ferns, provided drainage is adequate.

Establishment from container-grown plants is reliable, with new fronds emerging within weeks of planting. Divided rhizome segments establish more slowly but produce mature colony-sized plants within 2 to 3 years.

The species is relatively pest-free in cultivation, though slugs may damage new fiddleheads in damp spring conditions. Fungal leaf spot diseases are uncommon in garden settings with good air circulation.

Winter hardiness is excellent, with fronds dying back to the rhizome after the first hard frosts. New fronds emerge relatively late in spring, typically 2 to 3 weeks after Athyrium filix-femina, which extends the seasonal interest of fern plantings.

## Uses

Deparia acrostichoides has limited documented ethnobotanical use, though the young fiddleheads are edible when cooked and were likely consumed by Indigenous peoples within its range. The species is not widely known as a fiddlehead source compared to ostrich fern.

In ecological restoration, silvery glade fern is used for riparian buffer plantings and slope stabilization projects. Its robust rhizome system and tolerance of periodic flooding make it effective for stream bank revegetation. Established plantings develop dense root mats that resist scouring during high-flow events, providing long-term structural integrity to restored riparian corridors.

The species has received attention from the native plant movement as an underutilized North American woodland fern with considerable ornamental potential. Its silvery frond undersides and bold texture distinguish it from more commonly available fern species.

Horticulturists have identified Deparia acrostichoides as a promising species for combining with hostas, astilbes, and other shade perennials in mixed woodland borders. Its late-season frond expansion extends the period of interest in shade gardens.

The species combines well with spring ephemerals such as Trillium and Mertensia, whose early-season display is followed by the expanding fern fronds. This sequential bloom pattern maximizes the visual interest of shade garden plantings throughout the growing season.
