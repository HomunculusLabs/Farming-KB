# Campanula flammula

## Overview

Campanula flammula, commonly known as the flame bellflower, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Campanulaceae. It is endemic to the Caucasus Mountains, where it grows on limestone cliffs and rocky slopes at elevations of 1,000–2,500 m.

The specific epithet flammula means "small flame" in Latin, referring to the flame-like shape of the narrow corolla lobes when viewed from above. The name was coined by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, who first described the species from specimens collected in Georgia.

Campanula flammula is distinguished from most other bellflowers by its narrowly funnel-shaped corolla with reflexed lobe tips. This unusual corolla morphology gives the flowers a distinctive star-like appearance when fully open.

The species is considered one of the more ornamental Caucasian campanulas but remains little-known outside specialist alpine gardening circles. Its compact size and unusual flower form make it desirable for [[hamilton-rock-garden-design]] cultivation.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Clade: Angiosperms
- Clade: Eudicots
- Order: Asterales
- Family: Campanulaceae
- Genus: Campanula
- Species: C. flammula Lam.

The plant forms a compact, caespitose clump with numerous short, ascending stems arising from a woody rootstock. The overall habit is cushion-like, typical of alpine specialists adapted to exposed montane habitats.

Basal leaves are linear to narrowly oblanceolate, 10–25 mm long, entire or with a few small teeth, and glabrous or sparsely hairy. Stem leaves are similar but smaller, becoming bract-like toward the inflorescence.

The corolla is narrowly funnel-shaped (tubular-campanulate), 15–22 mm long, pale to deep violet-blue, with lobes that reflex outward at their tips. This reflexed-lobe character is diagnostic for the species.

Flowers are usually solitary or paired at the stem tips, occasionally in small clusters of three. Each flower is subtended by small, linear bracts that are shorter than the calyx lobes.

The calyx lobes are linear-lanceolate, 4–6 mm long, glabrous, and spreading at anthesis. The capsule is ovoid, nodding, and opens by three small basal pores.

Seeds are ellipsoid, light brown, approximately 0.8–1.0 mm long. Chromosome number has not been definitively reported but is expected to be 2n = 34 based on related species.

The species was first described by Lamarck in the Encyclopédie Méthodique (1786). The type collection was made by a French botanical expedition to Georgia during the late 18th century.

## Distribution and Habitat

Campanula flammula is endemic to the Greater and Lesser Caucasus mountain ranges. It occurs in Georgia, Armenia, Azerbaijan, and adjacent northeastern Turkey.

The species is concentrated in the central and western Greater Caucasus, particularly in the Svaneti and Racha regions of Georgia. Smaller populations occur in the Trialeti Range and the Lesser Caucasus of southern Georgia.

It grows exclusively on limestone substrates, inhabiting cliff faces, rock crevices, scree slopes, and stony montane grasslands. The species is a strict calcicole, absent from volcanic or granitic substrates within its range.

Populations occur at elevations between 1,000 and 2,500 meters, with optimal habitat at 1,500–2,000 m. The species is absent from the highest alpine zone and the lowest foothills.

The Caucasus is one of the world's [[psilocybe-global-biodiversity-hotspots]], harboring approximately 6,400 vascular plant species. C. flammula is one of an estimated 1,600 endemic plant species in the region.

The species has not been assessed by the IUCN but is considered rare throughout its range due to its strict habitat requirements. Several populations are protected within Georgian national parks.

[[dighton-fungal-responses-climate-change]] modeling suggests that suitable habitat for high-elevation limestone specialists like C. flammula may contract significantly by 2050. Upward migration is limited by the decreasing area of available habitat at higher elevations.

## Cultivation

Campanula flammula is cultivated by specialist alpine gardeners for its unusual flower form and compact habit. It is considered a choice plant for limestone crevice gardens and alpine troughs.

The species requires full sun, excellent drainage, and alkaline or neutral soil enriched with limestone chips. It is intolerant of winter wetness and requires a dry, cold dormancy period.

Propagation is by seed, which should be sown in autumn or given 4–6 weeks of cold stratification. Germination is often slow and irregular, occurring over 3–8 weeks at 15–18°C.

Plants are hardy to approximately USDA zones 5–7 when grown in sharply drained conditions. Established plants are drought-tolerant but may be short-lived in cultivation, persisting 3–5 years.

The species is very rarely available from commercial nurseries and is primarily maintained in botanical garden collections. The Tbilisi Botanical Garden and the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh hold living collections.

In rock [[community-garden-design]], C. flammula is particularly effective planted in vertical tufa crevices where its cushion habit can be displayed. It combines well with other Caucasian alpines such as Draba bryoides and Primula auriculata.

## Ecological Role

The narrowly funnel-shaped flowers restrict pollinator access to insects with sufficiently long mouthparts. Specialized long-tongued bumblebees (Bombus spp.) are the primary pollinators in Caucasian montane habitats.

Flowering occurs from July to August, during the warmest period of the short Caucasus alpine summer. The extended corolla tube may protect nectar from rain dilution during the frequent afternoon thundershowers.
