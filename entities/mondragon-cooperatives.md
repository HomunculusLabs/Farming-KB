## Mondragon Cooperatives

## Description

The Mondragon Cooperatives are a federation of worker cooperatives based in the
Basque region of Spain, founded in 1956. They represent one of the most successful
examples of [[cooperative-enterprise-and-formal-economy]] in the world, and are frequently cited in
permaculture literature as a model for community economic organisation. Bill
Mollison references Mondragon extensively in the village design chapter of
*Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* as a proven template for self-reliant
community economics.

## Classification

- **Type:** Cooperative federation / economic model
- **Location:** Basque Country, Spain
- **Domain:** Community economics, social enterprise, village design
- **Relevance:** [[permaculture-village-development]] planning, cooperative governance
- **Founded:** 1956 by Father José María Arizmendiarrieta

## Key Facts

- Founded in 1956 in the town of Mondragón, Basque Country, Spain
- Grew to 3,000–5,000 members in early stages, but later restructured into
  smaller cooperatives of 300–500 members to preserve individual identity
- Each small cooperative produces a component part, enabling the collective
  federation to manufacture complete products such as vehicles and machinery
- A 10% levy on net profits funds community infrastructure including banks,
  schools, libraries, research facilities, and recreational grounds
- The system demonstrates that small cooperative units can achieve industrial-scale
  production through federation and coordination
- Mondragon pioneered the concept of the "cooperative group" where multiple
  small co-ops operate semi-independently under a shared umbrella
- The model has been studied worldwide as an alternative to both capitalist
  and state-socialist economic organisation
- By the 2010s, the Mondragon Corporation employed over 70,000 people across
  multiple countries, demonstrating the scalability of the cooperative model

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

While the Mondragon Cooperatives are not directly a [[boil-a-bag-mushroom-cultivation-technique]], they
are highly relevant to [[permaculture-design]] for several reasons:

1. **Village-Scale Economics:** Permaculture designs often envision self-reliant
   villages of 100–500 people. Mondragon proves this scale can support diverse
   enterprises including food production, energy generation, and manufacturing.

2. **Cooperative Food Production:** The model applies directly to community-supported
   agriculture, shared food processing centres, cooperative dairies, and collective
   market gardening operations described in permaculture village design.

3. **Profit Sharing for Community Infrastructure:** The 10% tithe model funds
   community assets — directly applicable to funding shared permaculture
   infrastructure like glasshouses, nurseries, [[irrigation-systems]], and composting.

4. **Scale Lessons for Community Gardens:** The insight that cooperatives of 300–500
   members work better than large ones (3,000–5,000) has direct implications for
   community garden groups, CSAs, and cultivation cooperatives.

5. **Federated Specialisation:** Just as Mondragon cooperatives each produce a
   component, permaculture villages can specialise — one household runs the
   nursery, another the [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]], another seed saving — trading
   within the network.

## Application in Permaculture Village Design

Mollison specifically recommends the Mondragon model for structuring village
trusts and cooperatives. Key structural elements adapted from Mondragon:

- **Trust A (Land Bank):** Holds commons and village land for the common good.
  Developers act as foundation directors and retire once 10-12 residents are
  established, who then assume directorship.

- **Trust B (Trading Operations):** A separate unit discretionary trust for
  trading operations. Currently immune from company tax; reduces family tax
  and enables diverse enterprises. Trust A is a beneficiary of Trust B.

- **Community Services Council:** Elected to administer policy on publicly owned
  assets, collect lease monies, administer funds for schools and medical services.
