# Echinopsis bridgesii (Bolivian Torch)

**Echinopsis bridgesii**, commonly known as the **Bolivian Torch cactus**, is a fast-growing columnar cactus species in the family Cactaceae, native to the Bolivian Andes. It is one of the principal "san pedro-type" mescaline-containing cacti, valued both as a traditional entheogen and as an ornamental succulent in cultivation. The species is closely related to *[[echinopsis-peruvianus]]* (Peruvian Torch), and is often discussed alongside them in ethnobotanical literature, though it is genetically and morphologically distinct.

The taxonomic history of *E. bridgesii* is complex. It has been variously classified as *[[echinopsis-lageniformis]]*, reflecting the ongoing revision of Trichocereus taxonomy into the genus *Echinopsis*. Many cultivators and ethnobotanists continue to use the name *Trichocereus bridgesii*.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Caryophyllales
- **Family:** Cactaceae
- **Subfamily:** Cactoideae
- **Tribe:** Trichocereeae
- **Genus:** Echinopsis (syn. Trichocereus)
- **Species:** E. bridgesii (Salm-Dyck) Friedrich & G.D. Rowley

The species was first described by Joseph zu Salm-Dyck in 1850 as *Cereus bridgesii*, named in honor of the English botanist and plant collector George Bridges (1806–1883), who collected cacti in South America.

## Botanical Description

### Growth Habit
*E. bridgesii* is a columnar, tree-like cactus that grows upright with minimal branching unless the apical meristem is damaged. In cultivation, it typically reaches 2–5 meters in height and 8–15 cm in diameter. In its native habitat, mature specimens can exceed 6 meters. Growth rate is fast for a cactus — 30–60 cm per year under optimal conditions — making it one of the most vigorous Trichocereus species.

### Ribs
The cactus typically has 4–8 ribs (most commonly 5), which is fewer than *E. pachanoi* (typically 6–9 ribs). The ribs are broad and rounded, with deep furrows between them. This relatively low rib count is a key diagnostic feature distinguishing it from related species.

### Areoles and Spines
Areoles are large and prominent, spaced approximately 1.5–3 cm apart along the ribs. Spines are variable:
- **Radial spines:** 3–7, slender, yellowish-brown to honey-colored, 0.5–2 cm long
- **Central spines:** 1–4 (usually 1–2), strong, yellowish to brown, 2–6 cm long
- Spine length is highly variable; some populations produce notably long central spines (up to 10 cm), while spineless or nearly spineless forms also exist in cultivation
- Spines turn gray with age

### Flowers
Large, showy flowers emerge from near the apex of mature stems. Flowers are:
- **Size:** 15–22 cm long, 10–14 cm in diameter
- **Color:** White with a greenish throat, sometimes with pinkish-brown outer petals
- **Form:** Funnel-shaped, with numerous petals and a prominent cluster of stamens and style
- **Blooming:** Nocturnal to crepuscular, opening in the evening and remaining open through the following morning
- **Fragrance:** Sweet, floral fragrance that attracts pollinating moths and bats

### Fruit
Oval to club-shaped, green to yellowish-green, 5–7 cm long, splitting longitudinally when ripe to reveal white pulp containing numerous small, black seeds.

### Distinguishing Features from Related Species
| Feature | E. bridgesii | E. pachanoi | E. peruvianus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ribs | 4–8 (usually 5) | 6–9 | 5–8 (usually 6–7) |
| Spine length | Moderate to long | Short | Long |
| V-notching | Absent | Present | Present |
| Growth rate | Very fast | Fast | Moderate |
| Mescaline content | Moderate-high | Moderate | Variable |
| Areole spacing | 1.5–3 cm | 1–2 cm | 1–2 cm |

The absence of V-shaped notches between areoles on the ribs is a reliable distinguishing feature — *E. pachanoi* and *E. peruvianus* both exhibit characteristic V-notching.

### Distribution and Habitat
Native to Bolivia (departments of La Paz, Cochabamba, Santa Cruz), growing at 2,000–3,000 meters in Andean foothills on rocky hillsides, dry inter-Andean valleys with seasonal rainfall, and well-drained volcanic soils.

## Chemistry and Psychoactive Constituents

### Mescaline Content
*E. bridgesii* is one of the most consistently potent mescaline-containing Trichocereus species:

| Tissue | Mescaline Content (dry weight) |
|---|---|
| Outer green tissue (skin) | 0.3–2.0% (typically 0.5–1.2%) |
| Whole dried cactus | 0.05–0.5% |
| Fresh cactus | 0.01–0.1% |

Mescaline content varies significantly based on:
- **Genetics:** Specific clone or population; some specimens consistently test higher
- **Age:** Older, mature specimens tend to have higher alkaloid concentrations
- **Growing conditions:** Stress (drought, nutrient limitation, UV exposure) may increase alkaloid production as a defensive response
- **Harvest time:** Seasonal variation; some reports suggest higher mescaline in late summer/fall
- **Tissue section:** The dark green photosynthetic tissue immediately beneath the epidermis contains the highest concentrations; the white pith contains negligible amounts

### Other Alkaloids
In addition to mescaline (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine), *E. bridgesii* contains a complex alkaloid profile including:
- 3-Methoxytyramine
- Tyramine
- Hordenine
- Anhalonidine
- Anhalinine
- Anhalonine

These secondary alkaloids may contribute to the overall subjective experience, which some users distinguish from synthetic mescaline or San Pedro preparations.

## Traditional Use

*E. bridgesii* has been used traditionally in Bolivian Andean communities, particularly among the Kallawaya and Aymara peoples, in healing ceremonies (mesadas), divination, and veterinary applications. Documentation is less extensive than for *E. pachanoi*, partly due to limited Bolivian ethnobotanical research and cultural insularity of indigenous communities.

## Cultivation

### Propagation
- **Seed:** Germinates readily at 20–25°C in well-drained cactus soil (7–30 days); seed-grown plants show genetic variation
- **Cuttings:** Most common method — take from healthy stems, callus 2–6 weeks, root in dry soil in 4–8 weeks
- **Grafting:** Seedlings grafted onto *Pereskiopsis* or *Hylocereus* rootstock for accelerated growth

### Growing Conditions

| Parameter | Optimal Range |
|---|---|
| Hardiness | USDA Zones 9b–11 (can survive brief light frost to -2°C) |
| Light | Full sun to bright indirect light; acclimatize gradually |
| Temperature | 10–35°C optimal; tolerates 5–40°C briefly |
| Soil | Well-drained mineral mix: 50% pumice/perlite, 25% sand, 25% organic matter |
| Water | Moderate during growing season (spring–fall); withhold during winter dormancy |
| pH | 6.0–7.5 |
| Fertilizer | Low-nitrogen cactus fertilizer during active growth (optional) |
| Container | Deep pots with excellent drainage; terracotta preferred over plastic |

### Common Cultivars and Varieties
- **Standard E. bridgesii:** The typical wild-type form with moderate spination
- **SS01 (Short Spine):** A commercially distributed clone with reduced spine length, popular in cultivation
- **Monstrose forms:** Several monstrose (crested) varieties exist, prized by collectors for their unusual morphology
- **E. bridgesii × pachanoi hybrids:** Naturally occurring and artificially created crosses that combine characteristics of both species

## Legal Status

Mescaline is Schedule I under the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971). Live plants are legal to cultivate as ornamentals in most jurisdictions (US, UK, EU, Australia) — the restriction applies to preparation for consumption. In the US, extracting mescaline is illegal under the Controlled Substances Act. Traditional use by indigenous Bolivian communities is culturally protected.
