# Ergothioneine

Ergothioneine (2-mercaptohistidine trimethylbetaine) is a naturally occurring amino acid derivative and potent antioxidant found predominantly in mushrooms. It is considered a longevity vitamin due to its cytoprotective properties and the existence of a dedicated human transporter (OCTN1/SLC22A4) for its cellular uptake, suggesting evolutionary importance.

## Chemical Properties

Ergothioneine is a thiol/thione-containing amino acid with the molecular formula C9H15N3O2S. Unlike glutathione, the thiol group of ergothioneine exists predominantly in the thione tautomer at physiological pH, making it significantly more stable and less prone to auto-oxidation. This stability allows it to function as a persistent intracellular antioxidant.

The compound was first isolated in 1909 from the ergot fungus [[claviceps-purpurea]] by Charles Tanret. Its unique betaine structure distinguishes it from all other known biological thiols.

## Pharmacokinetics

Ergothioneine is absorbed in the small intestine via the OCTN1 transporter. Oral bioavailability is moderate (approximately 40-60% in animal models). Once absorbed, it is distributed to most tissues, with highest concentrations found in:
- Erythrocytes: 1-2 mg/L in healthy adults
- Liver: 2-5 mg/kg tissue
- Kidney: 1-3 mg/kg tissue
- Lens of the eye: up to 1 mM concentration
- Seminal fluid: 2-4 mg/L

The plasma half-life is approximately 30 days in humans, making it one of the longest-lived small molecules in blood. This exceptional longevity is attributed to efficient renal reabsorption via OCTN1 and resistance to metabolic degradation. Excretion occurs primarily via the kidneys as unchanged ergothioneine.

## Dietary Sources

Beyond mushrooms, ergothioneine is found in trace amounts in:
- Certain fermented foods (tempeh, miso)
- Black and red beans
- Oat bran
- Garlic
- Some organ meats (liver, kidney)

However, mushrooms remain by far the most concentrated dietary source, typically providing 10-100 times more ergothioneine per gram than other foods. Regular mushroom consumption (2-3 servings per week) can maintain plasma ergothioneine levels adequate for biological effects.

## Measurement and Biomarker Status

Plasma and whole-blood ergothioneine levels are measured by HPLC or LC-MS. Proposed reference ranges:
- Deficient: < 1 umol/L
- Low-normal: 1-3 umol/L
- Normal: 3-5 umol/L
- High: > 5 umol/L

Low ergothioneine levels have been independently associated with increased all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease risk, cognitive decline in elderly populations, and pre-eclampsia in pregnancy. These associations have led researchers to propose ergothioneine as a longevity vitamin.

## Current Research Directions

Active areas of ergothioneine research include clinical trials for neurodegenerative disease prevention, investigation of ergothioneine status as a biomarker for disease risk prediction, development of ergothioneine-enriched functional foods and supplements, synthesis of derivatives with enhanced bioavailability, and studies on mitochondrial function and cellular bioenergetics.

## Occurrence in Mushrooms

Mushrooms are the primary dietary source of ergothioneine. Among commonly consumed species:

- [[flammulina-velutipes]] (enokitake): among the highest concentrations reported
- [[pleurotus-eryngii]] (king oyster): significant levels
- [[lentinula-edodes]] (shiitake): substantial ergothioneine content
- [[agaricus-bisporus]] (button mushroom): moderate but consistent levels
- [[ganoderma-lucidum]] (reishi): detectable amounts

Concentrations vary significantly by species, substrate, and cultivation method. Wild-harvested mushrooms from the Boletaceae family also contain notable amounts.

## Biosynthesis

Fungi synthesize ergothioneine via a five-step pathway from histidine. The key enzyme is ergothioneine synthase (EgtB), a non-heme iron enzyme that catalyzes the oxidative coupling of hercynine (N-alpha-trimethylhistidine) with cysteine. This pathway was fully elucidated in [[neurospora-crassa]] and several bacterial species.

## Biological Activity

### Antioxidant Effects

Ergothioneine scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) including singlet oxygen, hydroxyl radicals, and hypochlorous acid. Its thione group can undergo redox cycling, and it acts synergistically with other antioxidants including ascorbic-acid and tocopherols.

### Cytoprotection

Ergothioneine protects cellular components from oxidative damage:
- DNA: reduces strand breaks and base modifications
- Proteins: prevents carbonylation and maintains enzyme function
- Lipids: inhibits peroxidation of cellular membranes
- Mitochondria: accumulates in mitochondria and protects against oxidative stress-induced apoptosis

### Anti-inflammatory Effects

Ergothioneine inhibits pro-inflammatory cytokine production (TNF-alpha, IL-6, IL-1 beta) and reduces neutrophil oxidative burst activity. These effects are mediated through modulation of NF-kB signaling pathways.

### Neuroprotection

Studies demonstrate protective effects against neuronal oxidative damage in models of Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, and ischemic stroke. The compound crosses the blood-brain barrier and accumulates in brain tissue.

## Human Transporter

The discovery of the specific organic cation transporter OCTN1 (encoded by SLC22A4) for ergothioneine uptake was a landmark finding. All human tissues express this transporter, with highest expression in kidney, liver, and erythrocytes. The existence of a dedicated transporter strongly suggests ergothioneine has essential physiological roles not fulfilled by other antioxidants.

## Therapeutic Potential

### Cancer

Ergothioneine demonstrates chemopreventive properties in several in vitro and animal models. It protects normal cells from chemotherapy-induced oxidative damage while potentially sensitizing cancer cells to treatment. Epidemiological studies suggest correlations between low plasma ergothioneine and increased cancer risk.

### Cardiovascular Disease

Ergothioneine reduces oxidized LDL formation, inhibits vascular inflammation, and improves endothelial function. Low plasma levels are associated with increased cardiovascular mortality.

### Eye Health

The lens and retina accumulate ergothioneine at high concentrations, suggesting a role in protection against cataract formation and age-related macular degeneration.

## Safety and Supplementation

Ergothioneine is Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS). No adverse effects have been reported at normal dietary or supplemental intake levels. Typical supplemental doses range from 5 to 50 mg per day. The compound has a long plasma half-life of approximately 30 days, allowing weekly dosing.
