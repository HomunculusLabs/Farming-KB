## Desert Drip Irrigation

## Description

[[drip-irrigation]] in desert permaculture encompasses a spectrum of technologies from extremely primitive to sophisticated, all sharing the principle of delivering water directly to the root zone of plants in minimal quantities with zero surface evaporation. Mollison identifies drip irrigation as "the great secret of growing plants in the desert" and describes implementations ranging from ostrich eggs with single holes to modern Israeli drip lines.

## Classification

- **Type**: Irrigation technology — arid land water delivery
- **Scale range**: Individual plant to settlement-wide systems
- **Technology spectrum**: Primitive (ostrich egg) to modern (pressurized drip line)
- **Water efficiency**: Near 100% delivery to root zone
- **Climate suitability**: All arid and semi-arid environments

## Key Facts

- The primitive form uses an ostrich egg with a single small hole bored near the plant base, leaking water in tiny drips directly to the roots.
- An equally primitive version uses discarded one-gallon wine flagons filled with water and inverted; water drips through a small hole in the cap.
- The sophisticated form is the modern Israeli drip irrigation line with engineered emitters.
- Without drip delivery, desert irrigation fails because surface-applied water evaporates before reaching roots, and mineral [[soil-ph]] rises to toxic levels.
- Drip irrigation must be combined with organic mulch: dripping into bare soil causes rapid pH rise as minerals concentrate around the wetting front, eventually becoming toxic to plants.
- When dripping into mulch, humic acids from decomposing organic matter buffer the pH indefinitely, preventing the toxic mineral accumulation.
- Lateral water spread from a single drip point extends approximately 2-2.5 feet in typical desert soils — this defines the maximum effective garden diameter of about 5 feet per emitter.
- Drip rates must be carefully calibrated to match soil absorption capacity — too fast causes surface pooling and evaporation losses.

## Primitive Implementations

### Ostrich Egg Dripper
- **Material**: Ostrich egg shell
- **Modification**: Single small hole bored near the base
- **Placement**: Buried near plant root zone
- **Capacity**: Approximately 1 liter
- **Duration**: Slow release over days to weeks
- **Advantage**: Uses locally available material, zero cost

### Wine Flagon Dripper
- **Material**: Discarded glass wine flagon (1 gallon/4 liters)
- **Method**: Fill with water, invert, place cap-down near plant
- **Flow rate**: Determined by hole size in cap
- **Common**: Many flagons litter desert camps, freely available
- **Advantage**: Larger capacity than eggs, reusable indefinitely

### Terracotta Pot Irrigation
- **Principle**: Unglazed clay pots allow water to seep through walls
- **Flow rate**: Self-regulating based on soil moisture tension
- **Advantage**: Automatically adjusts delivery to plant demand

## Advanced Implementations

### Israeli Drip Line
- **Technology**: Pressurized plastic tubing with engineered emitters
- **Flow rate**: Precisely controlled (1-4 liters/hour per emitter)
- **Advantage**: Uniform delivery, scalable to large areas
- **Limitation**: Requires pump or elevated water source, manufactured components

### Wick Systems (Atoll)
- **Technology**: Fibrous organic wick at drip point
- **Advantage**: Distributes moisture laterally without surface wetting
- **Application**: Ring gardens on coral atolls

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Drip irrigation is the enabling technology for all desert food production:

1. **[[water-conservation]]**: In environments where water is the absolute limiting factor, drip delivery achieves near-zero waste.

2. **pH management**: Combined with mulch, drip irrigation prevents the mineral toxicity that makes surface irrigation unworkable in desert soils.

3. **Scalability**: The same principle operates at every scale from individual plants to commercial orchards.

4. **[[appropriate-technology]]**: The spectrum from ostrich eggs to engineered systems means the technology is accessible at every economic level.

5. **Gravity compatibility**: Primitive drip systems work entirely on gravity — no pumps, electricity, or moving parts required.

## Design Principles

- Place emitters at or below soil surface to eliminate evaporation
- Combine every drip point with organic mulch cover
- Calibrate drip rate to soil type: faster for sand, slower for clay
- Space emitters based on 2-2.5 foot lateral spread radius
- Protect drip lines from termite damage in desert environments
- Plan for three-year drought when sizing water storage
