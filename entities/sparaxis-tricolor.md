## Overview

*Sparaxis tricolor* (Schneev.) Ker Gawl., commonly known as the harlequin flower, is a bulbous perennial in the family Iridaceae. It is native to the winter-rainfall region of the Western Cape of South Africa.

The species is renowned for its vivid, multicolored flowers that display concentric rings of red, yellow, and dark purple around a white or cream center. Each bloom measures 3–5 cm across and bears six broadly spreading tepals.

It has become a popular ornamental spring-blooming bulb in Mediterranean-climate gardens worldwide. Commercial seed mixes sold under the name "harlequin flower" often contain hybrid color selections derived from *S. tricolor* and related species.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Order: Asparagales
- Family: Iridaceae
- Subfamily: Ixioideae
- Tribe: Ixieae
- Genus: *Sparaxis*
- Species: *S. tricolor*

The genus *Sparaxis* comprises approximately 16 species, all endemic to the Western and Northern Cape provinces of South Africa. *S. tricolor* was first described by Schneevood and transferred by John Bellenden Ker Gawler in 1802.

Plants grow 15–30 cm tall from small, globose corms with fibrous tunics. Each stem produces 5–8 narrow, lanceolate leaves that are pale green and slightly ribbed.

The corms are 10–15 mm in diameter with dark brown, fibrous outer tunics. Corms replace themselves annually, producing a new corm above the old one at the end of the growing season.

Foliage typically emerges within two to three weeks of the first autumn rains. Leaves remain green through winter and spring before yellowing as the plant enters dormancy.

Flowers are stellate with six tepals arranged in two whorls. The distinctive color pattern includes an outer ring of bright crimson or orange, a central dark purple-black ring, and a yellow or white throat.

The stamens are asymmetrically arranged, with three curved filaments positioned to one side of the flower. This asymmetry is a key diagnostic feature that distinguishes *Sparaxis* from related genera.

Capsules are ovoid, 8–12 mm long, and contain angular brown seeds approximately 2 mm in diameter. Seeds are released in late spring as the capsule dries and splits open.

The chromosome number is 2n = 20, consistent with the base number reported for the genus. Polyploid forms have not been documented in *S. tricolor*, unlike some related *Sparaxis* species.

*S. tricolor* is most closely allied to *S. bulbifera* and *S. grandiflora*, which share similar flower morphology. It is distinguished by the combination of tricolor tepal markings and asymmetric stamens.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Sparaxis tricolor* is endemic to the Western Cape Province of South Africa. Its range extends from the Olifants River valley southward through the Piketberg and Clanwilliam districts.

It occurs in renosterveld and fynbos vegetation types at elevations of 100–600 m. The species grows in well-drained, sandy or gravelly clay soils derived from shale substrates.

Like other Cape bulbs, it follows a winter-growing, summer-dormant lifecycle adapted to the Mediterranean climate. Rainfall in its native habitat occurs primarily from May through August, totaling 300–500 mm annually.

Populations are fragmented due to extensive agricultural conversion of lowland renosterveld. Less than 5% of the original renosterveld in the species" range remains intact.

The species is classified as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List due to ongoing habitat loss. Climate change and invasive alien plants pose additional threats to remaining wild populations.

Several nature reserves in the Clanwilliam and Piketberg districts protect key populations. However, many populations occur on private farmland where conservation is voluntary.

Wildfire plays a role in the species' ecology, with flowering often enhanced in the first season after fire. Corms survive underground during fire events and respond to post-fire nutrient flushes.

## Cultivation

*Sparaxis tricolor* is widely cultivated as a spring-flowering ornamental bulb in USDA hardiness zones 8–10. Corms are planted in autumn at 5–8 cm depth in well-drained soil with full sun exposure.

The species prefers full sun to light shade and tolerates a range of soil types provided drainage is adequate. Plants require moderate water during the growing season and completely dry conditions during summer dormancy.

Propagation is by offset cormlets or fresh seed. Seed-grown plants typically flower in their second or third year after sowing.

Commercial cultivar mixes sold as "Sparaxis tricolor mix" often include hybrid color forms. These selections feature larger flowers and broader color ranges including pink, white, and lavender forms.

In cold-winter regions, corms can be lifted after foliage dies back and stored dry at room temperature. Replant in autumn when soil temperatures drop below 15°C.

The species is relatively pest-free but may be affected by aphids during flowering. Slugs and snails can damage emerging shoots in early spring.

Flowering occurs in mid-spring (September–October in the southern hemisphere). Each corm produces one to three flowering stems over a bloom period of two to three weeks.

## Uses and Ecology

The harlequin flower is primarily grown as an ornamental garden plant and for cut-flower production. Its vivid, contrasting colors make it a distinctive addition to rock gardens, container plantings, and spring borders.

In its native habitat, *S. tricolor* is pollinated by monkey beetles (Scarabaeidae: Hopliini). These beetles are attracted to the dark central markings that serve as visual mating-site cues.

The asymmetric stamen arrangement facilitates precise pollen placement on the beetle's body. This specialized pollination mechanism is shared across several Cape Iridaceae genera.

The species contributes to the exceptional bulb diversity of the Cape Floristic Region, one of the world's six floral kingdoms. Over 1,400 bulb species occur in this region, many of them endemic to narrow geographic ranges.

South Africa's Cape Floristic Region contains the highest density of geophyte species anywhere on Earth. The Iridaceae alone contribute over 650 species to this diversity.

Loss of renosterveld habitat to wheat and canola farming is the primary conservation concern. Several protected areas now shelter remaining populations of *S. tricolor* and other threatened Cape bulbs.

The species has been featured on South African postage stamps and in botanical art since the 18th century. Its striking appearance made it a favorite subject of Cape botanical illustrators.

In cultivation, the species naturalizes readily in suitable Mediterranean-climate gardens. It has been reported as established in parts of southern Australia and coastal California.

The corms are not known to have any traditional food or medicinal uses. Their primary human value is ornamental, where they contribute to the horticultural tradition of Cape bulb cultivation that dates to the 18th century.
