# Convolvulus pluricaulis

## Overview

**Convolvulus pluricaulis** Choisy, commonly known as **Shankhpushpi**, is a
small perennial herbaceous plant in the family Convolvulaceae. It is one of the
most important *medhya rasayana* (brain tonic) herbs in the Ayurvedic
pharmacopoeia, where it has been used for centuries to enhance memory,
cognition, and mental clarity.

The plant is native to the Indian subcontinent and is found throughout the
plains and foothills of northern and central India, as well as in parts of
Myanmar and Sri Lanka.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Division Tracheophyta → Class
Magnoliopsida → Order Solanales → Family Convolvulaceae → Genus *Convolvulus*
→ *C. pluricaulis*.

**Synonyms:** *Convolvulus microphyllus* Sieb. ex Spreng., *Convolvulus
pluricaulis* var. *microphyllus*.

The plant is a prostrate or ascending herb with slender, spreading stems 15–40
cm long. The stems are filiform, hairy, and root at the nodes where they
contact soil, forming dense mats.

Leaves are small, oblong-elliptic to lanceolate, 0.8–2.5 cm long and 0.3–1
cm wide, with entire margins and acute apices. Both surfaces are sparsely
pubescent.

Flowers are solitary or in few-flowered cymes, borne in leaf axils. The calyx
has 5 sepals; the corolla is funnel-shaped, 6–12 mm long, white to pale pink
or pale blue with a darker throat. Flowering occurs during the monsoon season
(July–October).

The fruit is a globose capsule, approximately 4 mm in diameter, containing
1–4 smooth, brown seeds.

## Distribution and Habitat

*C. pluricaulis* is distributed across the Indian subcontinent, from the
Indo-Gangetic plains to the Deccan plateau, at elevations up to 1,500 m. It
also occurs in Myanmar, Nepal, and Sri Lanka.

The plant grows as a weed in cultivated fields, grasslands, and along
roadsides. It prefers sandy or loamy soils with good drainage and thrives in
open, sunny locations with moderate rainfall.

It is commonly found in dry deciduous forests and wastelands, often forming
extensive ground cover during the monsoon season. The species is tolerant of
poor soils and moderate drought conditions.

## Traditional Uses

In Ayurvedic medicine, *Shankhpushpi* is classified as a *medhya rasayana*
— a rejuvenator of the mind and intellect. It is a primary ingredient in
classical formulations such as Shankhpushpi Ghrita, Shankhpushpi Rasayana, and
Unmada Gajankusha Rasa.

Traditional indications include memory impairment, anxiety, insomnia,
epilepsy, and cognitive decline associated with aging. It is also used as a
mild sedative for children and as a general tonic for the nervous system.

The herb is traditionally administered as a decoction of the whole plant,
often in combination with other medhya herbs such as *Bacopa monnieri*
(Brahmi), *Centella asiatica* (Mandukaparni), and *Withania somnifera*
(Ashwagandha).

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

The principal bioactive constituents include:

- **Alkaloids:** Shankhpushpine, convolvine, convoline, convolidine, and
  convolvuline — tropane-type alkaloids with reported CNS activity.
- **Flavonoids:** Kaempferol, quercetin, and their glycosides, contributing
  antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects.
- **Triterpenoids:** β-amyrin and lupeol derivatives.
- **Coumarins:** Scopoletin and umbelliferone.

Experimental studies have demonstrated nootropic activity in animal models,
including improved acquisition and retention in passive avoidance and maze
tests. The alkaloid fraction has shown anxiolytic and sedative effects
comparable to diazepam in some rodent assays.

Anti-inflammatory activity has been documented through inhibition of
carrageenan-induced paw edema and reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines.
Antioxidant assays demonstrate significant free radical scavenging activity,
primarily attributed to the flavonoid content.

## Cultivation

*C. pluricaulis* is cultivated commercially in several Indian states,
particularly Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, and Uttar Pradesh. Propagation is by
seed, sown directly in prepared beds after the onset of monsoon rains.

The crop requires minimal inputs and is well suited to marginal agricultural
land. Harvesting of the aerial parts occurs 90–120 days after sowing, during
the flowering stage when bioactive alkaloid content is highest.

Post-harvest processing involves shade-drying the whole plant, followed by
powdering or extraction. Average dry herb yield is approximately 2,000–3,000
kg/ha under rainfed conditions.
