# Leccinum scabrum

Leccinum scabrum, the brown birch bolete, is an edible ectomycorrhizal
mushroom in the family Boletaceae, found exclusively in association with
birch (Betula spp.) trees across temperate regions of Europe and North
America.

One of the most commonly collected boletes in northern Europe, L. scabrum
is valued as a good edible mushroom, though it is considered inferior in
flavor to the king bolete (Boletus edulis). Its obligate mycorrhizal
association with birch makes it a reliable indicator of healthy birch
forest ecosystems.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Boletales
- **Family:** Boletaceae
- **Genus:** Leccinum
- **Species:** L. scabrum (Bull.) Gray

**Synonyms:** Boletus scaber Bull., Krombholziella scabra (Bull.) Rauschert,
Leccinum scabrum var. scabrum.

The genus Leccinum was historically placed within Boletus but separated
based on scabrate stipe and other morphological differences. Phylogenetic
studies confirm Leccinum as a distinct clade within Boletaceae.

## Morphology

**Cap:** 5–15 (–20) cm, convex becoming plano-convex with age. Surface smooth
to finely velvety, pale brown to dark brown, sometimes olive or grey tints.
Does not peel. Margin inrolled when young.

**Pore surface:** Tubes 8–20 mm deep, adnexed. Pores 1–3 per mm, angular,
white when young becoming grey-olive to brown. Does not bruise blue.

**Stipe:** 8–18 cm × 1–3 cm, clavate to bulbous. Surface covered with dark
brown to black raised scales on whitish ground color — the diagnostic
"scabrosity." Solid when young, hollow with age.

**Flesh:** White, firm, unchanging when cut. No blue staining. Odor mild;
taste slightly nutty.

**Spore print:** Olivaceous-brown. Spores 14–18 × 4.5–6 µm, elongate-
fusiform, smooth.

## Distribution and Habitat

Widespread across Europe (Scandinavia to Mediterranean), east through Russia
to Siberia. Also in North America, though collections may represent a complex
of cryptic species.

Exclusively mycorrhizal with birch: B. pendula, B. pubescens, B. papyrifera,
B. alleghaniensis. Fruits June–November in birch forests, woodland edges,
heathland, and parks. Prefers acidic to neutral soils.

## Ecological Role

L. scabrum forms obligate ectomycorrhizal associations with birch roots,
exchanging soil minerals for photosynthates. Mycelium contributes to soil
aggregation, nutrient cycling, and carbon sequestration. Fruiting bodies
feed slugs, insects, and small mammals.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

L. scabrum is widely regarded as a good edible mushroom:

- **Flavor:** Mild, slightly nutty; less intense than Boletus edulis
- **Texture:** Firm when young, spongy when mature
- **Preparation:** Remove tube layer and peel stipe before cooking
- **Cooking:** Sautéing, frying, soups, stews, drying, pickling
- **Caution:** Must be thoroughly cooked; raw consumption causes GI upset

### Safety Notes

L. scabrum does not stain blue when cut, distinguishing it from some toxic
boletes. Proper identification requires attention to birch association and
scabrate stipe.

Some individuals report GI sensitivity to Leccinum species, particularly
with alcohol — more commonly associated with L. oxydabile and L. insigne.

## Nutritional Profile

Per 100 g fresh weight (approximate):

- **Calories:** 25–30 kcal
- **Protein:** 1.5–2.5 g
- **Carbohydrates:** 3–5 g
- **Fat:** <1 g
- **Dietary fiber:** 1–2 g
- **Potassium:** 300–400 mg
- **Phosphorus:** 40–60 mg
- **B vitamins:** Notable thiamin, riboflavin, niacin

Contains ergosterol (provitamin D₂), converting to vitamin D₂ upon UV
exposure during drying.

## Related Species

Several Leccinum species are similar and may be confused:

- **Leccinum versipelle** (orange birch bolete) — orange cap, larger, also
  on birch; superior edible
- **Leccinum aurantiacum** — similar, typically under aspen
- **Leccinum oxydabile** — on birch; stipe scales stain dark on handling
- **Leccinum insigne** — under aspen; reddish-orange cap; cook thoroughly
- **Leccinum variicolor** — on birch; cap with pink/red zones

North American collections may represent a complex of cryptic species with
different host preferences.

## Cultivation

L. scabrum has not been successfully cultivated commercially due to its
obligate mycorrhizal dependency on living birch roots. All collection is
from wild populations. Birch plantation with inoculated seedlings has been
experimentally attempted with limited success.
