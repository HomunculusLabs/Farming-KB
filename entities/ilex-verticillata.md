## Overview

**Ilex verticillata**, commonly known as winterberry, common winterberry, or black alder, is a deciduous holly native to [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]]. It is valued for its profuse display of bright red berries that persist through autumn and winter, providing dramatic color in the landscape after leaf drop.

The species is one of the most widely cultivated native [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] hollies, extensively used in ornamental plantings, rain gardens, and naturalistic landscapes. Its berries are an important winter food source for birds and other wildlife. Unlike most hollies, it is deciduous, shedding its leaves in autumn to reveal the berry-laden stems.

The genus name *Ilex* is the classical Latin name for the European holly (*[[ilex-aquifolium]]*). The specific epithet *verticillata* refers to the whorled or verticillate arrangement of leaves in some botanical descriptions, though the leaves of this species are actually alternate. The common name "winterberry" directly references the species' most distinctive horticultural feature.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomic classification:** Kingdom Plantae, Angiosperms, Eudicots, Aquifoliales, Aquifoliaceae, Ilex.

**Authority:** (L.) A.Gray.

**Synonyms:** *Ilex verticillata* var. *padifolia* (Willd.) H.Wendl., *Prinos verticillatus* (L.) DC., *Ilex verticillata* var. *tardifolia' H.Wendl.

*Ilex verticillata* is a slow-growing, multi-stemmed deciduous shrub typically reaching 1.5 to 3 meters in height, though exceptional specimens may approach 5 meters. The growth habit is upright and spreading, forming rounded to irregular crowns with age. The bark is thin, smooth, and grayish-brown, becoming slightly roughened on older stems.

Leaves are alternate, simple, elliptic to obovate, 5 to 10 cm long and 2 to 4 cm wide. The margin is finely serrate with acute to acuminate apices. The upper surface is dark green and glabrous, while the lower surface is paler with sparse pubescence along the midrib.

Autumn foliage color is variable, ranging from yellow-green to purplish tones, though color is often muted compared to more showy ornamental species.

Leaf abscission typically occurs in October to November, though timing varies with latitude and weather conditions. The foliage provides little fall color interest, as the species is grown primarily for its fruit display rather than its autumn leaves.

The species is dioecious, with male and female flowers borne on separate plants. Flowers are small, white to greenish-white, approximately 6 mm in diameter, with 4 to 6 petals. Male flowers are borne in clusters of 3 to 12 in the axils of leaves of the previous year's growth. Female flowers are solitary or in clusters of 2 to 3, appearing slightly later than male flowers in mid to late spring.

The dioecious condition of the species has important implications for landscape planting.

Without a compatible male pollinator, female plants produce no fruit, rendering them ornamentally ineffective during the winter months. Nursery labeling sometimes omits sex designation, leading to planting errors. Landscape professionals recommend planting male and female plants in a ratio of approximately 1:5 to 8.

Fruit is a spherical drupe, 6 to 9 mm in diameter, ripening from green to bright scarlet-red in late August to October. Each fruit contains 3 to 5 nutlets. The berries persist on the shrub well into winter and sometimes through early spring, unless consumed by birds. Yellow- and orange-fruited cultivars also exist.

The growth habit is upright and spreading, forming rounded to irregular crowns with age. The bark is thin, smooth, and grayish-brown, becoming slightly roughened on older stems. The root system is shallow and fibrous, spreading horizontally rather than forming a deep taproot.

Stems are slender to moderately stout, gray to grayish-brown, with lighter lenticels. New growth is green to greenish-purple, maturing to gray-brown by the second year. The plant produces numerous basal shoots, forming dense thickets over time if not managed through periodic renewal pruning.

The species is slow-growing in its early years, typically adding 15 to 30 cm of height per year. Growth rate increases with age and adequate moisture. Mature plants in favorable sites may reach full size within 10 to 15 years, though most ornamental specimens are maintained at smaller sizes through periodic renewal pruning.

The bright red berries are among the most persistent of any North American fruiting shrub, often remaining on stems through February or March. Persistence varies among cultivars, with some selections retaining fruit into April under mild winter conditions. The contrast of red berries against dark, bare winter stems creates the species' signature ornamental effect.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Ilex verticillata* is native to eastern North America, ranging from Newfoundland and Nova Scotia west to Ontario, Minnesota, and eastern Nebraska, and south to Georgia, Alabama, and eastern Texas. It is one of the most widespread hollies in North America, occurring throughout the eastern deciduous forest biome and adjacent regions.

The species is strongly associated with wetland habitats, including swamps, bogs, marshes, floodplain forests, and stream margins. It is a facultative wetland plant (FACW), occurring most frequently in saturated or seasonally inundated soils. However, it also grows in mesic upland forests, woodland edges, and old fields, demonstrating considerable ecological amplitude.

Several recognized varieties and forms exist within the species' range. *Ilex verticillata* var. *padifolia* (Willd.) H.Wendl. is a coastal plain variant with broader leaves and larger fruit. Hybrids with other *Ilex* species, particularly *Ilex serrata* (Japanese winterberry), have produced ornamental cultivars combining the hardiness of the American species with the heavier fruiting of its Asian relative.

Soil preferences include acidic, organic-rich loams and sandy loams, though the species tolerates clay and muck soils in wetland settings. Optimal soil pH ranges from 4.5 to 6.5. Full sun to partial shade is acceptable, with best [[query-how-do-i-prune-brambles-for-maximum-fruit-production]] occurring in full sun exposures.

The species tolerates a wide range of environmental conditions, including urban [[air-pollution-fungal-community-responses]], compacted soils, and road salt exposure. This adaptability has contributed to its widespread use in commercial and residential landscapes. It is hardy to USDA Zone 3, making it suitable for northern gardens where few other [[serviceberry-and-native-fruiting-shrubs]] thrive.

## Ecology

Winterberry provides significant ecological value in eastern North American ecosystems. The persistent berries are a critical winter food resource for numerous bird species, including American robin, northern mockingbird, cedar waxwing, eastern bluebird, and hermit thrush. Small mammals such as raccoon, opossum, and squirrels also consume the fruit. Birds typically prefer the berries after several freeze-thaw cycles have softened the flesh and reduced bitterness, which means the most ornamental display period often extends through early winter before significant consumption begins.

The berries are considered mildly toxic to humans due to the presence of saponins and ilicin glycosides. Ingestion may cause nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, though serious poisoning is uncommon. The bitter taste generally deters significant human consumption. [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] tribes reportedly used the bark in medicinal preparations, though documentation of specific applications is limited.

The dense, thicket-forming growth habit provides nesting cover and shelter for songbirds and small mammals. Deer browse the foliage, particularly in winter when other forage is scarce. The species is a larval host for the Henry's elfin butterfly (*Callophrys henrici*).

Pollination is primarily performed by bees, including honeybees and native bumblebees. The small, inconspicuous flowers produce modest nectar rewards, but the species is still considered valuable for early-season pollinator support. Flowering typically occurs in May to June, before many competing nectar sources are available.

In wetland restoration projects, winterberry is frequently planted for its [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]] capabilities, [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]] value, and tolerance of seasonally flooded conditions. Its root system is fibrous and helps stabilize stream banks and pond margins. The species establishes readily from container-grown stock and transplants with a high [[maitake-fruiting-body-success-rate-management]]. Companion species in wetland plantings often include *Clethra alnifolia*, *[[viburnum-dentatum]]*, and *Itea virginica*, which share similar cultural requirements and provide complementary seasonal interest.

## Cultivation and Uses

*Ilex verticillata* is one of the most popular native shrubs for ornamental landscaping in eastern North America. Its primary ornamental feature is the abundant display of persistent berries on bare winter stems, which are extensively harvested for floral arrangements and holiday decorations. Cut branches are a major commercial horticultural product, with significant wholesale and [[hallucinogenic-mushroom-online-retail-markets]].

The species has received numerous horticultural awards, including the Pennsylvania Horticultural Society Gold Medal and the Cary Award for landscape excellence. Its popularity has increased substantially since the 1990s as native plant landscaping has gained momentum in the horticultural industry.

Cultivation requires well-drained to moist acidic soils and adequate moisture. Full sun produces the heaviest fruit set, though partial shade is tolerated. The most critical cultural requirement is the presence of a male pollinator for female plants — one male plant is sufficient to pollinate approximately 6 to 8 female plants within a 15-meter radius. Male and female plants must bloom simultaneously for successful pollination.
