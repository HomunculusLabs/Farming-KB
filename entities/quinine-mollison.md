# Quinine

## Description

quinine mollison is a bitter alkaloid mentioned by [[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual* as an example of a crop with "special value" — a substance that commands unique importance beyond ordinary food production. Extracted from the bark of Cinchona trees, quinine was historically the primary treatment for malaria and remains one of the most significant medicinal compounds derived from plants.

## Classification

- **Type**: Alkaloid (medicinal compound)
- **Source plant**: *Cinchona* species (Family Rubiaceae)
- **Primary species**: *C. officinalis*, *C. ledgeriana*, *C. succirubra*
- **Chemical class**: Quinoline alkaloid
- **Molecular formula**: C20H24N2O2

## Key Facts

- Mollison lists quinine alongside vanilla and bamboo as crops with special value that justify inclusion in permaculture designs regardless of bulk yield.
- Quinine was the standard treatment for malaria from the 17th century until the development of synthetic alternatives in the mid-20th century.
- The compound is extracted from the bark of Cinchona trees, native to the Andes mountains of South America.
- Spanish colonisers learned of quinine's antimalarial properties from indigenous Quechua peoples of Peru, Bolivia, and Ecuador.
- Dutch plantations in Java (Indonesia) once produced 95% of the world's quinine supply before World War II.
- Quinine gives tonic water its characteristic bitter flavour.
- Modern uses include treatment of nocturnal leg cramps and as a flavouring agent.
- Resistance to synthetic antimalarials has renewed interest in quinine and its derivatives.

## Cultivation of Cinchona

- **Climate**: Tropical highland (1,500-2,500m elevation)
- **Rainfall**: 1,500-3,000mm annually
- **Soil**: Well-drained, acidic, rich in organic matter
- **Harvest**: Bark is stripped from trees 6-8 years old
- **Yield**: Approximately 5-15% quinine content in bark, varying by species and growing conditions

## Relevance to Permaculture and Mycology

Cinchona trees represent an ideal permaculture element: a long-lived perennial that produces a high-value product from bark (a renewable resource when harvested sustainably). As forest trees, Cinchona species fit naturally into agroforestry systems and [[tagari-publications]]. Chapter 2, section on Maximum Product Yield Concept.
