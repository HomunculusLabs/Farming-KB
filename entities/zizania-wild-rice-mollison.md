## Description

Zizania is a genus of tall aquatic grasses collectively known as wild rice. The most well-known species, *[[zizania-lacustris]]* (also called *Zizania palustris*), is an annual grass growing to 2.5 m tall, harvested as a nutritious grain for centuries by Indigenous peoples of North America. Several perennial species exist in the same genus. Wild rice is one of the most recently domesticated grain crops, having been brought into cultivation only within the last 30-50 years, making it a crop with enormous untapped breeding potential.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Poaceae (grass family)
- **Genus**: Zizania
- **Key species**: *Z. lacustris* (northern wild rice), *Z. aquatica*, *Z. texana* (Texas wild rice, endangered)
- **Growth form**: Tall annual aquatic grass (to 2.5 m); some perennial species
- **Habitat**: Cold swamps, shallows, lake margins; intense summer heat required

## Key Facts

- Native to North America, ranging from Minnesota to Florida and across Canada
- Approximately 3 million kg produced annually, mostly from the Minnesota region
- About 50% of total production is still wild-gathered by Indigenous peoples using traditional canoe-and-beating methods
- Commercial yields are low (50-100 kg/ha) due to mechanical harvesting constraints, but prices are high
- Home garden plots can be highly productive: 200-800 plants can supply a family
- Seed requires winter stratification; held over winter in bags under cold water or refrigerated at 40°F
- Sown in spring in fields flooded 16-20 cm deep; harvested from mid-autumn
- Fields dried 2-3 weeks before machine harvesting
- Ideal plant spacing is 20 x 35 cm
- Yield potential per selected head: 200-500 g when well cared for
- Wildfowl harvest perhaps 70% of total production as seed ripens slowly over 3-4 weeks
- Mechanical harvest gathers only seed ripe at harvest time, leading to low commercial yields
- Small home plots gathered over 3-4 weeks are in fact very productive

## Traditional and Innovative Harvesting

- Indigenous method: bundling and tying 10-20 heads together, untying periodically for threshing into canoes
- Mechanical harvesting captures only ripe seed at harvest time, as seed ripens gradually over 3-4 weeks
- Home plots gathered over 3-4 weeks are in fact very productive
- Innovative approach: packrats collect and store large quantities of grain in artificial shelters, insulated with cattail (Typha) seed silk
- [[bill-mackently]] uses grey squirrels similarly for hickory nuts and acorns, leaving harvesters ~15% of stores
- Small plots with selected heads can achieve excellent per-plant yields far exceeding commercial fields
- Breeding packrats could be supplied with alternative winter food and allowed to keep 15% of stores
- Packrats may be the most efficient and cleanest harvesters for the crop

## Polyculture Associations

Wild rice systems in Minnesota have developed self-generated polycultures including:
- Crayfish (Homarus) in ditches and margins, browsing 5-10 m into the crop causing some damage but are themselves a potential crop
- Mink and beaver populations along bunds
- Thousands of waterfowl (ducks harvest perhaps 70% of total production)
- Coyote on bunds; packrats store much of the seed
- Duck potato (Sagittaria) as a companion/occasional weed preferring deeper water
- Potential for coldwater fish (trout, bullheads) in canals and ditches, not yet developed
- [[azolla]] not currently used for manuring but has significant potential
- Supplementary crops: duck fattening, mussel meats, hops, silverberry ([[actinidia-arguta]])
- Restricted fur production and recreational fishing as additional income streams

## Relevance to Cultivation

Wild rice is uniquely suited to cold swamps and shallows with intense summer heat, making it an excellent crop for northern climates where few other grain crops thrive in wetland conditions. It is ideal for [[permaculture]] designs incorporating:
- Wildlife refuge food sources (no better autumn fodder for waterfowl)
- Integrated aquaculture-polyculture systems
- Small-scale home production in waterlogged or swampy areas
- Edge productivity in pond and lake margin designs
- Catch-and-restock management strategies

## Cultivation Notes

- Seed provenance should match climatic and water conditions of the culture site
- Some crops have been grown in the Southern hemisphere with mixed results
- Fields must be rested or sown to another crop when introducing new cultivars (self-seeding perpetuates first-sown variety)
- Fields rested dry in winter
- Diversity of harvest methods offers opportunities for innovative small-scale growers
- Pioneer growers Hubert and Leonard Jacobson of Aitken, Minnesota provided key cultivation data
- A small terrace or pond of wild rice should be the aim of every home gardener who can obtain seed

## Ecological Considerations

- Wild rice fields provide critical habitat for migratory waterfowl on the Mississippi flyway
- The plant's slow seed ripening over 3-4 weeks provides an extended food source for wildlife
- Self-generated polycultures in commercial fields demonstrate nature's tendency toward diversity
- The crop can serve as a transition species for restoring degraded wetland ecosystems
- Fields function as nutrient filters when integrated with upstream agricultural drainage

## Economic Notes

- At 1984 prices of $10/kg, fields of 100 hectares can return up to $100,000 annually in rice alone
- The price premium reflects limited supply and the difficulty of mechanical harvesting
- Home-scale production eliminates the need for expensive harvesting machinery ($300,000+)
- Supplementary products (fur, fish, crayfish, ducks) can substantially increase total returns
