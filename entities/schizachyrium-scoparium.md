The species is recognized by its slender blue-green foliage that turns striking shades of copper, orange, and red in autumn. It typically grows 60–120 cm tall and forms dense basal tussocks with an upright, columnar habit.

Little bluestem is the official state grass of Nebraska and Kansas, reflecting its ecological and cultural importance in the North American Great Plains. It is highly drought-tolerant and thrives in nutrient-poor, well-drained soils.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Poales
- **Family:** Poaceae
- **Subfamily:** Panicoideae
- **Tribe:** Andropogoneae
- **Genus:** Schizachyrium Nees

**Bas synonym:** Andropogon scoparius Michx. The species was moved from Andropogon to Schizachyrium by George Valentine Nash in 1903 based on inflorescence morphology.

### Morphology

Little bluestem is a warm-season (C4) perennial bunchgrass forming dense tussocks 40–100 cm in diameter at the base. Culms are erect, slender, and 60–120 cm tall at maturity.

Leaves are flat to involute, 10–30 cm long and 2–5 mm wide, with blue-green coloration during [[comparison-prairie-restoration-vs-silvopasture]] mixtures, it constitutes 10–25% of the seed mix by weight. Fall or dormant seeding is preferred in northern regions.

Establishment is slow, with plants investing heavily in root development during the first growing season. Flowering culms typically do not appear until the second or third year after seeding.

No fertilization is required or recommended once established. Excess nitrogen promotes weed competition and reduces the species' characteristic fall color. Controlled burning every 3–5 years stimulates vigorous regrowth and maintains stand health.

Numerous cultivars are available for ornamental use, including 'The Blues', 'Standing Ovation', 'Carousel', and 'Twilight Zone'. These selections are bred for compact habit, intensified autumn color, and strong upright posture.

## Ecological Role

Schizachyrium scoparium is a foundational species in North American [[mollison-permaculture-two-arid-water-harvesting-and-erosion-control]] on slopes, roadsides, and reclaimed mine lands. Root biomass often exceeds above-ground biomass by a ratio of 2:1.

Over 20 species of Lepidoptera use little bluestem as a larval host plant, including several grass skippers (Hesperiidae). Seeds are consumed by sparrows, finches, juncos, and wild turkeys throughout winter.

As a C4 species, it contributes significant carbon sequestration in prairie soils. The dense thatch layer moderates soil temperature, retains moisture, and facilitates nutrient cycling through decomposition.

It serves as a nurse crop in prairie restorations, protecting slower-establishing forbs and cool-season grasses during early succession. Its allelopathic properties suppress some weedy species, aiding native plant community development.

## Cultural and Economic Significance

Little bluestem was historically one of the most important native forage grasses of the North American prairie. Before European settlement and the widespread introduction of non-native pasture species, it supported vast herds of bison, elk, and pronghorn.
