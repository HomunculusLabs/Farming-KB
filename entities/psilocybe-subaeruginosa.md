# Psilocybe subaeruginosa

**Psilocybe subaeruginosa** is a potent psilocybin mushroom native to Australasia, where it is one of the most commonly encountered psychoactive fungi. It is closely related to the Northern Hemisphere [[psilocybe-cyanescens]] and shares many ecological and morphological characteristics with the "woody psilocybe" group.

## Taxonomy and Naming

The species was first described by John Burton Cleland in 1927 from material collected in South Australia. The epithet "subaeruginosa" means "somewhat dark blue-green," referring to the bluing reaction. Cleland originally placed it in *Psilocybe*, and its taxonomic placement has remained relatively stable, though it has been confused in the literature with *Psilocybe aucklandii* from New Zealand and other Australasian taxa. The full scope of the *P. subaeruginosa* species complex remains under investigation, and cryptic speciation is suspected given the broad geographic range and morphological variability.

## Morphology

The pileus is 1.5 to 6 cm in diameter, convex to broadly convex or nearly plane, sometimes with a slight umbo. The cap surface is smooth and viscid when moist, hygrophanous, reddish-brown to chestnut when wet, fading to pale ochraceous or yellowish-brown when dry. The margin is often slightly wavy or irregular, translucently striate when moist. The lamellae are adnate to slightly sinuate, crowded, grayish at first, becoming dark purple-brown at maturity. The stipe is 4 to 9 cm long and 2 to 5 mm thick, whitish to cream, bruising prominently blue to blue-green when damaged. The base is often covered with white to bluish mycelial threads. The spore print is dark purple-brown. Spores are ellipsoid to subovoid, 10 to 14 by 6.5 to 9 µm, with a distinct germ pore and moderately thick walls.

## Distribution and Habitat

*P. subaeruginosa* has a primarily Australasian distribution. It is widespread across southeastern Australia, including Victoria, New South Wales, South Australia, Tasmania, and southern Queensland. It is also found in New Zealand, particularly in the North Island. The species has been reported, more rarely, from parts of southern South America (Chile) and has been tentatively identified from collections in Europe, though these may represent introductions or misidentifications.

The species is strongly lignicolous, growing on decaying hardwood debris including logs, branches, woodchips, and sawdust of Eucalyptus and other native and introduced hardwoods. It fruits in the cooler months, typically from April through July in the Southern Hemisphere (autumn to early winter), often in large clusters on decomposing wood in forests, parks, gardens, and along waterways.

## Chemistry

*P. subaeruginosa* is a highly potent psilocybin mushroom. Reported psilocybin content ranges from approximately 0.3% to 1.5% of dry weight, with some collections approaching or exceeding 2%. Psilocin is present at 0.1% to 0.5%, and baeocystin has also been detected. The total alkaloid profile places it among the more potent species in the genus, comparable to [[psilocybe-azurescens]] and [[psilocybe-cyanescens]]. As with other wood-loving psilocybes, potency is influenced by substrate composition, with specimens from enriched woodchips often being more potent than those from more impoverished woody debris.

## Ecology

The species is a primary decomposer of hardwood in temperate and subtropical Australasian forests. It plays a significant role in breaking down fallen Eucalyptus branches and other woody litter. The association with introduced woodchip mulch in urban settings mirrors the behavior of [[psilocybe-cyanescens]] in the Northern Hemisphere, suggesting a broad ecological adaptability to anthropogenic lignicolous substrates.

## Ethnobotanical Significance

*P. subaeruginosa* is the most commonly foraged psilocybin mushroom in Australia and New Zealand. While traditional Indigenous use of psychoactive mushrooms in Australia is not well documented, the species has been widely used in the modern recreational and countercultural context since at least the 1970s. It is the primary species encountered by foragers in the Australasian autumn mushroom season.

## Legal Status

The species is illegal to possess, cultivate, or sell throughout Australia and New Zealand. In Australia, psilocybin and psilocin are Schedule 9 (prohibited) substances under the Standard for the Uniform Scheduling of Medicines and Poisons, making possession of the fresh or dried mushrooms a criminal offense.

## Identification Notes

In the field, *P. subaeruginosa* can be confused with several non-psychoactive or toxic species that share its lignicolous habit. The most dangerous lookalikes are species of [[galerina-marginata]] (the deadly galerina), which grow on similar decaying wood and can produce lethal amatoxin poisonings. Key distinguishing features include the Galerina's rusty-brown spore print (versus dark purple-brown in *P. subaeruginosa*) and its more orange-brown cap that lacks the strong bluing reaction. Other potential lookalikes include non-psychoactive *Hypholoma* and *Psathyrella* species found on wood. Microscopic examination of spore size and cheilocystidia morphology is recommended for definitive identification.

## Chemical Profile

Detailed analytical studies have quantified the alkaloid content of *P. subaeruginosa* more thoroughly than many other Australasian species:
- **Psilocybin**: 0.34% to 1.93% of dry weight (avg. ~0.8%)
- **Psilocin**: 0.05% to 0.51% of dry weight
- **Baeocystin**: 0.01% to 0.12% of dry weight
- **Norbaeocystin**: trace amounts detected in some collections

The alkaloid profile is notably variable between collections, with substrate type, geographic origin, and fruiting stage all influencing concentrations. Specimens from enriched hardwood mulch in urban parks of Melbourne and Sydney have consistently tested among the most potent. The total tryptamine content can exceed 2% in exceptional specimens, rivaling the potency of [[psilocybe-azurescens]] from the Pacific Northwest of North America.

## Cultivation

*P. subaeruginosa* can be cultivated using methods similar to those employed for other wood-loving Psilocybe species such as [[psilocybe-cyanescens]]. It colonizes hardwood sawdust and wood chip substrates readily, with Eucalyptus-based substrates producing particularly vigorous mycelial growth. Outdoor cultivation on buried woodchip beds has been successful in temperate climates with cool, wet autumns. The species requires a cold shock (near-freezing temperatures) to initiate fruiting, analogous to the fruiting triggers for Northern Hemisphere wood-loving psilocybes. Indoor cultivation is more challenging due to these specific environmental requirements, though some cultivators have achieved fruiting in controlled cold-environment chambers. Mycelial growth rate is moderate, with full colonization of supplemented sawdust blocks typically taking 3–5 weeks at 20–22°C.

## Taxonomic History

The species was first described by John Burton Cleland in 1927 from material collected in South Australia. Cleland (1878–1971) was one of Australia's most prominent mycologists and published extensively on the fungal flora of the continent. The epithet "subaeruginosa" means "somewhat dark blue-green," referring to the characteristic bluing reaction observed when the mushroom is damaged. Cleland originally placed it in *Psilocybe*, and its taxonomic placement has remained relatively stable, though it has been confused in the literature with [[psilocybe-aucklandii]] from New Zealand and other Australasian taxa. The full scope of the *P. subaeruginosa* species complex remains under investigation, and cryptic speciation is suspected given the broad geographic range and morphological variability. Molecular phylogenetic studies by [[gaston-guzman]] and others have helped clarify relationships within the section, but comprehensive sampling across the full range is still needed.

## Microscopic Features

Detailed microscopic examination reveals additional diagnostic characters. Spores are ellipsoid to subovoid, 10–14 × 6.5–9 µm, with a distinct germ pore and moderately thickened walls. Basidia are four-spored, clavate, 18–24 × 5–7 µm. Cheilocystidia are abundant, ventricose to lageniform with a narrow neck, sometimes with a slight capitate apex, 16–30 × 4–8 µm. Pleurocystidia are less common but similar in morphology. The pileipellis is an ixocutis — a gelatinized layer of hyaline to brownish, filamentous hyphae that gives the cap its viscid texture when moist. These microscopic features are consistent with other members of the *Psilocybe* sect. *Cyanescentes*.

## Similar Species

*P. subaeruginosa* can be confused with several species in the field:
- **Galerina marginata**: Deadly lookalike with rusty-brown spore print. See [[galerina-marginata]].
- **Psilocybe aucklandii**: New Zealand species with slightly smaller spores and less pronounced bluing. See [[psilocybe-aucklandii]].
- **Psilocybe weraroa**: Secotioid species from New Zealand with enclosed cap. Not a close lookalike but phylogenetically related.
- **Hypholoma species**: Non-psychoactive wood-inhabiting species with similar cap coloration but lacking blue bruising and having different spore color.
