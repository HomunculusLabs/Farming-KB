## Overview

Hericium laciniatum, commonly known as the bear's head tooth fungus or coral tooth mushroom, is a species of edible mushroom in the family Hericiaceae. It produces cascading, branched fruiting bodies with long, slender spines hanging from a central core, resembling a waterfall of white icicles.

The species is closely related to and often confused with lion's mane (Hericium erinaceus). It is valued both as a choice edible and for its potential medicinal properties, including neuroprotective and immunomodulatory compounds shared with other Hericium species.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Russulales
- **Family:** Hericiaceae
- **Genus:** Hericium
- **Species:** H. laciniatum

**Basionym:** Hydnum laciniatum Leers (1778).

**Synonyms:**

- Hericium ramosum (Merat) Banker (1906), widely used in North American literature.
- Hydnum ramosum Merat (1821).
- Merisma laciniatum (Leers) Kuntze (1891).

Some authorities consider H. laciniatum conspecific with H. coralloides, while others maintain them as distinct species based on spine morphology and substrate preference. The debate continues in modern mycological literature.

## Morphology

**Fruiting body:** Soft-fleshy, initially compact, expanding into a branched, coral-like structure. Individual branches arise from a short, thickened base or a rudimentary stipe. The overall mass can reach 5–20 cm across.

**Spines (teeth):** The most distinctive feature — long, slender, tapered spines measuring 5–25 mm in length and 0.5–1 mm in diameter. They hang downward in curtains or tufts from the branches and branch tips. Spines are white when fresh, turning yellowish-brown with age.

**Flesh:** White, soft, and watery when young. Becomes tough and fibrous in maturity. Has a mild, pleasant odor reminiscent of seafood, similar to H. erinaceus.

**Spore print:** White to cream.

**Spores:** Globose to subglobose, smooth, 5–7 × 5–6 µm, hyaline, amyloid, occurring on basidia at the tips of the spines.

**Chemical reactions:** Flesh stains brownish to reddish-brown when cut or bruised, especially in age. KOH on flesh produces a negative to weakly pinkish reaction.

## Distribution and Habitat

Hericium laciniatum has a broad distribution across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere. It is found throughout Europe, from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean, across much of North America (both eastern and western regions), and extends into parts of Asia.

The species is primarily a saprotrophic wood-decay fungus, though it may have a facultative parasitic stage on weakened trees. It fruits on dead or dying hardwoods, especially standing dead trunks and large fallen logs.

**Preferred substrates:**

- Oak (Quercus spp.), the most commonly reported host.
- Beech (Fagus spp.) in European forests.
- Maple (Acer spp.) and birch (Betula spp.) in North America.
- Occasionally on conifers, particularly spruce.

Fruiting occurs from late summer through autumn (August–November in the Northern Hemisphere), typically during cool, wet periods following heavy rains. The species favors old-growth forests with abundant coarse woody debris.

## Cultivation

Hericium laciniatum is cultivated on a smaller commercial scale than H. erinaceus but shares similar cultivation parameters. It is considered more challenging to fruit reliably in artificial conditions.

**Substrate:** Supplemented hardwood sawdust (oak, beech, or maple) at 65–75% moisture content. Wheat bran or rice bran supplementation at 10–20% improves yields. Some growers use a mixed substrate with small amounts of soy meal.

**Spawn run:** Optimal temperature 20–25°C. Full colonization typically takes 18–25 days, slightly slower than H. erinaceus.

**Fruiting conditions:** Requires a cold shock or temperature drop (to 15–18°C) to initiate primordia. High humidity (90–95%) is critical during pinning. Fresh air exchange is essential — elevated CO₂ leads to distorted, elongated growth.

**Yield:** Biological efficiency ranges from 50–85%, lower than the 100–150% achievable with H. erinaceus. The species is sensitive to contamination, particularly Trichoderma, during the spawn run.

**Harvest:** Fruit bodies should be harvested when spines are fully elongated but still white. Post-harvest shelf life is limited — 5–7 days refrigerated.

## Bioactive Compounds

Like other Hericium species, H. laciniatum produces a range of bioactive metabolites. Research has identified several classes of compounds with therapeutic potential.

**Erinacines:** Cytotoxic diterpenoid compounds isolated from the mycelium of Hericium species. Erinacine A, C, and H have been detected in H. laciniatum extracts. These compounds can cross the blood-brain barrier and stimulate nerve growth factor (NGF) synthesis.

**Hericenones:** Phenolic compounds found primarily in the fruiting body. Hericenones C and D, originally characterized from H. erinaceus, are also present in H. laciniatum in varying concentrations.

**Polysaccharides:** β-glucans and other immunomodulatory polysaccharides constitute 15–25% of the dried fruiting body weight. These compounds activate macrophages and NK cells through Dectin-1 receptor signaling.

**Other compounds:** The species contains small amounts of hericin, hericenols, and several fatty acids including oleic and linoleic acid.

## Culinary and Nutritional Use

Hericium laciniatum is considered a premium edible mushroom with a delicate texture and mild, slightly sweet flavor. The culinary profile is comparable to lobster or crab, shared across the Hericium genus.

**Preparation:** Young, fresh specimens are best. They can be sautéed, grilled, or incorporated into soups and cream sauces. The flesh absorbs flavors well and pairs excellently with butter, garlic, and white wine.

**Nutritional profile (per 100 g dried):**

- Protein: 15–22 g
- Carbohydrates: 45–60 g (including dietary fiber)
- Fat: 2–4 g
- Potassium, phosphorus, and zinc in notable amounts

The species is commercially available dried and as a dietary supplement in some European and Asian markets, though it remains less common than H. erinaceus in commercial production.

## Ecological Role

As a white-rot fungus, H. laciniatum plays an important role in forest nutrient cycling. It decomposes lignin and cellulose in dead hardwood, releasing nutrients back into the soil for uptake by plants and other organisms.

The species serves as a food source for various forest invertebrates and small mammals. Slugs and insects feed on the fruiting body, while squirrels have been observed consuming mature specimens.

In forest ecology, H. laciniatum is an indicator species for old-growth conditions. Its presence suggests a mature forest with significant coarse woody debris and minimal disturbance. It contributes to the structural complexity of dead wood habitats, providing microhabitats for insects and microorganisms.
