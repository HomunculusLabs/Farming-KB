## Overview

Cypripedium calceolus, commonly known as the lady's slipper orchid, is a perennial terrestrial orchid in the family Orchidaceae.

It is one of the most iconic and threatened orchid species in Europe.

The species is easily recognized by its large, slipper-shaped yellow labellum and maroon petals.

Cypripedium calceolus is the national flower of Sweden and holds protected status throughout most of its range.

It is the most widely distributed Cypripedium species in the Old World.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Cypripedium calceolus was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum.

The genus name derives from the Greek Kypris, a reference to Aphrodite, and pedion, meaning foot or slipper.

The specific epithet calceolus is Latin for "small shoe," describing the distinctive pouch-shaped labellum.

The species belongs to the subfamily Cypripedioideae, commonly known as the lady's slipper orchids.

Plants grow 25–60 cm tall from a creeping rhizome with thick, fibrous roots.

The stem bears 3–5 alternate, ovate to elliptic leaves, each 10–20 cm long.

The inflorescence produces 1–2 flowers, occasionally up to 3.

Each flower is 6–9 cm across, making it one of the largest European orchid flowers.

The dorsal sepal is erect and maroon to reddish-brown, 30–45 mm long.

Lateral sepals are fused into a synsepal behind the labellum, similar in color to the dorsal sepal.

The petals are twisted, narrow, and maroon, often 40–60 mm long.

The labellum is inflated, pouch-shaped, and bright yellow, 15–25 mm long.

The column is short and fused to the margins of the labellum opening.

Pollinia are granular and positioned on the stigma, requiring insect visitors for effective pollination.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cypripedium calceolus has a broad but fragmented distribution across Eurasia.

In Europe, it occurs from Spain and France eastward through central Europe to the Balkans and the Carpathians.

The species extends across Russia to Siberia, reaching the Pacific coast.

The lady's slipper orchid grows in calcareous, nutrient-poor soils in semi-shaded conditions.

Typical habitats include deciduous and mixed woodlands, forest margins, scrub, and limestone pavement.

The species is strongly associated with old-growth or long-undisturbed forest stands.

## Ecology

Cypripedium calceolus relies on a small number of medium-sized bees for pollination.

In Europe, the primary pollinators are Andrena species and certain Bombus workers.

The labellum functions as a trap, luring insects inside with scent and bright coloration.

Pollinia adhere to the insect's body and are transferred to the next flower visited.

The species is mycoheterotrophic during seedling establishment, relying on basidiomycete fungi.

Flowering is not annual; mature plants may remain vegetative for years between flowering events.

## Conservation

Cypripedium calceolus is classified as Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List.

The species is listed in Appendix II of CITES, regulating international trade.

It is protected by national legislation in virtually every European country where it occurs.

In the United Kingdom, a single wild population survives in Yorkshire, guarded and monitored since the 1970s.

Habitat loss through forestry, agricultural intensification, and urbanization remains the primary threat.

Reintroduction projects in the UK, Germany, and the Netherlands have achieved limited success.

A European Action Plan for the species was developed under the Bern Convention.

## Cultivation

Cypripedium calceolus is one of the most sought-after terrestrial orchids in horticulture.

Cultivation is considered difficult and is recommended only for experienced growers.

Plants require a humus-rich, well-drained, slightly acidic to neutral substrate.

A mix of loam, leaf mold, perlite, and fine bark has proven successful in cultivation.

Dappled shade or morning sun with afternoon shade is ideal.

Consistent moisture during the growing season is essential, but winter drainage must be excellent.

Propagation from seed requires mycorrhizal symbionts or sterile laboratory conditions.

Division of established clumps is possible but slow, with recovery taking several years.
