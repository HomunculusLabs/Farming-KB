## Taxonomy and Morphology

Cypripedium calceolus was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum.

The genus name derives from the Greek Kypris, a reference to Aphrodite, and pedion, meaning foot or slipper.

The specific epithet calceolus is Latin for "small shoe," describing the distinctive pouch-shaped labellum.

The species belongs to the subfamily Cypripedioideae, commonly known as the lady's slipper orchids.

Plants grow 25–60 cm tall from a creeping rhizome with thick, fibrous roots.

The stem bears 3–5 alternate, ovate to elliptic leaves, each 10–20 cm long.

The inflorescence produces 1–2 flowers, occasionally up to 3.

Each flower is 6–9 cm across, making it one of the largest European orchid flowers.

The dorsal sepal is erect and maroon to reddish-brown, 30–45 mm long.

Lateral sepals are fused into a synsepal behind the labellum, similar in color to the dorsal sepal.

The petals are twisted, narrow, and maroon, often 40–60 mm long.

The labellum is inflated, pouch-shaped, and bright yellow, 15–25 mm long.

The column is short and fused to the margins of the labellum opening.

Pollinia are granular and positioned on the stigma, requiring insect visitors for effective pollination.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cypripedium calceolus has a broad but fragmented distribution across Eurasia.

In Europe, it occurs from Spain and France eastward through central Europe to the Balkans and the Carpathians.

The species extends across Russia to Siberia, reaching the Pacific coast.

The lady's slipper orchid grows in calcareous, nutrient-poor soils in semi-shaded conditions.

Typical habitats include deciduous and mixed woodlands, forest margins, scrub, and limestone pavement.

The species is strongly associated with old-growth or long-undisturbed forest stands.

## Ecology

Cypripedium calceolus relies on a small number of medium-sized bees for pollination.

In Europe, the primary pollinators are Andrena species and certain Bombus workers.

The labellum functions as a trap, luring insects inside with scent and bright coloration.

Pollinia adhere to the insect's body and are transferred to the next flower visited.
