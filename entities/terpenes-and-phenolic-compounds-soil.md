## Description

Terpenes, phenolic compounds, and tannins are secondary plant metabolites found in woody material that serve as chemical defenses against pathogens. These compounds are difficult for microorganisms to break down and can persist in soil and [[compost]], sometimes providing beneficial weed suppression (as with black walnut terpenes) and sometimes inhibiting beneficial organisms. Managing these compounds through proper [[composting]] — allowing volatilization, promoting [[fungal-decomposition]], or deliberately leveraging their allelopathic properties — is an important aspect of soil biology management.

## Classification

- **Category**: Secondary plant metabolites / Allelopathic compounds
- **Types**: Terpenes (e.g., juglone from black walnut), tannins, phenolic compounds
- **Occurrence**: All woody material contains some level of these compounds
- **Persistence**: Resistant to rapid decomposition; may take 4+ weeks to volatilize from wood chips

## Key Facts

- All woody material contains hard-to-break-down defensive compounds
- These are the plant's natural protection against pathogen attack
- Black walnut produces a particularly effective terpene (likely juglone)
- Options for managing these materials:
  1. Chip and pile for 4+ weeks to allow terpenes/phenols to volatize (turning speeds the process)
  2. Apply fungal-rich compost tea to enhance biological decomposition
  3. Use aged wood chip material to inoculate fresh chips with decomposer organisms
  4. Leverage terpenes deliberately for weed suppression in specific areas
- Black walnut terpenes may provide weed suppressant properties — not scientifically documented
- Proper composting decomposes these toxic materials completely
- Only improperly composted material risks carrying toxins into the final product
- Compost tea with good fungal content can accelerate breakdown of recalcitrant plant compounds

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

For cultivators working with wood-based substrates (a common practice in [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]]), understanding terpenes and phenolic compounds is essential. Fresh wood chips and sawdust can contain sufficient terpenes to inhibit mycelial growth. The remediation strategies Ingham describes — aging, turning, and applying fungal-rich teas — are directly applicable to [[substrate-preparation]]. The allelopathic potential of compounds like juglone also offers opportunities: black walnut chips could be deliberately used as a weed suppressant mulch in outdoor cultivation beds. The key principle is that these compounds are biodegradable but require specific conditions (time, aeration, and fungal activity) to break down. Skipping the composting or aging step with woody materials risks introducing growth-inhibiting compounds into the growing environment.
