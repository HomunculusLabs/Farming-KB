# Amanita bisporigera

*Amanita bisporigera* is a deadly poisonous mushroom in the family
Amanitaceae, commonly known as the Eastern North American Destroying Angel.
It is among the most lethal mushrooms known, responsible for the majority of
fatal mushroom poisonings in eastern North America. A single mature cap
contains sufficient toxin to kill an adult human. Despite its lethality, its
pure white, innocuous appearance and mild odor make it one of the most
dangerous organisms that amateur foragers encounter in eastern forests.

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

Described by George Francis Atkinson in 1906 (*Botanical Gazette*, Vol. 42),
the species name derives from Latin *bis-* ("two") and *sporigera*
("[[spore]]-bearing"), referring to its characteristically two-spored basidia.
Early treatments placed it as *[[amanita-verna]]* var. *bisporigera* before
elevation to full species rank. Molecular phylogenetics (ITS, LSU, RPB2,
TEF1 markers) confirms it as distinct from European *A. virosa* and western
North American *A. ocreata*.

## Morphology

The cap (pileus) measures 2.5–10 cm diameter, ovate when young, expanding to
convex or plano-convex. Surface is pure white and smooth, margin slightly
appendiculate with veil remnants. Gills (lamellae) are free, crowded, narrow,
white, becoming cream-tinted with age. The stem (stipe) is 5–14 cm × 0.6–2
cm, white, solid, smooth above the annulus, slightly fibrillose below, with
a bulbous base enveloped by the volva. The ring (annulus) is membranous,
white, skirt-like, near the stipe apex — persistent but fragile. The volva
is saccate, white, membranous, cupping the stem base, often partially buried.
Flesh is white, thin, firm, and does not change color on exposure. Odor is
mild and pleasant — dangerously non-distinctive. [[spore-print]] is white.

## Spore Characteristics

Spores measure 7.0–9.5 × 6.0–9.0 µm, broadly ellipsoid to subglobose,
smooth, thin-walled, and inamyloid (non-reactive with Melzer's reagent).
Basidia are characteristically bisporic (two-spored), rarely four-spored —
the defining microscopic trait. Cheilocystidia are abundant, clavate to
subcylindrical, thin-walled (20–40 × 10–20 µm). Pleurocystidia are absent.
The pileipellis is a cutis of interwoven filamentous hyphae.

## Distinguishing Features from Related Species

*A. virosa* (European Destroying Angel) is morphologically similar but
typically four-spored with subtle cheilocystidia differences. *A. phalloides*
(Death Cap) typically has a greenish-yellow to olive cap, concentric-ring
volva on a bulbous base, and four-spored basidia. *A. ocreata* (Western
Destroying Angel) occupies western North America and is molecularly distinct.
*A. verna* (European Fool's Mushroom) is European with a more hemispherical
cap. Wolfe et al. (2012) and Cai et al. (2014) found *A. bisporigera*
phylogenetically closest to *A. ocreata*.

## Geographic Distribution

Eastern North America: southeastern Canada (Quebec, Ontario) through the
eastern and central United States, from Maine to Florida, west through the
Great Lakes states to Iowa, Missouri, eastern Texas, and Kansas. Rarely in
northeastern Mexico. Absent from the Pacific Northwest, where *A. ocreata*
fills the equivalent niche. Commonly encountered in eastern hardwood forests
during late summer and autumn.

## Habitat and Ecology

Ectomycorrhizal, primarily with oaks (*Quercus*) but also beech (*Fagus*),
birch (*Betula*), hickory (*Carya*), maple (*Acer*), and occasionally pines
(*Pinus*). The mycorrhizal association is obligate — cannot be cultivated.
Found in mixed hardwood, oak-hickory, and beech-maple forests, solitary or
in small groups. Fruits July–October, favored by warm, wet periods following
heavy rain on organic-rich soils.

## Toxicity Profile

Contains the highest amatoxin concentrations of any North American mushroom,
up to ~3.5 mg/g dry weight. Principal toxins: α-amanitin and β-amanitin
(bicyclic octapeptides; LD₅₀ ≈ 0.1 mg/kg mouse). A single cap may contain
5–8 mg total amatoxins, exceeding the human lethal dose of ~0.1 mg/kg.
Secondary toxins: phallotoxins (phalloidin, phalloin) and virotoxins — toxic
*in vitro* but poorly absorbed orally. Amatoxins are heat-stable, not
destroyed by cooking, boiling, drying, freezing, or acid.

## Mechanism of Poisoning

Amatoxins are absorbed from the GI tract and transported via portal
circulation to the liver. Enterohepatic recirculation concentrates them in
hepatocytes. α-Amanitin irreversibly inhibits RNA polymerase II, blocking
mRNA transcription. Protein synthesis ceases, hepatocytes cannot synthesize
essential proteins, and progressive hepatocellular necrosis ensues. This
leads to hepatic failure, coagulopathy, hypoglycemia, renal failure, and
multi-organ death.

## Clinical Presentation

A deceptive four-phase pattern: Phase I (6–24h): asymptomatic latent period.
Phase II (6–24h): sudden severe nausea, vomiting, profuse watery diarrhea,
cramping, dehydration. Phase III (24–72h): dangerous "lucid interval" —
symptoms subside while liver enzymes silently rise catastrophically. Phase IV
(72–96h+): frank hepatic failure with jaundice, transaminases >1000 IU/L,
coagulopathy, hypoglycemia, encephalopathy, renal failure, coma, death.
Historical mortality: 50–60%; modern: ~10–15%, approaching 50% with delayed
treatment or massive ingestion.

## Treatment

Activated charcoal if presenting within hours; repeated dosing may interrupt
enterohepatic recirculation. Aggressive IV fluids. Silibinin (silybin, from
milk thistle) inhibits hepatocellular amatoxin uptake by blocking OATP
transporters — IV formulation (Legalon® SIL) is European standard of care.
High-dose IV penicillin G competitively inhibits amatoxin uptake. NAC
provides antioxidant support. Liver transplantation for fulminant failure,
guided by King's College or Clichy criteria.

## Poisoning History and Cultural Significance

Destroying angels cause ~90% of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide. *A.
bisporigera* accounts for most wild-mushroom fatalities in eastern North
America. Victims often include experienced foragers who mistake it for edible
*[[volvariella-volvacea]]* ([[paddy-straw-mushroom]]) or *Amanita* sect. *Vaginatae*.
Several US fatalities documented annually. The name "Destroying Angel"
captures the juxtaposition of pure white beauty and lethal toxicity. It is
a cornerstone example in [[mycology]] education, underscoring: never eat a
white-gilled, white mushroom with a volva unless identified by an expert.
