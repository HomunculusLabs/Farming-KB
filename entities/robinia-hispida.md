## Overview

Robinia hispida, commonly known as rose acacia or bristly locust, is a deciduous shrub in the family Fabaceae native to the southeastern United States. It is closely related to black locust (Robinia pseudoacacia) but distinguished by its rose-pink flowers and densely bristly stems.

The species is valued as an ornamental for its showy floral display and has been widely planted outside its native range. In parts of the northeastern United States and beyond, it has escaped cultivation and become locally invasive.

Several named selections are available in the nursery trade, including compact forms suited to smaller gardens. The species is relatively short-lived for a woody plant, typically 15 to 25 years.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Robinia hispida was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The specific epithet hispida means "bristly," referring to the gland-tipped hairs covering the stems and petioles.

It belongs to the tribe Robinieae within subfamily Faboideae, closely allied with the genera Sophora and Sesbania.

The shrub typically grows 1.5 to 3 meters tall, forming dense thickets through root suckering. Stems are green to purplish-brown and densely covered with bristly glandular hairs, a diagnostic feature separating it from other Robinia species.

Growth rate is moderately fast, with new suckers capable of reaching 1 meter in a single growing season. Mature specimens develop a spreading, irregular crown form.

Leaves are alternate and pinnately compound, bearing 7 to 13 elliptic leaflets each 2 to 4 cm long. Leaflets are glabrous above and sparsely pubescent beneath, with entire margins and rounded to slightly emarginate tips.

Flowers are produced in pendulous racemes of 5 to 15 blooms from April through June. The papilionaceous flowers are rose-pink to purplish-pink, approximately 2 cm long, with a prominent standard petal and reflexed wing petals.

Fruits are flattened legume pods 5 to 8 cm long, covered in glandular bristles, containing 2 to 6 kidney-shaped seeds. Pods mature in late summer and autumn, dehiscing to release seeds.

The bark on older stems develops shallow fissures and turns gray-brown. Young twigs remain green and photosynthetic for several years, contributing to the plant's drought tolerance.

Wood anatomy is typical of the Robinieae tribe, with ring-porous vessels and broad rays. Heartwood is yellowish and moderately durable but too small-dimensioned for structural use.

Root systems are extensive and shallow, with abundant nitrogen-fixing nodules and prolific suckering roots. Clonal colonies can spread several meters from the parent plant.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is native to the southeastern United States, with a range centered on the Appalachian region from Alabama and Georgia north through Tennessee, Kentucky, and Virginia.

It occurs naturally on rocky slopes, forest margins, stream banks, and disturbed sites at elevations from 100 to 800 meters. It prefers well-drained soils and full sun but tolerates partial shade.

Outside its native range, R. hispida has naturalized widely across the northeastern United States, the Midwest, the Pacific Northwest, and parts of Europe and Japan. It spreads primarily through root suckering rather than seed dispersal.

In Europe, the species has been recorded as naturalized in Germany, France, Italy, and the British Isles. It is less aggressive than R. pseudoacacia in invasion potential but still poses a concern in sensitive habitats.

The species tolerates a wide range of soil pH, from acidic to mildly alkaline. It performs best on loamy, well-drained soils but survives on thin rocky substrates typical of its native Appalachian habitat.

Periodic disturbance such as road cuts, old fields, and stream bank erosion promotes colonization and spread. Once established, clones persist indefinitely through root suckering.

## Cultivation and Uses

Rose acacia is planted as an ornamental shrub for its abundant pink flowers and drought tolerance. It is hardy to USDA zones 4 through 9 and adapts to poor soils, making it useful for difficult landscape sites.

The species has been used in erosion control and mine reclamation due to its nitrogen-fixing root nodules and vigorous suckering habit. Dense thickets stabilize soil on steep slopes and disturbed ground.

Young shoots and seeds contain toxic robin lectins and should not be consumed. The wood is light and brittle, with no commercial timber value.

All parts of the plant contain toxalbumins similar to those found in R. pseudoacacia. Ingestion can cause nausea, vomiting, and gastrointestinal distress in humans and livestock.

Propagation is most commonly done by root cuttings or division of suckering offsets. Seeds require scarification and stratification for reliable germination.

Several horticultural cultivars exist, including 'Flore Pleno' with double flowers and 'Idaho' selected for enhanced cold hardiness. The straight species remains the most widely available form in the nursery trade.

In riparian buffer plantings, R. hispida has been used to stabilize stream banks alongside native grasses and forbs. Its nitrogen contribution improves soil fertility for understory plant establishment.

Pruning requirements are minimal, though removal of dead stems and thinning of overly dense thickets maintains plant health and flowering vigor. Renewal pruning every 3 to 5 years rejuvenates older plantings.

## Ecological Role

Like other Robinia species, R. hispida forms symbiotic associations with Rhizobium bacteria in root nodules, fixing atmospheric nitrogen. This enriches soils and benefits neighboring vegetation in mixed plantings.

The flowers attract a range of pollinators including bees, butterflies, and hummingbirds. Several native bee species specialize on Robinia pollen.

Browsing mammals generally avoid the bristly stems, though deer may consume young shoots when other forage is scarce. The dense thickets provide cover for small birds and mammals.

In areas where it has naturalized, R. hispida can form monocultures that displace native understory vegetation. Its aggressive suckering allows it to outcompete slower-growing native shrubs.

The species is listed as invasive in several northeastern states, including Connecticut, Massachusetts, and New York. Management includes mechanical removal of root systems and careful herbicide application to cut stumps.

Leaf litter from R. hispida contributes nitrogen-rich organic matter to the soil surface. Decomposition rates are relatively fast compared to other woody legumes, cycling nutrients quickly through the ecosystem.

## Related Species

Robinia pseudoacacia (black locust) is the most closely related species, differing in its white flowers, nearly hairless stems, and larger tree habit. R. hispida is sometimes considered a variety of R. pseudoacacia by some authors.

Robinia viscosa (clammy locust) has pink flowers and sticky glandular stems but lacks the dense bristles of R. hispida. It is also native to the southeastern United States.

Robinia neomexicana (New Mexico locust) is a western species with rose-pink flowers, found in the mountains of the southwestern United States. It occupies a similar ecological niche in arid regions.

Robinia ambigua is a hybrid of R. pseudoacacia and R. hispida, combining the tree habit of the former with the pink flowers of the latter. It is occasionally cultivated under the name 'Idaho locust'.

Intergeneric hybrids between Robinia and other Fabaceae genera are rare. The genus comprises approximately 10 species, all native to North America.

The chromosome number of R. hispida is 2n = 20, consistent with the base number for the genus. Cytological studies confirm its close relationship with R. pseudoacacia and support its treatment as a distinct species.

Among ornamental Fabaceae shrubs, R. hispida is often compared to Caragana arborescens (Siberian peashrub) for similar landscape uses. However, Caragana has yellow flowers and originates from Siberia and Central Asia.
