## Overview

*Geissorhiza radians* (Thunb.) Goldblatt, commonly known as the wine cup flower or wynkelkbessie, is a cormous perennial in the family Iridaceae. It is endemic to the Western Cape of South Africa.

The species is celebrated for its striking flowers that are deep crimson to purple with a brilliant green center. The cup-shaped blooms resemble small wine glasses, giving rise to the common name.

It is one of approximately 100 species in the genus *Geissorhiza*, most of which are endemic to the Cape Floristic Region. The wine cup flower is among the most visually dramatic members of the genus.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Order: Asparagales
- Family: Iridaceae
- Subfamily: Ixioideae
- Tribe: Ixieae
- Genus: *Geissorhiza*
- Species: *G. radians*

The genus name *Geissorhiza* derives from Greek *geisson* (tile) and *rhiza* (root), referring to the overlapping corm tunics. The species epithet *radians* refers to the radiating color pattern of the flower.

Plants grow 10–25 cm tall from asymmetrical, globose corms. The corm tunics are concentric and woody, forming a distinctive tiled pattern that is diagnostic for the genus.

Each plant produces 2–4 narrow, linear leaves that are usually prostrate or spreading. Leaves are 1–2 mm wide and 8–15 cm long, with moderately thickened margins and midribs.

Flowers are cup-shaped, 3–5 cm in diameter, with six tepals. The outer tepals are deep crimson to purple, while the center is bright green with a white or yellow ring.

The stamens are erect and clustered in the center of the flower. Anthers are purple, complementing the overall dark coloration of the bloom.

The style is filiform with three short branches. Each branch ends in a small stigma that receives pollen from visiting insects.

Capsules are globose to ovoid, 5–8 mm in diameter. Seeds are small, angular, and reddish-brown when mature.

The chromosome number is 2n = 26, which falls within the range reported for the genus. The base chromosome number for *Geissorhiza* is x = 13.

The species is most closely related to *G. splendidissima* and *G. inflexa*, which share similar deep-colored flowers. It is distinguished by the bright green central eye and cupped flower shape.

Variation in flower color has been observed across populations. Some individuals display darker purple flowers, while others show a more open, stellate tepal arrangement.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Geissorhiza radians* is endemic to the Western Cape Province of South Africa. Its range is centered on the Cape Town area, extending from the Cape Flats to the Stellenbosch and Paarl districts.

The species grows in seasonally wet, sandy flats and lower mountain slopes. It occurs in renosterveld and fynbos vegetation at elevations of 10–250 m.

Like many Cape geophytes, it is winter-growing and summer-dormant. The species flowers in spring (August–October) after the winter rains have provided sufficient soil moisture.

Much of its historical habitat on the Cape Flats has been lost to urban development. Remaining populations are scattered and often small.

The species is listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. Urban expansion, agricultural conversion, and invasive alien vegetation are the primary threats.

Several populations persist on road verges and in small nature reserves around Cape Town. These fragments represent the last remnants of the species' original lowland habitat.

Post-fire observations show enhanced flowering in burned areas. The species benefits from the removal of competing vegetation and nutrient release after fire.

## Cultivation

The wine cup flower is grown as an ornamental bulb by specialist collectors and in rock gardens. Its dramatic flower color makes it one of the most sought-after *Geissorhiza* species in cultivation.

Corms should be planted in autumn at 3–5 cm depth in sandy, well-drained soil. Full sun is essential for good flowering and compact growth.

Water moderately during the winter growing season, keeping soil evenly moist but never waterlogged. Reduce water as foliage yellows and cease entirely during summer dormancy.

Propagation is primarily by seed, as corm offsets are produced sparingly. Fresh seed germinates in 2–3 weeks at 12–15°C without special treatment.

Seed-grown plants typically flower in their second or third year. Plants grown from corm offsets may flower in the first season.

The species is best grown in deep containers where corms can be kept dry during summer dormancy. In-ground cultivation is possible only in Mediterranean climates with reliably dry summers.

Slugs and snails can damage emerging foliage in late autumn. Physical barriers or organic pellets applied at planting time provide effective protection.

Plants in containers benefit from a low-phosphorus fertilizer applied once during the growing season. Avoid high-nitrogen feeds, which promote soft, disease-prone foliage.

## Ecology

In the wild, *G. radians* is pollinated by short-tongued bees and hopliine monkey beetles. The bright green flower center acts as a visual attractant and landing platform.

The dark red-purple color is produced by anthocyanin pigments concentrated in the tepal epidermis. The green center results from chlorophyll retained in the tepal tissue.

The species is adapted to nutrient-poor, acid sands typical of the Cape lowlands. Mycorrhizal associations help the corms acquire phosphorus from these impoverished soils.

*Geissorhiza* corms are not known to be palatable to vertebrate herbivores. The fibrous, woody tunics may deter burrowing mammals from consuming the corms.

The genus *Geissorhiza* is most closely related to *Ixia* and *Sparaxis*, sharing the same tribe Ixieae. The distinctive corm tunic structure is the primary character separating it from these genera.

The species has no documented traditional food or medicinal uses. Its value to humans is entirely ornamental, both in gardens and as a subject for botanical illustration.

The wine cup flower was illustrated in Curtis's Botanical Magazine in the early 19th century. Its distinctive appearance made it a popular subject for Cape botanical artists.

Conservation efforts include seed collection from remaining wild populations for ex situ storage. The Kirstenbosch Botanical Garden maintains cultivated stocks as a genetic backup.
