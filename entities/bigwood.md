# Jeremy Bigwood

Jeremy Bigwood is an American mycologist and analytical chemist known for pioneering
quantitative analysis of [[psilocybin-compound]] and [[psilocin]] levels in psychoactive mushrooms.
His work provided the first systematic, species-by-species data on tryptamine alkaloid
content across a wide range of Psilocybe species and related genera, establishing
baseline pharmacological data that continues to be referenced decades after publication.

## Biography

Bigwood received his academic training in chemistry and applied analytical methods
to the study of naturally occurring psychoactive compounds.
His interest in fungal alkaloids emerged from the intersection of organic chemistry
and ethnobotany, fields undergoing significant growth in the 1980s and 1990s as
researchers sought to characterize the chemical profiles of traditionally used
entheogenic plants and fungi.

Throughout his career, Bigwood maintained an interdisciplinary approach,
combining rigorous laboratory analysis with field collection and collaboration
with taxonomic mycologists.
This dual perspective allowed him to connect chemical data with morphological
species concepts, contributing to both pharmacological understanding and taxonomic
refinement within the genus Psilocybe.

His work emerged during a period of renewed scientific interest in psychedelic
compounds, following decades of restricted research access due to regulatory
constraints.
By applying modern analytical chemistry techniques to these poorly characterized
natural products, Bigwood helped establish an evidence-based foundation for
understanding the pharmacological diversity of psilocybin-containing fungi.

Bigwood's approach to mycochemistry was notable for its quantitative rigor.
Rather than simply detecting the presence or absence of psychoactive alkaloids,
he sought to measure their concentrations precisely, enabling meaningful comparisons
between species, populations, and growing conditions.

## Research Contributions

Bigwood's most cited work is the 1992 study *Variation of Psilocybin and Psilocin
Levels*, co-authored with [[beug]].
This paper analyzed psilocybin and psilocin concentrations in dried specimens
from 12 species of psilocybin-containing mushrooms, establishing baseline
pharmacological data that remains widely referenced.

The study analyzed specimens from multiple collections and geographic origins for
each species, documenting not only average alkaloid content but also the range of
variation within and between species, providing a more nuanced picture of the
pharmacological landscape of psilocybin mushrooms than had previously been available.

### Key Findings

Key findings from this research include:

- Wide intra-species variation in alkaloid content, with [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]] showing the greatest range (0.05% to 1.3% psilocybin), a 25-fold variation
- [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-azurescens-species-guide]] and [[psilocybe-bohemica]] ranked among the most potent species, with total alkaloid content reaching approximately 2% dry weight
- Confirmed that [[baeocystin]] is present in most psilocybin-containing species but at lower concentrations, suggesting it may be a biosynthetic precursor
- Demonstrated significant variation depending on geographic origin, growing conditions, and specimen maturity
- Established that [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] contained relatively high psilocybin but low psilocin
- Noted that [[panaeolus-cyanescens-cultivation-guide]] showed consistently high total alkaloid content despite being from a different genus
- Documented that the ratio of psilocybin to psilocin varies significantly between species

### Impact on Dosage Guidelines

The quantitative data produced by Bigwood and Beug has been widely used to inform
dosage guidelines for therapeutic and recreational use of psilocybin mushrooms.
Their finding of significant variation within species highlighted the inherent
difficulty of precise dosing with natural fungal material, increasingly relevant
in modern clinical research into psychedelic-assisted therapy.

Clinical researchers studying psilocybin for depression, anxiety, and substance
use disorders have cited Bigwood and Beug's work when discussing the challenges
of standardizing natural mushroom preparations.
The variability they documented supports the argument for using chemically pure
psilocybin in clinical settings where precise dosing is essential.

## Analytical Methods

Bigwood employed high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with UV detection
for alkaloid quantification.
His methodology set standards for subsequent researchers including [[jochen-gartz]]
and others studying [[psilocybin-compound]] content variation, representing a significant
advance over earlier thin-layer chromatography (TLC) methods.

The analytical protocol involved extracting alkaloids from dried mushroom tissue
using methanol or ethanol, separating components on a reverse-phase C18 column,
and detecting psilocybin and psilocin based on UV absorption at approximately
267 nm.
This allowed simultaneous quantification of multiple alkaloids from a single
extract, providing a more complete chemical profile than earlier methods.

Calibration was performed using authentic psilocybin and psilocin standards, with
results expressed as percentage of dry weight.
Sample preparation addressed the non-uniform distribution of alkaloids within
fruiting bodies by homogenizing entire dried specimens before analysis.

## Relationship with Other Researchers

Bigwood's work built upon and complemented the research of several other prominent
figures in psychedelic mycology.
His collaboration with [[beug]] combined analytical chemistry
expertise with deep taxonomic knowledge, producing research that was both chemically
rigorous and mycologically informed.

His quantitative approach complemented the taxonomic work of gaston-guzman|Gastón Guzmán, whose worldwide monographs catalogued the diversity of psilocybin-containing
species.
[[jochen-gartz]] extended the analytical tradition established by Bigwood and Beug,
analyzing additional species and developing methods for detecting minor alkaloids
and biosynthetic intermediates.

## Later Work and Advocacy

Bigwood has also worked in drug policy analysis and served as an expert witness on
psychoactive substances.
His later publications addressed the challenges of regulating naturally variable
psychoactive products, the limitations of forensic identification methods for
mushroom material, and the scientific basis for scheduling decisions related to
psilocybin-containing fungi.

Bigwood has been critical of scientifically unjustified restrictions on research
into psychoactive fungi, arguing that regulatory barriers have impeded legitimate
scientific inquiry into compounds with significant therapeutic potential.

## Legacy

The Bigwood and Beug dataset remains one of the most frequently cited sources for
quantitative psilocybin content in mycological and pharmacological literature.
Their work bridged ethnobotanical observation with rigorous analytical chemistry,
establishing reliable benchmarks for potency comparisons across species.
Subsequent researchers have built upon their methodology but the original study remains foundational.
