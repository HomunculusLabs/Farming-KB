## Core Principles

Swale design operates on the principle that **slow water is productive water**. In undisturbed landscapes with intact vegetation, rainfall infiltrates the soil at rates of 50–200 mm/hour. In degraded, compacted, or bare landscapes, infiltration may drop below 5 mm/hour, causing rapid surface runoff, erosion, and aquifer depletion. Swales physically intercept this runoff and convert it from a destructive force into a restorative resource.

The four functions of a properly designed swale system:
1. **Capture:** Collect surface water flowing downhill during and after rainfall events
2. **Slow:** Reduce flow velocity to near-zero, preventing erosion and sediment transport
3. **Spread:** Distribute water laterally across the landscape via the level trench
4. **Sink:** Infiltrate captured water deep into the soil profile, recharging groundwater

## Swale Anatomy

A typical on-contour swale consists of the following components:

### The Berm (Downslope)
- A mound of excavated soil placed on the downhill side of the trench
- Height: 0.3–1.0 meters above original grade
- Width at base: 1.5–3.0 meters
- Functions as a raised planting bed, windbreak, and physical barrier to water flow
- Planted immediately with deep-rooted, fast-establishing species to stabilize the soil

### The Trench (Swale Proper)
- Level-bottomed excavation on the uphill side of the berm
- Width: 0.5–2.0 meters (wider for larger catchments)
- Depth: 0.3–0.6 meters (shallower in clay soils, deeper in sandy soils)
- Level along its entire length (0% grade, verified with A-frame, laser level, or water level)
- Temporarily holds water during and immediately after rainfall, allowing infiltration
- In arid climates, may be mulched heavily to reduce evaporative losses

### The Spillway
- A level section at the lowest point of the berm, designed as an overflow mechanism

## Design Process

### Step 1: Site Assessment
Before designing any swale system, a thorough assessment is essential:
- **Topography:** Map contour lines using a laser level, dumpy level, A-frame, or GPS survey. Identify keypoint, keyline, and valley positions. Slope angle determines swale spacing and design.
- **Hydrology:** Determine average annual rainfall, rainfall intensity-duration-frequency data, watershed area above each swale, existing drainage patterns, and seasonal flow variations.
- **Soils:** Conduct soil tests (texture, depth, permeability, infiltration rate, pH, [[swale-construction-guide]].
- **Vegetation:** Assess existing vegetation, root zones, and [[tree-canopy-fungal-sampling-techniques]] coverage.
- **Existing infrastructure:** Map buildings, roads, utilities, and drainage infrastructure. Swales must be positioned to protect, not threaten, existing structures.

### Step 2: Swale Spacing
Vertical spacing between successive swales on a slope depends on slope angle and soil type:
| Slope (%) | Approx. Angle | Vertical Spacing (m) | Horizontal Spacing (m, per 10% slope) |
|-----------|--------------|----------------------|---------------------------------------|
| 1–3% | 0.6–1.7° | 15–30 | 500–1000 |
| 3–5% | 1.7–2.9° | 10–20 | 200–667 |
| 5–10% | 2.9–5.7° | 5–15 | 50–300 |
| 10–20% | 5.7–11.3° | 3–10 | 15–100 |
| >20% | >11.3° | Not recommended for swales; use terraces, gabions, or other stabilization |
A common rule of thumb: **vertical spacing = 3–5 × slope percentage (in meters)**. In clay soils, use the closer spacing; in sandy soils, use the wider spacing.
