## Granitic Landscape Permaculture

## Description

Granitic landscapes present a specialized set of challenges and opportunities for [[permaculture-design]]. These are slab landscapes characterized by exposed granite rock, permeable sand (often 100+ feet deep), minimal water retention, and rapid organic matter decomposition. [[bill-mollison]] devotes an entire section of the [[mollison-permaculture-design-course-curriculum]] to strategies for these difficult but surprisingly productive environments. The entire Perth plain of Western Australia — home to millions — sits on granitic sand.

## Classification

- **Landscape type:** Residual granitic terrain
- **Soil characteristics:** Deep siliceous sand (100–200 ft), extremely permeable, no water retention
- **Climate range:** Semi-arid to humid (challenges persist regardless of rainfall)
- **Key feature:** Abundant exposed and subsurface rock with deep sand

## Key Facts

- **Water cannot be retained** in the soil profile — it passes straight through hundreds of feet of sand
- **Mulch disappears in 6 months** — organic matter breaks down into particles and acids that wash down to the water table
- **Lawns are total disasters** — requiring 90 inches of water per year on siliceous sands, with massive evaporative loss
- **Bore water is finite and degrading** — bores must go ever deeper (20 ft → 2,000 ft) while water becomes saltier
- Despite these challenges, **granitic areas can become more productive** than conventional agricultural soil through proper design
- Rocks provide **thermal mass, microclimate diversity, and growing niches**
- A 4-acre granitic site can outproduce 4 acres of promising agricultural soil with proper design

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Core Design Strategies

1. **Tree crop emphasis** — Trees perform disproportionately well on granitic sands (reason unclear to Mollison)
2. **Rock integration** — Incorporate granite into buildings as thermal mass; use rocks as heat stores under glass or shade houses
3. **Gley ponds and lined gardens** — Line garden areas with plastic sheeting to create "underground tanks" where humic acid can reach roots
4. **Pocket gardening** — Mulch directly on and beside rocks; use crevices and shelving areas as growing pockets
5. **Microclimate exploitation** — Rocks create light/heat reflectivity benefits; grow on both shaded and sun-exposed sides

### Plant Selection

Species that thrive on granitic landscapes include:
- **Palms** (nearly all species)
- **Figs** ([[ficus-carica]])
- **Grapes** ([[vitis-vinifera]])
- **Olives** (Olea europaea)
- **Date palms** ([[phoenix-dactylifera]])
- **Strawberries** (in shaded pockets among rocks)
- **Raspberries**
- **Tomatoes and cucumbers** (in rock niches)
- **Marigolds** (Tagetes — companion planting)

### Water Management

- **Gley ponds**: Small sealed ponds for water storage
- **Run-off tanks**: Capture whatever rainfall occurs
- **Modest bore use**: Minimal extraction to avoid saltwater intrusion
- **Windmills**: For pumping from depth
- **Keyline systems**: Direct what little water is available to growing areas

### Mycological Connections

- Deep siliceous sand presents challenges for mycorrhizal networks — rapid organic matter loss means limited carbon for fungal symbionts
- **Rock crevice microenvironments** may harbor unique fungal communities adapted to low-nutrient, well-drained conditions
- **Mulched pockets on rock** create concentrated zones of fungal activity [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]]
- **Below-garden gley systems** (proposed by Mollison): dig earth, install green mat layer, return earth — potentially creating favorable [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] soil fungi
- **Moss and fern establishment** on rocks (Mollison mentions moss-covered keylines) indicates the presence of moisture-retaining fungal-bacterial biofilms

## Design Principles

1. Accept and work with the rock — do not try to remove it
2. Tree crops replace annual crops as primary production
3. Every rock is a growing opportunity (shade, heat, moisture niche)
4. Water must be captured, stored, and recycled at the smallest scale
5. Deep sand areas should not be densely settled or crowded
6. Integrate rocks into architecture as thermal mass and [[bloomfield-mushroom-evaporative-cooling-and-thermal-regulation]]
7. Use morning sun for leaf production, afternoon sun for ripening
