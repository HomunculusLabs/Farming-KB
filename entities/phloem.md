# Phloem

## Description

Phloem is one of the two types of vascular tissue in plants, responsible for transporting sugars, amino acids, organic compounds, and even RNA from source locations (such as photosynthesizing leaves or storage cells in roots) to sink locations where these materials are needed. Unlike xylem, phloem consists only of living cells and operates bidirectionally, though not simultaneously in both directions.

## Classification

- **Tissue Type:** Vascular tissue (conducting tissue)
- **Cell Types:** Sieve elements, companion cells, parenchyma, fibers
- **System:** Part of the plant's transport infrastructure alongside xylem

## Structure

Phloem tissue is composed of specialized, elongated cells called sieve elements that lack lignin. These cells connect in vertical stacks to form sieve tubes, which are separated by sieve plates — perforated structures lined with callose, a polysaccharide. As sieve elements mature, they lose their nuclei, vacuoles, and ribosomes but retain plastids, endoplasmic reticulum, and mitochondria.

Each sieve element pairs with a companion cell that carries out the functions of its missing organelles. Sugar molecules dissolve in water and flow into the companion cell, where they are collected and then actively loaded into the sieve tubes — a process that requires energy (ATP).

## Key Facts

- Phloem distributes sap from source to sink — from chloroplasts in leaf cells or storage cells in roots to areas of low supply
- During summer, the source is typically in the leaves; sap flows through phloem to roots for winter storage
- In spring, stored nutrients move from roots upward through the xylem (not phloem), which is the source of maple and birch syrups
- Phloem depends on hydrostatic pressure (not transpiration) to move water containing sugars
- As sieve tubes fill with sugar, osmotic pressure draws water from adjacent xylem vessels, creating positive pressure that pushes sap through the system

## Mechanism of Transport

The pressure-flow hypothesis explains phloem transport:

1. Sugar is actively loaded into sieve tubes at the source (requires ATP)
2. High sugar concentration causes water to flow in from xylem via osmosis
3. Positive hydrostatic pressure builds inside phloem
4. Sap is pushed toward the sink where sugar is unloaded
5. Water returns to xylem at the sink, maintaining the pressure gradient

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Carbohydrate delivery:** Phloem is the primary pathway by which plants deliver photosynthate to roots, including the root exudates that feed mycorrhizal fungi and the rhizosphere microbiome
- **Mycorrhizal exchange:** Mycorrhizal fungi tap into the plant's carbon flow, which ultimately passes through phloem before being released as root exudates
- **Nutrient deficiency diagnosis:** Understanding phloem transport helps explain why mobile nutrient deficiencies appear in older leaves
- **Girdling damage:** Disrupting phloem cuts off sugar transport to roots, starving the root system and its microbial partners
- **Seasonal sap flow:** Spring sap flow in maples and birches moves through xylem, but phloem transport during growing season fuels root storage

## Seasonal Dynamics

Phloem transport direction changes with the seasons:

- **Summer:** Source is in the leaves (photosynthesis); sap flows down to roots for storage
- **Autumn:** Carbohydrates are actively transported to root storage tissues
- **Spring:** Stored nutrients move upward, but through the xylem, not phloem
- **Growing season:** Phloem continuously distributes sugars to growing tips, flowers, and fruits

## Agricultural Significance

Understanding phloem transport is critical for:

- **Fruit quality:** Sugar accumulation in fruits depends on phloem delivery
- **Root crop development:** Carbohydrate storage in roots (potatoes, carrots, beets) requires efficient phloem transport
- **Disease transmission:** Some plant viruses move through the phloem, spreading systemically
- **Systemic pesticide delivery:** Some systemic pesticides are transported via phloem

## Phloem Loading Mechanisms

Phloem loading occurs through two primary pathways:

- **Apoplastic loading:** Sugar moves through the cell wall space (apoplast) before being actively transported into sieve elements. This is energetically costly but allows high sugar concentrations.
- **Symplastic loading:** Sugar moves cell-to-cell through plasmodesmata without crossing membranes. This is more energy-efficient but limits the concentration gradient.
