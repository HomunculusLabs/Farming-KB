# Atrazine

## Description

Atrazine is a widely used herbicide of the triazine class, primarily employed to control broadleaf and grassy weeds in agricultural crops such as corn, sorghum, and sugarcane. It is one of the most commonly detected pesticides in groundwater and surface water in agricultural regions, making it a significant target for mycoremediation research. Atrazine is a chlorinated compound with a chemical structure that makes it resistant to natural degradation, persisting in soil and water for extended periods. Its environmental persistence and widespread use have made it a model compound for studying fungal [[singh-fungal-degradation-of-synthetic-dyes-and-textile-waste]] chemicals.

## Classification

- **Category**: Chemical / Herbicide
- **Chemical class**: Triazine (chlorinated s-triazine)
- **Chemical formula**: C8H14ClN5
- **CAS Number**: 1912-24-9
- **Mode of action**: Photosynthesis inhibitor (inhibits photosystem II)

## Key Facts

- One of the most widely used herbicides globally, particularly in corn production in the United States.
- A [[fungal-chlorinated-aromatic-degradation]] compound, making it structurally similar to lignin and therefore a candidate for degradation by [[white-rot-fungi]].
- Highly persistent in the environment, with a half-life ranging from weeks to months depending on soil conditions.
- Frequently detected as a groundwater and surface water contaminant in agricultural areas.
- Subject to regulatory restrictions or bans in the European Union due to concerns about groundwater contamination and endocrine-disrupting effects.
- Can be remediated by fungi through enzymatic degradation, particularly by species that produce ligninases, peroxidases, and laccases.

## Relevance to Mycoremediation

Atrazine is a prime target for mycoremediation for several reasons:

- **[[serotonin-psilocin-structural-similarity-hallucinogenic-mechanism]] to lignin**: The chlorinated aromatic structure makes it susceptible to the same fungal enzyme systems (ligninases, peroxidases, laccases) that white rot fungi use to decompose lignin in wood.
- **Adaptive enzyme production**: Fungi such as oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus) can rapidly shift their [[oner-cannabis-phenotypes-genetic-expression]] to produce enzymes capable of dismantling previously unencountered chemical compounds, including atrazine at concentrations three times the labeled application rate.
- **Training response**: When exposed to atrazine, fungal mycelium initially slows its linear growth within two days, then adapts its enzyme production over approximately eight days to initiate the disassembly of the compound.
- **Practical applications**: Myceliated wood chips or bales of wheat straw can be deployed as fungal filters to reduce atrazine contamination in agricultural runoff.

## Mycoremediation Training Protocol

The process of training fungi to degrade atrazine follows a predictable pattern:

1. **Exposure**: Oyster mushroom mycelium is exposed to atrazine at concentrations exceeding typical field application rates.
2. **Initial shock**: The mycelium stops linear growth within two days as it encounters the unfamiliar chemical.
3. **Adaptation phase**: Over approximately eight days, the fungus shifts its genetic expression to produce novel enzyme combinations.
4. **Degradation**: The adapted enzyme systems begin dismantling the atrazine molecule.
5. **Verification**: Testing confirms the extent of degradation and any remaining metabolites.

## Environmental and Health Concerns

- Classified as a possible human carcinogen (Group C) by the U.S. EPA.
- Suspected endocrine disruptor, with studies suggesting effects on hormone levels in amphibians and potentially humans.
- Highly mobile in soil, leading to widespread groundwater contamination in agricultural regions.
- Toxic to aquatic organisms, particularly amphibians, at low concentrations.
- Banned in the European Union since 2004 due to groundwater contamination concerns.

## Degradation Pathway

Fungal degradation of atrazine typically involves:
1. Initial enzymatic cleavage of the chlorinated ring structure by [[fungal-extracellular-enzymes]].
2. Breakdown into simpler, less toxic metabolites.
3. Further degradation by associated bacteria in a species sequence approach.
4. Testing is always critical to confirm complete contaminant removal, as partial degradation products can still be hazardous.

## Research Significance

Atrazine serves as a model compound for demonstrating the adaptive capacity of fungal enzyme systems. When Pleurotus ostreatus was exposed to atrazine at triple the labeled rate, the mycelium stopped growth after two days but then adapted within eight days to begin disassembling the compound. This illustrates the core principle: fungi can reprogram their enzyme production to target previously unencountered chemical structures. This finding has broad implications for mycoremediation of many persistent organic pollutants beyond just atrazine.

## Practical Deployment for Agricultural Runoff

For practical field application, mycoremediation barriers using atrazine-degrading fungi can be constructed from myceliated wood chips or wheat straw bales placed at the lowest point of agricultural fields. Rain washes atrazine-contaminated runoff into the fungal filter, where the mycelium traps and degrades the herbicide before it reaches local waterways. These living filters are simple to install, sustainable, and can process contamination passively without energy input. However, cultivators should never assume complete remediation without testing; regular monitoring is essential to verify that contamination levels are being adequately reduced.

## Connection to White Rot Fungal Enzymology

The ability of white rot fungi to degrade atrazine stems from their lignin-degrading enzyme systems. Lignin, the complex polymer that gives wood its structural rigidity, shares key [[metzner-shamanic-ceremony-versus-psychotherapy-structural-features]] with many synthetic chlorinated compounds, including the triazine ring of atrazine. The same extracellular enzymes that white rot fungi evolved to break down lignin — lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, and laccases — can cleave the chemical bonds in atrazine and similar herbicides. This is why white rot fungi are considered among the most promising organisms for mycoremediation of persistent organic pollutants.
