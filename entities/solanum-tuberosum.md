## Overview

**Solanum tuberosum**, commonly known as the potato, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Solanaceae cultivated worldwide for its starchy edible tubers. It is the world's fourth-largest food crop by production volume, after maize, wheat, and rice.

The species originated in the Andes Mountains of South America, where indigenous peoples domesticated it approximately 7,000–10,000 years ago. Spanish explorers introduced the potato to Europe in the late 16th century.

Potatoes are grown in over 160 countries across a wide range of climates and altitudes. Global annual production exceeds 370 million tonnes, with China and India as the leading producers.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Solanum tuberosum** L. belongs to the order Solanales, family Solanaceae, subfamily Solanoideae. The genus *Solanum* contains approximately 1,500 species, making it one of the largest genera of flowering plants.

The cultivated potato is a tetraploid (2n=4x=48) derived primarily from *Solanum tuberosum* subsp. *andigena*, a wild subspecies from the Andean highlands. A secondary domestication event produced *S. tuberosum* subsp. *tuberosum* in southern Chile.

Plants grow 30–100 cm tall with compound leaves consisting of 5–9 leaflets. Stems are angular and may be green or purplish-pigmented, depending on the cultivar.

The underground tubers are modified stems (stolons) that swell with stored starch. Tubers vary widely in size, shape, skin color, and flesh color among cultivars. "Eyes" on the tuber surface are axillary buds from which new shoots emerge.

Flowers are white, pink, purple, or blue, approximately 2–3 cm in diameter, with a yellow conical central cone. Many modern cultivars produce few or no flowers, as flowering is not required for tuber production.

True fruits are small green berries resembling cherry tomatoes, each containing 200–400 seeds. These berries are toxic due to high solanine concentrations and are not consumed.

## Distribution and Habitat

The wild progenitors of the potato are native to the Andes Mountains from Peru to northern Argentina, at elevations of 2,500–4,500 meters. The greatest diversity of wild *Solanum* species occurs in Bolivia and Peru.

Cultivated potatoes are grown from sea level to over 4,000 meters elevation. The crop performs best in temperate climates with moderate rainfall (500–700 mm during the growing season) and well-drained, fertile soils.

Optimal soil temperature for tuber initiation is 15–20°C. Temperatures above 25°C suppress tuber development and promote excessive foliage growth. Frost kills above-ground foliage but tubers can survive brief light freezes.

The species requires at least 120–150 frost-free days for full maturity in most cultivars. Short-day conditions favor tuberization in Andigena germplasm, while Tuberosum cultivars are largely day-neutral.

## Cultivation

Potatoes are propagated vegetatively from seed tubers ("seed potatoes") rather than true seeds. Certified disease-free seed tubers are cut into pieces bearing 2–3 eyes and planted 5–10 cm deep.

Seed rates range from 1.5–2.5 tonnes/ha depending on tuber size and row spacing. Mechanical planters place seed pieces at uniform depth and spacing in prepared ridges or beds.

Nitrogen requirements are relatively high at 100–200 kg/ha, with split applications recommended to avoid excessive foliage at the expense of tuber yield. Phosphorus and potassium are critical for tuber quality and disease resistance.

Major diseases include late blight (*Phytophthora infestans*), the pathogen responsible for the Irish Potato Famine, and early blight (*Alternaria solani*). ## Nutritional Value

Raw potato tubers contain approximately 17% carbohydrates, primarily in the form of starch, and 2% protein. They are a significant source of vitamin C, potassium, and vitamin B6 in human diets.
