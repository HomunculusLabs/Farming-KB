## Overview

Cynoglossum grande, commonly known as Pacific hound's tongue or grand hound's tongue, is a biennial to short-lived perennial herb in the family Boraginaceae. It is native to western North America, ranging from British Columbia to southern California.

The plant grows 30–90 cm tall with large, basal leaves that resemble a dog's tongue in shape, giving rise to the common name. Coarse hairs cover the leaves and stems, giving the foliage a rough texture.

Flowers are bright blue to purple with a white central ring, arranged in showy terminal clusters. The blooms appear in early to mid-spring (March–May), making it one of the more conspicuous spring wildflowers in Pacific coast forests.

After flowering, the plant produces distinctive barbed nutlets that attach to passing animals for dispersal. These burred fruits can be a nuisance to hikers and their dogs.

The plant is monocarpic or nearly so in some populations, flowering once and then dying after seed set. Other individuals may persist as short-lived perennials with a basal rosette producing flowering stems in successive years.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Clade: Tracheophytes
- Clade: Angiosperms
- Clade: Eudicots
- Clade: Asterids
- Order: Boraginales
- Family: Boraginaceae
- Genus: Cynoglossum
- Species: C. grande

Basal leaves are ovate to oblong-lanceolate, 10–30 cm long and 3–10 cm wide, with prominent veins. They are long-petioled and form a low rosette in the first year of growth.

Stem leaves are smaller, sessile, and clasping. The flowering stem is erect, unbranched or sparingly branched, and covered with soft, spreading hairs.

Flowers are 8–12 mm across with five rounded corolla lobes. The corolla is blue to purple with a conspicuous white or cream ring at the throat (the fornices).

The fruit consists of four depressed-globose nutlets, 4–6 mm across, densely covered with barbed prickles. These burred nutlets are a key diagnostic feature of the genus.

Each nutlet contains a single seed. The barbed prickles are stiff enough to embed in fabric and fur, making them effective for animal-mediated dispersal. A single plant may produce 50–200 nutlets.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to western North America from southern British Columbia through Washington, Oregon, and California. Occurs primarily west of the Cascade and Sierra Nevada crests, extending to the coast.

Found in coniferous and mixed forests, forest openings, chaparral edges, and along trails at elevations from sea level to 1,800 m. It prefers partial shade and grows on a variety of soil types.

The species colonizes disturbed ground readily, including road cuts, landslide scars, and burned areas. This pioneering habit reflects a seed bank strategy where buried nutlets germinate when exposed to light and reduced competition.

The species is most abundant in the Coast Ranges and western slopes of the Cascades. Populations in southern California are restricted to higher elevations and shaded canyons.

It is not currently considered rare or threatened across its range, though local populations may be affected by logging, development, and invasive species competition.

In the Oregon Coast Range, it is often found growing alongside sword fern (Polystichum munitum), trillium (Trillium ovatum), and bleeding heart (Dicentra formosa) in moist, shaded forest understories.

## Ecological Role

The early spring flowers provide an important nectar and pollen source for native bees, particularly mason bees (Osmia spp.) and mining bees (Andrena spp.). Butterflies also visit the flowers.

The barbed nutlets are adapted for epizoochorous dispersal, attaching to the fur of mammals. This mechanism allows colonization of new forest openings and disturbed sites along animal trails.

Several native Lepidoptera species feed on Cynoglossum as larvae. The plant supports a small but specialized community of herbivorous insects in Pacific coast forest ecosystems.

Deer generally browse the foliage lightly. The coarse hairs provide moderate deterrence, though hungry deer will consume the plant in late winter when other forage is scarce.

The species is a component of the early seral vegetation that colonizes forest gaps and burned areas. It is moderately tolerant of disturbance and can persist in logged areas for several years before being shaded out by later-successional species.

## Cultivation and Uses

**Native plant gardening:** Cynoglossum grande is occasionally cultivated in native plant gardens and woodland gardens on the Pacific coast. Its early blue flowers and bold foliage make it a showy spring specimen.

**Propagation:** From seed sown in autumn. Seeds require cold stratification for 4–8 weeks. Germination rates are moderate (40–60%). Transplanting is difficult due to the taproot.

**Caution:** The barbed nutlets can be a significant nuisance in gardens located near trails or pet areas. Gardeners should deadhead promptly to prevent seed dispersal.

The plant is best suited to naturalistic plantings rather than formal garden beds. It performs well under oaks and conifers where it receives winter sun and summer shade.

Like other Cynoglossum species, it contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids and is toxic if consumed in quantity. Livestock poisoning has been reported, particularly in cattle grazing on C. officinale.

## Chemistry and Toxicity

Cynoglossum grande contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) characteristic of the genus. The primary PAs identified include heliotrine and supinine, though concentrations are lower than in the invasive Cynoglossum officinale.

Pyrrolizidine alkaloids are hepatotoxic, causing veno-occlusive disease of the liver with chronic exposure. Livestock (particularly cattle and horses) are most at risk from grazing on dense stands.

The barbed nutlets can cause mechanical injury to the mouths and digestive tracts of animals that ingest them. Combined with PA toxicity, the plant is considered moderately toxic to livestock.

Human poisoning is rare, as the bitter taste and hairy texture deter consumption. Nevertheless, the plant should not be ingested and gardeners should wear gloves when handling mature fruiting stems.

No significant medicinal or pharmacological applications have been documented for this species. Its close relative Cynoglossum officinale has a more extensive ethnobotanical history in European traditions.
