# Split Plate Agar (Behavioral Gallery)

## Description

A split plate, also known as a behavioral gallery, is an agar cultivation technique where a single petri plate is divided into two halves, each containing a different agar formula. This creates a transitional growing surface that allows cultivators to observe how fungal [[mycelium]] reacts to different nutrient concentrations, supplements, or challenging media. The technique is used to study mycelial behavior, test substrate compatibility, and gradually acclimate fungi to new growing conditions.

## Classification

- **Category:** Laboratory cultivation technique
- **Field:** [[mycology]] / Tissue culture
- **Related techniques:** [[agar-plate-preparation]], substrate agar, enrichment medium

## Key Facts

- A split plate contains two different agar formulas in the same petri dish, creating a zone of transition where the two media meet.
- One side typically contains a familiar or preferred agar formula (such as [[potato-agar]]) that the mushroom has adapted to, while the other side contains a more challenging or experimental medium.
- The plate is made by setting the petri dish base at an angle (resting one end on a dowel or similar prop), pouring enough agar to fill half the dish, allowing it to gel, then flipping the base and filling the other half with the second formula.
- Mycelium is transferred to the "safe side" (familiar medium) first, giving it the opportunity to expand before reaching the midpoint where it encounters the different medium.
- The technique allows observation of how fungi react to changes in medium composition, revealing whether they thrive, stall, or adapt their growth patterns.
- Concentration of challenging additives can be increased with each consecutive transfer, testing the limits of what a particular mycelial strain can handle.
- Dyes can be added to mark zones on the plates, making it easier to observe mycelial behavior and growth in response to different formulas.
- Colored agar backgrounds are also used for photographing or observing species that produce faint mycelium, such as lion's mane ([[hericium-erinaceus]]).

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The split plate technique is a powerful tool for mushroom cultivators and researchers. It enables systematic testing of substrate compatibility, allowing growers to determine whether a particular strain can adapt to a new fruiting substrate before committing to larger-scale cultivation. By gradually increasing the concentration of challenging additives through consecutive transfers, cultivators can "train" mycelium to tolerate and thrive on substrates it would not naturally prefer. This approach is particularly valuable for hard-to-cultivate species and for developing new cultivation protocols. The technique also supports research into fungal enzyme expression, metabolic adaptation, and the effects of various supplements and additives on mycelial growth.

## Construction Method

1. Prepare two different agar formulas and sterilize them separately.
2. Place a small dowel or rod under one edge of a petri dish base to tilt it.
3. Pour the first agar formula to fill approximately half the dish.
4. Allow the first half to gel completely.
5. Flip the dish orientation and pour the second formula to fill the remaining half.
6. Allow the second half to gel completely.
7. Transfer mycelium to the familiar medium side.
8. Observe growth patterns as mycelium encounters the transitional zone.
