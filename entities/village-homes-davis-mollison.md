## Description

Village Homes is a pioneering suburban development in Davis, California, designed by Michael Corbett. It serves as a landmark example of integrated permaculture-style water management in residential design. The subdivision features swale systems that accept all road and excess roof runoff, supporting hundreds of productive trees throughout the settlement. Mollison cites it as a prime demonstration of how swale-based water management can transform suburban landscapes into productive, water-efficient communities.

## Classification

- **Category**: Residential development / Urban permaculture example

## Key Facts

### Water Management System

- Swales interpenetrate the entire suburban development
- All road runoff is directed into the swale system
- Excess roof water is accepted by swales
- No conventional storm sewer infrastructure needed
- Water penetrated soils to 6 m (19 feet) deep after a few years of operation
- Swales became self-shaded after 3–4 years of tree growth

### Productive Landscape

- Hundreds of productive trees supported by swale-captured water
- Trees produce fuel, mulch, and food products
- Tree lines shade pavement and reduce urban heat island effects
- Integrated food production within residential lots

### Performance Results

- Demonstrated deep soil moisture penetration (6 m / 19 feet)
- Self-sustaining after initial 3–4 year establishment period
- Reduced infrastructure costs compared to conventional stormwater systems
- Productive landscape that generates food and resources rather than consuming them

### Design Significance

Village Homes demonstrates that permaculture water management principles can be applied at subdivision scale: every paved surface becomes a water collection area, swales replace conventional drainage infrastructure, tree belts on swale banks create productive microclimates, water is stored in soil rather than piped away, and the system improves with age as trees mature.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Village Homes demonstrates that productive urban tree systems sustained by harvested runoff can support diverse fungal ecosystems. The deep soil moisture and accumulated organic matter in swale systems create favorable conditions for mycorrhizal fungi and saprophytic species. The model is directly applicable to urban mushroom cultivation using swale-irrigated wood chip beds, mycoremediation of urban stormwater through fungal biofilters in swale systems, community-scale mushroom production integrated with food forests on swale banks, and establishing fungal networks in residential soil ecosystems.
