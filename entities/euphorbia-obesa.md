## Overview

Euphorbia obesa is a small, globular succulent in the family Euphorbiaceae, endemic to a small area of the Eastern Cape Province of South Africa. It is commonly known as the baseball plant for its spherical, ribbed shape that resembles a stitched ball.

The plant is solitary, producing a single stem 8–15 cm tall and 6–12 cm in diameter. It has no spines, no leaves, and no branches, consisting entirely of a fleshy, greenish-grey body with prominent vertical ridges.

Euphorbia obesa is dioecious, with separate male and female plants. The tiny flowers (cyathia) appear in summer from the apex of the stem.

The species is classified as Endangered due to its extremely restricted natural range and historical over-collection. Wild populations are now protected, and most plants in cultivation are nursery-grown from seed.

Despite its endangered status in habitat, Euphorbia obesa is one of the most commonly cultivated succulents worldwide. Its unique form and ease of care make it a favorite among collectors.

The species has been in cultivation since the early 1900s and is now propagated by seed in nurseries around the world. Hundreds of thousands of nursery-grown plants exist, far exceeding the wild population.

The plant has become a symbol of succulent conservation, illustrating how commercial nursery propagation can rescue a species from extinction caused by over-collection.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Euphorbia obesa was described by Joseph Hooker in 1903 from material collected near Graaff-Reinet. The species epithet "obesa" means "fat" or "stout," referring to the plump, rounded body.

The plant belongs to Euphorbia subgenus Euphorbia, section Anthacantha. It is closely related to Euphorbia symmetrica and Euphorbia meloformis.

The stem is spherical to slightly cylindrical, typically with 8 vertical ribs. Young plants are nearly perfectly round, becoming more columnar with age.

Ribs are adorned with small, shallow tubercles rather than spines. The body color ranges from grey-green to brownish-green, often with horizontal banding in darker or lighter shades.

The plant contains a milky white latex sap typical of Euphorbia species. This sap is toxic and irritating to skin and mucous membranes.

Roots are fibrous and shallow, adapted to the thin soils of its native habitat. The root system is proportionally small relative to the above-ground body.

Cyathia (the characteristic Euphorbia inflorescence) are tiny, approximately 3 mm across. Male cyathia produce yellow pollen, while female cyathia bear a single tri-lobed pistil.

Chromosome number is 2n = 20. The species is diploid and shows limited genetic variation across its small natural range.

## Distribution and Habitat

Euphorbia obesa is endemic to the Graaff-Reinet district of the Eastern Cape, South Africa. Its entire natural range covers less than 50 square kilometers.

Plants grow on stony, gently sloping terrain in the Karoo vegetation type. They are found at elevations of 600–900 m on shallow, calcareous soils over mudstone and sandstone.

The climate is semi-arid with 200–350 mm annual rainfall, mostly in summer. Temperatures range from 35 °C in summer to −3 °C in winter.

The species grows partially embedded in gravel in habitat. Its cryptic coloration blends with surrounding stones, reducing detection by herbivores and collectors.

Associated plants include low Karoo shrubs, grasses, and other succulents such as Avonia, Crassula, and Stapelia species. The vegetation is sparse, with large areas of bare ground.

The species is effectively invisible in habitat when not in flower. Collectors historically relied on local guides who knew the precise locations of remaining populations.

## Cultivation

Euphorbia obesa requires a well-draining, mineral-based potting medium. A mix of 80% pumice or coarse sand with 20% loam provides suitable drainage.

Bright light is essential for maintaining compact growth and the characteristic banding patterns. Plants grown in shade become elongated and lose their ornamental markings.

Water moderately during the summer growing season, allowing the soil to dry completely between waterings. Withhold water entirely during the winter dormancy period.

The species is hardy to approximately 5 °C when kept dry. It is intolerant of frost, especially when combined with moist soil.

Both male and female plants are needed to produce seed. Hand pollination with a fine brush transfers pollen from male to female cyathia.

Seed capsules explode when ripe, scattering seed up to 2 m from the parent plant. Collect capsules just before ripening and allow them to open in a paper bag.

Seeds germinate in 7–14 days at 25–30 °C. Seedlings grow slowly and should be kept in bright, indirect light for their first year.

## Conservation

Euphorbia obesa is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List. Its extremely restricted range makes it vulnerable to any habitat disturbance.

The primary historical threat was over-collection by commercial plant collectors. Between 1900 and 1970, tens of thousands of plants were removed from habitat for the horticultural trade.

Current threats include illegal poaching, habitat degradation from overgrazing, and climate change. Livestock trampling has damaged several known population sites.

All remaining wild populations occur on private land or within protected areas. The species is listed on CITES Appendix II, regulating international trade in wild-collected specimens.

Nursery propagation from seed has substantially reduced collection pressure on wild populations. Most commercially available plants are now legally produced in cultivation.

The species is included in the living collections of Kirstenbosch National Botanical Garden and Kew Gardens as part of ex-situ conservation programs.

## Similar Species

Euphorbia symmetrica is the closest relative and is sometimes considered a subspecies of E. obesa. It has a more flattened, disc-shaped body with more prominent tubercles.

Euphorbia meloformis is another globose South African species but develops prominent, decorative banding and is generally larger. It is also from the Eastern Cape but occupies a different geological substrate.

Astrophytum asterias (star cactus) from Mexico has a remarkably similar flattened, ribbed body. The convergence in form between these unrelated species illustrates parallel evolution in arid environments.

Euphorbia obesa can also be confused with young Ferocactus specimens. The presence of milky latex sap (Euphorbia) versus spines (Ferocactus) easily distinguishes them.
