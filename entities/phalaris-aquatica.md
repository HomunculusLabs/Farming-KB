> *"Phalaris aquatica is among the most widely studied of the DMT-containing grasses, yet its extreme alkaloidal variability has frustrated nearly every attempt at standardized extraction or safe entheogenic use."*
> — Jonathan Ott, *Pharmacotheon* (1993)

Described by Linnaeus (1753). Once treated as *P. arundinacea* subsp. *aquatica*, molecular phylogenetics supports distinct species status. Tetraploid, 2n = 28.

## Etymology and Common Names

*Phalaris* from Greek *phalaros* (crested), referring to gleaming glumes. *aquatica* (Latin: "of water") indicates affinity for moist habitats and riparian zones. Named "Harding Grass" for Constantine C. Harding, who promoted it as California forage ~1890.

| Common Name | Region |
|-------------|--------|
| Harding Grass / Hardinggrass | United States |
| Toowoomba Canary Grass | Australia (Queensland) |
| Alpiste Criollo | Spanish-speaking Latin America |
| Bulbous Canary Grass | Older European usage |
| Hardgrass | Colloquial, California |

## Morphology

Coarse **perennial bunchgrass** forming dense tussocks from a short rhizomatous crown.

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Height | 0.5–2.0 m (1.5–6.5 ft) |
| Culms | Sturdy, erect, 3–5 mm diameter, unbranched |
| Leaves | Flat/loosely rolled, 5–15 mm wide, 15–40 cm, glaucous-green |
| Ligule | Membranous, truncate, 3–8 mm |
| Inflorescence | Dense cylindrical-ovoid panicle, 5–15 cm × 1–2 cm |
| Spikelets | 1-flowered, 4–6 mm, enclosed by hardened glumes |
| Rhizomes | Short, thick, creeping; enables vegetative spread |
| Roots | Deep fibrous, 1 m+ depth; confers drought tolerance |

Inflorescence resembles reed canary grass (*P. arundinacea*) but shorter, thicker, more erect. Semievergreen in mild winters.

## Distribution

Native to the **Mediterranean Basin** (S. Europe, N. Africa, W. Asia). Widely introduced:

| Region | Status |
|--------|--------|
| North America | Naturalized in CA, OR, TX, Southeastern US |
| South America | Planted in Argentina, Chile |
| Australia | Major forage species; naturalized in NSW, QLD, VIC |
| New Zealand | Established in pastures and roadsides |
| Southern Africa | Occasional introduction |

Thrives in Mediterranean-type climates (cool wet winters, hot dry summers). Occupies ~200,000 ha of California rangeland.

## Alkaloid Content

Notable for accumulating **indole alkaloids** in leaf and stem tissue. Profiles vary extremely:

| Alkaloid | Range (dry wt) | Notes |
|----------|---------------|-------|
| DMT | 0.00–0.34% | Presence depends on chemotype |
| 5-MeO-DMT | 0.00–0.05% | Often co-occurs with DMT |
| Bufotenine | Trace–0.01% | Less consistent |
| Gramine | 0.01–0.5% | Toxic; unrelated to tryptamine pathway |
| β-Carbolines | Trace | Harmala alkaloids occasionally detected |

Concentration varies by genotype, phenological stage, tissue, soil, season, and stress. Young spring shoots contain peak DMT. Cultivar 'AQ1' (AU) bred for **reduced gramine**. **DMT biosynthesis**: L-tryptophan → tryptamine → N-methyltryptamine → DMT, via decarboxylases and N-methyltransferases.

## Entheogenic History

Interest emerged 1977–1985 after alkaloid analyses showed DMT in *Phalaris* species. **Jonathan Ott**
documented it in *Pharmacotheon* (1993), noting DMT potential but strongly cautioning against use
due to gramine toxicity and extreme variability. Extraction experiments showed DMT content in
identical-looking plants ranged from zero to 0.34% dry weight. Briefly popular as a "legal" DMT
source, but **no safe preparation method** was established. Gramine contamination in crude extracts
poses genuine health risks. Key papers: Applewhite (1977), Smith (1977), Ott (1993), Letcher (2004).
The entheogenic potential remains **theoretical and unsafe in practice**.

## Permaculture and Agronomic Uses

| Use | Details |
|-----|---------|
| Erosion control | Deep roots stabilize slopes, banks, riparian zones |
| Livestock forage | High biomass; palatable when young; rotational grazing |
| Drought tolerance | Survives extended dry periods once established |
| Windbreak | Dense tussocks provide low-level wind shelter |
| Soil improvement | Organic matter input; fibrous roots improve structure |
| Riparian revegetation | Tolerates seasonal waterlogging; stream bank restoration |
| Biomass | 8–15 tonnes DM/ha/year under irrigation |

Australian cultivar **'Sirolan'** bred for low-gramine forage. Also used in constructed wetlands
and mine site rehabilitation.

## Toxicity Concerns

Gramine (3-dimethylaminomethylindole) is the primary concern. **Acute toxicity**: CNS depression,
ataxia, convulsions in livestock; sheep most sensitive. **Chronic exposure**: reduced weight gain,
reproductive impairment, death. Symptoms include salivation, bloat, muscle tremors, tachycardia.
Mechanism involves metabolites disrupting monoamine oxidase activity. Low-gramine cultivars 'AQ1',
'Sirolan', and 'Holdfast' mitigate risk. **Livestock should not graze during stress-induced
alkaloid spikes** (drought, frost, nutrient deficiency). Unpredictable alkaloid ratios make any
self-experimentation hazardous regardless of DMT's oral inactivity without MAOIs.

## Cultivation

| Parameter | Requirement |
|-----------|-------------|
| Propagation | Seed (primary); clump division |
| Seed rate | 5–10 kg/ha, broadcast or drilled 5–10 mm deep |
| Germination | 7–21 days at 15–20°C; stratification helps |
| Soil | Clay/loam/sand; pH 5.0–8.0 |
| Moisture | Prefers moist; drought-hardy after 6–8 weeks |
| Temperature | Optimal 15–25°C; hardy to −10°C |
| Fertilizer | N at 50–100 kg/ha/year for forage |
| Grazing | Rotate; allow 15–20 cm regrowth |
| Persistence | 10+ years; can become invasive |

Easily established from seed with minimal management. Self-seeds readily and can become aggressive
in riparian and coastal areas. California Invasive Plant Council rates it **"Moderate"** invasiveness.
