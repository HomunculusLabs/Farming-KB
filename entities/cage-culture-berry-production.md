## Description

Cage culture is an intensive berry production technique where crops such as blueberries, sweet cherries, raspberries, and table-quality bramble fruits are grown inside enclosed mesh cages (typically 10-20 m wide by 10-20 m long by 2-4 m high) to exclude birds and large insect pests. The method can yield up to 30 times that of open field conditions, making it a highly productive small-scale commercial strategy for cool humid climates.

## Classification

- **Category:** Agricultural technique / Protected cropping
- **Applicable crops:** Blueberry, raspberry, sweet cherry, bramble fruits (youngberry, boysenberry), nectarine, peach
- **Climate zone:** Cool humid (mesothermal) climates
- **Scale:** Small commercial to family livelihood
- **Pest exclusion method:** 10 mm mesh netting

## Key Facts

- Cage structures typically measure approximately 20 m x 10 m x 2-4 m high, using 10 mm mesh netting
- Reported yields up to 30x open field production for caged berry crops
- Supports polyculture of smallfruit plus espalier fruits (nectarine, peach) within the same enclosure
- Uses [[drip-irrigation]] installed for precise moisture management
- Deep mulch applied for pH adjustment and soil moisture conservation
- Careful schedule of liquid manures selected for specific crop nutritional needs
- Ridge construction essential for blueberries, which require well-drained acidic conditions

## Management Requirements

- Regular pruning of berry canes and brambles to maintain productivity
- Ongoing mulch addition to maintain soil health and moisture
- Liquid manure application on a crop-specific schedule
- Monitoring and maintenance of mesh integrity
- Management of beneficial organisms (frogs, lizards, quail) for biological pest control
- Seasonal adjustment of irrigation schedules
- Removal and replacement of aging cane growth

## Biological Pest Control

The cage ecosystem integrates natural pest management:
- **Tree frogs** — control flying insects and small pests
- **Small lizards** — consume insects at ground and low canopy level
- **Quail and insectivorous birds** — provide broad-spectrum insect control
- The enclosed environment concentrates beneficial predators alongside crops
- This eliminates or greatly reduces the need for chemical pesticides

## Economic Considerations

- Suited to family-livelihood scale production
- Targets high-quality fresh market sales where premium prices justify infrastructure
- Construction cost involves piping framework plus mesh netting
- High-value crops such as bell pepper, tomato, snow pea, and specialty berries justify the investment
- Potential for self-pick sales when located within 15-20 km of town markets
- Self-pick operations require parking, weigh-out centres, buckets for pickers, and produce tubs

## Alternative: Field Culture with Hawk Kites

Where cage culture is not feasible, field conditions can use tethered predator hawk kites above the crop at critical ripening periods. These realistic kites imitate local hawks or eagles and serve as effective bird repellents. They should be removed after harvest so birds do not become habituated. Commercially available in Australia.

## Self-Pick System Layout

For self-pick operations (alternative to cage culture):
- Berries planted double row on contour mounds 1-1.5 m high
- Grassed paths between rows for picker access
- Wider spacing of mounds than in cage culture for easy access
- Mulch or clover paths between rows
- Adequate irrigation and good drainage essential

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Deep mulch systems create favorable microhabitats for beneficial fungi
- Organic management practices (liquid manures, mulch) support soil fungal communities
- The protected environment could potentially be adapted for specialty mushroom production alongside berry crops
- Moisture management techniques are transferable to [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] bed preparation
- Integrated biological pest control principles apply equally to [[permaculture]]
