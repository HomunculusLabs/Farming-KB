## Acid Rain and Fungal-Faunal Interactions

## Description

Acid rain and acidifying pollutants alter the composition and biomass of fungal communities in forest soils, which in turn cascades through soil food webs to affect fungal-feeding fauna including nematodes, mites, and other microarthropods. Research by Esher et al. (1992), Ruess et al. (1993, 1996), and others has documented how acidification shifts faunal trophic groups in correlation with changes in the bacterial:fungal ratio of soils.

## Classification

- **Category:** Ecological interaction / trophic cascade
- **Pollutant types:** Sulfuric acid, nitric acid, oxalic acid, ammonium nitrate
- **Affected fauna:** Nematodes, oribatid mites, prostigmatid mites, astigmatid mites, collembola
- **Affected fungi:** Ectomycorrhizal, saprotrophic, and root-associated fungi

## Key Facts

### Pine Forest Studies (Southeastern US)

Esher et al. (1992) conducted a 2-year study in pine forests with acid precipitation (pH 4.3 and 3.6 in throughfall):

**No significant effects on:**
- Saprotrophic microbial communities (dilution plate counts)
- Nematode numbers
- Arbuscular mycorrhizal spore counts

**Significant effects on:**
- Fine root length and dry mass (reduced)
- Ectomycorrhizal short roots of loblolly pine (Pinus taeda) and longleaf pine (Pinus palustris) (reduced)
- Oribatid, prostigmatid, and astigmatid mite numbers (increased with acidity)

The authors suggested that changes in the balance between saprotrophic and ectomycorrhizal fungi may alter feeding preferences of faunal groups if saprotrophic fungi are more palatable or provide higher nutrient levels than mycorrhizal fungi (Shaw, 1988, 1992).

### Nematode Community Shifts

Ruess et al. (1993) correlated nematode trophic group changes to bacterial:fungal ratios in forest soils treated with sulfuric, nitric, and oxalic acids:

- **Shift observed:** From bacterial-feeding nematodes to fungal-feeding nematodes with acid addition
- **Interpretation:** Bacteria are more sensitive to soil pH changes and associated nutrient/toxic metal shifts than fungi
- **Maturity index** (Bongers, 1990) was used to quantify community-level changes

### Sitka Spruce Plantation (Scotland)

Ruess et al. (1996) conducted a 3-year acid precipitation experiment on Sitka spruce (Picea sitchensis) with equimolar sulfuric acid and ammonium nitrate:

**Soil changes:**
- Upper 2 cm soil acidity increased from pH 5.0 to 4.0

**Fungal responses:**
- Fungal biomass increased (measured by soil ergosterol content)
- Ectomycorrhizal colonization of roots increased

**Faunal responses:**
- Nematode populations elevated (supported by increased fungal biomass)
- Omnivorous and predatory nematodes (Filenchus spp., Aporcelaimellus obtusicaudatus) declined
- Fungal-feeding nematodes (particularly Aphelenchoides spp.) increased significantly

### Trophic Cascade Mechanisms

The acidification-fungal-faunal interaction operates through several pathways:

1. **Direct toxicity:** Acidification directly reduces bacterial populations, shifting the bacterial:fungal ratio
2. **Fungal community shifts:** Acidification alters the ectomycorrhizal:saprotrophic fungal ratio
3. **Food web restructuring:** Changed fungal communities support different faunal feeding guilds
4. **Top-down effects:** Loss of omnivorous and predatory nematodes reduces top-down regulation

### Implications for Soil Food Web Stability

The research suggests that:
- Acidifying pollutants simplify [[soil-food-web-structure]]
- Loss of higher trophic levels (predatory nematodes) may destabilize microarthropod regulation
- Increased fungal biomass under acidification does not necessarily indicate improved ecosystem function
- The palatability and nutritional quality of different fungal groups to fauna varies significantly
- Mycorrhizal fungi may be less palatable to fauna than saprotrophic fungi (Shaw, 1988, 1992)

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

1. **[[soil-food-web]] management** in cultivation should account for acidification effects on both fungal and faunal communities
2. **Nematode communities** can serve as bioindicators of soil health and pH balance
3. **Compost ecology:** Understanding fungal-faunal trophic interactions helps predict decomposition dynamics
4. **Integrated pest management:** Acidification effects on predatory nematodes may reduce natural pest regulation
5. **Mycorrhizal inoculation** in acidic soils should consider impacts on soil fauna and their grazing pressure on fungal hyphae
6. **Buffering capacity** of cultivation substrates should be maintained to prevent unintended food web disruption
