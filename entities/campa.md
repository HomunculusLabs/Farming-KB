## Description

The Campa (also known as Asháninka) are an indigenous Arawakan-speaking people of eastern Peru who practice [[ayahuasca]] ceremonies distinguished by the directive role of a shaman as leader of the rituals. Unlike the egalitarian Cashinahua or the individualistic Jivaro, the Campa model features a hierarchically organized ceremony in which the shaman directs the group experience. The [[campa-ayahuasca-ceremony]] has been studied by anthropologist [[gerald-weiss]] for its evolutionary implications — specifically, how the shaman"s directorial role may shed light on the historical transition from shamanism to priesthood.

## Classification

- **Language family**: Arawakan (Asháninka)
- **Geographic region**: Eastern Peru, upper Amazon Basin
- **Social organization**: Shaman-led communal ceremonies
- **Key distinction**: Directorial shaman (intermediate between egalitarian shamanism and hierarchical priesthood)

## Key Facts

- The Campa [[ayahuasca-ceremony]] features a shaman who plays a directive role as leader of the ceremonies, contrasting with the more egalitarian approach of the Cashinahua and Sharanahua and the individualistic approach of the Jivaro.
- Gerald Weiss, in his contribution to Harner's volume, discusses the Campa ceremony in terms of evolutionary implications for the development of priesthood from shamanism. The Campa model — where a shaman directs group hallucinogenic sessions — represents an intermediate form between individual shamanic practice and the institutionalized priestly role.
- Weiss's analysis implicitly raises questions about the possible use of hallucinogens in early priesthoods. Drugs that permit the achievement of trance states by otherwise uninspired persons in appointed positions are known to be employed by priesthoods elsewhere, such as the Zuni rain priests in the southwestern United States (Stevenson, 1915).
- The Campa practice illustrates how hallucinogenic plant use can be organized along different social principles — from individualistic to communal-egalitarian to hierarchically-directed — reflecting broader patterns of social organization.
- The Campa join together for *Banisteriopsis* sessions, but unlike the Cashinahua where each participant independently seeks visions, the Campa shaman actively directs and shapes the communal experience.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- The Campa model of directed ayahuasca ceremonies provides an important comparative case for understanding how psychoactive plant use is socially organized. The shift from individual to directed group use may parallel the historical development of more complex religious organizations.
- Weiss's suggestion that the Campa ceremony may illuminate the transition from shaman to priest has implications for understanding how hallucinogenic plant practices may have been institutionalized and eventually suppressed in state religions — a pattern also observed in European witchcraft (discussed in Chapter 8 of Harner's volume).
- The Campa case demonstrates that the pharmacological effects of *Banisteriopsis* preparations are sufficiently flexible to support a range of social patterns, from individual vision quests to shaman-directed communal rituals.
