## Overview

Carex lurida is a robust perennial sedge in the family Cyperaceae, commonly known as the swamp sedge or lurid sedge. It is one of the more conspicuous sedges of [[palmer-mushroom-hunting-field-identification]] straightforward even for non-specialists.

Carex lurida was described by the German botanist Carl Ludwig Willdenow in 1805. The specific epithet lurida refers to the pale yellow-green color of the inflorescence.

It belongs to Carex section Vignea, a group defined by bisexual spikes with both staminate and pistillate flowers. Within this section, Carex lurida is one of the more ecologically important species due to its wide distribution.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:** Kingdom Plantae, order Poales, family Cyperaceae, genus Carex, section Vignea, species C. lurida Wahlenb. The species has no commonly used synonyms in current North American botanical literature.

Plants form dense tussocks arising from stout, short-creeping rhizomes. Culms are firm and erect, 40 to 100 centimeters tall, and 1.5 to 3 millimeters thick at the base.

Rhizome growth is more vigorous than in most related species, allowing single clones to expand to 50 centimeters or more in diameter. This clonal growth contributes to the species' effectiveness at stabilizing wetland soils.

Leaves are basal and cauline, 4 to 10 millimeters wide, with prominent midribs and M-shaped cross-sections. Leaf sheaths are green at the summit and conspicuously purple-tinged at the base.

The inflorescence consists of 3 to 6 androgynous spikes, each 15 to 40 millimeters long. The terminal spike is stout and erect, while lower spikes are nodding on long, slender peduncles.

Perigynia are broadly ovoid to nearly spherical, conspicuously inflated, 5 to 8 millimeters long and 3 to 5 millimeters wide. They are pale green to straw-colored at maturity with distinct longitudinal nerves.

Pistillate scales are lanceolate to ovate, 4 to 6 millimeters long, with prominent green midribs and broad hyaline margins. The achenes are lenticular and tightly enclosed within the inflated perigynia.

Flowering occurs from May through July across most of the range. Fruit maturation takes approximately 4 to 6 weeks following anthesis.

## Distribution and Habitat

Carex lurida ranges from southern Ontario and Quebec south through the [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

Competition from invasive species such as [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]] in riparian and palustrine wetlands.

The inflated perigynia are adapted for hydrochory, or water dispersal. They float readily and can remain viable after extended periods of submersion, facilitating colonization of new wetland sites.

Field observations indicate that perigynia can remain afloat for 2 to 4 weeks, enabling downstream dispersal during flood events. This dispersal mechanism contributes to the species' ability to colonize newly created wetland habitats.

Numerous wetland birds feed on the seeds, including red-winged blackbirds, swamp sparrows, and rails. The dense foliage provides nesting cover for marsh-nesting bird species.

White-tailed deer browse the foliage during spring and early summer. Muskrats occasionally use the rhizomes as a food source in marsh habitats.

The species is considered secure across most of its range. However, wetland drainage and conversion have reduced populations in heavily agricultural landscapes, particularly in the Midwestern United States.

Several Lepidoptera species use Carex as larval host plants, including the Appalachian brown butterfly and various sedge moths. Carex lurida supports moderately diverse insect communities in wetland habitats.

Amphibians including spring peepers and pickerel frogs use the dense tussocks as refugia during non-breeding periods. The microtopographic variation created by tussock growth increases habitat heterogeneity in wetland systems.

## Cultivation and Uses

Carex lurida is increasingly used in [[mollison-designers-cloudburst-and-stormwater-management]] systems, and [[hamilton-vegetative-propagation]] through division of established tussocks is the most reliable method for large-scale planting. Transplants should be set at the existing soil line in saturated or shallowly flooded conditions.
