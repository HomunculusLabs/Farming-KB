# Hypsizygus (Genus)

## Description

Hypsizygus is a genus of hardwood-loving mushrooms that includes two commercially important but very different species: the elm oyster (H. ulmarius) and shimeji (H. tessulatus). Despite being closely related, these species differ significantly in appearance, taste, and texture. Both are relatively easy to cultivate on hardwood species and sterilized sawdust blends, preferring cooler fruiting temperatures during spring and fall. The genus is known for high yields, ease of cultivation, and versatility in both outdoor and indoor growing systems.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Lyophyllaceae
- **Genus:** Hypsizygus

## Key Species

- **H. tessulatus** — Shimeji mushroom, with marbled caps in white, brown, or tortoiseshell varieties
- **H. ulmarius** — Elm [[oyster-mushroom]], capable of producing massive individual caps over 2 feet across

## Key Facts

- Both species are [[brown-rot]] mushrooms, important for soil-building ecology
- Easy to cultivate both indoors and outdoors (difficulty 1/5 for both)
- Mycelium is white and linear, exuding a light yellow metabolite on overcolonized substrates
- Sweet to slightly anise-like odor
- [[spore-print]] is white for both species
- Fruiting temperature window: 55–70°F (13–21°C)
- Elm oysters can produce caps exceeding 2 feet across when primordia are limited
- Shimeji mushrooms display unique watermarked, "cracked glass" cap ornamentation
- Protein content: 4–8 percent (dry weight)
- Excellent companion species in garden environments due to compatibility with [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]

## Cultivation Notes

### Substrate Preferences

- Hardwood trees, logs, and stumps in the wild
- Supplemented sawdust for indoor cultivation
- Pasteurized agricultural waste also suitable
- Prefers high-density fruiting substrate — finely shredded straw works if densely packed

### Outdoor Cultivation

- Grow on logs, stumps, or wood chips
- Use fresh wood (cut no more than one month before inoculation)
- Elm oysters can also grow on straw-based substrate in garden settings
- Preferred spawn: sawdust, plug, or cardboard spawn
- Inoculation to fruiting time: six to ten months
- Expected yields: Good on stumps and rounds; poor in garden beds
- Two flushes about a month apart or when moisture becomes available

### Indoor Cultivation

- Use pasteurized agricultural waste, supplemented sawdust, or wood chips
- Preferred spawn: grain or supplemented sawdust spawn
- Inoculation to fruiting time: three to four weeks
- Heavy yielding on straw or supplemented sawdust
- Highly recommended for commercial production

### Fruiting Conditions

- Initiate fruiting at lower end of temperature window (55°F/13°C)
- Increase to 60–70°F (16–21°C) for maturation
- Shimeji strains available in white, brown, and tortoiseshell cap colors

### Wild Spawn Expansion

- Well suited for cardboard expansion methods
- Stem bases readily produce mycelium when wrapped in wet cardboard
- Within weeks, sheets of mycelium can be transplanted to logs or pasteurized media
- Similar to oyster mushroom (Pleurotus) expansion techniques

### Laboratory Culture

- Extremely easy to culture via tissue cloning and spore germination
- Crack caps open and peel apart from cap to stem base for best cottony, dry tissue
- Spore germination useful for creating genetic diversity, especially for mycoremediation strain development

## Harvesting

- Twist mushrooms away from growing substrate
- Trim before storing
- Shimeji: harvest as tight clusters
- Elm oyster: allow caps to flatten and expand laterally before harvesting

## Storage

- Fresh: two to three weeks at 38–42°F (3–6°C)
- Older elm oysters better dried and powdered or sautéed and frozen
- Both species store well with minimal degradation

## Marketing

- Elm oysters: resemble white [[oyster-mushrooms]] but larger with more anise-like flavor
- Shimeji: crunchier, forming tighter clusters with unique marbled caps
- Make distinctions clear to consumers — same genus but different in appearance, taste, and texture
- Both excellent sautéed in stir-fries, sauces, and soups

## Nutritional and Medicinal Properties

- Protein content: 4–8 percent (dry weight), varying by strain and conditions
- Currently being researched for antiviral and antibacterial properties
- Shown to inhibit many cancer cell lines in laboratory studies
- Brown rot decomposition contributes valuable compounds

## Mycoremediation Potential

- Efficient wood chip and stump recyclers as brown rot fungi
- Highly compatible with mycorrhizal fungi
- Excellent companion species on slopes and in eroded or disturbed habitats
- Valuable for rebuilding soil in degraded environments
- Brown rot chemistry helps create stable soil organic matter

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Hypsizygus is an outstanding genus for both beginner and commercial cultivators. Its ease of cultivation (rated 1/5 difficulty for both outdoor and indoor methods) combined with high yields makes it accessible and profitable. The genus's role as a brown rot fungus that bonds with nitrogen-fixing bacteria and mycorrhizae makes it ecologically valuable in garden and habitat restoration settings. The contrasting nature of its two main species — massive elm oysters versus delicate clustered shimeji — provides cultivators with diverse market options from a single genus.
