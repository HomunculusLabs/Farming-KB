## Description

Arbuscular mycorrhizae (AM) are the most widespread type of mycorrhizal association, formed between [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF) in the phylum Glomeromycota and the roots of approximately 95% of all vascular plant species. The association is characterized by the penetration of [[fungal-hyphae]] into the cortical cells of the host root, where they form highly branched arbuscules that serve as the primary sites of nutrient exchange. Some AMF also produce vesicles, which are swollen hyphal structures containing storage lipids. The name vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizae (VAM) is now reserved primarily for associations involving Glomus species that produce both structures.

## Classification

- **Category:** Mycorrhizal association type
- **Fungal phylum:** Glomeromycota (formerly Zygomycota)
- **Key fungal genera:** Glomus, Acaulospora, Scutellospora, Gigaspora, Entrophospora
- **Host plants:** Herbaceous plants, grasses, many tropical and some temperate trees
- **Structural features:** Arbuscules (always present), vesicles (genus-dependent), extraradical hyphae, intraradical hyphae

## Key Facts

- AM associations are formed by a large number of plant species with a relatively small diversity of fungal species. The fungal partners are obligate symbionts that cannot complete their life cycle without a host plant.
- The association is identified primarily by the anatomy of spores produced within or outside the host root, as these fungi do not produce large fruiting structures like basidiomycetes.
- AMF hyphae extend the nutrient depletion zone around roots from 10-20 mm (root hairs alone) to up to 110 mm in some species, dramatically increasing phosphorus acquisition capacity.
- Rousseau et al. (1994) showed that for ectomycorrhizal pine seedlings, extraradical mycelium accounted for only 5% of the absorbing system dry weight but 75% of the potential absorbing area and over 99% of the absorbing length, a relationship that also holds broadly for AM systems.
- Kabir et al. (1996) demonstrated that AMF mycelium colonizing corn (Zea mays) and barley (Hordeum vulgaris) roots accounted for more than 83% of soil fungal hyphae, underscoring the dominance of AMF in agricultural soils.
- Berta et al. (1993) showed that mycorrhizal plants produce significantly more lateral roots than non-mycorrhizal plants, suggesting dual benefits: enhanced root branching and fungal-mediated soil exploration.
- Fitter (1991) estimated the cost of maintaining AM infection at roughly 1-10% of fungal biomass per day (0.1-1% of root biomass per day), approximately equivalent to root maintenance costs (~1.5% of root biomass per day), suggesting the nutritional benefit is realized primarily during periods of peak phosphorus demand.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Arbuscular mycorrhizae are of paramount importance in agriculture, horticulture, and ecosystem management. They are the dominant mycorrhizal type in most crop systems, including cereals, legumes, vegetables, and many tropical fruit trees. Their capacity to enhance phosphorus uptake makes them critical for sustainable agriculture, particularly in low-input and organic farming systems where synthetic fertilizers are limited.

In cultivation, AMF inoculants are commercially available for improving crop establishment, nutrient use efficiency, and drought tolerance. The selection of appropriate AMF species depends on factors such as hyphal extension rate (e.g., Acaulospora laevis at ~20 mm/week vs. Glomus spp. at ~10 mm/week), host compatibility, and soil conditions.

AMF also contribute to soil structure through the production of glomalin, a glycoprotein that acts as a biological glue binding soil microaggregates into stable macroaggregates. This function links mycorrhizal biology directly to soil health, erosion resistance, and carbon sequestration. In restoration ecology, AMF are essential for re-establishing functional soil ecosystems on degraded lands, mine tailings, and disturbed habitats.

Research continues to explore the role of AMF in plant community dynamics, interplant nutrient sharing through [[common-mycorrhizal-networks]], and the potential for AMF to suppress soil-borne plant pathogens through competition and induced resistance.
