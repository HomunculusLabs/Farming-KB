# Tepary Bean (Phaseolus acutifolius)

## Description

The tepary bean is a small-seeded legume native to the arid and semi-arid regions of the southwestern United States and northern Mexico. It is one of the most drought-tolerant cultivated legumes in the world, having been grown for millennia by Indigenous peoples including the Tohono O'odham (Papago) and Hopi. Mollison recommends tepary beans as a hot-season successor crop in desert gardens, planted alongside moth beans to maintain continuous food production through the hottest periods.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Family:** Fabaceae
- **Genus:** Phaseolus
- **Species:** P. acutifolius
- **Common names:** Tepary bean, pawi, Yori mui (Tohono O'odham)
- **Growth habit:** Annual herb, bush or vining forms

## Key Facts

- Originated in the Sonoran Desert; domesticated approximately 5,000 years ago
- Extraordinarily drought-tolerant - can produce seed with as little as 250 mm of rainfall
- Seeds are small (roughly half the size of common beans), flat, and come in white, brown, black, and mottled varieties
- Protein content ranges from 22-30%, higher than common beans
- Cooks faster than most dry beans due to smaller seed size
- Day-length sensitive varieties exist; some are short-day, others day-neutral
- Root system develops rapidly to access deep soil moisture
- Tolerates high temperatures exceeding 35C during flowering and pod set

## Cultivation Requirements

- **Climate:** Arid to semi-arid, hot; performs poorly in humid or cool conditions
- **Soil:** Well-drained sands to loams; tolerates alkaline pH common in desert soils
- **Water:** Extremely low - 250-400 mm annual rainfall sufficient; overwatering reduces yield
- **pH:** Tolerates 5.5-8.0; well-adapted to alkaline desert soils
- **Planting:** Direct-seeded after soil warms; hot season crop in desert gardens

## Role in Permaculture Garden Sequences

Mollison positions tepary beans within a seasonal succession system:

- **Hot season:** Tepary beans paired with moth beans as staple legume crops
- **Cool season:** Replaced by fava beans and peas in the same beds
- **Companions:** Planted in beds alongside marigold ([[subsurface-irrigation]]

## Nutritional and Cultural Significance

- Traditional staple food of the Tohono O'odham, Akimel O'odham, and Hopi peoples
- Well-adapted to the desert monsoon season planting pattern
- Provides essential protein and complex carbohydrates in regions where few crops thrive
- Near-complete protein when paired with maize and squash (Three Sisters analog)
- Rich in fiber, iron, calcium, and potassium

## Relevance to Cultivation

The tepary bean is a cornerstone species for desert [[moth-bean]] - companion hot-season legume
- Fava bean - cool-season replacement in succession
- Tagetes - nematode-suppressing companion plant
- Hopi - Indigenous stewards of tepary bean cultivars

## Varieties and Cultivars

Numerous landraces exist, maintained by Indigenous communities and seed banks. Key cultivar groups include:

- **White tepary:** Large-seeded, mild flavor, preferred for direct consumption
- **Brown tepary:** Smaller seeds, nuttier flavor, often used for flour
- **Black tepary:** Strongest flavor, highest antioxidant content
- **SI-1 through SI-6:** Selected lines developed by USDA for agricultural use

Seed conservation efforts by Native Seeds/SEARCH (Tucson, Arizona) and the USDA National Plant Germplasm System maintain hundreds of accessions of tepary bean germplasm collected from Indigenous communities across the Southwest.

## Agronomic Data

- Days to maturity: 60-90 (bush types), 90-120 (vining types)
- Seed rate: 20-30 kg/ha under dryland conditions
- Yield potential: 500-1,500 kg/ha under drought conditions where common beans fail entirely
- Row spacing: 50-75 cm between rows, 10-15 cm within rows
- No nitrogen fertilizer required; modest phosphorus response on very poor soils
