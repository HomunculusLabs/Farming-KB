## Overview

**Rosmarinus officinalis** L., commonly known as rosemary, is a woody perennial herb in the family Lamiaceae. It is native to the Mediterranean basin and has been cultivated for centuries as a culinary herb, medicinal plant, and ornamental shrub.

The species is renowned for its aromatic evergreen leaves, which yield an essential oil rich in 1,8-cineole, camphor, α-pinene, and borneol. Rosemary has a long history of use in traditional medicine, particularly for cognitive enhancement, digestive complaints, and topical wound healing.

Modern pharmacological research has validated several traditional uses, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and hepatoprotective activities. The plant is one of the most commercially important aromatic herbs worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Rosmarinus officinalis** L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The genus name derives from the Latin *ros marinus*, meaning "dew of the sea," referring to its native coastal habitat.

It is placed within the Lamiaceae family, tribe Nepetoideae. Some authorities recognize a second species, *R. eriocalyx*, from North Africa, but most modern treatments consider rosemary a single variable species.

Rosemary is an evergreen shrub growing 1–2 m tall. Leaves are linear to lanceolate, 1.5–3 cm long, with revolute margins and a dense white tomentose abaxial surface.

The upper leaf surface is dark green and glabrous. Flowers are bilabiate, 8–12 mm long, ranging in color from white through pale blue to deep violet.

Flowering occurs from November to April in the Northern Hemisphere. The calyx is campanulate and bilabiate, and the corolla has a curved tube with a two-lipped limb.

The fruit is a schizocarp of four ovoid nutlets, each approximately 2–2.5 mm long. Root systems are extensive and relatively drought-tolerant once established.

## Distribution and Habitat

Rosemary is native to the Mediterranean region, ranging from Portugal and Spain eastward through the Balkans, Turkey, and Lebanon. It has naturalized in many temperate and subtropical regions worldwide.

The plant thrives in dry, calcareous, rocky soils and is a characteristic component of Mediterranean garrigue and maquis vegetation. It grows from sea level to approximately 1,500 m elevation.

Naturalized populations exist across southern Australia, California, South Africa, and parts of South America. The species is considered invasive in some coastal California ecosystems.

It prefers full sun exposure and well-drained soils with pH 6–8. Established plants are highly drought-tolerant, surviving on as little as 200–400 mm annual rainfall.

Frost tolerance is moderate, with mature plants surviving brief exposure to −15 °C. Prolonged freezing temperatures cause significant dieback or plant death.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

The essential oil of rosemary (yield 0.5–2.0% dry weight) is dominated by 1,8-cineole (20–50%), camphor (5–15%), α-pinene (9–14%), and borneol (1.5–5%). Chemotype variation correlates with geographic origin.

Phenolic diterpenes, particularly carnosic acid and carnosol, are the primary non-volatile bioactive constituents. These compounds exhibit potent antioxidant activity through free radical scavenging and metal chelation.

Rosmarinic acid, a caffeic acid ester, contributes additional anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. Rosemary extracts demonstrate IC₅₀ values of 5–15 μg/mL in DPPH radical scavenging assays.

Antimicrobial activity has been demonstrated against *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Escherichia coli*, *Candida albicans*, and *Aspergillus niger*. The essential oil shows MIC values of 0.5–2.0 mg/mL against Gram-positive bacteria.

Hepatoprotective effects have been confirmed in rodent models of CCl₄-induced liver injury. Carnosic acid administration (50 mg/kg) reduced serum ALT and AST levels by 40–60%.

Cognitive enhancement research has shown that rosemary aroma exposure improves memory performance and alertness in human trials. A 2012 study found that 4 drops of rosemary oil in a diffuser significantly increased prospective memory scores.

## Culinary and Nutritional Use

Rosemary is one of the most widely used culinary herbs in Mediterranean cuisine. The fresh or dried leaves impart a pungent, slightly astringent flavor described as piney, peppery, and camphoraceous.

It is a traditional companion to roasted meats, particularly lamb, poultry, and pork. Whole sprigs are used to flavor soups, stews, breads, and infused oils.

The dried herb contains approximately 1–3% essential oil, 4–7% tannins, and notable levels of calcium (317 mg/100 g), iron (6.7 mg/100 g), and vitamin B6 (0.34 mg/100 g).

Rosemary extract (E392) is approved as a natural food preservative in the European Union. It inhibits lipid oxidation in processed meats, extending shelf life by 30–50% compared to synthetic antioxidants.

The herb is a key ingredient in the French *herbes de Provence* blend and the Italian *gremolata*. It is also used to flavor vinegar, butter, honey, and various liqueurs.

## Cultivation

Rosemary is propagated commercially by seed, semi-hardwood cuttings, and tissue culture. Cuttings (10–15 cm) taken in autumn root within 3–4 weeks under mist propagation with IBA treatment.

Field planting density ranges from 8,000 to 15,000 plants per hectare depending on the production system. Plants begin significant leaf production in the second year and remain productive for 10–15 years.

Essential oil distillation typically occurs during full flowering when oil yield is highest. Steam distillation of aerial parts yields 0.5–2.0% oil, with quality varying by chemotype and harvest timing.

Major commercial producers include Spain, Morocco, Tunisia, Turkey, and France. Spain accounts for approximately 40% of global rosemary essential oil production.

Pests include spider mites (*Tetranychus urticae*), spittlebugs, and powdery mildew (*Podosphaera* spp.). Good air circulation and well-drained soils minimize disease pressure.

Companion planting benefits include pest deterrence for cabbage family crops and attraction of pollinators. Rosemary is considered compatible with sage, lavender, and thyme in herb gardens.

## Notable Cultivars

Numerous cultivars have been selected for ornamental and culinary purposes. 'Tuscan Blue' is a tall, upright form widely used in landscaping, reaching 1.5–2 m with deep blue flowers and strongly aromatic foliage.

'Prostratus' is a low-growing, spreading cultivar valued as a ground cover and for container cultivation. It rarely exceeds 30 cm in height but spreads 1–2 m wide.

'Arp' is a cold-hardy selection developed at Texas A&M, surviving temperatures to −20 °C. 'Salem' and 'Hill Hardy' are additional cold-tolerant cultivars suitable for USDA zone 7.

Golden-leaved cultivars such as 'Aureus' and 'Joyce DeBaggio' provide ornamental interest but are generally less vigorous than green-leaved forms.

## Historical and Cultural Significance

Rosemary has been associated with remembrance and fidelity since classical antiquity. Ophelia's line in Shakespeare's *Hamlet* — "There's rosemary, that's for remembrance" — is the most widely known literary reference.

In ancient Egypt, rosemary was used in burial rituals and incense. The Greeks and Romans regarded it as sacred to Aphrodite and Venus, and it featured prominently in wedding ceremonies and funerals.

During medieval plague epidemics, rosemary was carried in posies and burned as fumigant based on the miasma theory of disease transmission. Four Thieves Vinegar, a traditional plague remedy, included rosemary as a primary ingredient.

Rosemary water was used as a hair rinse and skin toner from the medieval period through the Victorian era. The plant remains a symbol of the Provence region of France.
