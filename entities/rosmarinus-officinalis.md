## Overview

**Rosmarinus officinalis** L., commonly known as rosemary, is a woody, perennial aromatic herb in the family Lamiaceae. Native to the Mediterranean basin, it has been cultivated for millennia as a culinary herb, medicinal plant, and ornamental shrub.

The species name derives from the Latin *ros marinus*, meaning "dew of the sea," referencing its native coastal habitat. Rosemary is one of the most extensively studied medicinal herbs, with robust evidence for antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cognitive-enhancing properties.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Lamiales
- **Family:** Lamiaceae
- **Genus:** Rosmarinus
- **Species:** R. officinalis L.
- **Authority:** Carl Linnaeus, 1753

Rosemary is an evergreen shrub growing 1–2 m tall. Leaves are evergreen, needle-like, 2–4 cm long and 2–5 mm wide, dark green above and white-felted beneath.

Flowers appear in clusters along the upper stems, ranging from white through pink to deep blue-violet. The calyx is bell-shaped with a two-lipped corolla typical of the Lamiaceae.

The root system is extensive and fibrous, contributing to rosemary's drought tolerance once established. Stems are square in cross-section when young, becoming woody and brown with age.

## Distribution and Habitat

Rosemary is native to the Mediterranean region, extending from the Iberian Peninsula to Turkey and North Africa. It thrives in dry, limestone-rich soils and full sun exposures typical of Mediterranean scrubland (garrigue and maquis).

The plant is hardy to approximately USDA zone 8 (−12 °C), though cultivars like 'Arp' and 'Hill Hardy' survive zone 7. It naturalizes readily in coastal California, Australia, and parts of South America.

Optimal growth occurs at 15–25 °C with well-drained, slightly alkaline soil (pH 6.5–7.5). Rosemary is highly tolerant of salt spray, wind, and drought but susceptible to root rot in poorly drained conditions.

## Bioactive Compounds

### Essential Oil Constituents

The essential oil (1.0–2.5% dry weight) is dominated by monoterpenes. Primary constituents include **1,8-cineole** (eucalyptol, 20–50%), **camphor** (5–15%), **α-pinene** (9–14%), **camphene** (5–8%), and **borneol** (2–6%).

Chemotype variation is significant: Spanish rosemary tends toward higher camphor (CT camphor), while Tunisian and Corsican populations favor cineole (CT cineole). A third chemotype (CT myrcene/α-pinene) occurs in North African populations.

### Phenolic Compounds

Dried rosemary leaves contain 1.5–3.0% phenolic diterpenes, primarily **carnosic acid** and its derivative **carnosol**. These compounds are potent lipid-soluble antioxidants that protect cell membranes from oxidative damage.

**Rosmarinic acid**, a caffeic acid ester, is the major water-soluble phenolic at 1.0–3.0% dry weight. It exhibits anti-inflammatory activity through inhibition of COX-2, 5-LOX, and NF-κB signaling pathways.

Additional phenolics include flavonoids (apigenin, luteolin, diosmetin) and phenolic acids (caffeic, chlorogenic acids).

## Pharmacology and Traditional Uses

### Cognitive Enhancement

Rosemary aroma (inhalation of 1,8-cineole) has demonstrated improved speed and accuracy in cognitive tasks in multiple controlled trials. A 2012 study by Moss and Cook found significant dose-dependent correlations between plasma 1,8-cineole levels and cognitive performance.

Carnosic acid crosses the blood-brain barrier and has shown neuroprotective effects in animal models of Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease. Mechanisms include activation of the Nrf2/ARE antioxidant response pathway and reduction of amyloid-β accumulation.

### Anti-inflammatory and Antioxidant Activity

Carnosic acid and rosmarinic acid inhibit multiple inflammatory mediators including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and prostaglandin E2. The antioxidant capacity of rosemary extract (ORAC 3,300 μmol TE/g) is among the highest of all culinary herbs.

In vitro studies demonstrate protection against lipid peroxidation at micromolar concentrations of carnosic acid, comparable to α-tocopherol in efficacy.

### Traditional Medicine

In European herbal medicine, rosemary preparations (infusion, tincture, essential oil) are indicated for dyspepsia, mild anxiety, and circulatory support. The German Commission E has approved rosemary leaf for internal use as a digestive aid.

Rosemary oil has a long history in Mediterranean folk medicine as a topical analgesic for joint pain and headache, and as a scalp tonic to stimulate circulation.

## Cultivation

Rosemary is propagated from seed (slow, low germination at 30–40%), semi-hardwood cuttings (preferred, 80–90% rooting with auxin treatment), or layering. Commercial nurseries rely almost exclusively on vegetative propagation to preserve chemotype and cultivar characteristics.

Harvesting can begin 12–16 months after planting. Leaves and young stems are harvested year-round in warm climates, with peak essential oil content occurring just before flowering. Drying at temperatures below 40 °C preserves phenolic content.

Major commercial producers include Spain, Morocco, Tunisia, Turkey, and France. Spain accounts for approximately 40% of global rosemary essential oil production.

## Culinary Use

Rosemary is one of the classic herbs of Mediterranean cuisine, paired with lamb, poultry, roasted vegetables, bread, and olive oil. The fresh or dried leaves are used whole (removed before serving) or finely chopped.

The essential oil is used as a food preservative (E number E907) owing to its potent antioxidant activity. Rosemary extracts (carnosic acid/carnosol mixtures) are commercially available as natural preservatives for fats, oils, and processed meats.

## Permaculture Applications

Rosemary serves multiple functions in permaculture design. As a drought-tolerant, long-lived perennial, it functions as a low-maintenance hedgerow or border plant. Its aromatic foliage deters certain insect pests, particularly cabbage moth and carrot fly.

The plant attracts pollinators when in bloom and provides year-round habitat structure. It is compatible with [[salvia-officinalis]], [[lavandula-angustifolia]], and [[origanum-vulgare]] in herb spiral and Mediterranean guild plantings.
