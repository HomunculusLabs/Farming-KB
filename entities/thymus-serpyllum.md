## Overview

**Thymus serpyllum**, commonly known as wild thyme, creeping thyme, or
mother-of-thyme, is a low-growing, woody-based perennial in the family
Lamiaceae. It is native to Europe and northern Africa, forming dense mats of
aromatic foliage in dry, sunny habitats.

Wild thyme is closely related to common thyme (*[[thymus-vulgaris-entity]]*) but is
distinguished by its prostrate growth habit, smaller leaves, and lower-growing
stature. It has been valued for centuries as a culinary herb, medicinal plant,
and ornamental [[fukuoka-citrus-orchard-ground-cover-cultivation]].

The species is one of the most important nectar sources for wild bees in
European grasslands and heathlands. Its [[permaculture-designers-manual-essential-oil-plants]], rich in [[thymol]] and
[[carvacrol]], has documented antimicrobial and antiseptic properties.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Thymus serpyllum* L. was described by Linnaeus in *Species Plantarum* (1753).
The genus name *Thymus* may derive from the Greek *thymon* (incense) or
*thymos* (courage), referencing both its aromatic qualities and traditional
associations with bravery.

The plant forms a dense, creeping mat 2–10 cm tall with woody, prostrate
stems that root at the nodes. Erect flowering stems rise 5–10 cm above the
foliage mat during the blooming period.

Leaves are opposite, elliptic to ovate, 4–10 mm long, with entire margins.
They are sessile or very short-petiolate, green to dark green above, and
grayish-green below due to a dense covering of glandular hairs. The leaves
are strongly aromatic when crushed, with a warm, herbaceous thyme scent.

Flowers are borne in dense, capitate whorls at the stem tips, forming
terminal heads approximately 8–15 mm in diameter. The corolla is 4–7 mm
long, pink to purple (occasionally white), with a two-lipped structure typical
of Lamiaceae. Flowering occurs from June through August.

The calyx is tubular, 2.5–3.5 mm long, with an upper lip divided into three
teeth and a lower lip with two teeth. The calyx is densely hairy and often
purple-tinged.

## Distribution and Habitat

Wild thyme is widely distributed across Europe, from the British Isles and
Scandinavia south to the Mediterranean, and eastward through central Europe to
western Russia. It also occurs in northern Africa and western Asia.

The species is a characteristic plant of dry, calcareous grasslands, heathlands,
rocky slopes, sandy soils, and open woodlands. It prefers well-drained, often
nutrient-poor soils in full sun and is drought-tolerant once established.

It is common in chalk and limestone grassland communities, coastal dune
systems, and mountain meadows up to approximately 2,400 meters elevation. In
the British Isles, it is widespread on dry grasslands, downs, and rocky
places, especially on calcareous soils.

Wild thyme is an indicator species for well-drained, base-rich soils in
European vegetation surveys. It is particularly abundant in the
*Xerobromion* and *Mesobromion* grassland communities.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

Wild thyme has a long history of culinary use throughout Europe, though it is
less commonly cultivated for kitchen use than common thyme (*Thymus vulgaris*).
The leaves and flowering stems are used fresh or dried as a seasoning.

The flavor is similar to common thyme but often milder and slightly sweeter,
with a more delicate aroma. Fresh leaves can be added to salads, soups,
stews, and marinades. Dried leaves retain their flavor well and are used in
herb blends, stuffings, and meat dishes.
