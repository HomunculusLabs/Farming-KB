## Taxonomy

Basidiomycota → Agaricomycetes → Agaricales → **Hydnangiaceae → *Laccaria → *L.
proxima***

Described by Jean-Baptiste Émile Boudier in 1887 from French collections. The genus
*Laccaria* contains approximately 75 species worldwide, characterized by
long-spined basidia and amyloid [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] to colonize
  disturbed or primary successional sites, *L. proxima* facilitates
  establishment of willow and other pioneer trees on nutrient-poor substrates.
- **Nutrient exchange**: The mycorrhizal mantle enhances host plant uptake of
  phosphorus, nitrogen, and micronutrients from poor soils in exchange for
  photosynthetically-derived carbon. This is particularly important in sand
  dune ecosystems where available phosphorus is limiting.
- **Stress tolerance**: *L. proxima* mycorrhizae confer enhanced drought
  tolerance and resistance to heavy metals (zinc, copper, lead) on host
  plants, making the association valuable in phytoremediation contexts.
- **Host specificity**: While primarily associated with *Salix* species,
  *L. proxima* shows moderate host breadth among early-successional trees,
  particularly in wet or sandy environments.

## Ecological Restoration and Permaculture

*Laccaria proxima* has direct applications in ecological restoration and
[[plant-nutrient-uptake]].
- **Dune stabilization**: Willows mycorrhized with *L. proxima* establish
  more rapidly on coastal dunes, contributing to sand stabilization and
  facilitating succession to more diverse plant communities.
- **Riparian restoration**: The species naturally colonizes willow plantings
  along degraded waterways, accelerating root system development and nutrient
  cycling.
- **Food forest edges**: In permaculture systems, willow edges can be
  inoculated with *L. proxima* to improve willow vigor for coppice, basketry,
  or biomass production in marginal sandy soils.

## Edibility

*Laccaria proxima* is technically edible but rarely collected for food due to
its small size and tough, fibrous texture. The flavor is mild and not
particularly distinctive. Young specimens can be added to mixed mushroom
dishes, but more palatable species are usually preferred. As with all
wild-collected mushrooms, positive identification is essential. No toxic
lookalikes within the *Laccaria* genus exist, but confusion with small brown
mushrooms from other genera is possible for inexperienced foragers.

## Related Species

- ***Laccaria laccata***: Very similar; found under conifers and hardwoods in
  forest soils rather than sandy/willow habitats. Slightly larger spores.
- ***Laccaria tortilis***: Smaller species (cap 1–3 cm) found in similar
  habitats; more delicate stipe and more pronounced lilac tones.
- ***Laccaria bicolor***: North American species with distinctive violet-blue
  tones on cap and stipe; associated with conifers and hardwoods.
- ***[[laccaria-amethystina]]***: Larger, with distinctive deep violet color
  throughout; found in forest settings, not typically in sandy dunes.
