# Anadenanthera peregrina (L.) Speg.

## Common Names

Yopo, Cohoba, Parica, Jopa, Niopo, Huilca, Sebil, Juju

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Fabales
- **Family:** Fabaceae (Leguminosae)
- **Subfamily:** Mimosoideae
- **Genus:** Anadenanthera
- **Species:** A. peregrina (L.) Speg.

## Synonyms

- Mimosa peregrina L.
- Acacia peregrina (L.) Willd.
- Piptadenia peregrina (L.) Benth.

## Description

Anadenanthera peregrina is a mimosa-like tree, occurring mainly in
open grasslands and savanna habitats. It attains an impressive height
of up to 65 ft (20 m) and develops a substantial trunk reaching
approximately 2 ft (60 cm) in diameter. The bark is characteristically
blackish and coarsely armed with conical mucronate projections, giving
the tree a distinctive appearance in the landscape.

The leaves are bipinnately compound, bearing 15 to 30 pairs of pinnae,
each pinna carrying many very small, hairy leaflets. This finely
divided foliage creates a feathery canopy typical of many mimosoid
legumes. The inflorescences consist of minute white flowers arranged
in dense spherical heads, which are pollinated by insects.

The fruit is a flattened, woody legume pod typical of the family
Fabaceae, containing the seeds that are the primary psychoactive
part of the plant. The seeds are broadly oval, flat, and dark brown
to nearly black when mature.

## Distribution

Anadenanthera peregrina is native to the Orinoco basin of Venezuela
and extends across northern South America. Its range includes
Colombia, the Guianas, northern Brazil, and portions of the Amazon
basin. The species occurs both naturally in wild populations and
in situations suggesting deliberate cultivation by indigenous peoples.
It favors open grasslands, gallery forests, and disturbed areas.

## Chemistry

The active principles of Anadenanthera peregrina belong to both
open-chained and ringed tryptamine derivatives, all members of the
class of indole alkaloids. The principal open-chained tryptamines are:

- **N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT):** A potent hallucinogenic compound
  found in numerous plant species and produced endogenously in mammals.

- **Bufotenine (5-hydroxy-DMT):** A tryptamine derivative also found
  in the skin secretions of toads of the genus Bufo, from which its
  name is derived. Bufotenine contributes significantly to the
  psychoactive effects of [[yopo-snuff]].

Tryptamine itself is the basic structural compound derived from the
amino acid tryptophane, a molecule widely distributed throughout the
Animal Kingdom and fundamental to many neurotransmitter systems.

The ringed tryptamine derivatives identified in A. peregrina seeds
include:

- 2-methyl-6-methoxytetrahydro-beta-carboline
- 1,2-dimethyl-6-methoxytetrahydro-beta-carboline

These beta-carboline alkaloids are structurally related to
[[harmala-alkaloids]] found in [[banisteriopsis-caapi]] and other
Malpighiaceous vines used in Amazonian ayahuasca preparations.
Beta-carbolines are known monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitors,
which may modulate the activity of DMT when both are consumed
simultaneously.

## Ethnobotanical Uses

Anadenanthera peregrina is one of the most widely used hallucinogenic
snuffs in South America. The seeds are harvested, dried, and ground
into a fine powder known by various indigenous names:

- **Yopo** (among many Orinoco basin tribes)
- **Cohoba** (among the Taino and Caribbean peoples historically)
- **Parica** (in parts of the Brazilian Amazon)

The snuff is typically prepared by toasting the seeds, grinding them
with a mortar and pestle, and sometimes mixing the powder with
alkaline plant ashes or lime (calcium hydroxide) to facilitate the
release and absorption of the active tryptamine alkaloids through the
nasal mucosa. The powder is then insufflated (blown) into the nostrils
using hollow tubes or by a blowing partner.

The psychoactive effects include vivid visual hallucinations, altered
perception of time and space, and intense spiritual or visionary
experiences. Indigenous users report contact with spirit beings,
communication with the natural world, and profound insights during
ceremonial sessions.

Numerous tribes throughout the Orinoco basin employ Yopo in shamanic
rituals, healing ceremonies, and rites of passage. The practice is
among the oldest documented psychoactive plant uses in South America,
with archaeological evidence of snuff inhalation dating back thousands
of years.

## Cultural Significance

The use of Anadenanthera snuff is deeply embedded in the cosmology
and spiritual practices of many South American indigenous cultures.
Shamans employ Yopo to diagnose illness, communicate with spirits,
predict the future, and guide communal decision-making. The visionary
states induced by the snuff are considered essential for maintaining
balance between the human community and the spirit world.

The pre-Columbian Taino people of the Caribbean used Cohoba in
ceremonies involving carved snuffing tablets and tubes, artifacts
that have been recovered from numerous archaeological sites. This
represents one of the most extensively documented entheogenic
practices of the pre-contact Americas.
