## Description

*Banisteriopsis rusbyana* (now reclassified as *Diplopterys cabrerana*) is a species of forest vine in the family Malpighiaceae, closely related to *[[banisteriopsis-caapi]]*. It is significant as an admixture plant in [[ayahuasca]] preparations throughout the upper Amazon, where it is valued for containing significant amounts of the fast-acting hallucinogenic alkaloid N,N-[[dimethyltryptamine]] ([[dmt]]). Unlike *B. caapi*, which primarily contributes β-carboline alkaloids (harmine, harmaline, d-tetrahydroharmine) that act as MAO inhibitors, *B. rusbyana* contributes the primary visionary compound DMT.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Malpighiaceae
- **Genus**: *Banisteriopsis* (now *Diplopterys*)
- **Species**: *B. rusbyana* (≡ *Diplopterys cabrerana*)
- **Growth form**: Tree-climbing forest vine
- **Common names**: Chaliponga, chagropanga, pucahuasca

## Key Facts

- *Banisteriopsis rusbyana* contains significant amounts of N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), a powerful, fast-acting hallucinogenic alkaloid structurally related to serotonin (Poisson, 1965; Agurell et al., 1968; Der Marderosian et al., 1968).
- In ayahuasca preparations, *B. rusbyana* is sometimes mixed with other *Banisteriopsis* species (particularly *B. caapi*) to produce a more potent visionary brew. The β-carboline alkaloids from *B. caapi* act as MAO-A inhibitors, rendering the orally inactive DMT from *B. rusbyana* bioavailable.
- The plant grows wild as a tree-climbing forest vine, and some indigenous tribes cultivate it alongside *B. caapi* in their gardens.
- Schultes (1960) noted that among the Cofán, Inga, and Sione Indians of the Rio Putumayo area of eastern Colombia, a second species of *Banisteriopsis* was used alongside the primary species — this is now understood to include *B. rusbyana*.
- The four primary alkaloids now known from ayahuasca preparations (harmine, harmaline, d-tetrahydroharmine, and DMT) are all structurally related indole derivatives, related to mescaline, psilocybin, and LSD.
- Modern reclassification places this species in the genus *Diplopterys* as *D. cabrerana*, though the older name *B. rusbyana* persists in ethnobotanical and anthropological literature.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- *B. rusbyana* demonstrates the sophisticated pharmacological understanding of indigenous Amazonian peoples, who combine two plants — one providing MAO inhibitors (*B. caapi*) and the other providing DMT (*B. rusbyana* or *[[psychotria-viridis]]*) — to create an orally active hallucinogenic preparation. This synergy was practiced for centuries before being understood by modern pharmacology.
- The plant is cultivated by indigenous groups, indicating established horticultural practices for this psychoactive vine species.
- As a source of DMT, *B. rusbyana* is relevant to the broader study of tryptamine-containing plants and their role in shamanistic practices across South America.
- The taxonomic reclassification from *Banisteriopsis* to *Diplopterys* illustrates the ongoing challenges in botanical identification of ayahuasca admixture plants, a problem documented by Schultes, Friedberg, and Pinkley.
