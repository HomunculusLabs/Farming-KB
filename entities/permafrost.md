# Permafrost

## Description

Permafrost is permanently frozen ground that occupies 47–50% of the land area of Canada,
Russia, and Greenland. It represents one of the most critical landscape factors in cold-climate
[[permaculture-design]], directly constraining building placement, agricultural systems, water
management, and infrastructure development. Understanding permafrost dynamics is essential
for any cold-climate food production strategy or sustainable settlement planning.

The term encompasses ground that remains at or below 0°C for at least two consecutive years.
Below the active layer (the seasonally thawing surface), the ground stays frozen year-round,
often to extraordinary depths measured in hundreds of metres.

## Classification

- **Type:** Geological / Cryospheric phenomenon
- **Category:** Soil condition, cold-climate design constraint
- **Scale:** Continental to local; fossil permafrost can reach 400 m thickness
- **Distribution:** Arctic and subarctic — Canada, Siberia, Greenland, Alaska, Scandinavia, Tibet
- **Active layer:** The seasonally thawing surface layer above permafrost, typically 0.5–4 m deep
- **Related terms:** Mollisols (thawed permafrost soils), gelifluction (ice-sorted soils), cryotic ground

## Key Facts

- Occupies 47–50% of land area in Canada, Russia, and Greenland
- Fossil permafrost reaches depths up to 400 m in ancient deposits
- Peaty surfaces over permafrost prevent melting and rapidly admit cold, preserving stability
- Earth heat from below reduces thickness only approximately 1 cm/year under intact peat
- Solar thaw is approximately 5,000 times more effective than geothermal heat when peat is removed
- Removal of peat layers by clearing, fire, or overgrazing triggers rapid, often irreversible thaw
- Thawed areas (mollisols) can reach 2–45 m deep within a few years of peat removal
- Erosion effects include 9% volume loss from ice melting, destroying upland grazing areas
- Soils form primarily through frost shattering of rock in permafrost zones
- Soils and rocks are sorted by ice action and thaw cycles (gelifluction)
- Ice particles at minus 40 degrees Celsius are as hard and abrasive as sand
- Frost heave on bare soils creates ideal seedbed conditions for clover and pioneer species

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Infrastructure:** Pipes buried below 1 m or trickled continuously; wells within insulated building fabric
- **Water storage:** Ponds freeze 15–90 cm in winter; storage must account for unusable frozen volume
- **Agriculture:** Frost penetration to 1.2 m common in Iceland pastures; open ditches at 50 m spacing needed
- **Soil fragility:** Vegetation clearing or overgrazing triggers irreversible thaw and permanent soil loss
- **[[psychrophilic-fungi]]:** Permafrost soils harbor cold-loving fungi for decomposition in cold biomes
- **Active layer gardening:** The seasonal thaw zone offers a narrow cultivation window
- **Climate change:** Warming accelerates thaw, releasing stored carbon in positive feedback loops
- **[[mushroom-cultivation]]:** Underground chambers provide natural cooling for cold-loving edible fungi
- **Design principle:** Cold-climate permaculture must account for permafrost depth and peat insulation

## Ecological Relationships

- Intact peat layers provide critical thermal insulation over frozen ground
- Overgrazing by livestock strips peat cover and triggers cascading thaw events
- Moss and lichen crusts add thermal protection and moisture regulation
- Gelifluction creates patterned ground (stone circles, stripes, polygons)
- Permafrost stability interdependent with vegetation cover, drainage, and snow patterns
- Peaty surfaces both prevent melting and rapidly admit cold, maintaining frozen equilibrium

## Design Strategies (from Mollison)

- Preserve and restore peat and vegetative cover over all permafrost zones
- Avoid clearing, burning, or intensive grazing on permafrost-adjacent lands
- Place water storage within insulated structures (house cellars, barns, greenhouses)
- Use earth-formed reflectors and snow management for passive heating
- Monitor active layer depth seasonally to inform planting and construction decisions
- Design roads and structures on thermally stable ground with adequate foundation insulation
- Use glasshouses and attached growing structures to extend season above permafrost ground
- Account for 9% volume expansion of freezing water in all storage and structural design
