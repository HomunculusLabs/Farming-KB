## Overview

**Tylecodon paniculatus** (L.f.) Tölken, commonly known as butter bush or rooisuikerbossie, is the largest species in the genus *Tylecodon*. This deciduous succulent shrub can reach heights of 1.5–2.5 m, making it one of the most imposing members of the Crassulaceae in southern Africa.

The species is native to the Western and Eastern Cape provinces of South Africa. It is immediately recognizable by its stout, fleshy stems and large, pendulous clusters of orange-red flowers that appear in summer when the plant is leafless.

Unlike many succulents, it develops a soft, peeling bark on mature stems that gives it a distinctive appearance year-round. The common name "butter bush" refers to the thick, buttery texture of its stems when cut.

It is the most widely cultivated species in the genus, valued for its architectural form and showy floral displays. However, like all Tylecodon species, it is highly toxic to livestock.

In Afrikaans, the plant is known as "botterboom" (butter tree) or "rooisuikerbossie" (red sugar bush). These names reflect both its fleshy stems and the nectar-rich flowers.

The species has been featured on South African postage stamps and is sometimes used as a landscape specimen in public gardens of the Western Cape.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Family: Crassulaceae
- Genus: *Tylecodon*
- Species: *T. paniculatus* (L.f.) Tölken

The species was originally described by Carl Linnaeus the Younger as *Cotyledon paniculata* in 1781. Tölken transferred it to *Tylecodon* in his 1978 revision of the group.

It is the type species most commonly referenced when describing the genus characteristics. Several subspecific taxa have been described but are not currently recognized.

It is a robust, sparingly branched succulent shrub reaching 1.5–2.5 m tall with a trunk diameter of up to 15 cm. The bark is smooth, grey-green to brownish, peeling in papery flakes on older growth.

Leaves are large, fleshy, obovate to elliptic, 40–100 mm long and 20–50 mm broad. They are bright green, glabrous, with entire margins, appearing in dense terminal rosettes during the winter growing season.

Flowers are tubular, pendulous, and bright orange-red, 18–25 mm long. They are borne in large, many-flowered, paniculate inflorescences at the tips of bare stems from November to February.

The corolla lobes are recurved and slightly twisted, giving the flowers an informal, ruffled appearance. Nectar is produced copiously, attracting sunbirds and other nectar-feeding birds.

## Distribution and Habitat

Tylecodon paniculatus occurs from the Richtersveld in the Northern Cape eastward through the Western Cape to the Eastern Cape. Its range follows the escarpment and inland mountain ranges.

It grows on rocky hillsides, in dry riverbeds, and on steep slopes in succulent Karoo and fynbos vegetation. The species favors well-drained, stony soils at elevations of 100–1,500 m.

Rainfall in its habitat is predominantly winter, ranging from 150–350 mm annually. Summer temperatures can exceed 40°C, while winter minimums rarely drop below 5°C.

Plants often grow in association with other large succulents such as *Aloe ferox*, *Euphorbia* species, and *Portulacaria afra*. The species can form localized thickets in favorable sites.

Populations are generally stable and locally abundant where habitat is intact. The species is not considered threatened, though urban expansion around some Western Cape populations has caused local declines.

The species shows remarkable fire resilience, re-sprouting from its thick, underground rootstock after veld fires. This adaptation allows it to persist in fire-prone fynbos habitats.

## Cultivation

Tylecodon paniculatus is relatively easy to cultivate compared to other members of the genus. It requires a well-drained growing medium, ideally a mix of coarse sand, gravel, and a small proportion of compost.

Watering should be withheld during the summer dormant period when the plant is in flower. Active watering from autumn through spring supports healthy leaf growth and stem development.

The species prefers full sun to light shade and tolerates temperatures as low as −3°C for brief periods. Young plants benefit from protection from intense afternoon sun in hot climates.

Propagation is straightforward from seed sown in autumn. Seedlings grow rapidly and may flower within 4–5 years. Stem cuttings taken during the growing season also root readily. Cuttings should be allowed to callus for 5–7 days before placing in a sandy rooting medium.

Plants respond well to light pruning to maintain shape and encourage branching. In containers, regular repotting every 2–3 years prevents root congestion and promotes healthy growth.

Large specimens can become top-heavy in containers and may require staking. Some growers prune the root system periodically to maintain a manageable size while keeping the architectural form.

Pest problems include mealybugs on stems and root mealybugs in the soil. Regular inspection during the growing season allows early treatment with systemic insecticides.

## Ecology and Toxicity

The species employs CAM photosynthesis, fixing CO₂ at night to minimize water loss in its arid habitat. This metabolic adaptation allows it to survive extended drought periods using stored water in its thick stems.

All parts of the plant contain cardiac glycosides, particularly cotyledontoxin, which are highly toxic to livestock and humans. Poisoning of sheep and cattle is well-documented in the Karoo farming regions.

Symptoms of poisoning include colic, labored breathing, and cardiac failure. The toxin acts directly on the heart muscle, and there is no specific antidote. Fatalities in livestock are reported annually.

Despite its toxicity to mammals, the flowers are an important nectar source for sunbirds and sugarbirds during the dry summer months. Several butterfly species also visit the flowers for nectar.

The peeling bark provides microhabitats for small invertebrates and lizards. Fallen stems decompose slowly, contributing organic matter to the nutrient-poor soils of the Karoo.

Traditional healers in some communities have used minute quantities of the plant in carefully controlled preparations. However, the narrow therapeutic margin makes such use extremely hazardous.

The species is not listed on any CITES appendix, reflecting its relatively stable conservation status. It is protected under South African provincial conservation legislation in several provinces.

## Related Species

*Tylecodon reticulatus* is a smaller species with distinctive net-patterned bark and greenish-yellow flowers. It shares the same winter-growing, summer-flowering cycle.

*Tylecodon wallichii* is a dwarf species with slender stems and yellowish-green flowers. It occupies similar habitats but is much less conspicuous in the landscape.

*Tylecodon racemosus* produces reddish flowers in looser inflorescences and has a more compact habit. It occurs in the western part of the genus range.

*Tylecodon pearsonii* is a dwarf, compact species with grey-green leaves and small tubular flowers. It is found primarily in the Little Karoo region.

*Tylecodon ventricosus* is another large species with a swollen trunk base, occurring in the western Karoo. It has cream-colored flowers that distinguish it from the orange-red blooms of *T. paniculatus*.
