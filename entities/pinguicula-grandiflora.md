## Overview

**Pinguicula grandiflora**, commonly known as the large-flowered butterwort, is a temperate carnivorous plant in the family Lentibulariaceae. It is native to western and central Europe, where it grows in damp, nutrient-poor habitats.

The species produces a rosette of sticky, glandular leaves that trap small flying insects. It is one of the most cold-hardy Pinguicula species, capable of surviving prolonged freezing temperatures during winter dormancy.

*P. grandiflora* is distinguished by its relatively large flowers, which are among the biggest in the genus. The violet-blue blossoms appear in spring and make this species popular among carnivorous plant collectors and alpine garden enthusiasts.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Angiosperms → Lamiales → Lentibulariaceae → Pinguicula.

The species was described by William Henry Harvey in 1818. The epithet *grandiflora* refers to the comparatively large corolla size relative to other European Pinguicula species. It is closely related to *P. vulgaris*, with which it shares much of its range.

The leaf rosette measures 5–12 cm in diameter, consisting of 6–12 succulent, ovate to spatulate leaves. Leaves are pale green to yellow-green, 3–8 cm long and 1–3 cm wide, with slightly curled margins.

Both leaf surfaces are covered with two types of glands: stalked mucilaginous glands that secrete a sticky, glistening droplet to trap prey, and sessile digestive glands that release proteolytic enzymes. The mucilage gives the leaves their characteristic buttery appearance.

The flower scape rises 8–20 cm above the rosette. The corolla is 20–30 mm long, violet-blue to purple, with a white or pale throat and a bilobed lower lip. A prominent spur extends 4–6 mm behind the flower.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Pinguicula grandiflora* occurs across western and central Europe, with populations in Ireland, western Britain, western France, the Pyrenees, the Alps, and the Carpathians. Ireland hosts some of the largest and most stable populations.

The species typically grows in damp, open habitats including peat bogs, wet heathland, flushes on mountain slopes, seepage areas, and the margins of streams and lakes. It requires consistently moist, acidic to neutral substrates.

Elevation ranges from near sea level in western Ireland to over 2,000 meters in the Alpine region. High-altitude populations tend to produce smaller rosettes and flowers compared to lowland forms.

The species shows a strong preference for open, unshaded conditions. In the Alps, it colonizes damp limestone ledges, seepage channels, and the margins of snowmelt streams. These microhabitats provide constant moisture while maintaining the low nutrient levels the species requires.

Soil substrates are typically peaty or mineral-rich but nutrient-poor, with pH ranging from 5.0 to 7.0. The presence of a water table within 10 cm of the surface appears to be a key habitat requirement across the species' range.

## Trap Mechanism

The sticky leaf surface functions as a flypaper trap. When an insect lands on the leaf, it becomes ensnared in the mucilage secreted by the stalked glands. Initial contact triggers increased mucilage secretion.

The mucilage of *P. grandiflora* is primarily composed of polysaccharides and water, forming an adhesive droplet 0.1–0.3 mm in diameter. The droplet's refractive index gives the leaf surface its characteristic glistening, buttery appearance that inspired the common name "butterwort."

The leaf margins slowly roll inward over a period of hours, enveloping the prey. This rolling motion is a thigmonastic response that brings additional digestive glands into contact with the captured organism.

## Related Species

*Pinguicula grandiflora* hybridizes naturally with *P. vulgaris* where their ranges overlap, producing *P. × scullyi*. This hybrid is intermediate in morphology and partially sterile, though it can persist vegetatively at sites where both parents occur.

Within the temperate Pinguicula group, *P. grandiflora* is most closely related to *P. vulgaris* and *P. alpina*. It can be distinguished from *P. vulgaris* by its larger flowers, longer spur, and more robust growth habit.

*Pinguicula alpina* is restricted to higher elevations and produces white flowers, while *P. lusitanica* is a smaller, annual species of western Atlantic coastal regions. *P. longifolia*, a species of the western Mediterranean, produces notably elongated leaves and reddish-purple flowers.

Studies of *P. grandiflora* prey capture have identified a diverse assemblage of small flying insects as the primary food source. Diptera (flies, midges, and [[query-how-to-control-fungus-gnats-organically]]) account for the majority of captured prey by both number and biomass.
