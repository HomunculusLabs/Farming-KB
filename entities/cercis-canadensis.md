## Taxonomy and Morphology

Cercis canadensis was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The authority is L.

It is placed in the order Fabales, family Fabaceae, subfamily Cercidoideae, which diverged early from the rest of the legumes.

Three varieties are recognized: var. canadensis (eastern redbud), var. texensis (Texas redbud), and var. mexicana (Mexican redbud).

Trees typically reach 6–9 meters in height with a spreading crown of similar width, rarely exceeding 15 meters.

Bark is dark brown to gray, smooth in young trees becoming scaly and fissured with age on older specimens.

Leaves are simple, cordate (heart-shaped), 7–12 cm long and wide, with entire margins and palmate venation.

New leaves are reddish-purple, maturing to glossy dark green, with yellow fall color.

The distinctive pea-like flowers are magenta to pink-purple, 1–1.5 cm long, borne in fascicles of 4–8 on bare wood.

Flowering occurs from March to May, with flowers appearing before or simultaneously with leaf emergence.

Fruit is a flattened legume pod, 5–10 cm long, containing 4–12 flattened brown seeds, maturing in late summer to fall.

## Edibility and Nutrition

The flowers of Cercis canadensis are edible and have a mildly sweet, slightly tart flavor with a crunchy texture.

They can be eaten raw in salads, pickled, candied, or used as a garnish, and are sometimes added to pancakes or fritters.

Young pods are edible when cooked and have been compared to snow peas in flavor and texture.

Seeds can be roasted and consumed, similar to other legume seeds, though they are small and labor-intensive to harvest.

The flowers contain vitamin C, anthocyanins (responsible for the purple color), and various flavonoid antioxidants.

No toxic compounds have been identified in the flowers or seeds of Cercis canadensis.
