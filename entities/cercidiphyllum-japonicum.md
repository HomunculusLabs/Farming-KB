## Overview

**Cercidiphyllum japonicum**, commonly known as the katsura tree, is a deciduous tree native to Japan and China. It is the sole extant species in the family Cercidiphyllaceae and is considered a living fossil with a lineage dating back to the Tertiary period.

The species is widely planted as an ornamental tree for its elegant heart-shaped leaves, attractive form, and distinctive autumn fragrance. The falling autumn leaves emit a sweet scent variously described as cotton candy, caramel, or burnt sugar.

Katsura is valued in both traditional Japanese gardens and Western landscapes. Its graceful branching habit and clean foliage give it a refined appearance throughout the growing season.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Saxifragales
- **Family:** Cercidiphyllaceae
- **Genus:** Cercidiphyllum
- **Species:** *C. japonicum*
- **Authority:** Siebold & Zucc.

**Synonyms:**
- *Cercidiphyllum japonicum* var. *sinense* Rehder & E.H. Wilson
- *Cercidiphyllum magnificum* (Nakai) Nakai (sometimes treated as a separate species)

The genus Cercidiphyllum contains only one species with two recognized varieties: var. *japonicum* (Japan) and var. *sinense* (China). Some authorities elevate the Chinese form to species rank as *C. sinense*.

The family Cercidiphyllaceae is monogeneric, making it one of the smallest plant families. Fossil records show the genus was once widespread across the Northern Hemisphere during the Paleogene and Neogene.

## Morphology

Katsura trees typically reach 15–25 meters in height with a broadly conical to rounded crown. In ideal conditions, specimens may exceed 30 meters with trunk diameters of 60–100 cm.

The leaves are opposite, broadly cordate (heart-shaped), and measure 5–10 cm in length and width. Leaf margins are finely serrated or crenate, with a slightly leathery texture.

New foliage emerges bronzy-red to purple, maturing to blue-green above and slightly paler below. Autumn coloration is exceptional, with leaves turning shades of apricot, gold, pink, and red, often on the same tree simultaneously.

Flowers are dioecious, with male and female flowers on separate trees. Both are small and inconspicuous, appearing in early spring before or with leaf emergence. Male flowers are reddish clusters; female flowers are greenish with purplish stigmas.

The fruit is a small cluster of 2–4 follicles, each 1–2 cm long. Follicles are dehiscent at maturity, releasing small winged seeds. Fruiting is rare in cultivation, particularly in cooler climates.

Bark on young trees is smooth and gray-brown, becoming rougher and exfoliating in shaggy strips on mature specimens. The branching pattern is graceful and symmetrical, with ascending primary branches.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Cercidiphyllum japonicum* is native to central and southern Japan (Honshu, Shikoku, Kyushu) and central China. In Japan, it occurs in mountainous regions from 500 to 2,700 meters elevation.

The species grows in mixed temperate forests on moist, well-drained soils along stream banks, ravines, and mountain slopes. It is often associated with [[fagus-grandifolia]], [[acer-saccharum]], and various conifers.

Optimal growth occurs on deep, fertile, loamy soils with consistent moisture and slightly acidic pH. The species is intolerant of drought and waterlogged conditions alike, requiring well-balanced soil moisture.

In its native range, katsura is a component of cool-temperate deciduous and mixed forests. It frequently colonizes disturbed sites and stream corridors where light availability is higher.

## Cultivation

Katsura is propagated from seed, softwood cuttings, and tissue culture. Seeds require cold stratification and lose viability quickly, so fresh seed should be sown promptly. Cuttings root most reliably under mist with bottom heat.

The species is hardy to USDA Zone 4–5, making it suitable for cold climates. However, young trees are susceptible to spring frost damage, and late freezes can kill emerging foliage.

It performs best in full sun to partial shade with deep, moist, fertile soil. Consistent moisture is critical during establishment; drought stress causes premature leaf drop and tip dieback.

Growth rate is moderate to fast, typically 30–60 cm per year. The species is relatively short-lived for a tree, with a typical lifespan of 100–150 years under cultivation.

Several cultivars are available, including 'Pendula' (weeping form), 'Rotfuchs' (red-leafed juvenile form), and 'Heronswood Globe' (compact rounded form). The species is widely planted in parks, botanical gardens, and large residential landscapes.

## Ecological Role

The sweet autumn scent of falling katsura leaves is produced by maltol, a naturally occurring organic compound. The intensity of the fragrance varies with weather conditions and individual tree genetics.

In its native forests, katsura provides food and habitat for various insects and birds. The seeds are eaten by small birds and mammals, though seed production is irregular in cultivation.

The fossil record of Cercidiphyllum extends back approximately 90 million years to the Late Cretaceous. The genus was once distributed across North America and Europe before retreating to its current East Asian refugium during Pleistocene glaciations.

As a relict species, katsura is of significant scientific interest for understanding Tertiary-era flora and paleoclimatic patterns. Its restricted modern distribution exemplifies the concept of a paleoendemic lineage.
