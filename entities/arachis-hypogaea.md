## Overview

**Arachis hypogaea** L., commonly known as the peanut, groundnut, or goober, is a leguminous crop species in the family Fabaceae. It is one of the world's most important oilseed and food crops, cultivated on approximately 30 million hectares globally.

The species is unique among major crops for its geocarpic fruit development — after pollination, the gynophore elongates and penetrates the soil, where the pod matures underground. This unusual reproductive strategy has profound implications for cultivation and harvest.

Global annual production exceeds 50 million tonnes, with China, India, Nigeria, and the United States as the largest producers. Peanuts provide protein, oil, and vitamins to billions of people worldwide.

The crop is particularly important in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where it serves as both a food staple and a cash crop for smallholder farmers. Peanut-based therapeutic foods are used in famine relief and malnutrition treatment programs.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Fabaceae, Faboideae, Dalbergieae, *Arachis*. The authority is Linnaeus (1753). The genus *Arachis* contains approximately 80 described species, all native to South America.

A. hypogaea is an allotetraploid (2n = 4x = 40), likely derived from a single hybridization event between *A. duranensis* (AA genome donor) and *A. ipaënsis* (BB genome donor). This polyploid origin has been confirmed by genomic sequencing.

The plant is an annual herb, 30–60 cm tall, with a well-developed taproot and extensive lateral root system. Stems are erect or procumbent depending on cultivar, with compound pinnate leaves having two pairs of leaflets.

Flowers are yellow, zygomorphic, borne in axillary clusters near the base of the plant. They are typically self-pollinated, with flowering beginning 30–40 days after planting and continuing for several weeks.

After fertilization, the pedicel elongates into a gynophore (peg) that grows downward 3–7 cm into the soil. The pod develops at the tip of the peg, containing 1–5 seeds (kernels) enclosed in a fibrous shell.

Two main botanical varieties are recognized: *var. hypogaea* (runner and virginia market types) with alternating branching, and *var. fastigiata* (valencia and spanish market types) with sequential branching. These differ in growth habit, pod size, and oil composition.

Spanish and Portuguese traders introduced peanuts to Africa and Asia during the 16th century. The crop spread rapidly in West Africa, where it became deeply embedded in local agricultural systems and cuisines.

## Distribution and Habitat

A. hypogaea originated in South America, likely in the region of present-day Bolivia, Argentina, and Paraguay. Domestication occurred at least 7,600 years ago, with the oldest archaeological remains found in Peru.

The species is now cultivated throughout the tropics and subtropics, from 40°N to 40°S latitude. It thrives in sandy, well-drained soils with pH 5.5–7.0 and requires 500–1200 mm of annual rainfall.

Optimal growth temperatures range from 25–30°C. The crop is sensitive to frost and waterlogging, requiring well-drained soils for proper pod development. Sandy loam soils are preferred for easier harvest.

The crop requires adequate calcium in the pegging zone for proper pod filling. Gypsum (calcium sulfate) is commonly applied at early pod development to ensure adequate calcium availability without raising [[soil-ph]].

## Chemistry and Nutrition

Peanut kernels contain approximately 45–55% lipid (oil), 25–30% protein, and 15–20% carbohydrate by dry weight. The oil is rich in oleic acid (35–70%) and linoleic acid (15–45%), making it one of the most important vegetable oils globally.
Peanut skin, often removed during processing, contains significant concentrations of resveratrol and other polyphenolic antioxidants.

The protein is rich in the amino acids arginine and glutamic acid but relatively low in methionine, cysteine, and tryptophan. Peanut protein has a Protein Digestibility Corrected Amino Acid Score (PDCAAS) of approximately 0.92.

Peanuts are a significant dietary source of niacin (vitamin B3), folate, vitamin E, magnesium, phosphorus, and potassium. They also contain resveratrol and other polyphenolic compounds with antioxidant activity.

Major allergenic proteins include Ara h 1 (vicilin-type), Ara h 2 (conglutin), and Ara h 3 (glycinin), which together account for the majority of peanut allergy cases. Peanut allergy affects 1–2% of the population in Western countries.

Aflatoxin contamination by *Aspergillus flavus* and *A. parasiticus* is a serious food safety concern, particularly in tropical regions with suboptimal [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]] through symbiosis with *Bradyrhizobium* species can provide 60–80% of the crop's nitrogen requirement, reducing fertilizer inputs. Inoculation with effective bradyrhizobial strains is practiced in many peanut-growing regions. In soils without established bradyrhizobial populations, inoculation can increase pod yield by 15–30%.

Major pests include the tobacco thrips (*Frankliniella fusca*), lesser cornstalk borer (*Elasmopalpus lignosellus*), and various species of root-knot nematodes (*Meloidogyne* spp.). [[integrated-pest-management]] strategies combine resistant cultivars with [[query-how-does-nitrogen-cycling-work-in-a-permaculture-system]]. Peanut rotations with cereals can significantly reduce nitrogen fertilizer requirements for subsequent crops.

The extensive root system improves soil structure and reduces erosion on sandy soils. Peanut production is particularly important in conservation agriculture systems in semi-arid tropics.

The crop provides habitat and forage for [[coleman-beneficial-insects-and-ecological-pest-control]] including pollinators and predatory species. Intercropping peanuts with cereals can enhance overall farmland biodiversity.
