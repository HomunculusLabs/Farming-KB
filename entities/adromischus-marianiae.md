## Overview

**Adromischus marianiae** (Marloth) A.Berger is a small, compact succulent in the family Crassulaceae, endemic to the Northern Cape province of South Africa. It is one of the most sought-after species in the genus by collectors, prized for its distinctive, heavily textured leaves.

The species is named after Rudolf Marloth, a pioneering South African botanist who made extensive collections in the Karoo. It was originally described as *Cotyledon marianiae* before Berger transferred it to *Adromischus* in 1930.

Plants form low, stemless rosettes of thick, ovate leaves that are grey-green to brownish in color. The leaf surfaces are covered with prominent papillae (bumps), giving them a rough, warty texture that is unique among South African Crassulaceae.

In its native habitat, it grows in crevices on rocky outcrops, often partially shaded by larger shrubs. The species is not commonly found in general horticulture but is a staple of specialist succulent collections worldwide.

Several varieties have been described based on leaf shape and coloration, though these are not formally recognized. The "alveolatus" form has deeply cupped leaves, while the "herrei" form has more elongated, cylindrical foliage.

Plants in habitat often develop a reddish-brown coloration due to intense sun exposure, while cultivated plants kept in shade tend to remain green. The color change is a stress response that helps protect the photosynthetic apparatus.

The species is highly variable across its range, with populations showing significant differences in leaf shape, texture, and coloration. This variability has led to confusion in identification among collectors.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Family: Crassulaceae
- Genus: *Adromischus*
- Species: *A. marianiae* (Marloth) A.Berger

The genus *Adromischus* comprises approximately 28 species, all endemic to southern Africa. *Adromischus marianiae* belongs to a group characterized by their thick, highly textured leaves.

Plants are dwarf succulents, typically 3–8 cm in diameter, forming solitary or slowly clumping rosettes. The stems are very short, often subterranean, with leaves crowded at the apex.

Leaves are thick, fleshy, ovate to broadly elliptic, 15–35 mm long and 12–25 mm broad. They are grey-green to reddish-brown, with surfaces densely covered in prominent, rounded papillae.

The leaf margins are entire but may develop a reddish tinge under stress conditions. The papillose texture is thought to reduce water loss and reflect intense sunlight.

Flowers are tubular, pale greenish-white, approximately 10–12 mm long, borne on slender, erect inflorescences up to 20 cm tall. Flowering occurs in mid-summer, typically December through February in habitat.

## Distribution and Habitat

Adromischus marianiae is endemic to the Richtersveld and southern Namibia, a region of extreme aridity along the lower Orange River valley. Its range is highly restricted, covering only a few hundred square kilometers.

It grows on quartzite and gneiss outcrops at elevations of 200–800 m, typically in crevices that provide partial shade and some protection from direct afternoon sun. The species favors south-facing aspects.

Rainfall is extremely low, averaging 50–100 mm annually, with most precipitation occurring in winter. Plants survive prolonged drought by drawing on moisture stored in their thick leaves.

Temperatures are extreme, with summer maxima regularly exceeding 45°C and winter minima occasionally dropping to near freezing. The species endures these conditions through its CAM photosynthetic pathway.

The quartzite gravel plains of the Richtersveld host a remarkable succulent community, with *Adromischus marianiae* growing alongside *Conophytum*, *Lithops*, and *Crassula* species.

Fog from the Atlantic coast occasionally reaches the Richtersveld, providing an additional moisture source. The papillose leaf surface may help capture and absorb moisture from these fog events.

The species does not tolerate disturbance of its root system and is difficult to transplant successfully. This sensitivity contributes to its rarity in cultivation compared to other Adromischus species.

## Cultivation

Adromischus marianiae is challenging to cultivate and is recommended only for experienced succulent growers. It requires extremely well-drained growing media, ideally pure pumice or a pumice-sand mix with minimal organic matter.

Watering should be very sparing, only when the leaves begin to show slight softening. Overwatering is the most common cause of death, leading to rapid root rot.

The species prefers bright, filtered light rather than full sun. In cultivation, it does well under 50% shade cloth that mimics the partial shade of its rocky crevice habitat.

Propagation is primarily by leaf cuttings, which should be detached cleanly from the stem and placed on dry medium. Roots typically form within 3–4 weeks without any supplemental moisture.

Seed propagation is possible but slow, with seedlings requiring 2–3 years to reach flowering size. Fresh seed germinates more reliably than stored seed.

Plants are susceptible to mealybugs, which can hide among the leaf papillae. Regular inspection and prompt treatment with systemic insecticides is essential.

## Ecology and Conservation

The CAM photosynthetic pathway allows Adromischus marianiae to operate efficiently in its extreme desert environment. The species fixes CO₂ at night when transpiration rates are minimal.

The papillose leaf surface may serve multiple functions, including light reflection, moisture capture from fog, and deterrence of herbivores. The rough texture makes the leaves less palatable to insects.

Pollination is likely carried out by small bees and flies attracted to the nectar produced by the flowers. The slender flower stalks elevate the blooms above the rosette, improving visibility to pollinators.

The species has a very limited geographic range, making it inherently vulnerable to habitat disturbance. Mining activities and overgrazing in the Richtersveld pose ongoing threats.

Illegal collecting by succulent enthusiasts is a significant concern. The species' small size and high collector demand make it a target for poaching from wild populations.

It is not currently listed on CITES appendices, but conservation organizations have recommended monitoring. The Richtersveld National Park provides some habitat protection for remaining populations.

## Related Species

*Adromischus cristatus* is a more common species in cultivation, distinguished by its crinkled, undulating leaf margins. It is less demanding in cultivation than *A. marianiae*.

*Adromischus maculatus* has flattened, spotted leaves and a more spreading growth habit. It occurs in the Western Cape and is relatively easy to grow.

*Adromischus filicaulis* is a smaller species with slender, elongated leaves and a more sprawling habit. It is found in the eastern Karoo region.

*Adromischus cooperi* produces distinctive, blue-green leaves with dark spots and a smoother texture. It is one of the more widely cultivated species in the genus.

*Adromischus sphenophyllus* has wedge-shaped leaves and produces pinkish flowers. It is found in the central Karoo and is uncommon in cultivation.

*Adromischus trigynus* is a small species with triangular leaves marked by reddish spots. It grows on sandstone outcrops in the Western Cape.
