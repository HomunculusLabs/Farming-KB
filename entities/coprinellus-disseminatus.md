## Overview

**Coprinellus disseminatus** (Pers.) J.E. Lange, commonly known as the fairy bonnet, non-inky cap, or little helmet, is a small saprotrophic basidiomycete mushroom in the family Psathyrellaceae.

It is one of the most widely distributed coprinoid fungi, found across temperate regions worldwide. Unlike many inkcap relatives, C. disseminatus does not deliquesce into ink at maturity, retaining its cap structure even as spores are shed.

The species is easily recognized by its habit of fruiting in dense clusters on decaying wood and tree stumps, often producing hundreds of fruiting bodies simultaneously. Despite its small size and ephemeral nature, it plays a significant ecological role in decomposing hardwood and softwood debris.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Fungi → Phylum Basidiomycota → Class Agaricomycetes → Order Agaricales → Family Psathyrellaceae → Genus *Coprinellus* → Species *C. disseminatus*.

**Authority:** Originally described as *Agaricus disseminatus* by Persoon (1795), later transferred to *Coprinellus* by J.E. Lange (1938). The epithet "disseminatus" refers to its prolific, scattered fruiting habit.

**Synonyms:** *Coprinus disseminatus* (Pers.) J.E. Lange, *Agaricus disseminatus* Pers., *Psathyrella disseminata* (Pers.) Kits van Wav.

**Cap:** 5–15 mm diameter, hemispherical to convex, becoming broadly umbonate with age. Surface is striate-sulcate from the margin inward, translucent when wet. Color ranges from pale ochre to grey-brown, hygrophanous, fading to near-white when dry.

**Gills:** Adnexed to free, crowded, white when young, darkening to grey then blackish as spores mature. Gill edges are smooth, not deliquescing.

**Stipe:** 15–40 × 1–2 mm, slender, fragile, hollow, white, silky-fibrillose, without a ring or volva. Base may have sparse white mycelial threads.

**[[spore-print]]:** Black. **Spores:** 7–10 × 4.5–6 µm, ellipsoid to amygdaliform, smooth, with a central germ pore, thick-walled, dark brown in deposit.

## Distribution and Habitat

C. disseminatus has a nearly cosmopolitan distribution in temperate and subtropical regions.

It has been recorded across Europe, [[fungal-succession-woody-debris-ecology]]. Common substrates include oak (*Quercus*), beech (*Fagus*), ash (*Fraxinus*), elm (*Ulmus*), and various fruit trees.

Fruiting occurs from spring through autumn in temperate zones, and year-round in warmer climates with adequate moisture. It appears in dense troops and clusters, often numbering in the hundreds on a single stump.

## Ecology and Role

C. disseminatus is a primary and secondary decomposer of woody substrates.

It colonizes dead wood after initial white-rot or brown-rot fungi have softened the substrate, contributing to the later stages of [[king-stropharia-ecological-interactions-permaculture]] include serving as a food source for various invertebrates, particularly springtails (Collembola) and fungus gnats (Mycetophilidae). The dense clusters create microhabitats for these organisms.

The mycelium contributes to soil formation by converting woody carbon into humic compounds. It is commonly found in garden settings, parks, and managed landscapes where tree stumps are left to decompose naturally.

## Edibility

C. disseminatus is considered edible but is generally regarded as not worthwhile for culinary purposes due to its extremely small size and insubstantial flesh.

Unlike some coprinoid species, it does not contain coprine and therefore does not cause the disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol associated with *Coprinopsis atramentaria*. There are no known toxic compounds.

The flesh is thin, watery, and without significant flavor. While technically edible, collection for food is impractical given the size of individual fruiting bodies.
