# Glomus fasciculatus

## Taxonomy

| Rank | Name |
|------|------|
| Phylum | Glomeromycota |
| Class | Glomeromycetes |
| Order | Glomerales |
| Family | Glomeraceae |

Glomus fasciculatus is a glomeromycete fungus within the
Glomeraceae family, belonging to the phylum Glomeromycota.
In modern taxonomic treatments, this species is often placed
in the genus Rhizophagus or related genera following
phylogenetic revisions of the [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]].
The traditional genus Glomus was historically used as a
broad taxonomic grouping for many arbuscular mycorrhizal
species, but molecular systematics has since revealed the
polyphyletic nature of this classification. Despite these
taxonomic changes, the name Glomus fasciculatus remains
widely used in the ecological literature, particularly in
reference to earlier experimental studies of mycorrhizal
function and soil ecology.

## Overview

Glomus fasciculatus is an arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungus
that forms obligate symbiotic associations with the roots of a
vast majority of terrestrial plant species. As an AM fungus,
it penetrates root cortical cells to form characteristic
arbuscules, which are the primary sites of nutrient exchange
between the fungus and its host plant. The species has been
extensively studied in the context of plant nutrition,
particularly phosphorus uptake, and its interactions with
soil microfauna. Research on G. fasciculatus has contributed
fundamentally to our understanding of mycorrhizal ecology and
the role of symbiotic fungi in agricultural and natural
ecosystems.

## Mycorrhizal Function

The mycorrhizal function of Glomus fasciculatus centers on
its ability to enhance host plant acquisition of nutrients,
particularly phosphorus, from soil. The fungus develops an
extensive extraradical hyphal network that extends far beyond
the root depletion zone, effectively increasing the volume of
soil explored for immobile nutrients such as phosphate. Within
root cells, the arbuscules formed by G. fasciculatus provide
an interface for bidirectional nutrient exchange, with
phosphorus and other mineral nutrients flowing from fungus to
plant and photosynthetically derived carbon compounds flowing
from plant to fungus.

Azcon et al. (1976) identified isolate E3 as G. fasciculatus
in their studies of mycorrhizal effects on plant growth,
demonstrating the positive impact of this fungal species on
host plant nutrition and biomass production. Their work was
among the early experimental demonstrations that specific AM
fungal isolates could be characterized and their effects on
plant growth quantified, contributing to the development of
targeted [[mycorrhizal-inoculation]] strategies for agricultural
and horticultural applications.

## Collembola Interactions

The interactions between Glomus fasciculatus and soil
microfauna were investigated by Warnock et al. (1982), who
studied the influence of the springtail Folsomia candida
(Insecta, Collembola) on the mycorrhizal association between
leek (Allium porrum) and G. fasciculatus. Their research,
published in the New Phytologist, demonstrated that
collembolan activity can significantly affect mycorrhizal
functioning, highlighting the complex trophic interactions
that occur between [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] and soil invertebrate
communities.

Springtails such as Folsomia candida are generalist
microarthropod detritivores that feed on fungal hyphae and
spores in the soil. Their grazing activity on the extraradical
hyphal network of AM fungi like G. fasciculatus can potentially
reduce the efficiency of the [[mycorrhizal-symbiosis]] by
diminishing the hyphal connections between soil and roots.
However, the net effect of collembolan grazing on mycorrhizal
function is complex and context-dependent, as moderate grazing
may stimulate hyphal growth and turnover while heavy grazing
can severely compromise nutrient delivery to the host plant.

## Agricultural Significance

Glomus fasciculatus has significant implications for
agricultural productivity due to its role in enhancing
phosphorus nutrition of crop plants. In many agricultural
soils, phosphorus is a limiting nutrient despite fertilizer
applications, because applied phosphorus rapidly becomes fixed
in forms unavailable to plant roots. The extensive hyphal
network of G. fasciculatus can access these otherwise
unavailable phosphorus pools, improving plant phosphorus
nutrition and reducing the need for phosphorus fertilizer
inputs. This function has economic and environmental benefits,
as phosphorus fertilizer production is resource-intensive and
excess phosphorus application contributes to water pollution
through agricultural runoff.

The interactions between G. fasciculatus and soil fauna such
as collembolans are also relevant to agricultural management.
Soil biological communities in agricultural fields influence
the persistence and effectiveness of indigenous and introduced
AM fungal populations, and management practices that support
healthy soil faunal communities may indirectly promote
mycorrhizal function and crop productivity. Understanding
these belowground interactions is essential for developing
integrated soil management strategies that harness the
beneficial functions of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi in
sustainable agriculture.
