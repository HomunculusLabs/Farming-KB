## Overview

Cryptomeria japonica, commonly known as Japanese cedar or sugi (杉), is a large evergreen conifer in the family Cupressaceae. It is endemic to Japan and is the country's most important timber species, constituting roughly 45% of all plantation forest area.

The species is culturally sacred in Japan and is widely planted around Shinto shrines and Buddhist temples. It is also a popular ornamental tree in temperate gardens worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Cryptomeria japonica is the sole extant species in the genus Cryptomeria. The family Cupressaceae places it alongside junipers, cypresses, and sequoias.

Some authorities recognize a second variety, *C. japonica* var. *sinensis*, from China, though this is often treated as a synonym. Molecular studies confirm the genus is most closely related to *Sequoiadendron* and *Athrotaxis*.

The genus name derives from Greek *krypto* (hidden) and *meros* (part), referring to the concealed reproductive structures. Fossil records indicate Cryptomeria was widespread across the Northern Hemisphere during the Tertiary period.

Mature trees reach 50–70 meters in height with trunk diameters of 2–4 meters. The bark is thick, reddish-brown, and peels in vertical strips.

The crown is conical in youth, becoming more columnar with age. In open settings, branches persist to near ground level; in forest stands, lower branches are naturally shed.

The needle-like leaves are 0.5–1.0 cm long, spirally arranged, and awl-shaped. They are decurrent at the base, giving branchlets a rope-like appearance.

Leaves are bright green above and have two stomatal bands beneath. In winter, foliage may develop bronze or purple tints in colder climates, though the species is generally evergreen.

Male and female cones are borne on the same tree (monoecious). Male cones are small, yellowish, and arranged in clusters at branch tips.

Female cones are globular, 1.5–2.5 cm in diameter, composed of 20–30 spirally arranged scales, and mature in the first year. Seeds are small and winged, dispersed by wind in autumn.

Root systems are shallow and widespreading, making large trees susceptible to windthrow in exposed sites.

This characteristic is a significant management concern in Japanese plantations located on steep slopes.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cryptomeria japonica is endemic to Japan, occurring naturally on Honshu, Shikoku, Kyushu, and Yakushima.

On Yakushima, the species forms ancient forests with specimens exceeding 2,000 years in age. The Yakushima cryptomeria grove is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and contains the famous Jomon Sugi, estimated at 2,000–7,200 years old.

It grows at elevations from sea level to approximately 2,000 meters. Natural stands favor moist, well-drained volcanic soils in regions with high annual precipitation (1,000–2,500 mm).

## Ecological Role

In natural forests, Cryptomeria provides habitat for numerous epiphytic mosses, liverworts, and ferns. The Yakushima old-growth stands support the endemic Yakushima macaque and Yakushima deer.

These old-growth cryptomeria forests are among the most species-rich coniferous ecosystems in [[dighton-pine-needle-litter-decomposition-fungal-succession]].

Soil under dense cryptomeria plantations becomes acidic and nutrient-poor over successive rotations. Conversion of broadleaf forests to cryptomeria plantations has been identified as a major driver of biodiversity loss in Japan.
