## Overview

**Dicentra cucullaria**, commonly known as Dutchman's breeches, is a perennial herbaceous plant native to the deciduous woodlands of [[query-how-to-attract-native-bees-and-beneficial-insects]], particularly long-tongued bumblebees.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Dicentra cucullaria** (L.) Bernh. is placed in the family Papaveraceae, subfamily Fumarioideae, tribe Fumarieae.
It was originally described by Linnaeus in 1753 as *Fumaria cucullaria* and later transferred to *Dicentra* by Bernhardi in 1833.
The genus name derives from Greek *dis* (two) and *kentron* (spur), referring to the two nectar spurs of the flowers.
The species epithet *cucullaria* refers to the hooded shape of the flower.
Plants grow 10–30 cm tall from a cluster of small, pinkish, ovoid tubers.
Each tuber is 1–2 cm long and produces a single basal rosette of finely dissected leaves.
Leaves are ternately compound, 10–20 cm long, divided into 3–5 deeply lobed leaflets that are pale green and fern-like in appearance.

The inflorescence is a leafless scape bearing 3–14 pendant flowers in a raceme.
Each flower is 10–15 mm long, white to creamy-white, with two inflated, laterally compressed spurs resembling a pair of tiny pantaloons.

The inner petals are yellow-tipped and form a small corona at the base of the flower.

Flowering occurs from March to May, typically lasting 2–3 weeks before the foliage senesces.

## Distribution and Habitat

Dutchman's breeches ranges from Nova Scotia and Quebec west to Minnesota, south to the Appalachian Mountains and as far south as northern Georgia.

It extends into the Ozark Mountains of Arkansas and Missouri.

The species is most abundant in rich, moist, deciduous forests on north-facing slopes and in floodplain terraces.

It prefers loamy soils high in [[papaver-somniferum]]*.

The entire plant is considered toxic to humans and livestock if ingested in quantity.

Symptoms of poisoning include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and in severe cases, neurological depression and seizures.

Cattle poisoning from grazing on Dutchman's breeches has been documented, though the plant's foliage is generally unpalatable and rarely consumed in harmful amounts.

The tubers contain the highest alkaloid concentrations and were used medicinally by some Indigenous peoples in very small doses.

Traditional applications included topical treatment of skin ailments and use as a mild diaphoretic.

## Ecology

Dutchman's breeches is a myrmecochorous species — its seeds bear elaiosomes (fatty appendages) that attract ants.

Ants carry the seeds to their nests, where the elaiosome is consumed and the viable seed is deposited in nutrient-rich soil.

This ant-mediated [[clay-pellet-seed-dispersal]] is critical for the species' ability to colonize new sites within forest habitats.

The flowers are pollinated almost exclusively by bumblebees (*Bombus* spp.), whose long tongues can reach the nectar at the base of the spurs.

Honeybees and short-tongued bees are generally unable to access the nectar.
