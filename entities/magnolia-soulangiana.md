## Overview

*Magnolia × soulangeana*, the saucer magnolia or Chinese magnolia, is a deciduous hybrid tree in the family Magnoliaceae. It originated as a cross between *Magnolia denudata* and *Magnolia liliiflora*, first raised by Étienne Soulange-Bodin near Paris around 1820.

It is one of the most widely planted ornamental magnolias in the world, valued for its large, showy flowers that appear in early spring before the leaves. The flowers are typically white to pink-purple, goblet-shaped, and up to 25 cm across.

The hybrid grows as a large shrub or small tree, reaching 6–10 meters in height with a rounded, spreading habit. Its bark is smooth and gray, and its branches are somewhat brittle, susceptible to damage from heavy snow or ice loads.

Numerous cultivars have been selected over two centuries, offering variations in flower color, size, and bloom timing. It is hardy in USDA Zones 4–9 and adapts to a wide range of temperate climates.

The species has received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit, confirming its reliability in cultivation. It is often used as a specimen tree in residential landscapes, public parks, and botanical gardens.

In Chinese and Japanese garden traditions, magnolias symbolize purity and nobility. The saucer magnolia continues this cultural association in Western gardens, where it is among the most recognized spring-flowering trees.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Clade Angiosperms → Clade Magnoliids → Order Magnoliales → Family Magnoliaceae → Genus *Magnolia* → Hybrid *M. × soulangeana* Soul.-Bod. (1826).

The "×" denotes its hybrid origin. The female parent is *Magnolia denudata* (Yulan magnolia) and the male parent is *Magnolia liliiflora* (purple lily magnolia). The original cross was made at Fromont near Paris.

Some authorities place this hybrid in the subgenus *Yulania* along with both parent species. Molecular studies confirm the hybrid's parentage and reveal variable backcrossing with *M. denudata* in garden populations.

Leaves are large, obovate to elliptic, 10–20 cm long and 5–10 cm broad. They emerge after flowering, dark green above and paler below, with entire margins and a bluntly acute apex.

Autumn color is generally a muted yellow to bronze, less showy than the spring floral display. Leaves drop cleanly in late October to November in most temperate regions.

Flowers are borne terminally on bare stems in early to mid-spring. Each flower consists of 9–12 tepals in two to three whorls, the outer tepals often reflexed, creating the characteristic saucer shape.

Flower color ranges from pure white to deep purplish-pink, often white inside and pink-purple on the outer surfaces. The flowers are fragrant, with a light, sweet scent detectable at close range.

Flowering duration is typically two to three weeks, depending on temperature. Cooler spring weather extends the display, while sudden heat causes rapid petal drop. Individual flowers last 5–7 days under optimal conditions.

Stamens are numerous, arranged spirally on a conical receptacle. The fruit is a cylindrical to ovoid aggregate of follicles, greenish-pink when ripe in late summer, containing red seeds suspended by thin threads.

The root system is fleshy and somewhat shallow, making the tree sensitive to root disturbance. Transplanting is best done when young, as mature specimens resent root disturbance and transplant poorly.

## Distribution and Habitat

As a cultivated hybrid, *Magnolia × soulangeana* does not occur naturally in the wild. Both parent species are native to China, where they grow in temperate forests and cultivated landscapes.

The original hybrid was created in France and rapidly spread across European gardens throughout the 19th century. Today it is planted worldwide in temperate regions of Europe, North America, East Asia, Australia, and New Zealand.

It is one of the most cold-hardy deciduous magnolias, surviving temperatures to −25°C in sheltered positions. This hardiness has contributed to its widespread adoption in continental climates where other magnolias fail.

It thrives in moist, well-drained, slightly acidic soils enriched with organic matter. It prefers a sheltered position with full sun to partial shade, protected from strong winds and late frosts.

The tree is remarkably adaptable, tolerating urban conditions including pollution and compacted soils. It is drought-sensitive when young but moderately tolerant once established.

In colder regions (Zones 4–5), flower buds may be killed by severe winter temperatures. Site selection against a warm, south-facing wall can improve flowering reliability in marginal climates.

Propagation is primarily by softwood cuttings in summer or grafting onto *Magnolia kobus* rootstock. Seed propagation is possible but produces variable offspring due to the hybrid nature of the parent.

## Ornamental Horticulture

*Magnolia × soulangeana* is arguably the most commonly planted deciduous magnolia in temperate gardens worldwide. Its popularity stems from reliable flowering, relatively compact size, and ease of cultivation.

The species is frequently used in foundation plantings, courtyard gardens, and as a focal point in mixed borders. Its moderate size makes it suitable for residential gardens where larger magnolias would be overwhelming.

Over 100 named cultivars exist. Notable selections include 'Alexandrina' (deep purple-pink outer tepals, white inner), 'Lennei' (deep magenta-purple, late flowering), 'Rustica Rubra' (rose-red), and 'Speciosa' (white with pink base, late).

'Picture' is a popular compact cultivar reaching only 4–5 meters, suitable for smaller gardens. 'Brodzonii' is a slow-growing form with heavily textured pink flowers. 'Alba Superba' produces pure white flowers on a vigorous frame.

Pruning should be minimal and done immediately after flowering to avoid removing next year's flower buds. The species responds poorly to severe pruning, often producing weak, watershoot growth.

Flowering typically occurs in March–April in the Northern Hemisphere, making it one of the earliest spectacular bloomers. In regions prone to late frosts, flower buds may be damaged, reducing the display.

The tree's bloom period overlaps with early daffodils and cherry blossoms, creating classic spring garden combinations. Landscape designers often pair it with understory bulbs for layered seasonal interest.

The tree is relatively pest-free. Scale insects, magnolia anthracnose, and coral spot canker are the most common problems. Good air circulation and proper site selection minimize disease issues.

Mulching with organic matter in spring helps retain soil moisture and suppress weeds. Avoid cultivating around the base, as the fleshy roots are easily damaged.

## Ecology

The large, showy flowers are adapted for beetle pollination, a primitive pollination syndrome characteristic of the Magnoliaceae. Beetles are attracted to the flower's scent and feed on pollen and tepal tissues.

This beetle-pollination mechanism is among the most ancient in flowering plants, predating the evolution of bee pollination by tens of millions of years. The tough, leathery tepals protect the reproductive organs from beetle mandible damage.

The flowers are protogynous, with the female receptive phase preceding the male phase by one to two days. This temporal separation promotes cross-pollination between different individuals.

The aggregate fruits develop by late summer, with the red-coated seeds attracting birds. Seeds are dispersed short distances by birds or gravity.

The foliage supports larvae of the tulip tree beauty moth (*Epimecis hortaria*) and other Lepidoptera. The dense canopy provides nesting sites for small birds in summer.

Fallen petals and leaves decompose quickly, enriching the soil with organic matter. The tree contributes to urban biodiversity by providing habitat structure and seasonal food resources.

In urban settings, the early flowers provide nectar and pollen for emerging bees when few other sources are available. This ecological role is increasingly valued in city parks and gardens.

The species is not considered invasive in any region where it is cultivated. Seedlings are rarely observed outside of cultivation due to the specific germination requirements of magnolia seeds.

Individual specimens can live 80–100 years with proper care. The oldest documented specimens in European botanical gardens date to the mid-19th century.
