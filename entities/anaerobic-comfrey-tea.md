## Anaerobic Comfrey Tea

## Description

Anaerobic comfrey tea is a traditional liquid plant fertilizer made by fermenting comfrey leaves (Symphytum spp.) in water without aeration. Elaine Ingham distinguishes this from [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT), noting that while comfrey tea provides nutrients, it lacks the beneficial aerobic organisms that make AACT effective for biological disease suppression.

## Classification

- **Category**: Plant-based liquid fertilizer
- **Type**: Anaerobic [[fermented-plant-extract]]
- **Primary plant**: Comfrey ([[comfrey-symphytum-officinale]], Symphytum x uplandicum)
- **Process**: Anaerobic fermentation (steeping without aeration)

## Key Facts

- **Nutrient profile**: Comfrey leaves are exceptionally rich in potassium (K), with significant nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P), plus trace minerals
- **Deep roots**: Comfrey's deep taproot (up to 3 meters) mines nutrients from subsoil that are unavailable to shallow-rooted plants
- **Traditional use**: One of the oldest liquid fertilizer preparations [[hamilton-kelp-and-seaweed-in-organic-gardening]] and farming
- **Anaerobic process**: Unlike AACT, comfrey tea is made by steeping leaves in water for 2-6 weeks without aeration
- **Odor**: Produces a strong, unpleasant smell during fermentation due to anaerobic decomposition

## Comparison: Anaerobic Comfrey Tea vs. AACT

| Characteristic | Anaerobic Comfrey Tea | AACT |
|----------------|----------------------|------|
| Aeration | None (anaerobic) | Continuous (aerobic) |
| Primary purpose | Nutrient supply | Biology + nutrients |
| Beneficial organisms | Few or none | Abundant and diverse |
| Brew time | 2-6 weeks | 18-24 hours |
| Odor | Strong, unpleasant | Earthy, pleasant |
| Disease suppression | Minimal | Significant |
| Nutrient density | Very high | Moderate |
| Pathogen risk | Possible anaerobic pathogens | Low if properly brewed |

## How It Is Made

1. **Harvest comfrey leaves** before flowering for maximum nutrient content
2. **Pack leaves** into a container (bucket or barrel)
3. **Add water** to cover the leaves completely
4. **Weight down** leaves to keep them submerged
5. **Cover** loosely to allow gas escape while keeping insects out
6. **Ferment** for 2-6 weeks until leaves have decomposed
7. **Strain** the liquid and dilute before application (typically 1:10 to 1:20)

## Nutrient Content

Comfrey is known as a [[comparison-hyperaccumulator-vs-dynamic-accumulator]], concentrating nutrients from deep soil layers:

- **Potassium (K)**: Exceptionally high - comfrey is one of the richest plant sources
- **Nitrogen (N)**: Moderate to high from protein-rich leaves
- **Phosphorus (P)**: Moderate levels
- **Calcium (Ca)**: Good levels from deep-root mining
- **Trace minerals**: Iron, manganese, zinc, and other micronutrients
- **Allantoin**: Comfrey contains allantoin, which promotes cell division and wound healing in plants

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Benefits
- **Nutrient boost**: Provides a rapid source of plant-available potassium and other nutrients
- **Foliar feed**: Can be applied as a foliar spray (diluted) for quick nutrient uptake
- **Soil drench**: Effective as a soil drench to boost nutrient levels around plant roots
- **Organic approved**: Fully compatible with organic farming standards
- **Cost-effective**: Free to produce from home-grown comfrey plants

### Limitations (Ingham's Perspective)
- **No biology**: Lacks the diverse beneficial microbial community of AACT
- **[[ingham-anaerobic-organisms-role-soil]]**: May contain anaerobic organisms that are harmful to plants
- **Nutrient only**: Provides nutrition without the disease-suppression benefits of aerobic biology
- **Phytotoxicity risk**: Undiluted tea can burn plant foliage
- **Pathogen potential**: Anaerobic conditions during fermentation may produce alcohols and organic acids harmful to plant roots

### Integration Strategy
Ingham suggests that comfrey tea can complement AACT programs but should not replace them:
1. **Use AACT** for biological benefits [[compost-tea-application-and-disease-suppression]]
2. **Use comfrey tea** as a supplemental nutrient source when potassium is needed
3. **Avoid anaerobic teas** when beneficial organism establishment is the primary goal
4. **Consider aerated comfrey extract** as an alternative that provides both nutrients and biology

## Damaging Microorganism Concerns

Ingham warns that anaerobic comfrey tea, like all anaerobic preparations, may harbor damaging microorganisms:
- Anaerobic bacteria that produce alcohols toxic to plant roots
- Opportunistic pathogens that thrive in low-oxygen environments
- Organisms that produce plant-toxic metabolic byproducts
