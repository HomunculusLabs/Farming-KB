# Naja naja

## Overview

Naja naja, commonly known as the Indian cobra or spectacled cobra, is one of the most medically significant venomous snakes in South Asia. It is responsible for a substantial proportion of snakebite envenomations across the Indian subcontinent, causing tens of thousands of deaths annually.

The species is readily recognized by its expandable hood, which often displays a distinctive spectacle-shaped pattern on the dorsal surface. It occupies a wide range of habitats from dense forests to agricultural landscapes and urban areas.

Indian cobras are deeply embedded in South Asian culture, religion, and mythology. They feature prominently in Hindu traditions and are associated with deities, festivals, and traditional medicine practices.

The festival of Nag Panchami, celebrated across India and Nepal, is dedicated to the worship of serpents. Naja naja is the species most commonly associated with this tradition, and live cobras are often displayed during the festivities.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Naja naja belongs to the family Elapidae, subfamily Elapinae. It was first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1758 as Coluber naja.

The current accepted taxonomy places it within the genus Naja, which contains approximately 30 species of true cobras. Recent phylogenetic studies have clarified relationships within the genus, confirming Naja naja as distinct from African and [[oner-southeast-asian-sativa-heritage-thai-vietnamese-cambodian]] cobra lineages.

Adult Indian cobras typically measure 1.0 to 1.5 meters in total length, though specimens exceeding 2.0 meters have been recorded. Males are generally longer than females.

The body is moderately robust with a distinct hood that can be expanded when the snake is threatened. The dorsal coloration varies from yellowish-brown to dark brown or nearly black, with the characteristic hood pattern ranging from a clear spectacle mark to a uniform color or partial marking in some populations.

The head is broadly ovate and barely distinct from the neck. The eyes are medium-sized with round pupils, and the nostrils are large. The dorsal scales are smooth and arranged in 15-17 rows at midbody.

The tail constitutes approximately 15-18% of total body length. The subcaudal scales are divided in males and undivided in females, a sexually dimorphic trait useful for [[psilocybin-psilocin-variation-across-flushes]] the species range. Populations from different regions may differ significantly in the relative abundance of neurotoxins versus cytotoxins, which has implications for antivenom efficacy.

Comparative proteomic studies have identified over 80 unique protein components in Naja naja venom from different geographical locations. Transcriptomic analysis has further revealed that venom gland gene expression varies seasonally, with higher neurotoxin expression during warmer months when prey availability increases.

## Clinical Significance

Naja naja is one of the Big Four venomous snakes of India, alongside Echis carinatus, Bungarus caeruleus, and Daboia russelii. Together, these species account for the vast majority of snakebite mortality in South Asia.

Envenomation typically presents with progressive neurotoxic symptoms. Ptosis (drooping eyelids) is often the earliest sign, followed by ophthalmoplegia, dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), dysarthria (slurred speech), and descending flaccid paralysis.

In severe cases, respiratory muscle paralysis leads to respiratory failure, the primary cause of death. This can occur within hours of the bite if left untreated. Neurotoxic signs may be delayed by several hours, creating a dangerous false sense of security.

Local effects at the bite site include pain, swelling, and occasionally tissue necrosis. Systemic effects may include vomiting, abdominal pain, and dizziness. Hemotoxic effects are generally minimal compared to viper envenomations.

Polyvalent antivenoms manufactured in India, such as those produced by the Haffkine Institute and the Central Research Institute, provide effective neutralization of Naja naja venom. However, antivenom dosage requirements and regional venom variation remain clinical challenges.

Traditional snake charmers and folk remedies continue to delay appropriate medical treatment in some rural areas. Public health education programs have improved outcomes but snakebite remains a significant burden, with an estimated 15,000 to 58,000 deaths annually in India attributed to snakebite overall.
