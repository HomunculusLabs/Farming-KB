# Hartebeest

## Description

The hartebeest (Alcelaphus buselaphus and related species) is a large African antelope
of open grasslands and savannah. Mollison references hartebeest in the stock unit system,
noting that 27 hartebeest are equivalent to one standard stock unit (450 kg European cow
and calf). As a native African grazer, the hartebeest represents part of the natural guild
of herbivores that sustain higher productivity on rangeland than imported domestic species,
requiring no external energy inputs. No known cultivated livestock system can surpass
natural savannah productivity without external inputs.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Animalia
- **Class:** Mammalia
- **Order:** Artiodactyla
- **Family:** Bovidae
- **Genus:** Alcelaphus
- **Species:** A. buselaphus (and subspecies)
- **Common names:** Hartebeest, kongoni
- **Size:** 120–150 cm shoulder height, 120–200 kg adult weight

## Key Facts

- Large African antelope of open grassland and savannah habitats
- 27 hartebeest equal one standard stock unit in the cow-equivalent system
- Ten standard stock units equal approximately 27 hartebeest
- Native to sub-Saharan Africa across diverse grassland ecosystems
- Primarily a grazer, feeding on medium to tall grasses in open savannah
- Can browse when grass is scarce, showing dietary flexibility
- Herding species; forms groups of 20–300+ individuals on open range
- Fast runner capable of sustained speeds to escape predators effectively
- Well-adapted to hot, dry conditions; can go without water for extended periods
- Standing crop of large herbivores on African savannah approximately 500 kg/ha

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Rangeland productivity:** Part of native guilds sustaining 500 kg/ha without inputs
- **Multi-species grazing:** One of 11 grazer species with specific food preferences
- **Dung ecology:** Large dung quantities support [[coprophilous-fungi]] and soil fertility
- **Dung beetle associations:** Dung supports beetle populations that aerate soil
- **No external inputs:** Native systems with diverse herbivores exceed cultivated yields
- **Game farming:** Early wildlife researchers suggested excellent game farm viability

## Ecological Relationships

- Part of complex savannah herbivore guild: 5 browsers, 11 grazers, 4 mixed feeders
- Competes minimally due to specific grass-height feeding preference
- Prey for lions, hyenas, wild dogs, leopards, and cheetahs
- Grazing stimulates new grass growth benefiting other herbivore species
- Seasonal migrations follow rainfall and fresh grass availability
- Dung deposits concentrate nutrients creating fertile patches in grassland

## Stock Unit Context (from Mollison)

Mollison's stock unit system provides equivalents for rangeland planning. One standard
stock unit (450 kg cow and calf) equals approximately 27 hartebeest. Ten standard units
equal 1 elephant, 4 black rhino, 15 zebras, or 27 hartebeest. Mollison argues that
imported European cultures prevented use of native antelope as farmed species despite
their being preferred local food with superior feed conversion in African conditions.

## Design Implications

- Natural multi-species systems always outperform single-species livestock production
- Importing European livestock prevents use of better-adapted native species in Africa
- [[rangeland-management]] should preserve or mimic natural herbivore diversity
- Stock unit equivalents enable planning multi-species stocking ratios on range
- Native species convert food to flesh more efficiently in local African conditions
