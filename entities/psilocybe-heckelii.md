# Psilocybe heckelii

## Overview

**Psilocybe heckelii** is a bluing, psilocybin-containing mushroom in the family Hymenogastraceae, native to the neotropical forests of **[[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]]** and northern **South America**. The species was first described from material collected in **Colombia** and has since been recorded across a range extending from southern Mexico through Panama, Colombia, and into Ecuador.

P. heckelii is closely allied with the **Psilocybe mexicana** species complex. It produces moderate levels of psilocybin and psilocin and has been used in traditional indigenous ceremonies in several Mesoamerican cultural contexts. The species is less well-characterized biochemically than its close relatives P. mexicana and P. caerulescens.

## Taxonomy

**Kingdom:** Fungi — **Phylum:** Basidiomycota — **Class:** Agaricomycetes

**Order:** Agaricales — **Family:** Hymenogastraceae — **Genus:** Psilocybe

**Species:** P. heckelii Guzmán

The species was formally described by Gastón Guzmán in 1976 based on specimens collected from the **Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta** in Colombia. The epithet honors the French mycologist **René Maire** (the original collector of related material). Some authorities have suggested P. heckelii may represent a tropical variant or subspecies of P. mexicana, but morphological and distributional differences support its status as a distinct species.

**Synonyms:** None currently accepted. Historically confused with P. mexicana in field identifications.

## Morphology

**Cap:** 8–25 mm diameter, conic to campanulate when young, expanding to broadly convex or plane with age. Surface is hygrophanous, brownish-orange to reddish-cinnamon when moist, fading to straw-yellow or pale ochre upon drying. Surface is smooth to slightly translucent-striate at the margin.

**Gills:** Adnexed to adnate, close to subdistant, grayish-brown becoming dark purplish-brown with spore maturity. Gill edges are fringed and whitish.

**Stipe:** 30–70 mm long, 1.5–3 mm thick, equal to slightly enlarged at the base. Color is pallid to yellowish, bruising distinctly blue to blue-green when handled. Surface is smooth, dry, with a persistent partial veil forming a thin annular zone.

**Spores:** Ellipsoid to somewhat rhomboid in face view, 8–11 × 5–7 μm, thick-walled with a distinct germ pore. Spore print is dark purple-brown.

**Taste and odor:** Farinaceous (mealy, flour-like), characteristic of the P. mexicana group.

## Distribution and Habitat

P. heckelii occurs in humid tropical and subtropical montane forests between 800–2,000 m elevation. It has been confirmed from **Mexico** (Chiapas, Oaxaca), **Guatemala**, **Costa Rica**, **Panama**, **Colombia**, and **Ecuador**.

The species is primarily a **dung-inhabiting** (coprophilous) fungus, fruiting on the dung of herbivorous mammals including cattle, horses, and deer. It also occurs on enriched soils and grasslands adjacent to forest margins.

Fruiting occurs during the rainy season, typically from May through October depending on latitude. Solitary to gregarious in habit.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The principal psychoactive constituents of P. heckelii are:

- **Psilocybin**: 0.25–0.55% dry weight (moderate potency)
- **Psilocin**: 0.02–0.10% dry weight
- **Baeocystin**: present in trace amounts
- **Norbaeocystin**: detected in some collections

These compounds are 5-HT2A [[cannabinoid-receptor-agonists-antagonists-synthetic-reference-blesching]], producing altered perception, synesthesia, introspective ideation, and in some users, mystical-type experiences. The onset is typically 20–60 minutes after ingestion, with effects lasting 4–6 hours.

Biochemically, P. heckelii is intermediate in potency between P. mexicana (which it closely resembles) and stronger tropical species such as P. cubensis. Variability in alkaloid content reflects differences in substrate, age at harvest, and geographic origin.

## Ethnobotanical Significance

The use of psilocybin mushrooms in Mesoamerican indigenous cultures is well-documented, with ceremonial consumption dating back at least 3,000 years. While P. mexicana and P. caerulescens are the species most frequently identified in ethnographic accounts, P. heckelii is believed to contribute to the general category of teonanácatl ("flesh of the gods") in regions where it occurs.

Among the **Mazatec** and **Mixtec** peoples of Oaxaca, Mexico, psilocybin mushrooms are used in healing ceremonies conducted by curanderos (traditional healers). The specific identity of species consumed is often not distinguished at the folk taxonomic level.

In Colombia, indigenous communities in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta region have been documented using Psilocybe species in ritual contexts, and P. heckelii is a likely candidate given its type locality in this region.

## Cultivation

Cultivation of P. heckelii has received limited attention compared to P. cubensis, but standard psilocybin [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] techniques are applicable. The species fruits on sterilized horse or cow dung, pasteurized straw, and enriched grain substrates.

Optimal fruiting parameters include temperatures of 22–26°C and high relative humidity (>90%). The species is somewhat slower to colonize than P. cubensis, with full substrate colonization requiring 3–4 weeks from inoculation.

 sclerotium formation (the production of hard, underground "truffle-like" masses known as "magic truffles") has been reported for P. heckelii, as it has for other members of the P. mexicana group. Sclerotia contain lower concentrations of psilocybin than fruiting bodies but are easier to cultivate in vitro.

## Legal Status

Psilocybin and psilocin are Schedule I controlled substances under the United Nations Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971). P. heckelii is consequently prohibited in most jurisdictions as a psilocybin-containing organism.

Notable exceptions include the **Netherlands** (where fresh psilocybin mushrooms were banned in 2008 but sclerotia remain legal), **Brazil** (where the species occurs naturally and is not specifically scheduled), and **Jamaica** (where psilocybin retreats operate in a legal gray area).

## Distinguishing from Similar Species

P. heckelii is most reliably distinguished from P. mexicana by its **larger spores** (8–11 μm vs. 6–8 μm for P. mexicana) and its **restricted neotropical distribution**. The cap color of P. heckelii tends toward reddish-cinnamon, whereas P. mexicana typically displays more golden-brown tones.

From P. caerulescens, P. heckelii differs in its **coprophilous habit** (P. caerulescens is primarily terrestrial and associated with undisturbed forest soils). The stipe of P. heckelii is more slender and lacks the robust, rooting base characteristic of P. caerulescens.

Microscopically, the cheilocystidia of P. heckelii are more abundant and less conspicuously swollen than those of P. mexicana. This character requires examination under 400× magnification with Melzer's reagent for reliable differentiation.

Field identification should always be confirmed with microscopic analysis, as multiple Psilocybe species overlap in the neotropical dung-inhabiting niche.
