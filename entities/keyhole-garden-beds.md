## Keyhole Garden Beds

## Description

Keyhole garden beds are a permaculture garden design technique that maximises the accessible growing edge for a given area of path and bed. The design derives its name from the shape of the bed when viewed from above, which resembles an old-fashioned keyhole — a circular or semi-circular raised bed with a narrow path or notch cut into one side, allowing the gardener to reach all parts of the bed from a compact access point.

## Classification

- **Type:** Garden design technique / permaculture method
- **Design principle:** Edge maximisation
- **Origin:** [[permaculture-design]] tradition
- **Application:** Home gardens, market gardens, small-scale food production

## Key Facts

- **Edge maximisation:** The keyhole shape is specifically designed to increase the ratio of accessible planting edge to total bed area. By eliminating the need for parallel paths between rectangular beds, more growing space is achieved with less pathway.

- **Access efficiency:** The central notch allows the gardener to stand in one position and reach plants across a much wider arc than conventional rectangular beds, reducing movement and labour.

- **Classic permaculture example:** Keyhole beds are cited by Holmgren as a classic example of permaculture Principle 11 (Use Edges and Value the Marginal) in action — literally designing to increase productive edge in the garden landscape.

- **Raised bed compatibility:** Keyhole beds are commonly constructed as raised beds, incorporating layers of organic material, compost, and soil to create deep, fertile growing environments with excellent drainage and root penetration.

- **Water efficiency:** The circular shape and central access point can incorporate a central compost basket or watering point, allowing efficient water and nutrient distribution to the entire bed.

## Design Variations

1. **Single keyhole:** A circular bed with one access notch, suitable for individual use
2. **Double keyhole:** A larger bed with two opposing notches, allowing access from both sides
3. **Keyhole mandala:** Multiple keyhole beds arranged in a circular pattern, creating a productive garden mandala with central access
4. **Combined with chicken domes:** [[keyhole-bed]] patterns integrated with movable chicken enclosures for pest control and fertility

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Keyhole garden beds embody several core [[permaculture-principles]]:

1. **Use Edges and Value the Marginal (Principle 11):** The design's primary purpose is to increase productive edge — the boundary between path and bed where access and growing space meet. Every curve and indent adds accessible edge.

2. **Small-Scale Intensive Systems (Principle 9):** Keyhole beds are most appropriate in Zone 1 (intensive garden areas close to the house) where the complexity of edge management is practical and the productivity gains justify the design effort.

3. **[[design-from-patterns-to-details]] (Principle 7):** The keyhole is a specific application of the general pattern of increasing edge to increase productivity — the natural pattern of coastlines, leaf margins, and intestinal villi applied at garden scale.

4. **Scale considerations:** Holmgren notes that the complexity of edge appropriate in Zone 1 may be inappropriate in Zone 2 and certainly in Zones 3 and 4. Keyhole beds are a Zone 1 technique; attempting to apply their level of edge complexity at farm scale would be impractical.

5. **Accessibility:** The compact access design makes keyhole beds particularly suitable for gardeners with mobility limitations, as reaching distance is minimised.

## Construction Notes

- Typical diameter: 1.5-3 metres for comfortable reach from the access notch
- Can be built at ground level or as raised beds (30-60 cm height common)
- Materials: stone, brick, timber, recycled materials, or earth banks
- Central compost basket (optional) for ongoing fertility and moisture
