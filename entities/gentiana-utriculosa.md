## Overview

Gentiana utriculosa, commonly known as the bladder gentian or swelled gentian, is a small annual gentian native to the mountains of central and southern Europe.

It is distinguished by its inflated, urn-shaped calyx tube that encloses the blue-violet corolla. This distinctive feature makes it one of the most easily recognized European gentians.

The species grows as a diminutive annual, rarely exceeding 10 cm in height. It inhabits alpine meadows, pasturelands, and damp scree slopes at elevations between 1,200 and 2,800 m.

Unlike many gentians that are perennial, G. utriculosa completes its entire [[agaricus-campestris-biology-and-life-cycle]] within a single [[extending-growing-season]]. It germinates in spring after snowmelt, flowers in July and August, and sets seed before the first frosts.

The species is of interest to botanists for its unusual calyx morphology and to alpine gardeners seeking diminutive, late-summer flowering subjects.

Gentiana utriculosa is sometimes confused with the similarly small [[gentiana-nivalis]], but the inflated calyx of G. utriculosa is diagnostic and immediately distinguishes the two species.

Gentiana utriculosa was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum. The specific epithet "utriculosa" means "bladder-like," referring to the inflated calyx tube.

The species belongs to Gentiana section Calathiana, a group of small annual gentians distributed across European mountains. Related species include Gentiana nivalis and Gentiana campestris.

No subspecies are currently recognized. The species shows moderate morphological [[psilocybin-psilocin-variation-across-flushes]] its range, particularly in flower size and stem branching.

## Morphology

Gentiana utriculosa is a glabrous annual herb with erect or ascending stems 3–10 cm tall.

The stems are simple or sparingly branched from the base, often tinged purple at the lower nodes. Root system is a slender taproot with few lateral branches.

Leaves are opposite, ovate to lanceolate, 5–15 mm long, and sessile with a somewhat clasping base. Upper leaves are broader than lower leaves and form a loose rosette beneath the flowers.

The calyx is the most distinctive feature: a prominently inflated, bladder-like tube 12–20 mm long with five narrow, spreading lobes 4–8 mm long. The inflated tube is pale green to purplish-green with darker veins.

The corolla is funnel-shaped to campanulate, 15–25 mm long, deep blue-violet, with five lobes bearing fringed scales between them. The tube is partially enclosed within the inflated calyx.

Flowers are terminal, usually solitary, though occasionally paired. Blooming occurs from July through August.

The fruit is an ovoid capsule 8–12 mm long, enclosed within the persistent calyx. Seeds are numerous, minute (0.5–0.8 mm), and light brown with a reticulate seed coat.

## Distribution and Habitat

Gentiana utriculosa occurs in the mountains of central and southern Europe. Its range extends from the Pyrenees through the Alps to the Carpathians and the mountains of the Balkan Peninsula.

Major population centers include the Swiss Alps, the Dolomites in Italy, the Austrian Tyrol, and the High Tatra mountains of Slovakia and Poland. Isolated populations occur in the Jura Mountains and the Vosges.

The species grows on damp, calcareous to mildly acidic substrates in alpine meadows, pastures, and scree slopes. It prefers open, sunny sites with reliable moisture from snowmelt or groundwater seepage.

Elevational range is 1,200–2,800 m, with most populations concentrated between 1,800 and 2,400 m. The species is absent from the highest nival zone and from lowland habitats.

It frequently co-occurs with other alpine gentians including [[gentiana-verna]] and Gentiana campestris, as well as with Sesleria caerulea, Carex sempervirens, and [[phyteuma-orbiculare]].

The species shows a preference for north-facing slopes where snow lingers longest, benefiting from delayed snowmelt that extends soil moisture availability into mid-summer.

## Ecology

Gentiana utriculosa is adapted to the short alpine growing season through its annual life cycle. Seeds germinate rapidly after snowmelt, and plants complete development in 8–12 weeks.

Pollination is primarily by bumblebees (Bombus spp.) and hoverflies (Syrphidae). The inflated calyx may serve to protect the developing flower from cold nighttime temperatures.

The species is self-compatible but produces more seed when cross-pollinated. Seed set is generally high, with individual capsules containing 50–100 seeds.

Dispersal occurs passively through wind and water, with the lightweight seeds capable of traveling moderate distances across open alpine terrain. Seed banking in the soil is likely but poorly studied.
