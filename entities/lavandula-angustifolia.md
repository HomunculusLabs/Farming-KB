# Lavandula angustifolia

**Lavandula angustifolia** Mill., commonly known as English lavender, common lavender, or true lavender, is an evergreen aromatic shrub in the mint family (Lamiaceae). It is one of the most widely cultivated and economically important lavender species, prized for its fragrance, essential oil, and ornamental value.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes, Angiosperms, Eudicots, Asterids
- **Order:** Lamiales
- **Family:** Lamiaceae
- **Subfamily:** Nepetoideae
- **Tribe:** Ocimeae
- **Genus:** *Lavandula*
- **Species:** *L. angustifolia* Mill.

The species was first formally described by Philip Miller in 1768. The epithet *angustifolia* derives from the Latin *angustus* (narrow) and *folium* (leaf), referring to the plant's characteristic narrow foliage.

## Morphology

*Lavandula angustifolia* is a compact, woody-based perennial subshrub typically reaching 30–60 cm in height and spreading 40–60 cm wide. Key morphological features include:

- **Stems:** Square (characteristic of Lamiaceae), woody at the base, green and herbaceous toward the tips.
- **Leaves:** Opposite, narrowly lanceolate, 2–6 cm long and 4–6 mm wide, silvery-grey-green due to a dense covering of stellate trichomes.Margins are entire and often revolute (rolled under).
- **Flowers:** Arranged in terminal spikes 2–8 cm long, each spike bearing 6–10 whorls of flowers (verticillasters). Individual flowers are bilabiate, approximately 8–12 mm long, with a tubular calyx and a corolla ranging from pale lilac to deep violet. Bracts are ovate and often similarly coloured.
- **Roots:** Fibrous and relatively shallow, extending laterally more than deeply.
- **Fruit:** A schizocarp of four nutlets, though seed set is often low under cultivation.

## Native Range

*L. angustifolia* is native to the Mediterranean region, specifically the western Mediterranean basin encompassing northern Spain, southern France, Italy, and the western Balkans. It is found naturally on dry, rocky limestone slopes, garrigue, and open scrubland at elevations of 300–1,800 m. The species has been widely naturalised beyond its native range across temperate regions worldwide.

## Cultivation Requirements

| Requirement | Details |
|---|---|
| **Hardiness** | USDA Zones 5–9 |
| **Sunlight** | Full sun (6+ hours direct); tolerates partial shade but becomes leggy |
| **Soil** | Well-drained, sandy or gravelly loam; pH 6.5–8.0 (alkaline preferred) |
| **Water** | Low to moderate; highly drought-tolerant once established; avoid waterlogging |
| **Spacing** | 30–45 cm apart for hedges; 60–90 cm for individual specimens |
| **Fertiliser** | Minimal; excessive nitrogen promotes soft, disease-prone growth |
| **Pruning** | Hard prune after flowering (remove one-third of growth) to maintain compact form |

Root rot caused by *Phytophthora* spp. is the most common cultivation problem, almost always attributable to poor drainage or overwatering.

## Major Cultivars

- **'Hidcote'** — Dense, compact mound to 40 cm; deep violet flowers; dark grey-green foliage; one of the most popular garden cultivars; introduced in the 1950s from Hidcote Manor, England.
- **'Munstead'** — Slightly taller and more open than 'Hidcote', to 45 cm; lighter violet-blue flowers; named after Gertrude Jekyll's garden at Munstead Wood; excellent heat tolerance.
- **'Phenomenal'** — Vigorous hybrid selection noted for exceptional winter hardiness (reliable in Zone 4); uniform habit; silvery foliage with medium violet flowers; developed for commercial production.
- **'Royal Velvet'** — Intense deep purple flowers on short stems; excellent for fresh and dried arrangements; compact to 35 cm.
- **'Melissa Lilac'** — Unusual pale pink flowers; delicate fragrance; less vigorous, suitable for containers.

## Essential Oil Composition

Lavender essential oil is steam-distilled from the flowering tops. The chemical profile of *L. angustifolia* oil is distinct from other lavender species, characterised by high levels of esters:

- **Linalool** (25–38%) — Monoterpene alcohol; sedative, anxiolytic, anti-inflammatory.
- **Linalyl acetate** (25–45%) — Ester; contributes the sweet, floral character; primary marker for quality grading.
- **β-Caryophyllene** (1–8%) — Sesquiterpene; anti-inflammatory.
- **Terpinen-4-ol** (2–6%) — Antimicrobial activity.
- **1,8-Cineole** (<2.5% in true *L. angustifolia*) — Low levels distinguish it from lavandin; high cineole indicates adulteration or misidentification.
- **Camphor** (<0.5% in high-grade oil) — Absence of camphor is a key quality indicator; elevated camphor suggests *L. latifolia* parentage or poor distillation practice.

The European Pharmacopoeia and ISO 3515 standards define acceptable ranges for these constituents in genuine *L. angustifolia* essential oil.

## Medicinal and Therapeutic Uses

Lavender has a long history of medicinal use dating to classical antiquity. Modern research supports several pharmacological activities:

- **Anxiety and stress relief:** Clinical trials have demonstrated that oral lavender oil preparation (Silexan) significantly reduces Generalised Anxiety Disorder symptoms, with efficacy comparable to benzodiazepines and SSRIs but with fewer side effects. Aromatherapy inhalation has shown modest reductions in state anxiety in clinical settings.
- **Sleep promotion:** Aromatherapeutic use of lavender oil before bedtime has been associated with improved sleep quality and increased slow-wave sleep in multiple randomised controlled trials.
- **Wound healing:** Topical application exhibits antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria (including *Staphylococcus aureus* and MRSA), and promotes wound contraction and granulation tissue formation in animal models.
- **Pain management:** Lavender oil aromatherapy has shown analgesic effects in post-operative and needle-procedure contexts, potentially mediated by opioid and cholinergic pathways.
- **Anti-inflammatory:** Linalool and linalyl acetate inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) in vitro.

## Culinary Uses

Lavender flowers impart a distinctive sweet, floral, and slightly peppery flavour. Culinary applications include:

- **Herbes de Provence:** Dried lavender flowers are a traditional (though sometimes optional) component of this southern French herb blend.
- **Lavender sugar and honey:** Infused granulated sugar or honey for use in baking, teas, and cocktails.
- **Baking:** Lavender shortbread, scones, cakes, and biscuits; pairs well with lemon, vanilla, and berries.
- **Beverages:** Lavender lemonade, herbal teas, and gin infusions.

Only culinary-grade *L. angustifolia* should be used; lavandin (*L. × intermedia*) contains camphor and tastes harshly medicinal.

## Pollinator Ecology

*L. angustifolia* is an excellent pollinator plant, attracting a diverse array of insect visitors:

- **Bees:** Honeybees (*Apis mellifera*) and numerous native bee species (especially bumblebees, *Bombus* spp.) are primary visitors. Lavender provides abundant nectar and pollen over a long midsummer bloom period. Lavender honey is a prized monofloral variety.
- **Butterflies:** Nectar source for a range of butterfly species.
- **Hoverflies and beneficial wasps:** Attracted to the flowers, contributing to biological pest control in adjacent plantings.

Mass lavender plantings serve as significant foraging resources in agricultural landscapes, particularly during the mid-summer nectar dearth.

## Permaculture Applications

Lavender offers multiple functions in permaculture design:

- **Companion planting:** Deters aphids, whiteflies, and cabbage moths when interplanted with vegetables; masks scent of neighbouring crops from pest insects.
- **Hedgerows and borders:** Low, drought-resistant hedges that define garden zones while providing nectar, fragrance, and harvestable material.
- **Pest deterrence:** The strong aromatic oils repel deer, rabbits, and some insect pests; sachets of dried lavender deter clothes moths in storage.
- **Erosion control:** Established plants stabilise soil on slopes and banks.
- **Ground cover:** Dense, low-growing cultivars suppress weeds effectively when planted at appropriate spacing.
- **Multi-yield polyculture integration:** Produces flowers (culinary/ornamental), essential oil (medicinal/cosmetic), and craft material (wands, sachets) from a single drought-hardy perennial.

## Harvesting and Distillation

- **Harvest timing:** Flowers should be harvested when approximately 50–100% of buds on each spike are open, typically mid-morning after dew has dried but before peak heat. Essential oil yield is highest at full bloom.
- **Method for dried flowers:** Cut stems with 10–15 cm of stem attached; bundle in small bunches (30–50 stems) and hang upside-down in a warm, dry, dark, well-ventilated area. Drying takes 2–4 weeks.
- **Steam distillation:** Fresh or slightly wilted flowering material is loaded into a still; steam passes through the plant matrix, vaporising volatile compounds. The vapour is condensed, and the essential oil separates from the hydrosol (floral water). Typical yield is 1.0–2.5% of fresh plant weight. Lavender hydrosol is also a valued product for cosmetic and therapeutic use.

## Propagation Methods

- **Seed:** Sow in spring, barely covered, at 21°C; germination in 14–28 days. Seed-grown plants are genetically variable; not recommended when cultivar fidelity is required.
- **Softwood cuttings:** Take 5–10 cm tip cuttings in spring or early summer from non-flowering shoots. Root in sandy propagating mix under mist or a humidity dome; rooting occurs in 3–6 weeks. This is the preferred method for commercial production.
- **Semi-hardwood cuttings:** Taken in late summer from current season's growth; slower but reliable.
- **Layering:** Low branches may be pegged to the ground in spring and will root by the following season; produces large, well-rooted plants.

## Related Species

- ***Lavandula × intermedia*** (Lavandin) — A natural hybrid of *L. angustifolia* × *L. latifolia*; more vigorous and higher-yielding in essential oil (3–5%), but with higher camphor content and a sharper fragrance. Cultivars include 'Grosso', 'Abrialii', and 'Super'. Not suitable for culinary use.
- ***Lavandula stoechas*** (Spanish Lavender) — Distinctive pineapple-shaped flower heads topped with elongated bracts; less cold-hardy (Zones 7–10); native to the western Mediterranean; higher camphor content.
- ***Lavandula latifolia*** (Spike Lavender) — Wider leaves, taller habit, higher camphor (8–15%); native to the western Mediterranean; oil used industrially in soaps and disinfectants.
- ***Lavandula dentata*** (French Lavender) — Toothed leaf margins, continuous but lighter flowering; less frost-hardy; popular as an ornamental in mild climates.
