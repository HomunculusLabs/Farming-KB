## Overview

**Physcia aipolia** (Ehrh. ex Humb.) Fürnr. is a foliose lichen in the
family Physciaceae, commonly known as the **hammer lichen** or **hammered
candleflame lichen**. It is widely distributed in temperate regions and is
one of the most frequently encountered corticolous lichens in urban and
suburban environments.

The species is named for its characteristic maculate thallus surface,
which bears scattered white punctate markings resembling hammer
indentations. It is moderately pollution-tolerant and serves as a
bioindicator for intermediate air quality conditions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Physcia aipolia* was originally described as *Lichen aipolius* by
Ehrhart ex Humboldt and transferred to *Physcia* by Fürnrohr in 1839.
The species epithet derives from Greek, referring to the distinctive
surface markings.

The thallus is foliose, forming rosettes 3-8 cm in diameter, loosely
attached to bark by scattered rhizines. Lobes are 1-3 mm wide, radiating
from the centre, with crenulate to slightly lobulate margins.

The upper surface is grey to greyish-white when dry, with a characteristic
pattern of white maculae (punctate spots) visible under hand lens. The
lower surface is black with simple to branched rhizines.

The medulla is white, and the cortex contains atranorin (K+ yellow). The
species is sorediate, with marginal or laminal soralia producing granular
to farinose soredia.

Apothecia are common, 1-3 mm diameter, with a black disc and a thalline
margin that becomes excluded with age. Spores are brown, 2-celled
(PPhyscia-type), 16-24 × 8-12 µm.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Physcia aipolia* has a cosmopolitan temperate distribution, occurring
across Europe, North America, eastern Asia, and parts of the Southern
Hemisphere. In Europe it is one of the most common foliose lichens on
tree bark.

The species is almost exclusively corticolous, growing on the bark of
deciduous and coniferous trees. It is particularly abundant on nutrient-
enriched bark in urban parks, gardens, hedgerows, and along roadsides.

## Overview

**Physcia stellaris** (L.) Nyl. is a foliose lichen in the family Physciaceae, commonly known as the star rosette lichen. It forms small, gray to pale blue-green rosettes on tree bark, fence posts, and occasionally on rock surfaces.

The species is one of the most common and widespread foliose lichens in temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere. Its tolerance of moderate air pollution makes it a frequent colonizer of urban and suburban trees.

*P. stellaris* is characterized by its sorediate upper surface, lack of rhizines on the lower surface, and the presence of atranorin and zeorin in its chemistry. It is an early to intermediate successional species in lichen community development.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Fungi → Phylum Ascomycota → Class Lecanoromycetes → Order Caliciales → Family Physciaceae → Genus *Physcia* → *P. stellaris*.

The basionym is *Lichen stellaris* L., described by Linnaeus in 1753. Nylander transferred it to *Physcia* in 1860. The species has been divided into several chemotypes and morphotypes, though most modern treatments recognize a single, variable species.

The thallus is foliose, forming small rosettes 1–4 cm in diameter. Lobes are 0.5–2 mm wide, radiating from the center, with rounded tips and crenulate margins. The upper surface is gray to pale blue-gray, often pruinose near the lobe tips.

The lower surface is white to pale brown, with sparse or absent rhizines — a key distinguishing feature from related *Physcia* species. Soralia are present along the lobe margins, producing fine, granular soredia for vegetative propagation.

Apothecia are uncommon but when present are 1–3 mm diameter, with a black disc and a thalline margin. Spores are brown, 2-celled (Physcia-type), measuring 16–22 × 7–10 µm. Pycnidia produce bacillar conidia approximately 5 µm long.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Physcia stellaris* has a nearly cosmopolitan distribution in temperate regions, occurring across Europe, North America, temperate Asia, and parts of the Southern Hemisphere. It is particularly abundant in lowland and urban environments.

The species is an epiphyte found on the bark of a wide range of deciduous and coniferous trees, including oak, elm, maple, ash, and fruit trees. It also colonizes wooden fence posts, gravestones, and shaded rock surfaces in nutrient-enriched environments.

*P. stellaris* is notable for its tolerance of moderate air pollution, including elevated nitrogen deposition. It thrives in urban parks, gardens, roadside trees, and agricultural hedgerows where more pollution-sensitive lichens cannot survive.

The species prefers well-lit to moderately shaded situations with moderate moisture. It is less common in dense forests with closed canopies, where it is outcompeted by shade-tolerant foliose and fruticose lichens.

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds

*Physcia stellaris* contains atranorin in the cortex and zeorin in the medulla. The cortex gives a K+ yellow reaction due to atranorin, while the medulla is K–, C–, and P– (negative for all spot tests).

The chemistry is relatively simple compared to many other foliose lichens. Some populations contain trace amounts of physodic acid or other secondary metabolites, but these are not consistent enough for chemotaxonomic purposes.

The species has been investigated for its ability to accumulate heavy metals from the atmosphere, making it a useful biomonitor for urban air quality studies. Lead, zinc, and cadmium concentrations in *P. stellaris* thalli correlate with local deposition levels.

## Ecology and Symbiosis

*Physcia stellaris* is a symbiotic association between an ascomycete fungus and a trebouxioid green algal photobiont. The photobiont is typically assigned to *Trebouxia* species, which provides carbohydrates through photosynthesis.

The species is an early successional lichen, often among the first to colonize young bark or newly exposed substrates. It facilitates the establishment of later-successional lichens by stabilizing the substrate surface and trapping moisture and organic debris.

*P. stellaris* supports a diverse microfaunal community including mites, springtails, and nematodes. Its soredia are dispersed by wind and rain splash, enabling rapid colonization of nearby suitable substrates.

The species responds positively to moderate nitrogen enrichment, which partly explains its abundance in agricultural and suburban landscapes. However, excessive nitrogen deposition can eventually lead to community simplification and decline.

## Similar Species

*Physcia stellaris* is most easily confused with *Physcia aipolia*, which is larger and has white maculae (pseudocyphellae) on the upper surface. *P. aipolia* also has a different chemistry, containing atranorin and zeorin but lacking the marginal soralia that characterize *P. stellaris*.

*Physcia tenella* is smaller, with narrower lobes and a more tightly appressed growth form. It typically grows in more shaded, humid microhabitats and has a more restricted distribution than *P. stellaris*.

*Xanthoria parietina* can appear similar when pale in color, but is distinguished by its bright orange apothecia and its chemistry, which contains parietin. *X. parietina* also prefers more nutrient-enriched, often bird-perched substrates.
