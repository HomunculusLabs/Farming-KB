## Overview

**Arisaema triphyllum** (L.) Schott, commonly known as jack-in-the-pulpit, is a herbaceous perennial plant in the family Araceae. It is native to [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-crystals]], which cause intense burning and irritation if ingested. The plant grows 30–65 cm tall from a rounded corm 2–5 cm in diameter. Leaves are compound, typically with three leaflets (occasionally five), each 8–15 cm long and 3–10 cm wide. The petiole is 15–40 cm long and often mottled with purple.

The spathe is 4–10 cm long, green to purple-brown, often striped or mottled. The spadix is club-shaped and extends beyond the spathe in male plants but remains enclosed in female plants.

Flowering occurs in mid-spring (April to June). Male plants produce a single leaf and a spadix with staminate flowers, while female plants typically produce two leaves and a spadix with pistillate flowers near the base.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Arisaema triphyllum* is widely distributed across eastern [[savory-soil-organic-matter-and-decomposition-dynamics]] dappled shade, typically found in floodplains, ravine bottoms, and north-facing slopes.

Elevation range extends from sea level to approximately 1,500 meters in the southern Appalachians. It is one of the more shade-tolerant spring ephemerals, persisting well after canopy closure.

The species is relatively adaptable and can colonize disturbed woodland edges and secondary [[fungal-calcium-oxalate-and-carbonate-precipitation]] raphides, needle-shaped crystals contained in specialized cells called idioblasts throughout all plant tissues. These crystals mechanically injure mucous membranes upon contact.

Additional compounds reported include trypsin inhibitors, saponins, and flavonoids such as quercetin and kaempferol derivatives. The corms also contain starch as a major storage carbohydrate.

Symptoms of ingestion include immediate burning and swelling of the mouth, tongue, and throat. Drooling, difficulty swallowing, and nausea may follow. Severe cases can cause airway compromise, though fatalities are extremely rare.

Native Americans processed the corms by prolonged drying, roasting, or boiling to destroy the oxalate crystals. The processed corms were used as a starchy food source and ground into flour.

Modern pharmacological interest has focused on the trypsin inhibitors found in the corms, which have shown anti-inflammatory and potential antitumor properties in vitro. However, clinical applications have not been developed due to the plant's inherent toxicity and the availability of safer alternatives.

The calcium oxalate crystals are among the sharpest found in any plant species, measuring 50–200 μm in length. They are bundled within specialized idioblast cells that rupture upon mechanical damage, releasing the raphides in a penetrating spray.
