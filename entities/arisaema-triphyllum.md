## Overview

**Arisaema triphyllum** (L.) Schott, commonly known as jack-in-the-pulpit, is a herbaceous perennial plant in the family Araceae. It is native to eastern North America, ranging from Nova Scotia west to Minnesota and south to Florida and Texas.

The species is notable for its distinctive inflorescence, which consists of a hooded spathe (the "pulpit") enclosing a finger-like spadix (the "jack"). This structure makes it one of the most recognizable spring wildflowers of eastern deciduous forests.

The specific epithet *triphyllum* means "three-leaved," referring to the characteristic trifoliate leaf arrangement. In older literature the plant is sometimes called Indian turnip, a reference to the edible (after processing) underground corm.

All parts of the plant contain calcium oxalate crystals, which cause intense burning and irritation if ingested. Despite its toxicity, Native American peoples developed methods to process the corms for food and medicine.

The plant is monoecious but functionally dioecious, with individual corms producing either male or female flowers in a given season. This unusual reproductive strategy is tied to corm size and resource availability.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Angiosperms → Monocots → Alismatales → Araceae → Arisaema → A. triphyllum.

The basionym is *Arum triphyllum* L., published by Linnaeus in 1753. Heinrich Wilhelm Schott transferred it to *Arisaema* in 1832.

Several infraspecific taxa have been recognized, including *A. triphyllum* subsp. *stewardsonii* (Bristol) M.C. Hotton and subsp. *pilosum* (Schott) M.C. Hotton. Some authorities treat these as distinct species, reflecting the considerable morphological variation across the species' range.

The plant grows 30–65 cm tall from a rounded corm 2–5 cm in diameter. Leaves are compound, typically with three leaflets (occasionally five), each 8–15 cm long and 3–10 cm wide. The petiole is 15–40 cm long and often mottled with purple.

The spathe is 4–10 cm long, green to purple-brown, often striped or mottled. The spadix is club-shaped and extends beyond the spathe in male plants but remains enclosed in female plants.

Flowering occurs in mid-spring (April to June). Male plants produce a single leaf and a spadix with staminate flowers, while female plants typically produce two leaves and a spadix with pistillate flowers near the base.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Arisaema triphyllum* is widely distributed across eastern North America. It occurs from Nova Scotia and southern Ontario south through the Appalachian Mountains to northern Florida and eastern Texas.

The species is most abundant in rich, moist deciduous forests. It favors loamy soils with high organic matter and dappled shade, typically found in floodplains, ravine bottoms, and north-facing slopes.

Elevation range extends from sea level to approximately 1,500 meters in the southern Appalachians. It is one of the more shade-tolerant spring ephemerals, persisting well after canopy closure.

The species is relatively adaptable and can colonize disturbed woodland edges and secondary growth forests. However, it is sensitive to prolonged drought and does not tolerate waterlogged soils or full sun exposure.

Population densities in optimal habitat can reach 50–100 plants per hectare. The species is considered secure across most of its range, though local populations may decline with habitat fragmentation and increased deer browsing pressure.

## Chemistry and Toxicity

The primary toxic principle is calcium oxalate raphides, needle-shaped crystals contained in specialized cells called idioblasts throughout all plant tissues. These crystals mechanically injure mucous membranes upon contact.

Additional compounds reported include trypsin inhibitors, saponins, and flavonoids such as quercetin and kaempferol derivatives. The corms also contain starch as a major storage carbohydrate.

Symptoms of ingestion include immediate burning and swelling of the mouth, tongue, and throat. Drooling, difficulty swallowing, and nausea may follow. Severe cases can cause airway compromise, though fatalities are extremely rare.

Native Americans processed the corms by prolonged drying, roasting, or boiling to destroy the oxalate crystals. The processed corms were used as a starchy food source and ground into flour.

Modern pharmacological interest has focused on the trypsin inhibitors found in the corms, which have shown anti-inflammatory and potential antitumor properties in vitro. However, clinical applications have not been developed due to the plant's inherent toxicity and the availability of safer alternatives.

The calcium oxalate crystals are among the sharpest found in any plant species, measuring 50–200 μm in length. They are bundled within specialized idioblast cells that rupture upon mechanical damage, releasing the raphides in a penetrating spray.

## Ecology and Cultivation

Jack-in-the-pulpit flowers in April through June, with a remarkable sex-changing life history. Small corms produce male flowers, while larger corms produce female flowers. Environmental conditions can cause sex reversal between years.

Pollination is primarily by fungus gnats (Mycetophilidae), which are attracted to the spathe's fungal odor and become trapped temporarily. Pollen is deposited on the insects as they escape through a narrow opening at the base.

The fruit is a cluster of bright red berries, each containing 1–5 seeds. These ripen in late summer to early fall and are dispersed by birds and small mammals, which are unaffected by the calcium oxalate.

The species serves as a minor food source for woodland wildlife. White-tailed deer occasionally browse the foliage, though it is not a preferred forage species.

Jack-in-the-pulpit is widely grown in shade gardens for its unusual flowers and attractive red fruit display. It prefers partial to full shade and consistently moist, humus-rich soil with a pH of 5.0–6.5.

Plants are hardy in USDA zones 4–9 and require minimal maintenance once established. Corms should be planted 5–8 cm deep in autumn. Division of offsets can be done in late summer when the plant is dormant.

Seeds require a warm stratification period followed by cold stratification for germination. Under natural conditions, seedlings may take 3–5 years to reach flowering size from seed. Vegetative propagation from corm offsets is faster and more reliable.

The species is generally pest-free but can be affected by slugs in damp conditions. Root rot may occur in poorly drained soils. It is not invasive and spreads slowly by corm offsets and seed.

## Ethnobotanical Uses

Native American tribes across the species' range used *Arisaema triphyllum* extensively. The Menominee, Potawatomi, and Ojibwa used the dried corm as a topical treatment for sore eyes and skin conditions.

The Iroquois prepared poultices from the corm for treating snake bites, swellings, and rheumatic pain. The Meskwaki used the root as a diaphoretic and expectorant in respiratory ailments.

In 19th-century eclectic medicine, a tincture of the fresh root was used as a treatment for chronic bronchitis, asthma, and rheumatism. However, the plant fell out of medicinal use due to the difficulty of safe dosing.

Some tribes used the powdered corm as a contraceptive or abortifacient. The Cherokee reportedly used it to treat ringworm and other fungal skin infections.

The specific name *triphyllum* refers to the three-part leaf, a diagnostic feature that distinguishes it from related Arisaema species with different leaf divisions.

The Penobscot used a decoction of the root to treat colds and coughs. The Abenaki applied the dried, powdered root to wounds as a styptic agent to stop bleeding.
