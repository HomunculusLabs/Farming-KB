## Overview

Acer pensylvanicum, known as striped maple or moosewood, is a small deciduous understory tree in the family Sapindaceae native to [[biodiversity-fungi-temperate-boreal-forests]].

Striped maple is one of the most shade-tolerant maples and is an important browse species for moose, white-tailed deer, and snowshoe hares.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Acer pensylvanicum was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The species epithet refers to the Pennsylvania region where it was first documented.

Striped maple is closely related to Acer spicatum (mountain maple), with which it shares habitat in the [[north-carolina-mycodiversity]].

It occurs primarily in cool, moist, forested areas at elevations from 300 to 1,400 meters in the southern Appalachians.

The species is most abundant in the understory of northern hardwood forests dominated by sugar maple, American beech, and yellow birch.

It is a common component of subalpine and boreal forests, often found on north-facing slopes and in ravines.

Striped maple prefers well-drained, acidic loamy soils with high organic matter content.

It is hardy in USDA zones 3 through 7 and is one of the more cold-tolerant maple species.

In the northern portion of its range, striped maple extends into the transition zone between temperate hardwood forests and boreal spruce-fir forests. It is notably absent from areas with calcareous bedrock.

## Ecology

The twigs and buds of striped maple are a highly preferred winter browse for moose, which consume them extensively in northern forests.

White-tailed deer and snowshoe hares also browse the twigs, though the species can become scarce in areas with high deer populations.

Ruffed grouse feed on the buds and catkins during winter months.

Striped maple serves as a host plant for the rosy maple moth (Dryocampa rubicunda) and several other Lepidoptera species.

The large leaves create significant shade at the forest floor level, influencing understory [[leake-mycorrhizal-carbon-sequestration-plant-community-composition]].

The species responds vigorously to canopy openings, with growth rates increasing dramatically after disturbance such as logging or windthrow.

It typically functions as a seral species that dominates understories for 20 to 40 years before being overtopped by taller canopy trees.

Striped maple is considered an indicator species for old-growth northern hardwood forests in some ecological classifications. Its presence signals limited recent disturbance.

## Cultivation and Uses

Striped maple is planted as an ornamental tree for its distinctive striped bark and large, attractive leaves.

It performs best in partial to full shade and requires consistently moist, well-drained soils. Full sun exposure can cause leaf scorch.

The species is relatively slow-growing in deep shade but can produce 30 to 60 centimeters of new growth per year in openings.

Fall color is generally pale yellow, though it is less reliable and less spectacular than that of sugar maple or red maple.
