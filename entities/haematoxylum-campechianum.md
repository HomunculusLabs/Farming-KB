## Overview

**Haematoxylum campechianum**, commonly known as logwood, campeachy wood, or palo de tinta, is a thorny leguminous tree native to [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-caribbean-central-america]] during the 16th–18th centuries.
The tree continues to be harvested for hematoxylin, which remains important in histology and microscopy as a biological stain.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Haematoxylum campechianum** L. belongs to the family Fabaceae, subfamily Caesalpinioideae, tribe Swartzieae.
It was described by Linnaeus in 1753 in *Species Plantarum*.
The genus name derives from Greek *haima* (blood) and *xylon* (wood), referring to the blood-red heartwood.
Trees typically reach 6–12 m in height, with a short, crooked trunk 30–50 cm in diameter.
In open conditions, the tree develops a spreading, irregular crown.
The bark is dark gray to brown, rough and fissured, with prominent spines on younger branches.
Leaves are bipinnate, 8–15 cm long, with 3–5 pairs of pinnae each bearing 4–8 pairs of small, oblong leaflets.
Leaflets are 8–15 mm long, 4–7 mm wide, dark green above and lighter below.
The inflorescence is a terminal or axillary raceme bearing numerous small, fragrant, yellowish flowers.
Flowers are papilionoid, approximately 1 cm long, with five petals and ten stamens.
The fruit is a flat, oblong pod 4–8 cm long and 1.5–2 cm wide, containing 2–4 brown, oval seeds.

Pods are green when young, turning brown at maturity, and may persist on the tree for several months.

## Distribution and Habitat

Logwood is native to the coastal regions of southern Mexico (particularly the Yucatán Peninsula, especially the area around Campeche), Belize, Guatemala, and Honduras.

It has been widely introduced throughout the tropics and is naturalized in many Caribbean islands, West Africa, and parts of [[fungal-biodiversity-tropical-forests]], coastal thickets, mangrove margins, and seasonally flooded areas.

It tolerates a range of soil types including saline, calcareous, and poorly drained soils.

Elevation range is from sea level to approximately 400 m.

Annual rainfall requirements are 750–2000 mm, with the tree exhibiting notable [[staycare-fungal-treatment-of-textile-industry-wastewater]].

Conflicts between Spanish, British, and French interests over logwood harvesting rights [[neurotropic-fungi-in-central-america]] were frequent from the 17th to 19th centuries.

British logwood cutters established settlements in Belize (then British Honduras) that eventually became the colony of British Honduras.

At its peak in the late 18th century, over 10,000 tons of logwood were exported annually from [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]].

The development of synthetic aniline dyes in the mid-19th century caused a sharp decline in logwood trade.

However, the unique properties of hematoxylin ensured continued demand for specialized applications.

## Dye Chemistry

The principal dye compound is hematoxylin (C₁₆H₁₄O₆), a colorless precursor stored in the heartwood.

Upon oxidation, hematoxylin converts to hematein (C₁₆H₁₂O₆), the actual pigment molecule.

This oxidation occurs naturally through aging or is accelerated by exposure to air and light.

Hematoxylin is extracted by boiling chopped heartwood in water, yielding a deep red solution.

Different mordants produce a remarkable range of colors: alum yields purples and mauves, iron produces deep blacks, chrome gives blues, and tin produces bright reds.

The combination of hematoxylin with iron mordants was the standard method for producing true black in European textile dyeing.
