## Overview

**Drosera intermedia**, commonly known as the oblong-leaved sundew or spoonleaf sundew, is a perennial carnivorous plant in the family Droseraceae. It is one of the most widely distributed sundew species, with a circumboreal range spanning [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], Europe, and South America.

The species is distinguished by its spoon-shaped leaves arranged in a flat rosette, with glandular tentacles that produce sticky mucilage for trapping small insects. It occupies boggy, nutrient-poor wetlands across its range and is a common component of temperate and subtropical carnivorous plant communities.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Drosera intermedia** was first formally described by August Heinrich Rudolf Grisebach in 1824. The specific epithet *intermedia* reflects its intermediate morphological position between other Drosera species with narrower and broader leaf forms.

The species is placed within the subgenus *Drosera*, section *Drosera*. Molecular phylogenetic analyses confirm its close relationship with **D. anglica** and **D. rotundifolia**, with which it frequently hybridizes in the wild where ranges overlap.

The plant forms a prostrate rosette of 4–12 leaves radiating from a central crown. Leaf laminae are spatulate (spoon-shaped), 10–30 mm long and 3–8 mm wide, narrowing gradually to a petiole that may be up to twice the length of the lamina.

The upper leaf surface is covered with two types of glandular trichomes. Stalked tentacles at the leaf margins are 3–8 mm long and produce sticky mucilage that glistens in sunlight. Shorter sessile glands across the leaf surface secrete digestive enzymes when triggered by prey contact.

The inflorescence scape is 5–25 cm tall, unbranched, and bears 3–15 white or pinkish flowers. Each flower has five petals, five stamens, and a three-lobed ovary. Flowering occurs from June through September in temperate populations.

Roots are relatively undeveloped, consisting of a few thin anchoring roots. The plant relies primarily on its carnivorous habit and photosynthesis for nutrition, with roots serving mainly for anchorage in the soft substrate.

## Distribution and Habitat

**D. intermedia** has one of the widest natural ranges of any sundew species. In North America, it occurs from eastern Canada (Newfoundland to Ontario) southward through the eastern United States to Florida and along the Gulf Coast into Texas.

European populations are distributed across western and central Europe, including the British Isles, Scandinavia, France, Germany, and Poland. The species extends into western Russia and the Baltic states. Disjunct populations also occur in South America, including Venezuela, Guyana, and Brazil.

The species inhabits shallow, nutrient-poor wetlands including bogs, fens, marsh edges, pond margins, and wet sandy shores. It often grows in standing water or on saturated substrates, with the rosette partially submerged during periods of high water.

It is frequently found growing alongside other carnivorous plants including **Sarracenia purpurea**, **Utricularia vulgaris**, and other Drosera species. These plant communities develop in habitats where low soil nitrogen favors the evolution of carnivorous strategies.

## Carnivory and Prey Capture

**D. intermedia** captures prey through the classic sundew flypaper mechanism. When an insect contacts the sticky mucilage on the tentacles, it becomes ensnared. Adjacent tentacles bend toward the prey through a process called thigmonasty, a rapid plant movement driven by changes in cell turgor pressure.

The tentacle bending response occurs within minutes of initial contact, progressively bringing additional glands into contact with the prey. This increases the area of digestive enzyme secretion and prevents the insect from escaping.

Digestive enzymes including proteases, phosphatases, and nucleases are secreted by the sessile glands. These enzymes dissolve the insect's soft tissues over a period of hours to days. The resulting [[cannabis-nutrient-solution-preparation]] is absorbed through the leaf surface via specialized transport cells.

Common prey items include small flying insects such as gnats, midges, and mosquitoes, as well as ants and small beetles. The species is particularly effective at capturing winged insects that land on the tentacle-covered leaves.

After digestion is complete, the leaf and tentacles unfurl and the tentacle glands resume mucilage production. Each leaf can capture multiple prey items over its lifespan, which typically spans 2–4 months before senescence.

## Cultivation

**D. intermedia** is a reliable and rewarding species for carnivorous plant growers. Its compact size and attractive dew-covered rosettes make it popular for bog garden and terrarium cultivation.

The standard carnivorous plant mix of equal parts peat moss and perlite or silica sand provides an ideal growing medium. Plants should be kept consistently moist using the tray method, with the pot sitting in 1–3 cm of water at all times during the growing season.

Outdoor cultivation in bog gardens works well in temperate climates. The species is fully winter-hardy in USDA zones 5–9, dying back to a hibernaculum (dormant bud) during cold months and resuming growth in spring. In colder zones, mulching or [[coleman-unheated-greenhouse-design]] protection may be needed.

Indoor growers should provide bright light, ideally 12–14 hours under fluorescent or LED grow lights. A sunny windowsill can work if humidity is adequate, but the sticky mucilage may dry out in centrally heated rooms. Terrarium culture with supplemental lighting often produces the best results.

The species can be propagated from seed, leaf cuttings, or natural division. Seeds require cold stratification (4–6 weeks at 2–5°C) for reliable germination. Leaf cuttings placed on moist peat produce plantlets within 4–8 weeks.

## Hybridization and Variation

**D. intermedia** freely hybridizes with several other Drosera species where their ranges overlap. The natural hybrid with **D. rotundifolia** is known as **D. × anglica** (though this is now generally treated as an allopolyploid species in its own right). The hybrid with **D. filiformis** is known as **D. × hybrida**.

The species exhibits considerable morphological variation across its geographic range. Tropical populations in South America tend to be larger and more robust than temperate North American and European forms. Some authorities have recognized these as distinct varieties or subspecies, though current consensus treats them as a single polymorphic species.

Leaf shape varies from narrow and elongated in shaded habitats to broad and compact in full sun. Coloration ranges from green with red tentacles to entirely deep red, depending on light intensity, temperature, and genetic factors.

North American populations from the southeastern coastal plain produce larger rosettes with longer petioles than northern inland populations. These differences appear to be primarily environmentally induced rather than genetically fixed.

## Ecological Role

Within its wetland habitats, **D. intermedia** contributes to [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] by capturing and digesting insect prey. In nutrient-poor bog ecosystems, this supplemental nitrogen source can significantly enhance the plant's growth and reproductive output compared to non-carnivorous competitors.

The species provides microhabitat structure for small invertebrates. Some species of [[comparison-predatory-mites-vs-ladybugs-biocontrol]] and small spiders use the tentacle-covered leaves as hunting platforms, feeding on insects attracted to the mucilage without becoming trapped themselves.

The nectar produced by the tentacle glands attracts a variety of pollinators when the plant is in flower, including small bees, flies, and wasps. The spatial separation between flowers (borne on tall scapes) and traps (at ground level) reduces the risk of pollinators becoming prey.

**D. intermedia** serves as an indicator species for the health of bog and fen ecosystems. Its presence typically indicates high water quality, low [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]], and intact hydrological conditions. Decline or disappearance of local populations often signals habitat degradation.

## Conservation

**D. intermedia** is globally secure due to its wide distribution. However, local populations face threats from habitat destruction, particularly the drainage of wetlands for agriculture and urban development. Peat extraction, a major industry in parts of Europe, has destroyed significant areas of suitable habitat.

In the United States, several state-level conservation programs list the species as threatened or of special concern, particularly in states at the southern edge of its range where wetland loss has been most severe.

In Europe, the species is protected under the Convention on the Conservation of European Wildlife and Natural Habitats (Bern Convention) and is listed in several national red lists. Conservation efforts focus on preserving and restoring bog habitats through rewetting and protection from development.

Collection from the wild is generally unnecessary, as the species is readily available from commercial carnivorous plant nurseries. Ethical growers should always purchase nursery-propagated material rather than wild-collected plants.
