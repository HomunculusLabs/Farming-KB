## Overview

**Xerula furfuracea** (Peck) Redhead, Ginns & Shoemaker, commonly known as the root-trouting mushroom or shaggy parasol root, is a saprotrophic basidiomycete fungus in the family Physalacriaceae.

It is characterized by its deeply rooted, long stipe that extends into the substrate and a pale grey-brown, radially wrinkled cap.

The species is primarily associated with decaying wood and roots of hardwood trees in temperate and subtropical forests.

Unlike many agarics, Xerula species fruit directly from buried wood or root systems, often appearing to emerge from bare soil.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Physalacriaceae
- **Genus:** Xerula
- **Species:** X. furfuracea

The species was originally described as *Agaricus furfuraceus* by Peck in 1873.

Redhead, Ginns, and Shoemaker transferred it to *Xerula* in 1987 based on microscopic features and substrate preference.

Synonyms include *Collybia furfuracea* (Peck) Sacc. and *Rhodophyllus furfuraceus* (Peck) Quél.

### Morphology

**Cap:** 3–10 cm diameter, convex to plane with age, pale grey-brown to tan, surface distinctly radially wrinkled (rugulose), often with a small umbo.

The cap margin is incurved in young specimens and becomes uplifted with maturity, occasionally splitting.

**Gills:** Adnexed to subfree, crowded, white to cream, with lamellulae of varying lengths interspersed between full gills.

**Stipe:** 8–20 cm long, 3–8 mm diameter, extremely elongated and rooting deeply into the substrate.

The stipe is tough, cartilaginous, pale grey-brown above and darker below, with a mealy or pruinose coating near the apex.

**Flesh:** Thin, white to pale buff, with no distinctive odor and a mild taste.

**Spores:** 8.5–12 × 5.5–7.5 µm, ellipsoid, smooth, hyaline, non-amyloid, deposit white.

## Distribution and Habitat

Xerula furfuracea has a wide distribution across [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], Europe, and parts of Asia.

In [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], it occurs from eastern Canada to the southeastern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], with scattered reports from the Pacific Northwest.

The species fruits from late spring through autumn, typically appearing after sustained rainfall.

It is almost always found growing from buried roots, stumps, or decaying wood of hardwood trees, particularly oaks (*Quercus* spp.), beech (*Fagus*), and maples (*Acer*).

The long rooting stipe allows it to access nutrients from deeply buried wood that other saprotrophs cannot reach.

It is rarely found in coniferous forests and shows a strong preference for sites with well-decomposed hardwood debris.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

Xerula furfuracea is generally considered edible but is not widely collected for the table.

The cap is reportedly palatable when cooked, though the stipe is excessively tough and fibrous, rendering it inedible.

Some foragers rate the species as mediocre in flavor, lacking the depth of more popular culinary mushrooms.

Caution is advised when collecting any Xerula species, as they can be confused with toxic lookalikes in forest settings.

There are no well-documented cases of poisoning from X. furfuracea specifically, but the genus is insufficiently studied for culinary recommendations.

The species is of minor interest to commercial foragers and is primarily of mycological rather than gastronomic importance.
