The species fruits from late spring through autumn, typically appearing after sustained rainfall.

It is almost always found growing from buried roots, stumps, or decaying wood of hardwood trees, particularly oaks (*Quercus* spp.), beech (*Fagus*), and maples (*Acer*).

The long rooting stipe allows it to access nutrients from deeply buried wood that other saprotrophs cannot reach.

It is rarely found in coniferous forests and shows a strong preference for sites with well-decomposed hardwood debris.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

Xerula furfuracea is generally considered edible but is not widely collected for the table.

The cap is reportedly palatable when cooked, though the stipe is excessively tough and fibrous, rendering it inedible.

Some foragers rate the species as mediocre in flavor, lacking the depth of more popular culinary mushrooms.

Caution is advised when collecting any Xerula species, as they can be confused with toxic lookalikes in forest settings.

There are no well-documented cases of poisoning from X. furfuracea specifically, but the genus is insufficiently studied for culinary recommendations.

The species is of minor interest to commercial foragers and is primarily of mycological rather than gastronomic importance.
