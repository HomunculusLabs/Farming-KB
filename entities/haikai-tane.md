# Haikai Tane

## Description

Haikai Tane is a New Zealand planner and resource ecologist known for his
pioneering work in integrated catchment management, ecographic modelling, and
innovative land tenure systems. He collaborated with [[david-holmgren]] in 1979 and
1984 on concepts connecting land tenure reform with broadacre [[permaculture]]
design, making significant contributions to how permaculture thinking extends
beyond site-scale design to landscape and regional planning.

## Classification

- **Domain**: [[slope-analysis-landscape-planning]], resource ecology, catchment management
- **Nationality**: New Zealand
- **Field**: Ecographic modelling, rural land use planning, land tenure innovation
- **Collaborators**: David Holmgren (1979, 1984)

## Key Facts

- Developed an ecographic model of rivers using patterns from Aboriginal dot
  painting, illustrating biological productivity along the Murray River floodplain
  in Australia
- Collaborated with David Holmgren to explore how strata and body corporate
  land tenure could enable integrated rural land use without subdivision
- Identified the potential for industrial estates to develop industrial ecology
  where outputs of one business become inputs for another
- His ecographic painting showed the location and nature of food resources along
  the Murray River floodplain, demonstrating that natural flooding patterns
  maintained ecosystems more productive of animal protein than the unsustainable
  flood-irrigated dairy farms that replaced them
- Recognised that body corporate title in shopping centres provided economies
  of scale for small businesses through shared marketing, accounting, and
  bargaining services with large chain stores
- His work directly influenced Holmgren's thinking on how modern legal land
  tenure mechanisms could be repurposed for permaculture-scale rural design

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

1. **Integrated Land Tenure**: He demonstrated how modern legal mechanisms
   (strata title, body corporate) could be repurposed to integrate forestry,
   horticulture, aquaculture, and apiculture into broadacre pastoral farms
   without the need for subdivision

2. **Catchment Productivity Modelling**: His ecographic models revealed that
   natural floodplain ecosystems (e.g., the Millewa and Barmah red gum forests)
   were more productive of animal protein than the unsustainable flood-irrigated
   dairy farms that replaced them

3. **Rural Eco-Village Vision**: His collaboration with Holmgren laid the
   groundwork for rural eco-villages using body corporate land tenure as a more
   sustainable economic, ecological, and social model for rural settlement

4. **Industrial Ecology**: He foresaw the potential for waste-sharing and
   resource-cycling between rural enterprises, a concept central to permaculture's
   "[[holmgren-produce-no-waste-waste-hierarchy-refuse-reduce-reuse-repair-recycle]]" principle

5. **Landscape-Scale Design**: Tane's work helped bridge the gap between
   site-scale [[permaculture-design]] and bioregional-scale planning, showing how
   catchment-level thinking can inform property-level design decisions

## Broader Significance

Tane's work represents a bridge between Western scientific [[broadscale-landscape-analysis-and-slope-design]]
and indigenous ecological knowledge. His use of Aboriginal dot painting as a
medium for ecological modelling demonstrated that [[wasted-human-resources-and-traditional-knowledge]] systems
encode sophisticated landscape understanding in visual and narrative forms that
complement Western cartographic and [[analytical-methods-psilocybin-psilocin-quantification]]. This approach resonates
with permaculture's emphasis on learning from indigenous land management
practices and integrating traditional ecological knowledge with modern design
science.

## Legacy

Holmgren credits Tane's influence as instrumental in developing his thinking on
how permaculture design could scale beyond individual properties to encompass
whole landscapes and rural communities. The concepts they explored together in
1979 and 1984 anticipated many of the challenges and opportunities that the
rural resettlement and eco-village movements would encounter in subsequent
decades.
