# Silver Spoon Test

## Description

The silver spoon test (and its variant, the gold ring test) was a popular folk method for detecting [[identifying-poisonous-mushrooms]], widely believed in the 19th century and still occasionally encountered today. The test held that if a silver spoon or gold ring turned black when cooked with mushrooms, the mushrooms were poisonous; if the metal remained bright, the mushrooms were safe to eat.

Palmer and his French authority, Dr. F. S. Cordier, both demonstrated that this test is completely unreliable and potentially dangerous.

## Classification

- **Type:** Debunked folk test
- **Field:** [[mycology]] / folklore
- **Purpose:** Purported detection of poisonous mushrooms
- **Status:** Completely unreliable and dangerous

## Key Facts

- One of the most persistent folk beliefs about mushroom safety, still circulating in Palmer's time and occasionally encountered today
- Based on the observation that decomposing [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] can tarnish silver through the release of sulphuretted hydrogen gas
- Dr. Cordier tested the method with *[[agaricus-bulbosus]]* (a deadly poisonous species) and found it never blackened silver or gold
- Palmer observed that any decomposing mushroom — edible or poisonous — could blacken silver through the release of [[decomposition]] gases
- A Boston physician recommended the test to a friend as recently as Palmer's writing, demonstrating how persistent the myth was among even educated people
- Cordier stated: "This is an error against which one cannot protest too strongly" (*on ne saurait trop protester*)

## Why the Test Fails

The scientific explanation for why the silver spoon test is unreliable:

- Decomposing mushrooms (even perfectly edible ones) release sulphuretted hydrogen and other gases that blacken silver
- Stale, maggot-infested mushrooms — commonly found in markets — are especially likely to produce these gases
- Many deadly poisonous mushrooms, including the *[[amanita]]* species, do not release gases that tarnish silver
- The test confuses decomposition with toxicity — it detects spoilage, not poison
- Palmer specifically noted that he had seen mushrooms in the markets of both Boston and London that would fail the silver test simply because they were old and decomposing

## Other Debunked Tests

Palmer and Cordier also debunked several related folk tests:

- **The onion test (Necker):** White onion cooked with mushrooms supposedly turns bluish or brown if poisonous. Not confirmed by experience.
- **The bulrush pith test:** Pith supposedly turns black near poisonous mushrooms. Also unconfirmed.
- **The snail/insect test:** Fungi not attacked by snails or insects supposedly indicate poison. Cordier demonstrated this is unreliable.

## Palmer's Campaign

Palmer made the debunking of the silver spoon test a recurring theme in his writings. He recounted being asked about the test "daily" and noted that even physicians continued to recommend it. His systematic campaign against this and other folk tests was part of his broader effort to replace superstition with scientific species knowledge as the only reliable path to safe [[mushroom-identification]].

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The persistence of the silver spoon test illustrates the enduring power of folk beliefs about mushroom safety. Modern mycology educators continue to encounter variants of this myth, making Palmer's 19th-century debunking as relevant today as it was when first written.

The lesson remains clear: no single test, rule, or characteristic can reliably distinguish edible from poisonous mushrooms. Only thorough knowledge of individual species provides safety.
