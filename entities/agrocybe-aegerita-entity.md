# Agrocybe aegerita

**Agrocybe aegerita**, commonly known as the **poplar mushroom**, **velvet pioppino**, or **black poplar mushroom**, is an edible basidiomycete fungus in the family Strophariaceae. It is widely cultivated for food and valued for its nutty flavor, firm texture, and medicinal properties.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Strophariaceae
- **Genus:** *Agrocybe*
- **Species:** *A. aegerita*

Originally described under the genus *Agaricus* by Albert Bernhard Frank in 1880, it was later transferred to *Agrocybe*. Several synonyms exist including *Pholiota aegerita*.

## Description

The cap is 3–10 cm in diameter, convex to plane with age, smooth and chestnut-brown when young, fading to pale tan. The surface is dry to slightly viscid in humid conditions. The stipe is 4–10 cm long, 0.5–1.5 cm thick, with a distinct membranous annulus (ring) that is often darkened by spore deposits. Gills are adnate to adnexed, initially white then turning cinnamon-brown as spores mature. The spore print is dark brown to tobacco-brown. Spores are ellipsoid, smooth, 10–14 × 6–8 µm with a germ pore.

## Habitat and Distribution

*A. aegerita* is a saprotrophic wood-decomposing fungus found naturally on dead hardwood trees, especially **poplar** (*Populus* spp.), from which it derives its common name. It also colonizes elm, oak, and willow. It is distributed across temperate regions of Europe, Asia, and North America. In the wild, it fruits from spring through autumn, often appearing in clusters on stumps and fallen trunks.

## Cultivation

The poplar mushroom is one of the most commercially important cultivated fungi, particularly in East Asia (China, Japan, Korea) and increasingly in Europe and North America.

### Substrate

- **Primary substrates:** Supplemented hardwood sawdust (poplar, oak, beech), wheat straw, cotton seed hulls
- **Supplements:** Wheat bran (10–20%), rice bran, soybean meal
- **Pasteurization:** 90–100°C for 8–12 hours or steam sterilization at 121°C for 60–90 minutes

### Growing Conditions

- **Temperature:** Optimal mycelial growth at 25–28°C; fruiting at 15–20°C
- **Humidity:** 85–95% relative humidity during fruiting
- **Light:** Indirect light, 200–500 lux; insufficient light causes elongated stipes
- **Fresh air exchange (FAE):** Moderate to high CO₂ tolerance but requires ventilation for normal cap development
- **pH:** 5.5–7.0 optimal

### Yield and Cycle

Spawn run takes 20–30 days. Primordia form 3–7 days after induction (temperature drop, increased humidity). Harvesting occurs in flushes every 7–10 days for 3–5 flushes. Biological efficiency on supplemented sawdust ranges from 60–120%. Fruiting bodies are best harvested when the cap is still convex and before the veil tears. Overmature specimens develop tough, fibrous stipes and reduced culinary quality. Post-harvest handling should minimize water contact to prevent bacterial soft rot, which is the primary post-harvest pathogen affecting this species.

### Strain Varieties

Commercial cultivation primarily utilizes selected strains with improved characteristics:
- **Italian poplar strain:** The most widely cultivated variety in Europe, characterized by compact clusters and good shelf life
- **Chinese PI菌株 series:** Several numbered strains selected for high yield and disease resistance
- **Hybrid strains:** Recent breeding programs have combined traits from wild collections across Europe and Asia, resulting in strains with shorter cropping cycles and improved biological efficiency

## Nutritional Value

Per 100 g fresh weight:
- Protein: 2.5–3.5 g
- Carbohydrates: 4–6 g
- Fat: 0.3–0.5 g
- Dietary fiber: 1.5–2.5 g
- Rich in B vitamins (B1, B2, niacin), ergosterol (provitamin D₂), and minerals (potassium, phosphorus, selenium)

## Medicinal Properties

- **Antioxidant activity:** Contains phenolic compounds and flavonoids that scavenge free radicals
- **Anti-inflammatory:** Polysaccharides demonstrate COX-2 inhibitory activity
- **Immunomodulatory:** β-glucans stimulate macrophage and NK cell activity
- **Antimicrobial:** Exhibits activity against *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Escherichia coli*
- **Hypocholesterolemic:** Feeding studies show reduction in serum LDL cholesterol
- **Antitumor:** Agrocybin, a bioactive peptide, shows selective cytotoxicity against tumor cell lines

## Bioactive Compounds

- **Agrocybin:** A novel antifungal protein/peptide
- **Lectins:** Hemagglutinating proteins with immunomodulatory properties
- **Ergosterol peroxide:** Anti-inflammatory and cytotoxic triterpenoid
- **Phenolic acids:** Including p-hydroxybenzoic acid and protocatechuic acid

## Culinary Uses

The velvet pioppino is prized in Italian, Chinese, and Japanese cuisines. Its firm texture holds well in stir-fries, soups, risottos, and pasta dishes. It pairs well with garlic, butter, thyme, and white wine. The mushroom should be cooked before consumption. Dried specimens rehydrate well and concentrate in flavor.

## Mycelial Characteristics

The mycelium of *A. aegerita* is robust and cottony-white, with moderate to fast colonization rates. It is relatively resistant to contamination compared to other cultivated species, making it suitable for both commercial and hobbyist cultivation. The mycelium produces a distinctive yellowish metabolic exudate on agar media, particularly on malt extract agar (MEA). Strain selection has led to commercial varieties with improved yield, shorter cropping cycles, and extended shelf life after harvest.

## Storage and Shelf Life

Fresh pioppino mushrooms store well at 2–4°C with 90–95% humidity for 10–14 days. Dried mushrooms retain their flavor and rehydrate well for up to 12 months when stored in airtight containers away from light. Vacuum-packed fresh mushrooms have a shelf life of 3–4 weeks at refrigeration temperatures. The firm texture makes it one of the better candidates for freezing among cultivated mushrooms.

## Economic Importance

In China, *A. aegerita* is among the top ten commercially cultivated mushrooms by production volume. Annual production in China exceeds 200,000 metric tons (fresh weight). European cultivation is expanding, particularly in Italy, Spain, and France, where it commands premium prices in specialty markets. The growing demand for exotic and medicinal mushrooms in Western markets has driven increased production in the United States and Canada as well.
