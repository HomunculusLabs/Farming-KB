## Description

Agaricus fabaceus is a species closely related to the common field mushroom (Agaricus campestris), distinguished primarily by its pronounced almond or amygdaline aroma. This species is mentioned in early mycological literature as one of the cultivated agarics, though it has received far less commercial attention than A. campestris or A. bisporus. The species name "fabaceus" references its association with bean-like aromas, though the predominant descriptor in cultivation literature is the strong almond character of its [[mycelium]] and fruiting bodies.

The species was recognized by early mushroom cultivators as distinct from the common field mushroom primarily through olfactory identification. While A. campestris spawn possesses a pleasant combined aroma of fresh mushrooms and mild almonds, the spawn of A. fabaceus produces a far more intense and unmistakable almond fragrance that serves as the primary diagnostic tool for identification in practice.

In the broader context of mushroom taxonomy, A. fabaceus represents one of several Agaricus species that early cultivators encountered and distinguished through practical observation rather than formal taxonomic study. The fact that multiple Agaricus species were recognized in cultivation underscores the natural diversity available to pioneering growers before the era of systematic breeding and variety selection.

## Key Facts

- Produces a spawn with a far more pronounced almond (amygdaline) odor compared to Agaricus campestris, whose spawn has a more subtle mushroom-almond combination aroma.
- The distinctive almond fragrance of its mycelium was considered a useful diagnostic characteristic for spawn identification in early cultivation practice, especially before modern laboratory methods were available.
- Like other Agaricus species, it grows on composted stable manure substrates and responds to similar environmental conditions for fruiting, including temperature ranges of 48-60F.
- The aromatic compounds responsible for the almond scent are largely lost upon drying, making fresh spawn identification essential for cultivators seeking to verify their spawn type before committing to full-scale production.
- Spawn of this species is visually similar to A. campestris spawn, requiring either aroma testing or sporophore production for confident identification in the field or grow room.
- The species was noted in Duggar's 1915 comprehensive manual on mushroom growing, indicating it had enough presence in cultivation circles to warrant distinction from the more common field mushroom.
- Other fleshy and woody fungi invade a great variety of substrata including rich earth, moist forest floor leaves, fallen timber, and living tree trunks, each species having specific habitat requirements that parallel the substrate specialization seen in Agaricus species.

## Morphology and Development

The mushroom follows the typical Agaricus developmental pattern observed across the genus. Under favorable composting conditions, the mycelium pervades the substratum in all directions, absorbing water and both organic and inorganic food materials required for growth. As nutrients accumulate to a considerable surplus, the organism prepares for reproduction.

When conditions for fruiting become satisfactory, minute cushion-like areas of growth appear on the larger mycelial threads. These become spherical in form, producing the snow-white pin heads that represent the first unmistakable signs of mushroom production. The pin heads grow fairly rapidly into recognizable buttons, and ultimately into mature mushrooms. Under optimal commercial conditions, approximately one week to ten days elapse between pin head formation and full maturity.

Commercial mushrooms are graded highest when picked just before the veil breaks, a principle that applies equally to A. fabaceus as to other cultivated Agaricus species.

## Spawn Characteristics

The fresh, undried spawn of Agaricus campestris has a pleasing aroma combining fresh mushroom scent with a slight powdered almond quality. By contrast, the spawn of A. fabaceus exhibits a far more pronounced almond or amygdaline character. This olfactory distinction was the primary practical method for distinguishing between the two species' spawn in commercial operations.

The odor is largely lost with drying, which means that spawn merchants and cultivators needed to work with fresh material for identification purposes. This limitation contributed to the confusion that historically plagued the spawn trade, where varieties were often mixed or misidentified.

It was emphasized in cultivation literature that true expertise in recognizing different types of spawn came only from direct experience with living material. Once a cultivator had grown mushrooms and carefully examined fresh spawn in beds, there would be no further difficulty in recognizing Agaricus spawn types by their characteristic appearance and aroma.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Spawn identification**: The strong almond aroma serves as a distinguishing feature when identifying different Agaricus spawn types, an important skill in traditional [[mushroom-cultivation]] before modern genetic identification methods became available.
- **Species diversity in cultivation**: The existence of A. fabaceus alongside A. campestris demonstrates the natural diversity within the Agaricus genus that was available to early cultivators, even before systematic variety selection began around 1900.
- **Aromatic compound research**: The amygdaline-type odor suggests the presence of benzaldehyde or related aromatic compounds, which may have implications for flavor development and chemotaxonomy within the genus.
- **Historical context**: Mentioned in Duggar's 1915 mushroom growing manual as a recognized species in cultivation circles, indicating it was considered commercially relevant enough to distinguish from A. campestris at that time.
- **Genetic relationships**: Modern molecular studies of Agaricus species diversity may benefit from knowing which species early cultivators considered distinct, as these observations were based on practical phenotypic differences.
