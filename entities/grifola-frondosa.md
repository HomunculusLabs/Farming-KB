# Grifola frondosa

*Grifola frondosa*, commonly known as maitake (舞茸, "dancing mushroom" in Japanese), is a large, showy polypore
fungus that is simultaneously a prized culinary mushroom and a potent medicinal organism. Native to temperate hardwood
forests of East Asia and eastern North America, maitake has been used in Japanese and Chinese traditional medicine for
centuries and has become one of the most commercially important medicinal mushrooms in global markets, with a growing
body of clinical evidence supporting its immunomodulatory and anti-tumor properties.

## Nomenclature

The genus name *Grifola* is derived from the Latin *grifa*, meaning "griffin," referring to the mythical creature —
an apt metaphor for the mushroom's large, ruffled, cascading form. The species epithet *frondosa* means "leafy" or
"frond-bearing." The Japanese name maitake (舞茸) literally translates as "dancing mushroom." The name is said to
derive from the traditional reaction of mushroom hunters upon finding the prized fungus — they would dance with joy.
An alternative etymology suggests the dancing, swaying appearance of the frond-like fruiting body. In Chinese, it is
called hui shu hua (灰树花, "gray tree flower"). In Italian-American communities in the northeastern United States,
it is sometimes called "hen of the woods," though this name is more commonly applied to the related *Polyporus
umbellatus*.

## Morphology

*G. frondosa* produces large, compound fruiting bodies consisting of multiple overlapping, fan-shaped caps (fronds)
that arise from a single, branching base or "core." A single mature specimen can reach 30–100 cm in diameter and
weigh 5–20 kg or more, making it one of the largest of the bracket fungi. Individual fronds are 2–10 cm wide,
grayish-brown to dark brown on the upper surface with a lighter, whitish pore surface underneath. The pores are small
and angular (1–3 per mm). The flesh is white, firm, and fleshy when young, becoming tough and corky with age. Spores
are broadly elliptical, 5–7 × 3–4 µm, with a white spore print. Young specimens are soft and edible with a
pleasant, earthy flavor and firm, meaty texture. The mushroom is a choice edible and is widely cultivated for both
culinary and medicinal markets.

## Distribution and Habitat

*G. frondosa* is found in temperate hardwood forests of eastern North America (from the Canadian Maritimes to the
Appalachian Mountains), Europe, and East Asia (Japan, China, Korea). It grows at the base of living or dead hardwood
trees, particularly oaks (*Quercus* spp.) but also beech, elm, and maple. The fungus forms a parasitic relationship
with living trees, causing a brown butt rot that can eventually kill the host, but it also fruits from dead stumps and
buried roots. Fruiting occurs from late summer through autumn, typically August through October in temperate regions,
and is favored by cool nights and warm days following significant rainfall. In Japan, wild maitake commands premium
prices, though cultivated material dominates the commercial market.

## Bioactive Compounds

The primary medicinal constituents of *G. frondosa* are beta-glucan polysaccharides, particularly the D-fraction (also
called MD-fraction or Grifolan), a protein-bound beta-1,6-glucan with beta-1,3 side branches. This compound is the most
extensively studied of maitake's bioactive constituents and is the basis of most commercial maitake extracts.
Additional polysaccharides include the SX-fraction, identified for its potential blood sugar-modulating effects.
Maitake also contains various proteins, lipids, ergosterol (vitamin D₂ precursor), and phenolic compounds with
antioxidant activity. The beta-glucan content of maitake fruiting bodies is typically 5–10% by dry weight,
concentrated primarily in the mycelial core and inner flesh.

## Immunomodulatory and Anti-Tumor Activity

Maitake's D-fraction has demonstrated potent immunomodulatory activity across a wide range of in vitro and in vivo
studies. Mechanisms include activation of macrophages, enhancement of NK cell cytotoxicity, potentiation of dendritic
cell maturation and antigen presentation, and modulation of T-cell responses including increased production of helper
T-cells and cytotoxic T-lymphocytes. In animal tumor models, maitake extracts have shown significant anti-tumor effects
against various cancer types including breast, lung, liver, and prostate cancers. The effects are primarily
immune-mediated rather than directly cytotoxic, with the [[beta-glucans]] acting as biological response modifiers
(BRMs) that enhance the host's innate anti-tumor defenses. Clinical studies, primarily from Japan, have reported
improvements in immune function markers, quality of life, and in some cases tumor response rates when maitake extracts
were used as adjuncts to conventional cancer therapy.

## Metabolic Effects

Maitake has demonstrated notable effects on glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity. The SX-fraction has been shown
to activate AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase) and enhance insulin receptor signaling in animal models, leading to
improved glucose uptake and reduced blood glucose levels. Human clinical trials have reported modest but significant
reductions in fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, and insulin resistance in patients with type 2 diabetes supplemented with
maitake extracts. Maitake has also shown lipid-modulating effects, including reductions in total cholesterol, LDL
cholesterol, and triglycerides, attributed to both the beta-glucan content (which can bind bile acids in the gut) and
triterpenoid constituents.

## Cultivation

Maitake cultivation was first achieved commercially in Japan in the mid-1980s and has since expanded globally. The
primary cultivation method uses supplemented sawdust substrates (typically hardwood sawdust with rice bran or wheat
bran additions) inoculated with grain spawn and incubated at 20–25°C. Fruiting requires a temperature drop to
15–18°C, high humidity (90–95%), and elevated CO₂ levels to promote the compact, high-quality morphology
preferred in commercial markets. The cultivation cycle from inoculation to harvest is approximately 60–90 days.
Maitake is also cultivated on oak logs using traditional outdoor methods, though this approach has lower yields and
longer production cycles. The species is considered one of the more challenging medicinal mushrooms to cultivate at
commercial scale, requiring precise environmental control for consistent results.

## Culinary Applications

Beyond its medicinal properties, maitake is one of the most highly regarded edible mushrooms in Japanese cuisine. Its
firm, meaty texture and rich, earthy-savory flavor make it versatile in cooking — it is excellent sautéed, grilled,
roasted, or used in soups and stews. The mushroom holds its texture well under cooking and does not release excessive
water, making it suitable for a wide range of preparations. In Japanese cuisine, maitake is used in nabemono (hot
pots), tempura, and as a grilled side dish. Its culinary value, combined with its medicinal reputation, has made it one
of the most expensive cultivated mushrooms in Japanese markets, with premium grades commanding prices comparable to
high-end matsutake.
