## Overview

Dr. Vigier was a French physician who, along with Dr. Currie in 1868, worked to isolate the toxic alkaloid from [[identifying-poisonous-mushrooms]], specifically using [[amanita-mappa]] (now [[amanita]] citrina, the false death cap). His pharmaceutical preparations from A. mappa represented early attempts to harness mushroom toxins for therapeutic use.

## Key Work

### Isolation of Amanita Alkaloid (1868)

Together with Dr. Currie, Dr. Vigier selected Amanita mappa as their source material, considering it the most poisonous species — a reflection of the [[growing-gourmet-pleurotus-sajor-caju-taxonomic-confusion]] of the era regarding the relative toxicity of different Amanita species. Modern science recognizes A. phalloides and A. virosa as far more deadly than A. citrina/mappa.

### Pharmaceutical Preparations

Dr. Vigier developed two pharmaceutical forms from Amanita mappa:

1. **Alcoholic Extract**: Prepared by macerating one kilogram of fresh poisonous mushrooms (crushed) with one kilogram of 90% alcohol for a fortnight (14 days). The mixture was then pressed and filtered to produce a tincture, administered in doses of 4-6 grammes per day for certain catarrhal affections.

2. **Pills**: Containing 5 centigrammes of extract obtained from distillation of the alcoholic mixture. These were taken in doses of 3-4 pills per day.

Dr. Currie was reported to be preparing publication of the therapeutic results from these preparations.

## Significance to Cultivation and Mycology

Dr. Vigier's work represents an early chapter in the pharmacological investigation of Amanita toxins. His approach — extracting the active principle into pharmaceutical forms for therapeutic application — paralleled contemporaneous work by [[schmiedeberg]] and Koppe (who isolated [[muscarine]] from A. muscaria) and Boudier (who extracted bulbosine from A. bulbosa).

While his [[psilocybin-therapeutic-applications]] (treating catarrhal affections) did not survive into modern medicine, his methodology for alkaloid extraction from fresh mushroom tissue using alcohol maceration was a standard pharmacognostic technique of the period.

## Historical Context of 19th-Century Alkaloid Extraction

Dr. Vigier's work occurred during the golden age of alkaloid chemistry (1850s-1880s), when physicians and pharmacists across Europe were systematically extracting active principles from natural sources. The isolation of morphine (1806), quinine (1820), caffeine (1820), and nicotine (1828) had demonstrated that plant and fungal materials contained powerful, isolable active compounds.

The 14-day alcohol maceration technique used by Vigier was standard pharmacognostic practice, relying on ethanol's ability to dissolve alkaloidal bases and their salts. The subsequent filtration and concentration steps produced tinctures and extracts of reproducible potency — an essential requirement for therapeutic dosing.

## Impact on Later Research

Vigier and Currie's work with Amanita mappa, while based on a mistaken assessment of that species' relative toxicity, nevertheless demonstrated that the toxic principles of Amanita species could be extracted, concentrated, and formulated into pharmaceutical preparations. This proof of concept encouraged later researchers to pursue more refined [[pf-tek-mushroom-extraction-techniques]] using more appropriate source species.

## Legacy

The pharmaceutical forms developed by Vigier (tincture and pills) represent early examples of standardized dosing for fungal-derived medicines. While the specific applications (catarrhal affections) did not persist, the methodology informed later developments in pharmacognosy and natural product drug discovery.

## The Amanita mappa Question

The selection of Amanita mappa (now A. citrina) as the "most poisonous" species warrants examination. A. citrina does contain small amounts of amatoxins, but at concentrations far lower than A. phalloides or A. virosa. However, its distinctive lemon-yellow cap coloration and abundance in European forests made it a conspicuous and easily identified species — practical advantages for bulk pharmaceutical extraction that may have influenced the choice.

Some 19th-century authors also confused A. mappa with other white or pale Amanita species, and the actual material used by Currie and Vigier may have included more toxic species mixed with the identified A. mappa specimens. This taxonomic uncertainty complicates retrospective evaluation of their work.

## Cultural Context

The Currie-Vigier collaboration exemplified the 19th-century French tradition of physician-pharmacist partnership in natural product research. France was a center of alkaloid chemistry during this period, and the French pharmacopoeia was enriched by numerous fungal and plant-derived preparations. The willingness to explore mushroom-derived pharmaceuticals reflected both the abundance of wild fungi in the French countryside and the established role of pharmacognosy in French medical education.

## Relevance to Modern Pharmacognosy

The Currie-Vigier methodology — maceration, extraction, concentration, and pharmaceutical formulation — remains the basic workflow of modern natural product research, though with refined solvents, chromatographic purification steps, [[shirota-psilocybin-crystal-form-spectral-data-and-analytical-characterization]] techniques. Their work with Amanita alkaloids anticipated the systematic investigation of [[biodiversity-fungal-secondary-metabolites]] that would become a major field in 20th-century pharmacology.

The therapeutic exploration of fungal toxins at sub-lethal doses also foreshadows modern approaches to drug development from natural poisons, including the use of botulinum toxin (Botox) and various venom-derived pharmaceuticals.

## Modern Understanding of Amanita citrina Toxicity

Modern chemical analysis has confirmed that Amanita citrina (the current name for A. mappa) does indeed contain amatoxins, but at much lower concentrations than A. phalloides. It also contains psilocybin-like compounds in some populations, contributing to its complex pharmacological profile. The species is generally considered poisonous but rarely fatal, consistent with the relatively modest therapeutic effects reported by Currie and Vigier.

This modern understanding vindicates Currie and Vigier's choice of species — they selected a genuinely toxic mushroom, just not the most dangerous one. Their work would have been even more productive had they used A. phalloides, but the results obtained with A. citrina were pharmacologically valid.
