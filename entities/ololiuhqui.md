## Ololiuhqui

## Description

Ololiuhqui (also spelled ololiuqui) is the Aztec name for the seeds of certain climbing plants of the family Convolvulaceae that were used in pre-Columbian times by the Aztecs and neighboring peoples in religious ceremonies and magical healing practices. The seeds contain lysergic acid amides, including lysergic acid amide (LA-111), lysergic acid hydroxyethylamide, and ergobasine, making them chemically related to LSD. Albert Hofmann succeeded in isolating these active principles in 1959-1960, completing what he called a "magic circle" of discoveries that began with ergot alkaloids and led through LSD to the Mexican magic drugs and back to lysergic acid compounds.

## Classification

- **Type**: Plant / entheogenic drug
- **Botanical source**: Turbina corymbosa (formerly Rivea corymbosa) and Ipomoea violacea
- **Family**: Convolvulaceae (morning glory family)
- **Active constituents**: Lysergic acid amide, lysergic acid hydroxyethylamide, ergobasine
- **Traditional names**: Ololiuhqui (Aztec), badoh (Zapotec), badoh negro (Zapotec, for Ipomoea violacea seeds), coatl xoxouhqui ("green snake"), la semilla de la Virgen ("seeds of the Virgin", Huautla)

## Key Facts

- Used in pre-Columbian Mexico in religious ceremonies and magical healing, as documented by Spanish chroniclers including Bernardino de Sahagun and Francisco Hernandez
- Richard Evans Schultes published a comprehensive monograph in 1941: "A Contribution to Our Knowledge of Rivea corymbosa, the Narcotic Ololiuqui of the Aztecs"
- Still used today by Zapotec, Chinantec, Mazatec, and Mixtec Indians in the remote mountains of southern Mexico
- Albert Hofmann isolated the active principles in 1959 with his laboratory assistant Hans Tscherter, discovering they were lysergic acid derivatives closely related to LSD
- The discovery that ergot alkaloids occurred in higher plants (Convolvulaceae) was initially met with skepticism, as these compounds were previously known only from lower fungi (ergot)
- Hofmann presented the findings at the IUPAC Natural Products Congress in Sydney, Australia in 1960
- The psychic effects differ from LSD: euphoric and hallucinogenic components are less pronounced, with a sensation of mental emptiness, anxiety, and depression predominating
- Lysergic acid amide (LA-111) requires a 10-20x greater dose than LSD to produce effects
- H. Osmond conducted the first modern self-experiment with Turbina corymbosa seeds in 1955, experiencing apathy, enhanced visual sensitivity, and subsequent well-being
- V.J. Kinross-Wright found no effects with up to 125 seeds in 1958
- The seeds were obtained through R. Gordon Wasson from Huautla and San Bartolo Yautepec
- Two species were identified: round light-brown seeds from Huautla (Turbina corymbosa) and black angular seeds from San Bartolo Yautepec (Ipomoea violacea)

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Ololiuhqui represents one of the three major Mexican magic plant traditions (alongside teonanacatl mushrooms and peyote) that were preserved in indigenous practice from pre-Columbian times. The discovery of lysergic acid derivatives in the seeds provided an extraordinary chemical link between the ergot alkaloids from which LSD was derived and the indigenous plant medicines of Mexico, completing what Hofmann called a "magic circle" of discovery. The chemical kinship between ololiuhqui and LSD demonstrates the profound interconnection between naturally occurring plant compounds and the synthetic pharmaceutical research that produced the most potent known psychedelic.

## The Magic Circle

Hofmann described his research on hallucinogenic drugs as forming a "magic circle": the starting point was the synthesis of lysergic acid amides, including the naturally occurring ergot alkaloid ergobasin, which led to the synthesis of LSD. The hallucinogenic properties of LSD led to the investigation of the Mexican magic mushroom teonanacatl, from which psilocybin and psilocin were isolated. This led to the investigation of ololiuhqui, in which lysergic acid amides including ergobasin were again encountered, closing the circle.

## The Morning Glory Seed Boom

After Hofmann published his findings, Dutch wholesale seed companies reported unusual sales of Ipomoea violacea seeds to hippie and drug-interested circles who sought a substitute for LSD. The boom was short-lived, as the seeds taste very bad, are difficult to digest, and produce undesirable effects including nausea, mental emptiness, and depression rather than the euphoric hallucinogenic experience LSD provides.

## Historical Documentation

The earliest records of ololiuhqui were written by Spanish chroniclers of the sixteenth century. The Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagun described it in his Historia General as an herb called coatl xoxouhqui (green snake) whose seeds "stupefy and deprive one of reason." Francisco Hernandez, sent by Philip II to study native medicaments (1570-1575), provided a detailed description and the first illustration in his monumental Rerum Medicarum Novae Hispaniae Thesaurus, published in Rome in 1651. He wrote: "When the priests of the Indians wanted to visit with the gods and obtain information from them, they ate of this plant in order to become inebriated. Thousands of fantastic images and demons then appeared to them." These colonial accounts confirmed that ololiuhqui was used alongside peyotl and teonanacatl in pre-Columbian religious practice.

## Chemical Investigation History

The pharmacologist C.G. Santesson of Stockholm first attempted chemical investigation of ololiuhqui in 1937 but failed to isolate the active substance in pure form. Hofmann's success in 1959 depended on the hypothesis that the active principles might be indole compounds, the same chemical family as LSD and psilocybin. This hypothesis was considered extremely improbable because the great majority of known plant compounds belong to other chemical families. However, the indole hypothesis was easily tested using colorimetric reactions that produce a characteristic blue color in the presence of indoles. The hypothesis proved correct, and the isolation proceeded rapidly. The identification of the compounds as lysergic acid derivatives was initially met with deep skepticism at the IUPAC Congress in Sydney, but was subsequently confirmed by laboratories in the United States, Germany, and Holland.

## Dual Chemical and Spiritual Influence

Hofmann eloquently described how the morning glory (Ipomoea violacea) demonstrates two possible ways of influencing the human mind-body: in Mexico it acts chemically as a magic drug, while in Japan it acts spiritually through the beauty of its flowers, as celebrated in the haiku by the poetess Chiyo (1702-75): "My trough is captivated by a morning glory blossom, so I ask after water."

## Zapotec Use: Badoh and Badoh Negro

The Zapotec Indians distinguish between the original ololiuhqui (seeds of Turbina corymbosa, called badoh) and badoh negro (seeds of Ipomoea violacea). Both species are used as entheogens but differ in their seed morphology and potency. T. MacDougall documented that the Zapotec utilize both species in their healing practices, with badoh negro being the more commonly available of the two. The round, light-brown seeds of Turbina corymbosa are native to tropical and subtropical climates, while Ipomoea violacea is distributed worldwide as an ornamental plant, growing in gardens throughout temperate zones with its characteristic blue or blue-red striped flowers.

## Modern Pharmacological Interest

Hofmann suggested that the strong narcotic, sedative effects of certain ololiuhqui constituents, or of chemical modifications of these compounds, might have useful medical applications. Unlike LSD, which produces pronounced euphoric and hallucinogenic effects, ololiuhqui's active principles produce a predominantly sedative and narcotic effect characterized by mental emptiness, enhanced auditory sensitivity, and physical lassitude leading to sleep. This pharmacological profile suggests potential applications in the treatment of anxiety, insomnia, and other conditions where sedation rather than psychedelic stimulation is desired. Dr. H. Solms conducted systematic psychiatric investigations of LA-111 (lysergic acid amide) confirming this profile.

## Botanical Identification History

The botanical identification of ololiuhqui occasioned many discussions in specialist circles despite the comparatively good description by Hernandez. The seeds were eventually identified as Rivea corymbosa (L.) Hall. f., with recent preference given to the synonym Turbina corymbosa (L.) Raf. The second species, Ipomoea violacea L., identified from the black angular seeds from San Bartolo Yautepec provided by the Zapotec guide Francisco Ortega "Chico," is the familiar ornamental morning glory found in gardens worldwide. The coexistence of two different species used interchangeably under the ololiuhqui name complicated both botanical identification and chemical analysis, as the two species contain somewhat different profiles of lysergic acid derivatives.
