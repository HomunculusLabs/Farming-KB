## Overview

Delphinium exaltatum Aiton, the tall larkspur or Allegheny larkspur, is a perennial herb in the family Ranunculaceae. It is native to [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] larkspurs. Its tall, spicate inflorescences of deep blue to violet-blue flowers are a showy feature of summer woodland edges.

Like all Delphinium species, D. exaltatum contains diterpenoid alkaloids that render it toxic to livestock. Cattle poisoning from tall larkspur is a significant concern on western rangelands.

The species is cultivated as a native ornamental perennial for wildflower gardens and naturalistic plantings. It is less commonly grown than the European D. elatum but valued for its adaptability to partial shade.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Class Magnoliopsida → Order Ranunculales → Family Ranunculaceae → Tribe Delphinieae → Genus *Delphinium* → Species *D. exaltatum* Aiton.

Aiton described the species in 1789 from material collected in the [[actaea-racemosa]]*, and *Solidago flexicaulis*. It is a component of the herb layer in Appalachian cove forests.

Soils are typically well-drained, circumneutral to alkaline loams with high organic content. The species does not tolerate poorly drained or strongly acidic sites.

Flowering occurs from July through September, later than most eastern woodland herbs. This late bloom period fills a phenological gap in shaded plantings.

## Toxicology

The primary toxic alkaloids include methyllycaconitine (MLA), 14-acetyltalatizamine, and deltaline. These C18-diterpenoid alkaloids are concentrated in leaves and immature seeds.

MLA is a potent neuromuscular blocker that acts at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. It causes respiratory paralysis in livestock at sufficient doses.

Cattle are the most susceptible livestock species, with lethal doses estimated at 0.5–1.0% of body weight in green plant material. Sheep are more resistant due to differential rumen metabolism.

Poisoning typically occurs when cattle graze the plant on rangelands or when D. exaltatum invades pastures. Early symptoms include trembling, stiffness, and labored breathing.

Fatalities result from respiratory failure due to neuromuscular blockade. Treatment with neostigmine can be effective if administered early.

Unlike western tall larkspurs (D. barbeyi, D. occidentale), D. exaltatum causes fewer documented livestock losses. Its eastern range coincides with less extensive grazing.

The species hybridizes occasionally with D. elatum in cultivation, producing intermediate forms with variable alkaloid profiles. These hybrids are of horticultural interest but rare in the wild.

## Cultivation

Delphinium exaltatum is cultivated as a native perennial for shade gardens, woodland borders, and pollinator plantings. It is hardy to USDA zones 4–8.

The species tolerates more shade than European delphiniums, thriving in light to moderate shade. Full sun is acceptable in cooler climates with adequate moisture.

Propagation is by seed or basal cuttings. Seeds require 4–6 weeks of cold stratification and should be sown in late autumn or early winter.
