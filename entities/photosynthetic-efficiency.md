## Photosynthetic Efficiency in Agriculture

## Description

Photosynthetic efficiency in agriculture refers to the effectiveness with which plants convert light energy into biomass, and how this efficiency is modulated by [[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]] including temperature, light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, wind, and latitude. Mollison draws heavily on climate science (particularly Chang, 1968 and Daubenmire, 1974) to demonstrate that the highest photosynthetic efficiency occurs not in the tropics (as commonly assumed) but in moderate-temperature, high-light conditions found at temperate latitudes during summer and in tropical shade conditions.

## Classification

- **Type**: Physiological concept / agricultural principle
- **Domain**: Plant physiology, agricultural climatology, [[permaculture-design]]
- **Key variables**: Temperature, light intensity, CO2 concentration, wind exposure, humidity
- **Optimal range**: 15-24 degrees C temperature, moderate light, elevated CO2 (up to 0.13%)

## Key Facts

- **Temperature optima**: 15 degrees C is optimum for adapted high-latitude species and cultivars; 20-24 degrees C for many useful food plants. Temperatures above 25 degrees C sharply decrease photosynthetic efficiency, meaning the hot conditions typical of tropical lowlands are actually suboptimal for most crop photosynthesis.
- **Light saturation paradox**: In intense equatorial light, photosynthesis may actually decline because plants become light-saturated — their biochemical machinery cannot process light faster than a certain rate, and excess light creates damaging [[reactive-oxygen-species-and-oxidative-stress]]. The energy built into plants may be less in full sunlight than in partial shade.
- **Shade benefits**: Shade down to 20% of full sunlight is beneficial in tropical deserts and sunny equatorial climates. Shadecloth with 50-70% light transmission greatly increases plant bulk and production, as demonstrated with sugar beet, taro, and other crops.
- **CO2 limitation**: At high light intensity, the ability of leaves to obtain CO2 becomes the limiting factor for photosynthesis. Supplying CO2 to 0.13% saturation achieves a 2-3 times increase in photosynthetic rate compared to ambient CO2 levels (approximately 0.04%).
- **CO2 sources in permaculture**: Composting operations and housing animals in greenhouses where light is more than sufficient can supply the needed CO2 enrichment, creating synergistic integrated systems.
- **Radiation by latitude**: Average radiation in temperate areas during summer is 1-5 times that of the tropics (Chang, 1968). Late summer daily totals: 440 Langleys (13 degrees N), 680 (36 degrees N), 450 (64 degrees N).
- **Leaf temperature exceeds air temperature** in bright sunlight, so diffuse light from overcast or cloudy days in high latitudes actually helps plant growth, especially after midday when direct sun would push leaf temperatures above the photosynthetic optimum.
- **Tropical vs. temperate crops**: True tropical plants can withstand much higher light before saturation and maintain photosynthesis at lower CO2 (0.10%), but non-adapted temperate crops persistently underperform in tropical conditions due to both temperature and light stress.
- **Rice yields**: 4-5 times better in temperate areas than tropical per crop, though multiple tropical crops per year partially compensate for lower per-crop efficiency.
- **Leaf damage and photosynthesis**: Torn or frayed leaf laminae and broken midribs from wind cause photosynthetic inefficiency ranging from 20-85% in crops like corn and bananas (Chang, 1968), demonstrating the critical importance of wind protection for maintaining photosynthetic capacity.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Integrated mushroom-greenhouse systems**: Mushrooms produce CO2 as a metabolic byproduct while decomposing substrate. Channelling this CO2-enriched air into adjacent greenhouses or polytunnels can double or triple photosynthetic rates of vegetable crops, creating a mutually beneficial integrated system where both mushrooms and plants benefit.
- **Shade-grown mushroom logs**: The need for shade in tropical and subtropical mushroom cultivation aligns with the photosynthetic shade requirement for understorey crops. Mushroom logs can be placed beneath the same permeable tree canopies (Prosopis, Inga, Acacia) that optimise photosynthesis for shade-tolerant understorey crops.
- **Tree-crop-mushroom polyculture**: Permeable-crowned leguminous trees that reduce light and temperature for tropical crops simultaneously create the humid, shaded conditions ideal for tropical mushroom species (Pleurotus, Volvariella, Auricularia) grown on logs or beds beneath them.
- **Substrate temperature management**: Understanding that both photosynthesis and fungal metabolism have temperature optima helps design integrated systems where the heat generated by composting substrates is captured to warm greenhouse growing spaces during cold periods.
- **Compost CO2 harvesting**: The CO2 generated by [[fao-spent-mushroom-substrate-composting-recycling]] (Phase I and II) represents a valuable resource for adjacent plant production, closing the carbon loop and increasing total system productivity by 2-3 times in the greenhouse component.
- **Wind protection for mushrooms**: Just as wind reduces photosynthetic efficiency in plants through leaf damage, wind increases evaporation from mushroom substrates and fruiting bodies, reducing yield. Windbreak design that protects both plant crops and mushroom beds provides compound benefits.

## Design Applications

- At high latitudes: maximise light interception with south-facing slopes, use thermal mass for temperature stability, extend season with greenhouses.
- At low latitudes: prioritise shade (50-70% shadecloth or tree canopies), ventilation, and [[bloomfield-mushroom-evaporative-cooling-and-thermal-regulation]] to keep temperatures in the photosynthetic optimum range.
- Couple mushroom cultivation with greenhouse production for CO2 enrichment, creating integrated systems that are more productive than either component alone.
- Use composting as a dual-purpose system producing both mushroom substrate and CO2 for plant growth.
- Select true tropical species for tropical sites rather than struggling with temperate crops that will be stressed by heat and light saturation.
- Protect all crops from wind to prevent the 20-85% photosynthetic losses documented by Chang in wind-damaged plants.

## Practical Applications Summary

- Greenhouse operations can increase yields 2-3x by enriching CO2 to 0.13% using compost or animal housing as CO2 sources.
- Tropical farms should interplant leguminous trees (Prosopis, Inga, Acacia, Leucaena) at 10-16 m spacing to provide 50-70% shade.
- High-latitude farms should maximise solar access and use long summer days for intensive biomass production.
- Temperate rice yields 4-5x more than tropical rice per crop, validating the focus on temperate grain production.
- [[windbreak-establishment-and-benefits]] is the single most cost-effective intervention for increasing photosynthetic efficiency on exposed sites, yielding 7-100% crop increases depending on species and exposure.
