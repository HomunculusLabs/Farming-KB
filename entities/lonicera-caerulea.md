# Lonicera caerulea

Lonicera caerulea, commonly known as haskap or honeyberry, is a deciduous
shrub in Caprifoliaceae native to cool temperate regions of the Northern
Hemisphere. Prized for its exceptionally early ripening and remarkable cold
hardiness, it has emerged as a commercially significant berry crop.

The berries are rich in anthocyanins, vitamin C, and phenolic compounds,
earning recognition as a "superfruit" in nutritional and functional food
contexts.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Dipsacales
- **Family:** Caprifoliaceae
- **Genus:** Lonicera
- **Species:** L. caerulea L.

Recognized subspecies include subsp. *caerulea* (European), subsp. *edulis*
(Japanese haskap), subsp. *kamtschatica* (Kuril/Kamchatka), and subsp.
*villosa* (North American). Common names vary: haskap (Japan), honeyberry
(commercial), blue honeysuckle, sweetberry honeysuckle.

## Morphology

A multi-stemmed deciduous shrub reaching 1.0–1.8 m in height and spread.

**Stems:** Slender, tan to brown, with peeling bark on older wood; produces
vigorous basal shoots.

**Leaves:** Opposite, simple, elliptic to ovate, 3–7 cm long, dark green
adaxially, paler abaxially. Leaf pairs fused at base forming a small cup —
diagnostic for the genus.

**Flowers:** Bisexual, protandrous, pale yellow to white, tubular, 8–12 mm
long, borne in pairs in leaf axils. Blooms extremely early (late March to
mid-April), making frost risk a concern.

**Fruit:** Elongated cylindrical berry, 1–2.5 cm, blue to dark blue-purple
with waxy bloom. Calyx persists at apex. Brix 12–18° depending on cultivar.

## Distribution and Habitat

Circumboreal distribution spanning eastern Europe, northern Asia (Siberia,
Kamchatka, Hokkaido), and North America (Alaska to Newfoundland).

Inhabits forest margins, riparian zones, bogs, wet meadows, and mountain
slopes from sea level to 1500 m. Tolerates winter temperatures to -40°C,
requiring 800–1200 chill hours for dormancy fulfillment.

## Bioactive Compounds

Among the richest fruit sources of anthocyanins:

- **Total anthocyanins:** 400–1800 mg/100 g FW (up to 3× blueberry)
- **Cyanidin-3-glucoside** — dominant anthocyanin
- **Cyanidin-3-rutinoside** — second most abundant
- **Vitamin C:** 30–80 mg/100 g FW
- **Phenolic acids:** Chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, ferulic acid
- **ORAC values:** 10–15× higher than cultivated blueberry

Bioavailability is enhanced by acylated anthocyanin forms unique to the
species.

## Cultivation

- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 2–7
- **Soil:** Well-drained loam, pH 5.5–7.0
- **Light:** Full sun to partial shade
- **Spacing:** 1.0–1.5 m within rows, 3–4 m between rows
- **Pollination:** Cross-pollination essential — at least two distinct
  cultivars required

Fruit ripens 6–8 weeks after flowering (late May to mid-June), 2–4 weeks
before strawberries. Yield begins year 2–3, full production (4–6 kg/plant)
by year 5. Commercial yields: 5–15 t/ha.

Annual pruning removes canes 3+ years old; shrub fruits on one-year-old wood.

**Major pests:** Powdery mildew, Botrytis fruit rot, birds (netting essential),
spotted wing drosophila (emerging concern).

## Permaculture Applications

- **Early food source** in spring food forests
- **Wildlife habitat** — dense shrub provides nesting cover
- **Erosion control** on slopes via fibrous root system
- **Pollinator support** — early nectar for emerging bees
- **Understory crop** — performs in partial shade beneath taller trees
- **Efficient nutrient cycling** from subsoil layers

Pairs well with seaberry, gooseberry, and nitrogen-fixing understory shrubs
in food forest design.
