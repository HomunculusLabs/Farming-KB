# Mushroom Casing

## Description

Casing is a fundamental technique in [[mushroom-cultivation]] that involves covering the fully spawned compost bed with a layer of soil or soil-like material. This practice, detailed extensively by [[benjamin-minge-duggar-mushroom-cultivation-research]] in his 1915 text "Mushroom Growing," is critical for initiating fruiting body (sporophore) formation and maintaining proper moisture conditions in the mushroom bed. The casing layer creates a microenvironment that stimulates the transition from vegetative mycelial growth to reproductive fruiting.

## Classification

- **Category**: Cultivation technique
- **Subcategory**: Bed management
- **Historical origin**: Practiced since early [[history-of-commercial-mushroom-cultivation]] (19th century)
- **Application stage**: Post-spawning, after mycelial colonization is confirmed

## Casing Layer Specifications

Duggar's recommended parameters for the casing layer:

- **Depth**: 1 to 1.25 inches of soil covering the entire bed surface
- **Timing**: Applied only after spawn is visibly running well through the compost
- **Quantity**: Sufficient to cover the entire bed surface uniformly

## Soil Selection and Preparation

### Recommended Materials
Duggar ranks casing materials from best to acceptable:

1. **Good garden loam** — preferred, readily available
2. **Potting soil** — excellent consistency and structure
3. **Soil from beneath grass sod** — excellent, rich in [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]]
4. **Calcareous loam mixture** (French method) — advantageous for sanitation purposes

### Materials to Avoid
- **Pure sand**: Loses moisture too readily, cannot maintain the humid microclimate needed
- **Heavy clay**: Subject to baking, especially when artificial heat is employed; poor aeration
- **Woods-mould**: Objectionable for two reasons — introduces competing fungi, and increases number of fruits at the expense of individual mushroom size

### Preparation Guidelines
- Secure casing material some time in advance of needing it
- Screen the soil if it contains numerous sticks and stones
- French growers mix calcareous (lime-rich) soil with the loam for improved sanitation
- The casing soil should be well moistened by repeated sprinkling before application

## Timing of Application

Casing should be delayed until spawn growth is confirmed throughout the bed. The correct sequence is:

1. Spawn is inserted into the compost
2. Wait 7-10 days for visible mycelial growth
3. Confirm spawn is "running well" throughout the bed
4. Apply casing layer only when colonization is satisfactory

However, Duggar notes an important exception: if house conditions make moisture retention difficult (e.g., in dry environments), earlier casing may be necessary to prevent the beds from drying out.

## Moisture Management After Casing

The moisture requirements after casing are critical and represent "one of the chief difficulties in mushroom growing":

- **Compost**: Must have correct moisture content at the time of casing
- **Casing soil**: Must be well moistened by repeated sprinkling
- **Common errors**: 
  - Amateurs often drench beds, destroying spawn
  - Others fear watering even during rapid drying, preventing surface mycelial growth

Duggar recommends the "hand test" for compost moisture: compress compost in the hand — it should distinctly moisten the hand but not release drops of water (approximately 65% water content).

## Functions of the Casing Layer

The casing layer serves multiple purposes:

1. **Moisture regulation**: Creates a humid microclimate at the compost surface
2. **Protection**: Shields spawn from direct desiccation and temperature fluctuations
3. **Microbiological stimulation**: Introduces beneficial soil microorganisms (later discovered to include bacteria like Pseudomonas putida that stimulate pinning)
4. **Physical support**: Provides a stable surface for mushroom emergence
5. **Fruit initiation**: The change in environment from compost to soil triggers reproductive development

## Historical Context

Duggar describes casing in Chapter VII, "Installation, Spawning, and Care of Beds," as a standard practice in both English (flat bed) and French (ridge bed) mushroom cultivation systems. The French practice of adding calcareous soil represents an early understanding of pH management in cultivation, even before the microbiological basis was fully understood.

## Modern Applications

Casing remains essential in commercial Agaricus cultivation worldwide. Contemporary practice follows Duggar's principles closely, though some operations use:
- Peat moss-based casing mixtures
- Automated casing application equipment
- Pasteurized casing to reduce contamination
- pH-adjusted casing materials (typically limed to pH 7.5-8.0)

The microbiological interaction between casing soil and mushroom mycelium was later discovered to be crucial — specific bacteria in the casing layer produce [[mycovolatiles-fungal-volatile-compounds]] that stimulate primordia formation.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Casing represents one of the most important innovations [[contamination-prevention-in-mushroom-cultivation]]. Without a casing layer, Agaricus species typically fail to fruit or produce greatly reduced yields. The technique demonstrates the importance of understanding the [[fungal-fruiting-body-formation-environmental-triggers]] for fungal reproduction.
