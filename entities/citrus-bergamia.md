## Overview

**Citrus bergamia**, commonly known as the bergamot orange or simply bergamot, is a small citrus tree prized primarily for its aromatic peel oil. The essential oil is a key ingredient in Earl Grey tea, perfumery, and aromatherapy.

The taxonomic origin of bergamot has been debated for centuries. Recent molecular evidence supports it as a hybrid of Citrus limetta (sweet lime) and [[citrus-aurantium]] (sour orange), though some studies propose [[citrus-aurantifolia]] as a parent.

Bergamot is cultivated almost exclusively in a narrow coastal strip of the Calabria region in southern Italy. This small area produces over 80% of the world's bergamot essential oil supply.

The fruit is rarely eaten fresh due to its intensely sour and bitter flavor. Its commercial value lies almost entirely in the cold-pressed essential oil extracted from the peel.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Plantae — **Order:** Sapindales — **Family:** Rutaceae

**Genus:** Citrus — **Species:** C. bergamia Risso & Poit.

Citrus bergamia was first formally described by Giovanni Antonio Risso and Pierre Antoine Poiteau in 1818 in their work *Histoire naturelle des orangers*.

The species is of hybrid origin and does not breed true from seed. [[hamilton-vegetative-propagation]] is required to maintain cultivar characteristics, and clonal selection has produced numerous named varieties.

The tree is medium-sized, typically 3–5 meters tall, with a rounded to somewhat irregular crown. It is more cold-sensitive than many other citrus species, damaged by temperatures below -3°C.

Leaves are elliptic to ovate, 6–12 cm long, with broadly winged petioles characteristic of the Citrus genus. The foliage is dark green, glossy, and aromatic when crushed.

Flowers are white, fragrant, and approximately 3 cm in diameter, appearing in clusters in spring. They possess five petals and numerous prominent stamens.

The fruit is a hesperidium (modified berry), 6–10 cm in diameter, roughly spherical to slightly pear-shaped. The peel is yellow to greenish-yellow, smooth, and exceptionally rich in oil glands.

The pulp is pale yellow, very acidic (pH 2.5–3.0), and moderately bitter. Segments contain seeds in many cultivars, though seedless selections have been developed.

## Distribution and Habitat

Bergamot is commercially cultivated in a narrow band along the Ionian coast of Calabria, southern Italy, from Reggio Calabria to the Gulf of Squillace. This microclimate provides the specific temperature and soil conditions the tree requires.

The Calabrian bergamot zone extends roughly 100 km along the coast, from sea level to about 300 m elevation. Annual rainfall is 600–800 mm, concentrated in autumn and winter.

Small-scale commercial production also occurs in southern France, Turkey, Morocco, Argentina, and Brazil. However, the oil profile of non-Calabrian fruit differs noticeably from the Italian standard.

The species requires a Mediterranean or [[query-how-do-i-grow-bananas-in-a-subtropical-climate]] with warm summers and mild, relatively frost-free winters. Temperatures below -3°C cause significant damage to foliage and fruit.

Optimal soils are well-drained, sandy to clay loams with pH 6.0–7.5. The Calabrian coastal soils, derived from metamorphic and sedimentary parent materials, are considered ideal.

## Cultivation

Bergamot trees are planted at densities of 300–500 trees per hectare in traditional Calabrian orchards. Spacing is typically 5 × 6 meters, allowing for the tree's spreading canopy.

Propagation is entirely vegetative, using budwood grafted onto sour orange (Citrus aurantium) rootstock. Selected clones such as 'Fantastico', 'Castagnaro', and 'Femminello' dominate commercial plantings.

Fertilization follows standard citrus recommendations: 150–250 kg/ha nitrogen, 50–80 kg/ha phosphorus, and 100–150 kg/ha potassium, split across multiple applications.

Irrigation is essential during the dry Mediterranean summer. Drip and micro-sprinkler systems are preferred, with water requirements of 4000–6000 m³ per hectare per season.

The primary pest is the citrus fruit fly (Ceratitis capitata), which can cause significant fruit drop. Citrus red mite (Panonychus citri) and scale insects also require management.

Harvest occurs from November through March, with peak quality in January–February. Fruit must be picked by hand at the correct maturity stage to optimize oil yield and composition.

Essential oil is extracted by cold pressing (sfumatrice or scarabatto machines) from the fresh peel. Yield ranges from 30–50 kg of oil per tonne of fruit, representing 3–5% of fresh peel weight.

## Chemistry and Uses

Bergamot essential oil is composed primarily of linalyl acetate (25–38%) and linalool (11–22%), which together define its characteristic floral-citrus aroma. These acetate esters are unusual in citrus oils.

Other significant volatile components include limonene (20–30%), gamma-terpinene (5–10%), and beta-pinene (5–8%). The specific ratio of linalyl acetate to linalool distinguishes high-quality Calabrian bergamot oil.

The peel contains flavonoid glycosides, particularly brutieridin and melitidin, which have demonstrated cholesterol-lowering activity in clinical studies. These compounds are structurally similar to statin drugs.

Bergamot juice is rich in ascorbic acid (vitamin C), flavonoids (naringin, neoeriocitrin, neohesperidin), and hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives. The juice has been studied for cardioprotective and anti-inflammatory effects.

The essential oil is the defining flavoring in Earl Grey tea, where it imparts a distinctive citrus-floral note. It is also used extensively in perfumery as a top note in over one-third of men's fragrances and one-third of women's fragrances.

In aromatherapy, bergamot oil is used for its purported anxiolytic and mood-enhancing properties. [[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-schizophyllan-spg-clinical-trials]] have shown modest reductions in anxiety scores with inhalation of the oil.
