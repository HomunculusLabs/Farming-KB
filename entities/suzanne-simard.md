## Suzanne Simard

## Description

Suzanne Simard is a Canadian forest ecologist and professor at the University of British Columbia whose groundbreaking research in the 1990s demonstrated that trees are connected below ground by mycorrhizal fungal networks and can share carbon, nutrients, and resources through these connections. Her 1997 publications provided the first direct field evidence of net carbon transfer between ectomycorrhizal tree species of different genera, fundamentally changing our understanding of plant community interactions. Her work popularized the concept of the "[[wood-wide-web]]" and challenged the prevailing paradigm that plant communities are structured primarily by competition.

## Classification

- **Profession**: Forest ecologist, professor
- **Affiliation**: University of British Columbia, Faculty of Forestry
- **Research focus**: [[mycorrhizal-networks]], forest ecology, plant-fungal interactions, below-ground carbon transfer
- **Key publications**: Simard et al. (1997a,b,c) in New Phytol. and Nature

## Key Facts

- Simard et al. (1997b) demonstrated reciprocal transfer of carbon isotopes between ectomycorrhizal [[betula-papyrifera]] (paper birch) and [[pseudotsuga-menziesii]] (Douglas fir) using dual-labeling with 13C and 14C isotopes.
- The 1997c Nature paper showed net transfer of carbon between ectomycorrhizal tree species in the field, providing the first definitive field evidence that mycorrhizal networks facilitate resource sharing.
- The 1997a paper examined effects of soil trenching on ectomycorrhizal occurrence, demonstrating the importance of intact mycelial connections for seedling establishment.
- Her research revealed that carbon flows from larger, photosynthetically active "mother trees" to shaded seedlings through mycorrhizal networks, with preferential transfer to related individuals.
- These findings fundamentally altered concepts about plant interspecific interactions, introducing the idea of a balance between competition and synergism in plant communities.
- Prior to Simard"s work, the dominant paradigm held that plant communities arose from competition among species; her research showed that cooperation through fungal networks is equally important.
- Dighton (2003) describes Simard's findings as altering our concepts regarding plant interspecific interactions and necessitating a rethink of competitive versus synergistic community dynamics.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Simard's research provides the scientific foundation for understanding why maintaining intact mycorrhizal networks in cultivation systems improves plant health and productivity.
- Her findings support agroforestry and polyculture designs where mixed plant species can share resources through [[common-mycorrhizal-networks]].
- The concept of "mother trees" nurturing seedlings through fungal networks has direct applications in reforestation, orchard establishment, and forest garden design.
- Soil disturbance practices (tilling, compaction) that sever mycorrhizal networks can be understood through Simard's framework as disrupting a critical resource-sharing infrastructure.
- Her work justifies the use of mycorrhizal inoculation to establish functional networks in degraded or newly established cultivation systems.
- Understanding carbon and nutrient transfer through mycorrhizal networks helps explain why diverse plant communities often outperform monocultures in terms of total productivity.
