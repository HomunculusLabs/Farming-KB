# Colchicum autumnale

**Colchicum autumnale**, the **autumn crocus** or **meadow saffron**, is a perennial geophyte in the family **Colchicaceae** native to Europe and the British Isles. Despite its common name, it is unrelated to true crocuses (*Crocus* spp., Iridaceae) and is instead one of the most poisonous plants in European flora.

The species is notorious as the natural source of **colchicine**, a potent alkaloid with a 2,000-year history in medicine. All parts of the plant are highly toxic, with fatalities recorded from [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

The fruit is a three-valved capsule containing numerous small, dark brown, spherical seeds approximately 2–3 mm in diameter. Each seed can remain viable in soil for several years, facilitating gradual colony expansion.

The underground corm is broadly ovoid, 3–5 cm in diameter, and covered with dark brown tunics. Unlike true crocuses, the corm contains toxic alkaloid concentrations throughout its tissues, with highest concentrations in the seeds and corm.

Corms contract annually through the action of specialized contractile roots, maintaining the plant at an optimal soil depth of 8–15 cm. This contractile mechanism is shared with many other geophytes and ensures protection from frost damage during the dormant period.

The species exhibits considerable morphological variation across its range, leading to the description of numerous infraspecific taxa. Some authorities recognize varieties based on flower color (white, pink, purple), tepal size, and corm morphology, though these are not universally accepted.

## Chemistry and Toxicology

The principal bioactive alkaloid is **colchicine**, a tricyclic tropolone alkaloid that binds tubulin and inhibits microtubule polymerization. This mechanism disrupts mitotic spindle formation, arresting cell division in metaphase — a property exploited in both medicine and cell biology.

Colchicine concentration varies by plant part: seeds contain 0.3–0.5% colchicine by dry weight, corms 0.2–0.4%, and flowers 0.1–0.2%. The therapeutic window is extremely narrow, with toxic effects appearing at doses only marginally above the therapeutic range.

Acute colchicine poisoning manifests as a delayed, multi-phase syndrome beginning with gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) within 2–24 hours. This is followed by multi-organ failure, bone marrow suppression, and potentially death within 2–7 days at doses exceeding 0.5 mg/kg.

No specific antidote exists for colchicine poisoning; treatment is entirely supportive. Activated charcoal may be effective if administered within one hour of ingestion. Fatality rates have improved significantly with modern intensive care, but the drug remains dangerous in overdose.

Fatalities in modern times are rare but continue to occur, typically from confusion between *C. autumnale* leaves and edible wild plants. The risk is greatest in early spring when the young leaves resemble those of ramsons (*Allium ursinum*) and wild leek.

Colchicine's anti-mitotic mechanism was first described by the Italian cytologist Leva in 1889, who observed its ability to arrest dividing cells in metaphase. This discovery made colchicine an indispensable tool in plant breeding, where it is used to induce polyploidy for crop improvement.

Additional alkaloids present in *C. autumnale* include colchiceine, demecolcine (colcemid), and N-formyl-N-deacetylcolchicine (NFDAC). Demecolcine shares the anti-mitotic mechanism but is used specifically as a chromosome-spreading agent in cytogenetic karyotyping.

## Taxonomy and Systematics

*C. autumnale* was reclassified from Liliaceae to Colchicaceae following molecular phylogenetic studies in the 1990s. The family Colchicaceae now includes approximately 15 genera and 285 species distributed across temperate and [[crocus-sativus]]*, *C. speciosus*) is a classic example of convergent evolution driven by similar pollination ecology. Both genera are pollinated by late-season bees and other insects foraging before winter dormancy.

Key morphological differences distinguish the two genera: *Colchicum* has six anthers (vs. three in *Crocus*), superior ovaries (vs. inferior in *Crocus*), and corms with a plaited tunic structure.
