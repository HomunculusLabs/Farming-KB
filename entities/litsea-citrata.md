## Overview

**Litsea citrata** is an aromatic evergreen shrub or small tree in the family Lauraceae, native to parts of East and Southeast Asia. It is commonly known as fragrant litsea, citrus litsea, or may chang, though the latter name is more properly applied to Litsea cubeba.

The species is valued primarily for its essential oil, which has a fresh, citrus-like aroma. The oil is used in perfumery, aromatherapy, and traditional medicine across its native range.

Litsea citrata typically grows 3 to 8 meters tall, with a spreading crown and smooth, grey-brown bark. Young branches are green and slightly pubescent, becoming glabrous with age.

The leaves are alternate, lanceolate to elliptic, 7 to 15 centimeters long and 2 to 5 centimeters wide. They are glossy green above and paler below, with prominent lateral veins.

When crushed, the leaves emit a strong lemon-citrus fragrance due to the presence of citral and other volatile terpenoids in the leaf oil glands. This characteristic scent distinguishes it from many related Litsea species.

The essential oil glands are visible as translucent dots on the leaf surface when viewed against strong light. This feature is characteristic of the Lauraceae and aids in field identification of the species.

The species is closely related to Litsea cubeba and Litsea glutinosa, with which it shares many morphological features and overlapping distributions. Molecular phylogenetic studies place it within the Tetranthera clade of Litsea.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Litsea citrata was described by Nees and later transferred to its current placement by Bartell. The taxonomy of the genus Litsea remains complex, with many species poorly delimited and frequent misidentification in herbarium collections.

The bark is smooth and grey-brown on mature trunks, becoming fissured on older specimens. The wood is light-colored, moderately hard, and diffusely porous with indistinct growth rings.

Inflorescences are umbellate, borne in the axils of fallen leaves or on short leafless branchlets. Each umbel contains 3 to 5 flowers that are functionally unisexual, with male and female flowers typically borne on separate plants.

Male flowers have 6 to 8 stamens with short filaments and conspicuous anthers. Female flowers have a superior ovary and a short style with a broad, capitate stigma.

The fruit is a small, globose drupe approximately 8 to 12 millimeters in diameter. It ripens from green to dark purple or black, seated on a shallow, cup-like perianth tube.

The fruit is consumed by a variety of forest birds, which serve as the primary dispersal agents. The pulp is thin but sweet, and the single seed passes through the digestive tract unharmed.

## Distribution and Habitat

Litsea citrata is distributed across southern China, northern Vietnam, Laos, and northern Thailand. It occurs at elevations from 200 to 1,500 meters in mountainous terrain.

The species grows in subtropical broadleaf evergreen forests, often as an understory component beneath taller canopy trees. It is also found in secondary growth and forest margins.

Preferred habitats include moist valleys, stream banks, and slopes with well-drained, acidic soils. The species tolerates a range of soil types but performs best on deep, fertile loams with high organic content.

The climate in its native range is characterized by warm, wet summers and mild, relatively dry winters. Mean annual temperatures range from 15 to 22 degrees Celsius with annual precipitation of 1,000 to 2,000 millimeters.

Natural regeneration is primarily through seed dispersal, with birds being the main agents of fruit distribution. The species can also regenerate from stump sprouts after disturbance.

The species plays a role in forest succession, often colonizing disturbed sites and contributing to the development of secondary forest. Its aromatic foliage may provide some degree of herbivore deterrence.

## Essential Oil Chemistry

The essential oil of Litsea citrata is commercially significant and has been the subject of extensive chemical analysis. The principal constituents are monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes derived from the mevalonate pathway.

Citral, a mixture of geranial and neral, is the dominant component, typically comprising 60 to 80 percent of the total oil. This is responsible for the characteristic lemon aroma.

Other notable constituents include limonene, linalool, citronellal, alpha-pinene, and beta-pinene. Minor components include geraniol, nerol, and various sesquiterpene hydrocarbons.

The oil yield from fresh leaves ranges from 0.5 to 2.0 percent by weight, depending on season, plant age, and growing conditions. Peak oil production occurs during the flowering period.

Hydrodistillation and steam distillation are the primary methods for essential oil extraction. Supercritical carbon dioxide extraction has been explored as an alternative, yielding oils with different terpene ratios.

Chemical composition varies significantly with geographic origin and harvest time. Populations from different provinces in China show distinct chemotype profiles, suggesting genetic differentiation.

The oil has demonstrated antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and various fungal pathogens. Antioxidant activity has also been documented, attributed primarily to the phenolic terpene constituents.

## Cultivation

Litsea citrata is cultivated for essential oil production in parts of China and Vietnam. Plantations typically use vegetatively propagated material to maintain consistent oil quality.

Propagation is achieved through seed, cuttings, or air layering. Seed germination is often poor and irregular, with dormancy requiring warm stratification for 2 to 3 months before sowing.

Semi-hardwood cuttings taken in late summer root readily under mist with auxin treatment. Air layering is a traditional method that produces well-rooted plants within 6 to 8 months.

Planting density in essential oil plantations ranges from 2,000 to 4,000 plants per hectare. First harvest of leaves typically occurs 2 to 3 years after planting, with subsequent harvests every 6 to 8 months.

Yield per hectare ranges from 20 to 50 kilograms of essential oil annually under managed cultivation. Intercropping with food crops or timber trees is common in traditional agroforestry systems.

The species is adapted to subtropical conditions and is sensitive to frost. Optimal growth occurs at temperatures between 18 and 28 degrees Celsius with annual rainfall exceeding 1,200 millimeters.

## Traditional Uses

In traditional Chinese medicine, Litsea citrata has been used as a warming, aromatic herb for centuries. The leaves and twigs are employed to dispel cold, promote circulation, and relieve pain.

A decoction of the leaves is used to treat digestive complaints including indigestion, flatulence, and abdominal cramping. The aromatic properties are believed to stimulate digestion and relieve nausea.

In Vietnamese folk medicine, the essential oil is applied topically to treat muscular aches, rheumatic pain, and skin conditions. The oil is also used as an insect repellent and in traditional steam bath preparations.

Clinical studies on related Litsea species have shown potential anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects of the essential oil. These findings support the traditional uses but require further validation through controlled trials.

The dried leaves are used as a spice and flavoring agent in regional cuisines, imparting a citrus-lemon flavor to soups, curries, and fish dishes. The fruit is occasionally used in traditional beverages.

Modern applications include the use of the essential oil in perfumery as a citrus note and in aromatherapy for its uplifting and stress-relieving properties. The oil is also used as a natural flavoring agent in the food industry.

Industrial applications of citral extracted from Litsea citrata include its use as a precursor for the synthesis of ionones and vitamin A. The global demand for natural citral has increased interest in cultivation of this and related species.
