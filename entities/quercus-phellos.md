## Overview

Quercus phellos, commonly known as willow oak, is a deciduous tree native to the southeastern and central United States.

It is one of the most commercially important oak species for timber production in the South.

The species is named for its narrow, willow-like leaves which distinguish it from most other oaks.

Willow oak is widely planted as a street and shade tree due to its tolerance of urban conditions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae, order Fagales, family Fagaceae, genus Quercus, section Lobatae (red oaks).

Quercus phellos was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum.

The species is placed in the red oak group based on its bristle-tipped leaf lobes and acorn morphology.

Hybridization with other red oaks including Quercus falcata and Quercus palustris is documented.

**Growth form** is a medium to large deciduous tree reaching 20-30 meters in height.

Trunk diameter at maturity ranges from 60-100 cm, occasionally exceeding 120 cm.

The crown is pyramidal in youth, becoming broadly rounded with age.

Bark is dark gray to brown, smooth on young trees, developing tight ridges with age.

Leaves are simple, alternate, narrowly lanceolate, 5-12 cm long and 1-2 cm wide.

Leaf margins are entire or with a few shallow, bristle-tipped lobes near the apex.

The upper leaf surface is dark green and glabrous; the lower surface is pale green.

Leaves turn yellow to yellow-brown in autumn, occasionally with russet tints.

Flowers are monoecious; male catkins are pendulous, 5-8 cm long, appearing with the leaves.

Female flowers are small and inconspicuous, borne in the axils of new leaves.

Acorns are small, 8-14 mm long and 6-10 mm wide, hemispherical to broadly ovoid.

The acorn cup is shallow, covering approximately one-quarter to one-third of the nut.

Acorns mature in two seasons, dropping in autumn of the second year.

## Distribution and Habitat

Quercus phellos is native to the southeastern and south-central United States.

Its range extends from coastal New Jersey southward to northern Florida and westward to eastern Texas.

The species extends northward along the Mississippi River valley to southeastern Missouri.

Isolated populations occur in the Ohio River valley of southern Illinois and Indiana.

It grows from sea level to approximately 450 meters elevation.

The species is a dominant component of bottomland hardwood forests along river floodplains.

It also occurs on moist upland sites, terraces, and well-drained alluvial soils.

Common associates include [[liquidambar-styraciflua]], and [[quercus-robur]], [[platanus-occidentalis]].

It prefers moist, well-drained, loamy to clay-loam soils with pH 4.5-6.5.

The species tolerates poorly drained sites and periodic flooding better than most oaks.
