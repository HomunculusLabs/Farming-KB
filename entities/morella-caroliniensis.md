## Overview

**Morella caroliniensis** (P.Wilson) Kartesz, known as the Carolina bayberry or southern bayberry, is a semi-evergreen to deciduous shrub in the family Myricaceae. It is native to the southeastern United States and is closely related to the widespread *M. cerifera*.

The species is distinguished from *M. cerifera* by its deciduous habit, smaller stature, and more restricted distribution in Atlantic coastal plain habitats. It was historically included within *Myrica* before being transferred to *Morella*.

Like other Myricaceae, it forms actinorhizal root nodules with *Frankia* bacteria, enabling atmospheric [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]] rates in *Morella* species can reach 2 to 20 kg per hectare per year depending on site conditions. ## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Fagales
- **Family:** Myricaceae
- **Genus:** *Morella* L.
- **Species:** *M. caroliniensis* (P.Wilson) Kartesz

The basionym is *Myrica caroliniensis* P.Wilson, published in 1921. John Kartesz transferred the species to *Morella* as part of a broader taxonomic revision of Myricaceae in the 1990s.

Some authorities have treated *M. caroliniensis* as a variety of *M. cerifera* (as *M. cerifera* var. *caroliniensis*), but molecular and morphological evidence supports its recognition as a distinct species. It hybridizes with *M. cerifera* where their ranges overlap.

### Morphology

*M. caroliniensis* is a multistemmed shrub typically 1 to 2.5 meters tall, occasionally reaching 3 meters. It forms loose, open thickets and spreads slowly by rhizomatous growth, though less aggressively than *M. cerifera*.

Leaves are alternate, obovate to oblanceolate, 3 to 8 cm long and 1 to 2 cm wide. The margins are entire or remotely toothed near the apex. Unlike *M. cerifera*, the leaves are deciduous to semi-evergreen, with most foliage abscising in winter.

The leaf surface is sparsely pubescent with simple hairs, particularly along the midrib beneath. Glandular punctations are present on both leaf surfaces, giving a slightly resinous appearance.

Flowers appear in early spring as unisexual catkins. Male catkins are 1 to 2 cm long with overlapping bracts. Female catkins are shorter, 4 to 8 mm, with reduced perianth.

Fruits are small drupes, 2 to 3 mm in diameter, covered with a waxy blue-white bloom. They are borne in dense axillary clusters and mature from mid-summer to early fall. Fruit wax content is lower than in *M. cerifera*.

The bark on young stems is smooth and gray, becoming darker and slightly rough with age. Branching is irregular and spreading, with slender twigs that bear prominent lenticels. The root system is fibrous and shallow, with extensive rhizomatous development near the soil surface.

Wood anatomy is typical of Myricaceae, with diffuse-porous xylem and inconspicuous growth rings. The wood is soft and light, with no commercial timber value.

## Distribution and Habitat

*M. caroliniensis* occurs along the Atlantic coastal plain from New Jersey southward to Florida and along the Gulf coast to Louisiana. It is most abundant in the Carolinas, Georgia, and northeastern Florida.

The species favors sandy soils in wetland margins, pine flatwoods edges, and coastal dune swales. It is frequently found in ecotones between upland pine forests and wetland communities.

Elevation ranges from sea level to approximately 50 meters. It is absent from the Piedmont and mountainous regions, being strictly a coastal plain taxon. Populations are generally associated with acidic, sandy, or peaty substrates.

The species tolerates periodic salt spray and brief saltwater inundation during storm events. It is more salt-sensitive than *M. cerifera*, which limits its distribution on exposed barrier islands. Optimal [[soil-ph]] ranges from 4.0 to 6.0.

Mean annual precipitation across its range is 1000 to 1500 mm. It is most competitive in areas with high water tables and frequent growing-season precipitation that maintains soil moisture.

## Ecology and Interactions

Root nodules containing *Frankia* actinomycetes enable *M. caroliniensis* to fix atmospheric nitrogen at rates comparable to other Myricaceae species. This nitrogen input benefits surrounding plant communities in oligotrophic coastal soils.

Bird species that consume the fruits include northern bobwhite, hermit thrush, and American robin. The persistent fruits provide a winter food source when other berry-producing shrubs have been depleted.

Fruiting density varies annually, with heavier crops produced following wet [[knf-leaf-mold-humus]] accumulation. The nitrogen-enriched litter supports higher microbial activity in the rhizosphere compared to surrounding non-fixing vegetation.
