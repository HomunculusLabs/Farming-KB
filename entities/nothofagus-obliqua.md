## Overview

Nothofagus obliqua (Mirb.) Oerst., commonly known as roble or roble pellín, is a deciduous tree in the family Nothofagaceae native to temperate forests of Chile and Argentina.

It is one of the most commercially important timber species in southern South America, valued for its moderately dense, reddish-brown wood suitable for furniture, flooring, and construction.

The species plays a keystone ecological role in the Valdivian temperate rainforest, forming pure stands or mixed forests with other Nothofagus species at elevations of 100–1000 m.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Fagales → Nothofagaceae → Nothofagus → N. obliqua.

**Authority:** (Mirbel) Oersted, 1871. Synonyms include Fagus obliqua Mirb. and Calucechinia obliqua (Mirb.) Kuntze.

The tree reaches 25–35 m in height with a straight trunk diameter of 1–1.5 m. The crown is broadly ovate and deciduous, dropping leaves in autumn.

Leaves are alternate, ovate to elliptic, 4–8 cm long and 2.5–4.5 cm wide, with coarsely serrate margins and prominent venation.

Bark on mature trees is dark gray to brownish, fissured into longitudinal plates. Young bark is smoother and lighter gray.

Flowers are unisexual and wind-pollinated, appearing with new leaves in spring (September–November). Male flowers are in pendulous catkins; female flowers are in small groups of 2–3.

Fruit is a small cupule containing three nuts (trigonal), approximately 5 mm long, maturing in March–May.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native range extends from approximately 33°S to 41°S in Chile (Coquimbo to Los Lagos regions) and the adjacent Andean slopes of Argentina (Mendoza to Neuquén).

It occurs in the Mediterranean-climate transition zone and the temperate rainforest belt, often on well-drained volcanic soils, alluvial deposits, and colluvial slopes.

Optimal annual precipitation ranges from 1000–2500 mm, though it tolerates drier mediterranean conditions at its northern range limit where it experiences summer drought.

Elevation range is primarily 100–1000 m, occasionally reaching 1500 m on Andean slopes. It is less cold-tolerant than N. pumilio but hardier than N. dombeyi.

## Wood Properties and Timber Use

Heartwood is pinkish-brown to reddish, moderately heavy (air-dry density 0.55–0.65 g/cm³), with a fine, uniform texture and straight to interlocked grain.

Mechanical properties include a modulus of rupture of 60–75 MPa and modulus of elasticity of 9,500–11,500 MPa, comparable to northern hemisphere oaks.

The wood is readily workable, glues and finishes well, and takes nails without splitting when pre-drilled. It seasons with moderate shrinkage (tangential 5.5%, radial 2.8%).

Primary uses include furniture, interior paneling, flooring, doors, window frames, veneer, and general construction. It is also used for cooperage and tool handles.

Heartwood has moderate natural durability against decay (Class 3), but sapwood is susceptible to insect attack and requires treatment for exterior use.

## Ecology and Silvics

N. obliqua is shade-intolerant in its juvenile phase, requiring canopy gaps or disturbance for successful regeneration. It is a pioneer to mid-successional species in montane forests.

Seed production is irregular (mast seeding every 3–5 years), with seeds dispersed primarily by wind. Seeds are short-lived and lose viability within 1–2 years.

Seedlings establish best on mineral soil exposed by disturbance. They are sensitive to frost in the first two years but develop cold hardiness with age.

The species forms ectomycorrhizal associations with numerous fungal genera, including Cortinarius, Descolea, and Laccaria, which are critical for nutrient uptake in volcanic soils.

Important animal associations include cavity-nesting birds (e.g., Magellanic woodpecker, Chilean pigeon), and browsing mammals (huemul deer, introduced red deer).

## Cultivation and Agroforestry

N. obliqua is increasingly planted in temperate regions worldwide, including New Zealand, Australia, the British Isles, and parts of western Europe, as a fast-growing ornamental and timber tree.

In New Zealand, it has naturalized in some areas and is considered a potential forestry species for areas too warm for radiata pine but too cold for eucalypts.

Seed germination requires cold stratification at 2–5°C for 30–60 days, followed by sowing in well-drained nursery beds. Germination rates are typically 60–80% from fresh seed.

In agroforestry systems, N. obliqua can serve as a component of silvopastoral systems in temperate highlands, providing shade for livestock and timber revenue.

Growth rates in plantations range from 1.0–1.5 m/year in height during the first decade, with mean annual increment of 12–20 m³/ha/year under favorable conditions.

## Related Species

N. obliqua hybridizes naturally with N. nervosa (roble de Neuquén) where their ranges overlap in Argentina, producing intermediates sometimes classified as N. leonii.

Other close relatives include N. dombeyi (evergreen coihue), N. pumilio (lenga), N. antarctica (ñire), and N. betuloides (guindo evergreen).

The genus Nothofagus, once placed in Fagaceae, is now recognized in its own family Nothofagaceae based on molecular phylogenetics, and is part of the Antarctic flora lineage.

## Conservation Status

N. obliqua is assessed as Least Concern by the IUCN, though its natural forests have been significantly reduced by conversion to agriculture and plantation forestry.

In Chile, an estimated 35–40% of original N. obliqua forests have been lost since European colonization. Remaining old-growth stands are concentrated in the Andes and coastal range.

Logging of natural stands is regulated in Chile under the Native Forest Law ( Ley 20.283, 2008), which requires management plans and restricts clearcutting in old-growth forests.

Ex situ conservation collections exist in arboreta worldwide, and provenance trials in New Zealand and Europe have identified fast-growing populations suitable for plantation development.
