## Magnolia macrophylla

## Overview

Magnolia macrophylla, the bigleaf magnolia, is a deciduous tree native to the southeastern and south-central United States. It produces the largest simple leaves of any tree native to North America, reaching up to 80 cm in length.

The species is prized in horticulture for its dramatic tropical-looking foliage and large, fragrant white flowers. Its common names include large-leaved cucumber tree and umbrella tree, the latter referring to the umbrella-like arrangement of its leaves at branch tips.

It was first described by André Michaux in 1803 from specimens collected in the Carolinas. The species is relatively uncommon in the wild but widely cultivated in temperate gardens worldwide for its striking ornamental character.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Magnolia macrophylla belongs to family Magnoliaceae, subfamily Magnolioideae, section Macrophylla. The full taxonomic authority is Michx. (1803), placing it among the most basal angiosperm lineages.

Key synonyms include Magnolia macrophylla var. ashei, now treated as the separate species Magnolia ashei (Ashe"s magnolia). Section Macrophylla contains only three species worldwide: M. macrophylla, M. ashei, and M. dealbata from the cloud forests of Oaxaca, Mexico.

Leaves are ovate to broadly elliptic, 30–80 cm long and 20–40 cm wide, green above and strikingly silvery-white beneath. They are clustered at branch tips in a pseudo-whorl arrangement that creates the characteristic umbrella-like canopy effect.

The petioles are stout, 5–12 cm long, and often flushed with pink or red. Leaf venation is prominently raised beneath, with 12–18 pairs of lateral veins visible on the lower surface.

Leaves emerge in mid-spring, unfolding from large, silvery-pubescent buds that are among the most conspicuous of any temperate tree. The tomentose bud scales leave a conspicuous ring of scars on the branch after abscission, providing a useful winter identification feature.

Fall color is typically a muted yellow-brown, occasionally golden-yellow in favorable years. The enormous leaves drop over a short period in late autumn, forming a thick mulch layer beneath the canopy.

Flowers are creamy white, strongly fragrant, 20–30 cm in diameter when fully open. Each flower has 6–9 tepals, with the three innermost bearing a distinctive purple or rose blotch at the base on the adaxial surface.

The fruit is an oblong-cylindric aggregate of follicles, 5–8 cm long, rose-pink when mature in late summer. Upon dehiscence, each follicle reveals a single scarlet seed suspended on a slender white thread, an adaptation for bird dispersal.

Bark on young trees is smooth, gray-brown, and thin. With age it develops shallow ridges and flat-topped plates, though it remains relatively smooth compared to other Magnolia species.

## Distribution and Habitat

The native range extends from southern Ohio and Kentucky through Tennessee, the Carolinas, Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, and into eastern Texas and Arkansas. Disjunct populations occur in the Sierra Madre Oriental of northeastern Mexico.

The species inhabits rich, mesic hardwood forests, ravine bottoms, limestone bluffs, and stream banks at elevations of 100–600 m. It shows a strong preference for deep, well-drained loamy soils with slightly acidic to neutral pH.

The species is most commonly found as a scattered understory component rather than forming pure stands. Individual trees are often widely separated, contributing to the species' reputation as locally uncommon throughout its range.

Habitat fragmentation poses a significant threat to populations at the periphery of the range. Small, isolated populations in Ohio and North Carolina have experienced declining recruitment due to altered disturbance regimes and deer herbivory on seedlings.

Conservation status varies by state. It is listed as S1 (critically imperiled) in Ohio and North Carolina, S2 in Virginia and Texas, and S3 in Kentucky. Globally it is ranked G4 (apparently secure) due to its broad overall range.

## Ecology

The flowers are beetle-pollinated, a trait consistent with the ancient evolutionary lineage of Magnoliaceae dating to the Cretaceous period. The large white petals and strong sweet fragrance attract primarily scarab beetles (Scarabaeidae) and longhorn beetles (Cerambycidae).

Beetles feed on the pollen and the carbohydrate-rich tissues of the inner tepals while simultaneously effecting pollination. The carpels are tough and leathery to withstand the destructive feeding behavior of the beetle pollinators.

Seeds are dispersed primarily by birds attracted to the bright red arils. Mockingbirds, thrushes, and catbirds are the most frequent consumers, digesting the fleshy aril and passing the hard seed intact.

The species is shade-tolerant in youth, persisting for years in the forest understory. However, it requires canopy gaps formed by treefall or blowdown to reach reproductive maturity.

Magnolia macrophylla serves as a larval host plant for several Lepidoptera species, including the tulip-tree beauty (Epimecis hortaria) and the sweetfern wave moth (Calocalpe undulata). The large leaves provide substantial forage for caterpillars.

## Cultivation

In cultivation, Magnolia macrophylla prefers partial shade to full sun in cooler climates with protection from hot afternoon sun. It requires consistently moist, well-drained soil and is intolerant of both drought and waterlogged conditions.

Hardiness spans USDA zones 5 through 8, though plants in zone 5 may suffer tip dieback on late-season growth. The species performs best in regions with warm, humid summers and reliable summer rainfall.

Young trees benefit enormously from wind protection due to the large, sail-like leaves that can be shredded by strong winds. A sheltered position near taller evergreens or buildings is ideal.

Growth rate is moderate, reaching 10–15 m in 20 years with a mature height of 12–18 m and a spread of 8–12 m. The crown is typically pyramidal in youth, becoming more rounded with age.

Propagation is by fresh seed sown in fall, which germinates the following spring after cold stratification. Seed loses viability quickly if allowed to dry and should be cleaned and sown immediately upon collection.

Grafting onto Magnolia × soulangeana rootstock is sometimes used for cultivar propagation. Cuttings are difficult to root, though semi-hardwood cuttings treated with IBA under mist have achieved limited success.

The species has no serious insect or disease problems in cultivation. Scale insects and southern root rot (Armillaria) can occasionally affect stressed trees, but healthy specimens are notably pest-resistant.

## Ethnobotany and Uses

The bark and roots were used by Cherokee and other southeastern Indigenous peoples for medicinal purposes, primarily treatment of fever, rheumatism, and coughs. Preparations were made as decoctions from the aromatic bark containing magnoflorine and other benzylisoquinoline alkaloids.

In modern horticulture, it is valued as a specimen tree in large gardens, public parks, and arboreta worldwide. The dramatic foliage and massive flowers make it a focal point in any temperate landscape.

Wood is soft, light, creamy-white, and close-grained, but has little commercial timber value due to the tree's rarity and slow growth. It has been used locally for crate construction, woodenware, and light woodworking projects.

The hybrid Magnolia × veitchii (M. macrophylla × M. grandiflora) combines the enormous leaves of M. macrophylla with the evergreen habit of M. grandiflora. This cross, first made at the Veitch Nurseries in England, demonstrates the species' importance in magnolia breeding programs.

Magnolia macrophylla has also contributed to the development of the "yellow magnolia" hybrids through crosses with subsection Mutelia species. These hybrids bring cold hardiness and large flower size into breeding lines that would otherwise lack these traits.

The species is diploid with 2n = 38 chromosomes, consistent with the base chromosome number for Magnoliaceae. This karyotype stability facilitates hybridization across sections of the genus.

Several cultivars have been selected for ornamental traits. 'Kaye's Clone' is notable for its particularly large flowers, while selections from the Ozark populations tend to have more compact growth habits.

The species received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit in 2012, confirming its value for UK and temperate gardens. It is featured prominently in the collections of the Arnold Arboretum, Kew Gardens, and the U.S. National Arboretum.
