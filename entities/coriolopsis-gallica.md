## Overview

*Coriolopsis gallica* (Fr.) Ryvarden is a polyporoid basidiomycete fungus in the family Polyporaceae, widely distributed across temperate and subtropical forests. It is a white-rot decomposer that colonizes dead hardwood, forming tough, sessile to effused-reflexed bracket fruiting bodies on trunks and branches.

The species has attracted pharmacological interest for its production of bioactive polysaccharides, triterpenoids, and phenolic compounds with antioxidant, antimicrobial, and immunomodulatory properties. Extracts of *C. gallica* have been investigated for antitumor and anti-inflammatory potential in preclinical studies.

Historically placed in the genus *Trametes*, molecular phylogenetic analyses support its placement in *Coriolopsis*, distinguishing it from *Trametes* species by pore morphology and genetic markers.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Basidiomycota → Agaricomycetes → Polyporales → Polyporaceae → *Coriolopsis* → *C. gallica*.

**Authority:** (Fr.) Ryvarden, 1972. Basionym: *Boletus gallicus* Fr., 1821.

**Synonyms:** *Boletus gallicus* Fr., *Polyporus gallicus* (Fr.) Fr., *Trametes gallica* (Fr.) Pat., *Coriolopsis occidentalis* (Klotsch) Murrill, *Coriolopsis polyzona* sensu auct.

**Fruiting body:** Sessile to effused-reflexed, 30–100 mm wide × 20–50 mm deep × 3–15 mm thick. Upper surface is concentrically zoned with alternating ochre, tawny, and dark brown bands. Surface texture is finely tomentose when young, becoming glabrous and often cracked with age.

**Pore surface:** Pale yellowish to cinnamon, pores angular to circular, 2–5 per mm. Tube layer is concolorous with the pore surface, up to 8 mm deep. Context is corky to tough-coriaceous, dual in structure with a darker thin upper layer.

**Spores:** 5–7 × 2–3 µm, cylindrical to allantoid, smooth, hyaline, inamyloid. Spore print is white. Basidia are clavate, 4-spored, with simple septa.

**Hyphal system:** Dimitic, with generative hyphae bearing clamp connections and skeletal hyphae that are thick-walled, unbranched, and dominant in the context.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Coriolopsis gallica* has a broad distribution spanning Europe, Asia, Africa, and the Americas. It is common in temperate deciduous forests of Europe and East Asia, and occurs in subtropical and tropical regions as well.

The fungus is exclusively saprotrophic, decomposing dead hardwood. Preferred substrates include oak, beech, birch, willow, and various fruit trees. It is commonly found on fallen logs, standing dead timber, and occasionally on worked wood including fence posts and garden structures.

Fruiting occurs year-round in mild climates, with peak abundance in autumn. It is one of the more persistent bracket fungi, often remaining on substrate for multiple seasons as a tough, weathered conk.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

Polysaccharides extracted from *C. gallica* fruiting bodies, particularly β-glucan-rich fractions, have demonstrated significant immunomodulatory activity. These compounds activate macrophages, enhance splenocyte proliferation, and stimulate cytokine production in murine models.

Methanolic extracts show potent antioxidant capacity through multiple mechanisms including DPPH radical scavenging, superoxide anion inhibition, and ferric-reducing antioxidant power. Total phenolic content of fruiting body extracts ranges from 15–35 mg GAE/g dry weight.

Antimicrobial screening has revealed activity against Gram-positive bacteria (*Staphylococcus aureus*, *Bacillus subtilis*) and several Candida species. The minimum inhibitory concentrations for bacterial targets range from 64–256 µg/mL for crude extracts.

Triterpenoids isolated from the species, including gallic acid derivatives and lanostane-type compounds, have shown cytotoxic activity against human cancer cell lines (HeLa, MCF-7) in vitro with IC50 values in the range of 20–80 µM.

Anti-inflammatory effects have been documented through inhibition of nitric oxide production in LPS-stimulated macrophages, mediated by downregulation of iNOS and COX-2 expression.

## Ecological Role

As a white-rot fungus, *C. gallica* degrades both cellulose and lignin in dead wood, playing a significant role in carbon cycling and soil formation in forest ecosystems. Its ability to persist on substrate for extended periods allows sustained decomposition activity.

The species contributes to deadwood biodiversity by providing microhabitat for invertebrates, including beetle larvae and mites that colonize the pore surface and context. It also serves as a substrate for bryophyte establishment in humid microsites.

In forest health contexts, *C. gallica* is not a pathogen of living trees, but its presence on orchard trees or ornamental timber may indicate advanced internal decay of already weakened or dead tissue.

## Cultivation and Biotechnological Potential

While *C. gallica* is not commercially cultivated for food, there is growing interest in its cultivation for medicinal extract production. Submerged fermentation techniques using liquid culture have been developed to produce mycelial biomass and extracellular polysaccharides at scale.

Optimal growth in liquid culture occurs at 25–28°C with pH 5.5–6.5, using glucose or maltose as carbon sources and yeast extract or peptone as nitrogen sources. Biomass yields of 15–25 g/L dry weight have been reported over 7–10 day fermentation cycles.

Solid-state cultivation on hardwood sawdust substrates has been explored for fruiting body production. Oak and beech sawdust supplemented with 10–20% wheat bran supports adequate colonization, though fruiting is less reliable than for established cultivated species.

The fungus has been investigated for bioremediation applications due to its lignin-degrading enzyme system. Laccase and manganese peroxidase production suggests potential for degradation of xenobiotic compounds including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and synthetic dyes.

## Similar Species

*Coriolopsis trogii* is a close relative with larger, more robust fruiting bodies and a darker, more uniformly brown upper surface. Genetic separation is confirmed by ITS sequence divergence.

*Trametes versicolor* (turkey tail) has a more conspicuously zonate upper surface with contrasting white and colored bands, a thinner context, and smaller pores (3–8 per mm). The two species can be difficult to distinguish in the field.

*Coriolopsis capitata* has a distinctly pileate habit with a more prominently cap-like upper surface, and is less commonly encountered.

## Traditional Use

*Coriolopsis gallica* has limited documented use in traditional medicine systems. In some regions of rural China, dried fruiting bodies are prepared as decoctions for general wellness and immune support, though this usage is less formalized than for *Ganoderma* or *Trametes* species.

In European folk traditions, bracket fungi including *C. gallica* were occasionally used as styptic agents and for wound dressing, applied directly to cuts as a hemostatic poultice. These applications likely derive from the absorbent, corky texture of the dried context rather than specific pharmacological knowledge.

Modern interest in the species is driven entirely by laboratory pharmacology rather than ethnomycological precedent, distinguishing it from many other medicinal polypores with established traditional use histories.
