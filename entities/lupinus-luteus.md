## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Lupinus luteus** L. belongs to the family Fabaceae, subfamily Faboideae, tribe Genisteae.

The species epithet *luteus* is Latin for yellow, referring to the characteristic flower color that distinguishes it from blue-flowered congeners.

It is an annual herb growing 30–90 cm tall with an erect, branching stem. The root system features a deep taproot that can extend 1–2 meters below the soil surface.

Leaves are palmately compound with 7–11 narrow leaflets. Leaflets are 3–6 cm long, linear to lanceolate, and sparsely pubescent on the lower surface.

Inflorescences are terminal, many-flowered racemes 10–30 cm long. Flowers are bright yellow, 12–16 mm long, with a reflexed banner petal.

The keel petal is strongly incurved. Flowering occurs from late spring through mid-summer, approximately 60–80 days after sowing.

Fruits are legume pods 4–6 cm long, densely pubescent, containing 4–6 large seeds. Seeds are mottled gray-brown, flattened-ovoid, and 6–8 mm in diameter.

Seed protein is rich in lysine but relatively low in sulfur-containing amino acids, complementing cereal-based rations.

## Distribution and Habitat

Yellow lupine is native to the western Mediterranean Basin, particularly the Iberian Peninsula, southern France, and northwestern Italy.

It has been naturalized in parts of central and northern Europe, the British Isles, southeastern Australia, and parts of [[comparison-fava-bean-vs-chickpea-vs-lentil-grain-legumes]] including white lupin and narrow-leafed lupin.

It is found in coastal dunes, scrubland, open woodland margins, and disturbed ground in its native range. It prefers Mediterranean climates with warm, dry summers and mild, wet winters.

Yellow lupine is moderately salt-tolerant and can be grown on marginally productive sandy soils that are unsuitable for cereals or other legumes.

## Ecology and Nitrogen Fixation

**Lupinus luteus** forms effective nitrogen-fixing symbioses with Bradyrhizobium lupini, nodulating freely on acidic soils where Rhizobium strains for other legumes may be ineffective.

It fixes an estimated 150–250 kg N/ha/year in agricultural systems, contributing significantly to soil nitrogen budgets in rotations with cereals.

The deep taproot mobilizes phosphorus and other nutrients from subsoil layers that are inaccessible to shallower-rooted crops.

This nutrient-scavenging ability makes yellow lupine particularly valuable in no-till farming systems on depleted sandy soils.

In its native Mediterranean habitat, yellow lupine colonizes sandy soils after disturbance and contributes to successional development of scrub communities.

The flowers are visited by bumblebees and other long-tongued bees, though the species is partially self-pollinating.

Foliage and seeds contain quinolizidine alkaloids (primarily lupinine and sparteine) that deter herbivory by insects and mammalian browsers.

## Cultivation and Uses

Yellow lupine is grown as a grain legume in parts of Poland, Germany, Belarus, and Australia, with total global production estimated at approximately 100,000 hectares annually.

It is sown in early spring in continental climates or as a winter annual in Mediterranean regions. Seeding rate is 80–120 kg/ha at 3–5 cm depth.

The crop matures in 110–140 days from sowing. Grain yields range from 1.5–3.5 t/ha depending on rainfall and soil fertility.

Seeds contain 35–45% protein and 10–15% oil, making them an excellent protein supplement for livestock rations.

Low-alkaloid "sweet" cultivars have been developed that contain less than 0.02% alkaloids, allowing direct use in monogastric feeds without heat treatment.
