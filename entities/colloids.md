## Description

[[soil-colloids]] are extremely fine particles of clay, organic matter, or long-chain
polymers that remain suspended in soil water unaffected by gravity. They form stable
aqueous gels or suspensions that serve as the primary sites for ionic bonding and
nutrient exchange in soil. Colloids are central to soil fertility because they hold
water reserves and bind nutrients that would otherwise leach away.

## Classification

- **Category:** Soil physical-chemical component
- **Types:** Organic colloids (humic), inorganic colloids (clay), synthetic colloids
- **Particle size:** 2–200 millimicrons (nanometres)
- **Charge:** Typically negative on particle surfaces

## Key Properties

- Colloid particles stay in suspension unaffected by gravity.
- They become active sites for ionic bonding and interchange.
- They form gels that hold soil water reserves.
- Surfaces are usually negatively charged — like root hairs — attracting positive ions.
- About 99% of nutrient ions in soil are held by colloids (Leeper, 1982).
- Humus-derived colloids are hundreds of times more effective than clay colloids in ion exchange.

## Natural Sources of Colloids

- Humus and humic decay products
- Bacterial secretions (polysaccharides forming soil gels)
- Finely ground graphite and silica
- Fine clays (illites, bentonite) deposited on clay pans from evaporation
- Grain flours and animal gelatinous flours (hooves, horns, sinew, albumen)

## Ionic Exchange on Colloids

- Colloids attract positive ions: sulphates, nitrates, and metals (sodium, calcium,
  magnesium, iron).
- Ammonia and sodium can dislodge bound ions, making them available to plant roots.
- Excessive sodium flushing leaches calcium and metallic ions to streams and seas.
- Ferric oxide colloids carry positive charges and stain water brown (typical of acid
  peat and pine forest waters).

## Synthetic Soil Conditioners

- Agrosoke, Ikedagel, Terrasorb — hydrophilic gels conserving up to 50% water.
- Most are acrylic-based (acrylamid polymers) as granules absorbing hundreds of times
  their weight in water.
- Applied at 6 kg/ha or more in nursery plants, row crops, desert plantings, transplanting.
- Useful in [[seed-pelleting]].

## Flocculation and Deflocculation

- Colloids can be flocculated (aggregated) by aluminium salts (sulphates).
- Acid rain can trigger flocculation naturally.
- Salt flocculates colloids until excess sodium deflocculates clays (above 1–5 ppm salt).
- This deflocculation is the mechanism behind dryland soil collapse.
- Gypsum and humus act as flocculating additives to maintain soil structure.

## What Destroys Colloids

- **Burning:** Destroys colloidal properties of surface clays permanently.
- **Excess sodium:** Deflocculates clays, disperses colloids, collapses soil structure.
- **Ploughing and clearing:** Oxidise organic colloids, expose them to degradation.
- **Fire and cultivation combined:** Create the cemented, dusty, hydrophobic conditions
  typical of desertified soils.

## Relevance to Cultivation

- Colloids are the primary nutrient-holding mechanism in soil.
- Very little clay is needed in sandy gardens to create a colloidal environment.
- Maintain humus and mulch to supply organic colloids.
- Apply gypsum on sodic soils to flocculate clays.
- Avoid burning and excessive sodium at all costs.

## Relevance to Mycology

- Fungi contribute to colloid formation through decomposition and polysaccharide secretion.
- Mycorrhizal networks interface with colloidal exchange sites.
- Colloid-rich soils support the food web sustaining fungal hyphae.
- Organic colloids from fungal breakdown are far more effective than clay colloids.
