# Harmane

## Overview

**Harmane** (1-methyl-9H-pyrido[3,4-b]indole) is a tricyclic
β-carboline alkaloid found in numerous plant species, foods, tobacco
smoke, and mammalian tissues including the human brain.

Its pharmacological significance centers on reversible MAO-A
inhibition, serotonin receptor agonism, and neurotoxic properties at
elevated concentrations. A principal β-carboline in *Banisteriopsis
caapi*, harmane enables oral activity of DMT in ayahuasca by
inhibiting peripheral MAO-A.

## Chemical Properties

**Chemical name:** 1-Methyl-9H-pyrido[3,4-b]indole

**Molecular formula:** C₁₂H₁₀N₂ | **MW:** 182.22 g/mol

**Structure:** Tricyclic β-carboline (indole + pyridine fusion) with
methyl substituent at N-1 position.

**CAS:** 486-84-0

**Physical properties:** Yellowish crystalline solid. MP 228–230°C
(dec.). Slightly soluble in water; soluble in ethanol, methanol,
chloroform, dilute acids. pKa 7.7.

**Spectroscopic:** UV-Vis λmax (MeOH) 253, 280, 330, 370 nm.
Fluorescence: ex 370 nm, em 440 nm.

## Pharmacology

**MAO inhibition:** Competitive, reversible MAO-A inhibitor (RIMA
profile), Ki 0.5–5 µM. Weak MAO-B inhibition (Ki > 50 µM).

By inhibiting peripheral MAO-A, allows DMT oral bioavailability in
ayahuasca — the pharmacological basis for the brew's oral activity.

**Serotonin receptors:** Agonist at multiple subtypes:
- **5-HT₂A** (Ki ~1–5 µM) — mild psychoactive/hallucinogenic effects
- **5-HT₂B** — cardiac valvulopathy risk with chronic use
- **5-HT₂C** — appetite and mood effects

**Other interactions:** Weak GABA_A benzodiazepine site binding
(Ki > 100 µM); imidazoline I₂ receptor binding (Ki ~5–20 µM);
CYP1A2/CYP2D6 inhibition.

**Neurotoxicity:** At elevated concentrations, induces apoptotic
neuronal death via oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction.
Elevated levels found in postmortem brain tissue of essential tremor
and Parkinson's patients; causality not established.

## Effects

**Low dose (5–25 mg oral):** Mild anxiolysis, mood elevation,
enhanced visual imagery (closed-eye), drowsiness.

**Moderate (25–100 mg oral):** Pronounced MAOI effects, potentiation
of tryptamines/phenethylamines, common nausea, mild visual
distortions at upper range.

**Duration:** 4–8 hours. **Safety:** Narrow therapeutic index. Above
100 mg: severe nausea, hypertensive crisis risk (tyramine + MAOI),
neurotoxic potential. Not used recreationally as standalone drug.

## Natural Occurrence

**Plant sources:** *Banisteriopsis caapi* (primary, 0.1–1.5% in
dried bark), *Peganum harmala*, *Passiflora incarnata* (trace),
*Tribulus terrestris*, tobacco smoke, coffee, rice, corn, soy sauce,
cooked meats (Maillard reaction product).

**Endogenous:** Detected in human brain, platelets, plasma at
nanomolar concentrations. Formed via Pictet-Spengler condensation of
tryptamine with acetaldehyde. Normal levels below pharmacological;
chronic dietary/smoking exposure can cause tissue accumulation.

## History

First isolated from *Peganum harmala* seeds in early 20th century.
Name from Hindi "harmal." Role in ayahuasca pharmacology elucidated
1960s–70s by Naranjo and colleagues. Neurotoxicity and movement
disorder associations researched since 2000s (Louis et al., Kansas
University — elevated harmane in essential tremor patients).

## Synthesis

**Pictet-Spengler route:** (1) Condensation of tryptamine with
acetaldehyde → tetrahydro-β-carboline; (2) Dehydrogenation/oxidation
using Pd/C or other oxidants → harmane. Alternative: Bischler-
Napieralski cyclization of N-acyl tryptamine derivatives.

## Legal Status

Unscheduled in most jurisdictions. Not listed under UN Convention on
Psychotropic Substances or US CSA. Pure form generally legal to
possess and research; sale for human consumption may be restricted
under novel psychoactive substance legislation (UK Psychoactive
Substances Act, etc.). Ayahuasca legality varies by jurisdiction.

## Metabolism

N-oxidation to harmane N-oxide, ring hydroxylation, glucuronidation/
sulfation of hydroxylated metabolites. Urinary excretion. Half-life
estimated 2–6 hours (animal data). Hepatic metabolism via CYP enzymes.
