## Overview

Aeonium arboreum, the tree aeonium or Irish rose, is a branching succulent shrub in the family Crassulaceae. Native to the Canary Islands, particularly Tenerife, it develops woody stems topped with rosettes of glossy, dark green to purplish-black leaves.

The species is widely cultivated in Mediterranean climates worldwide as a structural succulent. The dark-leaved cultivar 'Zwartkop' (syn. 'Schwarzkopf') is one of the most distinctive and sought-after succulents in horticulture, with near-black rosettes that provide dramatic contrast.

It produces large, conical panicles of bright yellow star-shaped flowers in late spring, after which the flowering rosette dies. First described by Philip Miller in 1768, the species name "arboreum" refers to its tree-like growth habit.

The genus Aeonium is almost entirely endemic to the Canary Islands archipelago, with approximately 35 species radiating from a single common ancestor that colonized the islands within the last few million years.

This rapid adaptive radiation is one of the most dramatic examples of island plant diversification, comparable to the Hawaiian silversword alliance and Darwin's finches in the Galápagos.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Order: Saxifragales
- Family: Crassulaceae
- Genus: Aeonium
- Species: A. arboreum (Mill.) Webb & Berthel. (1841)

Plants develop branching woody stems reaching 1–2 m tall in cultivation, occasionally taller in favorable conditions. Each branch terminates in a rosette 10–30 cm in diameter.

Leaves are spatulate to obovate, 5–15 cm long, with a small mucro at the tip. The species form has bright green leaves, while cultivar 'Zwartkop' produces deep purple to nearly black leaves, especially in full sun.

Rosettes are open and flat during active growth but close into tight cups during summer dormancy. This closing mechanism protects the growing point from desiccation during the dry season.

Flower panicles reach 20–40 cm tall, bearing hundreds of yellow flowers each with 6–9 petals. The inflorescence is a cymose corymb. After flowering, the rosette that produced the inflorescence dies, but the branch continues growing from lateral buds.

The roots are fibrous and shallow, adapted to capturing moisture from fog and brief rainfall in rocky volcanic substrates.

Stem height and branching pattern vary considerably among populations. Plants in exposed, windy sites remain compact with short internodes, while sheltered specimens develop elongated, arching branches.

## Distribution and Habitat

Aeonium arboreum is native to the Canary Islands, primarily Tenerife, where it grows on rocky cliffs, hillsides, and volcanic slopes from sea level to 1,200 meters elevation.

The Canary Islands have a Mediterranean-type climate with mild, wet winters and warm, dry summers. Aeonium arboreum has adapted to this by growing actively in winter and entering summer dormancy, when rosettes close tightly.

The species grows in full sun to partial shade, often colonizing north-facing cliffs where it receives protection from intense afternoon sun. It associates with other Canary Island endemics including Echium, Euphorbia canariensis, and various Asteraceae.

It has naturalized in coastal California, the Mediterranean basin, and parts of Australia. In some areas of coastal Southern California, it has become a common garden escape, growing on roadcuts and bluffs.

The volcanic soils of the Canary Islands are well-drained and mineral-rich but low in [[extending-growing-season]], allowing soil to dry between waterings. Reduce or cease watering entirely during summer dormancy, when plants close their rosettes and appear dormant.

The species is not frost-tolerant and is damaged below -2°C. In USDA zones 9–11, it can be grown outdoors year-round. Elsewhere, it is grown in containers that can be moved indoors during cold weather.

Propagation is by stem cuttings, which root readily in 2–3 weeks. Cuttings should be allowed to callus for 1–2 days before planting. [[jadam-pesticide-for-slugs-and-snails]] can damage young growth. Overwatering during dormancy [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]].

The flowers are a significant nectar source for Canary Island endemic insects and birds. The bright yellow color and abundant nectar production attract both bees and the Canary Island chiffchaff (Phylloscopus canariensis).

Aeonium species are an example of adaptive radiation on oceanic islands. The genus diversified from a single colonization event into approximately 35 species across the Canary Islands, adapting to different elevations, exposures, and moisture regimes.

The CAM photosynthetic pathway allows the plants to persist through the long, dry summer with minimal water input. During dormancy, [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]] drops significantly, conserving stored resources.
