## Chisel Plough Soil Conditioning

## Description

Chisel plough soil conditioning is a mechanical soil rehabilitation technique central to permaculture's approach to restoring degraded agricultural land. Unlike conventional mouldboard ploughing, which inverts and destroys soil structure, chisel ploughing uses narrow shanks with axe-edged profiles to vertically fracture compacted soil while leaving the surface largely undisturbed. [[geoff-wallace]]'s design:** A circular coulter slits the ground, followed by a steel shoe that opens it to create air pockets.
- Manufacturers: At least 6–7 makers in the USA, Europe, and Australia by the time of Mollison's writing.

## The Graze-Cut-Chisel Cycle in Detail

The full rehabilitation sequence over approximately one year proceeds as follows:

1. **First pass:** Chisel at shallow depth (5–10 cm) after rain. Soil fractures and air pockets form.
2. **Grazing response:** New growth emerges vigorously. Hard graze or slash this growth — the shock causes plants to shed up to 50% of their root mass, injecting organic matter directly into the soil profile.
3. **Second pass:** Chisel again at 23–30 cm on the same lines. Fractures extend deeper. Earthworm populations expand in the newly aerated zones.
4. **Repeat cycles:** Each graze-chisel cycle adds organic matter, deepens aeration, and expands the zone of biological activity.
5. **Mature result:** A 23 cm (9 inch) blanket of living, aerated soil with functioning water transport channels from valleys to ridges.

## Results

- 23 cm (9 inch) blanket of aerated, living soil develops over 1–2 growing seasons.
- Soil temperature increases up to 13°C in autumn on treated versus untreated soils.
- Dramatically improved water infiltration — all water flows into soil rather than running off.
- Increased nitrogen-fixing bacteria nodulation.
- Breakdown of subsoil and rock particles by carbonic and humic acids.
- Methane from decay aids seed germination.
- Previously boggy country supports vehicle traffic without sinking.
- Water filtered through soil runs clear into dams and rivers.

## Biological Succession After Treatment

The chisel plough creates the physical conditions for biological recovery, but the long-term maintenance of soil structure depends on establishing permanent deep-rooted vegetation. Without follow-up planting of deep-rooted perennials, trees, or vigorous pasture species, soil will gradually re-compact. Mollison emphasises that mechanical conditioning is a one-time intervention — the permanent solution is biological. The sequence typically moves from chisel-conditioned soil to pioneer [[plough-pan]] (compacted layer) at the base of each pass.
- **Rotary hoe:** Pulverises soil structure, destroying aggregates and creating a powdery surface crust prone to erosion.
- **Biological chisels ([[daikon-radish]], tree roots):** Achieve the same subsurface shattering slowly and without fossil fuel, but require more time — typically several years versus one to two seasons.

## Pitfalls

- Operating in too-dry conditions produces clods rather than clean fractures, leaving an uneven seedbed.
- Operating in too-wet conditions causes the fractures to collapse immediately, negating the aeration benefit.
- Without follow-up biological colonisation, the physical improvements degrade as soil re-compacts over 2–3 years.
- Heavy subsequent traffic (machinery or livestock) on treated areas will reverse the conditioning effect.
- On very steep slopes, tractor operation may be impractical — biological chisels are the appropriate alternative.

## Relevance to Cultivation

This technique represents one of the most effective transitional tools for converting degraded agricultural land into productive growing systems. For cultivators working with compacted sites, it offers a mechanised pathway to the same soil conditions that biological approaches (daikon radish, tree roots, earthworms) achieve more slowly. The critical insight is that mechanical conditioning is a one-time intervention — the long-term solution is permanent deep-rooted plants (forests or prairies) that maintain the open structure the chisel plough creates.
