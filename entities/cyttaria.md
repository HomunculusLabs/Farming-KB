## Cyttaria

## Description

*Cyttaria* is a genus of parasitic discomycete fungi ([[ascomycota]]) known
exclusively from galled branches and smaller twigs of 12 south-temperate
species of *[[nothofagus]]* (southern beech). The genus produces distinctive,
brightly colored, subglobose to conical stromata that emerge in clusters from
woody galls on the host tree. *Cyttaria* species are among the most visually
conspicuous [[fungal-parasites]] in the temperate forests of the [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]]
and have a unique coevolutionary relationship with their *Nothofagus* hosts.
The genus is also culturally significant as one of the few directly consumed
parasitic fungi, known as "llao-llao" in southern [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]].

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Leotiomycetes
- **Order:** Cyttariales
- **Family:** Cyttariaceae
- **Genus:** *Cyttaria*

## Key Facts

- **Host Specificity:** *Cyttaria* is restricted to *Nothofagus* species
  (southern beech), a genus of trees that dominates many temperate forests in
  the Southern Hemisphere. Twelve species of *Nothofagus* serve as hosts, and
  the association appears to be highly specific and coevolutionary.

- **[[geographic-distribution-of-psilocybe-species]]:** The genus is exclusively south-temperate in
  distribution, found in the *Nothofagus* forests of South America (Chile,
  Argentina, Tierra del Fuego), southeastern Australia (Tasmania, Victoria),
  and New Zealand. This distribution mirrors the Gondwanan distribution of
  *Nothofagus* itself and provides evidence for continental drift.

- **Gall Formation:** Infection by *Cyttaria* induces the formation of woody
  galls on branches and smaller twigs of *Nothofagus*. The galls are perennial
  and can be quite large, with multiple stromata emerging from each gall during
  the fruiting season. The galls may persist on the tree for many years.

- **Stromata:** The fruiting structures are distinctive and colorful, typically
  bright yellow, orange, or reddish, subglobose to conical, and produced in
  dense clusters. Each stroma contains numerous apothecial chambers (locules)
  in which asci and ascospores are produced. The stromata are fleshy and may be
  edible when young.

- **[[coprophilous-fungi-taxonomic-groups-distribution]]:** Korf (1983) recognized three groups within *Cyttaria*
  based on geographic position, host range, and other morphological features.
  This tripartite division corresponds roughly to the three major areas of
  *Nothofagus* distribution: South America, Australia, and New Zealand.

- **[[amanita-mushrooms-history-chemistry-and-cultural-significance]]:** In southern South America, *Cyttaria* species
  (locally known as "llao-llao" or "pan del indio" — bread of the Indian) are
  collected and eaten by local people. The fruiting bodies are consumed fresh or
  used in local dishes. This represents one of the few examples of a directly
  consumed parasitic fungus.

- **Nothofagus Forests:** *Nothofagus* forests of New Zealand can have sooty
  mold growth covering tree trunks up to 9 meters in height, demonstrating the
  rich fungal communities associated with these Southern Hemisphere ecosystems.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Southern Hemisphere Mycology:** *Cyttaria* is one of the iconic genera of
  Southern Hemisphere mycota, and its study has contributed significantly to
  understanding [[fungal-biogeography]] and the Gondwanan distribution pattern of
  organisms. The genus provides a compelling example of co-radiation between
  fungi and their host plants.

- **Coevolutionary Studies:** The strict host specificity and disjunct
  geographic distribution of *Cyttaria* make it an excellent model for studying
  coevolution, vicariance biogeography, and the historical biogeography of the
  Southern Hemisphere. The pattern of three species groups on three
  *Nothofagus* subgenera across three landmasses is a classic biogeographic
  pattern.

- **Forest Ecology:** In *Nothofagus* forests, *Cyttaria* galls can be abundant
  and affect tree growth and branch architecture. The galls themselves may
  serve as microhabitats for other organisms, including insects and secondary
  fungal colonizers.

- **Edible Fungi:** As one of the few edible parasitic fungi, *Cyttaria*
  represents an interesting case study in the intersection of mycology,
  ethnobotany, and food science. Its consumption is primarily local and
  traditional rather than commercial.
