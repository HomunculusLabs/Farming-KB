## Overview

**Magnolia × soulangeana**, the saucer magnolia, is a deciduous hybrid magnolia widely planted as an ornamental tree throughout temperate regions worldwide. It is one of the most recognized and commonly cultivated magnolias in gardens and public landscapes.

The hybrid originated around 1820 in the garden of Étienne Soulange-Bodin at Fromont, near Paris. It resulted from a cross between magnolia denudata (Yulan magnolia) and magnolia liliifera (lily magnolia), two Chinese species introduced to Europe in the late 18th century.

Saucer magnolia is a small to medium deciduous tree, typically reaching 5 to 8 meters in height with a spreading, rounded crown. Its large, cup-shaped flowers appear in early spring before the foliage, creating a dramatic floral display.

The species is valued for its adaptability to a wide range of temperate climates and soils. It has received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit and remains one of the most popular magnolias in cultivation.

The tree has been planted extensively in temperate cities around the world, from New York to Tokyo to Melbourne. Its combination of manageable size, spectacular bloom, and urban tolerance has made it perhaps the most widely planted ornamental magnolia globally.

## Taxonomy and Origin

The hybrid was first described by the French botanist Joseph Decaisne in the 1830s. The nothospecific epithet honors Étienne Soulange-Bodin, a Napoleonic cavalry officer who founded one of the first private botanical gardens in France.

Soulange-Bodin created the cross around 1820, though the exact date and circumstances are debated. The first flowering occurred in his garden at Fromont, and plants were distributed to nurseries across Europe within a decade.

The parent species, magnolia denudata and magnolia liliifera, are both native to central and eastern China. magnolia denudata contributes large, white, fragrant flowers and cold hardiness, while magnolia liliifera contributes the characteristic purplish-flushed tepal coloration.

The hybrid shows intermediate characteristics between its parents and is fully fertile. It backcrosses readily with both parent species, producing a complex range of intermediates that complicate taxonomic classification.

Several botanical varieties have been described based on flower color and form, but most modern horticultural treatments recognize the hybrid as a single, variable taxon. Cultivar selection has largely replaced formal botanical classification.

The natural variability of seedling-grown plants was a significant factor in the early popularity of the hybrid. Gardeners could select individual trees with preferred flower colors, leading to hundreds of informal selections.

## Morphology

Saucer magnolia is a deciduous tree with a broad, spreading crown and a relatively short trunk. Growth rate is moderate, typically 30 to 60 centimeters per year under good conditions.

Bark is smooth and grey on young trees, becoming shallowly fissured with age. Branching is ascending in the upper crown and spreading near the base, creating a symmetrical, vase-shaped silhouette in maturity.

Leaves are obovate to elliptic, 10 to 20 centimeters long and 6 to 12 centimeters wide. They are medium green above and slightly paler below, with a finely pubescent midrib on the underside. Autumn color is yellow-brown to bronze, generally modest.

Flowers are large, 10 to 15 centimeters in diameter when fully open, and borne before leaf emergence in early to mid-spring. Each flower has 9 to 12 tepals arranged in three whorls. The outer whorl is reflexed and often greenish, while the inner whorls are white flushed with pink to purplish-violet on the exterior.

Flower color varies considerably among seedlings and cultivars. The typical form is white inside with a distinctly purple-pink exterior, creating the characteristic bicolored "saucer" appearance that gives the plant its common name.

Individual flowers last 5 to 7 days under ideal conditions. A large tree in full bloom can produce hundreds of flowers simultaneously, creating one of the most conspicuous spring displays of any ornamental tree.

Fruit is an aggregate of follicles, 5 to 8 centimeters long, cylindrical to slightly curved. Individual follicles are green maturing to reddish-brown, dehiscing in autumn to reveal orange-red seeds.

Seed production is moderate, and germination follows the same warm-then-cold stratification requirements as other magnolias. However, seedling progeny are highly variable and rarely resemble the parent in flower color.

## Cultivation

Saucer magnolia is adaptable to a wide range of growing conditions, which contributes to its worldwide popularity. It is hardy in USDA zones 5 through 9, making it suitable for most temperate climates.

The tree performs best in deep, organically rich, well-drained loam soils. It tolerates clay and sandy soils if drainage is adequate, and prefers a slightly acidic pH of 5.5 to 6.5. Alkaline soils can cause chlorosis of the foliage.

Full sun to partial shade is acceptable, with best flowering in full sun. A sheltered site is important, as the early spring flowers are vulnerable to late frosts.

In regions prone to late spring freezes, the flowers of saucer magnolia frequently turn brown overnight, creating a disappointing display. This frost sensitivity has driven much of the breeding effort toward later-blooming selections.

Planting is best done in autumn or early spring. The root system is relatively coarse and transplanting larger specimens can be difficult. Young container-grown trees establish quickly and typically flower within 3 to 5 years of planting.

Pruning is best limited to the removal of dead, damaged, or crossing branches. Heavy pruning disrupts the natural form and reduces flowering. Any necessary pruning should be done immediately after flowering to avoid removing next year's buds.

Pest and disease problems are relatively minor. Coral spot fungus (Nectria cinnabarina) can infect damaged branches, and magnolia scale may occur in some regions. Good cultural practices generally prevent serious issues.

Bacterial leaf spot caused by Pseudomonas syringae can affect foliage in cool, wet springs. Affected leaves develop dark spots and may drop prematurely, but the condition rarely threatens tree health.

## Cultivars and Selections

Numerous named cultivars of Magnolia × soulangeana have been selected and introduced since the 19th century. Cultivars vary primarily in flower color, size, form, and timing of bloom.

'Alba Superba' is a popular white-flowered selection with large, pure white tepals flushed only faintly pink at the base. It is one of the most widely available cultivars and has been in commerce since the late 19th century.

'Brozzonii' has white flowers with a broad purple stripe on the exterior of each tepal. 'Lennei' produces large, deeply cupped flowers with rich purple-pink exteriors and a more compact growth habit.

'Rustica Rubra' is one of the darkest-flowered forms, with deep rose-purple tepals. 'Verbanica' is valued for its later flowering time, which reduces frost damage risk. 'Speciosa' has particularly large flowers and a vigorous, upright habit.

Modern breeding programs have produced cultivars with improved cold hardiness, later bloom times, and more stable flower colors. Many of these are complex hybrids involving backcrosses to the parent species or other magnolias.

Propagation of named cultivars is typically by summer softwood cuttings or grafting onto seedling rootstock. Chip budding in late summer is also effective and widely used in commercial nurseries.

## Landscape Uses

Saucer magnolia is a versatile ornamental tree suited to a wide range of landscape applications. Its moderate size, attractive form, and spectacular spring bloom make it a popular choice for residential, commercial, and public landscapes.

The tree works well as a specimen or focal point in lawns and garden borders. Its spreading crown provides light shade suitable for underplanting with shade-tolerant perennials and spring bulbs such as narcissus and scilla.

Mature trees develop significant landscape presence, with their smooth grey bark, wide-spreading branches, and dark green summer foliage providing visual interest beyond the spring bloom season.

In urban landscapes, saucer magnolia is widely planted in parks, median strips, and streetscapes. Its tolerance of pollution and compacted soils makes it a reliable choice for city environments, though drought stress can cause leaf scorch.

Saucer magnolia combines effectively with other spring-flowering trees and shrubs. It pairs well with [[forsythia-suspensa]], [[syringa-vulgaris]], and [[prunus-serotina]] for an extended spring display.

The tree is also a common feature in historic and estate landscapes, particularly in Europe and North America, where mature specimens over a century old can be found in many public gardens and arboreta.

The wood of saucer magnolia is soft, light, and pale yellow, with no significant commercial value. It has occasionally been used for carving and small turned objects in East Asia, where the parent species are more commonly utilized.
