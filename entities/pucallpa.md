## Description

Pucallpa is a major city in the Peruvian Amazon, situated on the Ucayali River. Along with Iquitos, it is one of the two primary urban centers where ayahuasca has received its greatest cultural elaboration as an adjunct to folk healing. Mestizo healers in Pucallpa gather together groups of patients, ranging in number from five or six to as many as twenty-five, in clearings at the edge of the Amazonian city for healing sessions involving ayahuasca.

## Classification

- **Type:** City / urban center
- **Location:** Ucayali Region, Peruvian Amazon
- **River:** Ucayali River (major tributary of the Amazon)
- **Significance:** Major center of mestizo ayahuasca healing traditions
- **Related city:** Iquitos (the other primary center of urban ayahuasca practice)
- **Accessibility:** Connected to Lima by road (unlike Iquitos)

## Key Facts

- Pucallpa, along with Iquitos, represents one of the two main urban centers where ayahuasca healing has been most elaborated in Amazonian mestizo culture
- Mestizo healers (ayahuasqueros) in Pucallpa gather groups of patients (5-25 people) for healing sessions in clearings at the edge of the city
- The city serves as a gateway to the central Peruvian Amazon and is home to both indigenous and mestizo populations
- Pucallpa is accessible by road from Lima (unlike Iquitos, which is river-accessible only), making it a significant point of contact between Amazonian plant medicine traditions and outside visitors
- The city"s healers practice traditional ayahuasca ceremonies including nighttime sessions, communal cup distribution, singing, whistling, and tobacco smoke rituals
- Pucallpa has become increasingly known internationally as a destination for ayahuasca tourism and ethnopharmacological research
- The Ucayali River region surrounding Pucallpa is rich in Banisteriopsis species used in ayahuasca preparation

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Pucallpa is one of the most important contemporary centers for the study and practice of Amazonian plant medicine, particularly ayahuasca. The city's healers maintain living traditions that connect modern practice to ancient Amazonian knowledge of psychoactive plants. For cultivators and researchers, Pucallpa represents a key location for understanding how ayahuasca traditions adapt to urban contexts -- the same healing practices that originated in remote forest communities continue to function in an urban setting with mixed indigenous, mestizo, and outsider populations. The city's accessibility makes it a practical entry point for field research into plant medicine cultivation, preparation, and therapeutic application. The Banisteriopsis vine used in ayahuasca preparations is cultivated and harvested from surrounding forest areas near Pucallpa.

## Cultural Context

- Mestizo healers conduct ceremonies in forest clearings at the city's edge
- Healing sessions always occur at night
- At about 10:00 p.m., the healer takes out a communal cup in which the ayahuasca potion is distributed
- Reciting orations and whistling to protect each person who drinks, he passes the cup around the circle
- He varies the amount of the potion in accordance with several factors, including his assessment of the patient's body weight or physical strength
- The optimal dose found by Rios Reategui was 7 mgm/kg of body weight (1962:48)
- Healers generally drink last, and an attempt is made by those present to reach a climax in their visions as close together as possible
- Dietary restrictions include abstaining from salt, lard, and sweets for 24 hours before and after sessions
- Sexual abstinence may also be required prior to ceremonies
- Sessions last until 2:00 or 3:00 a.m. -- some four to five hours of strong drug intoxication

## Ayahuasca Ceremony Structure

The ayahuasca healing sessions in Pucallpa follow a structured protocol documented in the ethnographic literature:
1. Patients gather in forest clearings with a healer (ayahuasquero) who conducts the drug ceremony
2. Healers carefully select their patients, tending to eliminate individuals who suffer from severe psychotic disorders
3. At about 10:00 p.m., the healer takes out a communal cup for distributing the ayahuasca potion
4. Reciting orations and whistling to protect each person who drinks, he passes the cup around the circle
5. He varies the amount of potion based on patient body weight and physical strength
6. Healers generally drink last
7. People sit around smoking and sometimes chatting for about half an hour, waiting for effects
8. Someone may rise to vomit or defecate off to the side -- no effort is made to hide these sounds
9. Healers use this time to stress the effectiveness of the purge
10. The healer moves around the circle with his schacapa rattle, contacting each person in turn
11. At 2:00-3:00 a.m., after four to five hours of strong drug intoxication, the session concludes

## Healer Training and Practice

Ayahuasqueros in Pucallpa spend years developing their practice:
- Learning to identify and harvest the correct Banisteriopsis species
- Mastering the preparation of the brew (cutting and boiling the vine for several hours)
- Memorizing healing songs (icaros) and whistling incantations
- Studying with established healers as apprentices
- Learning to diagnose different categories of magical illness through visionary experience
- Developing the ability to maintain an omnipotent stance during ceremonies
- Understanding dietary restrictions and their enforcement

## Significance for Ethnopharmacology

Pucallpa's accessibility has made it a key site for ethnopharmacological research into ayahuasca. The optimal dose of 7 mgm/kg of body weight was established by Rios Reategui (1962) working in this region. The additive plants used in ayahuasca preparations near Pucallpa have been partially identified: Datura suaveolens (toe) and chacruna (Banisteriopsis rusbyana leaves containing dimethyltryptamine). The city remains a living laboratory for studying how traditional plant knowledge adapts to urban and modernizing contexts.
