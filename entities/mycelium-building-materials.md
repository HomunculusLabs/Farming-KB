# Mycelium Building Materials

## Description

Mycelium building materials encompass a class of construction and insulation products manufactured from fungal mycelium grown on agricultural or waste substrates. The mycelium acts as a natural binding agent, colonizing and fusing substrate particles into solid, shaped forms. These materials represent a sustainable alternative to conventional construction products like concrete, fiberglass insulation, synthetic packaging, and plastic composites. The technology was pioneered commercially by companies such as Ecovative Design and has since expanded to include DIY and small-farm applications.

## Classification

- **Category:** Biocomposite materials
- **Primary component:** Fungal mycelium (chitin-based structural matrix)
- **Substrate base:** Agricultural waste (sawdust, straw, hemp hurds, corn stalks)
- **Product types:** Insulation boards, structural blocks, packaging, textiles

## Key Facts

### Insulation Properties

Dried mycelium exhibits excellent insulation characteristics:

- **R-value:** Approximately 2.9 at 2-inch thickness (comparable to commercial fiberglass insulation)
- **Non-conductive:** Natural thermal resistance from web-like cellular architecture
- **Fire-retardant:** Dried mycelium (skeletonized chitin) resists combustion
- **Hydrophobic:** Beads water away unless completely saturated
- **Lightweight:** Low density reduces structural load

### Structural Blocks

When air-dried or freeze-dried, colonized growing medium forms a durable building composite:

- **Formation:** Mycelium-colonized substrate shaped into blocks
- **Bonding:** Blocks fuse together with light moisture application between layers (no mortar or chemical adhesives needed)
- **Curing time:** Blocks naturally fuse within days
- **Strength:** Comparable tensile strength to concrete and steel
- **Flexibility:** Slight mycelium flexibility reduces fissure cracking
- **Longevity:** Lasts indefinitely when kept dry
- **Shaping:** Can be cut with band saws or formed in molds

### Production from Spent Substrate

Mushroom cultivators can produce mycelium building materials as a secondary revenue stream using spent growing substrate:

1. **Dried columns:** Spent oyster mushroom substrate columns dried and cut with band saw into boards
2. **Shredded insulation:** Dried substrate shredded into fluffy material for blow-in insulation
3. **Hydropulped products:** Substrate mixed with waste paper and hydropulped into cardboard/paper products
4. **Molded forms:** Freshly colonized substrate pressed into molds for custom shapes

### Vapor Barrier Requirement

A critical consideration for mycelium insulation: apply an exterior vapor barrier to prevent the material from absorbing sustained moisture. Without this barrier, the mycelium can rehydrate and return to active vegetative growth, potentially attacking the structure it was insulating. This only occurs under conditions of overwhelming continuous moisture, which would cause conventional mold and rot problems as well.

### Species Selection for Materials

Notable species for mycelium building applications:

- **Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.):** Fast colonization, good for insulation and packaging
- **Reishi (Ganoderma spp.):** Forms dense, rubber-like material; water-repellent; holds shape well
- **Shiitake (Lentinula edodes):** Identifiable by dark brown zones; good for antimicrobial applications
- **Birch polypore (Piptoporus betulinus):** High fiber content, durable
- **Various polypores:** Tough, fibrous, long-lasting in the environment

### Biotextile Potential

Cotter reports isolating fungal strains suitable as rubber replacements:

- Difficult to tear (high tensile strength)
- Moisture-resistant
- Possess natural antibiotic properties
- Can be expanded on sawdust and formed into molds or sheets
- Prototypes include flip-flops and similar consumer goods

### Paper and Packaging

Spent oyster columns or sawdust-based fruiting substrate can be:

- Shredded and mixed with waste paper
- Hydropulped into new cardboard or paper products
- Mycelium acts as natural binder, potentially replacing chemical binders used in paper industry
- Initially hydrophobic (water-repellent), only breaking down when soaked
- Gives controlled shelf life and compostability at end of use

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Waste Stream Monetization

Mushroom farms generate substantial spent substrate. Rather than composting or disposing of this material, cultivators can:

- Dry and cut insulation boards for sale
- Shred for blow-in insulation products
- Hydropulp into biodegradable packaging
- Form into structural blocks for non-load-bearing applications
- Create retail packaging for their own mushroom products

### Circular Economy Integration

Mycelium materials close the loop in agricultural production:

1. Agricultural waste → mushroom substrate
2. Substrate → mushroom production (food)
3. Spent substrate → mycelium building materials (shelter)
4. End-of-life materials → compost → soil amendments → crop production

### Low Technology Entry

Unlike many green building materials, mycelium products can be manufactured with minimal equipment:

- Growing containers (standard cultivation infrastructure)
- Drying space (passive air drying or food dehydrators)
- Band saw for cutting boards
- Hydropulper (available used) for paper/packaging products
- Simple molds for shaped products

## Safety and Limitations

- Keep mycelium insulation dry with vapor barriers
- Test prototypes before large-scale application
- Fire testing recommended for building code compliance
- Moisture management is the primary long-term maintenance concern
- Building code approval varies by jurisdiction
