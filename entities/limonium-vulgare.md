Despite its common name, sea lavender is not related to true lavenders but belongs to the leadwort family alongside sea pinks and plumbagos.

The plant is valued both ecologically for its salt marsh role and horticulturally for its long-lasting flowers used in dried arrangements.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Limonium vulgare Mill., family Plumbaginaceae, subfamily Limonioideae. The species was described by Philip Miller in 1768.

The genus Limonium contains approximately 350 species distributed across saline habitats worldwide, with a center of diversity in the Mediterranean.

Sea lavender is a herbaceous perennial growing 15 to 40 centimeters tall from a thick, woody rootstock.

The rootstock produces a basal rosette of leaves and multiple flowering stems arising from a persistent crown.

Leaves are basal, oblong to spoon-shaped, 5 to 15 centimeters long, with a leathery texture and entire margins.

## Salt Tolerance and Physiology

Limonium vulgare is a facultative halophyte capable of tolerating salinities from near-freshwater to full seawater strength.

The species employs salt excretion through specialized glandular trichomes on leaf surfaces, a mechanism shared with other Limonium species.

These salt glands actively secrete excess sodium and chloride ions, preventing toxic accumulation in leaf tissues.

Excreted salt crystals form visible deposits on leaf surfaces that are subsequently removed by wind and rain.

Sea lavender also accumulates compatible solutes including proline and glycine betaine to maintain cellular osmotic balance.

The succulent leaf texture partially results from water storage in vacuoles, which dilutes intracellular salt concentrations.
