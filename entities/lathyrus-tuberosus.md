# Earthnut Pea (Lathyrus tuberosus)

## Overview

Lathyrus tuberosus, commonly known as the earthnut pea, tuberous pea, or tine-tare, is a perennial climbing legume notable for producing edible underground tubers. Native to Europe and Western Asia, this species has a long history of human use as a food crop, particularly during periods of scarcity. Despite its historical importance, the earthnut pea has largely faded from commercial cultivation and is now considered an underutilized or orphan crop with significant potential for permaculture and [[phosphate-accumulators-sustainable-agriculture]] systems. The plant combines the nitrogen-fixing capabilities typical of legumes with the rare ability among its family to produce carbohydrate-rich edible tubers, making it a uniquely valuable species for food forest design sign and perennial polyculture systems.
Among its many common names, "earthnut pea" reflects the tubers' resemblance to small nuts, while "tine-tare" is an older English vernacular derived from the plant's association with wild tares. In some European regions it is called "groundnut pea," though this name invites confusion with the unrela apios americana cana. The species epithet *tuberosus* directly references its most distinctive feature: the production of underground tubers, a trait shared with only a handful of other legume species worldwide.

## Taxonomy

The earthnut pea belongs to the following taxonomic hierarchy:
- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Subkingdom:** Tracheobionta (vascular plants)
- **Division:** Magnoliophyta (flowering plants)
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida (dicotyledons)
- **Order:** Fabales
- **Family:** Fabaceae (legume family)
- **Subfamily:** Faboideae
- **Tribe:** Fabeae (formerly Vicieae)
- **Genus:** Lathyrus
- **Species:** L. tuberosus
The genus Lathyrus contains approximately 160 species of climbing or trailing herbs distributed across [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] worldwide. Lathyrus tuberosus was first formally described by Carl Linnaeus in his 1753 *Species Plantarum*, though the plant had been known and used by humans for millennia prior to formal taxonomic classification.
Several botanical varieties and subspecies have been described over the centuries, though modern taxonomic treatments tend to recognize L. tuberosus as a single, morphologically variable species without formal infraspecific taxa. The species has a chromosome number of 2n = 14, consistent with many other members of the genus. Molecular phylogenetic studies place L. tuberosus within the Lathyrus section Lathyrus, alongside species such as L. sativus and L. cicera, supporting the close evolutionary relationships inferred from morphology.

## Morphology

Lathyrus tuberosus is a herbaceous perennial vine that typically grows 30 to 120 centimeters in height, though it can exceed this range under ideal conditions with adequate support. The plant exhibits the following morphological characteristics:
**Stems:** Slender, climbing or trailing stems that are distinctly winged, a characteristic feature of many Lathyrus species. The wings run along the length of the stem's angles, providing [[comparison-silicon-vs-calcium-in-plant-defense-and-structural-support]] and increasing photosynthetic surface area.
**Leaves:** Pinnately compound leaves with 2 to 4 pairs of leaflets, each leaflet being ovate to lanceolate in shape and 2 to 5 centimeters long. The terminal point of each leaf terminates in a branched tendril that enables the plant to climb and scramble over surrounding vegetation and structures.
**Flowers:** Showy, irregular (papilionaceous) flowers typical of the Faboideae subfamily. Flowers are borne in racemes of 3 to 8 blooms and are rose-red to pink in color, measuring 12 to 20 millimeters in length. The standard petal is the largest and most conspicuous, while the keel petals enclose the reproductive organs. Flowering occurs from June through August in its native range.
**Fruit:** Linear pods 3 to 5 centimeters long and 6 to 8 millimeters wide, containing 4 to 10 seeds. The pods are brown and dehiscent at maturity, splitting along both sutures to release seeds. Seeds are roughly spherical, 3 to 5 millimeters in diameter, mottled brown or grey in color, and have a hard seed coat that contributes to their longevity in the soil seed bank.
**Tubers:** The most economically significant feature of the plant. A chain of small, rounded to oval tubers develops along the root system, each measuring 2 to 5 centimeters in diameter. Tubers are produced at the ends of swollen roots and have a smooth to slightly wrinkled skin, typically brownish or cream-colored. The flesh is firm and white to pale yellow, with a distinctive flavor often described as resembling chestnuts, sweet potatoes, or a cross between the two. Individual plants can produce dozens of tubers, though yields per plant remain modest compared to conventional root crops.

**Root System:** Extensive and deep, featuring both fibrous roots and the characteristic tuber-bearing roots. The root system contributes significantly to soil structure improvement and the plant's [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]].
**Rhizobial Symbiosis:** The roots form nitrogen-fixing nodules in association with Rhizobium leguminosarum biovar viciae, the same bacterial symbiont associated with peas, lentils, and vetches. Nodulation typically begins 3 to 4 weeks after seedling emergence and continues throughout [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]. The nitrogen contribution to the soil is estimated at 30 to 80 kilograms per hectare per year depending on growing conditions, making the species a meaningful contributor to soil fertility in mixed plantings.
**Pollination Biology:** Flowers are primarily bee-pollinated, with bumblebees (*Bombus* spp.) and honeybees (*Apis mellifera*) serving as the principal pollinators. The flowers exhibit a typical papilionaceous structure adapted for bee visitation, with the keel petals functioning as a landing platform. The plant is partially self-compatible but produces higher seed set and more vigorous offspring through cross-pollination. Flowering duration is typically 4 to 6 weeks per plant, providing an extended nectar and pollen resource for pollinators during the mid-summer period.

## Distribution

Lathyrus tuberosus is native to a broad swath of Europe and Western Asia, with its natural range extending from France and the British Isles eastward through Central Europe to Russia, and southward into the Mediterranean basin including the Iberian Peninsula, Italy, the Balkans, Greece, and Turkey. It is also found in North Africa, particularly in Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia. The species displays considerable ecological amplitude, occurring across a wide range of climatic conditions from the Atlantic coast to the continental interior.

Beyond its native range, the species has become naturalized in several regions, most notably in parts of [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]], where it occasionally escapes cultivation and establishes self-sustaining populations. It has also been introduced to Australia and New Zealand, though it is less widespread in the [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]].

The plant favors dry, open habitats including grasslands, meadows, scrubland, woodland edges, roadsides, and railway embankments. It shows a strong preference for well-drained, sandy or gravelly soils and is tolerant of calcareous and slightly alkaline substrates. It is typically found at elevations from sea level to approximately 1,500 meters, though it can occur at higher altitudes in southern parts of its range.
In parts of its native range, particularly in Eastern Europe and the Balkans, earthnut pea populations have declined due to agricultural intensification, habitat destruction, and the conversion of semi-natural grasslands to arable monocultures. Conservation organizations in several European countries have noted the species' declining status and have recommended protection of remaining wild populations and their grassland habitats.

## Nutrition

The tubers of Lathyrus tuberosus are notable for their nutritional profile, particularly their relatively high protein content compared to other tuber crops:

- **Protein:** Approximately 11 to 13 percent by fresh weight, which is exceptionally high for a tuber crop. By comparison, potatoes contain roughly 2 percent protein, and sweet potatoes about 1.6 percent. This makes earthnut pea tubers one of the most protein-rich tuberous foods available.
- **Carbohydrates:** Starch is the primary carbohydrate, providing the bulk of caloric content along with dietary fiber.
- **Dietary Fiber:** Moderate levels contribute to digestive health and satiety.
- **Vitamins and Minerals:** Tubers contain appreciable amounts of B vitamins, vitamin C, potassium, phosphorus, and iron, though comprehensive micronutrient analyses remain limited in the scientific literature.
- **Safety:** An important consideration is that the seeds (but not the tubers) of Lathyrus species contain β-N-oxalyl-L-α,β-diaminopropionic acid (ODAP, also known as β-ODAP or BOAA), a neurotoxic amino acid that can cause lathyrism, a debilitating neurological condition, when consumed in large quantities over extended periods. The tubers of L. tuberosus contain only trace, non-harmful amounts of ODAP and are considered safe for regular consumption. This distinction is critical: the seeds should be avoided as food, while the tubers present no known toxicity concerns.
- **Culinary Quality:** The tubers' culinary appeal extends beyond nutrition. When cooked, they develop a firm, waxy texture that holds together well in soups and stews, yet they can also be roasted to a crispy exterior. The flavor is sweet, nutty, and subtly earthy, with aromatic notes that distinguish it from conventional tuber crops. Some culinary comparisons liken the taste to a cross between roasted chestnuts and sweet corn, with a faintly peppery finish.

## Historical and Culinary Significance

The earthnut pea has a documented history of human use stretching back to classical antiquity. The Roman naturalist Pliny the Elder (23 to 79 CE) referenced the plant in his *Naturalis Historia*, noting its edible tubers and their consumption by rural populations. In sixteenth-century England, John Gerard described the earthnut pea in his influential *Herbal* (1597), praising the tubers as wholesome and nutritious when boiled or roasted. Other Renaissance herbalists including Rembert Dodoens and Leonhart Fuchs also documented the species, reflecting its widespread familiarity across European botanical and culinary traditions of the period.

Throughout European history, the earthnut pea served as an important supplementary and famine food. During periods of crop failure, war, and economic hardship, rural populations across France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and Eastern Europe turned to wild-harvested earthnut pea tubers as a reliable calorie source. The tubers were prepared in numerous ways: roasted in ashes, boiled, fried in butter or oil, mashed, or incorporated into stews and soups.
Archaeological evidence from European sites suggests that earthnut pea tubers were gathered and consumed during the Neolithic and Bronze Ages, long before the advent of systematic agriculture. Carbonized tuber fragments have been identified at several prehistoric settlement sites in Central Europe, indicating that the species has been part of the human diet for at least 5,000 years.

In France, the plant was historically known as *macque* or *glycine tubéreuse* and was cultivated to a limited extent as a root vegetable, particularly in the central and southern regions of the country. French culinary records indicate the tubers were sometimes pickled or candied as a delicacy. In Germany, the plant was called *Knollen-Platterbse* and the tubers were roasted and consumed as a coffee substitute during the Napoleonic Wars and World War II when imported coffee was unavailable.

The tubers have also been used to produce a modest alcoholic beverage through fermentation, similar to the way potatoes are used in some vodka production traditions. In rural Spain, the plant is known as *guisante de raíz* and was traditionally gathered from the wild rather than cultivated. Spanish ethnographic studies from the early [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]] document the tubers being sold in local markets in rural Catalonia and Aragon, where they were valued as a winter food source when fresh vegetables were scarce.

In Eastern Europe, particularly in Poland, Ukraine, and Belarus, the earthnut pea was known as *groch grubowy* or *pestrushka* and held a place in traditional peasant cuisine. Tubers were commonly roasted alongside root vegetables during autumn harvest festivals. In Italy, the plant is called *cicerchia tuberosa* and was historically foraged in the Apennine foothills, where it grew abundantly in dry meadows and olive grove margins. Italian ethnobotanical records document its use in rustic breads and savory pies.
