     7|     7|## Description
     8|     8|
     9|     9|The Carreira phosphorus-pollution model describes the mechanism by which acidifying atmospheric pollutants disrupt soil phosphorus cycling, creating phosphorus nutritional stress in forest ecosystems. Proposed by Carreira et al. (2000), the model identifies two interconnected pathways: (1) changes in soil acidity that alter both inorganic and organic phosphorus cycling, and (2) nitrogen saturation that increases plant phosphorus demand. The model emerged from experiments in which tree canopies were misted with combinations of sulfuric acid and ammonium nitrate to simulate occult deposition of acidifying pollutants.

    12|    12|
    13|    13|- **Category:** Ecological model / Biogeochemical framework
    14|    14|- **Domain:** Soil science, mycorrhizal ecology, forest decline
    15|    15|- **Key processes:** Phosphorus sorption, acid phosphatase activity, nitrogen-phosphorus interactions
    16|    16|
    17|    17|## Key Facts
    18|    18|
    19|    19|- Acidifying pollutants were shown to increase phosphorus sorption capacity in soil, reducing the concentration of labile (plant-available) inorganic phosphorus.
    20|    20|- Acid phosphatase activity in soil decreased from 382 mg pNP h⁻¹ g⁻¹ (control) to 263 mg pNP h⁻¹ g⁻¹ (acid-misted), indicating reduced capacity to mineralize organic phosphorus.
    21|    21|- The net effect is an accumulation of organic phosphorus in soil that plants and fungi cannot access, effectively creating phosphorus limitation even when total soil phosphorus is adequate.
    22|    22|- Nitrogen saturation compounds the problem by increasing plant demand for phosphorus (Liebig's Law of the Minimum), creating a dual limitation.
    23|    23|- The model predicts that fungal biomass and phosphatase activity decline as a consequence of pollutant exposure, reducing the ecosystem's capacity to recycle organic phosphorus.

    25|    25|## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology
    26|    26|
    27|    27|The Carreira model has direct implications for understanding [[phosphorus-availability]] in managed fungal and plant systems:
    28|    28|
    29|    29|1. **Mycorrhizal function:** The model explains why ectomycorrhizal fungi — which produce phosphatase enzymes to access organic phosphorus — are critical buffers against pollution-induced phosphorus stress. Loss of mycorrhizal diversity compounds the problem.
    30|    30|2. **Substrate management:** In cultivation systems using organic substrates, maintaining appropriate pH is essential for phosphorus availability. Acidification of substrate (e.g., from metabolic acids or fertilizer reactions) can trigger the same phosphorus immobilization described by the model.
    31|    31|3. **Nitrogen-phosphorus balance:** The model reinforces the principle that nitrogen fertilization without corresponding phosphorus availability creates nutritional stress — applicable to everything from forest management to mushroom substrate formulation.
    32|    32|4. **Enzyme-based monitoring:** Phosphatase activity can be used as a practical assay for soil/substrate health, with declining activity signaling phosphorus cycling disruption.
    33|    33|5. **Remediation strategy:** The model suggests that addressing both acidity (liming) and phosphorus availability (organic amendments, mycorrhizal inoculation) is necessary to restore phosphorus cycling in acidified systems.
