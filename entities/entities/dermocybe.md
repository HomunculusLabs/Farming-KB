## Description

Dermocybe is a genus of ectomycorrhizal basidiomycete fungi, historically treated as a subgenus or section within Cortinarius but often recognized at the genus level based on distinctive pigmentation and spore morphology. Species in this group are commonly known as "corts" and are characterized by brightly colored fruiting bodies — typically red, orange, or olive-green — due to the presence of anthraquinone pigments. Dermocybe species are important ectomycorrhizal symbionts in coniferous and deciduous forests, and they have been shown to be sensitive indicators of atmospheric pollution and soil acidification.

## Classification

- **[[kingdom-fungi]]
- **Division:** [[basidiomycota]]
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** [[agaricales]]
- **Family:** Cortinariaceae
- **Genus:** Dermocybe (sometimes included in Cortinarius)
- **Ecological role:** Ectomycorrhizal symbiont
- **Habitat:** Coniferous and mixed forests, temperate and boreal zones

## Key Facts

- Markkola and Ohtonen (1988) found that Dermocybe species were significantly reduced in the presence of acidifying pollutants in Finnish forests, making the genus a sensitive bioindicator for atmospheric deposition impacts.
- Alongside Piloderma, Hebeloma, and "type 03" ectomycorrhizae, Dermocybe was among the most pollution-sensitive ectomycorrhizal taxa identified.
- The genus produces distinctive anthraquinone dyes (e.g., dermocybin, dermorubin) that have been used traditionally for dyeing wool and textiles.
- Species are typically medium-sized agarics with a cortina (cobweb-like partial veil) that disintegrates as the cap expands.
- Some Dermocybe species contain the toxin orellanine, which can cause severe kidney damage — a characteristic shared with other Cortinarius subgenera.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Dermocybe species occupy an important ecological niche and serve multiple roles in mycological research and practice:

1. **Bioindicator value:** The sensitivity of Dermocybe to acidifying pollutants makes it an early-warning indicator for nitrogen deposition and soil acidification in forest monitoring programs.
2. **Dye production:** The anthraquinone pigments (reds, oranges, greens) make Dermocybe species prized among mushroom dyers for natural textile coloring.
3. **Taxonomic complexity:** The ongoing debate about whether Dermocybe deserves genus-level status or should remain within Cortinarius illustrates the challenges of fungal taxonomy and the importance of molecular phylogenetics.
4. **Toxicity caution:** Several Dermocybe species are poisonous (orellanine-containing), requiring careful identification before any use. This toxicity risk overlaps with edible Cortinarius species, creating identification challenges.
5. **Mycorrhizal function:** As sensitive ectomycorrhizal partners, Dermocybe decline under pollution may reduce the functional diversity of the mycorrhizal community, potentially impairing nutrient uptake in forest trees.
