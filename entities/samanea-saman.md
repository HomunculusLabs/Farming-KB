# Samanea saman

**Samanea saman** (Jacq.) Merr., commonly known as the **rain tree**, is a large, fast-growing, deciduous tree in the family Fabaceae. It is native to northern South America and [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]] but has been widely planted throughout the tropics as an ornamental and shade tree.

The common name "rain tree" derives from the rhythmic folding of its leaves at night, which can cause accumulated dew or rain to fall from the canopy. Another explanation is that grass beneath the canopy often remains greener, suggesting moisture benefits from the tree.

A third explanation comes from the observation that the tree's nectar-rich flowers attract abundant insect activity, which may increase localized humidity.

Rain trees are iconic landscape elements in many tropical cities, known for their massive, umbrella-shaped crowns that can exceed 50 m in diameter.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Fabales
- **Family:** Fabaceae
- **Subfamily:** Caesalpinioideae
- **Tribe:** Mimoseae
- **Genus:** *Samanea*
- **Species:** *S. saman*

The species was originally described as *Acacia saman* by Jacquin in 1753, later transferred through several genera before being placed in *Samanea* by Elmer Drew Merrill in 1916. Some authorities still place it in *Albizia* as *Albizia saman*.

**Morphological features:**

The trunk is short, often buttressed at the base, reaching 1.5–2.5 m in diameter. Bark is dark gray, rough, and deeply fissured.

Leaves are bipinnately compound, 20–40 cm long, with 2–6 pairs of pinnae. Each pinna bears 3–9 pairs of leaflets, each 2–5 cm long.

Leaflets exhibit **nyctinastic movement**, folding downward at night and reopening in the morning. This circadian rhythm persists even in constant darkness.

This leaf movement is controlled by changes in [[bloomfield-turgor-pressure-and-hyphal-invasion]] at the base of the leaflets, mediated by the phytohormone auxin.

Flowers are borne in dense, pinkish-white heads, approximately 5 cm in diameter, arranged on axillary peduncles. Flowering is profuse and visually striking.

The fruit is a flat, straight or slightly curved pod, 10–20 cm long and 2–2.5 cm wide. Pods are dark brown, leathery, and contain 5–20 seeds embedded in a sweet, sticky pulp.

Pods persist on the tree for several months and often fall during the dry season, providing a timely food source for herbivores.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Samanea saman* is native to the neotropics, from the Amazon Basin and Guianas through Central America to Mexico. Its natural range extends from about 10°N to 18°S latitude.

It has been introduced and naturalized throughout the tropics, including [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]], Africa, Hawaii, and the Pacific Islands. It is widely planted in India, Sri Lanka, and the Philippines.

The species grows from sea level to about 500 m elevation. It prefers seasonally dry climates with annual rainfall of 1,000–2,500 mm and a distinct dry season.

It tolerates a wide range of soils but grows best on deep, well-drained, fertile alluvial soils. It is also found on clay, loam, and even shallow rocky soils, though growth is slower on poor sites.

It is frost-sensitive and requires mean annual temperatures above 22°C. Young trees are particularly vulnerable to cold damage and may require protection during establishment.

## Ecology and Applications

Like most mimosoid legumes, *S. saman* forms **nitrogen-fixing nodules** on its roots through symbiosis with rhizobia. This capacity makes it valuable for soil improvement in agroforestry systems.

The tree is a major component of tropical silvopastoral systems, especially in Central and South America. Its broad canopy provides excellent shade for cattle in pastures.

The sweet pod pulp is palatable to livestock and wildlife. Cattle, horses, and goats consume fallen pods eagerly, making the species a dual-purpose shade-and-fodder tree.

In mixed cropping systems, the tree is interplanted with coffee, cacao, and various fruit trees. The nitrogen contribution from leaf litter and [[mycorrhizal-root-exudates-pathogen-interactions]] benefits companion crops.

The dense, wide-spreading canopy creates significant shade that suppresses weed growth and modifies microclimate beneath. However, it can also be overly competitive with understory crops if planted too densely.

## Cultivation and Uses

**Propagation:** Seeds require mechanical scarification or hot water treatment to break dormancy. Germination is rapid, typically within 3–7 days. [[comparison-seed-starting-indoors-vs-direct-seeding-techniques]] and [[mollison-designers-plant-nursery-seedling-production]] methods are both effective.

**Growth:** Rain trees grow rapidly, reaching 10–15 m in height within 10 years. Mature trees can attain 25–30 m in height with crown diameters of 40–60 m.

**Wood:** Heartwood is dark brown with a golden luster, moderately heavy (specific gravity 0.50–0.60), and durable. It is used for furniture, cabinetry, carving, and decorative veneers.
