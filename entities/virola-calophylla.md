## Overview

Virola calophylla is a large tree in the family Myristicaceae, native to the Amazon basin in South America. It is one of several Virola species whose bark resin is used by indigenous peoples of the Amazon to prepare psychoactive snuff powders known as epená, paricá, or nyakwana.

The bark resin of Virola calophylla contains N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT) and 5-methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (5-MeO-DMT) as its principal psychoactive constituents. When prepared as a nasal snuff, these compounds are rapidly absorbed through the mucous membranes, producing intense visionary experiences.

Virola calophylla is closely related to Virola theiodora and Virola surinamensis, which are also used in traditional snuff preparations. The species are often difficult to distinguish in the field, and indigenous users may not differentiate between them.

The tree plays an important ecological role in lowland Amazonian forests, providing food and habitat for numerous animal species. Its seeds are dispersed by birds and mammals that feed on the bright red arils surrounding the seeds.

The genus Virola contains approximately 60 to 70 species of evergreen trees distributed throughout tropical lowland forests of Central and South America, with the greatest diversity in the Amazon basin. The genus is the largest in the family Myristicaceae.

The specific epithet "calophylla" derives from the Greek "kalos" (beautiful) and "phyllon" (leaf), referring to the attractive, glossy foliage of the species. The type specimen was collected by the English botanist Richard Spruce during his extensive explorations of the Amazon region in the mid-19th century.

Virola calophylla belongs to the informally recognized "Virola calophylla group," which includes several closely related species with similar morphology and overlapping distributions. Members of this group are often difficult to distinguish using vegetative characters alone, and reproductive material (flowers or fruit) may be required for accurate identification.

The family Myristicaceae also includes the commercially important nutmeg tree (Myristica fragrans), which produces the spice nutmeg and contains the psychoactive compounds myristicin and elemicin.

## Morphology

Virola calophylla is a medium to large canopy tree, typically growing 15 to 35 meters tall with a straight, cylindrical trunk 40 to 80 centimeters in diameter. The trunk is buttressed at the base in larger individuals, a common feature of rainforest canopy trees.

The bark is smooth to finely fissured, grayish-brown to reddish-brown, and exudes a thin, blood-red to reddish-orange resin when cut. This resin is the part of the plant used in traditional snuff preparations. The red coloration is distinctive and helps distinguish Virola species in the field.

The leaves are simple, alternate, oblong to elliptic, 15 to 35 centimeters long and 6 to 14 centimeters wide, with an entire margin and a shortly acuminate apex. The upper leaf surface is dark green and glossy, while the lower surface is paler and may have a slightly waxy appearance. The leaves are clustered toward the ends of the branches.

The flowers are small, unisexual, and borne in dense axillary panicles. Male and female flowers are borne on separate trees (dioecious). The flowers are cream-colored to pale yellow, with a distinctive sweet fragrance that attracts insect pollinators.

The fruit is a fleshy, dehiscent capsule approximately 2 to 4 centimeters long, opening when ripe to reveal a single seed covered by a bright red or orange aril (fleshy appendage). The aril is eagerly consumed by birds and mammals, which disperse the seeds. The seed itself is dark brown, roughly ovoid, and approximately 1.5 to 2 centimeters long.

## Distribution and Habitat

Virola calophylla is distributed throughout the Amazon basin, occurring in Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, and the Guianas. It is one of the more widespread Virola species and is common in lowland tropical rainforest on well-drained upland soils (terra firme forest).

The species occurs at elevations from sea level to approximately 500 meters, in areas with high annual rainfall (2,000 to 4,000 millimeters) and no pronounced dry season. It is most abundant in mature, undisturbed rainforest but can also be found in secondary growth and forest edges.

Virola calophylla is a characteristic component of the Amazonian canopy, often occurring alongside other Virola species. The genus is one of the most important tree genera in terms of both species diversity and ecological dominance in lowland Amazonian forests.

The species is not currently considered threatened, as it is widespread and locally abundant throughout much of its range. However, deforestation and habitat fragmentation in the Amazon basin pose long-term conservation concerns for the species and its associated ecosystem.

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds

The bark resin of Virola calophylla contains tryptamine alkaloids as its principal psychoactive constituents. The two major alkaloids are N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT) and 5-methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (5-MeO-DMT).

DMT is the dominant alkaloid, typically present at concentrations of 0.1% to 0.5% of resin dry weight. 5-MeO-DMT is present at lower concentrations, usually 0.05% to 0.2%. The relative proportions of these two alkaloids can vary between individual trees and populations.

The resin also contains beta-carboline alkaloids, including harmine and related compounds, though at lower concentrations than the tryptamine alkaloids. The presence of beta-carbolines may contribute to the overall psychoactive profile of the snuff preparations, as these compounds are monoamine oxidase inhibitors.

When the resin is prepared as a nasal snuff, the tryptamine alkaloids are rapidly absorbed through the richly vascularized nasal mucosa. This route of administration bypasses first-pass hepatic metabolism and the gastrointestinal monoamine oxidase barrier, allowing the compounds to reach the brain in their active form. The onset of effects is typically rapid, occurring within minutes of insufflation.

The concentration of alkaloids in the bark resin varies with the season, the age of the tree, and the specific part of the bark harvested. Inner bark generally contains higher concentrations than outer bark. Indigenous users have developed traditional methods for selecting and preparing the most potent bark material.

## Ethnobotanical and Traditional Use

The use of Virola bark resin as a psychoactive snuff is among the most widespread and well-documented entheogenic practices in the Amazon basin. The practice extends across numerous indigenous groups including the Yanomamö, Waiká, Makú, and other peoples of the upper Orinoco and Rio Negro drainages.

The snuff is prepared by stripping the inner bark from the tree, collecting the exuded resin, and processing it into a fine powder. Traditional preparation methods vary between groups but generally involve scraping the resin from the bark, drying it over a fire, and grinding it with the ashes of specific tree barks (such as Elizabetha princeps) that serve as alkaline admixtures to facilitate absorption.

The snuff is administered by blowing it through long tubes (blowpipes) into the nostrils of the recipient. In some traditions, a second person administers the snuff using a blowpipe, while in others the user self-administers using a shorter tube. The snuff produces intense visionary experiences lasting approximately 15 to 30 minutes, with aftereffects persisting for an additional hour or more.

The visionary experiences induced by Virola snuff play important roles in indigenous shamanic practice. The snuff is used for divination, diagnosis and treatment of illness, communication with spirits, hunting magic, and initiation rites. The intensity of the experience is generally described as comparable to or exceeding that of ayahuasca, with more rapid onset and shorter duration.

Different indigenous groups have distinct names for Virola snuff preparations. Among the Yanomamö, the snuff is called ebena or yopo (a name also applied to Anadenanthera peregrina snuff, reflecting some confusion in the early ethnographic literature). Among the Waiká, it is called nyakwana or hakudufha.
