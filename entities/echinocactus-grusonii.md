## Echinocactus grusonii

## Overview

Echinocactus grusonii, commonly known as the golden barrel cactus or mother-in-law's cushion, is one of the most widely recognized and cultivated cacti in the world. Its distinctive spherical shape covered in dense golden-yellow spines has made it a staple of succulent collections and desert landscaping.

The species is endemic to east-central Mexico, where it is restricted to a relatively small geographic area in the states of Querétaro and Zacatecas. In its native habitat, Echinocactus grusonii is classified as Endangered due to habitat destruction and illegal collection.

The surface is divided into 20 to 35 prominent vertical ribs, each bearing closely spaced areoles that produce the characteristic spination. The ribs create a deeply corrugated texture that adds to the plant's sculptural appearance.

Spines are the most distinctive feature, forming dense clusters of 3 to 4 large central spines and 8 to 10 shorter radial spines per areole. Central spines are straight, rigid, and bright golden-yellow, reaching 3 to 5 centimeters in length.

Flowers emerge from the woolly crown at the apex of the plant during summer. The flowers are diurnal, bright yellow, and approximately 4 to 6 centimeters in diameter, forming a striking contrast against the golden spine mass.

The flowering period lasts approximately 7 to 10 days for each individual plant. Flowers open in the morning and close by late afternoon, with each flower remaining receptive for only a single day.

Following successful pollination, small oblong fruits develop at the crown, containing numerous tiny black seeds. The fruits are covered in woolly scales and dehisce when ripe to release seeds.

## Distribution and Habitat

Echinocactus grusonii is endemic to a small region of east-central Mexico, primarily in the state of Querétaro with extensions into adjacent Zacatecas. The total native range is estimated at less than 200 square kilometers.

The species grows on volcanic rocky slopes and in semi-arid thorn scrub at elevations between 1,400 and 1,800 meters. Populations are typically found on steep, well-drained hillsides with eastern or southeastern exposure.

Native habitats experience a pronounced dry season from November through April and a summer rainy season from May through October. Annual precipitation ranges from approximately 400 to 600 millimeters.

Associated vegetation includes other cacti such as Mammillaria, Opuntia, and Neobuxbaumia species, along with drought-deciduous shrubs and agaves. The rocky substrate provides microsites for seedling establishment.

Wild populations are concentrated in a few protected areas, including the Sierra Gorda Biosphere Reserve in Querétaro. However, many populations exist on unprotected private land subject to ongoing development pressure.

## Cultivation

Echinocactus grusonii is one of the easiest cacti to cultivate, tolerating a wide range of conditions provided its basic requirements for full sun and excellent drainage are met. It is widely grown in containers, rock gardens, and mass plantings.

The species prefers a mineral-based [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]]. Standard cactus potting soils are generally suitable.

Watering should follow a soak-and-dry cycle, allowing the [[extending-growing-season]] from spring through autumn. Water should be withheld almost entirely during winter dormancy.
