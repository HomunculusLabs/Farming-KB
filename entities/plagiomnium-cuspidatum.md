---# Taxonomy and Morphology

Plagiomnium cuspidatum was originally described as Mnium cuspidatum by Johann
Hedwig in 1801. It was transferred to the genus Plagiomnium by T. Koponen in
1968 based on differences in peristome structure and leaf architecture from the
type species of Mnium.

The gametophyte forms loose to dense mats of irregularly branched stems that
reach 4 to 10 centimeters in length. Stems are creeping to ascending, with
paraphyllia often present at branch axils.

Leaves are 3 to 5 millimeters long, ovate to broadly lanceolate, with a
distinct costa extending to or near the leaf apex. The leaf margins are
strongly and doubly toothed from base to tip, a key diagnostic character.

Laminal cells are uniformly small and hexagonal, 15 to 25 micrometers in
diameter, with distinct transverse wrinkles visible at 20x magnification.
This cellular texture distinguishes the species from similar Plagiomnium taxa.

The sporophyte bears a seta 15 to 30 millimeters tall, terminating in a
cylindrical, slightly inclined capsule. The operculum is long-rostrate, and
the double peristome is well developed with 16 teeth.

## Distribution and Habitat

Plagiomnium cuspidatum has a circumboreal distribution across Europe, Asia,
and North America. In Europe, it ranges from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean
and extends eastward through Siberia to Japan and the Russian Far East.

In North America, the species occurs across Canada and the northern United
States, extending southward in the Appalachian Mountains and along the Pacific
Coast. It is less common in the southeastern [[query-how-to-grow-gourmet-mushrooms-outdoors-on-logs-and-stumps]], cliff bases, and humus-rich forest floors.

It tolerates moderate disturbance and is frequently found in secondary growth
forests, forest edges, and shaded road cuts. Elevation ranges from sea level to
approximately 2,000 meters in mountainous regions.

## Ecology

Plagiomnium cuspidatum is a dominant component of the forest floor bryophyte
layer in many temperate deciduous forests. It contributes substantially to
soil moisture retention, intercepting rainfall and reducing evaporation from
the litter layer.

The mats created by this moss serve as microhabitats for diverse invertebrate
communities, including springtails, mites, nematodes, and various insect
larvae. These microarthropod communities are important [[hypnum-cupressiforme]] in heavily disturbed or open-canopy situations.

Epiphytic growth on tree bases is common, particularly on broadleaf trees
with deeply furrowed bark that retains moisture. The moss rarely ascends
above 1 meter on tree trunks, distinguishing it from truly corticolous taxa.
