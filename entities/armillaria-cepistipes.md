## Overview

Armillaria cepistipes is a species of honey fungus in the Physalacriaceae family. It is a widespread root-pathogenic and saprotrophic basidiomycete that attacks woody plants across Europe and parts of Asia.

The species was long confused with A. mellea and other Armillaria species due to morphological similarities. Modern molecular techniques have clarified its distinct status within the European Armillaria complex.

A. cepistipes is generally considered less virulent than A. mellea but can cause significant damage in stressed trees, orchards, and forest plantations. It is one of the most commonly encountered Armillaria species in central European forests.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Physalacriaceae
- **Genus:** Armillaria
- **Species:** A. cepistipes Velen. (1920)

The species epithet "cepistipes" refers to its association with onions, from the Latin "cepa" (onion) and "pestipes" (destructive stalk), as Velenovský first described it from specimens found damaging onion crops in Czechoslovakia.

Molecular phylogenetic studies by Kären and others have confirmed A. cepistipes as a distinct species within the European Armillaria clade, closely related to A. gallica and A. borealis.

## Morphology

Basidiocarps (mushrooms) are similar to other honey fungi, with convex to planar caps 3–10 cm in diameter. The cap surface is smooth to slightly scaly, honey-brown to brown, often with a darker center.

The stipe is central, firm, and tapers slightly upward, typically 5–12 cm long. A distinct persistent annulus (ring) is present on the upper stipe, a characteristic feature of the genus.

Gills are adnate to slightly decurrent, white to cream, and may develop pinkish tones with age. Spore print is white. Spores are ellipsoid, 7–9 × 5–6.5 µm, smooth, and non-amyloid.

Rhizomorphs (shoestring-like structures) are produced underground and are important for both identification and pathogenic spread. They are black on the surface and white internally.

## Distribution and Habitat

A. cepistipes is widely distributed across Europe, from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean, and extends eastward through Russia into parts of northern Asia. It has also been reported from North Africa.

The species occurs in diverse forest types including coniferous, broadleaf, and mixed woodlands. It is particularly common in urban and suburban environments where it attacks stressed ornamental trees.

Unlike some Armillaria species that prefer healthy vigorous trees, A. cepistipes shows a stronger preference for weakened, stressed, or recently dead hosts. This makes it a common problem in drought-stressed forests and neglected orchards.

## Pathogenicity

A. cepistipes is a white-rot fungus that attacks the root systems and lower stems of woody hosts. It spreads through root contact and rhizomorph growth, forming extensive mycelial networks in soil.

The species is considered a secondary pathogen — it typically colonizes hosts already weakened by drought, poor site conditions, or other stressors. However, once established, it can kill trees of all ages.

Above-ground symptoms include thinning crowns, chlorotic foliage, reduced growth, and eventually tree death. Below-ground, infected roots show white mycelial fans and rotting tissue.

## Host Range

The host range of A. cepistipes is broad, encompassing most European tree species. Common hosts include oak (*Quercus* spp.), beech (*Fagus sylvatica*), spruce (*Picea abies*), pine (*Pinus* spp.), and fruit trees.

In orchards, it is particularly damaging to apple, pear, and stone fruit trees. Vineyards are also susceptible, with Armillaria root rot causing significant economic losses in some European wine-growing regions.

Urban trees are frequently affected, especially street trees growing in compacted, poorly aerated soils. The species is one of the leading causes of tree mortality in urban forestry settings.

## Ecological Role

Despite its pathogenic reputation, A. cepistipes plays an important ecological role in forest ecosystems. As a saprotroph, it contributes to wood decomposition and nutrient cycling, particularly of coarse woody debris.

The species is involved in natural forest succession, killing weakened trees and creating canopy gaps that allow light-demanding species to establish. This disturbance role maintains forest structural diversity.

Armillaria species also serve as food sources for various invertebrates and fungi. The fruiting bodies are edible when thoroughly cooked, though they must be positively identified to avoid confusion with other Armillaria species of varying edibility.

## Management

Management of A. cepistipes in forestry and horticulture focuses on prevention and reducing host stress. Maintaining tree vigor through proper irrigation, nutrition, and soil management is the most effective defense.

Infected stumps and roots should be removed or mechanically destroyed to prevent rhizomorph spread. In high-value orchard settings, soil fumigation and biological control with Trichoderma species have shown some efficacy.

Chemical control options are limited. Barrier trenches around infection centers can slow rhizomorph spread but are labor-intensive. Research into resistant rootstock varieties is ongoing for fruit crop applications.

## Related Species

A. cepistipes is one of approximately 10 described Armillaria species in Europe. It is most commonly confused with A. gallica, from which it can be distinguished by its larger spores and more aggressive pathogenicity.

Compared to A. mellea, A. cepistipes has a more limited virulence range and prefers weaker hosts. A. mellea is generally more destructive in orchards and vineyards. A. ostoyae is the primary Armillaria pathogen of conifers.

A. cepistipes belongs to the same species complex as A. gallica and A. borealis. Molecular identification using ITS rDNA sequencing is often necessary for reliable species discrimination in field populations.

## Cultivation

Unlike several other Armillaria species, A. cepistipes is not commercially cultivated. The related A. mellea is cultivated at small scale in some Asian countries, but A. cepistipes has not been domesticated.

Laboratory cultures grow readily on standard fungal media including malt extract agar and potato dextrose agar. Optimal growth temperature is 20–25°C. The species forms rhizomorphs in culture, which can be used for species identification.

The edibility of A. cepistipes is generally considered good when thoroughly cooked, though some sources recommend caution due to potential confusion with toxic look-alikes. The flavor is mild and pleasant, similar to other honey fungi.
