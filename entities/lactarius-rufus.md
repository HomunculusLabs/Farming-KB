# Lactarius rufus

**Lactarius rufus** is an ectomycorrhizal basidiomycete fungus in the family Russulaceae, commonly known as the rufous milkcap. It is a common ectomycorrhizal associate of coniferous trees, particularly pines, and plays a significant role in soil food webs as a food resource for fungivorous soil animals. The species is notable for being the premier food source for fungivorous nematodes among ectomycorrhizal fungi studied.

## Taxonomy and Distribution

Lactarius rufus is found across boreal and temperate coniferous forests of the
Northern Hemisphere. It is primarily associated with pines (Pinus spp.) and is
commonly encountered in upland forest soils, peatland environments, and
coniferous plantations. The species is one of the characteristic
ectomycorrhizal fungi of pine-dominated ecosystems in Europe and North America.
Along with Inocybe longicystis, Lactarius rufus has been surveyed in upland
Britain as part of broader studies of ectomycorrhizal fungal diversity in
coniferous plantations.

The genus Lactarius is distinguished by the production of latex (milk) when the gills or flesh are cut or damaged. In Lactarius rufus, the latex is typically white to cream-colored and may change color upon exposure to air. The cap surface is typically reddish-brown to rufous, giving the species its common name.

## Soil Fauna Interactions

### Nematode Food Resource

Lactarius rufus is a highly valued food resource for fungivorous nematodes. Ruess and Dighton (1996) investigated the food value of mycorrhizal fungi for the nematode Aphelenchoides saprophilus and found that Lactarius rufus supported the largest mixed-species nematode populations of any mycorrhizal fungus tested. In petri plate experiments, Lactarius rufus supported 25,890 nematodes with 99% of the population being A. saprophilus. This dramatically exceeded the support provided by:

- Paxillus involutus: only 6 nematodes with 50% A. saprophilus
- Laccaria laccata: 2,633 nematodes with 100% A. saprophilus
- Chaetomium globosum: 1,427 nematodes with 99% A. saprophilus (saprotrophic)
- Agrocybe gibberosa: 349 nematodes with 95% A. saprophilus (saprotrophic)
- Mucor heimalis: 24 nematodes with 13% A. saprophilus (saprotrophic)

The capacity of Lactarius rufus to support nematode populations nearly ten times larger than Laccaria laccata and over four thousand times larger than Paxillus involutus highlights its exceptional nutritional quality as a fungal food resource. The near-exclusive dominance of A. saprophilus in the population indicates that Lactarius rufus provides specific nutritional conditions that favor this nematode species.

### Pure Culture Nematode Support

In pure culture studies, Lactarius rufus supported nematode populations of 1.4 times 10 to the fifth individuals with 7.8% mature females, indicating good nutritional quality for population maintenance and moderate fecundity. This placed Lactarius rufus in the mid-range for female proportion among mycorrhizal fungi tested:

- Laccaria laccata: 2.4 population, 11.4% females
- Hymenoscyphus ericae: 2.0 population, 6.8% females
- Lactarius rufus: 1.4 population, 7.8% females
- Hebeloma sacchariolens: 0.8 population, 2.8% females
- Amanita muscaria: 0.7 population, 2.0% females
- Amanita rubescens: 0.6 population, 1.3% females
- Paxillus involutus: 0.8 population, 5.8% females

The discrepancy between mixed-species and pure-culture results (where Lactarius rufus supported the highest mixed populations but only moderate pure-culture populations) may reflect synergistic interactions between nematode species that enhance population growth on Lactarius rufus biomass.

### Sustainable Nematode Communities

Ruess and Dighton (1996) demonstrated that nematode populations from a natural
mixed soil community performed best and with the highest proportion of A.
saprophilus when the nematodes fed on the ectomycorrhizal fungi Lactarius rufus
and Laccaria laccata. In contrast, a sustainable community could not be
maintained on a diet consisting solely of Paxillus involutus. This finding
suggests that the nutritional quality of Lactarius rufus is critical for
maintaining nematode biodiversity and community function in forest soils.

### Collembolan Feeding Preferences

Shaw (1988) studied the feeding preferences of the collembolan Onychiurus armatus when offered a range of fungal species grown in agar culture. Lactarius rufus was a moderately preferred fungal food source, with 55.7% of the colony area consumed and a mean fecal count of 64.5 per culture vessel. This placed it in the mid-upper range of preference among the 12 species tested:

- Marasmius androsaceus: 72.2% consumed, 74.8 fecal count
- Mycena galopus: 68.2% consumed, 19.2 fecal count
- Lactarius rufus: 55.7% consumed, 64.5 fecal count
- Suillus luteus: 50.7% consumed, 48.9 fecal count
- Laccaria proxima: 41.4% consumed, 70.8 fecal count
- Suillus bovinus: 18.2% consumed, 16.6 fecal count
- Hebeloma crustuliniforme: 1.7% consumed, 1.0 fecal count
- Pisolithus tinctorius: 0.2% consumed, 2.1 fecal count

## Nitrogen Enrichment Response

Dighton et al. studied the response of ectomycorrhizal fungal communities to
nitrogen enrichment in peat soils under Pinus contorta. Lactarius rufus showed
increased abundance with added nitrogen, consistent with the broader pattern
that nitrogen deposition favors certain ectomycorrhizal species while suppressing
others. Overall, species richness and diversity of ectomycorrhizal fungal
communities typically decline with nitrogen addition, but specific species like
Lactarius rufus benefit from the enhanced nitrogen availability.

This response has significant implications for understanding how atmospheric nitrogen deposition may alter ectomycorrhizal community composition and function in boreal forests. As nitrogen-sensitive species decline and nitrogen-responsive species like Lactarius rufus increase, the functional characteristics of the mycorrhizal community may shift, potentially affecting host tree nutrition, decomposition processes, and soil carbon storage.

## Peatland Occurrence

Lactarius rufus was recorded in MH86 peat soils under Pinus contorta lodgepole pine plantations, demonstrating its ability to colonize and function in acidic, organic-matter-rich peatland soils. This habitat tolerance suggests adaptation to low-pH, low-nutrient, and high moisture conditions typical of boreal and sub-boreal peatland ecosystems.

## Ecological Significance

Lactarius rufus occupies a significant position in coniferous forest soil food
webs. Its exceptional value as a nematode food resource means it serves as an
important conduit for carbon and nutrients flowing from the mycorrhizal pool
into the soil microfaunal food web. The conversion of mycorrhizal fungal biomass
into nematode biomass, and subsequently into higher trophic levels, represents
an important pathway for nutrient cycling in forest soils.

The species responds positively to nitrogen enrichment, which may lead to shifts in belowground community composition as atmospheric nitrogen deposition continues to increase in many boreal forest regions. These shifts could alter the flow of carbon and nutrients through soil food webs, with cascading effects on ecosystem processes. The dual role of Lactarius rufus as both a mycorrhizal mutualist and a keystone food web resource makes it a species of particular interest for understanding how anthropogenic nitrogen deposition may restructure boreal forest soil ecosystems.

## Key Studies

- **Shaw (1988):** Quantified collembolan feeding preferences, placing Lactarius rufus in the mid-upper range (55.7% colony consumed) among 12 fungal species tested.
- **Ruess and Dighton (1996):** Demonstrated that Lactarius rufus supports the highest mixed-species nematode populations (25,890 individuals) of any mycorrhizal fungus tested, over 4,000 times more than Paxillus involutus.
- **Ruess and Dighton (1996):** Showed that sustainable nematode communities could only be maintained on Lactarius rufus and Laccaria laccata diets, not on Paxillus involutus.
- **Dighton et al.:** Found that Lactarius rufus increases in abundance with nitrogen enrichment in peat soils under Pinus contorta.
