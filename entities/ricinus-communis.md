## Overview

**Ricinus communis**, commonly known as the castor bean or [[cannabis-honey-oil-production]], one of the world's most important industrial vegetable oils.

Ricinus communis is notoriously toxic: its seeds contain ricin, a type II ribosome-inactivating protein that is among the most potent naturally occurring toxins known. Ingestion of as few as 1–4 chewed seeds can be fatal to an adult.

Despite its toxicity, the plant has been used medicinally for millennia, and castor oil remains a commercially important product in pharmaceuticals, lubricants, and bio-based polymers.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:** Ricinus communis L., family Euphorbiaceae, subfamily Acalyphoideae. The genus *Ricinus* is monotypic, containing only this species.

The plant typically grows 2–5 m as a perennial in [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] reaching 1–2 m.

Stems are erect, hollow, glaucous, and often tinged with red or purple. The root system is extensive and fibrous.

Leaves are large (15–60 cm across), palmately lobed with 7–11 pointed lobes, alternate, and long-petioled. Leaf color ranges from deep green to dark reddish-purple, depending on cultivar.

The inflorescence is a terminal panicle bearing monoecious flowers: staminate (male) flowers at the base with prominent cream-colored anthers, and pistillate (female) flowers at the apex with red stigmas.

The fruit is a spiny, three-lobed capsule (2–3 cm) containing three seeds, each resembling an engorged tick — the origin of the genus name (*ricinus* is Latin for "tick").

Seeds are oval, approximately 1–1.5 cm long, mottled with brown, grey, and white patterns, and contain 40–60% fixed oil by weight.

## Distribution and Cultivation

Ricinus communis is believed to be native to northeastern Africa (Ethiopia region) but its original range is obscured by ancient cultivation and naturalization.

The species is now pantropical, occurring as a weed or ornamental in tropical, subtropical, and warm temperate regions worldwide. It has naturalized in parts of the Americas, Asia, and Australia.

Major castor seed producing countries include India, Brazil, China, Mozambique, and Ethiopia, with India accounting for over 80% of global production.

Cultivation is straightforward: the plant grows rapidly in a wide range of soils (preferring well-drained sandy loams), tolerates drought, and requires minimal inputs.

Optimal growth occurs at temperatures of 20–30 °C with annual rainfall of 500–750 mm, though the species tolerates considerable climatic variation.

Harvest occurs 3–5 months after sowing, with seed yields of 500–2000 kg/ha depending on cultivar and growing conditions.

The oil is unique among vegetable oils for its high ricinoleic acid content (85–95%), a hydroxylated fatty acid that confers unusual viscosity and lubricity.

Industrial applications include high-performance lubricants, hydraulic fluids, plasticizers, and the production of sebacic acid and undecylenic acid as polymer precursors.

In pharmaceuticals, castor oil is used as a laxative, emollient, and carrier oil. It is the primary component of some topical formulations and the basis for zinc oxide castor oil cream.
