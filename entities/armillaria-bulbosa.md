# Armillaria bulbosa

## Overview

armillaria bulbosa is a species of Basidiomycota fungus in the family Physalacriaceae, order Agaricales. It is one of the most ecologically significant root-rot pathogens of woody plants worldwide and has gained considerable fame as one of the largest and oldest [[savory-living-organisms-as-tools]] on Earth. The species is a clonal organism capable of forming extensive underground networks of rhizomorphs that can persist for centuries or even millennia.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Physalacriaceae
- **Genus:** Armillaria
- **Species:** A. bulbosa

The taxonomy of the [[armillaria-ostoyae]] species are capable of significant [[dighton-fungal-biosorption-heavy-metal-remediation]] accumulation. Rizzo et al. (1992) showed that despite the melanized outer cortex, rhizomorphs effectively concentrate metals from the environment, with concentrations reaching up to 3440 ppm for aluminum, 1930 ppm for zinc, and 1890 ppm for iron.

## Interactions with Other Organisms

### Plant Interactions

armillaria bulbosa is primarily pathogenic on a wide range of woody hosts, including oak, maple, pine, spruce, and many ornamental tree species. The fungus attacks roots and the root collar, causing a characteristic white mycelial fan beneath the bark and progressive wilting and dieback of the crown.

### Radiocesium Translocation

Studies using quantitative autoradiography have demonstrated that [[armillaria-ostoyae]] can cover enormous areas. The Michigan individual described by Smith et al. (1992) occupied approximately 15 hectares, while an individual of A. ostoyae in Oregon was reported to cover over 900 hectares, making it potentially the largest organism on Earth by area.

The mating system of armillaria bulbosa is bipolar (unifactorial), meaning that compatibility between mating types is controlled by a single genetic locus. This mating system influences the genetic diversity of populations and the potential for adaptation to different host species and environmental conditions.

## Economic and Practical Significance

armillaria bulbosa is one of the most economically damaging forest pathogens worldwide. It causes significant losses in timber production, orchard systems, and ornamental plantings. Economic impacts include:

- Reduced growth rates and timber volumes in infected forest stands
- Increased tree mortality requiring costly salvage operations
- Loss of orchard trees, particularly in apple and [[holzer-stone-fruit-varieties-and-growing]] production
- Expenses associated with root [[plant-disease-management-solomon]] and prevention
- Reduced property values in urban and suburban landscapes

Management strategies include removal of infected stumps and roots (stump removal or grinding), soil fumigation, [[fukuoka-textdoc-biological-control-fallacy-pheromone-critique]] using antagonistic fungi, improved site preparation before planting, and the use of resistant rootstock varieties where available.

## Record-Breaking Biology

Smith, Bruhn, and Anderson (1992) demonstrated in a landmark study published in Nature that a single genetic individual of armillaria bulbosa (now reclassified by some as A. gallica) covering approximately 15 hectares in a Michigan forest was estimated to be more than 1,500 years old and weigh over 10,000 kg. This finding established the species as among the largest and oldest living organisms on Earth, highlighting the remarkable capacity of fungal clonal organisms for persistence and growth.

## Research Notes

The species is referenced in Dighton's "Fungi in [[fungal-ecosystem-processes-overview]]" in the context of heavy metal accumulation by rhizomorphs, radiocesium translocation through fungal thalli, and its status as one of the largest and oldest known organisms. These references underscore the importance of armillaria bulbosa in understanding fungal contributions to ecosystem-level processes, including biogeochemical cycling and the long-term dynamics of forest ecosystems.
