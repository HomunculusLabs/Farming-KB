## Overview

**Lobelia cardinalis**, the cardinal flower, is a perennial herbaceous plant native to the Americas, renowned for its brilliant scarlet-red flower spikes. It is among the most striking native wildflowers of [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] gardens.

The Royal Horticultural Society has awarded *L. cardinalis* the Award of Garden Merit. It has been in continuous cultivation in Europe for over 400 years, making it one of the oldest North American native plants in the horticultural trade.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*L. cardinalis* was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 and placed in the family Campanulaceae. The genus *Lobelia* contains approximately 415 species distributed worldwide, with about 30 native to North America.

The plant grows 60–120 cm tall from a basal rosette of lanceolate leaves 8–15 cm long. The unbranched stem produces alternate leaves that decrease in size upward.

The inflorescence is a terminal raceme 15–30 cm long bearing 10–30 flowers. Each flower is tubular, approximately 3–4 cm long, with five fused petals forming a two-lipped corolla. The brilliant red coloration results from anthocyanin pigments, primarily pelargonidin.

The calyx is five-lobed with a narrow tube. Five stamens are fused into a tube surrounding the style, which protrudes beyond the anthers. The stigma is bifid and recurved.

Fruit is a many-seeded capsule 8–12 mm long. Seeds are numerous, tiny (less than 1 mm), oblong, and light brown. The species inhabits wet meadows, stream banks, marsh edges, roadside ditches, and woodland seeps. It requires consistently moist to saturated soils and tolerates shallow flooding.

Elevation ranges from near sea level to approximately 2,500 meters in the southwestern mountains. It is most abundant in low-elevation riparian corridors and calcareous wetlands.

The plant tolerates full sun to partial shade. In southern portions of its range, it is typically found in shaded stream corridors. In the north, it grows more openly in full-sun wetlands.

*L. cardinalis* is an opportunistic colonizer of disturbed wet sites. It frequently appears along recently constructed drainage ditches, road cuts, and beaver-created wetlands where open soil and saturated conditions coexist.

Soil pH tolerance ranges from mildly acidic (pH 5.5) to mildly alkaline (pH 7.5). The species shows a preference for calcium-rich substrates in limestone regions but is not restricted to them.

## Ecology

Ruby-throated hummingbirds (*Archilochus colubris*) are the primary pollinators. The long tubular flowers are perfectly adapted to hummingbird bill morphology, and the red color is highly visible to birds but nearly invisible to most insects.

Large swallowtail butterflies, particularly the spicebush swallowtail (*Papilio troilus*), occasionally visit flowers for nectar. However, their shorter proboscises limit effective pollen transfer compared to hummingbirds.

Bumblebees are frequent nectar robbers, chewing holes at the base of the corolla tube to access nectar without contacting the reproductive structures. This behavior provides no pollination service but reduces nectar availability for legitimate visitors.

Self-pollination rates are extremely low. Studies report outcrossing rates exceeding 90%, making the species strongly dependent on hummingbird visitation for reproductive success.

The flowering period extends from July through September, providing a critical late-season nectar resource for migrating ruby-throated hummingbirds. Individual flowers last 4–10 days before senescing.

White-tailed deer occasionally browse the foliage but generally avoid the plant, possibly due to alkaloid compounds in the leaves. The species benefits from reduced competition in areas where deer browsing suppresses neighboring vegetation.

The pungent alkaloid lobeline, concentrated in the foliage and roots, provides chemical defense against most mammalian herbivores. Lobeline is a piperidine alkaloid with documented effects on the central nervous system.

In cultivation, *L. cardinalis* prefers rich, organic soils with consistent moisture. It performs best in full sun in northern gardens and partial shade in the South. It is hardy in USDA zones 3–9.

The species is relatively short-lived as a perennial, typically persisting 3–5 years before senescing. However, it self-seeds prolifically in suitable sites, maintaining a persistent presence in the garden.

Several cultivars are available, including 'Queen Victoria' (deep red with dark foliage) and 'Alba' (white-flowered). These selections maintain the species' ornamental appeal while offering color variations.

Deadheading spent flower spikes can extend the bloom period by 2–4 weeks. Cutting plants back to the basal rosette after flowering often promotes a second, smaller flush of blooms in early autumn.
