## Overview

Cynoglossum officinale, known as common hound's tongue, is a biennial or short-lived perennial herb in the family Boraginaceae. Native to Europe and western Asia, it is notable for its medicinal history and its status as an invasive weed in [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]].

The plant produces a rosette of hairy, tongue-shaped leaves in its first year and erect flowering stems 40–120 cm tall in the second year. Flowers are deep reddish-purple, funnel-shaped, and produced in loose cymes from late spring through midsummer.

The common name "hound's tongue" refers to the shape and texture of the basal leaves, which resemble a dog's tongue. The species has been used in traditional European [[childrens-herbal-medicine]] for centuries despite containing toxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids.

It has also acquired the folk names "gypsy flower," "dog bur," and "woolmat" in different European regions. The strong, unpleasant smell of the crushed leaves is sometimes compared to mouse urine.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Clade Angiosperms → Clade Eudicots → Clade Asterids → Order Boraginales → Family Boraginaceae → Genus Cynoglossum → Species C. officinale.

The genus Cynoglossum contains approximately 75 species distributed across [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] worldwide. The name derives from the Greek "kyon" (dog) and "glossa" (tongue), describing the leaf shape.

Cynoglossum officinale is the most widespread European species in the genus. It is closely related to Cynoglossum germanicum and Cynoglossum montanum of southern Europe.

The species is tetraploid (2n=24) and shows considerable morphological [[psilocybin-psilocin-variation-across-flushes]] its range. Plants from Mediterranean populations tend to be smaller and more compact than northern European forms.

Basal leaves are oblong-lanceolate, 15–30 cm long, softly hairy, and grey-green. Stem leaves are narrower, sessile, and decrease in size toward the apex.

The plant is covered in fine, soft hairs (tomentose) that give the foliage a greyish appearance. Flowers are 6–8 mm in diameter, reddish-purple to occasionally pink or white.

The fruit consists of four prickly nutlets that attach readily to animal fur and clothing. Each nutlet is 5–7 mm wide with a flattened margin bearing barbed prickles that facilitate zoochorous dispersal.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cynoglossum officinale is native throughout most of Europe, from the British Isles and Scandinavia to the Mediterranean and eastward to western Siberia and the Caucasus. It occurs in a wide range of open habitats including grasslands, woodland edges, roadsides, and disturbed ground.

The species prefers dry, well-drained calcareous soils but tolerates a broad pH range. It is found from sea level to approximately 2000 m elevation in mountainous regions of southern Europe.

Introduced to [[berry-growing]] in the late 19th century, it has become a problematic invasive species across the northern and western [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] and southern Canada. It is listed as a noxious weed in several states including Washington, Colorado, and Montana.

Invasive populations establish readily on overgrazed rangelands, logged areas, and along trails. The plant's toxic properties reduce palatability to livestock, giving it a competitive advantage on grazed land.

The species is classified as a Category B noxious weed in several western US states. Control efforts focus on biological agents including the root-feeding weevil Mogulones cruciger.

It was likely introduced to North America as a contaminant in crop seed or hay shipments. First herbarium records from North America date to the 1890s in Ontario, Canada.

## Ecology and Toxicology

Cynoglossum officinale contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids, primarily heliosupine and its N-oxide, throughout all plant parts. These compounds are hepatotoxic and cause liver damage in livestock with chronic exposure.

Pyrrolizidine alkaloid concentrations are highest in young rosette foliage and decline as the plant matures. The compounds serve as chemical defenses against generalist herbivores.

Cattle and horses are most susceptible to poisoning, while sheep show greater tolerance at lower doses. Pyrrolizidine alkaloid levels are highest in young rosette leaves and flowering stems.

Toxicity manifests as progressive liver fibrosis, leading to weight loss, jaundice, and death in severe cases. There is no effective treatment once clinical signs appear in affected animals.

Despite its toxicity, the plant supports a specialized insect fauna in its native range. The weevil Mogulones cruciger feeds specifically on Cynoglossum species and has been introduced as a [[fukuoka-textdoc-arrowhead-scale-ladybug-biological-control]] agent in North America.

The flowers provide nectar for a range of pollinators including bees, butterflies, and moths. Several specialist moths in the genus Hadena use Cynoglossum as a larval host plant.

The plant has been documented as a nectar source for the scarce forester moth (Adscita globulariae) in southern England. This association highlights its ecological role in supporting specialist invertebrate fauna.

[[cannabis-seed-banks-and-genetics]] can persist for up to 3 years in the soil. Individual plants produce 300–600 seeds, with high germination rates in disturbed soil conditions.

The barbed nutlets are efficiently dispersed by animals, contributing to rapid range expansion in invaded territories. Populations can increase dramatically following disturbances that remove competing vegetation.

## Traditional Uses

Despite its documented toxicity, Cynoglossum officinale has a long history in European folk medicine. The species epithet "officinale" indicates its inclusion in official pharmacopoeias.

Traditional applications included treatment of [[cannabis-for-skin-conditions-detailed]], hemorrhoids, coughs, and wounds. The plant was applied externally as a poultice or salve rather than ingested internally.

The leaves were sometimes baked into cakes or steeped in wine for lung complaints in medieval herbal practice. These internal preparations are now recognized as unsafe due to cumulative liver damage.

Nicholas Culpeper's herbal (1653) recommended hound's tongue for "all inflammations" and as a sedative. The doctrine of signatures linked the hirsute leaves to treatment of dog bites.

Modern herbal practice has largely abandoned internal use due to well-documented hepatotoxicity. Homeopathic preparations use highly diluted extracts, which are considered safe due to extreme dilution.

The European Medicines Agency classifies pyrrolizidine-containing plants as requiring strict limits in [[honey-based-herbal-preparations]]. This [[mycoremediation-regulatory-framework]] has effectively removed Cynoglossum from commercial herbal products.
