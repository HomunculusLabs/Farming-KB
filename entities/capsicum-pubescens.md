## Overview

Capsicum pubescens is a perennial pepper species commonly known as rocoto or locoto, distinguished by its black seeds and dense pubescence on leaves and stems. It is one of the five domesticated Capsicum species and the most cold-tolerant of the cultivated peppers.

Native to the mid-elevation Andes of Bolivia and Peru, rocoto has been cultivated for over 5,000 years and remains a staple in Andean cuisine. The species is notable for its vigorous, vining growth habit and its preference for cool, humid highland environments.

Archaeological evidence from the Guitarrero Cave in Peru documents Capsicum pubescens cultivation by approximately 2500 BCE. The species was likely domesticated independently from the [[pepper-cultivation-and-variety-guide]] traditions.

Fruits are thick-walled, apple- or pear-shaped, and range from 50,000 to 250,000 SHU on the Scoville scale, placing them among the hotter cultivated peppers. ## Bioactive Compounds

The primary pungency compound is **capsaicin**, along with dihydrocapsaicin, nordihydrocapsaicin, and other capsaicinoids at total concentrations of 0.3–1.5% by dry weight. The capsaicinoid profile is similar to [[capsicum-annuum]], producing unique ratios of capsanthin to capsorubin.

Flavonoid glycosides including luteolin and quercetin derivatives have been identified in fruit extracts. These compounds exhibit documented antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties in vitro and contribute to the fruit's oxidative stability during storage.

The dense pubescence on leaves and stems contains **secretory trichomes** that exude oleoresins with documented antifungal activity. Extracts have shown activity against Colletotrichum and Fusarium species, suggesting a role in pre-harvest pathogen defense.
