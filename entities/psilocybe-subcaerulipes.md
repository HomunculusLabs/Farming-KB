# Psilocybe subcaerulipes

**Psilocybe subcaerulipes** is a rare and little-known psychoactive species of mushroom in the family Hymenogastraceae. It is native to East Asia, where it has been documented primarily in Japan and surrounding regions. The species is closely related to [[psilocybe-caerulipes]] (the blue-foot mushroom) of eastern North America, as reflected in its specific epithet *subcaerulipes* — meaning "somewhat resembling the blue-foot," a clear reference to its North American counterpart.

## Taxonomy

The species was originally described by Japanese mycologists based on collections from the deciduous and mixed forests of Japan. The name indicates its close morphological and phylogenetic relationship to [[psilocybe-caerulipes]], from which it is distinguished by subtle but consistent differences in cap morphology, stipe characteristics, and microscopic features, as well as by its distinct geographic distribution separated by the Pacific Ocean. The prefix *sub-* denotes its status as a closely allied species rather than a variety, subspecies, or form of [[psilocybe-caerulipes]].

Within the genus Psilocybe, P. subcaerulipes belongs to section Psilocybe (also called section Cordisporae by some authors), which includes many of the most well-known and widely distributed psychoactive species such as [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] (liberty cap), [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]], [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-azurescens-species-guide]], and [[psilocybe-caerulipes]]. This section is characterized by bluing reactions upon bruising, the presence of psilocybin|psilocybin and psilocin|psilocin as active compounds, reddish-brown to purplish-brown spore deposits, and typically thin-walled, ellipsoid spores with a germ pore.

## Description

### Cap (Pileus)
The cap is small, typically 1 to 3 cm in diameter, though occasional specimens may reach up to 3.5 cm. It is convex to broadly convex when young, becoming plane or slightly depressed with a low umbo at maturity. The surface is smooth, glabrous, and distinctly hygrophanous, with the margin being somewhat translucent-striate when moist. When fresh and hydrated, the cap color is a distinctive reddish-brown to warm caramel-brown, chestnut-brown, or hazel-brown, often with a lighter disc (center) and slightly darker margin. As it dries, the color fades to a dingy yellowish-buff, pale tan, or straw color, sometimes with a slight greenish tint. The cap surface may develop slight radial cracking (rimose) in very dry conditions. The flesh is thin, fragile, and whitish to pale brownish, bruising distinctly blue or blue-green when damaged, especially prominently near the base of the stipe.

### Gills
The gills are adnate to slightly adnexed (narrowly attached), moderately close, and narrow. They are initially pale grayish or whitish, becoming dark purplish-brown to nearly blackish-brown as the spores mature. The gill edges are paler than the faces and may be slightly whitish and fimbriate (fringed) in young specimens. The gills may also bruise a bluish or blue-green color where damaged or handled, though this reaction is typically less intense than the bluing at the stipe base.

### Stipe (Stem)
The stipe measures 3 to 6 cm long and 1.5 to 3 mm thick, cylindrical and equal or slightly enlarged at the very base. It is fragile, hollow, and whitish to pale brownish in color. The surface is smooth or very finely pruinose (frosted or powdery) near the apex, becoming more smooth lower down. The base of the stipe is the area where bluing is most pronounced, often developing a strong, vivid blue or blue-green discoloration that may spread upward along the stipe when the mushroom is handled or cut. This bluing at the stipe base is a key diagnostic feature and the origin of the species epithet (through comparison to [[psilocybe-caerulipes]], which is named for its characteristically blue-staining foot). There is no annulus (ring) at any stage of development.

### Spores and Microscopic Features
Spores are broadly ellipsoid to slightly rhomboid in face view and ellipsoid in side view, smooth, with a distinct germ pore at the apex. They measure approximately 9 to 12 µm by 6 to 7.5 µm, placing them in the medium size range for the genus. The spore deposit is dark purplish-brown to reddish-brown, typical of section Psilocybe. Basidia are four-spored and clavate, measuring 16 to 22 µm. Cheilocystidia are abundant at the gill edges, ventricose to lageniform (flask-shaped), with swollen bases and narrow, elongated necks, measuring 20 to 35 µm. Pleurocystidia are similar in shape but less common, scattered on the gill faces. The pileipellis is a cutis with occasional erect elements.

## Distribution and Habitat

P. subcaerulipes is known primarily from Japan, where it has been collected in various forested regions of the country including Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu. It has also been reported from other parts of East Asia, including South Korea and possibly parts of northeastern China, though documented collections from outside Japan remain scarce and poorly documented.

The species grows saprotrophically on decaying wood and woody debris in forested environments. It is typically found on fallen branches, rotten logs, and decomposing wood of hardwood trees in moist, shaded forest floor settings. It has been recorded in both deciduous broadleaf forests and mixed coniferous-broadleaf forests, often in association with oaks, beeches, [[maple-syrup-production]], and other hardwood species. Fruiting occurs during the warm, humid months of summer and early autumn, typically from June to September in Japan, coinciding with the rainy season (tsuyu) and its aftermath. The species prefers environments with consistently high humidity and adequate moisture.

## Psychoactive Properties

P. subcaerulipes has been confirmed to contain psilocybin|psilocybin and psilocin|psilocin, the classic indole tryptamine alkaloids responsible for the psychoactive effects of hallucinogenic mushrooms. As a member of section Psilocybe, its chemical profile is consistent with other members of this well-defined phylogenetic group.

### Chemical Analysis
Quantitative analyses of P. subcaerulipes are limited due to the rarity of collections and the difficulty of obtaining sufficient material for analysis. Based on the limited data available from Japanese researchers, psilocybin|psilocybin levels are reported to be moderate, comparable to other wood-inhabiting Psilocybe species of similar size and ecology. The presence of baeocystin|baeocystin and norbaeocystin|norbaeocystin — the common biosynthetic precursors found in most psilocybin-containing mushrooms — has also been detected in analyzed collections.

The bluing reaction observed upon bruising of the fruiting body is biochemically significant: it is indicative of the oxidation of psilocin|psilocin (or its phosphorylated precursor psilocybin|psilocybin, which is enzymatically dephosphorylated to psilocin|psilocin upon tissue damage) and correlates strongly with the presence of psychoactive tryptamine compounds.

## Similar Species

- **[[psilocybe-caerulipes]]** — The North American "blue-foot" species, from which P. subcaerulipes derives its name. The two species are morphologically very similar and are primarily separated by geography (North America versus East Asia) and subtle microscopic differences in spore size and cystidial morphology.
- **[[psilocybe-pelliculosa-profile]]** — A western North American species with a similar habitat on woody debris, but typically smaller in all dimensions with a more conical cap and less pronounced bluing.
- **[[psilocybe-silvatica]]** — Another wood-inhabiting species of similar size and coloration, found in both North America and East Asia, though typically with a more robust stature and slightly larger spores.
- **[[psilocybe-argentipes]]** — A Japanese species found in similar habitats, distinguished by its more silvery-gray cap color and different spore morphology.

## Cultural and Ethnobotanical Context

Japan has a rich and ancient tradition of mushroom foraging and mycology, and several Psilocybe species have been documented from the archipelago over the centuries. P. subcaerulipes is one of the less well-known psychoactive species from Japan in the Western mycological literature, overshadowed by more frequently discussed species such as [[psilocybe-yungensis]] and [[psilocybe-argentipes]].

The species received attention in Jochen Gartz's comprehensive global survey of psychoactive mushrooms, *Magic Mushrooms Around the World*, where it was noted as one of the East Asian representatives of the Psilocybe genus with confirmed psychoactive properties. Its inclusion in such surveys reflects its verified chemical status despite its relative rarity in mycological collections. In Japan, the possession and cultivation of psilocybin-containing mushrooms was legal until 2002, when changes to Japanese law brought the country's regulations in line with international drug control conventions.

## Ecological Role

As a saprotrophic wood-decay fungus, P. subcaerulipes contributes to the decomposition of woody material in forest ecosystems. Its role in breaking down lignin and cellulose in dead wood helps recycle nutrients locked in woody biomass and maintain soil health in the forest floor environment. The species appears to be a secondary decomposer, colonizing wood that has already begun to break down and has been softened by other fungal species or environmental factors. It shares its ecological niche with numerous other small wood-inhabiting mushrooms in East Asian forests.

## Conservation Status

P. subcaerulipes is considered rare, with relatively few documented collections even within its known range in Japan. This rarity may reflect genuine ecological scarcity, limited search effort by mycologists in its preferred habitats, difficulty in locating the small, inconspicuous fruiting bodies in dense forest litter and woody debris, or a combination of these factors. Habitat loss through deforestation, urbanization, and intensive forest management practices in Japan's increasingly developed landscape may pose a threat to the species, though no formal conservation assessment has been conducted and it is not listed on any conservation registries.

## Notes

The study of East Asian Psilocybe species remains an active and productive area of mycological research. Molecular phylogenetic studies using multi-locus DNA sequencing have begun to clarify relationships within the genus and between geographically separated but morphologically similar species pairs. P. subcaerulipes and its close North American relative [[psilocybe-caerulipes]] may represent an example of an East Asian-North American disjunct distribution pattern, a well-documented biogeographic phenomenon seen in numerous plant and fungal species pairs that were likely continuous across the Bering land bridge during the Tertiary period before being separated by continental drift and climatic change.

## Legal Status

In Japan, psilocybin-containing mushrooms were legal to possess and sell until 2002, when legislative changes prohibited them. P. subcaerulipes is now classified as a controlled substance in Japan. In most other jurisdictions, it is treated as a Schedule I controlled substance equivalent to other [[psilocybin]]-containing mushroom species. Its rarity makes enforcement encounters unlikely.
