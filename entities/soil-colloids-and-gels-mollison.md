## Description

Soil colloids are stable aqueous suspensions of clay, organic, or polymer particles so fine they remain suspended unaffected by gravity. They form the active chemical sites in soil where ionic bonding, nutrient exchange, and water retention occur. Colloids and the gels they produce are the primary mechanism by which soils bind water and nutrients against leaching.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil physical chemistry
- **Types**: Inorganic (clay colloids), organic (humus-derived), synthetic (polymer-based)
- **Particle size**: 2 to 200 millimicrons for effective colloidal behaviour

## Key Facts

### Natural Colloid Sources

- **Humus and humic decay products**: Most effective colloids, hundreds of times more effective than clay colloids in ion exchange
- **Fine clays**: Illites, bentonite (deposited on clay pans from evaporation of clay suspensions)
- **Graphite and silica**: Finely ground forms provide colloidal activity
- **Bacterial polysaccharides**: Secreted by soil bacteria to form soil gels
- **Animal-derived gels**: From hooves, horns, sinew wastes, and albumen

### Colloid Chemistry

- Colloid particle surfaces carry negative charge (same as root hairs)
- They attract positive ions: sulphates, nitrates, and metals (sodium, calcium, magnesium, iron)
- About 99% of nutrient ions are held by colloids in soils (Leeper, 1982)
- Ammonia and sodium can dislodge these ions, making them available to plant roots
- With excessive sodium flushing, calcium and metallic ions are lost to leaching
- Humus colloids are hundreds of times more effective than clay colloids in cation exchange
- Ferric oxide colloids carry positive charges and give water a brown stain (typical of acid peat and pine forest drainage)

### Synthetic Soil Conditioners

- Commercial products: Agrosoke, Ikedagel, Terrasorb (hydrophilic granules)
- Most artificial gels are acrylic-based (acrylamide polymers) supplied as granules
- Can absorb hundreds of times their weight in water
- Applied at 6 kg/ha or more; water conservation claims of up to 50%
- Used in nursery plants, row crops, desert tree plantings, transplanting, and seed pelleting

### Colloid Destruction and Flocculation

- **Burning**: Destroys colloidal properties of surface clays permanently
- **Excess sodium**: Deflocculates clays, releasing bound calcium and metallic ions to leaching
- **Cultivation and clearing**: Destroy organic colloids and soil structure
- Aluminium salts (sulphates) flocculate colloids causing them to settle out
- Salt flocculates colloids up to the point where excess sodium deflocculates clays
- Acid rain can induce flocculation of ferric oxide colloids

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Soil colloids directly mediate nutrient availability to mycorrhizal fungi and plant roots. The ion exchange capacity of colloids determines how effectively nutrients are retained in the root zone versus being leached. In [[mushroom-substrate-preparation]], understanding colloid behaviour informs casing material selection: peat-based casings function partly through their colloidal properties, which regulate water availability and micro-environmental conditions for pin initiation. In tropical soils, most colloids exist as cellular gels in the biomass or as bacterial sheathing material, making biological soil management more critical than mineral amendment.

## Colloid Formation in Soils

Mollison described soil colloids as the dynamic interface between mineral and
organic soil fractions. These sub-micrometer particles carry negative surface
charges that hold cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, K+, NH4+) against leaching, forming the
soil's [[cation-exchange-capacity]].

## Gel-Surface Interactions

Soil gels form when organic polymers (polysaccharides from bacterial slime,
root exudates, and humic compounds) bridge mineral particles. These gels create
water-stable aggregates essential for soil structure and porosity.

## Practical Implications

Understanding colloid behavior helps permaculture practitioners manage soil
fertility without synthetic inputs. Compost additions increase organic colloid
content, improving nutrient retention. Reduced tillage preserves existing
colloid-gel structures that take years to develop.
