# Pennisetum

## Description

Pennisetum is a large genus of tropical and subtropical grasses in the family Poaceae, commonly known as fountaingrasses or bristle grasses. The genus includes both highly productive forage grasses and significant cereal crops. Species range from low-growing ground covers to tall perennial bunch grasses reaching 3–5 meters in height. They are distributed across Africa, Asia, and the Pacific Islands.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Poaceae (Gramineae)
- **Subfamily**: Panicoideae
- **Genus**: Pennisetum
- **Notable species**: *P. purpureum* (elephant/Napier grass), *P. glaucum* (pearl millet), *P. setaceum* (crimson fountaingrass), *P. clandestinum* (kikuyu grass)

## Key Facts

- Elephant grass (*P. purpureum*) is one of the highest-yielding tropical forage grasses, producing 30–80 tonnes of dry matter per hectare per year under favorable conditions.
- Pearl millet (*P. glaucum*) is a critical staple cereal crop in arid and semi-arid tropical regions of Africa and South Asia, tolerant of drought and high temperatures.
- Pennisetum species are effective pioneer plants in ecological succession, often the first tall grasses to colonize disturbed sites in tropical grasslands.
- The genus includes both annual and perennial species adapted to a wide range of rainfall conditions.
- Many species produce abundant biomass suitable for mulch, thatch, and erosion control.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Mollison references Pennisetum in multiple contexts within the humid tropics chapter:

- **Ecological succession pioneer**: Listed as an early successional grass in humid areas, preceding shrubs like castor oil bean and Prosopis in the natural progression toward forest. Understanding this succession allows designers to accelerate or guide land rehabilitation.
- **Mulch production**: Pennisetum straw is listed in Mollison's mulch comparison trials (Table 10.1) where it produced strong crop yields when used as mulch for maize (3.3 t/ha), cowpea (1.2 t/ha), soybean (1.4 t/ha), and cassava (14.2 t/ha).
- **Soil stabilization**: Rapid root development and dense growth habit make it valuable for stabilizing swales, contour banks, and erosion-prone slopes in tropical earthworks.
- **Forage system integration**: Elephant grass is a cornerstone species in tropical cut-and-carry forage systems, supporting integrated livestock-crop production.

## Ecological Considerations

- Some Pennisetum species, notably *P. setaceum*, are considered invasive in Mediterranean and arid climates outside their native range.
- Elephant grass can become dominant and suppress other species if not managed through regular cutting or grazing.
- Kikuyu grass (*P. clandestinum*) is valued as a lawn and pasture grass but can be aggressive in garden settings.

## Cultivation Notes

- Propagates by seed (pearl millet) or stem cuttings and splits (elephant grass).
- Elephant grass is typically planted in rows 1 m apart with 0.5–1 m spacing within rows.
- Responds well to nitrogen fertilization; cutting height of 10–15 cm above ground promotes regrowth.
- Pearl millet matures in 75–90 days, making it one of the fastest-growing cereal crops.
