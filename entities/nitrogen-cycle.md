## Nitrogen Cycle

## Description

The nitrogen cycle is the biogeochemical process by which nitrogen is converted between its various chemical forms in the environment, enabling its uptake by plants and its return to the atmosphere. This cycle involves nitrogen fixation (converting atmospheric N2 to plant-available forms), ammonification, nitrification, assimilation, and denitrification. The nitrogen cycle is fundamental to all life on Earth, as nitrogen is a component of proteins, DNA, RNA, and chlorophyll.

## Classification

- **Type:** Biogeochemical cycle
- **Key Elements:** Nitrogen (N) in various forms: N2, NH4+, NO2-, NO3-
- **Participants:** Bacteria, Archaea, plants, fungi, geological processes, industrial processes

## Key Steps in the Cycle

### 1. Nitrogen Fixation
Atmospheric nitrogen (N2, making up 78% of the atmosphere) is converted to plant-available forms:

- **Biological fixation:** Diazotrophs (Rhizobia, Frankia, Azotobacter, and others) break the triple covalent bond of N2 using the enzyme nitrogenase
- **Atmospheric fixation:** Lightning and snow storms convert small amounts of N2 to reactive forms
- **Industrial fixation:** The Haber-Bosch process artificially fixes N2 into ammonia (NH3)

Today, only about 50–60% of available nitrogen comes from biological systems; the rest is industrially produced.

### 2. Ammonification
When plants and animals die, bacteria and fungi decompose their remains, producing ammonium (NH4+). This organic nitrogen is converted back to inorganic form, making it available again.

### 3. Nitrification
Ammonium (NH4+) is converted by nitrifying bacteria to nitrite (NO2-), which is then converted by bacteria and Archaea to nitrate (NO3-):

- NH4+ → NO2- (by ammonia-oxidizing bacteria)
- NO2- → NO3- (by nitrite-oxidizing bacteria and Archaea)

### 4. Assimilation
Plants take up nitrogen as ammonium (NH4+) or nitrate (NO3-) through their roots and assimilate it into amino acids, proteins, nucleotides (DNA/RNA), and chlorophyll.

### 5. Denitrification
When soil or water becomes anaerobic (lacking oxygen), nitrates are converted back to atmospheric nitrogen (N2) by anaerobic microbes that use nitrogen compounds as an energy source. This represents a loss of nitrogen from the soil system.

## Key Facts

- Atmospheric nitrogen (N2) makes up 78% of Earth's atmosphere but is unavailable to most organisms
- The triple covalent bond (N≡N) is extremely strong and requires enormous energy to break
- Up to 50% of garden nitrogen can come from nitrogen-fixing organisms
- Fixed nitrogen exists primarily as ammonium (NH4+) and nitrate (NO3-)
- Nitrification and denitrification complete the cycle, returning nitrogen to the atmosphere
- The Haber-Bosch process now provides the majority of agricultural nitrogen

## Soil pH Effects

Nitrogen forms have direct effects on soil pH:

- Adding NO3- causes hydroxyl ions (OH-) to be released, raising rhizosphere pH
- Higher pH increases solubility of iron and aluminum phosphates
- This pH shift affects the availability of other nutrients

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Mycorrhizal transport:** Mycorrhizal fungi transport nitrogen (as well as phosphorus) from soil to plant roots
- **Compost and organic matter:** Organic nitrogen in compost is mineralized by bacteria and fungi through ammonification
- **Legume rotation:** Planting nitrogen-fixing legumes (with Rhizobia) naturally enriches soil nitrogen
- **Cover crops:** Nitrogen-fixing cover crops reduce the need for synthetic nitrogen fertilizers
- **Denitrification loss:** Waterlogged, anaerobic soils promote denitrification, losing nitrogen to the atmosphere — good drainage helps retain soil nitrogen
- **Soil food web:** The nitrogen cycle is driven by soil microbes; maintaining healthy microbial populations supports efficient nitrogen cycling

## Agricultural Management

Gardeners and farmers can optimize the nitrogen cycle through several practices:

- **Crop rotation with legumes:** Legumes (beans, peas, clover) fix atmospheric nitrogen via Rhizobia, leaving residual nitrogen for subsequent crops
- **Composting:** Returns organic nitrogen to soil; microbial decomposition mineralizes it into plant-available forms
- **Cover cropping:** Nitrogen-fixing cover crops prevent nitrogen leaching and add biological nitrogen
- **Proper drainage:** Prevents anaerobic conditions that promote denitrification losses
- **Mulching:** Organic mulches slowly release nitrogen as they decompose
- **Mycorrhizal management:** Maintaining mycorrhizal fungal networks supports efficient nitrogen transport
