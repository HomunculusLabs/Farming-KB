## Helianthus maximiliani

## Overview

**Helianthus maximiliani** Schrad., commonly known as [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], ranging from southern Canada to northern Mexico.

The species is among the tallest native sunflowers, typically reaching 1.5–3.5 meters in height, with a robust central stem and numerous narrow leaves arranged in a distinctive whorled pattern along the upper stem. It produces clusters of bright yellow flower heads 5–8 cm across from late summer through autumn.

Maximilian sunflower is widely planted in native landscaping, prairie restorations, and wildlife habitat projects. Its late-season bloom provides critical nectar and pollen resources for migrating monarch butterflies and native bees when few other plants are flowering.

The species is named after Prince Maximilian of Wied-Neuwied, a German naturalist and ethnologist who explored the American Great Plains in the 1830s. He collected the type specimen during his expedition along the Missouri River.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Plantae — **Order:** Asterales — **Family:** Asteraceae — **Subfamily:** Asteroideae — **Tribe:** Heliantheae — **Genus:** Helianthus

**Authority:** Heinrich Friedrich Link, *Enumeratio Plantarum Horti Regii Berolinensis* (1822), based on material collected by Prince Maximilian.

*H. maximiliani* is a perennial herb growing from a thick, fleshy rhizome system that produces multiple stems from a single root crown. Stems are erect, stout, and usually unbranched below the inflorescence, covered with short stiff hairs.

Leaves are lanceolate to narrowly elliptic, 10–25 cm long and 1–4 cm wide, with entire or finely serrate margins. The most distinctive leaf feature is the tendency for leaves to occur in dense whorls of 3–6 at intervals along the upper stem, a trait unique among [[holmgren-ecological-succession-and-pulsing]].

Maximilian sunflower is hardy to USDA Zone 3 (−40 °C) and is drought-tolerant once established. It thrives in full sun and well-drained loam or clay-loam soils but tolerates rocky, shallow, and moderately alkaline conditions. It is considered weedy in agricultural settings due to its aggressive rhizomatous spread.

The species has been widely planted outside its native range for ornamental and ecological purposes and has naturalized in scattered locations across [[cervantes-seed-production-storage]] makes Maximilian sunflower one of the most productive native seed sources for wildlife in the Great Plains region.

## Cultivation and Ornamental Use

Maximilian sunflower is easily propagated by division of rhizomes in early spring or by seed. Cold stratification (30–60 days at 4 °C) improves germination rates. Seeds should be planted 1–2 cm deep in prepared soil; germination typically occurs within 14–21 days.

In garden settings, the species requires well-drained soil and full sun. It is highly drought-tolerant once established but benefits from occasional irrigation during prolonged dry spells. The tall stature (often exceeding 2.5 meters) may require staking in exposed locations.

The aggressive rhizomatous spread can make *H. maximiliani* invasive in formal garden beds. It is best suited to naturalistic plantings, meadow gardens, and large-scale prairie restorations where its spreading habit is an asset. Root barriers can be used to contain rhizome spread in smaller gardens.

Cultivars selected for shorter stature, enhanced flower production, and reduced rhizome aggression are available in the native plant nursery trade. The species pairs well with other tall prairie perennials including *Solidago* spp., *Andropogon gerardii*, and *Sorghastrum nutans*.

In prairie restoration projects, Maximilian sunflower is often included in seed mixes for its rapid establishment and ability to suppress invasive species through dense rhizomatous growth. It is particularly effective at competing with cool-season exotic grasses such as *Bromus inermis* and *Agropyron cristatum*.

## Edible and Traditional Use

The achenes are edible and similar in flavor and nutritional content to cultivated sunflower (*H. annuus*) seeds, though smaller. Indigenous peoples of the Great Plains, including the Lakota and Omaha, harvested Maximilian sunflower seeds as a food source.

Seeds were traditionally collected in autumn, dried, and ground into meal for use in porridges and flatbreads. The seeds were also eaten raw or roasted. Oil extracted from the seeds was used for cooking and as a hair dressing.

The Lakota used a decoction of the root as a remedy for chest pain and respiratory ailments. The Omaha applied poultices of crushed flower heads to burns and wounds. The Pawnee used the stems as a source of fiber for cordage and basket weaving.
