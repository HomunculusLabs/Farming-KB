## Description

[[ilya-prigogine]] (1917–2003) was a Russian-Belgian physical chemist and Nobel laureate whose work on dissipative structures and thermodynamics of irreversible processes fundamentally changed our understanding of how complex, organized systems emerge from chaos. Mollison quotes a *Newsweek* article from October 24, 1977, about Prigogine's Nobel Prize, noting that "in an open system, energy is gained or lost irreversibly" and that "biological systems tend to become increasingly complex and efficient" — seemingly violating the second law of thermodynamics' prediction of increasing entropy.

Prigogine resolved this apparent paradox by showing that open systems far from thermodynamic equilibrium can spontaneously self-organize into more complex structures (dissipative structures) by exporting entropy to their environment. This insight is foundational to understanding how living systems, ecosystems, and by extension designed cultivation systems can become more organized and productive over time.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Physical chemistry, thermodynamics, complexity science
- **Era**: Mid to late 20th century
- **Tradition**: European scientific tradition, Brussels school of thermodynamics

## Key Facts

- Awarded the 1977 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his contributions to non-equilibrium thermodynamics
- Developed the theory of dissipative structures — ordered systems that maintain themselves by exporting entropy
- Showed that the second law of thermodynamics (increasing entropy) applies to closed systems, but open systems can decrease local entropy by increasing it elsewhere
- Founded the Center for [[statistical-mechanics]] and Thermodynamics at the University of Texas at Austin
- Authored *Order Out of Chaos* (1984, with Isabelle Stengers) and *The End of Certainty* (1997)
- His work bridges physics, chemistry, biology, and philosophy of science

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Prigogine's work is directly relevant to understanding how cultivation systems function:

1. **Open-system cultivation**: Cultivation systems are open systems that exchange energy and matter with their environment. Prigogine's framework explains why they can become more organized and productive rather than running down.
2. **Self-organization**: Fungal mycelial networks are classic dissipative structures — they maintain their organization by continuously processing and transforming energy and materials from their environment.
3. **Irreversible processes**: In cultivation, processes like decomposition, nutrient uptake, and fruiting body formation are irreversible transformations. Understanding this irreversibility is key to system design.
4. **Complexity from chaos**: The principle that ordered structures emerge from far-from-equilibrium conditions explains how complex soil food webs and mycorrhizal networks develop in healthy cultivation systems.
5. **Energy management**: Mollison's design strategy of intercepting energy "from source to sink" is grounded in Prigogine's thermodynamic framework — the goal is to use energy to build system complexity before it dissipates as entropy.
