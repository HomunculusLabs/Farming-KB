# Burrowers and Soil Life

## Description

Burrowers and soil macrofauna are the "gardeners" of the earth — the moles, gophers, worms, prairie dogs, termites, ants, crustaceans, rodents, and larvae whose constant subterranean activity aerates, turns, fertilises, and structures the soil. Mollison devotes an entire section (8.20, "Life in Earth") to describing how these organisms maintain soil health and how [[permaculture]] designers can harness their activities.

## Classification

- **Type**: Ecological concept / soil biological community
- **Category**: Soil ecology, zoology, landscape design
- **Key organisms**: [[earthworms]], termites, ants, moles, gophers, prairie dogs, rodents, wombats, rabbits, crustaceans, insect larvae, bardi grubs, ghost moths

## Key Facts

- Before humans learned to cut open soil, it was "thoroughly dug, aerated, and overturned by multitudes of industrious burrowers." Unploughed meadows of Europe and America are "as soft as a great mattress" due to the eternal work of moles, gophers, worms, prairie dogs, rodents, and larvae.
- Termites and ants are the "earthworms of the deserts and drylands," carrying tons of organic material to underground [[compost]] piles and sometimes cultivating fungi to feed their colonies.
- Earthworms and crustaceans move up and down in their burrows "like a billion pump plungers, sucking in and expelling air (and thus nitrogen) to roots, and in effect giving the soil its daily breath."
- Many plants depend on burrowers for propagation: comfrey and sunroot ([[helianthus-tuberosus]]) spread via roots carried by moles and gophers, having lost the ability to produce viable seed.
- Foxes eat fruits and defecate on gopher mounds, creating the dug-over areas where new trees establish.
- Underground fungal spores eaten by squirrels or wallabies are activated by digestive enzymes, breaking hibernation to colonise new ground and assist root growth of acorns and eucalypts.
- Wombats tunnel, overturn, and topple many hectares of trees, leaving richly-manured beds for new forest.
- Engaeid "land crabs" or earth lobsters burrow to 2 m depth and 2 m horizontally, opening thousands of shafts to the air.

## Relevance to Cultivation

- **Soil aeration**: Burrowers provide free, continuous aeration that no mechanical system can match in scale or longevity.
- **Nutrient cycling**: Worms add alkaline manure; termites compost organic material underground; all burrowers transport and transform nutrients.
- **Seed dispersal and germination**: Many species bury, activate, and plant seeds and spores — effectively providing a free sowing service.
- **Water infiltration**: Burrow networks create preferential flow paths for water deep into the soil profile.
- **Design opportunities**: Mollison suggests it is valid to plant Acacia for the by-product of swift moth larvae, to use moles instead of mole ploughs, and gophers as daffodil gardeners.
- **Warning against eradication**: The Navajo warning — "If you kill off the prairie dogs, there will be no one to cry for rain" — was vindicated when burrower eradication near Chilchinbito, Arizona created a virtual wasteland through fierce runoff and soil compaction.

## Ecological Connections

- Root systems of plants parallel burrower networks: roots also have penetrations, patterns, schedules, and seasonal migrations.
- Some plant species secrete phenols or creosols to inhibit competitors (bracken, tamarisk, Juglandaceae, Brassicas), while others trap or poison nematodes (marigolds, fungi, Crotalaria).
- Trees act as long-term nutrient pumps, depositing minerals as leaves and bark that fungi and soil crustaceans convert to humus.

## Practical Applications

Burrowers Soil Life Mollison plays a critical role in sustainable land management and regenerative agriculture.
Understanding these processes enables practitioners to design systems that work with natural
soil biology rather than against it, reducing inputs while improving long-term productivity.

## Management Considerations

Effective management requires monitoring soil indicators and responding to changes in
soil structure, organic matter content, and biological activity. Key strategies include
minimizing soil disturbance, maintaining ground cover, and incorporating diverse plantings.
