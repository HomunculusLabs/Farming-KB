## Overview

Catuaba is a common name for the bark of several tree species native to the Brazilian Amazon, most commonly **Trichilia catigua** (Meliaceae). The bark has been used for centuries in Brazilian folk medicine as a tonic, aphrodisiac, and central nervous system stimulant.

The term "catuaba" derives from the Tupi language, meaning "what gives strength to the Indian." It is one of the most commercially important herbal remedies in Brazilian phytotherapy.

Commercial catuaba preparations may also include bark from **Anemopaegma arvense** (Bignoniaceae), known as "catuaba verdadeira" (true catuaba), leading to [[fungal-biodiversity-tropical-forests]] at low to moderate elevations.

The species is most abundant in the states of Pará, Amazonas, and Mato Grosso in Brazil. It thrives in well-drained, fertile soils of primary forest but tolerates secondary growth.

Anemopaegma arvense is distributed across the Brazilian cerrado (savanna) and caatinga (dry forest) biomes, reflecting broader ecological adaptation than the forest-dwelling T. catigua.

Both species are harvested primarily from wild populations, with limited cultivation of T. catigua for commercial bark production.

## Bioactive Compounds

The bark of Trichilia catigua contains a range of indole alkaloids, including catuabine D, catuabine E, and catuabine F. These tropane-related alkaloids are considered the primary bioactive constituents responsible for central nervous system effects.

Additional compounds include flavonoids (catechin, epicatechin), phenolic acids (chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid), and saponins. The total alkaloid content of dried bark ranges from 0.5–2%.

Anemopaegma arvense bark contains different compounds, notably flavonoid glycosides (enantiomers of kaempferol and quercetin derivatives) and the coumarin scopoletin. This chemical divergence explains the inconsistent pharmacological results across studies.

Antioxidant capacity of T. catigua extracts is attributed primarily to catechin and procyanidin content, with significant radical scavenging activity demonstrated in vitro.

## Pharmacology

Trichilia catigua extracts have demonstrated dopaminergic activity in preclinical studies, increasing dopamine levels in the central nervous system. This mechanism may underlie the traditional aphrodisiac and stimulant effects.

Animal studies show improvement in memory acquisition and retention, suggesting potential nootropic properties. Antidepressant-like effects have been observed in forced-swim and tail-suspension tests in rodents.

The bark extracts exhibit anti-inflammatory and analgesic activities in carrageenan-induced paw edema and hot-plate models, respectively. Nitric oxide modulation may contribute to vasodilatory effects relevant to [[mushroom-cancer-clinical-trials-overview]] are limited. A small open-label study of a multi-herb preparation containing catuaba reported improved sexual function scores in male participants.

## Traditional Use

In Brazilian folk medicine, catuaba bark is prepared as a decoction or maceration in alcohol (tincture). The traditional dose is approximately 1–2 cups of bark tea daily or 10–20 mL of tincture.

The primary traditional indication is as an aphrodisiac and sexual tonic for both men and women. It is also used for general fatigue, nervous exhaustion, and memory impairment.

Indigenous Amazonian communities use catuaba as a general stimulant and nerve tonic, often combined with guarana (*[[european-smartshop-economics-mushroom-supply-chain-emcdda-2006]].

Quality control in the catuaba trade is problematic. Adulteration with inert bark, substitution with A. arvense, and contamination with undeclared species are commonly reported by [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]] of commercial products.

Cultivation trials of T. catigua have shown that the tree can be grown from seed with bark harvest possible after 5–7 years. However, plantation-grown bark may have a different phytochemical profile than wild-harvested material.
