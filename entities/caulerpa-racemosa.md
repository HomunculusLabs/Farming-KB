## Overview

**Caulerpa racemosa** is a species of green macroalga in the family Caulerpaceae. It is widely distributed across tropical and subtropical waters and is best known for its distinctive grape-like branch clusters.

The species gained global notoriety when the invasive variety *C. racemosa* var. *cylindracea* spread rapidly throughout the Mediterranean Sea in the 1990s. This variety outcompetes native seagrasses and benthic algae, fundamentally altering benthic community structure across the basin.

Several varieties of *C. racemosa* are harvested as edible sea grapes, particularly in Southeast Asian cuisine. The thallus has a crisp, slightly salty flavor and is served raw in salads and cold dishes.

The genus name comes from the Greek "kaulos" (stem) and "erpos" (creeping), referencing the creeping stolon growth habit. The species epithet "racemosa" refers to the raceme-like arrangement of the vesicular branchlets.

The edible varieties are marketed commercially under names such as "sea grapes," "green caviar," and "umibudo." The Japanese market alone imports hundreds of tons annually, with growing demand across East and Southeast Asia.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomic classification:** Kingdom Plantae, Phylum Chlorophyta, Class Ulvophyceae, Order Bryopsidales, Family Caulerpaceae, Genus *Caulerpa*, Species *C. racemosa*.

The authority is (Forsskål) J. Agardh (1873). The basionym is *Fucus racemosus* Forsskål (1775), originally described from the Red Sea.

Multiple varieties are recognized, including var. *racemosa*, var. *cylindracea*, var. *lamourouxii*, and var. *occidentalis*. The invasive Mediterranean variety is formally designated as var. *cylindracea* (Sonder) Verlaque, Huisman & Boudouresque.

The thallus consists of a creeping stolon anchored by rhizoids that penetrate the substrate. Upright branches arise from the stolon at regular intervals, terminating in clusters of vesicular branchlets.

The vesicles are spherical to ovoid, typically 2–5 mm in diameter, arranged in compact grape-like clusters. In var. *cylindracea*, the branchlets are more cylindrical and less densely clustered than in the typical variety.

Color ranges from bright green to dark green depending on light conditions and depth. The alga is coenocytic, meaning the entire thallus is a single multinucleate cell without cross-walls.

This unicellular organization is unusual among multicellular-appearing algae and requires specialized cytoplasmic streaming for [[fungal-life-cycles-sexual-reproduction-metamorphosis]] and vegetative fragmentation. Even small fragments can regenerate into complete thalli, making mechanical removal difficult and facilitating dispersal via boat anchors and fishing gear.

Chemical defense compounds, primarily caulerpenyne, deter herbivory by generalist grazers. This allelopathic advantage allows the alga to outcompete native vegetation without significant grazing pressure from Mediterranean herbivores.

In its native range, the species plays a more balanced ecological role as one component of diverse algal communities. Specialist herbivores and natural competitors keep populations in check, preventing the monoculture formations seen in invaded habitats.

The invasive variety was likely introduced to the Mediterranean through shipping (ballast water or hull fouling) or through the Suez Canal as a Lessepsian migrant. Genetic studies suggest multiple independent introduction events rather than a single founding population.

## Chemistry and Nutrition

The characteristic [[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]] include racemosin A and B, caulerpicin, and various sulfated polysaccharides. These compounds contribute to chemical defense and have attracted interest for potential pharmaceutical development.

Nutritionally, *C. racemosa* is rich in dietary fiber, minerals, and vitamins. Key minerals include calcium, magnesium, iron, and iodine at concentrations comparable to other edible seaweeds.

Protein content is moderate at 8–12% of dry weight with a favorable amino acid profile. The sea grape varieties are notable for their high content of unsaturated [[stable-carbon-isotope-signature-fatty-acids-mycorrhizal-carbon-tracking]], including oleic and linoleic acid.

Vitamin content includes measurable amounts of vitamin A (as beta-carotene), vitamin C, and several B-group vitamins. The mineral profile is dominated by calcium, magnesium, iron, and zinc in bioavailable forms.

They also contain significant amounts of pectin, which contributes to their characteristic gelatinous texture when consumed raw. This texture, combined with the popping mouthfeel of the vesicles, makes them popular in Japanese and Filipino cuisine.
