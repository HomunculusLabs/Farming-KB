The species is notable for its distinctive cup-shaped gemmae-bearing structures, a reproductive feature uncommon among mosses.

Tetraphis pellucida is primarily a pioneer species, colonizing decaying wood, shaded rock surfaces, and disturbed mineral soil.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Tetraphis pellucida was described by the Swedish botanist Johan Hedwig in 1748.

The genus Tetraphis is the sole member of the family Tetraphidaceae and the order Tetraphidales.

The gametophyte forms small, loose to dense tufts, typically 0.5 to 2.0 centimeters in height.

Stems are slender, erect, and sparsely branched, with a reddish to brownish coloration at the base.

Leaves are ovate to broadly ovate, 1.0 to 1.5 millimeters long, with an acute to acuminate apex.

The leaf margins are entire or slightly toothed near the apex, and the costa extends to the mid-leaf or above.

Leaf cells are quadrate to rectangular in the upper portion, becoming more elongated toward the base.

A highly distinctive feature is the presence of specialized cup-shaped gemmae cups at stem apices.

These gemmae cups are 0.5 to 1.0 millimeters in diameter and contain numerous lens-shaped gemmae.

Each gemma is a multicellular propagule capable of developing into a new gametophyte individual.

The sporophyte features a capsule with a characteristic four-toothed peristome, the origin of the genus name "Tetraphis."

## Distribution and Habitat

Tetraphis pellucida has a wide circumboreal distribution across the Northern Hemisphere.

In North America, it occurs from Alaska and Newfoundland southward through the Great Lakes region to the Appalachian Mountains.

The species is also widespread across Europe, from Scandinavia and the British Isles to the Mediterranean region.

In Asia, it ranges from Siberia and Japan southward through the Himalayan region.

The species is most commonly found on decaying conifer [[wood-decomposition-by-fungi]] and other microorganisms.

On shaded rock surfaces, Tetraphis pellucida is among the first mosses to establish, often forming small pioneer communities.

The gemmae cups are a key adaptation for rapid colonization, allowing [[conidia-and-asexual-reproduction-in-filamentous-fungi]] without reliance on water for sperm transfer.

Gemmae are dispersed by rain splash, which ejects the small propagules several centimeters from the parent plant.

The species frequently co-occurs with other pioneer mosses such as Lophocolea heterophylla and Nowellia curvifolia on decaying wood.

In mature [[dighton-fungi-acidifying-pollutants-forest-ecosystems]], Tetraphis pellucida populations tend to decline as shading increases and competitor mosses become established.
