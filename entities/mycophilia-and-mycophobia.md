## Description

Mycophilia and mycophobia are two contrasting cultural attitudes towards mushrooms, concepts introduced by **R. [[gordon-wasson]]** and his wife **Valentina Wasson**. These terms describe the deep divide between cultures that revere and cherish mushrooms (mycophilia) and those that fear and avoid them (mycophobia). The distinction has profound implications for understanding how different societies have historically interacted with psychoactive mushroom species.

## Definitions

### Mycophilia
- **Love or strong appreciation** of mushrooms
- Cultures that cherish mushrooms as food, medicine, and sacrament
- Examples: Slavic countries (Russia, Poland, Czech Republic), Mexican Indian tribes, many Asian cultures
- Associated with **positive attitudes** toward psychoactive mushrooms
- Often includes detailed taxonomic knowledge passed through generations

### Mycophobia
- **Fear or aversion** towards mushrooms
- Cultures that view mushrooms with suspicion, associating them with poison or decay
- Examples: Britain, much of Western Europe, Anglo-American cultures
- Associated with **negative framing** of psychoactive effects (e.g., "insanity," "foolishness")
- May result from historical loss of mushroom knowledge

## Historical Context

Gartz argues that the development of early cultural taboos and prohibitions against psychotropic mushrooms may be the root cause of enduring mycophobic behavior. Key observations:

1. **European mycophobia** likely originated from the loss or destruction of early cultural knowledge about psychoactive plants and mushrooms, probably during the Christianization of Europe
2. **England** exhibits an entrenched dislike for mushrooms (mycophobia), reflecting traditional beliefs vastly different from those found in Slavic countries
3. **Mexico** demonstrates profound mycophilia — Mexican Indian tribes never causally linked [[psilocybin]] effects to mental illness; they were the only Indians in the Americas who also harvested large numbers of mushroom species for food
4. **Modern "official mycophobia"** was established in the mid-1960s through anti-drug legislation that effectively prevented scientific investigation of psilocybin

## The Wasson Hypothesis

R.G. Wasson and Valentina Wasson proposed that these divergent attitudes subdivide cultures with different traditional attitudes towards mushrooms into two groups. The origins and evolution of such diverging attitudes remain lost in history, but may be connected to:

- Survival of pre-Christian mushroom knowledge in certain regions
- Historical patterns of mushroom poisoning in particular countries
- Religious and cultural suppression of indigenous plant knowledge
- Individual childhood experiences with unusual mushrooms

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Understanding the mycophilia/mycophobia divide is essential for contextualizing the history of psychoactive mushroom research. Mycophilic cultures preserved practical knowledge about [[mushroom-identification]], preparation, and use across centuries, while mycophobic cultures lost or suppressed such knowledge. The current legal and social framework surrounding psychoactive mushrooms in Western countries reflects a continuation of institutionalized mycophobia.

## Key Facts

- Concepts introduced by R.G. Wasson and Valentina Wasson
- Mycophilia = love/appreciation of mushrooms (Slavic, Mexican, Asian cultures)
- Mycophobia = fear/aversion towards mushrooms (British, Anglo-American cultures)
- European mycophobia likely stems from destruction of pre-Christian knowledge
- Modern drug policy reflects institutionalized "official mycophobia"
- Mexican Indians were uniquely mycophilic among Americas indigenous peoples
