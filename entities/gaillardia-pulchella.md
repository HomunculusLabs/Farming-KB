## Overview

**Gaillardia pulchella**, commonly known as Indian blanket, firewheel, or blanketflower, is a short-lived annual or tender perennial in the family Asteraceae. It is native to the central and southern [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] and long bloom season.

The species exhibits remarkable phenotypic plasticity, producing compact plants under drought stress and taller, more branching forms when moisture is abundant.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Gaillardia pulchella was described by Louis Auguste Guillaume Bosc in 1817. The genus honors Antoine René Gaillard de Charentonneau, an 18th-century French botanist and patron of botany.

Plants grow 20 to 60 centimeters tall from a taproot, with one or multiple branching stems arising from a basal rosette. The stems are slender, often purplish-tinged, and covered with fine spreading hairs.

Leaves are alternate, lanceolate to oblanceolate, 3 to 8 centimeters long, with margins entire to pinnately lobed. The foliage is grayish-green due to a dense covering of fine hairs, which helps reduce water loss in hot, dry conditions.

Flower heads are solitary on long peduncles, 4 to 7 centimeters in diameter. The involucral bracts (phyllaries) are in 2 to 3 series, with the outer series ciliate and often purplish at the base.

Ray florets are typically 8 to 14, bicolored with a red to orange proximal zone and yellow distal zone, giving the characteristic "firewheel" appearance. Disk florets are numerous, reddish-purple to brown, forming a prominent central dome.

Achenes are 3 to 5 millimeters long, flattened, and crowned with a short, scale-like pappus. Chromosome number: 2n = 34. Flowering occurs from April through October, depending on latitude and moisture availability.

Natural hybridization with G. aristata (perennial blanketflower) is common where ranges overlap, producing G. × grandiflora, a widely cultivated hybrid group that combines the annual vigor of G. pulchella with the perennial habit of G. aristata.

The distinctive bicolor ray floret pattern is controlled by carotenoid pigments, with lutein derivatives producing the yellow tips and astaxanthin-related compounds responsible for the red-orange basal zone.

The pappus scales are rigid and truncate, a character that distinguishes Gaillardia from related genera with more elaborate pappus structures.

## Distribution and Habitat

Gaillardia pulchella is native to the central and southern Great Plains, the southwestern United States, and northern Mexico. Its native range extends from Kansas and Colorado south to Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona, and east to Louisiana and Mississippi.

The species has been widely introduced outside its native range and is naturalized in the eastern United States, California, Hawaii, [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] wildflowers to cultivate, thriving in USDA hardiness zones 2 through 11 as a warm-season annual. It is often direct-sown in meadow plantings and requires no special soil preparation.

Seeds should be sown in [[vegetable-storage-conditions-by-temperature-and-humidity]].

Plants flower approximately 60 to 90 days from sowing and continue blooming until frost. Deadheading extends the bloom period and reduces self-seeding, which can be prolific in favorable conditions.

In garden settings, Indian blanket performs best in full sun with minimal supplemental irrigation. Overwatering and overly rich soils produce floppy, short-lived plants with reduced flowering.

The G. × grandiflora hybrids ('Arizona Sun', 'Goblin', 'Burgundy') are perennial in zones 3 through 9 and are often preferred for permanent garden plantings. These cultivars are widely available and more cold-hardy than the species.

The species is susceptible to aster yellows phytoplasma, which can cause distorted growth and greenish, sterile flower heads. Affected plants should be removed and destroyed to prevent spread.

## Ecological Role

Gaillardia pulchella is an exceptional nectar and pollen source for butterflies, [[comparison-honey-bees-vs-native-solitary-bees]]. The flowers are visited by a wide range of pollinators, including monarchs, painted ladies, skippers, and various bee species.

The seeds are consumed by finches, sparrows, and other granivorous birds. The plants provide moderate cover value for ground-dwelling wildlife in open habitats.

In prairie and meadow ecosystems, Indian blanket serves as a pioneer species on disturbed sites, rapidly colonizing bare soil and providing early-season color. Its ability to thrive in poor soils makes it valuable for [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]] on degraded land.

The species has low palatability to mammalian herbivores due to the bitter sesquiterpene lactones present in the foliage. This unpalatability contributes to its persistence in heavily grazed rangelands.

The long bloom season provides nectar resources spanning several months, making the species particularly valuable for sustaining pollinator populations through periods of floral scarcity.
