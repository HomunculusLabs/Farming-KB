## Overview

Cladium jamaicense, commonly known as sawgrass, is a perennial sedge in the family Cyperaceae and the dominant plant species of the Florida Everglades.

Despite its common name, sawgrass is not a true grass but a sedge closely related to bulrushes and nutgrasses.

The species is named for the Jamaican parish where it was first scientifically described, though its range extends far beyond the Caribbean.

Sawgrass is considered an ecological keystone species of subtropical wetland ecosystems throughout the Americas.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Cladium jamaicense (Crantz) Kükenthal, family Cyperaceae, tribe Scirpeae, subfamily Cyperoideae.

The genus Cladium contains approximately five species distributed across tropical and subtropical regions worldwide.

Sawgrass typically grows 1.5 to 2.5 meters tall, with some individuals reaching 3 meters under optimal conditions.

The leaves are long, flat, and stiff with serrated margins containing silica deposits that give the plant its common name.

These serrated leaf edges can cut exposed skin, a characteristic that distinguishes sawgrass from softer marsh grasses.

The inflorescence is a compound panicle bearing numerous small spikelets, each 3 to 5 millimeters long.

Flowering occurs from April through October, with peak anthesis during the summer wet season.

The root system is extensively rhizomatous, forming dense monospecific stands that can persist for decades.

Rhizomes are thick and woody, typically 5 to 15 millimeters in diameter, and can extend several meters from the parent plant.

## Ecological Role

Sawgrass is the primary structure-forming species of the Everglades and defines the character of the marl prairie and ridge communities.

Dense sawgrass stands slow water flow during the wet season, contributing to sheet-flow hydrology that sustains the entire ecosystem.

Dead [[biodiversity-of-fungi-leaf-litter-microfungi-survey-methods]] accumulates as peat, building the organic soils that underlie much of the Everglades wetland complex.

These peat soils store significant quantities of carbon, making sawgrass marshes important carbon sinks in subtropical regions.

Sawgrass provides nesting habitat and cover for numerous wetland birds, including the endangered snail kite and wood stork.

The plant also offers refuge for fish, amphibians, and invertebrates during both wet and dry seasonal phases.

Fire is a natural and essential component of sawgrass ecology in the Everglades landscape.

Sawgrass is fire-adapted, with rhizomes surviving belowground while aboveground biomass burns during the dry season.

Post-fire regrowth is rapid, and burned areas typically recover full biomass within a single [[extending-growing-season]].

However, fires that burn into the peat substrate can be destructive, converting sawgrass marsh to open water sloughs.
