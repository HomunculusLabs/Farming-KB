# Plasmodesmata

## Description

Plasmodesmata (singular: plasmodesma) are microscopic intercellular tunnels that connect the cytoplasm of adjacent plant cells, forming a continuous living network called the symplastic pathway. These channels are essential for cell-to-cell communication, transport of nutrients, signaling molecules, and other substances throughout the entire plant. Through plasmodesmata, it is theoretically possible to travel from a root cell to the top of a leaf without leaving the symplastic space.

## Classification

- **Category:** Cellular structure
- **Domain:** Plant cell biology, intercellular transport
- **Location:** Cell membranes (plasmalemma) between adjacent cells

## Key Facts

### Physical Dimensions

- **Diameter:** Approximately 50 nanometers
- **Length:** 90 to 100 nanometers (spanning the distance between adjacent cells)
- **Quantity per cell:** 1,000 to 100,000 plasmodesmata per individual plant cell
- **Density:** Can range from 1 per square micrometer up to 15 per square micrometer in areas requiring high transport capacity

### Formation

Plasmodesmata form during cell division when fragments of the plasmalemma become trapped between the two newly separated daughter cells. These trapped membrane segments create permanent tunnels that bridge the dividing wall. An analogy is twisting a long balloon into sections—bits of the balloon get caught at each twist point, forming connections between the sections. Since every cell division creates new plasmodesmata, and all cells originate from a single fertilized cell, the entire plant is internally connected.

### Structure and Regulation

Each plasmodesma contains a narrower tubular structure called the desmotubule, which connects to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) of both adjacent cells. At both ends of the tunnel—and sometimes within it—are proteins that function like sphincters, opening and closing to regulate which molecules are allowed to pass through. This gating mechanism allows the plant to control intercellular traffic based on the size, charge, and identity of molecules.

### The Symplastic Pathway

Together, the network of plasmodesmata creates the symplastic pathway—the route through which water and dissolved molecules travel entirely within the cytoplasm of living cells. This contrasts with the apoplastic pathway (through cell walls) and the transcellular pathway (directly through membranes). The symplastic pathway allows regulated, selective transport of substances throughout the plant while bypassing the need to repeatedly cross cell membranes.

### Transport Capabilities

- Small molecules including water, ions, sugars, amino acids, and hormones pass freely
- Larger molecules including proteins and RNA can be actively transported through plasmodesmata
- Some plant viruses exploit plasmodesmata to spread infection throughout the plant
- The size exclusion limit varies by tissue type and can be dynamically regulated

### Relationship to Endoplasmic Reticulum

Each plasmodesma contains a desmotubule connected to the ER of adjacent cells, creating a continuous membrane network throughout the plant. This ER connection serves as an additional intracellular transport route, complementing the cytoplasmic flow through the plasmodesmata.

## Regulation Mechanisms

Plasmodesmata are not passive tunnels but actively regulated gateways with multiple control mechanisms:

### Gating and Sphincter Proteins
- **Callose deposition:** The plant can deposit callose (a complex carbohydrate) at the neck of each plasmodesma, narrowing or completely closing the channel. This is the primary mechanism for rapidly closing plasmodesmata.
- **Callose removal:** The enzyme β-1,3-glucanase can dissolve callose deposits to reopen closed plasmodesmata, allowing dynamic regulation.
- **Phosphorylation-dependent gating:** Protein kinases and phosphatases modify the sphincter proteins at plasmodesmata openings, changing their conformation and thus the effective aperture.
- **Cytoplasmic streaming:** The movement of cytoplasm within cells helps drive substances through plasmodesmata, and the rate of streaming can be modulated.

### Environmental Regulation
- **Light/dark cycles:** Plasmodesmata aperture changes with circadian rhythms, often opening wider during daylight when photosynthetic activity requires greater intercellular transport.
- **Temperature:** Cold temperatures can trigger callose deposition, restricting plasmodesmata transport.
- **Osmotic stress:** Changes in cellular water status can alter plasmodesmata conductivity.
- **Wounding:** Physical damage triggers rapid callose-mediated closure of nearby plasmodesmata to prevent loss of cellular contents and limit pathogen spread.

## Size Exclusion Limit (SEL)

The size exclusion limit is a critical parameter governing plasmodesmata function:

- **Baseline SEL:** Under normal conditions, plasmodesmata allow passage of molecules up to approximately 800–1,000 Daltons (roughly the size of a small sugar or amino acid).
- **Tissue-specific variation:** Plasmodesmata in different tissues have different SELs. Source tissues (mature leaves producing sugars) may have wider SELs than sink tissues.
- **Dynamic dilation:** The SEL can be temporarily increased to allow passage of larger signaling molecules (proteins, RNA, even small DNA fragments). Some plant-encoded proteins actively dilate plasmodesmata to facilitate their own intercellular movement.
- **Developmental regulation:** During embryonic development, plasmodesmata SELs are typically larger, allowing more extensive intercellular communication for patterning and differentiation signals.
- **Viral exploitation:** Plant viruses produce movement proteins that can increase the SEL of plasmodesmata, allowing viral genomes to pass between cells and establish systemic infection.

## Signaling Roles

Plasmodesmata serve as conduits for multiple classes of signaling molecules:

- **Phytohormones:** Auxin, cytokinins, gibberellins, and abscisic acid can move through plasmodesmata, coordinating growth and stress responses across tissues.
- **Transcription factors:** Some regulatory proteins move through plasmodesmata from cell to cell, controlling gene expression in neighboring tissues — a mechanism critical for leaf patterning and floral development.
- **Small RNAs:** Short-interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) travel through plasmodesmata as part of gene silencing pathways that coordinate development and defense.
- **Electrical signals:** Ion fluxes through plasmodesmata can propagate electrical signals across tissues, enabling rapid systemic responses to stimuli.
- **Calcium waves:** Calcium ion fluxes through plasmodesmata can propagate as waves, serving as rapid stress signals throughout the plant.

## Pathogen Exploitation

Many plant pathogens have evolved mechanisms to hijack plasmodesmata for their own spread:

- **Viral movement proteins:** Most plant viruses encode specific movement proteins that bind to the viral genome and actively increase the plasmodesmata SEL, allowing the viral RNA or DNA to pass from cell to cell.
- **Bacterial effectors:** Some bacterial pathogens inject effector proteins through plasmodesmata to suppress immune responses in neighboring cells.
- **Fungal strategies:** Fungal pathogens may produce compounds that alter plasmodesmata gating, facilitating the spread of fungal toxins or effectors.
- **Plant defense:** In response to pathogen attack, plants can rapidly deposit callose to close plasmodesmata in infected areas, creating a physical quarantine zone that limits pathogen spread.
- **Systemic acquired resistance (SAR):** Salicylic acid and other defense signals travel through plasmodesmata to activate immune responses in uninfected tissues throughout the plant.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Nutrient distribution:** Plasmodesmata are essential for distributing nutrients absorbed by root cells throughout the plant. Any factor affecting plasmodesmata function directly impacts nutrient availability to all tissues.
- **Mycorrhizal signaling:** Molecular signals exchanged between mycorrhizal fungi and plant roots must pass through plasmodesmata to reach the plant's vascular system, making these tunnels critical for establishing and maintaining symbiotic relationships.
- **Disease susceptibility:** Pathogens, particularly viruses, use plasmodesmata as highways to spread through plants. Understanding plasmodesmata regulation helps explain disease progression and resistance mechanisms.
- **Growth regulation:** Plant hormones and growth signals travel through plasmodesmata, coordinating development across tissues. Environmental stress can alter plasmodesmata function, affecting growth patterns.
- **Stress response:** Under drought, salt stress, or pathogen attack, plasmodesmata can be closed off (callose deposition) to protect uninfected tissues, demonstrating their role as dynamic regulatory checkpoints.
