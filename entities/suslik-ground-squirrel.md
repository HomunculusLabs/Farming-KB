# Suslik (Russian Ground Squirrel)

## Description

The suslik (*Spermophilus* spp.), also known as the Russian or European ground squirrel, is a burrowing rodent of the squirrel family (Sciuridae) native to Eurasian grasslands and steppes. Mollison highlights the suslik as a key "underground gardener" of natural meadow ecosystems, citing its extraordinary soil-turning capacity: a single suslik digs approximately 200 holes per acre and turns over 95 cubic feet of soil per year.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Animalia
- **Phylum**: Chordata
- **Class**: Mammalia
- **Order**: Rodentia
- **Family**: Sciuridae
- **Genus**: *Spermophilus*
- **Habitat**: Eurasian steppes, meadows, and grasslands

## Key Facts

- Digs approximately 200 holes per acre per year
- Turns over 95 cubic feet of soil annually per individual
- Creates burrow spoil mounds that serve as seed beds for meadow plants
- Stores seeds, bulbs, and root cuttings in shallow tunnels — many forgotten stores sprout voluntarily
- Hibernates through winter, emerging to aerate and mix soils in spring
- Colonially dwelling with interconnected burrow systems covering large areas

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Mollison positions the suslik within a broader guild of burrowing animals (moles, woodchucks, wombats, mice, voles, prairie dogs, rabbits) that collectively function as nature's deep-ploughing system. Their contributions include:

1. **Soil aeration and structure**: Deep tunnels create an absorbent, springy soil matrix in meadows. The "deep springy softness of the earth" in natural meadows is directly attributable to undisturbed burrower activity.

2. **Vegetative plant propagation**: Many bulb species with poor seed set (and root-set plants like comfrey) are effectively planted by burrowers who harvest, store, and forget corms, tubers, and root cuttings in shallow tunnels.

3. **Seed dispersal**: Seeds caught in the fur of prey species may sprout from owl regurgita (pellets), while neglected underground stores germinate in subsequent seasons.

4. **Nutrient cycling**: Burrow spoil brings subsoil minerals to the surface, while manure deposits concentrate nutrients.

Mollison notes that North American gardeners "can never rely on keeping comfrey in one place if gophers are in the area" — a testament to how effectively burrowers propagate vegetative plants through their caching behaviour.

## Ecological Interactions

- **Predator-prey relationships**: Susliks are prey for raptors, foxes, and mustelids; their burrows provide shelter for numerous invertebrate and vertebrate species
- **Plant association**: Their burrow mounds create microsites for pioneer species, blown seed establishment, and fox/dog-dispersed fruits (mulberry, plum, loquat, vine fruits)
- **Mycorrhizal networks**: Tunnelling activity may disrupt and redistribute mycorrhizal networks, potentially spreading beneficial fungal associations through meadow soils

## Agricultural Impact

In conventional agriculture, susliks and other burrowers have been largely eliminated by plough culture, removing their beneficial soil-building functions. Mollison argues that when these "underground gardeners" are eliminated, "hoofed animals [are left] to compact the earth without relief," leading to the soil degradation characteristic of modern intensive farming.

## Conservation Status

Many suslik species have experienced significant population declines due to agricultural conversion of steppe grasslands, pesticide use, and persecution as agricultural pests. Some species are now considered vulnerable or endangered, representing a loss of the ecosystem engineering functions Mollison describes.
