# Bjerkandera adusta

**Bjerkandera adusta**, the **smoky polypore**, is a cosmopolitan white-rot
basidiomycete in the order Polyporales. A saprotrophic decomposer of dead hardwood
in temperate and boreal forests, it is scientifically notable for simultaneously
producing all three major ligninolytic enzyme families — manganese peroxidase (MnP),
lignin peroxidase (LiP), and laccase — a rare capability among white-rot fungi that
makes it valuable for bioremediation, biobleaching, and wastewater treatment.

## Taxonomy

Originally described as *Boletus adustus* by Willdenow (1787) and transferred to
*Bjerkandera* by Karsten (1879), the genus honors Swedish botanist Clas Bjerkander.
The epithet *adusta* means "scorched," referring to the dark fruiting body. It is
sometimes confused with *B. fumosa*, which maintains a white pore surface that does
not darken with bruising.

## Morphology

Fruiting bodies are annual to semi-perennial brackets, sessile to effused-reflexed.
Caps measure 2–8 cm wide and 1–3 cm thick, with zonate surfaces grey to dark grey-
brown, characteristically smoky-grey at the margin. The pore surface is white to
pale grey initially, darkening to smoky grey with age or bruising — the key
diagnostic feature. Pores are angular to circular, 3–5 per mm. Context is corky,
white to cream. Spores are cylindrical to ellipsoid (4–6 x 1.5–2.5 um), smooth,
hyaline, inamyloid, producing a white spore print. Hyphal system is monomitic with
clamp connections.

## Ecology and Distribution

*B. adusta* decomposes dead hardwood — primarily birch, beech, oak, alder, willow,
and ash — colonizing fallen logs, stumps, and standing dead trees. It also grows on
treated timber and fence posts. Cosmopolitan across temperate Europe, North America,
and Asia, it fruits year-round but peaks autumn through spring. In forest ecosystems
it contributes to carbon and nutrient cycling by degrading lignin-cellulose,
typically appearing mid-to-late in wood decomposition succession.

## Ligninolytic Enzyme Systems

### Manganese Peroxidase (MnP)

The primary ligninolytic enzyme, catalyzing Mn(II) to Mn(III) oxidation. Diffusible
Mn(III)-organic acid complexes penetrate wood cell walls and oxidize phenolic lignin.
*B. adusta* produces 3–5 MnP isoenzymes with among the highest constitutive and
inducible production levels reported for white-rot fungi. MnP also oxidizes diverse
xenobiotic compounds relevant to bioremediation.

### Lignin Peroxidase (LiP)

*B. adusta* is one of few fungi co-producing LiP with MnP. LiP directly oxidizes
non-phenolic lignin units (~90% of lignin polymer) via veratryl alcohol-mediated
long-range electron transfer at ~1.5 V oxidation potential. Production is induced
by nitrogen limitation and aromatic compounds.

### Laccase

A multi-copper oxidase oxidizing phenolic subunits and aromatics with O2 reduction
to H2O. Strongly inducible by aromatic compounds, copper, and certain dyes, it
acts synergistically with MnP/LiP through mediators (ABTS, HBT) and is critical
for dye decolorization and phenol detoxification.

### Auxiliary Enzymes

Aryl alcohol oxidase and glyoxal oxidase generate H2O2 for MnP/LiP. Some strains
show versatile peroxidase (VP)-like activities. Low-molecular-weight iron chelators
(oxalate, malonate) participate in Fenton-like supplementary reactions.

## Bioremediation Applications

### Dye Decolorization

Highly effective against azo, anthraquinone, triphenylmethane, and phthalocyanine
dyes with 70–100% decolorization within days. Laccase-mediated oxidation breaks azo
bonds and chromophoric groups. Performs well in both free-cell and immobilized
bioreactor systems, tolerating high dye concentrations.

### Pharmaceutical Removal

Degrades diclofenac, carbamazepine, ibuprofen, sulfamethoxazole, and 17-beta-
estradiol with 50–95% removal efficiencies, relevant for wastewater treatment of
pharmaceutical micropollutants.

### PAH and Chlorophenol Degradation

Degrades low-molecular-weight PAHs (naphthalene, anthracene, phenanthrene, pyrene)
via MnP-mediated oxidation to quinones followed by ring cleavage. Also degrades
chlorophenols including pentachlorophenol (PCP) through laccase/MnP-catalyzed
dechlorination, effective at environmentally relevant concentrations.

## Industrial Applications

### Biobleaching and Biopulping

MnP and laccase partially replace chemical bleaching agents in pulp processing.
Enzymatic pretreatment of kraft pulp reduces kappa number and increases brightness
while reducing chlorinated organic compounds (AOX) in effluents. Biopulping reduces
mechanical pulping energy by 10–30% through selective lignin removal.

### Biosensors

*B. adusta* laccase and MnP power electrochemical biosensors for phenolic compound
detection. Enzyme immobilization on graphene, carbon nanotubes, or conducting
polymers enables micromolar to nanomolar detection for water quality monitoring.

## Comparison with Phanerochaete chrysosporium

While *P. chrysosporium* is the better-characterized model with extensive genetic
tools, *B. adusta* has a distinctive advantage: it produces strong laccase
alongside MnP and LiP, whereas *P. chrysosporium* typically lacks laccase. This
gives *B. adusta* superior performance in dye decolorization and phenol removal.
It also demonstrates higher xenobiotic tolerance and more stable MnP isoforms.

## Genome

Sequenced by the US DOE Joint Genome Institute (JGI MycoCosm portal). The ~35–40 Mb
genome encodes multiple MnP isoenzymes, numerous laccase and LiP genes, auxiliary
H2O2-generating enzymes, and an expanded cytochrome P450 complement for xenobiotic
metabolism. The CAZy profile is rich in AA1 laccases, AA2 peroxidases, and GH
families for cellulose/hemicellulose degradation.
