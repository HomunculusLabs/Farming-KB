## Overview

**Carya glabra**, the pignut hickory, is a large deciduous tree native to the eastern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] and one of the most common hickory species in Appalachian forests.

It is an important component of upland hardwood stands, valued for its durable wood and wildlife-supporting nut production.

The species typically reaches 18–24 m (60–80 ft) in height with a narrow to rounded crown.

Its nuts, though small and bitter compared to other hickories, are a significant food source for wildlife.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Carya glabra** (Mill.) Sweet belongs to the family Juglandaceae, closely related to pecan (*C. illinoinensis*) and shellbark hickory (*C. laciniosa*).

It was originally described as *Juglans glabra* by Philip Miller in 1768 and transferred to *Carya* by Robert Sweet in 1825.

The specific epithet *glabra* refers to the relatively smooth (glabrous) leaves and twigs compared to other hickories.

Hybridization with [[carya-ovata]] (shagbark hickory) and *C. tomentosa* (mockernut hickory) occurs occasionally where ranges overlap.

### Bark and Form

Bark on mature trees is tight, relatively smooth, and gray to dark gray, forming shallow interlacing ridges without the pronounced shaggy plates of shagbark hickory.

The crown is typically narrow and oblong, more compact than many other hickories.

Branching is ascending to spreading, with strong, well-developed laterals.

The trunk is straight and columnar in forest-grown specimens.

### Leaves

Leaves are alternate, pinnately compound, 20–30 cm (8–12 in) long with 5–7 leaflets.

Leaflets are 8–15 cm (3–6 in) long, lanceolate to obovate, with finely serrate margins.

The upper surface is dark yellow-green and glabrous; the lower surface is paler with scattered hairs along veins.

Rachis and petioles are typically glabrous or sparsely pubescent, a key diagnostic character.

Autumn color is golden yellow, often developing deep amber and orange tones.

### Flowers and Fruit

The species is monoecious, with male catkins and female spikes appearing separately on the same tree in spring.

Male catkins are pendulous, 5–8 cm (2–3 in) long, in clusters of three.

Female flowers are short spikes with 2–5 flowers, appearing at the base of new shoots.

The fruit is a nut enclosed in a thin husk 3–5 mm thick, splitting only partially at maturity.

Nuts are globose to ovoid, 2.5–4 cm (1–1.5 in) long, with a thick shell and small, sweet kernel.

## Distribution and Habitat

Pignut hickory ranges from southern Maine west to southeastern Minnesota, south to northern Florida, and west to eastern Texas.

It is absent from the lower Mississippi Delta and most of the Atlantic Coastal Plain.

The species occurs on a wide range of upland sites, from dry ridges and rocky slopes to moist coves and ravines.

It grows on acidic to neutral soils derived from sandstone, shale, and limestone parent materials.

Optimal development occurs on well-drained, loamy soils on north- and east-facing slopes.

It is classified as shade-tolerant in youth, becoming more light-demanding with maturity.

Common forest associates include [[quercus-rubra]], [[quercus-alba]], [[acer-saccharum]], [[fagus-grandifolia]], and [[carya-ovata]].

## Timber and Uses

Pignut hickory wood is heavy, hard, strong, and tough, with a Janka hardness of approximately 1,580 lbf.

Heartwood is light to medium brown; sapwood is nearly white and wide.

The wood shrinks considerably during drying but holds nails well and takes a smooth finish.

It is used for tool handles, ladders, sporting goods, flooring, and furniture frames.

The high shock resistance makes it ideal for axe handles, mauls, and other striking-tool handles.

It is also a preferred species for smoking meats, imparting a rich, hickory flavor.

Historically, the wood was used for wagon wheels, agricultural implements, and barrel hoops.

## Ecology

Pignut hickory nuts are a critical food resource for wildlife including squirrels, chipmunks, wild turkeys, deer, black bears, and various songbirds.

The nuts are high in fat and protein, providing essential energy reserves for winter survival.

The tree supports a diverse insect fauna, including hickory leaf stem gall aphids and hickory twig girdlers.

Cavity-nesting birds and mammals use hollow trunks and branches for shelter.

Root systems are deep and extensive, contributing to slope stability and watershed protection.

In oak-hickory forests, pignut hickory often succeeds oaks as a canopy dominant on mesic sites.

## Conservation

The species has no formal conservation status and is considered secure throughout its range.

However, forest fragmentation and land-use conversion have reduced the extent of mature oak-hickory ecosystems.

Hickory regeneration is often limited in logged or grazed forests due to heavy deer browsing of seedlings.

[[dighton-fungi-climate-change-feedback]] may shift the suitable range northward over the coming decades, particularly on the southern and western edges.

Pignut hickory is susceptible to hickory bark beetles (*Scolytus quadrispinosus*) and several fungal canker diseases.

The species is somewhat resistant to chestnut blight and Dutch elm disease, as it is not a host for either pathogen.
