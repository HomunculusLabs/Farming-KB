## Overview

**Carya glabra**, the pignut hickory, is a large deciduous tree native to the eastern [[carya-ovata]] (shagbark hickory) and *C. tomentosa* (mockernut hickory) occurs occasionally where ranges overlap.

### Bark and Form

Bark on mature trees is tight, relatively smooth, and gray to dark gray, forming shallow interlacing ridges without the pronounced shaggy plates of shagbark hickory.

The crown is typically narrow and oblong, more compact than many other hickories.

Branching is ascending to spreading, with strong, well-developed laterals.

The trunk is straight and columnar in forest-grown specimens.

### Leaves

Leaves are alternate, pinnately compound, 20–30 cm (8–12 in) long with 5–7 leaflets.

Leaflets are 8–15 cm (3–6 in) long, lanceolate to obovate, with finely serrate margins.

The upper surface is dark yellow-green and glabrous; the lower surface is paler with scattered hairs along veins.

Rachis and petioles are typically glabrous or sparsely pubescent, a key diagnostic character.

Autumn color is golden yellow, often developing deep amber and orange tones.

### Flowers and Fruit

The species is monoecious, with male catkins and female spikes appearing separately on the same tree in spring.

Male catkins are pendulous, 5–8 cm (2–3 in) long, in clusters of three.

Female flowers are short spikes with 2–5 flowers, appearing at the base of new shoots.

The fruit is a nut enclosed in a thin husk 3–5 mm thick, splitting only partially at maturity.

Nuts are globose to ovoid, 2.5–4 cm (1–1.5 in) long, with a thick shell and small, sweet kernel.

## Distribution and Habitat

Pignut hickory ranges from southern Maine west to southeastern Minnesota, south to northern Florida, and west to eastern Texas.

It is absent from the lower Mississippi Delta and most of the Atlantic Coastal Plain.

The species occurs on a wide range of upland sites, from dry ridges and rocky slopes to moist coves and ravines.

It grows on acidic to neutral soils derived from sandstone, shale, and limestone parent materials.

Optimal development occurs on well-drained, loamy soils on north- and east-facing slopes.

It is classified as shade-tolerant in youth, becoming more light-demanding with maturity.

Common forest associates include [[quercus-alba]], [[fagus-grandifolia]], and [[carya-ovata]].
