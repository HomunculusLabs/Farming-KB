## Overview

Iris versicolor is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Iridaceae, commonly known as blue flag iris or harlequin blueflag. It is native to wetland habitats across eastern North America.

The species produces showy blue-violet flowers with distinctive falls marked by yellow and white patterning. It is widely cultivated for water gardens and naturalized wetland plantings.

All parts of the plant, particularly the rhizome, contain toxic iridin compounds. Despite this toxicity, the species has a documented history of use in indigenous North American and European herbal medicine.

The species is the provincial flower of Quebec, where it is known as iris versicolore. It has also been adopted as the official wildflower of several U.S. states and conservation organizations.

The species is native to wetland habitats across eastern North America.

Iris versicolor has been widely planted beyond its native range for ornamental and ecological restoration purposes. It is naturalized in parts of western Europe and the Pacific Northwest of North America.

Numerous cultivars have been selected for flower color and garden performance. Notable selections include 'Kermesina' (deep crimson), 'Gerald Darby' (purple with blue tints), and 'Purple Flame'.

Iris versicolor is the type species of the subsection Versicolores within Limniris. The genus Iris contains approximately 300 species distributed across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

The specific epithet "versicolor" means "variously colored" in Latin, referring to the range of blue, purple, and violet shades found in natural populations.

Hybridization occurs naturally between Iris versicolor and other Limniris species where ranges overlap. Iris versicolor is believed to be an allopolyploid derived from hybridization between Iris virginica and Iris setosa.

## Morphology

Plants grow 60 to 100 centimeters tall from thick, horizontal rhizomes. The rhizome is fleshly, measuring 1 to 3 centimeters in diameter, and produces a network of fibrous roots.

Leaves are sword-shaped, 30 to 90 centimeters long and 1 to 3 centimeters wide, with parallel venation and a prominent midrib. Leaves arise basally in a fan arrangement typical of the Iridaceae.

Flowers are borne on branching stems with 2 to 4 flowers per stem. Each flower measures 7 to 10 centimeters across, with three upright standards and three reflexed falls.

The falls are prominently veined with purple and marked with a yellow-green signal patch surrounded by a white border. Bloom occurs from May through July depending on latitude.

Fruits are three-angled capsules 3 to 5 centimeters long containing numerous flattened, D-shaped seeds arranged in two rows per locule.

Seeds require cold stratification for germination and may remain viable in the seed bank for several years. Mature capsules split open along three seams to release seeds, which float briefly before sinking.

## Distribution and Habitat

Iris versicolor is native to eastern North America, ranging from Newfoundland and Labrador west to Minnesota and south to Virginia and Arkansas. It is most abundant in the Great Lakes region and northeastern United States.

The species inhabits marshes, wet meadows, shorelines, swamps, and stream margins. It tolerates water depths up to 15 centimeters during the growing season.

Preferred soils are organic-rich loams and mucks with pH ranging from slightly acidic to neutral. Plants perform best in full sun but tolerate partial shade in southern portions of their range.

The species spreads through both rhizome extension and seed dispersal. Vegetative propagation produces dense clumps within 3 to 5 years in favorable conditions.

## Ecological Role

Blue flag iris provides habitat structure in wetland communities, offering cover for amphibians, small mammals, and aquatic invertebrates. The dense root mat contributes to shoreline stabilization.

Pollinators include bumblebees and other native bees that access nectar through the tubular fall extensions. Hummingbirds occasionally visit the flowers, though bees are the primary pollination vector.

The flower structure is specialized for bee pollination, with falls serving as landing platforms and the style arms positioned to deposit pollen on the bee's back. Each flower remains open for approximately 2 to 3 days.

The iridin compounds in rhizomes deter herbivory from most mammalian species. These glycosides cause severe gastrointestinal distress, making the plant relatively unpalatable to deer and muskrats.

## Traditional Uses

Indigenous peoples of eastern North America used Iris versicolor rhizomes medicinally despite their toxicity. The Cherokee applied poultices of the root to wounds and swellings after thorough processing.

The Iroquois prepared decoctions of the root for treating digestive complaints and liver ailments. Preparations typically involved prolonged boiling or drying to reduce iridin concentrations.

The Penobscot used iris root externally for skin infections and as a poultice for swellings. Among the Ojibwe, the plant was considered spiritually significant and was used in ceremonial contexts alongside other wetland species.

European settlers adopted the plant into Thomsonian and eclectic medical practice during the 19th century. Iris versicolor was listed in the United States Pharmacopeia as a source of "iridin," used as a cathartic and diaphoretic.

Modern herbalism uses the dried rhizome cautiously in small doses as a choleretic and lymphatic stimulant. Professional supervision is recommended due to the narrow therapeutic window.

The roots contain iridin (also called irisin), a cathartic glycoside, along with iridolactone and other phenolic compounds. Fresh rhizome ingestion causes nausea, vomiting, and burning sensations in the mouth and throat.
