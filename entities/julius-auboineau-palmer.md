## Julius Auboineau Palmer

## Description

Julius Auboineau Palmer was an American mycologist and author based in Boston,
Massachusetts, active during the 1870s and 1880s. He is best known as the
author of *About Mushrooms*, a comprehensive work on [[classification-of-edible-and-poisonous-mushroom-families]]
fungi of New England.

Palmer was one of the earliest American advocates for public education on
[[mushroom-identification]] and the dangers of fungal poisoning. He wrote
extensively in the *Boston Evening Transcript*, *Forest and Stream*, and
the *Popular Science Monthly*.

His articles combined scientific observation with accessible prose aimed at
ordinary readers rather than specialists. He made it his mission to bring
mycological knowledge out of the academy and into the homes of ordinary
citizens of New England.

## Classification

- **Nationality:** American
- **Location:** Boston, Massachusetts

- **Role:** Author, public educator, field mycologist
- **Publications:** Boston Evening Transcript, Forest and Stream, Popular Science Monthly

## Key Facts

- Devoted years of leisure time to the study of edible and poisonous fungi in New England
- Estimated approximately 3,000 varieties of fungi in New England
- About one-third of those were of sufficient size and substance for practical study
- Personally tested perhaps 100 varieties of fungi on his own stomach
- This included some varieties long considered poisonous
- He was never deceived or harmed by these personal experiments
- Argued that approximately 50 common edible varieties could be learned by any person of good judgment
- Published a multi-part series titled 'Mushrooms and Toadstools' in the Boston Evening Transcript
- Acknowledged that no general rule or test exists to distinguish edible from [[identifying-poisonous-mushrooms]]
- Planned an illustrated work with colored chromos of edible and poisonous varieties
- Noted that no such American illustrated work had ever been issued
- Collected specimens from the same locations year after year (1874 and 1879)
- Demonstrated that fungi return to the same habitat with regularity
- Received specimens and correspondence from the public as a voluntary identifier
- Used the Boston Library of the [[stropharia-cubensis-identification-and-natural-history]] Society and Horticultural Hall for research

## Relationships with Contemporaries

Palmer acknowledged several key figures in his writings. **[[john-galvin]]**, the
Boston city forester, granted permission to collect specimens from city grounds.
**[[charles-james-sprague]]** was a Boston mycologist of rare attainments who donated
foreign works to the Natural History Society library.

**Charles Frost** of Vermont was described as a living authority in mycological
science. **Dr. Curtis** and **Mr. Ravenal** of South Carolina were both recognized
as experts worthy of the name. **Dr. F. S. Cordier** was the French mycologist
whose 1876 work Palmer considered the most complete in the world.

## Observations on Cultivation

Palmer provided detailed cultivation instructions based on his own experiments.
The horse-mushroom (*[[agaricus-arvensis]]*) was the only variety that could be
propagated by artificial means in his time.

He recommended using spawn from edges of old mushroom beds rather than purchased
spawn, which often contained no actual fungus. He advised planting beds in
sections at staggered intervals for continuous production.

He identified [[mushroom-bed-moisture-management]] as the most difficult problem in mushroom
cultivation. Water must come from beneath the soil, not from surface watering.
He recommended steam in greenhouses as the best method to maintain proper humidity.

He noted that mushroom beds often produced only one picking and then became
barren. He attributed failure to poor spawn quality, simultaneous spawning of
entire beds, and neglect of atmospheric conditions.

## Views on Popular Errors

Palmer systematically debunked numerous myths about mushrooms. These included
the [[silver-spoon-test]], the idea that mushrooms spring up overnight, and the
notion that there is such a thing as 'the common toadstool.'

He argued that the same variety of fungus returns to the same location year
after year with the reliability of a farmer's wheat crop. He also observed
that fungi succeed each other in seasonal rotation, like spring wildflowers
followed by summer roses.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Palmer occupies a unique position in [[palmer-mushroom-charts-american-mycological-education]] history as a bridge
between academic science and public knowledge. His observations on cultivation,
spawn management, and moisture control foreshadowed modern practices.

His warnings about the *Amanita* genus as the cause of nearly all fatal mushroom
poisonings remain current today. His advocacy for illustrated guides and public
education helped lay the groundwork for the field guides that would follow.
