# Kalaupapa, Moloka'i

## Description

Kalaupapa is a flat peninsula on the north shore of the island of Moloka'i in Hawai'i. It is a historically and agriculturally significant site known for its traditional Hawaiian windbreak and agricultural systems, which demonstrate sophisticated understanding of microclimate management in exposed coastal environments. Mollison cites Kalaupapa as a case study in wind-adapted garden design and windbreak architecture.

## Classification

- **Type**: Peninsula / settlement / historical agricultural site
- **Location**: North shore, Moloka'i, Hawaiian Islands, USA
- **Coordinates**: Approximately 21.18°N, 156.98°W
- **Climate**: Tropical, exposed to strong trade winds, salt spray
- **Terrain**: Flat peninsula below steep sea cliffs (pali)

## Key Facts

- Hawaiian people built an extensive network of tiny stone fences, 25–50 cm (10–20 inches) high and only 4.5–5 m (15–18 feet) apart, across the entire peninsula.
- These stone fences were used to grow sweet potato crops in their lee, with tough fern growing between the fences for mulch.
- On the seaward coast, wild date palms struggle to 4.5 m (15 feet) in the teeth of the trade winds.
- The site demonstrates how intensive micro-shelterbelt systems can enable food production even in extremely hostile wind and salt conditions.
- Salt spray crystallizes in pinkish ponds on the windward coast, mixed with red volcanic earth — this is gathered as "Hawaiian salt."
- Hawaiian salt is mixed with roasted kukui nut and chilies to make a traditional raw-fish condiment.
- The agricultural systems are now abandoned, largely due to displacement by tourism and graziers.
- The peninsula is backed by some of the world's tallest sea cliffs (over 1,000 m / 3,300 ft).

## Wind Design Lessons

The Kalaupapa system illustrates several [[permaculture-principles]]:

1. **Dense network shelter**: Unlike modern large-scale windbreaks, Hawaiians used closely-spaced low barriers to create continuous micro-shelter across an entire landscape.
2. **Stone as windbreak material**: Locally abundant volcanic rock used to create permanent, fire-proof, salt-resistant barriers.
3. **Multi-function rows**: Fence rows produced mulch (fern) while protecting food crops (sweet potato).
4. **Salt harvesting**: Wind-borne salt was captured as a resource rather than treated purely as a problem.
5. **Scale appropriate**: Small, closely spaced barriers suited the flat peninsula terrain and extreme salt-wind exposure.

## Relevance to Cultivation

- **Model for coastal permaculture**: Demonstrates viable food production in extreme coastal wind environments.
- **Windbreak spacing**: The 4.5–5 m spacing with 25–50 cm barriers is an effective ratio for ground-level crop protection.
- **Stone fence technique**: Applicable to any exposed coastal or arid site with available rock.
- **Cultural context**: Shows indigenous Hawaiian agricultural sophistication in adapting to harsh microclimates.
- **Abandonment lesson**: The loss of these systems to tourism and grazing illustrates the vulnerability of traditional agricultural knowledge.
