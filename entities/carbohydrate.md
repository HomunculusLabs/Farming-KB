# Carbohydrate

## Description

Carbohydrates are carbon-based molecules composed of combinations of carbon, oxygen, and hydrogen, typically with one water molecule for each carbon atom — hence the name "carbon water" (carbo + hydrate). Along with lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, carbohydrates are one of the four fundamental groups of molecules that constitute all living things. In plants, carbohydrates serve as the primary energy storage medium, structural material, and transport form for carbon energy.

## Classification

- **Type**: Macromolecule / biomolecule
- **Category**: One of the four molecules of life
- **Composition**: Carbon (C), Hydrogen (H), Oxygen (O)
- **General formula**: Typically (CH₂O)ₙ
- **Subtypes**: Monosaccharides, disaccharides, polysaccharides

## Types by Complexity

### Monosaccharides (Simple Sugars)

- The simplest carbohydrate units, containing up to six carbon atoms
- **Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)** is the most common monosaccharide in plants and animals
- Glucose serves dual functions: energy storage and base material for synthesizing other molecules
- Global plant photosynthesis produces enough glucose to fill a line of tanker trucks 25 to 30 million miles long annually

### Disaccharides

- Formed by connecting two monosaccharides
- **Sucrose (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁)** is the primary disaccharide in plants
- Sucrose is the usual form in which sugar is transported throughout the plant via the phloem
- Common example: maple sap contains sucrose transported from leaves to storage tissues

### Polysaccharides

- Long chains of monosaccharides linked together
- Much more complex than mono- or disaccharides
- Do not readily dissolve in water
- Two major plant polysaccharides:

#### Starch (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ
- The preferred form of sugar storage in plants
- Stored in chloroplasts, amyloplasts, and other plastids
- Formed from glucose during photosynthesis and transported to storage organs (tubers, seeds)
- Can be broken back down into glucose when energy is needed

#### Cellulose
- The structural material composing plant cell walls
- Made of the same glucose subunits as starch, but linked in a different configuration
- The difference in glucose linkage determines whether the polysaccharide is an energy store (starch) or structural material (cellulose)
- Extremely strong and resistant to degradation

## Role in Plant Metabolism

Carbohydrates occupy a central position in plant metabolism:
- **Photosynthesis** produces glucose from CO₂, water, and light energy
- **Translocation** moves sucrose from leaves to all parts of the plant via phloem
- **Storage** converts excess glucose into starch in plastids
- **Structure** incorporates glucose into cellulose for cell walls
- **Building blocks** — glucose carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms are used to synthesize nucleotides, lipids, and proteins

## Key Facts

- The only difference between starch and cellulose is how glucose molecules are linked together — different linkage, radically different function
- Plants produce over 100 billion tons of carbohydrates from atmospheric carbon annually through photosynthesis
- Glucose is not just an energy source — it is a fundamental building material for virtually everything in the cell
- Polysaccharides also serve as root exudates, assembled in the Golgi apparatus and secreted into the rhizosphere
- Carbohydrates are the most abundant organic compounds in the biosphere

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Carbohydrate production is the primary output of photosynthesis — optimizing light, CO₂, and water availability maximizes carbohydrate yield
- Starch storage in tubers, roots, and grains represents the harvestable portion of many crop plants
- Root exudates (polysaccharides) feed soil microorganisms and shape the rhizosphere microbiome — healthy carbohydrate production supports healthy soil biology
- In mycology, fungi break down cellulose and starch using specialized enzymes — this decomposition releases carbon and energy back into the soil food web
- Understanding carbohydrate metabolism explains why pruning excess foliage reduces the plant's photosynthetic capacity and total carbohydrate production
- Brix measurements (sugar content) of plant sap reflect carbohydrate status and are used as indicators of plant health and nutritional adequacy
