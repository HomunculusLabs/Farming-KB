## Overview

**Kalanchoe daigremontiana**, commonly called **mother of thousands** or **devil's backbone**, is a succulent perennial in the family Crassulaceae. It is notorious for producing profuse plantlets along the margins of its leaves, enabling rapid vegetative spread.

Native to southwestern Madagascar, this species has become naturalized in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide. It is widely cultivated as an ornamental houseplant but is considered invasive in many areas.

The species belongs to the Bryophyllum section of Kalanchoe, distinguished by viviparous leaf-margin plantlet production. All parts contain bufadienolide cardiac glycosides, making it toxic to humans, livestock, and pets.

First described by Raymond-Hamet and Perrier de la Bâthie in 1914, Kalanchoe daigremontiana has become one of the most widely distributed succulents globally.

Its common name "mother of thousands" refers to the extraordinary fecundity of its leaf-margin plantlets.

**Synonyms:** Bryophyllum daigremontianum (Raym.-Hamet & H.Perrier) A.Berger.

Plants grow 60–100 cm tall with an unbranched, erect, somewhat woody stem. Leaves are triangular-lanceolate, 10–20 cm long, fleshy, and bluish-green with purple spotting on the undersides.

The leaf margins bear notches from which plantlets (bulbils) develop, each producing roots while still attached to the parent leaf. A single leaf can produce 20–50 plantlets, and a mature plant may generate hundreds of offspring annually.

Inflorescences are terminal paniculate cymes bearing bell-shaped, pinkish-grey flowers 1.5–2 cm long. Flowering occurs in late winter to early spring under short-day conditions, though cultivated plants often fail to bloom.

The species is closely related to Kalanchoe delagoensis (mother of millions), which produces narrower cylindrical leaves with fewer plantlets per leaf.
