# Hericium coralloides

## Overview

Hericium coralloides, commonly known as the coral tooth fungus, comb tooth, or icicle mushroom, is a saprotrophic mushroom in the family Hericiaceae. It is closely related to the well-known lion's mane (*Hericium erinaceus*) and shares many of its medicinal properties, particularly regarding nerve growth factor stimulation.

The species is distinguished from its relatives by its branching, coral-like fruiting body with shorter spines and a more delicate structure. It is found across temperate forests of North America, Europe, and Asia, typically fruiting on dead or dying hardwoods.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Russulales
- **Family:** Hericiaceae
- **Genus:** Hericium
- **Species:** H. coralloides
- **Authority:** (Scop.) Pers. (1797)
- **Synonyms:** Hydnum coralloides Scop.; Hericium ramosum (Bull. ex Merat) Letellier; Hericium laciniatum (Leers) Banker

The epithet *coralloides* means "coral-like," referring to the branched morphology of the fruiting body. Some authorities treat *H. ramosum* and *H. laciniatum* as separate species, but modern molecular phylogenetics largely supports their inclusion within *H. coralloides*.

## Morphology

### Fruiting Body

Branching, coral-like mass, 5–40 cm broad, arising from a single base or short stalk. The overall structure forms irregular, cascading branches reminiscent of marine coral.

Unlike *H. erinaceus*, which forms a single unbranched mass with long pendant spines, *H. coralloides* produces distinct branches from the point of attachment, each bearing spines.

### Spines

Hanging in clusters or rows from the branches, 5–15 mm long and 1–2 mm thick. White to cream-colored when fresh, becoming yellowish with age.

The spines are shorter and more densely clustered than those of *H. erinaceus*. Spore production occurs on the spine surfaces.

### Flesh

White, soft, and watery when fresh. Becomes tough and brittle when dried. Has a mild, slightly sweet or seafood-like odor and taste.

### Spore Print

White. Spores are broadly ellipsoid to subglobose, 5–7 × 4–6 µm, smooth and amyloid.

## Distribution and Habitat

Widely distributed across temperate regions of North America, Europe, and Asia. In North America, found from eastern Canada to the southern Appalachian Mountains and westward to the Pacific Northwest.

Primarily a saprotroph on dead or dying hardwoods, especially oak (*Quercus*), maple (*Acer*), beech (*Fagus*), birch (*Betula*), and walnut (*Juglans*). Occasionally reported on conifers.

Fruits in late summer to autumn (August–November), often after cool, wet periods. Prefers shaded, moist forest environments on fallen logs, stumps, and standing dead trees.

## Bioactive Compounds

Like other *Hericium* species, *H. coralloides* contains hericenones and erinacines, compounds that stimulate nerve growth factor (NGF) synthesis in the brain.

Research has identified several terpenoid compounds unique to the genus, including coralloidin A and B, which have shown antibacterial and cytotoxic activity against cancer cell lines in preliminary in vitro studies.

The species is rich in beta-glucan polysaccharides, contributing to its immunomodulatory properties. Total polysaccharide content is typically 15–25% of dry weight.

## Pharmacology and Medicinal Uses

### Neuroprotective Effects

Hericenones and erinacines isolated from *Hericium* species cross the blood-brain barrier and promote NGF synthesis in astrocytes, suggesting potential applications in neurodegenerative conditions.

While most clinical research has focused on *H. erinaceus*, the shared phytochemistry of *H. coralloides* suggests similar neurological benefits. Preliminary animal studies have shown improved memory and reduced neuroinflammation.

### Immunomodulatory Properties

Beta-glucans from *H. coralloides* have demonstrated activation of macrophages and natural killer cells in vitro. These immunomodulatory effects are consistent with the broader medicinal mushroom literature.

### Antimicrobial Activity

Extracts have shown inhibitory activity against gram-positive bacteria and select fungi in laboratory studies, though clinical applications remain unestablished.

## Cultivation

Can be cultivated on hardwood sawdust substrates (oak, maple, beech) supplemented with wheat bran (10–20%). Optimal fruiting temperatures are 15–20°C with high humidity (85–95%).

Colonization time is typically 14–21 days, with fruiting beginning 7–14 days after induction. Yields of 50–100 g per 1 kg dry substrate are typical for home cultivation setups.

The species is less commonly cultivated commercially than *H. erinaceus* due to lower demand and slightly more complex fruiting requirements, but it is gaining interest among specialty mushroom growers.

## Ecological Role

As a white-rot decomposer, *H. coralloides* breaks down lignin and cellulose in dead hardwood, playing an important role in forest carbon cycling and nutrient recycling.

The fruiting bodies serve as food for various insects and mammals. Slugs are particularly attracted to the soft, watery flesh of fresh specimens.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

Hericium coralloides is an excellent edible mushroom with a texture and flavor profile similar to *H. erinaceus*. Young, fresh specimens have a mild, sweet taste often compared to lobster or crab meat.

Best prepared by sautéing in butter or light oil, with minimal seasoning to preserve the delicate flavor. The texture holds up well in soups and cream sauces.

Specimens should be harvested when the spines are still white; yellowing indicates age and deterioration in both texture and flavor. Drying is possible but less common than with other medicinal mushrooms due to the relatively thin flesh.
