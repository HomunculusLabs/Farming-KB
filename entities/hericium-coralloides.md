## Overview

*Hericium coralloides* (Scop.) Pers. (1801) is a species of edible mushroom in the family Hericiaceae, order Russulales. It is commonly known as the coral hedgehog mushroom or comb tooth fungus, named for its branching, coral-like fruiting body that hangs in cascading spines from dead or dying hardwood trees. Unlike its more commercially prominent relative *Hericium [[hericium-erinaceus|erinaceus]]* (lion's mane), *H. coralloides* forms distinctly branched structures with spines distributed along the branches rather than a single compact mass.

The species is a saprotrophic wood-decay fungus, primarily decomposing cellulose and hemicellulose in hardwood substrates. It produces a white-rot pattern of decay. *H. coralloides* is found across temperate and boreal forests of the Northern Hemisphere, fruiting in late summer to autumn on standing dead trees, fallen logs, and large branches of hardwoods, especially beech (*Fagus* spp.), oak (*Quercus* spp.), and birch (*Betula* spp.).

Like other *Hericium* species, *H. coralloides* has attracted research interest for its production of bioactive compounds including hericenones, erinacines, and β-glucans, which demonstrate neurotrophic, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory activities in preclinical studies. It is both foraged wild and cultivated commercially, though it remains less widely available than *H. erinaceus*.

## Morphology

The fruiting body of *H. coralloides* is a multiple-branched structure arising from a single basal attachment point on the substrate. Individual branches are 5–40 mm wide, cylindrical to slightly flattened, and repeatedly divide in an irregular, coral-like pattern. The entire fruiting body measures 5–25 cm across and may weigh 50–300 g at maturity.

Spines (teeth) are produced in clusters or rows along the undersides and sides of the branches, pendant and hanging downward. Individual spines are 5–15 mm long, 0.5–1 mm in diameter at the base, slender, tapered, and fragile. Spine color ranges from white when fresh to cream or pale yellowish with age. The spines are the hymenophore and bear the basidia.

The flesh is white, soft, and watery when fresh, becoming tough and rubbery when dried. It has no distinctive odor when raw. Context is monomitic, consisting of generative hyphae with clamp connections. The hyphal system lacks skeletal or binding hyphae.

Spores are white in deposit, subglobose to broadly ellipsoid, 5–7 × 4.5–6 μm, smooth, thin-walled, amyloid, and ornamented with low, rounded warts visible under scanning electron microscopy. Basidia are clavate, 25–35 × 5–7 μm, four-spored, with a basal clamp. Cystidia are absent.

Ecologically, *H. coralloides* is a white-rot saprotroph that degrades cellulose and lignin in hardwood wood and bark. It occasionally behaves as a weak parasite on stressed trees.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Hericium coralloides* has a broad distribution across the temperate and boreal zones of the Northern Hemisphere. In North America, it ranges from eastern Canada (Newfoundland to Ontario) south through the Appalachian Mountains to the southeastern United States and west to the Great Lakes region. In Europe, it occurs from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean, with notable populations in Central European beech forests. It is also reported from East Asia (Japan, Korea, northeastern China) and temperate Russia.

The species fruits predominantly on dead or moribund hardwoods. Preferred hosts include American beech (*Fagus grandifolia*), European beech (*F. sylvatica*), oaks (*Quercus* spp.), maples (*Acer* spp.), birches (*Betula* spp.), and occasionally conifers such as fir (*Abies* spp.) in mixed forests. It typically grows on standing dead trees, stumps, and fallen logs, often well above ground level on trunks and large branches.

Fruiting occurs from July through November in temperate regions, with peak abundance in September and October. It requires sustained high humidity and moderate temperatures (15–20°C). In warm, wet autumns, multiple flushes may occur on the same substrate over several weeks.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

*Hericium coralloides* is an excellent edible mushroom with a mild, sweet, slightly nutty flavor and a tender, seafood-like texture often compared to crab or lobster meat. It is considered one of the choice edible fungi and is prized by foragers for its culinary quality.

Fresh specimens should be harvested before the spines begin to discolor or dry. Preparation involves gently brushing away debris, trimming the tough basal attachment, and tearing or slicing the spiny branches. The mushroom should not be soaked in water, as the flesh readily absorbs moisture and becomes soggy. Cooking methods include sautéing in butter or olive oil, pan-frying with garlic and herbs, tempura-battering and deep-frying, and incorporating into cream sauces, risottos, and soups. It pairs well with shellfish, cream, thyme, tarragon, and white wine. Overcooking should be avoided as the texture becomes mealy. Dried specimens rehydrate well and retain flavor, making them suitable for long-term storage.

## Medicinal Properties

*Hericium coralloides* produces several classes of bioactive compounds of pharmacological interest:

**Hericenones and erinacines:** These cyathane-type sesquiterpenoids are known to stimulate nerve growth factor (NGF) synthesis in astrocytes. Erinacine A, first isolated from mycelial cultures of *Hericium* spp., promotes neurite outgrowth in PC12 cells and has shown neuroprotective effects in animal models of neurodegenerative disease. *H. coralloides* mycelium has been confirmed as a productive source of erinacines A and E.

**β-Glucans:** Polysaccharide β-(1,3)/(1,6)-glucans isolated from *H. coralloides* fruiting bodies demonstrate immunomodulatory activity, activating macrophages and enhancing NK-cell cytotoxicity. These compounds contribute to the species' traditional use as an immune tonic.

**Anti-inflammatory effects:** Ethanolic extracts of *H. coralloides* inhibit nitric oxide (NO) production and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, iNOS) in LPS-stimulated macrophage models, indicating potential applications in chronic inflammatory conditions.

**Neurological benefits:** Preclinical studies suggest that *Hericium* extracts may ameliorate cognitive decline by promoting myelination, reducing amyloid plaque formation, and enhancing hippocampal neurogenesis. Human clinical trials have primarily focused on *H. erinaceus*, but *H. coralloides* is expected to share similar neurotrophic properties given overlapping chemistry.

**Antioxidant activity:** Phenolic compounds and polysaccharides from *H. coralloides* exhibit significant free-radical scavenging activity in DPPH and ABTS assays.

## Cultivation

*Hericium coralloides* can be cultivated on supplemented hardwood sawdust substrates, though it is less commercially developed than *H. erinaceus*. Optimal substrate formulations include hardwood sawdust (oak, beech, or maple) supplemented with 10–20% wheat bran and 1–2% gypsum. Substrate moisture content should be 60–65%. Bags or bottles are sterilized at 121°C for 90 minutes, inoculated with grain spawn, and incubated at 22–25°C. Full colonization typically requires 25–35 days.

Fruiting is initiated by introducing fresh air exchange, maintaining 90–95% relative humidity, and reducing temperature to 15–18°C with 12-hour photoperiods of indirect light. Primordia form within 7–14 days of induction, and mature fruiting bodies develop in 7–10 additional days. Biological efficiency (fresh weight / dry substrate weight × 100) typically ranges from 50–80% over two to three flushes.

Outdoor cultivation on hardwood logs is also feasible, using a spawn-and-plant approach similar to shiitake cultivation. Inoculated beech or oak logs (10–20 cm diameter) are incubated in shaded, humid conditions for 6–12 months before natural fruiting in autumn.

## Similar Species

*Hericium coralloides* is most likely confused with two congeners:

**Hericium erinaceus** (lion's mane) forms an unbranched, globular to irregular mass of densely packed spines descending from a single, short stalk. It lacks the characteristic branching pattern of *H. coralloides*. *H. erinaceus* spines are longer (15–40 mm), denser, and form a single, rounded mass, whereas *H. coralloides* has clearly differentiated branches with shorter spines in rows or tufts. Both are edible and medicinally valuable.

**Hericium americanum** (bear's head tooth fungus), a North American species, is morphologically intermediate, forming multiple branches from a common base but with shorter, more tightly packed spines. *H. americanum* tends to produce fewer, thicker branches compared to *H. coralloides*. Some authorities treat *H. americanum* as conspecific with *H. coralloides*, while others maintain it as distinct based on molecular data and ITS sequence divergence.

Other potential lookalikes include species of *Creolophus* (e.g., *C. cirrhatus*, splitgill tooth), which are smaller, grow on conifer wood, and have a more flattened, shelf-like form with spines restricted to the pore surface.

## Taxonomic Notes

*Hericium coralloides* has a complex nomenclatural history. The basionym is *Hydnum coralloides* Scop. (1772), later transferred to *Hericium* by Persoon (1801). Major synonyms include *Hericium ramosum* (Merat) Letourneux (1870), which has been widely used in European literature, and *Hericium coralloides* var. *viridiflavum* (Schwein.) Sacc. (1888). Many authorities now consider *H. ramosum* conspecific with *H. coralloides*.

Molecular phylogenetic studies (Binder et al., 2013; Moncalvo et al., 2006) based on ITS and LSU rDNA sequences confirm that *Hericium* is monophyletic within Hericiaceae and that *H. coralloides* is distinct from *H. erinaceus* and *H. abietis*. The relationship between *H. coralloides* and *H. americanum* remains debated, with some analyses placing them as sister taxa and others suggesting they represent a single variable species complex.

The genus was historically placed in Cantharellaceae or Hydnaceae but was assigned to Hericiaceae (Corner, 1966) based on amyloid spores and monomitic hyphal structure. This placement is supported by multigene phylogenetic analyses.
