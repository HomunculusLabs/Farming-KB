# Amanita muscaria

**Amanita muscaria**, commonly known as the **fly agaric**, is a basidiomycete mushroom of the genus *Amanita*.
It is one of the most recognizable mushrooms in the world, featuring a bright red cap dotted with white warts.
Native throughout the temperate and boreal regions of the Northern Hemisphere, *Amanita muscaria* has been used in shamanic and spiritual practices across Siberia and northern Europe for millennia, and has become deeply embedded in popular culture, folklore, and children's literature.
The mushroom's iconic appearance has made it a universal symbol of fungi in visual media, video games, and decorative arts worldwide.

The species was originally described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 as *Agaricus muscarius* and later transferred to the genus *Amanita* by Christian Hendrik Persoon in 1797.
Several subspecies and varieties are recognized, including *A. muscaria* var. *muscaria* (typical red-capped Eurasian form), *A.
muscaria* var. *flavivolvata* (North American), *A. muscaria* var. *alba* (white-capped), *A. muscaria* var.
*formosa* (yellow-orange cap), and *A. muscaria* var. *persicina* (peach-colored, southeastern US).
Molecular phylogenetic studies using ITS rDNA sequencing suggest North American populations may represent distinct species within a species complex, with the global lineage diverging approximately 8–10 million years ago.

## Description and Morphology

The cap of *Amanita muscaria* is 5–20 cm (2–8 in) in diameter, initially hemispherical before flattening with age.
The cap surface is bright red to orange-red, covered with white to pale yellow warts (remnants of the universal veil) that can be washed off by heavy rain.
The cuticle can be peeled approximately one-third of the radius from the cap margin.
The gills are free (not attached to the stipe), white, and crowded, with lamellulae (short gills) of mixed lengths interspersed.
The stipe (stem) is 8–20 cm tall and 1–2 cm thick, white, and bears a membranous skirt-like annulus (ring) near the apex and a bulbous base with concentric rings of volval tissue.
The flesh is white and does not change color when cut or bruised.
The spore print is white, and spores measure 9–13 × 6.5–9 µm, are ellipsoid to broadly ellipsoid, smooth, and non-amyloid.
The mushroom emits a mild to strong odor described as slightly sweet or not distinctive.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Amanita muscaria* is widely distributed across temperate and boreal forests of the Northern Hemisphere, including Europe, Asia (Siberia, Japan, Korea, Himalayas), North America (Alaska to California, across Canada), and parts of North Africa (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia).
It has also been introduced to the Southern Hemisphere, including Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, and South America (Argentina, Chile), likely through importation of nursery stock with ectomycorrhizal tree roots.
The species forms obligate ectomycorrhizal associations with a broad range of trees, particularly birch (*Betula* spp.), pine (*Pinus* spp.), spruce (*Picea* spp.), oak (*Quercus* spp.), and fir (*Abies* spp.).
It fruits predominantly from late summer to early winter, favoring cool, moist conditions and acidic soils (pH 4.5–6.5).

## Chemistry and Active Compounds

The psychoactive constituents of *Amanita muscaria* differ fundamentally from those of psilocybin-containing mushrooms.
The primary active compounds are:

- **Ibotenic acid** (C₅H₆NO₄; IUPAC: (1R,2S,3R,4S)-2-amino-3-(3-hydroxyisoxazol-5-yl)propane-1,2,4-tricarboxylic acid; CAS 2552-55-8) — a glutamate receptor agonist acting primarily at ionotropic AMPA and NMDA receptors.
Acts as a potent neuroexcitotoxin. Concentrations in dried cap tissue: 500–1,000 mg/kg; stem tissue: 200–500 mg/kg.
- **Muscimol** (C₄H₆N₂O₂; IUPAC: 5-(aminomethyl)-3-hydroxyisoxazole; CAS 2763-96-4) — the principal psychoactive compound, a GABA_A receptor agonist producing sedative, hypnotic, and dissociative effects.
Formed by decarboxylation of ibotenic acid through drying, heating, or acidic conditions. Dried cap concentrations: 300–800 mg/kg.
- **Muscazone** (C₅H₆N₂O₄; CAS 2762-68-7) — a minor compound formed by UV-induced rearrangement of ibotenic acid, of uncertain pharmacological significance.
- **Muscarine** (C₉H₂₀NO₂⁺; CAS 300-54-9) — present in trace amounts only (<0.0002% dry weight), contrary to popular belief.
The species was named under the erroneous assumption that muscarine was the primary active compound.

## Uses and Applications

### Traditional and Ethnobotanical Use

*Amanita muscaria* has a long history of use in Siberian shamanism among the Koryak, Chukchi, Kamchadal, and Itelmen peoples of the Kamchatka Peninsula and eastern Siberia.
Shamans consumed the mushroom to enter trance states for divination, healing, and communication with spirit worlds.
The practice of drinking the urine of individuals who had consumed the mushroom was well-documented by European explorers including Georg Wilhelm Steller (18th century) and Waldemar Jochelson (early 20th century).
This was pharmacologically rational: muscimol is excreted largely unmetabolized in urine, while the more toxic ibotenic acid is metabolized.
Reindeer in Siberia are also known to seek out and consume the mushroom, exhibiting visible behavioral changes.

The mushroom has been proposed as a candidate for the identity of the ancient Vedic Soma, a sacred ritual drink described in the Rigveda (c.
1500 BCE), most notably by R. Gordon Wasson in his 1968 book *Soma: Divine Mushroom of Immortality*.
In Europe, the mushroom was traditionally used as an insecticide — small pieces crumbled into saucers of milk to attract and kill flies (hence "fly agaric"), documented since at least the 13th century.

### Modern Research

Modern research has explored muscimol as a potential therapeutic agent.
Its GABAergic mechanism suggests possible applications in anxiety, insomnia, and epilepsy treatment.
Microdosing preparations of decarboxylated *Amanita muscaria* extracts have gained commercial interest since approximately 2020, with several companies marketing dried cap preparations standardized to muscimol content.
A small open-label pilot study (n=12) in 2023 examined muscimol for treatment-resistant insomnia, reporting significant improvements in sleep onset latency.
However, rigorous large-scale clinical trials remain limited.
Preliminary in vitro studies on ibotenic acid analogs have investigated neuroprotective and nootropic properties through NMDA receptor modulation.

## Safety and Toxicity

*Amanita muscaria* is classified as poisonous.
Symptoms of ingestion include nausea, vomiting, dizziness, confusion, agitation, visual and auditory hallucinations, ataxia, dysarthria, myoclonic jerks, and in severe cases seizures and coma.
The toxicity profile is distinct from the lethal *Amanita phalloides* group — fatalities from *A.
muscaria* are exceedingly rare (only one well-documented fatality, an Italian child in 2007) and typically associated with consumption of very large quantities.
The onset of effects occurs within 30–120 minutes and may last 4–10 hours. Treatment is primarily supportive.
Activated charcoal may be administered if ingestion is recent (within 1–2 hours).
Benzodiazepines (diazepam or lorazepam) are used for agitation and seizures.
The LD₅₀ in mice for muscimol is approximately 45 mg/kg (oral) and for ibotenic acid is approximately 129 mg/kg (oral).
Children and pets are at particular risk due to the mushroom's attractive appearance and sweet taste.

## Legal Status

The legal status of *Amanita muscaria* varies by jurisdiction.
It is unscheduled and legal in most countries, including the United States (federal level), the United Kingdom, Canada, and most of Europe.
The Netherlands banned its sale in December 2008 under the Opium Act.
Australia classifies it as a prohibited substance in New South Wales and South Australia.
Louisiana specifically prohibits cultivation and sale under Louisiana Act 159.
In Russia, sale for human consumption is regulated under food safety law. The mushroom is not listed on any UN drug convention schedule.
