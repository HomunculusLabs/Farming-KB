# Forest Microorganisms

## Description

Forest microorganisms are beneficial indigenous microorganisms collected from the humus and litter layer of aged, healthy forests using a cooked-rice trapping technique. In natural farming, the deep humus layer (2 feet to 1 yard) found around old, robust forest trees represents the highest concentration of soil microbial biodiversity and fertility. These microorganisms are trapped using cooked rice as a carbohydrate attractant, then cultured through sugar/molasses fermentation to create a potent soil inoculant used in Korean Natural Farming and other natural agriculture systems.

## Classification

- **Category**: Beneficial microorganism culture
- **Collection source**: Aged forest humus and litter (preferably 2 feet to 1 yard deep)
- **Method**: Cooked-rice trapping technique
- **Field**: Natural farming, Korean Natural Farming, soil microbiology, forest ecology

## Key Facts

- Collection targets healthy old robust trees in local aged forests with deep humus accumulation (at least 2 feet to 1 yard), indicating high soil fertility and microbial biodiversity.
- The trapping technique exploits the attraction of microorganisms to carbohydrate food sources: cooked rice is placed in a flat container (plastic lunch box) with approximately 1 inch of rice and air space.
- The container is buried in the forest litter/humus, covered with metal netting to protect from animals, and left for 2-10 days (temperature dependent).
- After successful trapping, the rice shows contamination with white and colored molds indicating diverse microbial colonization.
- Culture process: add 1/3 volume of crude sugar or molasses to the infected rice, ferment for one week, then add equal amount sugar/molasses for storage.
- An alternative method involves simply collecting forest litter/humus and spreading it sparingly over cooked rice, or using forest leaf molds directly.
- For use, dilute the preserved serum 1:20 with non-chlorinated water to create the basic forest microorganism spray.
- Forest microorganisms are a key component of the Beneficial Indigenous Microorganism (BIM) formulation, contributing to the overall microbial diversity.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Forest microorganisms are directly relevant to both plant cultivation and mushroom growing. For cultivators, these organisms represent the most diverse and well-balanced soil microbiome found in nature, containing bacteria, actinomycetes, fungi, yeasts, and other organisms that have co-evolved over centuries in a stable forest ecosystem. For mushroom cultivators, the forest microorganism concept is particularly relevant because it demonstrates the importance of the complete soil food web in fungal fruiting. Many mushroom species require or benefit from the presence of companion microorganisms in the substrate or casing layer, and forest-derived inocula may provide these companions naturally.

## Collection and Culture Protocol

1. Select a healthy, old, robust tree in a local aged forest.
2. Verify deep humus/litter accumulation (2 feet to 1 yard) around the tree.
3. Place approximately 1 inch of cooked rice in a flat container with lid.
4. Bury the container in the humus/litter around the tree.
5. Cover with metal netting to protect from animals (rats, etc.).
6. Wait 2-10 days (temperature dependent) for microbial colonization.
7. When rice shows visible white and colored molds, dig up the container.
8. Add 1/3 volume crude sugar or molasses to the infected rice.
9. Ferment for one week until sticky and liquidy.
10. Add equal volume crude sugar or molasses for storage.
11. Dilute 1:20 with non-chlorinated water for application.

## Alternative Methods

- **Direct litter inoculation**: Spread collected forest humus/litter sparingly over the top of cooked rice in a container.
- **Leaf mold method**: Use forest leaf molds as the inoculant source, following the same fermentation protocol.

## Scientific Basis

The deep humus layer in old-growth forests contains the most mature and diverse soil microbiome in terrestrial ecosystems. Decades or centuries of leaf fall, woody debris decomposition, and root exudate accumulation create an environment rich in actinomycetes, mycorrhizal fungi, saprophytic fungi, bacteria (including lactic acid bacteria, photosynthetic bacteria, and nitrogen-fixers), and protozoa. By trapping these organisms and culturing them with carbohydrate substrates, the natural farming approach captures this biodiversity in a form that can be applied to agricultural soils.

## Role in BIM Formulation

Forest microorganisms serve as a critical diversity component of the Beneficial Indigenous Microorganism consortium:

- The BIM recipe combines 50% lactic acid bacteria with 50% other cultured organisms (forest, bamboo, and plant-specific microorganisms).
- The more diverse the microbial input, the more effective the BIM is considered to be.
- Forest microorganisms are particularly valued for their contribution of decomposer fungi and bacteria that accelerate organic matter breakdown in agricultural soils.

## Historical Context

The forest microorganism collection technique is part of the Korean Natural Farming tradition developed by Dr. Han Kyu Cho and popularized internationally by practitioners such as Gil Carandang. The approach embodies the natural farming philosophy of utilizing locally available biological resources and mimicking natural soil ecology rather than relying on purchased commercial inoculants. The technique reflects an understanding that the most successful agricultural microbiomes are those that most closely resemble the balanced, mature ecosystems found in undisturbed forests.
