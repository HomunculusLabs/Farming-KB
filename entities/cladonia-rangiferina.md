# Cladonia rangiferina (Reindeer Lichen)

**Cladonia rangiferina**, commonly called **reindeer lichen** (also known as grey reindeer lichen or Arctic moss), is a fruticose (bushy, branching) lichen species in the family Cladoniaceae. It is one of the most ecologically important lichens in circumpolar regions, serving as a primary winter food source for caribou and reindeer (*Rangifer tarandus*) across the Arctic tundra and boreal forests.

## Taxonomy and Biology

- **Family:** Cladoniaceae
- **Genus:** *Cladonia*
- **Species:** *C. rangiferina* (Linnaeus) Weber
- **Common names:** Grey reindeer lichen, reindeer moss, caribou moss, Arctic moss
- **Photobiont:** Trebouxia (green alga) — the fungal partner provides structure while the algal partner photosynthesizes

### Morphology
*Cladonia rangiferina* forms dense, branching mats of pale grey to white thalli, growing 4–10 cm tall. The branching pattern is intricately dichotomous (forking in pairs), with hollow, cylindrical branches that become increasingly branched toward the tips. The overall appearance resembles a miniature forest or coral colony.

### Key Identification Features
- Pale grey-white coloration when dry, greenish-grey when wet
- Dense, intricate dichotomous branching
- Hollow branch interior (unlike some similar *Cladonia* species)
- Podetia (upright stalks) arising from a primary squamulose (scale-like) thallus
- No visible fruiting bodies (apothecia are rare)
- Brittle and lightweight when dry

### Similar Species
- **Cladonia stellaris:** Forms rounded, star-shaped tufts; branches more robust and puffy
- **Cladonia arbuscula:** Branches are more slender and angled
- **Cladonia mitis:** More yellowish-green tinge; branches more erect
- **Cladonia stygia:** Darker coloration; grows in more humid habitats

The "reindeer lichen" complex is often difficult to distinguish in the field and these species frequently intergrade.

## Distribution and Habitat

### Geographic Range
Circumpolar distribution across:
- Northern Europe (Scandinavia, Iceland, British Isles)
- Northern Russia and Siberia
- Northern Canada (Yukon, NWT, Nunavut)
- Alaska
- Mountainous regions at lower latitudes (Alps, Rockies, Appenines)

### Habitat
- Arctic tundra (dominant ground cover in some areas)
- Boreal and taiga forests (forest floor, on humus)
- Open bogs and muskegs
- Rocky outcrops and barrens
- Sandy or gravelly soils
- Old fields and disturbed ground

Prefers well-lit, open habitats with acidic soils. Sensitive to air pollution and does not thrive near industrial areas.

## Ecology

### Caribou and Reindeer Forage
Reindeer lichen is a keystone species for *Rangifer tarandus* (caribou in North America, reindeer in Eurasia):

- **Winter staple:** Comprises up to 60–80% of winter diet when other forage is unavailable
- **Nutritional value:** High in carbohydrates (lichenin, isolichenin) providing energy; low in protein (3–6% crude protein)
- **Digestion:** Rangifer are uniquely adapted to digest lichen carbohydrates through specialized rumen microflora and the enzyme lichenase
- **Cratering:** Caribou dig through snow (cratering) to access lichen mats beneath
- **Recovery:** Lichen grows extremely slowly (3–5 mm/year), making overgrazed areas slow to recover

### Other Ecological Roles
- **Nitrogen fixation:** Associated cyanobacteria can fix atmospheric nitrogen in some *Cladonia* species
- **Soil formation:** Lichens are pioneer organisms that break down rock and contribute organic matter
- **Microhabitat:** Provides shelter for microarthropods, nematodes, and other soil organisms
- **Moisture retention:** Dense mats retain moisture and moderate soil temperature

### Growth Rate
Reindeer lichen is among the slowest-growing organisms. Primary growth rates range from 3–11 mm per year depending on climate, light, and moisture. Full recovery from overgrazing or disturbance can take 30–100+ years.

## Chemical Composition

### Lichen Acids
- **Usnic acid** (~1–3%): Major secondary metabolite; antibacterial, antifungal, anti-inflammatory
- **Atranorin** (~0.5–1.5%): Depsidone; UV-protective, allelopathic
- **Fumarprotocetraric acid** (trace): Depsidone with antioxidant properties

### Carbohydrates
- **Lichenin** (α-1,3/1,4-glucan): Primary storage carbohydrate, ~50% dry weight
- **Isolichenin** (α-1,4/1,3-glucan): Secondary glucan
- These are unique to lichens and not found in vascular plants

### Nutritional Profile (per 100 g dry weight)
- Carbohydrates: 50–65%
- Protein: 3–6%
- Fiber: 15–25%
- Fat: 1–3%
- Ash (minerals): 1–5%
- Vitamin C: trace amounts
- Carotenoids: trace (from photobiont)

## Traditional and Modern Uses

### Indigenous Peoples
- **Food:** Several Indigenous groups, including the Innu, Dene, and Sami, have traditionally used reindeer lichen as emergency food, often after prolonged boiling to remove bitter acids
- **Medicine:** Used as a poultice for wounds and skin infections (attributed to usnic acid)
- **Bedding and insulation:** Dried lichen used as absorbent bedding, diaper material, and insulation
- **Dye:** Produces brown, yellow, and purple dyes depending on the mordant used

### Culinary (Modern)
In Nordic and Scandinavian cuisine, reindeer lichen is occasionally used:
- Soaked and boiled in multiple water changes to remove bitterness
- Incorporated into breads, soups, and desserts
- Used as a garnish for its striking appearance
- Commercially available dried from specialty suppliers

**Warning:** Raw consumption is not recommended due to stomach irritation from usnic acid and other lichen acids.

### Medicine and Pharmacology
- **Usnic acid** has demonstrated in vitro antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria (including MRSA), antifungal activity, and anti-inflammatory properties
- **Atranorin** shows analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects in animal models
- Traditional use for wound healing is supported by antimicrobial activity
- Usnic acid has been investigated for weight loss but carries hepatotoxicity risk at therapeutic doses

### Industry
- **Taxidermy and model making:** Dried lichen used as naturalistic ground cover in dioramas and model landscapes
- **Floristry:** Used in dried flower arrangements
- **Perfumery:** Lichen extracts used as fixatives in some perfumes (oakmoss is a related *Evernia* species)
