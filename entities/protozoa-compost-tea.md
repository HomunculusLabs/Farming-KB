# Protozoa in Compost Tea

## Description

Protozoa are single-celled eukaryotic organisms that play a critical and often underappreciated role in [[soil-food-web]] and in [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT).
In the compost tea context, protozoa serve as the primary bacterial predators that cycle nutrients by consuming bacteria and releasing plant-available nitrogen in the form of ammonium (NH4+).
This process, known as the microbial loop, is fundamental to biological nutrient cycling.
Elaine Ingham's field guide devotes a specific section to protozoa, recognizing them as essential components of a complete and effective compost tea that delivers not just microorganisms but functional nutrient cycling capacity.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Eukarya
- **Major groups in soil and compost tea**: 
  - Flagellates (Mastigophora) — smallest and most numerous, move by flagella
  - Amoebae (Amoebozoa) — medium-sized, include naked and testate (shell-bearing) forms
  - Ciliates (Ciliophora) — largest soil protozoa, move by cilia, indicate high bacterial activity
- **Role in food web**: Primary consumers of bacteria; secondary consumers in the soil food web; prey for nematodes and microarthropods
- **Size range**: 5-500 micrometers depending on group
- **Abundance in healthy soil**: 10,000 to 100,000 per gram of soil; 10,000+ per mL in quality compost tea

## Key Facts

- Protozoa are the primary grazers of bacteria in soil and compost tea systems, with individual protozoa consuming hundreds to thousands of bacteria per hour
- When protozoa eat bacteria, they release excess nitrogen as ammonium (NH4+), making it immediately plant-available — this process is called the microbial loop or nutrient cycling and is central to biological agriculture
- A healthy compost tea should contain 10,000 or more protozoa per mL according to Ingham's standards — lower numbers indicate an incomplete or poorly brewed tea
- Three main types found in compost tea: flagellates (smallest, 5-20 um, most numerous), amoebae (medium-sized, 10-100 um, include testate forms with shells), and ciliates (largest, 20-500 um, indicators of very high bacterial populations)
- Ciliates are indicators of high bacterial activity because they require abundant bacterial prey — their presence in compost tea signals a robust bacterial community
- Protozoa also consume pathogenic bacteria and fungal spores on leaf surfaces, contributing directly to foliar disease suppression when compost tea is sprayed on crops
- Ingham emphasizes that protozoa inoculation comes from quality compost — if the source compost lacks protozoa, the resulting tea will also be deficient regardless of brewing technique
- Over-application of bacterial foods (like excessive molasses) can cause dissolved oxygen to drop, creating anaerobic conditions that kill protozoa while favoring undesirable anaerobic organisms
- Protozoa are particularly important in foliar applications of compost tea where they consume pathogen propagules (spores, conidia) on leaf surfaces, providing direct biological disease control
- The nutrient release from protozoan grazing can provide 40-70% of the nitrogen taken up by plants in natural ecosystems, making them one of the most important groups for nutrient cycling
- Protozoan populations in tea typically peak 18-24 hours into brewing, then may decline if bacterial food sources are exhausted

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

1. **Nutrient cycling**: Protozoa are the primary mechanism by which bacterial-bound nitrogen becomes plant-available, converting organic nitrogen in bacterial biomass into ammonium that roots can absorb
2. **Compost tea quality assessment**: Presence and diversity of protozoa is a key indicator of compost tea biological quality and brewing success
3. **Disease suppression**: Protozoa consume pathogen spores and bacteria on leaf surfaces, providing direct biological control that reduces infection potential
4. **Soil food web education**: Protozoa are central to Elaine Ingham's soil food web approach and her teaching about biological nutrient management as an alternative to synthetic fertilizers
5. **Mushroom substrate preparation**: Understanding protozoan activity helps explain nutrient dynamics in composting substrates used for mushroom cultivation, where bacterial-protozoan interactions influence nitrogen availability
6. **Compost quality indicator**: The protozoan community in finished compost indicates the maturity and biological quality of the compost, with mature thermal compost and vermicompost both supporting diverse protozoan populations
