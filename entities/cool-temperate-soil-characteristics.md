## Description

Cool temperate soils are distinguished by their exceptional ability to accumulate humus under natural regimes of forest and prairie or meadow. These soils differ fundamentally from both tropical and arid-area soils in their organic matter dynamics, pH profiles, and structural characteristics. They form the foundation of the world's most productive agricultural regions and support rich fungal communities.

## Classification

- **Category:** Soil type / Pedological classification
- **Climate zone:** Cool humid (mesothermal), temperate regions
- **Holdridge classification:** Cool temperate life zones
- **Parent materials:** Glacial deposits, loess, brown/black clay-loams, moraine

## Key Facts

- Characterized by high natural humus accumulation — the defining feature distinguishing them from tropical and arid soils
- pH values in poorly drained areas range from 3.5-4.5 due to humic acid accumulation
- [[cation-exchange-capacity]]) enabling nutrient retention
- When adjusted for pH, micronutrients, and trace elements, these are ideal crop soils
- Sandy coasts are an exception with poorer structure
- The 7-10 day sequence of frontal rains in the westerly wind belt maintains consistent moisture

## Glacial Influences

- Sheets of periglacial outwash cover vast northern hemisphere areas
- Compacted till, moraine, and glacio-fluvial outwash provide mineral diversity
- Downwind deposits of rock flour and fine particles (loess) create especially fertile zones
- Braided streams and complex sediment benches along valleys typify glacial-headwater rivers
- Recently glaciated areas are typically well-provided with lakes

## Water Chemistry of Cool Temperate Streams

- Low meltwater temperatures (4-6°C) maintained most of the year in glacial-headwater streams
- Acid waters from bogs and marshes contain humic acids, tannins, and saponins
- Even at pH 3.5, unpolluted streams can sustain good fish populations
- Low calcium availability in acidic streams limits mollusc populations
- Salmonid fish, suckers, carp, pike, and sturgeon inhabit cool temperate waters

## Historical Agricultural Context

- The region where plough agriculture developed with "high farming" traditions
- Crop rotation over 4-9 years with rest periods (ley of green crop or pasture)
- Development of manure spreading, hay feed stores, silage, and permanent pasture management
- Traditional grains: barley, oats, wheat, and rye
- Traditional root crops for winter storage: turnip, beet, potato, carrot, parsnip

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- High humus content creates excellent conditions for saprophytic and mycorrhizal fungi
- The acidic pH range (3.5-4.5) in poorly drained areas is favorable for acid-loving edible fungi
- Glacial and loess-derived soils provide diverse mineral substrates for fungal growth
- Clay fractions retain moisture critical for fungal fruiting
- Peat soils in acidic rock areas support specialized fungal communities including Sphagnum-associated species
- Traditional liming practices affect fungal community composition
- The accumulation of humus under natural forest regimes indicates strong saprophytic activity
- These soils are the natural habitat of many prized edible fungi including truffles and cultivated species
