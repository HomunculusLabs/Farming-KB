# Mycena purpureofusca

**Mycena purpureofusca** is a species of mushroom in the family Mycenaceae, noted for its potential psychoactive properties and its bioluminescent fruiting bodies. It is one of several *Mycena* species that have been investigated for the presence of tryptamine alkaloids, contributing to our understanding of the phylogenetic distribution of psilocybin biosynthesis across the fungal kingdom.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Mycenaceae
- **Genus:** Mycena
- **Species:** M. purpureofusca

The species was first described by mycologists working on the agaric flora of temperate and subtropical forest ecosystems. The epithet "purpureofusca" refers to the characteristic purplish-brown coloration of the cap and stem. The genus *Mycena* is one of the largest genera of agaric fungi, with hundreds of described species worldwide, many of which are small, fragile, and saprotrophic.

## Description

### Cap
The cap (pileus) is small, typically ranging from 5 to 20 mm in diameter. It is convex to bell-shaped (campanulate) when young, becoming broadly convex or nearly plane with age. The surface is smooth and slightly viscid when moist, colored deep purplish-brown fading to a lighter brown at the margins. The cap flesh is thin and fragile, often translucent-striate near the margin when moist. The center is often darker than the edges, creating a subtle two-toned appearance. In very young specimens, a small umbo may be present at the center of the cap.

### Gills
The gills are adnate to slightly decurrent, moderately spaced, and white to pale gray. They may develop a pinkish tinge with age. The gill edges are even and occasionally slightly fringed. There are typically 16 to 24 gills reaching the stem, with 1 to 3 tiers of shorter lamellulae between them. The gill attachment can be observed to pull away slightly as the cap expands during maturation.

### Stem
The stem (stipe) is slender, central, hollow, and fragile. It measures 20 to 50 mm in length and 1 to 2 mm in thickness. The stem surface is smooth, colored similarly to the cap or slightly paler, often with a whitish basal mycelium. The base may be slightly tomentose where it attaches to the substrate. The stem is insititious, arising directly from the substrate without a distinct bulb or root-like base structure.

### Spores and Microscopic Features
The [[spore-print]] is white. Basidiospores are ellipsoid to roughly amygdaliform, smooth, and non-amyloid, measuring approximately 7-9 × 4-5 µm. Basidia are typically four-spored, clavate, and measure 20-25 µm in length. Cheilocystidia are numerous, cylindrical to ventricose, often with projecting necks. The pileipellis is a cutis of interwoven hyphae with embedded pigments. Clamp connections are present on the hyphae, a useful diagnostic feature for distinguishing *Mycena* species from morphologically similar genera.

### Odor and Taste
The odor is mild and indistinct. The taste is mealy or slightly radish-like but not particularly distinctive. This species is not considered edible and should not be consumed without proper chemical analysis and expert identification.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Mycena purpureofusca* has been reported from temperate broadleaf and mixed forests in North America, Europe, and parts of Asia. It is a saprotrophic species, growing on decaying wood, leaf litter, and forest debris, particularly on hardwood substrates such as fallen oak and beech branches. Fruiting occurs from late summer through autumn, often appearing after periods of sustained rainfall. It can be found growing singly, in small scattered groups, or occasionally in dense clusters on well-decayed logs.

## Bioluminescence

Like several other species in the genus *Mycena*, *M. purpureofusca* exhibits bioluminescence. The gills and sometimes the stem base emit a faint greenish glow in darkness, which is visible to dark-adapted eyes. This phenomenon is caused by the enzyme luciferase acting on the substrate luciferin in an oxygen-dependent reaction. The bioluminescence is thought to serve ecological functions related to [[spore-dispersal]] or possibly as a deterrent to nocturnal invertebrate predators.

The intensity of bioluminescence varies with environmental conditions including temperature, humidity, and the age of the fruiting body. Freshly developed basidiocarps typically exhibit the strongest luminescence. The bioluminescent system in *Mycena* is distinct from that found in other [[bioluminescent-fungi]] such as Omphalotus and Armillaria, suggesting multiple independent evolutionary origins of fungal light emission.

## Chemistry and Psychoactive Properties

*Mycena purpureofusca* is among the *Mycena* species that have been screened for psychoactive tryptamine alkaloids. Some collections have been reported to contain psilocybin and/or psilocin, though the concentrations are generally lower than those found in primary psychoactive genera such as Psilocybe and Panaeolus.

The presence and concentration of active compounds can vary significantly depending on geographic origin, substrate, and environmental conditions. Chemical analysis by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) has confirmed the presence of tryptamine derivatives in some but not all tested specimens. The variable results highlight the importance of chemical analysis of multiple collections from different locations and seasons before drawing conclusions about a species' psychoactive potential.

### Known Compounds

- **Psilocybin** — Detected in some collections at low concentrations
- **Psilocin** — May be present as a metabolite of psilocybin
- **Baeocystin** — Possible minor constituent in some specimens

### Factors Affecting Potency

Multiple factors influence the tryptamine content of fungal collections:

1. **Geographic origin** — Specimens from different regions may show different chemical profiles
2. **Substrate** — The type of decaying wood or leaf litter may affect secondary metabolite production
3. **Seasonal timing** — Environmental conditions during growth influence compound synthesis
4. **Specimen age** — Tryptamine content may degrade as fruiting bodies mature and begin to decompose

## Similar Species

*Mycena purpureofusca* can be confused with several other small, purplish-brown *Mycena* species:

- **[[mycena-pura]]** — Similar coloration but typically larger and with a more distinctive radish-like odor
- **Mycena rosea** — Pinker overall coloration, found in similar habitats
- **Mycena galopus** — Common and widespread, brownish cap, exudes a white latex when cut
- **Mycena sanguinolenta** — Bleeds a reddish fluid when cut, typically smaller

Accurate identification requires [[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] of spore morphology and cheilocystidia characteristics, as macroscopic features alone are often insufficient for reliable species determination in this genus.
