## Transpiration

## Description

Transpiration is the process by which water evaporates from the surface of a plant, primarily through stomata in the leaves. This evaporation creates a negative pressure (tension) that pulls water upward through the xylem from the roots, serving as the primary driving force for water and nutrient transport throughout the plant. About 90 percent of a plant's water loss occurs through transpiration.

## Classification

- **Type:** Plant physiological process
- **Mechanism:** Evaporative water loss driving xylem transport
- **Primary Site:** Stomata on leaf surfaces
- **Related Forces:** Cohesion, adhesion, root pressure

## How Transpiration Works

Transpiration operates through a chain of physical forces:

1. **Stomata open** during the day to allow carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis
2. **Water in xylem** at the base of stomatal openings evaporates into the atmosphere
3. **Cohesion** — water molecules stick together due to hydrogen bonds (polar attraction)
4. Each evaporating molecule **pulls up** the molecule behind it, creating a chain reaction
5. **Negative pressure** at the bottom of the water column pulls new water molecules into the root system
6. Water in the roots is literally **pulled up** through the xylem like a rope

Water and dissolved ions can travel through the xylem at up to 30 meters (100 feet) per hour.

## Key Facts

- Transpiration requires no energy expenditure by the plant — it is a passive process driven by physical forces
- About 90% of plant water loss occurs through transpiration
- Trichomes near stomata speed evaporation by drawing water vapor away from the leaf surface
- At night, stomata close and transpiration ceases; plants respire using water and nutrients brought in during the day
- Cohesion and adhesion hold the water column in place even when transpiration stops at night
- The system is powerful enough to fill a redwood-sized xylem network

## The Three Driving Forces

### Transpiration Pull
Evaporation from stomata creates tension at the top of the water column, pulling water upward.

### Cohesion
Water molecules are polar and stick together through hydrogen bonds, allowing them to act as a continuous chain that can be pulled from the top.

### Adhesion
Water molecules stick to the walls of xylem tubes (and other surfaces), creating a concave meniscus. This adhesion, combined with cohesion, maintains the water column in tall plants.

## Role in Nutrient Transport

Transpiration is the primary mechanism moving dissolved nutrients from roots to shoots:

- Mineral ions absorbed by root hairs enter the xylem
- The transpiration stream carries these ions upward to leaves and growing tips
- Nutrients are delivered to cells along the entire length of the plant
- The rate of transpiration directly affects the rate of nutrient delivery

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Water management:** Understanding transpiration helps growers manage irrigation — high temperatures and low humidity increase transpiration and water demand
- **Humidity control:** In enclosed growing environments, managing humidity affects transpiration rate and thus nutrient uptake
- **Stomatal function:** Potassium is the key nutrient regulating stomatal opening and closing; potassium deficiency impairs transpiration regulation
- **Nutrient delivery:** Foliar nutrient sprays rely on stomatal uptake, which is linked to transpiration dynamics
- **Plant stress:** Excessive transpiration (from heat, wind, low humidity) can cause wilting and nutrient transport failure

## Factors Affecting Transpiration Rate

- **Temperature:** Higher temperature increases evaporation rate
- **Humidity:** Lower humidity increases the water vapor gradient, speeding transpiration
- **Wind:** Air movement removes the boundary layer of humid air around leaves, increasing transpiration
- **Light:** Stomata open in response to light; more light = more transpiration
- **Soil water availability:** Limited soil water reduces transpiration as stomata close
- **CO2 concentration:** Higher CO2 levels can cause partial stomatal closure, reducing transpiration

## Guttation: Related Process

When transpiration is low (e.g., at night) and soil moisture is high, excess water is exuded from leaf tips through hydathodes — a process called guttation. Unlike transpiration, guttation involves liquid water rather than water vapor and occurs through specialized structures (hydathodes) rather than stomata.
