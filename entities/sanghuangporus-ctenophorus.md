# Sanghuangporus ctenophorus

## Overview

**Sanghuangporus ctenophorus** (L.W. Zhou & Y.C. Dai) L.W. Zhou, B.K. Cui & Y.C. Dai is a perennial poroid fungus in the family Hymenochaetaceae. It belongs to the **Sanghuangporus** genus, the taxonomic group that encompasses the medicinally significant "sanghuang" mushrooms of traditional Chinese medicine.

The species was originally described as a member of the genus **Inonotus** before being transferred to the newly erected genus Sanghuangporus in 2016 based on molecular phylogenetic evidence. It is a rare wood-decay fungus associated with broadleaf trees in subtropical forests of East Asia.

## Taxonomy

**Kingdom:** Fungi — **Phylum:** Basidiomycota — **Class:** Agaricomycetes

**Order:** Hymenochaetales — **Family:** Hymenochaetaceae — **Genus:** Sanghuangporus

**Species:** S. ctenophorus (L.W. Zhou & Y.C. Dai) L.W. Zhou, B.K. Cui & Y.C. Dai

**Basionym:** Inonotus ctenophorus L.W. Zhou & Y.C. Dai

The specific epithet *ctenophorus* derives from the Greek *ktenos* (comb) and *phoros* (bearing), referring to the comb-like arrangement of the skeletal hyphae in the context. The species was described in 2012 from specimens collected in **Hainan Province**, China, and formally transferred to Sanghuangporus alongside the genus revision in 2016.

## Morphology

**Basidiocarp:** Perennial, sessile to effused-reflexed, woody-corky when dry. Individual fruiting bodies reach 4–12 cm wide, 2–5 cm thick, and 1–3 cm projecting from the substrate surface.

**Upper surface:** Concentrically zonate, rust-brown to dark brown or nearly black. Surface is coarsely tomentose when young, becoming glabrous and cracked with age. Margin is blunt and rounded.

**Pore surface:** Brown to cinnamon-brown, with circular to angular pores numbering 4–6 per mm. Tubes are stratified, 1–3 mm between layers, totaling up to 5 mm in thickness.

**Context:** Corky, duplex. Upper layer is golden-brown, 1–2 mm thick. Lower layer is darker brown and fibrous.

**Spores:** Subglobose to broadly ellipsoid, 3.5–5 × 3–4 μm, hyaline, thin-walled, smooth, IKI-negative.

**Setal hyphae:** Present in the context and tubes, dark brown, thick-walled, with a sharply pointed or occasionally branched apex. These are a diagnostic feature of the Hymenochaetaceae.

## Distribution and Habitat

S. ctenophorus is known from **southern China** (Hainan, Guangxi, Yunnan, Guangdong provinces) and has been tentatively recorded from northern **Vietnam** and **Thailand**. It occurs in tropical and subtropical monsoon forests at elevations of 200–1,200 m.

The species is a **white rot** fungus, causing a selective delignification of host wood. It fruits almost exclusively on living or recently dead standing trunks and fallen logs of broadleaf trees, particularly species in the families **Fagaceae** (Castanopsis, Lithocarpus) and **Moraceae** (Morus, Artocarpus).

Collections are strongly seasonal, with fruiting bodies most commonly encountered during the warm, humid months from May to October.

## Chemistry and Bioactivity

As a member of the Sanghuangporus clade, S. ctenophorus produces a profile of bioactive metabolites characteristic of the sanghuang group. The principal classes of bioactive compounds include:

**Polysaccharides:** β-D-glucans with immunomodulatory properties. Water-soluble polysaccharide fractions from related Sanghuangporus species demonstrate significant anti-tumor activity in murine sarcoma 180 and Lewis lung carcinoma models through macrophage activation and NK cell enhancement.

**Polyphenols and hispidin derivatives:** These compounds exhibit strong antioxidant activity, scavenging DPPH and hydroxyl radicals with IC50 values comparable to ascorbic acid.

**Triterpenoids:** Lanostane-type triterpenes with documented anti-inflammatory activity. Inonotusol and related compounds inhibit NF-κB signaling pathways.

Specific pharmacological activities reported for Sanghuangporus species include:

- Anti-tumor activity through immune potentiation and direct cytotoxicity
- Hepatoprotective effects against carbon tetrachloride–induced liver injury
- Anti-diabetic activity via α-glucosidase inhibition and glycemic regulation
- Antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli

Note: Most published pharmacological data pertains to the better-studied S. sanghuang and S. linteus; S. ctenophorus-specific bioassays remain limited.

## Cultivation

Artificial cultivation of S. ctenophorus remains in early research stages. Like other Sanghuangporus species, it is a slow colonizer with exacting substrate requirements. Current research has focused on two approaches.

**Solid-substrate cultivation** uses supplemented hardwood sawdust (oak, beech, or Castanopsis) blended with wheat bran at 10–20% by weight. Substrates are sterilized at 121°C for 90 minutes and inoculated with grain spawn. Colonization at 25–28°C requires 60–90 days.

Fruiting induction involves cold shock (4–6°C for 48–72 hours), followed by transfer to a high-humidity (>85%) fruiting chamber at 22–25°C with diffuse light. First fruiting bodies may appear 2–4 weeks after induction, but yields and consistency remain variable.

**Liquid fermentation** offers a more controllable production method for biomass and polysaccharide extraction. Submerged cultures in glucose-yeast extract medium achieve peak mycelial biomass at 7–10 days, with polysaccharide yields of 1.5–3.0 g/L. This method bypasses the challenges of fruiting body induction.

The primary bottleneck for commercial cultivation is the species' slow growth rate and the difficulty of inducing consistent fruiting. For Sanghuangporus generally, biological efficiency (fresh fruiting body weight per dry substrate weight) typically ranges from 5–15%, compared to 50–100% for easily cultivated species like Pleurotus ostreatus.

## Conservation Status

Sanghuangporus species are generally rare in nature due to their specific host-tree associations and slow growth rates. S. ctenophorus has not been formally assessed by the IUCN, but the destruction of tropical and subtropical broadleaf forests in southern China and Southeast Asia threatens its habitat.

The growing commercial demand for sanghuang products has intensified collection pressure on wild populations. Artificial cultivation of Sanghuangporus species remains technically challenging, though liquid fermentation and solid-substrate methods are under active development.

## Distinguishing from Related Sanghuangporus Species

S. ctenophorus is differentiated from other Sanghuangporus species by a combination of morphological and host-range characters.

Compared to **S. sanghuang**, S. ctenophorus has smaller pores (4–6 per mm vs. 5–8 per mm) and a more restricted southern distribution. The context of S. ctenophorus is distinctly duplex, while S. sanghuang typically shows a more homogeneous context structure.

From **S. linteus** (formerly Phellinus linteus), S. ctenophorus differs in its lighter pore surface color and the absence of a true hymenial setae layer. S. linteus also tends to occur on different host genera (primarily Prunus and Quercus).

**S. baumii** shares the same general habitat type but can be distinguished by its larger spores (5–7 μm vs. 3.5–5 μm) and its association with mulberry (Morus) hosts. Molecular identification via ITS sequencing is considered the most reliable method for species-level determination within Sanghuangporus.
