Populations from opposite sides of the Pacific are fully cross-fertile, producing viable spores in artificial hybridization experiments. This genetic compatibility supports the interpretation that the disjunction is relatively recent in geological terms.

The species colonizes a wide range of wet to moist habitats including marsh edges, swamp margins, alluvial floodplains, stream banks, ditches, roadside swales, and damp thickets. It tolerates both full sun in open wetlands and partial shade in woodland seeps.

Soil preferences range from acidic peat to neutral alluvial loam. The species is intolerant of prolonged drought but can survive brief dry periods through its deep rhizome system.

In cultivation, the fern has naturalized beyond its native range in parts of western Europe, where it is occasionally considered invasive in bog gardens and along waterways. Its spread is primarily vegetative through rhizome fragmentation.

Rhizome fragments can survive flooding and subsequent deposition on new sites, facilitating downstream colonization along watercourses. This dispersal mechanism, combined with human-mediated transport in horticultural materials, accounts for the species' spread beyond its native range.

In Europe, O. sensibilis was first recorded naturalized in the British Isles in the 19th century and has since spread to parts of France, Germany, and Scandinavia. It is most invasive in disturbed wetland habitats with reduced native competition.

## Ecological Role

The sensitive fern plays a significant role in wetland succession, colonizing open disturbed sites and forming dense mats that stabilize soil and reduce erosion. Its extensive rhizome network binds alluvial sediments along stream banks and floodplains.

Fronds provide cover and foraging habitat for wetland wildlife including frogs, salamanders, and ground-nesting birds. The dense sterile frond canopy creates humid microclimates at ground level that benefit amphibian populations.

White-tailed deer occasionally browse sterile fronds, though the coarse texture makes it a low-preference forage. The persistent fertile fronds provide limited winter cover for small mammals and ground-dwelling birds.
