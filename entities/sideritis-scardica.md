## Overview

**Sideritis scardica** is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Lamiaceae, commonly known as **Greek mountain tea** or **Pirin mountain tea**.

It is native to the mountainous regions of the Balkan Peninsula, where it has been consumed as a traditional herbal tea for centuries.

The species is valued for its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and mild anxiolytic properties, and has attracted research attention as a potential cognitive enhancer.

*Sideritis scardica* is one of the most commercially important *Sideritis* species, forming the basis of a growing export market from Greece and Bulgaria.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Phylum:** Angiosperms
- **Class:** Eudicots
- **Order:** Lamiales
- **Family:** Lamiaceae
- **Genus:** *Sideritis*
- **Species:** *S. scardica*

Plants grow 20–50 cm tall with erect, woody-based stems that are densely white-tomentose.

Leaves are opposite, ovate to lanceolate, 2–5 cm long, with crenate margins and a woolly white underside.

Inflorescences are terminal, congested verticillasters forming a dense spike-like raceme, 3–10 cm long.

Calyx is campanulate, 5–7 mm, densely covered in white hairs, with five nearly equal teeth.

Corolla is yellow or pale yellow, 8–12 mm long, bilabiate with a curved upper lip and spreading lower lip.

Flowering occurs from June to August; fruit is a group of four brown nutlets, each approximately 2 mm long.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Sideritis scardica* is endemic to the mountainous regions of the central and southern Balkans.

Its native range extends through the Pirin, Rila, Rhodope, and Pindus mountain ranges across Bulgaria, Greece, North Macedonia, and Albania.

The species grows at elevations of 800–2,200 meters on limestone and siliceous substrates.

Preferred habitats include dry rocky slopes, alpine meadows, scree fields, and open forest clearings.

It is well-adapted to harsh mountain conditions including intense UV exposure, temperature extremes, and poor, shallow soils.

Wild populations have declined due to overharvesting, and the species is classified as near-threatened in parts of its range.

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds

The primary bioactive constituents are flavonoids, phenylethanoid glycosides, and terpenoids.

Major flavonoids include **hypolaetin-8-O-glucoside**, **isoscutellarein-7-O-glucoside**, **isoscutellarein-8-O-glucoside**, and **apigenin** derivatives.

Phenylethanoid glycosides include **verbascoside** (acteoside) and **forsythoside B**, both with significant anti-inflammatory activity.

Essential oil composition is dominated by **monoterpene hydrocarbons** including α-pinene, β-pinene, and limonene.

Diterpenes of the **ent-kaurane** type are characteristic of the genus, including siderol and sideridiol.

Total phenolic content ranges from 40–80 mg GAE/g dry weight depending on origin and harvest time.

Antioxidant capacity (DPPH IC50) is comparable to green tea, primarily attributable to the phenylethanoid glycoside fraction.

## Pharmacology and Health Effects

Anti-inflammatory activity has been demonstrated in vitro via inhibition of COX-2, iNOS, and NF-κB signaling pathways.

The methanolic extract shows neuroprotective effects in cellular models of oxidative stress, reducing ROS accumulation by 40–60%.

Animal studies indicate memory-enhancing effects, potentially mediated by acetylcholinesterase inhibition and increased BDNF expression.

A double-blind crossover study found significant improvements in cognitive performance in elderly subjects consuming *S. scardica* tea daily for 4 weeks.

The herb shows mild anxiolytic effects in rodent models, comparable to low-dose diazepam, without sedative side effects.

Antimicrobial activity has been documented against Gram-positive bacteria including *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Streptococcus pyogenes*.

## Traditional and Modern Use

Greek mountain tea (*τσάι του βουνού*) is one of the most popular traditional beverages in Greece, consumed daily by millions.

Traditional preparation involves steeping the dried flowering aerial parts in boiling water for 5–10 minutes, often with honey and lemon.

It is traditionally used for colds, respiratory infections, digestive complaints, and as a general tonic for fatigue and low mood.

Modern use focuses on its adaptogenic and nootropic potential, with commercial products including dried herb, tinctures, and standardized extracts.

The European Medicines Agency has not yet issued a monograph, but the herb is sold as a traditional herbal tea across the EU.

## Cultivation

Cultivation of *S. scardica* has expanded significantly to reduce pressure on wild populations.

Seeds require cold stratification (4°C for 30–60 days) and light for germination; germination rates are 60–80% under optimal conditions.

Plants prefer full sun to partial shade, well-drained calcareous soils, and moderate watering.

Harvest occurs during full flowering (July–August); aerial parts are dried at 35–40°C to preserve volatile compounds.

Yields of dried herb range from 200–400 kg/ha under cultivation, with essential oil content of 0.3–0.8% v/w.

The species is suitable for organic cultivation and shows good pest resistance due to its high terpenoid content.
