## Description

Endomycorrhizal fungi (also called [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]], or AMF) are a group of [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] that physically penetrate and grow inside plant root cells, forming an intimate symbiotic association. Unlike ectomycorrhizal fungi, which form sheaths around root surfaces, endomycorrhizae actually invade root tissue while simultaneously extending a vast network of hyphae outward into the surrounding soil.

These fungi are preferred by most vegetables, annuals, grasses, shrubs, perennials, and softwood trees. They represent one of the most widespread and ecologically significant symbioses on Earth, associating with approximately 80–90% of all terrestrial plant species.

## Classification

Endomycorrhizal fungi belong primarily to the phylum **Glomeromycota**. Key taxonomic groups include:

| Category | Details |
|----------|---------|
| Phylum | Glomeromycota |
| Classes | Glomeromycetes, Paraglomeromycetes, Archaeosporomycetes |
| Key Genera | *Glomus*, *Rhizophagus*, *Gigaspora*, *Acaulospora*, *Scutellospora* |
| Common Name | [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]] (AMF), vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizae (VAM) |

The term "arbuscular" refers to the tree-like structures (arbuscules) formed inside root cortical cells, which are the primary sites of nutrient exchange between fungus and plant.

## Mechanism of Action

### Root Colonization

1. **Spore germination**: Fungal spores in the soil detect chemical signals from plant root exudates
2. **Hyphal growth**: Germinating hyphae grow toward the root, guided by strigolactones and other root exudate compounds
3. **Appressorium formation**: The hypha forms a pressurized attachment structure on the root surface
4. **Penetration**: The fungus enters the root through the epidermis or between cortical cells
5. **Arbuscule formation**: Inside root cortical cells, the fungus forms highly branched arbuscules—the primary nutrient exchange interfaces
6. **Extraradical hyphal expansion**: Simultaneously, hyphae extend outward from the root into the surrounding soil

### Nutrient Exchange

The arbuscule functions as a bidirectional exchange system:

- **Plant → Fungus**: The plant supplies carbohydrates (up to 10–20% of photosynthate) from root exudates
- **Fungus → Plant**: The fungus delivers water, phosphorus, nitrogen, and micronutrients mined from soil

### Root Surface Area Enhancement

Endomycorrhizal fungi can increase the effective surface area of a tree"s root system by an extraordinary **700 to 1,000 times**. This massive expansion allows plants to access water and nutrients from soil volumes far beyond the reach of roots alone.

## Key Functions

### Phosphorus Acquisition

Retrieving phosphorus appears to be a major function of many endomycorrhizal fungi. The organic acids produced by mycorrhizal hyphae can unlock, retrieve, and transport chemically locked-up phosphorus back to the host plant. This is particularly important because phosphorus is one of the most immobile major nutrients in soil, often fixed in forms unavailable to plant roots.

### Micronutrient Mobilization

Beyond phosphorus, endomycorrhizal fungi free up several essential micronutrients for plant use:

- **Copper** (Cu): Essential for enzyme function and lignin synthesis
- **Calcium** (Ca): Critical for cell wall structure and signaling
- **Magnesium** (Mg): Central atom in chlorophyll, essential for photosynthesis
- **Zinc** (Zn): Required for auxin synthesis and enzyme activation
- **Iron** (Fe): Essential for chlorophyll synthesis and [[electron-transport-chain-mitochondrial-respiration]]

### Water Relations

The extensive hyphal network acts as an auxiliary water transport system, accessing soil moisture in micropores too small for roots to penetrate. This drought-buffering capacity is particularly valuable in sandy or well-drained soils.

### Pathogen Protection

[[mycorrhizal-colonization-thresholds]] provides physical and chemical barriers against root pathogens:
- **Physical barrier**: Dense hyphal networks around roots impede pathogen access
- **Chemical defense**: Colonized plants often show enhanced production of defense compounds
- **Competitive exclusion**: Mycorrhizal fungi occupy root space that pathogens might otherwise colonize

## Role in Nutrient Cycling

### Carbon Flow

Endomycorrhizal fungi receive 10–20% of a plant's total photosynthetic carbon output. This carbon flows through the fungal network into [[the-soil-food-web]], supporting bacteria, microarthropods, and other organisms that feed on fungal hyphae and exudates.

### Nutrient Retention

Any nutrient compounds not immediately delivered to plant roots are locked up in fungal tissue and released only when the fungi die and decompose. This prevents nutrient leaching and maintains a slowly cycling nutrient pool in the soil.

### Inter-Plant Nutrient Transfer

Remarkably, mycorrhizal fungi can form connections between the roots of different plants, carrying water and nutrients from one plant to another. A mycorrhizal fungus associated with one plant may help neighboring plants simultaneously, creating an underground nutrient-sharing network sometimes called the "[[wood-wide-web]]."

## Relevance to Cultivation

### Host Plant Range

Endomycorrhizal fungi associate with an exceptionally broad range of host plants:

- **Vegetables**: Tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, lettuce, carrots, onions, and most others
- **Annuals and perennials**: Virtually all garden flowering plants
- **Grasses**: Lawn grasses, cereal crops, and [[ornamental-grasses-guide]]
- **Shrubs and softwood trees**: Most landscape shrubs and many tree species

Notable exceptions include plants in the Brassicaceae (cabbage family), Chenopodiaceae (beet family), and Proteaceae, which generally do not form [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]].

### Management Considerations

**Practices that promote endomycorrhizal fungi:**
- Minimize soil disturbance (reduced or no tillage)
- Maintain continuous plant cover (avoid fallow periods)
- Limit inorganic phosphorus fertilizer (high P reduces colonization)
- Use diverse plantings (different plants support different fungal species)
- Apply [[query-how-do-i-use-mycorrhizal-inoculants-when-growing-cannabis]] establishing new plantings

**Practices that harm endomycorrhizal fungi:**
- Rototilling and double digging (physically sever hyphal networks)
- High rates of inorganic fertilizer (especially phosphorus)
- Fungicide applications (directly toxic to mycorrhizae)
- Soil compaction (crushes fungal tubes and hyphae)
- Extended fallow periods (fungi die without host plant exudates)
- Exposure to air pollution containing nitrogenous substances
