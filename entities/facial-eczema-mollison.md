## Facial Eczema

## Description

[[bill-mollison]] in *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* as one of the intractable illnesses that arises from forced high-production regimes in pastoral agriculture. It is a condition affecting ruminants, particularly cattle and sheep, caused by toxicity from fungal spores (primarily *Pithomyces chartarum*) ingested from pasture. The disease exemplifies the cascading consequences of industrial farming practices that prioritise maximum yield over system health.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Animal disease (fungal toxin origin)
- **Category**: Livestock health / Mycotoxicosis
- **Causative organism**: *Pithomyces chartarum* (saprophytic fungus)
- **Toxin**: Sporidesmin
- **Affected species**: Cattle, sheep, goats, deer, alpacas and other ruminants
- **Geographic distribution**: Primarily New Zealand, also parts of Australia, South Africa, and South America

## Key Facts

- Bill Mollison cites facial eczema as a consequence of stressed, high-production farming systems in New Zealand, where veterinary costs reached $120 per stock unit in intensive operations.
- The disease damages the liver of affected animals, leading to photosensitization (skin lesions, especially on the face), weight loss, and reduced productivity.
- On less-stressed pastures and farms, veterinary costs drop dramatically to around $20 per unit, and herds remain healthier.
- The condition is directly linked to over-fertilisation and intensive [[fungal-ecology]] directly impacts agricultural sustainability. Permaculture approaches — diverse pasture mixes, rotational grazing, reduced chemical inputs — create environments less favorable to the pathogenic fungus and more resilient livestock systems. Understanding this disease reinforces the permaculture principle that diverse, low-stress systems are more productive in the long term than forced monocultures.

## Management in Permaculture Systems

- Diverse pasture species reduce fungal load by breaking the monoculture conditions favorable to *P. chartarum*
- Rotational grazing prevents buildup of dead litter where the fungus sporulates
- Integration of trees and shrubs provides shade, reducing the photosensitization symptoms and breaking the humid microclimate
- Reduced nitrogen fertilisation lowers fungal substrate availability
- Breeding for natural resistance rather than relying on chemical prophylaxis
- Introduction of competitive decomposer fungi and beneficial soil organisms that break down dead litter faster
- Monitoring spore counts and adjusting grazing schedules accordingly

## Broader Implications

Mollison uses facial eczema as a case study in why the "Big Pumpkin Fallacy" — the pursuit of maximum product yield — is fundamentally flawed. When production is forced beyond natural limits, the resulting health problems in plants, soils, and animals become "economically and ecologically unsustainable, and a danger to public health." The permaculture alternative — diverse, low-input systems that produce moderate but reliable yields — avoids these cascading failures entirely.
