## Description

HEX-1 is the core structural protein of Woronin bodies in Euascomycete fungi. It self-assembles into a dense proteinaceous crystalline matrix that forms the characteristic osmophilic core of these septal-pore-associated organelles.

## Classification

- **Type**: Protein
- **Gene**: hex-1
- **Phylogenetic distribution**: Exclusive to Euascomycetes (present in all Euascomycete genomes examined, absent in other fungal groups)
- **Cellular location**: Woronin body core
- **Origin**: Peroxisomal (Woronin bodies are peroxisome-derived organelles)

## Key Facts

- Encoded by the hex-1 gene, which is considered diagnostic of the Euascomycete lineage
- Self-assembles into a dense crystalline core that gives Woronin bodies their characteristic electron-dense appearance under transmission electron microscopy
- Woronin bodies are bounded by a unit membrane derived from the peroxisomal membrane system
- The dense HEX-1 core enables Woronin bodies to function as physical plugs for septal pores
- When hyphal damage occurs, Woronin bodies rapidly move to and seal septal pores, preventing catastrophic loss of cytoplasm and allowing the syncytial [[mycelium]] to cellularize affected compartments
- The phylogenetic distribution of hex-1 supports a single evolutionary origin of Woronin bodies in the ancestral Euascomycete
- hex-1 is absent from Hemiascomycetes, Archiascomycetes, Basidiomycota, Zygomycota, and Chytridiomycota genomes
- Woronin bodies exist in two morphological forms: oval and hexagonal, both containing the HEX-1 core
- Tethering filaments connect Woronin bodies to the septal pore region, positioning them for rapid pore closure
- The evolution of HEX-1 and Woronin bodies is correlated with the development of complex multicellular fruiting bodies in the Euascomycetes

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The HEX-1 protein and the Woronin bodies it forms are essential for the multicellular integrity of Ascomycete fungi. This system allows filamentous Ascomycetes, including many commercially cultivated species such as morels (Morchella spp.), truffles (Tuber spp.), and Cordyceps, to maintain organized hyphal networks capable of cellular differentiation. Understanding HEX-1 function provides insight into the cellular mechanisms that support fruiting body formation and the ability of Ascomycete mycelia to survive and recover from physical damage during substrate colonization.
