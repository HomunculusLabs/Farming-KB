# Granitic Landscape Permaculture

## Description

Granitic landscapes are slab-and-rock landscapes presenting peculiar cultivation challenges: typically hundreds of feet of permeable sand, virtually no water retention, and rapid loss of all organic material through the soil profile. Millions of people live on these landscapes worldwide, including the entire Perth plain of Australia. [[bill-mollison]] dedicated Pamphlet VI of his [[permaculture-design]] Course to the unique strategies required for productive cultivation on granitic terrain, where conventional agriculture fails entirely but creative design can create productive systems.

## Classification

- **Landscape type**: Residual granitic—slab landscapes with extensive rock outcrops
- **Soil type**: Deep siliceous sands (often 100–200+ feet of clean sand)
- **Water characteristics**: Extremely high permeability, virtually no retention, water table often deep and increasingly saline
- **Climate range**: Can occur in reasonably humid to semi-arid zones
- **Geographic examples**: Perth plain (Western Australia), ancient river plains near granitic formations

## Key Facts

1. **Water impossibility**: On the Perth plain, there is approximately 200 feet of clean sand. Water applied to the surface simply descends indefinitely. Even thick mulch breaks down and washes through the sand profile in six months.

2. **Saline bore water**: Groundwater bores must go increasingly deep (from 20 feet historically to 2,000 feet now), and the water becomes progressively saltier because fresh water floats over denser saline layers. As fresh water is pumped off, bores must go deeper into saltier strata.

3. **Tree crop emphasis**: Trees perform surprisingly well on granitic sands, making tree crops the primary replacement for annual crops. Palms, figs, grapes, olives, date palms, and raspberries all perform well with minimal work.

4. **Rock microclimates**: Around granite outcrops, microclimates range from date-palm conditions to strawberry conditions. The reflectivity of granite provides light and heat benefits; incorporating rock mass into buildings creates efficient heat stores.

5. **Rock-surface cultivation**: Mulch can be placed directly on top of rocks and beside rocks below, creating productive growing pockets. Granite flakes off in large sheets that can be laid out with crowbar assistance to create mulched growing areas.

6. **Morning vs. afternoon sun**: Plants can be grown on both sides of rocks—morning sun for leaf production, afternoon sun for ripening. This creates remarkably diverse habitat niches in small spaces.

7. **Lined garden beds**: Garden areas should be completely lined with plastic sheeting to create underground tank-like conditions where humic acid can reach plant roots rather than washing away.

8. **Gley ponds**: Small gley ponds, tanks, and modest bore water use are essential for survival on these landscapes. Below-garden gley systems (dig earth, lay green mat layer, return earth) may also work.

9. **Lawns are impossible**: It takes approximately 90 inches of water per year to maintain a lawn on siliceous sands. Lawns represent total disasters on granitic landscapes.

10. **Productive potential**: A four-acre granitic property managed with rock gardening techniques can become more productive than an equivalent area of promising agricultural soil, using stones for ripening, heat storage, and microclimate creation.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Rock-sheltered mushroom beds**: The crevices, shaded areas, and moss-covered zones around granite outcrops provide natural humidity chambers for [[mushroom-cultivation]]. The constant moisture from condensation on cool rock surfaces creates ideal [[fruiting-conditions]].

- **Decomposition on sand**: In deep siliceous sands, organic matter decomposes and washes away rapidly. Introducing fungal mycelium as decomposition agents could help retain nutrients in the upper soil profile by forming [[mycelial-networks]] that bind organic material before it washes through.

- **Lined bed substrate systems**: The plastic-lined garden concept translates directly to contained mushroom cultivation beds where substrate must be isolated from the surrounding sand and maintained at specific moisture levels.

- **Log and rock niche cultivation**: Piles of granite rocks and logs create the edge habitats and niches where mushroom cultivation logs can be integrated. Mollison specifically noted that "a pile of logs is great—big logs, I mean, a huge amount of niches, and a great potential."

- **Mulch decomposition management**: On granitic sands, mulch disappears rapidly. Fungal inoculation of mulch could accelerate the conversion of mulch to stable humus before it washes through the sand profile.

- **Vine cultivation on rock**: Mollison recommended places for vines to grow and climb on rock instead of fencing. Mushroom-producing vines or companion plantings could be integrated into this rock-vine system.
