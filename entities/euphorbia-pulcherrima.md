## Overview

Euphorbia pulcherrima, commonly known as poinsettia, is a species of flowering
plant in the family Euphorbiaceae. It is indigenous to Mexico and Central
America, where it grows as a perennial shrub reaching 1-4 metres in height.

The plant is renowned worldwide as a ornamental houseplant associated with the
Christmas holiday season. Its brilliant red bracts, which surround small yellow
cyathia (true flowers), are the most recognisable feature.

Euphorbia pulcherrima was named by Johann Friedrich Klotzsch in 1834. The
specific epithet pulcherrima means "most beautiful" in Latin, reflecting the
striking appearance of its coloured bracts.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Euphorbia pulcherrima belongs to the genus Euphorbia, one of the largest
genera of flowering plants with over 2,000 species. It is placed in the
subgenus Euphorbia, section Poinsettia.

The species was first described by the German botanist Karl Willdenow in 1812
as Poinsettia pulcherrima, honouring Joel Roberts Poinsett. Klotzsch later
transferred it to the genus Euphorbia.

The plant is a shrub or small tree with alternate, dark green, lobed leaves
measuring 7-16 cm in length. The leaf margins are typically dentate or
slightly lobed.

The showy "flowers" are actually modified leaves called bracts. The bracts are
bright red in the wild type, though cultivated varieties range from white,
pink, orange, marbled, and cream.

The true flowers are small, yellow-green cyathia borne at the centre of the
bract cluster. Each cyathium contains several male flowers (stamens) and a
single female flower (pistil).

All parts of the plant exude a milky white latex when damaged. This latex is
characteristic of the Euphorbiaceae and serves as a chemical defence against
herbivory.

## Distribution and Habitat

Euphorbia pulcherrima is native to the Pacific coast of Mexico, from
Guerrero to Chiapas, extending into Guatemala. It grows in tropical
deciduous forests and seasonally dry scrublands at elevations of 600-2,200
metres.

Wild populations are found as scattered individuals or small colonies in
disturbed areas, canyon edges, and steep hillsides. The species thrives in
well-drained, slightly acidic volcanic soils.

The Aztec name for the plant was cuetlaxochitl, meaning "flower that grows in
residue" or "leather flower." It was cultivated in pre-Columbian gardens and
used for decorative and medicinal purposes.
