## Overview

*Liquidambar formosana*, commonly known as Formosan sweetgum or Chinese sweetgum, is a large deciduous tree in the family Altingiaceae. It is native to southern China, Taiwan, Vietnam, Laos, and northern Myanmar.

The species is closely related to the American sweetgum (*Liquidambar styraciflua*) and shares many ornamental traits including brilliant autumn foliage in shades of crimson, orange, and purple. It reaches 30–40 meters in height with a straight trunk and broadly conical crown.

Its bark exudes a fragrant, amber-colored resin called liquidambar or storax, used in traditional Chinese medicine and perfumery. The timber is valued for furniture-making and construction across East Asia.

In the horticultural trade, it is sometimes listed under the synonym *Altingia formosana*. The species was first described by the British botanist Hance in 1866 from specimens collected in Taiwan.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Clade Angiosperms → Clade Eudicots → Order Saxifragales → Family Altingiaceae → Genus *Liquidambar* → Species *L. formosana* Hance (1866).

Synonyms include *Altingia formosana* (Hance) Rehder & E.H.Wilson and *Liquidambar acerifolia* Maxim. The genus *Liquidambar* contains four to six species distributed across eastern Asia, western Asia, and eastern North America.

Leaves are palmately lobed with three (sometimes five) triangular lobes, 7–17 cm long and 8–20 cm broad. Leaf margins are finely serrated, and the leaf base is truncate to slightly cordate.

The petioles are slender, 5–12 cm long, often turning reddish before leaf fall in autumn. Venation is palmate with three primary veins diverging from the base of each blade.

Leaves turn brilliant shades of crimson, deep red, orange, and occasionally yellow in autumn. Fall color develops earlier at higher elevations and in more exposed sites. The display typically lasts two to three weeks before leaves drop.

The bark is dark gray-brown, developing deep longitudinal fissures with age. Twigs are glabrous, reddish-brown, with scattered lenticels.

Terminal buds are ovoid, with reddish-brown scales that are slightly resinous to the touch. Lateral buds are smaller and appressed against the twig surface.

Inflorescences are unisexual, appearing in early spring before or with the expanding leaves. Male flowers form pendulous terminal racemes up to 5 cm long; female flowers are borne in globose heads on long peduncules.

The distinctive globular, spiny fruiting heads are 2–3 cm in diameter and persist through winter. Each head contains numerous small, flattened seeds with narrow wings for wind dispersal.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Liquidambar formosana* is native to a broad region of subtropical and warm-temperate East and Southeast Asia. Its range extends from central and southern China through Taiwan and into Vietnam, Laos, and northern Myanmar.

In China, it is common in provinces including Fujian, Guangdong, Guangxi, Guizhou, Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi, Sichuan, Yunnan, and Zhejiang. It is also found in the Ryukyu Islands of southern Japan.

In Taiwan, it occurs at elevations of 400–1,500 meters, often forming nearly pure stands on hillsides. The species is a characteristic component of subtropical broadleaved forests below 1,800 meters.

It grows in moist, well-drained soils along stream banks, in valley forests, and on mountain slopes. It prefers full sun to partial shade and is moderately drought-tolerant once established.

The species thrives in loamy, slightly acidic soils with a pH of 5.5–6.5. It does not tolerate prolonged waterlogging or highly alkaline conditions.

The species acts as a pioneer in disturbed forests, colonizing clearings and abandoned agricultural land. It associates with *Cunninghamia lanceolata*, *Pinus massoniana*, and various broadleaf species in mixed stands.

Genetic studies reveal distinct population structure between mainland China and Taiwanese populations. Taiwanese populations show higher genetic diversity, suggesting long-term in situ persistence during Pleistocene glacial cycles.

## Chemistry and Uses

The aromatic resin (storax) collected from bark wounds contains cinnamic acid, styrene, and vanillin. In Chinese herbal medicine, the resin is known as "feng xiang zhi" and is used to promote blood circulation and relieve pain.

Traditional preparations apply the resin externally for skin conditions and internally as an anti-inflammatory. Modern pharmacological studies have confirmed antimicrobial and antioxidant activity in the resin compounds.

Essential oil distilled from the leaves contains α-pinene, β-pinene, and limonene as major constituents. These volatile compounds contribute to the tree's pest-resistant properties in forest settings.

The timber is moderately hard and fine-grained, with light brown to reddish-brown heartwood. It is used for furniture, interior trim, veneer, and tea chests throughout East Asia.

In Taiwan, the wood is traditionally used for making storage chests due to its natural insect-repellent properties. The timber seasons well with minimal warping and takes stains and finishes evenly.

The bark has been used as a source of tannin for leather production in rural areas. The leaves are occasionally used as a natural dye, producing yellow to brown colors depending on the mordant used.

## Ornamental Value

As an ornamental, Formosan sweetgum is prized for its reliable autumn color in shades of crimson, orange, and gold. It is widely planted as a street and park tree in Chinese cities, Japan, and increasingly in temperate gardens worldwide.

The species tolerates heat and humidity better than the American sweetgum, making it suitable for warmer climates. Selected cultivars emphasize deeper red fall color or narrower crown form.

In China, it has been planted along roadsides and in temple grounds for centuries. Several historically significant specimens exceeding 300 years old are protected in Fujian and Taiwan.

It grows best in USDA Hardiness Zones 7–9, preferring slightly acidic, moist, well-drained soils. Young trees transplant readily and establish quickly with regular watering in the first two years.

Mature trees require little maintenance beyond occasional crown thinning. The species is resistant to most serious diseases, though coral spot canker may affect weakened specimens.

The spiny fruiting heads can be a nuisance on paved surfaces, similar to those of the American sweetgum. The species is generally pest-free, though scale insects and leaf spot may occur on stressed specimens.

Propagation is primarily by seed, which requires cold stratification for 30–60 days before sowing. Germination rates are typically 60–80% with proper treatment.

## Ecology

*Liquidambar formosana* is wind-pollinated, with pollen dispersed by early spring breezes before canopy closure. The spiny fruiting heads persist through winter, releasing winged seeds gradually over several months.

The tree serves as a host plant for various Lepidoptera larvae in its native range. Fruiting heads provide winter food for seed-eating birds including finches and tits.

Deer and other browsing mammals feed on young shoots and seedlings, which can limit regeneration in overbrowsed forests. The resin-rich bark provides some defense against bark-boring insects.

Fallen leaves decompose rapidly, enriching the forest floor with nutrients. It forms ectomycorrhizal associations with a range of fungal partners, facilitating nutrient uptake in nutrient-poor soils.

The species is moderately shade-intolerant, requiring canopy gaps for successful seedling establishment. In its native range, it contributes to watershed protection and soil stabilization on steep mountain slopes.

*Liquidambar formosana* can live 150–200 years under favorable conditions. Mature specimens develop wide-spreading root systems that help stabilize stream banks and prevent erosion.

The species is not currently considered threatened. However, habitat conversion for agriculture and urbanization has reduced its extent in lowland areas of southern China.
