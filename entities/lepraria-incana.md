# Lepraria incana

## Description

Lepraria incana is a crustose (crust-like) [[lichen]] species belonging to the family Stereocaulaceae. It is one of the most common and widespread leprose [[lichens]], found growing as a powdery or granular crust on a variety of substrates including tree bark, stone walls, soil, and mosses. L. incana has gained attention as a bioindicator species because of its observed recovery in areas where industrial air pollution has declined, making it a positive indicator of improving air quality.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Lecanoromycetes
- **Order:** Lecanorales
- **Family:** Stereocaulaceae
- **Genus:** Lepraria
- **Species:** L. incana

## Morphology

L. incana forms a leprose (powdery) thallus without a clearly defined cortex or organized structural layers. The thallus appears as a granular to powdery crust, typically pale grey-green to bluish-green in color. Unlike many lichens, it does not produce distinct fruiting bodies (apothecia); instead, it propagates primarily through soredia - small bundles of [[fungal-hyphae]] wrapped around algal cells that are dispersed by wind and rain.

The granules (granular areoles) are 30-100 micrometers in diameter, and the thallus may form extensive patches several centimeters across. No prothallus (pre-thallus boundary) is usually visible. Chemical spot tests reveal the presence of atranorin and other secondary metabolites characteristic of the species.

## Ecology and Habitat

L. incana is a cosmopolitan lichen found on all continents except Antarctica. It grows on a remarkably wide range of substrates:

- Tree bark (especially on the shaded, rain-protected side of trunks)
- Mosses and other lichens (overgrowing them)
- Acidic rock surfaces and stone walls
- Soil and humus
- Concrete and other man-made substrates

The species prefers humid, shaded microhabitats and is tolerant of moderate levels of nutrient enrichment. It is commonly found in woodlands, hedgerows, churchyards, and urban environments.

## Recovery from Air Pollution and Bioindicator Role

Bates et al. (2001) recorded the revival of L. incana communities in the greater London area, attributing the recovery to the reduction of sulfur dioxide (SO2) levels following the implementation of pollution controls and the decline of heavy industry. Their study involved a 70-km transect from central London, documenting the recolonization of substrates by this crustose lichen.

The recovery of L. incana is particularly significant because:

1. **It demonstrates ecosystem recovery:** The return of pollution-sensitive organisms indicates genuine improvement in air quality.
2. **It contrasts with other species:** While L. incana recovered, [[lecanora-conizaeoides]] (which thrived in polluted conditions) actually retreated, suggesting that L. incana is outcompeted by L. conizaeoides under high-SO2 conditions.
3. **It provides a measurable indicator:** The distance from urban centers at which L. incana first appears serves as a quantitative measure of air quality improvement.

## Pollution Sensitivity

L. incana is moderately sensitive to sulfur dioxide, being less tolerant than Lecanora conizaeoides (which tolerates up to 150 mg/m3 of SO2) but more tolerant than highly sensitive species such as [[ramalina-fastigiata]] (tolerating only 10 mg/m3). Its intermediate sensitivity makes it valuable for tracking moderate changes in air quality - it disappears when pollution is severe but returns when conditions improve.

The species is also affected by nitrogen deposition, showing complex responses depending on the form and amount of nitrogen. In some contexts, moderate nitrogen enrichment may favor L. incana growth, while excessive deposition can be harmful.

## Key Facts

- Crustose leprose lichen forming powdery grey-green crusts
- Propagates through soredia rather than fruiting bodies
- Cosmopolitan distribution on bark, stone, soil, and mosses
- Demonstrated recovery in London area following SO2 reduction
- Moderate sensitivity to sulfur dioxide pollution
- Useful bioindicator of improving air quality in post-industrial landscapes
- Prefers humid, shaded microhabitats
- Contains atranorin and other characteristic secondary metabolites
- Outcompeted by Lecanora conizaeoides under high-SO2 conditions

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

L. incana is primarily relevant to environmental monitoring and ecology:

1. **Air quality biomonitoring:** Its recovery in formerly polluted areas provides a living record of air quality improvement and serves as a validation tool for pollution control policies.

2. **Succession studies:** The competitive dynamics between L. incana and pollution-tolerant species like L. conizaeoides provide insights into lichen community succession following environmental change.

3. **Urban ecology:** As a species that recolonizes urban environments when conditions improve, L. incana contributes to understanding the ecology of cities and the potential for biodiversity recovery in anthropogenic landscapes.

4. **Climate-chemistry interactions:** Changes in lichen communities, including L. incana, may reflect complex interactions between air pollution, climate change, and habitat alteration.

5. **Conservation assessment:** The presence and abundance of L. incana can be used as one metric in assessing the ecological quality of woodland and urban habitats.
