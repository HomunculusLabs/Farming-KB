## Overview

**Cyathea medullaris** (G.Forst.) Sw., commonly known as **mamaku**, black tree fern, or pīngao in some dialects, is the tallest native fern species in New Zealand. It is a iconic member of the Cyatheaceae, immediately recognizable by its thick black trunk and massive spreading frond crown.

Mamaku can reach heights of **20 meters** or more, making it one of the tallest tree fern species in the world. Its dramatic silhouette with arching fronds up to 5 meters long is a defining feature of New Zealand's temperate rainforests and a popular ornamental in subtropical gardens worldwide.

The species holds deep **cultural significance** for Māori, who have traditionally used the trunk pith as a food source and the fronds for medicinal and practical purposes. The koru (unfurled frond) of mamaku appears frequently in Māori carving and art as a symbol of new life and growth.

Mamaku is one of the fastest-growing tree ferns, capable of reaching landscape-defining heights within a few decades. Its ecological role as a pioneer and canopy species makes it fundamental to the structure and regeneration of New Zealand's native forests.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Cyatheaceae → Cyatheales → Polypodiopsida. The genus *Cyathea* is the largest genus of tree ferns with approximately 280 species worldwide. *Cyathea medullaris* was described by Georg Forster in 1786 and later transferred to *Cyathea* by Olof Swartz in 1801.

It belongs to the subgenus *Cyathea* section *Phanerophlebia*, a group characterized by dark-scaled stipes and cup-shaped indusia. The closest relatives are other Australasian species including *Cyathea cunninghamii* and the Australian *Cyathea australis*.

The trunk is erect, 10–20 m tall and 15–30 cm in diameter, covered with a distinctive **black or very dark brown** stipe base mantle. This dark coloration is the source of the common name "black tree fern" and distinguishes it from the related *Cyathea dealbata* (silver tree fern).

Fronds are bipinnate to tripinnate, 3–5 m long, forming a large spreading crown. The stipe is stout, dark brown to black, and densely covered with dark scales and small black stipule-like structures at the base.

Pinnae are 50–100 cm long, with pinnules that are narrowly oblong with serrate margins. Sori are borne on the lower surface of pinnule segments, each protected by a small cup-shaped indusium characteristic of the Cyatheaceae.

Spores are trilete, globose-tetrahedral, 55–75 μm in diameter, with a finely granulate perispore. The chromosome number is 2n = 138, a high polyploid count consistent with the ancient evolutionary lineage of the Cyatheaceae.

The dark stipe bases that persist on the trunk create a distinctive textured surface, unlike the smoother trunks of *Dicksonia* species. New fronds emerge from the crown apex as tightly coiled koru, unfurling over 2–4 weeks to reach their full impressive span.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Cyathea medullaris* is **endemic to New Zealand**, occurring throughout the North Island, South Island (mostly western and southern districts), Three Kings Islands, and the Kermadec Islands. It is absent from the driest eastern regions of the South Island.

It grows from sea level to approximately **600 m elevation** in the North Island and up to 450 m in the South Island. The species is most abundant in the wetter western and northern districts, where annual rainfall exceeds 1500 mm.

Its range extends from the subtropical **Kermadec Islands** (29°S) south to **Fiordland** (46°S), spanning a remarkable latitudinal range of over 17 degrees. This broad range reflects the species' adaptability to diverse temperature and rainfall regimes.

Preferred habitats include **lowland and montane forests**, stream margins, gullies, disturbed forest margins, and regenerating bush. It thrives in areas with high rainfall and humidity, often forming dense stands along riverbanks and in damp valleys.

Unlike many tree ferns that require deep shade, mamaku is relatively **light-demanding** and responds vigorously to canopy gaps. It frequently colonizes landslide scars, road cuttings, and other disturbed sites, acting as a pioneer species in forest succession.

It often forms pure stands in damp gullies and along stream margins, creating a distinctive tree fern forest type. These mamaku groves are important habitat for native birds such as **kererū** (New Zealand pigeon) and **tūī**, which perch in the frond crowns.

## Ecology

*Cyathea medullaris* is a **keystone species** in New Zealand's temperate rainforest ecosystems. Its massive frond crown creates a distinct subcanopy layer that modifies light availability and microclimate for understory plants.

The trunk surface and persistent stipe bases provide substrate for a rich epiphyte community. Common epiphytes include the fern *Asplenium polyodon*, various moss species, and the orchid *Drymoanthus adversus*.

The persistent dead stipe bases also trap leaf litter and organic debris, creating a moist microhabitat that supports invertebrate communities and fungal decomposers. This **detritus-trapping function** contributes to nutrient cycling within the forest ecosystem.

The trunk's fibrous root mantle supports colonies of **mycorrhizal fungi** that facilitate nutrient uptake from the poor soils typical of New Zealand forests. Fallen fronds contribute significantly to forest floor litter, decomposing relatively quickly and cycling nutrients.

The koru (young fiddleheads) are browsed by introduced mammals including **possums** (*Trichosurus vulpecula*) and **goats**, which can defoliate and kill young plants. This browsing pressure represents a significant conservation concern in some areas.

Spore production is prolific, with mature plants releasing millions of spores annually. Dispersal is primarily wind-mediated, and spores can travel considerable distances, explaining the species' ability to colonize remote islands.

Despite this prolific spore production, successful establishment requires specific microsite conditions: consistently moist substrate, adequate light, and freedom from heavy herbivore browsing. Recruitment is therefore episodic, tied to years with favorable weather patterns and low possum densities.

## Cultivation

Mamaku is widely cultivated as an ornamental tree fern in frost-free and mild climates worldwide. It requires **abundant moisture**, **high humidity**, and prefers **partial shade to full sun** once established.

Plants are surprisingly fast-growing compared to other tree ferns, capable of adding **10–15 cm** of trunk height per year under optimal conditions. This rapid growth makes it one of the most rewarding tree fern species for cultivation.

The species tolerates brief temperature drops to −3°C but suffers frost damage below this threshold. In cooler climates, it can be grown in large containers that are moved under protection during winter.

Wind exposure is a significant limitation for cultivated mamaku. The large frond crown acts as a sail, and plants in exposed positions suffer frond breakage and desiccation. Sheltered sites with overhead canopy provide the best growing conditions.

Propagation is by **spores**, which germinate readily on sterile peat at 18–22°C. Prothalli develop within 6–10 weeks, and juvenile fronds appear after 4–6 months.

Transplanting established specimens is challenging due to the sensitive root mantle but can succeed if the trunk is kept continuously moist during the recovery period. Plants respond well to regular application of balanced fertilizer during the growing season.

In the horticultural trade, mamaku is valued for its dramatic scale and rapid growth. However, its large mature size makes it suitable only for spacious landscapes, and it can dominate smaller garden settings within a decade.

## Ethnobotany and Cultural Significance

The **pith (inner trunk tissue)** of mamaku was an important traditional food source for Māori. The mucilaginous pith was extracted from felled trunks and eaten raw or roasted, providing a bland but nutritious food rich in carbohydrates.

The pith has a distinctive slimy texture when raw, described as similar to **natto or okra**. When roasted over a fire, it develops a more palatable, slightly sweet flavor. Mamaku pith was particularly valued as a survival food during times of scarcity.

Modern interest in mamaku pith has increased with the growth of the **New Zealand native foods movement**. Chefs have experimented with the mucilaginous texture in contemporary cuisine, using it as a thickening agent and flavor base in sauces and desserts.

Medicinally, Māori used the mucilaginous pith as a **poultice for wounds, boils, and skin conditions**. The gel-like substance was applied directly to soothe irritation and promote healing. Modern analysis confirms the pith contains polysaccharides with moisturizing properties.

The fronds were used practically for thatching temporary shelters and as bedding material. The strong, flexible stipes were fashioned into frameworks for containers and fishing traps.

The **koru** (unfurled frond spiral) of mamaku is a fundamental motif in Māori art, symbolizing new beginnings, growth, and the interconnectedness of all living things. This spiral form appears prominently in **whakairo** (carving), **tā moko** (tattoo), and contemporary Māori design.

Mamaku also features in **Māori whakataukī** (proverbs) and traditional narratives. The resilience of the tree fern — capable of resprouting after damage and thriving in disturbed sites — makes it a powerful metaphor for endurance and renewal in Māori cultural expression.
