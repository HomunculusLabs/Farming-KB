# Stachybotrys chartarum

Stachybotrys chartarum is a cellulolytic ascomycete fungus commonly
known as black mold or toxic black mold. It is one of the most
notorious indoor moulds, frequently associated with water-damaged
buildings and concerns about adverse health effects in building
occupants.

The species thrives in warm, damp conditions and is notably absent
from cooler climates where dry [[comparison-white-rot-fungi-vs-brown-rot-fungi-biodegradation]] like Serpula lacrymans
predominate. S. chartarum has become a focal point of indoor air
quality concerns and building biology research.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Euascomycetes (Sordariomycetes)
- **Order:** Hypocreales
- **Family:** Stachybotryaceae
- **Genus:** Stachybotrys
- **Species:** S. chartarum

The taxonomy of S. chartarum has been subject to revision, with some
authorities recognizing multiple species within what was formerly
considered a single species complex. The species was first described
in the early 19th century and has since become one of the most studied
indoor mould fungi.

## Ecology and Habitat

S. chartarum is a warmth-loving mould requiring sustained moisture for
growth. Unlike dry rot fungi that transport water through mycelial
structures, S. chartarum obtains water directly from damp
surroundings, requiring water-damaged conditions with high water
activity.

The species prefers temperatures above 20 degrees C for vigorous
growth, explaining its relative rarity in cooler climates like
Britain. It grows primarily on cellulose-rich materials including
wallpaper, ceiling tiles, cardboard, and gypsum board paper facing.
The high cellulose content provides the primary carbon source for
growth and metabolism.

### Contrast with Dry Rot Fungi

Indoor moulds like S. chartarum are opportunistic colonizers of wet
materials that cannot function in dry conditions. Dry rot fungi like
Meruliporia incrassata and Serpula lacrymans actively transport water
through specialized mycelial structures and can cause decay in
relatively dry timber by conducting water from moisture sources.

The prevalence of dry rot and absence of S. chartarum in Britain
reflects these temperature preferences. S. chartarum is more common in
warmer regions such as California, where building conditions more
frequently support its growth and establishment.

## Mycotoxin Production

S. chartarum produces mycotoxins including satratoxins (macrocyclic
trichothecenes), stachylysin (a hemolytic protein), and other
[[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]]. Notably, not all strains produce mycotoxins,
and the health significance of exposure remains debated in the
scientific literature.

The potential for mycotoxin production has nevertheless made S.
chartarum a focus of indoor [[cervantes-air-quality-ventilation]] concerns, though the presence
of the fungus does not necessarily indicate mycotoxin production or
exposure risks to building occupants.

## Building Damage

S. chartarum contributes to [[fungal-building-material-deterioration]] degradation through
cellulolytic degradation of paper-based materials, discoloration and
staining of surfaces (dark greenish-black patches), and production of
unpleasant musty odors from microbial [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]] compounds
(MVOCs).
