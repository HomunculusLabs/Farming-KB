# Althaea officinalis

## Overview

Marshmallow (*Althaea officinalis*) is a herbaceous perennial plant in the family
Malvaceae, native to Europe, western Asia, and North Africa. Revered since
antiquity as a medicinal herb, its name derives from the Greek *altho* ("to
heal"), reflecting its extensive use in traditional medicine spanning Greek,
Roman, Egyptian, and medieval European practices. The plant is best known for its
abundant mucilage content, concentrated primarily in the root, which provides
soothing, protective, and anti-inflammatory effects on mucous membranes. The
original confection known as "marshmallow" was historically made from the plant's
gelatinous root sap combined with sugar and egg whites. In contemporary herbalism,
marshmallow root is a foundational demulcent remedy for respiratory, digestive,
and urinary tract conditions, while the leaves and flowers offer milder mucilaginous
properties. The plant also serves ecological functions in permaculture as a
pollinator attractor, dynamic accumulator, and edible landscape species.

## Taxonomy and Identification

- **Family:** Malvaceae (mallow family)
- **Genus:** *Althaea*
- **Common names:** Marshmallow, marsh mallow, white mallow, sweet weed,
  Mortification root, hock herb, wymote
- **Life cycle:** Hardy perennial; zones 3–9
- **Height:** 3–5 feet (90–150 cm) at maturity
- **Stem:** Erect, stout, slightly woolly with stellate hairs; branched in
  upper portions
- **Leaves:** Alternate, cordate (heart-shaped) to ovate, 3–5 lobed, softly
  pubescent on both surfaces, 2–4 inches long with crenate-serrate margins
- **Flowers:** Pale pink to white, 1.5–2 inches across, with five petals;
  borne in axillary clusters; blooming July–September; attractive to bees and
  butterflies
- **Root:** Thick, fleshy, tapering taproot up to 30 cm long; white and
  fibrous internally when fresh, yellowish when dried; high mucilage content
- **Fruit:** Circular schizocarp of 15–20 mericarps arranged in a ring

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Native range:** Europe (especially coastal and marshland areas), western
  Asia, North Africa
- **Naturalized range:** Eastern North America, parts of Australia and New
  Zealand
- **Habitat:** Marshes, damp meadows, riverbanks, tidal flats, roadside
  ditches; tolerates both wet and moderately dry conditions once established
- **Soil preference:** Moist, well-draining loam; tolerant of saline and
  clay soils; prefers full sun to partial shade
- **Invasiveness:** Not considered invasive; spreads slowly by seed and
  short rhizomes

## Active Constituents

- **Mucilage:** 20–35% in root (galacturonorhamnan, arabinogalactan, glucuronoxylan);
  5–10% in leaves; forms a gelatinous protective layer on mucous membranes
- **Flavonoids:** Hypolaetin-8-glucoside, isoquercitrin, kaempferol — antioxidant
  and anti-inflammatory
- **Phenolic acids:** Caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid, ferulic acid — anti-inflammatory
  and antimicrobial
- **Scopoletin:** Coumarin derivative with anti-inflammatory properties
- **Pectin:** Water-soluble polysaccharide contributing to soothing effects
- **Starch and sugars:** Nutritional value and additional demulcent action
- **Asparagine:** Free amino acid in root; mild diuretic; low tannin concentrations
  provide mild astringency

## Medicinal Uses

- **Respiratory:** Root cold maceration for dry coughs, bronchitis, sore throat,
  laryngitis; mucilage coats inflamed airway tissue, reducing irritation
- **Digestive:** Root tea for gastritis, peptic ulcers, acid reflux, IBD;
  protective barrier on gastric and intestinal mucosa; mild bulk laxative
- **Urinary:** Soothing for cystitis, urethritis, kidney stone irritation; mucilage
  coats urinary tract to reduce pain during urination
- **Skin:** Topical poultice for burns, wounds, insect bites, abscesses;
  anti-inflammatory and wound-healing properties
- **Oral:** Gargle for mouth ulcers, gingivitis, sore gums; traditional
  toothache remedy; some evidence of immunomodulatory activity via polysaccharides

## Preparation and Edible Uses

- **Cold maceration:** Preferred method for mucilage; soak 1–2 tsp dried root
  in cold water 4–8 hours (heat degrades polysaccharides); strain and drink
- **Decoction:** Simmer root gently 10–15 minutes; less mucilage than cold
  maceration but more convenient
- **Powder/glycerite:** Dried root ground for encapsulation or poultice; use
  glycerite (not alcohol tincture) to preserve mucilaginous properties
- **Edible parts:** Root boiled as vegetable (origin of marshmallow candy);
  young leaves raw in salads or cooked; flowers as garnish; seeds sprouted or
  ground; all parts become mucilaginous when wet, useful for thickening soups

## Permaculture and Cultivation

- **Propagation:** Seed (stratify 4–6 weeks at 40°F) or root division in
  spring or autumn; seeds germinate in 2–3 weeks
- **Spacing:** 18–24 inches apart; plants form substantial clumps over 2–3
  years
- **Companion planting:** Attracts bees, butterflies, and beneficial insects;
  good companion for fruit trees and vegetable gardens
- **Soil building:** Deep taproot brings minerals from subsoil; mulch with
  leaves to return nutrients to surface
- **Ornamental value:** Attractive cottage garden plant with soft foliage and
  delicate flowers; suitable for rain gardens and boggy areas
- **Harvest:** Root harvested in autumn of second or third year from
  two-year-old or older plants; leaves harvested throughout growing season
