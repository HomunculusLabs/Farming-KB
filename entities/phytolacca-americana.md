## Overview

**Phytolacca americana**, commonly known as pokeweed or American pokeweed, is a large perennial herbaceous plant in the family Phytolaccaceae.

It is native to eastern North America and has become widely naturalized in temperate regions worldwide, including Europe and Asia.

Pokeweed is notable for its striking magenta stems, dark purple berries, and the extreme toxicity of its roots and mature berries.

Despite its toxicity, the species has a long history of medicinal use and its young shoots were traditionally consumed as a spring vegetable in the American South.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Magnoliopsida → Caryophyllales → Phytolaccaceae → Phytolacca → P. americana.

The species was described by Carl Linnaeus in *Species Plantarum* (1753). The genus name *Phytolacca* derives from Greek *phyton* (plant) and *lacca* (lacquer), referring to the red berry dye.

Pokeweed is a robust perennial herb growing 1.5–3 meters tall from a large, fleshy, parsnip-like taproot.

The root can exceed 15 cm in diameter and weighs several kilograms in established plants.

Stems are erect, smooth, reddish-purple to magenta, prominently ridged, and often branched in the upper portion.

Leaves are alternate, simple, ovate to lanceolate, 10–30 cm long and 5–15 cm wide, with entire margins and conspicuous pinnate venation.

Flowers are arranged in erect racemes 10–20 cm long, composed of numerous small pedicellate flowers.

Individual flowers have 5 greenish-white to pink sepals (no true petals), 10 stamens, and a superior ovary of 8–10 carpels.

Flowering occurs from May through October, with plants often bearing flowers and fruit simultaneously.

The fruit is a 10-lobed purple-black berry, approximately 8–10 mm in diameter, containing 8–10 black seeds.

Berries are produced in drooping clusters and contain a deep magenta juice that serves as a natural dye.

Each seed is lens-shaped, approximately 2–3 mm in diameter, with a glossy black testa.

## Distribution and Habitat

Phytolacca americana is native to eastern North America, from New England and Ontario south to Florida and west to Texas and Minnesota.

It has been widely introduced and naturalized in Europe, Asia, North Africa, and parts of South America.

The species colonizes a broad range of disturbed habitats including forest clearings, old fields, fence rows, roadsides, and waste ground.

It thrives in rich, moist soils with high organic content and tolerates both full sun and partial shade.

Pokeweed is a pioneer species that readily establishes in disturbed sites and can become aggressive in garden and agricultural settings.

It is commonly found at woodland edges, streambanks, and in riparian corridors where soil moisture is adequate.

The deep taproot allows established plants to survive drought periods and makes manual removal challenging.

Pokeweed is considered a noxious or invasive weed in several jurisdictions outside its native range.

## Toxicity and Chemistry

All parts of Phytolacca americana contain toxic triterpene saponins, with the roots and seeds containing the highest concentrations.

The primary toxic compounds are phytolaccigenin and phytolaccatoxin, triterpenoid glycosides that act as potent mitogens.

Pokeweed mitogen (PWM) is a lectin protein isolated from the roots that acts as a potent T-cell and B-cell activator.

PWM is widely used as a laboratory reagent in immunology research to stimulate lymphocyte proliferation.

Ingestion of mature berries, roots, or other plant parts causes severe gastrointestinal distress including nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and diarrhea.

Severe poisoning can lead to hypotension, bradycardia, convulsions, and in extreme cases, respiratory paralysis and death.

The juice of ripe berries is less toxic than the roots and seeds but can still cause significant illness if consumed in quantity.

Children are most commonly affected due to the attractive appearance of the dark berries.

Cooking does not reliably destroy the triterpenoid saponins, though the traditional practice of boiling young shoots with multiple water changes reduces toxicity.

The red berry juice has historically been used as an ink and fabric dye; a single berry yields enough pigment to stain fabric permanently.

Contact with the sap can cause dermatitis in sensitive individuals, particularly on sun-exposed skin.

## Traditional Uses and Pharmacology

Pokeweed has an extensive history of medicinal use among both Indigenous peoples of North America and European settlers.

The Cherokee used a root poultice for breast inflammation and a root decoction as a topical treatment for rheumatism.

The Delaware applied crushed berries to boils and used a root tea to treat hemorrhoids.

The Iroquois employed the plant as a purgative and treated skin conditions with root preparations.

The Powhatan used pokeweed as a ceremonial emetic and for the treatment of worms.

European herbalists adopted pokeweed in the 19th century as an alterative, anti-inflammatory, and lymphatic decongestant.

The dried root was an official drug in the United States Pharmacopeia from 1820 to 1916 under the name "Radix Phytolaccae."

Eclectic physicians prescribed pokeweed for tonsillitis, mastitis, enlarged lymph nodes, and rheumatic complaints.

Modern pharmacological studies have confirmed anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and antiproliferative activities of pokeweed extracts.

The ribosome-inactivating protein pokeweed antiviral protein (PAP) from the leaves demonstrates antiviral activity against HIV, herpes, and influenza in vitro.

Research interest continues in PAP as a potential immunotoxin conjugate for targeted cancer therapy.

Internal use of pokeweed is strongly discouraged in modern herbal practice due to the narrow therapeutic index and severity of toxicity.

## Ecological Role

Despite its toxicity, pokeweed plays an important ecological role in eastern North American ecosystems.

The berries are an important late-season food source for numerous bird species, including mockingbirds, catbirds, thrushes, and waxwings.

Birds are largely unaffected by the triterpenoid toxins and disperse the seeds widely through their droppings.

The deep taproot of pokeweed helps break up compacted soils and contributes to soil aeration in disturbed sites.

The large, robust stems provide structural support for climbing vines in successional plant communities.

Pokeweed flowers attract bees, flies, and beetles, contributing to pollinator diversity in disturbed habitats.

The plant serves as a host for several specialist insect herbivores, including the pokeweed borer moth (*Megacyllene decora*).
