## Overview

*Stipa tenuissima*, commonly known as Mexican feather grass or fine needle grass, is a fine-textured ornamental [[savory-perennial-grass-and-desertification]] in the family Poaceae.

It is native to open grasslands and rocky slopes in the southwestern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], Mexico, and parts of [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]].

The species is widely cultivated for its delicate, hair-like inflorescences that sway gracefully in the slightest breeze.

Despite its ornamental appeal, it has become an environmental weed in parts of Australia, New Zealand, and California.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Stipa tenuissima* Trin. & Rupr. is placed in the tribe Stipeae within the subfamily Pooideae of Poaceae.

Some authorities now classify it within *Nassella*, referring to it as *Nassella tenuissima* (Trin.) Barkworth.

Plants form dense, finely textured mounds 40 to 60 cm tall in foliage, with flowering stems reaching 60 to 90 cm.

Leaves are extremely narrow, thread-like, and 40 to 60 cm long, with a diameter of less than 1 mm.

The inflorescence is an open, feathery panicle 15 to 25 cm long, appearing from late spring through summer.

Awns are exceptionally long and slender, 8 to 12 cm, giving the flower heads a silky, flowing appearance.

The whole plant has a luminous, golden-green quality in summer, bleaching to a straw color in autumn.

Roots are dense and fibrous, forming a compact crown without aggressive rhizomatous spread.

## Distribution and Habitat

The native range extends from southern Texas and New Mexico through Mexico to Guatemala, and south into Argentina and Chile.

It typically occurs in dry, open grasslands, rocky hillsides, and disturbed sites at elevations from 200 to 2,400 meters.

The species is well-adapted to seasonally arid conditions and poor, well-drained soils.

It has been widely introduced outside its native range as an ornamental, particularly in Mediterranean and warm-temperate climates.

Naturalized populations are established in Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, and coastal California.

In Australia, it is classified as a Weed of National Significance due to its impact on native [[biodiversity-fungi-grassland-ecosystems]].

It invades roadsides, pastures, and conservation reserves, displacing native understory species.

## Cultivation

Mexican feather grass thrives in USDA hardiness zones 6 through 10, preferring warm, dry conditions.

It performs best in full sun with very well-drained soil, tolerating sandy, gravelly, and rocky substrates.

Once established, plants are extremely drought-tolerant and require little to no supplemental irrigation.

Overwatering or planting in heavy, moisture-retentive soils leads to [[cannabis-root-rot-prevention-and-treatment]] and rapid decline.

Grooming involves combing out dead foliage with a rake or cutting back to near ground level in late winter.

Plants self-seed freely in favorable conditions, which can lead to unwanted spread in gardens and adjacent natural areas.

[[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]] issues are rare, making it a low-maintenance choice for xeriscaping and gravel gardens.

In regions where the species is invasive, responsible cultivation includes deadheading before [[clay-pellet-seed-dispersal]].

## Ecology and Invasive Potential

In its native range, Mexican feather grass provides forage for livestock and wildlife during the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].

The fine, hair-like awns can cause mechanical injury to grazing animals, particularly in the eyes and mouth of livestock.

Invasive populations in Australia form dense mats that suppress native wildflower diversity in grassland ecosystems.

The species is highly flammable when dry, increasing fire risk in invaded grassland and chaparral communities.
