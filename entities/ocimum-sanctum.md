## Etymology and Common Names
- **Sanctum** (Latin): "sacred," reflecting the plant's central role in Hindu religious practice.
- **Tenuiflorum** (Latin): "slender-flowered," an earlier and widely used synonym.
- **Tulsi** (Sanskrit तुलसी): derived from *tulasā*, meaning "the incomparable one."
- **Holy Basil**: English common name; references its sacred status in Indian culture.
- **Sacred Basil**: alternate English name, synonymous with Holy Basil.
- **Krueng** (Acehnese): used in northern Sumatra and parts of Indonesia.
- **Thai Holy Basil** (กะเพรา, *kaphrao*): used in Thai cuisine; often treated as O. tenuiflorum var.
- **Tulasi** (Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam): Dravidian-language variants of Tulsi.
- **Rehan** (Arabic): used in Unani (Greco-Arabic) medicine traditions.
The name "Tulsi" is shared across most South Asian languages, underscoring the plant's pan-regional cultural importance from Sri Lanka to Bangladesh.

## Morphology
Ocimum sanctum is an erect, many-branched, aromatic **annual or short-lived perennial subshrub** growing 30–100 cm in height under cultivation, occasionally reaching 150 cm in ideal tropical conditions.
| Plant Part | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Stem | Square (quadrangular), pubescent, green to purplish-green |
| Leaves | Simple, opposite, ovate, 2–5 cm long, serrate margin, gland-dotted |
| Leaf surface | Pubescent on both sides; strong aromatic scent when crushed |
| Leaf venation | Pinnate, prominent midrib, reticulate secondary veins |
| Inflorescence | Verticillaste (whorled) racemes, 10–20 cm long |
| Flowers | Bilabiate, zygomorphic, calyx 5-toothed, corolla 2-lipped |
| Flower color | White to pale purple; stamens 4, didynamous |
| Fruit | Nutlet (schizocarp), ellipsoid, ~1.5 mm, black at maturity |
| Roots | Taproot system with fibrous lateral branches; moderately deep |
Two major **cultivars** are widely recognized:
1. **Krishna Tulsi** (purple-leaved): darker pigmentation, higher eugenol content.
2. **Rama Tulsi** (green-leaved): lighter foliage, milder flavor, more common.

Ocimum sanctum is native to the Indian subcontinent and has been deliberately spread across the tropics and subtropics through trade, migration, and religious practice over at least 3,000 years. It is now cultivated commercially in India, Thailand, Madagascar, and parts of Africa for essential oil and dried leaf markets.

## Traditional Use in Ayurveda
In classical Ayurveda, Tulsi is classified as a **Rasayana** (rejuvenative tonic) and is one of the most important medicinal plants in the pharmacopoeia.
| System | Traditional Application |
|--------|------------------------|
| Nervous system | Medhya rasayana (nootropic); reduces stress, anxiety, and mental fatigue |
| Respiratory system | Treats cough, cold, bronchitis, asthma; expectorant and bronchodilator |
| Digestive system | Stimulates appetite, relieves bloating, mild carminative |
| Immune system | Enhances host defense (vyādhi kṣamatva); used during seasonal illness |
| Skin | Applied topically for insect bites, fungal infections, and wound healing |
| Cardiovascular | Supports healthy blood pressure; mild hypotensive in traditional texts |
| Metabolic | Used in managing blood sugar; referenced in diabetes management formulas |
Classical formulations containing Tulsi include **Tulasi Kvatha** (decoction), **Tulasi Ghrita** (medicated ghee), and **Panch Tulasi Ras** (a polyherbal preparation combining five Ocimum varieties). The dried leaves are consumed as a daily tea across Indian households, and fresh leaves are placed in food and water offerings in Hindu temples.

## Phytochemistry
The essential oil (0.5–1.9% dry weight) and leaf extracts contain a complex mixture of bioactive compounds:
| Compound | Class | Approximate Content | Primary Activity |
|----------|-------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Eugenol | Phenylpropanoid | 40–70% of essential oil | Antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant |
| Ursolic acid | Pentacyclic triterpene | 0.5–2.5% dry leaf | Anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, anticancer |
| Rosmarinic acid | Phenolic acid | 0.3–1.2% dry leaf | Antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiviral |
| Oleanolic acid | Pentacyclic triterpene | 0.3–1.0% dry leaf | Hepatoprotective, cardioprotective, anti-inflammatory |
| Linalool | Monoterpene alcohol | 2–8% of essential oil | Anxiolytic, sedative, antimicrobial |
| β-Caryophyllene | Sesquiterpene | 5–15% of essential oil | Anti-inflammatory (CB2 agonist), analgesic |
| Apigenin | Flavone | Trace–0.1% dry leaf | Anxiolytic, anticancer, antioxidant |
| Luteolin | Flavone | Trace–0.1% dry leaf | Anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective |
| Methyl eugenol | Phenylpropanoid | Variable | Aromatic; regulatory limits in some markets |
| Vitamin C | Ascorbic acid | 20–30 mg/100g fresh leaf | Antioxidant, immune support |
Eugenol content is significantly higher in Krishna Tulsi compared to Rama Tulsi, contributing to its stronger clove-like aroma and more potent antimicrobial activity.

## Pharmacology
Modern research has validated many traditional claims through in vitro, in vivo, and limited clinical studies:
- **Adaptogenic / Anti-stress**: Normalizes cortisol levels in rodent stress models; reduces oxidative stress markers (MDA, SOD, CAT) in chronic stress paradigms.
- **Anti-inflammatory**: Inhibits NF-κB, COX-2, and TNF-α pathways; ursolic acid is the primary triterpenoid responsible.
- **Antimicrobial**: Essential oil shows broad-spectrum activity against *S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, C. albicans*; MIC values typically range 50–500 µg/mL.
- **Antioxidant**: DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging (IC₅₀ 15–45 µg/mL for leaf extract); comparable to ascorbic acid at equivalent concentrations.
- **Cardioprotective**: Reduces serum cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL in hyperlipidemic animal models; mild antihypertensive effect via vasodilation.
- **Hypoglycemic**: Lowers fasting blood glucose by 15–30% in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats; enhances peripheral glucose uptake.
- **Hepatoprotective**: Reduces liver enzyme elevation (ALT, AST) in toxin-induced hepatotoxicity models; ursolic and oleanolic acids are primary agents.
- **Neuroprotective**: Reduces cognitive deficits in scopolamine-induced amnesia models; anti-cholinesterase activity supports memory function.
- **Radioprotective**: Reduces radiation-induced chromosomal damage and lipid peroxidation; investigated as an adjunct in radiotherapy.
Clinical trials remain limited in sample size and rigor. A 2017 meta-analysis noted moderate evidence for anxiolytic and stress-reduction effects, with weaker evidence for glycemic control. Larger RCTs are needed.

## Cultivation
| Parameter | Requirement |
|-----------|------------|
| Hardiness | USDA Zones 10–12; grown as annual elsewhere |
| Temperature | Optimal 20–35 °C; sensitive to frost (dies below 5 °C) |
| Rainfall | 500–2500 mm annually; drought-tolerant once established |
| Soil | Well-drained loamy soil; pH 6.0–7.5; tolerates poor soils |
| Light | Full sun (6–8+ hours); tolerates partial shade with reduced vigor |
| Propagation | Primarily by seed; softwood cuttings root in 7–14 days |
| Germination | Seeds germinate in 7–14 days at 20–25 °C; light aids germination |
| Spacing | 20–30 cm between plants; 40–50 cm between rows |
| Growth cycle | Harvestable in 60–75 days from sowing; flowers in 90–120 days |
| Yield | Fresh leaf: 10–20 tonnes/hectare; essential oil: 10–25 kg/ha |
| Pests | Generally pest-resistant; occasional aphids, whitefly, or leaf miners |
Tulsi is one of the easiest medicinal herbs to cultivate. It self-seeds readily in tropical climates and can become weedy in disturbed areas. For essential oil production, flowering tops are harvested at full bloom and steam-distilled within 24 hours of cutting for maximum eugenol retention.
