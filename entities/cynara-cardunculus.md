# Cynara cardunculus

## Overview

Cynara cardunculus, commonly known as the cardoon, is a perennial thistle-like plant in the family Asteraceae. It is native to the Mediterranean Basin and has been cultivated for centuries for its edible leaf stalks, flower buds, and seeds.

The species is the wild ancestor of the globe artichoke (Cynara scolymus) and is closely related to cultivated cardoon varieties grown as vegetables. Cardoon has also gained modern attention as a biomass crop for bioenergy production.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Cynara cardunculus L. belongs to the tribe Cynareae within the Asteraceae family. The species comprises three botanical varieties: C. cardunculus var. cardunculus (wild cardoon), C. cardunculus var. scolymus (globe artichoke), and C. cardunculus var. altilis (cultivated cardoon).

Cardoon is a robust perennial herb reaching 1–1.5 meters in height. It forms a large rosette of deeply lobed, silvery-grey leaves that are 50–80 cm long and covered in a dense white tomentum.

The stems are thick, fleshy, and ribbed, typically harvested when blanched for culinary use. Flower heads are large (5–10 cm diameter), globe-shaped, and composed of purple-blue florets surrounded by spiny bracts.

## Ecological Role

Cardoon flowers are highly attractive to pollinators, particularly bees, butterflies, and hoverflies. The large nectar-rich florets provide an important late-season food source in [[fungal-diversity-mediterranean-ecosystems]].

The deep root system improves soil structure and can help prevent erosion on slopes. As a member of the Asteraceae, cardoon contributes to plant biodiversity in semi-arid grassland communities.
