# Coprosma repens

## Description

Coprosma repens, commonly known as taupata or mirror plant, is an evergreen shrub or small tree native to coastal New Zealand. It is recognised in permaculture as an exposure-tolerant, frontline coastal species used in windbreak configurations. Its thick, glossy leaves resist salt burn and wind abrasion, making it invaluable for coastal shelterbelt design. Mollison specifically includes it as a shrub-layer component in compound high-density coastal windbreak systems.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Rubiaceae
- **Genus**: Coprosma
- **Species**: C. repens
- **Common names**: Taupata, mirror plant, looking-glass bush
- **Growth form**: Evergreen shrub to small tree, 2-8 m tall

## Key Facts

- Native to the coastal cliffs and dunes of New Zealand's North and South Islands.
- Grows as a dense, spreading shrub 2-8 m tall with thick, leathery, oval leaves that reflect salt spray and resist desiccation.
- Produces small orange-red drupes attractive to birds, supporting coastal biodiversity corridors and providing food for native wildlife.
- Rated as **exposure tolerant** in permaculture windbreak classification: yields are reduced in strong winds but dry matter yield is less affected than in wind-sensitive plants.
- Functions as a **low shrub layer** in compound windbreak systems (Configuration C), planted alongside ground-covers like Mesembryanthemum and trees like Cupressus and Lycium.
- Root systems stabilise coastal dunes and sandy soils, reducing wind erosion on exposed shorelines and preventing sand blow onto productive land.
- Tolerates salt-laden winds, poor sandy soils, and periods of drought, making it ideal for frontline sea-coast plantings where few other species survive.
- Can be propagated from seed or semi-hardwood cuttings; establishes rapidly in coastal conditions and reaches effective windbreak height within 2-3 years.
- Provides habitat for beneficial insects and birds that contribute to integrated pest management in nearby crop areas.
- Dioecious species: both male and female plants needed for fruit production; male flowers are in clusters, female flowers solitary or in pairs.

## Role in Windbreak Design

Mollison places Coprosma repens in the shrub layer of **compound, high-density windbreaks** designed for eroding beaches and exposed coastlines. These systems:

- Lift wind smoothly over the beach berm and trap sand effectively.
- Are estimated to be 50% cheaper than stout artificial fences and far more durable.
- The shrub layer sits between ground-covers (Convolvulus, Phyla, Mesembryanthemum) and taller tree species (Lycium, Cedrus, Cupressus), creating a dense, multi-tiered barrier.
- Configuration C windbreaks are also effective in dust-storm areas as dust traps, settling out 50-70% of dusts within 100 m of tree clumps.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Coastal permaculture design**: Essential for establishing productive growing zones in maritime climates where salt burn and wind damage limit conventional agriculture.
- **Microclimate creation**: The shelter provided by Coprosma-based windbreaks reduces evapotranspiration by up to 50% in strong winds, raises soil moisture by up to 4% within four times the height of the break, and extends the effective growing season for sensitive crops.
- **Mycological interest**: The dense, humid microclimate created beneath and leeward of Coprosma hedges may support wood-decomposer and mycorrhizal fungal communities. Leaf litter and twig fall contribute organic matter to sandy coastal soils, building humus layers that favour saprophytic fungi.
- **Agroforestry integration**: Can be interplanted with nitrogen-fixing species (Acacia, Leucaena) to create multi-functional coastal hedgerows producing fodder, mulch, and wildlife habitat simultaneously.
- **Soil food web**: The accumulation of organic matter beneath Coprosma stands gradually builds fungal-dominant soil food webs in otherwise depauperate sandy coastal soils.

## Cultivation Notes

- Plant in full sun to partial shade; tolerates exposure but grows denser with some protection when young.
- Hardy to approximately -5 degrees C; best suited to USDA Zones 9-11 and coastal maritime climates.
- Prefers well-drained sandy or loamy soils but tolerates poor, salty substrates and exposed coastal conditions.
- Responds well to pruning and can be shaped into formal hedgerows or allowed to grow naturally as an informal screen.
- Dioecious: plant both male and female specimens for fruit production and maximum biodiversity benefit.
- Water requirements are low once established; occasional deep watering during extended dry periods improves foliage density.
- Feed with balanced fertiliser or compost in spring for maximum growth rate in ornamental or hedgerow applications.
