---# Silicon

## Overview
Silicon is the chemical element with symbol Si and atomic number 14.
It is a hard, brittle, blue-gray crystalline solid in its elemental form.
Chemically it belongs to group 14 of the [[crystal-structure-and-crystal-defects]]: diamond cubic.
- Electronic class: semiconductor.
- Band-gap character: indirect gap.
- Wiki relevance: baseline material for semiconductor band-structure comparisons.

## Natural Occurrence
Silicon rarely occurs as pure elemental silicon in nature.
It has a strong chemical affinity for oxygen.
Most natural silicon is bound in silica and silicate minerals.
Silicon dioxide appears as quartz, sand, and many rock-forming phases.
Silicate minerals make up a very large fraction of Earth's crust.
By mass, silicon is commonly described as the second most abundant element in the crust after oxygen.
This abundance helps explain why silicon-based glass, ceramics, cement, and stone have ancient histories.
Electronic-grade silicon, however, requires purification far beyond ordinary mineral use.

## Discovery and Naming
Human use of silicon compounds is prehistoric because stones, clays, and silica sands are everywhere.
Glass containing silica was made in [[mckenna-club-des-hachischins-and-nineteenth-century-literary-hashish]].
Jons Jakob Berzelius is credited with preparing and characterizing relatively pure silicon in 1823.
The English name silicon reflects its relation to silex, the Latin root for flint.
The naming history mirrors a scientific transition from mineral craft to chemical element theory.

## Physical and Chemical Identity
Crystalline silicon uses a diamond cubic lattice.
Each silicon atom is tetrahedrally bonded to four neighboring silicon atoms.
This covalent network makes the material hard and brittle rather than ductile like a metal.
At room temperature pure silicon conducts poorly compared with metals.
Its conductivity rises strongly with temperature and with intentional doping.
Silicon's native oxide, silicon dioxide, is one of its most important technological partners.
A stable, high-quality oxide can electrically insulate and chemically protect the surface.
This oxide compatibility is a major reason silicon outcompeted many early semiconductor alternatives.

## Semiconductor Behavior
Silicon is an indirect band-gap semiconductor.
The valence-band maximum and conduction-band minimum occur at different crystal momenta.
An optical transition across the gap usually needs a phonon to conserve momentum.
As a result, ordinary silicon is inefficient at emitting light.
The same band structure is still excellent for electronic switching, rectification, and sensing.
Doping with donor atoms produces n-type silicon with extra mobile electrons.
Doping with acceptor atoms produces p-type silicon with mobile holes.
P-n junctions, MOS capacitors, and MOSFET channels all rely on this controllable carrier population.

## Device Importance
Silicon is the base material of most integrated circuits.
It supports complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor technology, usually abbreviated CMOS.
CMOS logic enables dense, low-power digital circuits.
Silicon wafers can be manufactured at large diameters with extreme crystalline perfection.
Photolithography, ion implantation, oxidation, deposition, and etching are highly optimized for silicon.
The material is also central to power electronics, MEMS devices, photodiodes, and image sensors.
In photovoltaics, crystalline silicon remains the dominant solar-cell material by deployed capacity.
The same infrastructure that makes silicon cheap also makes incremental improvements economically powerful.

## Silicon Dioxide and Process Advantage
Silicon's native oxide is a defining advantage.
Silicon dioxide can be grown thermally with good interface quality.
It acts as a gate dielectric, passivation layer, field oxide, and masking material.
