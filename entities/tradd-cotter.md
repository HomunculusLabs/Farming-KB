---# Tradd Cotter

## Overview

Tradd Cotter is an American mycologist, mushroom cultivator, author, and educator.
He is best known as the author of *[[synthetic-log-cultivation-methods-guide]] with experimental ecological applications.
Cotter is closely associated with Mushroom Mountain in South Carolina.
Mushroom Mountain functions as a farm, laboratory, training site, and spawn operation.
His public work emphasizes low-cost, accessible fungal techniques.
The emphasis makes his material especially relevant to small farms, homesteads, schools, and community projects.
Within this wiki, Cotter appears in pages on laboratory setup, classroom demos, mushroom honey, mushroom beer, and low-tech spawn.
Those references show him as a practical bridge between gourmet cultivation and applied mycology.

## Key details

- Primary field: applied mycology.
- Main roles: author, teacher, researcher, cultivator, and entrepreneur.
- Known organization: Mushroom Mountain.
- Known book: *Organic Mushroom Farming and Mycoremediation*.
- Publisher: Chelsea Green Publishing.
- Publication year: 2014.
- Book format: practical manual with photographs and experiments.
- Main audience: mushroom growers from beginner through advanced levels.
- Agricultural focus: organic mushroom production.
- Ecological focus: mycoremediation and waste conversion.
- Educational focus: demonstrations that make fungal biology visible.
- Practical style: frugal, experimental, and field-tested.

## Organic mushroom farming

Cotter's cultivation writing covers both indoor and [[mycoremediation-basics]] and [[mushroom-classroom-education-demos]] describes a grade-level framework attributed to him.
The activities teach fungal ecology, recycling, cultivation, and mycoremediation.
They can connect biology, environmental science, chemistry, and food systems.
Mushrooms are unusually effective for education because their growth is visible over days to weeks.
Students can observe colonization, contamination, fruiting, decomposition, and spore dispersal.
Cotter's approach makes mycology less abstract and more participatory.
It also supports citizen science and local experimentation.
This educational role is a major reason his work travels beyond commercial mushroom farms.
