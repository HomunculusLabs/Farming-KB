## Overview

Alnus serrulata, commonly known as hazel alder or smooth alder, is a deciduous shrub or small tree native to eastern North America.

It is a actinorhizal plant capable of fixing atmospheric nitrogen through symbiosis with Frankia bacteria.

The species is ecologically important in riparian and wetland habitats throughout its range.

Hazel alder is used in streambank restoration, erosion control, and riparian buffer plantings.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae, order Fagales, family Betulaceae, genus Alnus, subgenus Clethropsis.

Alnus serrulata was described by Carl Ludwig von Willdenow in 1805.

The species was originally named Alnus serrulata by William Aiton in 1789.

It is diploid with 2n = 28 chromosomes.

**Growth form** is a multi-stemmed deciduous shrub reaching 3-8 meters in height.

The plant occasionally develops a single trunk and can assume a small tree form.

Trunk diameter rarely exceeds 20 cm at maturity.

Bark is thin, smooth, dark gray to brown, developing shallow fissures on older stems.

Twigs are slender, reddish-brown, with scattered white lenticels and a slightly zigzag form.

Leaves are simple, alternate, ovate to elliptic, 4-11 cm long and 3-7 cm wide.

Leaf margins are finely serrate (double-toothed) with acuminate tips.

The upper leaf surface is dark green and glabrous; the lower surface is pale green.

Petioles are slender, 5-15 mm long, often slightly winged near the base.

Male catkins are pendulous, cylindrical, 5-10 cm long, formed in the previous growing season.

Female catkins are small, erect, cone-like structures 8-15 mm long, borne in clusters of 2-5.

Fruit is a small, woody, cone-like strobile 10-18 mm long containing numerous winged nutlets.

## Distribution and Habitat

Alnus serrulata ranges from southern Maine westward to southern Indiana and Missouri.

It extends southward through the Atlantic coastal plain to eastern Texas and northern Florida.

The species is absent from most of the Appalachian Mountains above 600 meters elevation.

It is found from sea level to approximately 600 meters in the Piedmont and coastal plain.

Hazel alder is strongly associated with riparian corridors, streambanks, and swamp margins.

It commonly grows along the edges of ponds, marshes, and other freshwater wetlands.

The species frequently colonizes disturbed sites including road cuts, old fields, and gravel bars.

Common associates include [[salix-alba]], [[betula-pendula]], and [[quercus-robur]].

Other companion species include [[vaccinium-corymbosum-entity|Vaccinium corymbosum]], [[kalmia-latifolia]], and [[rhododendron-maximum]].

It prefers moist to wet soils with pH 5.0-7.0 and tolerates seasonal inundation.

## Ecology and Uses

Alnus serrulata forms root nodules containing the nitrogen-fixing actinomycete Frankia.

This symbiosis enables the species to enrich nitrogen-poor soils, benefiting neighboring plants.
