## Description

Connate salt is salt that was trapped in sedimentary rock formations during their original deposition, typically from ancient marine or lacustrine (lake) environments. The term "connate" means "born with" — these salts have been present in the rock since its formation, often for millions of years. When groundwater flows through these salt-bearing formations, it dissolves the connate salts and can transport them to the surface, where they become a major contributor to soil salinisation.

## Origin and Formation

- Connate salts originate from ancient seas, lakes, and evaporite deposits that were buried under subsequent sediment layers.
- As marine sediments accumulated over geological time, salt was trapped within the pore spaces and mineral structures of the forming rock.
- In Australia, much of the landscape was covered by inland seas during the Cretaceous and earlier periods, leaving extensive salt deposits in sedimentary basins.
- These deep salt reserves can remain dormant for millennia until mobilised by changes in groundwater hydrology.

## Key Facts

- Groundwater dissolves connate salts from old sea beds, carrying large loads of dissolved salts through subsurface aquifers.
- Connate salt is distinct from [[soil-collapse]] described by the WA S.A.L.T. theory.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Geological awareness**: Understanding the connate salt content of underlying formations helps predict salinisation risk when planning land use.
- **Deep-rooted perennials**: The primary defence against connate salt mobilisation is maintaining deep-rooted perennial vegetation that keeps water tables below critical thresholds.
- **Groundwater monitoring**: Regular monitoring of groundwater depth and salinity provides early warning of connate salt mobilisation.
- **Landscape reading**: Salt-tolerant vegetation patterns in low-lying areas often indicate connate salt influence at the landscape scale.
- **Irreversibility consideration**: Once connate salts have been mobilised to the surface, returning them to depth requires decades of consistent tree cover and [[fukuoka-irrigation-topsoil-erosion-salt-accumulation]]
