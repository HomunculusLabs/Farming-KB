# Melissa officinalis (Lemon Balm)

## Overview

*Melissa officinalis*, universally known as lemon balm, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the mint family (Lamiaceae) with a long and distinguished history of use in medicine, cuisine, and beekeeping. Native to the Mediterranean basin and Central Asia, lemon balm has been cultivated for over 2,000 years as a calming nervine, digestive aid, and culinary flavoring. Its intensely lemon-scented leaves — which contain volatile oils including citral, citronellal, and geranial — give the plant its common name and make it one of the most recognizable herbs in traditional European herbalism. The genus name *Melissa* derives from the Greek word for "honeybee" (*melissa*), reflecting the plant's legendary attraction to bees and its historical use as a beekeeping companion.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Lamiales
- **Family:** Lamiaceae (mint family)
- **Genus:** *Melissa*
- **Species:** *M. officinalis*
- **Author:** L.
- **Common names:** Lemon balm, balm, balm mint, common balm, sweet balm, bee balm (archaic), melissa

### Etymology

The generic name *Melissa* (Greek μέλισσα, "honeybee") was assigned by Carl Linnaeus, referencing the ancient belief that bees were drawn to lemon balm more than any other herb. The species epithet *officinalis* indicates its long-standing status as an official medicinal plant in European pharmacopeias. "Balm" derives from the Hebrew *balsam*, suggesting its historical reputation as a healing balm for wounds and emotional distress.

## Botanical Description

### Growth Habit
- **Type:** Herbaceous perennial
- **Height:** 30–90 cm (12–35 inches)
- **Spread:** 45–60 cm (18–24 inches); spreads slowly by rhizomes and self-seeding
- **Lifespan:** 5–10 years in cultivation; plants become woody and less productive with age
- **Root system:** Fibrous root system with shallow rhizomes; does not form deep taproots
- **Growth rate:** Vigorous in spring and summer; dies back to ground level in winter in temperate climates

### Morphology
- **Leaves:** Opposite, ovate to cordate, 3–8 cm long and 2–5 cm wide; coarsely toothed (crenate) margins; rugose (wrinkled) surface texture; bright green; covered in fine glandular trichomes that produce aromatic oils; pronounced lemon scent when crushed or brushed
- **Stems:** Square (characteristic of Lamiaceae), branched, 4-angled; green to reddish-green; pubescent
- **Flowers:** Small, bilabiate, white to pale yellow or pale pink, 8–12 mm long; borne in whorled clusters (verticillasters) in the leaf axils; bloom from June to October; attractive to bees, butterflies, and other pollinators
- **Seeds:** Small nutlets (mericarps), brown to black, 1.5–2 mm, four per flower; produced abundantly; remain viable in soil for several years

### Distribution and Habitat
- **Native to:** Southern Europe, the Mediterranean basin, Central Asia, and Iran
- **Naturalized in:** North America, South America, Australia, New Zealand, and much of temperate Europe
- **Habitat:** Forest clearings, roadsides, waste ground, stream banks, and disturbed soils; prefers moist, well-drained soils in partial shade to full sun
- **Hardiness:** USDA zones 4–9; tolerates temperatures to -30°C
- **Invasiveness:** Can become weedy in gardens due to prolific self-seeding and rhizomatous spread; not typically considered ecologically invasive

## Phytochemistry

### Essential Oil Composition
Lemon balm leaves contain 0.1–0.5% essential oil, with significant variation based on chemotype, harvest time, and growing conditions:

- **Citral** (geranial + neral): 30–40% of essential oil — primary source of lemon aroma; antimicrobial, antifungal
- **Citronellal:** 10–30% — lemon scent; insect repellent properties
- **Geraniol:** 5–15% — floral, rose-like; antimicrobial, antioxidant
- **Linalool:** 3–10% — floral; anxiolytic, sedative
- **β-Caryophyllene:** 2–8% — spicy; CB2 receptor agonist, anti-inflammatory
- **Germacrene D:** 2–5% — woody; insecticidal
- **Eugenol:** Trace–2% — clove-like; analgesic, antimicrobial

### Rosmarinic Acid
- The most significant non-volatile compound; concentrations of 3–6% in dried leaf
- Potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent
- Inhibits COX-1 and COX-2 enzymes; modulates NF-κB inflammatory pathway
- Contributes to the anxiolytic and cognitive-enhancing effects documented in clinical studies
- Also found in high concentrations in rosemary, peppermint, and perilla

### Other Constituents
- **Flavonoids:** Luteolin, apigenin, quercetin, rhamnocitrin, and their glycosides — antioxidant, anxiolytic
- **Tannins:** Moderate astringency; contribute to traditional use for digestive complaints
- **Triterpenes:** Ursolic acid and oleanolic acid — anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective
- **Phenolic acids:** Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid — antioxidant

## Medicinal Uses

### Nervine and Anxiolytic
Lemon balm has its strongest clinical evidence base as a calming nervine:
- **Anxiety reduction:** Multiple clinical trials demonstrate significant anxiolytic effects. A 2004 study by Kennedy et al. found that a single 600 mg dose of *M. officinalis* extract improved mood and cognitive performance while reducing anxiety
- **Insomnia:** A fixed combination of valerian (*Valeriana officinalis*) and lemon balm (e.g., Euvegal®) has shown efficacy in improving sleep quality and reducing sleep onset latency in randomized controlled trials
- **Stress modulation:** Mild adaptogenic properties; modulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis response to acute stress

### Cognitive Enhancement
- **Alzheimer's disease:** A landmark 2003 double-blind, randomized trial by Akhondzadeh et al. demonstrated that *M. officinalis* extract (60 drops/day for 4 months) produced significant improvements in cognitive function in patients with mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease, without the serious side effects associated with cholinesterase inhibitor drugs. The effect is attributed to rosmarinic acid's mild cholinesterase inhibition and GABA-transaminase inhibition
- **Memory and attention:** Acute dosing studies show improved accuracy and speed on cognitive tasks, particularly under conditions of induced stress

### Antiviral Activity
- **Herpes simplex virus (HSV):** Topical lemon balm cream (1% Loibel®) has demonstrated efficacy in reducing the frequency, duration, and severity of herpes labialis (cold sores) outbreaks in multiple clinical trials. The antiviral effect is attributed to tannins, polyphenols, and rosmarinic acid, which interfere with viral attachment and penetration
- **Other viruses:** In vitro activity against HIV-1, influenza, and enterovirus has been demonstrated, though clinical evidence is lacking

### Gastrointestinal Applications
- Carminative and antispasmodic properties; traditionally used for indigestion, bloating, flatulence, and colic
- Mild bitter principle stimulates digestive secretions
- Combined with chamomile or peppermint in traditional formulations for irritable bowel syndrome

### Thyroid Effects
- **Caution:** Lemon balm has documented antithyroid activity. It inhibits thyroid peroxidase (TPO), the enzyme responsible for iodination of thyroglobulin, and may interfere with thyroid hormone synthesis. This property has been investigated as a potential treatment for hyperthyroidism (Graves' disease), but lemon balm should be used cautiously by individuals with hypothyroidism or those taking thyroid medications

### Other Applications
- **Topical:** Wound healing, insect bites, minor burns (traditional); antimicrobial properties support these uses
- **Febrifuge:** Traditional use in reducing fever, particularly in children (as a mild, safe diaphoretic)
- **Menstrual cramps:** Antispasmodic effects may provide mild relief of dysmenorrhea

## Permaculture Applications

### Companion Planting
- **Bee attractant:** Lemon balm is one of the best bee-forage plants in the garden; its nectar-rich flowers attract honeybees, bumblebees, and native solitary bees throughout the growing season
- **Pest deterrence:** The strong lemon-scented essential oils may help repel certain insect pests when interplanted with vegetables; some evidence suggests deterrence of cabbage moths and squash bugs
- **Cabbage family companion:** Traditionally planted near brassicas to improve flavor and deter pests

### Ecological Functions
- **Ground cover:** Dense, low-growing habit provides weed suppression in perennial polycultures
- **Erosion control:** Fibrous root system helps stabilize soil on gentle slopes
- **Pollinator support:** Extended bloom period (4+ months) provides consistent forage for pollinators
- **Dynamic accumulator:** Moderate mineral accumulator; mines potassium, calcium, and trace minerals from subsoil

### Cultivation
- **Site selection:** Prefers partial shade in hot climates; tolerates full sun in cooler regions; moist, well-drained, fertile loam with pH 6.0–7.5
- **Propagation:** Easily grown from seed (stratification improves germination), stem cuttings, or division of established clumps; division every 3–4 years maintains vigor
- **Harvest:** Leaves can be harvested throughout the growing season; highest essential oil concentration occurs just before flowering; cut stems 5–10 cm above ground for regrowth
- **Maintenance:** Cut back after flowering to prevent self-seeding and encourage fresh growth; remove old woody stems in early spring
- **Companion plants:** Grows well with tomatoes, peppers, squash, brassicas, and fruit trees; avoid planting near basil (can cross-compete)

## Culinary Uses

- **Fresh leaves:** Added to salads, fruit salads, cold soups, and summer drinks; infused in water, lemonade, or iced tea for a refreshing lemon flavor
- **Dried leaves:** Used in herbal tea blends (often combined with chamomile, mint, and lavender); incorporated into baked goods, custards, and fruit desserts
- **Pesto:** Lemon balm pesto (with pine nuts, Parmesan, garlic, and olive oil) is a popular alternative to traditional basil pesto
- **Preserves:** Lemon balm jelly, syrup, and infused vinegar are traditional preparations
- **Medicinal beverages:** "Melissa water" (eau de mélisse des Carmes) is a traditional European herbal cordial combining lemon balm with lemon peel, nutmeg, cloves, and coriander

## Historical and Cultural Significance

- **Paracelsus (1493–1541):** Called lemon balm "the elixir of life" and prescribed it for all ailments
- **Dioscorides:** Described lemon balm in *De Materia Medica* (c. 50–70 CE) for wounds, bites, and menstrual complaints
- **Avicenna (Ibn Sina):** Recommended lemon balm for melancholy, heart palpitations, and digestive disorders in *The Canon of Medicine*
- **Carmelite monks:** Created "Carmelite water" (Melissengeist), a lemon balm-based spirit, in the 14th century; the recipe remains a proprietary secret of the Carmelite order
- **Shakespeare:** Referenced in *The Merry Wives of Windsor* and *Antony and Cleopatra*
- **Thomas Jefferson:** Grew lemon balm extensively at Monticello; noted it in his garden journals

## Contraindications and Interactions

- **Thyroid disorders:** Avoid in hypothyroidism; may potentiate antithyroid medications
- **Sedative interactions:** May potentiate the effects of benzodiazepines, barbiturates, and other CNS depressants
- **Glaucoma:** Theoretical concern based on anticholinergic activity at high doses
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** Generally considered safe in culinary amounts; avoid therapeutic doses due to insufficient safety data
- **Children:** Well-tolerated in traditional use; commonly administered as a mild, calming tea for restlessness and digestive upset
