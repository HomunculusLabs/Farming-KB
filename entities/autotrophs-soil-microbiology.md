## Description

In soil microbiology, autotrophs are organisms capable of synthesizing their
own organic compounds from inorganic sources. In the context of compost tea
and [[soil-food-web]] management discussed by [[elaine-ingham]], autotrophs are
specifically noted as organisms that "breathe" carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrate
(NO3), nitrite (NO2), sulfate (SO4), and other materials containing oxygen
bound in chemical forms rather than gaseous oxygen. Ingham uses the colloquial
term "solid oxygen" to describe these compounds, making the concept accessible
to non-scientists.

These are organisms that cannot be present in compost tea and are considered
problematic when [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]] develop during tea brewing.

## Classification

Autotrophs can be classified by their energy source:

- **Photoautotrophs**: Use light energy to fix carbon (e.g., cyanobacteria,
  algae, green plants, photosynthetic bacteria)
- **Chemoautotrophs**: Use chemical energy from inorganic compound oxidation
  (e.g., nitrifying bacteria like Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter,
  sulfur-oxidizing bacteria like Thiobacillus, iron-oxidizing bacteria)

By carbon source, autotrophs use CO2 as their primary carbon source, in
contrast to heterotrophs which require organic carbon compounds. This
distinction is critical in soil ecology because it determines which organisms
thrive under different soil conditions.

## Key Facts

- Autotrophs in the compost tea context are organisms that utilize oxygen from
  [[neurotropic-fungi-chemical-compounds]] rather than dissolved gaseous oxygen
- Elaine Ingham notes these organisms are undesirable in compost tea brews
  because they produce plant-toxic metabolites
- The problematic aspect is not what autotrophs breathe but what they produce
  as metabolic byproducts when growing under anaerobic conditions
- Many autotrophic processes in anaerobic conditions produce compounds toxic
  to plants including organic acids, alcohols, and reduced sulfur compounds
- Nitrifying bacteria (a type of chemoautotroph) convert ammonia to nitrite
  and nitrate in soil, playing a crucial role in [[query-how-does-the-nitrogen-cycle-work-in-a-garden]]
- Autotrophs are fundamental to global biogeochemical cycles, particularly
  carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles
- In healthy aerobic soil, autotrophic nitrifiers are beneficial; the problem
  arises specifically in anaerobic tea brewing or waterlogged soil conditions

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

In [[soil-food-web]] framework, understanding autotrophs is important for
compost tea management. Ingham explains that the organisms we cannot have in
tea are largely autotrophs that thrive under anaerobic conditions. When oxygen
levels in compost tea drop below 5.5-6 mg per liter, conditions shift to
favor [[anaerobic-organisms-compost]], including many autotrophic species.

The key insight is that these autotrophic anaerobes produce substances that
cause leaf-curl, root-death, and burning of plants. Even if fermentative
bacteria (rather than putrefying species) dominate the anaerobic conditions,
the low pH organic acids produced still harm beneficial fungi, kill protozoa
and [[ingham-beneficial-nematodes-soil-food-web]], and undermine soil health over time.

[[grass-seed-substrate-for-mushroom-cultivation]] and soil management, the takeaway is clear:
maintaining aerobic conditions during compost tea production and in soil
ensures that beneficial heterotrophic organisms (the ones plants need)
outcompete undesirable autotrophic and anaerobic species. This is why actively
aerated compost tea (AACT) is preferred over anaerobic brewing methods.

The distinction between autotrophs and heterotrophs in soil management also
explains why adding simple sugars and fungal foods to compost tea feeds the
beneficial heterotrophs that will protect plants, rather than the autotrophic
organisms that thrive on inorganic chemistry alone.

The practical implication for compost tea brewers is straightforward: if you
smell alcohol, putrid odors, or sulfur (rotten egg smell) in your tea, you
have gone anaerobic and autotrophic organisms have begun producing undesirable
metabolites. The tea should smell sweet and earthy, like good forest soil or
compost. Any off-odors indicate the tea has gone anaerobic and the autotrophs
have taken over from the beneficial heterotrophs you were trying to grow.
