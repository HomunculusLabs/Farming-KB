## Description

Phyla nodosa (formerly classified as Lippia nodosa) is a low-growing, spreading perennial plant in the Verbenaceae (verbena) family, recommended by Bill Mollison as a key marginal planting for taro terrace bunds in wetland agriculture systems. It functions as a nitrogenous mulch source, remaining naturally short and trimmed at water level by herbivorous fish such as Tilapia (Oreochromis spp.) and grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella). Its prostrate growth habit, tolerance of saturated soils, and ability to fix nitrogen make it an ideal living mulch for the edges and bunds of flooded agricultural terraces.

This species exemplifies the permaculture design strategy of selecting plants that maintain themselves through ecological feedback loops rather than requiring constant human management and trimming inputs. The fish trim the plant at the waterline, the plant feeds the fish with trimmed material, and nitrogen fixed by the plant enriches the entire aquatic system.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Verbenaceae (verbena family)
- **Genus**: Phyla
- **Species**: P. nodosa
- **Synonym**: Lippia nodosa
- **Growth habit**: Prostrate, spreading perennial herb
- **Habitat**: Wet margins, bunds, water edges, saturated soils
- **Native range**: Tropical and subtropical wetland margins worldwide
- **Role**: Nitrogen-fixing living mulch for aquaculture bunds and terrace edges

## Key Facts

### Role in Taro Polyculture Systems
- Planted on bunds (terrace walls) of wet taro terraces as a primary nitrogenous mulch source
- Remains naturally short due to continuous grazing by fish (Tilapia, grass carp) at the water level
- Provides persistent living groundcover on terrace edges and bunds without any manual trimming required
- Contributes fixed nitrogen and organic matter to both terrestrial and aquatic components of the system
- Fully compatible with the wet, humid, frost-free conditions required for taro cultivation year-round

### Companion Planting on Taro Bunds
Mollison recommends bunds planted to a mix of two complementary nitrogenous groundcovers:
- **Phyla nodosa** — primary nitrogenous living mulch, self-maintained by fish grazing
- **Dolichos hosei** — additional nitrogenous mulch and groundcover species

Both species remain manageable in the taro terrace system, naturally kept in check by fish grazing at the waterline. This eliminates the need for manual mowing, scything, or trimming of bund vegetation entirely.

### Ecological Functions in Aquaculture Systems
- Biological nitrogen fixation in wetland margin environments, enriching both soil and water
- Erosion control on bunds and terrace edges, preventing structural failure during heavy tropical rains
- Habitat for beneficial insects and arthropods near water margins, supporting natural pest management
- Fish forage when trimmed material enters the water column as decomposing organic matter
- Organic matter contribution to aquatic nutrient cycles supporting plankton, detritus, and invertebrate food webs
- pH buffering through decomposing leaf litter in marginal zones
- Weed suppression through dense prostrate growth habit that outcompetes unwanted volunteer species

### Growth and Management Characteristics
- Low, spreading habit ideally suited for bund and terrace-edge planting
- Highly tolerant of saturated soils, periodic flooding, and continuous root-zone waterlogging
- Persistent perennial that rapidly regrows after fish grazing or physical cutting
- Dense enough growth to suppress unwanted volunteer vegetation on bunds
- Self-maintaining through the natural fish-grazing feedback loop with no human intervention needed

## Relevance to Cultivation

Phyla nodosa exemplifies the permaculture principle of stacking functions, where every element in the system serves multiple purposes simultaneously. In a taro terrace system, it provides nitrogen fixation, erosion control, living mulch, fish fodder, and weed suppression — all while maintaining itself through a natural feedback loop where herbivorous fish keep its growth in check at the waterline.

The plant is particularly valuable in integrated aquaculture-agriculture systems where terrestrial and aquatic production are combined on the same site. Its use demonstrates how careful selection of marginal plants can close nutrient loops: nitrogen fixed by Phyla on the bunds enters the water system through leaf drop and fish grazing, fertilizing both the taro crop and aquatic organisms including prawns, Tilapia, grass carp, and crayfish.

For mycology and specialty cultivation practitioners, Phyla nodosa illustrates the importance of edge plantings in any water-based growing system. The nitrogen-rich leaf litter produced by this species could serve as a substrate component for margin-dwelling fungal species. The principle of using fish-grazed living mulch on water margins can be applied to any integrated system combining plant cultivation with aquaculture, including those incorporating fungal cultivation in humid margin environments.

The species also demonstrates a broader permaculture design principle: selecting organisms that regulate each others growth through natural ecological interactions (fish grazing Phyla, Phyla feeding fish) creates self-maintaining systems that reduce human labor while increasing total system productivity. This same principle can be applied to fungal cultivation systems where fungal mycelium interacts with plant roots and soil microbiota in mutually beneficial relationships.
