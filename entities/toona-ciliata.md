## Toona ciliata

## Overview

**Toona ciliata** (Australian red cedar) is a large deciduous tree in the family Meliaceae, native to Australia and widely distributed across South and Southeast Asia. It is one of the most valued timber species in the Asia-Pacific region, prized for its rich reddish-brown heartwood that resembles true mahogany (*Swietenia* spp.). The species can reach 40–60 m in height with a straight, cylindrical bole.

Australian red cedar was the first timber species commercially exploited by European settlers in eastern Australia during the 1820s–1840s. Intensive logging decimated natural populations, and the species is now considered depleted throughout much of its native range.

The species earned the colloquial name "red gold" during the colonial era due to its commercial value. Logging teams known as "cedar getters" penetrated deep into rainforest valleys to harvest the largest trees. By the early 20th century, accessible stands were largely exhausted, and the industry shifted to other species. The legacy of this exploitation is evident in the species' current conservation status and the prevalence of young, regenerating stands rather than old-growth forests. It remains commercially important in plantation forestry, agroforestry, and ornamental horticulture across tropical and subtropical regions worldwide.

The genus *Toona* contains five to seven species distributed across South Asia, Southeast Asia, and eastern Australia. Molecular phylogenetic studies place *Toona* within the Cedreloideae subfamily of Meliaceae, closely related to *Cedrela* and *Swietenia*. The species epithet *ciliata* refers to the ciliate (hairy) leaf margin, a distinguishing morphological character.

Chromosome number is 2n = 28. Genetic studies have revealed significant population structure across the species' range, with Australian populations showing marked differentiation from Asian populations. Infraspecific classification is contentious; some authorities recognize varieties based on [[geographic-distribution-of-psilocybe-species]]. The Australian populations are sometimes treated as a distinct variety, *T. ciliata* var. *australis*, distinguished by larger leaflets and more pronounced ciliation. However, molecular evidence does not strongly support formal varietal status.

## Morphology

Australian red cedar is a large, buttressed deciduous tree. The trunk is straight and cylindrical, often branchless for the first 15–20 m. Bark on mature trees is dark brown to gray, rough and fissured, with reddish-brown inner bark that exudes a bitter-tasting sap when cut. Buttresses are prominent on large trees in lowland rainforest sites.

Leaves are pinnately compound, 30–70 cm long, with 8–16 pairs of leaflets plus a terminal leaflet. Leaflets are lanceolate to ovate-lanceolate, 6–15 cm long and 2–5 cm wide, with ciliate margins. The upper surface is dark green and glabrous; the lower surface is paler with scattered glands. New foliage is bright pink to reddish, a distinctive ornamental feature. This coloration results from anthocyanin pigments that provide photoprotection to developing leaves. The pink color fades to green over 2–4 weeks as the leaf matures.

Branching pattern is typically horizontal with ascending tips, forming a spreading crown in open-grown trees. In forest conditions, the crown is narrower and more columnar. Root systems are shallow and widespreading in well-drained soils, with a moderately developed taproot on deep alluvial sites.

Flowers are small, white to pale pink, 5–6 mm in diameter, borne in large terminal panicles 20–40 cm long. Flowering occurs from October to December in Australia (spring). The species is monoecious, with male and bisexual flowers on the same inflorescence. Pollination is by insects, particularly bees and flies. Nectar production is abundant, making the flowers attractive to a wide range of pollinators. Fruit set is typically high under open-pollinated conditions.

Capsules mature 3–4 months after flowering. Dehiscence is explosive in dry conditions, projecting seeds up to 20 m from the parent tree. Wind dispersal carries seeds further, though effective dispersal distance is limited by the relatively heavy seed weight. Pre-dispersal seed predation by insects can be significant, reducing viable seed output by 30–50% in some populations.

Fruit is a woody capsule, 2–3.5 cm long, containing numerous winged seeds. Capsules dehisce at maturity, releasing seeds that are dispersed by wind.
