# Adenium obesum

## Overview

Adenium obesum is a succulent shrub or small tree in the family Apocynaceae, native to the [[amanita-muscaria]] across its range. In Oman, it is planted near homes as a protective ornamental and its wood is carved into small decorative objects. The species appears on the coat of arms of several Saharan nations.

## Taxonomy

Adenium obesum (Forssk.) Roem. & Schult. was originally described as Nerium obesum by Pehr Forsskal in 1775 based on specimens collected in Yemen. It was transferred to the genus Adenium by Josef August Schultes in 1819.

The species is placed in the order Gentianales, family Apocynaceae, subfamily Apocynoideae, tribe Nerieae. The genus Adenium comprises five to twelve species depending on taxonomic treatment, with several recently described species from East Africa and the Arabian Peninsula.

Several subspecies are recognized including subsp. obesum (Arabian Peninsula and East Africa), subsp. boehmianum (Namibia and Angola), subsp. oleifolium (South Africa), and subsp. somalense (Somalia and Kenya). Subspecies boehmianum is the most toxic and is used traditionally as an arrow poison.

The taxonomy of the genus was substantially revised by Dimmitt and colleagues in 2013 based on molecular phylogenetic analysis. Several former species including Adenium arabicum and Adenium multiflorum were reclassified as subspecies or varieties within Adenium obesum.

Chromosomal analysis reveals a base chromosome number of n equals eleven for the genus. Polyploid cultivars with doubled chromosome counts have been developed for the ornamental trade, producing larger flowers and more vigorous growth than diploid forms.

## Morphology

Adenium obesum forms a thick, bottle-shaped caudex that serves as a water storage organ, reaching diameters of thirty to one hundred centimeters at maturity. The caudex tapers upward into slender, spreading branches that bear sparse clusters of leathery leaves.

Leaves are spirally arranged, obovate to oblanceolate, five to fifteen centimeters long and two to five centimeters wide. They are glossy dark green above, lighter below, with entire margins and a prominent midrib. Leaves are deciduous and are shed during extended dry periods.

Leaf venation is brochidodromous with an intramarginal vein running parallel to the leaf margin. The epidermis bears scattered stomata on the adaxial surface and dense trichomes on the abaxial surface, reducing transpirational water loss.

The bark is smooth and pale gray-green on young growth, becoming rough and fissured with age. The inner bark and sapwood contain the highest concentrations of cardiac glycosides.

Flowers are terminal, salverform, measuring four to seven centimeters in diameter with five spreading lobes. Petal color ranges from deep crimson to pink, white, and bicolor patterns. The corolla tube is narrow and elongated, adapted for pollination by long-tongued hawkmoths.

Nectar is produced at the base of the corolla tube and accumulates in a narrow chamber accessible only to insects with proboscides exceeding three centimeters. Hawkmoths of the genus Hyles and Agrius are the primary nocturnal pollinators.

The fruit is a paired follicle fifteen to twenty-five centimeters long containing numerous linear seeds with a tuft of silky coma hairs at each end for wind dispersal.

The species grows in arid and semi-arid environments including rocky hillsides, dry savanna, thorn scrub, and sandy alluvial plains.
