# Gammarus pulex

## Description

Gammarus pulex, commonly known as the freshwater shrimp or river shrimp, is an amphipod crustacean in the family Gammaridae. It is one of the most abundant and ecologically important macroinvertebrates in freshwater ecosystems across Europe. G. pulex plays a central role in aquatic food webs as both a detritivore processing leaf litter (often mediated by aquatic fungi) and as prey for fish and other predators. The species has been used in ecotoxicology studies demonstrating heavy metal bioaccumulation through aquatic food chains involving fungi.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Animalia
- **Phylum:** Arthropoda
- **Class:** Malacostraca
- **Order:** Amphipoda
- **Family:** Gammaridae
- **Genus:** Gammarus
- **Species:** G. pulex

## Morphology

G. pulex is a laterally compressed crustacean typically 10-20 mm in length. The body is divided into a cephalothorax, a seven-segmented pereon (thorax), and a six-segmented pleon (abdomen). The animal is semi-transparent to grey-brown in color, with prominent antennae and large gnathopods (claw-like appendages) used for grasping food. The first two pairs of pleopods are modified for swimming, while the posterior uropods form a tail fan.

Males are typically larger than females with proportionally larger gnathopods. The species can be distinguished from the closely related G. duebeni and invasive G. tigrinus by antennal and coxal plate characteristics.

## Ecology and Habitat

G. pulex inhabits a wide range of freshwater habitats across Europe, including streams, rivers, ponds, lakes, and canals. It prefers well-oxygenated waters with moderate flow and abundant shelter in the form of stones, leaf litter, and aquatic vegetation.

Ecologically, G. pulex is a key species in freshwater ecosystems:

- **Leaf litter processing:** Shreds leaf litter that has been conditioned (colonized and softened) by [[bloomfield-aquatic-hyphomycete-leaf-decomposition]] fungi, accelerating [[decomposition]]
- **Food web linkage:** Serves as a major prey item for fish (trout, bullhead), aquatic insects, and birds
- **Nutrient cycling:** Processes [[savory-soil-organic-matter-and-decomposition-dynamics]] releases nutrients through excretion and fecal production
- **Biomass dominance:** Can constitute 30-50% of macroinvertebrate biomass in some stream systems

## Heavy Metal Bioaccumulation Through Fungal Food Chains

Duddridge and Wainwright (1980) demonstrated significant transfer of cadmium (Cd) from [[fungal-hyphae]] to G. pulex through feeding, providing evidence for heavy metal bioaccumulation through aquatic food chains. In their experiments:

- G. pulex was fed on fungal hyphae (Pythium species) containing 150-170 micrograms/g cadmium
- No shrimp survived after 13 days when fed Cd-contaminated fungi
- In contrast, 60% of shrimp survived to 21 days when fed control (uncontaminated) fungi
- Dead animals from the Cd-exposed group contained 22 micrograms/g cadmium

These findings demonstrate that:

1. **Cadmium transfers efficiently from fungi to grazing invertebrates:** The metal is not detoxified during passage through the fungal food chain
2. **Lethal concentrations accumulate rapidly:** Cd at these levels in fungal food is fatal to the grazer within two weeks
3. **Food chain bioaccumulation occurs:** Heavy metals accumulated by fungi from the environment are passed up the food web to invertebrate consumers
4. **Ecological risk extends beyond direct toxicity:** The impact of heavy metals on [[dighton-fungal-biomass-production-aquatic-ecosystems]] includes indirect effects through food web contamination

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

While G. pulex is a crustacean rather than a fungus, its [[king-stropharia-ecological-interactions-permaculture]] with aquatic fungi make it relevant to mycological research:

1. **Fungal-animal interactions:** The feeding of G. pulex on fungi-conditioned leaf litter is a textbook example of fungal-animal mutualism [[fungal-decomposition-in-aquatic-ecosystems-concluding-perspectives]], where fungi increase the palatability and nutritional value of leaf material.

2. **Bioaccumulation studies:** The fungal-Gammarus food chain is a model system for studying how heavy metals and other contaminants move through ecosystems, with implications for understanding pollutant dynamics in aquatic fungi.

3. **Ecosystem ecotoxicology:** The sensitivity of the fungal-shrimp-fish food chain to heavy metals demonstrates the cascading effects of pollution on aquatic ecosystem function, where impacts on fungi propagate to higher trophic levels.

4. **Leaf litter decomposition:** Understanding the synergistic role of fungi and shredders like G. pulex in leaf litter decomposition is fundamental to stream ecology and the management of aquatic ecosystem health.

5. **Biomonitoring:** G. pulex is a standard test organism in ecotoxicology, and its responses to contaminants help assess the ecological risk of heavy metal pollution in aquatic systems.
