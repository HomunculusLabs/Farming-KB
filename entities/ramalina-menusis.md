## Overview

**Ramalina menziesii** Taylor, commonly known as the lace lichen or fishnet lichen, is a fruticose lichen in the family Ramalinaceae. It is renowned for its distinctive net-like or lace-like thallus structure that hangs in intricate, curtain-like drapes from the branches of trees along the Pacific coast of North America.

The species is the state lichen of California, designated in 2015, reflecting its ecological and [[ethnomycology-cultural-significance-sacred-fungi]]. It is one of the most abundant and conspicuous lichens in coastal California, forming extensive drapes that can exceed a meter in length on mature oaks and other hardwoods.

Indigenous peoples of California used R. menziesii for various purposes, including as absorbent bandage material, diaper padding, and menstrual padding. The lichen's natural absorbency and soft texture made it a practical material for everyday use. These traditional applications reflect a deep ethnobotanical knowledge of the species' properties.

Ramalina menziesii is ecologically important as a source of nesting material for birds, habitat for microarthropods, and a bioindicator of air quality in coastal ecosystems. Its sensitivity to air pollution makes it a valuable monitor species in urbanizing coastal regions.

Unlike most fruticose lichens, R. menziesii lacks a central cord and instead forms a flattened, net-like mesh. This unique morphology gives it exceptional flexibility and allows it to sway freely in wind, which may enhance spore dispersal across open landscapes. The net-like structure also maximizes surface area for photosynthesis and atmospheric nutrient absorption.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Ramalina menziesii** was described by Thomas Taylor in 1848 and is named in honor of Archibald Menzies, the Scottish naturalist and botanist who served as surgeon and naturalist on the Vancouver Expedition (1791-1795). The species has no widely accepted synonyms, though it has occasionally been placed in the genus *Usnea* by early lichenologists.

Molecular phylogenetic studies have confirmed the placement of R. menziesii within the Ramalinaceae and its close relationship to other pendulous *Ramalina* species. The unique net-like morphology appears to have evolved once within the genus, making it a phylogenetically distinctive lineage within the family.

The thallus is fruticose, pendulous, and conspicuously net-like, forming broad, flat branches that repeatedly dichotomize and anastomose to create an intricate lacework pattern. The mesh is typically irregular, with openings ranging from a few millimeters to several centimeters across. Thallus color ranges from pale grey-green to yellowish-grey, becoming darker when wet.

Branches are flattened, strap-shaped, and 1-4 mm wide, with a smooth to slightly wrinkled surface. The medulla is loosely organized, which accounts for the flexible, draping habit. There is no central supporting cord, distinguishing R. menziesii from *Usnea* species that possess a distinct central axis.

Growth rates of R. menziesii have been measured at approximately 1-3 cm per year under favorable conditions. The species can persist for decades on stable substrates, with some individual thalli estimated to be over 50 years old based on growth ring analysis of attached branches.

Apothecia are lecanorine, terminal or marginal on branch tips, and 2-6 mm in diameter. They are relatively uncommon compared to [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]], with discs that are pale brown to tan and often slightly pruinose. The thalline margin is well-developed and concolorous with the thallus surface.

Spores are ellipsoid to broadly ellipsoid, colorless, simple or occasionally one-septate, measuring approximately 10-16 × 5-8 µm. Pycnidia occur on the thallus surface and produce bacillariform conidia approximately 5-7 × 1 µm.

The photobiont is a trebouxia-like green alga. Vegetative reproduction occurs primarily through fragmentation of the net-like thallus, with broken pieces establishing on new substrates. This mode of dispersal is highly effective in the windy coastal environments where the species is most abundant. Fragment dispersal may be facilitated by birds that carry lichen pieces between trees during nest construction.

## Distribution and Habitat

Ramalina menziesii has a restricted distribution along the Pacific coast of North America, ranging from southeastern Alaska through coastal British Columbia, Washington, Oregon, and California to northern Baja California, Mexico. The species is most abundant in the California Floristic Province, where it dominates lichen communities in coastal oak woodlands.

In California, R. menziesii occurs from sea level to approximately 1500 meters elevation. It reaches its greatest abundance in the fog belt of coastal California, where summer fog provides regular moisture and reduces desiccation stress. Inland populations occur in riparian corridors and moist canyon habitats that receive sufficient humidity.

The fog-dependent ecology of R. menziesii makes it particularly vulnerable to [[dighton-fungi-climate-change-feedback]]. Models predict significant range contraction in southern California as summer fog frequency decreases. Along the northern margin of its range, the species may benefit from warmer conditions but could face competition from more aggressive lichen taxa.

The species is almost exclusively corticolous, growing on the branches and twigs of a wide variety of hardwood and conifer trees. Preferred hosts include *Quercus agrifolia* (coast live oak), *Quercus douglasii* (blue oak), *Quercus lobata* (valley oak), *Sequoia sempervirens* (coast redwood), *Pseudotsuga menziesii* (Douglas-fir), and various *Salix* species. It is most conspicuous on exposed branches of open-canopy trees.

Coastal scrub, chaparral edges, oak savannas, and redwood forests are the primary habitat types. The species is largely absent from the interior valleys of California, the Central Valley, and arid regions of southern California, where summer drought and low humidity limit establishment. It is also sensitive to sulfur dioxide and other air pollutants, restricting its distribution to areas with relatively clean air.

Seasonal patterns of moisture availability strongly influence the species' physiological activity. During the summer fog season, R. menziesii remains photosynthetically active and continues growth, while in the drier winter months it enters a dormant state. This reversed phenological pattern contrasts with most Mediterranean-climate lichens that are active during winter rains.

## Chemistry and Secondary Metabolites

The medulla of Ramalina menziesii contains usnic acid as the primary secondary metabolite, along with smaller amounts of atranorin, evernic acid, and ramalinic acid derivatives. Usnic acid is responsible for the characteristic yellowish tinge visible on dry thallus surfaces and contributes to the species' antibiotic properties.

Spot tests for the cortex show K+ yellow (atranorin) and C+ yellow (atranorin). The medulla shows K+ yellow (usnic acid), C+ pink or KC+ pink (evernic acid), and P- or weakly P+ pale. These spot test reactions help distinguish R. menziesii from other fruticose lichens with different chemistries.

The absence of thamnolic acid and other depsidones in the medulla separates R. menziesii from several *Ramalina* species that contain these compounds. Chemical differentiation is particularly useful when morphological identification is complicated by atypical growth forms on unusual substrates.

Thin-layer chromatography reveals the usnic acid chemosyndrome as the dominant pattern. Geographic variation in secondary chemistry is minimal, though specimens from more northern populations may show slightly reduced evernic acid content. The chemical profile is consistent across the species' range.

Usnic acid has well-documented antibacterial, antifungal, and anti-inflammatory properties. Traditional uses of R. menziesii by Indigenous peoples of California may be partly attributable to these [[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]]. Modern research has investigated usnic acid for wound healing and antimicrobial applications.

The total lichen biomass contributed by R. menziesii in some coastal oak woodlands is remarkably high, with estimates exceeding several hundred kilograms per hectare. This substantial standing biomass makes the species one of the most ecologically important epiphytes in the Pacific coastal lichen flora.

## Ecology and Symbiosis

Ramalina menziesii is a mutualistic symbiont with trebouxia-like green algae contained within the medullary layer. The algal partner provides fixed carbon through photosynthesis, while the fungal mycobiont offers protection from environmental stress and facilitates nutrient and water uptake from the atmosphere.

The species is a dominant member of epiphytic lichen communities in coastal California woodlands. In oak savannas of the Coast Range, R. menziesii can account for a substantial proportion of total lichen biomass, forming extensive curtains that draped over entire tree canopies. Individual thalli may exceed one meter in length on old-growth oaks in favorable locations.

Birds use R. menziesii extensively as nesting material. The bushtit (*Psaltriparus minimus*) and other small passerine species weave the lace-like thallus into their hanging nests, taking advantage of its flexibility and abundance. This ecological interaction provides a significant ecosystem service, as the lichen's moisture-retaining properties may help regulate nest humidity.

Other bird species documented using R. menziesii include the Allen's hummingbird, Anna's hummingbird, and various warblers and vireos. The lichen may also provide camouflage for nests built in exposed locations. The abundance of R. menziesii in oak woodlands directly influences the breeding success of multiple bird species that depend on it as a construction material.

The species serves as microhabitat for diverse invertebrate communities, including mites, springtails, nematodes, and various lichen-feeding insects. These microarthropod communities contribute to [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] and energy flow within the forest canopy ecosystem.

Ramalina menziesii is sensitive to air pollution, particularly sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides. Declining populations near urban areas and agricultural operations have been documented, making the species a useful bioindicator for monitoring air quality changes along the Pacific coast.
