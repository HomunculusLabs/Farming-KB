## Overview

Ixia maculata is a cormous perennial in the Iridaceae family, endemic to the winter-rainfall region of the Western Cape, South Africa. It produces clusters of star-shaped flowers in shades of yellow, orange, or red with dark centers.

Known commonly as the spotted ixia, it is valued in horticulture for its vivid coloration and long-lasting cut flowers. The species epithet "maculata" refers to the dark, often blackish center marking each bloom.

Plants grow from a small tunicate corm and bloom in spring (September–October in the Southern Hemisphere). They naturalize readily in Mediterranean-climate gardens worldwide.

The species has been cultivated in European gardens since the late 18th century. It was first described by Linnaeus in 1753 from material collected near the Cape of Good Hope.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Order Asparagales → Family Iridaceae → Subfamily Ixioideae → Genus Ixia → Species I. maculata.

Authority: L. (Linnaeus, 1753, Species Plantarum). Recognized synonyms include Ixia autumnalis Ker Gawl. and Ixia lutea Eckl.

Plants reach 20–50 cm tall with narrow, sword-shaped leaves 3–5 mm wide. Each stem bears a lax spike of 4–12 flowers on slender pedicels.

Tepals are broadly ovate, 15–25 mm long, spreading to form a flat star shape. Flower color ranges from golden yellow to deep orange-red, always with a contrasting dark eye at the center.

The filaments are united into a short column, a key diagnostic feature of the genus Ixia. Anthers are linear, 4–6 mm long, and purple-black in color.

Corms are small (8–15 mm diameter), globose, with fibrous brown tunics. They reproduce vegetatively by producing small offset cormlets at the base.

The style divides into three short branches, each extending between the stamens. This arrangement ensures efficient pollen transfer by visiting insects.

## Distribution and Habitat

Ixia maculata is restricted to the Western Cape Province of South Africa. Its range extends from the Cape Peninsula eastward to Caledon and Bredasdorp.

It occurs within the Cape Floristic Region, one of the world's six floral kingdoms and a global biodiversity hotspot. Populations are scattered across renosterveld and lowland fynbos communities.

Plants prefer seasonally damp sites on clay or loam soils derived from Malmesbury shale formations. Elevations range from near sea level to approximately 500 m.

The species is adapted to winter-rainfall patterns followed by summer dormancy. Corms remain dry underground from November through March, surviving the arid months.

Habitat loss to agriculture and urban expansion has reduced several populations. Local declines have been documented near Stellenbosch and Somerset West, though the species is not currently IUCN-listed.

## Cultivation

Ixia maculata requires well-drained soil and a Mediterranean climate with dry summers. Plant corms in autumn at 5–8 cm depth, spaced 5–8 cm apart.

Full sun exposure promotes the strongest flowering display. Plants tolerate light shade but produce fewer and smaller blooms in shaded conditions.

Water moderately during autumn and winter growth, tapering off as foliage yellows in late spring. Corms must be kept completely dry during summer dormancy.

In colder climates (USDA zones 8–9), corms can be lifted after foliage dies back. Store them dry in paper bags at room temperature until autumn replanting.

Container cultivation works well in deep terracotta pots with a gritty, free-draining mix. A blend of equal parts loam, sharp sand, and fine bark is ideal.

Propagation is by fresh seed (sown in autumn) or separation of cormlets. Seed-raised plants typically flower in their second or third season.

Common pests include slugs and snails feeding on new growth. Botrytis can affect overcrowded plantings in humid, poorly ventilated conditions.

## Ecology

Ixia maculata is primarily pollinated by monkey beetles (Hopliini, Scarabaeidae). These beetles are drawn to the dark floral centers and feed on pollen while using the flowers as mating platforms.

The species forms part of the geophyte layer in renosterveld vegetation. Its phenology is precisely timed to exploit the wet winter growing season.

Corms are adapted to survive periodic grass fires common in fynbos and renosterveld ecosystems. Smoke-derived compounds such as butenolides may stimulate seed germination in related Ixia species.

Ixia maculata hybridizes naturally with Ixia scillaris and Ixia viridiflora where ranges overlap. These hybrids show intermediate flower colors and tepal markings.
