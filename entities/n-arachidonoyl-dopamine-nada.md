# N-Arachidonoyl-dopamine (NADA)

**Type:** Chemical Compound (Endocannabinoid)
**Category:** Endogenous Cannabinoid / Endovanilloid
**Also known as:** NADA, n arachidonoyl dopamine nada

## Overview
N-Arachidonoyl-dopamine (NADA) is one of five identified endogenous
cannabinoids produced naturally by the human body. Discovered in 2000,
NADA is unique among endocannabinoids because it combines the
arachidonic acid chain characteristic of cannabinoid signaling
molecules with a dopamine moiety, effectively bridging the
endocannabinoid and dopaminergic neurotransmitter systems. NADA acts
as both a CB1 receptor agonist and a potent TRPV1 (vanilloid)
receptor agonist, making it a dual-function signaling molecule.

## Key Details

### Chemical Properties
- **Molecular formula:** C24H37NO2
- **Classification:** Endocannabinoid / Endovanilloid
- **Molecular weight:** 371.5 g/mol
- **Structure:** Arachidonic acid conjugated to dopamine via an amide
  bond, combining features of both endocannabinoid and catecholamine
  signaling molecules
- **Tissue distribution:** Found in the brain, particularly in
  regions rich in both dopaminergic and cannabinoid systems including
  the striatum, hippocampus, and cerebellum

### Pharmacology
- **CB1 receptor:** NADA acts as an agonist at CB1 cannabinoid
  receptors, though its affinity is lower than that of anandamide
  or 2-AG. It activates the same signaling cascades as other CB1
  agonists
- **TRPV1 receptor (vanilloid):** NADA is a potent agonist at the
  TRPV1 receptor, also known as the capsaicin or vanilloid receptor.
  This receptor mediates pain sensation, body temperature
  regulation, and inflammatory responses. NADA's TRPV1 activity is
  actually more potent than its CB1 activity
- **Dual signaling:** The ability to activate both CB1 and TRPV1
  receptors positions NADA at the intersection of cannabinoid and
  vanilloid [[plant-signaling-pathways]]

### Physiological Functions
- **Pain modulation:** Through both CB1 and TRPV1 receptor
  activation, NADA plays a complex role in pain signaling. TRPV1
  activation typically promotes pain sensation, while CB1 activation
  tends to suppress it, suggesting NADA may have a modulatory role
  in pain processing
- **Neuroprotection:** NADA has demonstrated neuroprotective
  properties in experimental models, potentially through both
  cannabinoid and vanilloid receptor-mediated pathways
- **Inflammation:** TRPV1-mediated effects of NADA may contribute
  to the regulation of inflammatory responses in peripheral tissues
- **Body [[blesching-cannabis-fever-temperature-regulation]]:** Via TRPV1 receptor activation,
  NADA may participate in thermoregulatory processes
- **Neurotransmitter interaction:** The dopamine moiety of NADA
  suggests potential interactions with dopaminergic signaling
  systems, though this is still being investigated

### Discovery and Significance
NADA was first identified in 2000 by Bisogno et al. and represented
a significant finding because it demonstrated that the body produces
hybrid molecules that combine endocannabinoid and neurotransmitter
features. This discovery expanded understanding of cross-talk
between different neurotransmitter systems and suggested that the
endocannabinoid system may interact with dopaminergic pathways in
ways not previously appreciated.

## Relevance
NADA is significant because it represents a molecular bridge between
the endocannabinoid system and the dopaminergic and vanilloid
neurotransmitter systems. Its dual-receptor pharmacology (CB1 + TRPV1)
makes it relevant to understanding pain processing, neuroinflammation,
and neuroprotection. For cannabis therapeutics, NADA's existence
suggests that some of cannabis's [[cervantes-terpenoid-pharmacology-therapeutic-effects]] may involve
modulation of multiple receptor systems beyond just CB1 and CB2,
including the TRPV1 pathway.
