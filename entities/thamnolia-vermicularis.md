## Overview

Thamnolia vermicularis, commonly known as the whiteworm lichen or arctic worm lichen, is a distinctive fruticose lichen found in arctic and alpine regions worldwide. It forms unbranched, hollow, white tubes that grow upright from soil, moss, or debris.

The species is immediately recognizable in the field by its worm-like appearance and pure white coloration. Despite its simple morphology, T. vermicularis is genetically diverse and has been the subject of significant taxonomic investigation.

T. vermicularis rarely produces sexual fruiting bodies, reproducing primarily through fragmentation. Its bipolar distribution in both hemispheres has generated interest in its biogeographic history and dispersal mechanisms.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Thamnolia vermicularis was first described by Olof Peter Swartz in 1784. The genus name derives from the Greek *thamnos* (bush) and *helios* (sun), while *vermicularis* means "worm-like," referring to its distinctive growth form.

The thallus consists of unbranched, hollow, tubular podetia 2–8 cm tall and 1–2 mm in diameter. The tubes are erect to arcuate, arising from a basal pad or directly from the substrate surface.

The outer surface is smooth to slightly grooved longitudinally, white to chalky-white, with a powdery or pruinose appearance. The inner surface is also white but may show faint longitudinal striations.

The podetia are hollow throughout, with a thin cortical layer enclosing a central cavity. This hollow construction distinguishes T. vermicularis from solid-stemmed Cladonia species.

Apothecia are extremely rare and have been documented only a few times in the species' taxonomic history. When present, they are terminal, brown, and 1–2 mm in diameter with simple asci.

Pycnidia are also rare, producing bacillar conidia approximately 4–5 μm long. The scarcity of both sexual and asexual fruiting structures reflects the species' primary reliance on vegetative reproduction through thallus fragmentation.

## Distribution and Habitat

Thamnolia vermicularis has a bipolar distribution, occurring in arctic and alpine regions of both hemispheres. In the Northern Hemisphere, it is circumpolar across North America, Europe, and Asia.

In North America, T. vermicularis ranges from arctic Alaska and northern Canada south through the Rocky Mountains and Sierra Nevada to New Mexico. In Europe, it occurs in Scandinavia, the Alps, Carpathians, and Scottish Highlands.

[[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]]. Chemotype I contains thamnolic acid, while Chemotype II contains squamatic acid.

Thamnolic acid produces the spot test reactions K+ yellow → deep red, C–, PD+ orange-red. Squamatic acid gives K+ yellow → orange, C+ faint pink, PD+ orange. Both chemotypes are morphologically indistinguishable.

Geographic distribution of chemotypes shows some correlation with latitude and climate. Thamnolic acid chemotypes are more common in oceanic regions, while squamatic acid chemotypes predominate in continental interiors.

Both compounds have documented antimicrobial and antioxidant properties, though these have been more extensively studied in thamnolic acid. The ecological function of these compounds is likely UV protection and anti-herbivore defense.
