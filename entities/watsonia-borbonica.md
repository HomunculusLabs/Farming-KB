## Overview

Watsonia borbonica is a tall, showy cormous geophyte native to the Western Cape of South Africa. It is one of the most spectacular members of the genus, producing spikes of pink to salmon flowers that can exceed 1.5 m in height.

The species was formerly known as Watsonia pyramidata and is still sold under that name in parts of the horticultural trade. It is widely grown as a garden ornamental and cut flower in Mediterranean-climate regions worldwide.

Watsonia borbonica is valued for its architectural form and long-lasting flower spikes. It blooms in late spring to early summer, providing color after many other Cape bulbs have finished flowering.

The species has naturalized in parts of California, Australia, and New Zealand. In these regions it is sometimes found growing along roadsides and disturbed sites.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Monocots
- **Order:** Asparagales
- **Family:** Iridaceae
- **Genus:** Watsonia
- **Species:** W. borbonica (Pourr.) Goldblatt

The species was originally described as Antholyza borbonica by Pourret in 1788. Goldblatt transferred it to Watsonia in 1989 during his revision of the genus.

The synonym Watsonia pyramidata Andrews is still encountered in older horticultural literature. The species epithet "borbonica" refers to the Île de Bourbon (now Réunion), though the species is not native there.

Watsonia is a genus of approximately 52 species, almost entirely endemic to southern Africa. W. borbonica belongs to the section Watsonia, characterized by large, showy flowers.

The genus is closely related to Gladiolus but differs in its consistently fan-shaped leaf arrangement. Molecular studies confirm Watsonia as monophyletic within the tribe Watsonieae.

## Morphology

Watsonia borbonica grows from a large, flattened corm 30–50 mm in diameter. The corm tunic is of hard, woody layers that persist for several years.

The stem is erect, stout, and reaches 1.0–1.8 m tall including the inflorescence. It is branched only at the base in robust specimens.

Leaves are sword-shaped (ensiform), 6–10 in number, and 40–80 cm long. They are gray-green, tough, and arranged in a fan at the base.

The inflorescence is a dense, cylindrical spike bearing 15–30 flowers. Bracts are dry, brownish, and 20–35 mm long.

Flowers are zygomorphic, pink to salmon-orange, and 50–65 mm long. The perianth tube is curved and 25–40 mm long.

The three lower tepals are slightly narrower and may have deeper pink markings. Stamens are unilateral, arching below the upper tepal.

The style is slender, dividing into three filiform branches. The ovary is inferior, oblong, and densely pubescent.

Capsules are oblong, 15–25 mm long, with a short beak. Seeds are flattened, winged, and light brown, approximately 5–8 mm across.

## Distribution and Habitat

Watsonia borbonica is endemic to the Western Cape Province of South Africa. Its range extends from the Cape Peninsula east to the Hottentots Holland and Klein River Mountains.

The species grows on damp, south-facing slopes and along stream banks. It is typically found at elevations of 100–600 m in areas receiving 600–1200 mm annual rainfall.

Associated vegetation types include mountain fynbos and moist proteoid shrubland. The species prefers acidic, nutrient-poor soils derived from Table Mountain sandstone.

Populations are often locally abundant where conditions are suitable. Large colonies can form spectacular floral displays during peak bloom from November to January.

The species occasionally hybridizes with Watsonia tabularis where their ranges overlap. Natural hybrids show intermediate flower color and spike density.

The species is adapted to winter rainfall with summer dormancy. Corms survive the dry season underground, resprouting with the first autumn rains.

## Cultivation

Watsonia borbonica is one of the easiest Watsonia species to grow in cultivation. It requires a Mediterranean climate with moist winters and dry summers.

Plant corms in autumn at a depth of 8–12 cm in well-drained soil. Full sun exposure produces the strongest stems and best flowering.

The species benefits from regular watering during the growing season but resents waterlogging. A deep watering once per week during active growth is generally sufficient.

Mulching with compost in autumn provides nutrients without requiring high-phosphorus fertilizers. The species is adapted to low-nutrient fynbos soils.

Staking may be required in exposed sites where wind can topple the tall flower spikes. Cut flower stems last 7–10 days in water.

Propagation is by seed or division of corm clumps. Large clumps should be divided every 3–4 years to maintain vigor.

The species is frost-tender and sustains damage below −3 °C. In colder regions, corms can be lifted and overwintered in a frost-free location.

Pests include aphids on young flower buds and slugs on emerging foliage. Good air circulation around plantings helps minimize fungal problems.

## Ecological Role

The long tubular flowers are adapted for pollination by long-proboscid flies and sunbirds. The primary pollinator is the long-tongued fly Philoliche rostrata.

Watsonia borbonica provides nectar for a range of nectar-feeding birds during the late spring flowering period. This food resource is important when few other nectar sources are available.

The tall, dry flowering stems persist through summer and provide perching sites for small birds. Dead foliage contributes to the thatch-like ground cover typical of fynbos.

The species is not currently considered threatened. However, habitat loss from urban expansion and invasive alien plants affects some populations.

Invasive species such as Australian acacias alter fire regimes and soil nitrogen levels, potentially displacing Watsonia populations. Active management of invasive species benefits this and other Cape geophytes.
