# Comfrey (Symphytum officinale)

## Overview

Comfrey is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Boraginaceae, native to Europe and naturalized across temperate regions worldwide. Renowned in permaculture as one of the most valuable dynamic accumulator plants, comfrey is prized for its deep taproot system that mines minerals from deep subsoil layers and brings them to the surface in its nutrient-dense foliage. It has been used medicinally for millennia (dating back to ancient Greek physicians) and remains one of the most important multi-functional plants in sustainable agriculture and permaculture design.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Angiospermae
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Boraginales
- **Family:** Boraginaceae
- **Genus:** Symphytum
- **Species:** S. officinale

### Common Names

Common comfrey, boneset, knitbone, bruisewort, slippery root, wallwort, gum plant, blackwort, healing herb. The name "knitbone" reflects its traditional use in promoting bone healing.

### Cultivated Varieties

- **Bocking 14:** Russian comfrey (*S. × uplandicum*) — sterile hybrid, most widely cultivated in permaculture; high biomass production; cannot self-seed (non-invasive)
- **Bocking 4:** Another sterile Russian comfrey cultivar; deeper rooted, preferred for medicinal use (though internal use is now discouraged)
- **Common comfrey (*S. officinale*):** Seeds freely; considered invasive in some regions; lower biomass than Bocking 14
- **Prickly comfrey (*S. asperum*):** Less commonly cultivated; can hybridize with common comfrey

## Botanical Description

Comfrey is a robust, clump-forming perennial growing 60–150 cm tall with a spread of 60–120 cm. It produces a thick, branching, black-skinned root system that can extend 2–3 meters deep into the soil, with some roots reported reaching depths exceeding 3 meters in well-drained soils.

**Stems:** Erect, hollow, angular, covered in coarse spreading hairs (bristly texture). Stems are winged at the base and branch in the upper portions.

**Leaves:** Large, broadly lanceolate to ovate, 15–40 cm long, arranged alternately along the stem. Lower leaves have winged petioles; upper leaves are sessile. Surfaces are rough and hairy. The leaves are the primary harvested part for mulch, compost, and liquid feed production.

**Flowers:** Bell-shaped (campanulate), pendulous, borne in terminal scorpioid cymes (characteristic of Boraginaceae). Colors range from creamy-white to purple, pink, or blue, depending on variety. Each flower is approximately 1.5 cm long. Flowers bloom from May through July and are attractive to bees, particularly bumblebees.

**Roots:** Thick, fleshy, branching taproot system. The root is black externally and white internally, containing allantoin (up to 0.8% dry weight), a compound that promotes cell proliferation.

## Dynamic Accumulation

Comfrey's primary value in permaculture derives from its role as a dynamic accumulator — a plant that mines nutrients from deep soil layers and concentrates them in its above-ground biomass.

### Mineral Content (per 100 g dried leaves)

| Mineral | Content |
|---------|---------|
| Potassium (K) | 2,500–3,500 mg |
| Nitrogen (N) | 2,500–3,200 mg |
| Calcium (Ca) | 1,500–2,000 mg |
| Phosphorus (P) | 200–350 mg |
| Magnesium (Mg) | 200–400 mg |
| Iron (Fe) | 15–50 mg |
| Manganese (Mn) | 5–20 mg |
| Zinc (Zn) | 3–10 mg |
| Copper (Cu) | 1–5 mg |
| Selenium (Se) | Trace |

The exceptionally high potassium content makes comfrey especially valuable as a potassium source for fruiting and flowering plants. Its deep root system accesses minerals beyond the reach of most annual crops, effectively bringing subsoil fertility to the surface.

## Permaculture Functions

### Mulch Material

Comfrey leaves can be cut 4–6 times per growing season, producing 3–5 kg of fresh biomass per plant per cut. The leaves break down rapidly when applied as surface mulch, releasing nutrients into the soil within 2–4 weeks. This "chop and drop" approach provides a sustained nutrient release equivalent to a slow-release organic fertilizer.

### Compost Activator

Adding comfrey leaves to compost piles accelerates decomposition due to their high nitrogen and moisture content. The leaves also contribute significant potassium and trace minerals, producing a balanced, nutrient-rich compost.

### Comfrey Liquid Feed (Tea)

Comfrey tea is one of the most potent homemade liquid fertilizers. Preparation involves steeping comfrey leaves in water for 2–6 weeks:

- **Standard method:** Fill a container with comfrey leaves, weight down with a stone, cover, and let decompose in water. Dilute 1:10 with water before application.
- **No-smell method:** Stuff leaves into a pipe with drainage holes; collect the dark liquid that drains out over several weeks. This concentrated liquid is diluted 1:15–20.
- The resulting liquid feed is particularly rich in potassium, making it ideal for tomatoes, peppers, fruiting vegetables, and fruit trees.

### Nutrient Mining

Planted beneath fruit trees, comfrey's deep roots access leached minerals and bring them within the root zone of shallower-rooted trees. This creates a biological nutrient cycling system that reduces or eliminates the need for imported fertilizers.

### Erosion Control

Comfrey's dense root mass helps stabilize soil on slopes, banks, and swale edges. The plant tolerates periodic waterlogging and can be used in riparian zone plantings.

### Bee Forage

Comfrey flowers are prolific nectar and pollen sources, blooming over an extended period. They are particularly valued by bumblebees and long-tongued bee species. Planting comfrey in polyculture systems supports pollinator populations that benefit nearby crops.

### Weed Suppression

Mature comfrey plants form dense clumps that shade out competing vegetation. The plant's rapid spring growth gives it a competitive advantage over most weeds, though it will not suppress established aggressive perennials like bindweed.

## Propagation

### Root Cuttings (Preferred)

Bocking 14, being sterile, can only be propagated vegetatively. Root cuttings are the standard method:

1. Dig up an established plant in spring or autumn
2. Cut roots into 2–5 cm sections
3. Plant horizontally 2–5 cm deep in prepared soil
4. Keep moist; sprouting occurs in 2–4 weeks
5. A single mature plant can yield 50–100 root cuttings

### Crown Division

Divide established clumps in early spring or autumn. Each division should contain at least one growing bud and a section of root. Space divisions 60–90 cm apart.

### Seed (Common comfrey only)

Common comfrey seeds require cold stratification (30 days at 4°C) for reliable germination. Sow in spring, 1 cm deep. Seed viability is generally low (30–50%). Note: common comfrey can be invasive via self-seeding — Bocking 14 is strongly preferred.

## Cultivation

- **Soil:** Tolerates most soil types; performs best in deep, moist, fertile loams. Tolerates clay and acidic soils.
- **pH:** 5.0–7.5 (tolerates acidic conditions better than most perennials)
- **Light:** Full sun to partial shade; tolerates significant shade, making it suitable for food forest understories
- **Water:** Moderate to high water needs; thrives with consistent moisture but tolerates drought once established due to deep root system
- **Spacing:** 60–90 cm between plants
- **Lifespan:** 20+ years; plants are extremely long-lived once established
- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 3–9

## Medicinal Use — Safety Warning

Comfrey has been used for centuries to heal wounds, fractures, and skin conditions. The root contains allantoin (a cell proliferant), rosmarinic acid (anti-inflammatory), and mucilage (soothing). **However, comfrey contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs), specifically lycopsamine and intermedine, which are hepatotoxic, mutagenic, and potentially carcinogenic.**

- **External use only:** Modern herbal practice restricts comfrey to topical applications (ointments, poultices, salves) for sprains, strains, bruises, and minor wounds
- **Internal use is not recommended** by regulatory agencies in the EU, UK, Canada, Australia, and the US (FDA advisory)
- PA-free comfrey cultivars exist but are not widely commercially available
- Do not use on deep wounds or broken skin, as PAs can be absorbed systemically
- Avoid during pregnancy and breastfeeding

## Pests and Diseases

Comfrey is remarkably pest- and disease-resistant. The coarse, hairy foliage deters most insect herbivores. Occasionally affected by:
- **Comfrey rust (*Melampsorella symphyti*):** Fungal disease causing orange pustules on leaves; remove and destroy affected foliage
- **Slugs and snails:** May damage young shoots in spring but rarely a serious problem on established plants
- **Root rot:** Can occur in waterlogged, poorly drained soils

## Integration in Polyculture Design

Comfrey is one of the most frequently recommended plants in permaculture food forests and polyculture systems. Standard placement includes:
- **Fruit tree guilds:** Planted in the drip line of fruit trees as a nutrient miner and mulch source
- **Swale edges:** Planted on berms to stabilize soil and mine nutrients from the water-infiltrated zone
- **Companion planting:** Near tomatoes, peppers, and potatoes for potassium supplementation
- **Chicken forage zones:** Chickens will eat young comfrey leaves; the high protein content is beneficial
- **Compost corner planting:** Dedicated comfrey beds adjacent to composting areas for easy harvest
