# Comfrey (Symphytum officinale)

## Overview

Common comfrey (*Symphytum officinale*) is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Boraginaceae, widely regarded as one of the most valuable plants in **permaculture**, **organic gardening**, and **biodynamic agriculture**. Renowned for its exceptional ability to accumulate nutrients from deep soil layers — earning it the designation **"dynamic accumulator"** — comfrey has been called "the permaculture practitioner's best friend." It serves simultaneously as a nutrient-mining companion plant, a high-protein animal fodder, a medicinal herb (external use), a compost accelerator, and a liquid fertilizer factory. Despite its importance in regenerative agriculture, internal medicinal use is controversial due to the presence of **pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)**, which are hepatotoxic.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Eudicots
- **Clade:** Boraginales
- **Family:** Boraginaceae
- **Genus:** *Symphytum*
- **Species:** *S. officinale*

### Related Species & Cultivars

- ***Symphytum × uplandicum*** (Russian Comfrey, Quaker Comfrey) — the most commonly cultivated variety in permaculture; sterile triploid hybrid (*S. officinale* × *S. asperum*); does not set seed, propagated vegetatively; higher biomass yield than common comfrey; varieties include **'Bocking 4'** (narrow leaves, deep roots, highest protein) and **'Bocking 14'** (broad leaves, highest biomass yield)
- ***Symphytum asperum*** (Prickly Comfrey) — parent of Russian comfrey; spreads by seed; considered invasive in some regions
- ***Symphytum tuberosum*** (Tuberous Comfrey) — smaller species, native to Europe; lower nutrient accumulation

## Botanical Description

Comfrey is a robust, clump-forming perennial:

- **Height:** 60–150 cm (2–5 feet)
- **Spread:** 60–120 cm (2–4 feet); forms dense, expanding clumps
- **Roots:** Deep taproot system reaching **1.5–3 meters** (5–10 feet) into subsoil; thick, fleshy, black-skinned with white interior; nearly impossible to eradicate once established (any root fragment regenerates)
- **Stems:** Hairy, angular, winged at base, branched above; exude mucilaginous sap when cut
- **Leaves:** Large, lanceolate to ovate, 15–30 cm long; rough-hairy on both surfaces; lower leaves petiolate, upper sessile; form a dense basal rosette
- **Flowers:** Bell-shaped, pendulous, 12–18 mm long; colors range from cream-white to purple, pink, or blue (depending on cultivar); borne in scorpioid cymes; bloom May–July (Northern Hemisphere)
- **Seeds:** Four nutlets per flower; Russian comfrey (*S. × uplandicum*) is **sterile** — produces no viable seed, which prevents unwanted spread

## Nutrient Accumulation (Dynamic Accumulator)

Comfrey's most celebrated property is its ability to mine **macro- and micronutrients** from deep subsoil horizons and concentrate them in its leaves:

### Leaf Composition (Dry Weight Basis)

| Nutrient | Percentage (dry weight) | Comparison |
|----------|------------------------|------------|
| **Nitrogen (N)** | 4.0–8.0% | Higher than farmyard manure |
| **Potassium (K)** | 3.0–7.0% | 2–3× farmyard manure; comparable to concentrated kelp |
| **Calcium (Ca)** | 1.5–3.0% | Excellent for preventing blossom end rot |
| **Phosphorus (P)** | 0.3–0.8% | Moderate; supplemental P may be needed |
| **Magnesium (Mg)** | 0.2–0.5% | |
| **Iron (Fe)** | High | |
| **Trace minerals** | Significant | Includes boron, copper, zinc, selenium |

### Why This Matters

Comfrey's deep taproot (up to 3 m) accesses **nutrient pools unavailable to shallow-rooted crops**. It effectively "mines" calcium, potassium, and trace minerals from subsoil layers and brings them to the surface in bioavailable form through leaf decomposition and liquid extract. This makes comfrey an exceptional:

- **Comfrey tea (liquid fertilizer):** Fermented leaf extract provides a balanced NPK feed (~3:1:5 ratio)
- **Chop-and-drop mulch:** Leaves decompose rapidly (high water content), releasing nutrients to surface-feeding crops
- **Compost activator:** High nitrogen content accelerates decomposition of carbon-rich materials (wood chips, straw, cardboard)

## Permaculture Applications

### 1. Liquid Fertilizer (Comfrey Tea)

The most common permaculture use:

1. Harvest comfrey leaves (avoid flowering stems)
2. Pack leaves into a container (bucket or barrel); weight down with a stone
3. Fill with water (optional — some prefer anaerobic fermentation without water for a more concentrated product)
4. Cover loosely and let ferment for 3–6 weeks
5. Dilute 1:10 (with water) for foliar feed; 1:5 for soil drench
6. **Warning:** Fermentation produces extremely strong odor — place away from living areas

**Alternative (non-smelly method):** Steep chopped leaves in water for 24–48 hours; use immediately. Less concentrated but odor-free.

### 2. Chop-and-Drop Mulch

- Cut comfrey 3–5 times per growing season (established plants)
- Lay fresh leaves as a mulch layer around nutrient-hungry crops (tomatoes, peppers, potatoes, fruit trees)
- Leaves decompose in 2–4 weeks, releasing nutrients directly to soil
- Yields of 2–5 kg of fresh leaf matter per plant per cut are common

### 3. Compost Accelerator

- Layer comfrey leaves between carbon-rich materials in compost piles
- The high nitrogen and moisture content kickstarts thermophilic decomposition
- Particularly effective for breaking down woody materials, cardboard, and autumn leaves

### 4. Biodynamic Preparation (BD 505)

In biodynamic agriculture, oak bark compost preparation (BD 505) is sometimes associated with comfrey due to its calcium-accumulating properties. Comfrey itself is valued in biodynamic practice for its silica and potassium content.

### 5. Companion Planting

- **Fruit trees:** Plant comfrey in the drip line to mine nutrients and provide mulch; reduce grass competition
- **Potato beds:** High potassium demand of potatoes matches comfrey's K-rich leaf profile
- **Tomato beds:** Calcium from comfrey helps prevent blossom end rot; potassium improves fruit quality
- **Pest attraction:** Comfrey flowers attract **pollinators** (bees, bumblebees) and **beneficial predatory insects** (hoverflies, parasitic wasps)

## Cultivation

### Establishment

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Propagation** | Root cuttings (most common); crown division; offsets |
| **Root cuttings** | 5–8 cm sections of root, planted 5 cm deep, spring or autumn |
| **Spacing** | 60–90 cm apart |
| **Soil preference** | Tolerates most soils; thrives in deep, moist, fertile loam |
| **pH** | 6.0–7.5 |
| **Water** | Moderate to high; tolerates waterlogging better than most herbs |
| **Sun** | Full sun to partial shade |
| **Hardiness** | USDA zones 3–9 |
| **Time to first harvest** | Year 1: light cut; Year 2+: full harvest (3–5 cuts/season) |
| **Lifespan** | 20+ years once established |

### Cultivar Selection for Permaculture

| Cultivar | Key Features | Best For |
|----------|-------------|----------|
| **Bocking 14** | Broad leaves, highest biomass yield, sterile | Mulch, compost, liquid feed |
| **Bocking 4** | Narrow, deep-rooted leaves, highest protein (~35% dry weight) | Animal fodder, highest nutrient mining |
| **Common comfrey (S. officinale)** | Medicinal tradition, sets seed | Heritage gardens (caution: can self-seed aggressively) |

### Maintenance

- Cut before flowering for maximum leaf nutrient content (flowering redirects energy to seed production)
- Leave at least 10 cm of growth above ground to allow regrowth
- Divide clumps every 4–5 years to maintain vigor
- **Eradication warning:** Do not rototill comfrey — root fragments will propagate aggressively. To remove, dig out all root material or repeatedly cut to exhaust root reserves over 1–2 seasons

## Safety & Toxicity

### Pyrrolizidine Alkaloids (PAs)

Comfrey contains hepatotoxic PAs, primarily **symphytine**, **echimidine**, and **lycopsamine**:

- **Hepatotoxicity:** PAs cause **hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD)** — irreversible damage to liver veins
- **Carcinogenicity:** PAs are genotoxic and classified as possible human carcinogens (IARC Group 2B)
- **Internal use:** **Not recommended.** The German Commission E, UK MHRA, and FDA have all issued warnings against internal consumption of comfrey preparations
- **External use:** Generally regarded as safe for short-term topical application (compresses, salves) for wounds, sprains, and bruises. PAs are poorly absorbed through intact skin
- **Russian comfrey (Bocking varieties):** Contains lower PA levels than common comfrey but is not PA-free

### Safe Use Guidelines

- **External only:** Use as poultice, compress, or infused oil for topical applications
- **Duration:** Limit topical use to 4–6 weeks per year
- **Avoid** if pregnant, breastfeeding, or with liver disease
- **Do not** use on open wounds where PA absorption may be enhanced
