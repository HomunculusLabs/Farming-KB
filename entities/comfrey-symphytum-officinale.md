# Comfrey (Symphytum officinale)

## Overview

Comfrey (*Symphytum officinale*) is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Boraginaceae, native to Europe and naturalized across temperate regions worldwide. Renowned in permaculture for its role as a **dynamic accumulator** — a deep-rooted plant that mines subsoil nutrients and deposits them in bioavailable form through its leaves — comfrey is one of the most widely cultivated multipurpose plants in sustainable food systems. Its traditional medicinal use for wound healing and bone repair spans over two millennia, though internal use is now strongly cautioned due to hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs). In permaculture design, comfrey serves as a nutrient miner, compost activator, mulch crop, liquid fertilizer source, and pollinator support plant.

## Taxonomy & Botany

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Boraginales
- **Family:** Boraginaceae
- **Genus:** *Symphytum*
- **Species:** *S. officinale* L.
- **Common names:** Common comfrey, knitbone, boneset, bruisewort, slippery root

The genus *Symphytum* comprises approximately 25–35 species. *S. officinale* is the type species and is distinguished from its close relative *S. × uplandicum* (Russian comfrey) by flower color, chromosome count, and sterility characteristics.

## Morphology & Identification

Comfrey grows 0.6–1.5 m tall from a thick, branching, black-skinned taproot that may extend 2–3 m vertically. Basal leaves are large (15–30 cm long), broadly lanceolate, covered in coarse hairs, and form a spreading rosette. Stem leaves are progressively smaller and decurrent. Flowering stems bear cymose inflorescences of bell-shaped flowers, typically cream to pale purple with a narrow corolla tube, blooming May through July. The fruit consists of four glossy brown nutlets. The entire plant is notably mucilaginous when bruised.

## Chemical Constituents

- **Allantoin (0.1–1.0%):** A cell-proliferant and wound-healing compound; the principal bioactive constituent responsible for comfrey's traditional reputation in tissue repair.
- **Rosmarinic acid:** A phenolic compound with significant anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity.
- **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs):** Hepatotoxic alkaloids including lycopsamine, intermedine, and their N-oxide derivatives. Concentrated primarily in roots (up to 0.3%) and, to a lesser extent, in young leaves. These are the compounds of primary toxicological concern.
- **Mucilage (up to 30%):** Polysaccharides providing demulcent and soothing properties for topical applications.
- **Tannins:** Astringent polyphenols contributing to wound-drying and mild antimicrobial effects.
- **Triterpenes and saponins:** Present in minor quantities with various pharmacological activities.
- **Vitamins B12 and C:** Notable for a plant source, though amounts are variable and not nutritionally significant at typical intake levels.

## Permaculture Applications

### Dynamic Accumulator

Comfrey is the prototypical dynamic accumulator in permaculture literature. Its deep taproot mines potassium (K), calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), magnesium (Mg), iron (Fe), and trace minerals from subsoil horizons inaccessible to shallow-rooted plants. These nutrients are concentrated in the leaf biomass, which can be harvested 4–6 times per growing season and cycled back into the system.

### Comfrey Tea (Liquid Fertilizer)

Fermented comfrey tea is a high-potassium liquid organic fertilizer widely used in permaculture and organic horticulture. Typical approximate NPK analysis of comfrey leaf material and its preparations:

| Preparation              | N      | P₂O₅   | K₂O    |
|--------------------------|--------|--------|--------|
| Fresh leaf dry weight    | ~3.0%  | ~0.8%  | ~8.0%  |
| Comfrey tea (fermented)  | ~0.5%  | ~0.1%  | ~3.0%  |

The high potassium-to-nitrogen ratio makes comfrey tea particularly valuable for fruiting and flowering crops (tomatoes, peppers, berries, fruit trees). To prepare: pack fresh comfrey leaves into a container, weight them, cover with water, and ferment for 3–6 weeks. Dilute 1:10 before application. The odor is notoriously pungent.

### Compost Activator

Comfrey leaves added to compost piles supply nitrogen and act as a biological activator, accelerating microbial decomposition. A layer of comfrey between green and brown materials is a standard composting technique.

### Mulch

Fresh comfrey leaves laid as a living or harvested mulch around crops suppress weeds, retain soil moisture, and slowly release nutrients as they decompose. The high potassium content is particularly beneficial for potassium-hungry crops.

### Companion Planting

Comfrey planted beneath fruit trees serves as a nutrient pump, drawing minerals from deep soil and making them available to shallow tree feeder roots via leaf decomposition. It also suppresses competitive grasses through its dense canopy.

### Pollinator Support

The prolonged flowering period (May–July) provides nectar and pollen for bumblebees, long-tongued bees, and other pollinators during a period when many other nectar sources are scarce.

## Traditional Medicinal Uses

Comfrey has a documented medicinal history spanning over 2,000 years, from Dioscorides through medieval herbalism to modern phytotherapy. Its principal traditional indications include:

- **Wound healing:** Allantoin promotes cell proliferation and tissue regeneration in minor wounds, cuts, and abrasions.
- **Bone knitting (symphytum = "grow together"):** Traditional use for fractures and sprains, supported by limited in vitro evidence for osteoblast stimulation. The homeopathic preparation *Symphytum* is still used for bone injuries.
- **Anti-inflammatory:** Rosmarinic acid and allantoin provide anti-inflammatory effects for bruises, sprains, and arthritic joints.
- **Demulcent:** Mucilage soothes irritated skin and mucous membranes.

**Safety warning:** Internal use of comfrey-containing products is banned or restricted in many countries (EU, Australia, Canada) due to confirmed hepatotoxicity and carcinogenicity of pyrrolizidine alkaloids. Topical use on unbroken skin for short durations is generally regarded as lower risk, though PA absorption through damaged skin remains a concern. Never use during pregnancy or lactation.

## Cultivation & Propagation

Comfrey thrives in USDA Hardiness Zones 3–9 in full sun to partial shade. It prefers deep, moist, fertile loams but tolerates clay, poor soils, and waterlogged conditions. Once established, comfrey is extremely persistent and difficult to eradicate — site selection should be permanent.

- **Root cuttings:** The most common propagation method. Sections of root 2–5 cm long are planted horizontally 2–5 cm deep in spring. Virtually every root fragment can generate a new plant, making eradication of unwanted stands very challenging.
- **Crown divisions:** Established clumps can be divided in early spring or autumn. Each division should contain viable crown buds and root mass.
- **Seed:** *S. officinale* produces viable seed, though germination can be uneven. Cultivars such as Bocking 14 are sterile and must be vegetatively propagated.
- **Spacing:** Plant 60–90 cm apart for dense biomass production.
- **Harvest:** Begin cutting 5–8 cm above ground once plants reach ~60 cm tall. Allow regrowth between cuts; typically 4–6 harvests per year.

## Ecological Benefits

- **Soil structure improvement:** The deep taproot creates channels through compacted subsoil, improving water infiltration and aeration. This "biological subsoiler" effect is valuable in no-till and minimum-tillage systems.
- **Micronutrient cycling:** By drawing minerals from deep soil profiles and depositing them in decomposable leaf litter at the surface, comfrey participates in a vertical nutrient cycling mechanism unavailable to most crop plants.
- **Erosion control:** Dense root systems and rapid above-ground growth stabilize banks, slopes, and riparian margins.
- **Biomass production:** Yields of 2–5 kg fresh leaf matter per plant per year make comfrey one of the highest-yielding perennial biomass crops in temperate climates.

## Safety & Toxicity

The primary safety concern is **pyrrolizidine alkaloid (PA) hepatotoxicity**. PAs are metabolized by hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes into reactive pyrrolic esters that cause veno-occlusive disease (VOD) of the liver. Chronic or high-dose exposure has been linked to hepatic fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatic veno-occlusive disease. PAs are also genotoxic and classified as possible human carcinogens (IARC Group 2B). Key safety points:

- Internal ingestion of any comfrey preparation is not recommended.
- PA content varies among species and cultivars; *S. × uplandicum* cv. 'Bocking 14' is reported to have lower PA concentrations than wild *S. officinale*.
- Topical application to intact skin carries lower but non-zero risk of systemic absorption.
- Always consult a healthcare professional before using comfrey medicinally.

## Species & Cultivars

### *Symphytum officinale* (Common Comfrey)

The wild species, diploid (2n=24), producing viable seed. Flowers range from cream to purplish. Higher PA content in leaves compared to cultivated varieties.

### *Symphytum × uplandicum* (Russian Comfrey)

A natural hybrid (*S. officinale* × *S. asperum*), triploid (2n=36), generally sterile. More vigorous, larger biomass, and taller stature than *S. officinale*. Flowers are typically blue-violet. Several PA-free or PA-reduced cultivars have been developed:

- **Bocking 4:** Vigorous, deep-rooted, high biomass; preferred for permaculture biomass production.
- **Bocking 14:** The most widely available sterile cultivar. High biomass, deep taproot, relatively lower PA content. Developed by Lawrence D. Hills at the Henry Doubleday Research Association (HDRA) in the 1950s. Standard choice for comfrey tea and permaculture applications.
- **Bocking 2 and 16:** Additional selections for specific conditions, less commonly available.
