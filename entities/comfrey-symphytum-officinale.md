# Comfrey (Symphytum officinale)

## Overview

Comfrey is a perennial herbaceous plant in the Boraginaceae family that has earned legendary status in both traditional herbal medicine and modern permaculture practice. Native to Europe and temperate Asia, it has naturalized widely across [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]] and other temperate regions. Its deep taproot system—capable of reaching depths of 3 meters or more—makes it one of the most effective **dynamic accumulators** in the permaculture toolkit, mining minerals from deep subsoil layers and bringing them to the surface through its leaves.

In permaculture, comfrey is valued as a nutrient-mining biomass factory, a compost activator, a mulch crop, a livestock fodder, and a companion plant. In herbal medicine, it has been used for millennia for wound healing, bone repair, and skin conditions—though internal use is now controversial due to the presence of hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs). The distinction between wild *Symphytum officinale* and cultivated hybrids (particularly the 'Bocking' cultivars) is important for both safety and horticultural purposes.

### Related Species and Cultivars

- ***Symphytum × uplandicum*** (Russian Comfrey): A natural hybrid (*S. officinale* × *S. asperum*); more vigorous and sterile; basis of the Bocking cultivars
- **Bocking 4:** The most commonly cultivated comfrey; sterile triploid; developed by Lawrence D. Hills at the Henry Doubleday Research Association; large leaves, high biomass yield, non-invasive
- **Bocking 14:** Similar to Bocking 4 with slightly narrower leaves; also sterile; preferred by some growers for easier harvesting
- **Wild comfrey (*S. officinale*):** Fertile, can spread by seed; contains higher levels of pyrrolizidine alkaloids
- **Prickly comfrey (*S. asperum*):** Rough-haired leaves; less commonly cultivated

## Botanical Description

### Growth Habit

Comfrey forms large, robust clumps 60–150 cm tall and up to 120 cm wide. It has a thick, branching, deep-penetrating root system that is nearly impossible to remove once established—hence the common name "knitbone," referring both to its medicinal use and its tenacious roots.

### Leaves and Flowers

Large, broadly lanceolate leaves (15–60 cm) with rough hairs; tubular, bell-shaped flowers
in scorpioid cymes. Color varies: purple-violet (wild), violet-blue (Bocking cultivars).

### Root System

The defining feature is its extraordinary root system:

- **Taproot depth:** 2–3 meters in well-drained soils
- **Regeneration:** Any root fragment >2 cm can regenerate into a new plant

## Dynamic Accumulation

Comfrey's role as a **dynamic accumulator** is its most valued permaculture function. The deep taproot mines minerals from subsoil and deposits them in above-ground biomass, which can then be harvested and redistributed through mulch, compost, or liquid feed.

### Mineral Content of Dried Leaves

| Mineral | Concentration | Significance |
|---------|--------------|--------------|
| Potassium (K) | 3.0–4.8% | Excellent source; comparable to banana ash |
| Nitrogen (N) | 3.0–4.0% | High protein content in leaves |
| Phosphorus (P) | 0.3–0.8% | Moderate accumulator |
| Calcium (Ca) | 2.0–3.2% | Excellent for soil amendment |

## Permaculture Applications

### Biomass Production

Under optimal conditions, Bocking 14 comfrey can produce:

- **Annual yield:** 3–5 kg of fresh leaf material per plant (4–6 cuts per season)
- **Per hectare:** 50–100+ tonnes of fresh biomass per year
- **Dried yield:** Approximately 20–25% of fresh weight

### Comfrey Tea (Liquid Fertilizer)

Pack leaves in a container, cover with water (1:1 ratio), steep 3–6 weeks sealed. Dilute
1:10 and apply as liquid feed. Rich in potassium — ideal for flowering and fruiting plants.

### Mulch

Cut leaves laid directly as mulch release nutrients slowly, [[query-how-do-cover-crops-suppress-weeds]], and benefit
potassium-hungry crops like tomatoes, potatoes, and currants.

Comfrey leaves can be cut and laid directly as mulch around garden plants. As they decompose, they release nutrients slowly and suppress weeds. The high potassium content particularly benefits potassium-hungry crops like tomatoes, potatoes, currants, and fruit trees.

### Compost Activator

High nitrogen content accelerates compost decomposition. Layer with carbon-rich materials
for optimal thermophilic composting.

Adding comfrey leaves to compost piles accelerates decomposition. The high nitrogen content provides energy for compost microorganisms, and the mineral diversity enriches the finished compost. Layering comfrey with carbon-rich materials (straw, cardboard, wood chips) creates optimal C:N ratios for thermophilic composting.

## Cultivation

### Growing Conditions

Full sun to partial shade; adaptable to most soils (prefers deep, moist loam); pH 5.0–8.0;
tolerates drought once established; hardy to USDA Zone 3 (-40°C).

### Propagation

Bocking cultivars are sterile and must be propagated vegetatively by root cuttings (2–5 cm
sections, 90%+ success rate), crown divisions, or offsets. Best done in spring.

## Medicinal Use and Safety

### Traditional Applications

Used for over 2,000 years for wound healing, bone repair (hence "knitbone"), burns, ulcers,
arthritis, gout, muscle pain, eczema, and psoriasis.
### Allantoin

The primary wound-healing compound is **allantoin** (0.3–1.0% in dried root), a cell proliferant that stimulates cell division and tissue regeneration. Allantoin promotes granulation tissue formation, accelerates wound contraction, and has keratolytic properties.

### Pyrrolizidine Alkaloid (PA) Warning

All *Symphytum* species contain hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) including symphytine,
echimidine, and lycopsamine. PAs cause hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD). Internal use is
**not recommended** and banned in many countries. External use of PA-free preparations is
generally considered safe. Bocking 14 has lower PA levels but is not PA-free.
