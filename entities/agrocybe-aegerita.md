# Agrocybe aegerita

## Introduction

**Agrocybe aegerita** (Brigantini) Singer, commonly known as the Black Poplar
Mushroom, Swordbelt Agrocybe, Pioppino (Italian), Yanagi-matsutake (Japanese),
or South Poplar Mushroom (Chinese), is a substantial and flavorful edible
mushroom prized across southern Europe and Asia. It is a variable fungus which
may eventually be split into several taxa.

Once classified as *Pholiota aegerita*, it has also been known under the
synonym *Agrocybe cylindracea* (DC. ex Fr.) Maire, a name preferred by many
Asian mycologists. Additional synonyms include *Pholiota cylindracea* Gillet
(Singer 1986). Related species *Agrocybe molesta* and *A. praecox* exist but
are not considered as flavorful as *A. aegerita*.

## Description

This is a substantial mushroom, often reaching up to 12 inches in diameter when
fully mature. The cap is convex to hemispheric in youth, expanding to plane
with age. The cap surface is smooth, ranging in color from yellowish gray to
grayish brown to tan to dingy brown, typically darker towards the center.

The gills are gray at first, becoming chocolate brown with spore maturity. The
stem is white and features a well-developed membranous ring, which is usually
colored brown from spore fall. The overall stature of the mushroom is robust
and meaty, making it an attractive species for both cultivation and
foraging enthusiasts.

**Microscopic features:** Spores are smooth, ovoid to slightly ellipsoid, brown
in deposit, measuring 9-11 x 5-6.5(7) μm, and lack a distinct germ pore. Clamp
connections are present. The spore print is dark brown.

## Distribution

In North America, *Agrocybe aegerita* is not known outside the southeastern
United States, specifically reported from Mississippi, Louisiana, and Georgia.
It is common across southern Europe and in similar climatic zones of the Far
East, where it is widely cultivated and commercially appreciated. The species
appears to be restricted to warm-temperate and subtropical regions.

## Natural Habitat

*Agrocybe aegerita* grows saprophytically, often in dense clusters, on stumps
and logs of various hardwood species. It strongly prefers hardwood substrates,
particularly cottonwoods, willows, poplars, maples, and box elders. In China,
it has been recorded growing on tea-oil trees.

The mycelium is longitudinally linear, becoming cottony but usually not aerial.
It is white at first, soon develops spotted brown discoloration, and eventually
becomes tan brown. Primordia typically form readily on malt extract [[agar]]. The
fragrance of the mycelium is mealy and farinaceous, not particularly pleasant.

## Cultivation

*Agrocybe aegerita* is an excellent candidate for stump recycling, especially
in the southeastern United States. The willow-populated swamps of Louisiana are
considered ideal for deliberate outdoor cultivation. Large diameter willow,
poplar, and cottonwood stumps could sustain massive fruitings for multiple
years. Outdoor stump cultivation represents a low-maintenance approach to
growing this species in suitable climates.

When cultivated on supplemented sterilized sawdust, chips, and bran blocks,
yields can reach up to 1 lb of fresh mushrooms per 5-6 lb of substrate. The
mushroom benefits from a 1/2 inch casing layer, and cluster formation is
encouraged for best results. Harvesting before the veils break ensures a longer
shelf life. The mushroom is marketed fresh.

## Medicinal Properties

No medicinal properties are currently known for *Agrocybe aegerita*, though
closely related species within the genus have been documented to produce unique
antibiotics. Further research into the bioactive compounds of this species may
reveal therapeutic potential. [[paul-stamets]] has noted the genus warrants
further pharmacological investigation given its ecological success on hardwood
substrates and its widespread use in traditional food cultures.

## Flavor and Preparation

*Agrocybe aegerita* offers a mild but satisfying flavor often described as
pork-like. It is versatile in the kitchen and may be finely chopped and
stir-fried, cooked in white sauce served over fish or chicken, or baked in
stuffing. Its substantial texture holds up well to various cooking methods.

In Italian cuisine, where it is known as Pioppino, it is often paired with
pasta and rich sauces. In Japanese cuisine, as Yanagi-matsutake, it is valued
for its firm texture and nutty undertones. Comparable in culinary appeal to
species like [[pleurotus-ostreatus]] and [[lentinula-edodes]].
