## Description

Table Mountain is a prominent flat-topped mountain forming a major landmark overlooking the city of Cape Town, South Africa. Rising approximately 1,085 meters (3,558 feet) above sea level, it is cited by Mollison as a key example of fog condensation significantly exceeding local rainfall. Fog drip measurements on Table Mountain have recorded 130-330 cm of annual precipitation from condensation alone, demonstrating the enormous water-harvesting potential of mountains that intercept moist onshore airflows.

## Classification

- **Category:** Place — Mountain / Geographic feature
- **Location:** Cape Town, Western Cape, South Africa
- **Elevation:** 1,085 m (3,558 ft)
- **Geology:** Sandstone (Quartzitic Table Mountain Sandstone) over shale base
- **Climate:** Mediterranean climate with strong orographic and advection fog effects
- **Vegetation:** Fynbos (Cape Floral Kingdom, exceptionally diverse heathland)

## Key Facts

- Fog drip has been measured at 130-330 cm annually on Table Mountain, and in these measurements condensation exceeds rainfall as a water source.
- The mountain"s flat top and steep cliffs intercept humid airflows from the Atlantic Ocean and False Bay, creating ideal conditions for orographic condensation and fog drip.
- The fynbos vegetation on Table Mountain is adapted to capture moisture from frequent cloud cover and fog, functioning as a natural condensation surface similar to cloud forests.
- Table Mountain's water-condensing capacity illustrates the general principle that wherever mountains or foothills face onshore night winds, fog condensation will probably exceed rainfall.
- The mountain's unique shape (flat plateau) maximizes horizontal interception of moving fog and cloud, making it a textbook example of topographic moisture harvesting.
- Part of the Cape Floral Region UNESCO World Heritage Site, containing over 1,470 plant species, many endemic.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Proof of condensation precipitation:** Table Mountain provides quantitative evidence that fog condensation can far exceed rainfall, validating [[permaculture]] strategies that rely on tree-planting to capture atmospheric moisture rather than depending solely on rain.
- **Topographic water harvesting:** The mountain demonstrates how landform shape determines moisture capture, informing the design of swales, keyline systems, and tree plantings on slopes that face prevailing moist winds.
- **Vegetation as condensation surface:** The fynbos of Table Mountain shows how plant communities adapt to and enhance moisture condensation, supporting the design principle of matching vegetation to condensation-zone conditions.
- **Coastal site design:** For permaculture sites near coastlines with mountain or hill features, the Table Mountain example shows that the primary water source may be atmospheric rather than pluvial (rain-based), fundamentally changing the design approach.
- **Water security:** In regions where rainfall is seasonal or declining, designing landscapes to maximize fog and condensation capture provides a drought-resistant supplementary water source that operates independently of rainfall patterns.

## Broader Context

Table Mountain is part of a global pattern of condensation-dominated mountain ecosystems that includes the redwood forests of California, the laurel forests of the Canary Islands, cloud forests of Hawaii, and the temperate rainforests of Tasmania and Chile. All share the characteristic that their water budget depends more on fog interception than on direct rainfall, and all are threatened by climate change and deforestation.
