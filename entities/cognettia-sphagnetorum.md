## Description

**Cognettia sphagnetorum** is a species of enchytraeid worm (potworm) commonly found in acidic soils, particularly in coniferous forests and heathlands. It is an important omnivorous soil mesofauna organism that feeds on fungi, bacteria, and decaying organic matter, playing a significant role in detrital food webs.









## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Animalia
- **Phylum**: Annelida
- **Class**: Clitellata
- **Order**: Enchytraeida
- **Family**: Enchytraeidae
- **Genus**: Cognettia
- **Species**: C. sphagnetorum









## Key Facts

- Omnivorous enchytraeid species commonly used in [[soil-food-web]] experiments
- Setala (2000) studied its role in ectomycorrhizal Scots pine systems, finding it had a positive influence on pine growth particularly in the absence of [[mycorrhizal-fungi]]
- Densities of 83-98 individuals per experimental system reported in mycorrhizal studies
- Contributes to soil structure modification through burrowing and organic matter processing
- Considered a keystone species in acidic forest soil ecosystems









## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

C. sphagnetorum influences fungal community dynamics through grazing on both mycorrhizal and [[saprotrophic-fungi]]. In cultivation systems with acidic substrates (common in mycological operations), enchytraeids may affect mycelial establishment and fruiting body formation. Their role in decomposition and [[nutrient-mineralization]] makes them relevant to understanding substrate ecology in [[mushroom-cultivation]]. The species' positive influence on plant growth in the absence of mycorrhizae suggests it may enhance nutrient availability through microbial grazing and organic matter turnover.









## Ecological Role

As an ecosystem engineer in acidic soils, C. sphagnetorum enhances decomposition rates and nutrient cycling. It forms an important link in detrital food webs between microbial decomposers and higher trophic levels. Its presence has been shown to interact with mycorrhizal fungal colonization, though the complexity of these interactions depends on the broader soil faunal community.
