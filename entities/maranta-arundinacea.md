## Overview

**Maranta arundinacea**, commonly known as arrowroot, is a perennial tropical plant in the family Marantaceae. It is cultivated primarily for its starchy rhizomes, which yield a fine, easily digestible culinary starch.

The species is native to the Amazon basin and the Caribbean. Indigenous peoples of South America and the Caribbean have used arrowroot for food and medicine for at least 7,000 years.

Arrowroot starch is valued for its neutral flavor, smooth texture, and high gel clarity. It is used as a thickening agent in sauces, gravies, desserts, and infant foods.

Commercial production is concentrated in the Caribbean (St. Vincent and the Grenadines is the leading exporter), Brazil, and [[bloomfield-rust-germ-tube-tactile-sensing-leaf-surface-navigation]] is dark green; the underside is pale green with fine pubescence.

The pulvinus enables nyctinastic leaf movement — leaves fold upward at night, a feature common to the Marantaceae. This movement is driven by [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]] to the Caribbean islands. Naturalized populations occur throughout tropical Africa, Southeast Asia, and the [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-hawaii-pacific-islands]].

The species thrives in warm, humid tropical environments with annual rainfall of 1200–2000 mm. It grows best at elevations below 500 m in partial shade to full sun.

Natural stands often develop in forest clearings and along riverbanks. The plant's tolerance for partial shade allows it to be cultivated as an understory crop in agroforestry systems.

Optimal soils are well-drained, loamy to sandy loam, with pH 5.0–6.5 and high [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] content. The plant is intolerant of waterlogging and heavy clay soils.

Temperature requirements include a mean annual minimum of 18°C and maximum of 30°C. Growth ceases below 15°C, and rhizomes are damaged by temperatures below 10°C.

## Cultivation

Arrowroot is propagated vegetatively from rhizome pieces (setts) weighing 30–60 g each. Setts are planted 5–10 cm deep at spacing of 60–90 cm within rows and 90–120 cm between rows.

The crop cycle is typically 10–12 months from [[query-how-do-i-grow-garlic-organically-from-planting-to-harvest]]. Rhizomes reach maximum starch content 9–11 months after planting, when lower leaves begin to yellow.

Harvesting is done by hand, using digging sticks or mechanized diggers. Care is required to avoid damaging the fragile rhizomes, which are easily bruised.

Yields range from 3–12 tonnes of fresh rhizomes per hectare. Starch extraction ratio is approximately 20–25% of fresh rhizome weight.

Weed control is critical during the first 3–4 months of growth. Once the canopy closes, arrowroot effectively suppresses most weed species through dense ground cover.

## Starch Extraction and Processing

Fresh rhizomes are washed, peeled, and ground into a pulp in clean water. The fibrous residue is separated by passing the slurry through sieves or cloth.

The starch milk is allowed to settle in settling tanks for 6–12 hours. The supernatant water is decanted and the settled starch is washed repeatedly to remove impurities.

Final drying is done in the sun or in low-temperature ovens below 50°C. Excessive heat gelatinizes the starch granules and reduces quality.

Arrowroot starch granules are small (15–70 μm), oval to rounded, and produce a clear gel upon heating. This distinguishes it from cornstarch, which produces an opaque gel.

## Culinary Use

Arrowroot starch is prized as a thickening agent for its neutral taste, smooth mouthfeel, and resistance to breakdown under acidic conditions. It produces clearer sauces than flour or cornstarch.

The starch thickens at a lower temperature than cornstarch and does not become cloudy or stringy when cooled. This makes it preferred for delicate fruit glazes, custards, and Asian-style stir-fry sauces.

In infant nutrition, arrowroot is used as a weaning food and easily digestible carbohydrate source. It is naturally gluten-free and considered hypoallergenic.

Indigenous Caribbean communities traditionally prepared arrowroot as a flatbread. The starch was mixed with water, formed into cakes, and baked on hot stones.

## Nutritional Value

Arrowroot starch is composed of approximately 85% carbohydrate (primarily amylose and amylopectin), with minimal protein (0.2–0.5%) and fat (0.1%). It contains no gluten, making it suitable for celiac and gluten-intolerant diets.
