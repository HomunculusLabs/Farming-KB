## Overview

**Dicksonia sellowiana** Hook., commonly known as the South American tree fern or xaxim, is a large arborescent fern native to the montane forests of southern Brazil, Paraguay, Argentina, and Uruguay. It is one of the most iconic and threatened tree fern species in South America.

The species forms an erect trunk up to 8 meters tall, crowned with large arching fronds reaching 2–3 meters in length. It is a hallmark species of the Atlantic Forest (Mata Atlântica) biome.

Dicksonia sellowiana is listed on CITES Appendix II, restricting international trade due to overharvesting for horticultural use. The trunk fiber (xaxim) was commercially exploited for decades as a potting substrate.

The species is critically threatened by habitat destruction and illegal extraction. It serves as a flagship species for Atlantic Forest conservation efforts in southern Brazil.

In its native habitat, Dicksonia sellowiana can form nearly pure stands on steep, mist-shrouded slopes. These tree fern groves create a distinctive microclimate with reduced light, elevated humidity, and moderated temperatures.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Polypodiophyta
- **Class:** Polypodiopsida
- **Order:** Cyatheales
- **Family:** Dicksoniaceae
- **Genus:** Dicksonia
- **Species:** Dicksonia sellowiana Hook.

The species was described by William Jackson Hooker in 1844 from specimens collected in southern Brazil. The specific epithet honors Friedrich Sellow, a German botanist who collected extensively in Brazil.

Dicksonia sellowiana belongs to the Dicksonia antarctica alliance, a group of southern hemisphere tree ferns with similar morphology. It is most closely related to Dicksonia antarctica of Australia and Dicksonia squarrosa of New Zealand.

The species has no widely accepted synonyms, though it has occasionally been confused with Dicksonia berteroiana in herbarium collections. Molecular data confirm its distinct status within the genus.

Genetic diversity studies using microsatellite markers reveal moderate to high variation within Brazilian populations, suggesting that conservation of multiple populations is needed to preserve the species' full genetic range.

## Morphology

The trunk is erect, typically 10–18 cm in diameter, reaching heights of 4–8 meters in mature specimens. The surface is covered with a dense layer of adventitious roots and persistent stipe bases.

Fronds are bipinnate, 2–3 meters long, forming a spreading crown. The stipe is robust, dark brown at the base, densely covered with long reddish-brown to golden hairs and narrow scales.

Pinnae are lanceolate, 40–60 cm long, with pinnules that are deeply lobed and serrate. The rachis bears scattered hairs and small scales similar to those on the stipe.

Sori are marginal, borne on the underside of fertile pinnule lobes. Each sorus is protected by a bivalvate indusium consisting of an outer modified leaf lobe and an inner true indusium.

Spores are trilete, golden-yellow when fresh, produced in large quantities during the austral summer from December to February. The dense hair covering on croziers provides protection during frond development.

Growth rates in the wild average 3–5 cm of trunk height per year under favorable conditions. The root mantle can reach 8–12 cm thick, storing water and providing structural stability on steep slopes.

## Distribution and Habitat

Dicksonia sellowiana is native to southern and southeastern Brazil (states of Minas Gerais, Rio de Janeiro, São Paulo, Paraná, Santa Catarina, Rio Grande do Sul), extending into eastern Paraguay, northeastern Argentina (Misiones), and northern Uruguay.

The species inhabits montane rainforest, Araucaria forest, and cloud forest at elevations of 500–1,800 meters. It is most abundant on steep, shaded slopes with high humidity and annual rainfall above 1,500 mm.

In Brazil, the largest remaining populations occur in the Serra do Mar and Serra Geral mountain ranges. Scattered populations persist in the Serra da Mantiqueira and Serra do Espinhaço.

The species grows on well-drained, organic-rich soils derived from granitic or basaltic parent material. It favors south-facing slopes and ravine bottoms where cold air drainage and moisture retention create suitable microclimates.

In Araucaria forest, Dicksonia sellowiana grows beneath the canopy of Araucaria angustifolia, forming a characteristic understory layer. The two species are considered indicators of high-quality montane forest habitat.

## Ecology and Conservation

Dicksonia sellowiana is listed on CITES Appendix II, reflecting international concern about trade-driven population declines. In Brazil, it is classified as Endangered under the national red list.

The primary threats are habitat destruction through deforestation and illegal extraction of trunks for horticultural use. The xaxim trade was historically significant, with trunks harvested and shredded for orchid potting media.

Atlantic Forest loss has been severe, with less than 12% of the original biome remaining. Remnant fragments often retain Dicksonia sellowiana populations, but isolation reduces genetic connectivity.

The species is slow to colonize new habitat due to its dependence on continuously moist microsites for gametophyte establishment. Fragmented populations separated by cleared land may be effectively isolated with no spore exchange.

The species plays important ecological roles as a nurse plant and watershed protector. Its trunk supports diverse epiphyte communities including bromeliads, orchids, and filmy ferns.

Conservation efforts include habitat protection in national parks (Serra dos Órgãos, Aparados da Serra), cultivation programs to reduce wild harvesting, and CITES enforcement at international borders.

Reintroduction trials in southern Brazil have shown moderate success, with spore-grown seedlings establishing in protected sites after 3–5 years of nursery growth.

Brazilian federal law prohibits extraction of Dicksonia sellowiana from wild populations. Enforcement is inconsistent, however, and illegal harvesting continues in remote montane areas where oversight is limited.

## Cultivation and Uses

Dicksonia sellowiana is valued as an ornamental specimen in subtropical and temperate gardens. It is hardier than many tropical tree ferns, tolerating brief frosts to -3°C with frond damage but trunk survival.

The species requires rich, moisture-retentive soil, dappled shade, and shelter from wind. Regular watering during dry periods is essential, as drought causes rapid frond desiccation and crown stress.

Propagation from spores is straightforward but slow. Spores germinate in 4–8 weeks on sterile medium. Sporophytes develop over 6–12 months before transplanting to individual containers.

In cultivation, Dicksonia sellowiana responds well to regular feeding with diluted liquid fertilizer during the growing season. Mulching around the trunk base helps maintain soil moisture and protect surface roots.

The species can be propagated by trunk cuttings in a manner similar to Dicksonia antarctica. Sections of trunk placed horizontally in moist substrate will produce new shoots from dormant buds, though this method is slower than spore propagation.

Historically, the trunk fiber (xaxim) was commercially harvested and processed into slabs, baskets, and shredded potting media for orchid cultivation. This practice is now illegal in Brazil but continues on a limited scale.

Sustainable alternatives to xaxim have been developed, including coconut fiber and pine bark substrates. Public education campaigns in Brazil have reduced consumer demand for wild-harvested tree fern products.
