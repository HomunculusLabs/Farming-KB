# 5,6-MDO-MIPT

5,6-Methylenedioxy-N-Isopropyl-N-Methyltryptamine

## Chemical Identity

5,6-MDO-MIPT is N-isopropyl-N-methyl-5,6-methylenedioxytryptamine, a synthetic tryptamine carrying the methyl-isopropyl nitrogen substitution pattern combined with a methylenedioxy bridge across the 5 and 6 positions of the indole ring.

The full chemical name is 5,6-methylenedioxy-3-[2-(isopropylmethylamino)ethyl]indole, also designated as 5H-1,3-dioxolo-[4,5-f]indole-7-ethaneamine, N-methyl-N-isopropyl.

The free base has a melting point of 87-89 degrees Celsius. Mass spectral data shows the characteristic C4H12N+ fragment at m/z 86 (100%), the indolemethylene+ fragment at m/z 174 (7%), and the parent ion at m/z 260 (9%).

This compound is the N-methyl-N-isopropyl member of the "methylenedioxy trilogy" described in the TIHKAL commentary, which also includes [[5-6-mdo-dmt-entity-tihkal]] and [[5-6-mdo-dipt-entity-tihkal]]. The methyl-isopropyl nitrogen pattern is the same one found in the much more active compounds [[5-meo-mipt-entity-tihkal]] and [[4-ho-mipt-entity-tihkal]].

5,6-MDO-MIPT is documented in [[tihkal-introduction-and-methodology]] as entry #32. It has been tested in humans but shows only marginal threshold activity at high doses.

## Dosage and Administration

The estimated threshold dosage is greater than 50 milligrams when taken orally.

Duration is unknown.

At 35 mg orally, the subject reported "Some paresthesia noted. Nothing else."

At 50 mg orally, the subject reported "Maybe a trace of activity after an hour. Certainly nothing at three hours."

At 60 mg orally, the subject reported "There is something going on there, but I can't tell what it is. Very vague."

At 75 mg orally, the subject reported "Just a teasing smell of light-headedness in twenty minutes, and maybe a bit more light-headedness in an hour. I can suspect the chronology, but the character of the effects remains nebulous. It is certainly less dramatic than the 5-methoxy-compound."

## Pharmacology and Activity

5,6-MDO-MIPT demonstrates only marginal threshold activity at doses significantly higher than those required for active tryptamines with the same nitrogen substitution pattern. For comparison, [[5-meo-mipt-entity-tihkal]] and [[4-ho-mipt-entity-tihkal]] are both quite active at 4-6 milligrams orally, making 5,6-MDO-MIPT at least 10-15 times less potent than its more active counterparts.

The qualitative character of the threshold effects is vague and nebulous, described as a "teasing smell of light-headedness" rather than any clearly defined psychedelic or psychoactive experience. This suggests that the compound either has very low affinity for the relevant receptors or that any activity at those receptors is blocked or modulated by the 5,6-methylenedioxy substitution.

The results with 5,6-MDO-MIPT, combined with the inactivity of [[5-6-mdo-dmt-entity-tihkal]] and the untested status of [[5-6-mdo-dipt-entity-tihkal]], strongly suggest that the 5,6-methylenedioxy substitution pattern is detrimental to tryptamine psychoactivity. The 6-position appears to be "the kiss of death" for psychedelic activity in the tryptamine series.

This is particularly striking given that the methyl-isopropyl nitrogen substitution pattern, which is one of the most effective patterns for oral activity in tryptamines, cannot overcome the negative effect of the 5,6-methylenedioxy bridge. The 4,5-dimethoxy and 5,6-dimethoxy analogues would be "fantastic tools" for unraveling the role of ring substitution position, but these compounds have not been explored.

The 4,5-methylenedioxy compounds are described as having "the rich smells of interest" compared to the disappointing 5,6-series, supporting the hypothesis that the 4- and 5-positions are the critical sites for oxygen substitution in tryptamine psychoactivity.

## Synthesis

The synthesis of 5,6-MDO-MIPT requires the preparation of 5,6-methylenedioxyindole as a key intermediate, which is itself a substantial synthetic undertaking. The indole is prepared from piperonal through a three-step sequence involving nitration, Henry reaction, and reduction.

Piperonal is nitrated with concentrated nitric acid at 0 degrees Celsius to give 2-nitro-4,5-methylenedioxybenzaldehyde as lemon-yellow crystals with a melting point of 97-98 degrees Celsius in approximately 80% yield. This is then condensed with nitromethane in glacial acetic acid with ammonium acetate to give 2,2'-dinitro-4,5-methylenedioxystyrene as yellow crystals with a melting point of 121-122 degrees Celsius.

The dinitrostyrene is reduced to 5,6-methylenedioxyindole by two alternative methods. The iron reduction in acetic acid gives the product as colorless plates with a melting point of 108-110 degrees Celsius in 33% yield. Catalytic hydrogenation over palladium on carbon under 55 psi hydrogen in ethanol, acetic acid, and ethyl acetate gives the same product with a melting point of 107-110 degrees Celsius in 64% yield.

5,6-methylenedioxyindole is converted to the glyoxyl chloride by treatment with oxalyl chloride in diethyl ether at 0 degrees Celsius. The red crystalline acid chloride is collected by filtration and reacted with N-methyl-N-isopropylamine in tetrahydrofuran under nitrogen. The resulting glyoxylamide is recrystallized from acetone to give 5,6-methylenedioxy-N-methyl-N-isopropylglyoxylamide with a melting point of 203-204 degrees Celsius.

The glyoxylamide is reduced with lithium aluminum hydride in dry tetrahydrofuran under reflux for 2 hours. After workup and Kugelrohr distillation, the colorless distillate is recrystallized from benzene and cyclohexane to give 5,6-MDO-MIPT with a melting point of 87-89 degrees Celsius in 33% yield.

## Library Research Anecdote

The entry for 5,6-MDO-MIPT includes a notable anecdote about the difficulty of accessing scientific literature. Shulgin needed a 1967 article in Monatsh. Chem. describing the nitration of piperonal for the synthesis section. The volume had been relocated from the UCSF medical library to a remote storage facility in Richmond, California, apparently because older German-language journals were deemed to require special preservation.

Shulgin used this experience to argue against restrictions on access to scientific literature, framing such restrictions as a form of censorship and "the superimposition of your principles upon mine." His plea to university authorities was simple: "Let me find what I want to find. Let me read what I want to read."
