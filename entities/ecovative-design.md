# Ecovative Design

## Description

Ecovative Design is a pioneering biotechnology company that commercialized the use of myceliated biomass for packaging and consumer products. Founded in Green Island, New York, the company developed proprietary processes for growing mycelium into specific shapes and densities, creating biodegradable alternatives to synthetic packaging materials such as expanded polystyrene foam (Styrofoam). Their work opened the door to broader experimentation in mycelium-based product development by both industrial researchers and home growers. The company demonstrated that fungal mycelium, when grown on agricultural waste substrates in molded forms, can produce materials with properties comparable to conventional petroleum-based plastics and foams.

## Classification

- **Type**: Biotechnology company
- **Industry**: Biomaterials, sustainable packaging, mycotechnology
- **Focus**: Mycelium-based materials for packaging, insulation, and consumer goods
- **Headquarters**: Green Island, New York, USA
- **Founded**: 2007
- **Key innovation**: Mycelium-based composite materials grown in molds

## Key Facts

- Pioneered the commercial use of myceliated biomass as a replacement for petroleum-based packaging foams
- Their technology uses fungal mycelium to bind agricultural waste (such as husks, stalks, and seed hulls) into rigid, customizable forms
- Products are fully biodegradable and compostable at end of life, breaking down in home composting systems
- The company's work inspired a wave of product development in fungal biomaterials worldwide
- Their process involves growing mycelium in molds filled with agricultural substrate, where the fungus colonizes and binds the material into the desired shape over several days
- The finished products are dried to halt mycelial growth, creating stable, inert materials that are fire-resistant and water-repellent
- Oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus) and reishi (Ganoderma spp.) are among the species used for biomaterial production
- The technology has been licensed to companies including IKEA and Dell for sustainable packaging solutions

## Mycelium-Based Material Properties

Mycelium-based materials produced using techniques pioneered by Ecovative exhibit several remarkable properties. Dried mycelium insulation achieves an R-value of approximately 2.9 at 2-inch thickness, comparable to commercial fiberglass insulation. The material is fire-retardant due to the chitin skeleton of the mycelium cell walls, and hydrophobic when dried, beading water away unless completely saturated. The skeletonized chitin structure is what provides the water-beading property.

The mycelia of many mushroom species also possess antifungal and insect-repelling properties, offering a significant advantage over traditional insulation materials that may harbor mold or pests. Colonized substrate can be cut with a band saw into boards of any thickness, or shredded to make fluffy insulation suitable for blow-in applications. However, a vapor barrier must be applied externally to prevent the mycelium from absorbing enough moisture to reactivate and potentially attack the structure it was insulating.

## Applications Beyond Packaging

The applications of mycelium-based materials extend well beyond packaging. When air-dried or freeze-dried, colonized growing medium exhibits the characteristics of a durable building composite. It can be formed or cut into shapes such as blocks suitable for construction. These blocks fuse together with a light moisture application between adjoining layers rather than chemical adhesives or mortar. Once the blocks naturally fuse over a matter of days, they achieve tensile strength comparable to concrete and steel, with less tendency for fissures due to the slight flexibility of the mycelium even when completely dried. If kept dry, blocks of mycelium can last indefinitely.

Spent oyster mushroom columns or sawdust-based fruiting substrate can be shredded, mixed with waste paper, and hydropulped into new cardboard or paper products. The mycelium acts as a natural binder that may replace the chemical adhesives used by the paper industry. One advantage of myceliated paper products is that the fibers are initially hydrophobic, repelling water until soaked, giving them a well-timed shelf life and compostability at end of life.

Cotter has also isolated fungal strains whose fruitbodies are difficult to tear, resist moisture, and possess antibiotic properties, making them candidates for rubber replacements. These cultures are expanded into biomass on sawdust and formed into molds or sheets that can be cut to shape, with prototypes including flip-flops and other consumer goods.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Ecovative Design demonstrated that mushroom cultivation skills translate directly into biomaterials manufacturing. The same principles of substrate preparation, inoculation, colonization, and environmental control used in mushroom fruiting are applied to grow mycelium into functional products. Cotter (2016) highlights Ecovative as proof that small-scale cultivators can participate in this emerging field.

For mushroom growers, mycelium-based product development offers additional revenue streams from existing infrastructure and spent substrate that would otherwise be composted. Cotter encourages bioprospecting among the thousands of fungal species for traits such as high tensile strength or natural antibiotic properties that could yield novel biomaterials.

The possibilities for replacing short-lived consumer goods that accumulate in landfills, including plastic bottles, disposable cups, food packaging, and dog chew toys, are enormous. Mycelium-based products represent a fundamentally different approach to material consumption, where products are grown rather than manufactured, and return to the earth rather than persisting for centuries.
