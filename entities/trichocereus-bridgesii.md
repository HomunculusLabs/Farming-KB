# Trichocereus bridgesii

The **Bolivian torch** is a columnar cactus native to the high deserts of Bolivia. It is a well-known [[mescaline]]-containing species valued for its psychoactive properties and distinctive morphology. Among psychoactive cactus enthusiasts, T. bridgesii is often regarded as one of the more potent Trichocereus species.

## Taxonomy and Description

Trichocereus bridgesii (syn. Echinopsis lageniformis) is a fast-growing columnar cactus reaching heights of 2-5 meters. The stems are bluish-green to dark green, typically 8-12 cm in diameter, with 4-7 prominent ribs.

The most distinctive morphological feature of this species is its long, honey-colored to brown spines, which can reach 4-7 cm in length on mature specimens. This gives the plant a formidable appearance that sets it apart from the relatively spineless [[trichocereus-pachanoi]].

Young areoles bear 4-7 radial spines and 1-4 central spines. The spines are robust, slightly curved, and can be quite sharp. The flowers are large, white, nocturnal, and fragrant, reaching 15-20 cm in length.

The fruit is an oval green berry containing numerous small black seeds. Several named cultivars and forms exist within the species. The most notable being the SS02 clone (collected by Seedman in Bolivia) and the Monstrose form.

The monstrose form exhibits abnormal, contorted growth patterns that are highly prized by collectors. It produces irregular, compressed, and folded stems that create sculptural shapes, making it popular in ornamental cultivation despite being unsuitable for alkaloid extraction.

The species was first described by the British botanist John Lindley in the 19th century and was named in honor of the English botanist and plant collector George Bridges, who explored South America extensively.

## Distribution and Habitat

T. bridgesii is native to Bolivia, primarily found in the departments of La Paz and Cochabamba at elevations of 2,000-3,000 meters. It grows in dry, rocky slopes, valleys, and scrubland environments.

The species has been widely introduced as an ornamental and is cultivated throughout the world, with particular popularity in the southwestern United States and Mediterranean climates. Its natural habitat overlaps with [[trichocereus-pachanoi]] in southern Peru and northern Bolivia.

Natural hybrids between the two species have been documented. These hybrids, sometimes called Backberg clones, can exhibit intermediate characteristics in both morphology and alkaloid content, making species-level identification challenging in overlapping zones.

The species is more cold-tolerant than T. pachanoi, surviving temperatures down to approximately -8°C for brief periods. This cold tolerance, combined with its distinctive spination, makes T. bridgesii a popular ornamental choice in xeriscaping and desert landscaping.

In cultivation, T. bridgesii is sometimes confused with T. pachanoi due to their general similarity, but the longer, more prominent spines and bluer coloration of T. bridgesii serve as reliable distinguishing features in most cases.

## Chemical Composition

[[mescaline]] is the primary psychoactive alkaloid in T. bridgesii, with reported concentrations ranging from 0.05% to as high as 4% by dry weight. Many experienced practitioners consider T. bridgesii to be among the most consistently potent of the Trichocereus species.

The variability in reported mescaline content likely reflects genuine genetic diversity within the species, differences in growing conditions, and differences in sampling methodology. Some growers have reported that certain clones produce consistently high mescaline levels, suggesting a significant genetic component to alkaloid production.

Additional alkaloids reported in T. bridgesii include:

- **3-Methoxytyramine** — a phenethylamine compound
- **Tyramine** — a biogenic trace amine found in many cacti
- **Hordenine** — an alkaloid with sympathomimetic properties
- **Anhalaninine** and **Anhalonidine** — tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloids

Some researchers have noted that the alkaloid profile of T. bridgesii may differ qualitatively from that of T. pachanoi and T. peruvianus, with potentially higher relative proportions of certain minor alkaloids. This may contribute to the subjective differences reported by experienced users.

Many users describe T. bridgesii as producing a particularly clear, visionary quality to the experience, with less physical heaviness and more pronounced visual effects compared to other Trichocereus species. However, individual responses vary considerably.

## Traditional Use

The traditional ceremonial use of T. bridgesii is less well documented than that of [[trichocereus-pachanoi]], though it is known to have been used by indigenous communities in the Bolivian Andes for healing and divinatory purposes.

The plant is referred to locally by various names including Achuma and Cactus de San Pedro, reflecting its shared cultural context with San Pedro ceremonies. Among the Kallawaya people of the Bolivian Andes, a group renowned for their traditional medical knowledge (recognized by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage), cacti of the Trichocereus genus have been used in healing practices for centuries.

While the specific use of T. bridgesii is less well documented than T. pachanoi, the overlapping ranges and cultural practices suggest shared ceremonial traditions that date back thousands of years.

In contemporary practice, T. bridgesii is widely used in both traditional Andean mesada ceremonies and in modern therapeutic and exploratory contexts. It is frequently preferred over T. pachanoi by experienced practitioners due to its reputation for higher and more consistent potency.

The growing international interest in T. bridgesii has led to increased cultivation and availability, with numerous nurseries and online vendors offering live plants and cuttings for ornamental and educational purposes.

## Cultivation

T. bridgesii is considered one of the easiest and most rewarding Trichocereus species to cultivate. Growth rates are comparable to [[trichocereus-pachanoi]], with healthy plants adding 20-30 cm per year under optimal conditions.

The species is highly tolerant of neglect, poor soils, and irregular watering, making it an excellent choice for novice growers. Propagation is most commonly achieved through cuttings, which root readily when calloused and placed in well-draining cactus soil.

Seed propagation requires standard cactus germination conditions: warm temperatures, high humidity, and well-draining soil. The species is slightly more cold-tolerant than T. pachanoi and is commonly used as a rootstock for grafting slower-growing species, including [[lophophora-williamsii]].

## Preparation and Dosage

Traditional preparation of T. bridgesii follows methods similar to those used for other psychoactive cacti. The most common approach involves boiling sliced cactus tissue in water for several hours, reducing the resulting liquid to a manageable volume.

Due to the typically higher mescaline content of T. bridgesii, less plant material is generally required compared to [[trichocereus-pachanoi]]. Experienced practitioners typically use 15-25 cm of mature stem material, compared to 30-40 cm of San Pedro, though potency varies significantly between individual plants.

The intense bitterness of the cactus tissue can be partially masked by adding citrus juice, honey, or ginger to the preparation. Some practitioners report that the nausea associated with cactus preparations is less pronounced with T. bridgesii compared to other species, though this is not universally confirmed.

## Legal Status

T. bridgesii is uncontrolled in most countries and is primarily sold and grown as an ornamental specimen. As with other [[mescaline]]-containing cacti, the legal status of the plant itself often differs from the status of extracted alkaloids.

In the United States, the plant is legal to possess and grow, while extraction of mescaline for consumption is prohibited. Buyers should be aware that while the plant is legal, processing it for its psychoactive content may violate local and federal laws.

International legal status varies widely. In the United Kingdom, T. bridgesii is legal to grow and sell as an ornamental, while in some other European countries it may be subject to restrictions. Prospective growers should research their local regulations before acquiring the plant.

## Notable Cultivars and Forms

Several named cultivars of T. bridgesii are circulated among collectors and growers. These are typically propagated vegetatively through cuttings and maintain the specific characteristics of the parent plant.

The SS02 clone is one of the most sought-after forms, reportedly collected from wild populations in Bolivia by the vendor Seedman. It is reputed to have consistently high mescaline content and distinctive morphological features including a bluish coloration and prominent spination.

The monstrose form of T. bridgesii is highly valued by ornamental collectors for its irregular, sculptural growth pattern. Monstrose growth occurs due to a genetic mutation that disrupts normal apical dominance, causing the plant to grow in compressed, folded, and contorted shapes. This form is not suitable for alkaloid extraction.

The crested (cristate) form, while less common, produces a fan-shaped growing tip that is also prized by collectors. Both monstrose and crested forms grow more slowly than typical specimens and are typically propagated by cutting and grafting to maintain their unusual growth patterns. These ornamental forms represent some of the most sought-after cactus specimens in the collector market, with established monstrose plants commanding significant prices.

## Differences from Related Species

T. bridgesii can be reliably distinguished from T. pachanoi by its longer, more prominent spines and bluer stem coloration. While T. pachanoi typically has 1-4 short spines per areole, T. bridgesii often has 4-7 spines reaching 4-7 cm in length. The ribs of T. bridgesii also tend to be slightly more pronounced and fewer in number (4-7 vs. 4-8 in T. pachanoi).

The growth habit of T. bridgesii is also subtly different, tending to be slightly more compact and slower-growing than T. pachanoi, though this difference is not always reliable for identification. When in doubt, spine characteristics provide the most consistent diagnostic feature.

T. bridgesii is generally considered more cold-hardy than T. pachanoi, making it a preferred choice for outdoor cultivation in marginal climates. It also tends to be more resistant to pests and diseases, particularly mealybug infestations.

The combination of ornamental appeal, cold tolerance, and ease of cultivation has made T. bridgesii one of the most popular columnar cacti in the international collector market. It is widely available from specialty nurseries and online vendors.
