# Bacillus thuringiensis

## Taxonomy

- **Domain:** Bacteria
- **Phylum:** Firmicutes
- **Class:** Bacilli
- **Order:** Bacillales
- **Family:** Bacillaceae
- **Genus:** Bacillus
- **Species:** B. thuringiensis

## Key Characteristics

- Gram-positive, facultatively anaerobic, rod-shaped soil bacterium
- Spore-forming; produces endospores and parasporal crystal inclusions during sporulation
- Closely related to B. cereus and B. anthracis (B. cereus group)
- First isolated by Shigetane Ishiwata in 1901 from diseased silkworm larvae (*Bombyx mori*)
- Formally named by Ernst Berliner in 1915 from a Mediterranean flour moth isolate in Thuringia, Germany

## Crystal (Cry) Proteins

- Parasporal crystalline inclusions composed of δ-endotoxin proteins (Cry and Cyt families)
- Over 1000 cry gene variants identified; grouped into 70+ primary ranks (Cry1–Cry10, Cyt1–Cyt3, etc.)
- Hierarchical nomenclature: primary rank, secondary rank, tertiary rank (e.g., Cry1Aa, Cry2Ab)
- Each Cry protein exhibits specific insecticidal activity against particular insect orders
- Cyt (cytolytic) proteins have broader hemolytic activity; synergize with Cry proteins in some strains
- Crystal proteins encoded on large plasmids (often 100–300 kb); can be horizontally transferred

## Mode of Action

1. **Ingestion:** Insect larva consumes Cry protein (protoxin) on treated foliage or in Bt crop tissue
2. **Solubilization:** Protoxin dissolves in the alkaline midgut environment (pH 8–11 in most Lepidoptera)
3. **Proteolytic activation:** Gut proteases (trypsin, chymotrypsin) cleave protoxin into active ~60 kDa toxin fragment
4. **Receptor binding:** Activated toxin binds specifically to cadherin-like proteins, aminopeptidase-N, or alkaline phosphatase receptors on midgut brush border membrane
5. **Oligomerization and insertion:** Toxin monomers oligomerize, insert into the membrane, and form cation-selective pores
6. **Cell lysis:** Ion imbalance → cell swelling → lysis of midgut epithelial cells
7. **Septicemia:** Gut bacteria invade hemocoel → septicemia → larval death (typically within 24–72 hours)
- High specificity: a given Cry protein is active against a narrow range of related insect species
- Target insect orders: primarily Lepidoptera (Cry1), Diptera (Cry4, Cry11), Coleoptera (Cry3, Cry8)

## Distribution

- Ubiquitous soil bacterium found on every continent, including Antarctica
- Isolated from: soil, leaf surfaces (phyllosphere), aquatic environments, grain dust, stored products, insect cadavers
- Particularly abundant in agricultural soils and habitats with high insect populations
- Environmental persistence: spores persist in soil for months to years; vegetative growth is transient

## Agricultural and Permaculture Significance

- Most widely used biological insecticide worldwide
- Registered for organic agriculture in the US (OMRI listed), EU, and most other countries
- **Transgenic Bt crops:** Bt cotton, Bt corn, Bt soy, Bt eggplant — engineered to express Cry genes
  - First commercial Bt crop: Bt cotton approved in the US in 1996
  - >80% of US corn and cotton acreage is Bt varieties as of 2024
  - Significant yield gains and reduction in synthetic insecticide use documented
- **Foliar Bt sprays:** Standard tool in organic and integrated pest management (IPM) programs
- **Resistance management:** High-dose/refuge strategy mandated by EPA; stacked/pyramided traits (multiple Cry genes) delay resistance evolution
- Compatibility with biological control: minimal impact on parasitoids, predators, and pollinators

## Safety Profile

- Non-toxic to mammals, birds, fish, amphibians, and most beneficial insects
- Cry protoxins require alkaline gut pH for solubilization and activation — mammalian stomach is acidic (pH 1.5–3.5)
- US EPA toxicity classification: Category IV (practically non-toxic) for acute oral, dermal, and inhalation exposure
- No evidence of carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, or reproductive toxicity
- Specific concerns:
  - Potential impact on non-target Lepidoptera from Bt crop pollen drift (e.g., monarch butterfly — risk found to be negligible in field studies)
  - Soil ecosystem effects from repeated large-scale application; effects on soil arthropods and microbial communities are generally transient
  - Development of insect resistance to single Cry proteins — mitigated by gene pyramiding and refuge strategies

## Production and Formulation

- Industrial production via submerged fermentation in large-scale bioreactors (10,000–100,000 L)
- Fermentation yields a mixture of spores, crystal proteins, and cell debris
- After fermentation: centrifugation, concentration, drying, and milling
- Formulation types:
  - Wettable powders (WP)
  - Water-dispersible granules (WG)
  - Emulsifiable concentrates (EC)
  - Granular baits (for Bti mosquito control)
- Bti formulations applied to standing water for larval mosquito control; effective at very low doses
- UV degradation is the primary limitation for foliar applications; UV-protectants and stickers extend field persistence to 3–7 days

## Genetics and Genetic Engineering

- Cry genes are located on large conjugative plasmids; mobilizable between strains
- Cry genes cloned and expressed in major crop plants since the early 1990s
- Common transgenic events: MON810 (Cry1Ab), Bt11 (Cry1Ab), TC1507 (Cry1F), MON87701 (Cry1Ac), Bollgard II (Cry1Ac + Cry2Ab)
- Gene pyramiding (stacking 2–3 Cry genes targeting different binding sites) is the primary resistance management strategy
- Next-generation Bt crops under development: vegetative insecticidal proteins (Vip), RNAi-based traits combined with Cry genes
- Genome sequencing of many Bt strains has revealed extensive plasmid diversity and novel toxin gene clusters

## Ecological Interactions

- Natural component of soil microbiomes; density correlated with insect population density
- Some strains colonize plant roots as endophytes, providing systemic protection
- Plant growth-promoting properties reported in some strains: [[indole]]-3-acetic acid (IAA) production, phosphate solubilization
- Can synergize with entomopathogenic nematodes and other biocontrol agents
- Spore persistence in soil creates a natural reservoir; crop residue incorporation returns Bt to soil
- Horizontal gene transfer of cry plasmids to B. cereus and other soil bacteria occurs in nature, though ecological significance is debated
