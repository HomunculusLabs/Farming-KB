## Description

*Placentonema gigantissima* holds the distinction of being the largest known nematode on Earth, capable of reaching an astonishing 30 feet (9 meters) in length. Mentioned in *[[teaming-with-microbes-actinomycetes]]* as a striking contrast to the [[soil-nematodes]] that gardeners encounter, this enormous roundworm inhabits the placenta of sperm whales rather than soil. Its existence dramatically illustrates the incredible size range found within the phylum Nematoda — from microscopic soil-dwellers measuring fractions of a millimeter to this colossal marine parasite.

Lowenfels and Lewis use *P. gigantissima* to make a memorable point: while most soil nematodes are tiny, the phylum encompasses an astonishing diversity of body sizes and ecological niches.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Animalia
- **Phylum:** Nematoda
- **Class:** Chromadorea
- **Order:** Ascaridida
- **Family:** Atractidae (classification debated; some sources place in other families)
- **Genus:** *Placentonema*
- **Species:** *P. gigantissima*

## Key Characteristics

- Length up to 30 feet (9 meters), making it the largest known nematode species
- Diameter proportionally thick for its length
- Parasitic lifestyle — obligate parasite of sperm whales (*Physeter macrocephalus*)
- Inhabits the placenta of its whale host
- Represents the extreme upper end of nematode body size
- Relatively little studied due to the difficulty of accessing specimens from deep-diving marine mammals

## Habitat

Unlike the soil nematodes discussed extensively in *Teaming with Microbes*, *Placentonema gigantissima* is a marine parasite with a highly specialized habitat:

- **Host:** Sperm whales (*Physeter macrocephalus*)
- **Location within host:** Placental tissue
- **Geographic range:** Presumably follows the [[global-distribution-of-neurotropic-fungi]] sperm whales — deep ocean waters worldwide
- **Life cycle details:** Largely unknown, though presumably involves egg/larval stages that may be released into the marine environment

The deep-diving habits of sperm whales and their pelagic lifestyle make studying *P. gigantissima* extremely challenging. Much of what is known comes from examinations of whale carcasses and placentas recovered from whaling operations.

## Ecological Context

*P. gigantissima* occupies a niche entirely different from the soil nematodes that are central to [[the-soil-food-web]]:

- As a parasite of a large marine predator, it exists at the apex of a very different food web
- Its impact on sperm whale populations is not well documented, though placental parasitism could potentially affect reproductive success
- It demonstrates the remarkable adaptive radiation of nematodes — the phylum has colonized virtually every habitat on Earth

## Comparison to Soil Nematodes

The contrast between *P. gigantissima* and typical soil nematodes is dramatic:

| Characteristic | *P. gigantissima* | Soil Nematodes |
|---|---|---|
| Length | Up to 30 feet (9 m) | ~2 mm average |
| Diameter | Substantial | ~50 micrometers |
| Habitat | Whale placenta | Soil, roots, [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]] |
| Diet | Parasitic (host tissue) | Bacteria, fungi, plants, other soil organisms |
| Visibility | Easily visible | Require microscope; look like moving hairs |
| Ecological role | Parasite | Decomposers, mineralizers, predators, herbivores |
| Reproduction | Not well studied | Some parthenogenetic, many sexual |

## Significance in Nematode Diversity

*Placentonema gigantissima* is significant for several reasons:

- It demonstrates the extraordinary size range within Nematoda — spanning several [[event-classification-and-orders-of-magnitude]] from the tiniest free-living species to this massive parasite
- It underscores that the 20,000+ described nematode species (with estimates of up to 1 million total species) occupy an incredibly diverse array of ecological niches
- It provides a memorable illustration of the fact that soil nematodes, while essential to soil food webs, represent only one facet of nematode biology
- The species highlights how much remains to be discovered about nematode biology, particularly in marine and parasitic contexts

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

While *P. gigantissima* has no direct relevance to cultivation or [[mycology]], its mention in *Teaming with Microbes* serves an important pedagogical purpose:

- It captures reader attention, drawing gardeners into the world of nematode biology
- By establishing the extremes of nematode diversity, it makes the discussion of soil nematodes more engaging and contextual
- It illustrates that the [[soil-food-web]] organisms discussed in the book are part of a much larger phylum with [[global-ecological-crisis-soils-and-deforestation]] importance

## Interesting Facts

- If laid end to end, a single *P. gigantissima* specimen could span the length of a school bus
- The species name *gigantissima* translates roughly to "the most gigantic," a fitting descriptor
- Most gardeners will never encounter *P. gigantissima*, but they share a phylum with this remarkable organism
- Sperm whales dive to depths exceeding 2,000 meters, meaning *P. gigantissima* lives in one of the most [[fungal-biodiversity-in-extreme-environments]] on Earth
- Despite being the largest nematode, it remains one of the least studied due to the inaccessibility of its host
