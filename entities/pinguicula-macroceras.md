## Overview

**Pinguicula macroceras**, commonly known as the **California butterwort** or **large-spurred butterwort**, is a carnivorous perennial plant in the family Lentibulariaceae. It is native to western [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] and Eurasian lineages likely diverged during the Pliocene or early Pleistocene.

Chromosomal studies show a diploid count of 2n = 32 for P. macroceras, consistent with other members of the section. Polyploid populations have not been documented, in contrast to the variable ploidy levels seen in some Eurasian Pinguicula species.

### Morphology

The plant forms a flat rosette of 5–12 succulent leaves, each 3–8 cm long and 1–3 cm wide. Leaves are oblong-spatulate to elliptic, pale green to yellow-green, with margins that curl slightly inward. The upper surface is covered in two types of glandular trichomes.

Stalked mucilage glands produce the sticky droplets that trap insects, while sessile glands on the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]], with older leaves dying from the base.

The winter hibernaculum is a compact bud of tightly overlapping scale-like leaves, 8–15 mm in diameter. This structure protects the growing point during freezing conditions and desiccation. The hibernaculum forms in autumn and breaks dormancy in early spring.

The inflorescence is a scape 5–15 cm tall bearing 1–3 flowers. Flowers are 12–20 mm across with a two-lipped corolla: the upper lip is two-lobed and the lower lip is three-lobed with a prominent palate. The spur is long and conical (8–15 mm), often exceeding the corolla length. Flower color ranges from pale violet to deep purple with white markings near the throat.

## Distribution and Habitat

P. macroceras occurs along the Pacific coast of North America from the Aleutian Islands of Alaska through British Columbia, Washington, and Oregon to northwestern California. Disjunct populations exist in the Rocky Mountains of Montana and Alberta.

The species occupies a broader geographic range than most other North American butterwort species, reflecting its tolerance for diverse climatic conditions. Coastal populations experience mild, wet winters while inland populations endure much harsher continental conditions.

It grows in moist, nutrient-poor environments including seepage slopes, cliff faces, stream banks, bogs, and wet meadows.

Elevation ranges from sea level to approximately 2500 m in the Rocky Mountains. Coastal populations grow in hypermaritime climates with heavy winter rainfall and cool summer fog. Inland mountain populations experience colder winters with significant snowpack.

The species is associated with mineral-rich substrates, often growing directly on rock surfaces or in thin organic mats overlying serpentine, limestone, or basaltic rock. This distinguishes it from many butterwort species that prefer peaty, acidic substrates.

In the Rocky Mountain disjunct populations, the species is often found in hanging seepage gardens on limestone cliffs. These populations may represent relicts from a broader distribution during glacial periods, now isolated by warmer, drier intervening lowlands.

## Trapping Mechanism

P. macroceras employs a passive flypaper trapping mechanism using the mucilage-secreting glands on its leaf surface. The stalked glands produce a viscid, glossy secretion that forms droplets at the gland tips.

When small insects (primarily dipterans, springtails, and small hymenopterans) land on the leaf surface, they become trapped in the mucilage. As the insect struggles, additional mucilage flows over it from surrounding glands, further entrapping it.

Unlike Drosera, the leaf margins of Pinguicula do NOT curl around prey. Instead, the leaf surface slowly rolls inward over a period of hours, bringing additional sessile digestive glands into contact with the prey. This movement is driven by turgor changes in specialized cells.

The sessile glands secrete proteases, esterases, and phosphatases that dissolve the soft tissues of the trapped insect. Digestion typically takes 1–3 days depending on prey size.

Isotopic studies on related Pinguicula species have demonstrated that prey-derived nitrogen can constitute 20–50% of total plant nitrogen during the growing season. This nutritional subsidy is particularly important during flowering and seed production, when demand for nitrogen is highest. Nutrients are absorbed through the gland surfaces and transported to the rest of the plant.

The trapping mechanism operates continuously during the growing season. Each leaf remains active for 2–4 weeks before being replaced by new growth. Older leaves with accumulated prey remains are shed from the rosette base.

Ants and larger insects occasionally become trapped but are not effectively digested due to their hard exoskeletons. In some cases, these larger insects actually damage the leaf tissue, causing localized necrosis around the capture site.

## Cultivation

P. macroceras is one of the easier temperate butterworts to cultivate, as it tolerates a wider [[query-what-is-the-ideal-temperature-range-for-growing-cannabis]] than many alpine species. A peat-sand-vermiculite mix (1:1:1) kept consistently moist works well.

The species requires a distinct cold dormancy period of 3–4 months. Winter temperatures of 0–10°C trigger hibernaculum formation. Without this chilling period, plants may weaken over successive growing seasons and eventually decline.
