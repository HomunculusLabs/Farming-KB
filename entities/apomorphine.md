## Apomorphine

## Overview

Apomorphine is a short-acting dopamine receptor agonist derived from [[morphine]] through acid-catalyzed rearrangement.

It was first synthesized in 1869 by Arata and Matsumoto and was subsequently identified as a potent emetic agent in veterinary medicine.

Despite its structural origin from morphine, apomorphine has no [[salvinorin-a-kappa-opioid-receptor-pharmacology]] activity and acts exclusively on dopamine receptors.

Apomorphine was introduced as a treatment for Parkinson disease in the 1950s and remains in clinical use as a rescue therapy for motor fluctuations.

The drug is administered primarily by subcutaneous injection or continuous subcutaneous infusion, though sublingual and inhaled formulations have been developed more recently.

## Chemical Properties

Apomorphine has the molecular formula C17H17NO2 with a molar mass of 267.32 g/mol.

Its IUPAC name is (6aR)-6-methyl-5,6,6a,7-tetrahydro-4H-dibenzo[de,g]quinoline-10,11-diol.

The compound is a tetracyclic aporphine alkaloid formed by the acid-catalyzed rearrangement of morphine, which eliminates the morphinan C4–C5 ether bridge.

Apomorphine is a white to gray crystalline powder that darkens upon exposure to air and light due to oxidation of the catechol moiety.

It is slightly soluble in water and freely soluble in dilute acids; the hydrochloride salt is the standard pharmaceutical form.

The catechol group makes apomorphine highly susceptible to oxidation, requiring formulation with antioxidants such as sodium metabisulfite.

## Pharmacology

Apomorphine is a potent agonist at both D1-like (D1, D5) and D2-like (D2, D3, D4) dopamine receptor families.

Its anti-Parkinsonian activity is primarily mediated through D2 receptor stimulation in the striatum, restoring dopaminergic tone in the nigrostriatal pathway.

Apomorphine has a rapid onset of action, typically producing clinical benefit within 5–15 minutes of subcutaneous administration.

The duration of effect is relatively short, approximately 40–90 minutes, necessitating multiple daily injections or continuous infusion for sustained benefit.

The emetic effect of apomorphine is mediated through D2 receptor activation in the chemoreceptor trigger zone of the area postrema.

Apomorphine also stimulates [[first-pass-metabolism-and-oral-bioavailability]], precluding oral administration.

Metabolism occurs primarily through [[glucuronidation]], N-demethylation, and catechol O-methylation by catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT).

## Synthesis and Derivatives

Apomorphine is synthesized from morphine through treatment with concentrated hydrochloric acid at elevated temperatures, a rearrangement first described by Matthiessen and Wright in 1869.

The reaction involves dehydration and rearrangement of the morphinan skeleton to form the aporphine ring system, converting the ether linkage to a direct carbon-carbon bond.

Modern industrial synthesis uses morphine or [[codeine]] as [[psilocybin-pharmacology-and-serotonin-receptors]] and adrenergic receptors.

Biotechnological production using engineered microorganisms has been explored as an alternative to morphine-derived synthesis.

## Medical Applications

Apomorphine is approved for the acute treatment of "off" episodes in patients with advanced Parkinson disease experiencing motor fluctuations.

It is typically administered as intermittent subcutaneous injections of 2–6 mg or as continuous subcutaneous infusion via a portable pump.

Pre-treatment with the antiemetic trimethobenzamide is recommended for the first few days of therapy to manage initial nausea and vomiting.
