# Nasturtium officinale (Watercress)

## Overview

Nasturtium officinale, commonly known as **watercress**, is a perennial aquatic or semi-aquatic flowering plant in the family Brassicaceae (the mustard family). Native to Europe and Asia, it has become naturalized worldwide in cool, flowing freshwater habitats. Watercress is one of the oldest known leaf vegetables consumed by humans, with documented use dating back to ancient Persian, Greek, and Roman civilizations — the Greek general Xenophon reportedly fed watercress to his soldiers to maintain stamina, and Hippocrates located his first hospital near a stream to ensure fresh watercress availability for patients. ## Safety Considerations

- **Fascioliasis risk**: Wild watercress from grazing land may harbor liver fluke (Fasciola hepatica) metacercariae. Cooking destroys the parasites, but raw wild-harvested watercress from areas with livestock should be avoided or thoroughly washed and inspected
- **Vitamin K interaction**: Extremely high vitamin K content can interfere with warfarin (Coumadin) anticoagulant therapy; patients on blood thinners should consult physicians before regular consumption
- **Goitrogenic potential**: Like all Brassicaceae, watercress contains goitrogenic compounds that may interfere with thyroid function in susceptible individuals when consumed in very large quantities raw; cooking reduces this effect
- **Waterborne pathogens**: Commercially grown watercress should carry food safety certification; home growers should ensure water source quality

## Relevance

Nasturtium officinale holds a unique position at the intersection of nutrition science, traditional medicine, and sustainable agriculture. As the most nutrient-dense commonly consumed vegetable, it represents an underutilized crop in modern food systems with significant potential for scaling in aquaponic and permaculture contexts. Its bioactive glucosinolates, particularly PEITC, place it among the most promising dietary sources of natural chemopreventive compounds. For permaculture practitioners, watercress exemplifies multi-functional plant design: food production, water purification, erosion control, and habitat creation in a single compact, perennial species.
