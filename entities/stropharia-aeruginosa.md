## Overview

**Stropharia aeruginosa**, commonly called the verdigris roundhead or blue-green stropharia, is a saprophytic agaric fungus in the family Strophariaceae. It is recognized by its distinctive blue-green to turquoise cap that fades with age.

The species fruits on soil, decaying wood debris, and mulched pathways in temperate woodlands and parklands. It is widely distributed across Europe and parts of North America.

It is generally considered inedible and may cause gastrointestinal distress if consumed. Despite its toxicity, the species plays a valuable ecological role in decomposition and nutrient cycling within forest floor ecosystems.

Its vivid coloration makes it one of the more visually striking woodland mushrooms encountered during autumn forays. The species is of interest to mycologists studying pigmentation and secondary metabolite production in the Strophariaceae.

The blue-green coloration is caused by aeruginosin and related pigments that are chemically related to the bisbolane sesquiterpenes. These pigments have been the subject of research into novel antifungal and antibacterial natural products.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Stropharia aeruginosa was first described as Agaricus aeruginosus by William Curtis in 1784. Lucien Quélet transferred it to the genus Stropharia in 1872.

The species epithet "aeruginosa" refers to the blue-green patina characteristic of weathered copper. Several historical synonyms exist, including Agaricus cyaneus and Stropharia cyanea.

Molecular phylogenetic studies have confirmed its placement within Stropharia sensu stricto, closely allied with [[malt-extract-agar]] and produces a characteristic sweet odor. The species is relatively easy to culture, making it accessible for physiological and biochemical studies.

Like other Stropharia species, it may have potential applications in mycoremediation and mycofiltration systems. Research into its enzymatic capabilities and bioactive metabolite production remains ongoing.

The fruiting bodies serve as a food source for various invertebrates including slugs, fungus gnats, and beetles. Its presence on the forest floor indicates healthy decomposition activity within the woodland ecosystem.

In permaculture systems, Stropharia species are sometimes encouraged as part of the decomposer community in woodchip mulched pathways. While Stropharia aeruginosa itself is not the preferred species for this role, its presence signals active mycelial networks at work.

The mycelium of Stropharia aeruginosa produces a dense white mat that binds soil particles and organic matter together. This soil-aggregating effect improves water retention and aeration in the upper soil horizons.

## Similar Species and Identification

Several species can be confused with Stropharia aeruginosa in the field. Stropharia caerulea is similar but tends to be a deeper blue and has a thinner, more fragile fruiting body.

Stropharia hornemannii is larger and more robust, with a scaly cap surface and darker coloration. It is also considered inedible and occurs in similar coniferous woodland habitats.

The blue-green coloration may initially suggest a Psilocybe species to inexperienced foragers. However, Psilocybe species typically bruise vivid blue when damaged and lack the prominent ring found on Stropharia stipes.

Hypholoma species such as Hypholoma fasciculare (sulphur tuft) share habitat preferences but differ in coloration. Sulphur tuft has a bright yellow-green cap that darkens and lacks the bluish tones entirely.

Accurate identification requires attention to spore print color, ring characteristics, and the absence of bluing reactions. Microscopic examination of spore morphology provides definitive confirmation.

The species has been reported from Japan and temperate East Asia, though some Asian records may represent closely related but distinct taxa. Further molecular work is needed to clarify the global species boundaries within the Stropharia aeruginosa complex.
