## Overview

**Asarum canadense** is a low-growing perennial herb in the family Aristolochiaceae, native to deciduous forests of [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]], and ability to suppress weeds in low-light conditions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Asarum canadense** L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The genus *Asarum* contains approximately 80–100 species distributed across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere, with centers of diversity in East Asia and eastern North America.

The family Aristolochiaceae also includes the genus *Aristolochia* (birthworts) and *Houttuynia*. Many family members contain aristolochic acid, a nephrotoxic and potentially carcinogenic compound.

*A. canadense* is a herbaceous perennial growing 10–15 cm tall. The plant spreads via fleshy, branching rhizomes that grow horizontally just below the soil surface, forming extensive mats over time.

Leaves are basal, long-petioled, cordate (heart-shaped), 7–15 cm wide, with a velvety pubescence on both surfaces. The leaf blades are dark green above and paler beneath, with palmate venation.

The flowers are solitary, produced at the base of the leaf petioles at ground level in April–May. Each flower has three calyx lobes that are brownish-purple to maroon, triangular, and pointed, 1.5–3 cm long.

The calyx tube is urn-shaped and constricted at the mouth, adapted for ground-dwelling pollinators. There are no true petals; the calyx lobes serve the visual attractant function.

The fruit is a six-valved capsule containing numerous small seeds with fleshy elaiosomes that attract ants for dispersal. Seed set is often low compared to vegetative spread.

Root anatomy is distinctive: the fleshy rhizomes are cream-white internally with a pungent, aromatic flesh. The aroma intensifies when rhizomes are dried and stored, making them more potent as a spice.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Asarum canadense* ranges from southeastern Canada (Manitoba to Nova Scotia) south through the Great Lakes region to North Carolina and Tennessee, and west to Minnesota and Iowa. It is most abundant in the Ohio River Valley and the central Appalachian region.

The species occurs in rich, moist deciduous forests, often on north-facing slopes, stream terraces, and ravine bottoms. It prefers deep, loamy soils high in [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. It is intolerant of waterlogged conditions and compacted soils.

The plant is strictly a shade species, rarely found in sites receiving more than a few hours of direct sun. It is a reliable indicator of mature, closed-canopy forest conditions.

In the southern portions of its range, *A. canadense* is restricted to higher elevations and cooler microsites. It is replaced by other *Asarum* species in the southeastern coastal plain and the Gulf states.

## Cultivation

Propagation is most commonly by division of rhizomes in early spring or fall. Each division should include several growth buds and a segment of healthy rhizome. Division every 3–4 years maintains vigor in garden settings.
