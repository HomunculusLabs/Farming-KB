## Overview

**Oxalis tuberosa**, commonly known as oca, is a perennial herbaceous plant grown for its edible tubers in the Andean highlands of South America.

It is one of the most widely grown root crops in the Andes after potato, with cultivation extending from Venezuela to northern Argentina. Oca tubers come in a striking range of colors including white, yellow, orange, red, pink, and purple.

The crop is valued for its productivity at high altitudes (2,500–4,000 m) where few other crops can reliably produce food. Oca has gained attention internationally as a novel food crop for temperate regions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Oxalis tuberosa** Molina belongs to the family Oxalidaceae and is one of approximately 800 species in the genus *Oxalis*. It is placed in section *Ionoxalis* alongside other tuber-bearing [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]]. In New Zealand it has become naturalized and is sold commercially.

## Cultivation

Oca is propagated by planting whole tubers or tuber pieces at the start of the rainy season, typically October–November in the Andes. Spacing of 60–80 cm between rows and 40–60 cm within rows is standard.

The crop requires 6–9 months to mature, with tuber initiation triggered by shortening daylength in autumn. This photoperiod requirement has historically limited oca's adaptation to higher latitudes outside the tropics.

Day-neutral oca varieties have been developed through breeding programs in New Zealand and Europe, enabling cultivation at latitudes up to 50°N. These varieties represent a significant breakthrough for commercialization in temperate regions.

Yields range from 5 to 30 tonnes per hectare depending on variety, soil quality, and management. Intercropping with legumes, cereals, or other tubers is common in traditional Andean farming systems.

## Nutritional Value and Culinary Use

Oca tubers contain approximately 70–80% moisture, 12–20% carbohydrate, 1–2% protein, and less than 0.5% fat. They are a good source of vitamin C, potassium, and dietary fiber.

Fresh tubers contain oxalic acid, which gives them a tangy, lemon-like flavor. Traditional Andean processing involves sun-drying tubers for several days to produce *caya*, a sweet, dried product with reduced oxalate content.

In Andean cuisine, oca is boiled, roasted, or added to stews. Sweet varieties are eaten raw as a snack. In New Zealand, oca is prepared similarly to potatoes and used in salads, roasts, and stir-fries.

Commercial production in New Zealand has expanded since the 2010s, with oca available in mainstream supermarkets during autumn. The crop has also gained popularity among home gardeners in the United Kingdom and Pacific Northwest of North America.

The diversity of pigmented varieties provides a range of antioxidant compounds. Red and purple varieties contain significant levels of anthocyanins and betalains with potential health benefits.

Yellow and orange varieties are highest in carotenoids, particularly beta-carotene. White varieties have the mildest flavor and lowest oxalate content, making them preferred for fresh consumption.

## Ecology and Applications

Oca plays an important role in traditional Andean polyculture systems, often intercropped with potatoes, quinoa, beans, and ulluco. Its sprawling growth habit provides ground cover that suppresses weeds and reduces soil erosion.

The crop contributes nitrogen to the soil through leaf [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]], oca suits cool-climate food forests and high-altitude garden systems. Day-neutral varieties have expanded its potential for temperate-zone perennial vegetable beds.

Oca's ornamental value is increasingly recognized, with colorful tubers sold through specialty seed companies. Its clover-like foliage and late-season harvest extend the productive season in home gardens.

## Pests and Diseases

Oca is relatively pest-resistant compared to potato, though it can be affected by the Andean potato weevil (*Premnotrypes* spp.) and various nematode species in areas of continuous cultivation.

Foliar diseases include downy mildew (*Peronospora* spp.) and leaf spot fungi, particularly under humid conditions. These rarely cause significant yield losses but can reduce plant vigor.

[[cervantes-viral-diseases-cannabis]] are considered the most serious long-term threat because oca propagation is entirely vegetative. Virus accumulation over successive clonal generations can lead to gradual yield decline, a phenomenon known as cultivar degeneration.
