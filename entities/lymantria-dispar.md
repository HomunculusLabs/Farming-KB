## Description

**Lymantria dispar**, the gypsy moth, is a species of tussock moth in the family Erebidae that is one of the most destructive defoliators of hardwood forests in North America. Originally introduced from Europe, its populations are subject to dramatic fluctuations driven in part by [[entomopathogenic-fungi]].

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Animalia
- **Phylum**: Arthropoda
- **Class**: Insecta
- **Order**: Lepidoptera
- **Family**: Erebidae
- **Genus**: Lymantria
- **Species**: L. dispar

## Key Facts

- Sudden population decline in Appalachian forests in 1996 attributed to [[entomophaga-maimaiga]] (Butler and Strazanac, 2000)
- Invasive species in North America causing widespread hardwood defoliation
- Population dynamics regulated by fungal pathogens, predators, parasitoids, and abiotic factors
- Epizootic events caused by E. maimaiga can crash populations within a single season
- Larvae are polyphagous, feeding on over 300 species of trees and shrubs

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The gypsy moth system demonstrates how fungal pathogens can regulate insect herbivore populations at landscape scales. For [[mycology]], L. dispar is relevant as a model for understanding how entomopathogenic fungi can be used as biocontrol agents. The environmental conditions that trigger fungal epizootics (warm, wet conditions) have applications in timing biocontrol applications in agriculture and forestry.

## Ecological Role

L. dispar is a major defoliator of deciduous forests in eastern North America. Its population cycles drive oak and other hardwood mortality, altering forest composition. The moth's interaction with entomopathogenic fungi creates a classic pathogen-host dynamic that influences forest structure at landscape scales.
