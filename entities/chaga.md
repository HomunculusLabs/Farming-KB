# Chaga

Chaga (*Inonotus obliquus*) is a sterile conk (sclerotium) that forms on living trees of the genus *Betula* (birch) as a parasitic fungal growth. Despite being one of the most commercially popular medicinal mushrooms globally, the visible chaga "conk" is not the fruiting body — it is a dense mass of mycelium and wood tissue formed as a pathogenic response to the fungus *I. obliquus* colonizing the living tree. The actual fruiting body, a shelf-like polypore, develops only after the host tree dies and is rarely encountered in commerce. Chaga is native to the circumboreal forests of the Northern Hemisphere, occurring across Siberia, Scandinavia, Canada, the northern United States, and parts of Central and Eastern Europe.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Division**: Basidiomycota
- **Class**: Agaricomycetes
- **Order**: Hymenochaetales
- **Family**: Hymenochaetaceae
- **Genus**: *Inonotus*

*Inonotus obliquus* was first described by the Finnish mycologist Petter Adolf Karsten in 1871. The species epithet *obliquus* refers to the oblique pores of the rare fruiting body. Molecular phylogenetic studies place chaga within a clade of white-rot fungi capable of degrading lignin via lignin peroxidase and manganese peroxidase enzymes.

## Morphology

### Sterile Conk (Sclerotium)

The commercially harvested chaga mass is a dark, cracked, charcoal-like exterior (**sterile conk or sclerotium**) protruding from the trunk or major branches of infected birch trees. Key characteristics:

- **Exterior**: Hard, deeply fissured, black to dark brown crust (3–5 mm thick) formed by a protective layer of melanin-rich hyphae
- **Interior**: Rusty amber to golden-brown, corky-textured tissue with concentric layers
- **Size**: Typically 10–40 cm in diameter, though specimens exceeding 60 cm and 10 kg have been recorded
- **Growth rate**: The conk expands at approximately 2–5 cm per year; large specimens may represent 10–20 years of growth

### Fruiting Body

The actual fruiting body is a sessile or effused-reflexed polypore, 5–30 cm wide, with a brown to dark pore surface producing ellipsoid basidiospores (7–9 × 5–6 µm). It develops on dead standing trees or fallen logs after the host has been killed by the fungal infection.

## Host Range and Ecology

Chaga is primarily associated with **birch species** (*Betula pendula*, *B. papyrifera*, *B. platyphylla*), which provide the highest concentrations of the bioactive compound betulin/betulinic acid that the fungus converts to inotodiol. The fungus has also been documented on:

- *Alnus* (alder) — reduced medicinal quality
- *Fagus* (beech) — rare
- *Quercus* (oak) — rare
- *Fraxinus* (ash) — occasional

Chaga functions as a **white-rot pathogen**, causing a slow heartwood decay that may kill the host over 10–80 years. In healthy forest ecosystems, the resulting deadwood habitat supports diverse saproxylic communities.

## Chemical Composition

### Polysaccharides

Beta-glucans (β-(1→3)(1→6)-D-glucans) constitute the primary immunomodulatory component, accounting for 30–50% of the dry weight of chaga extract. These polysaccharides activate immune responses via dectin-1 receptor signaling and NF-κB pathway modulation.

### Triterpenoids

The triterpenoid fraction is unique to chaga and includes:

| Compound | Concentration | Activity |
|----------|--------------|----------|
| Betulin | 2–4% (from host) | Anti-inflammatory, antitumor |
| Betulinic acid | 0.5–2% | Potent antitumor (apoptosis induction) |
| Inotodiol | 0.3–1% | Antitumor, anti-inflammatory |
| Lanosterol | Trace | Membrane stabilizer |
| Ergosterol peroxide | Trace | Antitumor, antiviral |

### Melanin

The black exterior crust is composed of 15–30% melanin, a complex heteropolymer with potent **antioxidant and radioprotective** properties. Chaga melanin demonstrates dose-dependent free radical scavenging and DNA protection against UV and ionizing radiation.

### Other Compounds

- **Polyphenols**: Protocatechuic acid, caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid — antioxidant
- **Sterols**: Ergosterol, ergosterol peroxide
- **Lanostane-type triterpenes**: 3β-hydroxy-lanosta-8,24-dien-21-al and related compounds
- **Vitamins and minerals**: Vitamin D2, potassium, zinc, iron, selenium, manganese

## Traditional and Modern Use

### Siberian and Russian Folk Medicine

Chaga has been used for centuries across Siberia, Russia, and Northern Europe as a general tonic, digestive aid, and treatment for:
- Gastrointestinal disorders (gastritis, ulcers)
- Tuberculosis (historical)
- Cancer (historical adjunct therapy)
- Cardiovascular conditions
- Skin conditions (eczema, psoriasis)
- Fatigue and immune support

The Khanty people of western Siberia consumed chaga as a tea and also used it as a soap substitute. In 16th-century Russia, chaga was officially listed in the Moscow Pharmacopoeia.

### Contemporary Use

Chaga is marketed globally as a dietary supplement in various forms:
- **Powdered conk**: Dried, ground sclerotium for tea or decoction
- **Dual extraction**: Combined hot-water extraction (polysaccharides) and ethanol extraction (triterpenoids)
- **Tinctures**: Ethanol-based liquid extracts
- **Chaga coffee substitutes**: Roasted chaga powder blended with other mushrooms

### Scientific Evidence

- **Immunomodulation**: Beta-glucans stimulate macrophage activity, NK cell cytotoxicity, and cytokine production (IL-2, TNF-α, IFN-γ)
- **Antitumor activity**: Inotodiol and betulinic acid induce apoptosis in cancer cell lines (melanoma, hepatoma, colon carcinoma) via mitochondrial pathways; in vivo studies show tumor growth inhibition in mice
- **Antioxidant**: ORAC values among the highest of any natural product tested (52,000 µmol TE/g in some extracts)
- **Antiviral**: Demonstrated activity against HIV-1 protease, HSV-1, and hepatitis C in vitro
- **Anti-inflammatory**: Inhibition of COX-2, iNOS, and NF-κB signaling pathways
- **Antidiabetic**: Reduction of blood glucose levels in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats

## Harvesting and Sustainability

Wild chaga populations face increasing pressure from commercial harvest. Key sustainability concerns:

- **Overharvesting**: Rising global demand has led to unsustainable collection in accessible boreal forests, particularly in Siberia and Canada
- **Harvest method**: Sustainable harvest involves taking no more than 20–30% of a conk, leaving enough for continued fungal growth
- **Host tree mortality**: Removing entire conks accelerates tree death; responsible harvesters never harvest from trees under 15 cm DBH
- **Cultivation**: *Inonotus obliquus* can be cultivated on birch substrates, though cultivated chaga differs in chemical profile from wild-harvested material, particularly in betulin-derived triterpenoid content
- **Adulteration**: Products labeled "chaga" are sometimes adulterated with other dark fungal material or bulked with ground birch bark
