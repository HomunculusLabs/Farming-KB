## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Angiospermae → Eudicots → Rosids → Brassicales →
Brassicaceae → Brassica → B. napus L.

The species was described by Linnaeus in 1753, with the epithet referring to
the turnip-like appearance of some morphological forms.

Two major morphotypes are recognized: the oleiferous type (oilseed rape/canola)
and the root-crop type (rutabaga/swede), which differ in [[root-development-and-plant-competition]]
cultivation purpose.

**Growth habit:** An erect, branched annual or biennial herb reaching 0.5–1.8 m
in height, with a stout taproot and well-developed lateral root system.

The stem is glaucous, bluish-green, and typically branched in the upper
portion, producing multiple flowering racemes.

**Leaves** are alternate, glaucous, and lyrate-pinnatifid in the rosette stage,
with the upper cauline leaves becoming sessile and clasping the stem.

Leaf size ranges from 10–30 cm in the rosette to 5–10 cm on the flowering stem,
with margins that are dentate to entire.

**Flowers** are borne in terminal racemes, with each flower 1.5–2.5 cm in
diameter and consisting of four bright yellow petals arranged in a cross
pattern characteristic of the Brassicaceae.

Six stamens (four long, two short) surround a superior bicarpellary ovary.

The flowering period lasts 3–5 weeks, with plants capable of producing
200–400 pods per individual.

**Fruit** is a silique (elongated capsule), 5–10 cm long, containing 15–30
small spherical seeds arranged in two rows.

Seeds are 1.5–2 mm in diameter, brown or black, with a seed coat containing
mucilage that aids in water absorption during germination.

## Distribution and Habitat

*B. napus* is not known from the wild as a primary species; it arose through
human-mediated hybridization of its diploid progenitors in agricultural settings.

The oilseed form was first cultivated in Europe during the Middle Ages,
primarily as a source of lamp oil before its use as a food oil was developed.

Modern canola cultivars were developed in Canada during the 1970s through
conventional breeding to reduce erucic acid in the oil and glucosinolates in
the meal to levels safe for human and animal consumption.
