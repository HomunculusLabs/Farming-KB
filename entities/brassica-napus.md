## Overview

**Brassica napus**, known as rapeseed, oilseed rape, or canola (low-glucosinolate
cultivars), is an annual or biennial oilseed crop in the family Brassicaceae.

The species is an amphidiploid (allotetraploid, 2n=38) formed by natural
hybridization between *B. rapa* (2n=20) and *B. oleracea* (2n=18), likely
in European gardens or fields during the Middle Ages.

Canola oil, extracted from low-erucic-acid, low-glucosinolate cultivars, is one
of the most widely used vegetable oils globally for cooking, biodiesel
production, and industrial applications.

Global rapeseed production exceeds 75 million tonnes annually, with the European
Union, Canada, China, and India as the leading producers.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Angiospermae → Eudicots → Rosids → Brassicales →
Brassicaceae → Brassica → B. napus L.

The species was described by Linnaeus in 1753, with the epithet referring to
the turnip-like appearance of some morphological forms.

Two major morphotypes are recognized: the oleiferous type (oilseed rape/canola)
and the root-crop type (rutabaga/swede), which differ in root development and
cultivation purpose.

**Growth habit:** An erect, branched annual or biennial herb reaching 0.5–1.8 m
in height, with a stout taproot and well-developed lateral root system.

The stem is glaucous, bluish-green, and typically branched in the upper
portion, producing multiple flowering racemes.

**Leaves** are alternate, glaucous, and lyrate-pinnatifid in the rosette stage,
with the upper cauline leaves becoming sessile and clasping the stem.

Leaf size ranges from 10–30 cm in the rosette to 5–10 cm on the flowering stem,
with margins that are dentate to entire.

**Flowers** are borne in terminal racemes, with each flower 1.5–2.5 cm in
diameter and consisting of four bright yellow petals arranged in a cross
pattern characteristic of the Brassicaceae.

Six stamens (four long, two short) surround a superior bicarpellary ovary.

The flowering period lasts 3–5 weeks, with plants capable of producing
200–400 pods per individual.

**Fruit** is a silique (elongated capsule), 5–10 cm long, containing 15–30
small spherical seeds arranged in two rows.

Seeds are 1.5–2 mm in diameter, brown or black, with a seed coat containing
mucilage that aids in water absorption during germination.

## Distribution and Habitat

*B. napus* is not known from the wild as a primary species; it arose through
human-mediated hybridization of its diploid progenitors in agricultural settings.

The oilseed form was first cultivated in Europe during the Middle Ages,
primarily as a source of lamp oil before its use as a food oil was developed.

Modern canola cultivars were developed in Canada during the 1970s through
conventional breeding to reduce erucic acid in the oil and glucosinolates in
the meal to levels safe for human and animal consumption.

Today the crop is cultivated across temperate zones worldwide, from 35°N to
55°N latitude, thriving in regions with cool, moist growing seasons.

Winter types require vernalization and are sown in autumn for harvest the
following summer, while spring types complete their lifecycle in a single
growing season.

## Cultivation

Rapeseed requires fertile, well-drained clay or loam soils with pH 6.0–7.5 and
moderate to high nitrogen availability.

Optimal growing temperatures range from 15–22°C, with the crop showing
moderate frost tolerance during the rosette stage of winter types.

Seeding rates are 3–6 kg/ha for winter types and 6–10 kg/ha for spring types,
with row spacing of 12–25 cm in modern intensive systems.

Nitrogen fertilization requirements are high, typically 150–250 kg N/ha,
with split applications to reduce leaching losses.

Major diseases include blackleg (*Leptosphaeria maculans*), stem rot
(*Sclerotinia sclerotiorum*), clubroot (*Plasmodiophora brassicae*), and
light leaf spot (*Pyrenopeziza brassicae*).

Yields range from 1.5–4.0 tonnes per hectare, with Western European systems
achieving the highest average yields due to favorable climate and intensive
management.

## Nutritional Value

Canola oil is characterized by a favorable fatty acid profile: approximately
7% saturated fat, 63% monounsaturated (predominantly oleic acid), and 28%
polyunsaturated (linoleic and α-linolenic acid).

The oil contains significant amounts of phytosterols and vitamin E, and is
low in erucic acid (<2% in canola cultivars, compared to 30–50% in
traditional rapeseed).

The seed meal remaining after oil extraction contains 35–40% protein and is
used as a high-quality livestock feed, though glucosinolate hydrolysis
products limit its use in monogastric diets unless low-glucosinolate
cultivars are processed.

## Ecological Role

*B. napus* is a valuable break crop in cereal rotations, disrupting pathogen
and pest cycles that build up under continuous cereal production.

The crop provides an early-season nectar source for pollinators, with its
massive yellow-flowered fields supporting large populations of honeybees
and wild bees during the spring bloom.
