Plants grow 30 to 60 cm tall from a thick woody taproot. The habit is typically erect and sparingly branched, with multiple flowering stems arising from a basal rosette.

The taproot can extend 60 cm or deeper, anchoring plants in loose sandy soils and providing drought resilience. Root systems are also colonized by mycorrhizal fungi.

Leaves are palmately compound with 7 to 11 narrow leaflets radiating from a central point. Leaflets are 3 to 7 cm long, glabrous above and sparsely pubescent beneath.

Inflorescences are terminal racemes 10 to 25 cm long, densely packed with blue to violet pea flowers. A white-flowered form occurs occasionally in natural populations.

Each flower is approximately 1 cm long with a typical papilionaceous structure. The banner petal is reflexed at maturity, and the keel petals enclose the stamens and pistil.

The common name "sundial lupine" refers to the leaf orientation, which follows the sun throughout the day. This heliotropic movement is most pronounced in young leaves and developing inflorescences.

Fruits are hairy legume pods 2 to 3 cm long containing 4 to 8 seeds. Pods dehisce explosively when mature, catapulting seeds several meters from the parent plant.

Seeds are mottled gray-brown, approximately 4 mm in diameter, and contain bitter alkaloids that deter seed predation. Seed viability persists for several years in the soil seed bank.

## Distribution and Habitat

The native range extends from Maine and Ontario west to Minnesota and south to Florida and eastern Texas. It is most abundant in the Great Lakes and northeastern regions.

Characteristic habitats include dry sandy soils of pine barrens, oak openings, sandplain grasslands, and lakeshore dunes. These are typically fire-maintained ecosystems with sparse canopy cover.

The species is intolerant of heavy shade and competition from taller vegetation. Fire suppression has allowed woody species to encroach on many historically open habitats, reducing lupine populations.

It is listed as endangered in Maine, New Hampshire, and possibly extirpated from Illinois. Significant populations persist in Wisconsin, Michigan, Minnesota, and parts of New York and New England.

Elevation ranges from sea level to approximately 600 meters. Plants in higher-elevation sites tend to be shorter and produce fewer flower spikes than lowland populations.

The species is most abundant on glacial outwash sands and lacustrine deposits. These nutrient-poor substrates reduce competition from more aggressive plant species.

Fire return intervals of 5 to 15 years maintain optimal habitat conditions. Prescribed burning is a primary management tool in Karner blue butterfly recovery programs.

## Ecological Role

Lupinus perennis is the obligate larval host for the Karner blue butterfly, a federally endangered species. Karner blue caterpillars feed exclusively on L. perennis leaves, making lupine conservation essential for the butterfly's survival.

Adult Karner blue butterflies nectar on lupine flowers and other spring-blooming species. Females lay eggs on or near lupine plants during the first flight period in May and June.

The species also hosts larvae of the frosted elfin (Callophrys irus) and the wild indigo duskywing (Erynnis baptisiae). Several generalist Lepidoptera species occasionally feed on the foliage.

Nitrogen fixation by root-associated rhizobia enriches the sandy soils where lupines grow. This nitrogen contribution benefits neighboring plants in species-rich barrens and savanna communities.

Flowers attract diverse pollinators including bumblebees, solitary bees, and butterflies. The pollen is loosely held and easily collected by short-tongued bee species.
