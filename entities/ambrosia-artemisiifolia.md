The species has spread globally as an invasive weed, establishing populations across Europe, Asia, Australia, and [[artemisia-absinthium]], hence the specific epithet artemisiifolia.

The species belongs to the tribe Heliantheae within Asteraceae. It is closely related to the larger giant ragweed (Ambrosia trifida) and the perennial western ragweed (Ambrosia psilostachya). Natural hybridization with A. psilostachya has been documented in parts of the central United States.

Chromosome number is 2n = 36. Molecular phylogenetic analyses confirm that the genus Ambrosia is nested within the Heliantheae and is sister to the genus Xanthium, with which it shares similar fruit morphology.

Plants grow 10–200 cm tall, though heights exceeding 150 cm are common in nutrient-rich sites. Stems are erect, branching from the base and upper nodes, green to reddish-green, and covered with short hispid hairs.

### Leaf morphology

Leaves are opposite in the lower portion of the plant and alternate above. They are deeply 1–2 pinnately lobed, 5–15 cm long, with lanceolate segments that have serrate margins.

## Allergenicity

The pollen of A. artemisiifolia contains the major allergen Amb a 1, a nonglycosylated protein belonging to the pectate lyase family. Sensitization rates among exposed populations reach 10–15% in urban areas of central Europe.

Additional allergens include Amb a 5, Amb a 6, and Amb a 8 (a lipid transfer protein), which contribute to cross-reactivity with food allergens in the Rosaceae family. This cross-reactivity can trigger oral allergy syndrome in sensitized individuals who consume apples, peaches, or melons.

Symptoms of ragweed pollen allergy include rhinitis, conjunctivitis, and allergic asthma. The pollen season extends from August to October in the Northern Hemisphere, overlapping with the start of the school year and creating significant public health burdens.
