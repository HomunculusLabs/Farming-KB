## Overview

Lespedeza virginica, commonly known as Virginia lespedeza or slender bush-clover, is a perennial herbaceous legume native to [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]]. It is a member of the Fabaceae family and the Desmodieae tribe, closely related to other native bush-clovers and ticktrefoils.

The species is valued for its role in native prairie and savanna restorations, its nitrogen-fixing capability, and its importance as a forage and wildlife food source. Virginia lespedeza typically grows 60 to 120 centimeters tall and produces clusters of small purplish-pink flowers from midsummer through early autumn.

Its range overlaps with several other native Lespedeza species, but Virginia lespedeza can be distinguished by its taller growth habit and more open inflorescence. It is less aggressive in spread than sericea lespedeza, making it a safer choice for sensitive ecological restorations.

Unlike some introduced lespedeza species such as sericea lespedeza, Lespedeza virginica is native and non-invasive, making it a preferred choice for ecological plantings in its native range. The species has received increasing attention from restoration ecologists as native seed supply has improved.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Lespedeza virginica (L.) Britt. was originally described by Linnaeus as Hedysarum virginicum in 1753 and later transferred to Lespedeza by Nathaniel Lord Britton. It belongs to the family Fabaceae, subfamily Faboideae, and tribe Desmodieae.

The genus Lespedeza contains approximately 40 species distributed across East Asia and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], with about 15 species native to the eastern United States.

Virginia lespedeza is an erect to ascending perennial herb growing from a woody taproot and branching caudex. Stems are slender, angular, and typically covered with fine spreading hairs, especially when young.

Leaves are trifoliolate with lanceolate to elliptic leaflets 2 to 5 centimeters long. Leaflet margins are entire, and the upper surface is green with sparse hairs while the lower surface is lighter with denser pubescence.

Flowers are borne in axillary racemes or short panicles in the upper leaf axils. Each flower is small, roughly 6 to 8 millimeters long, with a papilionaceous corolla that is typically pale pink to purplish.

The calyx is deeply lobed with the lower lobe longer than the others. Flowering occurs from July through September depending on latitude and local conditions.

Fruits are small loments that break into single-seeded segments at maturity. Each segment is approximately 3 to 4 millimeters long, flattened, and brown when ripe, with a distinctive reticulated surface pattern.

Root morphology includes a prominent taproot that can extend 60 centimeters or more into the soil profile. Lateral roots branch extensively in the upper 30 centimeters, forming associations with Bradyrhizobium spp. for [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]]. The root crown develops a woody caudex from which new stems arise each [[extending-growing-season]].

## Distribution and Habitat

Lespedeza virginica is native to the eastern United States, ranging from New York and Massachusetts west to Wisconsin and Iowa, and south to Georgia, Alabama, and eastern Texas. It is most abundant in the central and southern portions of its range.

The species is characteristic of open habitats including prairies, savannas, glades, open woodlands, old fields, and roadside margins. It tolerates a range of soil conditions but is most common on dry to mesic, well-drained soils.

Virginia lespedeza is frequently found in oak-hickory savannas and tallgrass prairie remnants where it occupies the mid-successional herbaceous layer. It also colonizes disturbed sites such as abandoned agricultural fields and utility rights-of-way.

Soil pH tolerance ranges from slightly acidic to neutral, approximately 5.5 to 7.0. It is less common on strongly acidic soils and poorly drained sites.

Elevation range extends from near sea level in the coastal plain to approximately 600 meters in the Appalachian foothills. It is absent from the deep South's coastal plain and from the high-elevation portions of the Appalachian Mountains.

In the western portion of its range, Virginia lespedeza transitions into tallgrass prairie communities dominated by [[andropogon-gerardii]] and [[sorghastrum-nutans]]. It is often associated with other native legumes including [[baptisia-australis]] and [[desmodium-canadense]].

## Ecology

As a legume, Lespedeza virginica forms root associations with rhizobial bacteria, enabling atmospheric nitrogen fixation. This capability enriches soil [[nitrogen-availability-in-legumes]] and makes the species ecologically important in nutrient-poor prairie and savanna systems.

Virginia lespedeza serves as a nectar and pollen source for a variety of native bees, particularly long-tongued species. The flowers are also visited by butterflies, skippers, and occasional hummingbirds during the late-summer bloom period.

The foliage is palatable to white-tailed deer and is browsed moderately throughout [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]]. Cottontail rabbits also consume young shoots, especially in the spring when more succulent growth is available.

Seeds are consumed by several species of ground-feeding birds including wild turkey, bobwhite quail, and various sparrows. The loment segments persist on the plant into winter, providing a late-season food resource.

Seed dispersal is primarily by animals, with the hooked hairs on loment segments facilitating attachment to fur and feathers. Water transport along drainage channels also contributes to local seed movement. Germination rates in untreated seed are relatively low, typically 30 to 50 percent, due to a hard seed coat that requires mechanical or chemical scarification.

Virginia lespedeza contributes to plant community diversity in restored prairies by occupying a distinct niche between warm-season grasses and shorter forbs. Its deep taproot system helps prevent soil erosion on steep or disturbed sites.

Fire ecology plays a significant role in maintaining Virginia lespedeza populations. The species resprouts readily from the caudex following prescribed burns, and fire suppression in eastern prairies has contributed to local population declines. Periodic burning at 2- to 4-year intervals promotes vigorous regrowth and reduces woody plant competition.

## Cultivation and Uses

Virginia lespedeza is readily established from seed in native prairie and [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]] plantings. Seed should be scarified or inoculated with the appropriate rhizobial strain to ensure reliable nodulation and nitrogen fixation.

Recommended seeding rates for conservation plantings range from 5 to 8 kilograms per hectare when drilled or 7 to 11 kilograms per hectare when broadcast. Seeds are small and should be planted no deeper than 1 centimeter in a firm, weed-free seedbed.

The species performs best in full sun but tolerates light shade. It is drought-tolerant once established and can persist on low-fertility sites where many other legumes fail.

In ecological restoration, Virginia lespedeza is included in native seed mixes for tallgrass prairie, savanna, and meadow plantings throughout its native range. It is valued for its low maintenance requirements and adaptability to site conditions.

Establishment is slow during the first growing season as the plant allocates resources to root development. By the second year, however, plants typically reach full stature and begin producing significant seed crops. Competition from aggressive cool-season grasses can suppress seedling establishment, so companion grasses should be chosen carefully.

The species has limited ornamental use but can be incorporated into naturalistic landscapes and wildflower meadows where its late-summer blooms provide color and pollinator support. A few ornamental cultivars have been selected for darker flower color and more compact habit, though commercial availability remains limited.
