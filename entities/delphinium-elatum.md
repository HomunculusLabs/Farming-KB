# Delphinium elatum

## Overview

Delphinium elatum, commonly known as candle larkspur or Alpine delphinium, is a tall herbaceous perennial in the Ranunculaceae family. It is the primary parent species of most garden delphiniums and is prized for its dramatic spires of blue, purple, pink, or white flowers.

Native to subalpine and alpine meadows of Europe and Asia, the species typically reaches 1.2–2.0 m in height when in bloom. The common name "larkspur" refers to the spur-shaped nectary that extends backward from each flower.

Delphinium elatum is one of the most important sources of tall garden delphinium cultivars. The Pacific Giant and Elatum Group hybrids derive their impressive flower spikes and color range from this species.

The species has been cultivated in European gardens since at least the 16th century. Early herbalists valued it for both its ornamental qualities and its medicinal applications, despite the inherent toxicity.

All parts of the plant are toxic, containing diterpenoid alkaloids that can cause serious poisoning if ingested. Despite this toxicity, the species has been used in traditional medicine in its native range.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Delphinium elatum was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The genus Delphinium encompasses approximately 365 species distributed across the northern hemisphere and tropical African mountains.

Molecular studies support the separation of Delphinium from the closely related genus Consolida (larkspurs), though some taxonomists include Consolida within Delphinium. Delphinium elatum belongs to the section Elatopsis, characterized by tall, perennial habit and tuberous root systems.

The plant forms a rosette of deeply palmate basal leaves with 5–7 lobed segments. Leaves are dark green, 15–25 cm across, on long petioles. Stem leaves are smaller and less deeply divided.

The flowering stem is erect, hollow, and bears a terminal raceme of 20–50 flowers. Each flower is 3–5 cm across with five petal-like sepals, the uppermost forming a backward-pointing spur.

The spur contains the nectaries and is 12–18 mm long. True petals are reduced to two small structures called "bees" that sit at the flower center and are often a contrasting color.

Flower color in wild populations ranges from deep blue to violet-blue, occasionally white. Cultivated selections expand this range to include pink, lavender, and near-black forms.

## Distribution and Habitat

Delphinium elatum is native to montane and subalpine regions of central and southern Europe. Its range extends from the Pyrenees through the Alps, Carpathians, and into the mountains of the Balkans.

Isolated populations occur in the Altai Mountains of Central Asia and the Sayan Mountains of southern Siberia. These eastern populations show slight morphological differences but are not recognized as separate subspecies.

The species typically grows at elevations between 1,200 and 2,400 m. It inhabits moist subalpine meadows, stream banks, and open woodland clearings where snowmelt provides consistent moisture.

Delphinium elatum prefers cool summer conditions and struggles in hot, humid climates. It thrives in regions with cool nights, moderate daytime temperatures, and reliable summer rainfall.

The species is classified as a hemicryptophyte, surviving winter as a basal bud at ground level. Spring emergence is relatively late compared to many alpine species, typically beginning in April or May.

## Cultivation

Delphinium elatum is the foundation of the tall garden delphinium trade. The species is hardy to USDA zones 3–7 and performs best in regions with cool summers.

Plants require full sun to light shade and fertile, moisture-retentive but well-drained soil. A [[soil-ph]] of 6.5–7.0 is ideal. Heavy clay or waterlogged conditions lead to crown rot and plant loss.

The Elatum Group hybrids represent the tallest garden delphiniums, reaching 1.5–2.5 m. Notable series include the Pacific Giants, Magic fountains, and New Zealand hybrids bred for improved disease resistance.

Staking is essential for tall cultivars, as the hollow flower stems are vulnerable to wind and rain damage. Many gardeners use bamboo canes or grow-through plant supports placed early in the season.

Propagation is by seed, basal cuttings, or division. Seeds require darkness and cool temperatures for germination. Fresh seed germinates more reliably than stored seed.

## Toxicity

All parts of Delphinium elatum contain toxic diterpenoid alkaloids, primarily delphinine, ajacine, and related compounds. These alkaloids act as neuromuscular blockers affecting the central nervous system.

Ingestion causes nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, muscle weakness, and in severe cases, respiratory paralysis. Fatalities have been reported in livestock that grazed on delphinium in mountain pastures.

Cattle are particularly susceptible to delphinium poisoning, and the plant is considered a significant cause of livestock loss in western North America. Native Delphinium species pose a similar risk.

Gardeners should wear gloves when handling the foliage, as the alkaloids can cause skin irritation in sensitive individuals. Contact with sap followed by eye rubbing has caused corneal irritation.
