## Description

Avenue cropping is a tropical agroforestry technique where regularly spaced rows
of coppiced **legume trees** are integrated with food crops planted in the avenues
between them. The system simultaneously produces fuelwood, nitrogen-rich mulch,
and food crops from the same land area. Developed by **Ray Wijewardene** (1981)
in Sri Lanka and widely promoted in permaculture design.



## Classification

- **Category**: Technique — Agroforestry / Land management
- **Domain**: Tropical and subtropical agriculture
- **System type**: Integrated multi-strata production system





## Key Facts

- **Spacing**: Trees are spaced to shade the ground fully while still allowing
  cropping between the legume rows.
- **Coppicing**: Legume trees are lopped to leave stems 0.5–1.0 m above ground,
  providing a flush of nitrogen-rich mulch material. Suitable species include
  *Leucaena leucocephala*, *Gliricidia sepium*, *Cassia* spp., and *Acacia* spp.
- **Timing**: Crop is sown just before rain or irrigation to outcompete weeds
  stimulated by the mulch.
- **Two-crop sequence**: Possible with mulch also returned from crop wastes,
  creating a closed-loop nutrient cycle.
- **Limitations**: Constant coppicing weakens legume trees over 5–8 years,
  requiring replanting. Coppicing should be confined to warm wet periods, leaving
  mature leaf to carry plants through dry, cool, or frosty periods.
- **African variant**: Uses *Gliricidia* as the primary legume.
- **New Zealand variant**: Uses *tagasaste* (*Chamaecytisus palmensis*) as the
  green crop.





## Design Principles

1. Permanent high legume cover can provide mulch year-round from a variety of
   non-legume hedge and understory species.
2. Perennial thin-crowned leguminous trees can be spaced throughout orchards
   and gardens as an alternative to coppicing.
3. Fast-growing, short-lived legumes can be allowed to grow and die, or be
   ring-barked / felled on a 2–5 year cycle.
4. Mixed non-coppiced tall legumes (*Erythrina*, *Tipuana tipu*) provide seasonal
   leaf drop alongside non-legume ground mulch species.





## Relevance to Cultivation / Mycology

- The deep, continuous mulch layers from coppiced legume prunings create excellent
  **fungal habitat**, supporting decomposer and mycorrhizal communities.
- Spent legume wood and prunings can serve as **substrate for wood-decomposer
  mushrooms** such as *Pleurotus* spp. (oyster mushrooms) and *Ganoderma* spp.
- The zero-tillage approach preserves soil structure and mycelial networks.
- Nitrogen-rich leaf litter accelerates **composting** and creates favourable
  C:N ratios for fungal decomposition.
- The system supports soil food web complexity: bacteria, fungi, protozoa,
  nematodes, and microarthropods all benefit from constant organic matter inputs.
