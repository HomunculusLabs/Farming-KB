# Serpula himantioides

**Serpula himantioides** (synonym *Serpula himantioides*, also spelled *S. himantioides*) is a basidiomycete fungus in the family Serpulaceae, closely related to the notorious dry rot fungus *Serpula lacrymans*. It causes a type of brown rot in timber, typically classified as a **wet rot** rather than true dry rot, and is encountered in building structures where persistent moisture problems exist. Although less destructive than *S. lacrymans*, it is nevertheless a significant cause of timber decay in buildings and shares many morphological and ecological characteristics with its more famous relative.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** [[agar]]icomycetes
- **Order:** Boletales
- **Family:** Serpulaceae
- **Genus:** Serpula

The genus *Serpula* has been the subject of phylogenetic investigation, with studies exploring the origins and evolutionary relationships within the genus. Molecular evidence suggests that *S. himantioides* and *S. lacrymans* are sister species, having diverged from a common ancestor. The taxonomic origins of *Serpula* within the Boletales indicate an evolutionary transition from ectomycorrhizal ancestors to saprotrophic brown rot fungi.

## Morphology

### Mycelium
- **Mycelial Growth:** Produces white to cream-colored mycelial mats on wood surfaces, though generally less extensive and conspicuous than those of *S. lacrymans*
- **Rhizomorphs:** Can produce rhizomorph-like strands, though these are typically less well-developed than those of *S. lacrymans*. The strands may facilitate limited water transport and substrate exploration
- **Guttation:** Like *S. lacrymans*, may produce water droplets (guttation) on actively growing mycelial surfaces under humid conditions
- **Odor:** A characteristic damp, musty fungal odor

### Basidiocarps
- **Fruiting Body:** Produces resupinate to effused-reflexed (crust-like to shelf-like) basidiocarps on decayed wood surfaces
- **Upper Surface:** When reflexed, the upper surface is wrinkled and folded, with colors ranging from pale buff to ochraceous or light brown
- **Hymenophore:** The spore-bearing surface is smooth to shallowly wrinkled or folded, lacking the distinct pores or gills seen in other agarics
- **Flesh:** Thin, waxy to leathery in consistency
- **Basidiospores:** Ellipsoidal, smooth, approximately 7 to 10 by 4 to 6 μm, hyaline, producing a white spore print

### Decay Characteristics
- **Decay Type:** Brown rot, characterized by selective removal of cellulose and hemicellulose from wood cell walls while leaving lignin largely unmodified
- **Appearance of Decayed Wood:** Affected timber becomes dark brown, develops longitudinal and transverse cracking (cubical fracture), and loses structural integrity. The residual wood is dry, lightweight, and crumbles easily
- **Decay Rate:** Generally slower and less aggressive than *S. lacrymans*, but can still cause significant damage over time

## Distinguishing Features from Serpula lacrymans

The accurate distinction between *S. himantioides* and *S. lacrymans* is important in building pathology, as the management implications differ:

| Feature | *S. himantioides* | *S. lacrymans* |
|---------|-------------------|----------------|
| Aggressiveness | Moderate | Very high |
| Mycelial sheets | Limited | Extensive, conspicuous |
| Rhizomorph development | Limited | Well-developed |
| Water transport capacity | Limited | Extensive |
| Moisture requirement | Higher (>25 percent) | Can initiate at ~20 percent |
| Building impact | Localized | Can spread widely |
| Economic significance | Moderate | Very high |
| Common name association | Wet rot | Dry rot |

## Habitat and Distribution

- **Natural Habitat:** In nature, *S. himantioides* is found on dead conifer wood (particularly pine, spruce, and fir) in forest environments. It can also be found on some hardwoods
- **Building Environment:** In buildings, it is typically associated with timber that is in direct contact with persistent moisture sources, such as:
  - Timber embedded in damp masonry
  - Structural timbers affected by plumbing leaks
  - Floor joists and wall plates in poorly ventilated crawl spaces
  - External timbers exposed to repeated wetting
- **Geographic Distribution:** Widespread in temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere, including Europe, North America, and parts of Asia. It is more commonly encountered in natural environments than *S. lacrymans*, which is relatively rare outside buildings

## Biochemistry of Wood Decay

*Serplula himantioides* employs brown rot decay mechanisms similar to other brown rot fungi in the Boletales:

- **Fenton Chemistry:** The primary decay mechanism involves the generation of hydroxyl radicals through Fenton-type reactions. The fungus produces low-molecular-weight iron-reducing compounds that reduce Fe3+ to Fe2+, which then reacts with hydrogen peroxide to produce highly destructive hydroxyl radicals
- **Non-Enzymatic Cellulose Degradation:** Hydroxyl radicals diffuse into the wood cell wall ahead of the fungal hyphae, depolymerizing cellulose and hemicellulose non-enzymatically
- **Limited Lignin Degradation:** Lignin is not extensively removed but may undergo chemical modification, including demethoxylation and oxidation
- **Oxalic Acid Production:** Secretion of oxalic acid contributes to the acidification of the wood environment and participates in metal chelation relevant to the Fenton chemistry cycle

## Control and Management

Management of *S. himantioides* decay in buildings follows principles similar to those for other wet rot fungi:

1. **Moisture Elimination:** Identify and rectify the source of moisture. *S. himantioides* cannot grow without sustained elevated wood moisture content, typically above 25 percent
2. **Timber Replacement:** Severely decayed structural timber should be replaced with preservative-treated timber or naturally durable heartwood
3. **Promote Drying:** Improve ventilation and heating in affected areas to promote drying of timber and prevent recurrence
4. **Chemical Treatment:** Fungicidal treatments may be applied to adjacent timber as a preventive measure, though they are not a substitute for moisture control
5. **Monitoring:** Regular inspection of previously affected areas and other moisture-prone locations

The key difference from *S. lacrymans* management is that extensive removal of apparently unaffected timber and masonry treatment is generally not required, as *S. himantioides* does not spread as aggressively through building structures.

## Research and Ecological Significance

*Serpula himantioides* contributes to several areas of scientific research:

- **Comparative Brown Rot Biology:** As the sister species to *S. lacrymans*, it provides a comparative model for understanding the genetic and biochemical basis of brown rot decay and the adaptations that make *S. lacrymans* such an aggressive building pathogen
- **Forest Ecology:** In natural forest ecosystems, *S. himantioides* contributes to the decomposition of coarse woody debris, playing a role in carbon cycling and nutrient release
- **Evolution of Saprotrophy:** The genus *Serpula*, along with related genera in the Boletales, represents an important case study in the evolution of saprotrophic lifestyles from ectomycorrhizal ancestors
- **Building Science:** Understanding the moisture requirements and decay characteristics of *S. himantioides* informs building design and maintenance practices aimed at preventing timber decay

## Genomics and Comparative Analysis

Genomic studies have illuminated the evolutionary relationship between *S. himantioides* and *S. lacrymans*:

- **Genome Sequencing:** The genome of *S. himantioides* has been sequenced, providing a comparative reference to the *S. lacrymans* genome and enabling identification of genes associated with the enhanced aggressiveness of dry rot
- **Gene Family Contrasts:** Compared to *S. himantioides*, *S. lacrymans* shows expansions in gene families encoding cellulose-active enzymes (particularly GH5, GH6, and GH7 glycoside hydrolases), iron acquisition proteins, and stress-response factors. These differences likely underlie the greater decay capacity and building adaptability of *S. lacrymans*
- **Brown Rot Machinery:** Both species share the core Fenton chemistry machinery for brown rot, including genes for iron reduction, oxalate production, and hydroxyl radical generation, inherited from their common ancestor
- **Population Genomics:** Natural populations of *S. himantioides* show greater genetic diversity than building-associated *S. lacrymans* populations, consistent with the hypothesis that *S. lacrymans* experienced a population bottleneck during its adaptation to the building environment

## Biochemical Compounds

Key metabolites produced by *S. himantioides* during wood decay:

- **Oxalic Acid:** The principal organic acid secreted during decay, reaching concentrations of 5 to 15 mM in the wood matrix. Functions in pH reduction, calcium chelation, and participation in Fenton chemistry
- **Iron Chelators:** Low-molecular-weight extracellular compounds that reduce Fe3+ to Fe2+, including catecholate and hydroxyquinolate-type siderophores. These chelators are essential for sustaining hydroxyl radical generation
- **Cellulolytic Enzymes:** Secretes endoglucanases, cellobiohydrolases, and beta-glucosidases that complete the degradation of cellulose after the initial oxidative depolymerization by Fenton chemistry
- **Xylanases:** Endo-beta-1,4-xylanases that degrade hemicellulose, particularly the arabinoxylan abundant in softwood cell walls
- **Melanin:** Produced in hyphal cell walls, providing UV protection, mechanical strength, and resistance to microbial competitors in soil and wood environments
