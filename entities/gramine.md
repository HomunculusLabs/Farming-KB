# Gramine

Gramine, also known as donaxine or 3-(dimethylaminomethyl)indole, is a naturally occurring indole alkaloid found in several plant species. It is of interest in the chemistry of [[tryptamine]] and [[psilocybin]] derivatives as a structural analog and potential chemical intermediate. Gramine contains an indole ring with a dimethylaminomethyl substituent at the 3-position, making it a close structural relative of [[tryptamine]] and [[dimethyltryptamine]].

## Chemical Structure and Properties

Gramine has the molecular formula C₁₁H₁₄N₂ and a molecular weight of 174.24 g/mol. The IUPAC name is 3-(dimethylaminomethyl)-1H-indole. The structure consists of an [[indole]] ring system with a dimethylaminomethyl group attached to the 3-position (the carbon atom adjacent to the nitrogen in the five-membered pyrrole ring).

The compound is a white crystalline solid at room temperature with a melting point of approximately 133 to 135 °C. It is moderately soluble in water, ethanol, and chloroform. Gramine is a weak base due to the tertiary amine functionality, with a pKa of approximately 9.7 for the conjugate acid.

Structurally, gramine differs from [[dimethyltryptamine]] in the nature of the 3-position substituent: gramine bears a dimethylaminomethyl group, while DMT bears a 2-(dimethylamino)ethyl chain. This one-carbon difference is significant for receptor binding and biological activity.

## Natural Occurrence

Gramine is found in several plant species across different families:

- **Phalaris grasses**: Species of Phalaris, including reed canary grass (P. arundinacea), contain gramine along with [[tryptamine]] and [[dimethyltryptamine]] derivatives. The presence of gramine in these forage grasses is a concern for livestock toxicity.
- **Lupinus species**: Certain lupine species accumulate gramine as a defensive alkaloid.
- **Hordeum species**: Barley and related grasses produce gramine as a phytochemical.
- **Arundo donax**: Giant reed, the source of the synonym "donaxine," contains gramine in its tissues.

In plants, gramine is believed to function as a chemical defense compound against herbivores and pathogens. Its toxicity to insects and mammals contributes to its protective role.

## Toxicity and Pharmacology

Gramine exhibits notable pharmacological and toxicological properties:

- **Acute toxicity**: Gramine is toxic to mammals, with reported LD₅₀ values in the range of 50 to 100 mg/kg (oral, mouse). Symptoms of gramine poisoning in livestock include ataxia, tremors, convulsions, and respiratory depression.
- **Monoamine oxidase inhibition**: Gramine has been reported to inhibit monoamine oxidase (MAO) enzymes, which is relevant to its interaction with other indole alkaloids. MAO inhibition is a key component of the pharmacological synergy in ayahuasca preparations.
- **Serotonin receptor activity**: Gramine shows some affinity for [[serotonin]] receptors, though its activity profile differs significantly from that of [[dimethyltryptamine]] or [[psilocin]] due to the different side chain geometry.
- **Cytotoxicity**: Gramine has demonstrated cytotoxic and antiproliferative effects against certain cancer cell lines in vitro, though it is not developed as a therapeutic agent.

## Role in Tryptamine Chemistry

Gramine is a valuable intermediate in the synthesis of tryptamine derivatives and related indole alkaloids:

- **Tryptamine synthesis**: Gramine can be converted to [[tryptamine]] through a Strecker degradation or through the intermediate formation of 3-indolecarboxaldehyde followed by reductive amination.
- **Side chain extension**: The Mannich reaction product gramine can be used as a starting point for building more complex indole alkaloids by modifying or extending the dimethylaminomethyl group.
- **[[psilocybin]] analog synthesis**: While gramine itself is not an intermediate in [[psilocybin]] biosynthesis, the chemistry of gramine and related Mannich bases is relevant to the synthetic construction of the tryptamine side chain in laboratory routes to [[psilocin]] and [[psilocybin]].

The structural relationship between gramine and the tryptamine class of compounds makes it a useful reference compound in studies of structure-activity relationships among indole alkaloids.

## Agricultural Significance

The presence of gramine in forage grasses, particularly Phalaris species, has agricultural implications:

- **Livestock poisoning**: Grazing animals that consume gramine-containing grasses may experience acute or chronic toxicity, particularly during periods of rapid plant growth or stress when alkaloid concentrations are highest.
- **Breeding programs**: Low-gramine varieties of Phalaris have been developed for forage use to reduce the risk of livestock poisoning.
- **Allelopathy**: Gramine may contribute to the allelopathic properties of certain grasses, inhibiting the growth of competing plant species.

## Chemistry and Derivatives

Gramine participates in a variety of chemical transformations that make it a versatile building block in indole chemistry:

- **Mannich reaction**: Gramine is itself the product of a Mannich reaction between [[indole]], formaldehyde, and dimethylamine. This reaction is one of the most important methods for functionalizing the 3-position of the indole ring and is widely used in the synthesis of indole alkaloids.
- **Cyanide displacement**: Treatment of gramine quaternary salts with cyanide ion (Sommelet-Hauser rearrangement conditions) can extend the side chain by one carbon, providing access to homologated tryptamine derivatives.
- **N-Oxide formation**: Gramine can be oxidized to its N-oxide, which undergoes the Meisenheimer rearrangement to yield 3-(N-methyl-N-hydroxylaminomethyl)indole.
- **Metalation and cross-coupling**: The indole ring of gramine can be metalated at various positions using directed ortho-metalation or halogenation followed by cross-coupling reactions (Suzuki, Stille, etc.) to introduce additional substituents.

These transformations have been exploited in the synthesis of a wide range of natural products and pharmacologically active compounds, including carbazole alkaloids, beta-carbolines, and various indole-containing pharmaceuticals.

## Ecological Role

The ecological functions of gramine in the plants that produce it are diverse and well-studied:

- **Insect deterrence**: Gramine is toxic to a range of insect herbivores, acting as a feeding deterrent and growth inhibitor. It has been shown to reduce the performance of aphids, caterpillars, and other insects that feed on gramine-containing grasses.
- **Fungal resistance**: The compound exhibits antifungal activity against several plant pathogenic fungi, contributing to the disease resistance of gramine-producing species.
- **Allelopathy**: Gramine released from decomposing plant material or root exudates can inhibit the germination and growth of competing plant species, providing a competitive advantage to the producing plant.
- **Mammalian toxicity**: The toxicity of gramine to grazing mammals may have evolved as a defense against herbivory, though it poses problems for agricultural use of gramine-containing forage grasses.

## Historical Context

Gramine was first isolated and characterized in the early 20th century from Arundo donax (giant reed), which is the source of its synonym "donaxine." Its identification in Phalaris grasses followed, and it became an important subject of study in plant biochemistry and allelopathy research. The discovery of gramine's toxicity to livestock stimulated breeding programs to develop low-alkaloid varieties of forage grasses.

In the context of psychedelic research, gramine's significance lies in its structural relationship to [[tryptamine]] and [[dimethyltryptamine]], which are found in the same plant genera (particularly Phalaris). This co-occurrence has led to interest in Phalaris species as potential botanical sources of psychedelic tryptamines, though the presence of toxic gramine complicates such use.

## Analytical Detection

Gramine can be identified and quantified using several analytical methods:

- **HPLC with UV detection**: Gramine absorbs strongly in the UV range (maximum near 225 nm and 280 nm), allowing sensitive detection by HPLC-UV. Reverse-phase chromatography with a C18 column and water/acetonitrile mobile phase provides adequate separation from other indole alkaloids.
- **GC-MS**: Gramine can be analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry after derivatization. The mass spectrum shows a molecular ion at m/z 174 and characteristic fragments at m/z 159 (loss of methyl) and m/z 144 (loss of two methyl groups).
- **LC-MS/MS**: Liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry provides the highest sensitivity and selectivity for gramine analysis in complex matrices such as plant extracts and biological samples.
- **Colorimetric assays**: Several colorimetric methods for gramine detection have been developed for field testing of forage grasses, though these are less specific than chromatographic methods.

## Relationship to Tryptamine Chemistry

Gramine occupies a unique position in tryptamine chemistry due to its structural features:

- **Mannich product**: Gramine is the archetypal Mannich reaction product of [[indole]], and its chemistry is extensively studied as a model for understanding the reactivity of indole Mannich bases.
- **One-carbon homolog**: Gramine is a one-carbon homolog of [[dimethyltryptamine]], lacking the methylene group that connects the dimethylamino nitrogen to the indole ring. This single carbon difference dramatically alters the biological activity and receptor interactions.
- **Synthetic utility**: The ability to convert gramine to [[tryptamine]] derivatives through cyanide displacement or other side-chain modifications makes it a practical starting material for the synthesis of tryptamine alkaloids.
- **Metabolic relevance**: The structural similarity between gramine and [[tryptamine]] allows gramine to interact with some of the same enzymes and transporters, including monoamine oxidase and the [[serotonin]] transporter, though with generally lower affinity.

The study of gramine metabolism in both plants and animals provides insights into the broader field of indole alkaloid biochemistry. Understanding how organisms process and detoxify gramine can inform approaches to managing gramine-containing forage and to predicting potential drug interactions between gramine-derived products and pharmaceutical agents.

## Toxicology in Detail

The toxicological profile of gramine has been studied in several animal models:

- **Acute oral toxicity**: LD₅₀ values range from approximately 45 mg/kg (mice) to 200 mg/kg (rats), with species-dependent sensitivity. Ruminants appear to be more sensitive than monogastric animals, possibly due to differences in rumen metabolism.
- **Subchronic toxicity**: Chronic administration of sub-lethal doses produces weight loss, reduced feed intake, liver pathology, and neurological signs including tremors and ataxia.
- **Mechanism of toxicity**: Gramine toxicity is believed to involve disruption of monoamine neurotransmitter systems, interference with mitochondrial function, and direct neurotoxic effects on specific brain regions.
- **Treatment**: There is no specific antidote for gramine poisoning. Treatment is supportive, including gastrointestinal decontamination (if recent ingestion), seizure management, and fluid therapy.
