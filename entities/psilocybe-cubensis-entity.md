# Psilocybe cubensis

## Overview

**Psilocybe cubensis** is the most widely known and cultivated psychoactive mushroom species in the world. A coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) basidiomycete in the family Hymenogastraceae, it contains the psychoactive alkaloids psilocybin and psilocin. Originally described from Cuba, it has a pantropical distribution and is the species most commonly encountered in illicit cultivation, mycological research, and contemporary clinical trials investigating psychedelic-assisted therapy.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae (formerly Strophariaceae)
- **Genus:** *Psilocybe*
- **Species:** *P. cubensis*

First described as *Stropharia cubensis* by Franklin Sumner Earle in 1906 from specimens collected in Cuba. Rolf Singer transferred it to *Psilocybe* in 1949. The specific epithet *cubensis* refers to Cuba, the type locality. Guzmán's 1983 monograph placed it in section *Psilocybe* (subsection *Cubensae*). Molecular phylogenetics has confirmed its placement in the *Psilocybe* genus sensu stricto, distinct from the former *Deconica* segregate.

## Morphology

### Cap (Pileus)
- Diameter: 1.5–8 cm, occasionally up to 10 cm in cultivated specimens
- Shape: Conic to convex when young, expanding to broadly convex or plane with age; often with a distinct umbo
- Color: Cinnamon-brown to golden when young, fading to pale yellowish or nearly white with age; hygrophanous — becomes significantly lighter as it dries
- Surface: Smooth to slightly viscid when moist, often with remnants of the partial veil attached as whitish patches
- Bruising: Distinctly blue to blue-black when handled or injured, a key diagnostic feature caused by oxidation of psilocin

### Gills (Lamellae)
- Attachment: Adnate to adnexed, sometimes slightly sinuate with age
- Color: Grayish to dark purplish-brown at maturity with white edges
- Spacing: Close to crowded

### Stipe (Stem)
- Length: 4–15 cm; Thickness: 0.4–1.5 cm
- Color: Whitish to yellowish, bruising blue
- Features: Hollow, equal to slightly thickened at base; partial veil leaves a membranous annulus (ring) that is persistent but often becomes darkened by falling spores
- Context: Fibrous, no distinctive odor

### Spores
- Print: Dark purplish-brown to nearly black
- Shape: Ellipsoid to ovoid in side view, rhomboid in face view
- Size: 11.5–17 × 8–11.5 µm
- Features: Thick-walled, with a distinct germ pore

## Distribution and Habitat

*P. cubensis* is pantropical and subtropical, found naturally in:
- Central and South America (Mexico, Guatemala, Colombia, Brazil, Ecuador)
- The Caribbean (Cuba, Jamaica, Puerto Rico, Trinidad)
- Subtropical regions of the United States (Gulf Coast states, Florida)
- Southeast Asia (Thailand, Cambodia, Vietnam, Indonesia)
- Sub-Saharan Africa, Australia, and parts of Oceania

It is a **coprophilous** species, fruiting primarily on the dung of herbivorous mammals — especially cattle (*Bos taurus*), but also horses, elephants, and other ruminants. It prefers warm, humid environments with temperatures between 24–30°C (75–86°F) and high relative humidity (>85%). Fruiting occurs during rainy seasons in tropical climates.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

### Active Constituents
- **Psilocybin (4-PO-DMT):** The primary prodrug; a serotonin 5-HT2A receptor agonist that is dephosphorylated in vivo to the active compound psilocin. Typical concentration: 0.5–1.2% dry weight (wild), 0.3–1.8% (cultivated)
- **Psilocin (4-HO-DMT):** The active metabolite of psilocybin; directly responsible for the psychedelic effects. Typical concentration: 0.1–0.5% dry weight
- **Baeocystin (4-PO-NMT):** A minor alkaloid, present at 0.01–0.3% dry weight; pharmacological role not fully elucidated
- **Norbaeocystin (4-OH-NMT):** Trace alkaloid; pharmacological significance uncertain

### Mechanism of Action
Psilocybin/psilocin act primarily as partial agonists at the serotonin 5-HT2A receptor, with additional activity at 5-HT2C, 5-HT1A, and other serotonergic receptor subtypes. This receptor activation modulates cortical activity, increases entropy in brain networks, and reduces activity in the default mode network (DMN), producing the characteristic alterations in perception, cognition, and sense of self.

### Potency Variation
Significant variability exists between strains and growing conditions. Notable variations:
- Wild specimens from subtropical climates tend toward moderate potency
- Cultivated specimens show wide variation depending on substrate, genetics, and flush number
- Notable strains include Golden Teacher, B+, Cambodian, Ecuadorian, Amazonian, Penis Envy (notably high potency), and Thai Koh Samui

## Ethnobotanical and Cultural Significance

- **Mesoamerican Use:** *P. cubensis* is believed to be one of the species used in pre-Columbian Mesoamerican ceremonies, possibly identified with the Aztec *teonanácatl* ("flesh of the gods"), though *P. mexicana* and *P. caerulescens* are more strongly associated with the historical record
- **Maria Sabina:** The Mazatec curandera's veladas (healing ceremonies) in Huautla de Jiménez, Oaxaca, Mexico, brought *Psilocybe* species to Western attention through R. Gordon Wasson's 1957 *Life* magazine article
- **Western Counterculture:** Following Albert Hofmann's identification and synthesis of psilocybin in 1958, *P. cubensis* became the most widely distributed psychoactive mushroom globally
- **Contemporary Research:** The species (or isolated synthetic psilocybin derived from it) is used in modern clinical trials for depression, anxiety, PTSD, end-of-life distress, and substance use disorders

## Cultivation

*P. cubensis* is the easiest psychoactive mushroom to cultivate, which accounts for its prevalence:
- **Substrates:** Grain spawn (rye, wheat, brown rice) followed by bulk substrates including pasteurized straw, coco coir, vermiculite, composted manure, and wood-based substrates
- **PF Tek:** The "Psilocybe Fanaticus Technique" — a simple brown rice flour and vermiculite method — popularized home cultivation in the 1990s
- **Temperature:** Colonization at 24–27°C; fruiting triggered by fresh air exchange, high humidity, and light exposure
- **Yields:** Commercial methods produce 100–300 g fresh per kg of substrate
- **Legal Status:** Cultivation is illegal in most jurisdictions; the species is scheduled under the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971)

## Key Identification Notes

- **Blue bruising** is the single most important field identification feature
- Always check spore print color (dark purple-brown to black)
- Compare with potentially toxic lookalikes: *Gymnopilus* spp. (orange spores), *Panaeolus* spp. (black spores, different morphology), and deadly *Galerina marginata* (which can grow in similar habitats but has a brown spore print and ring)
- Never consume any wild mushroom without positive identification by an experienced mycologist
