# Charles Frost

## Description

[[charles-frost]] was an American mycologist based in Vermont, active

during the 1870s. He was described by [[julius-auboineau-palmer]] as 'a

living authority in this science,' placing him among the small number

of recognized American experts on fungi during the late 19th century.

Frost represents the tradition of gentleman-naturalists who contributed

to [[palmer-mushroom-charts-american-mycological-education]] knowledge through dedicated field study and

observation, despite the relative scarcity of institutional support.

## Classification

- **Nationality:** American
- **Location:** Vermont, New England
- **Era:** 1870s
- **Field:** [[mycology]]
- **Role:** Field mycologist, authority on New England fungi

## Key Facts

- Recognized by Palmer as a 'living authority' in mycological science
- Based in Vermont, contributing to study of New England fungi
- One of few American authorities cited by Palmer
- His recognition indicates extensive field experience and respected observations
- Represented the Vermont and northern New England mycological tradition
- His work likely documented species in Vermont's higher elevations
- Helped build a broader picture of New England [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity]]
- Connected to Boston mycologists through correspondence and publications

## Role in American Mycology

Frost's inclusion highlights the decentralized nature of American

mycology in the 19th century. Unlike England with Kew Gardens,

American mycologists worked independently, connected through

correspondence and publications in popular journals.

Palmer's recognition of a Vermont-based mycologist suggests Frost's

work was known through published observations or shared collections

that circulated among New England naturalists.

## Regional Context

Vermont's forests and pastures provided rich territory for fungal

study. The state's mix of deciduous and coniferous forests, combined

with agricultural landscapes, supported diverse fungal flora.

Vermont's colder climate and higher elevations host species adapted

to those conditions, giving Frost a unique perspective on fungal

distribution across different environmental zones in New England.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Frost's work contributes to the historical record of New England fungal

distribution. Modern mycologists studying changes in fungal populations

may find value in comparing Frost's observations with current data.

His recognition as an authority illustrates the importance of regional

expertise in mycology that remains relevant today, as fungal species

and fruiting patterns vary significantly by geography and climate.
