# Lupinus albus (White Lupin)

## Overview

Lupinus albus is an annual legume in the family Fabaceae, domesticated in the eastern
Mediterranean basin for at least 3,000 years. Among temperate grain legumes it stands
out for its exceptional nitrogen fixation capacity, deep taproot system, and high seed
protein content. Historically cultivated for green manure and animal feed across
southern Europe, North Africa, and the Near East, white lupin has seen renewed interest
as a low-input protein crop and biological tool for soil rehabilitation in permaculture
and regenerative agriculture systems. Modern "sweet" cultivars with reduced alkaloid
levels have opened markets for direct human consumption as a plant-based protein source.

## Taxonomy and Naming

- **Kingdom:** Plantae — **Order:** Fabales — **Family:** Fabaceae — **Subfamily:** Faboideae
- **Genus:** Lupinus — **Species:** L. albus L.
The specific epithet *albus* (Latin: "white") refers to the seed coat color of wild
and many cultivated forms. Common names include white lupin, lupini bean (Italian:
*lupino*), and *altramuz* (Spanish). The genus comprises roughly 270 species, most
native to the Americas; L. albus belongs to the Old World clade alongside L.
angustifolius (narrow-leaf lupin) and L. luteus (yellow lupin).

## Description

White lupin is an erect, bushy annual herb reaching 60–150 cm in height. The stem is
stout, hollow, and ribbed, typically branching from the base. The root system consists
of a deep taproot extending 1–1.5 m vertically, with lateral roots exploring a wide
soil volume — making it exceptionally effective at accessing subsoil nutrients.
- **Leaves:** Palmately compound, 5–9 foliolate; leaflets 4–8 cm × 1–2 cm,
  oblanceolate to spatulate, glabrous to sparsely pubescent.
- **Flowers:** Terminal racemes 10–30 cm long; flowers 12–18 mm, white to pale
  blue, papilionaceous. Self-pollinating, also visited by bees.
- **Seed pods:** Flattened, oblong, 5–10 cm × 1.5–2.5 cm, densely villous,
  containing 4–6 seeds. Wild types dehisce explosively; cultivated forms show
  reduced shattering.
- **Seeds:** Large, 8–14 mm diameter, 120–180 mg each; coat white to cream or
  mottled brown. Cotyledons yellow, oil-rich.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to the Balkan Peninsula and Aegean region, L. albus has been spread by
cultivation throughout the Mediterranean, into the Nile Valley, and across temperate
Europe. Now naturalized in parts of South America, South Africa, and Australia. Thrives
in Mediterranean climates with cool, moist winters and warm, dry summers. Tolerates
sandy and loamy acidic to neutral soils (pH 4.5–7.5) better than most grain legumes;
moderately drought-tolerant once established. Sensitive to waterlogging and heavy clay.
Young plants survive brief freezes to −5 °C.

## Agricultural and Permaculture Applications

### Nitrogen Fixation

L. albus forms a symbiotic relationship with Bradyrhizobium spp. in root nodules,
fixing 200–300 kg N/ha/year — among the highest rates reported for any annual legume.
This exceeds the fixation capacity of [[alfalfa-medicago-sativa]] under comparable
conditions and rivals perennial legumes including [[trifolium-repens]]. Inoculation
with appropriate Bradyrhizobium strains is recommended on soils without prior lupin
cropping history.

### Cover Cropping

As a winter annual cover crop in Mediterranean and warm-temperate zones, white lupin
produces 6–12 t/ha of above-ground dry matter. Rapid early growth suppresses winter
weeds; the deep root system breaks plow pans and compacted layers, improving soil
structure and water infiltration. When terminated at early flowering, residue C:N ratio
(15–20:1) allows rapid decomposition. It serves as an effective rotation partner for
[[crocus-sativus]] (daikon radish) in multi-species cover crop mixes for compaction
remediation.

### Soil Improvement

Beyond nitrogen, white lupin mobilizes soil phosphorus through proteoid (cluster)
roots that exude citrate and malate into the rhizosphere, solubilizing calcium, iron,
and aluminum phosphates unavailable to most crops. This phosphorus-mining effect
benefits subsequent plantings. Root channels persist for multiple seasons as macropores,
improving drainage and root penetration for following crops.

### Companion Planting

In permaculture designs, L. albus integrates well in guild plantings with fruit and
nut trees. Its nitrogen contribution supports establishing [[moringa-oleifera]] in
subtropical systems. In temperate food forests, interplanting beneath [[robinia-
pseudoacacia]] or [[acacia-confusa]] combines deep nitrogen inputs from both taprooted
and nodal-fixing species. Dense biomass provides weed suppression and mulch. Flowering
supports pollinators that also service neighboring [[tagetes-erecta-entity]] plantings.
[[tithonia-diversifolia]] cuttings interplanted with lupin benefit from elevated soil
nitrogen during decomposition.

## Alkaloid Content and Debittering

Wild-type and traditional cultivars contain 1.5–3.0% quinolizidine alkaloids (QAs),
primarily lupanine and sparteine, which confer bitterness and toxicity. Chronic
ingestion causes lupinosis in livestock; human toxicity manifests as nausea, tremor,
and respiratory depression at high doses.

| Alkaloid   | Proportion of QA fraction | Toxicity (approx.)     |
|------------|--------------------------|------------------------|
| Lupanine   | 50–70%                   | LD50 (rat, oral) ~1100 mg/kg |
| Sparteine  | 10–25%                   | LD50 (rat, oral) ~430 mg/kg  |

Debittering involves prolonged water soaking (3–7 days) with frequent water changes,
leaching QAs into solution. "Sweet" cultivars (e.g., 'Ultra', 'Kiev Mutant') carry
recessive *iucundus* alleles limiting QA accumulation to <0.02%, safe for direct
consumption. Industrial processing uses aqueous alkaline extraction followed by acid
neutralization.

## Relevance

White lupin occupies a niche at the intersection of soil remediation, low-input
agriculture, and protein crop development. Its nitrogen fixation rate surpasses most
annual legumes, and its phosphorus-mobilizing root exudates address a nutrient bottleneck
common on weathered and compacted soils. For permaculture practitioners, L. albus
provides a dual-function cover crop that simultaneously builds soil fertility and
produces a harvestable protein-rich seed. Breeding efforts continue to improve yield,
reduce alkaloid content, and expand climatic adaptation.
