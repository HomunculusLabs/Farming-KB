## Overview

**Lupinus albus** (white lupin) is an annual legume in the family Fabaceae, native to the Mediterranean basin and the Balkans. It is cultivated widely for its protein-rich seeds, which serve as both human food and animal feed. The species is notable for its ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen through root nodules, making it valuable in [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] and nitrogen use efficiency.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Lupinus albus** L. belongs to the genus *Lupinus*, subfamily Faboideae, tribe Genisteae. The specific epithet *albus* refers to the white seeds characteristic of the species.

The plant is an erect annual herb reaching 30–120 cm in height. Stems are stout, ribbed, and pubescent. Leaves are palmately compound with 5–9 leaflets, each 2–7 cm long, glabrous above and pubescent beneath.

Vegetative growth is initially slow but accelerates after the rosette stage. Branching is basal, with 3–6 primary branches per plant. The foliage is a rich green and can serve as a moderate-quality forage when grazed or cut before flowering.

Inflorescences are terminal racemes 10–30 cm long, bearing whorled flowers. Each whorl contains 2–4 flowers arranged decussately along the rachis. The standard petal is white to pale blue, 12–18 mm long, with a central greenish patch or keel spot. The keel is beaked and spirally twisted after pollination, facilitating tripping by bee visitors.

Pods are 5–10 cm long, densely pubescent, containing 3–6 large white seeds. The pod wall is leathery and dehiscent at maturity in wild types, while cultivated forms have been selected for indehiscence to reduce seed shattering. Seeds are broadly ovate, 8–14 mm in diameter, with a flat hilum and smooth seed coat. Thousand-seed weight ranges from 200–500 g depending on cultivar.

Root architecture is distinctive, with a deep taproot and cluster roots (proteoid roots) that enhance phosphorus uptake from poor soils. Nitrogen-fixing nodules form in symbiosis with *Bradyrhizobium* species.

The species is diploid (2n = 50), with a genome size of approximately 1.0 Gb. Genetic mapping has identified quantitative trait loci for alkaloid content, flowering time, seed protein, and anthracnose resistance. The reference genome was published in 2017, facilitating marker-assisted breeding.

## Distribution and Habitat

White lupin is native to the Balkan Peninsula, Italy, Greece, and the eastern Mediterranean coast. It has been naturalized across much of Europe, North Africa, and temperate South America. Cultivation extends to Australia, South Africa, and parts [[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]] lupin seeds include tocopherols (vitamin E), phytosterols, and carotenoids. Total tocopherol content ranges from 30–60 mg/kg seed oil, with γ-tocopherol as the dominant form. These antioxidants contribute to the oxidative stability of lupin oil and may provide health benefits when consumed as part of the diet.

## Agriculture and Uses

White lupin is grown as a grain legume for seed harvest and as a green manure or cover crop. As a grain crop, seed yields range from 1.5–4.0 t/ha under rainfed conditions, with irrigated crops reaching 5 t/ha. The crop requires minimal [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]], contributing 100–200 kg N/ha to the soil.

Sowing occurs in autumn in Mediterranean climates and in spring in cooler [[legal-responses-to-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-in-the-european-union]] has approved lupin-derived proteins as novel food ingredients since 2006.

As a cover crop, white lupin provides excellent biomass production (5–10 t DM/ha), weed suppression, and soil phosphorus mobilization through its cluster roots. It is increasingly used in [[fungi-in-the-environment-symbiotic-relationships]] with rhizobial bacteria (*Bradyrhizobium* spp.) in root nodules, converting atmospheric N₂ into plant-available ammonium. This nitrogen fixation contributes substantially to soil fertility in rotational systems, reducing dependency on synthetic nitrogen inputs.

Effective nodulation depends on inoculation with compatible *Bradyrhizobium* strains in soils lacking native lupin-nodulating rhizobia. Commercial inoculants are widely available. The symbiosis is sensitive to soil nitrate levels, with nitrogen fixation suppressed when available soil nitrogen exceeds 50 kg/ha.

The proteoid root clusters exude organic acids (primarily citrate and malate) that solubilize bound phosphorus in the rhizosphere. This mechanism allows white lupin to access phosphorus from soils that are unavailable to most crop species. The phosphorus mobilization effect can benefit subsequent crops in rotation.
