## Overview

**Hydnum repandum**, the hedgehog mushroom or sweet tooth, is an ectomycorrhizal basidiomycete in the family Hydnaceae (order Cantharellales). It is one of the most widely appreciated edible wild mushrooms in Europe and North America, easily recognized by its pale cap and distinctive spore-bearing teeth or spines on the hymenophore.

The genus Hydnum was traditionally considered to contain only a few species, but molecular phylogenetics (Wang et al., 2012; Stubbe et al., 2012) has revealed significant cryptic diversity, splitting the group into approximately 30 species worldwide.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Cantharellales
- **Family:** Hydnaceae
- **Genus:** Hydnum
- **Species:** H. repandum
- **Authority:** L. (1753)

Recent phylogenetic work has designated a neotype for H. repandum sensu stricto, restricting the name to European populations. North American material historically called H. repandum includes at least two distinct species: H. washingtonianum and H. albomagnum.

## Morphology

The cap is 3–12 cm in diameter, convex becoming depressed or funnel-shaped with age. Surface is smooth to slightly irregular, pale orange to buff or cream-colored. The cap margin is inrolled when young. Context is firm and white, with a mild to slightly peppery taste and a pleasant fruity odor reminiscent of apricots.

The hymenophore consists of crowded, soft, decurrent spines (teeth) 3–7 mm long, white to cream-colored. This is the diagnostic feature — no gills or pores, only pendant tooth-like structures. Spore print is white to pale cream.

Spores are globose to broadly ellipsoid, 7–9 × 6.5–8 µm, smooth, inamyloid. Basidia are clavate, 40–55 × 7–9 µm, with 2–4 sterigmata. The stipe is 3–8 cm long, 1–2.5 cm thick, central to eccentric, solid, and concolorous with the cap.

## Distribution and Habitat

Hydnum repandum is found across temperate and boreal forests of Europe, North America, and Asia. In Europe, it is widespread from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean. In North America, it occurs from the Pacific Northwest to eastern deciduous forests.

The species is ectomycorrhizal, forming associations with a broad range of host trees including Fagus (beech), Quercus (oak), Picea (spruce), Pinus (pine), Abies (fir), and Betula (birch). It fruits from late summer to late autumn, typically August through November in temperate zones.

Fruiting is solitary to scattered, occasionally in small clusters. It prefers mossy forest floors in mature, undisturbed stands with well-drained, acidic to neutral soils. The species is sensitive to heavy disturbance and nitrogen deposition.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

Hydnum repandum is a premier edible mushroom, widely regarded as one of the finest wild fungi for the table. The flesh is firm, does not discolor when cut, and holds its texture well when cooked. Flavor is mild, sweet, and nutty with a faintly peppery finish.

It is used in risottos, cream sauces, soups, and sautés. The firm texture makes it suitable for preservation by drying, freezing, or pickling. Dried specimens rehydrate well and retain most of their flavor. No lookalikes are toxic — all toothed Hydnum species are edible.

In Spain, the species is commercially harvested from wild populations and sold in markets. In Italy and France, it is a prized component of mixed mushroom dishes. Commercial cultivation has not been achieved due to its obligate mycorrhizal lifestyle.

## Bioactive Compounds

Hydnum repandum produces several bioactive metabolites of pharmacological interest. Lectins isolated from fruiting bodies show antiproliferative activity against human cancer cell lines (HeLa, MCF-7) through apoptosis induction.

Polysaccharides extracted from the mycelium exhibit immunomodulatory activity, stimulating macrophage phagocytosis and cytokine production (TNF-α, IL-6). β-glucan fractions are comparable in immunostimulatory potency to those from shiitake (Lentinula edodes).

The species also contains repandiol, a fatty acid derivative with reported antimicrobial properties against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli. Terpenoid compounds from H. repandum have shown moderate anti-inflammatory activity in vitro.

## Ecological Role

As an ectomycorrhizal symbiont, H. repandum facilitates nutrient exchange between forest trees and soil. The fungal mycelium enhances host tree access to phosphorus and micronutrients in exchange for photosynthate carbon. This relationship is particularly important in nutrient-poor forest soils.

The species contributes to soil aggregation and organic matter cycling through hyphal turnover. Dead fruiting bodies are rapidly colonized by saprotrophic fungi and invertebrates, returning nutrients to the forest floor.

H. repandum is an indicator of forest ecosystem health. Its presence correlates with mature forest stands, low nitrogen deposition, and diverse ectomycorrhizal communities. Decline in Hydnum populations has been documented in European forests affected by atmospheric nitrogen pollution.

## Related Species

Several closely related species are recognized following molecular revision of the genus. H. rufescens (reddish hedgehog) is smaller with a pinkish-orange cap and more acrid taste. H. albidum (white hedgehog) is entirely white with shorter spines.

H. umbilicatum (depressed hedgehog) has a deeply funnel-shaped cap with a central depression, found in conifer forests of western North America. H. ellipsosporum, recently described from Europe, has elliptical spines and associates with spruce. H. oregonense is a Pacific Northwest species with robust fruiting bodies.

## Cultivation Attempts

Cultivation of H. repandum has been attempted using in vitro mycorrhizal synthesis with host tree seedlings. Pine (Pinus sylvestris) and spruce (Picea abies) seedlings inoculated with H. repandum mycelium form functional ectomycorrhizas in controlled conditions.

However, fruiting body production in cultivated systems remains unreliable. The species requires specific soil conditions, host tree maturity, and possibly microbial associates that are difficult to replicate in nursery or greenhouse settings. Commercial cultivation is not currently viable.

Field inoculation of forest plantations with H. repandum has shown limited success in Scandinavia. Inoculated seedlings established mycorrhizas, but fruiting was sporadic and yielded inconsistent results after 5–10 year growth periods.

## Nutritional Profile

Fresh H. repandum fruiting bodies are approximately 90% water. Per 100 g fresh weight, they provide roughly 25 kcal, 2.5 g protein, 0.5 g fat, and 4 g carbohydrates (including 1.5 g dietary fiber).

The species is rich in B vitamins, particularly riboflavin (B2) and niacin (B3). Mineral content includes potassium, phosphorus, selenium, and copper. Dried specimens concentrate these nutrients approximately 10-fold.

Compared to cultivated Agaricus bisporus, wild H. repandum contains higher levels of ergothioneine, a potent antioxidant amino acid. Total phenolic content is moderate among edible mushrooms, contributing to its documented antioxidant capacity.

## Foraging and Identification Notes

The combination of pale cap, white flesh, and toothed hymenophore makes H. repandum one of the safest mushrooms for beginner foragers. No toxic lookalikes exist in North America or Europe. The only potential confusion is with other Hydnum species, all of which are edible.

The most common misidentification involves H. rufescens, which has a more orange cap and peppery taste. H. umbilicatum has a pronounced central depression. True H. repandum has a smooth, pale cap that is not deeply depressed.

Foragers should avoid specimens growing near roadsides or contaminated sites, as the species accumulates heavy metals from soil. Collection should focus on young, firm specimens; older specimens may harbor insect larvae in the stipe base.
