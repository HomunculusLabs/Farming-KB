# Muscarine

**Muscarine** (2,5-dimethyl-3-furyl-trimethylammonium) is a quaternary ammonium alkaloid that
acts as a parasympathomimetic agent by selectively activating muscarinic acetylcholine
receptors. It was the first known cholinomimetic alkaloid isolated from a natural source and
remains one of the most important mushroom toxins from both a pharmacological and historical
perspective.

Despite its enduring association with the fly agaric ([[amanita-muscaria]]), the name is
somewhat misleading, as [[amanita-muscaria]] contains only trace quantities of muscarine
that are insufficient to produce significant toxicity. The true clinical significance of
muscarine lies in other, less famous mushroom species that contain it in far greater
concentrations.

## Chemical Properties

Muscarine has the molecular formula C9H20NO2+ and a molecular weight of 174.26 g/mol (as the
cation). It exists as a quaternary ammonium salt and is typically isolated as the chloride or
iodide. Muscarine is a water-soluble, crystalline solid that is stable under normal conditions
of temperature and pH.

It is a relatively small molecule consisting of a furan ring substituted with methyl groups
at positions 2 and 5, bearing a trimethylammoniumethyl side chain at position 3.

The quaternary ammonium structure means that muscarine carries a permanent positive charge at
physiological pH, which has important pharmacological and pharmacokinetic implications.
Because of its permanent charge and high polarity, muscarine is poorly absorbed across the
blood-brain barrier and does not readily cross lipid membranes.

Unlike nicotine, which acts on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in both the central and
peripheral nervous systems, muscarine acts exclusively on peripheral muscarinic receptors
and produces no central nervous system effects. It is also poorly absorbed from the
gastrointestinal tract, which partially mitigates its toxicity when ingested.

## History of Discovery

Muscarine was first isolated in 1869 by the German pharmacologist Oswald Schmiedeberg and
his colleague Richard Koppe from the red fly agaric mushroom, [[amanita-muscaria]]. This
work was among the earliest examples of the successful isolation of an active pharmacological
principle from a natural product and represented a landmark achievement in the emerging
field of pharmacology.

The name derives from the Latin "musca" (fly), reflecting the traditional use of muscaria as an insecticide, whereby milk laced with pieces of the mushroom was left out
to attract and poison flies.

Schmiedeberg and Koppe demonstrated that extracts of [[amanita-muscaria]] produced effects
similar to those of parasympathetic nerve stimulation, including slowing of the heart rate,
increased glandular secretions, and constriction of the pupils. Their discovery established
the foundation for the systematic study of mushroom alkaloids.

Subsequent research, particularly in the mid-twentieth century, revealed that muscaria contains only minute quantities of muscarine (approximately 0.0002 to 0.003% of
fresh weight), and that its psychoactive effects are primarily due to other compounds,
notably ibotenic acid and its decarboxylation product muscimol.

## Natural Occurrence

While muscarine is most famously associated with [[amanita-muscaria]], the species with the
highest muscarine concentrations belong to entirely different genera:

- **Inocybe species:** Certain Inocybe species contain up to 0.4% muscarine by dry
  weight, making them the richest natural sources. inocybe patouillardii and
  inocybe fastigiata are particularly noteworthy for their high muscarine content.
  These are small, dull-colored mushrooms unlikely to be deliberately collected but may be
  accidentally encountered during foraging.

- **Clitocybe species:** clitocybe dealbata (the sweating mushroom) and
  clitocybe rivulosa contain significant muscarine concentrations sufficient to cause
  clinical poisoning. These are white to pale buff mushrooms that can resemble edible
  Tricholoma species.

- **[[amanita-muscaria]]:** Contains only trace amounts (0.0002 to 0.003%), far below the
  threshold needed to produce significant muscarinic symptoms.

- **[[amanita-pantherina]]:** Similarly contains only trace muscarine, with its effects
  attributable to ibotenic acid and muscimol.

- **Mycena species:** Some species contain low but detectable levels of muscarine.

- **Hebeloma and Omphalotus species:** Certain species have been reported to
  contain muscarine.

The concentration of muscarine varies considerably among species and even within a single
species depending on growing conditions, geographic location, substrate, and the
developmental stage of the fruiting body.

## Pharmacology

Muscarine is a direct-acting parasympathomimetic agent that selectively activates
muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (mAChRs), which are G-protein-coupled receptors found on
effector cells innervated by parasympathetic postganglionic fibers. Five subtypes of
muscarinic receptors have been identified (M1 through M5):

- **M1 receptors:** Predominantly in the CNS and gastric parietal cells; Gq-coupled
- **M2 receptors:** Cardiac tissue and smooth muscle; Gi-coupled (bradycardia)
- **M3 receptors:** Exocrine glands and vascular and visceral smooth muscle; Gq-coupled
- **M4 receptors:** CNS and lung; Gi-coupled
- **M5 receptors:** Limited distribution, primarily CNS; Gq-coupled

Muscarine is a non-selective agonist, activating all five subtypes with varying but
significant affinity. The activation of these receptors produces the characteristic "SLUDGE"
syndrome of parasympathomimetic toxicity:

- **S**alivation (excessive)
- **L**acrimation (excessive tearing)
- **U**rination (involuntary)
- **D**efecation (involuntary)
- **G**I distress (nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, diarrhea)
- **E**mesis (vomiting)

Additional effects include bradycardia, bronchoconstriction, bronchorrhea, miosis,
increased sweating (diaphoresis), hypotension, and increased gastrointestinal motility.
In severe cases, muscarine poisoning can cause pulmonary edema and cardiac arrhythmias.
