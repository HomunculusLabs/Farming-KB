## Overview

**Larrea tridentata** (Sessé & Moc. ex DC.) Coville, known as **creosote bush**, **chaparral**, or **gobernadora**, is an evergreen shrub in the family Zygophyllaceae. It is the dominant plant species of the warm deserts of [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], forming extensive monospecific stands across the Sonoran, Mojave, and Chihuahuan deserts.

The species is among the longest-lived plants on Earth, with individual clonal colonies estimated at 11,000–12,000 years old based on radiocarbon dating of preserved wood. Its resinous foliage emits a characteristic creosote-like odor, especially after rainfall, due to volatile terpenoid compounds.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae > Tracheophyta > Magnoliopsida > Zygophyllales > Zygophyllaceae > **Larrea** > *L. tridentata*.

The synonym *Larrea divaricata* Cav. has been applied to [[chiric-caspi-brunfelsia-south-american-hallucinogen]] populations, now recognized as a separate species complex. *L. tridentata* is restricted to [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] deserts.

The shrub grows 1–3 m tall with an open, spreading crown. Leaves are opposite, compound (bifoliate), with each leaf divided into two oval leaflets 7–18 mm long. Leaflets are dark green, resinous, and leathery, with a waxy cuticle that minimizes water loss.

Flowers are solitary, 2–3 cm in diameter, with five yellow petals that persist on the plant long after flowering. Flowering peaks in spring (March–May) but can occur opportunistically after summer rains.

The fruit is a spherical capsule covered in dense white pubescence, 5–8 mm in diameter, splitting open to release 5–8 seeds. Seeds are covered in sticky mucilage that aids in water retention during germination.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Larrea tridentata* is endemic to the warm deserts of the southwestern United States and northern Mexico, ranging from southeastern California and southern Nevada to western Texas and the central Mexican plateau. Its range extends from sea level to approximately 1,800 m elevation.

The species dominates on well-drained sandy or gravelly soils on alluvial fans, bajadas, and desert pavements. It is extremely drought-tolerant, capable of surviving with as little as 100 mm annual precipitation, though optimal growth occurs at 200–300 mm.

Clonal colonies spread slowly via lateral roots, with individual ramets connected by shared root systems. The species produces allelopathic compounds that inhibit germination of competing plants within its root zone, contributing to its dominance.

## Bioactive Compounds

The primary bioactive compounds are **nordihydroguaiaretic acid** (NDGA), a lignan constituting 5–10% of leaf dry weight, and numerous flavonoid aglycones including quercetin, kaempferol, and larreatin.

NDGA is a potent antioxidant and lipoxygenase inhibitor. It has been studied extensively as an anti-inflammatory agent and was historically used as a food preservative due to its radical-scavenging properties. However, NDGA is also hepatotoxic and nephrotoxic at high doses.

The resinous leaf coating contains volatile terpenes including camphor, α-pinene, and limonene. These compounds contribute to the characteristic rain odor and provide insecticidal and antimicrobial activity.

**Lignan glycosides** and additional phenolic compounds have been isolated from stem and root tissue, including justicidin B and larreaside, which show cytotoxic activity in vitro.

## Traditional and Ethnobotanical Use

Indigenous peoples of the southwestern United States and northern Mexico have used *L. tridentata* for millennia. The Pima prepared a decoction of leaves for respiratory ailments, kidney problems, and as a topical antiseptic.

The Coahuiltecan and other groups used the resin as a waterproofing agent for pottery and baskets, and as an adhesive for tool hafting. The dried stems served as fuel and construction material.

In contemporary herbal medicine, chaparral tea and capsules are marketed for their purported antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. However, the FDA has issued warnings about hepatotoxicity associated with chaparral consumption, and the German Commission E has rejected its internal use.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

NDGA acts primarily through inhibition of 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) and 12-lipoxygenase (12-LOX), enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of inflammatory leukotrienes. The IC₅₀ for 5-LOX inhibition is approximately 2–5 µM in cell-free systems.

The compound also demonstrates iron-chelating activity, reducing Fenton reaction-mediated oxidative damage. This dual mechanism (enzyme inhibition + radical scavenging) accounts for its potent anti-inflammatory profile in vitro.

However, NDGA metabolism produces reactive quinone intermediates that deplete hepatic glutathione and cause centrilobular necrosis. Chronic oral administration at doses exceeding 500 mg/day has been linked to cases of acute liver injury requiring transplantation.

Topical application is safer and remains the primary route in traditional medicine. Poultices of mashed leaves are applied to wounds, burns, and skin infections, exploiting the antimicrobial activity of the resin.

## Clonal Longevity

Individual *Larrea tridentata* clones are among the oldest known [[savory-living-organisms-as-tools]]. The "King Clone" in the Mojave Desert has been radiocarbon dated to approximately 11,700 years, originating in the early Holocene following the retreat of Pleistocene lakes.

Clonal reproduction occurs through root suckering and layering of branches that contact soil. New ramets establish independently while remaining connected to the parent root system, forming expanding rings of genetically identical stems.

Growth rings in the woody stems are poorly defined due to the absence of distinct dormant seasons in desert climates, making conventional dendrochronological dating difficult. Radiocarbon dating of preserved branch segments within clone centers provides the primary age estimates.

## Similar Species

*Larrea divaricata* Cav. sensu stricto occurs in South American arid regions (Argentina, Patagonia). It is morphologically similar but differs in leaf size (smaller leaflets), flower structure, and flavonoid profile.

*Larrea nitida* Cav. is another South American species with a more restricted range in the Atacama Desert. It is distinguished by its glossy leaf surface and more [[fukuoka-ideal-rice-plant-morphology-compact-growth-high-yield]] habit.
