# Briza media

## Overview

**Briza media** L., commonly known as quaking grass or common quaking grass, is a perennial bunchgrass native to Europe, western Asia, and North Africa. It is named for its distinctive pendulous spikelets that tremble and quiver in the slightest breeze, creating a shimmering effect in meadows and gardens.

The species is widely cultivated as an ornamental grass for its delicate, heart-shaped spikelets and graceful airy habit. It grows 30–60 cm tall and forms loose, open tufts with fine-textured foliage.

Quaking grass is a characteristic component of traditional hay meadows and calcareous grasslands across much of Europe. It has become naturalized in parts [[berry-growing]], New Zealand, and southeastern Australia.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Poales
- **Family:** Poaceae
- **Subfamily:** Pooideae
- **Tribe:** Poeae
- **Subtribe:** Brizinae
- **Genus:** Briza L.
- **Species:** B. media L.

The genus Briza contains approximately 12 species distributed across Eurasia, Africa, and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]]. Briza media is the type species and the only member of the genus widely distributed in temperate Europe.

### Morphology

Briza media is a cool-season (C3) perennial bunchgrass forming loose, open tussocks 20–40 cm in diameter. Culms are slender, erect to geniculate at the base, and 30–60 cm tall at maturity.

Leaf blades are flat, soft, and 5–20 cm long by 2–5 mm wide, with a characteristic bright green color. The ligule is membranous, 3–6 mm long, and acute to obtuse at the tip.

The inflorescence is an open, pyramidal panicle, 5–15 cm long, with slender nodding branches. Spikelets are broadly ovate to cordate (heart-shaped), 8–14 mm long, pendulous on long slender pedicels, and pale green to purplish in color.

Glumes are ovate, equal in length, 8–14 mm, with 7–11 prominent nerves. The lemma is similar in size and texture to the glumes, enclosing a single bisexual floret. The distinctive heart-shaped spikelet is unique among European grasses and makes identification straightforward.

## Distribution and Habitat

Briza media is native to most of Europe, from the British Isles and Scandinavia south to the Mediterranean, and eastward through the Balkans, Turkey, and the Caucasus. Its range extends to northwestern Africa (Morocco, Algeria) and western Iran.

The species is characteristic of dry to mesic calcareous grasslands, neutral hay meadows, open woodland margins, and rocky slopes. It tolerates a wide range of soil pH but shows a strong preference for calcareous or neutral substrates.

Elevation ranges from near sea level to approximately 2,000 m in the Alps and Apennines. It is more abundant in lowland and submontane zones than at high elevations.

Briza media has been introduced and naturalized in scattered locations across North America (particularly the northeastern United States and eastern Canada), New Zealand, and southeastern Australia. In some areas it persists as a casual garden escape without becoming invasive.

It is classified as a species of least concern by the IUCN, though it has declined significantly in intensively managed agricultural landscapes where traditional hay meadow management has been abandoned.

## Cultivation

Quaking grass is easily grown from seed, which germinates readily without stratification. Optimal germination occurs at 15–20°C with light exposure, typically reaching 70–85% within 14–21 days.

Seeds are sown on the soil surface or lightly covered, as they require some light for germination. Spring sowing is standard, though autumn sowing is possible in mild climates. Transplanting of container-grown seedlings is also common.

In the garden, Briza media thrives in full sun to partial shade and well-drained soils of average to low fertility. It tolerates drought once established but performs best with consistent moisture during [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

Plants are relatively short-lived as perennials, typically persisting 3–5 years before the center of the clump dies out. Division every 2–3 years maintains vigorous growth and provides propagation material.

The species is [[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]] resistant under most conditions. [[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]] may occur during humid summers but is rarely severe. No regular fertilization is needed; in fact, high nitrogen promotes floppy, lax growth.

## Ecological Role

Briza media provides habitat and forage for a variety of invertebrates in European meadow ecosystems. Its open, airy growth form creates microhabitats within the sward that benefit ground-dwelling arthropods.

The seeds are consumed by several species of small granivorous birds, including skylarks (Alauda arvensis) and various finches. The foliage serves as a food source for the larvae of several grass-feeding moths.

As a cool-season species, it provides early-season green forage in mixed meadows before warm-season grasses become active. Its relatively low productivity makes it a minor component of modern hay production, but it contributes to species diversity in semi-natural grasslands.

The pendulous spikelets facilitate [[clay-pellet-seed-dispersal]] by wind and by attachment to animal fur. Seeds remain viable in the soil seed bank for 3–5 years, allowing the species to persist through periods of unfavorable conditions.

In calcareous grassland restoration, Briza media is a useful indicator species for successful management. Its presence signals that traditional low-intensity grazing or cutting regimes have been maintained.
