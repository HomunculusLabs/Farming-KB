## Description

Disease-causing Streptococcus species in soil are bacterial pathogens that dominate when soil biology becomes severely imbalanced. High population levels of pathogenic Streptococcus are a clear indicator of "sick soil" — soil that has lost its microbial diversity to the point where one or two bacterial strains can monopolize resources. Ingham identifies these organisms as particularly problematic in dairy operations where accumulated manure creates ideal conditions for their proliferation.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Bacteria
- **Phylum**: Bacillota (Firmicutes)
- **Genus**: Streptococcus
- **Habitat**: Anaerobic microenvironments in disturbed or manure-laden soils
- **Role**: Opportunistic pathogen; indicator of severe soil biological degradation

## Key Facts

- High populations indicate severely degraded [[soil-food-web]]
- Dominance occurs when diversity drops enough for one or two strains to "win"
- Problems develop gradually over years, not overnight
- Selective [[duggar-mushroom-environmental-factors-temperature-moisture-light]] include:
  1. Reduced oxygen conditions (e.g., from rapid bacterial growth on waste residues)
  2. High nitrate levels (from undecomposed manure)
  3. High concentrations of undigested sugars, proteins, and short-chain anaerobic organic acids
- Lime application to raise pH is only a "symptom cure," not a real solution
- When soil gets waterlogged, lime cannot counteract anaerobic [[staycare-fungal-organic-acid-production-metal-leaching]]
- Associated pathogens in the same conditions: E. coli, fecal coliforms, Fusarium, blights, wilts, root rots
- Adding lime without improving fungi causes calcium to leach into drinking water

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Streptococcus dominance in soil illustrates the endgame of biological mismanagement: the complete collapse of microbial diversity. For cultivators, this is a cautionary example of what happens when soil is repeatedly treated with chemicals that kill beneficial organisms without replacing them. The solution requires restoring the complete food web — not just bacteria, but [[beneficial-fungi-trichoderma-and-mycorrhizae]], protozoa, nematodes, and [[microarthropods]]. Ingham notes that Gary Wegner of Natural Aeration has demonstrated repeatedly that correcting the biology eliminates Streptococcus problems entirely. The key insight is that the conditions favoring Streptococcus (low oxygen, high nitrates, [[hamilton-concentrated-organic-fertilizer-products]] waste) must be changed, not just the bacteria itself killed. [[compost]] tea applications that introduce competing beneficial organisms, combined with practices that improve soil aeration and structure, address the root cause.
