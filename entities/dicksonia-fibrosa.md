## Overview

**Dicksonia fibrosa** (Colenso) Cheeseman, commonly known as **wheki-ponga** or the woolly tree fern, is a stout tree fern endemic to New Zealand. It is distinguished from the related wheki (*Dicksonia squarrosa*) by its thicker trunk, persistent skirt of dead fronds, and the thick fibrous root mantle that gives the species its name.

Wheki-ponga reaches heights of 3–7 meters with a trunk diameter of 15–30 cm, making it more robust than wheki but substantially shorter and stockier than the towering mamaku (*Cyathea medullaris*). The dense fibrous trunk and pendulous dead frond skirt create a distinctive silhouette in the forest understory.

Unlike wheki, *Dicksonia fibrosa* does **not produce suckers** and grows as a solitary stem. It is more shade-tolerant and cold-hardy than most New Zealand tree ferns, occurring at higher elevations and in cooler, wetter forests than its congeneric relative.

The species is classified as **Not Threatened** under the New Zealand threat classification system, reflecting its broad distribution and stable populations throughout the main islands.

Wheki-ponga is one of the most recognizable tree ferns in New Zealand's montane forests, where its thick trunk and pendant frond skirt create a distinctive silhouette that differs markedly from the slender colonial habit of wheki.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Dicksoniaceae → Cyatheales → Polypodiopsida. *Dicksonia fibrosa* was described by William Colenso and later formally transferred to *Dicksonia* by Thomas Cheeseman. The specific epithet *fibrosa* refers to the thick fibrous root mantle clothing the trunk.

The trunk is erect, 3–7 m tall and 15–30 cm in diameter. It is covered with a **thick, fibrous root mantle** up to 5 cm deep, giving the trunk a soft, felted appearance distinct from the rough stipe bases of *Dicksonia squarrosa*.

Fronds are bipinnate, 1.5–2.5 m long, forming a compact crown. The stipe is stout, brown, and covered with **stiff reddish-brown hairs** and persistent scales. New fronds emerge covered in dense brown tomentum that gradually wears away.

A distinctive feature is the persistent **skirt of dead fronds** that hangs pendant from the crown, often reaching 1–2 m below the living fronds. This dead frond skirt is absent or minimal in *Dicksonia squarrosa*.

Pinnules are oblong, 2–5 cm long, with crenate margins and rounded tips. Sori are marginal, enclosed in the characteristic bivalved Dicksoniaceae indusium.

Spores are trilete, tetrahedral-globose, 35–50 μm in diameter, with a granulate perispore. The chromosome number is 2n = 130.

The thick fibrous root mantle distinguishes this species from all other New Zealand tree ferns. This mantle consists of adventitious roots growing from the vertical rhizome, creating a dense insulating layer that protects the growing apex from cold and desiccation.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Dicksonia fibrosa* is **endemic to New Zealand**, occurring throughout the **North Island**, **South Island**, and **Stewart Island/Rakiura**. It is absent from the Chatham Islands and the Kermadec Islands.

It grows from sea level to approximately **950 m elevation**, extending to higher altitudes than most New Zealand tree ferns. Populations in the southern South Island occur at lower elevations but experience colder winter temperatures.

Preferred habitats include **montane forests**, cloud forests, subalpine scrub margins, and cold, damp gullies. The species is particularly characteristic of **cool temperate rainforests** dominated by kamahi (*Weinmannia racemosa*) and southern beech (*Nothofagus* species).

It is more shade-tolerant than wheki and mamaku, often persisting in deep forest understory where other tree ferns fail to establish. The species tolerates snowfall and repeated frost, making it one of the most cold-hardy tree ferns in the world.

In the southern parts of its range, *Dicksonia fibrosa* frequently co-occurs with **silver fern** (*Cyathea dealbata*) in cool, wet forests. The two species can be distinguished at a distance by the wheki-ponga's pendant dead frond skirt and stockier trunk profile.

## Ecology

The thick fibrous trunk and dead frond skirt of *Dicksonia fibrosa* create a **unique microhabitat** in the forest understory. The fibrous mantle retains moisture and provides substrate for epiphytic bryophytes, lichens, and small ferns.

The pendant dead frond skirt functions as a **perch and shelter** for forest birds. Species such as **toutouwai** (robin, *Petroica australis*) and **miromiro** (tomtit, *Petroica macrocephala*) use wheki-ponga trunks as foraging posts.

The root mantle hosts diverse fungal communities, including **mycorrhizal associates** that enhance nutrient uptake from the infertile soils typical of montane New Zealand forests. Fallen fronds decompose slowly in the cool conditions, contributing to deep humus layers.

Unlike *Dicksonia squarrosa*, the solitary growth habit means individual plants cannot recover from trunk damage through suckering. However, the thick fibrous trunk provides excellent insulation against cold, allowing survival at higher elevations.

Spore production occurs annually, with mature plants producing moderate quantities of viable spores. Recruitment is limited by the requirement for consistently moist, shaded microsites for gametophyte establishment.

Despite this recruitment limitation, populations are generally stable because adult plants are extremely long-lived. Individual wheki-ponga specimens may persist for **80–100+ years** in favorable montane forest conditions, with slow but steady growth throughout their lifespan.

## Cultivation

*Dicksonia fibrosa* is cultivated as an ornamental tree fern, valued particularly for its cold hardiness and distinctive appearance. It is one of the most frost-tolerant *Dicksonia* species, surviving temperatures to **−8°C** or lower with minimal damage.

In cultivation, it prefers **deep shade to partial shade** and consistently moist, humus-rich soil. The thick trunk should never be allowed to dry completely, as the fibrous root mantle requires constant moisture to support the plant.

The species grows slowly, adding only 1–2 cm of trunk height per year. This slow growth makes it a long-term investment in garden design but also means it requires less space management than faster-growing tree ferns.

Propagation is exclusively by **spores**, as the species does not sucker or produce offsets. Spore germination follows the standard procedure for Dicksoniaceae: surface-sow on sterile peat at 15–20°C under high humidity.

Germination typically occurs within 6–12 weeks, somewhat slower than *Dicksonia squarrosa*. Juvenile fronds develop after 3–5 months, and plants reach transplantable size in 18–24 months from sowing.

The dead frond skirt, while distinctive in the wild, is sometimes trimmed in formal garden settings. However, retaining the skirt enhances the plant's cold tolerance by insulating the growing crown.

In European and North American cultivation, wheki-ponga is valued for its ability to thrive in **cool maritime climates** where other tree ferns fail. It performs well in the Pacific Northwest, western Scotland, and similar regions with mild, wet winters.

## Ethnobotany and Cultural Significance

The **thick fibrous trunk** of wheki-ponga was used by Māori as a building material and for crafting containers and vessels. The fibrous outer layer could be peeled in sheets and used as a **natural wrapping material** for food storage and transport.

The trunk pith was occasionally consumed as an emergency food, though it was less favored than mamaku pith. The fibrous root mantle was also used as **packing material** and as a substrate for growing seedlings in traditional Māori horticulture.

Dead fronds were used as **temporary roofing** for shelters and as insulation. The persistent dead frond skirt that distinguishes wheki-ponga from wheki provided a ready source of dry material for fire-starting and bedding.

In contemporary ecology, wheki-ponga is recognized as an important component of **montane forest restoration** planting mixes. Its cold hardiness and shade tolerance make it suitable for revegetation projects at higher elevations where other tree ferns struggle.

The species' ability to establish in deep shade makes it particularly valuable for **understory enrichment planting** in degraded forests, where it can persist for decades while slowly improving microsite conditions for other native species.

The species is sometimes planted in **memorial and ceremonial gardens** as a long-lived, resilient presence that connects cultural landscapes to the native forest ecology of New Zealand.

Unlike many tree ferns, wheki-ponga is not commercially harvested for horticultural trade at significant scale. Most cultivated specimens originate from spore propagation by specialist fern nurseries within New Zealand.
