## Overview

**Penstemon digitalis**, commonly called foxglove beardtongue or talus slope penstemon, is a herbaceous perennial native to eastern and central North America. It is one of the most widely cultivated penstemon species in temperate horticulture.

The species produces tall spikes of tubular, white to pale pink flowers from late spring through midsummer. The flowers superficially resemble those of true foxglove (*Digitalis*), which accounts for both the common and specific epithets.

Each plant produces multiple flowering stems from a basal rosette. Individual stems bear 30–60 flowers, opening sequentially from the base upward over a bloom period of 4–6 weeks.

Foxglove beardtongue is valued in gardens for its architectural flower spikes, long bloom period, and exceptional [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] once established. It has become a staple of prairie-style and naturalistic plantings.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Penstemon digitalis* belongs to the family Plantaginaceae, tribe Cheloneae.

**Full classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Magnoliopsida → Lamiales → Plantaginaceae → *Penstemon* Schmidel → *P. digitalis* Nutt. ex Sims.

The species was described by John Sims in 1823, based on material collected by Thomas Nuttall. The genus *Penstemon* is the largest genus endemic to North America, with approximately 270 species.

The genus name derives from the Greek *pente* (five) and *stemon* (stamen), referring to the five fertile stamens. The specific epithet *digitalis* (finger-like) refers to the tubular flowers resembling those of *[[digitalis-purpurea]]*.

**Stem:** Erect, glabrous to slightly hairy, 60–120 cm tall, often with a purplish tint. Stems are unbranched or sparingly branched near the inflorescence.

**Leaves:** Basal rosette of elliptic to ovate leaves, 10–20 cm long. Cauline leaves are opposite, lanceolate to ovate, clasping the stem, progressively reduced upward.

In the cultivar 'Husker Red', new foliage emerges burgundy-red and matures to a dark green with reddish overtones. Fall color can be attractive, with leaves turning shades of red and bronze before senescence.

**Flowers:** Arranged in a terminal panicle or thyrse, 15–30 cm long. Individual flowers are tubular, 20–30 mm long, white to pale pink, with a two-lipped corolla. The lower lip has three lobes; the upper lip has two.

The distinctive "beardtongue" feature is a sterile staminode (staminodial filament) covered in dense yellow hairs, located on the lower surface of the corolla tube. This structure aids in pollinator positioning.

**Fruit:** Capsule, ovoid, 8–12 mm long, containing numerous small, angular brown seeds.

Capsules mature 4–6 weeks after flowering and split open to release seeds. Wind dispersal is limited; most seeds fall within a meter of the parent plant. Seed viability in soil is reported at 2–3 years.

Seeds are small (approximately 2 mm long) and require light for optimal germination. Direct sowing on bare soil in autumn or [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] produces the best results.

## Distribution and Habitat

*P. digitalis* is native to the eastern and central [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], from Maine and Ontario west to South Dakota and Texas, and south to Florida.

The species occupies a broad range of open habitats including prairies, meadows, woodland edges, rocky slopes, and roadside verges. It tolerates both dry and mesic conditions.

Foxglove beardtongue thrives in full sun to partial shade and is adapted to a wide range of soil types. It is notably tolerant of clay soils and poor, rocky substrates where many other perennials struggle.

The species has a deep taproot system that contributes to its drought tolerance. Established plants can survive extended dry periods without supplemental irrigation, making them suitable for xeriscaping.

## Cultivation

Foxglove beardtongue is easily grown in well-drained soil in full sun. It is exceptionally drought-tolerant once established and requires minimal supplemental irrigation in most [[fukuoka-natural-farming-temperate-climates]].

Plants prefer lean, well-drained soils; overly rich or moisture-retentive soils can promote floppy growth and reduce winter survival. Good drainage is essential to prevent crown rot.

Propagation is by seed or division. Seeds germinate readily without stratification and bloom in the second year. Division of established clumps in early spring or autumn is the most reliable vegetative method.

The cultivar 'Husker Red' is the most widely grown selection, noted for its deep burgundy-red foliage and stems. It was introduced by the University of Nebraska and received the Perennial Plant Association's Plant of the Year award in 1996.

Other notable cultivars include 'Dark Towers' (darker foliage than 'Husker Red'), 'Mystica' (bronze-red new growth), and 'Pocahontas' (compact habit, pink flowers). Seed strains are also commercially available.

'Husker Red' remains the industry standard and is widely used in public landscapes across temperate North America and Europe. Its burgundy foliage provides color contrast even when the plant is not in flower.

## Ecology and Wildlife Value

The tubular flowers are adapted for pollination by long-tongued bees, particularly bumblebees (*Bombus* spp.). Honeybees, hummingbird moths (*Hemaris* spp.), and occasional hummingbirds also visit the flowers.

Nectar is produced at the base of the long corolla tube, rewarding long-tongued pollinators while excluding less efficient short-tongued visitors. This morphological specialization supports native bee diversity in planted landscapes.

Studies have shown that penstemon-rich plantings support higher native bee abundance and species richness compared to equivalent areas planted with non-native ornamentals. The early summer bloom period fills a temporal gap between spring ephemerals and late-summer composites.

The foliage provides cover for small insects and ground-dwelling arthropods. Several moth species use penstemons as larval host plants, though specific associations for *P. digitalis* are not well documented.

Deer and rabbit browse is generally low due to the slightly bitter foliage. This resistance makes the species valuable in gardens where herbivore pressure is a significant concern.
