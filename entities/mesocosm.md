## Mesocosm

## Description

A mesocosm is an experimental research system that occupies an intermediate position between highly controlled laboratory microcosms and unmanipulated field studies. Mesocosms are designed to simulate real-world ecosystem complexity while retaining some degree of experimental control. The concept was formalized by Eugene P. Odum (1984), who advocated for the use of mesocosms as a bridge between the unrealistic simplicity of microcosms and the uncontrollable complexity of natural ecosystems. In mycological research, mesocosms allow the study of fungal community interactions, [[decomposition]] dynamics, and nutrient cycling under semi-natural conditions.

## Classification

- **Concept type**: Experimental methodology / ecological research design
- **Scale**: Intermediate between microcosm (small, highly controlled) and natural ecosystem
- **Origin**: Odum, E. P. (1984). The mesocosm. BioScience 34:558–562
- **Application domain**: Ecosystem ecology, [[mycology]], environmental science

## Key Facts

- Mesocosms were formalized as a research concept by Odum (1984) as a way to increase ecological realism in controlled experiments.
- They contain a relatively large number of organisms and allow complex interspecific interactions to develop, unlike microcosms which may contain only one or a few species.
- The trade-off with increased complexity is a reduction in experimental control compared to microcosms, but this is offset by greater ecological realism.
- Anderson (1995) and Lawton and Jones (1995) advocated mesocosms for studying soil organisms as ecosystem engineers within more realistic settings.
- Mesocosm studies reveal fungal functional diversity and community dynamics that cannot be observed in pure-culture microcosm studies.
- They are particularly valuable for studying fungal succession on decomposing substrates, mycorrhizal community development, and the effects of environmental perturbations on fungal communities.
- Dighton (2003) describes a research progression from microcosm to mesocosm to natural ecosystem studies as essential for understanding fungal roles at multiple scales of resolution.
- Mesocosms bridge the gap between the micrometer scale of individual hyphal activity and the meter-to-hectare scale of ecosystem processes.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Mesocosm approaches can be used to test cultivation substrate formulations, composting protocols, and [[mycorrhizal-inoculation-guide]] strategies under semi-realistic conditions before scaling to field trials.
- They allow controlled comparison of fungal community development in different cultivation media or soil types while maintaining ecological complexity.
- Mesocosms can be used to study the impact of environmental stressors (drought, heavy metals, temperature changes) on fungal communities relevant to cultivation.
- The approach is valuable for optimizing [[mushroom-cultivation]] conditions: testing how [[saprotrophic-fungi]] interact with competing microorganisms in substrate at a manageable scale.
- Mesocosm experiments can validate whether laboratory findings about individual fungal species translate to community-level outcomes in cultivation systems.
- Understanding the mesocosm concept helps cultivation researchers design experiments that are ecologically meaningful rather than oversimplified.
