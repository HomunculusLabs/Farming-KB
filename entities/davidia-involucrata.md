## Overview

Davidia involucrata (dove tree, handkerchief tree) is a deciduous tree in the family Nyssaceae, native to south-central and southwestern China. It is renowned for its showy white bracts that resemble hanging handkerchiefs or doves in flight.

The tree was named after Armand David, the French Père David who first described it from specimens near Mupin (Baoxing) in Sichuan in 1869. It is the sole species in its genus, making it a monotypic taxon of considerable botanical interest.

Mature trees reach 12–20 m in height with a broad, rounded crown. The species is considered a relict element of the Tertiary flora that once covered much of the Northern Hemisphere.

Fossil records of Davidia pollen dating to the Paleogene have been found in Europe and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]]. The genus was once widespread across the Northern Hemisphere before Pleistocene glaciations restricted it to refugia in central China.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Davidia involucrata is the only species in the genus Davidia, placed in family Nyssaceae (formerly Cornaceae). Some authorities include it within a broadly defined Cornaceae, but molecular phylogenetics supports its placement in Nyssaceae alongside Nyssa and Camptotheca.

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Family:** Nyssaceae
- **Genus:** Davidia
- **Species:** D. involucrata Baill.

Two varieties are recognized: var. involucrata (large bracts, widespread) and var. vilmoriniana (smaller bracts, more western distribution). The latter is sometimes elevated to species rank as D. vilmoriniana by Chinese botanists.

Leaves are broadly ovate to cordate, 8–15 cm long, with sharply serrate margins and a distinctive acuminate tip. They are bright green above and densely pubescent beneath when young.

The true flowers are small, purple-green heads about 2 cm across, surrounded by two large unequal white bracts. The larger bract reaches 15–20 cm long, creating the dramatic display for which the species is celebrated.

Fruit is a globose, greenish drupe approximately 3 cm in diameter, containing a single hard seed. The fruit ripens in autumn and superficially resembles a small walnut.

Chromosome number is 2n = 42. The polyploid nature of the species has been noted as unusual within Nyssaceae, where most genera have lower chromosome numbers.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to south-central and southwestern China, including Sichuan, Hubei, Hunan, Guizhou, Yunnan, and Gansu provinces. Populations occur at 1,100–2,600 m elevation in montane mixed forests.

The species grows as a canopy or sub-canopy tree in association with Acer spp., Betula spp., Tilia spp., and various conifers. It prefers moist, well-drained, acidic soils on sheltered valley slopes.

Climate in native range features warm, humid summers and cool winters with moderate snowfall. Annual rainfall ranges from 1,000–1,800 mm, much of it falling during the summer monsoon.

The dove tree often occurs alongside other notable Chinese endemics such as [[metasequoia-glyptostroboides]] and [[cercidiphyllum-japonicum]]. These associations form distinctive relict plant communities of high conservation value.

Populations are highly fragmented due to the complex topography of the Hengduan Mountains and surrounding ranges. [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] is highest in Sichuan populations, suggesting a glacial refugium in this region.

The dove tree is considered a paleoendemic species, having survived multiple glacial cycles in the diverse topography of southwestern China. Its persistence underscores the importance of the Hengduan Mountains as a biodiversity refuge.

## Cultivation

Hardy in USDA zones 6–8. Requires sheltered positions with protection from cold, drying winds, especially when young.

Prefers deep, fertile, humus-rich, acidic to neutral soils that remain consistently moist. Intolerant of drought and compacted or poorly drained conditions.

Trees grown from wild-collected Chinese seed often display greater vigor than those from cultivated sources. This vigor likely reflects the genetic diversity maintained in wild populations across fragmented mountain habitats.

Flowering typically begins at 10–15 years from seed, though some specimens take 20+ years. Peak flowering occurs from May to June, with individual trees producing thousands of bracts.

Propagation is primarily by seed, which requires 3–6 months of warm stratification followed by 3 months of cold stratification. Germination rates are often erratic and may be spread over two [[jeavons-climate-adaptation-growing-seasons]].

Seeds should be sown fresh in autumn in a cold frame. The hard endocarp can be nicked with a file to improve water uptake and accelerate germination.

Grafting onto rootstocks of [[nyssa-sylvatica]] has been attempted with limited success. Cuttings are generally difficult to root, with success rates below 20% even under mist with hormonal treatment.

The variety vilmoriniana is considered slightly hardier and more compact than the type variety. It is sometimes preferred for smaller gardens in colder climates.

Autumn color is variable, ranging from yellow to orange-red on exposed leaves. Fall display is most reliable in regions with warm summers and cool autumn nights.

## Ecological Role and Uses

In native Chinese forests, Davidia involucrata is a component of the mixed mesophytic forest community. Its flowers attract pollinators including bees and flies during the spring flowering period.

The fruit is consumed by various birds and small mammals, which serve as seed dispersal agents. Fallen fruits contribute to the forest floor ecosystem and support invertebrate communities.

Seed predation by rodents and insects is significant in native habitats, with estimated predation rates of 40–60% of annual seed production. This natural attrition limits natural regeneration in disturbed sites.

In horticulture, the dove tree is among the most sought-after ornamental trees for temperate gardens. It was introduced to Western cultivation in 1904 by Ernest Wilson, who called it "the most interesting and beautiful of all trees of the north temperate flora."
