# Ochrolechia tartarea (Cudbear Lichen)

## Overview

*Ochrolechia tartarea* is a widespread crustose lichen in the family Ochrolechiaceae, historically one of the most important dye lichens in Europe. It produces lecanoric acid, which yields purple and red dyes through ammonia fermentation.

The species was the principal source of commercial cudbear and orchil dyes from the 16th through the 19th centuries. These dyes colored wool, silk, and liturgical textiles across Scandinavia, Scotland, and continental Europe.

Its thallus forms large, pale to medium grey crusts with distinctive rounded [[cervantes-air-quality-ventilation]] but has declined significantly in polluted regions.

- *[[psilocybe-semilanceata-worldwide-temperate-distribution-guzman-1998]]. In Europe, it ranges from Fennoscandia and the British Isles across Central Europe to the Mediterranean mountains and eastward through the Carpathians and Balkans.

The species occurs across [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] through the boreal forest to the Great Lakes region and Appalachian Mountains. It extends southward in mountainous areas.

It is strongly associated with the bark of mature, shade-tolerant trees. Preferred phorophytes include *Quercus* spp., *[[palmer-mushroom-hunting-field-identification]] character.

The dye chemistry of *O. tartarea* involves alkaline hydrolysis of lecanoric acid to orcinol and orsellinic acid. Traditional ammonia fermentation (urine treatment) converts these precursors to litmus and orchil dyes.

Cudbear dye production was industrialized in Scotland from the mid-18th century under the patent of Cuthbert Gordon. The lichen was fermented in a warm, alkaline solution for several weeks to produce purple-red dye solids.

## Traditional and Medicinal Uses

The primary historical use of *O. tartarea* was as a source of purple and red dyes. Scottish cudbear, Norwegian orchil, and French litmus were all produced principally from this species and close relatives.

In Scotland, commercial cudbear production centered on the Highlands and islands from the 1750s through the late 1800s. Lichens were collected from rocks and tree bark, fermented with ammonia (often urine), and processed into dye cakes.

Orchil dye was widely used in the European [[staycare-fungal-treatment-of-textile-industry-wastewater]] for dyeing wool and silk. It produced a range of reds, purples, and violets depending on the mordant, pH, and fermentation duration.

The litmus derived from *Ochrolechia* species became the standard pH indicator in chemistry from the 17th century onward. Litmus paper was manufactured commercially from lichen extracts well into the 20th century.
