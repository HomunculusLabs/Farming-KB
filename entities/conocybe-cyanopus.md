# Conocybe cyanopus

**Conocybe cyanopus** is a small but remarkably potent psilocybin-containing mushroom in the family Bolbitiaceae. Despite its diminutive size, it has been shown to contain significant concentrations of psychoactive alkaloids, making it one of the more potent mushrooms relative to its mass. The species is among the lesser-known members of the psilocybin mushroom group and is notable for the extreme identification risks it presents to foragers.

## Taxonomy and Discovery

The species was first described by mycologist R. Watling in the 1960s from European collections. The epithet "cyanopus" means "blue foot" or "blue stem," referring to the characteristic blue bruising that develops on the base of the stem. The genus Conocybe contains approximately 100 species, the vast majority of which are small, inconspicuous, non-psychoactive saprotrophic mushrooms. Only a handful of Conocybe species are known to contain psilocybin, with Conocybe cyanopus being the most notable. Other psilocybin-containing Conocybe species include *Conocybe kuehneriana* (see [[conocybe-smithii]]), and *[[guzman-conocybe-siligineoides-lost-sacred-mushroom-oaxaca-never-recollected]]* (see [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] in terms of alkaloid density. The high potency per unit weight is noteworthy given the tiny size of individual fruiting bodies. The [[amanita-phalloides]] (the death cap). conocybe filaris, in particular, has been responsible for fatal poisonings and contains amatoxins at levels comparable to lethal Amanita species. The mechanism of amatoxin toxicity involves inhibition of RNA polymerase II, leading to hepatic and renal failure. Symptoms typically onset 6–24 hours after ingestion, with an initial asymptomatic period that can falsely suggest the mushroom was harmless. Conocybe cyanopus is visually very similar to non-psychoactive and potentially deadly Conocybe species. The differences between species within this genus are often microscopic — requiring examination of spore morphology, cheilocystidia shape, and pileipellis structure. There is no reliable macroscopic feature that distinguishes Conocybe cyanopus from deadly Conocybe species in the field. The blue bruising at the stem base is suggestive but not definitive, and some toxic species may show similar discoloration.

For these reasons, Conocybe cyanopus is widely considered one of the most dangerous mushrooms for amateur foragers to pursue. No responsible mycological source recommends collecting any Conocybe species for consumption without expert microscopic verification and [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]]. The risk of fatal [[bloomfield-amatoxin-poisoning-mechanism]] far outweighs any potential psychoactive benefit from foraging in this genus.

## Research Importance

The study of psilocybin biosynthesis in Conocybe species has contributed to understanding the genetic and biochemical basis of [[gottlieb-psilocybin-production-extraction-methods]] across the fungal kingdom. The fact that psilocybin occurs in distantly related genera like Psilocybe, Panaeolus, Inocybe, Gymnopilus, and Conocybe suggests that the biosynthetic pathway for psilocybin has either ancient origins in the basidiomycete lineage or has been transferred horizontally between fungal lineages. Research on the [[psilocybin-biosynthesis-pathway]] has evolved independently in a genus only distantly related to the core Psilocybe clade. The Conocybe psilocybin gene cluster shares the same core genes (psiD, psiK, psiM, psiH) found in Psilocybe species, but comparative genomic analyses suggest that these genes were acquired through either deep ancestral inheritance followed by differential loss in most Conocybe lineages, or through more recent horizontal gene transfer events. The fact that only a handful of the approximately 100 Conocybe species contain psilocybin supports the latter hypothesis, as widespread retention from a common ancestor would likely result in a broader distribution of the trait within the genus. The study of psilocybin biosynthesis in Conocybe species has important implications for understanding the evolution of [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]] pathways across the basidiomycete fungi.
