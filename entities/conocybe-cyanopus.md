# Conocybe cyanopus

**Conocybe cyanopus** is a small but remarkably potent psilocybin-containing mushroom in the family Bolbitiaceae. Despite its diminutive size, it has been shown to contain significant concentrations of psychoactive alkaloids, making it one of the more potent mushrooms relative to its mass. The species is among the lesser-known members of the psilocybin mushroom group and is notable for the extreme identification risks it presents to foragers.

## Taxonomy and Discovery

The species was first described by mycologist R. Watling in the 1960s from European collections. The epithet "cyanopus" means "blue foot" or "blue stem," referring to the characteristic blue bruising that develops on the base of the stem. The genus Conocybe contains approximately 100 species, the vast majority of which are small, inconspicuous, non-psychoactive saprotrophic mushrooms. Only a handful of Conocybe species are known to contain psilocybin, with Conocybe cyanopus being the most notable. Other psilocybin-containing Conocybe species include *Conocybe kuehneriana* (see [[conocybe-kuehneriana]]), *Conocybe smithii* (see [[conocybe-smithii]]), and *Conocybe siligineoides* (see [[conocybe-siligineoides]]), all of which are similarly small and present comparable identification challenges. The taxonomic placement of Conocybe within the Bolbitiaceae has been supported by molecular phylogenetics, though some authorities have suggested merging the family into the Agaricaceae.

## Morphology

Conocybe cyanopus is a very small mushroom, easily overlooked in the field. The cap is only 8 to 20 mm in diameter, conic to campanulate (bell-shaped), with a smooth surface that is hygrophanous. When moist, the cap is ochre to reddish-brown, fading to pale buff or straw-colored as it dries. The gills are adnate, moderately spaced, and darken from pale gray-brown to rusty-brown with spore maturity. The stem is slender, 30 to 70 mm long and only 1 to 2 mm thick, fragile, and whitish to pale brown. The most distinctive feature is the blue to bluish-green bruising that develops at the base of the stem, which gives the species its name. A membranous partial veil may be present in very young specimens but is evanescent. The spore print is rusty-brown to cinnamon-brown. The overall stature of the mushroom is remarkably delicate — individual fruiting bodies are easily damaged by wind, rain, or even light handling.

## Microscopic Features

Microscopic examination is essential for reliable identification. Spores are ellipsoid to slightly amygdaliform, 10–14 × 6–8 µm, with a distinct germ pore and thickened walls. Basidia are two- or four-spored. Cheilocystidia are abundant, ventricose to lageniform, often with a long neck, and may be slightly capitate. Pleurocystidia are absent or rare. The pileipellis is a cutis of hyaline to pale brown, filamentous hyphae. These features distinguish *C. cyanopus* from other Conocybe species, particularly the deadly conocybe filaris, which has different cheilocystidia morphology and thinner-walled spores.

## Chemical Profile

Despite its small size, Conocybe cyanopus contains surprisingly high concentrations of psilocybin and psilocin. Published analytical data include:
- **Psilocybin**: 0.3% to 1.02% of dry weight
- **Psilocin**: 0.1% to 0.56% of dry weight
- **Baeocystin**: 0.05% to 0.15% of dry weight

These concentrations place Conocybe cyanopus among the more potent psilocybin mushrooms known, comparable to [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] in terms of alkaloid density. The high potency per unit weight is noteworthy given the tiny size of individual fruiting bodies. The [[psilocybin-biosynthesis-pathway]] in Conocybe species is of particular evolutionary interest, as it demonstrates independent acquisition of psilocybin production in a genus only distantly related to the core Psilocybe clade.

## Habitat and Distribution

Conocybe cyanopus grows in grassy habitats, including pastures, lawns, meadows, and disturbed ground. It is found in temperate regions of North America and Europe, though it is generally less common than larger and more conspicuous psilocybin mushrooms. In North America, it has been documented in the Pacific Northwest, the Rocky Mountains, and parts of the central United States. In Europe, it is found in scattered locations but appears to be relatively rare. The species fruits from late spring through autumn and can appear after warm rains in nutrient-rich grasslands. Its small size and inconspicuous appearance mean it is almost certainly under-reported, as few people notice or collect such tiny mushrooms. The species may be more widespread than current records suggest, as systematic surveys of grassland mycota often overlook the smaller Bolbitiaceae in favor of more conspicuous agarics. Climate change may also be affecting its distribution, as warming temperatures could be shifting the suitable range for temperate grassland fungi.

## Toxicity and Identification Risks

The genus Conocybe presents extreme identification dangers. Several Conocybe species contain amatoxins — the same deadly toxins found in [[amanita-phalloides]] (the death cap). conocybe filaris, in particular, has been responsible for fatal poisonings and contains amatoxins at levels comparable to lethal Amanita species. The mechanism of amatoxin toxicity involves inhibition of RNA polymerase II, leading to hepatic and renal failure. Symptoms typically onset 6–24 hours after ingestion, with an initial asymptomatic period that can falsely suggest the mushroom was harmless. Other toxic Conocybe species include [[conocybe-tenera]], which is widespread and common in grasslands but not known to contain psilocybin. [[conocybe-siligineoides]] is another Conocybe species found in similar habitats. Conocybe cyanopus is visually very similar to non-psychoactive and potentially deadly Conocybe species. The differences between species within this genus are often microscopic — requiring examination of spore morphology, cheilocystidia shape, and pileipellis structure. There is no reliable macroscopic feature that distinguishes Conocybe cyanopus from deadly Conocybe species in the field. The blue bruising at the stem base is suggestive but not definitive, and some toxic species may show similar discoloration.

For these reasons, Conocybe cyanopus is widely considered one of the most dangerous mushrooms for amateur foragers to pursue. No responsible mycological source recommends collecting any Conocybe species for consumption without expert microscopic verification and chemical analysis. The risk of fatal amatoxin poisoning far outweighs any potential psychoactive benefit from foraging in this genus.

## Research Importance

The study of psilocybin biosynthesis in Conocybe species has contributed to understanding the genetic and biochemical basis of psilocybin production across the fungal kingdom. The fact that psilocybin occurs in distantly related genera like Psilocybe, Panaeolus, Inocybe, Gymnopilus, and Conocybe suggests that the biosynthetic pathway for psilocybin has either ancient origins in the basidiomycete lineage or has been transferred horizontally between fungal lineages. Research on the [[psilocybin-biosynthesis-pathway]] has identified conserved gene clusters that appear to be responsible for the independent evolution of psilocybin production in these different genera. The Conocybe psilocybin gene cluster shares the same core genes (psiD, psiK, psiM, psiH) found in Psilocybe species, but the flanking regions and regulatory elements differ, supporting the hypothesis of convergent evolution or horizontal gene transfer events. This independent evolution of psilocybin production in multiple lineages is one of the most striking examples of convergent biochemical evolution in the fungal kingdom.

## Legal Status

Conocybe cyanopus is treated as a controlled substance in most jurisdictions due to its psilocybin content. In the United States, psilocybin and psilocin are Schedule I substances under the Controlled Substances Act, making possession of the fresh or dried mushrooms illegal. Similar restrictions apply in most European countries, Australia, New Zealand, and Canada. However, because of the species' extreme rarity and the identification risks associated with collecting any Conocybe species, it is virtually never encountered in law enforcement contexts. The legal status is primarily of academic interest given that no one deliberately seeks this species for recreational use.

## Seasonality and Collection Notes

Conocybe cyanopus fruits from late spring through autumn (May to October in the Northern Hemisphere), with peak fruiting typically during the warmest and wettest months. Individual fruiting events are brief — the delicate fruiting bodies often persist for only 1–3 days before deteriorating, particularly in hot or wet conditions. This ephemeral nature, combined with the species' tiny size, means that it is almost certainly encountered far more often than it is noticed or collected. Systematic mycological surveys that include careful examination of grassland microfungi would likely reveal a broader distribution than current records suggest. The species may also fruit in multiple flushes within a single season, triggered by successive rainfall events.
## Amatoxin Toxicity Context

The risk of fatal amatoxin poisoning from misidentified Conocybe species cannot be overstated. Amatoxins (primarily α-amanitin and β-amanitin) are bicyclic octapeptides that inhibit RNA polymerase II, leading to inhibition of protein synthesis and ultimately to hepatic and renal failure. The clinical course of amatoxin poisoning follows a characteristic pattern:
- **Latent phase** (6–24 hours): No symptoms, creating a false sense of security
- **Gastrointestinal phase** (24–48 hours): Severe nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and watery diarrhea
- **Apparent recovery** (48–72 hours): Temporary improvement as gastrointestinal symptoms resolve
- **Hepatic/renal failure** (3–7 days): Rising liver enzymes (AST, ALT), jaundice, coagulopathy, encephalopathy, and potentially death

Mortality rates for untreated amatoxin poisoning range from 10% to 60%, depending on the species ingested and the dose. Even with aggressive medical treatment (including intravenous silibinin, N-acetylcysteine, and in severe cases liver transplantation), outcomes can be poor. The combination of delayed onset, initial asymptomatic period, and potential lethality makes amatoxin-containing mushrooms among the most dangerous organisms that foragers can encounter.
## Phylogenetic Significance

The presence of psilocybin in Conocybe cyanopus is phylogenetically significant because it demonstrates that the [[psilocybin-biosynthesis-pathway]] has evolved independently in a genus only distantly related to the core Psilocybe clade. The Conocybe psilocybin gene cluster shares the same core genes (psiD, psiK, psiM, psiH) found in Psilocybe species, but comparative genomic analyses suggest that these genes were acquired through either deep ancestral inheritance followed by differential loss in most Conocybe lineages, or through more recent horizontal gene transfer events. The fact that only a handful of the approximately 100 Conocybe species contain psilocybin supports the latter hypothesis, as widespread retention from a common ancestor would likely result in a broader distribution of the trait within the genus. The study of psilocybin biosynthesis in Conocybe species has important implications for understanding the evolution of secondary metabolite pathways across the basidiomycete fungi.
