# Pennisetum purpureum (Napier Grass / Elephant Grass)

## Description

Pennisetum purpureum, commonly known as Napier grass or elephant grass, is a large, perennial tropical grass species native to the grasslands of Africa. It is one of the highest-yielding forage grasses in the world, capable of producing enormous volumes of biomass per hectare. In Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual*, it is listed among the important grass species used in permaculture systems.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Poaceae (Grass family)
- **Genus**: Pennisetum
- **Species**: P. purpureum
- **Common names**: Napier grass, elephant grass, Uganda grass, merker grass

## Key Facts

- **Growth habit**: Perennial bunchgrass reaching 2–6 meters in height under favorable conditions, with stout stems up to 3 cm in diameter.
- **Native range**: Tropical and subtropical Africa; now widely naturalized across the tropics worldwide.
- **Climate adaptation**: Thrives in warm, humid tropical and subtropical regions between 0–2000 m elevation. Sensitive to frost but can survive mild cold spells through its root crown.
- **Soil requirements**: Adaptable to a wide range of soils from sandy loams to heavy clays. Prefers well-drained, fertile soils with pH 5.0–8.0. Tolerates moderately acidic conditions and some waterlogging.
- **Propagation**: Primarily propagated vegetatively through stem cuttings or splits, as seed production is often poor. Can also be established from rooted tillers.
- **Growth rate**: Among the fastest-growing terrestrial plants, capable of producing 40–85 tonnes of dry matter per hectare per year under optimal management.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

### Forage and Livestock Feed

Napier grass is a cornerstone forage species in tropical livestock systems worldwide. Its high biomass yield makes it invaluable for cut-and-carry feeding systems, particularly for dairy cattle. When harvested at 6–8 week intervals, it provides nutritious fodder with crude protein levels of 8–12%. It is often intercropped with legumes such as Desmodium spp. or Centrosema to improve protein content and soil fertility simultaneously.

### Soil Conservation and Erosion Control

The deep, dense root system of Pennisetum purpureum makes it an excellent species for erosion control on steep slopes and along waterways. It is widely planted as contour hedgerows in alley cropping systems, where it serves as both a physical barrier to soil movement and a source of mulch biomass when cut. The hedgerows can be cut back regularly and the material applied as green manure to adjacent crop beds.

### Biomass and Bioenergy

Due to its extraordinary growth rate, Napier grass has attracted significant interest as a bioenergy feedstock. It can be used for biogas production through anaerobic digestion, as a feedstock for cellulosic ethanol, or directly combusted for heat and power generation. In permaculture energy systems, it offers a renewable on-site energy resource.

### Windbreaks and Microclimate Modification

The tall, dense growth of Napier grass makes it effective as a windbreak in exposed tropical sites. When planted in multiple rows, it can significantly reduce wind speed and create sheltered microclimates for more sensitive crops. This function is particularly valuable in establishing new permaculture sites in windy tropical lowlands.

### Integration with Push-Pull Pest Management

In East Africa, Pennisetum purpureum is a key component of the push-pull intercropping system developed by ICIPE. When planted as a border crop around maize fields, it attracts stemborer moths (the "pull"), while Desmodium intercropped within the maize repels them (the "push") and suppresses striga weed. This system dramatically reduces crop losses without chemical pesticides.

### Companion Planting and Guild Design

In permaculture guilds, Napier grass serves multiple functions simultaneously: biomass production, wind protection, mulch generation, and habitat for beneficial insects. It pairs well with nitrogen-fixing trees and shrubs, which can provide shade that prevents the grass from becoming too rank during peak growing season.

## Management Considerations

Napier grass requires regular cutting (every 6–8 weeks) to maintain palatability and nutritional quality for livestock. If left uncut, stems become woody and unpalatable. It responds well to nitrogen fertilization but can persist on moderately fertile soils without additional inputs when grown in association with legumes. The species can become invasive in some contexts due to its vigorous growth, so its placement in permaculture designs should be planned with containment in mind, using physical barriers or strategic harvesting regimes.

### Riparian Buffer Strips

In permaculture water management designs, Napier grass is an excellent candidate for riparian buffer strips along streams, ponds, and waterways. Its deep roots stabilize banks while its above-ground biomass filters sediment and nutrient runoff from upslope areas before it reaches water bodies. When combined with leguminous shrubs, these buffer strips become multi-functional elements in the landscape design.

### Nutrient Cycling

The rapid growth and subsequent decomposition of Napier grass biomass creates an efficient nutrient cycling mechanism. When slash-and-mulch management is practiced, nutrients captured from deep soil layers by the grass roots are redistributed to the surface as decomposing mulch, making them available to shallower-rooted crop plants. This "nutrient pumping" effect is particularly valuable on heavily leached tropical soils.

## Cultivars and Breeding

Notable cultivars include 'Bana' (Uganda selection, high yield), 'French Cameroon' (disease-resistant), 'Pusa Napier' (Indian hybrid), and various Pennisetum purpureum × P. typhoides hybrids (King grass, Mott grass) that combine elephant grass vigor with improved disease resistance and palatability. Hybrid selections often provide better nutritional profiles and management characteristics for smallholder systems.

## Toxicity Notes

Napier grass can accumulate nitrates under heavy nitrogen fertilization, particularly during drought periods. Livestock producers should be cautious of nitrate poisoning when grazing heavily fertilized stands during dry spells. Regular cutting and wilting before feeding reduces this risk.
