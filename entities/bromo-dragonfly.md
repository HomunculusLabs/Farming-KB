## Overview

Bromo-DragonFLY (1-(4-bromofuro-2,5-dimethyl-4-ethylbenzyl)propan-2-amine)
is an extremely potent synthetic psychedelic compound of the DOx
(dimethoxyamphetamine) family. A substituted phenethylamine with a
difuranyl ring system, it dramatically exceeds related compounds like DOI
and DOC in both potency and duration.

First synthesized by David E. Nichols at Purdue University in the late
1990s during serotonin receptor structure-activity studies, it was never
intended for human consumption. It entered the recreational market as a
research chemical in the mid-2000s and has been associated with multiple
deaths due to its exceptional potency, delayed onset, and prolonged
duration — making it one of the most dangerous psychedelic compounds known.

## Chemical Properties

**Molecular formula:** C15H14BrNO2. **Molecular weight:** 320.19 g/mol.
**IUPAC name:** 1-(8-Bromobenzo[1,2-b;4,5-b']difuran-4-yl)propan-2-amine.

The compound features a benzodifuran ring system fused to the core
phenethylamine structure, with a bromine atom at the 8-position and
methyl groups at the 4- and 5-positions. The difuranyl substitution
pattern is responsible for its extraordinary receptor affinity — the
extended aromatic system increases 5-HT2A binding interactions far beyond
what dimethoxy substitutions achieve.

It exists as a free base, commonly encountered as an off-white to tan
powder. It is not water-soluble and is typically applied to blotter paper
or sold as a powder for oral or sublingual administration.

## Pharmacology

Bromo-DragonFLY acts primarily as a potent agonist at the 5-HT2A serotonin
receptor, with significant affinity for 5-HT2B and 5-HT2C subtypes. Its
binding affinity (Ki) at 5-HT2A is approximately 0.04 nM — roughly 100
times more potent than DOI (Ki ≈ 4 nM) and approximately 10,000 times
more potent than mescaline.

This extreme potency is attributed to the difuranyl ring system, which
creates an extended planar aromatic structure with optimal geometry for
receptor binding. The compound also shows moderate affinity for alpha-1
adrenergic receptors, contributing to pronounced vasoconstrictive effects
that are a major factor in its toxicity and the severe tissue necrosis
observed in overdose cases.

## Effects and Dosage

Active doses are extraordinarily low: threshold 100–200 µg, light dose
200–500 µg, common dose 500 µg – 1 mg, strong dose 1–2 mg.

Onset is characteristically slow, typically 2–6 hours after oral
administration. This delayed onset is a major hazard — users who believe
they have taken an inactive substance may redose, leading to dangerous
cumulative intake.

Duration is extremely long: primary effects last 12–24 hours, with
aftereffects persisting 12–48 additional hours. The total experience can
span 2–3 days, with residual stimulation and perceptual distortions
extending well beyond the primary effects. Subjective effects include
intense visual hallucinations, profound cognitive alteration, time
distortion, ego dissolution, and synesthesia.

## Harm Reduction and Toxicity

Bromo-DragonFLY has been directly implicated in multiple fatalities.
Critical harm reduction information:

- **Vasoconstriction:** Severe peripheral vasoconstriction can cause
  tissue ischemia, gangrene, and limb amputation. Multiple documented
  amputations have followed overdose.
- **Delayed onset danger:** The 2–6 hour onset leads to frequent
  redosing. Users should NEVER redose before at least 6 hours elapse.
- **Extreme duration:** Prepare for a 24+ hour experience. Do not drive
  or handle responsibilities for at least 48 hours.
- **Dosage accuracy:** Sub-milligram active doses require analytical
  equipment for accurate measurement. Eyeballing is extremely dangerous.
- **Misidentification risk:** Has been sold as LSD on blotter paper,
  a substitution that is potentially lethal.

There is no specific antidote. Treatment is supportive, emphasizing
management of vasoconstriction and prevention of tissue necrosis.

## Legal Status

Bromo-DragonFLY is controlled in most major jurisdictions: Schedule I
in the United States (under the Federal Analog Act), Class A in the
United Kingdom, controlled across EU member states following the 2009
EMCDDA risk assessment, and Schedule 9 in Australia. Sweden classified it
as a hazardous substance following multiple overdose incidents.
