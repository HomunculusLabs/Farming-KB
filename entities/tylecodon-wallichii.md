## Overview

**Tylecodon wallichii** (Harv.) Tölken is a deciduous succulent shrublet in the family Crassulaceae, endemic to the Western and Northern Cape provinces of South Africa. It is one of the most distinctive species in the genus, recognized by its thick, tuberous base and slender, pale-green stems.

The species produces dense clusters of tubular, yellowish-green flowers during the summer months when the plant is leafless. It is named after Nathaniel Wallich, a prominent Danish botanist who directed the Royal Botanic Garden Calcutta.

Like all members of the genus, it is winter-growing and summer-dormant, shedding its leaves as it enters flower. Tylecodon wallichii is popular in specialist succulent collections but requires careful handling due to its toxicity.

In cultivation, it is sometimes referred to by the common name "Wallich's butter bush," though this name is not widely used. Its compact size and attractive caudex make it a favorite among caudiciform enthusiasts.

The species is easily distinguished from other Tylecodon by its very slender stems and small, narrow leaves. In the wild, plants may form low, spreading mats rather than the more upright habit seen in cultivation.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Family: Crassulaceae
- Genus: *Tylecodon*
- Species: *T. wallichii* (Harv.) Tölken

The species was originally described as *Cotyledon wallichii* by Harvey in 1862 before Tölken transferred it to the genus *Tylecodon* in 1978 during his revision of the Crassulaceae of southern Africa.

It is a dwarf succulent shrublet reaching 8–20 cm in height. The plant develops a thick, partially exposed tuberous caudex from which multiple slender, erect stems arise.

Leaves are spirally arranged, linear to lanceolate, and covered with a fine waxy bloom. They are glaucous green, 20–40 mm long and 3–5 mm broad, appearing during the winter growing season.

Flowers are tubular, pendulous, and pale yellowish-green, borne in dense terminal clusters on bare stems. The corolla tube is approximately 12–15 mm long with recurved lobes.

Flowering occurs from November through January in the Southern Hemisphere. The flowers are pollinated by sunbirds and certain bee species that are attracted to the nectar-rich corollas.

The fruit consists of a cluster of follicles that split open when mature, releasing numerous tiny seeds. Each seed is less than 1 mm long and dispersed by wind.

## Distribution and Habitat

Tylecodon wallichii is endemic to the succulent Karoo and fynbos biomes of South Africa. Its range extends from the Richtersveld in the Northern Cape southward through Namaqualand to the Western Cape.

It grows on rocky slopes, quartzite gravel plains, and in crevices of exposed outcrops at elevations of 200–1,200 m. The species favors well-drained, mineral-poor soils derived from quartzite and granite.

Rainfall in its native habitat occurs primarily in winter, averaging 100–250 mm annually. Plants endure extreme summer heat, often exceeding 40°C, by relying on their tuberous root reserves.

The species co-occurs with other succulent specialists including *Conophytum*, *Crassula*, and *Lithops* species in the wild.

Populations are generally small and localized, with individual colonies numbering fewer than 100 plants. The species shows limited gene flow between disjunct populations due to its restricted pollinator assemblage.

Climate change poses a long-term threat as increasing temperatures and shifting rainfall patterns alter the winter-rainfall regime on which the species depends.

Illegal collecting by succulent enthusiasts has also been reported at several accessible sites. The species is not currently protected under CITES appendices.

## Cultivation

Tylecodon wallichii requires a well-drained, mineral-based growing medium. A mix of pumice, coarse sand, and a small proportion of loam provides ideal conditions.

Watering should commence in autumn when new leaves emerge and continue through the winter growing season. Water should be withheld entirely once leaves begin to yellow in late spring.

The plant prefers full sun to partial shade with excellent air circulation. It is hardy to approximately −2°C for brief periods but requires frost-free conditions in cultivation.

Propagation is primarily by seed, which should be sown in autumn on the surface of a sandy medium. Seedlings are slow-growing and may take 3–5 years to develop a characteristic caudex.

Stem cuttings are occasionally successful if allowed to callus for several days before planting. However, plants grown from cuttings typically do not develop the impressive tuberous base of seed-grown specimens.

Mealybugs and root mealybugs are the most common pests in cultivation. Systemic insecticides applied during the growing season are effective, but care should be taken to avoid treating dormant plants.

The species is well-suited to container culture and bonsai-style presentation. Specimens with exposed caudexes are highly prized by collectors and regularly appear in succulent exhibitions.

## Ecology and Toxicity

Tylecodon wallichii is adapted to arid conditions through its CAM (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism) photosynthetic pathway. This allows the plant to fix carbon dioxide at night, minimizing water loss through transpiration.

The species contains cardiac glycosides, particularly cotyledontoxin, which are toxic to livestock and humans. Ingestion can cause nausea, cardiac arrhythmia, and in severe cases, death.

Livestock poisoning by Tylecodon species is a recognized problem in the Karoo grazing lands. Sheep and goats are most commonly affected, particularly during drought conditions when other forage is scarce.

Despite its toxicity, the plant is an important food source for certain specialist insects. Some species of aphids and mealybugs feed on the sap without apparent ill effect.

In its native habitat, the species contributes to soil stabilization on steep, rocky slopes. Its tuberous root system helps prevent erosion in the fragile Karoo ecosystem.

The conservation status of Tylecodon wallichii has not been formally assessed by the IUCN. However, its restricted range and small population sizes warrant monitoring, particularly in areas affected by mining and overgrazing.

## Related Species

The genus *Tylecodon* comprises approximately 50 species, all endemic to southern Africa. Several related species are also cultivated as ornamental succulents.

*Tylecodon reticulatus* is a close relative distinguished by its more robust growth habit and reticulate (net-like) pattern on the bark. It is similarly toxic and shares the winter-growing cycle.

*Tylecodon paniculatus*, known as butter bush, is a much larger species forming shrubs to 2 m tall. It is the most commonly cultivated member of the genus and shares the same tubular flower morphology.

*Tylecodon racemosus* produces distinctive reddish flowers and has a more compact growth form than *T. wallichii*. It occurs in overlapping range areas of the Western Cape.

*Tylecodon sulphureus* is distinguished by its sulphur-yellow flowers and more robust, branching stems. It is found primarily in the Richtersveld region.

*Tylecodon cordiformis* is a recently described species from the Knersvlakte with heart-shaped leaves. It represents the closest morphological ally to *T. wallichii* within the genus.
