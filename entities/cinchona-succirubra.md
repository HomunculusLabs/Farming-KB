## Overview

Cinchona succirubra, commonly known as the red cinchona or red bark tree, is a species of flowering tree in the family Rubiaceae. It is one of the most commercially significant of the cinchona species due to its high bark yield and relative ease of cultivation.

The species was a major source of quinine throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries, contributing substantially to global anti-malarial efforts. Its bark contains a characteristic reddish pigment that distinguishes it from other cinchona species.

Native to the montane forests of the Andes, Cinchona succirubra has been widely planted [[strain-isolation-from-wild-specimens]] can exceed 20 meters in optimal cloud forest habitat.

The leaves are broadly elliptic to ovate, 15 to 30 centimeters long, with a glossy dark green adaxial surface and a paler abaxial surface. The leaf margins are entire or slightly undulate, and the petioles are stout and 2 to 4 centimeters long.

The inflorescences are terminal or axillary panicles bearing numerous small, tubular, fragrant flowers. The corolla is white to pale pink or rose-colored, 10 to 15 millimeters long, with five lobes.

The fruit is a small ovoid capsule, approximately 1.5 to 2.5 centimeters long, containing numerous flat, winged seeds. The capsule splits open at maturity to release the seeds, which are dispersed by wind.

The bark is thick, deeply fissured, and characteristically red when freshly cut, producing a distinctive reddish-brown exudate. This red pigment is due to anthocyanin compounds and is the origin of the common name "red bark tree."

Root morphology includes a well-developed taproot system in seedlings, transitioning to a shallower but extensive lateral root network in mature trees. This root architecture supports growth on steep, well-drained Andean slopes.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cinchona succirubra is native to the eastern slopes of the Andes in Ecuador, Peru, and possibly southern Colombia. It occurs at elevations of 1,000 to 2,500 meters above sea level in cloud forest and montane rainforest ecosystems.

Associated native flora includes other Rubiaceae genera, Melastomataceae, and cloud forest epiphytes such as bromeliads and orchids. The species is frequently found near stream courses and on windward slopes with persistent cloud cover.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The bark of Cinchona succirubra is the primary economic product, containing a complex mixture of quinoline alkaloids. Total alkaloid content in the dried bark ranges from 3 to 7 percent, depending on the age of the tree and growing conditions.

The principal alkaloids are quinine, quinidine, cinchonine, and cinchonidine. Cinchona succirubra typically contains a higher proportion of cinchonine and cinchonidine compared to Cinchona ledgeriana, which is valued for its higher quinine yield.

Quinine from this species was the standard anti-malarial drug for over three centuries, acting by interfering with the heme detoxification pathway in Plasmodium parasites. Quinidine, also present in the bark, is used in cardiac pharmacology as a Class Ia antiarrhythmic agent.

The bark also contains quinovic acid glycosides and tannins, which contribute to its traditional use as a bitter tonic and digestive aid.
