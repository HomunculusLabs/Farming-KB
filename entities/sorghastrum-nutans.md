## Taxonomy and Morphology

Sorghastrum nutans (L.) Nash belongs to the family Poaceae, subfamily Panicoideae, tribe Andropogoneae.

It was originally described as Holcus nutans by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 and later transferred to Sorghastrum by George Valentine Nash in 1903.

The species epithet "nutans" refers to the nodding habit of its mature inflorescence.

Plants form dense bunches or tussocks reaching 1.2 to 2.1 meters in height at maturity.

Culms are solid, erect, and often covered with a distinctive bluish-white waxy bloom near the base.

Leaf blades are 30 to 60 cm long and 4 to 8 mm wide, flat to slightly involute, with a prominent midrib.

The ligule is a fringed membrane 3 to 6 mm long, a useful diagnostic character distinguishing it from similar Andropogoneae.

The inflorescence is a narrow, contracted panicle 15 to 30 cm long that arches or nods at maturity.

Spikelets are paired, with one sessile and one pedicellate, a characteristic feature of the Andropogoneae tribe.

Each spikelet is 8 to 12 mm long, golden-yellow to bronze at anthesis, producing a conspicuous plume.

The grain is a caryopsis approximately 3 mm long, light brown, and released gradually as it matures.

Root systems are deep and extensive, with individual roots penetrating 1.5 to 3 meters into the soil profile.

## Distribution and Habitat

Sorghastrum nutans is native to central and [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]], offering cover, nesting sites, and seeds for numerous bird and small mammal species.

The seeds are consumed by granivorous birds including field sparrows, slate-colored juncos, and various finch species.

Deer and elk browse the foliage in spring and early summer when it is young and succulent.

As a warm-season species, it exhibits peak growth during the hot months of June through August.

It is well-adapted to frequent fire regimes, resprouting vigorously from basal buds after prescribed or wild fire.

Fire suppression in the eastern tallgrass prairie region has contributed to the decline of indiangrass and its replacement by woody vegetation.
