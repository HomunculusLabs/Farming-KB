## Campanula medium

## Overview

Campanula medium, commonly known as Canterbury bells, is a robust biennial species in the family Campanulaceae. It is native to southern Europe, from France to the Balkans, and has been cultivated for centuries as an ornamental garden plant.

The species produces a rosette of basal leaves in its first year, then sends up tall flowering spikes in the second year. The large, showy campanulate flowers are typically violet-blue, though pink and white forms exist in cultivation.

Canterbury bells are among the most recognizable garden flowers in the temperate world. The common name derives from the bell-shaped flowers thought to resemble the bells carried by pilgrims to Canterbury Cathedral.

Plants reach 60–120 cm in height when in flower, making them one of the tallest cultivated bellflowers. The flowering period extends from late spring through midsummer.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Campanula medium belongs to the section Medium of the genus Campanula. It is a diploid species with 2n = 32.

The species is distinguished from other European bellflowers by its large flower size (4–6 cm long), calyx segments that are broadly ovate and spreading, and biennial life cycle. No subspecies are formally recognized.

First-year basal rosettes consist of ovate to lanceolate leaves 10–20 cm long with crenate margins. The leaves are light green, softly hairy, and form a flat rosette close to the ground.

In the second year, the flowering stem is stout, erect, and covered with spreading hairs. Stem leaves are smaller, sessile, and clasping, gradually reduced in size upward.

The inflorescence is a tall, loose raceme or panicle bearing numerous large, pendant to horizontal flowers. Each flower is broadly campanulate, 4–6 cm long, with five rounded lobes.

The calyx is notable, with five broadly triangular lobes that spread outward between the corolla lobes. This "calyx between corolla lobes" character is a key diagnostic feature.

The fruit is a large, nodding capsule dehiscing by three basal pores. Seeds are numerous, small, light brown, and ellipsoid.

These naturalized populations are usually short-lived and dependent on regular reseeding.

In its native mountain habitats, the species is typically found on limestone-derived soils. It associates with other subalpine forbs such as Geranium sylvaticum and [[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]] in humid conditions. Providing good air circulation and avoiding overhead watering reduces disease problems.

Staking is advisable in exposed sites, as the tall flowering stems can be top-heavy and prone to wind damage. Bamboo canes or grow-through supports work well.

## Ecological Role

The large, pendant flowers are pollinated primarily by bumblebees, which can navigate the broad corolla tube. The flowers produce copious nectar at the base of the ovary.

The long flowering period provides an extended nectar source for pollinators in early to midsummer. This makes Canterbury bells valuable in wildlife gardens and pollinator plantings.

As a biennial, the species plays a role in early successional plant communities in its native habitat. The basal rosette occupies ground in the first year, while the flowering stem provides vertical structure in the second year.

The numerous small seeds are dispersed by wind from the dehiscent capsules. In garden settings, self-seeding is common and can produce naturalized colonies where conditions are favorable.
