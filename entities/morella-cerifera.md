# Morella cerifera

## Overview

**Morella cerifera** (southern wax myrtle) is an evergreen to semi-evergreen shrub or small tree native to the southeastern United States, the Caribbean, and parts of [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] extends to the Oligocene. Pollen and leaf fossils indicate that the genus was once more widely distributed across temperate [[guzman-global-distribution-patterns-neurotropic-fungi]] reflect post-glacial expansion from southern refugia.

## Morphology

Southern wax myrtle is a fast-growing, multi-stemmed evergreen shrub or small tree. The bark is smooth and gray-brown on young stems, becoming rougher and darker with age. Branching is dense and ascending, forming an oval to rounded crown.

Leaves are simple, alternate, narrowly elliptic to oblanceolate, 3–10 cm long and 1–2.5 cm wide. Leaf margins are entire or remotely serrate toward the tip. The upper surface is dark green, glossy, and dotted with amber resin glands; the lower surface is yellowish-green with dense glandular pubescence. When crushed, leaves emit a spicy, bayberry-like fragrance. The essential oil composition varies seasonally and geographically, with myrcene, limonene, and cineole as the dominant volatiles.

Plants are evergreen in the southern portion of the range and semi-evergreen to deciduous in the northern portion. Leaf persistence depends on winter severity; temperatures below -12°C cause significant defoliation. In mild climates, foliage retention approaches 80–100% year-round. Northern populations are effectively semi-evergreen, losing 40–60% of leaves during cold winters.

Flowers are unisexual and appear in early spring (February–April). Male catkins are yellowish-green, 1–2.5 cm long, borne in dense clusters at leaf axils. Female flowers are tiny and greenish, borne in short axillary spikes. Wind pollination requires male and female plants in close proximity.

Fruit is a small drupe, 3–5 mm in diameter, covered in a thin, bluish-white waxy coating. Fruit maturity from August to October and persists into winter. Each fruit contains a single hard seed. The waxy coating, composed primarily of triglycerides of myristic and palmitic acids, constitutes approximately 15–25% of fruit dry weight. Wax melting point is approximately 45–48°C, similar to that of northern bayberry but with slightly different fatty acid ratios.

Seed dispersal is primarily by birds. Germination rates are highest after passage through avian digestive tracts, which removes the waxy coating. Seeds remain viable in the soil for 1–3 years.

Root systems are extensive and fibrous, with lateral roots extending 2–3 m from the crown. Actinorhizal nodules are abundant on fine roots in well-aerated soils, appearing as small, coral-shaped outgrowths 2–8 mm in diameter. Nodulation is reduced on waterlogged or compacted sites where soil oxygen is limited.

## Distribution and Habitat

Southern wax myrtle is distributed from southern New Jersey along the Atlantic coast to Florida, westward along the Gulf coast to Texas, and southward through Mexico to [[dighton-fungi-acidifying-pollutants-forest-ecosystems]], southern wax myrtle often forms the dominant shrub layer beneath a canopy of live oak (*[[pinus-elliottii]]*). These wax myrtle thickets are critical habitat components for the endangered red-cockaded woodpecker and serve as winter cover for white-tailed deer along the Atlantic coast.

## Ecological Role

Like all Myricaceae, southern wax myrtle forms actinorhizal root nodules with *Frankia* symbionts. [[fungal-habitat-diversity]] of the host plant. Cross-inoculation studies have shown that effective Frankia strains from wax myrtle can nodulate *Myrica gale* and *Alnus* species, though the reverse is less consistent.

## Cultivation

Southern wax myrtle is one of the most widely planted native ornamental shrubs in the southeastern United States. Its rapid growth rate (0.5–1.5 m/year), evergreen foliage, salt tolerance, and drought resistance make it suitable for screening hedges, foundation plantings, and coastal landscapes.
