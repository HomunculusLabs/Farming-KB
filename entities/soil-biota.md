## Description

Soil biota refers to the complete community of living organisms inhabiting the soil: bacteria, fungi, yeasts, algae, protozoa, nematodes, mites, springtails, earthworms, arthropods, molluscs, and larger burrowing animals. In permaculture, soil biota are recognised as the primary engine of soil fertility. They decompose organic matter, cycle nutrients, build crumb structure, create colloids, regulate gas exchange, and make minerals available to plant roots. The biomass of soil organisms often vastly exceeds the visible above-ground animal biomass on the same land.

## Classification

Based on body size (after Anderson, *New Scientist*, 6 Oct 1983):

### Microflora and Microfauna (1-100 millimicrons)
- Bacteria
- Fungi
- Nematodes
- Protozoa
- Rotifers

### Mesofauna (100 millimicrons - 2 mm)
- Mites
- Springtails (Collembola)
- Small myriapods
- Enchytraeid worms
- False scorpions
- Termites

### Macrofauna (2-20 mm)
- Woodlice (Isopoda)
- Harvestmen
- Amphipods
- Centipedes and millipedes
- Earthworms
- Beetles
- Spiders
- Slugs and snails
- Ants

### Megafauna (20 mm and above)

- Moles
- Rodents
- Wombats
- Rabbits

## Key Facts

### Biomass Scale
- On semi-arid poor pasture stocked at 3-6 sheep/ha, the same land may support 2-5 tonnes of pasture grubs or up to 65 tonnes of earthworms per hectare.
- At Rothamsted (UK), after 140 continuous wheat cropping cycles, the plough layer still supports 0.5 tonnes of living microbial biomass per hectare.
- In forest topsoil, every square metre can contain a thousand species of animals and 1-2 km of fungal hyphae.

### Composition of Soil Biomass (Rothamsted data)
- **50% fungi** — the dominant biomass component
- **20% bacteria**
- **20% yeasts, algae, and protozoa**
- **10% macrofauna** — earthworms, nematodes, arthropods, molluscs and their larvae

### Abundance per Square Metre (temperate grassland)
- Nematodes: 120 million
- Mites: 100,000
- Springtails: 45,000
- Enchytraeid worms: 20,000
- Molluscs: 10,000

### Functional Roles
- **Nutrient cycling:** Soil organisms gather, store, and concentrate easily leached nutrients (nitrogen, sulphur). Their death releases these at multiple microsites for slow root uptake.
- **Colloid production:** Bacteria secrete polysaccharides that form soil gels; fungi contribute to organic colloid formation.
- **Structure building:** Gums and gels from soil organisms create crumb structure, aerate soil, and darken it for faster spring warming.
- **Decomposition:** Fungi are more effective at breaking down wood and large plant material. They can transport nutrients, e.g., move nitrogen into decaying wood.
- **Mycorrhizal associations:** Fungi form intimate contact with root cells, exchanging minerals and nutrients for plant sugars.
- **Gas exchange:** Burrowers (ants, termites, worms, prairie dogs, land crayfish) create tunnels that act as Pitot tubes, drawing air through soils via pressure differentials.
- **Calcium concentration:** Termites store calcium from subsoils in their mounds.

### Fungi vs Bacteria Balance
- Disturbance, aeration, low oxygen, and compaction favour bacteria over fungi.
- Acid soils of coniferous and oak forests yield few earthworms.
- Fungi can send hyphae many metres to invade trees as pathogens and decomposers.
- Gross disturbance by large fauna colonies (e.g., desert rodents at 1,000-10,000 kg/ha) shifts balance from fungi to bacteria.

### Impact of Agriculture
- Plough cropping in Canada has reduced humus levels to 1% of original levels over much of wheat country.
- Aeration from ploughing oxidises humus to CO2, permanently lost to atmosphere.
- About 30% of plant energy may be lost as sugars or compounds released to soil via roots, feeding the soil food web.
- Humus at Rothamsted has a mean age of 1,400 years, deriving from forests that preceded the wheat.

### Earthworm Recycling Rates
- Soil recycled by worms varies from 2-150 t/ha/year (0.5-25 cm depth of soil per year).
- Turnover of nitrogen by earthworms exceeds that of the litter fall of plants.
- Worm populations vary dramatically: few in acid conifer/oak soils and humic peats, abundant in rich loams.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Mollison argues that the health of the soil biota IS the health of the soil. The tradition of treating soil as dead mineral matter, burning off the organic component in analyses, misses the entire living dimension. Sound farmers, biodynamic practitioners, mulch gardeners, and no-dig croppers focus on the quantity and quality of soil life as the primary indicator of soil health. In permaculture, every design decision should favour soil biology: permanent vegetation, surface mulch (not incorporated), minimal cultivation, diverse plantings, legume integration, and the return of organic wastes including composted humanure. The soil biota, when protected and fed, perform the work that industrial agriculture attempts with expensive and often destructive chemical inputs.
