# Inocybe corydalina

Inocybe corydalina is a mycorrhizal mushroom in the family Inocybaceae, notable as one of the relatively few Inocybe species confirmed to contain [[psilocybin]]. Found primarily in Europe, it grows in association with various trees in deciduous and mixed forests. The species is of particular interest to mycologists and psychopharmacologists because it represents a rare instance of psilocybin biosynthesis outside the Hymenogastraceae and Cortinariaceae families, providing evidence for the broader phylogenetic distribution of this biosynthetic pathway. Among the psilocybin-containing Inocybe species, I. corydalina is considered the most common, though its alkaloid concentrations are low compared to dedicated psychoactive genera.

## Historical Context

Inocybe corydalina was originally described by the French mycologist Lucien Quélet in the late 19th century. The specific epithet "corydalina" is derived from the genus name of the fishfly (Corydalis), likely referencing a perceived resemblance in coloration between the mushroom and certain insects. The species has undergone various taxonomic revisions, with some authorities recognizing varieties or subspecies based on differences in coloration and distribution. Two varieties are formally recognized: I. corydalina var. corydalina (the type variety, reported from both Europe and North America) and I. corydalina var. erinaceomorpha (described by Stangl and Veselský, later transferred by Kuyper, known only from Europe). The latter is distinguished by dark brown scales without greenish hues, whereas the type variety exhibits characteristic greenish gray fibrils near the disc.

The discovery of [[psilocybin]] in this species was confirmed through analytical chemistry by researchers including [[jochen-gartz]], T. Stijve, and Th. Kuyper in the mid-1980s. Gartz documented its presence alongside other Inocybe species known to contain tryptamines, such as [[inocybe-crocata]], [[inocybe-aeruginascens-profile]], [[inocybe-haemacta]], and I. coelestium. The confirmation of psilocybin in I. corydalina added an important data point to the emerging picture of psilocybin's phylogenetic distribution across agaric fungi. Stijve and Kuyper (1985) were among the first to document its chemistry systematically, finding it "weakly active" — a designation that reflects its consistently low alkaloid levels compared to more potent relatives like [[inocybe-aeruginascens-profile]].

## Taxonomy and Naming

The genus Inocybe is one of the larger agaric genera, with over 500 described species worldwide. Most are mycorrhizal associates of forest trees and are characterized by their brown spore prints, fibrous to scaly caps, and prominent sterile cells (cystidia) on the gill edges. The genus is notoriously difficult for amateur identification, with many species requiring microscopic examination for reliable determination. The presence of psilocybin in Inocybe is phylogenetically significant, as it suggests either convergent evolution or an ancient origin of psilocybin biosynthesis predating the divergence of these fungal lineages.

## Description

### Cap (Pileus)
The pileus is 20 to 52 mm in diameter, conic to obtusely conic or campanulate when young, expanding to convex or broadly umbonate with age. The margin is initially incurved, often denticulate (toothed), and may split radially with age, creating a slightly ragged appearance. The surface is dry and covered with a distinctive radial fibrillose to squamulose (scaly) pattern, more densely appressed toward the disc. Coloration varies from brown to buff brown, with greenish gray tones and darker brown to greenish blue, sometimes nearly black, near the center. The cap surface does not stain blue when bruised, unlike many psilocybin-containing species. Flesh is white.

### Gills (Lamellae)
Lamellae are narrowly adnate, crowded, and broad, with a minutely fringed (fimbriate) margin. They are pale brown to buff or pale grayish brown, developing a brownish color with age but never achieving the dark purplish brown typical of Psilocybe species. The cheilocystidia form a conspicuous sterile band along the gill edges.

### Stipe (Stem)
The stipe is 24 to 95 mm long and 5 to 15 mm thick, cylindrical, solid, and equal or slightly enlarged near the base. The surface is smooth above and fibrillose to longitudinally striate below. Coloration is whitish to dull gray above, becoming grayish ochraceous brown to sordid brown overall, with the base often showing grayish greenish tinges. Flesh is white to gray toward the base, slightly reddening upon exposure. The partial veil is cortinate (cobweb-like), soon disappearing. The scent is aromatic, described as similar to Peruvian balsam.

### Spores and Microscopic Features
Basidiospores are brown in deposit, smooth, and lemon-shaped to almond-shaped (amygdaliform), measuring 7 to 13 × 5 to 8 μm depending on the source. The bean-shaped to amygdaliform spores are characteristic of the genus Inocybe and distinguish it from the ellipsoid spores of Psilocybe and Gymnopilus. The spore print is dull brown to tobacco brown, distinctly different from the purplish brown of Psilocybe species or the rusty brown of Gymnopilus. Basidia are 4-spored. Pleurocystidia are 33 to 70 × 9 to 21 μm, cylindrical to clavate-cylindrical. Cheilocystidia are reportedly rare and similar to pleurocystidia in some descriptions, though other sources note them as numerous and clavate to ventricose with a mucronate or capitate apex. The diversity of cystidial shapes is a key diagnostic feature of the genus.

## Identification Keys

I. corydalina can be differentiated from other Inocybe species using the following key characters:

- **Cap with greenish gray to greenish blue tones near the disc** — distinguishes var. corydalina from var. erinaceomorpha (dark brown scales, no greenish hues) and most other Inocybe species.
- **Aromatic scent resembling Peruvian balsam** — unusual among Inocybe and a useful field character when present.
- **Spores lemon to almond-shaped, 7–13 × 5–8 μm** — consistent with the Inocybe morphology but smaller than some close relatives.
- **Narrowly adnate, crowded gills with fringed margin** — helps separate from species with sinuate or free gills.
- **Cortinate partial veil, soon disappearing** — typical of the genus but notable for the lack of persistent annulus.
- **Mycorrhizal under deciduous trees (Fagus, Quercus, Carpinus)** — habitat association with beech, oak, and hornbeam is characteristic.
- **No blue bruising reaction** — despite containing psilocybin, I. corydalina does not exhibit the blueing reaction common to many Psilocybe species.

Accurate identification to species level requires microscopic examination of spore morphology and cystidial characteristics, and should only be attempted by experienced mycologists.

## Distribution and Habitat

Inocybe corydalina is primarily a European species, found across much of the continent from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean. It has been reported from France, Germany, the Netherlands, Denmark, Sweden, the United Kingdom, Switzerland, Austria, Poland, and other European countries. I. corydalina var. corydalina has additionally been reported from North America, though these reports require confirmation and may represent similar but distinct species. The variety erinaceomorpha has thus far only been reported from Europe.

The species is mycorrhizal, forming obligate symbiotic associations with the roots of various trees. It is found in deciduous and mixed forests, particularly under beech (Fagus sylvatica), oak (Quercus spp.), hornbeam (Carpinus betulus), birch (Betula spp.), and to a lesser degree under spruce (Picea abies). It fruits from August through October, growing singly or in scattered groups on forest floors with rich organic soil, often among moss or deep leaf litter. The mycorrhizal habit means the species cannot be found far from its host trees. Fruiting is dependent on adequate rainfall and moderate temperatures, and fruiting bodies are relatively short-lived, often persisting for only a few days under favorable conditions. The species is considered uncommon to rare across most of its range, though it may be locally abundant in suitable habitats.

## Chemistry and Active Compounds

Inocybe corydalina contains [[psilocybin]] and [[baeocystin]], though typically at low concentrations. Jochen-Gartz|Gartz, Stijve, and Kuyper confirmed the presence of these tryptamines through analytical methods including TLC and HPLC. Reported alkaloid concentrations vary by variety:

- **I. corydalina var. corydalina**: Up to 0.032% psilocybin, no psilocin detected, and 0.034% baeocystin (% of dried material). One analysis revealed a psilocybin content of only 0.011–0.1%, a concentration so low that the species would become clinically relevant only if large amounts were eaten.
- **I. corydalina var. erinaceomorpha**: 0.10% psilocybin, no psilocin detected, and 0.034% baeocystin (based on limited sampling of only three collections).

The absence of detectable [[psilocin]] in analyzed collections is notable, as many psilocybin-containing mushrooms also contain its dephosphorylated active metabolite. The psilocybin content is generally lower than that of dedicated psychoactive genera like Psilocybe or Gymnopilus, and the psychoactive effects would be mild and potentially overshadowed by other compounds present.

## Warning: Genus-Wide Toxicity Risk

The genus Inocybe contains numerous species that are dangerously toxic, and accurate species-level identification requires microscopic examination by an expert. This warning cannot be overstated: **consumption of any Inocybe species for recreational purposes is extremely dangerous and strongly discouraged.**

Several Inocybe species contain high concentrations of [[muscarine]], a parasympathomimetic alkaloid that activates muscarinic acetylcholine receptors. Symptoms of muscarine poisoning typically onset within 30 minutes to 2 hours of ingestion and include:

- **SLUDGE syndrome**: Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, Gastrointestinal distress, and Emesis
- Excessive sweating (diaphoresis)
- Blurred vision with constricted pupils (miosis)
- Abdominal cramps, nausea, severe vomiting, and diarrhea
- Bradycardia (slow heart rate) and hypotension (low blood pressure)
- Bronchoconstriction and bronchorrhea
- Muscle weakness and tremors
- Confusion, dizziness, and in severe cases, collapse

In the most severe cases, respiratory depression, cardiac arrhythmias, and potentially fatal respiratory failure can occur, particularly in children, the elderly, or immunocompromised individuals. Deaths from muscarine poisoning are rare with prompt medical treatment but have been documented.

These symptoms closely resemble those of organophosphate insecticide exposure or nerve agent intoxication. Medical treatment may require emergency intervention including administration of atropine (a muscarinic antagonist) and supportive care. Hospitalization is frequently necessary. There is no reliable way to distinguish muscarine-containing Inocybe species from psilocybin-containing ones in the field — they are virtually indistinguishable macroscopically.

Crucially, Gurevich and Nezoiminogo (1994) reported that a collection of I. corydalina var. corydalina tested **negative for psilocybin but positive for muscarine**. While this result has not been confirmed by other researchers, it underscores the variable and unpredictable chemistry within the species and the very real possibility that individual collections may contain dangerous toxins rather than, or in addition to, psilocybin. Inocybe species are collectively responsible for numerous cases of mushroom poisoning in Europe each year, making this genus one of the most hazardous for amateur foragers.

## Medical Importance

Beyond its toxicity risk, I. corydalina has limited direct medical importance. The species is of pharmacological interest as a natural source of [[psilocybin]] and [[baeocystin]], though the low concentrations make it impractical as a source for extraction compared to dedicated psilocybin-producing species. The presence of psilocybin in Inocybaceae contributes to the broader understanding of the distribution and evolution of psychoactive tryptamine biosynthesis in fungi. The variable chemistry of the species (with one collection testing positive for muscarine rather than psilocybin) also highlights the importance of chemical analysis in mycotoxicology and the potential for dangerous misidentification. Clinically, awareness of I. corydalina and related psilocybin-containing Inocybe species is important for differential diagnosis in cases of suspected mushroom poisoning, as the initial symptoms of psilocybin intoxication (nausea, anxiety, confusion) may overlap with early muscarine poisoning, potentially delaying appropriate treatment.

## Etymology

The specific epithet "corydalina" refers to the genus Corydalis (fishflies or dobsonflies), though the exact morphological basis for this comparison by Quélet remains unclear. It may reference the fibrous, scale-like cap surface reminiscent of insect wings, or the overall brownish coloration. This naming convention is consistent with many Inocybe species whose epithets reference insects or animal features (e.g., I. erinaceomorpha, referring to hedgehog-like scales).

## Phylogenetic Significance

The presence of [[psilocybin]] in Inocybe species like I. corydalina is of considerable scientific interest. Inocybe belongs to the family Inocybaceae (order Agaricales), which is phylogenetically distinct from the Hymenogastraceae (which contains Psilocybe) and the Cortinariaceae (which contains psychoactive Gymnopilus). The independent evolution of psilocybin biosynthesis in at least three unrelated fungal lineages suggests either remarkable convergent evolution driven by similar ecological pressures, or a much more ancient origin of the psilocybin biosynthetic pathway with subsequent loss in many descendant lineages.

Recent molecular phylogenetic studies have identified the psilocybin biosynthetic gene cluster (psi genes) and found evidence for both horizontal gene transfer and convergent evolution in its distribution across fungal lineages. The occurrence of psilocybin in Inocybe supports the hypothesis that the biosynthetic pathway has evolved multiple times independently, possibly as a defense mechanism against insect predation or as a metabolic byproduct of other biochemical processes. The fact that psilocybin levels in Inocybe are generally much lower than in Psilocybe or Panaeolus may reflect a more recent or incomplete acquisition of the pathway, or different selective pressures acting on ectomycorrhizal versus saprotrophic fungi. Additionally, the unique compound [[aeruginascin]] found in [[inocybe-aeruginascens-profile]] suggests that the genus has evolved its own distinct complement of bioactive indole alkaloids, further supporting the case for independent biochemical evolution within Inocybaceae.
