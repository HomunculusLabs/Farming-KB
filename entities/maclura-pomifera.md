# Maclura pomifera — Osage Orange

## Overview

- **Common names:** Osage Orange, Hedge Apple, Bois d'Arc, Bodark, Bowood, Horse Apple
- **Growth habit:** Medium-sized deciduous tree, dioecious, often multi-stemmed when coppiced
- **Lifespan:** 75–150 years; some specimens exceed 200 years
- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 5–9; tolerates extreme heat, cold, drought, and poor soils
- **Root system:** Deep taproot with extensive lateral roots; soil-binding and erosion-controlling
- **Growth rate:** Moderate to fast (1.5–3 ft/year in ideal conditions)
- **Note:** Tagged nitrogen-fixing for guild reference but *M. pomifera* is not a true
  nitrogen-fixer; it contributes via deep nutrient cycling and heavy leaf litter return

## Taxonomy

- **Binomial:** *Maclura pomifera* (Raf.) C.K.Schneid.
- **Family:** Moraceae (mulberry family)
- **Authority:** Charles Konstantin Gabriel Schneid., 1906 (basionym: *Ilex pomifera* Raf., 1818)
- **Order:** Rosales
- **Synonyms:**
  - *Maclura aurantiaca* Nutt.
  - *Toxylon pomiferum* Raf.
  - *Ilex pomifera* Raf.
  - *Joxylon pomiferum* (Raf.) Skeels
  - *Cardioxyylon aurantiacum* (Nutt.) Prenant
- **Type:** Collected in Arkansas; original Rafinesque description based on Osage Nation material

## Morphology

- **Tree size:** 30–60 ft tall, 1–2 ft DBH; open crown, rounded to irregular form
- **Bark:** Dark brown to orange-brown, deeply furrowed with ridges, scaly on mature trunks
- **Branches:** Stout, zigzag at nodes, armed with 0.5–1 in sharp thorns (esp. young growth)
- **Leaves:** Simple, alternate, 3–5 in × 2–3 in; ovate to lanceolate; glossy dark green above,
  paler below; entire or slightly serrate near apex; pinnately veined; milky latex on break
- **Flowers:** Dioecious; apetalous, greenish, in tight globular clusters (May–June);
  male flowers in racemes, female flowers in globular heads
- **Fruit:** Large globose syncarp, 4–6 in diameter, bright green turning yellow-green;
  rough-textured, citrus-scented; aggregate of many small drupelets around a central core;
  not edible raw (causes GI distress); seeds dispersed by former megafauna
- **Wood:** Bright yellow-orange heartwood deepening to amber with age; extremely dense
  (SG 0.77–0.86); hard, heavy, strong; highest rot resistance of any North American wood

## Distribution & Habitat

- **Native range:** Red River Valley of Oklahoma, Texas, Arkansas, Louisiana;
  southwestern Missouri, southeastern Kansas
- **Naturalized:** All 48 contiguous US states; Europe, Asia, Australia, New Zealand
- **Habitat:** River bottoms, prairie edges, open woodlands, fencerows, old fields;
  highly adaptable to disturbed sites, compacted soils, alkaline or acidic conditions
- **Soil tolerance:** Clay, loam, sandy, limestone-derived, rocky soils; pH 5.0–8.0
- **Moisture:** Drought-tolerant once established; tolerates seasonal flooding

## Historical Significance

- **Native American bow wood:** Osage Nation and Plains tribes prized the wood for war
  and hunting bows; considered superior to all other NA woods; trade spread species
  far beyond native range; French name "Bois d'Arc" = "wood of the bow"
- **Hedge planting era (1850s–1900s):** Primary living fence species in US Midwest
  and Great Plains before barbed wire; planted in dense hedgerows pruned into
  impenetrable barriers; promoted by USDA; hundreds of thousands of miles by 1880
- **Railroad ties:** Heartwood rot resistance made it preferred material for ties
  and telegraph poles in the 19th century
- **Dye production:** Wood and root bark yield bright yellow-orange dye; used
  commercially in the 1800s
- **Barbed wire (1874)** ended the hedge era but existing hedgerows persist today

## Permaculture Applications

- **Hedgerow / living fence:** Dense thorny barriers at 12–18 in spacing; effective
  livestock exclusion; coppices vigorously
- **Windbreak:** Fast establishment, dense branching; effective in multi-row systems
- **Erosion control:** Deep roots stabilize slopes and riparian zones
- **Dye source:** Heartwood and root bark produce osage orange dye — brilliant gold-yellow
  on wool, cotton, linen; mordant with alum for best results; valued by fiber artists
- **Rot-resistant timber:** Posts last 25–50+ years in ground contact without treatment;
  ideal for hugelkultur frames, raised bed borders, garden edging
- **Firewood:** Extremely high BTU (~32 million BTU/cord); burns hot and long; coals
  persist overnight; difficult to split when green
- **Insect repellent:** Fruit traditionally placed around foundations to deter spiders
  and insects (anecdotal; limited scientific evidence)
- **Pollinator habitat:** Male flowers attract bees and other pollinators in spring
- **Guild roles:** Deep nutrient miner, dynamic accumulator via leaf litter; nurse tree

## Chemistry

- **Heartwood compounds:**
  - **Morin:** Flavonol with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, metal-chelating properties
  - **Maclurin:** Benzophenone derivative; antityrosinase and antioxidant activity
  - **Osajin:** Prenylated isoflavone; antifungal; specific to *Maclura* genus
  - **Pomiferin:** Prenylated isoflavone; cytotoxic and antifungal activity
- **Fruit compounds:**
  - **Xanthones:** Including macluraxanthone; antioxidant and antimicrobial activity
  - **Osajin, pomiferin** concentrated in fruit and seeds
  - **Triterpenes:** Various pentacyclic triterpenoids in fruit pulp
  - **Limonene and volatiles:** Responsible for characteristic citrus scent
- **Latex:** Milky sap with proteolytic enzymes and rubber-like polymers
- **Dye chemistry:** Morin and maclurin complex with metal mordants (Al³⁺, Fe²⁺, Cu²⁺)
  to produce yellows, golds, greens, blacks; direct dye without mordant yields pale yellow

## Field Identification

- **Key ID 1:** Bright yellow-orange heartwood on cut or broken branches — single most
  diagnostic feature; no other NA tree has this wood color
- **Key ID 2:** Large spherical green fruit (4–6 in) resembling a brain or green orange;
  appears Sept–Oct; drops and persists on ground through winter
- **Key ID 3:** Stout zigzag branches with 0.5–1 in sharp thorns at nodes; thorns
  prominent on young wood, reduced on mature canopy branches
- **Key ID 4:** Simple alternate leaves, entire margins, glossy above, white milky latex
  when torn
- **Key ID 5:** Dark deeply furrowed bark with orange-brown underbark
- **Distinguishing:** Not confused with *Citrus* (unrelated Rutaceae); fruit is unique
  among NA trees; wood color separates from black locust, mulberry, all other timber
