# Cut and Feed System (Zero Grazing)

## Description

The cut and feed system (also called zero grazing or stall feeding) is a livestock management method where animals are permanently housed and fed from cut forage brought to them, rather than being allowed to graze pasture directly. All manure is collected and returned to the land, preferably as subsoil-injected sludge. Mollison describes this as an excellent system for small-scale farmers in tropical, semi-arid, and developing-area contexts.

## Classification

- **Category**: Agricultural technique — confined livestock management
- **Scale**: Small — 2 to 20 cows
- **Climate suitability**: Tropics, semi-arid areas, villages in poor regions
- **Key feature**: Complete manure capture and return
- **Alternative name**: Zero grazing, stall feeding

## Key Facts

- Ideal for milk cows and small herds of 2-20 cows
- Suited to tropics, semi-arid areas, and smallholder village systems
- Mixed forage is cut and brought to shedded cattle daily
- Forage species mentioned: Leucaena, Pennisetum, comfrey, browse plants
- All manure is returned to the cut-over area, preferably as subsoil sludge
- Hay can be cut from surplus growth in good periods for dry season feed
- Not sustainable as broadscale feedlot due to excessive energy costs and waste

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Mollison positions the cut and feed system as the ideal approach for specific contexts:

1. **Nutrient cycling efficiency**: Because all manure is collected and returned, nutrient losses are minimized. Subsoil sludge injection (as described in Figure 12.16) prevents nitrogen loss to atmosphere, deters pollution, and maximizes pasture response. Manure is injected at 140 cubic metres/ha, 18 cm down, with best results when applied in autumn.

2. **Forage diversity**: The system encourages mixed forage harvesting from diverse plantings — Leucaena (tree legume), Pennisetum (elephant grass), comfrey (deep-rooted perennial), and various browse plants. This diversity exceeds what animals would self-select on pasture, allowing the farmer to optimize nutrition.

3. **Land protection**: Because animals never touch the pasture directly, soil structure is never damaged by trampling, pugging, or overgrazing. The forage area is managed purely as a cutting regime, maintaining optimal plant health and root reserves.

4. **Smallholder economics**: The system is "essentially a small farmer system" — the labour of daily cutting and feeding is manageable at household scale and eliminates the need for fencing, large acreage, or expensive infrastructure.

5. **Contrast with feedlots**: Mollison explicitly distinguishes this from broadscale feedlots, which are "not sustainable... due to excessive energy costs, waste of manures, pollution, and high overheads." The cut and feed system works precisely because it operates at a scale where manure is a resource, not a waste problem.

## Implementation Guidelines

- Establish diverse forage plantings including legume trees, grass, and herbaceous species
- Build simple shelter for 2-20 head of cattle
- Cut forage daily or every two days, rotating through forage blocks
- Collect all manure and urine from the shelter
- Process manure as liquid slurry for subsoil injection
- Allow 3-4 weeks rest between cuts on any forage block
- Store surplus as hay during peak growth periods

## Equipment and Setup

Basic setup requires cutting tools, collection bins, and transport infrastructure.
Scale ranges from hand tools for small holdings to mechanized systems for larger operations.
Proper maintenance of cutting equipment ensures clean cuts and reduces plant damage.

## Management Practices

Timing of cut-and-feed cycles aligns with plant growth stages for optimal nutrition.
Rotation between cutting areas allows regrowth and maintains long-term productivity.
Monitoring animal response guides adjustments to cutting frequency and species selection.

## Integration with Grazing Systems

Complements direct grazing by utilizing areas unsuitable for livestock access.
Provides feed during periods when pasture growth is insufficient for direct grazing.
Reduces waste by converting surplus growth into stored or immediate feed resources.
