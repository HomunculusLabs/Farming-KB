## Description

The Hartig net is a specialized fungal structure formed during [[ectomycorrhizal-associations]], consisting of a network of hyphae that penetrate between the cortical cells of the host plant root without entering the cells themselves. Named after the German forestry scientist Robert Hartig, who first described it in the 19th century, the Hartig net is the primary site of nutrient and carbohydrate exchange between the ectomycorrhizal fungus and its plant host. It is one of the defining anatomical features that distinguishes ectomycorrhizae from other [[mycorrhizal-types]].

## Classification

- **Category:** Mycorrhizal anatomical structure
- **Mycorrhizal type:** Ectomycorrhizae only
- **Fungal associates:** Basidiomycetes, Ascomycetes, and some Zygomycetes
- **Host plants:** Primarily coniferous and deciduous trees (Pinaceae, Fagaceae, Betulaceae, Salicaceae)

## Key Facts

- The Hartig net forms when [[fungal-hyphae]] grow intercellularly between the root cortical cells, typically extending from the epidermis to the endodermis, but never penetrating the cell walls of the host.
- This intercellular network creates an extensive surface area for bidirectional exchange: the fungus transfers mineral nutrients (especially phosphorus and nitrogen) and water to the plant, while receiving photosynthetically derived carbohydrates in return.
- The Hartig net co-occurs with the fungal sheath or mantle, a layer of fungal tissue that encases the root surface externally. Together, these structures define the ectomycorrhizal morphology.
- The depth of Hartig net penetration varies among host species. In some conifers, it may extend through multiple layers of cortical cells, while in angiosperms it is often restricted to the outermost cortical layer.
- The formation of the Hartig net suppresses root hair development and alters root branching patterns, changing the overall architecture of the root system to favor fungal-mediated nutrient acquisition.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The Hartig net is fundamental to understanding how [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] enhance nutrient uptake in forest trees. Its large intercellular surface area enables efficient exchange at rates far exceeding what root hairs alone could achieve. In forestry and silviculture, the health and development of the Hartig net directly influences seedling establishment, tree growth rates, and resistance to environmental stress.

In mushroom cultivation and [[mycorrhizal-inoculum-production]], understanding Hartig net formation is essential for developing effective ectomycorrhizal products for truffle cultivation (Tuber spp.), chanterelle production, and reforestation efforts. The structure also serves as a diagnostic feature for identifying ectomycorrhizal associations in root samples, using microscopy to confirm successful colonization.

Research on the Hartig net has implications for understanding carbon cycling in forest ecosystems. Since this structure is the conduit through which significant quantities of plant-derived carbon enter the fungal pathway, it plays a role in soil carbon sequestration. The turnover rate of Hartig net tissue contributes to soil organic matter formation and the regulation of nutrient cycling in temperate and boreal forests.
