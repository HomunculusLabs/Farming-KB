## Guttation

## Description

Guttation is a **plant physiological process** in which water is exuded from the tips and margins of leaves through specialized structures called **hydathodes**. Unlike dew, which condenses from atmospheric moisture onto leaf surfaces, guttation water originates from within the plant itself — pushed out by root pressure when soil moisture is high and transpiration is low (typically at night or in early morning). Mollison distinguishes guttation from dew and distillation as part of his detailed treatment of precipitation and moisture sources.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Plant Physiology
- **Category:** Water transport phenomenon
- **Mechanism:** Root pressure forcing xylem sap through hydathodes
- **Timing:** Night and early morning when transpiration is minimal

## Key Facts

- Guttation occurs when **root pressure** exceeds transpiration pull, forcing water out through leaf hydathodes.
- The water droplets appear at the **tips and margins of leaves**, distinguishing them from dew which coats the entire leaf surface.
- Guttation fluid contains dissolved **minerals, sugars, and organic compounds** — it is not pure water.
- It occurs most commonly on warm, humid nights after rain or irrigation when soil moisture is abundant.
- Mollison emphasizes that guttation (along with ground distillation on warm, damp, cloudy nights) represents **no net gain to total precipitation** — it is water already in the system being redistributed.
- Common in many cultivated plants including grasses, strawberries, tomatoes, and numerous tropical species.

## Distinguishing Guttation from Dew

Mollison provides a clear diagnostic distinction:

| Feature | Guttation | Dew |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| **Source** | Internal plant water (xylem sap) | Atmospheric moisture condensation |
| **Cause** | Root pressure exceeding transpiration | Radiative heat loss from surfaces on clear nights |
| **Distribution on leaf** | Tips and margins only | Entire leaf surface evenly |
| **Timing** | Warm humid nights with moist soil | Clear calm nights |
| **Composition** | Water with dissolved minerals and sugars | Pure condensed water |

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Understanding guttation has practical implications:

- **Irrigation assessment**: Guttation indicates that soil moisture is abundant and root systems are functioning well — a visual indicator of adequate watering.
- **Not a water source**: Unlike dew collection or fog harvesting, guttation cannot contribute to net water gain in a system. Designers should not count it as precipitation.
- **Disease risk**: Persistent guttation can create conditions favorable for **fungal and bacterial pathogens**, particularly in dense crop canopies with poor air circulation.
- **Nutrient cycling**: Guttation fluids deposited on leaf surfaces can be reabsorbed or utilized by epiphytic organisms, contributing to nutrient cycling in forest ecosystems.
