# Corridor Farming

## Description

Corridor farming is a dryland agricultural strategy that concentrates food production along natural moisture corridors rather than attempting area-wide cropping across arid landscapes. Mollison presents this as a fundamental paradigm shift for desert agriculture: instead of clearing and planting large uniform fields, food systems are designed as linear assemblies following rivers, dune series, floodplains, and other naturally moist pathways through the landscape. The approach is both more water-efficient and ecologically appropriate than conventional broadscale desert agriculture.

## Classification

- **Type:** Agricultural technique / land use strategy
- **Domain:** Dryland [[swale]] systems:** Human-made contour trenches that harvest and infiltrate runoff

## Species Selection for Corridors

The corridor approach demands a different species palette than conventional agriculture:

- **Staple trees:** Date palms, olives, doum palms, citrus, apricots planted along swales
- **Hardy ground covers:** Very drought-resistant bulbs, tubers, and yams in semi-wild plantings
- **Broadcast crops:** Sunflower, millet, sorghum, panicgrass, melons for opportunistic surplus
- **Native species:** Retained and managed rather than cleared for monoculture
- **Leguminous support:** [[permaculture]] to dryland management. It rejects the European model of area-wide field cropping in favor of a landscape-informed strategy that works with natural moisture distribution. This approach is directly relevant to anyone designing food systems in arid or semi-arid environments, offering a more resilient, water-efficient, and ecologically appropriate framework than conventional agriculture. The concept scales from individual property design to regional planning and has been validated by Indigenous management practices spanning millennia.

## Historical Precedent

Mollison argues that corridor farming has deep historical roots in Indigenous desert management:

- Aboriginal Australian peoples managed food corridors along waterways and damp sand areas for millennia
- The Papago and Hopi managed desert food plant assemblages along wash corridors
- Saharan peoples maintained oasis corridor systems linking isolated productive patches
- The concept is not new but rather a formalization of traditional ecological knowledge

## Design Considerations

Key factors for successful corridor farm design:

- Map all natural water flow paths before designing corridor layout
- Assess soil depth and salinity along each proposed corridor segment
- Maintain connectivity between corridor segments for wildlife movement
- Plan for both wet year surplus production and dry year minimal maintenance
- Integrate permanent infrastructure (swales, check dams, fencing) with seasonal opportunistic planting
- Preserve existing native vegetation as the foundation of corridor plantings
