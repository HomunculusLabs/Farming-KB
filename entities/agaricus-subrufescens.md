# Agaricus subrufescens

Agaricus subrufescens, commonly known as the Almond Agaricus or the Brazil Mushroom, is a gourmet and medicinal mushroom distinguished by its pronounced almond aroma. Originally described from North America, this species has gained significant attention for both its culinary qualities and its medicinal properties, particularly its immune-enhancing beta-glucan content. It is closely related to [[agaricus-brunnescens]] (the commercial button mushroom) and [[agaricus-blazei]], a name often used interchangeably in commercial contexts. See [[agaricus-brunnescens]] for compost-based cultivation methods and [[ganoderma-lucidum]] for another widely studied medicinal mushroom.

## Taxonomy and Description

- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Agaricaceae
- **Synonyms:** Agaricus blazei Murrill (often used commercially), Agaricus brasiliensis Wasser et al.
- **Common names:** Almond Agaricus, Brazil Mushroom, Royal Sun Agaricus, Himematsutake (Japanese, "Princess Matsutake"),Cogumelo do Sol (Portuguese, "Mushroom of the Sun")

### Morphology

- **Cap:** White to cream colored, smooth, convex expanding to plane, typically 5-15 cm in diameter.
- **Gills:** Free from stem, initially pink, darkening to chocolate brown with maturity.
- **Stem:** White, sturdy, with a well-developed membranous annulus (ring).
- **Flesh:** White, firm, with a distinctive **almond-like aroma** when cut or bruised. This almond fragrance is the key identifying feature.
- **Spore print:** Dark brown.
- **Habit:** Often grows in clusters or groups.

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Global range:** Originally described from North America (New Jersey). Now widely cultivated, especially in Brazil, Japan, China, and other countries with commercial medicinal mushroom industries.
- **Natural habitat:** Rich soils, composted substrates, grasslands, and areas with decomposing organic matter. In nature, associated with warm-temperate to subtropical climates.
- **Cultivation range:** Grown commercially in controlled environments worldwide, particularly in Brazil and Japan.

## Medicinal Properties

A. subrufescens has become one of the most studied medicinal mushrooms in recent decades:

- **Beta-glucans:** Contains high concentrations of beta-1,3/1,6-D-glucans, polysaccharides known to modulate immune function
- **Anti-tumor activity:** Numerous studies have demonstrated anti-tumor effects in vitro and in animal models, particularly against sarcoma 180
- **Immunomodulation:** Enhances natural killer (NK) cell activity, stimulates cytokine production, and activates macrophages
- **Anti-inflammatory:** Demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties in various studies
- **Anti-diabetic effects:** Some evidence suggests blood sugar regulation benefits
- **Hepatoprotective:** May support liver function and protect against chemical-induced liver damage

The species is widely marketed as a dietary supplement, particularly in Japan where it was popularized as Himematsutake. The commercial trade under the name Agaricus blazei is extremely common, though taxonomically disputed.

## Cultivation

A. subrufescens is a secondary decomposer and requires composted substrates similar to those used for [[agaricus-brunnescens]]:

- **Substrate preparation:** Requires a composting process involving microbial succession of primary and secondary decomposers
- **Base materials:** Straw, horse manure, poultry manure, and various organic amendments
- **Pasteurization:** Compost must be pasteurized before inoculation
- **Casing:** A peat-based casing layer is typically required for fruiting
- **Temperature:** Prefers warmer growing conditions than A. brunnescens, typically 75-85°F (24-29°C) for spawn run
- **pH:** Optimal pH range around 6.5-7.0

## Flavor and Culinary Use

The Almond Agaricus has a distinctive, pleasant almond aroma and a nutty, savory flavor. It can be used in any recipe calling for button mushrooms but provides a more complex and aromatic profile. It is excellent sauteed, grilled, added to soups, or used in stuffings. The almond scent is most pronounced when the mushroom is fresh and raw.

## Economic Significance

The global market for A. subrufescens products (fresh, dried, and extracts) is substantial, particularly in Japan, Brazil, China, and the United States. The species is one of the top-selling medicinal mushrooms worldwide, often marketed alongside [[ganoderma-lucidum]] (Reishi) and [[grifola-frondosa]] (Maitake) in functional food and supplement products.

## Beta-Glucan Content and Biochemistry

The medicinal reputation of A. subrufescens rests largely on its polysaccharide content:

- **Beta-1,3-glucans and beta-1,6-glucans:** These are the primary bioactive compounds, found in both the fruiting body and the mycelium. Concentrations are comparable to or higher than those found in [[ganoderma-lucidum]] (Reishi) and [[grifola-frondosa]] (Maitake).
- **Protein-bound polysaccharides:** A. subrufescens contains unique protein-polysaccharide complexes that may contribute to its immunomodulatory effects.
- **Ergosterol:** Contains significant amounts of ergosterol (vitamin D2 precursor), which converts to vitamin D2 upon UV exposure.
- **Other compounds:** Contains various phenolic compounds, steroids, and lectins with potential biological activity.
- **Extraction methods:** Hot water extraction is the traditional method for preparing A. subrufescens supplements. Dual extraction (hot water followed by alcohol) may capture a broader range of bioactive compounds.

## Cultivation Techniques

A. subrufescens is a secondary decomposer and requires composted substrates similar to those used for [[agaricus-brunnescens]]:

- **Substrate preparation:** Requires a composting process involving microbial succession of primary and secondary decomposers. Phase I composting takes 7-14 days, with thermophilic temperatures reaching 160°F (71°C).
- **Phase II pasteurization:** Compost is pasteurized at 140°F (60°C) for several hours, then conditioned at lower temperatures to select for beneficial microorganisms.
- **Spawning:** Grain spawn is mixed into the compost at approximately 1 quart per 10-15 square feet of growing surface.
- **Spawn run:** Mycelium colonizes the compost over 14-21 days at 75-85°F (24-29°C) with high CO2 levels (>5,000 ppm).
- **Casing:** A peat-based casing layer is typically required for fruiting. The casing provides essential moisture and microbial stimulation.
- **Fruiting temperature:** Prefers warmer growing conditions than A. brunnescens, typically 70-80°F (21-27°C) for fruiting initiation.
- **pH:** Optimal pH range around 6.5-7.0
- **Yield:** Commercial yields are lower than for A. brunnescens, typically 2-4 lbs per square foot over 2-3 flushes.
- **Challenges:** Susceptible to various competitors and diseases common in Agaricus cultivation, including Trichoderma green mold, bacterial blotch, and virus diseases.

## Market and Commercial Significance

The global market for A. subrufescens products (fresh, dried, and extracts) is substantial, particularly in Japan, Brazil, China, and the United States:

- **Japan:** Marketed as Himematsutake, one of the top-selling medicinal mushrooms in Japanese health food stores and pharmacies.
- **Brazil:** Widely cultivated and consumed domestically, with significant export to Japan and other Asian markets.
- **China:** Large-scale cultivation operations produce both fresh mushrooms for food and dried material for supplement manufacturing.
- **United States:** Available primarily as dietary supplements (capsules, extracts, teas) in health food stores and online.
- **Forms:** Sold fresh, dried, as extracts (hot water, dual extraction), in capsule form, and as functional food ingredients.
- **Quality concerns:** The commercial market has issues with product quality consistency, species authentication, and labeling accuracy. DNA barcoding has revealed that some products labeled as A. subrufescens may contain other Agaricus species.

## Taxonomic History and Controversy

The taxonomy of this species has been contentious. Originally described by Peck in 1893 as Agaricus subrufescens from New Jersey, it was later synonymized with A. blazei Murrill, described from Florida. Some researchers argue these are distinct species, while others treat them as conspecific. The name A. brasiliensis was proposed by Wasser et al. to resolve the nomenclatural confusion. The commercial industry largely uses the name A. blazei regardless of taxonomic correctness.
