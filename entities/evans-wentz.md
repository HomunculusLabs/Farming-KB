## W. Y. Evans-Wentz

## Description

Walter Yeeling Evans-Wentz (1878–1965) was an American anthropologist, writer, and
pioneering scholar of Tibetan Buddhism. He is best known as the editor and publisher
of *The Tibetan Book of the Dead* (1927), the first English-language translation of
the Bardo Thodol, produced in collaboration with Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup. His edition
became the foundational text through which Western readers — including Timothy Leary,
R. C. Zaehner, and Aldous Huxley — first encountered Tibetan Buddhist teachings on
consciousness, death, and the intermediate state. Evans-Wentz devoted his mature years
to the study of Tibetan mystical traditions, literally sitting at the feet of Tibetan
lamas for years to acquire their wisdom.

## Biography

- **Born**: 1878 in Trenton, New Jersey, United States
- **Education**: Stanford University (A.B.); Oxford University, where he studied under
  the anthropologist R. R. Marett
- **Travels**: Extensive fieldwork in India, Sikkim, Bhutan, and Tibet during the early
  20th century
- **Key collaboration**: Met Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup in Gangtok, Sikkim; the lama served
  as his primary Tibetan language teacher and collaborator on the Bardo Thodol translation
- **Affiliation**: Oxford University Press, which published his four major treatises on
  Tibetan mysticism
- **Intellectual orientation**: Influenced by Theosophical ideas and comparative religion;
  sought to make esoteric Tibetan Buddhist doctrines accessible to Western lay readers
- **Died**: 1965

Evans-Wentz"s approach was shaped by his background in Theosophy and comparative
religion. He did not merely translate but editorially framed the texts with extensive
introductions, notes, and supplementary commentaries designed to contextualize Tibetan
practices for a Western audience. His translation philosophy emphasized making guarded
esoteric doctrines "more or less intelligible to the layman," with practical spiritual
application as the goal.

## Key Works

1. **The Tibetan Book of the Dead** (1927) — The landmark English translation of the
   Bardo Thodol, produced with Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup. Included Carl G. Jung"s
   "Psychological Commentary," which provided a Western analytical psychology
   interpretation of the Tibetan text.

2. **Tibetan Yoga and Secret Doctrines** (1935) — A collection of translated Tibetan
   yogic and meditation texts with editorial commentary.

3. **The Tibetan Book of the Great Liberation** (1954) — A translation of a Tibetan
   Dzogchen text concerned with the nature of mind and the attainment of liberation.

4. **The Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries** (1911) — His earlier Oxford dissertation,
   a comparative study of fairy beliefs across Celtic cultures, reflecting his broader
   interest in the anthropology of the supernatural.

## Contributions to Tibetan Buddhist Studies

Evans-Wentz pioneered the academic study of Tibetan Buddhism in the West at a time
when virtually no English-language scholarship on the subject existed. His contributions
include:

- Producing the first widely accessible English translation of the Bardo Thodol, making
  Tibetan death and consciousness teachings available to a broad Western audience
- Establishing the Oxford University Press series on Tibetan religious texts, which set
  a precedent for scholarly publication of Asian philosophical works
- Including supplementary commentaries — most notably Jung"s "Psychological Commentary"
  — that framed Tibetan Buddhist concepts in terms accessible to [[mckenna-decentralizing-bender-western-intellectual-history-consciousness]]
  traditions
- Drawing connections between Tibetan death practices and the initiation rites of
  classical antiquity, situating the Bardo Thodol within a comparative framework of
  mystical traditions
- Emphasizing the practical spiritual dimension of the texts rather than treating them
  purely as ethnographic curiosities

Leary described Evans-Wentz as acting "like an RNA molecule" between Tibet and the West,
"activating the latter with the coded message of the former." Leary drew a parallel
between Evans-Wentz and Marpa ("The Translator") of the eleventh century, who rendered
Indian Buddhist texts into Tibetan and thereby preserved them from extinction.

## Connection to Psychedelic Literature

Evans-Wentz"s edition of the Tibetan Book of the Dead was the direct source text for
Leary, Metzner, and Alpert's *The [[leary-psychedelic-experience-bardo-mapping]]* (1964):

- **Structural foundation**: Leary explicitly modeled the [[psychedelic-session-instructions]] guide on
  the three-bardo structure of Evans-Wentz's Bardo Thodol — Chikhai Bardo (transcendence),
  Chonyid Bardo ([[the-second-bardo-and-hallucinatory-visions]]), and Sidpa Bardo (return to ordinary consciousness)
- **Direct quotation**: Leary quoted extensively from Evans-Wentz's own commentary,
  particularly the passage on "the Art of Dying" (Evans-Wentz, p. xiii-xiv), which became
  a central thematic element of the psychedelic manual
- **Jung"s commentary**: The Evans-Wentz edition included Carl G. Jung's "Psychological
  Commentary" (referenced on p. xiii of the edition), which Leary cited as one of three
  key interpretive frameworks alongside Evans-Wentz"s own commentary and Lama Govinda's
  introduction
- **Conceptual vocabulary**: Terms like "Clear Light," "ego-loss," "bardo," and "karmic
  apparitions" that Evans-Wentz introduced to English-language readers became standard in
  psychedelic discourse
- **Guide tradition**: Evans-Wentz"s emphasis on the role of a "reader" guiding the
  deceased through the bardos was directly adapted by Leary into the concept of a
  psychedelic session guide

## Relevance

Evans-Wentz"s editorial work stands as the critical bridge between Tibetan Buddhist
consciousness teachings and the [[shamanic-vs-western-psychedelic-healing]] movement. His translation and
commentary framework provided the structural, philosophical, and terminological
foundation upon which Leary built *The Psychedelic Experience* — one of the most
influential psychedelic session manuals ever written. The conceptual vocabulary of
bardos, Clear Light, and ego-loss that Evans-Wentz helped introduce to the West
became embedded in psychedelic culture and remains in use today in therapeutic,
academic, and ceremonial contexts.

His insistence that "the Art of Dying is quite as important as the Art of Living"
resonated deeply with the psychedelic movement"s focus on ego dissolution and
[[metzner-non-drug-methods-consciousness-transformation]]. The Jung commentary he commissioned for his edition
further legitimized Tibetan Buddhist texts as psychologically — not merely
religiously — significant, creating the interdisciplinary space in which works like
Leary's could flourish. Evans-Wentz's legacy endures wherever the bardo framework
is used to understand or guide entheogenic experiences.
