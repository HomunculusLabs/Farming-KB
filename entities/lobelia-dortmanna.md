The species is a classic indicator of pristine, nutrient-poor freshwater habitats. Its presence signals high water quality and minimal nutrient enrichment from agricultural or urban runoff.

Water lobelia grows partially or fully submerged, with a rosette of fleshy, hollow leaves anchored in sandy or gravelly lake sediments. It produces small white to pale violet flowers on emergent stalks in midsummer.

The species has declined significantly across its European range due to eutrophication and shoreline development. It is listed as threatened or endangered in several European countries.

In the United Kingdom, water lobelia has been lost from over 70% of its historical sites in England since 1950. Remaining strongholds are in the Scottish Highlands and Welsh uplands, where oligotrophic conditions persist.

The species is listed on the EU Habitats Directive as a characteristic species of the "oligotrophic waters containing very few minerals" habitat type (Natura 2000 code 3110).

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Family: Campanulaceae
- Subfamily: Lobelioideae
- Genus: Lobelia
- Species: L. dortmanna L.

The plant forms a compact basal rosette, 5–10 cm in diameter, of hollow, cylindrical leaves. Leaves are pale green, semitransparent, and contain lacunae (air chambers) that facilitate [[hamilton-garden-ponds-and-aquatic-plants]] or aquariums.

Successful cultivation demands oligotrophic water with very low nutrient levels, fine sand or gravel substrate, and clear water with good light penetration. Even moderate nutrient enrichment causes algal overgrowth and plant decline.

Propagation is by seed, sown on moist sand in shallow trays of dechlorinated water. Germination occurs in 2–4 weeks at 15–20 °C under cool-white fluorescent lights.

The species is unsuitable for garden ponds enriched with fish waste or plant fertilizers. It persists only in nutrient-poor, rain-fed water features or carefully managed botanical displays.

Transplanting from wild populations is strongly discouraged due to conservation concerns and poor establishment success. Rosettes damaged during collection rarely recover.

## Ecological Role

Lobelia dortmanna plays a significant role in the [[dighton-fungi-elevated-co2-global-carbon-cycling]] of oligotrophic lakes. Its hollow leaves conduct oxygen from the atmosphere to the roots, creating an oxidized rhizosphere in otherwise anoxic sediments.

This radial oxygen loss supports nitrification bacteria in the root zone, maintaining low ammonium concentrations in the sediment. The plant effectively engineers its own microenvironment.

The species is among the few aquatic plants that use bicarbonate as a carbon source for photosynthesis, giving it a competitive advantage in softwater lakes where dissolved CO₂ is limited.
