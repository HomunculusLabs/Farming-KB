## Overview

Haworthia fasciata, commonly called zebra plant or zebra haworthia, is a small stemless succulent in the family Asphodelaceae. Endemic to the Eastern Cape Province of South Africa, it forms tight rosettes of stiff, dark green leaves marked by distinctive white horizontal tubercles that create a zebra-stripe pattern.

The species is among the most popular and widely cultivated haworthias, prized for its compact size, striking leaf markings, and tolerance of indoor conditions. It rarely exceeds 10–15 cm in diameter and 8–12 cm tall.

Unlike many succulents, Haworthia fasciata prefers partial shade and is well-adapted to the lower light levels found in homes and offices. This makes it one of the few succulents that genuinely thrives as a houseplant without supplemental lighting.

The genus Haworthia was named for Adrian Hardy Haworth (1768–1833), an English entomologist and botanist. Recent molecular phylogenetic studies have split the genus, with H. fasciata remaining in Haworthia sensu stricto.

The closely related species Haworthia attenuata is frequently confused with H. fasciata in cultivation. The key difference is that H. attenuata has tubercles on both leaf surfaces, while H. fasciata has tubercles only on the lower (outer) surface.

Commercially sold "Haworthia fasciata" are frequently mislabeled H. attenuata. True H. fasciata has a smoother upper leaf surface and more regular, bolder white banding on the lower surface.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Order: Asparagales
- Family: Asphodelaceae
- Subfamily: Asphodeloideae
- Genus: Haworthia
- Species: H. fasciata (Willd.) Haw. (1812)

Plants form stemless, solitary or slowly offsetting rosettes of 30–80 stiff, triangular leaves. Each leaf is 5–8 cm long, 1.5–2.5 cm wide at the base, and tapering to an acute tip.

The upper (adaxial) leaf surface is smooth and dark green. The lower (abaxial) surface bears raised white tubercles arranged in transverse bands, creating the characteristic zebra-stripe pattern.

Leaf tips may turn dry and brown with age, a normal feature not indicative of poor health. The leaves are firm and fibrous rather than soft and fleshy, containing less water storage tissue than many succulents.

Flowers are borne on slender, wiry inflorescences reaching 25–40 cm tall. Individual flowers are small, white with greenish-brown veins, tubular, and bilabiate (two-lipped), typical of the tribe Aloeae.

The chromosome number is 2n = 14. The genus Haworthia sensu stricto comprises approximately 40 species, all endemic to South Africa, with the Eastern Cape as the center of diversity.

## Distribution and Habitat

Haworthia fasciata is endemic to the Eastern Cape Province of South Africa, occurring in a relatively small area around Port Elizabeth and Uitenhage. It grows in thicket vegetation on gently sloping terrain.

Plants are found growing under shrubs and in rock crevices, typically in partial shade. They are often partially buried in [[neurotropic-fungi-endemic-species-and-restricted-ranges-guzman-1998]] and are considered vulnerable.

The species is remarkably pest-resistant. Mealybugs occasionally appear in leaf axils but are easily controlled with [[isopropyl-alcohol]]. Fungal problems are rare if proper watering practices are followed.

Root mealybugs are a more serious problem, feeding on roots below the soil line where they go unnoticed. Infested plants show stunted growth and may develop corky root lesions. Systemic insecticide drenches are the most effective treatment.

Indoor temperatures of 15–25°C are ideal. The species tolerates brief exposure to -1°C but should be protected from frost. Avoid placing near heating vents or air conditioning that could cause rapid temperature fluctuations.

## Ecological Role

In its native Eastern Cape habitat, Haworthia fasciata grows as an understory plant in Albany thicket vegetation. Its cryptic coloration among quartz gravel provides camouflage against herbivores including tortoises and small mammals.

The white tubercles on the leaf undersides may serve multiple functions beyond camouflage. They scatter light into the leaf interior, enhancing photosynthesis in the shaded habitat, and may also reflect excess radiation.

The small, tubular flowers are pollinated by [[comparison-honey-bees-vs-native-solitary-bees]] and other small native insects. Unlike many Aloe relatives that attract sunbirds, Haworthia flowers are too small and produce insufficient nectar for avian pollinators.
