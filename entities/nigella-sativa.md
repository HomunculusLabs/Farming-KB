# Nigella sativa

## Overview

**Nigella sativa**, commonly known as black seed, black cumin, kalonji (Hindi/Urdu), habbat al-barakah (Arabic: "seed of blessing"), or Roman coriander, is an annual flowering plant in the family Ranunculaceae (buttercup family). Native to Southern Europe, North Africa, and Southwest Asia, it has been cultivated for over 3,000 years as both a culinary spice and a traditional [[medicinal-herb-growing-guide]]. ## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Magnoliophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Ranunculales
- **Family:** Ranunculaceae
- **Genus:** *Nigella*
- **Species:** *N. sativa*

First described by Linnaeus in 1753. The genus name *Nigella* derives from Latin *niger* (black), referring to the seed color. The species has a long history of taxonomic confusion — *N. sativa* should not be confused with *Bunium bulbocastanum* (black cumin of India), *Nigella damascena* (love-in-a-mist, an ornamental species), or *Carum carvi* (caraway), despite overlapping common names.

## Botanical Description

### Growth Habit
- **Life cycle:** Annual, completing its lifecycle in one growing season
- **Height:** 20–60 cm (8–24 inches)
- **Root:** Taproot system, moderately deep
- **Stem:** Erect, branching from the base, glabrous to finely pubescent
- **Leaves:** Finely divided, 2–3 times pinnatisect, with linear to lanceolate lobes; alternate arrangement; light green

### Flowers
- **Inflorescence:** Terminal, solitary pale blue to white flowers; 2–3 cm in diameter
- **Petals:** 5–7 (technically sepals, as the true petals are reduced to nectaries), pale blue to white, sometimes tinged with green
- **Nectaries:** 8–12 small, honeycomb-shaped nectaries between the petal bases
- **Stamens:** Numerous (15–20)
- **Blooming period:** Summer (June–August in Northern Hemisphere)
- **Pollination:** Primarily entomophilous (insect-pollinated), especially by bees

### Fruit and Seeds
- **Fruit:** An inflated, compound capsule (follicle), composed of 5–7 united carpels, 1.5–3 cm long; green when immature, turning straw-colored and papery at maturity
- **Seeds:** Numerous (hundreds per capsule), small (2–3 mm), trigonal, black, with a rough surface texture and a distinctive sharp, peppery aroma
- **Seed composition:** Fixed oil (30–35%), proteins (20–25%), carbohydrates (25–35%), fiber, ash, and bioactive compounds
- **Seed yield:** 500–1000 kg/ha under good conditions

## Phytochemistry

### Primary Bioactive Compound: Thymoquinone
- **Chemical name:** 2-Isopropyl-5-methylbenzo-1,4-quinone
- **Molecular formula:** C₁₀H₁₂O₂
- **Molecular weight:** 164.20 g/mol
- **Concentration in seeds:** 0.1–0.5% of seed weight (variable by cultivar, origin, and processing)
- **CAS:** 490-91-5
- **Solubility:** Lipophilic; soluble in organic solvents, poorly soluble in water

Thymoquinone is the most pharmacologically significant constituent, though it is not the only active compound. The therapeutic effects of *N. sativa* are attributed to the synergistic action of multiple constituents.

### Other Significant Constituents
- **Thymohydroquinone:** Reduced form of thymoquinone; antioxidant properties
- **p-Cymene (4-isopropyltoluene):** Monoterpene; anti-inflammatory
- **Carvacrol:** Monoterpene phenol; antimicrobial, antioxidant
- **4-Terpineol:** Monoterpene alcohol; antimicrobial, insecticidal
- **Longifolene:** Sesquiterpene; anti-inflammatory
- **α-[[pinene]], β-pinene:** Monoterpenes; bronchodilatory, anti-inflammatory
- **T-anethole:** Phenylpropene; antimicrobial
- **Nigellicine and nigellidine:** Alkaloids unique to *N. sativa*; immunomodulatory
- **Fixed oil fatty acids:** Linoleic acid (C18:2, ~55%), oleic acid (C18:1, ~20%), palmitic acid (C16:0, ~12%), stearic acid (C18:0, ~3%)

### Fixed Oil (Black Seed Oil)
Cold-pressed black seed oil is the most common commercial preparation. It contains:
- Essential oil fraction (0.4–0.5%): Volatile compounds including thymoquinone
- Fixed oil fraction: Rich in unsaturated fatty acids, particularly linoleic acid (omega-6)
- Phytosterols: β-Sitosterol, [[stigmasterol]], campesterol
- Tocopherols (vitamin E): α-Tocopherol, γ-tocopherol
- Squalene: Triterpene with antioxidant properties

## Pharmacological Activities

### Anti-inflammatory
Thymoquinone inhibits NF-κB signaling, COX-2 expression, and pro-inflammatory cytokine production (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6). These effects have been demonstrated in multiple in vitro and in vivo models of inflammation, including arthritis, colitis, and encephalomyelitis.

### Antioxidant
Thymoquinone and other constituents enhance endogenous antioxidant enzyme activity (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase) while directly scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS). The antioxidant capacity contributes to hepatoprotective, nephroprotective, and neuroprotective effects.

### Immunomodulatory
* N. sativa* demonstrates bidirectional immunomodulation — enhancing immune responses in immunocompromised states while suppressing excessive immune activation in autoimmune conditions. It modulates T-cell differentiation, cytokine balance, and natural killer cell activity.

### Antidiabetic
Multiple clinical trials have demonstrated that black seed supplementation (1–3 g/day) significantly reduces fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, and insulin resistance in patients with type 2 diabetes. Mechanisms include enhanced insulin sensitivity, pancreatic β-cell protection, and inhibition of hepatic gluconeogenesis.

### Anticancer
Preclinical studies show thymoquinone induces apoptosis in cancer cell lines (breast, colon, prostate, lung, pancreatic, leukemia) through multiple mechanisms: ROS generation, mitochondrial disruption, caspase activation, and inhibition of PI3K/Akt and NF-κB pathways. Clinical trials remain limited.

### Antimicrobial
Black seed oil and essential oil exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (including MRSA), fungi (including *[[candida-albicans]]*), and parasites (*Plasmodium*, *Schistosoma*).

### Respiratory and Gastroprotective
Traditional respiratory use is supported by bronchodilatory and antiasthmatic effects; thymoquinone reduces airway inflammation. Also demonstrates gastroprotective effects against gastric ulcers via antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms.

## Traditional and Ethnobotanical Use

- **Islamic medicine:** Prominent in Prophetic medicine (Tibb al-Nabawi). The Prophet Muhammad stated: "In the black seed there is healing for every disease except death" (Sahih Bukhari 5688), making it one of the most widely used medicinal plants in the Islamic world
- **Unani medicine:** Used as a carminative, stimulant, diuretic, emmenagogue, and galactagogue
- **Ayurveda:** Known as *krishna jiraka*; used for digestive issues, skin conditions, and as a general tonic
- **Culinary:** Seeds used as a spice in breads, pastries, pickles, and cheese throughout Middle Eastern, South Asian, and Ethiopian cuisines

## Cultivation

- **Climate:** Adapted to Mediterranean, semi-arid, and temperate climates; tolerates drought and poor soils
- **Soil:** Well-drained, sandy loam to clay loam; pH 6.0–8.0; tolerates saline conditions
- **Propagation:** Direct seeding; seed rate 10–15 kg/ha; sowing in spring or autumn at 1–2 cm depth
- **Water requirements:** Low to moderate; 300–500 mm annual rainfall adequate; avoid waterlogging
- **Fertilization:** Moderate NPK (40–60/30–40/20–30 kg/ha)
- **Harvest:** 100–130 days after sowing; harvest when capsules turn straw-colored; thresh and sun-dry

## Dosage and Preparations

### Common Preparations
- **Whole seeds:** 1–3 g/day, ground and mixed with food or honey; grinding increases bioavailability
- **Cold-pressed oil:** 250–500 mg (1–2 mL), 1–3 times daily
- **Standardized extracts:** Thymoquinone-standardized capsules (500 mg extract containing 2–5 mg thymoquinone)
- **Tea/decoction:** 1–2 g crushed seeds steeped in hot water for 10 minutes

### Safety Profile
- Generally well tolerated at traditional and studied doses
- Mild GI discomfort possible at higher doses; possible drug interactions with antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and anticoagulant medications
- Avoid in pregnancy at medicinal doses (uterine stimulant effects in animal studies)
