## Overview

Tigridia pavonia, commonly called tiger flower or peacock tiger-flower, is a bulbous perennial native to Mexico and [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]].

Molecular studies confirm Tigridia pavonia as closely related to Tigridia durangense and Tigridia galanthoides. Hybridization among these species has produced many cultivated forms available from specialty nurseries.

Chromosome number is 2n = 28. Polyploid cultivars with enhanced flower size are occasionally reported in the horticultural literature.

## Morphology

Plants grow 45–90 cm tall from globose corms 3–6 cm in diameter. The corm tunic is papery, brown, and splits into fibrous strands at the base.

Leaves are equitant, broadly linear, 30–60 cm long and 1.5–3 cm wide. The foliage is light green with a slightly glaucous bloom and conspicuous parallel venation throughout.

Flowering stems produce 3–7 successively opening flowers over a 3–4 week period in mid to late summer. Each stem branches once or twice, bearing terminal flowers at each branch apex.

Individual flowers are 10–15 cm across when fully open, among the largest in Iridaceae. Three outer tepals (falls) are flat and broad in shades of pink, red, orange, yellow, or white.

Three inner tepals form a central cup 3–5 cm deep, densely spotted in dark red, purple, or maroon against a lighter background. This bold pattern gives the flower its common name.

Three stylar branches arch over the cup, each bearing a fringed, petaloid stigma. Stamens are positioned beneath the style branches for direct pollinator contact during visits.

The fruit is an ellipsoid capsule 2–3 cm long containing numerous angular, reddish-brown seeds. Seeds lack appendages and disperse by wind and water during the rainy season.

Plants in cultivation often produce larger flowers than [[guzman-central-america-neurotropic-fungi-species-distribution]] and the Andes where it has escaped cultivation. It typically establishes in disturbed sites at appropriate elevations.

Mexico is the center of diversity for Tigridia, harboring nearly all 35 species. Many species are narrow endemics restricted to single mountain ranges or valleys in the Sierra Madre.

The species is not considered threatened in its native range. Some highland meadow habitats face pressure from agricultural expansion near Mexico City and surrounding states.

Seeds are dispersed locally by wind and water during the rainy season. Long-distance dispersal is rare, contributing to the [[neurotropic-fungi-endemic-species-and-restricted-ranges-guzman-1998]] of many Tigridia species.

## Cultivation

Tiger flowers require full sun and well-drained soil for best performance. Plant corms 8–10 cm deep in late spring after all frost danger has passed.

The species is hardy to approximately USDA zone 7 with winter mulch. In colder zones, lift corms in autumn, dry them, and store at 5–10 °C until spring replanting.

Corms produce 1–3 flowering stems per season under good conditions. Flowering begins 10–12 weeks after spring planting, peaking in July and August.

Water regularly during active growth but reduce watering as foliage yellows in autumn. Excess moisture during dormancy promotes corm rot, the most common cause of cultivation failure.

Propagation is by corm division or seed. Offsets form at the base of the mother corm and can be separated at lifting time in autumn before storage.
