## Overview

Amanita vernus (sometimes written A. vernus or [[amanita-verna]]) is a historical name applied to a deadly white Amanita species that was responsible for numerous fatal poisonings in 19th-century America. The name refers to what modern taxonomists generally classify as a white form of [[amanita-phalloides]] (the death cap) or [[amanita-bisporigera]] (the [[bloomfield-destroying-angel-amanita-virosa-amatoxin-poisoning-clinical-course]]). It was one of the most feared mushrooms in early [[palmer-mushroom-charts-american-mycological-education]] literature.

## Historical Description

Based on Palmer's and McIlvaine's accounts, A. vernus was characterized by:

- **Cap**: Pure satiny white, convex to flat, sometimes showing remains of the universal veil as warts or scales
- **Gills**: White at all stages of growth (a key distinguishing feature from the common mushroom, which has pink or purple gills)
- **Stem**: Free from the gills, with a kid-like ring (annulus) near the top
- **Base**: Distinctive bulb covered with a sheath or volva — the critical identifying feature
- **Flesh**: White, tasteless, and nearly scentless in young specimens; developing a strong, unpleasant odor with age
- **Habitat**: Woods, uncultivated ground, margins of pastures near woods, fence corners

### Confusion with Common Mushroom

Young specimens closely resembled the common mushroom ([[agaricus-campestris]]), leading to frequent misidentification. Key differences:
- The common mushroom never has a volva and is seldom found in woods
- Common mushrooms have pink or purple gills (darkening with age), while Amanita gills remain white
- Common mushrooms have a much shorter stem than Amanita species

## Toxicology

Amanita vernus contained the deadly alkaloid amanitine (modern: amatoxins, primarily alpha-amanitin). Clinical picture:

1. **Latent period**: 6-18 hours (sometimes up to 24 hours)
2. **Initial symptoms**: Abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, foul breath, coated tongue
3. **Gastrointestinal phase**: Intense vomiting, violent diarrhea, tenesmus
4. **Neurological**: Stupor, coma, coma-vigil, cold extremities, delirium
5. **Vital signs**: Tachycardia (120-140 bpm), temperature elevation (up to 104 F), imperceptible pulse in terminal phase
6. **Death**: By asthenia — progressive failure of nerve centers governing respiration and cardiac function

The longer the latent period, the more alkaloid was absorbed and the more severe the outcome.

## Historical Cases

### The Farris Family, Shenandoah, PA (August 1885)
- Five persons poisoned after gathering A. vernus from a chestnut-dominated woods
- Two fatalities: Thomas R. (13) at 56 hours; Mrs. F. (22) at 63 hours
- Three survived after atropine treatment by Dr. S. E. Shadle

### The W. Family, Chelsea (September 1878)
- Five family members poisoned after confusing Amanita with puffballs
- All survived with varying symptom severity

## Modern Taxonomic Note

The name Amanita vernus as used by 19th-century authors most likely corresponds to [[amanita-phalloides-death-cap]] (white forms), Amanita bisporigera ([[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] American destroying angel), or potentially Amanita virosa (European destroying angel). Modern taxonomy has largely abandoned vernus as a species epithet for North American white Amanita species.

## Modern Understanding of Amatoxin Poisoning

Modern toxicology has clarified the mechanism that 19th-century physicians observed but could not fully explain:

- **Alpha-amanitin** inhibits RNA polymerase II, halting protein synthesis in affected cells
- The liver is the primary target organ (hepatocytes accumulate the toxin via OATP transporters)
- The characteristic latent period (6-24 hours) corresponds to the time required for irreversible cellular damage to accumulate
- The gastrointestinal phase represents the body's response to direct toxin exposure in the GI tract
- The apparent improvement after GI symptoms ("false recovery") precedes fulminant hepatic failure

### Modern Treatment Protocol
1. Aggressive GI decontamination (activated charcoal)
2. Intravenous fluid resuscitation
3. Silibinin (intravenous milk thistle extract) — blocks hepatocyte uptake
4. High-dose penicillin G — displaces amatoxin from protein binding
5. N-acetylcysteine — hepatoprotective antioxidant
6. Liver transplantation for fulminant hepatic failure

The atropine treatment advocated by 19th-century physicians addresses only the muscarinic symptoms and does not counteract the amatoxin mechanism itself, though it remains useful for managing the acute cholinergic crisis.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Division**: Basidiomycota
- **Class**: Agaricomycetes
- **Order**: Agaricales
- **Family**: Amanitaceae
- **Genus**: Amanita
- **Historical species**: A. vernus (now generally A. bisporigera or A. phalloides)
- **Toxicity**: Deadly — contains amatoxins
