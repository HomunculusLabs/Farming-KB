## Overview

**Carex hirta**, commonly known as hairy sedge or bottle sedge, is a perennial sedge of the family Cyperaceae. It is widespread across Europe and parts of western Asia.

The species is one of the most commonly encountered sedges in lowland Britain and western Europe. It is readily identified by the dense hairs covering its leaf sheaths and utricles, a character unique among British Carex species.

Hairy sedge is a plant of disturbed ground, meadows, roadsides, and waste places. It is tolerant of a wide range of soil conditions and is often one of the first sedges to colonise newly disturbed habitats.

The specific epithet hirta refers to the hairy nature of the plant's vegetative parts. This distinctive pubescence makes the species immediately recognisable even to novice botanists.

The species is diploid with 2n = 56 chromosomes. Cytological studies have revealed some population-level variation in chromosome morphology across its European range.

In [[biodiversity-fungal-herbarium-collections]], Carex hirta is well-represented due to its conspicuousness and wide distribution. The pressed specimens retain their characteristic hairy covering, making historical identification reliable.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Carex hirta** L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum. It belongs to Carex section Carex within subgenus Carex, a group characterised by unisexual spikes.

The plant grows 30–90 cm tall from short, creeping rhizomes. Culms are stout, trigonous, and rough-textured towards the apex.

Leaf blades are 4–8 mm wide, flat, and greyish-green. The leaf sheaths are conspicuously hairy, a key identification feature that distinguishes this species from all other British sedges.

The inflorescence consists of 3–5 spikes, with the upper spike male and the lower spikes female. Female glumes are dark brown with a greenish keel and are shorter than the utricles.

Uricles are 5–7 mm long, broadly ovoid, and densely covered in short hairs. They taper to a bidentate beak with two conspicuous teeth at the apex.

Flowering occurs from May to July, with fruiting extending through August. The achenes are small, brown, and enclosed within the persistent utricles.

## Distribution and Habitat

Hairy sedge is native to most of Europe, extending from the British Isles eastward to western Siberia. Its range includes the Mediterranean basin, where it occurs at higher elevations.

In the British Isles, it is widespread and common throughout lowland England and Wales. It becomes scarcer in Scotland and is rare in Ireland, where it is largely confined to the [[east-coast-sour-diesel-clone-lineage-chemdawg-family-tree]].

The species occupies a remarkably broad range of habitats, including damp meadows, woodland rides, roadside verges, railway banks, and waste ground. It is particularly characteristic of moderately fertile, moist but well-drained soils.

It is absent from permanently waterlogged sites and very acidic soils. It thrives on clay loams and alluvial soils in open or semi-shaded conditions.

Hairy sedge has been introduced to parts [[berry-growing]] and New Zealand. In these areas it is generally uncommon and not considered invasive.

The species shows a preference for neutral to slightly alkaline soil pH, typically ranging from 6.0 to 7.5. It is absent from strongly calcareous and strongly acidic substrates.

In montane regions of southern Europe, hairy sedge occurs at elevations up to 1,500 metres. At these altitudes it is restricted to moist meadows and stream margins.

Urban populations are common, reflecting the species' tolerance of nitrogen enrichment and compacted soils. It frequently occurs along pavement edges and in neglected urban green spaces.

## Ecology

**Carex hirta** is a generalist species with wide ecological amplitude. It occurs in both semi-natural and strongly anthropogenic habitats, reflecting its tolerance of disturbance.

The species is an important component of neutral grassland communities in lowland Britain. It is characteristic of the MG8 Crested Dog's-tail grassland type of the National Vegetation Classification.

Larvae of several moth species feed on the leaves and inflorescences, including the marsh oblique-barred and twin-spot carpet. The seeds provide food for small granivorous birds.

The hairy utricles may serve as a defence against seed predation by making the fruits less palatable. This morphological adaptation is unusual among temperate Carex species.

In agricultural landscapes, hairy sedge can become a weed of arable fields and pastures. Its tolerance of herbicides varies, and it persists in intensively managed grasslands where other species decline.

The species responds positively to moderate [[gadd-mycelial-network-resilience-grazing-pressure]] and can increase in swards that are grazed by cattle or sheep. Heavy grazing or frequent cutting reduces its abundance.

[[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] have been documented in Carex hirta roots, though the functional significance of these relationships in sedges remains less well understood than in grasses.

The species can serve as a phytometer for assessing soil nitrogen status in grassland monitoring schemes. Its abundance increases with moderate nitrogen enrichment.

In wetland margin communities, hairy sedge co-occurs with species such as meadowsweet, ragged-robin, and creeping buttercup. It is a component of the Fen-meadow community complex.

## Cultivation and Uses

Hairy sedge has limited ornamental value but is occasionally grown in wildflower meadows and ecological plantings. Its robust growth and tolerance of poor soils make it useful for habitat restoration projects.

In traditional European [[childrens-herbal-medicine]], the roots were used as a mild astringent and diuretic. These applications are largely historical and the species has no significant modern pharmacological use.
