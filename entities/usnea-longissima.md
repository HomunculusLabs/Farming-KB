## Overview

**Usnea longissima** is a fruticose lichen in the family Parmeliaceae, commonly known as the **old man's beard lichen** or **Methuselah's beard**. It is one of the longest lichen species in the world, with thalli frequently exceeding one meter in length.

The species forms distinctive pendulous strands that drape from tree branches in old-growth and mature forests. It is widely regarded as an indicator species for forest continuity and air quality due to its sensitivity to atmospheric pollution and habitat disturbance.

Usnea longissima has a circumpolar distribution in boreal and temperate forests of the Northern Hemisphere. Despite its wide range, it is declining across much of its distribution due to habitat loss from forestry and air pollution.

The species has been used medicinally by indigenous peoples [[huckleberries-and-wild-berries-of-north-america]] and Scandinavia. Contemporary interest in its [[clematis-vitalba]]*.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Usnea longissima was first described by Erik Acharius in 1810. The taxonomy of the genus *Usnea* has been substantially revised with molecular phylogenetics, but *U. longissima* remains a well-defined species.

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Fungi
- Division: Ascomycota
- Class: Lecanoromycetes
- Order: Lecanorales
- Family: Parmeliaceae
- Genus: *Usnea* Adans.
- Species: *U. longissima* Ach.

The thallus is fruticose, highly elongated, and pendulous, typically 20–100 cm long but occasionally reaching 200 cm or more. It consists of a single central axis with sparse, irregular lateral branching.

The central axis contains a tough, elastic central cord (medulla) that provides [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of North America, Europe, and Asia. In North America, it ranges from Alaska to northern California and east across Canada to the Maritime Provinces.

European populations are found in Scandinavia, the British Isles, [[psilocybe-bohemica-central-european]] mountain ranges, and the Carpathians. Asian distribution extends through Siberia, the Russian Far East, and into Japan.

The species is an obligate epiphyte, growing almost exclusively on coniferous trees. Preferred hosts include Douglas-fir, western hemlock, Sitka spruce, and various pine species in the Pacific Northwest, and Norway spruce and Scots pine in Europe.

It shows a strong preference for old-growth and late-successional forests with high humidity, stable microclimates, and minimal disturbance. Thalli are most abundant in the lower canopy on branches with rough bark and in sheltered coves.

Elevational range varies geographically. In coastal Pacific Northwest forests, the species occurs from sea level to approximately 800 meters. In interior mountain ranges and Scandinavia, it is found primarily above 500 meters where humidity is consistently high.

The species is absent from recently disturbed or young forests. It typically requires 80–150 years of forest continuity before substantial colonization occurs.

## Ecology and Reproduction

Usnea longissima is a slow-growing lichen with estimated annual elongation rates of 1–3 cm per year under optimal conditions. A one-meter thallus may therefore represent several decades of uninterrupted growth.

The species is highly sensitive to atmospheric sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides. It disappears from areas with even moderate air pollution, making it a reliable bioindicator for air quality monitoring programs.

Vegetative reproduction through thallus fragmentation is the primary dispersal mechanism. Fragments become entangled in animal fur or bird feathers and are transported to suitable substrates.

Wind dispersal of fragments is also significant during storm events, when long thalli are broken from canopy branches and scattered across the forest floor. Fragments landing on suitable host branches can establish new colonies if moisture conditions permit.

The lichen provides habitat and foraging substrate for various invertebrates, including mites, springtails, and bark lice. Some bird species, notably the marbled murrelet and various flycatchers, incorporate *U. longissima* strands into nest construction.

Competition with faster-growing bryophytes and foliose lichens limits colonization on branches where these groups are already established. *U. longissima* persists in the forest interior where light levels are low enough to suppress bryophyte competitors.

## Ethnobotany and Uses

Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest used *U. longissima* medicinally. The Makah and Quinault applied poultices of the lichen to wounds and burns, while the Tlingit used it as a dressing for cuts and sores.

Scandinavian folk medicine employed the species as a wound healing agent and treatment for respiratory ailments. The lichen was steeped in water or alcohol to extract [[cordyceps-cancer-bioactive-compounds]] before application.

Modern pharmacological research has focused on usnic acid, the primary [[fungal-elicitors-enhanced-secondary-metabolite-production]] of *Usnea* species. Usnic acid demonstrates broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, particularly against Gram-positive bacteria.
