## Strip Grazing

## Description

Strip grazing is an intensive rotational grazing technique that uses permanent or movable electric fences to allocate small strips of pasture to livestock on a frequent rotation basis (average 2-5 day cycle). The method confines animals to a narrow band of fresh pasture, ensuring uniform grazing and preventing selective overgrazing of preferred species. Mollison describes strip grazing as one of seven management strategies for grasslands and rangeland.

## Classification

- **Category**: Agricultural technique — rotational grazing management
- **Equipment**: Electric fencing (permanent or movable)
- **Stocking intensity**: Intensive — suitable only for improved pastures
- **Primary application**: Dairy cattle on improved pastures
- **Rotation frequency**: 2-5 day average rotation

## Key Facts

- Uses electric fences to control livestock access to narrow strips of pasture
- Average rotation period of 2-5 days per strip
- Grass should be at 15 cm height or better before grazing
- Suitable only for improved pastures and dairy cattle
- Akin to tethering for a few milk cows or goats on limited improved pastures
- One of seven management strategies Mollison outlines for livestock on grasslands

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Strip grazing is positioned within Mollison's spectrum of grassland management strategies ranging from natural wild systems (lightest management) through to fixed stocking (heaviest). Strip grazing falls on the intensive end:

1. **Controlled defoliation**: By restricting animals to small strips, grazing pressure is concentrated enough to prevent selective avoidance of less palatable species. This maintains a more balanced sward composition.

2. **Rest and recovery**: Once animals are moved to the next strip, the previously grazed area has a defined recovery period before re-grazing. This prevents the continuous defoliation that degrades pasture under set stocking.

3. **Integration with mowing**: Fields not well-grazed can be mown 3-6 weeks before stock return, preventing rank growth and maintaining forage quality. Many good farmers "mow and mulch to improve soils."

4. **Dairy optimization**: Strip grazing is particularly suited to dairy production where consistent, high-quality forage intake directly affects milk yield. The method ensures fresh, leafy forage is always available.

5. **Comparison with other methods**: Mollison contrasts strip grazing with short-rotation stocking (3-7 day paddock rotation with 3-5 week rest), cut-and-feed (zero grazing with manure return), and [[mixed-livestock-rotation]] (multi-species succession). Each method suits different scales, climates, and production goals.

## Context in Mollison's Grazing Spectrum

| Method | Scale | Stocking | Rotation | Best For |
|--------|-------|----------|----------|----------|
| Natural wild | Extensive | Light | Migration | Wildlife lands |
| Long rotation | Extensive | Very light | 7-9 year | Semi-arid range |
| Short rotation | Improved | 15-25/ha | 3-5 weeks | Humid pastures |
| Strip grazing | Intensive | Variable | 2-5 days | Dairy cattle |
| Cut and feed | Small-scale | 2-20 cows | Daily | Villages, tropics |
| Mixed rotation | Mixed | Multi-species | Variable | Permaculture farms |
| Fixed stocking | Any | Risk if heavy | None | Only with light density |
