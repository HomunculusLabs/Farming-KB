# Panaeolus africanus

**Type:** Species (Fungus)
**Category:** Basidiomycota, Agaricales, Bolbitiaceae, Panaeolus
**Also known as:** None widely used

## Overview
Panaeolus africanus is a psychoactive mushroom species originally
described from central and southern Africa. It is notable for its
specialized habitat on hippopotamus and elephant dung, from which it
may be spreading globally through the distribution of "zoo-doo" as
garden fertilizer. It contains psilocybin and psilocin in variable
amounts according to Ola'h (1969). The specific epithet "africanus"
denotes its continent of origin.

## Key Details
- **Cap:** 1.5-2 cm broad. Obtusely conic, hemispheric, rarely broadly
  convex. Surface smooth (may crack in sun), viscid when wet,
  reticulated along margin. Grayish, creamy white, sometimes
  reddish brown towards disc, becoming grayish brown.
- **Gills:** Adnate to adnexed, sometimes sinuate, rarely subdecurrent,
  widely spaced, irregular. Grayish at first, grayish black to blackish
  with age, mottled as spores mature.
- **Stem:** 30-50 mm by 4-6 mm thick. Equal, firm, pruinose. Whitish
  to white with pinkish tones, generally lighter than cap. No veil
  remnants.
- **Spores:** Nearly black in deposit, 11.5-14.5 x 7.9-10 um,
  lemon-shaped, often variable. Basidia 2- and 4-spored. Cheilocystidia
  clavate. Pleurocystidia present with extended sharp apices, 25-50
  (60) x 10-17 (20) um.
- **Habitat:** On hippopotamus and elephant dung in spring or rainy
  seasons. Reported from central Africa to southern Sudan. Probably
  more widely distributed. Found on elephant dung at Seattle Zoo.
- **Chemistry:** Contains psilocybin and psilocin in irregular amounts
  (Ola'h 1969). Exact potency data limited.
- **Comparison:** Similar to Panaeolus antillarum (= P. phalaenarum)
  but with smaller spores. Distinguished by its habitat preference for
  large herbivore dung.

## Distribution and Ecology
Panaeolus africanus was originally described from the heartland of
the African continent, specifically from central Africa through
southern Sudan. Its strict association with dung of large herbivores
(hippopotamus and elephant) reflects a highly specialized ecological
niche. The species has been found on elephant dung at the Seattle Zoo,
demonstrating its ability to colonize exotic substrates far from its
native range. The commercial distribution of "zoo-doo" as garden
fertilizer provides a plausible mechanism for the species' spread to
