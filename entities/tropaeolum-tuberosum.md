## Overview

**Tropaeolum tuberosum** Ruiz & Pav., commonly known as **mashua**, **añu**, or **isasño**, is a perennial tuberous plant in the family Tropaeolaceae. Native to the Andean highlands of Peru, Bolivia, and Ecuador, it has been cultivated as a food crop for over 8,000 years.

Mashua is one of the most important traditional Andean root crops alongside potato, oca (*[[cannabis-disease-pest-resistance-breeding-clarke]], and pungent flavor that diminishes with cooking.

The tubers are rich in vitamin C, glucosinolates, and antioxidants. They exhibit remarkable pest resistance due to natural isothiocyanate compounds, making them a promising low-input crop for sustainable agriculture.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Order Brassicales → Family Tropaeolaceae → Genus *Tropaeolum* → Species *T. tuberosum*.

**Synonyms:** *Tropaeolum tuberosum* subsp. *tuberosum* (autonym), *Tropaeolum pendulum* Vell.

**Tubers:** Clusters of 5–20 tubers are produced on underground stolons. Individual tubers are spindle-shaped to elongated, 5–15 cm long and 2–5 cm in diameter. Skin coloration is extremely variable — white, yellow, orange, red, purple, and bicolor forms exist.

The flesh is firm, crisp, and yellow to orange. Raw mashua has a strong, peppery, radish-like flavor due to glucosinolate content. Cooking significantly reduces the pungency and reveals a mild, slightly sweet taste.

**Vines:** Slender, climbing or trailing stems up to 2–3 m in length. The plant is not self-supporting and scrambles over neighboring vegetation or trellises. Leaves are peltate (shield-shaped), 4–8 cm diameter, with long petioles.

**Flowers:** Showy, tubular, 2–3 cm long. Colors range from orange to red, yellow, or bicolor. Flowering typically occurs in the [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], and Japan, though it remains a niche crop outside the Andes.

## Cultivation

Mashua is propagated vegetatively from tuber pieces (seed tubers). Each planting piece should contain at least one bud and weigh approximately 30–50 grams. Planting occurs at the onset of the rainy season.

Plants are spaced 30–40 cm apart in rows 60–80 cm apart. They require 6–8 months to reach maturity. Yields range from 10–30 tonnes per hectare, comparable to or exceeding potato yields at equivalent altitudes.

Mashua is remarkably pest-resistant. Foliage is rarely damaged by insects or disease, and tubers show resistance to nematodes and soil-borne pathogens. This resistance is attributed to glucosinolate-derived isothiocyanates.

The crop requires minimal inputs — no fertilizer, pesticides, or irrigation beyond natural rainfall in its native range. This makes it an excellent candidate for low-input and permaculture systems.

## Nutritional Profile

Mashua tubers are approximately 75–80% water. Per 100 g fresh weight, they provide roughly 70 kcal, 14–16 g carbohydrates (primarily starch), 1–2 g protein, and negligible fat.

They are an excellent source of vitamin C (40–60 mg/100 g), exceeding potato levels. They also contain significant amounts of B vitamins, potassium, and iron.

The glucosinolate content (primarily glucotropaeolin and benzyl glucosinolate) is responsible for the pungent flavor and contributes antioxidant and potential anti-cancer properties. These compounds are reduced 70–90% by cooking.

## Permaculture Applications

Mashua offers several advantages for permaculture and sustainable farming systems. Its climbing growth habit makes it suitable for vertical stacking in food forests, using existing plants as living trellises.

The crop's complete pest resistance eliminates the need for any pesticide inputs. Its ability to produce high yields on marginal, nutrient-poor soils reduces or eliminates fertilizer requirements. These traits make it one of the lowest-input root crops available.

In Andean traditional agriculture, mashua is intercropped with oca, olluco, and potatoes in complex polyculture systems. These intercropping arrangements optimize land use and reduce disease pressure through botanical diversity.
