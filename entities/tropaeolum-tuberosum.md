## Overview

**Tropaeolum tuberosum** Ruiz & Pav., commonly known as **mashua**, **añu**, or **isasño**, is a perennial tuberous plant in the family Tropaeolaceae. Native to the Andean highlands of Peru, Bolivia, and Ecuador, it has been cultivated as a food crop for over 8,000 years.

Mashua is one of the most important traditional Andean root crops alongside potato, oca (*Oxalis tuberosa*), and olluco (*Ullucus tuberosus*). It is notable for its high productivity at high altitudes, pest resistance, and pungent flavor that diminishes with cooking.

The tubers are rich in vitamin C, glucosinolates, and antioxidants. They exhibit remarkable pest resistance due to natural isothiocyanate compounds, making them a promising low-input crop for sustainable agriculture.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Order Brassicales → Family Tropaeolaceae → Genus *Tropaeolum* → Species *T. tuberosum*.

**Synonyms:** *Tropaeolum tuberosum* subsp. *tuberosum* (autonym), *Tropaeolum pendulum* Vell.

**Tubers:** Clusters of 5–20 tubers are produced on underground stolons. Individual tubers are spindle-shaped to elongated, 5–15 cm long and 2–5 cm in diameter. Skin coloration is extremely variable — white, yellow, orange, red, purple, and bicolor forms exist.

The flesh is firm, crisp, and yellow to orange. Raw mashua has a strong, peppery, radish-like flavor due to glucosinolate content. Cooking significantly reduces the pungency and reveals a mild, slightly sweet taste.

**Vines:** Slender, climbing or trailing stems up to 2–3 m in length. The plant is not self-supporting and scrambles over neighboring vegetation or trellises. Leaves are peltate (shield-shaped), 4–8 cm diameter, with long petioles.

**Flowers:** Showy, tubular, 2–3 cm long. Colors range from orange to red, yellow, or bicolor. Flowering typically occurs in the southern hemisphere autumn (March–May). The flowers are edible and ornamental.

## Distribution and Habitat

Mashua is native to the Andean altiplano, where it is cultivated between 2,500 and 4,000 meters elevation. It is primarily grown in Peru (especially the departments of Puno, Cusco, and Huancavelica), Bolivia, and Ecuador.

The plant thrives in cool, moist conditions with short day lengths typical of tropical highland environments. It is adapted to poor, rocky, and acidic volcanic soils where few other crops perform well.

Mashua has been introduced to New Zealand, where it has naturalized in some regions. Experimental cultivation has occurred in Europe, North America, and Japan, though it remains a niche crop outside the Andes.

## Cultivation

Mashua is propagated vegetatively from tuber pieces (seed tubers). Each planting piece should contain at least one bud and weigh approximately 30–50 grams. Planting occurs at the onset of the rainy season.

Plants are spaced 30–40 cm apart in rows 60–80 cm apart. They require 6–8 months to reach maturity. Yields range from 10–30 tonnes per hectare, comparable to or exceeding potato yields at equivalent altitudes.

Mashua is remarkably pest-resistant. Foliage is rarely damaged by insects or disease, and tubers show resistance to nematodes and soil-borne pathogens. This resistance is attributed to glucosinolate-derived isothiocyanates.

The crop requires minimal inputs — no fertilizer, pesticides, or irrigation beyond natural rainfall in its native range. This makes it an excellent candidate for low-input and permaculture systems.

## Nutritional Profile

Mashua tubers are approximately 75–80% water. Per 100 g fresh weight, they provide roughly 70 kcal, 14–16 g carbohydrates (primarily starch), 1–2 g protein, and negligible fat.

They are an excellent source of vitamin C (40–60 mg/100 g), exceeding potato levels. They also contain significant amounts of B vitamins, potassium, and iron.

The glucosinolate content (primarily glucotropaeolin and benzyl glucosinolate) is responsible for the pungent flavor and contributes antioxidant and potential anti-cancer properties. These compounds are reduced 70–90% by cooking.

## Permaculture Applications

Mashua offers several advantages for permaculture and sustainable farming systems. Its climbing growth habit makes it suitable for vertical stacking in food forests, using existing plants as living trellises.

The crop's complete pest resistance eliminates the need for any pesticide inputs. Its ability to produce high yields on marginal, nutrient-poor soils reduces or eliminates fertilizer requirements. These traits make it one of the lowest-input root crops available.

In Andean traditional agriculture, mashua is intercropped with oca, olluco, and potatoes in complex polyculture systems. These intercropping arrangements optimize land use and reduce disease pressure through botanical diversity.

Mashua's cold tolerance and frost resistance extend the growing season in temperate climates. The plant can survive light frosts and continues tuber development at temperatures as low as 5°C.

## Traditional Uses

In Andean communities, mashua is consumed boiled, roasted, or added to soups and stews. The raw tubers are sometimes soaked in water or exposed to sunlight before cooking to reduce pungency.

Traditional medicine attributes diuretic, anti-inflammatory, and aphrodisiac-reducing properties to mashua. Colonial-era records note that Inca soldiers were fed mashua specifically to reduce libido during military campaigns.

The flowers are used as a colorful garnish and are edible raw. Dried tubers can be stored for several months under cool, dark conditions.

Traditional Andean farmers recognize numerous named landrace varieties, distinguished by tuber color, shape, flavor intensity, and maturity period. This varietal diversity represents an important genetic resource for crop improvement programs.

## Limitations and Challenges

Despite its advantages, mashua faces several barriers to wider adoption outside the Andes. The strong pungent flavor of raw tubers is unfamiliar and often unappealing to consumers accustomed to mild root vegetables like potatoes.

The climbing growth habit complicates mechanical harvesting. Tubers produced along the length of stolons are distributed through the soil profile rather than concentrated in a compact root zone, making efficient harvest difficult at scale.

Day-length sensitivity restricts cultivation to specific latitudes. Mashua requires short days (12–13 hours or less) to initiate tuberization, which limits production in temperate zones without careful variety selection or photoperiod manipulation.

Limited seed availability outside South America is another constraint. Most cultivated mashua exists as landraces maintained by smallholder farmers, and formal breeding programs are virtually nonexistent.

## Global Interest and Experimental Cultivation

Mashua has attracted growing interest from researchers and sustainable agriculture advocates seeking climate-resilient, low-input crops. Experimental plots in New Zealand, the Pacific Northwest of North America, and parts of Europe have demonstrated viable yields.

In New Zealand, mashua has escaped cultivation and naturalized in some areas, particularly on the South Island. It is sometimes considered a minor weed in pastures but is also valued by home gardeners for its ornamental flowers and edible tubers.

The Crop Trust has identified mashua as a priority underutilized crop for genebank conservation. Efforts are underway to characterize and preserve the genetic diversity of Andean mashua landraces, which face erosion from modernization of Andean agriculture.
