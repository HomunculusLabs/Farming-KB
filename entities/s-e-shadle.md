## Dr. S. E. Shadle

## Description

Dr. S. E. Shadle was a physician practicing in Shenandoah, Pennsylvania, who achieved the first documented successful clinical use of atropine as an antidote in human Amanita mushroom poisoning. His treatment of the Farris family in August 1885 — five patients poisoned by Amanita verna, with three survivors — provided the critical real-world confirmation of the atropine-muscarine antagonism that had previously been demonstrated only in laboratory animals by Dr. Isaac Ott. Dr. Shadle's detailed case report, written in response to inquiries from Charles McIlvaine, remains one of the most thorough clinical descriptions of Amanita poisoning progression and atropine treatment in the historical literature.

## Classification

- **Type:** Historical figure — physician
- **Location:** Shenandoah, Pennsylvania
- **Active period:** 1880s
- **Specialty:** General practice (rural/industrial community physician)
- **Notable achievement:** First successful clinical application of atropine in human Amanita poisoning

## Key Facts

### The Farris Family Poisoning (August 1885)

**Circumstances:**

- **Date:** Sunday, August 30 (ingestion) through Monday, August 31 and following days
- **Location:** Shenandoah, Pennsylvania, a coal mining community
- **Victims:** Five individuals who consumed Amanita verna mushrooms gathered from a chestnut-dominated woodland

**Patients:**

1. **Mr. F.** — Male, 29, miner, "intemperate life" — SURVIVED
2. **Mrs. F.** — Female, 22, "delicate body," neurotic tendency — FATAL (63 hours post-ingestion)
3. **Mrs. R.** (mother-in-law) — Female, 45, "nervo-bilious" — SURVIVED
4. **Thomas R.** (brother-in-law) — Male, 13, "well developed" — FATAL (56 hours post-ingestion)
5. **Mrs. B.** (neighbor) — Female, 40, "tall, spare" — SURVIVED

**Timeline:**

- **Sunday afternoon:** Mr. F. and Thomas R. gathered mushrooms from nearby woods
- **Sunday ~9 PM:** Mrs. F. cooked three pints stewed in milk with butter, pepper, salt
- **Monday before dawn:** First symptoms appeared — abdominal distress, cerebral disturbance
- **Monday ~10 AM (12 hours post-ingestion):** Dr. Shadle arrived to find all five in grave condition

**Symptoms Observed:**

- Foul breath, coated tongue, stomach pain, nausea
- Intense vomiting (four patients); violent retching (Mrs. R.)
- Gastro-intestinal irritation progressing to tenesmus
- Pulse: 120–140 BPM, soft and compressible, intermittent
- Temperature rise to 104°F (axillary)
- Progressive stupor, cold extremities, extreme prostration
- Thomas R. progressed to coma; Mrs. F. to coma-vigil
- Shrunken facial appearance, sunken eyes, dusky skin
- Imperceptible pulse at wrist hours before death
- Visual disturbances — transient blindness despite pupillary light response

### Treatment Protocol

**Gastrointestinal evacuation:** Emetic to Mrs. R.; mild purge to others

**Symptomatic relief:**

- Cracked ice for burning mouth and throat
- Bismuth subnitrate and creosote mixture with mucilage of acacia and peppermint water
- 1/8 grain morphine sulfate hypodermically for abdominal pain

**Circulatory support:**

- Alcoholic stimulants freely administered
- Tincture of digitalis by mouth and subcutaneously
- Heated bricks and hot water bottles to warm cold body surfaces

**Antidotal treatment (the key innovation):**

- Subcutaneous injections of sulfate of atropia (atropine sulfate)
- Repeated doses ranging from 1/120 to 1/60 grain
- "The injections invariably were followed by a perceptible improvement"
- Heart's action became stronger, pulse returned, respiration deepened

### Results

- **Three of five patients survived (60%)**
- Two fatalities likely due to large quantities consumed and treatment delay
- Atropine injections consistently produced measurable vital sign improvement

### Shadle's Conclusions

1. Poisoning by Amanita verna is slow in manifesting effects
2. It destroys life by a process of asthenia (progressive loss of vital function)
3. Atropine is an antidote and "should be pushed heroically from the earliest inception"

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Dr. Shadle's case report is historically invaluable because it:

- Confirmed in humans what Dr. Ott had demonstrated in frogs — atropine counteracts Amanita alkaloids
- Established the clinical protocol of repeated subcutaneous atropine dosing
- Documented the natural history of Amanita verna poisoning in unprecedented detail
- Demonstrated the critical importance of early intervention

The Shenandoah case was brought to wider attention by Charles McIlvaine, who contacted Shadle upon reading newspaper reports and arranged for mushroom specimen identification, confirming the species as Amanita verna.
