# Gymnopilus Earlei

## Overview

*Gymnopilus earlei* is a large, wood-inhabiting mushroom in the family Cortinariaceae (or Hymenogastraceae under some classifications), notable as one of the psilocybin-containing species within the genus *Gymnopilus*. It is commonly known as "Earle's Gymnopilus" and is found in tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas.

The species is one of the larger members of its genus, frequently producing striking orange to reddish-orange fruiting bodies on decaying hardwoods. Like other psilocybian *Gymnopilus* species, it develops characteristic greenish-blue bruising on the stipe and pileus when handled.

*G. earlei* is primarily of interest to mycologists studying the convergent evolution of psilocybin biosynthesis across distant fungal lineages.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Cortinariaceae (sensu Hesler 1969)
- **Genus:** *Gymnopilus* P. Karst.
- **Species:** *G. earlei* Murrill

The species was first described by William Alphonso Murrill in 1913 from collections made in Jamaica. The specific epithet honors Franklin Sumner Earle, an American mycologist who conducted extensive fungal surveys in the Caribbean and southern United States.

The genus *Gymnopilus* derives from Greek *gymnos* (naked) and *pilus* (cap), referring to the typically smooth, non-viscid cap surface characteristic of the genus.

## Morphology

### Pileus

Cap is 40–120 mm in diameter, convex to broadly convex, sometimes plano-convex in maturity. Surface is dry, smooth to finely fibrillose, lacking a gelatinous pellicle.

Color is bright orange to reddish-orange or orange-brown, often with a slightly darker disc. The margin is initially inrolled, later decurved, and sometimes slightly appendiculate with partial veil remnants in young specimens.

### Lamellae

Gills are adnate to adnexed, close to subdistant, with 2–4 tiers of lamellulae. Color is yellow-orange to rusty-orange in youth, becoming cinnamon-brown to rusty-brown as spores mature.

Gill edges are often irregular and slightly fimbriate, a characteristic trait of the genus. The rusty-brown spore print distinguishes *Gymnopilus* from psilocybian *Psilocybe* species.

### Stipe

Stipe is 50–150 mm long and 8–20 mm thick, central, cylindrical to slightly clavate. Surface is dry, fibrillose, and colored similarly to the pileus or slightly paler.

A membranous partial veil is present in young specimens, leaving a superior annular zone or evanescent ring. The stipe base often develops pronounced greenish-blue bruising when handled or damaged.

### Spores

Spore print is rusty-brown to ferruginous. Spores are elliptical to amygdaliform, with moderately thick walls, smooth or finely roughened.

Spore dimensions: (7)8–10(11) × (4.5)5–6.5(7) µm. Q range: 1.4–1.8. Basidia are 4-spored, clavate. Cheilocystidia are ventricose to cylindrical with narrow necks.

## Distribution and Habitat

*G. earlei* occurs in tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas, from the Caribbean (Jamaica, Cuba, Puerto Rico) through Central America and into northern South America. It has also been reported from the Gulf Coast region of the southeastern United States (Florida, Louisiana, Texas).

The species is a saprotrophic wood decomposer, fruiting on decaying hardwood logs, stumps, and fallen trunks. Preferred substrates include tropical hardwoods such as mahogany, ceiba, and various leguminous trees.

Fruiting occurs during warm, wet periods, typically from late spring through autumn in tropical climates, with peak abundance during the rainy season. Fruiting can occur year-round in consistently warm, humid environments.

## Chemistry

### Bioactive Compounds

- **Psilocybin:** Present in measurable concentrations, though variable between collections
- **Psilocin:** Present in fresh tissue; responsible for the bluing reaction
- **Baeocystin:** Minor constituent
- **Gymnopiline:** Characteristic bis-naphthoquinone pigment of the genus (non-psychoactive)

The total psilocybin content of *G. earlei* is reported as low to moderate compared to [[gymnopilus-spectabilis]], with significant variability between geographic populations and individual collections.

### Pigments

The genus *Gymnopilus* produces distinctive orange and yellow pigments derived from bis-naphthoquinone compounds, including gymnopiline, gymnophilin, and related derivatives. These pigments are responsible for the characteristic orange coloration of the fruiting body.

## Effects

Psychoactive effects are consistent with other psilocybin-containing species: visual distortion, altered perception, euphoria, and introspective cognitive effects.

The species is rarely consumed due to its rarity and the prevalence of better-known alternatives. Onset occurs within 30–60 minutes of ingestion, with effects lasting 4–6 hours.

⚠️ **Caution:** Correct identification requires microscopic examination. *Gymnopilus* species as a whole are poorly known to most foragers, and misidentification is a significant risk.

## Ecological Role

As a primary wood decomposer, *G. earlei* contributes to nutrient cycling in tropical forest ecosystems by breaking down lignin and cellulose in dead hardwood.

The species likely serves as a food source for various invertebrates and may form associations with wood-boring insects. Its ecological interactions in tropical ecosystems remain understudied.
