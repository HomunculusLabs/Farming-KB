## Overview

**Parasola plicatilis** (Curtis) Redhead, Vilgalys & Moncalvo, commonly known as the **pleated inkcap** or **little Japanese umbrella**, is a small, delicate agaric fungus in the family Psathyrellaceae. It is one of the most widespread coprinoid mushrooms in temperate regions worldwide.

The species is immediately recognized by its deeply plicate (radially grooved) cap that expands to a shallow parasol shape before dissolving into ink-like deliquescence at maturity. Unlike many inkcaps, it is not associated with dung but grows on bare soil, grasslands, and disturbed ground.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

*Kingdom*: Fungi → *Division*: Basidiomycota → *Class*: Agaricomycetes → *Order*: Agaricales → *Family*: Psathyrellaceae → *Genus*: **Parasola**

The species was originally described as *Agaricus plicatilis* by Curtis in 1788 and later transferred to *Coprinus*. Redhead, Vilgalys, and Moncalvo established the genus *Parasola* in 2001 based on molecular phylogenetic analysis, moving this species along with several others out of *Coprinus* sensu lato.

The epithet *plicatilis* derives from Latin *plicare* (to fold), referring to the deeply grooved cap surface.

### Morphology

**Cap**: 1–3 cm diameter when expanded, ovoid when young, becoming broadly convex to plane with a pronounced umbo. Surface is deeply plicate with 16–24 radial grooves extending nearly to the center.

Cap color is grey-brown to fuscous, paler at the margin. The margin extends slightly beyond the gills and is prominently radially wrinkled. Flesh is extremely thin, less than 1 mm, membranous and fragile.

**Gills**: Free to subfree, crowded, broad. Color is grey initially, becoming black and deliquescent (auto-digesting) from the margin inward. Spore print is black.

**Stipe**: 3–7 cm tall × 1–2 mm diameter, cylindrical, hollow, fragile. Surface is white to pale grey, silky-fibrillose, with a slightly swollen base. No ring or volva present.

**Spores**: 10–14 × 7–9 µm, ellipsoid to lemon-shaped in face view, with a central germ pore and a prominent apical hilar appendage. Spore walls are thickened and dark brown in deposit.

Microscopic features include cheilocystidia that are utriform (flask-shaped) to lageniform, and pileipellis composed of a cutis of cylindrical hyphae with intracellular pigments.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Parasola plicatilis* has a cosmopolitan distribution across temperate and subtropical regions. It is reported from Europe, [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], South America, Asia, Africa, and Australasia.

The species fruits from spring through autumn, with peak fruiting in late summer and early autumn in temperate zones. In mild climates it may fruit year-round following rainfall.

Fruiting occurs on bare or sparsely vegetated soil, often in lawns, gardens, pastures, roadbanks, and disturbed ground. It is particularly common on compacted, nutrient-poor soils and does not show a strong preference for dung or woody substrates.

Unlike many coprinoid species, *P. plicatilis* can appear in large troops, with dozens to hundreds of fruiting bodies arising simultaneously from the same area after rain. Individual fruiting bodies are extremely short-lived, typically persisting for only 6–12 hours before complete deliquescence.

## Ecology

*Parasola plicatilis* is a saprotrophic fungus that decomposes [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] in soil. It plays a minor role in [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] in grassland and disturbed-ground ecosystems.

The species is a primary colonizer of disturbed soils, often appearing within days of soil disturbance or after the first significant rainfall of a season. Its rapid life cycle — from primordium to spore release in under 24 hours — allows it to exploit brief windows of favorable moisture conditions.

[[aquatic-fungi-spore-dispersal-ingold]] occurs primarily through the deliquescing gill mechanism. As the cap and gills liquefy, a black, spore-laden fluid drips from the cap margins, depositing spores onto the soil surface below.

The fungus is occasionally parasitized by *Volvariella surrecta*, a rare bolete parasite that fruits directly from the decaying inkcap. This relationship is one of the best-documented examples of [[symbiotic-fungi-fungal-parasitism-and-pathogenicity]] in Agaricales.

## Edibility

*Parasola plicatilis* is generally considered edible but is not regarded as a worthwhile edible species due to its extremely small size and thin, insubstantial flesh. The entire fruiting body typically weighs less than 1 gram fresh.

Some field guides list it as inedible or caution against consumption, not due to toxicity but because of the difficulty of positive identification among the many similar *Parasola* species. The genus contains several lookalikes, though none are known to be dangerously toxic.

The species is not cultivated commercially. Its rapid deliquescence makes it impractical for culinary use, as fruiting bodies must be harvested and cooked within hours of emergence.

## Related Species

*Parasola plicatilis* is part of a complex of morphologically similar species that were historically lumped under *Coprinus plicatilis* sensu lato. Molecular studies by Uljé (2005) and others have resolved several cryptic taxa:

- **Parasola lactea** — cap smooth, not plicate; grows on dung
- **Parasola hemerobia** — larger spores (13–17 µm); grows on wood chips
- **Parasola leiocephala** — cap surface granulose when young; broader spores
- **Parasola auricoma** — orange-brown cap coloration; larger fruiting body

*Parasola plicatilis* is distinguished from these by its deeply plicate cap, relatively small spores (10–14 µm), and non-lignicolous habitat. [[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] of spore size and cheilocystidia morphology is typically required for certain identification.

## Cultivation Notes

*Parasola plicatilis* is not commercially cultivated due to its minute size, fragile constitution, and rapid autodeliquescence. It has no recognized culinary or economic value as a cultivated species.

Laboratory fruiting has been achieved on sterilized soil and compost-based substrates under controlled humidity and temperature. Colonization is rapid, typically completing within 7–10 days, but fruiting requires very high ambient humidity (95%+) and occurs unpredictably.
