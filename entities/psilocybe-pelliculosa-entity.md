# Psilocybe pelliculosa

Psilocybe pelliculosa is a small, psilocybin-containing mushroom found in the coniferous forests of the Pacific Northwest of North America. It is a common member of the section Semilanceatae, often encountered in the autumn months growing on forest debris, wood chips, and along trails. Despite being widespread and locally abundant, its small size, modest potency, and lack of prominent blue bruising make it one of the less conspicuous psychoactive species in the region.

Gaston-Guzman|Guzmán confirmed its placement in section Semilanceatae in his comprehensive monograph of the genus Psilocybe, noting its affinity with other bluing and non-bluing members of this section from temperate regions.

## Taxonomic History

Psilocybe pelliculosa was first described by American mycologist Alexander H. Smith in 1946 from collections made in Washington state. The specific epithet "pelliculosa" refers to the thin, gelatinous pellicle (skin) on the cap surface that can be peeled off, a characteristic feature of many species in section Semilanceatae.

The species was later transferred by Singer and Smith to its current combination as *Psilocybe pelliculosa* (Smith) Singer & Smith, though the original Smith combination remains the most commonly cited authority in North American literature.

For many years, P. pelliculosa was confused with [[psilocybe-silvatica]], and the two were often treated as synonymous in field guides and checklists. Gaston-Guzman|Guzmán eventually recognized them as distinct species based on differences in spore size, cheilocystidia morphology, and ecological preferences. P. pelliculosa has slightly smaller spores and is restricted to coniferous forest habitats, while P. silvatica favors hardwood debris and has larger, broader spores.

This taxonomic clarification was important for accurate field identification and chemical analysis of Pacific Northwest mycoflora.

In 1961, V.E. Tyler became the first investigator to report the detection of [[psilocybin]] in P. pelliculosa, establishing it as one of the earliest confirmed psychoactive mushrooms of the Pacific Northwest. This finding predated the discovery of psilocybin in [[psilocybe-baeocystis-profile]] by one year and helped draw scientific attention to the region's diverse assemblage of psychoactive fungi.

## Description

### Macroscopic Features

#### Cap (Pileus)
The pileus is 10 to 30 mm in diameter, conic to broadly conic or campanulate when young, expanding to convex or broadly convex with age, sometimes retaining a slight umbo. The surface is smooth and covered with a thin, gelatinous pellicle that gives it a silky sheen when dry.

It is strongly hygrophanous: dark reddish brown to chestnut brown when moist, fading to ochraceous tan or straw yellow when dry. The margin is translucently striate when moist, showing the gill outlines. The cap flesh is thin and fragile, whitish to pale brown, and does not change color significantly when cut or bruised. Blue bruising is usually absent or very faint.

#### Gills (Lamellae)
Lamellae are adnate to slightly adnexed, subclose to close, with several tiers of lamellulae (20–30 per gill). They are pale grayish at first, becoming dark purplish brown with spore maturity. Gill edges are whitish and finely fringed with cheilocystidia, giving them a slightly fimbriate appearance.

The lamellulae are of mixed lengths, creating an irregular gill pattern characteristic of the species. Gill attachment is sometimes described as subdecurrent near the stipe in mature specimens.

#### Stipe (Stem)
The stipe measures 40 to 100 mm in length and 1 to 2.5 mm thick. It is cylindrical, equal, hollow, and fragile. The surface is smooth to slightly pruinose (frosted) near the apex, with a whitish to pale brown coloration that may darken slightly toward the base.

A cortinate partial veil is present in young specimens but is evanescent, leaving at most a faint fibrillose annular zone that is easily overlooked. Blue bruising on the stipe is minimal or absent. The stipe base may be slightly covered in white mycelial threads. The stipe context is fibrous and tough for its diameter, often bending without breaking cleanly.

#### Odor and Taste
Odor is mild to slightly farinaceous (mealy or flour-like). Taste is not distinctive. There are no reports of strongly aromatic or unpleasant odors associated with fresh specimens.

### Microscopic Features

Basidiospores are (8)9 to 12(13) × 6 to 7.5(8) μm, ellipsoid to slightly amygdaliform in face view, with moderately thick walls (approximately 0.5–0.8 μm) and a distinct germ pore. Spores are smooth and thick-walled, with a dark purplish brown spore print. Q (length/width ratio) ranges from 1.3 to 1.7.

Cheilocystidia are lageniform to ventricose with narrow necks, 18 to 28 × 4 to 7 μm, forming a conspicuous sterile band on gill edges. Pleurocystidia are absent or very rare, which is an important diagnostic feature distinguishing P. pelliculosa from some similar species.

Basidia are four-spored, clavate, 16 to 22 μm long. Pileipellis is a cutis of narrow, gelatinous hyphae characteristic of section Semilanceatae, with individual hyphae 1.5 to 3.5 μm wide. Clamp connections are present on hyphae. Subhymenial tissue is loosely interwoven.

## Distribution and Habitat

Psilocybe pelliculosa is widely distributed in the Pacific Northwest of North America, ranging from coastal and insular British Columbia (including Vancouver Island) through Washington, Oregon, and into northern California. Inland reports extend to Idaho and western Montana.

Specific well-documented locations include the Olympic Peninsula, the Cascade Range foothills, the Puget Sound lowlands, the Willamette Valley, and coastal forests from Tillamook to Coos Bay. Elevations range from near sea level to approximately 1,200 meters, with greatest abundance in lowland and montane coniferous forests.

The species fruits abundantly in the autumn, typically from September through November, with peak fruiting occurring after the first significant autumn rains when temperatures have dropped and humidity is high. It is one of several wood chip-dwelling species reported from the Pacific Northwest, along with [[psilocybe-stuntzii-profile]] and [[psilocybe-baeocystis-profile]]. Fruiting typically ceases with the first hard frosts of late November or early December.

It is found in coniferous forests, particularly those dominated by Douglas fir (*Pseudotsuga menziesii*), western hemlock (*Tsuga heterophylla*), western redcedar (*Thuja plicata*), and Sitka spruce (*Picea sitchensis*). The mushrooms grow on decaying conifer needles, twigs, forest duff, woody debris, sawdust, and decaying pine cones.

They are commonly encountered along forest trails, roadcuts, in clearings, and in landscaping areas where wood chip mulch has been applied. They often fruit in large troops or scattered groups, sometimes forming extensive carpets across suitable habitat.

The species appears to be adapted to the cool, wet maritime climate of the Pacific Northwest and is less common in areas with continental climates. Its requirement for coniferous forest habitats and specific moisture conditions may limit its potential distribution compared to more generalist species like [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]].

## Chemistry and Active Compounds

Psilocybe pelliculosa contains [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]], though at relatively low concentrations compared to more potent species in the genus. [[baeocystin]] has also been detected in trace amounts, consistent with the chemical profile of other members of section Semilanceatae.

Beug and Bigwood furnished analytical proof that P. pelliculosa is weaker in its psychotropic effects than comparable species. According to Jochen-Gartz|Gartz, P. pelliculosa contains approximately 30–50% of the amount of [[psilocybin]] found in [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-cyanescens-species-guide]], a common and potent species across the Pacific Northwest.

Total psilocybin content ranges from approximately 0.05% to 0.2% of dry weight, depending on collection location, age of specimens, and analytical methodology. This is substantially less than [[psilocybe-semilanceata]], which can reach 1% or more total psilocybin by dry weight.

Variability in alkaloid content has been noted between collections, with specimens from more northern latitudes and higher elevations sometimes showing slightly elevated psilocybin concentrations. The psilocin content is typically very low or undetectable in dried specimens, as this compound degrades more rapidly than psilocybin during storage and drying.

The relatively low potency of P. pelliculosa, combined with its lack of prominent blue bruising, may partly explain why it has not attracted the same level of attention as its more potent relatives. V.E. Tyler's 1961 detection of psilocybin in this species nonetheless marked an important milestone in the chemical study of North American psychoactive fungi.

## Similar Species

Accurate identification of Psilocybe pelliculosa requires attention to several similar species that share its forest habitat and general appearance:

- [[psilocybe-silvatica]] — Very similar appearance but with larger spores (12 to 16 μm long) and found primarily on decaying hardwood logs and debris rather than conifer needles. Formerly considered conspecific; distinguished by cheilocystidia shape and spore dimensions.
- [[psilocybe-semilanceata]] — The liberty cap; macroscopically extremely difficult to distinguish from P. pelliculosa, but larger overall with a more pronounced papilla, found in grasslands and pastures rather than forest duff. More potent and often more strongly bluing.
- [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-cyanescens-species-guide]] — Much larger and more robust with a strongly undulating cap margin and intense blue bruising; fruits in wood chip mulch in gardens and parks. Contains roughly 2–3 times the psilocybin of P. pelliculosa.
- [[psilocybe-stuntzii-profile]] — More robust with a persistent annulus; favors mulched beds and disturbed areas rather than undisturbed forest duff.
- [[psilocybe-baeocystis-profile]] — Found inland on wood debris and lawns; a strongly bluing species with a distinctly shaped cap and higher potency.
- [[psilocybe-montana]] — Small, non-bluing species found in similar habitats but lacking psychoactive compounds entirely.

P. pelliculosa is distinguished by the combination of its small size, coniferous forest habitat, thin gelatinous pellicle, absence of significant blue bruising, adnate gills with lageniform cheilocystidia, and absent pleurocystidia.

## Field Identification Tips

- **Look for the pellicle:** Fresh caps have a translucent, gelatinous skin that can be carefully peeled with a needle — a hallmark of section Semilanceatae.
- **Check the habitat:** P. pelliculosa grows on conifer duff, sawdust, and wood chips in forested settings, not in open grasslands. Finding it on a forest trail among Douglas fir needles is a strong indicator.
- **Bruising is unreliable:** Unlike many Psilocybe species, P. pelliculosa typically does not blue significantly. Do not rely on blue bruising as a primary identification character for this species.
- **Size matters:** The small cap (rarely exceeding 30 mm) and slender, fragile stipe help separate it from more robust species like P. cyanescens or P. baeocystis.
- **Spore print:** A dark purplish brown spore print confirms placement in Psilocybe, but microscopic examination is needed to separate it from close lookalikes.
- **Season:** Autumn fruiting after first rains; rarely seen in spring. Peak abundance in October and November in the Pacific Northwest.
- **Growth pattern:** Often fruits in large scattered troops rather than isolated individuals; this gregarious habit is characteristic.
- **Veil remnant:** Check carefully for a faint fibrillose annular zone near the stipe apex — it is present but extremely subtle and easily missed.

## Foraging Notes

P. pelliculosa is generally considered a low-priority target for foragers due to its modest potency, small size, and the large quantities needed to produce noticeable effects. However, it can be remarkably abundant in suitable habitat, sometimes fruiting in extensive carpets that make collection in bulk feasible. Its tendency to grow on trail sides and roadcuts makes it accessible to casual foragers.

Care should be taken to avoid confusion with non-psychoactive Galerina species, some of which contain deadly amatoxins and fruit in similar forest habitats. The dark purplish brown spore print of P. pelliculosa contrasts with the rusty brown spore print of most dangerous Galerina species, making spore printing an essential safety step. Always make a spore print before consuming any foraged mushroom.

Due to the low potency, foragers seeking this species should expect to collect large numbers of specimens for effective dosing. Drying and careful storage are recommended to preserve the limited psilocybin content.

## Ecological Role

As a saprotrophic fungus, Psilocybe pelliculosa plays a role in decomposing coniferous forest litter. It contributes to nutrient cycling by breaking down needles, twigs, and other organic matter on the forest floor. The species is one of many small litter-decomposing mushrooms that form an essential component of the Pacific Northwest forest mycoflora. Its fruiting in large troops suggests it may be a significant contributor to the autumn fungal biomass in coniferous forests.

## Conservation Status

Psilocybe pelliculosa is common and widespread within its range and faces no known conservation threats. The coniferous forests it inhabits are extensive, though logging and habitat fragmentation could potentially impact local populations. The species has not been formally evaluated for conservation status but is considered secure due to its abundance and wide distribution.
