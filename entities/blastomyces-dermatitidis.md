# Blastomyces dermatitidis

**Blastomyces dermatitidis** is a thermally dimorphic fungal pathogen that causes blastomycosis, also known as Gilchrist's disease or North American blastomycosis. It is one of the endemic dimorphic fungi, capable of existing as a mold in the environment and converting to a pathogenic yeast form at mammalian body temperature. The fungus is a significant cause of systemic mycosis in parts of North America, particularly the Mississippi and Ohio River valleys, and the Great Lakes region.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Eurotiomycetes
- **Order:** Onygenales
- **Family:** Ajellomycetaceae
- **Genus:** Blastomyces

The teleomorph (sexual) state was long unknown but was eventually identified as *Ajellomyces dermatitidis*. Phylogenetic studies have confirmed that *Blastomyces* is closely related to *Histoplasma capsulatum* and *Paracoccidioides brasiliensis*, forming a monophyletic clade of dimorphic fungal pathogens within the Onygenales.

## Dimorphic Life Cycle

The ability to switch between two morphological forms is central to the pathogenicity of *B. dermatitidis*:

### Mold Phase (Environmental Form, 25°C)
- **Growth:** Filamentous growth on Sabouraud dextrose [[agar]] and other fungal media at room temperature
- **Morphology:** White to tan, cottony colonies. Microscopically, hyphae produce small, single-celled conidia (2 to 5 μm) on short conidiophores. These conidia are the infectious particles.
- **Habitat:** Found in moist soil rich in organic debris, particularly near waterways, in wooded areas, and in animal barns. The fungus thrives in acidic soils with high organic content.

### Yeast Phase (Pathogenic Form, 37°C)
- **Growth:** Budding yeast cells in host tissue or when cultured on enriched media at body temperature
- **Morphology:** Thick-walled, broadly based budding yeast cells, 8 to 15 μm in diameter. The characteristic broad-based bud (with a wide attachment to the mother cell) is the diagnostic feature distinguishing *Blastomyces* from other yeasts.
- **Cell Wall:** The yeast form cell wall contains alpha-1,3-glucan as a major component, which is critical for virulence. Alpha-1,3-glucan helps mask beta-glucan from host immune recognition by dectin-1 receptors.

## Epidemiology

### Geographic Distribution
Blastomycosis is endemic in several regions:

- **North America:** The Mississippi River Valley, Ohio River Valley, and the Great Lakes region (particularly Wisconsin, Minnesota, and areas around the Great Lakes). Endemic areas extend into parts of Canada (Ontario, Manitoba) and the southeastern United States.
- **Africa:** Sporadic cases reported from sub-Saharan Africa
- **Rare Elsewhere:** Occasional cases in India, the Middle East, and Central/South America

### Transmission
- **Inhalation:** Infection occurs primarily through inhalation of aerosolized conidia from disturbed soil or decaying organic matter
- **Environmental Disturbance:** Outbreaks have been associated with activities that disturb contaminated soil, such as construction, excavation, hunting, and recreational activities near waterways
- **Zoonotic Potential:** Dogs are highly susceptible to blastomycosis and can serve as sentinels for human exposure

## Pathogenesis

The pathogenic mechanism of *B. dermatitidis* involves multiple virulence factors:

1. **Thermal Dimorphism:** The temperature-dependent switch to yeast form is essential for establishing infection
2. **WI-1 Adhesin:** A 120 kDa cell surface protein (also known as BAD-1) that functions as both an adhesin and an immune evasion molecule. WI-1 binds to complement receptors on macrophages and neutrophils, facilitating uptake while simultaneously suppressing the oxidative burst response
3. **Alpha-1,3-Glucan:** Masks pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) from innate immune detection, particularly hiding beta-glucan from dectin-1 recognition
4. **Melanin:** Production of melanin in conidia may provide resistance to environmental stresses and UV radiation
5. **Immune Evasion:** The yeast form is resistant to killing by alveolar macrophages and can survive and replicate within phagocytic cells

## Clinical Manifestations

Blastomycosis can present as a spectrum of disease:

- **Pulmonary Blastomycosis:** The most common form, ranging from asymptomatic infection to acute or chronic pneumonia. Symptoms include fever, cough, chest pain, weight loss, and night sweats. Can mimic tuberculosis or lung cancer on radiographic imaging
- **Disseminated Blastomycosis:** Hematogenous spread from the lungs to skin, bone, genitourinary system, and central nervous system. Skin lesions (verrucous or ulcerative) are the most common extrapulmonary manifestation
- **CNS Blastomycosis:** Meningitis or brain abscess, carries a poor prognosis
- **Osteomyelitis:** Lytic bone lesions, particularly in vertebrae and long bones

## Diagnosis

Diagnostic approaches include:

- **Microscopy:** Direct examination of sputum, tissue, or exudate showing characteristic broad-based budding yeast cells (KOH preparation)
- **Culture:** Growth of the mold phase on fungal media at 25°C, with conversion to yeast form at 37°C
- **Antigen Detection:** Urine antigen testing by enzyme immunoassay (EIA) is the most sensitive non-invasive test, though cross-reactivity with *Histoplasma* antigens occurs
- **Serology:** Antibody detection by complement fixation and immunodiffusion, though sensitivity is limited
- **Histopathology:** Granulomatous inflammation with suppurative features; Gomori methenamine silver (GMS) stain highlights the yeast cells
- **Molecular Detection:** PCR-based methods for direct detection from clinical specimens are increasingly available

## Treatment

Antifungal therapy for blastomycosis depends on disease severity:

- **Mild to Moderate Pulmonary Disease:** Itraconazole (200 to 400 mg daily for 6 to 12 months) is the treatment of choice
- **Severe or Disseminated Disease:** Amphotericin B (lipid formulation preferred) initially, followed by step-down to itraconazole
- **CNS Blastomycosis:** Lipid amphotericin B followed by an azole with good CNS penetration (fluconazole or voriconazole)
- **Lifelong Suppressive Therapy:** May be needed for immunocompromised patients

## Research Directions

Active areas of research include:

- **Vaccine Development:** No licensed vaccine exists, though experimental vaccines targeting WI-1 and other surface antigens have shown promise in animal models
- **Genomics:** The *B. dermatitidis* genome has been sequenced, providing insights into dimorphism regulation and virulence gene expression
- **Environmental Detection:** Improved methods for detecting *Blastomyces* in soil and water, including PCR-based environmental surveillance
- **Immune Response:** Characterization of the host immune response, particularly the role of T-helper cell subsets and cytokine profiles in determining disease outcomes

## Research History

The study of blastomycosis spans over a century of medical mycology:

- **1894:** Thomas Casper Gilchrist first describes the organism causing the disease now bearing his name, based on a case of disseminated cutaneous infection in Baltimore, Maryland
- **1896:** Gilchrist and William Stokes publish a detailed account of the disease, establishing blastomycosis as a distinct clinical entity
- **1921:** The dimorphic nature of the organism is demonstrated, showing that the yeast form in tissue and the mold form in culture represent the same species
- **1968:** The first reliable skin test antigen for blastomycosis is developed, aiding in epidemiological studies
- **1980s:** Improved culture media and techniques lead to increased isolation rates from environmental samples
- **2000s:** Urine antigen testing by EIA revolutionizes diagnosis, providing a sensitive non-invasive method. The assay is initially developed for histoplasmosis but cross-reacts with *Blastomyces* antigens
- **2011:** Phylogenetic analysis reveals that North American isolates previously classified as *B. dermatitidis* actually comprise two species: *B. dermatitidis* (clade largely restricted to western North America) and *B. gilchristii* (associated with eastern North America and the Great Lakes region)
- **2010s-2020s:** Whole-genome sequencing of clinical and environmental isolates elucidates population structure, virulence determinants, and transmission patterns

## Biochemical Compounds and Virulence Factors

Key biochemical features underlying *B. dermatitidis* pathogenicity include:

- **Alpha-1,3-Glucan:** The dominant cell wall polysaccharide of the yeast form, constituting up to 40% of cell wall dry weight. It masks underlying beta-1,3-glucan from detection by host dectin-1 receptors, enabling immune evasion
- **WI-1 (BAD-1):** A 120 kDa surface adhesin and immune modulator. Binds complement receptor 3 (CR3) on macrophages, facilitating phagocytosis while simultaneously suppressing the respiratory burst. Deletion of the BAD-1 gene abolishes virulence in murine models
- **Melanin:** Produced via the DHN-melanin pathway, melanin deposition in conidial cell walls provides resistance to UV radiation, enzymatic degradation, and oxidative killing by host phagocytes
- **Ergosterol and Sterols:** Standard ergosterol-based cell membrane composition makes the fungus susceptible to echinocandin, azole, and polyene antifungal agents that target the fungal sterol pathway
