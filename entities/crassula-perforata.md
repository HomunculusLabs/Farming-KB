## Overview

**Crassula perforata** Thunb., commonly called string of buttons or necklace vine, is a succulent subshrub in the family Crassulaceae. It is native to the Western Cape and Eastern Cape of South Africa.

The species is named for its distinctive leaf arrangement, where pairs of triangular leaves appear to be strung along the stem like beads. Each leaf pair is rotated 90° from the previous pair, creating a stacked, perforated appearance.

Plants form spreading to trailing stems 30–60 cm long, making them effective ground covers and hanging basket subjects. The species is widely grown as a houseplant and garden ornamental.

The foliage displays gray-green leaves with reddish margins, intensifying in bright light. This color variation adds ornamental value to container plantings.

It is one of the most commonly cultivated Crassula species, valued for its ease of growth and distinctive geometric foliage. Specimens are readily available from most succulent nurseries.

The species received the Royal Horticultural Society Award of Garden Merit. This recognition confirms its reliability as a garden and container plant.

Several horticultural cultivars have been selected for enhanced color and form. 'Variegata' with cream-edged leaves is the most widely grown selection.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Crassula perforata was described by Carl Peter Thunberg in 1778. The species epithet "perforata" refers to the perforated or pierced appearance of the stems.

The species belongs to the section *Globulea* within *Crassula*, characterized by fused leaf bases that clasp the stem. Close relatives include *Crassula rupestris* and *Crassula brevifolia*.

Stems are succulent, erect at first then becoming trailing or decumbent, reaching 30–60 cm. Older stems become woody at the base with peeling bark.

Leaves are opposite, triangular to ovate, 1–2.5 cm long and 0.8–1.5 cm wide. Leaf bases are fused around the stem, creating the characteristic perforated look.

Leaf surfaces are gray-green with a powdery bloom, and margins develop red edges under strong light. The leaves are smooth to slightly papillate.

Flowers are small, tubular, cream to pale pink, borne in terminal thyrses. Each inflorescence bears 10–30 flowers on a short peduncle.

Flowering occurs in late spring to summer (November–January in the southern hemisphere). Flowers are mildly fragrant and attract small flies and bees.

The fruit is a small follicle containing numerous dust-like seeds. Seed production is modest compared to other Crassula species in the wild.

Hybridization with *Crassula rupestris* occurs in habitat where their ranges overlap. These natural hybrids display intermediate leaf shapes and growth habits.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is endemic to South Africa, occurring from the Western Cape eastward through the Eastern Cape. Its range follows the Cape Floristic Region and Albany Thicket biome.

It grows on rocky slopes, in dry riverbeds, and among thicket vegetation at elevations of 100–800 m. Plants are frequently found growing in the partial shade of larger shrubs.

The native climate is semi-arid with winter rainfall of 200–500 mm annually. Summer temperatures reach 30–35 °C, while winter minima rarely drop below 2 °C.

Populations are locally common but not invasive. The species is not considered threatened, as much of its range falls within protected areas.

It co-occurs with other succulent shrublets including *Crassula ovata*, *Portulacaria afra*, and various *Aloe* species. The mixed succulent thicket community supports high biodiversity.

## Cultivation

Crassula perforata is straightforward to grow in bright, indirect light with some direct morning sun. It tolerates a range of soil conditions provided drainage is excellent.

Use a well-draining succulent mix with added perlite or pumice. Standard commercial cactus mix works well when amended with 30% additional grit.

Water when the soil is dry to the touch during spring and autumn. Reduce watering in summer and winter when growth slows.

The species tolerates temperatures down to approximately −2 °C for brief periods if kept dry. In cold-winter climates, grow as a houseplant or in containers.

Propagation is by stem cuttings, which root within 1–2 weeks. Individual leaf cuttings also root but produce plants more slowly.

Pinch growing tips to encourage branching and maintain a bushy form. Unpruned plants become leggy with bare lower stems over time.

Plants benefit from a light application of diluted balanced fertilizer in spring. Avoid over-fertilizing, which produces soft, etiolated growth.

Repot every 2–3 years to refresh the growing medium. Crassula perforata is relatively pest-resistant but may attract scale insects on older stems.

## Ecological Role

In its native thicket habitat, Crassula perforata forms part of the succulent understory layer. It contributes to ground cover and soil stabilization on rocky slopes.

The small flowers attract generalist pollinators including hoverflies and small bees. Flowering coincides with the warmer months when insect activity peaks.

CAM photosynthesis enables efficient water use in the seasonally dry thicket environment. The succulent leaves store water for extended dry periods.

The species is relatively pest-free in habitat due to its tough, leathery leaf texture. In cultivation, mealybugs may colonize leaf axils and stem joints.

Birds occasionally use the dense growth for nesting material and shelter. The trailing stems provide cover for small ground-dwelling invertebrates.

## Uses and Ethnobotany

Crassula perforata is grown exclusively as an ornamental plant. It has no documented traditional medicinal or culinary uses.

The species is popular in container gardens, rock gardens, and succulent landscapes. Its trailing habit makes it especially effective in hanging baskets and retaining wall plantings.

It is frequently used in living walls and green roof installations due to its drought tolerance. The shallow root system adapts well to thin substrate layers.

Several cultivars exist, including 'Variegata' with cream-striped leaves and 'Giant' with larger, more robust foliage. These are propagated vegetatively by specialty nurseries.

The Royal Horticultural Society has noted the species for its garden merit. It performs reliably in Mediterranean-climate gardens worldwide.
