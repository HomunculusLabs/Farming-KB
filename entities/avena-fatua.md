## Overview

**Avena fatua** is an annual grass in the family Poaceae, commonly known as wild oat.

It is native to the Mediterranean region and western Asia but has become one of the most widespread and economically damaging agricultural weeds in temperate cereal-growing regions worldwide.

Wild oat infests cereal crops including wheat, barley, oats, and canola, competing aggressively for water, nutrients, and light.

The species is closely related to cultivated oat (*Avena sativa*) and is considered one of its primary progenitors.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Avena fatua belongs to the tribe Aveneae within the subfamily Pooideae.

The species was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in *Species Plantarum*.

The specific epithet *fatua* means "foolish" or "insipid" in Latin, likely referring to its occurrence as an unwanted weed in cultivated fields.

Wild oat is a hexaploid with 2n = 6x = 42 chromosomes, sharing the same ploidy level as cultivated oat.

The genus *Avena* includes both the cultivated oat and several weedy species that hybridize readily.

The plant grows 60 to 150 centimeters tall with erect, hollow culms arising from a fibrous root system.

Leaf blades are 10 to 40 centimeters long and 4 to 12 millimeters wide, with a prominent midrib and hairless or sparsely hairy margins.

The ligule is a conspicuous white membrane 4 to 8 millimeters long.

The inflorescence is a large, open panicle 15 to 40 centimeters long, with spreading branches bearing pendulous spikelets.

Each spikelet contains two or three florets, the lower one or two sterile and reduced, the uppermost fertile.

The characteristic feature distinguishing wild oat from cultivated oat is the disarticulation scar at the base of the spikelet.

In wild oat, the spikelet separates from the panicle at maturity as a unit, while cultivated oat spikelets remain attached to the rachis.

The fertile floret bears a long, twisted awn 20 to 40 millimeters arising from the lemma back.

Awns are geniculate, bent at a distinct knee, and dark brown to black at maturity.
