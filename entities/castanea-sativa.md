# Castanea sativa

**Type:** Species (Tree)
**Category:** Nut-bearing tree
**Family:** Fagaceae
**Also known as:** Spanish chestnut, [[holzer-sweet-chestnut-and-nut-trees]], European chestnut

## Overview
Castanea sativa is a large, spreading deciduous tree reaching up to
35 m tall, native to southern Europe and western Asia. It is closely
related to oaks and is one of the most important nut-producing trees
in temperate regions. The species has been cultivated for millennia
and can live for over 1,000 years, with some ancient coppice stools in
Europe still actively producing nuts and timber after many centuries of
management. Chestnuts were a staple carbohydrate food across southern
Europe before the introduction of potatoes.

## Taxonomy
- **Order:** Fagales
- **Family:** Fagaceae
- **Genus:** Castanea
- **Species:** C. sativa
- **Common relatives:** C. crenata (Japanese chestnut), C.
  mollissima (Chinese chestnut), C. dentata (American chestnut)

## Key Details
- **Growth rate:** Moderate
- **Height:** Up to 35 m, girth 1-3 m
- **Lifespan:** Extremely long lived (centuries to millennia)
- **[[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]]:** High once established
- **Soil preference:** Well-drained, light soils; fair lime tolerance;
  does not tolerate salinity
- **Nutritional value (fresh, per 100g):** Energy 194 cal,
  Carbohydrate 42.1 g, Fat 1.5 g, Protein 2.9 g, Potassium 454 mg,
  Phosphorus 88 mg, Vitamin B1 0.22 mg, Vitamin B2 0.22 mg
- **Nutritional value (dried, per 100g):** Energy 377 cal,
  Carbohydrate 78.6 g, Fat 4.1 g, Protein 6.7 g, Potassium 875 mg,
  Iron 3.3 mg, Phosphorus 162 mg
- **Digestion time:** 2.75 hours (fresh)
- **Yield:** Up to 5,000 kg per hectare from mature groves; 0.77
  t/ha minimal irrigation, 1.7 t/ha full irrigation by year 10
- **Timber:** Young timber is tough and durable; [[fungal-roles-in-old-growth-forests]]
  primarily used as firewood; coppices well for sustainable
  production; prized in Europe for traditional timber uses
- **Culinary:** Eaten raw, roasted, boiled, or preserved by drying
  and pickling; high carbohydrate content makes it a staple food
  crop rather than a supplementary nut

## Cultivation
Planted at densities of 56-70 trees per hectare. Prefers well-drained
soils and tolerates drought well once established. Does not tolerate
salinity. Propagation is by seed, grafting, or stooling. The
coppice system is traditional in many European regions, with stools
cut on 10-20 year rotations for both timber and fuel production.

## Related Species
- **C. crenata** (Japanese chestnut): Smaller tree, coppices well;
  developed strains with excellent nut crops
- **C. mollissima** (Chinese chestnut): Grows to 20 m, sweeter nut,
  much more resistant to root fungus Phytophthora spp than
  European chestnut
- **C. dentata** (American chestnut): Once a major deciduous tree
  of [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]]; destroyed by bark fungus (chestnut blight) in
  early 1900s; nuts said to be sweetest of any species

## Relevance
Sweet chestnut is a cornerstone species in European agroforestry and
a valuable addition to temperate food forests. Its ability to coppice
for centuries while producing both nuts and timber makes it one of
the most sustainable tree crops available. The high carbohydrate
content of dried chestnuts made them a staple food across southern
Europe for centuries, and they remain an important element of
traditional food systems.
