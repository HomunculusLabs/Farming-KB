## Description

**Steccherinum fimbriatum** (syn. *Steccherium fimbriatum*) is a cord-forming basidiomycete fungus that decomposes wood and organic matter in forest soils. It is notable for its ability to shift between distinct mycelial growth forms in response to environmental conditions, particularly arthropod grazing pressure. This morphological plasticity has made it an important model for studying how soil fauna influence fungal growth strategies.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Eukaryota
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Polyporales
- **Family:** Meruliaceae
- **Genus:** *Steccherinum*
- **Species:** *S. fimbriatum*

## Key Facts

### Growth Form Plasticity

Dowson et al. (1988) demonstrated that *S. fimbriatum* exhibits dramatic shifts in mycelial morphology in response to arthropod grazing. In ungrazed conditions, it forms a slow, dense mycelium. Under arthropod grazing, it shifts to a fast-growing diffuse mycelium that allows the fungus to escape grazing pressure by rapidly colonizing new areas.

### Cord-Forming Ability

*S. fimbriatum* belongs to a group of saprotrophic basidiomycetes that form mycelial cords — aggregated bundles of hyphae that function as transport networks for nutrients and carbon, exploration structures extending through soil to locate new resources, and persistence structures more resistant to environmental stress than individual hyphae.

### Ecological Role

- Decomposes woody debris and leaf litter in forest ecosystems
- Contributes to nutrient cycling by translocating phosphorus and nitrogen through cord systems
- Provides food for soil microarthropods (collembola, mites)
- Interacts competitively with other cord-forming and non-cord-forming decomposer fungi
- Can persist in soil for extended periods due to the durability of cord structures

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Mycelial Morphology and Cultivation

The growth form plasticity has practical implications:

1. **Substrate colonization:** Understanding what triggers diffuse vs. dense growth can help optimize colonization rates
2. **Yield optimization:** Dense mycelial forms may be more productive for fruiting, while diffuse forms are better for rapid substrate colonization
3. **Pest interactions:** The morphological response to arthropod grazing suggests soil fauna in cultivation systems could alter mycelial growth patterns

### Research Significance

*S. fimbriatum* is important for fundamental mycological research:

- **Morphological plasticity:** Demonstrates that fungal growth form is not fixed but responds to biotic conditions
- **Indirect grazing effects:** Shows how faunal grazing indirectly alters ecosystem processes by changing fungal morphology
- **Cord system dynamics:** Provides insights into how [[mycelial-networks]] transport nutrients across heterogeneous soil environments
- **Foraging ecology:** Illustrates the trade-offs between exploration and exploitation in fungal foraging strategies

### Phosphorus Translocation

Cord-forming fungi like *S. fimbriatum* are important for phosphorus cycling:

- Mycelial cords can translocate phosphorus from nutrient-rich patches to growing margins
- Wells and Boddy (1990, 1995) demonstrated extensive phosphorus translocation in similar cord-forming systems
- Understanding this process has implications for nutrient management in mycorrhizal and saprotrophic systems
