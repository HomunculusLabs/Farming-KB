# Trametes versicolor

**Turkey Tail** — one of the most extensively researched medicinal mushrooms and source of two prescription anticancer drugs: **PSK (Polysaccharopeptide Krestin)** and **PSP (Polysaccharopeptide)**. Named for concentric color zones resembling a wild turkey's tail feathers, *T. versicolor* is a cosmopolitan wood-decay fungus. Its immunomodulatory polysaccharides have generated billions in pharmaceutical sales in Asia.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi · **Division:** Basidiomycota · **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Polyporales · **Family:** Polyporaceae · **Genus:** *Trametes* · **Species:** *T. versicolor*

**Synonyms:** *Boletus versicolor*, *Polyporus versicolor*, *Coriolus versicolor* (common in Asian pharmaceutical literature). The current accepted name is *Trametes versicolor*.

## Morphology

- **Form:** Thin, flexible, semicircular bracket, 2–10 cm wide × 1–5 cm deep × 1–3 mm thick
- **Upper surface:** Concentrically zoned — white, grey, brown, orange, blue, green, red bands. Finely hairy when young. Colors vary by substrate, light, and age.
- **Pore surface:** White to cream, 3–8 pores/mm, slightly angular
- **Flesh:** Thin (1–2 mm), tough, leathery-corky, white. Not edible due to texture.
- **Spores:** Cylindrical, smooth, 5–7 × 1.5–2.5 µm, white print

**Distinguishing features:** Extremely thin bracket, white pore surface, leathery flexibility. Unlike *Stereum ostrea* (false turkey tail) which lacks pores entirely.

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Range:** Cosmopolitan — every continent except Antarctica. Most common wood-decay fungi worldwide.
- **Substrates:** Primarily hardwoods (oak, maple, beech, birch, alder, willow, eucalyptus). Occasionally conifers.
- **Role:** **White rot decomposer** — degrades cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin using laccase, lignin peroxidase (LiP), and manganese peroxidase (MnP).
- **Season:** Year-round in temperate regions; most prolific in autumn.
- **Longevity:** Perennial — brackets persist months to years.

## Bioactive Compounds

### PSK (Polysaccharopeptide Krestin)

The most clinically significant compound:

- **Source:** Mycelial extract, isolated in Japan (1960s, Kureha Chemical Industry)
- **Nature:** Protein-bound β-glucan, ~100 kDa (~30% protein, ~70% polysaccharide). Primarily β-(1→4)-linked glucose with β-(1→3)/(1→6) branching.
- **Mechanism:** Activates NK cells, cytotoxic T lymphocytes, macrophages; increases IL-2, IL-12; enhances dendritic cell maturation; shifts Th1/Th2 balance.
- **Status:** Prescription drug in Japan since 1977 (Krestin®). Annual sales >$400M. Not FDA/EMA approved; available as dietary supplement in the West.

### PSP (Polysaccharopeptide)

- **Source:** Strain COV-1, isolated in China (1980s). Higher protein content (~40%) than PSK.
- **Status:** Approved as adjunct cancer therapy by China's SFDA.

### Other Compounds

- **β-glucans** — (1→3)/(1→6)-linked, immunostimulant
- **Triterpenoids, steroids** (ergosterol, ergosterol peroxide) — anti-inflammatory
- **Phenolic compounds** — antioxidant
- **Laccase** — industrially significant lignin-modifying enzyme

## Clinical Applications

### Cancer Immunotherapy (Adjuvant)

*PSK and PSP are adjuncts to conventional therapy, not standalone treatments.*

- **Colorectal cancer:** Meta-analysis (Sakamoto et al., 2016) — PSK + chemo vs. chemo alone: significant DFS and OS improvement
- **Gastric cancer:** PSK + S-1 chemo showed significant 5-year survival benefit (Ohwada et al., 2017)
- **Breast cancer:** PSP improved immune parameters, NK cell activity, reduced chemo side effects (Torkler et al., 2017)
- **Prostate cancer:** Phase I trial showed immunomodulatory effects (Twardowski et al., 2013)
- **Lung cancer:** Japanese RCTs show improved survival with PSK + standard chemo in NSCLC

### Immune Restoration

Counteracts immunosuppression from chemo/radiation. Reduces treatment-related fatigue, nausea, weight loss. Enhances immune function in immunocompromised patients.

### Antiviral Activity

Preliminary in vitro inhibition of HIV replication and HPV. Research ongoing for chronic viral infections.

## Permaculture Applications

### Mycoremediation

- **Lignin degradation:** Powerful white rot enzymes break down complex pollutants
- **PAH degradation:** Degrades polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in contaminated soil
- **Dye decolorization:** Laccase breaks down textile dyes
- **Pesticide degradation:** Metabolizes organochlorine/organophosphate pesticides
- **Wood decomposition:** Accelerates decomposition in hugelkultur and woody beds

### Garden Integration

- **Log inoculation:** Easy to grow on hardwood logs. Provides medicinal mushrooms while accelerating log decomposition.
- **Chip beds:** Inoculate hardwood chip mulch for passive cultivation. Often appears spontaneously on fresh chips.
- **Soil building:** Releases nitrogen and minerals as wood decomposes, building soil organic matter
- **Companion fungi:** Pairs with shiitake on same logs (different timing). Often first fungus on fresh chips, paving the way for other decomposers.

## Cultivation

| Method | Details |
|--------|---------|
| Log cultivation | Drill-and-fill hardwood logs (5–15 cm). Seal with wax. Incubate 6–12 months. |
| Wood chip beds | Mix sawdust spawn into fresh hardwood chips in shaded beds. 4–8 months to fruit. |
| Supplemented blocks | Hardwood sawdust + 10–20% bran. Sterilize, inoculate. 14–21 day fruiting cycle. |
| Natural | Inoculate stumps in situ; zero maintenance, variable yield. |

**Environmental parameters:** Colonization 20–25 °C, fruiting 15–25 °C, humidity 80–95%, moderate-high fresh air.

## Dosing

| Form | Typical Dose |
|------|-------------|
| Dried powder | 2–3 g/day |
| Hot water extract | 1–2 g equivalent, 2–3× daily |
| Dual extract (water + ethanol) | 1–2 mL tincture 2× daily |
| PSK (pharmaceutical) | 3 g/day (standard Japanese dose) |
| PSP (pharmaceutical) | 1–3 g/day (standard Chinese dose) |

## Safety

Excellent safety profile — no significant adverse effects in decades of clinical use in Japan and China. No known toxicity. No clinically significant drug interactions reported, though immunomodulatory effects may theoretically interact with immunosuppressants.
