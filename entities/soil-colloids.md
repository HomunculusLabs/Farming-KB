## Description

Soil colloids are ultra-fine particles of clay, organic matter, or long-chain polymers that remain suspended in soil water unaffected by gravity. They form stable aqueous gels or suspensions and serve as the primary active sites for ionic bonding and nutrient exchange in soil. Colloids are the mechanism by which soils hold water and nutrients against leaching, making them indispensable to soil fertility and plant health. Without colloids, soil minerals rapidly wash out and the soil becomes nutrient-poor.

## Classification

- **Category:** Soil physical chemistry component
- **Types:** Organic colloids (humus-derived), inorganic colloids (clay particles), synthetic colloids (polyacrylamide gels)
- **Particle size:** 2-200 millimicrons (nanometres)
- **Charge:** Typically negatively charged on particle surfaces

## Key Facts

### Natural Sources of Colloids
- **Humus and humic decay products:** The most effective colloids in soil, hundreds of times more effective than clay colloids in ion exchange.
- **Bacterial polysaccharides:** Secreted by soil bacteria, forming gels that bind soil particles.
- **Fine clays:** Particles of 2-200 millimicrons including illites and bentonite (deposited on clay pans from evaporating suspensions).
- **Finely ground graphite and silica.**
- **Grain flours and animal gelatinous flours** (hooves, horns, sinew, albumen).

### How Colloids Work
- Colloid particles carry negative surface charges, as do root hairs.
- These negative charges attract positive ions (cations): sulphates, nitrates, and metal ions (sodium, calcium, magnesium, iron).
- Approximately 99% of nutrient ions in soil are held by colloids (Leeper, 1982).
- Plant roots attract these ions by producing negative hydrogen charges, displacing them from colloid surfaces.
- Ammonia and sodium ions can displace bound cations, making them available to roots or, in excess, causing leaching loss.

### Hydrophilic vs Hydrophobic Colloids
- **Hydrophilic colloids** attract water and form gels that hold soil moisture reserves. These include natural grain gels, seaweed extracts, and synthetic products like Agrosoke, Ikedagel, and Terrasorb.
- **Hydrophobic colloids** repel water. Produced by some clays and certain soil fungi. In normal soils these are less common.

### Synthetic Soil Conditioners
- Most artificial gels are acrylic-based (acrylamid polymers), supplied as granules.
- Each granule can absorb hundreds of times its weight in water.
- Applied at rates of 6 kg/ha or more.
- Used in nursery plants, row crops, new tree plantings in deserts, and transplanting.
- Water conservation claims of up to 50% reduction in irrigation needs.

### Colloids and Soil Destruction
- **Fire** destroys the colloidal properties of surface clays. Burnt clay particles no longer form colloids and become mineral-poor.
- **Excess sodium** deflocculates colloids, causing soil collapse. Common in dryland soils with salt concentrations above 1-5 ppm.
- **Ploughing and cultivation** destroy organic colloids by oxidation.
- **Clearing and burning** remove the biomass that generates colloids.

### Flocculation
- Colloids can be aggregated (flocculated) by adding aluminium salts (sulphates) or gypsum.
- Flocculation causes colloids to settle out of suspension, improving soil structure.
- Acid rain can also induce flocculation of ferric oxide colloids.
- Salt flocculates colloids up to a threshold, beyond which excess sodium deflocculates clays. This is the mechanism of dryland soil degradation.

### Colloids in the Tropics
- Most colloids in tropical soils exist in the biomass of the soil as cellular gels or are produced as sheathing material by soil bacteria.
- Without colloids, soil minerals rapidly leach out.
- Fire, clearing, ploughing, and cultivation destroy these colloids and soil structure.
- Very little clay is needed in sandy gardens to create a colloidal environment if biological activity is maintained.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Colloids are the living heart of soil fertility. In permaculture systems, the emphasis on mulching, composting, and perennial vegetation directly supports colloid formation, especially through humus and bacterial polysaccharide production. Mollison stresses that very little clay is needed in sandy gardens to create a colloidal environment if the biological component (humus, mulch, root exudates) is maintained. The key insight is that colloids are not just chemical but biological: soil bacteria, fungi, and root systems continuously produce them. Maintaining permanent plant cover and avoiding tillage preserves the colloid matrix that holds water and nutrients, making the difference between productive and degraded land.

## Types of Soil Colloids

Soil colloids include layer silicate clays (montmorillonite, kaolinite, illite),
iron and aluminum oxides (gibbsite, goethite), and organic colloids (humic
and fulvic acids). Each type has distinct charge properties and cation exchange
capacity that influence nutrient availability.
