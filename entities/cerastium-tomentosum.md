## Taxonomy and Morphology

Cerastium tomentosum belongs to the genus Cerastium, which contains approximately 100 species distributed across temperate and arctic regions worldwide. The species name "tomentosum" refers to the dense, woolly (tomentose) hair covering the leaves and stems.

The genus Cerastium is distinguished from the related genus Stellaria by its cylindrical (rather than compressed) capsule and the presence of five (rather than ten) styles. Cerastium species are commonly known as mouse-ear chickweeds.

Stems are procumbent to ascending, 15–30 cm long, forming dense mats 5–10 cm tall. The foliage is covered with fine, white, woolly hairs that give the entire plant a distinctive silvery appearance.

Leaves are narrowly lanceolate to linear, 1–3 cm long and 3–6 mm wide. They are arranged in opposite pairs along the stem.

Flowers are produced in loose cymes at the stem tips from late spring through early summer. Each flower is 1.5–2 cm in diameter with five white, deeply notched petals.

The fruit is a cylindrical capsule containing numerous small, brown seeds. Seeds are approximately 0.5 mm in diameter with a minutely bumpy surface.

The root system consists of a network of fibrous roots that spread horizontally from rooting nodes. This growth habit allows the plant to form extensive, soil-binding mats.

Cerastium tomentosum var. columnae is a naturally occurring variety with slightly larger flowers. It is sometimes listed in nursery catalogs as a distinct cultivar.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cerastium tomentosum is native to the mountains of central and southern Europe. Its range extends from the Pyrenees through the Alps to the Apennines and the mountains of the Balkan Peninsula.

It grows naturally on rocky slopes, scree, limestone outcrops, and dry alpine meadows at elevations of 500–2,500 meters. The species prefers calcareous substrates and excellent drainage.

## Landscape Uses

Cerastium tomentosum is primarily used as a groundcover in sunny, well-drained sites. Its silver foliage provides excellent contrast with darker-leaved plants in perennial borders and rock gardens.

The species is ideally suited for planting between stepping stones, along dry stone walls, and on sunny banks. It tolerates light foot traffic and recovers quickly from occasional trampling.

It combines well with other drought-tolerant perennials such as sedums, thymes, and dwarf irises. The white flowers harmonize with virtually any garden color scheme.

In alpine and trough gardens, Cerastium tomentosum cascades attractively over container edges. Its compact habit and silver foliage make it a staple of miniature landscape designs.

The plant is deer-resistant and rarely bothered by rabbits. Its woolly, bitter-tasting foliage deters most mammalian herbivores.
