## Cut-and-Come-Again

## Description

Cut-and-come-again is a harvesting technique for leafy green vegetables in which the outer or upper leaves are harvested while the growing point (apical meristem) is left intact, allowing the plant to regrow for subsequent harvests. This method is central to the economic viability of winter salad production and is referenced throughout *The Winter Harvest Handbook* at pp. 3, 50, and 85 as a foundational technique for continuous harvest from limited greenhouse space.

## Classification

- **Category**: Harvesting technique
- **Domain**: Market gardening, greenhouse vegetable production
- **Crops**: Leafy greens, salad mix components, braising greens
- **Alternative names**: Multiple harvest, leaf-by-leaf harvest, repeat harvest

## Key Facts

- Allows multiple harvests (typically 3-5) from a single sowing over several weeks
- The growing point must remain undamaged for regrowth to occur
- Most applicable to leafy greens: lettuce, mache, spinach, mizuna, arugula, claytonia, radicchio, endive
- Referenced in *The Winter Harvest Handbook* as a core technique for winter salad production (pp. 3, 50, 85)
- Reduces seed costs and labor compared to single-harvest whole-plant removal
- Compatible with salad mix production where leaves of various sizes are harvested together
- Particularly important during the [[persephone-period]] (low-light winter months) when growth is slowest

## Technique Variations

### Leaf-by-Leaf Harvesting

Individual outer leaves are picked by hand or cut with a knife, leaving the center rosette intact. Best for:

- Lettuce (pp. 75-78) — harvest 3-4 outer leaves per plant every 7-10 days
- Endive and escarole (p. 72, 77) — similar approach, slightly bitter flavor intensifies with age
- Radicchio (p. 77) — outer leaves harvested, sometimes the head regrows after initial cut
- Chard (p. 78) — outer stalks and leaves pulled or cut, inner leaves continue developing

### Shearing / Mowing

The entire top of the plant is cut at a uniform height (2-3 inches above soil level) using a knife. Best for:

- Mache (pp. 84-85) — excellent regrowth from cut stems, can yield 3+ harvests
- Spinach — regrows from remaining leaf axils after cutting
- Salad mix greens (pp. 75-78) — a blend of greens cut together at varying heights
- Mizuna and mustard greens (pp. 76, 78) — vigorous regrowth after cutting

### Mixed Harvest

In salad mix production, a blend of greens at different growth stages is cut together:

- Combines leaves from multiple species and maturity levels in a single pass
- Natural variation in regrowth rates creates built-in succession within the bed
- Different species are harvested at different heights, preserving their respective growing points
- Creates a more interesting and nutritionally diverse salad mix (p. 75)

## Application in Winter Harvest Systems

In Coleman's winter greenhouse system, cut-and-come-again is especially valuable because:

- **Slow winter growth**: Growth rates are slower due to reduced daylight (Persephone period, pp. 41-44), making each sowing a greater time investment
- **Maximum harvests per sowing**: With each planting taking longer to mature, maximizing harvests per planting is economically critical
- **Natural suitability**: Winter greens like mache, claytonia, and minutina are naturally well-suited to repeated harvesting
- **Continuous supply**: The technique allows continuous production from a limited greenhouse footprint throughout the low-light season
- **Crop holding**: Winter greens hold well in cold conditions and regrow slowly, providing a flexible harvest window

## Economic Significance

Cut-and-come-again harvesting directly impacts farm economics:

- **Reduced seed costs**: Fewer sowings needed for the same total yield
- **Reduced labor**: Less time spent on soil preparation, sowing, and early cultivation
- **Extended revenue**: Each bed generates income over a longer period
- **Risk mitigation**: If one harvest is delayed by weather, the greens simply hold in the field

## Relevance to Cultivation

Cut-and-come-again is fundamental to the economics of winter market gardening. By extending the productive life of each crop planting, it reduces the number of sowings needed during the low-light winter months when germination and growth are slowest. This technique, combined with cold greenhouse protection and successive planting schedules, enables year-round harvest from a relatively small growing area — a core principle of Coleman's [[winter-harvest-system]].
