# Gymnopilus sapineus

**Flammulina sapinea** | *Gymnopilus sapineus* (Fr.) Maire ex Singer

Gymnopilus sapineus is a wood-inhabiting mushroom in the family cortinariaceae (or hymenogastraceae under modern classification) that has been reported to contain [[gymnopilus-spectabilis-entity]].

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Cortinariaceae (or Hymenogastraceae)
- **Genus:** Gymnopilus

The species was originally described as Agaricus sapineus by Elias Magnus Fries in 1821 and was later transferred to the genus Gymnopilus by René Maire and subsequently validated by Rolf Singer. The taxonomy of Gymnopilus has undergone significant revision, and some authorities consider G. sapineus to be part of a species complex that includes gymnopilus penetrans and gymnopilus liquiritiae, with unclear boundaries between these taxa. Molecular phylogenetic studies are needed to resolve the relationships within this genus.

## Morphology

### Cap
The pileus measures 3 to 8 cm in diameter, convex to broadly convex, sometimes with a slight umbo in age. The cap surface is dry, covered with fine appressed fibrils or small scales, and ranges in color from yellow-orange to rusty orange or brownish-orange, often darker toward the center. The margin is inrolled when young, becoming plane to slightly uplifted with age, and may be slightly striate at maturity.

### Gills
The gills are adnate to adnexed or slightly sinuate, close to subdistant, and initially pale yellow before developing a rusty-brown to cinnamon color as the spores mature. This characteristic rust-colored spore deposit is a defining feature of the genus Gymnopilus and helps distinguish it from superficially similar genera.

### Stem
The stipe is 4 to 10 cm long and 0.5 to 1.5 cm thick, cylindrical, and often slightly curved. It is pale yellow at the apex, becoming darker and more brownish below. The surface may have scattered fibrillose scales, particularly near the base. An annulus (ring) is often present but may be evanescent, leaving only a fibrillose zone. The base may be slightly enlarged but does not form a distinct bulb.

### Flesh and Spores
The flesh is thin, yellowish, and may stain brownish when cut or bruised, though this reaction is not always pronounced. The odor is mild to slightly farinaceous or rancid. The taste is bitter to strongly bitter, a characteristic that, combined with the rusty spore color, helps identify the genus. The [[comparison-spore-print-collection-vs-spore-syringe-production]] is rusty brown to ferruginous. Basidiospores are ellipsoid to amygdaliform, 7 to 10 by 5 to 6.5 µm, with a rough to warty ornamentation, and are dextrinoid.

## Distribution and Habitat

Gymnopilus sapineus has a wide distribution across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere. It has been reported from Europe, [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], and parts of Asia. The species is saprotrophic, growing on dead wood, stumps, logs, and branches of both coniferous and broadleaf trees. It shows a preference for conifer substrates, particularly pine, spruce, and fir, but can also be found on hardwoods including oak and beech.

Fruiting occurs from late summer through autumn, typically from August to November in temperate regions, depending on local climate conditions. It often grows in clusters or tufts on the same piece of woody substrate, sometimes producing substantial fruitings that can cover large areas of fallen logs.

## Chemistry and Psychoactive Properties

Gymnopilus sapineus has been reported to contain [[psilocin]], though the concentrations are generally lower than those found in the more potent members of the genus such as [[gymnopilus-purpuratus]]. The presence of baeocystin has also been detected in some Gymnopilus species, though specific analysis of G. sapineus for this compound is limited.

[[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]] of Gymnopilus species is complicated by the presence of bitter, non-psychoactive compounds that may mask or interact with the indole alkaloids. The bitter taste characteristic of the genus may be related to sesquiterpene compounds rather than the tryptamine alkaloids.

The psychoactive effects reported from Gymnopilus species are broadly similar to those of psilocybe species, including visual hallucinations, altered perception of time and space, and changes in mood and cognition. However, the experience is often described as less predictable and sometimes more dysphoric, possibly due to the presence of additional [[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]].

## Ecological Role

As a saprotrophic wood-decay fungus, Gymnopilus sapineus plays an important role in forest ecosystems by breaking down dead woody material and contributing to [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]]. The species is primarily a white-rot decomposer, capable of degrading both cellulose and lignin in wood. This process releases nutrients bound in dead wood back into the soil, making them available for uptake by living plants.

The preference of G. sapineus for conifer substrates means it is particularly important in coniferous forest ecosystems where dead wood accumulation can be significant. It is one of the earlier successional saprotrophs on freshly fallen conifer logs, often appearing within a year or two of tree death.

## Similar Species

Several Gymnopilus species are morphologically similar and can be difficult to distinguish in the field:

- **gymnopilus penetrans**: Very similar, growing on conifer wood; some authorities consider it synonymous with G. sapineus
- **gymnopilus liquiritiae**: Grows primarily on broadleaf wood and has a darker cap; also contains psilocybin
- **[[gymnopilus-aeruginosus]]**: Has a distinctive greenish-blue coloration and grows on conifer wood
- **[[gymnopilus-purpuratus]] and [[psilocybin]] and [[gymnopilus-spectabilis-entity]] — the type species for the genus, known as the "big laughing gym"
- gymnopilus-luteofolius — a small, brightly colored species found in the [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]]
- [[psilocybin]] — the primary psychoactive indole alkaloid
- cortinariaceae — the fungal family to which Gymnopilus belongs
