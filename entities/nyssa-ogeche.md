The tree typically grows 9 to 15 meters tall with a narrow, conical crown. The trunk is straight and can reach 50 to 60 centimeters in diameter at maturity.

Bark is dark gray to brown, developing deep fissures and ridges with age. Young bark is relatively smooth with scattered lenticels.

Leaves are alternate, simple, elliptic to obovate, 5 to 12 centimeters long and 3 to 5 centimeters wide. Leaf margins are entire or slightly toothed near the apex.

The upper leaf surface is dark green and glossy, while the lower surface is lighter with scattered pubescence along the veins. Fall color is variable, often yellow to orange-red.

Flowers are small, greenish-white, and borne in dense clusters from the axils of fallen leaves of the previous season. Flowering occurs from March through May, one of the earliest spring-blooming trees in its range.

The fruit is a drupe 2 to 3 centimeters long, elliptic to ovoid, and dark purple to black when ripe. The flesh is thin and tart, with a single large seed.

Ripe fruits drop from the tree in late summer and early autumn. They are collected from the water surface in floodplain habitats where the species commonly grows.

The tart, acidic fruit was historically important as a food source for indigenous peoples of the southeastern coastal plain. The fruit can be stored for extended periods in water, providing an off-season food resource.

## Distribution and Habitat

Nyssa ogeche is distributed along the Atlantic and Gulf coastal plains of the southeastern United States. Its range extends from southeastern South Carolina through Georgia and northern Florida to southeastern Louisiana.

The species is most abundant in the Ogeechee, Altamaha, and Suwannee River drainages of Georgia and Florida. Disjunct populations occur in the Apalachicola and Choctawhatchee River systems.

Ogeechee tupelo is found exclusively in wetland habitats. It grows along the margins of blackwater rivers, in cypress-tupelo swamps, and around the edges of ponds and Carolina bays.

The species requires sites with seasonal flooding and acidic, organic-rich soils. It is typically found at elevations below 30 meters in flat terrain subject to periodic inundation.

USDA hardiness is Zone 7 to Zone 9. The species is tolerant of flooding but intolerant of drought and fire. The tupelo honey flow lasts only 2 to 3 weeks in early spring. This short harvest window, combined with the species' restricted range, contributes to the honey's high market value.

Ogeechee tupelo honey is light amber with a greenish cast and a distinctive mild, buttery flavor. It is prized for its resistance to crystallization, remaining liquid for years without processing.

The tart fruit can be used to make preserves, syrups, and beverages. Early settlers used the fruit as a substitute for limes, giving rise to the common name Ogeechee lime.

The species is occasionally planted as an ornamental tree in wetland gardens and along water features. Its narrow form, glossy foliage, and fall color make it an attractive landscape specimen.

Transplanting success is limited because Ogeechee tupelo develops a deep taproot that is easily damaged during digging. Container-grown specimens are recommended for landscape installations.

## Ecology and Wildlife Value

Nyssa ogeche provides important [[fungal-melanin-biosynthesis-ecological-functions]] in southeastern wetland ecosystems. The flowers are a critical early-season nectar source for honeybees and native pollinators.

Beekeepers from across the United States transport hives to Ogeechee tupelo swamps each spring for the honey harvest. The economic value of tupelo honey supports rural communities throughout the species' range.

The fruit is consumed by numerous bird species, including wood ducks, pileated woodpeckers, and various songbirds. Mammals including raccoons, opossums, and black bears also feed on the fruit.

The tree provides nesting sites and cover for wildlife in swamp and riparian habitats. Wood ducks frequently nest in cavities within mature Ogeechee tupelo trunks.
