# Matthew Carpenter

## Description

Matthew Carpenter is a New Zealand farmer who pioneered the field-drilling of tagasaste (*Chamaecytisus palmensis*) for summer sheep forage on the cold Canterbury Plain of southern New Zealand. His work is cited by Mollison as a practical demonstration of tree-based forage systems extending the productive season of grassland pastures into summer deficit periods.

## Classification

- **Type**: Person — innovative farmer and agricultural practitioner
- **Location**: Canterbury Plain, South Island, New Zealand
- **Field**: Forage tree establishment, sheep production, dryland farming
- **Era**: Late 20th century (contemporary with 1988 publication of the Manual)

## Key Facts

- Successfully field-drilled tagasaste on the cold Canterbury Plain for summer sheep forage
- Demonstrated an "unforeseen benefit": grasses between tagasaste strips dry out much later and produce more growth than grasses in open field situations
- Operated in a challenging environment — the Canterbury Plain is a cold, dry region where conventional summer pasture typically dries off
- Provided proof of concept for tagasaste as a dryland alternative to irrigated lucerne

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Carpenter's work validates several key permaculture principles for temperate grassland design:

1. **Tree-forage integration**: By drilling tagasaste strips into existing pasture, Carpenter created a two-tier forage system that produces leaf fodder during summer when grass is dormant or dried off. Tagasaste (unirrigated) produces the equivalent forage weight of irrigated lucerne — a dramatic improvement in water-use efficiency.

2. **Microclimate modification**: The unexpected benefit — that inter-row grasses remained greener and more productive — demonstrates the windbreak, shade, and moisture-conservation effects of tree strips in open pasture. This is a textbook example of edge effects and companion benefits in polyculture design.

3. **Low-establishment cost**: Field-drilling tagasaste uses standard seed drilling equipment, making it accessible to conventional farmers. Seed can be sourced cheaply from soil beneath mature trees using shaker tray systems.

4. **Multi-function stacking**: Tagasaste simultaneously provides sheep forage, chicken feed (abundant seed), bee forage (honey production), and summer shelter — multiple yields from a single establishment cost.

## Practical Implications

Carpenter's demonstration suggests that for cool temperate dryland regions (annual rainfall 400-600 mm), tagasaste at 1.0-1.5 m spacing for cut forage (stems kept cut at 1 m high) or 4-5 m spacing for seed and bee production can transform summer-deficit pastures into year-round productive systems without irrigation infrastructure.
