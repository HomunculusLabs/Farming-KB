## Overview

*Cerrena unicolor* (Bull.) Murrill is a white-rot basidiomycete bracket fungus in the family Polyporaceae. It is one of the most efficient lignin-degrading fungi known, producing potent laccase enzymes used in biotechnology and bioremediation.

The species colonizes dead and dying hardwood, particularly fallen branches and stumps of oak, beech, and birch. It fruits as tough, semicircular brackets with a distinctive zonate upper surface and a gray-to-cream pore layer underneath.

C. unicolor has attracted significant industrial interest for its laccase production capacity, which rivals that of *Trametes versicolor*. Its enzymes are studied for applications in pulp bleaching, textile dye decolorization, and degradation of organic pollutants.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Basidiomycota → Agaricomycetes → Polyporales → Polyporaceae → *Cerrena* → *C. unicolor*.

The species was originally described as *Boletus unicolor* by Bulliard in 1788 and has undergone multiple generic transfers. Murrill placed it in *Cerrena* in 1903.

Synonyms include *Daedalea unicolor* (Fr.) and *Polyporus unicolor* (Bull.) Fr. Some authorities recognize *C. meyenii* as a separate species, but molecular work places it within the *C. unicolor* complex.

**Fruiting body:** Semicircular to fan-shaped brackets, 2–8 cm wide and 1–3 cm thick. Upper surface is distinctly zonate with concentric zones of gray, brown, and white, often coated with a fine bloom.

Pore surface is white to cream, with angular to maze-like (daedaleoid) pores 1–3 per mm. Context is tough and corky, white when fresh, drying hard and woody.

Spores are cylindrical to allantoid, smooth, hyaline, measuring 5–7 × 1.5–2 µm. The hyphal system is dimitic with generative and skeletal hyphae. Cystidia are absent or very rare.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Cerrena unicolor* has a broad temperate and subtropical distribution across North America, Europe, and Asia. It is particularly common in eastern North American hardwood forests and throughout temperate Europe.

The species is almost exclusively found on dead or moribund hardwood. Preferred substrates include species of *Quercus* (oak), *Fagus* (beech), *Betula* (birch), *Acer* (maple), and *Alnus* (alder).

Fruiting occurs year-round in mild climates but peaks in late summer through autumn in temperate zones. It typically appears on fallen branches, small logs, and standing dead timber where moisture retention is adequate.

## Ecology and Decomposition

*C. unicolor* is a primary white-rot decomposer that selectively degrades lignin while leaving cellulose largely intact. This white-rot strategy contrasts with brown-rot fungi that primarily attack cellulose.

The fungus produces a consortium of lignin-modifying enzymes: laccases (multicopper oxidases), manganese peroxidases, and lignin peroxidases. These extracellular enzymes catalyze the oxidative depolymerization of lignin's complex polyphenolic structure.

Its ecological role is significant in forest carbon cycling. By breaking down lignin, C. unicolor releases trapped cellulose and hemicellulose for other decomposers and contributes to soil humus formation.

The species also acts as a weak pathogen on stressed trees, colonizing through wounds. It is not considered a major cause of timber decay in standing trees compared to more aggressive polypores like *Ganoderma lucidum*.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

The most commercially significant metabolite of *C. unicolor* is laccase, a blue multicopper oxidase. Laccases from this species demonstrate high thermal stability and broad substrate specificity, making them preferred candidates for industrial enzyme production.

Antimicrobial compounds have been isolated from fruiting bodies and mycelial extracts. These include sesquiterpenoids and phenolic derivatives with activity against Gram-positive bacteria including *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Bacillus subtilis*.

Anti-inflammatory effects have been documented in vitro through inhibition of nitric oxide production in LPS-stimulated macrophages. Polysaccharide extracts show immunomodulatory activity via NF-κB pathway modulation.

Antioxidant activity of methanol and aqueous extracts is attributed to phenolic compounds and evaluated by DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assays. The total phenolic content correlates with antioxidant capacity.

## Cultivation

*C. unicolor* is cultivated primarily for laccase enzyme production rather than as an edible mushroom. Submerged fermentation in bioreactors yields peak laccase titers after 5–7 days at 25–28°C with agitation at 150–200 rpm.

Solid-state fermentation on agricultural residues (wheat straw, rice bran, sawdust) provides cost-effective enzyme production. Supplementation with copper (0.5–2.0 mM CuSO₄) and aromatic inducers (guaiacol, 2,5-xylidine) significantly enhances laccase yields.

Optimal pH for enzyme production is 4.5–6.0, with pH 5.0 being standard for most strains. Carbon sources such as glucose, sucrose, and maltose support robust mycelial growth, while nitrogen sources like ammonium tartrate or peptone enhance enzyme secretion.

Fruiting body cultivation is rarely attempted commercially. Laboratory fruiting has been achieved on supplemented hardwood sawdust under high humidity (85–95%) at 20–25°C with 12-hour light cycles.

## Bioremediation Applications

Laccase from *C. unicolor* degrades a wide range of environmental pollutants including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), chlorophenols, synthetic dyes, and endocrine-disrupting chemicals. This makes the species valuable for bioremediation of contaminated soils and wastewater.

Immobilized laccase preparations from C. unicolor show enhanced stability and reusability compared to free enzymes. Support materials such as alginate beads, activated carbon, and chitosan have been used successfully for enzyme immobilization.

The fungus has demonstrated ability to decolorize industrial dyes from textile effluents, achieving 70–95% color removal within 24–72 hours under optimized conditions. This has practical implications for treatment of dye-containing wastewater.

## Related Species

*Cerrena unicolor* is most easily confused with *Trametes versicolor* (turkey tail), which shares a similar bracket morphology and white-rot ecology. Key distinctions: T. versicolor has distinctly bicolored (concentric zones of brown and white) upper surfaces and round pores, while C. unicolor has more subtle gray-zoned caps and angular pores.

*Daedaleopsis confragosa* (thin-maze polypore) is another lookalike with daedaleoid pores. It differs in having a more reddish-brown upper surface, turning pinkish when bruised, and grows preferentially on willow and birch.

*Stereum hirsutum* (hairy curtain crust) can appear similar from above but lacks a true pore layer, instead having a smooth to wrinkled spore-bearing surface (hymenium).
