## Overview

**Vigna unguiculata**, commonly known as cowpea, black-eyed pea, or southern pea, is an annual herbaceous legume in the family Fabaceae. It is one of the most important food legumes in the semi-arid tropics, providing essential protein, calories, and micronutrients to hundreds of millions of people across sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Latin America.

The species is exceptionally drought-tolerant and thrives in poor soils where many other crops fail. Its dual-purpose nature — producing both edible grains and nutritious leafy greens — makes it a cornerstone of subsistence farming systems.

Cowpea was domesticated in West Africa approximately 5,000–6,000 years ago and subsequently spread to Asia and the Americas through trade and the slave trade. It now encompasses several well-defined cultivar groups adapted to diverse agroecological zones.

## History and Domestication

Cowpea was domesticated in West Africa, likely in the region encompassing present-day Nigeria and Niger, approximately 5,000–6,000 years ago. The wild progenitor (*V. unguiculata* subsp. *dekindtiana*) is still found across the African savanna.

Archaeological evidence from rock shelters in Ghana and central Ghanaian sites documents cowpea cultivation dating back to the second millennium BCE. The crop spread eastward to the Horn of Africa and South Asia, and westward to the Americas during the transatlantic slave trade.

In the Americas, cowpea became deeply embedded in Southern U.S. cuisine, Caribbean cooking, and Brazilian food traditions. The term "black-eyed pea" reflects the distinctive seed pattern that became iconic in American culinary culture.

## Cultivar Groups

The species encompasses considerable morphological diversity organized into several recognized cultivar groups. The *Unguiculata* group (common cowpea) includes bush and semi-climbing types with short to medium pods grown primarily for dry grain harvest.
