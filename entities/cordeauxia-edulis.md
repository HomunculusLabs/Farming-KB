## Overview

**Cordeauxia edulis**, commonly known as yeheb, yeheb nut, or the Somali nut, is a monotypic species of evergreen shrub in the family Fabaceae (subfamily Caesalpinioideae). It is endemic to the arid and semi-arid regions of the Horn of Africa, primarily Somalia and eastern Ethiopia.

The species produces edible seeds with a flavor resembling roasted chestnuts or cashews, making it a prized food source for pastoralist communities in one of the world's harshest environments. Its ability to produce nutritious food under extreme drought conditions makes it a species of significant interest for dryland agriculture research.

Yeheb is classified as endangered due to over-harvesting of wild populations, habitat degradation from overgrazing, and the absence of cultivation programs. It is one of the few perennial food crops native to the Somali-Masai semi-desert ecosystem.

*Cordeauxia* is a monotypic genus, meaning *C. edulis* is its sole species. The genus was named after Captain H.M. Cordeaux, a British colonial administrator who collected the first botanical specimens in southern Somalia during the late 19th century.

The plant is an evergreen shrub or small tree, typically reaching 2–5 meters in height. It develops a deep, extensive root system that enables survival in regions receiving as little as 100–300 mm annual rainfall.

Leaves are bipinnately compound, 10–20 cm long, with 3–6 pairs of pinnae each bearing 4–8 leaflets. The foliage has a distinctive silvery-green appearance due to a dense covering of fine hairs. Leaves produce a red pigment called cordeauxiaquinone, which leaches from fallen leaves and stains the ground red.

Flowers are small, pale yellow to cream-colored, borne in axillary racemes. The pods are oblong, 3–6 cm long, flattened, and contain 1–3 seeds each. Seeds are ovoid, approximately 1.5–2 cm long, brown to reddish-brown, and enclosed in a hard testa.

## Distribution and Habitat

Yeheb is endemic to the Horn of Africa, with its natural range restricted to southern Somalia, the Ogaden region of eastern Ethiopia, and possibly bordering areas of northeastern Kenya. It occurs at elevations between 100 and 500 meters.

The species inhabits semi-desert and arid bushland on sandy or stony soils, often associated with *Acacia*-*Commiphora* woodland and *Aloe* scrub. It is adapted to [[kava-traditional-preparation-ceremonial-context]] involves sun-drying followed by roasting over open fires, which develops the characteristic chestnut-like flavor.

## Ecological Role

*Cordeauxia edulis* is an important component of the arid-zone ecosystem in the Horn of Africa. It provides shade and microhabitat for understory plants and contributes to soil stabilization in fragile desert soils.

The species fixes atmospheric nitrogen through root-associated rhizobial symbiosis, though [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] from deep soil horizons.

Yeheb provides browse for livestock, particularly camels and goats, during the dry season when other forage is scarce. However, excessive browsing by [[synthetic-log-cultivation-methods-guide]] has been limited but promising. Preliminary trials in Somalia and Ethiopia suggest that yeheb can be propagated from seed and established in managed plantations, though [[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]] may take several years.

## Ethnobotanical Use

Yeheb has been a staple food for Somali pastoralist communities for centuries. The seeds are traditionally collected from wild stands during the dry season (December–March), when other food sources are scarce, providing a critical nutritional safety net.

Somali communities value yeheb for both subsistence and trade. Dried seeds can be stored for extended periods, making them a reliable famine reserve. The species holds significant cultural importance and is referenced in Somali poetry and oral traditions as a symbol of resilience.

Leaves and young shoots are occasionally browsed by livestock, and the red pigment (cordeauxiaquinone) from fallen leaves has been used as a [[natural-dye-plants]]. The wood, though small in dimension, has been used for fuel and tool handles.

## Chemistry

The most notable chemical constituent of *C. edulis* is cordeauxiaquinone, a red anthraquinone pigment found in the leaves, stems, and seed coats. This compound leaches from fallen foliage and accumulates on the soil surface, creating characteristic red staining beneath established plants.
