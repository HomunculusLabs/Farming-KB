# Geronimo

## Description

Geronimo (Goyaałé, "the one who yawns"; c. 1829–1909) was a prominent [[chiricahua-apache]] leader and medicine man who led resistance against Mexican and United States military expansion into Apache lands in the American Southwest and northern Mexico during the late nineteenth century.

## Classification

- **Type:** Historical figure / indigenous leader
- **Tribal affiliation:** Chiricahua Apache (Bedonkohe band)
- **Roles:** Military leader, medicine man, resistance figure
- **Historical period:** Apache Wars (1850s–1886)

## Historical Significance

Geronimo's leadership and eventual surrender marked a pivotal turning point in Apache history. The Chiricahua Apache were taken as prisoners of war in 1886 after Geronimo's capitulation, ending the period of armed resistance and beginning the era of forced relocation and reservation life.

When the Chiricahuas were freed from prisoner-of-war status in 1913, the majority chose to move to the [[mescalero-indian-reservation]] in New Mexico and became part of the Mescalero tribe, though some elected to remain at Fort Sill, Oklahoma.

## Connection to Shamanism

Geronimo was known as a medicine man (shaman) as well as a military leader. In Apache culture, supernatural power approaches people through plants, animals, or natural phenomena via dreams or hallucinatory experiences. Geronimo's reputation included claims of supernatural abilities, consistent with the Apache understanding that power has no intrinsic moral attribute — its virtue resides in its potency.

## Impact on Apache Tribes

Geronimo's surrender and the subsequent imprisonment of the Chiricahuas had lasting consequences:

- **Population displacement:** The Chiricahuas were held as prisoners of war for 27 years (1886–1913)
- **Cultural disruption:** The forced relocation disrupted traditional practices, including shamanistic traditions
- **Reservation consolidation:** After 1913, Chiricahuas joined the Mescalero Indian Reservation alongside Mescalero and [[lipan-apache]]
- **Continued identity:** Despite consolidation, Chiricahua descendants maintained distinct cultural traditions

## Key Facts

- Geronimo's surrender in 1886 ended major Apache armed resistance in the Southwest
- The Chiricahuas were the last major Apache group to be confined to reservations
- After release in 1913, the majority of Chiricahuas chose the Mescalero Reservation over Fort Sill
- By the time of the Boyer studies (1958–1971), no one on the Mescalero Reservation had knowledge of peyote use specifically by the Chiricahuas
- Geronimo died in 1909 at Fort Sill, Oklahoma, still a prisoner of war

## Legacy

Geronimo's legacy is deeply intertwined with Apache identity on the Mescalero Reservation, where Chiricahua descendants continue to maintain elements of their distinct cultural and religious traditions alongside those of the Mescaleros and Lipans. His name has become one of the most recognized in Native American history, symbolizing both fierce resistance and the tragic consequences of colonial expansion.

## Relevance to Ethnobotany

While Geronimo is not directly associated with hallucinogenic plant use in the source material, his role as a Chiricahua Apache medicine man places him within the broader context of Apache [[shamanism]], which historically included peyote use (among the Mescaleros and Lipans, if not the Chiricahuas). The Apache understanding of supernatural power — accessible through dreams and visions, mediated by plants and animals — represents an important framework in the ethnobotany of the American Southwest.
