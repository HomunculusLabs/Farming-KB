## Overview

Corylus maxima, commonly known as the filbert or giant filbert, is a deciduous shrub or small tree in the family Betulaceae. It is closely related to the common hazel (*C. avellana*) and is cultivated commercially for its edible nuts, which are larger and more elongated than those of its relative.

The species is native to southeastern Europe and western Asia, with a natural range extending from the Balkan Peninsula through Turkey to the Caucasus. It has been cultivated for nut production for at least two millennia and remains an important crop in several European regions.

The common name "filbert" is thought to derive from the Old French "philbert," named after Saint Philibert whose feast day on August 20 coincides with the beginning of the filbert harvest season in Europe. The nuts are widely used in confectionery, baking, and as a snack food.

The distinction between "filbert" and "hazelnut" varies regionally. In European usage, filberts are the larger, elongated nuts of *C. maxima* while hazelnuts are the rounder nuts of *C. avellana*. In [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]] distributed across the north temperate zone. The genus is placed in the birch family Betulaceae, subfamily Coryloideae.

Phylogenomic studies have confirmed that *Corylus* is sister to *Ostryopsis* and *Carpinus* within Betulaceae. The evolutionary origin of the genus dates to the Eocene, with fossil pollen records extending the genus history to approximately 50 million years.

The species typically grows as a large, multi-stemmed shrub reaching 5–8 meters in height, though single-trunked tree forms up to 10 meters occur under cultivation. The growth habit is upright and spreading, with a dense, rounded crown formed by numerous basal shoots.

Bark is smooth and grayish-brown on young stems, becoming fissured and peeling in longitudinal strips on older trunks. Branches are stout and ascending, with prominent lenticels. The species produces numerous basal suckers, forming dense thickets when unmanaged.

Leaves are alternate, broadly ovate to cordate, 8–16 cm long and 6–12 cm wide, with doubly serrate margins and a pointed apex. The upper surface is dark green and rough-textured, while the lower surface is paler with pubescence along the veins.

Male catkins are pendent, cylindrical, and 5–10 cm long, forming in autumn and expanding in late winter to early spring before leaf emergence. Female flowers are small, reddish, and barely visible, borne in clusters of 2–8 at the tips of short shoots.

The fruit is a nut enclosed in a tubular involucre (husk) that extends 2–4 cm beyond the nut, distinguishing it from *C. avellana* where the husk is shorter than the nut. Filbert nuts are 1.5–2.5 cm long, ovoid to oblong, with a thick, hard shell and sweet kernel.

Kernel color ranges from cream to golden-brown depending on cultivar and roasting degree. The skin of the kernel is thin and papery, easily removed by blanching in hot water for commercial processing.

Root systems are extensive and fibrous, with a moderate taproot and numerous lateral roots near the soil surface. The species forms symbiotic associations with [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]].

## Distribution and Habitat

The native range of *C. maxima* extends from southeastern Europe through the Balkan Peninsula, Turkey, the Caucasus, and into northern Iran. It is particularly abundant in the Black Sea coastal region of Turkey, which remains a center of [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]] and the [[north-american-dmt-containing-plants-phalaris-desmanthus-peganum]] production region for filberts and hazelnuts. The mild, wet winters and dry summers approximate the climatic conditions of the species' native range in the Black Sea region.

Archaeological evidence from European sites suggests that hazelnuts have been collected and consumed by humans since the Mesolithic period. The deliberate cultivation of filberts likely began during the Roman era, when the nuts were valued both as food and as a trade commodity.

## Cultivation

Filberts are commercially cultivated in Turkey, Italy, Spain, France, and the Pacific Northwest United States. Turkey is the world's largest producer of filbert-type hazelnuts, with the majority of production centered in the Black Sea region.

[[fukuoka-citrus-orchard-establishment-bare-hillside]] typically uses spacing of 4–6 meters between plants within rows and 5–7 meters between rows. Training to a single trunk or multi-trunk vase form is practiced, depending on regional tradition and cultivar characteristics.

Major commercial cultivars include 'Barcelona', 'Ennis', 'Butler', 'Tonda di Giffoni', and 'Tonda Gentile delle Langhe'. The 'Tonda Gentile' cultivar from Piedmont, Italy, is particularly prized for its flavor and is protected under European PDO designation.

Pollination is required from a compatible cultivar, as the species is self-incompatible. Male catkins release pollen in late winter, and female flowers are receptive over a 2–4 week period. Wind is the primary pollen dispersal vector, making orchard layout and pollinizer placement critical for adequate nut set.

The timing of pollen shed and female receptivity varies among cultivars, creating a need for careful pollinizer selection. Inadequate pollination is one of the most common causes of poor nut set in commercial orchards, particularly during years with unfavorable spring weather conditions.

Harvesting occurs in late summer to early autumn when the nuts fall naturally or are mechanically dislodged. Post-harvest processing includes washing, drying to approximately 8–10% moisture content, and sorting by size and quality grade.

Global production of all hazelnut types exceeds one million metric tons annually, with Turkey accounting for approximately 70% of world production. Italy is the second-largest producer, followed by the United States, Spain, and Georgia.

Ornamental cultivars of *C. maxima* are grown primarily for their showy catkins and purple-leaved forms. The cultivar 'Purpurea' (red filbert) has deep burgundy foliage and is widely planted in gardens for its distinctive color.

## Nutritional and Culinary Value

Filbert nuts are nutritionally dense, containing approximately 60–65% fat, 12–17% protein, and 10–15% carbohydrates. The fat profile is dominated by monounsaturated oleic acid, similar to olive oil, which contributes to cardiovascular health benefits.

Vitamin E content is notably high at approximately 15–20 mg per 100 g, making filberts one of the richest plant sources of this antioxidant vitamin. The nuts also contain significant amounts of manganese, copper, magnesium, and B vitamins.

In culinary applications, filberts are used extensively in European confectionery. They are a primary ingredient in Nutella, praline, gianduja, and numerous chocolate products. The distinctive flavor profile combines sweetness with a slightly earthy, roasted character.
