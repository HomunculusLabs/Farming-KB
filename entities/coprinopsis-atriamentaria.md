## Overview

Coprinopsis atramentaria, commonly known as the common inkcap or tippler's
bane, is a widely distributed saprotrophic basidiomycete in the family
Psathyrellaceae. It is one of the most recognizable coprinoid fungi due to
its deliquescent gills that dissolve into a black, ink-like liquid at
maturity.

The species is infamous for containing coprine, an amino acid derivative
that causes a disulfiram-like reaction when consumed with alcohol. This
pharmacological interaction has made it one of the best-documented examples
of mushroom-ethanol toxicity in mycology.

Despite this notable hazard, C. atramentaria is considered edible when
prepared without concurrent alcohol consumption and has a long history of
culinary use in Europe.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Originally described by Jean Baptiste François Pierre Bulliard in 1786 as
Agaricus atramentarius, the species was transferred to Coprinus by Persoon
in 1801. Molecular phylogenetics prompted its reclassification to
Coprinopsis by Redhead, Vilgalys and Moncalvo in 2001.

The cap is 3–8 cm in diameter, ovate when young, expanding to campanulate
or broadly convex. The surface is smooth, grey to grey-brown, often with a
silvery sheen when dry. The margin is prominently plicate-striate,
extending nearly to the disc.

Gills are crowded, free, initially white then progressing through grey to
black as deliquescence proceeds from the margin inward. The stipe is
6–17 cm tall and 5–12 mm thick, white, hollow, with a fragile ring zone
that often disappears.

Spores are ellipsoid, 7–10 × 5–6 µm, with a central germ pore, dark brown
in deposit. Basidia are four-spored, and cheilocystidia are numerous,
cylindrical to lageniform.

## Distribution and Habitat

C. atramentaria has a cosmopolitan distribution across temperate zones of
Europe, North America, Asia, and Australia. It fruits from late spring
through autumn, often in large clusters.

The species is strongly saprotrophic, found primarily on disturbed ground,
garden soils, and nutrient-enriched substrates. It has a notable affinity
for sites with buried wood, old tree stumps, and areas amended with manure
or compost.

It frequently appears in urban and suburban settings, including lawns,
parkland, and roadsides. Clusters of 20–50 fruiting bodies are common, and
large trooping groups can number in the hundreds.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

The defining bioactive constituent of C. atramentaria is coprine
(N5-1-hydroxycyclopropyl-L-glutamine), a glutamine derivative with a
unique cyclopropanol ring. Coprine itself is not directly toxic but is
hydrolyzed in vivo to 1-aminocyclopropanol, which inhibits acetaldehyde
dehydrogenase (ALDH).

This ALDH inhibition mirrors the mechanism of disulfiram (Antabuse), the
pharmaceutical used to treat alcohol dependence. When ethanol is consumed
after coprine ingestion, acetaldehyde accumulates rapidly, producing the
characteristic acetaldehyde syndrome: facial flushing, throbbing headache,
palpitations, nausea, vomiting, tachycardia, and hypotension.

Symptoms typically onset within 15–30 minutes of alcohol consumption and
persist for 30–120 minutes. Severe reactions can include respiratory
distress and, rarely, cardiovascular collapse. The sensitivity window
extends from 48–72 hours after mushroom ingestion.

The coprine content varies by developmental stage, geographic population,
and substrate. Young specimens contain higher concentrations, and the
compound is concentrated in the cap and gills.

## Culinary Use and Precautions

C. atramentaria has been consumed as an edible mushroom in Central and
Eastern Europe for centuries, particularly in Poland, Czech Republic, and
Russia. The flavor is mild and agreeable, described as earthy and slightly
nutty.

Safe preparation requires strict abstinence from alcohol for a minimum of
72 hours before and after consumption. The species should be collected
young, before gill deliquescence begins, as both flavor and texture
deteriorate rapidly at maturity.

Confusion with the closely related Coprinus comatus (shaggy inkcap) is a
significant risk. C. comatus is an excellent edible without coprine, but
the two species share similar habitats and phenology. Key distinguishing
features include the shaggy, scaly cap surface of C. comatus versus the
smooth, silvery cap of C. atramentaria.

## Ecological Role

As a primary decomposer of organic matter in disturbed soils, C.
atramentaria plays an important role in nutrient cycling. Its preference
for nutrient-enriched substrates positions it as an early successional
species, colonizing recently disturbed ground before more competitive
fungi establish.

The deliquescent fruiting body is an adaptation for efficient spore
dispersal. As gills liquefy, the resulting ink-like fluid flows down the
stipe and cap, depositing spores in a thin film that dries and is
disseminated by wind and rain splash.
