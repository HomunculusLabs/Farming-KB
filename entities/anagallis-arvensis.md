The species is renowned for its distinctive flowers, which are typically bright scarlet-orange with a darker center. A less common blue-flowered form also occurs naturally in some populations.

Scarlet pimpernel gained lasting cultural fame as the emblem of the fictional character Percival Blakeney in Baroness Orczy's 1905 novel The Scarlet Pimpernel. The plant's habit of closing its flowers in poor weather contributed to its symbolic association with concealment.

Anagallis arvensis contains toxic triterpenoid saponins that can cause gastroenteritis if ingested in quantity. Despite this toxicity, the species has a long history of use in traditional European folk medicine.

The plant is a common weed of arable land, gardens, and disturbed ground across much of the temperate world. It is one of the most widely distributed annual weeds of cultivated fields.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Anagallis arvensis was first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The genus Anagallis was historically placed in its own family, Myrsinaceae, but molecular phylogenetic studies have firmly placed it within Primulaceae.

The generic name derives from the Greek words ana meaning again and agallein meaning to delight, possibly referring to the flowers opening and closing responsively. The specific epithet arvensis means of the fields.

The plant is a low-growing annual with prostrate or ascending stems 10 to 30 centimeters long. Stems are square in cross-section, slender, and freely branched from the base.

Leaves are opposite, sessile, and broadly ovate to elliptic in shape. Each leaf measures 8 to 20 millimeters long with entire margins and a somewhat fleshy texture.

The [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], [[bloomfield-fungal-pathogens-and-parasites]] include downy mildew caused by Peronospora anagallidis, which produces yellowing and necrosis on the upper leaf surface. Rust fungi of the genus Uromyces also infect the species.

The plant serves as an alternate host for certain plant viruses that affect economically important crops. Tomato spotted wilt virus has been detected in natural populations.

## Toxicology

All parts of Anagallis arvensis contain triterpenoid saponins, with the highest concentrations found in the roots and seeds. The primary toxic compounds are anagalligenins and their glycosidic derivatives.
