# Anagallis arvensis

## Overview

Anagallis arvensis, commonly known as scarlet pimpernel, is a small annual plant in the family Primulaceae. It is native to Europe, North Africa, and western Asia and has become widely naturalized across [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] worldwide.

The species is renowned for its distinctive flowers, which are typically bright scarlet-orange with a darker center. A less common blue-flowered form also occurs naturally in some populations.

Scarlet pimpernel gained lasting cultural fame as the emblem of the fictional character Percival Blakeney in Baroness Orczy's 1905 novel The Scarlet Pimpernel. The plant's habit of closing its flowers in poor weather contributed to its symbolic association with concealment.

Anagallis arvensis contains toxic triterpenoid saponins that can cause gastroenteritis if ingested in quantity. Despite this toxicity, the species has a long history of use in traditional European folk medicine.

The plant is a common weed of arable land, gardens, and disturbed ground across much of the temperate world. It is one of the most widely distributed annual weeds of cultivated fields.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Anagallis arvensis was first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The genus Anagallis was historically placed in its own family, Myrsinaceae, but molecular phylogenetic studies have firmly placed it within Primulaceae.

The generic name derives from the Greek words ana meaning again and agallein meaning to delight, possibly referring to the flowers opening and closing responsively. The specific epithet arvensis means of the fields.

The plant is a low-growing annual with prostrate or ascending stems 10 to 30 centimeters long. Stems are square in cross-section, slender, and freely branched from the base.

Leaves are opposite, sessile, and broadly ovate to elliptic in shape. Each leaf measures 8 to 20 millimeters long with entire margins and a somewhat fleshy texture.

The [[aact-foliar-sticker-spreaders-leaf-surface-attachment-ingham]] is smooth or sparsely hairy with conspicuous translucent gland dots. The presence of these pellucid dots is a diagnostic character for the genus Anagallis.

Flowers are solitary in the leaf axils and are borne on slender pedicels. The calyx is deeply five-lobed with lanceolate segments approximately 6 millimeters long.

The corolla is rotate and 8 to 14 millimeters in diameter with five rounded petals. Petals are typically scarlet-orange with a purplish spot at the base.

A naturally occurring blue-flowered form, formerly recognized as Anagallis foemina, is now considered a color morph of the same species. The blue form is more common in Mediterranean populations.

The fruit is a globose capsule approximately 5 millimeters in diameter enclosed by the persistent calyx. Seeds are numerous, small, brown, and angular with a rough surface.

## Distribution and Habitat

Anagallis arvensis is native to Europe, the Mediterranean basin, and western Asia. It has been introduced to virtually every temperate region of the world through human activity.

The species is now naturalized across [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], southern Africa, Australia, and New Zealand. It is particularly common in agricultural landscapes worldwide.

The plant occupies a wide range of open, disturbed habitats including arable fields, gardens, vineyards, waste ground, and roadsides. It is tolerant of a broad spectrum of soil types and conditions.

In its native range, scarlet pimpernel is characteristic of annual weed communities on light, sandy, or gravelly soils. It is a component of the arable weed flora of cereal crops.

The species is an early colonizer of disturbed ground and is frequently one of the first species to appear on newly exposed soil. Seed longevity in the soil seed bank can exceed 20 years.

Anagallis arvensis occurs from sea level to approximately 1500 meters in elevation in southern Europe. It is intolerant of deep shade and is absent from closed-canopy habitats.

The plant is drought-tolerant once established and can complete its life cycle in areas with relatively low rainfall. This adaptability contributes to its success as a cosmopolitan weed.

## Ecology

The flowers of Anagallis arvensis open in the morning and close in the evening, and also close during dull or rainy weather. This nyctinastic response is mediated by turgor changes in specialized cells at the base of the petals.

This closing behavior is so reliable that the plant was traditionally used as a weather indicator by farmers and shepherds. The common name poor man's weather-glass reflects this folk use.

Pollination is carried out by small bees, hoverflies, and other generalist insects attracted to the nectar. Self-pollination can also occur if cross-pollination fails.

The small seeds are dispersed by wind, water, and human activities. Seeds adhere to soil particles and are readily transported on farm machinery and animal fur.

In agricultural settings, the species is primarily a weed of spring-sown crops. It competes minimally with established crops due to its low growth habit.

Several insect herbivores have been recorded on Anagallis arvensis. The flea beetle Longitarsus parvulus feeds on the foliage in European populations.

[[bloomfield-fungal-pathogens-and-parasites]] include downy mildew caused by Peronospora anagallidis, which produces yellowing and necrosis on the upper leaf surface. Rust fungi of the genus Uromyces also infect the species.

The plant serves as an alternate host for certain plant viruses that affect economically important crops. Tomato spotted wilt virus has been detected in natural populations.

## Toxicology

All parts of Anagallis arvensis contain triterpenoid saponins, with the highest concentrations found in the roots and seeds. The primary toxic compounds are anagalligenins and their glycosidic derivatives.
