## Overview

Taxodium mucronatum, commonly known as Montezuma cypress or ahuehuete, is a large coniferous tree native to Mexico and Central America.

It is the national tree of Mexico and holds profound [[taxodium-distichum]] but is now widely accepted as distinct.

Key differences from Taxodium distichum include semi-evergreen foliage and a more southern distribution.

**Growth form** is a large to massive deciduous to semi-evergreen tree reaching 25-40 meters in height.

Exceptional specimens exceed 50 meters in height with trunk diameters of 10-14 meters.

The crown is broadly conical to spreading, becoming irregular and open with great age.

Bark is thick, reddish-brown to gray, deeply fissured into long, fibrous ridges.

Leaves are spirally arranged, 5-15 mm long, 1-2 mm wide, lanceolate and slightly flattened.

Foliage is deciduous in northern populations but semi-evergreen to evergreen in tropical lowlands.

Unlike Taxodium distichum, the leaves are not strictly two-ranked on the branchlets.

Male pollen cones are small, 3-5 mm long, borne in pendulous catkins 5-10 cm long.

Female seed cones are spherical, 15-25 mm in diameter, composed of 6-12 woody, peltate scales.

Cones are green at maturity, turning brown, and disintegrate to release 12-24 triangular seeds.

## Distribution and Habitat

Taxodium mucronatum is native to Mexico and extends southward through Guatemala, Belize, and Honduras.

In Mexico it occurs from the states of Sonora and Chihuahua southward through the central highlands.

The species is found along riverbanks, in canyons, and in seasonally flooded riparian forests.

It grows from 300 to 2,400 meters elevation in mountain valleys and plateaus.

The largest populations occur along the Rio Grande watershed in Nuevo Leon and Tamaulipas.

It is commonly associated with riparian [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-vegetation-types-mesophytic-cloud-forest]] edges.

Companion species include [[platanus-occidentalis]], and [[bloomfield-birds-nest-fungi-peridiole-splash-herbivore-dispersal]] in the branches and mammals use the hollow trunks of ancient specimens for shelter.

The root systems stabilize streambanks and reduce erosion in mountainous watersheds.

In [[shroom-mesoamerican-civilization]] the tree was sacred to the Aztec god Quetzalcoatl.
