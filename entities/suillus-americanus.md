# Suillus americanus

**American Slipper Jack** | *Boletus americanus* Peck

Suillus americanus is a pored mushroom in Suillaceae, known as a mycorrhizal
associate of five-needle pines across eastern and central North America.
It produces medium-sized fruiting bodies with a viscid orange-yellow cap and a
membranous partial veil ring on the stipe. Though edible, it is not widely
collected due to soft, bland flesh. It serves as an indicator species for
healthy pine forest ecosystems and has been studied for its role in fungal
succession after disturbance.

## Taxonomy

Basidiomycota → Agaricomycetes → Boletales → **Suillaceae → *Suillus* → *S.
americanus***

Described by Charles Horton Peck in 1888 as *Boletus americanus* from New
York. Transferred to *Suillus* by Otto Kuntze in 1898. Belongs to the *S.
luteus* clade, characterized by glandular dots on the stipe and associations
with Pinus subsection *Strobus*. Some older guides treated it as a variety of
*S. granulatus*, but molecular phylogenetics confirms it as distinct.

## Morphology

- **Cap:** 3–10 cm, broadly convex to plane; viscid when wet; bright
  yellow-orange to amber; cuticle easily peels.
- **Pore surface:** Adnexed to decurrent; tubes 5–12 mm; pores angular,
  1–2/mm, pale yellow aging to ochre; slowly bruises brownish.
- **Stipe:** 4–8 × 0.8–1.5 cm, central, equal; dry, finely fibrillose,
  pale yellow above ring, brownish below; conspicuous glandular dots
  exude milky fluid when young; superior membranous ring from partial veil.
- **Flesh:** Pale yellow, soft and spongy; unchanging or slowly brown when
  cut; mild odor and taste.
- **[[ectomycorrhizal-associations]] with five-needle pines, facilitating
nutrient exchange between fungal mycelium and tree roots. The hyphal mantle
extends root absorptive surface area, improving phosphorus and nitrogen
uptake for the host. In post-fire and post-logging succession studies, *S.
americanus* is among the first Suillus species to colonize disturbed sites,
appearing within 2–3 years of stand establishment. Its spores persist in
soil, forming a resilient bank that responds to compatible pine roots.
Rodents and slugs consume fruiting bodies, aiding [[suillus-granulatus]]** — Lacks partial veil and ring; pores exude milky
  droplets when young; associated with two-needle pines.
