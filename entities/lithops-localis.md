## Overview

Lithops localis, formerly known as Lithops terricolor, is a small stem-succulent in the Aizoaceae family. Native to the Great Karoo of South Africa, it is one of the most distinctive "living stone" species in the genus.

The species is characterized by its truncate, slightly convex leaf pairs that are grey-green to brownish in color. The upper surface bears fine, branching grooves and numerous tiny dark-green dots (windows) that allow light to penetrate the buried photosynthetic tissue.

Flowers are bright yellow, 2–3 cm in diameter, emerging from the fissure between the leaf pairs in late summer to autumn. The species is among the earlier-flowering Lithops, often blooming in February–March in the Southern Hemisphere.

Lithops localis is popular among succulent collectors for its compact size and attractive patterning. It is considered one of the easier Lithops species to maintain in cultivation.

The species was first described by N.E. Brown in 1914 based on material collected near Graaff-Reinet in the Eastern Cape.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Angiosperms — Eudicots — Caryophyllales
- **Family:** Aizoaceae (subfamily Ruschioideae)
- **Genus:** Lithops
- **Species:** Lithops localis (N.E.Br.) Schwantes
- **Synonyms:** Lithops terricolor (N.E.Br.) N.E.Br., Mesembryanthemum localis N.E.Br.

Each plant consists of a single pair of thick, fleshy leaves that are almost entirely buried in the soil. Only the flattened upper surface is exposed, typically measuring 2–3 cm wide by 1.5–2 cm tall.

The leaf surface color ranges from grey-green to brownish-pink, closely matching surrounding quartzite pebbles. This cryptic coloration provides effective camouflage against herbivores.

The upper surface features a network of fine, branching channels (grooves) dotted with numerous translucent green window spots. These windows channel sunlight to the buried chlorophyll-bearing tissue.

Flowers are daisy-like, bright yellow with a white center, 2–3 cm across. They open in late afternoon and close at dusk, persisting for five to seven days.

Fruit is a five-lobed capsule that opens when wetted (hygrochastic), releasing tiny seeds. Seed capsules may remain viable for several years in dry conditions.

## Distribution and Habitat

Lithops localis is endemic to the Great Karoo region of South Africa. Its range extends from Graaff-Reinet in the Eastern Cape northward into the southern Free State.

The species occurs on flat, open terrain at elevations of 900–1,500 meters. Plants grow partially buried in gravelly, quartzitic soils among pebbles that they closely resemble.

The habitat receives 150–300 mm of annual rainfall, predominantly in summer (December–March). Winters are cold and dry with frequent frost, and summer temperatures regularly exceed 35°C.

Lithops localis grows in exposed positions with no shade from other vegetation. The surrounding plant community consists of sparse Karoo shrubland with low grasses and scattered dwarf shrubs.

Populations are typically small and localized, restricted to specific geological formations. Several subspecies and varieties have been described based on subtle differences in leaf patterning and flower size.

The species is part of the L. localis complex, which includes several morphologically similar taxa. Distinguishing between them requires examination of leaf surface texture, flower size, and capsule structure.

Plants in the southern part of the range tend to have more pronounced dark window dots. Northern populations are generally greyer with less contrasting markings.

## Cultivation

Lithops localis requires a well-drained, mineral-based potting mix with minimal organic content. A mix of coarse sand, perlite, and a small amount of loam provides ideal growing conditions.

Water only during the active growing season (spring to early autumn) and cease completely during winter dormancy. Overwatering is the single most common cause of death in cultivation.

Plants need strong light to maintain compact growth and authentic coloration. Insufficient light causes etiolation (stretching) and loss of the characteristic flat-topped profile.

Temperature range should reflect the natural Karoo climate: warm summers (25–35°C) and cool winters (5–15°C). A period of winter cold helps initiate proper spring growth cycles.

Repot only when the plant has outgrown its container, typically every three to four years. Handle carefully as the brittle roots break easily during repotting.

Propagation is primarily from seed, sown in spring on the surface of a fine, sandy mix. Seedlings require careful misting and protection from strong sun during their first year.

Vegetative propagation is not possible for Lithops species, as each plant produces only one leaf pair per growth cycle. Division of clumps (multiple heads) is the only vegetative option.

## Conservation

Lithops localis is listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. However, several populations face pressure from illegal collection by succulent enthusiasts.

The species occurs in some protected areas including the Karoo National Park. Populations on private farmland are generally secure due to the plant's cryptic nature and low impact on agriculture.

Climate change poses a long-term threat through altered rainfall patterns in the Karoo. Reduced summer rainfall could shrink suitable habitat and increase drought stress on wild populations.

CITES Appendix II listing regulates international trade in wild-collected specimens. Most commercial plants are nursery-grown from seed, reducing pressure on wild populations.

## Ecology

The cryptic stone-mimicry of Lithops localis is a defense against herbivory by tortoises, ostriches, and small mammals. The species is virtually invisible when growing among quartzite pebbles in its natural habitat.

Window-like translucent spots on the leaf surface allow photosynthesis to occur in the buried leaf tissue. This adaptation reduces water loss by keeping most of the plant below the soil surface.

Flowers are pollinated by small bees and flies attracted to the yellow petals and sweet scent. The late afternoon opening time coincides with peak insect activity in the Karoo.

Seed dispersal relies on rain splash: the hygrochastic capsule opens when wetted, flinging seeds outward. This mechanism disperses seeds only a few centimeters from the parent plant.

The species survives extreme drought by storing water in its succulent leaves. During prolonged dry periods, the old leaf pair shrivels as a new pair develops within, drawing on stored reserves.

Growth cycles are triggered by seasonal rainfall patterns in the Karoo. New leaf pairs emerge after summer rains, with the old leaves gradually drying and forming a protective sheath.

Individual plants can live for 20–30 years in ideal conditions. Multi-headed clumps form slowly as new leaf pairs occasionally produce two growth points instead of one.
