## Description

The thermal belt is a zone of moderated temperature found on mid-slope positions of hills and mountains, situated above the cold air drainage of valley floors and below the exposed, windswept conditions of ridge tops. It is one of the most important microclimatic features for site selection in [[permaculture]] and agriculture.

## Classification

- **Category:** Microclimatic phenomenon
- **Domain:** [[permaculture-design]], horticulture, architecture
- **Also known as:** Thermal zone, frost-free zone, mid-slope thermal band
- **Related concepts:** Temperature inversion, cold air drainage, adiabatic lapse rate

## Key Facts

### Formation Mechanism

The thermal belt forms because cold air, being denser than warm air, drains downhill at night via gravity and pools in valleys and hollows. Meanwhile, ridge tops lose heat rapidly through radiation to the open sky. The mid-slope position benefits from:

1. **Cold air drainage below:** Dense cold air slides past downward.
2. **Reduced sky view factor:** The hill mass behind limits radiant heat loss.
3. **Warm air advection:** Gentle upslope daytime heating creates a reservoir of warmth.

### Typical Elevations

- **Hill country and mountains:** 1,000-5,000 m (3,280-16,400 ft) elevation.
- **Lower hill slopes:** 100-200 m (330-650 ft) above valley floor.
- **Hot deserts:** Frost-free thermal belt may extend only 10-15 m (33-49 ft) up the slopes of mesas.

### Design Implications

Siting houses and gardens on sun-facing slopes within the thermal belt is described by Mollison as a critical energy-conservation strategy in all but tropical climates. Sun-facing slopes in the thermal belt absorb more solar radiation, drain cold air at night, lie below chilly hilltops, and sit above the cold night air pooled in valleys.

The shaded side of hills accentuates cold, reduces evaporation, and supports very different plant communities compared to the sun-facing thermal belt.

### Observation Methods

- **Local anecdotes:** Long-time residents often know where frost occurs.
- **Existing vegetation:** Frost-sensitive species indicate frost-free zones.
- **Trial plantings:** Deliberate testing of frost-susceptible species to map the belt.

## Relevance to Cultivation

1. **House siting:** Maximizes passive solar heating while minimizing frost exposure and wind chill.
2. **Frost-sensitive crops:** Subtropical and tropical species can be grown at higher latitudes when placed within the thermal belt.
3. **Energy savings:** Homes in the thermal belt require significantly less heating fuel.
4. **Orchard planning:** Fruit trees prone to blossom frost damage are best located within this zone.
5. **Animal husbandry:** Livestock sheltered in the thermal belt avoid the worst of cold stress and frost.
