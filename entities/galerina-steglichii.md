# Galerina steglichii

*Galerina steglichii* is a rare species of wood-inhabiting mushroom in the family cortinariaceae (or hymenogastraceae) that has been reported to contain [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]]. It is one of only a handful of Galerina species found to contain psychoactive indole alkaloids, making it a scientifically noteworthy but potentially dangerous species. The genus Galerina is primarily notorious for containing deadly poisonous species that produce amatoxins, the same toxins found in [[amanita-phalloides-death-cap]] (the death cap). This dual identity — some species hallucinogenic, others lethal — makes Galerina one of the most important genera for foragers and mycologists to understand thoroughly.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Cortinariaceae (or Hymenogastraceae)
- **Genus:** Galerina

Galerina steglichii was described relatively recently and is named in honor of Wolfgang Steglich, the German chemist who contributed significantly to the study of fungal secondary metabolites. The species was identified during chemical screening programs that analyzed Galerina species for their alkaloid content. It was included in Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz's (1998) worldwide survey as the only Galerina species confirmed to contain psilocybin, alongside Galerina species that are noted for their deadly amatoxin content.

The taxonomy of Galerina is complex, with approximately 300 described species worldwide. Molecular phylogenetic studies have shown that the genus is polyphyletic, and several species groups have been proposed for reclassification. The small brown mushrooms of Galerina, psilocybe, and conocybe are notoriously difficult to distinguish morphologically, and microscopic examination is usually required for accurate identification.

## Morphology

### Cap
The pileus is small, typically measuring 1 to 3 cm in diameter, broadly convex to plane with age. The cap surface is hygrophanous, brown to reddish-brown when moist and pale tan to buff when dry. It may be slightly striate at the margin when wet. The surface is smooth to finely fibrillose. Unlike some better-known Galerina species, G. steglichii does not have a prominently viscid or glutinous cap surface.

### Gills
The gills are adnate to adnexed or slightly sinuate, close, and narrow. They are initially pale brown, becoming rusty brown to ferruginous as spores mature. The rusty-brown spore color is a key feature of the genus Galerina, shared with Gymnopilus and some Cortinarius species. The gill edges may be whitish and slightly fimbriate.

### Stem
The stipe is slender, 3 to 6 cm long and 1 to 3 mm thick, cylindrical, and equal. It is pale to brownish, often with a slightly lighter apex. The surface may be pruinose or finely fibrillose. A membranous annulus (ring) is typically present, though it may be fragile and easily lost. The annulus is an important feature that helps distinguish Galerina from many similar-looking small brown mushrooms.

### Flesh and Spores
The flesh is thin, watery, and pale brownish. The odor and taste are not well documented but are generally mild. The spore print is rusty brown to ferruginous. Basidiospores are ellipsoid to amygdaliform, with a somewhat roughened surface and a distinct apical germ pore. Spore dimensions and ornamentation patterns are critical for species-level identification within Galerina.

## Distribution and Habitat

Galerina steglichii is a rare species with a limited number of documented collections. It has been reported from Europe, where most of the initial collections and chemical analyses were made. The species is saprotrophic, growing on decaying wood, wood debris, and woody litter, particularly in coniferous forests. It may also be found on moss-covered wood and among forest floor debris.

Due to its rarity and inconspicuous appearance, G. steglichii is likely undercollected and may have a wider distribution than currently documented. The species fruits in autumn in temperate regions.

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds

Galerina steglichii is notable for being one of the few Galerina species confirmed to contain [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]] rather than the deadly amatoxins characteristic of most other species in the genus. This chemical distinction is critically important:

**Psychoactive compounds detected:**
- [[psilocybin]] — the phosphorylated prodrug that is converted to psilocin in the body
- [[psilocin]] — the active psychoactive compound, a serotonin 5-HT2A receptor agonist

**Toxic compounds NOT detected:**
- Alpha-amanitin — the primary amatoxin found in deadly Galerina species
- Beta-amanitin — a secondary amatoxin
- Phalloidin — a phallotoxin that disrupts cell membranes

The concentrations of psilocybin and psilocin in G. steglichii have not been extensively quantified, but available analyses suggest moderate levels, lower than those found in potent species like [[psilocybe-azurescens]] or [[copelandia-cyanescens]] but comparable to some psilocybe species.

The evolutionary question of why some Galerina species produce psilocybin while closely related species produce amatoxins is of considerable scientific interest. It has been suggested that the two biosynthetic pathways may have evolved independently, with different selective pressures driving the production of compounds that serve distinct ecological functions, potentially related to defense against herbivory or competition with other microorganisms.

## Danger: The Amatoxin Risk

The most important public health message regarding Galerina steglichii is the extreme danger of confusion with amatoxin-containing Galerina species. Several Galerina species contain lethal concentrations of amatoxins:

- **[[galerina-autumnalis]]** (also known as Galerina marginata) — one of the most deadly mushrooms in North America and Europe
- **Galerina venenata** — a small grassland species containing amatoxins
- **Galerina fasciculata** — found in clusters on wood, resembling some psilocybe species

Amatoxin poisoning causes delayed-onset liver and kidney failure that is frequently fatal without aggressive medical intervention, including liver transplantation. The symptoms follow a characteristic pattern:

1. **Latent phase (6 to 24 hours):** No symptoms, creating a false sense of security
2. **Gastrointestinal phase (24 to 48 hours):** Severe nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain
3. **Apparent recovery phase (48 to 72 hours):** Temporary improvement as liver damage progresses silently
4. **Hepatic failure phase (3 to 7 days):** Liver failure, jaundice, coagulopathy, hepatic encephalopathy, death

There is NO reliable visual way to distinguish psilocybin-containing Galerina species from amatoxin-containing ones. Microscopic examination and chemical analysis are required. **No Galerina species should ever be consumed for psychoactive purposes without definitive chemical identification.**

## Similar Species

The small brown habit of Galerina steglichii makes it superficially similar to numerous other small brown mushrooms:

- **[[psilocybe-semilanceata]]**: Grows in grasslands (not on wood), has a conical cap, and has a dark purple-brown spore print (not rusty brown)
- **[[conocybe-cyanopus]]**: Also contains psilocybin but has a conical cap and different spore ornamentation
- **[[galerina-autumnalis]]**: Visually very similar but contains lethal amatoxins — this is the most dangerous confusion possible
- **Gymnopilus species**: Larger and more orange, with a distinctly bitter taste
- **Mycena species**: Typically more fragile with white spores

## Research Significance

Galerina steglichii is of particular interest to mycologists and chemists because it represents an evolutionary bridge point between two very different chemical strategies within the same genus. Understanding the biosynthetic pathways that produce psilocybin in some Galerina species and amatoxins in others could provide insights into:

- The evolution of fungal secondary metabolism
- The ecological functions of fungal toxins and psychoactive compounds
- The genetic basis for the divergent biosynthetic pathways
- Potential pharmaceutical applications of the compounds involved

The species also serves as a cautionary example in mycological education, illustrating why accurate identification to species level is essential when dealing with mushrooms that could be either psychoactive or lethal.

## Conservation Status

Galerina steglichii is not formally assessed for conservation status, but as a rare species with limited documented collections, it may warrant monitoring. The general decline of dead wood in managed European forests poses a potential threat to all wood-inhabiting fungi. Conservation of dead wood habitats through retention of fallen logs and standing dead trees in forests would benefit G. steglichii along with many other saprotrophic species.

## Historical and Etymological Notes

The species is named for Professor Wolfgang Steglich (born 1933), a distinguished German natural products chemist at the University of Bonn and later the Technical University of Munich. Steglich made major contributions to the chemistry of fungal metabolites, including the isolation and characterization of psychoactive compounds from numerous mushroom species. His work on the chemistry of psilocybe species and related genera was foundational to the modern understanding of fungal indole alkaloids. The naming of G. steglichii in his honor reflects the importance of his contributions to mycochemistry.

The broader context of Galerina taxonomy has been complicated by the small, nondescript appearance of most species in the genus. Many early descriptions were based on limited material, and the advent of molecular phylogenetics has revealed that the genus, as traditionally defined, is polyphyletic. This has led to proposals for splitting the genus into several smaller, monophyletic groups, though these changes have not yet been universally adopted.

## Seasonality and Collection Notes

Galerina steglichii is an autumn-fruiting species in temperate regions, typically appearing from September through November. Due to its rarity, it is unlikely to be encountered by casual foragers. When searching for Galerina species on wood, the following precautions are essential:

- Never consume any Galerina species without definitive chemical analysis
- The rusty-brown spore print alone does not distinguish between psilocybin and amatoxin species
- A microscope is required for species-level identification
- When in doubt, leave the specimen untouched

## Legal Status

Galerina steglichii is not specifically regulated. However, its content of [[psilocybin]] could subject it to the same legal restrictions as other psilocybin-containing fungi in jurisdictions where such laws apply. Given the extreme danger of misidentification with amatoxin-containing species, possession for consumption purposes would be exceptionally ill-advised regardless of legal status.
