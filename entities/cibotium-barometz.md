## Overview

**Cibotium barometz** (L.) J.Sm., commonly called the **vegetable lamb fern**, golden chicken fern, or East Asian tree fern, is a striking member of the Dicksoniaceae native to subtropical and tropical Asia. It is renowned for the dense golden-brown hair covering its trunk and crown, historically interpreted as a mythical "vegetable lamb" in medieval European folklore.

The species is listed on **CITES Appendix II**, reflecting international concern about overharvest of its hairy trunk scales for traditional medicine. Its trunk hairs have been traded commercially for centuries as a wound-healing and hemostatic remedy across East and Southeast Asia.

This fern reaches heights of 1–3 meters with a prostrate or erect trunk and large bipinnate fronds extending 2–3 meters in length. It thrives in humid montane forests and shaded ravines from northeastern India through southern China to peninsular Malaysia.

The species has considerable cultural and economic importance beyond its ornamental value. Its CITES listing reflects ongoing concerns that wild populations cannot sustain current harvest levels of medicinal trunk hairs.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Dicksoniaceae → Cyatheales → Polypodiopsida. The genus *Cibotium* contains approximately 11 species distributed across Asia, Hawaii, and Central America. *Cibotium barometz* was originally described by Linnaeus as *Polypodium barometz* in 1753.

It was later transferred to *Cibotium* by J. Smith in 1842. The specific epithet *barometz* derives from the Persian word for the legendary "vegetable lamb" of Tartary.

Synonyms include *Polypodium barometz* L., *Aspidium barometz* (L.) Willd., and *Dicksonia barometz* (L.) Link. The species is closely related to *Cibotium chamissoi* of Hawaii and *Cibotium glaucum* of the same archipelago.

The trunk is decumbent to erect, 1–3 m tall, densely covered with long golden-brown silky hairs (setae) up to 10 cm in length. These hairs are among the most distinctive features of the species and the primary target of commercial harvest.

Fronds are bipinnate to tripinnate, 2–3 m long, with a stout straw-colored stipe bearing dark brown basal scales. The lamina is tripinnate at the base, with pinnae 40–80 cm long.

Pinnules are oblong-lanceolate with serrate margins and obtuse tips. Sori are borne on the margins of reduced pinnule segments, enclosed in two types of indusia: an outer firm valve and an inner delicate lip characteristic of the Dicksoniaceae.

Spores are trilete, tetrahedral-globose, 40–55 μm in diameter, with a granulate perispore surface. The chromosome number is 2n = 136, consistent with other polyploid members of the Dicksoniaceae.

The caudex (trunk) is not a true stem but a vertical rhizome surrounded by a dense mantle of adventitious roots. This root mantle provides structural support and moisture absorption, a feature shared with other tree ferns in the order Cyatheales.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Cibotium barometz* occurs across a broad swath of subtropical and tropical Asia. Its range extends from **northeastern India** (Assam, Sikkim) through **Myanmar**, **Thailand**, **Vietnam**, **Laos**, and **southern China** (Yunnan, Guangxi, Guangdong, Guizhou, Hainan, Fujian, Taiwan).

The distribution continues southward to **peninsular Malaysia** and **Indonesia** (Sumatra). Throughout this range, populations are often localized to specific montane habitats rather than being continuously distributed.

It grows in **montane forests** between 200–1800 m elevation, favoring shaded ravines, stream banks, and steep forested slopes with high humidity. The species prefers acidic, well-drained soils rich in organic matter.

It often establishes on mossy banks and rocky outcrops in old-growth forest understories. Populations are fragmented due to habitat loss from deforestation and agricultural expansion, particularly in lowland areas of southern China.

The species is now rare or locally extirpated in parts of its range where collection pressure is heaviest. Several Chinese provinces report significant population declines over the past 50 years.

Conservation assessments in China categorize the species as **Vulnerable** at the national level. The combination of habitat loss and medicinal harvest represents a dual threat that has reduced many formerly robust populations to scattered remnants.

## Ecology

As an understory fern, *Cibotium barometz* plays a significant role in montane forest structure and microhabitat creation. Its large spreading fronds create shaded microenvironments that support diverse epiphyte communities.

These communities include mosses, liverworts, and small filmy ferns on the frond surfaces and trunk. The golden trunk hairs provide nesting material and shelter for invertebrates, particularly beetles and springtails.

The fibrous root mantle at the trunk base harbors mycorrhizal fungi and provides germination sites for other fern spores and bryophytes. It functions as a **nurse substrate** in the forest understory, facilitating establishment of other plant species.

Spore dispersal is wind-mediated, with peak spore release during the dry season when humidity drops sufficiently for sporangia to dehisce. Like most tree ferns, it shows slow growth rates.

Trunk elongation averages 1–3 cm per year under favorable conditions. Individual plants may live for several decades, with some specimens estimated at over 50 years old.

The species shows limited capacity for vegetative reproduction. Unlike some *Dicksonia* species that produce offsets from the base, *Cibotium barometz* relies entirely on spore production for recruitment, making population recovery from disturbance slow.

## Cultivation

*Cibotium barometz* is cultivated as an ornamental fern in subtropical and tropical gardens worldwide. It requires **consistent moisture**, **high humidity**, and **shade to partial shade** for optimal growth.

Plants tolerate brief temperature drops to −2°C but are damaged by prolonged frost. In cultivation, the trunk should never be allowed to dry out completely.

Regular overhead watering maintains the humidity that the trunk hairs and surface roots require. Well-drained, humus-rich acidic soil is essential; waterlogged conditions promote root rot and crown decay.

Propagation is exclusively by **spores**, as the species does not produce offsets or suckers. Spore germination occurs on sterile peat or finely chopped sphagnum under high humidity at 20–25°C.

Prothalli appear in 4–8 weeks, with juvenile fronds developing after 3–6 months. Growth from spore to a plant with a visible trunk takes 5–8 years under ideal conditions.

Container-grown specimens perform well in large pots with a mix of coarse bark, peat, and perlite. The golden trunk hairs make this species particularly attractive as a specimen plant in shaded courtyards and ferneries.

## Ethnobotany and Uses

The **golden hairs** (golden wool, *jinmao gouji*) of *Cibotium barometz* have been used in traditional medicine for over 1500 years. In **Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)**, the hairs are classified as a hemostatic and wound-healing agent.

They are applied externally to stop bleeding from cuts, wounds, and surgical sites. Phytochemical studies have identified **flavonoids**, **phenolic acids**, and **protocatechuic acid** in the trunk hairs.

These compounds exhibit anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity in vitro. Extracts demonstrate measurable hemostatic effects in animal models, supporting the traditional use.

The "vegetable lamb" legend made *Cibotium barometz* one of the most famous plants in European botanical mythology. Medieval travelers to the Far East described a plant that grew sheep as fruit, connected to the earth by a stalk.

The golden-haired trunk, emerging from a prostrate base, was imagined to be this mythical creature. The legend persisted in European natural history from the 11th to the 18th century.

In parts of Southeast Asia, the starchy trunk pith is occasionally eaten as a famine food. The young uncurled fronds (fiddleheads) are consumed in some regions, though they require thorough cooking to remove potential toxins.

CITES Appendix II listing now regulates international trade in wild-harvested material. Legal exports require permits documenting sustainable sourcing.

Despite CITES protection, illegal harvesting continues in parts of southern China and Southeast Asia. Cultivation for medicinal supply remains limited, with most commercial material still sourced from wild populations.
