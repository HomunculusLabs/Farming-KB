## Overview

**Cornus florida**, the flowering dogwood, is a deciduous understory tree native to [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America. It is one of the most celebrated ornamental trees in temperate landscapes, prized for its large white bracts, red autumn foliage, and bright red drupes.

The species is the state tree of Missouri and the state flower of Virginia and [[north-carolina-mycodiversity]]. It occupies a central place in the ecology of eastern deciduous forests as both a food source and structural component of the understory.

In the horticultural trade, flowering dogwood is one of the top-selling ornamental trees in eastern North America. Its multi-season interest — spring flowers, summer foliage, autumn color, and winter bark texture — makes it exceptionally versatile in designed landscapes.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*C. florida* was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. It belongs to the family Cornaceae, which contains approximately 85 species across two genera. The specific epithet *florida* means "flowering," distinguishing it from less showy relatives.

Mature trees typically reach 6–9 meters in height with a crown spread of 6–10 meters. The trunk is short, often leaning, with distinctive alligator-hide bark that breaks into small rectangular blocks.

In exceptional sites, trees may reach 12–15 meters with trunk diameters of 30–40 cm. Champion trees have been recorded at heights exceeding 18 meters. Maximum age is approximately 125 years, though most trees in managed landscapes live 40–80 years.

The branching pattern is characteristically horizontal, producing a broad, flat-topped crown. This distinctive architecture makes dogwood recognizable even in winter, when the bark and branch pattern are the primary identification features.

Leaves are opposite, simple, ovate to elliptic, 6–13 cm long and 3–6 cm wide. Leaf margins are entire, and the upper surface is dark green with a lighter underside. Autumn color is a reliable deep red to purplish-red.

The conspicuous "flowers" are actually four showy bracts, 3–5 cm long, that subtend the small true flower head. Bracts are typically white but pink and red forms occur naturally and in cultivation.

The true flowers are tiny, greenish-yellow, arranged in a dense head 12–18 mm across. Each flower has four petals, four stamens, and a single pistil. Flowering occurs in early spring, before or with leaf emergence.

Fruit is a fleshy drupe 8–12 mm long, bright scarlet to orange-red at maturity. Each drupe contains a single hard seed. The fruit ripens in late summer to early fall and persists until consumed by wildlife.

Individual trees may produce 10,000–50,000 drupes in a mast year. Masting behavior is irregular, with heavy fruit crops occurring every 3–5 years. Seeds are primarily dispersed by birds.

The wood is extremely hard, dense, and fine-grained, with a specific gravity of approximately 0.75. It has been used for weaving shuttle blocks, golf club heads, tool handles, and small turned objects.

## Distribution and Habitat

*C. florida* ranges from southern Maine west to southern Ontario and Michigan, south to eastern Texas and northern Florida. It is absent from the Gulf Coastal Plain except for disjunct populations.

These disjunct populations in the Florida panhandle and southern Alabama are genetically distinct and may represent relicts from a broader pre-Pleistocene distribution.

The species grows as an understory tree in mixed hardwood forests, often on well-drained, acidic soils. It is most abundant on north- and east-facing slopes with rich loam and partial shade.

Elevation ranges from near sea level to approximately 900 meters in the Appalachian Mountains. It is sensitive to drought and heat, which limits its distribution in the southeastern coastal plain.

The tree tolerates full sun in cooler climates but requires partial shade in warmer regions. Urban heat islands can stress established trees, leading to reduced vigor and increased susceptibility to disease.

*C. florida* is considered a climax understory species but also colonizes forest edges and disturbed sites. It grows slowly, typically adding 15–30 cm of height per year under optimal conditions.

The species forms [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] with [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] fungi, which enhance nutrient uptake in the shaded understory. These associations are particularly important for [[comparison-phosphorus-solubilizing-microorganisms-vs-mycorrhizal-phosphorus-acquisition]] on acidic forest soils.

## Ecology

The drupes are a critical food resource for at least 36 species of birds and numerous mammals. Wild turkey, northern cardinal, robin, and cedar waxwing are among the most frequent avian consumers. White-tailed deer, gray fox, and black bear also feed on the fruit.

Deer browsing of seedlings and saplings is intense and represents a major recruitment bottleneck. In forests with high deer density, dogwood regeneration is often severely limited, shifting the age structure toward older, senescing individuals.

Exclosure studies have demonstrated dramatic recovery of dogwood recruitment when deer are excluded. In one long-term study, dogwood seedling density increased 40-fold within 15 years of deer exclusion in a Pennsylvania forest.

The foliage serves as a larval host for the spring azure butterfly (*Celastrina ladon*) and several moth species. The flowers provide nectar and pollen for early-season native bees and flies when few other woody plants are in bloom.

Fallen dogwood leaves decompose rapidly and contribute significant calcium to the forest floor. This [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] function enhances soil fertility and benefits neighboring plants.

The root system is shallow and spreading, typically concentrated in the upper 30 cm of soil. This shallow rooting makes the species sensitive to drought and soil compaction from construction or heavy foot traffic.

## Diseases and Conservation

Dogwood anthracnose, caused by the fungus *Discula destructiva*, is the most serious threat to *C. florida*. First identified in the late 1970s, the disease has killed millions of trees across the [[bolete-fungi-appalachian-region]] and spread throughout most of the species' range.

The pathogen infects leaves, twigs, and trunk, causing tan leaf spots with purple margins, twig dieback, and cankers on the main stem. Trees in moist, shaded, high-elevation sites are most severely affected.

The disease spreads through rain-splashed spores and is most virulent during cool, wet spring conditions. Trees growing in full sun or on dry sites generally suffer less damage than those in deep shade.

[[weed-management-strategies]] include pruning infected branches, improving air circulation, and applying fungicides. Resistant selections, particularly the 'Appalachian Spring' cultivar, have been developed from surviving trees in the Great Smoky Mountains.

The 'Appalachian Spring' cultivar was discovered as a surviving specimen in the Cades Cove area of the Great Smoky Mountains National Park. It has shown exceptional resistance to anthracnose in long-term field trials and is now widely available in the nursery trade.
