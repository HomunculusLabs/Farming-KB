# Mycorrhiza

## Description

A mycorrhiza (literally "fungus root") is a mutualistic symbiotic association between a fungus and the roots of a higher plant. Approximately 90% of all higher plant species form mycorrhizal associations, making it one of the most widespread symbioses on Earth. In this relationship, the fungus benefits from a direct carbohydrate supply from the plant, while the host plant benefits from increased efficiency of nutrient uptake. Mycorrhizal fungi also provide protection against root pathogens and influence plant fitness in subtle ways beyond simple nutrition.

## Classification

- **Type**: Biological symbiosis / ecological relationship
- **Literal meaning**: "Fungus root"
- **Prevalence**: ~90% of all higher plant species
- **Benefits to plant**: Nutrient uptake, pathogen protection, enhanced fitness
- **Benefits to fungus**: Direct carbohydrate supply from host plant
- **Key references**: Pirozynski and Malloch (1975), Harley (1969), Smith and Read (1997)

## Key Facts

- Named literally "fungus root" from the Greek
- Some 90% of all higher plant species are associated with mycorrhizae
- Fungus benefits from direct carbohydrate supply from the plant
- Plant benefits from increased efficiency of nutrient uptake
- Mycorrhizal fungi protect host plants from root pathogens
- Benefits extend beyond nutrition to interactions with pathogens and herbivore fitness
- Association enhances plant nutrition and provides pathogen defense simultaneously
- Critical role in plant primary production and soil ecology

## Nutrient Uptake Mechanism

The mycorrhizal association dramatically increases the effective surface area for nutrient absorption beyond what plant roots alone can achieve. Fungal hyphae extend far into the soil, accessing nutrients and water that are beyond the depletion zone of uncolonized roots. This is particularly important for phosphorus, which is relatively immobile in soil. The fungal hyphae transport acquired nutrients back to the plant root, where they are exchanged for carbohydrates produced by photosynthesis.

## Pathogen Protection

Mycorrhizal fungi provide physical and chemical barriers against soil-borne root pathogens. The fungal mantle around roots creates a physical barrier, while fungal antibiotics and other secondary metabolites provide chemical defense. Evidence shows that mycorrhizal benefits include protection from root pathogens even in the absence of visible disease symptoms. This subtle form of biological control is an important ecosystem service provided by mycorrhizal associations.

## Effects on Herbivores

Research by Goverde et al. (2000) demonstrated that mycorrhizal associations can influence the fitness of herbivores grazing on host plants. These effects may be mediated through changes in plant tissue chemistry, including altered levels of defensive compounds or nutritional quality. This demonstrates that mycorrhizal effects cascade through multiple trophic levels in the ecosystem, affecting not just the plant and fungus but also the herbivores and potentially their predators.

## Role in Ecosystem

Mycorrhizal associations are fundamental to terrestrial ecosystem function. By enhancing nutrient uptake in the vast majority of plant species, mycorrhizae effectively control the rate of primary production in most ecosystems. The fungal component of mycorrhizae also contributes to soil structure through hyphal networks that bind soil particles into aggregates. The mycorrhizal symbiosis thus links plant production, soil formation, and nutrient cycling in a single relationship.

## Historical Study

The study of mycorrhizae has a long history, with key contributions from Harley (1969), Harley and Smith (1983), and Smith and Read (1997). Pirozynski and Malloch (1975) established the ubiquity of the association. Marx (1973) and Duchesne (1994) documented pathogen protection. Newsham et al. (1994, 1995) showed subtle fitness effects. Dighton (1997, 2003) placed mycorrhizae in the broader context of fungal ecosystem services.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Understanding mycorrhizal associations is essential for both plant cultivation and mushroom cultivation. In agriculture and horticulture, managing mycorrhizal populations can reduce fertilizer requirements and improve crop resilience. In mushroom cultivation, many edible fungi are mycorrhizal (e.g., truffles, chanterelles, porcini), making their cultivation dependent on host plant relationships. The study of mycorrhizae is central to applied mycology.
