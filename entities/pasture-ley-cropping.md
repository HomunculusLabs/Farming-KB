## Pasture Ley Cropping

## Description

Pasture ley cropping is an agricultural system that alternates fertility-demanding annual crops with longer rotations (leys) of soil-improving perennial pastures. In moderate rainfall temperate climates, mixtures of clovers, perennial grasses, and other herbaceous plants improve soil organic matter, structure, and fertility during the ley phase, especially when combined with managed grazing. Ploughing under the pasture releases accumulated fertility, leading to high yields in the annual crop phase. David Holmgren presents pasture ley cropping as an important example of the pulsing ecosystem model applied to broadacre agriculture.

## Classification

- **Category:** Agricultural System / Technique
- **Domain:** Broadacre farming, soil management, temperate agriculture
- **Related Practices:** Crop rotation, green manuring, cell grazing, organic farming
- **Geographic Association:** Australian cropping zones, temperate regions worldwide

## How It Works

### Pasture Ley Phase
Perennial pasture species — including clovers, grasses, and herbs — are established and managed with controlled grazing. During this phase, typically lasting several years, soil organic matter accumulates, soil structure improves, nitrogen is fixed by leguminous species, and overall fertility builds gradually. The grazing regime, if following cell grazing principles, can further enhance soil health through nutrient cycling and biological stimulation.

### Transition
The pasture is ploughed under, incorporating the accumulated organic matter and releasing the nutrients (particularly nitrogen from legume fixation and phosphorus from mineralised organic matter) into the soil in plant-available forms. This transition creates a pulse of fertility analogous to the burning phase in slash-and-burn agriculture.

### Cropping Phase
Fertility-demanding annual crops are grown, utilising the soil fertility built up during the ley phase to achieve high yields. The crops mine the accumulated fertility, producing grain or other harvestable products. Yields are typically highest in the first year after ploughing the ley and decline progressively in subsequent crop years.

### Return to Ley
As fertility declines over successive crop years, the land is returned to pasture ley to begin the cycle of soil rebuilding again. The timing of this return is critical — waiting too long depletes soil reserves, while returning too soon sacrifices the productive potential of accumulated fertility.

## Key Facts

- Variations of this system prevailed in the Australian cropping zones until the pasture phase supporting sheep became uneconomic due to falling wool prices and rising costs.
- The modern replacement — direct drill seeding without cultivation, using herbicides to control weeds and synthetic fertilisers to provide nutrients — compensates for the loss of the pasture phase but raises serious sustainability concerns.
- Organic farmers still use green manure and legume pasture leys as a more sustainable method of soil improvement that reflects the pulsing ecosystem model.
- Organic sources of bagged nitrogen fertiliser are too expensive for broadacre organic farms, making ley systems essential for organic grain production.
- If the sequence of annual crops is maintained for too many years without a ley phase, fertility declines progressively and soil structure deteriorates.
- Whether modern intensive direct-drill systems will rebuild organic matter and mineral fertility in cropping soil or lead to another downward spiral of land degradation remains contentious, with the evidence not encouraging.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Pulsing ecosystem model:** Pasture ley cropping is a direct agricultural application of the pulsing model — separating the production phase (crop growth) from the accumulation phase (pasture ley) to increase total system productivity and resilience.

- **Soil health:** The ley phase rebuilds soil organic matter, structure, and biological activity — functions that synthetic inputs cannot fully replicate and that are essential for long-term productivity.

- **Nutrient cycling:** The integration of grazing animals during the ley phase accelerates nutrient cycling through manure deposition, returning minerals and organic matter to the soil in plant-available forms.

- **Sustainability assessment:** The decline of ley systems in favour of input-dependent agriculture provides a clear case study in the unsustainability of systems that pulse too frequently without adequate biological recovery.

- **Biological resources:** The system demonstrates the permaculture principle of using biological resources (legumes, pasture, grazing animals) to replace non-renewable inputs (synthetic nitrogen fertilisers derived from fossil fuels).

- **Design integration:** Ley systems can be integrated with permaculture designs at various scales, from market gardens and smallholdings to broadacre farms.

## Connections

- Related to cell grazing as another pulsing agricultural practice that separates production from consumption
- Connects to bush regrowth as a natural analogue of the ley phase in high-rainfall hill country
- Parallels the slash-and-burn system in using biological accumulation followed by productive exploitation
- Demonstrates the permaculture principle of catching and storing energy (in soil organic matter during the ley phase)
- Illustrates the generalised model of land degradation through over-pulsing when the ley phase is shortened or eliminated
