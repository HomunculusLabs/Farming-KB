# Keyline Design

**Keyline Design** is a landscape planning technique for the sustainable development and management of agricultural and pastoral land, originally developed by Australian farmer and engineer **Percival Alfred (P.A.) Yeomans** in the 1950s.
The system focuses on the systematic management of water across a landscape by identifying and working with the natural topographic features — specifically the "keyline" — to maximize rainfall absorption, soil moisture retention, and overall land productivity.
Keyline design integrates water harvesting, soil building, and land shaping into a cohesive approach that has profoundly influenced modern [[permaculture]], regenerative agriculture, and holistic land management practices worldwide.
Yeomans published his foundational ideas in *The Keyline Plan* (1954), *Water for Every Farm* (1965), and *The Challenge of Landscape* (1958), establishing principles that remain central to sustainable land management more than seven decades later.

## Historical Development

### Origins with P.A. Yeomans

- Developed by Percival Alfred Yeomans (1904–1984) on "Nevallan" property in New South Wales, Australia
- Yeomans, a mining engineer, applied engineering principles to agricultural land management
- Motivated by severe soil degradation and drought vulnerability across Australian farmland
- His wife Jane Yeomans contributed significantly to practical implementation

### Key Publications

| Publication | Year | Significance |
|-------------|------|-------------|
| *The Keyline Plan* | 1954 | First comprehensive description of the keyline system |
| *The Challenge of Landscape* | 1958 | Expanded philosophical and ecological framework |
| *Water for Every Farm* | 1965 | Most widely read; practical implementation guide |
| *Water for Every Farm* (revised) | 1993 | Updated by Ken Yeomans with modern applications |

### Influence on Permaculture

- [[bill-mollison]] and [[david-holmgren]] explicitly acknowledged keyline as foundational to [[permaculture]]
- [[geoff-lawton]] has been a prominent advocate; Darren J. Doherty's Regrarians platform modernized keyline globally
- Working with natural landscape contours is now standard in permaculture site design

## Core Concepts

### The Keypoint and Keyline

The fundamental insight of keyline design revolves around two topographic features:

- **Keypoint:** The point in a valley where the slope transitions from steeper (convex) to flatter (concave) — the highest point where cultivation is practical without erosion risk
- **Keyline:** An imaginary line extending from the keypoint across the landscape at the same contour elevation, typically with a slight 1–2% grade for controlled water movement

The keyline is optimal for water management because water above it concentrates in valleys, water below it disperses toward ridges — allowing systematic moisture distribution across the entire landscape.

### Water Flow Patterns on Slopes

Understanding how water naturally moves across slopes is essential:
- **Above keypoint (valley):** Water concentrates in valleys — area for drainage, avoid cultivation
- **At keyline:** Transition zone — ideal for primary cultivation and water storage
- **Below keyline (ridge/flats):** Water disperses toward ridges — maximize irrigation and absorption
- **Ridge tops:** Naturally dry — plant trees, windbreaks

## Design Principles

### 1. Parallel Keyline Cultivation

- Cultivation lines established parallel to the keyline, not to contour
- Lines above keyline gradually move uphill toward ridges (away from valleys)
- Lines below keyline gradually move downhill toward valleys
- This pattern *diverts water from saturated valleys to dry ridges*, evening out moisture distribution
- A Yeomans plow (shank/subsoiler) is used to deepen soil without inversion, preserving soil structure

### 2. Water Storage

- **Keypoint dam:** Constructed at or near keypoint in primary valley — largest catchment, gravity-feed to lower areas
- **High contour dams:** Along ridges above keyline for gravity-fed irrigation
- **Pump-back systems:** Water from lower dams pumped to high dams using inexpensive energy
- Goal: maximize water storage at highest practical elevations for gravity-fed distribution

### 3. Tree Planting

- Tree belts planted along ridge lines for windbreaks, evaporation reduction, and nutrient cycling
- Deep roots access minerals from subsoil unavailable to pasture; fallen leaves contribute organic matter
- Species selection prioritizes deep-rooted, nitrogen-fixing, multipurpose trees
- Provides wildlife corridors, livestock shade, and potential timber/fodder harvests

### 4. Soil Development

- Shallow subsoiler (Yeomans plow) fractures compacted layers without inversion
- Increases water infiltration, root penetration depth, and soil aeration
- Over time, increased organic matter and biological activity build deeper topsoil
- Accelerates natural soil formation; grazing management integrated to maintain ground cover

## Practical Implementation Steps

1. **Analyze topography:** Identify valleys, ridges, and slopes using contour maps or laser leveling
2. **Locate keypoints:** Find where slope transitions from convex (steep) to concave (flat) in each primary valley
3. **Establish keylines:** Mark keylines extending from keypoints across the full property width
4. **Design water storage:** Position dams at keypoints and along high contours for gravity-fed distribution
5. **Plan cultivation lines:** Design parallel cultivation lines based on keyline orientation
6. **Implement tree systems:** Plant deep-rooted, nitrogen-fixing tree belts along ridges
7. **Execute subsoiling:** Perform keyline pattern cultivation using a Yeomans-style plow
8. **Integrate grazing:** Implement rotational grazing to maintain soil cover and stimulate biology
9. **Monitor and adapt:** Observe water movement, soil changes, and vegetation response

## Modern Applications and Relevance

- **Broadacre farming:** Applied to large-scale cereal and livestock operations across Australia, North America, and Africa
- **Permaculture design:** Standard element in permaculture site planning for water management on sloped land
- **Drought resilience:** Keyline-designed properties consistently outperform conventional neighbors during drought
- **Carbon sequestration:** Increased soil organic matter contributes to carbon storage in agricultural soils
- **Mine rehabilitation:** Used in landscape restoration of mined and degraded lands
- **Urban/rural residential:** Scaled-down keyline principles applied to homesteads and small properties

## Criticisms and Limitations

- Initial implementation requires investment in earthmoving equipment (dams, cultivation)
- Most effective on properties with significant slope — limited on flat terrain
- Requires careful contour analysis and planning — not a casual weekend project
- The "parallel to keyline" concept can be confusing for beginners
- Prolonged multi-year drought can overwhelm even well-designed systems
- Less studied in academic literature compared to other land management approaches
- Benefits typically take 3–5 years to fully manifest
