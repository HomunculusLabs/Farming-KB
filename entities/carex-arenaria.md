## Overview

**Carex arenaria**, commonly known as sand sedge, is a rhizomatous perennial sedge of the family Cyperaceae. It is native to coastal dunes and sandy soils across Europe and western Asia.

The species is a vigorous coloniser of unstable sand, forming extensive mats through its creeping rhizome system. It plays a major role in [[desert-dune-stabilisation-and-forestry]] is considered a key framework species in [[coastal-plant-communities-permaculture]].

Sand sedge is a distinctive component of strandline and foredune vegetation across its range. It can form near-monocultures spanning hundreds of square metres where conditions are favourable.

The plant is highly tolerant of salt spray, wind exposure, and substrate instability. These adaptations make it one of the most successful dune-building species in temperate [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

Flowering occurs from April to June across most of its range. Pollination is wind-mediated, and seeds are dispersed by wind and surface water flow.

Seeds are small, lens-shaped achenes that can remain viable in the soil seed bank for several years. Germination is stimulated by disturbance and exposure of bare sand surfaces.

## Distribution and Habitat

Sand sedge is widely distributed across Europe, from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean. It extends eastward through western Russia and occurs in parts of North Africa.

The species has been introduced to North America, where it is locally naturalised along the Atlantic coast. In parts of its introduced range it can become invasive, displacing native dune vegetation.

Its primary habitat is coastal sand dunes, from embryonic shifting dunes to fixed grey dunes. It also colonises sandy grasslands, railway embankments, and disturbed sandy ground inland.

Populations in the British Isles are concentrated along the coasts of England, Wales, and eastern Scotland. Inland records are scattered and often associated with glacial sand deposits or former heathland.

Optimal conditions are well-drained, nutrient-poor sandy soils in full sun. It tolerates salt spray and periodic burial by wind-blown sand.

Inland populations occur on sandy heaths, disturbed ground, and post-industrial sites. These populations are often relictual, persisting on sandy substrates left after glacial retreat.

The species is sensitive to waterlogging and does not persist on compacted or clay-rich soils. It requires open conditions and declines sharply as competing vegetation closes the canopy.

Elevation range extends from sea level to approximately 500 metres in mountainous areas. At higher elevations it is restricted to exposed, south-facing slopes with thin sandy soils.

## Ecology

**Carex arenaria** is a primary coloniser on coastal dunes and is often among the first vascular plants to establish on bare sand. Its rhizome network helps bind loose substrate and facilitates succession.

The species provides habitat and food for various invertebrates. Sand-dune specialist insects, including weevils and moth larvae, feed on the foliage and seeds.

It competes aggressively with other dune plants through both rhizomatous spread and dense above-ground cover. In dense stands it can significantly reduce plant species diversity.

The rhizomes form a dense mat that influences soil development by trapping organic matter. This process contributes to dune stabilisation and the gradual formation of humus-rich soils.

Rabbits and hares occasionally graze the foliage, though the tough leaves make it a relatively unpalatable food source. Cattle grazing can help control excessive dominance in managed dune systems.

Fungal associates include [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]]. It is planted as a sand-binding species on reclaimed land and artificial dune systems.

In traditional medicine, a decoction of the rhizomes was used as a diuretic and for treating digestive complaints. These uses are largely historical and not supported by modern clinical evidence.

The tough rhizomes were traditionally woven into mats and baskets in parts of northern Europe. This practice has largely disappeared with the availability of synthetic materials.
