## Overview

**Quercus palustris**, commonly known as pin oak or swamp Spanish oak, is a
deciduous tree in the family Fagaceae. It is native to eastern and central
[[jeavons-climate-adaptation-growing-seasons]].

The leaves are 10–18 cm long and 8–13 cm wide, with 5–7 deep lobes separated
by wide U-shaped sinuses. Each lobe terminates in one or more bristle-tipped
teeth. The upper surface is glossy dark green; the lower surface is paler with
small tufts of hairs in the axils of veins.

Autumn color is highly variable, ranging from bronze to brilliant scarlet and
russet. The bark is smooth and dark gray-brown on young trees, developing
shallow ridges and furrows with age. The lower branches characteristically
droop downward, middle branches are horizontal, and upper branches ascend,
creating a distinctive layered appearance.

Acorns are 12–18 mm long, broadly ovoid, and enclosed at the base by a
shallow, saucer-shaped cup covering approximately one-third of the nut. They
mature in the second year and are dispersed from September through November.

## Distribution and Habitat

Pin oak occurs naturally from southern New England and southern Ontario west
to eastern Nebraska and Kansas, and south to the Appalachian foothills,
northern Arkansas, and eastern Oklahoma. It is absent from the southeastern
Coastal Plain and the upper Mississippi Valley.

The species is most commonly associated with bottomland forests, floodplain
swamps, and the margins of ponds and streams. It thrives in poorly drained,
acidic clay soils with a pH of 4.5–6.0. It is frequently a dominant canopy
tree in swamp hardwood forests of the central [[leaf-tissue-analysis-crop-nutrient-status]] while veins remain green.

## Cultivation and Landscaping

Pin oak is among the most commonly planted shade trees in the United States
and parts of Europe. Its naturally symmetrical, pyramidal crown requires
minimal pruning when young, making it popular for street plantings, parking
lot buffers, and institutional grounds.

The tree transplants easily as a balled-and-burlapped specimen due to a
fibrous, shallow root system. It prefers full sun and tolerates wet
conditions better than most landscape oaks, including periodic flooding of
up to 30 days during the dormant season.

Cultivars are uncommon; the species form is overwhelmingly preferred in
nursery trade.
