# Hallucinogenic Plants of the New World

Based on [[richard-evans-schultes-updated]]"s Golden Guide. The New World has far more hallucinogenic species in active cultural use (90+) than the Old World (15-20). This disparity is not explained by botanical distribution — Old World plants like Cannabis and Amanita have been largely abandoned for hallucinogenic use. See hallucinogenic plants new world for Old World species.

---

## Solanaceous Hallucinogens (Tropane Alkaloids)

### Datura Species (Angel's Trumpet, Jimsonweed)

**Datura arborea, D. candida, D. dolichocarpa, D. sanguinea** — Brugmansia-type species (tree daturas)
**Datura stramonium, D. ferox, D. innoxia** — herbaceous daturas

- **Distribution**: Widely cultivated and naturalized across Americas, especially Andes
- **Use**: Among the most important New World hallucinogens. Used by Atacama, Chibcha, Aymara of Chile and Peru; also Mapuche (Chile) as "toloache"; used in Chile and Colombia for sorcery and divination
- **Chemistry**: Scopolamine, hyoscyamine, atropine. In D. sanguinea, scopolamine is present in amounts up to 0.4% of dry weight
- **Effects**: Onset of effects may be preceded by unconsciousness. Narcotic properties noted by early chroniclers. Requires restraint during initial violent phase, followed by sleep and hallucinations
- **Other**: All daturas contain similar tropane alkaloids; some cultivated as ornamentals

### Tree Daturas — Methysticodendron amesianum
- A monotypic genus sometimes merged into Datura
- Used by Colombian Indians; has a strong narcotic effect
- Scopolamine comprises 80% of total alkaloids

## Cactus Hallucinogens (Phenylethylamines)

### Peyote — Lophophora williamsii (Lem. ex Salm-Dyck) Coulter
- **Family**: Cactaceae; Cereoideae
- **Distribution**: Endemic to south Texas and central-northern Mexico (Rio Grande drainage); found in Laredo, Starr County, Zapata County
- **Use**: Central to Huichol (Wixáritari), Tarahumara, Cora, and other Mexican cultures. Considered a deity; extensive ceremonial use. Harvested by peyoteros for religious use
- **Chemistry**: Contains more than 30 alkaloids, principally mescaline (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenylethylamine). Total alkaloid content: 1-2% fresh, up to 8% dried
- **Effects**: Vivid visual hallucinations with eyes open or closed; kaleidoscopic plays of color in constant motion. Symptoms include nausea, chills, motor excitation, then dreamlike state

### San Pedro — Trichocereus pachanoi Britt. et Rose
- **Distribution**: Andean Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia; widely cultivated
- **Use**: Ceremonial use in Peru dating back at least 3,000 years. The name "San Pedro" is Christianized. Used by curanderos for divination, diagnosis, and healing. Often consumed in the evening at all-night ceremonies called "mesadas"
- **Chemistry**: Contains mescaline (up to 2% fresh weight); total alkaloids similar to peyote
- **Preparation**: Usually prepared by boiling slices of the cactus for several hours. Used both for diagnosis of illness and treatment

## Fungal Hallucinogens

### Psilocybin Mushrooms — Psilocybe, Stropharia, Conocybe, Panaeolus
- **Distribution**: Cosmopolitan; highest diversity in subtropical and tropical Americas
- **Use**: Mesoamerican mushroom cult (teonanacatl — "flesh of the gods") documented from pre-Columbian times through present among Mazatec, Mixtec, Zapotec, Chatino, and other Oaxacan peoples. Richard Evans Schultes identified teonanacatl as a mushroom in 1938. See [[r-gordon-wasson]] for the famous 1955 Velada Maria Sabina experience.
- **Species**: Psilocybe mexicana (Schultes"s original identification), P. caerulescens, P. zapotecorum, P. cubensis, Stropharia cubensis, Conocybe cyanopus, Panaeolus sphinctrinus, among others
- **Chemistry**: Psilocybin (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) and psilocin. Psilocybin is an acidic phosphoric acid ester — a completely new type of natural structure. First isolated from European P. semilanceata by [[albert-hofmann]] in 1958
- **Effects**: Similar to LSD; colorful visual hallucinations. Dosage varies by species and preparation

### Fly Agaric — Amanita muscaria
- **Distribution**: Boreal and temperate Northern Hemisphere; Siberia, North America, Europe
- **Use**: Used by Siberian tribes (Koryak, Kamchadal, Chukchi) — see hallucinogenic plants new world. Also used in Oaxaca, Mexico by Mazatec peoples. The Siberian and Mexican use are culturally independent, representing convergent discovery
- **Chemistry**: Ibotenic acid, muscimol (muscazone). Not tryptamine-based — unique chemistry
- **Effects**: Narcotic, intoxicating, inebriating. The dried mushroom is eaten or boiled in water to make an intoxicating beverage

## Vine and Snuff Hallucinogens (Tryptamines + Beta-Carbolines)

### Ayahuasca — Banisteriopsis caapi (Spruce ex Griseb.) Morton
- **Family**: Malpighiaceae
- **Distribution**: Western Amazonia — Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Brazil
- **Use**: Widely used by 70+ Amazonian tribes. Also known as "yage" (Colombia), "caapi" (Brazil), "ayahuasca" (Ecuador/Peru). Served in all-night ceremonies by shamans. Preparations vary enormously by tribe — some add 15-20 admixture plants. See [[ayahuasca-preparation]] for detailed preparation methods
- **Chemistry**: Beta-carboline alkaloids — harmine, harmaline, tetrahydroharmine. These are MAO inhibitors, not hallucinogenic alone, but enable oral activity of DMT from admixture plants
- **Cultural context**: Described by Richard Spruce in 1851. First alkaloid isolated by Chen and Chen in 1939. B. inebrians (Ecuador) is a separate but related species

### Chacruna — Psychotria viridis Ruiz et Pavon
- **Family**: Rubiaceae
- **Distribution**: Amazon basin
- **Use**: The most common DMT-containing admixture plant in ayahuasca. Added to the Banisteriopsis caapi brew to provide the visionary component
- **Chemistry**: N,N-DMT (up to 0.66% of dry leaves). See [[dmt-reference]]

### Chaliponga — Diplopterys cabrerana (Cuatr.) Anderson
- **Family**: Malpighiaceae
- **Distribution**: Colombian and Ecuadorian Amazon
- **Use**: Alternative to Psychotria viridis in ayahuasca preparations, especially in Putumayo region
- **Chemistry**: N,N-DMT (0.6%), 5-MeO-DMT (0.13%), bufotenine (0.10%)

### Virola — The Snuff Hallucinogen
- **Species**: V. theiodora, V. calophylla, V. calophylloidea, V. elongata, among others
- **Family**: Myristicaceae (nutmeg family)
- **Distribution**: Northwestern Amazon — Colombia, Venezuela, Brazil
- **Use**: The most recently discovered hallucinogenic complex. Prepared as snuff (epena, ebena, nyakwana, yakee) from inner bark resin. Used by Waika (Yanomamo), Bora, Witoto, and other tribes. The bark is stripped, beaten, boiled, and the resin kneaded into snuff. Sometimes used sublingually as pellets. Snuff is blown into nostrils through long tubes (up to 2 feet)
- **Chemistry**: 5-MeO-DMT (up to 8% in dried resin of V. theiodora), N,N-DMT (2-4%), bufotenine. Also N-methyltryptamine
- **Discovery**: First identified by Schultes in 1954 from Waika territory

### Yopo — Anadenanthera peregrina (L.) Speg.
- **Synonyms**: Piptadenia peregrina
- **Distribution**: Orinoco basin (Venezuela/Colombia) and Caribbean
- **Use**: One of the most widely used hallucinogenic snuffs in South America. Used by Yanoama, Waika, Guahibo, and other tribes. Also archaeological evidence from pre-Columbian Caribbean (snuff tubes found). The seeds are toasted, ground, mixed with alkaline ash (lime or shells) to liberate free base
- **Chemistry**: Bufotenine, N,N-DMT, 5-MeO-DMT, N-methyltryptamine, N-monomethyltryptamine. Bufotenine is present but apparently not hallucinogenic. 5-MeO-DMT and DMT are the active constituents
- **Note**: A. colubrina (vilca, cebil) serves the same purpose in southern Peru and Bolivia, where snuff is called "vilca" or "cebil"

## Morning Glories (Ergoline Alkaloids)

### Ololiuqui — Turbina corymbosa (L.) Rafinesque
- **Synonyms**: Rivea corymbosa (L.) Hall. fil.
- **Family**: Convolvulaceae
- **Distribution**: Native to Mexican tropics; cultivated widely
- **Use**: One of the most important Mesoamerican hallucinogens since pre-Columbian times. Aztec priests consumed seeds in rituals. Spanish chroniclers confused it with Datura. Still used by Oaxacan healers (curanderos). The name "ololiuqui" is Nahuatl, from "ololiuhqui" (round thing)
- **Chemistry**: Ergine (d-lysergic acid amide), isoergine, chanoclavine, elymoclavine, lysergol. First identified as hallucinogenic morning glory in 1937 by Schultes; alkaloids identified by Hofmann in 1960 — a "sensational discovery" that these compounds previously known only from ergot fungus existed in plants

### Badoh Negro — Ipomoea violacea L.
- **Family**: Convolvulaceae
- **Distribution**: Mexican tropics; widely cultivated ornamental (Heavenly Blue, Pearly Gates varieties)
- **Use**: Known to the Aztecs as "tlitliltzin" (black thing). Seeds used similarly to ololiuqui, often preferred by Zapotec curanderos
- **Chemistry**: Same ergoline alkaloids as T. corymbosa but total alkaloid content is 5 times greater (0.06% vs 0.012% in T. corymbosa)

## Lamiaceae

### Salvia divinorum Epling et Jativa
- **Family**: Lamiaceae (mint family)
- **Distribution**: Endemic to Sierra Mazateca, Oaxaca, Mexico; found only in a few localities at 1000-1800 ft elevation
- **Use**: Used by Mazatec curanderos for divination and healing. Called "ska María Pastora" (leaves of Mary the Shepherdess). Often used as a substitute when psilocybin mushrooms are unavailable. See [[salvia-divinorum]]
- **Chemistry**: Salvinorin A (neoclerodane diterpene) — the first non-alkaloid, non-nitrogenous plant hallucinogen known (before cannabinoid mechanisms were fully understood). Not a tryptamine, ergoline, or tropane. Discovered as psychoactive in the 1960s by Wasson; chemical structure identified by Ortega et al. (1982); salvinorin A isolated by Valdes (1994)
- **Cultivation**: Propagated vegetatively; rarely sets seed.极易 propagated from cuttings
