## Phytoremediation

## Description

Phytoremediation is the use of plants (and sometimes the microbes they attract) to detoxify soil or water contaminated with pollutants such as heavy metals, pesticides, and crude oil. This technique leverages the natural ability of certain plants to accumulate, degrade, or volatilize contaminants, often in partnership with [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] and soil microbes that enhance the remediation process.

## Classification

- **Type:** Environmental remediation technique
- **Approach:** Biological (plant-based and microbe-assisted)
- **Targets:** Heavy metals, organic pollutants, pesticides, petroleum hydrocarbons
- **Mechanisms:** Phytoextraction, phytovolatilization, phytodegradation, rhizofiltration, phytostabilization

## Mechanisms

### Phytoextraction / Hyperaccumulation
Certain plants take up contaminants (especially metals) in extraordinary quantities. Hyperaccumulation occurs when a specific metal makes up 1% or more of the plant's dry mass. The plants are then harvested and the contaminants are removed from the site.

**Example:** The fern *Pteris vittata* takes up large quantities of arsenic, making it a model organism for arsenic phytoremediation.

### Phytovolatilization
Some plants convert contaminants into gaseous forms and release them through transpiration. The nonessential element is transformed into a volatile compound and dispersed into the atmosphere, typically at concentrations low enough to be harmless.

### Phytodegradation
Plants and their associated microbes break down organic pollutants (pesticides, petroleum) into less toxic or nontoxic compounds through metabolic processes.

### Rhizostabilization
Plants immobilize contaminants in the root zone, preventing their migration into groundwater or dispersal by wind and water.

## Key Facts

- Some plants can hyperaccumulate metals to over 1% of their dry mass
- Mycorrhizal fungi can sequester nonessential nutrients, accumulating them to protect their plant partner
- Rice plants let in arsenite through silicon membrane transport proteins (arsenite has a similar shape to silicic acid)
- Arsenic (AsO3-) can move through the same membrane transport protein as phosphorus
- Plants have developed membrane transport systems that are specific but sometimes allow nonessential elements with similar shapes to pass through
- Nonessential elements not used metabolically are sequestered in vacuoles or converted to insoluble crystalline forms

## Transport Mechanisms for Nonessential Elements

Plants have evolved sophisticated transport proteins that are generally specific for essential nutrients, but nonessential elements can slip through:

- **Shape mimicry:** Arsenite mimics silicic acid; arsenate mimics phosphate
- **Charge similarity:** Elements with similar ionic radii and charge compete for the same transport channels
- **Evolutionary compromise:** Transport proteins cannot be perfectly selective; some nonessential elements inevitably enter

## Role of Mycorrhizal Fungi

Mycorrhizal fungi play important roles in phytoremediation:

- Bring metal nutrients (phosphorus, copper, nickel) to plants
- Can also bring nonessential elements like lead to plants
- Some mycorrhizal fungi secrete glomalin, which ties up metals in soil, reducing bioavailability
- Can sequester nonessential nutrients, accumulating them away from the plant partner
- [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] are particularly important for metal immobilization

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Bioremediation applications:** Phytoremediation offers low-cost, environmentally friendly cleanup of contaminated sites
- **Mycorrhizal partnerships:** Selecting appropriate mycorrhizal inoculants can enhance phytoremediation effectiveness
- **Soil safety:** Understanding plant uptake of nonessential elements is important for food safety in contaminated areas
- **Metal-tolerant crops:** Breeding or selecting plants for phytoremediation is an active area of research
- **Glomalin applications:** Glomalin-producing mycorrhizal fungi can be used to stabilize heavy metals in contaminated soils

## Advantages and Limitations

### Advantages
- Low cost compared to conventional remediation (excavation, chemical treatment)
- Environmentally friendly — uses natural biological processes
- Aesthetically pleasing — plants improve site appearance
- Can treat large areas of low-to-moderate contamination
- Minimal site disruption
- Public acceptance is generally high

### Limitations
- Slow process — can take years to decades depending on contamination level
- Limited to the root zone depth of plants
- Not suitable for extremely contaminated sites
- Risk of contaminants entering the food chain through herbivores
- Requires long-term monitoring and maintenance
- Climate and seasonal conditions affect performance
