## Overview

**Desmodium illinoense** is a perennial herbaceous legume in the family Fabaceae, commonly known as Illinois tick-trefoil or Illinois beggarlice.

It is native to central and [[biodiversity-fungi-grassland-ecosystems]].

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Desmodium illinoense** A. Gray belongs to the family Fabaceae, subfamily Faboideae, tribe Desmodieae.

The genus name derives from the Greek *desmos* (bond or chain), referring to the jointed, chain-like loment fruits characteristic of the group.

It is a perennial forb growing 60–150 cm tall from a thick woody rootstock. Stems are erect, unbranched or sparingly branched above, and usually covered with spreading hairs.

Leaves are trifoliolate with petioles 3–8 cm long. Leaflets are 5–12 cm long, ovate to lanceolate, with acute tips and finely toothed margins.

The upper leaflet surface is green and sparsely hairy, while the lower surface is paler with denser pubescence along the veins.

Stipules are lanceolate, 5–8 mm long, and persistent, a useful diagnostic character for distinguishing Desmodium species.

Inflorescences are terminal or axillary racemes 10–25 cm long, producing 20–50 flowers. Individual flowers are papilionaceous, 6–8 mm long, pinkish-purple to lavender.

The calyx is deeply two-lipped, and flowering occurs from July through September. The banner petal is reflexed and often marked with darker purple veins.

The fruit is a segmented loment 3–6 cm long, breaking into 3–6 one-seeded joints at maturity. Each joint is covered with minute hooked hairs that readily attach to animal fur and clothing.

This effective epizoochorous dispersal mechanism allows the plant to colonize new sites far from parent populations.

## Distribution and Habitat

Illinois tick-trefoil occurs in the central United States from southern Wisconsin and Minnesota west to eastern Nebraska and Kansas, and south to Oklahoma, Arkansas, and northern Louisiana.

It is also found in the Midwest from Ohio and Indiana westward, with scattered populations in Missouri and Illinois where it was first described.

The species is most common in mesic to dry prairies, savannas, open woodlands, and glades. It is a characteristic forb of the tallgrass prairie assemblage.

It prefers well-drained [[loam]] to clay-loam soils with a pH of 5.5–7.0. It tolerates moderate drought but is less common on very sandy or very wet sites.

Illinois tick-trefoil is frequently associated with dominant prairie grasses such as big bluestem ([[sorghastrum-nutans]]).

It is also found along roadsides, railroad rights-of-way, and in old fields undergoing succession to prairie vegetation.

## Ecology and Wildlife Value

**Desmodium illinoense** fixes atmospheric nitrogen through root nodule symbiosis with Bradyrhizobium bacteria, contributing nitrogen to the prairie soil nutrient pool.

The hooked loment segments are an effective dispersal mechanism, attaching to the fur of mammals and feathers of birds for long-distance transport.

The foliage provides moderate forage value for white-tailed deer, rabbits, and ground-nesting birds. [[comparison-prairie-restoration-vs-silvopasture]] throughout the tallgrass prairie region of the central United States.

Seed requires scarification for reliable germination, as the hard seed coat causes strong physical dormancy. Mechanical scarification or acid treatment is effective.

Seeding rate for restoration is 2–4 kg/ha pure live seed, planted in early spring or as a dormant seeding in late fall.
