## Overview

**Sambucus cerulea**, commonly known as blue elderberry or blue elder, is a deciduous shrub or small tree in the family Adoxaceae.

It is native to western [[sambucus-nigra]]*) and shares many of its traditional and pharmacological properties.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Magnoliopsida → Dipsacales → Adoxaceae → Sambucus → S. cerulea.

The species was originally described by Rafinesque in 1808 and has undergone several taxonomic revisions. The epithet *cerulea* refers to the blue (cerulean) color of the ripe berries. Some authorities treat it as a variety of Mexican elderberry (*Sambucus mexicana*), though most modern floras recognize it as distinct.

Blue elderberry is a large, fast-growing deciduous shrub typically reaching 3–6 meters in height, occasionally forming a small tree to 9 meters.

The bark is smooth and grey-brown on young stems, becoming fissured and furrowed with age.

Young twigs are green and covered with a distinctive waxy bloom, containing a prominent pith.

Leaves are opposite, pinnately compound, 15–30 cm long, with 5–7 lanceolate leaflets 5–15 cm in length.

Leaflets have serrate margins and are dark green above with a paler, sometimes glaucous underside.

Inflorescences are large, flat-topped corymbs 10–25 cm across, composed of numerous small creamy-white flowers.

Each flower is 4–6 mm across with five petals, five stamens, and a three-chambered inferior ovary.

The fruit is a small globose drupe, 4–6 mm in diameter, blue to purple-black with a white waxy coating when ripe.

Berries are borne in dense pendulous clusters that often weigh down branches under the weight of the crop.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

The ripe berries of Sambucus cerulea are edible when cooked and have been used for food by Indigenous peoples and settlers for centuries.

Raw berries contain trace amounts of cyanogenic glycosides and should not be eaten in quantity uncooked.

Cooking neutralizes the glycosides and develops the berries' pleasant, slightly tart flavor.

Traditional preparations include elderberry jelly, syrup, wine, pies, and dried berry cakes.

The berries are rich in anthocyanins, particularly cyanidin-3-glucoside, which give them their deep blue-purple color.

Elderflower clusters can be used to make cordials, fritters, and teas, similar to the European *S. nigra* tradition.

The flowers have a sweet, floral aroma and are collected in late spring before fruit set.

Modern artisanal producers in the Pacific Northwest use blue elderberries for preserves, shrubs, and craft beverages.
