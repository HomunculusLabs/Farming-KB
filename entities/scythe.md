## Scythe

## Description

The scythe is an ancient hand tool consisting of a long curved blade mounted on a snath (handle), used for cutting grass, weeds, and crop residues by hand. In *The Winter Harvest Handbook*, scythes are referenced at pp. 139, 156, and 192 as part of the tool inventory at [[four-season-farm]], serving roles in both weed management and [[cover-crop-termination]]. The scythe embodies the low-input, appropriate-technology philosophy central to Coleman's approach to market gardening.

## Classification

- **Category**: Hand tool
- **Domain**: Market gardening, [[cover-crop]] management, weed control
- **Type**: Cutting / mowing tool
- **Power source**: Human muscle — no fossil fuels required
- **Historical origin**: One of the oldest agricultural tools, used since Roman times

## Key Facts

- Used at Four Season Farm for cutting cover crops and managing crop debris (p. 139)
- Part of the organic tool suite alongside hoes, wire weeders, and flame weeders (pp. 156, 192)
- Silent, fossil-fuel-free alternative to gas-powered mowers and string trimmers
- Particularly effective for cutting cover crops before incorporating them into soil
- European scythes (Austrian-style) are preferred over American patterns for their lighter weight and superior cutting geometry
- Listed in the tool functionality discussion at pp. 192, 194
- Requires skill to use effectively — the mowing stroke is a full-body motion combining hip rotation and arm swing
- Blade requires regular maintenance: peening (hammering the edge thin) and honing (whetstone sharpening)

## Applications in Market Gardening

Within Coleman's integrated organic system, scythes serve several functions:

### Cover Crop Termination

- Cutting cover crops like rye, vetch, clover, and buckwheat before tarping or soil incorporation
- Allows precise cutting height control to leave root mass in place for soil structure
- Timing can be adjusted to catch cover crops at optimal C:N ratio for decomposition

### Crop Debris Management

- Clearing spent crop residues from beds before replanting (pp. 139-140)
- Faster and more thorough than hand-pulling for large-stemmed crop residues
- Debris can be collected for composting after cutting

### Border and Pathway Maintenance

- Keeping grass and weeds around greenhouse perimeters managed without powered equipment
- Mowing weeds between beds in field production without soil disturbance
- Maintaining access paths and drainage channels

## Equipment and Maintenance

### Types

- **Austrian/European scythe**: Lighter, thinner blade; preferred for mowing grass and fine vegetation
- **American scythe**: Heavier, stamped blade; suited for heavier brush but more fatiguing
- **Bush scythe**: Heavier blade for woody vegetation and saplings

### Maintenance Requirements

- **Peening**: Hammering the blade edge on a small anvil to thin and shape the cutting edge (done every 4-8 hours of use)
- **Honing**: Whetstone sharpening in the field during use (every 15-30 minutes)
- **Oiling**: Blade wiped with oil after use to prevent rust
- **Snath fitting**: Handle length and grip positions adjusted to the user's height

## Advantages

- **No fuel, oil, noise, or exhaust** — fully sustainable and pleasant to use
- **Physical exercise** — connects the farmer to the land through embodied labor
- **Precise cutting height** — adjustable by changing blade angle and stroke technique
- **Edge precision** — can cut right up to crop edges without damaging adjacent plants
- **Low maintenance cost** — only requires a peening jig, whetstone, and occasional blade replacement
- **Reliability** — no engine to maintain, starts every time, works in all weather

## Relevance to Cultivation

The scythe represents a deliberate choice for sustainability and simplicity in small-scale agriculture. In market gardens where beds are typically 30 inches wide and 100 feet long, the scythe provides an efficient means of managing vegetation without the capital expense and environmental impact of powered equipment. Its use reflects the broader philosophy of [[appropriate-technology]] that pervades Coleman's approach — choosing the simplest effective tool for each task.

For organic growers, the scythe also offers a practical advantage in soil health: unlike rotary mowers that pulverize vegetation into small pieces that decompose too quickly, the scythe leaves longer stems that decompose gradually, providing a more sustained release of organic matter and nutrients.
