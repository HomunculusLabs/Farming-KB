# Amanita verna

**Amanita verna** (Bull.) Lam., commonly known as the **fool's mushroom** or **spring
[[palmer-poisonous-mushrooms-deadly-species]] in the world. It is a member
of the Amanita section Phalloideae and contains the same lethal amatoxins as the [[amanita-phalloides]], and
molecular phylogenetic studies have shown that the two species are very closely related.

However, it is generally maintained as a distinct species due to its consistently pure white
coloration, earlier fruiting season, and subtle but consistent morphological differences. It
is closely related to the [[amanita-phalloides]], typically appearing
from late spring to early summer (May through July), which is the origin of both its specific
epithet and its common name "fool's mushroom" (so called because fools who emerge in spring
seeking early mushrooms are most likely to encounter it).

It is found in deciduous and mixed forests, parks, gardens, and along forest edges, often in
the same locations where [[alpha-amanitin]] is the predominant and most toxic component, acting as a potent and
selective inhibitor of RNA polymerase II.

The toxins are heat-stable and are not destroyed by any conventional cooking method, including
boiling, frying, baking, or drying. This is a critically important point, as many poisoning
victims have consumed amanita verna after cooking it, believing that cooking would
neutralize any toxins.

### Clinical Course of Poisoning

The clinical presentation of amanita verna poisoning is identical to that of phalloides, following the classic biphasic pattern:

1. **Gastrointestinal phase (6 to 24 hours post-ingestion):** Profuse vomiting, severe
   abdominal cramps, and watery diarrhea leading to significant dehydration and electrolyte
   imbalance. The onset of symptoms is notably delayed, which is both a diagnostic clue and a
   danger, as victims may not associate their illness with mushrooms consumed many hours
   earlier.

2. **Latent phase (1 to 3 days):** A deceptive period of apparent recovery during which
   hepatic necrosis progresses silently. Liver enzymes (AST, ALT) may rise into the thousands
   or tens of thousands. Patients may feel well enough to refuse or leave medical care during
   this phase.

3. **Hepatic and renal failure phase (3 to 7 days):** Rapid deterioration with hepatic
   necrosis, jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy, disseminated intravascular coagulation, acute
   kidney injury, hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis, and potentially death.
