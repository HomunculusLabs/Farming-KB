## Description

The Brush Turkey (*Alectura lathami*), also known as the Australian Brush-turkey, is a large ground-dwelling bird native to Australia. It belongs to the megapode family (Megapodiidae), a group of birds distinguished by their unique incubation strategy: rather than sitting on eggs with body heat, they construct massive [[decomposition]].

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Animalia
- **Phylum**: Chordata
- **Class**: Aves
- **Order**: Galliformes
- **Family**: Megapodiidae (Megapodes)
- **Genus**: *Alectura*
- **Species**: *A. lathami*

## Key Facts

- The male brush turkey compacts a heap of rain-wet vegetation in an excavated pit, covers it, and allows a female to approach and lay eggs when the mound temperature reaches approximately 35°C (95°F).
- The male continuously attends the nest, adding or removing material to regulate incubation temperature, which stabilises at about 30°C internally.
- The nest functions as a large underground compost pile, generating consistent heat through decomposition.
- Mound-building creates a nutrient-rich microenvironment that can persist for thousands of years.

## Ecological Role in Forest Generation

Mollison describes a remarkable phenomenon observed in the dry savannahs of Central Cape York Peninsula (Australia): a rainforest clump approximately one mile across, with a megapode compost-mound nest at its centre, carbon-dated to roughly 5,000 years old. The rainforest was oldest near the nest and progressively younger at the periphery.

This observation suggests that megapode nests provide concentrated soil nutrients that serve as nucleation sites for rainforest establishment, with plant seeds likely introduced by fruit pigeons on migration. The implication is profound: these birds act as ecosystem engineers capable of initiating forest communities in otherwise dry landscapes.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Compost analog**: The brush turkey's mound-building behaviour demonstrates natural principles of thermophilic composting, showing how organic matter, moisture, and microbial activity can generate reliable heat for biological processes.
- **Dryland reforestation model**: The compost-mound strategy directly inspired Mollison's proposal for reforesting deserts by burying mixed organic material (chips, logs, manure, straw) under sand in contour swales, mimicking the megapode's approach.
- **Nucleation planting**: The concept of starting reforestation from small, nutrient-rich nuclei that expand outward is a core [[mallee-fowl]]** (*Leipoa ocellata*): Arid-area megapode building nests of 5–6 × 1–2 m, deeply buried in sand.
