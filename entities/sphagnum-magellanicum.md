## Overview

**Sphagnum magellanicum**, commonly known as Magellanic bog moss or short-leaved bog moss, is a widespread and ecologically important peat moss species.

It is one of the dominant *Sphagnum* species in boreal and subarctic peatlands, forming extensive carpets and hummocks across the circumboreal region.

The species is named for its original collection locality near the Strait of Magellan, though it is far more abundant in northern latitudes.

*S. magellanicum* is a major contributor to peat formation and plays a central role in global carbon cycling through peatland ecosystems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Sphagnum magellanicum* Brid. was described by Bridel in 1798 and is placed in *Sphagnum* section *Sphagnum*.

The gametophyte forms large, dense carpets or low hummocks that are typically green, yellow-green, or reddish-brown depending on light exposure.

Stems are stout, 5–15 cm tall, with fascicles of spreading branch leaves and pendant branch leaves at each node.

Stem leaves are triangular-ovate, 1.5–2.5 mm long, with a broad, rounded apex and no fibrils on the stem cortex.

Branch fascicles contain 2–3 spreading branches and 2–4 pendant branches per fascicle.

Branch leaves are ovate, 1.5–2 mm long, with a concave apex and moderately serrulate margins.

The leaf lamina consists of a single layer of alternating chlorophyllose cells and large hyaline cells.

Hyaline cells on the convex surface bear large, round pores, while those on the concave surface are smaller and less numerous.

The species is monoicous, with both male and female reproductive structures on the same stem.

Sporophytes are produced infrequently. Capsules are spherical, 1.5–2 mm in diameter, on a short pseudopodium.

Spores are 20–30 μm in diameter, finely papillose, and yellowish-brown.

## Distribution and Habitat

*S. magellanicum* has a broad circumboreal distribution across North America, Europe, and Asia.

It occurs from Alaska and northern Canada southward through the northern United States, the British Isles, Scandinavia, and Russia.

Southern disjunct populations exist in the Andes of South America, New Zealand, and southeastern Australia.

The species is a dominant component of ombrotrophic (rain-fed) bogs and minerotrophic (groundwater-fed) fens.

It occupies a range of microtopographic positions, from wet hollows to moderate hummocks, showing broad ecological amplitude.

In ombrotrophic bogs, it often forms the transition zone between wet *Sphagnum* hollows and dry hummock tops.

Preferred pH ranges from moderately acidic (pH 4.5–5.5) to strongly acidic (pH 3.5–4.5) in ombrotrophic sites.

Elevation ranges from sea level in boreal regions to over 3,000 m in tropical montane peatlands.

## Ecology and Peatland Function

*Sphagnum magellanicum* is a keystone species in peatland ecosystems and a major driver of peat accumulation.

Like all *Sphagnum* species, it acidifies its surroundings through cation exchange, creating conditions unfavorable for most vascular plants.

The hyaline cells in its leaves can absorb and retain water up to 20 times the moss's dry weight, acting as a living sponge.

This water-holding capacity maintains the waterlogged, anaerobic conditions necessary for peat formation and carbon storage.

*S. magellanicum* contributes significantly to global carbon sequestration through the accumulation of partially decomposed plant material as peat.

Northern peatlands dominated by this and related *Sphagnum* species store an estimated 30% of global soil carbon.

The species hosts diverse microbial communities, including methanotrophic bacteria that modulate methane emissions from peatlands.

It provides substrate and microhabitat for a range of peatland invertebrates, including specialized mites, springtails, and insect larvae.

The dense carpets reduce evaporation and help maintain the high water table essential for peatland function.

## Economic Uses and Applications

*S. magellanicum* is harvested commercially as a component of horticultural peat in parts of Scandinavia, Canada, and Russia.

The harvested material is used as a soil amendment, growing medium, and seed-starting substrate in professional horticulture.

Its superior water retention and acidic pH make it particularly valuable for cultivation of acid-loving ornamental plants.

The species has been investigated as a bioindicator for heavy metal contamination in peatland environments.

Peat blocks containing *S. magellanicum* have been used historically as insulation material and as an absorbent in wound dressings.

Dried *Sphagnum* material from this species has antiseptic properties due to the presence of phenolic compounds.

The moss is used in the florist trade for decorative purposes and as a packing material for transporting live plants.

## Cultivation and Conservation

*S. magellanicum* can be cultivated artificially through fragmentation of donor material spread on prepared peat beds.

Commercial *Sphagnum* farming has been developed as a sustainable alternative to harvesting from natural peatlands.

Cultivation requires permanently waterlogged, acidic conditions with high humidity and minimal competition from vascular plants.

Growth rates under cultivation are slow, typically 1–3 cm of vertical accumulation per year.

The species faces significant conservation challenges due to peatland drainage for agriculture, forestry, and peat extraction.

An estimated 15–20% of global peatlands have been drained or degraded, reducing habitat for *S. magellanicum* and associated species.

Climate change poses additional threats through altered precipitation patterns and increased frequency of summer drought.

Conservation efforts focus on peatland restoration, including rewetting drained sites and reintroducing *Sphagnum* from cultivated stock.
