The plant has been used as food and animal fodder in various cultures. Young fronds and sporocarps are edible and were historically collected as a vegetable in parts of Europe and Asia.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Marsilea quadrifolia is the type species of the genus Marsilea, which contains approximately 65 species of aquatic and semi-aquatic ferns. The family Marsileaceae is one of three families in the order Salviniales, alongside Salviniaceae and Azollaceae.

The rhizome is long-creeping, slender (1–2 mm diameter), and rooted at nodes. It grows horizontally through the substrate at depths of 5–50 cm, producing fronds at regular intervals from the upper surface.

Floating fronds consist of a slender petiole 5–30 cm long bearing four obovate leaflets at the apex. Each leaflet is 5–15 mm long and 3–12 mm wide, with entire margins and a small notch at the rounded apex.

Submerged fronds may differ markedly from floating fronds, sometimes bearing only two leaflets or undivided linear blades. This heterophylly is an adaptive response to water depth and light availability.

Reproductive structures are sporocarps, hard, bean-shaped structures 3–5 mm long borne on short stalks at the base of the petioles. Each sporocarp contains both mega- and microsporangia, a condition known as heterosporagy.

Spore release is triggered when the sporocarp imbibes water and the suture opens, releasing a gelatinous structure called the sorophore that bears the sporangia. This mechanism can remain viable for decades, with documented germination from century-old sporocarps.

## Distribution and Habitat

M. quadrifolia is native to much of Europe, from the British Isles and southern Scandinavia south to the Mediterranean basin and eastward through central Asia to China and Japan. It also occurs in North Africa (Morocco, Algeria) and the Caucasus.

In North America, the species is considered introduced and has been recorded in several northeastern states, particularly in coastal plain ponds and slow-moving waterways of New England and the mid-Atlantic region. Australian populations are also introduced.

The species inhabits shallow, still or slow-moving freshwater habitats including ponds, marshes, ditches, canal margins, and the sheltered edges of lakes and streams. It tolerates both acidic and alkaline waters but prefers nutrient-moderate to nutrient-rich conditions.

Water clover grows rooted in mud, sand, or clay substrates at depths of 5–60 cm. In deep water, the petioles elongate to reach the surface, while in shallow water or exposed mud, the fronds adopt an erect terrestrial form.

Seasonal behavior includes winter dormancy [[dighton-fungal-biomass-production-aquatic-ecosystems]]. Its floating fronds provide shade that moderates water temperature and reduces algal growth in small water bodies.

The dense mat of floating leaves creates habitat complexity for aquatic invertebrates, including dragonfly nymphs, water beetles, and mosquito larvae. Small fish and amphibian larvae use the plant for shelter and foraging.

Waterfowl including mallards, teal, and swans feed on the fronds and rhizomes. The sporocarps are consumed by waterfowl and may be dispersed to new habitats through their digestive tracts, facilitating colonization of isolated water bodies.

The species can form dense floating mats that reduce light penetration to submerged vegetation. In introduced ranges, this competitive ability has raised concerns about impacts on native aquatic plant communities.

No significant toxicity has been reported for M. quadrifolia. However, as with all aquatic plants collected from the wild, contamination from agricultural runoff, heavy metals, or waterborne pathogens should be considered before consumption.

Modern interest in the species as a food plant is limited, though it has been investigated as a potential aquatic vegetable for integrated aquaculture systems. Its rapid vegetative growth and ease of harvest make it theoretically suitable for small-scale cultivation.
