## Overview

**Adenophora triphylla** (Thunb.) A.DC., commonly known as ladybell or three-leaf ladybell, is a herbaceous perennial in the family Campanulaceae. It is native to [[glehnia-littoralis]] (bei sha shen or "northern sha shen"). Adenophora roots are designated "southern sha shen" (nan sha shen) and are considered more effective for resolving phlegm, while Glehnia is preferred for yin deficiency patterns.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Plantae · **Clade:** Angiosperms · **Clade:** Eudicots · **Clade:** Asterids · **Order:** Asterales · **Family:** Campanulaceae · **Genus:** Adenophora · **Species:** A. triphylla

The genus Adenophora contains approximately 60 species distributed across Eurasia, with the greatest diversity in East Asia. Adenophora triphylla belongs to section Adenophora, characterized by pendulous flowers and a well-developed style disk.

The species was first described by Carl Peter Thunberg as Campanula triphylla in 1784, later transferred to Adenophora by Augustin Pyramus de Candolle in 1830. The specific epithet triphylla refers to the whorled arrangement of leaves at the stem nodes.

Plants grow 40–100 cm tall from a thick, carrot-like taproot. The root is white to pale yellow, fleshy, and 5–20 cm long with a diameter of 1–3 cm at maturity.

Lower leaves are opposite or whorled in groups of three to four, while upper leaves become alternate. Leaf blades are ovate to lanceolate, 3–10 cm long, with serrated margins and a dark green upper surface.

Flowers are pendulous, narrowly campanulate, 1.5–2.5 cm long, and pale blue to lavender-white. They are borne in loose racemes or panicles at the stem apex and upper leaf axils.

The style disk (gynostegium) is a critical diagnostic feature, forming a distinct ring or cup around the base of the style. This structure is absent in Campanula and present in all Adenophora species.

The fruit is an ovoid capsule opening by three apical valves. Seeds are numerous, small (0.6–0.9 mm), brown, and winged at the margins, facilitating wind dispersal.

## Distribution and Habitat

Adenophora triphylla is native to East Asia, with a range spanning Japan (Hokkaido, Honshu, Shikoku, Kyushu), the Korean Peninsula, northeastern China (Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning), and the Russian Far East (Primorsky Krai).

The species grows in montane meadows, forest margins, grassy slopes, and along streambanks at elevations of 100–2,000 m. It prefers moist, well-drained soils in partially shaded to open sites.

In Japan, it is common in the montane zone of central and northern Honshu, where it grows in tall-herb meadow communities alongside species such as Cacalia delphinifolia and Aconitum japonicum.

In Korea, Adenophora triphylla is widespread in mountainous areas and is one of the most commonly encountered Adenophora species. It grows in forest clearings, along trails, and in moist grassland from valley floors to ridgelines.

The species tolerates a range of soil types from acidic to mildly alkaline but performs best on humus-rich, well-drained loams. It is intolerant of waterlogging and drought during [[apigenin]] and [[guzman-magic-mushrooms-chemical-constituents]] from Adenophora triphylla roots, with triterpenoid saponins being the most extensively studied class for biological activity.

Clinical applications in traditional medicine focus on treating [[ophiopogon-japonicus]] and Scrophularia ningpoensis in the formula known as Saengmaek-san, used for treating thirst, dry cough, and depletion of body fluids after illness.

## Cultivation

Adenophora triphylla is hardy in USDA zones 4–8 and prefers partial shade to full sun in humus-rich, moist but well-drained soil. It is well-suited to woodland-edge gardens, east-facing borders, and naturalized meadow plantings.

Propagation is by seed or division. Seeds require 4–6 weeks of cold stratification and should be surface-sown in [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]]. Germination rates are moderate (50–70%), with seedlings developing slowly in the first year.

Division of mature plants is best performed in early spring before new growth begins. The deep taproot makes division challenging, and plants may take one to two seasons to re-establish after root disturbance.

For medicinal root production, plants are typically grown for 2–3 years before harvest. Roots are dug in late autumn after the foliage dies back, cleaned, and either used fresh or dried for storage.
