## Streptomycin Sulfate

## Description
[[streptomycin-sulfate]] is an antibiotic used in mycological culture media to suppress bacterial contamination while allowing mushroom mycelium to grow. It is a key component of antibiotic-supplemented agar formulations used for purifying bacteria-contaminated cultures, particularly in the [[antibiotic-sandwich]] technique.

## Classification
- **Category**: Antibiotic / Laboratory chemical
- **Domain**: Mycology, microbiology
- **Chemical class**: Aminoglycoside antibiotic
- **Function**: Bacterial growth suppression in [[fungal-culture-media]]

## Key Facts

### Use in Mycology
- Added to antibiotic agar formulations for screening and purifying contaminated cultures
- Used at 0.01 gram per liter in agar media
- Added after autoclaving and cooling to 113-122F (45-50C) as it is heat-sensitive
- Effective against broad spectrum of gram-negative and some gram-positive bacteria

### Antibiotic Agar Formula (Dr. Bob Blanchette, University of Minnesota)
Base ingredients (autoclaved 20 min):
- 1000 ml distilled/deionized water
- 15g malt extract
- 15g agar
- 15g yeast extract
- 0.06g Benlate (50% wettable powder; fungicide)

Added after cooling to 113-122F (45-50C):
- 0.01g streptomycin sulfate
- 2 ml lactic acid

### Applications
- Antibiotic sandwich technique: pour antibiotic plates, place contaminated mycelium on one side, flip agar wedge over it. Mycelium drills through; bacteria cannot follow.
- Culture purification: screen through 1-2 transfers, then switch to antibiotic-free medium
- Rescuing contaminated isolates: valuable for saving rare wild-collected cultures

### Limitations
- Does not eliminate all bacteria — some may be resistant
- Not a substitute for proper aseptic technique
- Should be temporary for purification, not permanent in media
- Cannot be used in streak tests which require antibiotic-free media

## Relevance to Cultivation
Streptomycin sulfate is essential for rescuing and purifying valuable mushroom cultures contaminated with bacteria. The antibiotic sandwich technique can save rare wild isolates that would otherwise be lost.
