# Glehnia littoralis

## Overview

Glehnia littoralis, commonly known as beach silvertop, glehnia, or sha shen (沙参) in Chinese and haerbang (해랑뱅이) in Korean, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Apiaceae. It is one of the few truly coastal medicinal plants in East Asian pharmacopoeias.

The species is native to sandy beaches and coastal dunes along the Pacific coast of East Asia, ranging from northern China, Korea, and Japan to the Russian Far East and Alaska. Its natural habitat is restricted to the upper beach zone above the high-tide line.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), the dried root (Bei Sha Shen, 北沙参, "Northern Glehnia Root") is valued as a yin-nourishing tonic. It is used to moisten the lungs, generate fluids, and clear heat, particularly in conditions of yin deficiency with dry cough and thirst.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomic classification:**

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Apiales
- **Family:** Apiaceae
- **Genus:** Glehnia
- **Species:** G. littoralis Fr. Schmidt ex Miq.

**Synonyms:** Glehnia littoralis F. Schmidt, Sium littorale (Fr. Schmidt) Koso-Pol., Phellopterus littoralis (Fr. Schmidt) Benth. The genus Glehnia is monotypic, containing only this single species.

The genus was named in honor of Johann August Carl Glehn (1815–1888), a German botanist and plant collector who worked extensively in East Asia.

**Morphology:** G. littoralis is a perennial herb growing 20–60 cm tall. The plant produces a thick, fleshy, cylindrical taproot 10–30 cm long and 1–3 cm in diameter, with a characteristic aromatic odor.

Leaves are 2–3 ternate or bipinnate, with thick, leathery, ovate to rhombic leaflets 2–6 cm long. The leaf surface is glabrous and dark green, with a waxy cuticle that reduces water loss in the exposed coastal environment.

Inflorescences are compound umbels, 5–15 cm across, with 8–20 unequal rays. Flowers are small and white, with petals approximately 1 mm long. Flowering occurs from June to August.

The fruit is an ovoid to suborbicular cremocarp, 4–6 mm long, with prominent corky ribs. The corky rib tissue aids in water dispersal and may contribute to the species' ability to colonize distant shorelines.

The corky pericarp enables the fruit to float in seawater for extended periods, facilitating long-distance dispersal along ocean currents. This dispersal mechanism explains the species' wide but disjunct coastal distribution from China to Alaska.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The root contains a diverse array of secondary metabolites. The principal bioactive compound classes are **coumarins**, **polyacetylenes**, **polysaccharides**, and **essential oil components**.

**Coumarins** are the most extensively studied constituents. Key compounds include imperatorin, bergapten, xanthotoxin, psoralen, isoimperatorin, and osthol. Total coumarin content in dried root ranges from 0.3–1.2%.

**Polyacetylenes** include falcarindiol, falcarinol, and panaxynol. Falcarindiol has demonstrated significant cytotoxic activity against several human cancer cell lines in vitro, with IC50 values in the low micromolar range.

**Polysaccharides** extracted from the root (GLPs) are water-soluble heteroglycans with immunomodulatory properties. They enhance macrophage phagocytic activity and stimulate lymphocyte proliferation at doses of 50–200 μg/mL.

**Pharmacological activities:**

- **Yin-nourishing and fluid-generating:** In TCM terms, the root moistens lung yin and generates body fluids. Modern pharmacology supports this through demonstrated mucoprotective and secretagogue effects on respiratory epithelium.

- **Anti-inflammatory:** Imperatorin and bergapten inhibit COX-2 expression and NF-κB activation in LPS-stimulated macrophages, reducing production of TNF-α, IL-6, and prostaglandin E2.

- **Antioxidant:** The methanolic extract shows DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activity. Oral administration reduced lipid peroxidation and elevated SOD and catalase activities in carbon tetrachloride-treated rats.

- **Immunomodulatory:** Root polysaccharides enhanced both cell-mediated and humoral immune responses in mice, increasing splenocyte proliferation and NK cell activity.

- **Anticancer preliminary data:** Falcarindiol and imperatorin have shown pro-apoptotic effects in lung, liver, and colon cancer cell lines. In vivo validation is limited.

## Traditional Medicine

In TCM, Bei Sha Shen is classified as sweet, slightly bitter, and slightly cold. It enters the lung and stomach meridians. Its primary indications are lung yin deficiency with dry cough, chronic bronchitis, and stomach yin deficiency with dry mouth and poor appetite.

Standard dosage is 9–15 g of dried root per decoction. It is commonly combined with Mai Men Dong (Ophiopogon tuber) and Shu Di Huang (prepared Rehmannia root) in formulas for yin deficiency patterns.

In traditional Korean medicine, the root (Gaesam, 개삼) is used for respiratory conditions, digestive disorders, and as a general tonic. It is included in several traditional herbal formulations alongside other coastal medicinal plants.

In Japan, the root (Hamabofu) has historically been used in Kampo medicine for cough and thirst, though it is less commonly prescribed today than in Chinese or Korean practice.

The young leaves and stems are also consumed as a coastal vegetable in some Japanese regions, where they are blanched and served with soy sauce or miso. This culinary use is relatively localized and not widely known outside coastal communities.

## Ecology and Applications

G. littoralis is a psammophyte (sand-loving plant) specialized for coastal dune environments. It stabilizes sand through an extensive root system and contributes to dune succession by trapping wind-blown sand and creating microhabitats for other species.

The species is adapted to saline conditions, salt spray, and periodic sand burial. Its thick waxy leaves and deep taproot enable survival in the nutrient-poor, drought-prone, and physically dynamic beach environment.

The plant is pollinated by a variety of generalist insects including flies, small bees, and beetles. Its coastal habitat means it provides nectar and pollen resources in an ecosystem where flowering plants are relatively scarce.

Conservation status varies by region. In parts of China, wild populations have declined due to coastal development and overharvesting for the medicinal root trade. The species is listed as a protected plant in several Chinese provinces.

In coastal restoration projects, G. littoralis has been planted for dune stabilization and as a component of native coastal vegetation restoration. Its ornamental value (attractive white umbels and silvery foliage) has also led to limited use in seaside gardens.
