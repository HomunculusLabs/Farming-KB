## Overview

**Dudleya viscida** (S. Watson) Britton & Rose, commonly called the sticky liveforever or viscid dudleya, is a rare succulent in the family Crassulaceae. It is endemic to a narrow stretch of coastal bluffs in southern Orange County, California.

The species is distinguished by its sticky, glandular leaf surfaces — an unusual trait in the genus Dudleya. The specific epithet "viscida" refers to this characteristic stickiness.

Dudleya viscida forms compact rosettes of pale green to whitish leaves that clasp tightly to coastal cliff faces. It is one of the rarest Dudleya species, with a highly restricted natural range.

The species is protected under the California Endangered Species Act and the federal Endangered Species Act. All wild populations occur on protected lands including state parks and nature reserves.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Dudleya viscida was first described by Sereno Watson in 1882 as Cotyledon viscida, based on material collected near San Juan Capistrano. It was transferred to the genus Dudleya by Britton and Rose in 1903.

The plant forms a stemless or nearly stemless rosette 5–12 cm in diameter. Rosettes are usually solitary or sparsely offsetting in age.

Leaves are 3–7 cm long, 1–2.5 cm wide, and shaped oblong-lanceolate to oblanceolate. The leaf surfaces are covered with a sticky, glandular exudate that gives them a tacky texture.

Leaf color ranges from pale glaucous green to almost white due to a thick epicuticular wax. The wax layer is less pronounced than in some Dudleya species, allowing the green leaf tissue to show through.

Flowering stems emerge from May through July, reaching 15–30 cm tall. The inflorescence is a compact cyme with 3–7 ascending branches.

Flowers are small, with petals 8–12 mm long, colored pale yellow to cream. The petals are marked with fine red or orange lines on the keel.

The sticky leaf surface is unique among Dudleya species and likely serves to trap insects or reduce herbivory. The glandular exudate has a faint resinous scent.

Chromosome number is 2n = 34, a tetraploid count shared with several other Dudleya species in subgenus Dudleya. Polyploidy has played a significant role in the evolution of the genus.

## Distribution and Habitat

Dudleya viscida is endemic to a narrow 15-km stretch of coastal bluffs in southern Orange County, California. All known populations occur between Dana Point and San Clemente.

The species grows exclusively on steep, north-facing sandstone and shale cliffs overlooking the Pacific Ocean. Plants occupy crevices and ledges on nearly vertical rock faces.

Elevational range is from near sea level to approximately 100 m. All populations are within 1 km of the coastline, where they receive frequent marine fog.

The maritime climate provides moderate temperatures year-round, with summer fog supplementing rainfall. Annual precipitation averages 250–350 mm, falling mostly from November through April.

Associated species include other cliff-dwelling plants such as Coreopsis gigantea, Eriogonum parvifolium, and various fern species. The specialized cliff habitat limits competition from faster-growing vegetation.

The species is restricted to a specific geological formation known as the San Onofre Breccia. This substrate provides the mineral composition and drainage characteristics the plant requires.

The total wild population is estimated at fewer than 10,000 individual plants across all known sites. Population size fluctuates with annual rainfall patterns, with drought years causing noticeable declines.

## Cultivation

Dudleya viscida is rarely cultivated and remains a specialist plant for experienced succulent growers. It requires precise conditions that mimic its native coastal bluff habitat.

The growing medium must be extremely well-draining, composed primarily of mineral grit. A mix of 80% pumice or decomposed granite with 20% loam provides suitable drainage.

Plants prefer bright indirect light or morning sun with afternoon shade. They should never be exposed to hot afternoon sun, which quickly scorches the leaves.

Watering should be infrequent but deep during the winter growing season. Summer watering should be minimal, as the plant naturally enters dormancy during the warmest months.

Overhead watering must be avoided to prevent rosette rot. Water should be directed at the soil surface, keeping the leaves as dry as possible.

Temperature requirements are moderate, with preferred growing temperatures of 10–22 °C. The species has limited frost tolerance and should be protected below -1 °C.

Propagation is primarily by seed, as the species rarely produces offsets. Seed should be sown on the surface of moist, sterile grit in fall.

Germination occurs within 2–4 weeks at 15–20 °C. Seedlings grow very slowly and require several years to reach flowering size.

Plants in cultivation are sensitive to humidity and poor air circulation. A well-ventilated growing area is essential to prevent fungal infections that can quickly kill the rosette.

Botanical gardens maintain small conservation collections of this species. These cultivated specimens serve as genetic reservoirs and educational displays for public awareness of rare endemic plants.

The species is generally unsuitable for standard succulent collections due to its exacting requirements. Most successful cultivation occurs in coastal California gardens that naturally provide the cool, foggy conditions it requires.

## Ecology and Conservation

Dudleya viscida is listed as Threatened under the U.S. Endangered Species Act and as a threatened species by the California Department of Fish and Wildlife. Its extremely limited range makes it vulnerable to any habitat disturbance.

Primary threats include coastal development, recreational rock climbing on habitat cliffs, and invasive plant species. Several populations have been impacted by trail construction and bluff stabilization projects.

The sticky leaf exudate may serve to deter insect herbivory and reduce water loss. Research suggests the glands secrete secondary metabolites that discourage feeding by common succulent pests.

Pollination is likely performed by small native bees and flies. The compact flower structure and pale coloration suggest generalist insect pollination rather than specialized bird or moth pollination.

All known populations occur within protected areas, including state parks and county nature reserves. Active monitoring programs track population sizes and reproductive success.

Climate change poses a long-term threat through sea-level rise and increased coastal erosion. Several populations grow on bluffs that are actively eroding, and accelerated erosion could eliminate habitat within decades.

Recovery efforts include habitat protection signage and exclusion of recreational activities from cliff areas. Seed banking programs maintain genetically diverse material as insurance against population loss.

The species has become a symbol of coastal conservation in Orange County. Its protection has contributed to broader efforts to preserve remaining coastal bluff habitats in southern California.

Dudleya poaching for the horticultural trade has affected several Dudleya species. While D. viscida has been less targeted due to its rarity and remote habitat, increased enforcement monitors all known sites.
