## Overview

**Curcuma phaeocaulis** is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Zingiberaceae, native to southern China and mainland Southeast Asia. It is one of the three primary species of **E Zhu** (郁金) used in Traditional Chinese Medicine, alongside *[[curcuma-wenyujin]]* and *[[curcuma-kwangsiensis]]*.

The dried rhizome is the principal medicinal organ, valued for its ability to invigorate blood circulation, alleviate pain, and resolve stagnation. Modern pharmacological studies have confirmed significant anti-inflammatory, antitumor, and hepatoprotective activities.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Liliopsida → Zingiberales → Zingiberaceae → Zingiberoideae → Curcuma → C. phaeocaulis.

**Authority:** Valeton (1918). The species was originally described from specimens collected in Yunnan, China.

The plant reaches 0.8–1.5 m in height. Leaves are broadly lanceolate, 30–60 cm long and 10–18 cm wide, with a prominent purple-tinged midrib. The leaf sheaths form a distinct pseudostem.

Inflorescences arise from the rhizome as separate flowering spikes, 12–20 cm long, with pale green bracts tipped in pink or purple. Flowers are yellow, funnel-shaped, with a labellum marked by a central yellow-orange blotch.

Rhizomes are ellipsoidal to irregularly shaped, 3–8 cm in diameter, with a dark brown exterior and yellowish-orange interior. The aroma is camphoraceous and slightly spicy, less pungent than common turmeric.

### Antimicrobial Properties

The essential oil shows moderate activity against Gram-positive bacteria (*Staphylococcus aureus*, *Bacillus subtilis*) and select [[bloomfield-candida-albicans-yeast-hyphal-dimorphism]]*). MIC values range from 125–500 μg/mL depending on the organism.

## Traditional Medicine

In TCM, *C. phaeocaulis* rhizome is classified as **pungent, bitter, and warm**, entering the liver and spleen meridians. It is prescribed to break up blood stasis, promote menstruation, and relieve epigastric and abdominal pain.

Common clinical applications include dysmenorrhea, amenorrhea, traumatic injuries with bruising, and hepatosplenic enlargement. It is frequently combined with *Curcuma wenyujin* and *Salvia miltiorrhiza* in formulae for cardiovascular and gynecological conditions.

The standard daily dose in decoction is 3–10 g of dried rhizome. Overdose may cause gastrointestinal irritation due to the high volatile oil content.

## Chemistry and Extraction

The essential oil profile varies by geographic origin and harvest time. Germacrone content peaks during flowering (July–September), while curdione accumulates more steadily through the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].

Steam distillation is the standard extraction method for volatile constituents. Supercritical CO₂ extraction yields higher germacrone and curdione recovery with less thermal degradation of labile sesquiterpenoids.

The rhizome also contains phenolic acids (ferulic acid, p-coumaric acid), flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol derivatives), and polysaccharides that contribute to antioxidant activity. Total polysaccharide content reaches 8–12% in dried rhizome.

### Key Sesquiterpenoids

Curdione is a furanosesquiterpenoid and the principal bioactive marker compound. It is used as a quality control standard in Chinese Pharmacopoeia monographs for *C. phaeocaulis* rhizome, with a minimum content requirement of 0.5%.

Germacrone is a monocyclic sesquiterpene ketone with broad pharmacological activity. Its crystal structure was resolved in 2012, facilitating SAR studies that identified the α,β-unsaturated carbonyl as essential for cytotoxicity.

## Distribution and Habitat

*C. phaeocaulis* is distributed across southern China (Sichuan, Yunnan, Guizhou, Guangxi, Fujian), northern Myanmar, northern Thailand, and northern Vietnam. It grows at elevations of 800–1,600 m.

The species favors warm, humid, shaded environments in evergreen broadleaf forests, forest margins, and valleys with well-drained, organic-rich loamy soils. Natural populations are declining due to overharvesting for medicinal trade.

## Cultivation

Propagation is primarily by rhizome division. Rhizome segments with 2–3 buds are planted in spring at a spacing of 30–40 cm within rows, with 50–60 cm between rows. The crop requires partial shade (40–60% light reduction) and consistent moisture.
