## Overview

Piper lanceaefolium is a shrubby perennial plant in the family Piperaceae, native to the tropical and subtropical forests of Mexico and [[cannabis-for-inflammatory-conditions]].

The species is part of the large genus *Piper* (over 1,000 species), which includes black pepper (*P. nigrum*) and many ethnobotanically significant tropical plants used across Latin America, [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]] worldwide. Fruits are small drupes, 2–3 mm in diameter, green ripening to dark purple or black, borne tightly along the spike. Each fruit contains a single seed.

The species is distributed from southern Mexico (Veracruz, Oaxaca, Chiapas, Tabasco) through Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, and into Nicaragua and Costa Rica.

It occurs in humid tropical and subtropical forests, cloud forests, and secondary growth, typically at elevations between 100 and 1,500 m.

The species shows a preference for calcareous soils in some regions, though it is adaptable to a range of substrates. Populations in cloud forest habitats tend to have larger leaves and higher essential oil content compared to lowland populations.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The essential oil of *P. lanceaefolium* leaves is dominated by phenylpropanoid compounds, particularly **safrole**, **methyleugenol**, and **eugenol**, which are characteristic of the genus *Piper*.

Safrole is the principal constituent, comprising 40–70% of the essential oil in some populations. It is also the precursor compound used in the industrial synthesis of MDMA and MDA, which has led to regulatory scrutiny of safrole-rich *Piper* species.

Other significant volatile constituents include **linalool**, **α-pinene**, **β-caryophyllene**, and **limonene**. The oil yield from dried leaves ranges from 0.5–2.0% (v/w).

Pharmacological studies on related *Piper* species demonstrate anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antimicrobial, and bronchodilatory activities, largely attributed to the phenylpropanoid and sesquiterpene components.

**Safety note:** Safrole and methyleugenol are classified as possible carcinogens (IARC Group 2B) based on rodent studies. Regular consumption of safrole-rich plant material is not recommended without processing to reduce safrole content.
