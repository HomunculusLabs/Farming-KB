# Paulownia tomentosa

Paulownia tomentosa, commonly known as [[permaculture]]tree, princess tree, or foxglove
tree, is a deciduous hardwood species in the family Paulowniaceae. Native to central and
western China, it has become naturalized across temperate and subtropical regions
worldwide, valued in permaculture and agroforestry for its extraordinarily rapid growth,
coppice ability, and large leaves that produce nutrient-rich mulch.

## Taxonomy and History

The genus Paulownia is named in honor of Anna Pavlovna, daughter of Tsar Paul I of
Russia and queen consort of the Netherlands. The species epithet tomentosa refers to the
densely hairy (tomentose) undersides of the leaves. Paulownia has a deep cultural
significance in China, where it has been planted near homes for centuries. According to
tradition, a paulownia tree is planted when a daughter is born, and the timber is
harvested to fashion her dowry chest upon marriage. The wood is also the traditional
material for the guqin, an ancient Chinese zither-like stringed instrument with over 3,000
years of continuous use.

## Botany and Identification

Paulownia tomentosa is a medium to large tree reaching 10 to 25 meters in height, with
a spreading crown and a straight cylindrical trunk that can exceed 60 cm in diameter at
maturity. The bark is smooth and gray on young trees, becoming rough, fissured, and dark
gray-brown with age. The branching pattern is somewhat irregular, with major limbs
emerging at wide angles from the trunk.

The leaves are large, broad, and heart-shaped (cordate), measuring 15 to 40 cm across,
arranged oppositely on young shoots but sometimes appearing whorled due to the compressed
internodes of rapid extension growth. Leaf surfaces are green and glabrous above, with
dense pubescence (tomentose) beneath, giving the species its epithet. The leaf margin is
entire or shallowly lobed, and the petioles are long and stout, 10 to 20 cm in length. The
foliage is late to emerge in spring, one of the last trees to leaf out, and drops early in
autumn. Individual leaves are so large that they are sometimes used as makeshift umbrellas
or food wrappers in rural China.

The flowers are striking and conspicuous, appearing in early spring (March to April)
before or with the new leaves in erect panicles of 10 to 30 cm length. Individual flowers
are tubular, approximately 4 to 6 cm long, violet-blue to lavender in color with yellow
internal markings and a delicate vanilla-like fragrance. Each flower has a bell-shaped
calyx covered in brown tomentum, five fused petals forming the tubular corolla, four
didynamous stamens, and a superior ovary. They are highly attractive to pollinators,
particularly bees, producing abundant nectar that yields a premium pale honey in China.

The fruit is a dry, ovoid, leathery capsule 3 to 4 cm long, persisting on the tree
through winter and splitting open in spring to release numerous tiny winged seeds. A
single tree can produce millions of seeds annually. The seeds are extremely light
(approximately 0.16 mg each) and wind-dispersed, contributing to the species' colonization
ability. Seeds can remain viable in the soil for several years.

## Growth Rate and Cultivation

Paulownia tomentosa is renowned as one of the fastest growing temperate hardwood trees
in the world. Under optimal conditions, seedlings can reach 3 to 5 meters in their first
growing season, and mature trees may add 2 to 3 meters of height per year. Some documented
specimens have achieved 6 meters of growth in a single season. Growth is strongly
influenced by site conditions, with deep, well-drained, fertile loams producing the best
results. The species is tolerant of a wide range of soil pH (5.0 to 8.5) but performs
poorly on waterlogged or compacted soils, where root rot can become an issue.

It is hardy to approximately -15 to -20 degrees Celsius when dormant, though young
growth and spring shoots are frost-tender. It thrives in full sun and is remarkably
drought-tolerant once established, owing to its deep and extensive root system that can
extend several meters below the soil surface. Propagation is most commonly achieved
through root cuttings (sections of 5 to 10 cm length planted horizontally), which produce
true-to-type clones and avoid the genetic variability of seed. Seed propagation requires
light for germination (surface sowing) and careful moisture management, as the tiny seeds
are easily washed away or buried too deeply. Tissue culture methods have been developed
for commercial plantation establishment.

For maximum timber production, intensive management regimes have been developed
involving site preparation, fertilization, weed control, and spacing of approximately 4 to
6 meters between trees. Under such management, harvestable timber (30 to 40 cm diameter)
can be produced in 8 to 12 years, compared to 30 to 60 years for comparable temperate
hardwoods like oak or walnut.

## Timber Properties

The wood of Paulownia tomentosa is pale blonde to light brown, sometimes with a pinkish
or purplish tint, straight-grained, and relatively lightweight with an air-dry density of
approximately 280 to 320 kg per cubic meter, making it one of the lightest commercial
hardwoods. Despite its low density, it has remarkable strength-to-weight ratios, good
dimensional stability, low shrinkage (approximately 3 percent radial, 5 percent
tangential), and excellent resistance to warping and splitting. The timber seasons rapidly
with minimal checking when properly stacked.

Paulownia wood is highly prized for traditional Japanese furniture (known as kiri),
where it has been used for centuries for tansu chests, geta (wooden sandals), and musical
instruments. The wood is also used for surfboards, skis, model aircraft, lightweight
packaging, Venetian blinds, and as a core material in composite panels. It has natural
resistance to rot and insect attack due to the presence of tannins and other secondary
compounds. The timber is easily worked with both hand and machine tools, takes stains and
finishes well, glues satisfactorily, and has excellent acoustic properties.

The low thermal conductivity of paulownia wood makes it suitable for insulated
containers and sauna [[permaculture-design]] also used in traditional Chinese coffin
manufacture, and the wood's resistance to moisture absorption has led to its use in boat
building for lightweight components.

## Permaculture and Agroforestry Applications

In permaculture design, Paulownia tomentosa serves multiple functions as a [[windbreak-and-shelterbelt-design]] and [[nitrogen-fixing-trees]] companion. As a nurse tree,
its rapid growth and large canopy provide quick shade and wind protection for establishing
understory crops and slower-growing climax species. The large leaves decompose rapidly,
contributing significant quantities of organic matter and nutrients to the soil surface.
Leaf fall in autumn creates a thick mulch layer that suppresses weeds, moderates soil
temperature, and improves moisture retention. The nutrient content of fallen paulownia
leaves is relatively high, containing significant nitrogen, potassium, calcium, and
magnesium.

Paulownia is noted for its strong [[permaculture-designers-manual-coppice-systems]] response, regrowing vigorously from the stump
after harvest. A single planting can produce multiple timber crops through coppice
rotations of 8 to 12 years. The species can also be pollarded for fodder production, as
the leaves are palatable and nutritious for livestock, containing approximately 18 to 20
percent crude protein on a dry weight basis. In some regions, paulownia leaves are
harvested as supplemental animal feed, particularly for cattle, sheep, and poultry.

The deep root system of paulownia is effective at breaking through compacted subsoil
layers, improving soil structure and drainage for subsequent plantings. In alley cropping
systems, paulownia can be planted in rows with agricultural crops between, providing shade
during hot summer months while allowing full light penetration during the dormant season.

## Invasive Potential and Ecological Considerations

The combination of rapid growth, prolific seed production, effective wind dispersal,
and tolerance of disturbed sites has led Paulownia tomentosa to become invasive in parts
of the southeastern United States, particularly along roadsides, riparian corridors,
mining sites, and abandoned agricultural land. It is classified as an invasive species in
several US states including Tennessee, North Carolina, and Virginia. In its native range,
it is a natural component of forest succession on disturbed sites.
