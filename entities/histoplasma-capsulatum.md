# Histoplasma capsulatum

Histoplasma capsulatum is a dimorphic [[histoplasma-capsulatum-ohio-valley-disease-dimorphic-fungus]] disease. It is one of the most important systemic [[blastomyces-dermatitidis]], it represents the most dangerous group of endemic fungal pathogens known.

## Taxonomy and Classification

H. capsulatum belongs to the Onygenales within the Ascomycota. Its teleomorph is classified as ajellomyces-capsulatus. The Onygenales are a predominantly keratinophilic order that includes several important human and animal pathogens. H. capsulatum is closely related to B. dermatitidis and C. immitis, all of which are biohazard class 3 organisms and share the dimorphic growth habit that is central to their pathogenicity. The genus Histoplasma is monotypic, with H. capsulatum as the sole species, though the existence of distinct varieties (var. capsulatum and var. farciminosum) reflects significant genetic and phenotypic diversity within the species.

## Dimorphism

H. capsulatum exhibits temperature-dependent dimorphism, a hallmark of the pathogenic Onygenales and a critical factor in its ability to cause disease:

### Mold Phase (Saprotrophic)

At ambient temperatures (25 to 30 degrees C), the fungus grows as a filamentous mold producing both macroconidia (8 to 16 micrometers, thick-walled, tuberculate) and microconidia (2 to 5 micrometers, thin-walled, smooth). The microconidia are the primary infectious propagules, being small enough to reach the alveoli of the lungs when inhaled. The mold phase is the environmental survival form of the fungus.

### Yeast Phase (Parasitic)

At mammalian body temperature (37 degrees C), the fungus converts to a budding yeast form that survives and multiplies within host macrophages. This [[fungal-virulence-factors-host-immune-evasion]] responses by surviving and replicating within the very cells (macrophages) that are supposed to destroy invading microorganisms. The yeast cells are small (2 to 4 micrometers) and ovoid, typically found within macrophage phagolysosomes.

This dimorphic switch is regulated by temperature-sensitive signaling pathways involving cAMP, calcium signaling, and specific transcription factors that control the expression of phase-specific genes.

## Clinical Manifestations

The clinical spectrum of histoplasmosis ranges widely depending on the inoculum size and the immune status of the host:

### Acute Pulmonary Histoplasmosis

Most infections are asymptomatic or produce a mild, self-limiting influenza-like illness 3–17 days after exposure. Symptomatic cases present with fever, non-productive cough, chest pain, fatigue, and myalgia. Chest radiographs may show patchy infiltrates or hilar lymphadenopathy. In otherwise healthy individuals, symptoms typically resolve within 2–4 weeks without specific antifungal therapy, though fatigue may persist for weeks to months.

### Chronic Pulmonary Histoplasmosis

This form occurs primarily in older adults with pre-existing pulmonary disease such as emphysema. It presents as a slowly progressive cavitary lung disease resembling tuberculosis, with chronic cough, weight loss, low-grade fever, and hemoptysis. Chest radiographs reveal apical cavities and fibrosis. Without treatment, chronic pulmonary histoplasmosis can progress to respiratory failure and death over months to years.

### Disseminated Histoplasmosis

The most severe form of the disease, disseminated histoplasmosis occurs when the yeast form spreads beyond the lungs to involve the reticuloendothelial system. It is most common in immunocompromised patients, particularly those with HIV/AIDS, and in infants and the elderly. Clinical features include prolonged fever, weight loss, hepatosplenomegaly, pancytopenia, and mucocutaneous lesions. Central nervous system involvement, including meningitis, may occur and carries a poor prognosis. Without treatment, disseminated histoplasmosis is frequently fatal.

### Ocular and Pericardial Complications

Less common manifestations include ocular histoplasmosis syndrome (presumed ocular histoplasmosis), which can cause choroidal neovascularization and vision loss, and pericarditis, which may follow acute pulmonary infection. These complications highlight the diverse tropism of the organism beyond the respiratory and reticuloendothelial systems.
