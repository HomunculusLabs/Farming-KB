## Overview

**Drosera rotundifolia**, the common sundew, is a perennial carnivorous plant in the family Droseraceae.

It is the most widely distributed sundew species, found across circumboreal regions of [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], Europe, and Asia.

The species is named for its round, spoon-shaped leaf blades covered in glandular tentacles that secrete sticky mucilage.

Insects are attracted to the glistening droplets, become trapped, and are slowly digested by proteolytic enzymes.

Drosera rotundifolia has a centuries-long history of medicinal use, particularly in European herbal medicine for respiratory complaints.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Drosera rotundifolia was first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum.

The genus Drosera contains approximately 200 species, making it one of the largest genera of carnivorous plants.

D. rotundifolia belongs to the subgenus Drosera, section Drosera, characterized by fibrous roots and winter resting buds (hibernacula).

**Leaves:** Arranged in a basal rosette, 3–8 cm in diameter. Each leaf has a round blade (lamina) 4–10 mm diameter.

The lamina is borne on a slender petiole 15–50 mm long, covered with downward-pointing hairs on the upper surface.

The upper surface of the lamina is densely covered with reddish glandular tentacles (trichomes) in two size classes.

Stalked glands (10–20 per leaf) are 2–4 mm long and produce the sticky mucilage that captures prey.

Sessile glands are shorter and responsible for secreting digestive enzymes after prey capture.

**Flowers:** Small, white to pink, 8–12 mm diameter, borne on a scape 5–25 cm tall, in a one-sided raceme.

Flowering occurs June through August. Each flower produces a capsule with numerous tiny seeds.

**Hibernacula:** In autumn, the plant forms a tight resting bud of tightly curled leaves to survive freezing temperatures.

## Distribution and Habitat

Drosera rotundifolia has a circumboreal distribution, occurring across northern Europe, northern Asia, and northern North America.

In North America, it ranges from Alaska across Canada to the northeastern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] and the Great Lakes region.

Disjunct populations exist in the Appalachian Mountains and in mountainous areas of the western United States.

The species is found in acidic, nutrient-poor wetland habitats including bogs, fens, marsh edges, and wet heathland.

It requires full sun to partial shade and consistently moist, waterlogged soils with a pH of 4.0–6.0.

The carnivorous habit is an evolutionary adaptation to nitrogen and phosphorus limitation in its native bog habitats.

## Carnivory and Digestion

The trapping mechanism of D. rotundifolia is a flypaper-type trap, one of five recognized carnivorous plant trap types.

When an insect contacts the mucilage on the glandular tentacles, it becomes stuck and triggers a slow thigmonastic response.

Adjacent tentacles bend toward the prey over a period of minutes, increasing contact and adhesion.

The leaf blade itself may fold inward to envelop the prey, maximizing the digestive surface area.

Digestion is carried out by a suite of proteases including droserain, a cysteine protease unique to the genus.

The plant absorbs nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients from the digested insect body.

Experiments have shown that fed plants grow significantly larger and produce more flowers than unfed controls.

Charles Darwin conducted extensive experiments on D. rotundifolia, detailed in his 1875 work "Insectivorous Plants."

## Medicinal Uses

Drosera rotundifolia has a long tradition of use in European phytotherapy, particularly as an antispasmodic and expectorant.

The primary historical application was for treating respiratory conditions including coughs, bronchitis, whooping cough, and asthma.

The aerial parts are harvested during flowering and dried for use in teas, tinctures, and pharmaceutical extracts.

The German Commission E and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) have both recognized Drosera for respiratory indications.

Active compounds include naphthoquinones (plumbagin, droserone), flavonoids, and ellagic acid derivatives.

Plumbagin exhibits antibacterial, antifungal, and anti-inflammatory properties in pharmacological studies.

Modern herbal preparations of Drosera are included in cough syrup formulations, often combined with thyme and ivy extracts.

## Cultivation

Drosera rotundifolia is cultivated by carnivorous plant enthusiasts and requires specific conditions to thrive.

Plants must be grown in a nutrient-free medium, typically a mix of sphagnum peat moss and silica sand or perlite.

Water must be low in dissolved minerals — distilled, rain, or reverse-osmosis water is required.

The tray method (sitting the pot in a shallow tray of water) is the standard cultivation technique.

Full sun produces the deepest red coloration in the glandular tentacles.

Plants require a winter dormancy period of 3–4 months at temperatures of 2–5°C to maintain long-term health.

Propagation is typically from seed (which requires cold stratification) or by leaf cuttings in sphagnum moss.
