## Overview

**Sium sisarum**, commonly known as skirret, is a perennial plant in the family Apiaceae cultivated for its cluster of sweet, edible tuberous roots. It was once a widely grown root vegetable in Europe before being largely displaced by the potato.

The species produces a rosette of pinnately compound leaves and umbels of small white flowers. Below ground, it develops a crown of 8–15 finger-sized tubers that are harvested in autumn.

Skirret roots have a sweet, nutty flavor often compared to parsnip and carrot. They can be boiled, roasted, or eaten raw, and were historically valued for their sweetness before the widespread availability of cane sugar.

The plant appears in the writings of Pliny the Elder, who described its cultivation in Roman gardens. Medieval herbals consistently recommend skirret as a nutritious and easily digested food.

The rise of the potato in the 18th century led to a rapid decline in skirret cultivation, as potatoes offered higher yields and easier propagation. By the 19th century, skirret had become a rarity even in traditional kitchen gardens.

The English word "skirret" derives from the Old French "cirée," itself from the Latin "siser," meaning a kind of water parsnip. This etymology reflects the plant's preference for moist growing conditions.

Tudor and Stuart-era banquets frequently featured skirret among the root vegetable courses. Samuel Pepys mentions eating skirret in his diary entries from the 1660s.
