## Overview

**Utricularia gibba**, commonly called the humped bladderwort, is an aquatic carnivorous plant in the family Lentibulariaceae. It is one of the most widely distributed bladderwort species, found on every continent except Antarctica.

The species is notable for its remarkably small genome, sequenced at approximately 82 megabases. Despite its genetic compactness, U. gibba possesses a complete set of typical angiosperm gene families.

U. gibba is a rootless, free-floating aquatic plant with subterranean and aquatic bladder-bearing stolons. It produces numerous microscopic traps that capture tiny aquatic organisms.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Lamiales → Lentibulariaceae → Utricularia → U. gibba.

Authority: L. (1753). The epithet "gibba" means "humped," referring to the shape of the flower's lower corolla lip.

**Vegetative structure:** The plant lacks true roots and leaves. It consists of filiform stolons 5–30 cm long that float freely in water or creep along the substrate.

**Bladders (traps):** Numerous microscopic bladder-like traps 1–3 mm in diameter are produced along the stolons. These are among the most sophisticated carnivorous structures in the plant kingdom.

**Leaves:** True leaves are absent. Photosynthetic tissue is distributed along the stolons, which perform both photosynthetic and absorptive functions.

**Flowers:** Emergent above the water surface on slender scapes 5–20 cm tall. Flowers are yellow, approximately 8–10 mm across, with a prominent spurred lower lip and two-lobed upper lip.

## Distribution and Habitat

U. gibba has a [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] worldwide. It is found in North, Central, and [[biodiversity-fungi-freshwater-aquatic-habitats]]. These include ponds, ditches, marshes, slow-moving streams, rice paddies, and temporary pools.

U. gibba tolerates both acidic and alkaline conditions. It frequently grows as a weed in artificial water bodies including aquaria, water gardens, and irrigation canals.

Its tolerance of eutrophic conditions and ability to spread vegetatively make it a successful colonizer. Fragmented stolons readily establish new populations in suitable habitat.

## Trapping Mechanism

The bladderwort trap operates as one of the fastest known movements in the plant kingdom. Each bladder maintains negative internal pressure through active pumping of water out of the trap interior.

When trigger hairs on the trap door are touched by prey, the door snaps open in approximately 0.5 milliseconds. The pressure differential sucks water and prey into the bladder in a fraction of a second.

The trap door then reseals, and the digestive process begins. Enzymes secreted by glandular cells within the bladder [[ligninolytic-enzyme-production-bioremediation]] was assembled from pre-existing genes.

## Cultivation

U. gibba is among the easiest carnivorous plants to grow and often appears uninvited in aquaria and water gardens. Many growers consider it a nuisance due to its prolific growth.

The species requires no special substrate. It grows freely floating or anchored to aquatic plants, rocks, or driftwood in still or slowly moving water.

It thrives in full sun to partial shade and tolerates a wide [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] and [[fukuoka-textdoc-microclimate-mite-scale-population-dynamics]] in freshwater ecosystems.

The species can reach high population densities in nutrient-enriched waters. Dense mats of U. gibba stolons provide habitat and shelter for small aquatic organisms including insect larvae.

In some regions, U. gibba is used as a bioindicator of water quality. Its presence and abundance correlate with specific nutrient and pH conditions in freshwater habitats.

Research has demonstrated that U. gibba traps undergo developmental plasticity in response to prey availability. Plants in prey-rich environments produce more numerous and larger bladders than those in prey-poor conditions.
