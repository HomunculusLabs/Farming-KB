# Symphytum officinale (Common Comfrey)

## Overview

**Symphytum officinale**, commonly known as common comfrey, knitbone, or boneset, is a vigorous perennial herbaceous plant in the family Boraginaceae. Native to Europe and temperate regions of Asia, comfrey has earned a legendary status in both permaculture and traditional herbalism. In permaculture systems, comfrey is valued as a **dynamic nutrient accumulator**, biomass generator, compost activator, mulch crop, and bee forage plant. It is considered one of the most useful and multifunctional plants in the sustainable agriculture toolkit.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Eudicots
- **Clade:** Boraginales
- **Family:** Boraginaceae
- **Genus:** *Symphytum*
- **Species:** *S. officinale*

The genus *Symphytum* contains approximately 35 species. The most commonly cultivated are:

- **S. officinale:** True comfrey — may produce viable seeds (can become invasive)
- **S. × uplandicum (Russian comfrey):** A naturally occurring hybrid (*S. officinale* × *S. asperum*); sterile, clonal propagation only; higher biomass production; preferred for permaculture
- **'Bocking 14':** The most widely grown cultivar, developed by Lawrence D. Hills at the Henry Doubleday Research Association. Sterile hybrid, high protein and potash content, disease-resistant
- **'Bocking 4':** Deep-rooting variety, better for mineral extraction from subsoil

## Botanical Description

Comfrey is a robust, clump-forming perennial producing large rosettes of leaves and tall flowering spikes.

- **Roots:** Extensive, deep taproot system reaching 2–3 meters (6–10 feet) into the subsoil. This exceptional root depth is the basis for comfrey's role as a dynamic accumulator — it mines minerals from deep soil layers that are inaccessible to most crop roots. The roots are thick, black, and branching, with a mucilaginous interior.

- **Leaves:** Large, broadly lanceolate to ovate, 15–60 cm long and 5–20 cm wide. Hairy (bristly) on both surfaces. Lower leaves are petiolate with winged stems; upper leaves clasp the stem. Leaves emerge from a basal crown in early spring and continue growing throughout the growing season.

- **Stems:** Erect, hollow, angled, 60–150 cm tall, bearing reduced leaves and terminal clusters of flowers. Stems are bristly and may branch.

- **Flowers:** Bell-shaped (campanulate), 12–18 mm long, in nodding cymes. Colors range from creamy-white to purple, violet, pink, or blue, depending on variety. Flowers produce abundant nectar and are highly attractive to bees. Blooming period: May–July (temperate regions), with occasional repeat flowering in autumn.

- **Seeds:** Four nutlets per flower, produced only by *S. officinale* (Russian comfrey hybrids are sterile). Seeds can persist in soil for years, contributing to volunteer populations.

## Role in Permaculture

Comfrey is often described as the "Swiss Army knife" of permaculture plants due to its extraordinary range of functions within a food forest or sustainable growing system.

### Dynamic Nutrient Accumulation

Comfrey's deep taproot mines subsoil minerals — particularly **potassium (K), phosphorus (P), calcium (Ca), and trace minerals** — and concentrates them in its leaves. When those leaves are cut and applied as mulch, compost, or liquid feed, these minerals become available to shallow-rooted crops.

Typical leaf nutrient content (dry weight basis):

| Nutrient | Content | Significance |
|----------|---------|--------------|
| **Nitrogen (N)** | 3.0–7.0% | Comparable to animal manure |
| **Potassium (K₂O)** | 3.0–8.0% | Exceptionally high — excellent for fruiting crops |
| **Phosphorus (P₂O₅)** | 0.3–1.0% | Moderate — supports root development |
| **Calcium (Ca)** | 2.0–4.0% | Important for soil structure |
| **Iron (Fe)** | 200–500 ppm | Micronutrient |
| **Magnesium (Mg)** | 0.3–0.8% | Chlorophyll component |

The high potassium content makes comfrey particularly valuable as a feed for potassium-hungry crops like tomatoes, potatoes, peppers, and fruit trees.

### Biomass Production

Comfrey is among the most productive perennial biomass crops in temperate climates:

- **'Bocking 14'** can produce 4–7 kg (9–15 lbs) of fresh leaf material per plant per year
- **3–5 cuts per growing season** are possible in favorable conditions
- Yields of **10–20 tonnes/hectare** of fresh matter annually have been recorded
- Biomass production continues for 20+ years from a single planting

### Comfrey Liquid Feed

One of the most popular uses is "comfrey tea" — a nutrient-rich liquid fertilizer:

1. Harvest comfrey leaves and pack into a container (bucket or barrel)
2. Weight down with a stone or board
3. Add water to cover (optional — some practitioners prefer to let leaves decompose without water for a more concentrated product)
4. Cover and let ferment for 3–6 weeks
5. Dilute 1:10 (with water) for foliar feed or soil drench

The resulting liquid is dark brown, with a strong smell (similar to manure), and contains readily available potassium, nitrogen, and trace minerals. It is particularly effective as a feed for:

- Fruiting vegetables (tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers)
- Root vegetables (potatoes, carrots, beets)
- Fruit trees and berry bushes
- Hungry annuals (squash, corn)

### Mulch and Compost Accelerator

Fresh or wilted comfrey leaves make excellent mulch:

- Spread 5–10 cm thick around plants; decomposes within 2–4 weeks
- Higher nutrient density than straw or leaf mold
- Accelerates compost decomposition when layered into compost piles (the high nitrogen content acts as a compost activator)
- Particularly effective as a mulch under fruit trees and in perennial beds

### Companion Planting

Comfrey serves several companion planting functions:

- **Fruit tree understory:** Planted in the drip line of fruit trees, comfrey mines nutrients from the root zone, reduces grass competition, and provides mulch material in-situ
- **Pest deterrent:** The presence of comfrey may help repel certain pests; the flowers attract predatory insects
- **Bee forage:** Extended flowering period provides nectar for bees and other pollinators during a critical gap period (late spring to midsummer)
- **Barrier plant:** Dense clumps suppress weeds effectively around garden edges and between beds

### Erosion Control

The extensive root system helps stabilize soil on slopes, banks, and waterway edges. Comfrey is sometimes planted in swales and on contour to prevent erosion while providing biomass.

### Livestock Fodder

Comfrey has a long history as animal fodder, particularly for poultry and pigs:

- High protein content (20–30% of dry matter)
- Palatable to chickens, pigs, and rabbits when wilted
- Can be fed fresh or ensiled
- **Note:** The presence of pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) has raised concerns about livestock feeding — see Safety section below

### Bioremediation

Comfrey has been investigated for its ability to accumulate heavy metals from contaminated soils (phytoremediation). It shows particular affinity for zinc, lead, and cadmium. This application requires careful management, as contaminated plant material must be disposed of safely and not composted.

## Cultivation

### Site Requirements

- **Sun:** Full sun to partial shade
- **Soil:** Tolerant of most soil types; prefers moist, fertile, deep loams. Tolerates clay and poor soils but performs best with adequate fertility and moisture.
- **pH:** 6.0–7.5 (adaptable)
- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 3–9; fully winter-hardy to -30°C
- **Water:** Moderate; established plants are drought-tolerant due to deep roots but produce maximum biomass with consistent moisture

### Propagation

- **Root cuttings (preferred):** 5–10 cm sections of root, planted 5 cm deep, horizontally, in spring or autumn. Each cutting produces 3–5 new plants. This is the standard propagation method for sterile cultivars like 'Bocking 14'.
- **Crown division:** Divide established clumps in early spring or autumn. Each division should have at least one growing bud.
- **Offsets:** Small plantlets that emerge from the root system can be carefully separated and transplanted.

**Important:** Once established, comfrey is extremely difficult to eradicate. Its deep root system regenerates from even small root fragments. Choose planting locations carefully and consider using root barriers in formal garden settings.

### Spacing

- **In dedicated biomass beds:** 60–90 cm apart (grid or row planting)
- **Under fruit trees:** 3–6 plants per mature tree, spaced in the drip zone
- **Barrier rows:** 30–45 cm apart for dense hedge/border plantings

### Maintenance

- **Cutting:** First cut when plants reach 40–60 cm tall (usually late spring). Subsequent cuts every 6–8 weeks until early autumn. Leave at least 10 cm of growth above the crown for rapid regrowth.
- **Mulching:** Benefits from annual mulch with compost or manure for maximum biomass production.
- **Division:** Re-divide clumps every 5–7 years to maintain vigor.
- **Pests/diseases:** Remarkably pest-free. Occasional issues with comfrey rust (*Melampsorella symphyti*) in humid conditions; remove affected leaves. Resistant to slugs and most common garden pests.

## Safety Concerns: Pyrrolizidine Alkaloids

### The PA Problem

All *Symphytum* species contain **pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)**, primarily:

- **Symphytine**
- **Echimidine**
- **Lycopsamine**
- **Intermedine**
- **7-Acetylintermedine**

PAs are **hepatotoxic** (liver-damaging) and **hepatocarcinogenic** (can cause liver cancer) with chronic exposure. They cause veno-occlusive disease of the liver — obliteration of small hepatic veins leading to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and potentially liver failure.

### Implications for Use

- **Internal use of comfrey is not recommended** without professional supervision. The German Commission E and multiple regulatory agencies have withdrawn approval for internal medicinal use of comfrey products containing PAs.
- **Topical use** is considered safer (limited systemic absorption through intact skin) but should not be applied to broken skin or open wounds over large areas.
- **PA-free comfrey preparations** (cultivated under controlled conditions to minimize PA content, or chemically extracted) exist but are not universally available.
- **Do not use comfrey internally** during pregnancy, breastfeeding, or in children.
- **For permaculture use** (external, as mulch/fertilizer), the PA content in leaves is not a concern for plants, as PAs are broken down during composting and are not taken up by food crops in significant quantities.

### PA Content Variability

PA content varies significantly between species and cultivars:

- *S. officinale* (true comfrey): Higher PA content
- *S. × uplandicum* (Russian comfrey): Lower PA content
- 'Bocking 14': Lowest PA content among commonly available cultivars

**For permaculture purposes, 'Bocking 14' is the recommended cultivar** — it combines maximum biomass production with the lowest PA content, is sterile (no seed spread), and is widely available.
