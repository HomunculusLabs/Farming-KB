## Overview

Phellodon niger, commonly known as the black tooth fungus, is a species of
tooth fungus in the family Bankeraceae (order Thelephorales). It is a
mycorrhizal associate primarily of coniferous trees, forming ectomycorrhizal
partnerships in temperate and boreal forest ecosystems.

The species is recognized by its dark, funnel-shaped fruiting body with a black
undersurface bearing spiny teeth, and its distinctive spicy or peppery odor.

P. niger is found across Europe and parts of North America, though it is
uncommon and considered threatened in several European countries due to habitat
loss and air pollution impacts on mycorrhizal networks.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Thelephorales
- **Family:** Bankeraceae
- **Genus:** Phellodon
- **Species:** P. niger
- **Authority:** (Fr.) P. Karst. (1881)

The basionym is Hydnum niger Fries (1821). The genus name Phellodon derives
from Greek "phellos" (cork) and "odon" (tooth), referring to the corky texture
of the teeth-bearing surface.

P. niger is closely related to P. melaleucus, with which it has sometimes been
confused, though P. niger is darker and typically associated with conifers.

## Morphology

The fruiting body is a tough, leathery, funnel-shaped to centrally depressed
basidiocarp measuring 3–8 cm in diameter.

The upper surface (pileus) is dark brown to black, smooth to finely felted,
often with concentrically zoned margins. The margin is initially inrolled and
may become wavy with age.

The undersurface bears crowded, decurrent teeth (spines) that are dark grey to
black, measuring 2–5 mm long. These teeth are the spore-producing hymenophore.

The flesh is tough, corky, and dark brown throughout, with no distinct zone
between pileus and hymenophore. Context is thin (1–2 mm).

The odor is strongly spicy, peppery, or like fenugreek, which is a key field
character for identification. The taste is acrid and peppery.

Spores are spherical to broadly ellipsoidal, ornamented with tuberculate
spines, measuring 4–5.5 μm in diameter. Spore print is white to pale buff.

## Distribution and Habitat

P. niger is widely distributed across temperate Europe, from Scandinavia to
the Mediterranean, and extends into parts of western Russia. Records from North
America exist but may represent a distinct species complex.

The fungus is mycorrhizal, forming obligate associations with coniferous trees
especially pine (Pinus sylvestris, P. sylvestris), spruce (Picea abies), and
fir (Abies spp.). It is occasionally found under deciduous trees, particularly
oak.

It fruits from late summer to autumn, typically in mature, relatively
undisturbed forest stands on acidic soils. The species prefers old-growth or
late-successional forests with well-developed mycorrhizal networks.

P. niger is considered rare to uncommon throughout much of its range and is
listed as threatened or endangered in several European countries, including
Germany, the Netherlands, and parts of Scandinavia.

## Ecological Role

As an ectomycorrhizal fungus, P. niger forms symbiotic associations with tree
roots, exchanging mineral nutrients and water for photosynthetic carbon from
its host. It plays a role in forest nutrient cycling and tree health.

Species in the genus Phellodon are known to form long-lived, persistent
ectomycorrhizal connections that contribute to belowground carbon storage and
inter-tree resource sharing through common mycorrhizal networks.

P. niger is sensitive to atmospheric nitrogen deposition and habitat
disturbance, making it a potential bioindicator of forest ecosystem health and
continuity.

## Bioactive Compounds

Phellodon species are known to produce bioactive secondary metabolites including
thelephoric acid, a pigment compound with reported antioxidant and
anti-inflammatory properties.

Thelephoric acid has been studied for its potential cytotoxic effects against
cancer cell lines and its ability to modulate immune cell activity.

P. niger and related species also produce atranorin and other depside
derivatives that contribute to their chemical defense against herbivory and
microbial competition.

## Similar Species

**Phellodon melaleucus** is similar but paler, with a grey-brown to tan upper
surface and typically found under both conifers and deciduous trees.

**Phellodon tomentosus** has a distinctly tomentose (woolly) upper surface and
a less acrid taste. **Bankera fuligineoalba** is a close relative with a
smoother hymenophore and lighter coloring.

**Sarcodon species** are generally larger with longer teeth and different
mycorrhizal preferences.
