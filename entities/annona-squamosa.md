## Overview

**Annona squamosa**, commonly known as sugar apple, sweetsop, or custard apple, is a small deciduous tree in the Annonaceae family native to tropical [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]], where it was introduced by Spanish and Portuguese traders in the 16th and 17th centuries. India, Thailand, and Indonesia are among the largest producers.

All parts of the plant — leaves, bark, seeds, and unripe fruit — contain bioactive acetogenins and alkaloids with documented insecticidal, antiparasitic, and cytotoxic properties.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Magnoliophyta → Magnoliopsida → Magnoliales → Annonaceae → *Annona squamosa* L. The species was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum.

The Annonaceae family contains approximately 2,400 species in 130 genera, many of which produce edible fruits. *A. squamosa* is one of five commercially significant *Annona* species alongside *A. muricata*, *A. cherimola*, *A. reticulata*, and the hybrid *A. × atemoya*.

Sugar apple trees are small, reaching 3–8 m in height with a spreading, open canopy and relatively short trunk. Branches are slender and spreading, with grayish-brown bark that is smooth when young.

Leaves are alternate, oblong to elliptic, 10–20 cm long and 3–6 cm wide, with a smooth upper surface and finely pubescent underside. When crushed, the leaves emit a characteristic aromatic scent.

Flowers are pendulous, solitary or in small clusters on old wood, with three green outer petals and three pale-yellow inner petals. Flowers are protogynous, with the female phase preceding the male phase by 1–2 days.

The fruit is a syncarpium (aggregate fruit), 6–12 cm in diameter, composed of numerous fused carpels forming a rounded or conical shape. The surface is composed of distinct, slightly projecting areoles (tubercles), giving a characteristic knobby appearance.

Fruit weight ranges from 100 to 500 g depending on cultivar and growing conditions. Under optimal management, a mature tree can produce 50–100 fruits per season. Commercial orchards in India report yields of 8–12 tonnes per hectare.

Ripe pulp is white, creamy, and fragrant, containing 20–60 shiny dark brown seeds embedded in the sweet flesh. Sugar content ranges from 14–20° Brix depending on cultivar and growing conditions.

## Cultivation

Sugar apple grows best in tropical and subtropical lowlands below 800 m elevation, where annual rainfall exceeds 1,000 mm. The tree is moderately drought-tolerant once established but produces best with regular irrigation during flowering and fruit development.

Optimal soils are well-drained sandy loams with pH 6.0–7.5. The species is intolerant of waterlogging and saline conditions. Trees are typically spaced 4–6 m apart in orchards.

Propagation is primarily by seed, which produces fruit-bearing trees in 3–4 years. Seedling trees show considerable genetic variation. Vegetative propagation by approach grafting, patch budding, or softwood cuttings is used to preserve selected cultivars.

Fruit matures 20–25 weeks after flowering. Harvest is timed by skin color change from dark green to lighter green-yellow and by the appearance of slight separation between areoles.

The most serious pest is the annona seed borer (*Bephratelloides cubensis*), whose larvae tunnel into the fruit, rendering it unmarketable. Fruit flies, mealybugs, and scale insects are additional constraints. Anthracnose caused by *Colletotrichum* spp. affects leaves and fruit.

## Nutritional Value

Sugar apple pulp provides approximately 95 kcal per 100 g, with carbohydrates (primarily sugars) comprising about 23 g. The fruit is a good source of vitamin C (36 mg per 100 g), vitamin B6, thiamin, and potassium.

The fruit contains moderate levels of dietary fiber (4.4 g per 100 g), magnesium, and phosphorus. Its protein content is low (2 g per 100 g), typical of sweet tropical fruits.

Seeds contain approximately 25% fixed oil composed of linoleic, oleic, and palmitic acids, plus a complex mixture of annonaceous acetogenins and alkaloids.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

Annonaceous acetogenins, particularly squamocin (annonacin), squamotatin, and squamosten, are the principal bioactive constituents. These long-chain fatty acid derivatives exhibit potent cytotoxic activity against multiple human [[fukuoka-indian-agriculture-observations]].

Aqueous leaf extracts show antidiabetic activity in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats, reducing blood glucose and improving lipid profiles. Bark extracts exhibit antimicrobial activity against *[[staphylococcus-aureus]]* and *Escherichia coli* in disc-diffusion assays.

## Ecology and Applications

Sugar apple is adapted to seasonally [[mollison-wet-dry-tropical-design-strategies]] forests in its native range. The sweet pulp attracts frugivorous birds, bats, and primates, which disperse the large seeds. The tree is relatively fast-growing and can begin fruiting within 3 years from seed.

In [[agroforestry]] systems, sugar apple serves as a component of homegardens and mixed fruit orchards in tropical Asia and Latin America. Its relatively small stature allows interplanting with taller tree crops such as mango or jackfruit.

The bark, leaves, and seeds have extensive applications in traditional medicine across the species' cultivated range. In Ayurveda, the unripe fruit is used for dysentery and diarrhea, while leaf decoctions treat fever and respiratory ailments.

Seed oil and acetogenin extracts are investigated as natural biopesticides and antifungal agents for crop protection. Dried seed powder is used as a lice treatment and fish poison in traditional fishing practices.

### Related Annona Species

The atemoya (*Annona × atemoya*) is a natural hybrid between sugar apple (*A. squamosa*) and cherimoya (*A. cherimola*), combining the heat tolerance of sugar apple with the superior fruit quality of cherimoya. It is commercially important in Australia, Israel, and Florida.
