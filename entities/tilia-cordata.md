# Tilia cordata

## Overview
- Common name: Littleleaf Linden, Small-leaved Lime
- Also known as: Lime tree (European usage), Tilleul (French)
- Growth habit: Medium-sized deciduous tree, broadly pyramidal crown when young, oval-rounded at maturity
- Lifespan: Long-lived; specimens exceeding 500 years documented in Europe
- Primary importance: Ornamental landscaping, medicinal flower harvest, beekeeping forage
- Medicinal part: Dried flowers and bracts (Tiliae flos), harvested in early summer at peak bloom
- Cultivars: 'Greenspire', 'Chancellor', 'Euclid', 'Norlin' widely planted
- Historical significance: Sacred tree in Germanic and Slavic folklore; associated with justice and community gatherings
- Monofloral honey: Produces prized pale, aromatic linden honey valued across Europe and North America

## Taxonomy
- Binomial: Tilia cordata Mill.
- Authority: Philip Miller (1768)
- Family: Malvaceae (subfamily Tilioideae); historically placed in Tiliaceae
- Order: Malvales
- Genus: Tilia L. (approx. 30 species worldwide)
- Synonyms:
  - Tilia parvifolia Ehrh. ex Hoffm.
  - Tilia microphylla Vent.
  - Tilia sylvestris Desf.
  - Tilia ulmifolia Scop.
  - Tilia cordata var. vulgaris Hayne
- Hybrid: Tilia × europaea (T. cordata × T. platyphyllos) — Common Lime, widely planted in Europe
- Note: Most cold-hardy Tilia species, contributing to its northern landscape popularity

## Morphology
### Tree
- Height: Typically 20–30 m; can reach 40 m in ideal conditions
- Crown spread: 12–18 m at maturity
- Trunk: Straight, cylindrical; bark smooth gray when young, becoming ridged and fissured with age
- Root system: Deep with extensive laterals; not considered invasive to foundations
- Growth rate: Moderate; 30–60 cm/year under good conditions
### Leaves
- Size: 3–8 cm long, 3–6 cm wide (smaller than T. platyphyllos)
- Shape: Cordate (heart-shaped), finely serrated margins, abruptly acuminate tip
- Surface: Dark green and glossy above, paler below with brown hair tufts in vein axils
- Petiole: 1.5–3 cm, glabrous or nearly so (distinguishes from pubescent-petioled T. platyphyllos)
- Autumn color: Pale yellow; not considered showy
### Flowers
- Inflorescence: Cymose clusters of 3–7 flowers on a peduncle subtended by a conspicuous oblong bract
- Bract: 5–9 cm, pale green, fused to peduncle for half its length; aids wind dispersal of fruit
- Corolla: Pale yellow to creamy white, 5 petals, 6–10 mm; strongly fragrant
- Bloom period: June–July (later than T. platyphyllos)
- Pollination: Entomophilous; exceptionally attractive to bees — a premier nectar source
### Fruit
- Type: Globose to ovoid drupaceous nutlet, 5–7 mm, ribbed, gray-brown
- Maturation: Late summer to early autumn
- Dispersal: Bract acts as wing; fruit falls spinning aerodynamically

## Distribution & Habitat
- Native range: Europe from British Isles eastward to western Siberia and the Caucasus
- Northern limit: Southern Scandinavia (~63°N in Finland); most cold-tolerant Tilia
- Naturalized: Widely naturalized in eastern North America, New Zealand, temperate South America
- Habitat: Mixed deciduous forests, forest edges, stream banks, limestone outcrops
- Soil preference: Calcareous, well-drained loams; adapts to both acidic and alkaline substrates
- Elevation: Lowland to submontane, up to ~1,500 m in southern range
- Urban adaptability: Extremely tolerant of pollution, compacted soils, drought once established
- Shade tolerance: Moderate — saplings tolerate shade but accelerate with increased light
- Invasive potential: Low; NA naturalized populations not ecologically aggressive

## Ethnobotanical Use
### Medicinal — Linden Flower Tea
- Central European tradition: Tisane de Tilleul (FR), Lindenblütentee (DE), Lipový čaj (CZ/SK)
- Indications: Mild anxiety, nervous tension, insomnia, colds with fever, digestive upset, hypertension
- Preparation: Infuse 1.5–3 g dried flowers in 250 mL boiling water, covered, 10–15 minutes
- Dosage: 2–4 cups/day; traditionally taken in the evening for sleep support
- Regulatory status: ESCOP monograph; approved in Germany (Commission E) for cold-associated cough
- Safety: Generally recognized as safe; rare allergic reactions reported
### Wood — Carpentry & Craft
- Timber: Fine-grained, pale, lightweight but strong; works easily and takes detail well
- Historical uses: Carving (Gothic altarpieces, woodcuts), model-making, piano keys, furniture, cooperage
- Bark: Bast fiber historically used for rope and mat weaving; practice persists in some regions
### Beekeeping & Honey
- Nectar value: Extremely high — one of the most productive honey trees in temperate Europe
- Honey characteristics: Pale amber to white, crystallizes quickly, delicate spicy-aromatic flavor
- Market: "Linden honey" (Miel de Tilleul) commands premium prices in European markets
## Active Compounds
### Flavonoids (primary bioactive class)
- Quercetin, kaempferol, and their glycosides (quercitrin, isoquercitrin, astragalin)
- Rutin (quercetin-3-O-rutinoside) — antioxidant and vascular support
- Tiliroside (kaempferol-3-O-p-coumaroylglucoside) — characteristic of Tilia species
### Mucilage Polysaccharides
- Arabinogalactans, rhamnogalacturonans — contribute to soothing/demulcent properties
- Concentration higher in bracts than in flowers proper
### Volatile Oils
- Farnesol (sesquiterpene alcohol) — major aromatic component, contributes sedative activity
- Other terpenoids: eugenol, geraniol, linalool derivatives
- Responsible for distinctive honey-sweet fragrance of fresh and dried flowers
### Phenolic Acids & Tannins
- Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, p-coumaric acid derivatives
- Condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins) — astringent and anti-inflammatory
### Other Constituents
- Amino acids (including GABA precursors), trace vitamin C, minerals (K, Ca, Mg)
## Pharmacology
- Anxiolytic / mild sedative: Flavonoids and farnesol act on GABA_A receptors; validated in animal models
- Diaphoretic: Promotes mild sweating; traditionally used at onset of febrile illness
- Anti-inflammatory: Flavonoids inhibit COX-2 and NF-κB pathways
- Antioxidant: Scavenges free radicals; rutin strengthens capillary walls
- Spasmolytic: Reduces smooth muscle contraction in GI and respiratory tracts
- Cardiovascular: Mild hypotensive effect reported in traditional and preliminary clinical contexts
- Traditional cold remedy: Combined diaphoretic + anti-inflammatory + mucilaginous action supports early-stage respiratory infections
- Clinical evidence: Moderate; strongest data for anxiolytic/sedative effects; human trials generally small
- ESCOP indication: Common cold (catarrh), cough; traditionally anxiety and sleep
## Permaculture Applications
- Bee forage: Premier mid-summer nectar source; supports honey production and native pollinators
- Shade tree: Dense canopy provides excellent summer shade for livestock, seating areas, buildings
- Coppice / pollard: Responds vigorously to cutting; traditional European management for firewood and fodder
- Windbreak: Suitable as mid-canopy component in shelterbelts and mixed windbreak plantings
- Urban tolerance: Exceptional — thrives along streets, in plazas, and under compacted conditions
- Companion planting: Understory of shade-tolerant herbs (Allium spp., Galium odoratum, Viola spp.)
- Edible landscape: Flowers edible (tea, fritters, syrups); young leaves mildly mucilaginous, edible in salads
- Soil improvement: Deep roots cycle nutrients; leaf litter enriches soil organic matter
- Wildlife value: Seeds eaten by birds and small mammals; canopy supports nesting; catkins provide early pollen
- Long-term yield: Decades of flower harvests, coppice rotations, and honey flows once established
- Guild role: N-fixing legumes benefit from dappled shade; linden draws pollinators benefiting all guild plants
- Food forest: Good for second-stage plantings; establishes well in open ground
## Cultivation Notes
- Hardiness: USDA Zones 3–7; tolerates -35°C or lower once established
- Heat tolerance: Performs well to Zone 7; struggles in prolonged extreme heat (Zone 8+)
- Light: Full sun to partial shade; best flowering in full sun
- Soil: Adaptable to clay, loam, sandy loam; tolerates pH 5.5–8.0
- Moisture: Moderate; drought-tolerant once established but prefers consistent moisture
- Propagation — seed: Requires 3–6 months cold stratification; germination slow and uneven
- Propagation — vegetative: Softwood cuttings under mist (difficult); layering; T-budding and grafting onto seedling rootstock
- Planting: Transplant as balled-and-burlapped or container-grown; fall planting preferred
- Pruning: Formative pruning when young; mature trees need little; avoid heavy spring pruning (bleeding)
- Pests/diseases: Generally pest-free; aphids produce honeydew (sooty mold); linden gall mite occasionally
- Fertilization: Light applications of balanced fertilizer in early spring benefit young trees
- Mulch: 5–8 cm organic mulch over root zone, kept away from trunk
