# Pinus sylvestris

## Overview

**Pinus sylvestris**, the Scots pine, is a coniferous tree in the family
Pinaceae native to Eurasia. It is the most widely distributed pine species
in the world, ranging from western Scotland to eastern Siberia.

The species holds immense ecological and economic importance across its
range. It is a keystone species in boreal and temperate [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity-patterns-distribution]] communities.

Scots pine timber has been used for millennia in construction, shipbuilding,
and paper production. It remains one of the most commercially significant
softwoods in European forestry.

Its distinctive orange-red upper bark and blue-green needles make it one of
the most recognizable conifers in the Northern Hemisphere.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Pinophyta → Pinopsida → Pinales → Pinaceae →
*Pinus*, subgenus *Pinus*, section *Pinus* → *Pinus sylvestris* L.

The specific epithet *sylvestris* means "of the forest" in Latin. Linnaeus
described the species in 1753.

Scots pine is an evergreen reaching 25–35 m in height, with trunk diameters
of 0.5–1.2 m. The crown is conical in youth, becoming broadly domed with age.

Bark is thick, scaly, and dark grey-brown at the base, transitioning to thin,
flaky, orange-red plates on the upper trunk and branches. This color pattern
is diagnostic for [[ectomycorrhizal-associations]] with over 200 fungal
species across its range. Common partners include *[[paxillus-involutus]]*, and *[[cenococcum-geophilum]]*.

The genus *Suillus* is especially strongly associated with Pinus, with
several species showing host specificity to Scots pine. These relationships
are essential for phosphorus and nitrogen uptake in nutrient-poor soils.
