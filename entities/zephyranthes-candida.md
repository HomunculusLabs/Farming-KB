## Taxonomy and Morphology

Zephyranthes candida was described by John Lindley in 1823 based on specimens collected in South America. The genus Zephyranthes comprises approximately 70 to 90 species distributed primarily in the Americas, with a few species in Africa and Asia. The genus name derives from the Greek words zephyros (west wind) and anthos (flower), referring to the delicate, wind-blown appearance of the flowers.

The plant forms a clump of narrow, linear leaves 15 to 30 centimeters long and 3 to 5 millimeters wide, arising from a small ovoid bulb 1.5 to 3 centimeters in diameter. The leaves are bright green, semi-erect, and appear simultaneously with or shortly before the flowers, unlike many other Amaryllidaceae species where foliage and flowers are temporally separated.

Flowering stems (scapes) are 15 to 25 centimeters tall, bearing a single terminal flower. The perianth is white, funnel-shaped, 4 to 6 centimeters long, with six tepals that are fused at the base into a short tube. The flowers are slightly fragrant and open widely in bright conditions, closing at night or during overcast weather. This nyctinastic movement is controlled by turgor changes in the tepal cells and serves to protect the reproductive organs from nighttime moisture and potential damage.

The stamens are equal to or slightly shorter than the perianth segments, with yellow anthers. The style is filiform and exceeds the stamens, terminating in a three-lobed stigma. Flowers are typically self-compatible and produce viable seed without cross-pollination.

Fruits are globose capsules approximately 1 centimeter in diameter, containing several black, flattened seeds. The capsules dehisce irregularly at maturity, releasing seeds that may be dispersed by water or animals.
