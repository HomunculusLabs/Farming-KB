# Avalanche

## Description

Avalanches are rapid flows of snow down a slope, representing one of the most dangerous natural hazards in cold mountainous terrain. In [[permaculture-design]], avalanche risk determines settlement placement, infrastructure routing, and forest management strategies in steep cold-climate landscapes. Mollison draws extensively on snow physics research (particularly G. M. Kuaeva, 1975) to characterize avalanche types, triggers, dynamics, and defensive strategies.

## Classification

- **Category**: Natural hazard / cryogenic mass movement
- **Types**: Episodic (every approximately 35 years), Systematic (yearly), Periodic (rare heavy snow events)
- **Speed range**: Snow slides at 4-5 m/sec; major avalanches accelerate to 6.5+ m/sec
- **Preceding air blast**: Up to 330 m/sec (speed of sound)

## Key Facts

- Avalanches occur on slopes exceeding 6 degrees where snow accumulates to 15 cm or more
- Three main frequency types: Episodic (approximately 35 year cycles), Systematic (annual), Periodic (rare)
- Avalanche triggers: strong winds (>13-18 km/sec), sudden freeze followed by thaw, warm winds, spring thaw, gunshots, cornice collapse, wind gusts, animals, skiers
- 80% of avalanches occur when old, compact, or dry snows are dislodged by condensation of moisture from air >70% humidity
- The compressed air blast preceding the avalanche mass can travel at 330 m/sec
- Snow-dust avalanches produce waves of 10-100 m/sec and develop pressures of 35 atmospheres
- Impact force ranges from 4-54 tonnes per square metre depending on total mass and carried load
- Basal melting from earth (soil) heat reduces snow-mass cohesion and can trigger slides
- Avalanche chutes are often delineated by pioneer species such as aspen ([[populus-tremuloides]])
- Meltwater lubrication typically triggers avalanches between midday and 3 p.m. in fine weather
- Sudden snowfalls of 50-69 cm followed by cloud-free periods create unstable conditions

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Avalanche dynamics affect cultivation and [[fungal-ecology]] in mountain environments:

1. **Forest protection**: High-slope forests are the primary defense against avalanches — these same forests host diverse fungal communities including commercially valuable ectomycorrhizal species
2. **Pioneer fungal communities**: Avalanche chutes colonized by aspen and other pioneer species develop distinct fungal succession sequences
3. **Substrate deposition**: Avalanches deposit organic material, woody debris, and soil down-slope, creating new substrates for saprotrophic fungal colonization
4. **Habitat disruption**: Avalanche paths create canopy gaps that alter microclimate, moisture, and light conditions for fungal fruiting
5. **Soil disturbance**: The mechanical disturbance of soil by avalanche can break [[mycorrhizal-networks]] and initiate fungal community succession
6. **Acid rain threat**: Death of steep-slope forests by acid rain removes the primary avalanche defense and eliminates the fungal habitat simultaneously

## Avalanche Prevention and Defense

Mollison outlines several defensive strategies:
- Preservation of high-slope forests (primary defense)
- Placement of essential services in tunnels
- V-shaped barriers to split avalanche fronts
- Prevention of settlement in high-risk areas
- Snow barriers on plateaus above slopes to hold snow in place
- Cross-slope terraces, walls, and barriers (limited effectiveness)
- Monitoring and mapping of avalanche-prone areas

## Natural Indicators of Avalanche Risk

- Trees with downslope lean
- Lack of lower limbs and understory on trees
- Bare trains of different age classes or pioneer species (especially aspen)
- Downslope strips of blown-down forest radiating from avalanche snouts
- These indicators persist for 20-30 years in coniferous forests
