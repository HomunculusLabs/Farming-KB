## Overview

GBL (gamma-butyrolactone) is a cyclic ester and industrial solvent that functions as a prodrug for GHB in the human body. It is rapidly converted to GHB via peripheral lactonase enzymes upon ingestion.

The compound is used legitimately as a solvent in paint stripping, [[amanita-muscaria-legal-alternative]] to GHB during the 1990s and early 2000s. It was sold online and in head shops under names like "Blue Nitro" and "Renewtrient" before most jurisdictions regulated it.

The substance has a distinctive chemical taste described as slightly salty or soapy. Unlike GHB, it has a faint characteristic odor detectable at higher concentrations.

Industrial grade GBL typically has purity exceeding 99%. Pharmaceutical contamination with toxic impurities is rare in industrial product but has been documented in clandestine preparations.

GBL is chemically stable at room temperature and does not degrade significantly over time. This stability distinguishes it from GHB solutions, which can degrade under certain conditions.

## Chemical Properties

GBL has the molecular formula C₄H₆O₂ and a molecular weight of 86.09 g/mol. Its IUPAC name is dihydrofuran-2(3H)-one.

The compound is a colorless to pale yellow liquid at room temperature. It is miscible with water and most organic solvents.

GBL has a boiling point of 204°C and a density of 1.13 g/mL. These physical properties make it useful as a high-boiling-point polar aprotic solvent.

In aqueous solution, GBL is in equilibrium with its hydrolysis product GHB. The equilibrium strongly favors the lactone form at acidic pH but shifts toward the hydroxy acid at alkaline pH.

Industrial production exceeds one million tons annually worldwide. Major manufacturers produce GBL from maleic anhydride or via dehydrogenation of 1,4-butanediol.

GBL serves as a precursor in the manufacture of N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP). These industrial applications provide cover for illicit diversion of the chemical.

The compound"s miscibility with both water and organic solvents makes it useful in [[blesching-cannabis-safety-profile-adverse-effects-and-overdose-risk]].

Dose equivalency is approximately 1 mL of pure GBL equating to roughly 1.6 grams of GHB. This conversion ratio is critical for understanding dose-response relationships.

GBL has no known direct receptor activity of its own. All pharmacological effects are mediated through its conversion to GHB and subsequent activation of GHB and GABA-B receptors.

The elimination kinetics mirror those of GHB once conversion is complete. Total duration of effects is 2–4 hours at recreational doses.

Food in the stomach can delay but not prevent GBL absorption. Users who take GBL on an empty stomach experience faster onset and potentially more intense effects.

Animal studies have demonstrated that chronic GBL exposure produces similar neuroadaptations to GHB. These include downregulation of GABA-B receptors and altered GHB receptor density.

## Effects

Effects are identical to those of GHB once metabolic conversion occurs. These include euphoria, increased sociability, muscle relaxation, and at higher doses, significant sedation.

GBL is considered more dangerous than GHB dose-for-dose due to its faster onset and higher bioavailability. Users may underestimate the potency because effects begin before full conversion is complete.

In the 1990s, GBL emerged as a "legal high" after GHB was scheduled in multiple countries. Marketers promoted it as a dietary supplement, cleaning solvent, or nail polish remover to circumvent drug laws.

The United States passed the Hillory J. Farias and Samantha Reid Date-Rape Drug Prohibition Act in 2000, which included GBL as a listed chemical. The UK followed with GBL classification in 2009 under the Misuse of Drugs Act.

Online sales of GBL have persisted despite regulation, often marketed as industrial cleaning products. Law enforcement agencies in multiple countries have conducted operations targeting these distribution networks.
