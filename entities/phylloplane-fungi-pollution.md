## Phylloplane Fungi and Atmospheric Pollution

## Description

Phylloplane (leaf surface) fungi are a diverse community of saprotrophic, endophytic, and weakly parasitic fungi that colonize living leaf surfaces. These communities are directly exposed to atmospheric pollutants, making them sensitive indicators of air quality. Research by Magan et al. (1995), Newsham et al. (1992a,b), and others has documented how sulfur dioxide (SO2) and ozone (O3) alter phylloplane [[mineralogical-controls-fungal-community-structure]], with implications for plant health [[fungal-biodiversity-and-ecosystem-function]].

## Classification

- **Category:** Ecological interaction / fungal habitat
- **Habitat:** Living leaf surfaces (phyllosphere)
- **Fungal groups:** [[saprotrophic-fungi]], endophytes, weak pathogens, yeasts
- **Key pollutants:** SO2, O3, acid mist (H2SO4:HNO3)

## Key Facts

### Phylloplane Fungal Community Composition

Common phylloplane fungi include:
- **Saprotrophs:** [[aureobasidium-pullulans]], [[cladosporium-cladosporioides]], Epicoccum nigrum, Phoma exigua, Mycena galopus
- **Yeasts:** Pink yeasts, white yeasts
- **Weak parasites:** Cladosporium spp.
- **Conifer specialists:** [[sclerophoma-pythiophila]], [[rhizosphaera-kalkhoffii]]

These fungi derive nutrition from leaf wax cuticles, surface exudates, and atmospheric deposition of nutrients and carbon.

### SO2 Effects on Phylloplane Communities

Magan et al. (1995) used open-air fumigation systems over 3 years and found:

**Tree-specific responses:**
- Sitka spruce: Low SO2 markedly reduced total phylloplane fungal populations
- Scots pine: O3 caused an increase in fungal colonization (Epicoccum nigrum increased)
- Norway spruce: Sclerophoma pythiophila increased with SO2

**Method-dependent results:**
- Dilution plating and direct plating methods gave different community profiles
- Pink yeasts decreased on Sitka spruce with SO2 (dilution plating)
- Aureobasidium pullulans increased on Norway spruce with O3 (direct plating)

### SO2 Effects on Leaf Litter Fungi

Newsham et al. (1992a,b) fumigated leaf litters of ash (Fraxinus excelsior), birch (Betula spp.), hazel (Corylus avellana), oak (Quercus robur, Q. petraea), and sycamore (Acer pseudoplatanus) at 10–30 nl l⁻¹ SO2:

**Decreased by fumigation:** Cladosporium spp., Epicoccum nigrum, Fusarium spp., Phoma exigua
**Increased by fumigation:** Coniothyrium quercinum, Cylindrocarpon ortosporum, Penicillium spp.

However, no change in leaf litter niche occupancy was observed, likely because resource utilization between these fungi is similar.

### Cereal Crop Responses

Magan and McLeod (1991) demonstrated on barley flag leaves:
- Significant reduction in pink and white yeasts under elevated SO2
- Cladosporium spp. (weakly parasitic) increased under SO2
- Suggests SO2 may increase susceptibility to pathogenic invasion in leaves with less surface wax

### Decomposition Impacts

Dursun et al. (1996a,b) showed:
- Sulfite at 12.5–100 mM negatively affected mycelial growth and spore germination of Mycena galopus, Phoma exigua, Cladosporium cladosporioides, and Aureobasidium pullulans
- Mycelia were more sensitive than spores to sulfite
- SO2 at 40 nl l⁻¹ reduced respiration of Mycena galopus on Sitka spruce litter by over 50%
- Effects were litter-species dependent: significant on Sitka spruce, less on Scots pine and ash

### Acid Mist Effects on Fungal Activity

Shaw (1996) exposed plants and leaf litter to ozone and acid mist (2:1 H2SO4:HNO3) in mesocosm experiments:
- Fluorescein diacetate-stained (metabolically active) fungal hyphae were significantly greater in acid-misted than control systems
- The nitrogen component of acid mist likely stimulated fungal activity on high C:N litter
- This stimulation may offset some negative effects of acidity on fungal growth

### Lichen Sensitivity

Lichens (fungal-algal symbioses) are among the most pollution-sensitive organisms:
- Species vary in tolerance to pollutant loading (Richardson, 1988)
- Distribution maps in the US show lichen absence correlating with industrial areas
- Recovery has been documented as air quality improves (Gilbert, 1992; Bates et al., 2001)
- Brown (1996) reviewed multiple pollutant impacts on lichen communities

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

1. **Bioindicators:** Phylloplane fungal community composition can serve as an early warning system for air quality degradation
2. **Leaf surface management** in cultivation should consider fungal community health
3. **Pathogen vulnerability:** Pollutant-stressed plants may be more susceptible to weak pathogens like Cladosporium
4. **Spore resilience:** Spores are more resistant to sulfite toxicity than mycelia, informing sterilization protocols
5. **Lichen monitoring** near cultivation sites can indicate local air quality status
6. **Compost ecology:** Understanding how SO2 affects saprotrophic fungi informs management of outdoor composting operations
