The species is of immense ecological, cultural, and economic significance in New Zealand. Kauri forests once dominated much of the northern North Island but were extensively logged during the 19th and 20th centuries.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Agathis australis was described by David Don in 1832. The genus Agathis comprises approximately 20 species distributed across [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]] characteristic of kauri forests.

Male and female cones are borne on the same tree. Male cones are cylindrical, 2 to 5 cm long, and clustered at branch tips. Female cones are globose, 5 to 8 cm in diameter, and disintegrate at maturity to release winged seeds.

Seeds are flattened and winged, approximately 1 cm long. They are wind-dispersed and germinate readily on exposed mineral soil or decaying [[biodiversity-of-fungi-decomposition-nutrient-cycling]] that shapes the entire plant community.

The thick bark and shedding habit create a fire-resistant outer layer that protects mature trees from low-intensity wildfires. This fire tolerance gives kauri a competitive advantage over many angiosperm tree species.

Kauri litter has a notably low [[fungal-decomposition]]. Old-growth kauri stores enormous quantities of carbon in both above-ground biomass and below-ground root systems. The loss of mature kauri forests to historical logging has had lasting effects on the regional carbon balance.

## Timber and Uses

Kauri timber is among the most valued softwoods in the world, prized for its straight grain, light weight, and exceptional durability. The heartwood is resistant to rot and insect damage, making it ideal for ship-building, construction, and furniture.

The 19th and early 20th centuries saw intensive kauri logging that removed an estimated 90 to 95 percent of the original forest area. Massive logs were transported from forests via kauri dams and driving streams, a logging method unique to New Zealand.

Kauri gum, a fossilized resin exuded by injured trees, was extensively harvested from both living trees and subterranean deposits. At its peak in the 1890s, kauri gum was New Zealand's most valuable export after gold.

The gum was used commercially for varnishes, linoleum, and high-quality finishes. Indigenous Maori used fresh kauri gum for chewing, as a fire starter, and for waterproofing containers.

Maori culture holds kauri in high regard as a taonga (treasure). The largest trees were given individual names and considered rangatira (chiefs) of the forest. Kauri wood was used for carving waka (canoes), wharenui (meeting houses), and weapons.

Contemporary use of kauri timber is restricted to salvaged and recycled wood, as logging of standing trees is prohibited on public land. Swamp kauri, ancient logs preserved in peat swamps for up to 50,000 years, is commercially harvested for specialty wood products.
