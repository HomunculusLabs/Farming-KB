# Allan Savory

Allan Savory is a Zimbabwean ecologist, farmer, and environmentalist best known as the originator of holistic management and holistic planned grazing. His work has been instrumental in demonstrating that properly managed livestock can be used to restore degraded grasslands and desertifying ecosystems, challenging the prevailing view that cattle are inherently destructive to the environment.

## Biography

Born in Rhodesia (now Zimbabwe) in 1935, Savory began his career as a research biologist and game ranger in the colonial-era Department of National Parks and Wildlife Management. His early observations of African rangelands led him to question conventional range management theories. After serving as a member of parliament in Rhodesia, he was exiled and eventually settled in the United States, where he co-founded the Center for Holistic Management (now the Savory Institute) in 1984. He has dedicated his career to developing and promoting practices that reverse desertification and restore grassland ecosystems worldwide.

## Holistic Management Framework

Holistic management is a comprehensive decision-making framework that addresses the complexity of ecological, social, and economic systems simultaneously. Unlike conventional management approaches that treat these domains separately, holistic management requires managers to consider all three simultaneously in every decision.

### The Holistic Goal

The foundation of the framework is the holistic goal, which defines:

- **Quality of life**: What the people involved in the management effort ultimately want from their lives.
- **Forms of production**: What must be produced to sustain the desired quality of life.
- **Future resource base**: The landscape and community that must be sustained to support production and quality of life over the long term.

All management decisions are tested against the holistic goal using a series of seven context-checking questions designed to identify decisions that are likely to be socially, environmentally, and economically unsound.

### The Four Ecosystem Processes

Savory identified four fundamental ecosystem processes that must function properly for a healthy environment:

1. **Water cycle**: Effective water capture, retention, and distribution through the soil. Healthy grasslands with adequate ground cover maximize rainfall infiltration and minimize runoff and evaporation.

2. **Mineral cycle**: The movement and cycling of nutrients through living organisms and the soil. Healthy ecosystems cycle minerals rapidly through decomposition and biological activity.

3. **Energy flow**: The capture and conversion of solar energy through photosynthesis and its movement through the food web. More diverse plant communities capture more solar energy.

4. **Community dynamics**: The constant change and succession of plant and animal communities. Biodiversity is both an indicator and a driver of ecosystem health.

### Seven Testing Questions

Every management decision is evaluated through seven questions:

1. Cause and effect: Does this action address the root cause of the problem?
2. Weak link (social): Will this action create or strengthen a weak link in the social fabric?
3. Weak link (biological): Will this action create or strengthen a weak link in the biological community?
4. Marginal reaction: Will the return from this action be worth the effort?
5. Gross profit: Will this action lead to the desired financial result?
6. Energy/money source and use: Does this action use the most appropriate form of energy or money?
7. Sustainability: Will this action lead toward or away from the holistic goal?

## Holistic Planned Grazing

Savory's most influential and controversial contribution is his approach to livestock management. Based on his observation that large herds of grazing animals co-evolved with grasslands and are essential for their health, he developed a grazing planning methodology that mimics the behavior of wild herds.

### Key Principles

- **High animal density**: Livestock are concentrated in large herds, mimicking the natural behavior of wild grazing animals. This ensures uniform grazing and trampling rather than selective grazing of preferred species.
- **Short grazing periods**: Animals move frequently, often daily, preventing overgrazing of individual plants. Plants are grazed once and then given adequate recovery time.
- **Long recovery periods**: After grazing, plants are allowed full recovery before being grazed again. Recovery time varies with season, rainfall, and plant growth rates.
- **Adaptive planning**: Grazing plans are adjusted continuously based on monitoring of plant recovery, animal performance, and changing conditions.
- **Full recovery**: The key insight is that overgrazing is caused by animals returning to a plant before it has recovered from the previous grazing, not by the number of animals per se.

### The Role of Grazing Animals

Savory argues that grasslands evolved under the influence of large herding herbivores and their predators. The herd behavior — bunching tightly, moving frequently, and creating disturbance through grazing and trampling — maintained grassland health by:

- Breaking up soil crusts and incorporating organic matter
- Cycling nutrients rapidly through dung and urine
- Preventing any single plant species from dominating
- Maintaining a soil surface that maximizes water infiltration
- Stimulating root growth and biological activity

Removing grazing animals, or managing them in ways that do not mimic natural herd behavior, leads to grassland deterioration and eventual desertification.

## Desertification

Savory's work addresses the global crisis of desertification — the process by which productive land degrades into desert. He argues that conventional understanding of desertification is incomplete because it fails to account for the role of livestock management. In his view:

- Overgrazing in the conventional sense (too many animals) is less damaging than allowing animals to remain in one area for extended periods (over-rest combined with overgrazing).
- Resting land, the standard prescription for degraded rangelands, can actually accelerate desertification in brittle environments by allowing dead plant material to accumulate and oxidize, shading new growth and creating a thatch layer that inhibits water penetration.
- Properly managed livestock can reverse desertification by restoring the four ecosystem processes.

## Criticisms and Scientific Debate

Savory's claims have generated significant controversy within the scientific community:

- Some range scientists argue that his results are not replicable at scale and that controlled studies have not consistently supported his claims about carbon sequestration and vegetation improvement.
- Critics note that the specific carbon sequestration rates Savory has claimed are not supported by peer-reviewed research.
- The complexity of the holistic management framework makes it difficult to isolate variables in scientific studies.
- Some ecologists argue that Savory overstates the role of grazing and understates other factors in grassland health, such as fire and climate.
- Concerns have been raised about the potential for overstocking when practitioners focus on high animal density without adequately monitoring plant recovery.

Despite these criticisms, many ranchers worldwide report improved soil health, increased vegetation cover, and improved profitability after adopting holistic planned grazing practices. Recent research has provided some support for Savory's core claims about the benefits of adaptive multi-paddock grazing systems compared to continuous grazing.

## Applicability to Different Climates

Savory's framework has been applied across a wide range of ecosystems:

- **Brittle environments**: Arid and semi-arid grasslands where the method was originally developed and where Savory argues it is most needed due to the risk of desertification.
- **Non-brittle environments**: More humid environments where the method adapts with different recovery periods and stocking densities.
- **Mixed farming systems**: Integration of grazing with crop production through rotational designs.
- **Rangeland restoration**: Application to degraded public and private rangelands in North America, Africa, and Australia.

The Savory Institute's global hub network has documented results from diverse climate zones, providing case studies for practitioners in different regions.

## The Savory Institute

Founded in 2009, the Savory Institute is a non-profit organization dedicated to promoting holistic management globally. Key activities include:

- **Hub network**: Accredited training centers worldwide that teach and support holistic management practitioners.
- **Land to Market program**: An outcomes-based verification system for regenerative agriculture, connecting producers with consumers who value ecological outcomes.
- **Research partnerships**: Collaborations with academic institutions to study the effects of holistic management on soil health, biodiversity, and carbon sequestration.
- **Educational programs**: Online and in-person training for farmers, ranchers, and land managers.
