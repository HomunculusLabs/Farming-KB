## Description

W.A.S.A.L.T. is an acronym for the **Whittington Interceptor Salt-Affected Land Treatment Society**, a farmer-led organisation founded in March 1978 in Brookton, West Australia. The society was established by Harry Whittington, who developed a practical field-based theory of desertification centred on soil collapse rather than simple deforestation. W.A.S.A.L.T. trains consultants and contractors in the construction of interceptor banks -- carefully engineered earthworks designed to halt and reverse desertification on farmland. At the time of Mollison's writing, the society had 4,100 farmer-members.

## Key Facts

- Founded by Harry Whittington based on decades of practical field work addressing desertification on his own and neighbouring properties.
- Developed the **soil collapse theory** of desertification, which attributes dryland salting primarily to clearing, cultivation, and hoofed animal compaction, with secondary effects from surface water swamping.
- Trains consultants and contractors in the correct construction of interceptor banks -- a specialised form of graded earthwork.
- Even bulldozers are modified to create a 1-metre blade drop specifically for delving ditches and ramming banks to seal them.
- Emphasises that great care must be taken in the size, spacing, grading, and construction of interceptor banks.
- A period of formal training with W.A.S.A.L.T. practitioners is considered necessary for both farmers and contractors before attempting interceptor bank construction.
- Maintains extensive data supporting both the theoretical explanations and the remedial earthworks used to reverse desertification.
- Mollison personally inspected their work ("I peered into miles of canals and ditches") and was fully convinced of the correctness of their theories and the effectiveness of their land therapy.

## The Soil Collapse Theory

The W.A.S.A.L.T. approach differs from the simpler "groundwater rising" model of dryland salting by identifying a more complex causal chain:

1. **Primary causes**: Clearing removes vegetation; cultivation destroys soil structure; hoofed animals compact the soil surface.
2. **Secondary effects**: Compacted soils cannot absorb rainfall, generating overland flow that carries small quantities of cyclic salt.
3. **Chemical process**: Salt (sodium chloride) dissociates, releasing sodium ions that displace calcium on clay particles, causing **deflocculation** when sodium exceeds 15% of exchangeable cations.
4. **Structural collapse**: Clay crumb structure disintegrates; dispersed clay particles migrate downward and cement the subsoil into a water-repellent block.
5. **Cascade effect**: Sealed surface and subsoil create ever-increasing surface waterlogging, worsening all initial problems.
6. **Wind effects**: On cleared areas exceeding 500 hectares (5 square kilometres), hot winds begin drying out additional soils and vegetation.

## Interceptor Bank Technology

The key remedial earthwork developed by W.A.S.A.L.T.:

- **Purpose**: Intercept and redirect surface water flows before they can waterlog susceptible soils.
- **Construction**: Precisely graded banks with sealed surfaces, built using modified bulldozers.
- **Spacing and sizing**: Determined by detailed site assessment of soil type, slope, and hydrology.
- **Integration**: Combined with gypsum application, strategic tree planting, and modified grazing management.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

W.A.S.A.L.T. represents a powerful model for permaculture practitioners:

- **Community-scale earthworks**: Demonstrates that landscape-scale problems require coordinated, community-based responses with trained practitioners.
- **Modified equipment**: Shows that appropriate technology -- even modified bulldozers -- is essential for effective earthwork construction.
- **Evidence-based practice**: The society's emphasis on training, data collection, and proper construction standards provides a rigorous model for permaculture earthworks.
- **Integrated approach**: Interceptor banks alone are insufficient; the W.A.S.A.L.T. approach combines earthworks with chemical remediation (gypsum), biological solutions (tree planting), and management changes (grazing modification).
- **Farmer-led innovation**: Whittington's work demonstrates that practical landholders can develop and disseminate solutions more effective than those proposed by distant researchers.
