# Passiflora incarnata

**Passiflora incarnata**, commonly known as **passionflower**, **maypop**, or **wild passion vine**, is a perennial climbing vine in the family Passifloraceae, native to the southeastern United States and extending into Central and South America.
It is one of the most widely used herbal anxiolytics and sedatives in Western herbal medicine, with a documented history of use spanning over 400 years among Native American peoples and European settlers.
The species produces striking, complex flowers with a distinctive corona of filaments, edible fruits, and has been the subject of extensive pharmacological research validating its traditional use as a mild anxiolytic, sedative, and antispasmodic agent.

The species was first described by Linnaeus in 1753. The genus name *Passiflora* ("passion flower") was bestowed by early Jesuit missionaries in South America, who saw in the flower's complex structure a visual representation of the Passion of Christ: the five petals and five sepals representing the ten faithful apostles, the corona filaments symbolizing the crown of thorns, the three stigmas representing the nails of the crucifixion, and the five stamens representing the five wounds.
The specific epithet *incarnata* means "flesh-colored," referring to the pale pink-purple flower color.
Approximately 550 species are recognized within the genus *Passiflora*, though *P. incarnata* is among the most medicinally significant.

## Description and Morphology

*Passiflora incarnata* is a vigorous woody-based perennial vine that can climb 3–9 meters using axillary tendrils.
The leaves are palmately lobed with 3–5 lobes, 6–15 cm long and wide, finely serrate margins, and alternate arrangement on slender green stems.
The flowers are among the most complex in the plant kingdom, measuring 5–9 cm in diameter, with five greenish-white sepals, five white to pale lavender petals, and a prominent corona composed of multiple series of filaments — the innermost banded purple and white, the outermost white.
The androgynophore (a stalk bearing both male and female parts) extends above the corona, bearing five stamens with large anthers and a tripartite style with three capitate stigmas.
The fruit is a fleshy berry (pepodio) 5–7 cm long, green maturing to yellow-orange, containing numerous seeds embedded in an aromatic, mucilaginous aril.
The root system consists of a deep, fleshy taproot and extensive rhizomes, enabling vegetative spread and persistence through winter dormancy in temperate climates.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Passiflora incarnata* is native to the southeastern United States, ranging from Florida and Texas northward to Virginia, Kansas, and Ohio, and extending into northeastern Mexico.
It has naturalized widely beyond its native range in California, the Pacific Northwest, Hawaii, parts of South America, the Caribbean, Europe, and Asia.
The vine thrives in disturbed habitats, roadsides, fencerows, forest edges, meadows, and riparian zones, preferring full sun to partial shade and well-drained sandy or loamy soils.
It is drought-tolerant once established but produces the most abundant foliage and flowers with regular moisture.
Hardy to USDA Zone 6 (−23°C), the above-ground vegetation dies back with frost while the root system survives to resprout in spring.
The species can become aggressively invasive in favorable conditions, spreading rapidly via rhizomes and self-seeding.

## Chemistry and Active Compounds

The pharmacological activity of *Passiflora incarnata* is attributed to a complex mixture of flavonoids, alkaloids, and other constituents:

- **Chrysin** (5,7-dihydroxyflavone; CAS 480-40-0) — the most extensively studied flavonoid in passionflower, a selective aromatase inhibitor and partial agonist at central benzodiazepine receptors. Concentrations in dried aerial parts: 0.1–0.8%.
- **Vitexin** (apigenin-8-C-glucoside; CAS 3681-93-4) — a C-glycosylflavone with anxiolytic, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties.
- **Orientin** (luteolin-8-C-glucoside; CAS 28608-75-5) — a luteolin derivative with documented anxiolytic and anti-inflammatory activity.
- **Isovitexin** (apigenin-6-C-glucoside; CAS 38665-49-1) — isomer of vitexin with similar pharmacological profile.
- **Schisantherin A and B** — dibenzocyclooctadiene lignans with neuroprotective effects.
- **Harman** and **harmaline** (β-carboline alkaloids) — present in trace amounts, monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitory activity. Controversial: some studies question whether these alkaloids are consistently present in commercial preparations.
- **GABA** (γ-aminobutyric acid) — the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter, found in measurable quantities in the dried herb, potentially contributing to direct GABAergic effects.
- **Maltol** and **ethylmaltol** — flavor compounds with mild sedative activity in animal models.

## Traditional and Ethnobotanical Use

*Passiflora incarnata* has a rich history of use among indigenous peoples of the southeastern United States.
The Cherokee used the root as a poultice for boils, wounds, and inflammation, and prepared infusions of the leaves for liver problems and earaches.
The Houma people used the plant as a blood tonic, and the Creek applied poultices of crushed leaves to boils.
Early European settlers adopted the plant, and by the late 19th century, passionflower had entered the Eclectic medical pharmacopoeia as a remedy for insomnia, anxiety, epilepsy, and hysteria.
The Eclectic physician Dr. Edwin M. Hale (1897) wrote extensively about its use, noting it was "superior to opium in insomnia" without the dangers of addiction.
Passionflower was listed in the United States National Formulary from 1916 to 1936.
In contemporary herbal medicine, *P. incarnata* is one of the most prescribed herbs for anxiety and insomnia in North America and Europe.

## Clinical Research

### Anxiety Disorders

Multiple double-blind, randomized controlled trials have evaluated passionflower for anxiety.
A 2001 RCT (n=36) comparing passionflower extract (45 drops/day) with oxazepam (30 mg/day) over 4 weeks found no significant difference in anxiolytic efficacy, though the passionflower group showed lower job performance impairment.
A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that passionflower demonstrates moderate anxiolytic effects in generalized anxiety disorder, with effect sizes comparable to low-dose benzodiazepines but with a superior safety profile.

### Insomnia

A well-cited 2011 RCT (n=41) comparing passionflower tea with placebo over 7 nights found significant improvements in sleep quality as measured by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).
Passionflower is frequently combined with valerian (*Valeriana officinalis*) in commercial sleep formulations, with combination products showing synergistic effects on sleep latency and quality in several trials.

### Opiate Withdrawal

A notable 2001 double-blind RCT (n=65) compared clonidine alone with clonidine plus passionflower extract for managing opiate withdrawal symptoms over 14 days.
The passionflower adjunct group showed significantly greater improvement in anxiety, irritability, and sleep disturbance scores, suggesting potential utility as a withdrawal management aid.

### Safety and Toxicity

Passionflower is generally well-tolerated with an excellent safety profile.
The most commonly reported side effects are mild drowsiness, dizziness, and confusion, particularly at high doses.
No serious adverse effects have been reported in clinical trials at standard therapeutic doses (250–900 mg dried herb equivalent or 0.5–2 mL tincture 1:5, 3× daily).
Theoretically, passionflower may potentiate the sedative effects of benzodiazepines, barbiturates, alcohol, and other CNS depressants, and should be used with caution in combination with these agents.
Pregnant and breastfeeding women are advised to avoid use due to insufficient safety data, though the herb was traditionally used to promote uterine relaxation during labor.
The β-carboline alkaloids (harmala alkaloids) are MAO inhibitors, but concentrations in typical preparations are too low to produce clinically relevant MAO inhibition.

## Cultivation and Harvesting

*Passiflora incarnata* is easily cultivated from seed, cuttings, or root divisions.
Seeds require stratification (cold treatment for 30–90 days) for optimal germination, or can be scarified and soaked in warm water before planting.
The vine prefers full sun and well-drained soil with pH 6.1–7.5, though it tolerates poor sandy soils and drought once established.
For medicinal harvest, aerial parts (leaves, stems, and flowers) are collected during the flowering and fruiting period (June–September) and dried at temperatures below 40°C to preserve flavonoid content.
The dried herb has a characteristic slightly sweet, grassy aroma and a mildly bitter taste.
