## Nitrobacter

## Description

*Nitrobacter* is a genus of Gram-negative, chemoautotrophic bacteria responsible for the second step of nitrification: the oxidation of nitrite (NO2-) to nitrate (NO3-). Working in tandem with *Nitrosomonas* (which performs the first step), *Nitrobacter* completes the biological conversion of ammonia to nitrate — a transformation that is central to nitrogen availability in agricultural and natural ecosystems. Without *Nitrobacter*, toxic levels of nitrite would accumulate in soil.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Bacteria
- **Phylum:** Pseudomonadota (Proteobacteria)
- **Class:** Alphaproteobacteria
- **Order:** Rhizobiales
- **Family:** Nitrobacteraceae
- **Genus:** *Nitrobacter*

## Key Facts

- Mentioned on page 49 of *Teaming with Microbes* alongside *Nitrosomonas* as a critical nitrifying bacterium.
- **Chemoautotrophs:** Like *Nitrosomonas*, *Nitrobacter* derives energy from an inorganic chemical reaction — the oxidation of nitrite to nitrate — and uses this energy to fix carbon dioxide.
- **Second step of nitrification:** *Nitrobacter* converts nitrite (NO2-) to nitrate (NO3-). This step is essential because nitrite is toxic to plants at even moderate concentrations, while nitrate is the primary nitrogen source for most crops.
- **Close association with *Nitrosomonas*:** The two genera form functional consortia in soil. *Nitrobacter* depends on the nitrite produced by *Nitrosomonas*, and in healthy soils their populations are closely coupled, preventing nitrite accumulation.
- **Inhibited by free ammonia:** High concentrations of ammonium (NH3) can inhibit *Nitrobacter* activity. This means that sudden large applications of ammonium-based fertilizers can temporarily suppress nitrite-to-nitrate conversion, leading to potentially toxic nitrite buildup.
- **Slow-growing and sensitive:** Like all nitrifiers, *Nitrobacter* has slow growth rates due to the modest energy yield of nitrite oxidation. Recovery from soil disturbance can take weeks.
- **Facultative chemolithotrophs:** Some *Nitrobacter* species can also grow heterotrophically on organic compounds, giving them more metabolic flexibility than *Nitrosomonas*.
- Prefer **aerobic, neutral to slightly alkaline** conditions (pH 7.0-8.0) with adequate moisture.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Plant nitrogen nutrition:** *Nitrobacter* produces nitrate, the nitrogen form preferentially taken up by most annual vegetables and grasses. Active *Nitrobacter* populations are essential for productive annual crop systems.
- **Nitrite toxicity prevention:** Without adequate *Nitrobacter*, nitrite accumulates to phytotoxic levels, causing root damage, poor growth, and increased susceptibility to disease. This is a particular risk after heavy ammonium fertilizer applications.
- **Soil health diagnostics:** Testing for nitrification rates can indicate the health of the *Nitrosomonas-Nitrobacter* consortium. Low nitrification suggests damaged soil biology from compaction, chemicals, or anaerobic conditions.
- **Compost maturity:** Active nitrification in finished compost indicates biological maturity. Immature compost may contain mostly ammonium; mature compost shows significant nitrate, reflecting a healthy nitrifier community.
- **Organic vs. conventional management:** Synthetic nitrogen fertilizers can inhibit *Nitrobacter* through ammonia toxicity. Organic nitrogen sources (compost, cover crops, organic amendments) release nitrogen gradually, supporting sustained nitrifier activity without inhibition events.
- **[[mushroom-substrate-preparation]]:** Understanding nitrification is important for formulating mushroom substrates, as some fungi prefer ammonium while others tolerate nitrate. *Nitrobacter* activity in compost-based substrates determines the nitrogen form available.

## Ecological Role

*Nitrobacter* is the essential partner in the two-step nitrification process. While *Nitrosomonas* initiates the conversion of ammonia, *Nitrobacter* completes it, ensuring that nitrogen flows through the ecosystem in plant-available forms rather than accumulating as toxic intermediates. Together, these nitrifiers form one of the most critical functional groups in [[the-soil-food-web]], linking decomposition and mineralization to plant nutrition.
