# Agaricus brasiliensis

**Type:** Species (Fungus)

**Category:** Edible and medicinal agaric, commercially cultivated

**Family:** Agaricaceae

**Also known as:** Almond mushroom, Agaricus blazei (sensu Heinemann), Agaricus subrufescens (correct name), Himematsutake, Cogumelo do Sol (Mushroom of the Sun)

## Overview

Agaricus brasiliensis is a commercially important edible and medicinal mushroom in the family Agaricaceae, widely known for its distinctive almond aroma and its extensively studied immunomodulatory and anti-tumor properties. The species has one of the most tangled taxonomic histories in mycology: originally described by Charles Horton Peck in 1893 from New York as Agaricus subrufescens, it was independently rediscovered in Brazil near Piedade, São Paulo, in the 1960s and described by Heinemann in 1972 as Agaricus blazei — an illegitimate homonym of Murrill's 1947 Florida species. Wasser proposed A. brasiliensis in 1989, but molecular studies by Kerrigan (2005) confirmed all three names refer to Peck's original A. subrufescens, which has nomenclatural priority. Despite the taxonomic resolution, the names "Agaricus blazei" and "Agaricus brasiliensis" remain ubiquitous in the commercial medicinal mushroom trade and scientific literature. The species is native to subtropical regions of Brazil and the eastern United States but is now cultivated worldwide at massive scale, primarily in China and Japan, for the medicinal mushroom supplement market valued at hundreds of millions of dollars annually.

## Taxonomy

- **Division:** Basidiomycota

- **Class:** Agaricomycetes

- **Order:** Agaricales

- **Family:** Agaricaceae

- **Genus:** Agaricus

- **Subgenus:** Agaricus (section Spissicaules)

- **Species:** A. subrufescens Peck (1893) — correct accepted name

- **Authority:** Peck (1893); Bull. N.Y. State Mus. 2: 47

- **Synonyms:** Agaricus blazei sensu Heinemann (1972, illegitimate homonym); Agaricus brasiliensis Wasser (1989); Agaricus rufotegulis Nascimento et al.

**Taxonomic resolution:** A. subrufescens Peck (1893) = A. brasiliensis Wasser (1989) = A. blazei sensu Heinemann (1972). These are all the same species. The true A. blazei Murrill (1947) is a different species from Florida with smaller spores (3.5-5 x 2.5-3.5 micrometers). The commercial name "Himematsutake" (Princess Matsutake) is a Japanese marketing term with no taxonomic basis; the species has no relationship to true matsutake (Tricholoma matsutake). Multilocus phylogenetic analysis places A. subrufescens in Agaricus section Spissicaules, closely related to A. macrosporus and A. augustus.

## Morphological Description

- **Cap (pileus):** 5-15 cm diameter (up to 25 cm in some cultivated forms); hemispherical to convex when young, expanding to broadly convex at maturity; surface dry, white to cream, covered with fine fibrillose to small squamulose scales especially toward center, becoming brownish with age; margin inrolled when young

- **Flesh:** Thick, white, firm; stains bright yellow when cut or bruised (key identification character due to phenoloxidase reaction)

- **Gills (lamellae):** Free, crowded; color progression from white to pink to pinkish-brown to chocolate brown at maturity; edge even or slightly floccose

- **Stem (stipe):** 4-10 cm long, 1-3 cm thick; cylindrical, clavate at base; white, solid to stuffed; well-developed membranous double annulus (ring) that is white and hanging

- **Spore print:** Chocolate brown to dark brown

- **Spores:** Broadly ellipsoid, smooth, thick-walled with germ pore, 5.5-7.5 x 4-5.5 micrometers

- **Odor:** Strong, characteristic almond fragrance (due to benzaldehyde and benzoic acid derivatives) — diagnostic feature, one of the few Agaricus species with distinctly almond-like aroma

- **Taste:** Mild, nutty, pleasant

- **Chemical reactions:** Flesh turns bright yellow with KOH; yellow staining on bruising or cutting

## Distribution and Habitat

**Native range:** Originally described from Greenport, Long Island, New York (Peck 1893) and the subtropical Atlantic Forest of southeastern Brazil, particularly near Piedade, São Paulo state at elevations of 800-1500 m. Also reported from Florida, northern Argentina, Uruguay, and Paraguay.

**Current cultivation:** Massively cultivated worldwide. China is the largest producer with large-scale facilities in Fujian and Zhejiang provinces. Japan has been a major cultivator since the 1980s. Korea, Taiwan, Indonesia, and Thailand also produce commercially. Brazil maintains both wild harvest and cultivated production. Small-scale cultivation exists in the US and Europe for specialty markets.

**Habitat:** In the wild, found in subtropical and temperate grasslands, pastures, and forest margins. Often associated with rich organic soils. The Piedade population grew in the mountainous Atlantic Forest region.

## History of Discovery and Commercialization

Local inhabitants of Piedade, São Paulo, traditionally consumed the mushroom and called it "Cogumelo do Sol" (Mushroom of the Sun) or "Cogumelo de Deus" (Mushroom of God). The Piedade population reportedly had unusually low rates of certain chronic diseases, which locals attributed to regular consumption of this mushroom.

In the 1960s, a Japanese immigrant in Brazil (sometimes identified as Furumoto) noticed the health of the Piedade population and sent spore samples to Japanese researchers. A popular but unsubstantiated story involving Albert Einstein is fabricated — Einstein died in 1955 and had no connection to this mushroom.

Japanese mycologists at Mie University and the Iwade Fungi Institute began cultivation trials in the 1970s-1980s. Successful cultivation was developed in the 1980s, and the mushroom was marketed as "Himematsutake" (Princess Matsutake) — a deliberately misleading name implying kinship with the prized matsutake. Companies like Piedade Co. and Kyowa Medex commercialized extracts and dried products heavily promoted as immune-boosting and anti-cancer supplements. Cultivation expanded to China and other Asian countries in the 1990s-2000s, and the global market for A. subrufescens products grew to hundreds of millions of dollars annually. Scientific interest expanded worldwide with hundreds of peer-reviewed studies on bioactive compounds.

## Cultivation

Cultivation follows methods similar to the button mushroom (A. bisporus) but at higher temperatures. The primary substrate is composted straw (wheat or rice) supplemented with horse or chicken manure, gypsum, rice bran, and wheat bran. Composting requires 14-21 days of aerobic fermentation with temperatures reaching 50-80 degrees Celsius, followed by pasteurization at 60 degrees Celsius for 8-12 hours and conditioning at 50-55 degrees Celsius for 5-7 days. Final substrate pH is 6.5-7.0 with 60-65% moisture content.

This is a thermophilic species requiring fruiting temperatures of 22-28 degrees Celsius (optimum 25-27 degrees Celsius), significantly warmer than button mushrooms. Relative humidity of 85-95% is needed during fruiting. Spawn run takes 20-30 days, followed by a fruiting cycle of 3-5 flushes over 60-90 days. Biological efficiency ranges from 30-60% (kg fresh mushrooms per kg dry substrate). Production is primarily in plastic bags or bottles (Japanese method). China dominates production with large-scale facility operations.

## Chemical Composition

**Macronutrients (per 100g dry weight):** Protein 25-40%, carbohydrates 35-50%, fiber 8-12%, fat 2-5%, ash (minerals) 7-10%.

**Key bioactive compounds:**

- **Beta-glucans:** The primary immunologically active compounds at 5-8% of dry weight, with both (1-3)-beta-D-glucan and (1-6)-beta-D-glucan linkages. These activate macrophages, NK cells, and dendritic cells via Dectin-1 and complement receptor 3 (CR3) pathways

- **Ergosterol and vitamin D2:** Ergosterol at 0.5-1.0% dry weight; UV irradiation converts ergosterol to vitamin D2. Also present: ergosterol peroxide, dihydroergosterol

- **Phenolic compounds:** p-hydroxybenzoic acid, p-coumaric acid, protocatechuic acid, ferulic acid with strong antioxidant activity (3-8 mg GAE/g total phenolics)

- **Steroids and triterpenes:** Lanostane-type triterpenes (agaricosterols) and ergosterol derivatives

- **Agaritine:** A phenylhydrazine derivative present in most Agaricus species; potential carcinogenic concern but reduced 80-90% by cooking; levels may be lower than in A. bisporus

- **Almond aroma compounds:** Benzaldehyde and benzoic acid derivatives responsible for the characteristic fragrance

- **Other:** Nucleotides (adenosine, guanosine), lectins, ribonucleases, mannitol, B vitamins (B1, B2, B3, B5, B6, B7, B9, C, D2), minerals (K, P, Mg, Ca, Zn, Se, Fe), predominantly unsaturated fatty acids (linoleic acid most abundant)

## Medicinal Properties

**Immune modulation (best-studied effect):** Beta-glucans stimulate production of cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, IL-6, IL-12, IFN-gamma) and enhance phagocytic activity of macrophages and neutrophils. Evidence level: strong — supported by in vitro studies, animal models, and several human clinical trials.

**Anti-tumor properties:** Animal studies demonstrate tumor growth inhibition (sarcoma 180, Ehrlich carcinoma, MM-46 adenocarcinoma) with oral administration. Proposed mechanisms include immune activation (immune surveillance), direct apoptosis induction, and anti-angiogenesis. Some small Japanese clinical studies showed improved quality of life in cancer patients receiving extracts alongside conventional treatment. Human clinical data remains limited and preliminary; this mushroom is not a proven cancer treatment and should not replace conventional therapy.

**Anti-diabetic effects:** Animal studies (STZ-induced diabetic rats) show blood glucose reduction through improved insulin sensitivity and pancreatic beta-cell protection. Evidence level: moderate (animal) / limited (human).

**Anti-inflammatory:** Reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-6, NF-kappaB pathway) in animal models of colitis and arthritis. Evidence level: moderate.

**Cholesterol-lowering:** Reduces total and LDL cholesterol while increasing HDL in animal studies, possibly through HMG-CoA reductase inhibition and increased bile acid excretion. Evidence level: moderate (animal) / preliminary (human).

**Antioxidant properties:** Strong DPPH, ABTS, and hydroxyl radical scavenging activity; reduces oxidative stress markers in animal studies. Evidence level: good (in vitro and animal).

**Hepatoprotective:** Protects against CCl4-induced and alcohol-induced liver damage in animal models, reducing ALT and AST enzyme elevation. Evidence level: moderate.

## Safety Notes

Generally recognized as safe when consumed as food; no serious adverse effects reported in clinical trials at standard doses of 500-3000 mg/day. Rare allergic reactions (skin rash, asthma-like symptoms from spore exposure) have been reported. Gastrointestinal upset is uncommon at high doses. Agaritine content is a theoretical concern but reduced significantly by cooking and found acceptable at normal dietary levels by regulatory agencies. Possible immunostimulatory effects could interact with immunosuppressant drugs — caution for organ transplant patients. Heavy metal accumulation from contaminated substrate is a quality control concern. Insufficient data for pregnancy and breastfeeding — caution advised. Not a replacement for conventional medical treatment.
