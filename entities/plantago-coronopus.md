# Plantago coronopus

## Description

*Plantago coronopus*, commonly known as buckshorn plantain or buckthorn plantain, is a low-growing perennial herb in the family Plantaginaceae. Mollison identifies it as a key **surface indicator species** for [[hordeum-maritimum]]*) on agricultural land where rising groundwater and soil salinity are beginning to affect crop production.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Family:** Plantaginaceae
- **Genus:** *Plantago*
- **Species:** *P. coronopus*
- **Common names:** Buckshorn plantain, buckthorn plantain, star of the earth, minutina
- **Growth habit:** Low-growing perennial or biennial herb, 5–25 cm tall

## Role as Salinity Indicator

Mollison specifically identifies buckthorn plantain (*Plantago coronopus*) alongside sea barley grass (*Hordeum maritimum*) as surface indicators of dryland salting:

- Appears as **brownish patches** in pasture and cropland
- Colonizes areas where salt concentrations are rising but not yet visible as surface salt crusts
- Replaces conventional pasture species as soil salinity increases
- Presence signals that the subsoil is becoming saturated and anaerobic

## Diagnostic Value

In Mollison's dryland salinity assessment framework, the appearance of *Plantago coronopus* indicates:

- **Rising water tables** from upslope deforestation and increased infiltration
- **Salt mobilization** through the soil profile
- **Impending crop failure** if salinization continues unchecked
- **Anaerobic soil conditions** developing in the root zone
- **Need for interceptor bank construction** and upslope [[juncus]]* (rushes) dominate; bare soil expands

## Culinary Note

*Plantago coronopus* is also cultivated as a salad green in some European countries, known as "minutina" or "erba stella." The young leaves are edible raw or cooked, with a mild flavor. This dual role as both edible plant and salinity indicator adds permaculture value.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

*Plantago coronopus* is a valuable indicator species for permaculture designers in dryland regions. Its appearance provides an early visual warning of salinity problems that may otherwise remain hidden until crops fail. Unlike sea barley grass, it also offers direct food value, making it a functional addition to saline-affected permaculture sites even as it signals underlying problems. Designers should train landowners to recognize both indicator species and use their presence as triggers for diagnostic investigation.
