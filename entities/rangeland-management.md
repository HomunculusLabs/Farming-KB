# Rangeland Management

## Description

Rangeland management is the systematic approach to maintaining and improving extensive natural grazing lands through careful balance of plant and animal species. Mollison presents rangeland management as fundamentally different from intensive [[pasture-management]], emphasising multi-species grazing, wildlife integration, and the preservation of soil and water resources as primary functions over livestock production.

## Classification

- **Category**: Land management system
- **Domain**: Extensive grazing, wildlife management, conservation
- **Related concepts**: Stock unit system, browse levels, polyculture grazing, range condition

## Key Facts

### Natural Productivity vs. Intensive Systems

Brown (1972) estimated the standing crop of large herbivores on the African savannah at approximately 500 kg/ha — comparable to improved pastures in humid temperate areas. The savannah system sustains guilds of browsers (5 species), grazers (11 species), and mixed feeders (4 species), each with specific food preferences. This natural polyculture system surpasses any known cultivated livestock system without external inputs.

### Stock Unit System

The standard stock unit for range assessment is a European cow and calf (~450 kg liveweight). Approximate equivalents:

- 1 standard stock unit = 2 African cows = 5 European sheep = 20 African sheep = 25 Thompson gazelles = 125 [[dikdik-antelope]]
- 10 standard stock units = 1 elephant = 4 black rhino = 15 zebras = 27 [[hartebeest]]

One European cow browse year = 266 black-tailed jackrabbits = 164 antelope jackrabbits = 18 kangaroo = 6 sheep = 385 ground squirrels.

### Multi-Species Advantages

Different species feed at different browse levels, enabling far greater production than single-species systems. Kangaroo and jackrabbits have significantly higher assimilable protein content (58% for kangaroo vs. 35% for cattle) and can be continuously harvested. A judicious balance of species always exceeds monoculture yield and keeps rangeland in better productive condition.

### Range Diet Variation

Range diets vary dramatically by species and season:

- **Elk**: Browse 32%, Grasses 65% (max 85%), Mosses 5%, Forbs 3%
- **Antelope**: Browse 74%, Grasses 4%, Forbs 22%

Deer may utilise 92% of a range area, compared to only 52% for cattle. Deer also convert food to flesh 2-3 times more efficiently than cattle depending on species.

### Water and Range Behaviour

Water placement is critical for range management:

- Cattle range only 3-7 km from water
- Deer, kangaroo, goats, and small antelope can survive without free surface water except in extreme drought
- Fencing from valleys to ridges controls cattle movement and reduces erosion from tracking

### Vegetation Cover and Water Infiltration

Vegetation cover profoundly affects water infiltration during the first 30 minutes of rainfall:

- 5% cover: 1.2% of rainfall soaks in
- 15% cover: 1.3% soaks in
- 25% cover: 1.7% soaks in
- 35% cover: 2.4% soaks in

After only 9 years of stocking, soil pore space was reduced 44% in the top 5 cm and 60% in the lower 60 cm layer. 65-70% total plant cover is needed to prevent erosion entirely.

### Range Condition Indicators

| Vegetation Type | Runoff (% rain) | Soil Erosion (t/ha) | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wheat grass (good climax) | 0.4% | 0.001 | Sustainable |
| Lupin and needle grass | 4.9% | 0.963 | Exceeds soil creation rate |
| Annual brome grass | 2.55% | 0.425 | Transitional — holds soil |
| Annual weed species | 6.08% | 3.094 | Equal to plough culture losses |

### Insect Plagues and Range Health

Canadian researchers found a direct correlation between range depletion and grasshopper outbreaks (Stoddart, 1955). Rangelands infested with grasshoppers, plague locusts, or Mormon crickets can lose 67% of browse to insects. Fish, guinea fowl, and turkeys on range efficiently convert plague insects into usable protein. In Botswana, drought harvest of locusts yields four times the food value of stored grains by weight.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Rangeland management illustrates the [[permaculture]] principle that polyculture systems outperform monocultures. The integration of multiple animal species with careful attention to browse levels, water placement, and predator-prey dynamics creates productive, sustainable systems. The primary function of rangelands in a permaculture context may be preserving upland soils and water for more intensive use downstream, with careful low-polyculture stocking as a secondary benefit.
