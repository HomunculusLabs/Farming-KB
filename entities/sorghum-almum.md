## Sorghum almum (Silk Sorghum / Columbus Grass)

## Description

Sorghum almum, commonly known as silk sorghum or Columbus grass, is a robust perennial forage grass species originating from South America. In Bill Mollison"s *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual*, it is listed as "Silk sorghum, Sorghum almum" among the useful grass species for designed agricultural systems.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Poaceae (Grass family)
- **Genus**: Sorghum
- **Species**: S. almum
- **Common names**: Silk sorghum, Columbus grass, five-year sorghum
- **Note**: Sometimes considered a subspecies or hybrid derivative of Sorghum halepense (Johnson grass).

## Key Facts

- **Growth habit**: Tall, rhizomatous perennial grass growing 1.5–3 meters in height, forming dense clumps from an extensive rhizome system. Produces stout, erect stems with broad leaves.
- **Native range**: South America, particularly Argentina and surrounding regions. Now distributed across warm-temperate and subtropical regions worldwide, including Australia, southern Africa, and the southern United States.
- **Climate adaptation**: Adapted to warm-temperate to subtropical climates with annual rainfall of 500–1200 mm. Exhibits good drought tolerance through its deep rhizome system and can survive extended dry periods in a dormant state. Moderately frost-tolerant compared to purely tropical grasses.
- **Soil requirements**: Adaptable to a wide range of soils, including heavy clays where many other forage species struggle. Prefers fertile, well-structured soils but tolerates moderately saline and alkaline conditions. Deep, cracking clays are particularly suitable.
- **Persistence**: As a true perennial, silk sorghum persists for many years through its rhizome system, unlike annual forage sorghums that require reseeding each season.
- **Regrowth**: Excellent regrowth after grazing or cutting, producing multiple growth cycles per season from the rhizome reserve.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

### Perennial Forage Alternative

Silk sorghum offers a perennial alternative to annual forage sorghums, eliminating the need for yearly reseeding. This permanence aligns with [[permaculture-design-principles]] that favor perennial systems over annual ones. Once established, a silk sorghum pasture can produce forage for 5+ years, reducing labor, seed costs, and soil disturbance.

### Heavy Clay Soil Forage

One of the key advantages of Sorghum almum is its adaptation to heavy clay soils that are challenging for many tropical and subtropical grasses. In permaculture designs on vertisol (cracking clay) soils, silk sorghum can be a productive forage component where species like Rhodes grass or Guinea grass underperform. It tolerates the seasonal cracking and swelling cycles of these soils.

### Biomass Production

Silk sorghum produces substantial biomass, making it useful as a mulch source in permaculture systems. When cut before seed set, the material can be applied as a thick mulch layer to garden beds, providing weed suppression and organic matter input. Multiple biomass harvests per growing season are possible with adequate moisture.

### Erosion Control

The extensive rhizome system of Sorghum almum provides excellent soil binding, making it useful for erosion control on banks, contour strips, and disturbed sites. The rhizomes spread horizontally through the soil, creating a dense underground network that resists water erosion and mass movement.

### Integration with Legumes

Like other tall tropical grasses, silk sorghum can be grown in association with climbing or twining legumes such as siratro (Macroptilium atropurpureum) or glycine (Neonotonia wightii). The grass provides structural support for the legume vines while the legumes contribute nitrogen to the system.

### Windbreak Function

The tall, dense growth of silk sorghum makes it effective as a seasonal windbreak in permaculture designs. When planted in multiple rows perpendicular to prevailing winds, it reduces wind speed and provides shelter for more sensitive crops, small animals, and microclimate modification.

## Management Considerations

Sorghum almum can become weedy due to its aggressive rhizome spread. Regular grazing or cutting is essential to prevent unwanted invasion of adjacent plantings. The species can cross-pollinate with grain sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), potentially creating weedy hybrid populations in areas where both are grown. Prussic acid (hydrogen cyanide) poisoning risk exists in young regrowth following stress (drought, frost), so grazing should be managed carefully during these periods.

## Relationship to Sudan Grass

Sorghum halepense (Sudan grass/Johnson grass), listed separately in Mollison"s plant species index as "Sudan, Sorghum halapense," is a closely related species with similar forage value but greater weed potential. Sorghum almum is generally preferred in permaculture contexts for its slightly better persistence and more manageable spread.

### Integration with Rotational Grazing

In permaculture livestock systems using rotational grazing, Sorghum almum provides an excellent recovery-phase forage. Its rapid regrowth following grazing allows it to fill gaps in rotational grazing schedules, providing feed when other pastures are in their rest phase. The deep root system also helps break up compacted soil layers, improving infiltration and soil structure for subsequent pasture species in the rotation.

### Potential as a Bioenergy Crop

The high biomass yield of perennial silk sorghum has attracted interest for bioenergy applications. In permaculture energy systems, it could serve as a locally produced feedstock for biogas digesters, providing both energy and nutrient-rich digestate for fertilizer. This dual-purpose function — forage and energy — exemplifies the permaculture principle of stacking functions.

### Water Management in Permaculture

Silk sorghum's deep rhizomes and extensive root system make it useful in permaculture water management strategies. Planted along contour swales, it helps slow, spread, and sink water into the soil profile. The species' ability to thrive on heavy clays means it can be used in areas where water tends to pool temporarily, utilizing that moisture before it evaporates or runs off.
