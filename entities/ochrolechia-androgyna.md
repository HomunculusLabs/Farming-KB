## Overview

**Ochrolechia androgyna** (L.) A. Massal. is a crustose lichen in the family Ochrolechiaceae, characterized by its pale greyish to white thallus and distinctive brownish or greenish soralia. The species is widespread across boreal and temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

It typically grows on the bark of deciduous and coniferous trees, particularly in humid forest environments. The specific epithet "androgyna" refers to the presence of both soredia and apothecia, giving the species dual reproductive capabilities.

This dual reproductive strategy is relatively uncommon within the genus *Ochrolechia*, where most species rely predominantly on either sexual or vegetative reproduction. The combination of both modes provides O. androgyna with enhanced colonizing ability across diverse forest environments.

Ochrolechia androgyna produces gyrophoric acid as its primary [[fungal-diversity-boreal-forests-taiga]] where they contribute significantly to bark-dwelling lichen diversity.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Ochrolechia androgyna** was originally described by Linnaeus as *Lichen androgynus* in 1753 and later transferred to *Ochrolechia* by Massalongo in 1852. The species has a complex taxonomic history with several synonyms reflecting historical disagreements about species boundaries within the genus.

Some authorities have treated populations from eastern Asia as a distinct taxon based on subtle differences in soralia morphology and spore size. However, molecular studies have generally supported the recognition of a single widespread species with moderate morphological [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].

In coastal temperate rainforests of the Pacific Northwest, O. androgyna is particularly abundant on the trunks and branches of large conifers. The persistent fog and drip from canopy interception create ideal moisture conditions for crustose lichen growth.

Elevation ranges from sea level to approximately 2000 meters, with the species becoming more restricted to montane and subalpine zones in the southern parts of its range. It is relatively sensitive to air pollution and serves as a bioindicator of forest air quality.

The species shows moderate shade tolerance but can also persist in partially open canopy conditions along forest edges and in woodland clearings. Growth rates are slowest in deeply shaded interiors and fastest in locations with dappled light and consistent humidity.

## Chemistry and Secondary Metabolites

The secondary chemistry of Ochrolechia androgyna is dominated by gyrophoric acid, which is present as the major metabolite throughout the thallus. This depside is readily detected by thin-layer chromatography and is a key character for species identification within the genus.

Spot tests show C+ red for the medulla, which is the diagnostic reaction for gyrophoric acid. The cortex reactions are typically K-, C-, KC-, and P-. The strong C+ red reaction in the medulla distinguishes O. androgyna from species containing lecanoric acid (which gives a C+ red reaction but has different chromatographic profiles).

Some specimens may also contain trace amounts of lecanoric acid or other related depsides. Chemical variation within the species is generally minor, and the gyrophoric acid chemosyndrome is consistent across its geographic range.

Gyrophoric acid has been investigated for its antibacterial and antifungal properties in laboratory studies. While these bioactivities are not exploited commercially, they may contribute to the species' competitive ability on bark surfaces by inhibiting microbial competitors.

The soralia contain the same chemical compounds as the main thallus, and the gyrophoric acid chemistry is maintained in vegetatively dispersed propagules. This chemical consistency supports the identification of sorediate material in the field using spot tests.

## Ecology and Symbiosis

Ochrolechia androgyna forms a mutualistic symbiosis with trebouxioid green algae (Chlorophyta) housed within the medullary layer of the thallus. The photobiont provides photosynthetically fixed carbon to the fungal partner, while receiving protection, water retention, and mineral nutrients in return.

The species is a mid-successional crustose lichen that typically colonizes bark after pioneer species such as *Lecanora* spp. have established. It contributes to epiphytic lichen community diversity and often co-occurs with foliose lichens including *Flavoparmelia caperata* and *Hypogymnia physodes*.

Vegetative dispersal through soredia allows O. androgyna to colonize nearby bark surfaces efficiently. Soredia are dispersed by wind and rain splash, establishing new thalli on suitable substrates within the same tree or on adjacent trees.

The soredia contain both fungal hyphae and algal cells, allowing establishment of a complete symbiotic thallus upon landing on a suitable substrate. Dispersal efficiency is highest during wet periods when soredia are more easily detached from the thallus surface.

The species provides microhabitat for various invertebrates, including mites and springtails, which inhabit the thallus surface and feed on associated microorganisms. It contributes to [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] through the retention and slow release of atmospheric particles and dissolved nutrients.

In boreal forest ecosystems, O. androgyna is one of the more abundant crustose lichens on mature *Picea* and *Abies* bark. Its presence is often associated with forest stands that have been undisturbed for 50 years or more, making it a useful indicator of forest continuity.
