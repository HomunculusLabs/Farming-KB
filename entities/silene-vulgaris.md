The species is valued as a wild edible green in many European and Mediterranean cuisines. Young shoots and leaves have a mild, slightly sweet flavor and are used in salads, soups, and cooked greens.

Silene vulgaris is highly adaptable and occurs in a broad range of habitats, from coastal dunes and roadside verges to agricultural fields and mountain meadows. This ecological plasticity has made it a successful colonizer on every continent except Antarctica.

The genus name Silene is thought to derive from Silenus, the Greek god of wine, possibly referencing the sticky, frothy exudate on the stems. The specific epithet vulgaris simply means common.

### Floral Morphology

The most distinctive feature is the inflated, bladder-like calyx, 12 to 18 millimeters long, with prominent purple-green or straw-colored venation. The calyx is ovoid to urceolate and becomes papery in fruit.

Petals are white, bifid, and exerted well beyond the calyx mouth, creating a fringed or ragged appearance. Each petal is divided into two narrow lobes with a small scale or coronal appendage at the base.

Flowers are arranged in open, spreading dichasia at the stem tips and upper leaf axils. The inflorescence is typically loose and airy, contrasting with the dense cymes of related Silene species.

Blooming occurs from May to September, with peak flowering in mid-summer. The calyx persists and becomes conspicuously inflated as the capsule matures, making the plant easily identifiable even in fruit.

In traditional European herbalism, bladder campion was occasionally applied as a poultice for minor skin irritations. However, it never achieved the medicinal prominence of related Caryophyllaceae species.

The roots have been used in folk medicine as a mild diuretic and expectorant. These applications are not well-supported by modern pharmacological research, and the species is primarily valued as a food plant rather than a medicinal one.

## Distribution and Habitat

Silene vulgaris is native to Europe, North Africa, and western Asia, with a natural range extending from the British Isles and Scandinavia to the Mediterranean and eastward through the Caucasus and into Central Asia.

The species has been widely introduced and naturalized in North America, [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] and southern Canada.

Habitats include grasslands, meadows, roadsides, railway embankments, waste ground, cultivated fields, coastal dunes, and open woodlands. It tolerates a wide pH range but shows preference for calcareous or neutral soils.

The plant is moderately salt-tolerant and occurs on coastal cliffs and shingle beaches in western Europe. It is also found at elevations up to 2,400 meters in alpine and subalpine meadows.
