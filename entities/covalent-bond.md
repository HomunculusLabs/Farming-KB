# Covalent Bond

## Description

A covalent bond is a chemical bond formed when two atoms share electrons in their outer (valence) shells to complete electron pairs. This sharing creates a force that holds the two atoms together as a molecule. Covalent bonds are the strongest type of chemical bond important in biological systems and form the structural basis of all organic molecules — the carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids that constitute living organisms.

## Classification

- **Type**: Chemical bond
- **Mechanism**: Electron sharing between atoms
- **Strength**: Strongest of the three biologically important bonds (covalent > ionic > hydrogen)
- **Subtypes**: Nonpolar covalent, polar covalent
- **Variations**: Single, double, and triple covalent bonds

## Formation

Covalent bonds form based on the principle that electrons in the outer shell (valence orbit) of atoms prefer to pair up:
- The outer shell of most atoms can hold up to four pairs of electrons (eight total) — the octet rule
- Atoms with fewer than four pairs in their valence orbit are highly reactive
- These atoms seek out electrons of other atoms to complete their pairs
- When two atoms share electrons, both achieve more stable electron configurations

## Bond Types by Strength

### Single Covalent Bond

One pair of electrons shared between two atoms. Common in organic molecules.

### Double Covalent Bond

Two pairs of electrons shared. Stronger than a single bond.

### Triple Covalent Bond

Three pairs of electrons shared — extremely strong. Example: the nitrogen molecule (N₂), where two nitrogen atoms share three electron pairs, making atmospheric nitrogen very stable and difficult to break apart (which is why biological nitrogen fixation requires so much energy).

## Subtypes by Charge Distribution

### Nonpolar Covalent Bond

- Shared electrons spend equal time orbiting each atom
- Both parts of the molecule carry the same charge
- No electrical poles are created
- Atoms of the same element always form nonpolar covalent bonds
- Nonpolar molecules are not electrically attracted to other molecules

### Polar Covalent Bond

- Electrons spend more time orbiting one atom than the other due to differences in electronegativity
- The atom holding electrons longer becomes slightly negatively charged
- The other atom becomes slightly positively charged
- The molecule develops opposite charges at different locations (poles)
- **Water (H₂O)** is the most famous polar molecule — negative charge on oxygen, positive charges on hydrogen
- Like charges repel; opposite charges attract — this polarity drives molecular interactions

## Key Facts

- The atoms of organic molecules (carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen) are joined by covalent bonds
- Carbon's ability to form four covalent bonds makes it the backbone of all organic molecules
- Triple covalent bonds are extremely strong — atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) is difficult to split, which explains why plants cannot use atmospheric nitrogen directly
- The strength of a covalent bond depends on the number of shared electron pairs
- Polar covalent bonds explain why water is such an excellent solvent for polar and charged molecules (including most plant nutrients)
- Lewis diagrams are used to visualize electron configuration and predict bonding behavior

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- All plant nutrients are incorporated into organic molecules through covalent bonding — understanding these bonds is fundamental to understanding plant nutrition
- The polarity of water molecules (due to polar covalent bonds) makes water the universal solvent that carries dissolved nutrients to plant roots
- The extreme strength of the nitrogen molecule's triple covalent bond explains why nitrogen fixation (breaking N₂) requires either specialized bacteria or industrial processes (Haber-Bosch)
- In mycology, fungal enzymes break and form covalent bonds to decompose complex organic matter into simpler compounds
- Understanding bond polarity helps explain why some nutrients (charged ions) dissolve readily in water while others (nonpolar molecules) require different transport mechanisms
- The carbon backbone of organic molecules, formed through four covalent bonds per carbon atom, determines the vast diversity of plant secondary metabolites, including terpenes, flavonoids, and alkaloids
