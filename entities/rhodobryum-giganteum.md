## Overview

**Rhodobryum giganteum** (giant moss or *da juan xue* in Chinese) is a large, conspicuous moss species in the family Bryaceae, widely distributed across temperate and subtropical regions of Asia, Africa, and the Americas. It is notable for its large, rosette-forming sterile stems that superficially resemble tiny fern fronds.

The species has a long history of use in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) as a hemostatic and cardiovascular tonic. It is one of the few moss species with well-documented ethnopharmacological applications, prescribed under the TCM name *juan bai* for bleeding disorders and cardiovascular conditions.

R. giganteum is a characteristic component of moist, shaded forest floors in montane and submontane regions. Its large size (up to 10 cm tall) and distinctive morphology make it one of the most easily recognized forest mosses in its range.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Bryophyta → Bryopsida → Bryidae → Bryales → Bryaceae → **Rhodobryum giganteum** (Schwägr.) Paris.

The species was originally described as *Mnium giganteum* by Christian Friedrich Schwägrichen in 1816 and later transferred to the genus *Rhodobryum* by Édouard Ernest Prillieux in 1909. The taxonomic placement of Rhodobryum within Bryaceae has been debated, with some authors treating it as a subgenus of Bryum based on molecular phylogenetic evidence. Recent phylogenomic analyses using chloroplast and mitochondrial markers support the recognition of Rhodobryum as a distinct lineage within Bryaceae, closely allied to the genus Mnium.

The genus Rhodobryum comprises approximately 40 species worldwide, with the greatest diversity in tropical and subtropical mountain regions of Southeast Asia and South America. R. giganteum is the type species of the genus and the most widely distributed member.

R. giganteum exhibits strong dimorphism between sterile and fertile stems. Sterile stems are tall (3–10 cm), erect, forming loose rosettes of large, broadly spathulate leaves 6–12 mm long and 3–5 mm wide. The leaf margins are recurved near the apex, and the costa (midrib) extends to near the leaf tip.

Fertile stems are shorter (1–3 cm) with smaller, ovate-lanceolate leaves. The sporophyte consists of a solitary, cylindrical capsule 3–5 mm long on a reddish seta 1.5–3 cm. Capsules are pendulous when mature, with a well-developed double peristome facilitating spore dispersal.

The leaf lamina is loosely arranged with elongated, thick-walled laminal cells. A distinctive feature of the genus is the presence of paraphyllia (filamentous structures between leaves) in some species, though these are reduced or absent in R. giganteum.

## Distribution and Habitat

R. giganteum has a broad but discontinuous distribution across temperate and subtropical regions. In Asia, it occurs in China (Yunnan, Sichuan, Guizhou, Hubei, Zhejiang, Taiwan), Japan, Korea, and the Himalayan region (Nepal, Bhutan, northern India). In Africa, populations are known from the Ethiopian highlands, East African mountains, and South Africa.

The species also occurs in Central and South America (Mexico, Costa Rica, Colombia, Brazil) and has been reported from isolated locations in southeastern North America. The disjunct distribution pattern suggests an ancient origin with subsequent range fragmentation during Pleistocene climatic oscillations. Large colonies create a moist microhabitat favorable for germination of vascular plant seedlings and for soil invertebrates including springtails and nematodes.

The species serves as a substrate for epiphytic algae, fungi, and micro-arthropods. In montane cloud forests, moss mats of R. giganteum and related species intercept fog drip, channeling water to the forest floor and contributing significantly to local hydrological cycles.

As a relatively large and productive moss species, R. giganteum accumulates organic carbon in its persistent biomass.
