## Description

Mepkin Abbey is a Trappist monastery located in South Carolina where [[tradd-cotter]] helped develop a mushroom production system using repurposed refrigerated shipping trucks. The facility demonstrates how abandoned or underutilized infrastructure can be converted into productive mushroom cultivation spaces, particularly in urban environments where real estate is limited. The Mepkin Abbey system became a model for modular, scalable mushroom production using shipping containers.

## Classification

- **Category**: Cultivation facility / Place

## Key Facts

- Trappist monastery in South Carolina
- Cotter helped develop a mushroom production system using old refrigerated shipping trucks on the property
- The shipping trucks had lost their air-conditioning units to vandals but retained their insulation
- Interiors were covered with plastic and sealed to make units watertight
- A regular air-conditioning unit was installed on one end for climate control
- A series of overhead pipes was installed for hanging oyster mushroom columns
- Automatic misting tubes ran the length of the pipes with micronizing misting heads
- Misting was timed for 30 seconds every hour
- Each 40-foot unit could produce an average of 65 pounds of mushrooms per week
- The system is modular and can be stacked to minimize footprint
- Applicable to urban environments where real estate is limited

## Infrastructure Design

The conversion of refrigerated shipping trucks into [[mushroom-fruiting-chambers]] leverages several advantages:
- Pre-existing insulation maintains stable temperatures
- Watertight sealing allows high-humidity operation
- Standardized dimensions enable modular deployment
- Units can be transported and relocated as needed
- Multiple units can be stacked vertically to maximize space utilization

## Broader Applications

The Mepkin Abbey model demonstrates that mushroom cultivation infrastructure does not require purpose-built facilities. Abandoned refrigerated trucks, shipping containers, and similar structures can be converted with minimal investment (plastic sheeting, air conditioning, misting systems). This approach is particularly relevant for:
- Urban farming operations with limited space
- Disaster relief and food security programs
- Low-capital startup operations
- Mobile cultivation systems

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Mepkin Abbey represents a practical example of adaptive reuse in mushroom cultivation infrastructure. The 65 lbs/week production from a single 40-foot unit demonstrates that commercial-scale yields are achievable with repurposed equipment and modest investment. The model has influenced the broader movement toward container-based farming and vertical agriculture, showing that mushroom cultivation is uniquely suited to modular, space-efficient production systems.
