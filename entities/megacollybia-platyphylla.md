## Overview

**Megacollybia platyphylla** (formerly Tricholomopsis platyphylla) is a saprophytic agaric in the family Tricholomataceae. It is commonly known as the broad-gilled collybia or the whitelaced mushroom.

The species is recognized by its large, broadly convex cap, widely spaced gills, and distinctive white rhizomorphs at the base of the stipe. It fruits on decaying hardwood logs and stumps.

Megacollybia platyphylla is widely distributed across temperate forests of the Northern Hemisphere. It is one of the larger collybioid species encountered during the summer and fall mushroom season.

Its edibility is disputed, with some sources listing it as edible and others cautioning against consumption. Most contemporary guides classify it as inedible or of poor quality.

The species is occasionally confused with edible species of [[entoloma-abortivum]], which share similar habitats on decaying wood. Careful attention to gill spacing and the presence of rhizomorphs distinguishes it.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

The species was originally described as Agaricus platyphyllus by Christian Hendrik Persoon in 1796. It was transferred to Megacollybia by Rolf Singer in 1949.

The genus Megacollybia is monotypic, containing only this single species. Molecular phylogenetics has confirmed its distinct status from Tricholomopsis and other related genera.

Phylogenetic analysis places Megacollybia in the marasmioid clade of the Tricholomataceae. Despite its collybioid appearance, it is more closely related to [[armillaria-tabescens]].

The species occasionally appears terrestrial when growing from buried roots or buried wood. In such cases, the rhizomorphs provide the crucial identification clue.

It is encountered in both old-growth and second-growth forests, though it is more abundant where large amounts of coarse woody debris are present. Forest management practices that remove dead wood reduce its habitat.

## Edibility

The edibility of Megacollybia platyphylla is a matter of debate among mycological authorities. Some older European texts list it as edible when thoroughly cooked.

However, most contemporary field guides classify it as inedible or not recommended. The flesh is described as tough, bland, and unappealing.

The species lacks the toxic amatoxins and orellanine found in genuinely dangerous mushrooms. Its poor reputation is based on culinary quality rather than acute toxicity.

There are isolated reports of gastrointestinal upset from consuming this species. These adverse reactions may be due to individual sensitivity rather than inherent toxicity.

Reports of adverse effects are most commonly associated with eating raw or undercooked specimens. The tough flesh of mature fruiting bodies is particularly difficult to digest.

Given the availability of many superior edible species in the same habitat, there is little reason to collect Megacollybia platyphylla for the table. Beginners should treat it as inedible.

Some foragers in eastern Europe reportedly collect young specimens after thorough cooking. This practice is not widely recommended and should be approached with caution.

## Ecological Role

Megacollybia platyphylla is a white-rot decomposer that breaks down the cellulose and lignin in dead hardwood. Its extensive rhizomorph system allows it to transport nutrients across considerable distances.

The species contributes to the degradation of coarse woody debris in mature forests. This activity is essential for nutrient cycling and soil formation.
