## Overview

Lantana camara L. is a species of flowering plant in the family Verbenaceae, native to the tropical and subtropical Americas. It is one of the world's most invasive woody weeds, listed among the 100 worst invasive alien species by the IUCN.

Despite its notoriety as an invasive plant, L. camara has a long history of use in traditional medicine across Africa, Asia, and the Americas, with documented antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antipyretic properties.

The species is a prolific bloomer, producing clusters of small, brightly colored flowers that change color as they age, making it popular as an ornamental hedge plant in gardens worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Lamiales → Verbenaceae → Lantana → L. camara.

**Authority:** Carl Linnaeus, Species Plantarum (1753). The taxonomy of the L. camara complex is complicated by extensive hybridization; over 600 varieties and cultivars have been described.

L. camara is a sprawling, thicket-forming shrub, typically 1–3 m tall but occasionally reaching 5 m as a scandent climber supported by neighboring vegetation.

Stems are square in cross-section (quadrate), armed with recurved prickles along the angles, and densely pubescent when young, becoming woody and glabrescent with age.

Leaves are opposite, simple, ovate, 5–12 cm long and 3–6 cm wide, with serrate margins, rough upper surfaces, and pubescent lower surfaces. They emit a strong, pungent aroma when crushed.

Inflorescences are terminal or axillary heads, 2–4 cm across, composed of numerous small tubular flowers. Flowers are heterostylous and change color after pollination — commonly from yellow to orange to red.

Fruit is a small, fleshy drupe, 5–8 mm in diameter, green when immature, turning glossy purple-black at maturity. Each drupe contains two seeds.

## Distribution and Invasive Biology

Native range extends from southern Texas (USA) through [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-hawaii-pacific-islands]]. In many regions it has become the dominant woody weed of pastures, forests, and disturbed land.

Invasiveness is driven by multiple factors: high [[clay-pellet-seed-dispersal]], [[permaculture-designers-manual-essential-oil-plants]] (0.1–0.3% yield) contains β-caryophyllene, α-humulene, and 1,8-cineole as major constituents.

Alkaloids have been reported in some chemotypes, including lantanine and lantanolic acid, though their pharmacological significance is less studied than the triterpenoid fraction.

## Pharmacology and Ethnomedicine

In Indian Ayurvedic medicine, leaf decoctions are used externally for wound healing and skin infections, and orally for fever, rheumatism, and digestive disorders.

African traditional medicine employs L. camara roots for snakebite, leaf infusions for malaria-like fevers, and leaf poultices for boils and skin ulcers.

In vivo studies have confirmed anti-inflammatory activity of leaf extracts in carrageenan-induced paw edema models, with potency comparable to indomethacin at equivalent doses.

Antimicrobial activity has been demonstrated against [[bloomfield-candida-albicans-yeast-hyphal-dimorphism]], with MIC values ranging from 100–500 µg/mL.

Antioxidant activity (DPPH IC₅₀ = 45–80 µg/mL for leaf extracts) is attributed primarily to the flavonoid and phenolic acid content. Hepatoprotective effects against CCl₄-induced liver damage have also been documented.

## Toxicity

L. camara is hepatotoxic to livestock, particularly cattle, sheep, and goats. Chronic poisoning causes photosensitization, jaundice, weight loss, and eventual death.

Hepatogenous photosensitization (secondary photosensitization) occurs when liver damage impairs phylloerythrin metabolism, leading to severe sunburn-like lesions on unpigmented skin.

The ripe fruits are eaten by birds without ill effect, but are toxic to humans and most mammals. Human ingestion causes vomiting, abdominal pain, and in severe cases, hepatic failure.
