## Overview

**Centrolobium paraense** is a Neotropical [[fungal-biodiversity-tropical-forests]].

Natural populations have declined due to selective logging, as the species is targeted for its premium heartwood. It is listed as near-threatened in several Brazilian state red lists, though not yet on the IUCN global Red List.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Centrolobium paraense** was described by Louis René Tulasne and published in 1844. The genus Centrolobium contains approximately 6–8 species, all restricted to Neotropical forests.

Recent phylogenetic analyses place Centrolobium within the dalbergioid clade of Faboideae, closely allied with Platymiscium and Machaerium. Morphological synapomorphies include the characteristic winged samara and the presence of bipinnate leaves in some species.

- **Order:** Fabales

- **Subfamily:** Faboideae

The tree is deciduous during the dry season, shedding its foliage before the onset of flowering. Its compound leaves are pinnate with 7–13 leaflets, each elliptic to obovate and 4–8 cm long.

Young branches are pubescent, becoming glabrous with age. The leaf rachis is slightly winged, a characteristic that helps distinguish it from related Centrolobium species in the field.

The inflorescences are axillary panicles bearing showy yellow flowers typical of many Faboideae. Flowers are papilionaceous, with a standard petal 15–20 mm long and a keel enclosing the stamens.

The fruit is a flattened, winged samara 5–8 cm long, with a single central seed. A distinctive circular wing facilitates wind dispersal over considerable distances in open canopy conditions.

Bark is gray-brown, fissured, and exfoliating in older specimens. The inner bark is fibrous and reddish, producing a weakly colored exudate when cut.

## Distribution and Habitat

C. paraense occurs naturally across the Brazilian Amazon, extending into eastern Peru, southern Colombia, and the Guianas. It is found at elevations from sea level to approximately 500 meters.

Specimens have been recorded from Acre, Amazonas, Pará, and Rondônia states in Brazil, with disjunct populations in the upper Orinoco basin of Venezuela. Populations are generally scattered at low densities rather than forming pure stands.

The species grows in both terra firme (non-flooded) and seasonally flooded lowland [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]].

The wood is also valued for musical instruments, particularly guitar backs and sides, owing to its tonal resonance. It is sometimes marketed internationally as "Brazilian tulipwood," though this name is more properly applied to unrelated Dalbergia species.

Sawdust from araribá can cause mild dermatitis in some workers, a common trait among tropical hardwoods containing quinone-type extractives. Proper dust extraction and protective equipment are recommended during machining.

## Cultivation and Ecology

C. paraense is propagated from seed, which requires scarification or hot-water treatment to break physical dormancy. Seed viability declines rapidly — storage beyond 6 months at ambient conditions significantly reduces germination rates.

Germination rates of 70–85% are achievable with freshly collected, scarified seed sown in well-drained nursery beds. Seedlings are ready for outplanting at 4–6 months when they reach 25–35 cm in height.

Plantations are established at spacings of 3 × 3 to 5 × 5 meters. Growth in managed plantations is moderate, with mean annual increment of 1.5–3.0 cm in diameter under favorable conditions.

Rotation lengths in plantation forestry range from 25 to 40 years for [[honey-locust-pod-fodder-timber-production]]. Thinning is recommended at 8–10 years to reduce competition and promote straight bole development.
