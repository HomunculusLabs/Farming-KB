## Overview

**Alpinia zerumbet**, commonly known as **shell ginger**, **pink porcelain lily**, or **variegated ginger**, is a rhizomatous perennial herb in the family Zingiberaceae. It is native to East Asia and is widely cultivated throughout the tropics and subtropics as an ornamental and medicinal plant.

The species is valued for its striking shell-shaped flowers with pink and yellow striped petals, as well as its variegated foliage. The leaves and rhizomes contain bioactive diarylheptanoids and essential oils with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and diuretic properties.

In Okinawan folk medicine, A. zerumbet leaves are consumed as a daily health tea, contributing to the region's reputation for longevity. The species is one of the most culturally significant medicinal plants in the Ryukyu Islands.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

A. zerumbet was described by (Pers.) B.L.Burtt & R.M.Sm. and is the most widely cultivated species in the genus *Alpinia*, which contains approximately 230 species. The genus is named after Prospero Alpini (1553–1617), an Italian physician and botanist.

The plant forms dense clumps from stout, creeping rhizomes. Aerial stems (pseudostems) reach 1.5–3 m in height, arising from the rhizome nodes. Each pseudostem consists of tightly wrapped leaf sheaths.

Leaves are lanceolate to oblong, 30–60 cm long and 5–12 cm wide, with a prominent central vein and parallel lateral veins. The wild type has solid green leaves; the popular ornamental cultivar has leaves striped with yellow or cream variegation.

The inflorescence is a pendant, terminal raceme producing clusters of fragrant flowers. Each flower has three pink to white petals with a yellow lip marked with red streaks. The buds resemble seashells, giving the plant its common name.

Fruits are globose capsules 1–1.5 cm in diameter, orange-red when ripe, containing small black seeds embedded in white arils.

## Distribution and Habitat

A. zerumbet is native to eastern India, southern China, Taiwan, and the Ryukyu Islands of Japan. It has been introduced and naturalized throughout tropical and subtropical Asia, the Pacific Islands, and parts of the Americas.

The species grows naturally in forest margins, thickets, and disturbed areas at low to middle elevations (0–800 m). It prefers warm, humid conditions with annual rainfall exceeding 1500 mm.

Shell ginger is frost-sensitive and does not tolerate temperatures below 5°C for extended periods. In subtropical climates, the aerial stems die back in winter and regrow from the rhizome in spring. It performs best in partial shade to full sun with rich, well-drained soils.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The leaves contain significant quantities of phenolic compounds, primarily diarylheptanoids. The major bioactive constituents include **caftaric acid**, **5-O-caffeoylquinic acid**, and various flavonoid glycosides.

The essential oil is composed mainly of monoterpenes, with **1,8-cineole** (eucalyptol), **α-pinene**, **β-pinene**, and **limonene** as principal components. The oil content ranges from 0.1–0.5% of fresh leaf weight.

Antioxidant activity has been demonstrated in multiple in vitro assays. Leaf extracts show strong DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activity, comparable to standard antioxidants at equivalent concentrations.

Anti-inflammatory effects have been documented in animal models of carrageenan-induced paw edema. The diarylheptanoids are considered the primary anti-inflammatory agents.

Diuretic and natriuretic activities have been confirmed in rats, where aqueous leaf extracts increased urinary volume and sodium excretion without affecting potassium balance.

## Culinary and Traditional Uses

In Okinawa, the leaves of A. zerumbet are commonly used to wrap rice cakes (*muchii*) and to flavor local dishes. The young shoots and flower buds are edible and have a mild ginger-like flavor.

The leaves are brewed into a popular herbal tea (*getto-cha*) consumed daily in Okinawan households. The tea is traditionally used to promote digestion, relieve fatigue, and support cardiovascular health.

In traditional Chinese medicine, the seeds (known as *yi-zhi-ren* or *cardamom of the mountains*) are used to warm the spleen, stop diarrhea, and promote digestion. The rhizome is used similarly to other *Alpinia* species.

In Southeast Asian folk medicine, leaf poultices are applied to wounds and skin infections. A decoction of the rhizome is consumed for stomach ache and intestinal parasites.

The essential oil of A. zerumbet has shown insecticidal and larvicidal activity against mosquito vectors of dengue and malaria. This has generated interest in the plant as a source of natural biopesticides for vector control programs.

## Cultivation

A. zerumbet is propagated by division of the rhizome or from seed. Division is the preferred method, as seeds are slow to germinate and variegated cultivars do not breed true from seed.

Plants are established at 0.5–1 m spacing in fertile, organic-rich soil. Regular irrigation and partial shade produce the best leaf coloration in variegated cultivars. Full sun can bleach the variegation.

The species is relatively pest-free but may be affected by mealybugs, spider mites, and fungal leaf spot diseases in humid conditions. Good air circulation and well-drained soil minimize disease pressure.

In temperate climates, A. zerumbet is grown as a container plant and overwintered indoors. The rhizomes survive light frosts if well-mulched, but extended freezing kills the plant.

## Ethnobotany and Cultural Significance

A. zerumbet holds particular cultural importance in Okinawa, Japan, where it is one of the most commonly used medicinal plants. Its consumption as a daily herbal tea is cited as a contributing factor to the exceptional longevity of Okinawan populations.

The plant is incorporated into traditional Okinawan festivals and rituals. Its flowers are used in seasonal decorations, and the leaves are used to wrap ceremonial foods. The species is considered a symbol of resilience due to its ability to recover quickly from typhoon damage.

In Hawaii, A. zerumbet has become naturalized and is known as "shell ginger." It is used in traditional Hawaiian healing practices and is commonly planted around homes for its ornamental value and cultural associations.

## Landscape and Ecological Value

Shell ginger is widely used in tropical and subtropical landscaping as a specimen plant, mass planting, and container specimen. The variegated cultivar is particularly popular for its year-round colorful foliage.

The flowers are attractive to butterflies, bees, and hummingbirds, providing nectar resources in garden settings. The dense clumping habit provides cover for small wildlife.

The species has naturalized in parts of Hawaii, Florida, and the Caribbean, where it can form dense stands along waterways. It is not currently listed as a major invasive threat, but its spread is monitored in sensitive habitats.
