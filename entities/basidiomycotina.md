## Description

Basidiomycotina (equivalent to the modern phylum Basidiomycota in current taxonomy) is a major subdivision of the fungal kingdom encompassing the "club fungi." This group includes all mushroom-forming fungi, as well as rusts, smuts, jelly fungi, and shelf/bracket fungi. Dighton references Basidiomycotina as one of the two groups of "higher fungi" (along with Ascomycotina) that produce large, visible fruiting bodies bearing spores on specialized structures.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Basidiomycota (formerly subdivision Basidiomycotina)
- **Subphyla**: Agaricomycotina (mushrooms, jelly fungi), Pucciniomycotina (rusts), Ustilaginomycotina (smuts)
- **Key feature**: Production of basidia — specialized club-shaped cells on which sexual spores (basidiospores) are produced
- **Spore production**: Basidiospores formed externally on basidia (typically 4 per basidium)

## Key Facts

- Includes the vast majority of fungi that produce macroscopic, fleshy fruiting bodies commonly recognized as "mushrooms" — members of the Agaricales (gilled mushrooms), Polyporales (bracket/shelf fungi), Boletales (pored mushrooms), and many other orders
- Distinguished from Ascomycotina by the production of spores on the exterior of the basidium rather than within a sac-like ascus
- Includes many ecologically critical groups: ectomycorrhizal fungi (e.g., *Amanita*, *Boletus*, *Russula*, *Laccaria*), wood-decay fungi (white-rot and brown-rot), plant pathogens (rusts and smuts), and saprotrophic litter decomposers
- The fruiting bodies (basidiocarps) produced by many basidiomycetes are energy-intensive structures that serve as sinks for internally translocated carbon and nutrients, making them highly nutritious food sources for animals and humans
- Basidiomycete mycelia frequently form rhizomorphs, cords, and strands — linear aggregations of hyphae specialized for long-distance transport of water and nutrients
- Many basidiomycetes produce secondary metabolites including toxins (amatoxins, phallotoxins), pigments, and bioactive compounds with pharmaceutical potential (polysaccharides, terpenoids)
- Play dominant roles in forest ecosystem processes: wood decomposition, nutrient cycling, mycorrhizal associations with trees, and food web support through fruiting body production
- Rust fungi (Pucciniomycotina) include some of the most devastating agricultural pathogens in history (e.g., wheat stem rust *Puccinia graminis*)

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Nearly all commercially cultivated mushroom species are basidiomycetes: *Agaricus bisporus* (button mushroom), *Pleurotus* species (oyster mushrooms), *Lentinula edodes* (shiitake), *Ganoderma* species (reishi), *Flammulina velutipes* (enokitake), *Hericium erinaceus* (lion's mane), and many others
- Understanding basidiomycete biology — particularly the formation and maturation of fruiting bodies (basidiocarps) — is fundamental to mushroom cultivation science
- The energy cost of fruiting body production means basidiomycetes require substantial substrate colonization and nutrient accumulation before fruiting can be induced — this is the biological basis for the colonization period required before initiating pinning in cultivation
- White-rot basidiomycetes (which degrade both cellulose and lignin) are the primary decomposers used in wood-based mushroom cultivation substrates
- Ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes (e.g., truffles, porcini, chanterelles) remain largely uncultivable in pure culture, representing one of the major challenges in mycological science
- The production of basidiospores on exposed surfaces of the fruiting body is the basis for spore print collection and spore-based cultivation techniques

## Ecological Importance

Basidiomycotina represents the fungal group most directly responsible for the ecosystem services that humans associate with "fungi" — mushroom formation, wood decomposition, mycorrhizal partnerships with trees, and the complex food webs that depend on fungal fruiting bodies. Their ecological dominance in forest ecosystems makes them central to understanding nutrient cycling, carbon sequestration, and ecosystem stability.
