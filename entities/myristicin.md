## Overview

Myristicin (5-allyl-1-methoxy-2,3-methylenedioxybenzene) is a naturally occurring phenylpropene compound and a weak monoamine oxidase inhibitor found primarily in nutmeg (*Myristica fragrans*). It is responsible for the mild psychoactive and deliriant effects associated with nutmeg consumption.

Myristicin has been used recreationally as a readily available hallucinogen, though its effects are generally described as unpleasant, with prolonged duration and significant physical side effects.

The compound is structurally related to [[elemicin]] and [[safrole]], sharing the allylbenzene backbone common to several natural psychoactive phenylpropenes.

## Chemical Properties

**IUPAC name:** 5-Allyl-1-methoxy-2,3-(methylenedioxy)benzene.

**Molecular formula:** C₁₁H₁₂O₃.

**Molecular weight:** 192.21 g/mol.

**CAS number:** 607-91-0.

Myristicin is a colorless to pale yellow oily liquid at room temperature with a characteristic aromatic odor resembling nutmeg.

It is slightly soluble in water and freely soluble in ethanol, diethyl ether, and most organic solvents.

The compound contains a methylenedioxy bridge and an allyl side chain, both of which are critical to its pharmacological activity.

## Pharmacology

Myristicin acts as a weak reversible inhibitor of monoamine oxidase (MAO), primarily MAO-A.

This MAOI activity potentiates endogenous monoamine neurotransmitters, contributing to its psychoactive effects.

It also shows affinity for serotonin 5-HT₂A receptors, though at significantly lower potency compared to classical psychedelics like LSD or psilocin.

In vitro studies suggest myristicin may inhibit certain cytochrome P450 enzymes, particularly CYP1A2 and CYP2A6.

The compound is metabolized primarily by O-demethylation and side-chain oxidation in the liver.

Its metabolic pathway can produce 3-methoxy-4,5-methylenedioxyamphetamine (MMDA), a psychoactive amphetamine derivative, though this conversion occurs at very low rates in vivo.

## Effects

Onset of effects after nutmeg ingestion occurs at 2-6 hours due to slow gastrointestinal absorption and metabolic conversion.

Peak effects typically last 12-24 hours, with residual aftereffects persisting for up to 48 hours in some individuals.

The effective dose from whole nutmeg is approximately 5-15 grams of seed material, corresponding to an estimated 0.2-1.0 g of myristicin.

Subjective effects include mild visual distortions, altered time perception, depersonalization, and in higher doses, deliriant effects with confusion and hallucinations.

Physical side effects are common and include nausea, vomiting, dry mouth, tachycardia, hypertension, dizziness, and urinary retention.

The experience is frequently described as dysphoric rather than euphoric, with a heavy body load and prolonged recovery.

There is no established lethal dose in humans, though severe toxicity has been reported at high nutmeg doses.

## Natural Sources

Myristicin is most abundant in the seeds of nutmeg (*Myristica fragrans*), where it constitutes 0.5-1.4% of the essential oil.

It is also found in lower concentrations in black pepper (*Piper nigrum*), parsley (*Petroselinum crispum*), carrots, celery, and several other Apiaceae family members.

The essential oil of nutmeg contains approximately 4-13% myristicin depending on origin, cultivar, and processing method.

Nutmeg butter, the fixed oil extracted from nutmeg, contains lower myristicin concentrations than the volatile essential oil.

## History

Nutmeg has been used as a spice and medicine for over 2,000 years in Southeast Asian and Indian traditional medicine systems.

Recreational use of nutmeg for psychoactive effects was first documented in the Western medical literature in the 16th century.

Use became notable in the United States during the 1960s counterculture movement as a legal and accessible intoxicant.

The psychoactive compound responsible was identified as myristicin in the early 20th century through chemical investigation of nutmeg constituents.

Myristicin poisoning remains a recurring clinical presentation in emergency departments, particularly among adolescents seeking legal intoxicants.

## Legal Status

Myristicin is unscheduled in most countries as it occurs naturally in common culinary spices.

Nutmeg itself is unregulated and widely available as a food product worldwide.

Some jurisdictions have implemented age restrictions on bulk nutmeg sales, though enforcement is rare.

In several countries, extraction of myristicin from nutmeg for concentrated use may fall under analog drug legislation.

The compound is not listed on the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances.
