## Black Spot Disease

## Description

Black spot is one of the most common and damaging diseases of roses and other
ornamental plants, caused by the fungal pathogen *Diplocarpon rosae* (sexual
stage) / *Marssonina rosae* (asexual stage). It produces characteristic black
circular spots on upper leaf surfaces, surrounded by yellow halos, leading to
premature defoliation and weakened plants. [[elaine-ingham]] specifically discusses
black spot in the context of using [[compost-tea-for-specific-crops]] [[aact-banana-cultivation-tropical-crop-disease-suppression-ingham]].

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Ascomycota
- **Class**: Leotiomycetes
- **Order**: Helotiales
- **Family**: Dermateaceae
- **Genus**: *Diplocarpon*
- **Species**: *Diplocarpon rosae*

## Key Facts

- Produces circular black spots (2–12 mm diameter) on upper leaf surfaces,
  often with fringed margins and yellow halos.
- Infected leaves turn yellow and drop prematurely, causing progressive
  defoliation from the bottom of the plant upward.
- Severe defoliation weakens plants, reduces flowering, and can kill
  susceptible varieties over time.
- Spores are dispersed by water splash; infection requires at least 7 hours
  of continuous leaf wetness.
- Overwinters on infected fallen leaves and canes; ascospores and conidia
  are produced in spring.
- Most active during warm, humid conditions with frequent rain or overhead
  irrigation.
- Primarily affects roses but can occur on other Rosaceae family members.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Compost Tea Applications

Elaine Ingham includes black spot among the rose diseases that respond to
[[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) treatment:

1. **Foliar colonization**: [[beneficial-fungi-trichoderma-and-mycorrhizae]] and bacteria from AACT colonize
   leaf surfaces, physically occupying niches that *D. rosae* spores would
   otherwise infect.
2. **Antibiotic production**: Beneficial microbes produce antibiotic compounds
   that inhibit black spot [[spore-germination-and-commercial-brick-spawn-production]] hyphal growth.
3. **Nutrient competition**: Foliar microbes consume leaf exudates that the
   pathogen requires for initial infection and colonization.
4. **[[mycorrhiza-induced-resistance-defense-priming]]**: Beneficial organisms on leaf surfaces can trigger
   plant defense responses.

### Scale Rhododendrons

Ingham also discusses black spot in the context of rhododendrons, where
similar AACT-based approaches can manage foliar diseases on acid-loving
plants. The key is matching the biology to the plant's natural conditions.

## Management Notes

- Apply AACT to foliage every 7–14 days during [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].
- Use fungal-dominated compost tea for best results against this fungal
  pathogen.
- Remove and compost infected leaves to reduce overwintering inoculum.
- Avoid overhead watering that creates prolonged leaf wetness.
- Ensure adequate air circulation through proper plant spacing and pruning.
