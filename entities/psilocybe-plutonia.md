# Psilocybe plutonia

**Psilocybe plutonia** is a small, dung-inhabiting [[psilocybin]]-containing mushroom in the
family Hymenogastraceae. It is one of the less commonly documented species within the genus
Psilocybe, found primarily in tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas. The species
belongs to the first group of neurotropic fungi, containing [[psilocybin]] and related indole
alkaloids. It was originally described by the British mycologists Miles Joseph Berkeley and
Moses Ashley Curtis.

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

The species epithet "plutonia" derives from Pluto, the classical Greco-Roman god of the underworld.
The name may refer to the dark coloration of the mushroom when fresh, its preference for growing
on decaying and buried substrates, or the subterranean aspects of its habitat (dung beneath grass
in pastures). The species has sometimes been placed in synonymy or confused with other small,
dark-spored, dung-inhabiting psilocybian species due to its nondescript appearance and small size.

In the comprehensive classification system of Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (2000), which cataloged
216 known neurotropic fungal species worldwide, *P. plutonia* is recognized as one of the 116
species within the genus Psilocybe that contain or are suspected to contain [[psilocybin]]
and related indole alkaloids. Within the genus, *P. plutonia* belongs to section Psilocybe
(the [[psilocybin]]-containing core group), which is characterized by dark spore prints,
the presence of a pleurocystidia (sterile cells on gill faces), and the
absence of a true partial veil (annulus) in most species.

## Morphology

### Macroscopic Features

*Psilocybe plutonia* is a small, delicate, and easily overlooked species. The cap (pileus) is
typically 5 to 15 mm in diameter (occasionally up to 20 mm), initially conical to convex when
young, becoming broadly convex to nearly plane with age. In some specimens, a small umbo
(a central nipple-like protrusion) may be present. The cap is hygrophanous, meaning it changes
color significantly as it dries: dark brown to reddish-brown or olive-brown when moist, fading
to a lighter tan, ochraceous, or straw-yellow color as it dries. The cap surface is smooth
and slightly viscid (sticky) when wet due to a thin gelatinous layer (pellicle), becoming
dull and matte when dry. The cap margin is often slightly translucent-striate at maturity,
meaning thin parallel lines are visible when the cap is moist and backlit. This translucency
is a common feature of hygrophanous Psilocybe species.

The gills (lamellae) are adnate to adnexed (attached to the stipe with a slight notch or
just barely touching), moderately close, and narrow. They are initially pale gray to whitish,
darkening progressively to dark purplish-brown to nearly black as the spores mature. This
color transition from light to dark is a characteristic feature of Psilocybe species.

The stipe is slender, fragile, and easily broken, measuring 15 to 40 mm long and 0.5 to
1.5 mm thick. It is central (attached at the center of the cap), equal in width throughout,
or slightly enlarged at the base. The stipe surface is whitish to pale brown, smooth or
slightly silky-fibrillose, and sometimes has a slightly bluish tint at the base in bruised
or aged specimens. A thin, ephemeral annulus (ring) may be present in some collections but
is often absent or so fragile that it disappears quickly. The stipe is hollow.

The flesh (context) is very thin, pale, and fragile. It does not have any distinctive odor.
The taste has been variously described as farinaceous (mealy or flour-like) or mild. Like
many [[psilocybin]] species, *P. plutonia* may exhibit slight bluish bruising on the stipe
base or cap when damaged, though this reaction is often faint and may be absent in young
or dried specimens.

### Microscopic Features

The spore print is dark purplish-brown to nearly black, typical of the genus Psilocybe.
Basidiospores are ellipsoid to slightly rhomboid or amygdaloid (almond-shaped) in face view,
and slightly inequilateral (asymmetric) in side view. They measure approximately 8 to 11 μm
long by 5 to 7 μm wide, with a distinct germ pore at the apical end. The spore wall is
smooth and moderately thick. Basidia are 2- or 4-spored, clavate (club-shaped), and measure
approximately 12 to 18 by 4 to 6 μm.

Cheilocystidia (sterile cells on the gill edges) are abundant and distinctive, typically
ventricose (swollen in the middle) with a long, narrow neck, sometimes with a slightly
clavate or capitate (head-like) apex. They measure approximately 15 to 30 by 4 to 8 μm.
Pleurocystidia (sterile cells on the gill faces) may be present but are less common and
less conspicuous than the cheilocystidia. The gill trama is regular to subregular, and
the pileipellis (cap cuticle) is a thin ixocutis (a gelatinous layer of interwoven hyphae).

## Distribution and Habitat

*Psilocybe plutonia* is found in tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas. Documented
collections include the southeastern United States (particularly Florida, Georgia, and Alabama),
Central America (Mexico, Guatemala, Costa Rica), the Caribbean islands, and northern South
America (Colombia, Venezuela, Brazil). The species may also occur in tropical Africa and
southeast Asia, though records from these regions require confirmation.

The species is a coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) fungus, typically fruiting on herbivore
dung, especially that of cattle, horses, and other large grazing mammals. It may also occur
on enriched soil in pastures and grasslands where dung has decomposed into the substrate.
The mushrooms often fruit in small clusters or scattered groups on individual dung pats.

Fruiting occurs during warm, humid periods, particularly during the rainy season. In tropical
climates with adequate year-round moisture, *P. plutonia* can be found fruiting in virtually
any month. In more subtropical areas like Florida, it is most common from late spring through
early autumn. The species is relatively short-lived; individual fruiting bodies typically
persist for only 1 to 3 days before deteriorating.

## Chemistry

Like other [[psilocybin]]-containing species in section Psilocybe, *P. plutonia* contains
[[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]] as its primary bioactive indole alkaloids. Psilocybin
(4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) serves as a prodrug, being dephosphorylated in the
body to the pharmacologically active [[psilocin]] (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine). Psilocin
acts primarily as a 5-HT2A serotonin receptor agonist, producing its
characteristic psychoactive effects.

The species may also contain [[baeocystin]] (4-phosphoryloxy-N-methyltryptamine) and
[[norbaeocystin]] (4-phosphoryloxytryptamine) in lower concentrations, as is common among
small, coprophilous Psilocybe species. These compounds are structurally related to
psilocybin but are generally considered less potent, though their pharmacological contributions
to the overall effects are not fully understood.

Due to the small size of the fruiting bodies and the relative rarity of collections in the
scientific literature, quantitative chemical analyses specific to *P. plutonia* are limited.
The psilocybin content is presumed to be moderate, comparable to other small coprophilous
species such as [[psilocybe-coprophila]] and [[panaeolus-cyanescens]], though definitive
data are lacking.

## Ecological Role

As a coprophilous species, *P. plutonia* plays a role in the decomposition and nutrient
cycling of herbivore dung in tropical and subtropical grassland ecosystems. The spores of
coprophilous fungi are adapted to survive passage through the digestive tracts of herbivores,
a process that both disperses the spores across the landscape and stimulates germination by
exposing them to the chemical environment of the gut. The fungus contributes to the breakdown
of complex organic compounds (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin) in dung, facilitating nutrient
return to the soil and supporting subsequent plant growth.

## Similar Species

*Psilocybe plutonia* can be confused with several other small, dark-spored, dung-inhabiting
mushrooms:

- [[psilocybe-coprophila]] — another small coprophilous Psilocybe species, typically with a
more conical cap, slightly larger spores, and differences in cystidia morphology.
- [[psilocybe-cubensis]] — much larger and more robust species growing on the same substrates;
immediately distinguishable by size (cap 2-8 cm vs. 0.5-1.5 cm).
- Species of Panaeolus and Copelandia — also coprophilous with dark spore prints;
distinguished by their black spore prints, mottled gills (spots of different maturity), and
different cystidia types.
- Conocybe species — some of which grow on dung and contain psilocybin; distinguished by
their conical caps and rusty-brown to cinnamon-brown spore prints.
