## Description

Karen K. Nakasone is an American mycologist affiliated with the USDA Forest Service, Forest Products Laboratory, Center for Forest Mycology Research in Madison, Wisconsin. She is recognized as a leading expert on fungal culture preservation, maintenance, and distribution, and co-authored the comprehensive chapter on these topics in *Biodiversity of Fungi: Inventory and Monitoring Methods*.

## Classification

- **Profession**: Mycologist, culture collection specialist
- **Affiliation**: USDA Forest Service, Forest Products Laboratory, Center for Forest Mycology Research, Madison, Wisconsin, USA
- **Specialty**: Fungal culture preservation, [[basidiomycota|basidiomycete]] systematics, wood-inhabiting fungi
- **Era**: Late 20th century–present

## Culture Preservation Methods

Nakasone's comprehensive treatment covers the full spectrum of preservation techniques:

### Short-Term Methods

1. **Serial transfer:** Regular subculturing onto fresh agar media. Simple but risks genetic drift, contamination, and loss of fruiting ability over time.
2. **Mineral oil overlay:** Covering agar slants with sterile mineral oil to reduce metabolism and desiccation. Effective for months to years with periodic checking.
3. **Distilled water storage:** Storing agar plugs or mycelial fragments in sterile distilled water at room temperature. Surprisingly effective and very low cost.
4. **Organic substrata:** Preserving fungi on natural substrates (wood chips, straw, cereal grains). Particularly useful for wood-inhabiting [[basidiomycota|basidiomycetes]].
5. **Soil/sand storage:** Inoculating sterile soil or sand with fungal spores or mycelium. Provides a naturalistic storage medium.
6. **Silica gel:** Preserving spores or mycelial fragments in silica gel at low temperatures. Nakasone provided detailed protocols for both sporulating and non-sporulating strains.

### Long-Term Methods

1. **Liquid nitrogen cryopreservation:** Storage at -196°C in vapor or liquid phase. The gold standard for long-term preservation with minimal genetic change.
2. **Lyophilization (freeze-drying):** Removing water under vacuum after freezing. Excellent for sporulating fungi but less reliable for non-sporulating basidiomycetes.
3. **Ultra-freezer storage:** Storage at -80°C in mechanical freezers, with cryoprotectant solutions. A compromise between accessibility and preservation quality.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Nakasone's work on [[fungal-culture-preservation|culture preservation]] is directly relevant to mushroom cultivation. Commercial operations must preserve production strains without genetic drift or loss of fruiting ability. Her documentation that [[basidiomycota|basidiomycetes]] (which include most cultivated mushrooms) are more challenging to preserve than ascomycetes highlights the need for careful strain management in cultivation facilities. Her comprehensive treatment of accessible methods (distilled water, oil overlay, silica gel) versus permanent methods (liquid nitrogen, lyophilization) gives cultivators a decision framework for balancing cost, convenience, and genetic stability.

### Specific Applications in Cultivation

1. **Strain banking:** Cultivation operations should maintain backup cultures of production strains using Nakasone's recommended preservation hierarchy, with liquid nitrogen for master stocks and simpler methods for working stocks.
2. **Genetic stability:** The choice of preservation method directly affects whether a strain maintains its fruiting characteristics over years of storage.
3. **Culture distribution:** Nakasone's protocols for safe shipping and distribution of fungal cultures enable reliable exchange of strains between research institutions and commercial operations.
4. **Quality assurance:** Regular viability testing following her recommended protocols helps detect culture degradation before it affects production.

## Contribution to Fungal Systematics

Beyond culture preservation, Nakasone has contributed to the systematics of wood-inhabiting basidiomycetes:

1. **Species delimitation:** Her taxonomic work on wood-decay fungi has clarified species boundaries in morphologically variable groups.
2. **Culture-based identification:** She advanced the use of cultural characteristics (growth rate, colony morphology, microscopic features in culture) as supplementary characters for identifying wood-inhabiting fungi.
3. **Herbarium connections:** Her work bridges the gap between herbarium specimens (dried fruiting bodies) and living cultures, ensuring that taxonomic identities can be verified through both morphological and molecular means.
