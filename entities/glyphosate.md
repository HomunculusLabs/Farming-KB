## Glyphosate

## Description

Glyphosate (N-phosphonomethyl glycine) is a broad-spectrum systemic herbicide, commercially available to gardeners as Roundup, that kills plants by interfering with their nutrient and metabolic system. It is the most widely used herbicide in the world and sits at the center of modern agricultural debates about genetically modified organisms (GMOs), environmental contamination, food safety, and sustainable farming practices. Understanding how glyphosate works requires understanding how plants take up and utilize nutrients — it exploits a metabolic pathway unique to plants and microorganisms.

## Classification

- **Type:** Synthetic herbicide (broad-spectrum, systemic)
- **Chemical class:** Organophosphorus compound
- **Commercial name:** Roundup (originally Monsanto, now Bayer)
- **Mechanism:** Blocks the shikimate acid pathway
- **Mode of action:** Systemic — absorbed through foliage and translocated throughout the plant
- **Selectivity:** Non-selective (kills most plants)

## Mechanism of Action

Glyphosate kills plants by **blocking the shikimate acid pathway** (also called the shikimate pathway), a metabolic route that plants and microorganisms use to synthesize three essential aromatic amino acids:

- **Phenylalanine**
- **Tyrosine**
- **Tryptophan**

These amino acids are precursors to a vast array of critical plant compounds, including proteins, growth hormones (auxin), pigments, lignin, and defensive compounds. When glyphosate inhibits the enzyme EPSPS (5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase) in this pathway, the plant cannot produce these amino acids and gradually starves to death.

Crucially, the shikimate pathway **does not exist in animals**. Animals obtain aromatic amino acids from their diet rather than synthesizing them. This is why glyphosate was long considered relatively safe for human and animal exposure — its target pathway simply does not exist in mammalian biochemistry.

## GMO Connection

Many genetically modified (GM) crops are engineered with a bacterial gene (CP4 EPSPS from Agrobacterium) that produces a version of the EPSPS enzyme that is **resistant to glyphosate**. This allows farmers to spray glyphosate over entire fields, killing weeds while leaving the modified crop unharmed. The major glyphosate-resistant crops include:

- Roundup Ready soybeans
- Roundup Ready corn
- Roundup Ready canola
- Roundup Ready cotton
- Roundup Ready alfalfa

As more GM crops are grown globally, increasing amounts of glyphosate are applied to agricultural land, with growing quantities making their way into the natural environment.

## Environmental and Health Concerns

### Soil Microbiology
Glyphosate does not only affect plants. The shikimate pathway exists in many soil microorganisms, including bacteria and fungi. There are concerns that glyphosate application can:

- Harm beneficial soil bacteria that drive nutrient cycling
- Damage mycorrhizal fungi that form symbiotic partnerships with plant roots
- Disrupt [[the-soil-food-web]] that organic cultivation depends on
- Alter microbial community composition in ways that affect plant health

### Environmental Contamination
Ubiquitous use has led to glyphosate residues being detected in:

- Agricultural soils (persistent residues between growing seasons)
- Surface water and groundwater
- Food products (cereals, grains, produce)
- Human urine samples (detected in population-wide studies)

### Regulatory Debate
Regulatory assessments of glyphosate safety remain intensely contested. Different agencies have reached different conclusions:

- The EPA has generally found glyphosate unlikely to be carcinogenic
- The IARC (International Agency for Research on Cancer, part of WHO) classified glyphosate as "probably carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2A)
- The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) concluded it is unlikely to be carcinogenic
- Legal proceedings have resulted in multi-billion-dollar verdicts against Bayer/Monsanto

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Glyphosate directly affects the **soil food web** that organic cultivators and mycologists depend on. Its impact on soil microbiology — particularly mycorrhizal fungi — can persist long after initial application.
- Understanding glyphosate's mechanism of action provides insight into how chemical agents affect fungal metabolism, since fungi share the shikimate pathway targeted by glyphosate.
- In [[outdoor-mushroom-cultivation]], glyphosate drift or soil residue can affect mycelial growth and the health of companion plants in integrated growing systems.
- For organic cultivators, glyphosate represents the antithesis of the soil food web approach — it exemplifies the chemical-dependent paradigm that Von Liebig himself came to reject late in life when he saw its effects on soil biology.
- The herbicide illustrates the principle that interventions targeting one organism's biochemistry often have cascading effects throughout the entire soil ecosystem.
