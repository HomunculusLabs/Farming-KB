# Trichocereus peruvianus

**Trichocereus peruvianus** (syn. *[[trichocereus-pachanoi]]* (San Pedro). The plant is commonly referred to as the [[trichocereus-bridgesii]]* has been the subject of considerable taxonomic debate. These three species form a morphologically similar group that has been difficult to delimit cleanly, particularly in cultivation where hybridization may occur. Some authorities have suggested that *T. peruvianus* and *T. pachanoi* represent a single variable species complex, while others maintain them as distinct species based on differences in spine characteristics, rib count, flower morphology, and alkaloid profile.

The cactus is a large, tree-like columnar plant that can grow up to 3 to 6 meters tall in the wild, though cultivated specimens rarely exceed 2 to 3 meters. Individual stems are bluish-green to glaucous, typically 8 to 12 cm in diameter, with 6 to 8 rounded ribs. The areoles are large and woolly, spaced approximately 2 cm apart, and bear 1 to 4 brownish-yellow central spines up to 4 cm long, plus numerous shorter radial spines. Young stems are more heavily spined than mature specimens, and the spines may become less pronounced with age.

The flowers are large, white, fragrant, and nocturnal, opening at dusk and remaining open through the night. They are among the largest flowers in the Cactaceae, reaching 20 to 25 cm in length and 10 to 12 cm in diameter. The pericarpel and floral tube are covered with dark brown hairs and small scales. The outer petals are greenish-white to brownish, while the inner petals are pure white. The flowers are pollinated by bats and large sphinx moths. The fruit is a spherical to ovoid berry, 3 to 5 cm in diameter, green to yellowish, containing numerous small black seeds embedded in a white pulp.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Trichocereus peruvianus* occurs at elevations of 2,000 to 3,000 meters on the western slopes of the Andes. It is found in dry inter-Andean valleys and on rocky hillsides in regions characterized by significant diurnal temperature fluctuations and low annual rainfall (typically 200 to 500 mm per year). The species is tolerant of drought and grows in some of the most arid environments in South America.

Its range extends from southern Ecuador through central Peru into Bolivia and northern Chile. In Peru, the species is found in the departments of Amazonas, Ancash, Cajamarca, Huánuco, La Libertad, Lambayeque, and Piura. It often grows in association with other columnar cacti, including *Cereus hildmannianus*, *Armatocereus rauhii*, and *Browningia candelaris*, as well as with *Trichocereus pachanoi*, *Trichocereus bridgesii*, and various *Opuntia* species. The vegetation communities where it occurs are classified as Andean desert scrub and inter-Andean thorn scrub.

## Chemistry

The principal psychoactive alkaloid in *Trichocereus peruvianus* is mescaline (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine). [[mckenna-shamanic-tobaccos-and-nicotiana-rustica]]*, and various aromatic herbs. The resulting brew is consumed by the patient and participants under the guidance of the curandero.

The ceremonial use of *Trichocereus peruvianus* is deeply integrated into the cosmovision of Andean peoples, where the cactus is regarded as a teacher plant (planta maestra) that facilitates communication with the spirit world and provides insight into the nature of health, illness, and the cosmos. The ceremony typically involves chanting (icaros), the blowing of tobacco smoke, ritual purification, and the invocation of spiritual allies before the ingestion of the preparation.

In Andean medicinal practice, *Trichocereus peruvianus* is also used topically as a treatment for [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] (spring through autumn). It should be kept dry during the winter dormancy period. The species is hardy to approximately -5°C for short periods when kept completely dry. Growth rates of 30 to 60 cm per year have been reported under optimal conditions, making it one of the fastest-growing columnar cacti.

## Comparison with Related Species

*Trichocereus peruvianus* can be distinguished from *Trichocereus pachanoi* (San Pedro) by several morphological features. *T. peruvianus* typically has longer, more robust spines (up to 4 cm), fewer ribs (6 to 8), and a more pronounced bluish-green (glaucous) coloration. *T. pachanoi* usually has shorter spines (often 1 to 2 cm or less), more ribs (6 to 9), and a greener appearance. The areoles of *T. peruvianus* are more widely spaced and more prominently woolly.

From a chemical perspective, *T. peruvianus* is often reported to have higher average mescaline content than *T. pachanoi*, though there is considerable overlap between the two species. *T. bridgesii* (Bolivian torch) has been reported to have variable but sometimes very high mescaline content, and is distinguished by its longer spines and more robust growth habit.

*Trichocereus macrogonus* is another species that has been confused with *T. peruvianus* in the literature. Some authorities consider *T. macrogonus* to be conspecific with *T. peruvianus*, while others maintain it as a separate species based on its more robust spines and more rounded ribs. The taxonomic status of this entity remains unresolved.

## History of Botanical Exploration

The earliest European botanical collections of *Trichocereus peruvianus* were made by explorers and naturalists in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

Richard Spruce, the English botanist who spent 15 years exploring the Amazon and Andes (1849 to 1864), encountered Trichocereus species in Ecuador and Peru and documented their use by indigenous peoples.

Later, the Czech botanist Alberto Vojtěch Frič made extensive collections of Trichocereus species during his travels in South America in the early 20th century.

The American botanists Lyman Smith and others contributed to the understanding of the genus through their work in the mid-20th century.

More recently, the field work of Karel Kníže, a Czech cactus collector and explorer, significantly expanded the known diversity and distribution of Trichocereus species in Peru and Bolivia during the 1970s through 1990s.

## Hybridization and Cultivar Complexes

In cultivation, extensive hybridization has occurred between *Trichocereus peruvianus*, *T. pachanoi*, and other species, making definitive identification of cultivated specimens extremely difficult. Many of the plants sold commercially as "Peruvian torch" are likely hybrids or may actually be *T. pachanoi* or intermediate forms. The horticultural trade has done little to maintain genetic purity of these species.

Named cultivars and clones of *Trichocereus peruvianus* exist in the collector community, including selections prized for their growth rate, spine characteristics, flower color, or reputed alkaloid content. Some clones, such as the "Matucana" clone and the "Torch" clone, are widely circulated among collectors and have been the subject of informal chemical assays.
