## Description

Michael James Harner (1929–2018) was an American anthropologist
and founder of the Foundation for Shamanic Studies. He was a
leading figure in the ethnographic study of hallucinogenic
plant use in Amazonian [[shamanism]], particularly among the [[jivaro]]
and Conibo-Shipibo peoples. Harner edited the seminal volume
*[[hallucinogens-and-shamanism]]* (1973) and authored *The Jivaro:
People of the Sacred Waterfalls* (1972).

## Classification

- **Profession:** Cultural anthropologist, ethnographer
- **Institutional affiliation:** New School for Social Research
  (Associate Professor, Graduate Faculty); also taught at
  Columbia University, Yale University, UC Berkeley
- **Research area:** Upper Amazon ethnology, hallucinogenic
  plants, shamanism
- **Field sites:** Ecuadorian and [[ayahuasca-tourism-economics-peruvian-amazon]]

## Key Facts

### Academic Career

Harner held faculty positions at several major universities:
- Associate Professor of Anthropology, Graduate Faculty,
  New School for Social Research, New York
- Lecturer/visiting positions at Columbia, Yale, UC Berkeley

### Fieldwork

Harner conducted four expeditions to the Upper Amazon rain
forest, engaging in ethnological research among:
- **Jivaro** (Ecuadorian Amazon) — primary research subjects
- **Achuara** (related Jivaroan group)
- **Conibo-Shipibo** (Peruvian Amazon, Rio Ucayali)

His fieldwork spanned from 1956–57 (initial Jivaro research)
through 1964 (return to Jivaro focusing on hallucinogen use).

### Personal Hallucinogenic Experience

A [[fukuoka-textdoc-post-season-cultivation-kochi-turning-point]] in Harner's career came in 1961 when he drank
the Banisteriopsis brew during fieldwork with an Upper Amazon
tribe. His experience was profound:

> "I found myself, although awake, in a world literally beyond
> my wildest dreams. I met bird-headed people, as well as
> dragon-like creatures who explained that they were the true
> gods of this world."

This experience led him to realize that anthropologists had
"profoundly underestimated the importance of the drug in
affecting native ideology" and prompted his 1964 return to
the Jivaro to focus specifically on hallucinogen use.

### Major Publications

- **[[hallucinogens-and-shamanism-harner-anthology]]** (1973, ed.) — collected papers
  from a symposium at the American Anthropological Association;
  remains a foundational text in the ethnography of
  hallucinogenic plant use
- **The Jivaro: People of the Sacred Waterfalls** (1972) —
  comprehensive ethnography of Jivaro culture including
  shamanism and hallucinogen use
- **"The Sound of Rushing Water"** (Natural History Magazine,
  1968) — influential account of [[harner-jivaro-shamanism-yage]] from the
  "inside view"

### Theoretical Contributions

Harner argued that anthropologists had systematically
underestimated the importance of hallucinogenic plants in
shamanism and religious experience because very few had
personally partaken of the native psychotropic materials.
He proposed that the more powerful hallucinogens "tend to
strongly reinforce a belief in the reality of the supernatural
world" and may have played a role in the original innovation
of such beliefs.

### Foundation for Shamanic Studies

After his academic career, Harner founded the Foundation for
Shamanic Studies, dedicated to the preservation and study of
shamanic knowledge worldwide. He developed "core shamanism"—
a synthesized approach to shamanic techniques stripped of
cultural specificities.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Harner's work is foundational for understanding how
hallucinogenic plants function within their cultural contexts.
His insistence on "participant observation"—personally
experiencing the hallucinogens he studied—pioneered a more
phenomenologically grounded approach to ethnobotany. His
documentation of Jivaro *Banisteriopsis* use provides crucial
data on [[amanita-preparation-methods]], dosage, experiential effects,
and cultural integration of hallucinogenic plants.
