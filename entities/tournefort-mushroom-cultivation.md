## Description

In 1707, the French botanist Joseph Pitton de Tournefort (1656-1708) documented early [[fungal-life-cycles]], spore function, and basic mycological biology.
- No mention is made of cave cultivation in Tournefort's account, suggesting that underground quarry mushroom growing near Paris developed later, likely around the beginning of the 19th century, representing a significant technological advance over earlier methods.
- The cultivation documented by Tournefort occurred during the reign of Louis XIV, when mushroom growing was already considered an established practice in France, indicating that the industry's origins stretch back even further than 1707.
- The original document was later reproduced in a late 19th-century French mycological publication, preserving the historical record for future reference and enabling modern historians to trace the development of cultivation techniques.
- Earlier Greek and Roman authors showed no evidence of special consideration for this species in their writings, suggesting that organized mushroom cultivation was a relatively late development in Western agricultural history.
- Mushroom cultivation was considered a luxury pursuit during this era, with the product commanding premium prices available primarily to the French aristocracy and wealthy classes.
- The species cultivated was the field agaric (Agaricus campestris) and related forms, the same species that would later form the basis of the massive French cave cultivation industry.

## Historical Significance

The Tournefort document provides critical evidence for several important historical claims about mushroom cultivation:

1. **French origin**: The account supports the assertion that mushroom culture had its origin in France, which from the beginning remained the chief home of the industry for centuries.
2. **Pre-cave methodology**: The absence of cave references confirms that the transition to underground cultivation was a later innovation that dramatically expanded the industry's scale and reliability.
3. **Empirical sophistication**: Even without understanding the underlying biology, cultivators had developed practical methods that reliably produced mushrooms — a testament to the power of careful observation and experimentation.
4. **Continuity of practice**: The methods described by Tournefort likely represented the culmination of generations of practical knowledge passed down through French gardening traditions.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Historical origins of cultivation**: Tournefort's account establishes that organized mushroom cultivation in France predates 1707, with the species having been considered a luxury a century or two earlier according to Duggar's research.
- **Pre-cave era methods**: The absence of cave cultivation references indicates that early French mushroom growing was conducted above ground, likely in prepared beds or sheltered locations similar to those still used in some English operations during Duggar's time.
- **Empirical knowledge**: The fact that successful cultivation was achieved without understanding the organism's biology demonstrates that empirical observation of environmental conditions (temperature, moisture, substrate) was sufficient for practical success.
- **Foundation for French industry**: These early methods eventually evolved into the massive Parisian cave-based mushroom industry that by the early 1900s produced over 5 million kilograms annually through the Halles Centrales market alone.
- **Spawn technology evolution**: The account provides context for understanding how spawn technology evolved from early empirical methods to the pure culture techniques developed around 1900 in both France and the United States.
- **Variety development lag**: The historical record shows that no progress was made in maintaining or developing varieties until approximately 1900, despite centuries of cultivation. This remarkable gap illustrates how critical pure culture methodology was for advancing mushroom genetics.
