# Lepista nuda (Wood Blewit)

**Lepista nuda**, commonly known as the **wood blewit** or **blue stalk mushroom**, is an edible species of agaric fungus in the family **Tricholomataceae** (sometimes placed in **Lyophyllaceae**). It is widely distributed across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere and is one of the most recognizable late-season [[palmer-edible-mushrooms-north-america]], appreciated for its distinctive bluish-violet coloration and pleasant aroma.

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Tricholomataceae (or Lyophyllaceae)
- **Genus:** *Lepista*
- **Species:** *L. nuda*

The species was originally described as **Agaricus nudus** by the Swedish mycologist **Elias Magnus Fries** in 1818. It was later transferred to the genus *Lepista* by **Mordecai Cubitt Cooke** in 1871. The genus name derives from the Greek *lepistes*, meaning "wine vessel," referring to the purplish color. The epithet *nuda* means "naked" or "bare," referring to the smooth, hairless cap surface.

Some authorities place this species in the genus **Clitocybe** as *Clitocybe nuda*, reflecting ongoing taxonomic revision within the Tricholomataceae complex.

## Description

### Cap
- **Diameter:** 4–15 cm
- **Shape:** Convex when young, expanding to broadly convex or nearly flat with age, sometimes developing a shallow central depression
- **Color:** Distinctive violet-blue to lilac when fresh, fading to brownish or tan with age; often with a wavy, inrolled margin
- **Surface:** Smooth, bare (nuda), slightly tacky when moist; may develop radial wrinkles

### Gills
- **Attachment:** Adnexed to slightly decurrent
- **Color:** Pale lilac to pinkish when young, becoming buff to pale tan with age
- **Spacing:** Crowded, with many short gills (lamellulae)
- **Edge:** Even, sometimes slightly serrate

### Stipe (Stem)
- **Dimensions:** 4–10 cm tall × 1–3 cm thick
- **Shape:** Cylindrical to slightly clavate, solid
- **Color:** Bluish-violet when young, fading to pale lilac or brownish with age
- **Surface:** Smooth to finely fibrillose, often with a slightly swollen base
- **Ring:** Absent

### Flesh
- **Color:** Pale lilac to whitish
- **Texture:** Firm, thick
- **Odor:** Distinctive, pleasant, fruity or perfumed — often described as reminiscent of frozen orange juice concentrate or violet flowers
- **Taste:** Mild, slightly nutty

### Spore Print
- **Color:** Pale pink to salmon-pink
- **Spore dimensions:** 6–8 × 3.5–5 µm, elliptical, smooth, slightly thick-walled

## Habitat and Distribution

*Lepista nuda* is a **saprotrophic** species, decomposing leaf litter and other organic matter in:

- **Deciduous and mixed woodlands**, particularly under oak, beech, and birch
- **Coniferous forests**, especially with spruce and pine
- **Leaf litter piles**, compost heaps, and garden mulch in urban settings
- **Grasslands** and hedgerows adjacent to wooded areas

It is found across **Europe, [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], Asia, and parts of Australasia**. Fruiting occurs in **autumn through early winter** (September to January in the Northern Hemisphere), often persisting through light frosts. It is one of the few edible mushrooms that continues fruiting well into cold weather.

## Culinary Value

The wood blewit is considered a **good edible mushroom** when properly cooked:

- **Must be cooked thoroughly** — raw or undercooked specimens can cause gastrointestinal distress in some individuals
- **Flavor profile:** Mild, nutty, and slightly sweet with a pleasant aromatic quality
- **Texture:** Firm and meaty, holds up well in cooking
- **Preparation methods:** Sautéing, braising, soups, stews, and drying for later use
- **Cautions:** Some people experience allergic reactions; always try a small portion first
- **Not recommended raw** — contains thermolabile irritants

## Similar Species and Look-Alikes

Accurate identification is critical. Species that could be confused with *L. nuda* include:

| Species | Distinguishing Features |
|---------|----------------------|
| ***Lepista sordida*** | Smaller, more fragile; found in grasslands; cap often more brown |
| ***Clitocybe nebularis*** (Clouded Agaric) | Larger, gray, no blue coloration; has a strong farinaceous odor |
| ***Cortinarius* spp.** | Many have violet hues; distinguished by rusty-brown spore prints and cobweb-like partial veil |
| ***Inocybe* spp.** | Some species have lilac tones; many are toxic with brown spore prints and fibrous caps |
| ***Entoloma* spp.** | Some have bluish colors; distinguished by pink angular spores; many are toxic |

The **pink spore print** is the most important diagnostic feature separating *L. nuda* from toxic *Cortinarius* and *Inocybe* species, which have rusty-brown spore prints.

## Cultivation

*Lepista nuda* can be cultivated but is less commonly grown commercially than species like *Pleurotus ostreatus* or *[[genetic-improvement-agaricus-bisporus-molecular-approaches]]*:

- **Substrate:** Composted straw, leaf litter, or [[gourmet-mushroom-enriched-sawdust-cultivation]]
- **Temperature:** Prefers cool temperatures (12–18°C / 54–64°F)
- **Fruiting:** Typically 2–4 months after inoculation
- **Yield:** Moderate, but reliable once established
- **Outdoor beds:** Can be established in shaded garden areas with leaf litter mulch

## Chemical Constituents

Research has identified several [[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]] in *L. nuda*:

- **Lepista nuda lectins** — carbohydrate-binding proteins with demonstrated antimicrobial and antiproliferative properties
- **β-glucans** — immunomodulatory polysaccharides
- **Antioxidant compounds** including phenolic acids and flavonoids
- **Lovastatin** — a statin compound found in some concentrations

## Ecological Role

As a primary decomposer of leaf litter, *L. nuda* plays a significant role in:

- **[[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]]** in temperate forest ecosystems
- **Breaking down cellulose and lignin** in fallen leaves
- **Supporting soil microbiome diversity** through competitive interactions
- **Serving as a food source** for various invertebrates and small mammals
