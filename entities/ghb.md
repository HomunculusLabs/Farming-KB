## Overview

GHB (gamma-hydroxybutyric acid) is a naturally occurring neurotransmitter and psychoactive substance. It is a structural analog of GABA, the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the mammalian central nervous system.

Synthetic GHB is used recreationally for its euphoric, prosocial, and sedative effects. It also sees limited medical use as sodium oxybate (Xyrem) for treating narcolepsy with cataplexy.

First synthesized in 1874 by Alexander Zaytsev, GHB remained pharmacologically uncharacterized until Henri Laborit investigated it in the 1960s as a GABA analog. It was briefly sold as a dietary supplement before scheduling in most jurisdictions.

The substance has been called "liquid ecstasy" and "G" in recreational contexts. It occupies a unique pharmacological niche, combining stimulant-like effects at low doses with potent sedation at higher doses.

GHB is found naturally in small quantities in wine, beef, citrus fruits, and nearly all living organisms. [[blesching-cannabis-sleep-disorders]] is well-established in clinical trials.

## Chemical Properties

GHB has the molecular formula C₄H₈O₃ and a molecular weight of 104.10 g/mol. Its IUPAC name is 4-hydroxybutanoic acid.

The compound exists as a colorless, odorless, slightly viscous liquid in its free acid form. The sodium salt (sodium oxybate) appears as a white crystalline powder soluble in water.

GHB is endogenously produced from GABA via succinic semialdehyde. It also occurs as a minor metabolite of 1,4-butanediol (BDO) and gamma-butyrolactone ([[gbl]]).

The substance has a pKa of approximately 4.7, making it predominantly ionized at physiological pH. This property affects its absorption, distribution, and blood-brain barrier penetration.

Illicit GHB is often produced by hydrolyzing GBL with sodium hydroxide in a simple kitchen-chemistry reaction. The resulting product's purity and concentration vary widely, contributing to dosing unpredictability.

## Pharmacology

GHB acts on at least two distinct receptor systems in the brain. It binds to specific high-affinity GHB receptors and also activates GABA-B receptors at higher concentrations.

At low doses (10–30 mg/kg), GHB receptor agonism produces mild stimulant and euphoriant effects. At higher doses (30–60 mg/kg), GABA-B receptor activation predominates, producing sedation, muscle relaxation, and eventually unconsciousness.

The biphasic dose-response curve distinguishes GHB from most other sedatives. This stimulant-to-sedative transition explains the narrow therapeutic window that contributes to overdose risk.

Endogenous GHB concentrations in human blood typically range from 0.5 to 4.0 mg/L. Forensic thresholds for GHB-positive samples are generally set above 10 mg/L in blood and 20 mg/L in urine.

Metabolism occurs primarily via GHB dehydrogenase, converting GHB to succinic semialdehyde, then to succinic acid, which enters the Krebs cycle. The elimination half-life is 30–60 minutes.

The rapid metabolism makes forensic detection challenging. Blood samples must be collected within 8–12 hours of ingestion for reliable quantification.

A minor pathway converts GHB to GABA directly via GABA-transaminase. This retrograde conversion may contribute to some of the compound's prolonged neurological effects.

## Effects

Recreational doses (1–3 grams orally) typically produce effects within 15–30 minutes. Initial effects include mild euphoria, increased sociability, and enhanced tactile sensitivity.

Peak effects last 1.5–3 hours depending on dose. Higher doses produce significant sedation, motor impairment, and ataxia. Overdose results in deep sleep, bradycardia, hypotension, and potentially fatal respiratory depression.

The steep dose-response curve means the difference between a recreational dose and overdose can be as little as 1–2 grams. Combining GHB with alcohol or other depressants dramatically increases overdose risk.

Withdrawal from chronic heavy GHB use can be severe and life-threatening. Symptoms include anxiety, tremor, insomnia, delirium, and autonomic instability resembling alcohol withdrawal.

Tolerance develops rapidly with regular use. Some chronic users report dosing every 2–4 hours around the clock to avoid withdrawal, a pattern that significantly increases overdose and dependence risk.

GHB produces characteristic anterograde amnesia at sedative doses. This property, combined with its colorless and odorless nature, has made it notorious as a drug-facilitated assault agent.

Repeated dosing at short intervals is particularly dangerous. Because GHB effects are short-lived, users may re-dose before the initial dose has fully cleared, leading to staggered overdose.

Emergency department presentations for GHB toxicity often involve coma with preserved brainstem reflexes. Patients typically regain consciousness spontaneously within 2–6 hours without specific antidotal therapy.

## History

Alexander Zaytsev first synthesized GHB in 1874. Henri Laborit began studying its pharmacology in the early 1960s while researching GABA analogs for anesthetic applications.

During the 1980s, GHB gained popularity among bodybuilders who believed it stimulated growth hormone release. It was also marketed as a dietary supplement for sleep enhancement and weight loss.

The U.S. DEA placed GHB in Schedule I of the [[medicinal-mushroom-dietary-supplements]]. Legislation eventually closed these precursor loopholes in most countries.
