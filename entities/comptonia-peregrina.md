## Comptonia peregrina

## Overview

**Comptonia peregrina** (sweetfern) is a monotypic species within the Myricaceae, native to [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]], and role in early-successional plant communities.

The plant typically reaches 0.3–1.2 m in height and spreads aggressively by rhizomes to form extensive colonies. Its aromatic foliage releases a sweet, resinous fragrance when crushed, due to volatile oils including myrcene and limonene. The species is dioecious and produces small, inconspicuous catkins followed by burr-like fruits. Sweetfern is widely recognized as an indicator species of acidic, nutrient-poor sandy soils and is often among the first woody plants to colonize disturbed ground in its native range. Its combination of ornamental, ecological, and practical values has sustained interest in the species across several centuries of North American botanical exploration.

*Comptonia* is a monotypic genus, containing only this single species. The genus name honors Rev. Henry Compton (1632–1713), Bishop of London and a noted botanical patron. The specific epithet *peregrina* means "wandering" or "foreign," possibly referencing the plant's wide-ranging distribution or its superficial resemblance to unrelated fern groups. Molecular phylogenetic studies place *Comptonia* as sister to *Myrica* sensu stricto within Myricaceae.

The fossil record of *Comptonia* extends back to the Eocene, with leaf impressions found across North America and Europe. These fossils indicate that the genus was once much more widely distributed, becoming restricted to eastern North America during Pleistocene glaciations. The monotypic status of the genus likely reflects extinction of related species rather than an ancient evolutionary isolation.

Chromosome number is 2n = 32, consistent with the base number for the family. The species exhibits moderate genetic variation across its range, with northern populations showing slightly higher frost tolerance than southern ones. Pollen morphology is tricolporate, typical of wind-pollinated Fagales.

Flowering occurs from March to May, with catkins expanding before or simultaneously with leaf emergence. Male plants produce abundant pollen that is dispersed by wind over distances of several hundred meters.

## Morphology

Sweetfern is a low, spreading shrub with multiple stems arising from an extensive rhizome system. Stems are slender, brown, and covered with small, resinous glandular dots. The bark is smooth on young shoots and becomes slightly fissured on older stems.

Leaves are alternate, simple, 5–15 cm long and 2–5 cm wide, deeply pinnately lobed with 6–12 pairs of irregular lobes. The lobes are toothed and somewhat resemble fern pinnae, hence the common name. The upper leaf surface is dark green and sparsely glandular; the lower surface is paler and densely covered with resinous dots. Foliage is deciduous, turning yellow-brown in autumn.

When crushed, the foliage emits a strong, sweet, balsamic fragrance. The essential oil contains myrcene, limonene, α-pinene, and various other terpenoids. This aromatic quality makes the leaves unpalatable to most mammalian herbivores.
