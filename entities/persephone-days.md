# Persephone Days

## Description

The Persephone days (or [[persephone-period]]) refers to the weeks during winter when day length drops below ten hours, causing a significant slowdown in plant growth. The term was coined by [[eliot-coleman]], drawing on the Greek myth of Persephone — daughter of Demeter who was required to spend winter months in the underworld — as a poetic framing for the biological reality that limited winter sunlight constrains vegetable production even under protected cultivation.

## Classification

- **Category**: Agricultural concept / photoperiod management
- **Type**: Seasonal growing benchmark
- **Origin**: Named by Eliot Coleman after the Greek myth of Persephone
- **Threshold**: Day length below 10 hours

## Key Facts

### The Ten-Hour Threshold

At Coleman's Maine farm (44.33N, 68.58W), the period when day length is shorter than ten hours runs from approximately November 5 to February 5. This is the period during which overall light energy available diminishes to the extent that it significantly affects the rate of plant growth.

### Latitude-Dependent Timing

The Persephone period varies significantly by latitude:
- **44th parallel** (Maine, Avignon France, Genoa Italy): ~November 5 to February 5
- **39th parallel** (Washington D.C.): ~November 17 to January 24
- **35th parallel** (Charlotte, NC): ~December 1 to January 10
- **33rd parallel** (Atlanta, GA): ~December 7 to January 5
- **32nd parallel** (Savannah, GA to El Paso, TX): Shortest day never drops below 10 hours
- **49th parallel** (U.S./Canada border): Extended Persephone period
- **51st parallel** (London, England): ~October 27 to February 16 (per W. J. C. Lawrence)
- **61st parallel** (Anchorage, AK): ~October 16 to February 24

### The Greek Myth

The concept draws on the myth where Hades abducted Persephone to live with him in the netherworld. Demeter, the earth goddess and Persephone's mother, threatened to shut down all plant growth. Zeus brokered a deal whereby Persephone would spend only the winter months with Hades, and Demeter, saddened by her daughter's absence, made the earth barren during that time.

### Practical Implications

The Persephone period has critical implications for winter harvest planning:

1. **Planting deadline**: Crops must reach a certain minimum size before day length drops below the ten-hour mark. Even true winter annuals like mache, claytonia, and spinach are slow to establish if sown during short days.

2. **Harvest vs. growth**: During the Persephone months, growers are not extending the growing season but rather extending the harvest season. Plants are semi-hibernating, waiting to be harvested.

3. **Days-to-harvest expansion**: A crop that takes 40 days to mature for a September or June harvest requires 80 days for a midwinter harvest in a cool house and 120 days in a cold house.

4. **[[succession-planting]]**: The cutoff point for new sowings is approximately the end of October. Seeds germinate after this date but grow very slowly until the ten-hour day returns in February.

## Relevance to Cultivation

### Planting Date Calculation

The Persephone concept drives the biological imperative behind calculating precise planting dates for fall and winter crops. Growers must work backward from desired harvest dates, accounting for the increased days-to-maturity during short-day periods.

### Cut-and-Come-Again Crops

Perennial and [[cut-and-come-again]] winter crops like spinach, chard, claytonia, and tatsoi continue productive regrowth throughout the Persephone months as long as they have well-established root systems. The established root system is key — growth above ground slows but the plants remain harvestable.

### Connection to the Celtic Calendar

Coleman noted that the end of the Persephone period aligns closely with Imbolc (February 1-2), the Celtic cross-quarter festival celebrating the growing of the light. February 1 is when more rapid spring growth begins in the greenhouses as the Persephone period draws to a close.

### Scientific Confirmation

W. J. C. Lawrence, the eminent British greenhouse researcher, confirmed the ten-hour threshold from his greenhouses at Merton near London on the 51st parallel. Reporting in *Science and the Glasshouse* (1948), Lawrence documented that plant growth slowed during the period from October 27 to February 16 when days are shorter than ten hours.

## Relationship to Other Concepts

- **Ten-hour day**: The specific day-length threshold defining the Persephone period
- **Succession planting**: Timing plantings to ensure harvestable size before Persephone days
- **Cold house / cool house**: Protected environments that moderate temperature but cannot compensate for light
- **Imbolc**: Celtic festival marking the approximate end of the Persephone period
- **Day length and latitude**: Geographic factors determining the length of the Persephone period
