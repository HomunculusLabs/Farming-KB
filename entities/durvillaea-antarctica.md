The species is remarkable for its buoyant, honeycomb-filled fronds and its role in long-distance rafting dispersal. It is culturally significant as a traditional food source for Māori and Mapuche peoples and is commercially harvested in Chile.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Chromista
- **Phylum:** Ochrophyta
- **Class:** Phaeophyceae
- **Order:** Fucales
- **Family:** Lessoniaceae
- **Genus:** Durvillaea
- **Species:** D. antarctica (Chamisso) Hariot

Fronds are thick, leathery, and can exceed 10 m in length. The holdfast is large, conical, and firmly attached to rocks in the high-intertidal and splash zones. The stipe is short and thick, merging into a broad, dichotomously branched blade.

A distinctive feature is the internal honeycomb structure of gas-filled vesicles (aerenchyma) within the medulla. This air-filled tissue provides exceptional buoyancy, allowing detached fronds to float at the sea surface for extended periods.

The thallus is dark olive-brown to nearly black when wet. Surface texture is smooth and rubbery, becoming stiff when dried. Growth occurs from a single apical cell at the blade tip, producing the characteristic dichotomous branching pattern.

## Distribution and Habitat

D. antarctica is distributed across the Southern Ocean from approximately 30°S to 56°S. It occurs along the entire coastline of Chile, throughout New Zealand (including the Chatham and subantarctic islands), the Falkland Islands, South Georgia, and the Kerguelen Islands.

The species occupies the high-intertidal to shallow subtidal zone, often in extremely exposed locations. It is a dominant component of the "bull kelp zone" — the uppermost band of large brown algae on wave-exposed rocky shores.

D. antarctica is exceptionally tolerant of desiccation and wave stress. Its flexible, rubbery thallus can withstand repeated pounding by heavy surf, and the honeycomb internal structure cushions mechanical impacts.

## Chemistry and Bioactivity

D. antarctica contains significant quantities of fucoidan, a sulfated polysaccharide with reported anticoagulant, antiviral, and immunomodulatory activities. The fucoidan from D. antarctica has a distinctive composition with high sulfate content relative to other brown algae.

The species also accumulates iodine, bromine, and various [[kava-traditional-preparation-ceremonial-context]] involves beating and drying the fronds, which can be stored for extended periods.

The species is investigated as a source of fucoidan for nutraceutical and pharmaceutical applications. Alginate extraction has been explored but is not commercially competitive with Macrocystis-derived alginate. Its rapid growth and accessibility make it attractive for integrated multi-trophic aquaculture systems.
