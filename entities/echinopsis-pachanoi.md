## Echinopsis pachanoi

**Echinopsis pachanoi**, commonly known as **San Pedro**, is a fast-growing columnar cactus native to the Andes mountains of Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, and northern Chile. It has been used as a sacramental entheogen for at least 3,000 years in Andean traditional medicine and shamanic practice. The cactus contains a complex profile of phenethylamine alkaloids, with **mescaline** as the primary psychoactive compound. San Pedro is one of the most culturally significant psychoactive plants in the Americas, with a living tradition of ceremonial use that predates the Inca Empire and continues unbroken to the present day.

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

- **Family:** Cactaceae (the cactus family, with approximately 1,400–2,000 species in 100–200 genera)
- **Subfamily:** Cactoideae
- **Tribe:** Trichocereeae
- **Genus:** Echinopsis (formerly *Trichocereus*; the genus was expanded to include Trichocereus following molecular phylogenetic studies by Ritz et al. in 2007)
- **Distribution:** Andean cordillera from approximately 2,000 to 3,000 m elevation in Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, and northern Chile. Widely cultivated and naturalized in other regions including the Canary Islands, the Mediterranean, East Africa, parts of the [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]]). Flowers are pollinated by bats and large moths.
- **Fruit:** Ovoid, green to yellowish, 5–6 cm long, edible, containing numerous small black seeds. The fruit is mildly sweet and eaten locally.

The species is characterized by its rapid growth rate—up to 30–60 cm per year under optimal conditions. Mature specimens produce lateral branches (pups) that can be removed and rooted as new plants. Individual specimens can live for decades or longer. The root system is relatively shallow and spreading, adapted to the well-drained volcanic soils of the Andean slopes.

## Phytochemistry

The primary psychoactive alkaloid in E. pachanoi is **mescaline** (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine), typically found at concentrations of 0.1–1.0% by dry weight in the green outer tissue of the stem. The full alkaloid profile includes numerous related phenethylamines:

- Mescaline (primary active compound; target concentration for psychoactive use)
- 3-Methoxytyramine (possible precursor in the biosynthetic pathway; may have cardiovascular effects)
- Tyramine (trace amine with cardiovascular effects at higher doses)
- Hordenine (also found in barley sprouts; weak MAOI activity)
- Anhalaninine (tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloid; pharmacological activity not fully characterized)
- Anhalonidine (tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloid; pharmacological activity not fully characterized)
- 3,4-Dimethoxyphenethylamine (DMPEA; originally thought to be the active principle of peyote, later shown to be inactive)
- 3,5-Dimethoxy-4-hydroxyphenethylamine (structurally related to mescaline with one methoxy group replaced by hydroxyl)

Total alkaloid content varies significantly with age, growing conditions, genetics, and the specific tissue analyzed. The green cortical layer just beneath the epidermis is substantially richer in alkaloids than the white inner pith. Older, more established specimens tend to have higher alkaloid concentrations than young, rapidly growing plants. Environmental stressors including UV exposure, drought, and nutrient limitation may increase alkaloid production as a defensive response. Different populations (clones) of cultivated E. pachanoi can differ dramatically in their alkaloid content, leading some enthusiasts to select specifically for higher-potency genetics.

## Historical and Ethnobotanical Use

San Pedro is one of the oldest known psychoactive plants in the Americas. Archaeological evidence from the Chavín de Huántar culture (circa 900–200 BCE) in Peru includes stone carvings (the Lanzón monolith, the Tello Obelisk) depicting the cactus and anthropomorphic figures holding cactus staffs. Ceramic effigies depicting the cactus have been found at numerous pre-Columbian sites along the north coast of Peru, including the Moche, Lambayeque, and Chimú cultures. These artifacts demonstrate a continuous tradition of ceremonial cactus use spanning at least 3,000 years.

In the Andean worldview, San Pedro is considered a sacred being—a teacher and healer with its own consciousness and personality. The cactus is personified as *Abuelo San Pedro* (Grandfather San Pedro) and is addressed with prayers and offerings in traditional ceremonies. The relationship between the curandero and the cactus is understood as one of mutual respect and reciprocity.

[[entheogen-cimora-folk-healing-ceremony]]), San Pedro ceremonies (mesadas) are conducted by curanderos for purposes including:

- Physical healing (treatment of various ailments, especially those attributed to spiritual causes such as *susto* or *mal aire*)
- Divination and diagnosis of illness through the visionary states induced by the cactus
- Spiritual cleansing and energetic protection (*limpia*)
- Community cohesion and collective celebration
- Connection with nature and the sacred landscape (*apus*, the mountain spirits; *pachamama*, the earth mother)

The ceremony typically involves the consumption of the cactus brew, often accompanied by music (harp, violin, percussion), prayer, and ritual objects arranged on a *mesa* (ceremonial altar). The experience is described as gentle, heart-opening, and connective, with strong visual and emotional components.

## Cultivation

E. pachanoi is among the easiest and fastest-growing columnar cacti. It is widely cultivated by ethnobotanical enthusiasts, spiritual practitioners, cactus collectors, and commercial nurseries. [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].
- **Seed** (less common): Seeds germinate readily in warm, moist conditions but seedlings are slow-growing for the first year or two. Seed-grown plants exhibit greater genetic diversity than clones.

The cactus tolerates a wide range of conditions but prefers full sun to partial shade, moderate water during the growing season (with dry rest periods in winter), and protection from hard frost (can tolerate brief exposure to -5°C but sustained freezing is fatal). Well-draining, slightly acidic to neutral soil is ideal. Fertilization during the growing season promotes vigorous growth.
