# Buffalofish (Ictiobus spp.)

## Description

Buffalofish are large freshwater fish of the sucker family (Catostomidae), native to North America. In permaculture aquaculture, they are significant as a polyculture partner for common carp, where their complementary feeding habits produce dramatic yield increases compared to either species alone.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Animalia
- **Phylum**: Chordata
- **Class**: Actinopterygii
- **Order**: Cypriniformes
- **Family**: Catostomidae (suckers)
- **Genus**: Ictiobus
- **Common species**: I. cyprinellus (bigmouth buffalo), I. niger (black buffalo), I. bubalus (smallmouth buffalo)

## Key Facts

- Native to the Mississippi River basin and Gulf Coast drainages of North America
- Bottom-feeding fish consuming detritus, benthic invertebrates, algae, and zooplankton
- Can grow very large -- adults commonly reach 5-15 kg, with specimens exceeding 35 kg
- Long-lived species; some individuals may survive 100+ years
- Market size typically around 250 g minimum in aquaculture, averaging 590-636 g at moderate stocking rates

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Mollison uses buffalofish to demonstrate one of the most dramatic examples of polyculture synergy in aquaculture:

### Polyculture with Common Carp
- Common carp alone (2,500/ha): 314 kg/ha
- Buffalofish alone (2,500/ha): 486 kg/ha (approximate)
- Buffalofish (2,500) plus carp (250/ha): 925 kg/ha -- a **300% increase** over carp alone

This remarkable synergy arises because buffalofish and carp occupy different feeding niches despite both being bottom-oriented species, allowing efficient partitioning of food resources.

### Stocking Rate Response
Stocking rate data (marketable size defined as approximately 250 g average):
- 300/ha: 152 kg, averaging 636 g/fish
- 600/ha: 273.5 kg, averaging 603 g/fish
- 1,080/ha: 656.5 kg, averaging 590 g/fish

Unlike Tilapia, buffalofish had not yet reached an excessive stocking rate at the highest density tested, suggesting potential for even higher yields at greater densities.

### Design Implications
The buffalofish-carp polyculture demonstrates Mollison's principle that the most important reason for adding species to a pond system is **utilisation of different foods**. By selecting fish with non-overlapping feeding preferences, total pond productivity can multiply far beyond monoculture potential.

## Cultivation Notes

- Tolerant of warm, turbid water conditions
- Can be grown in clay-based ponds common in [[permaculture-design]]
- Compatible with mussels and aquatic plants for water quality management
- May require supplemental feeding at high stocking densities
- Less commonly cultured commercially than carp or catfish, but highly productive in polyculture

Effective buffalofish requires attention to site-specific
factors including climate, water availability, and local
regulations. Integration with existing water management

## Ecological Significance

Recent research has revealed that bigmouth buffalo (Ictiobus cyprinellus) can live for over 100 years, making them one of the longest-lived freshwater fish species. This extreme longevity has conservation implications, as populations may be slow to recover from overharvest. In permaculture systems, this longevity translates to long-term stability -- once established, a buffalofish population can persist for decades with minimal intervention.

Buffalofish are also remarkable for their ability to filter feed on zooplankton in addition to their benthic foraging, giving them access to food resources unavailable to purely bottom-feeding species. This dual feeding strategy may partly explain the extraordinary polyculture synergy observed with carp.

## Design for the Mosaic Landscape

In Mollison's integrated landscape approach, buffalofish fit naturally into the deeper open-water component of the pond mosaic. Their large adult size and preference for deeper water complements the shallow-water species like Tilapia and the edge-dwelling plants, creating a three-dimensional polyculture that utilises food resources at every depth level of the pond.
