## Description

The Jivaro (also Jívaro) are an indigenous people of the
Ecuadorian Upper Amazon rain forest, renowned in
anthropological literature for their head-shrinking practice
(tsantsa) and their intense shamanistic traditions. They are
among the most extensively studied Amazonian groups regarding
hallucinogen use.

Related sub-groups include the Achuara (Achuar), with whom
Harner also conducted field research.

## Classification

- **Language family:** Jivaroan
- **Location:** Eastern Ecuadorian Amazon
- **Related groups:** Achuara, Shuar, Huambisa, Aguaruna
- **Subsistence:** Horticulture (manioc, plantains), hunting, fishing

## Key Facts

### Reality View

The Jivaro maintain a radical dual-reality cosmology in which
the normal waking life is considered a "lie" or illusion. The
true forces determining daily events are supernatural and can
only be seen and manipulated with the aid of hallucinogenic
drugs. This worldview creates intense demand for shamans who
can cross into the supernatural world at will.

### Natemä (Hallucinogenic Brew)

The Jivaro name for their Banisteriopsis preparation is
*natemä* (also rendered *[[natema]]*). It is brewed primarily from
*[[banisteriopsis-caapi]]* vine segments boiled with leaves of a
similar vine, likely another *Banisteriopsis* species. The
brew contains [[harmaline]], harmine, d-tetrahydroharmine, and
possibly N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT).

### Shamanism

Approximately one out of every four Jivaro men is a shaman—an
extraordinarily high proportion, directly attributable to the
accessibility of the hallucinogenic natemä. Women rarely become
shamans but are considered especially powerful when they do,
believed to possess special *tsentsak* (spirit helpers).

Two categories of shaman exist:
- **Bewitching shamans** — send spirit darts into victims
- **Curing shamans** — extract enemy darts from patients

### Tsentsak (Spirit Helpers/Darts)

The central supernatural forces in Jivaro shamanism are
*tsentsak*, tiny spirit helpers that function as both weapons
and shields. They are:
- Normally invisible to non-shamans
- Perceivable only under the influence of natemä
- Transferable from master shaman to apprentice
- Regurgitated by shamans in a brilliant substance
- Capable of causing illness, death, or protection

### Training

To become a shaman, an adult (male or female) presents a gift
to an established practitioner. The master administers the
Banisteriopsis drink and transfers some of his own tsentsak
to the apprentice. The hallucinogen makes it possible for
virtually anyone to achieve the trance state required for
shamanic practice.

### Social Structure

The Jivaro are described as individualistic and feuding,
preferring to take natemä individually rather than communally
(contrasting with the Cashinahua). They use the hallucinogen
primarily to cope supernaturally with enemies.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The Jivaro demonstrate how a hallucinogenic plant preparation
can fundamentally shape a society's epistemology, medical
system, and social structure. Their high shaman-to-population
ratio (~1:4 adult males) is directly linked to the availability
of *Banisteriopsis caapi*. Their practice illustrates the
"inside view" of reality perceived through hallucinogens—
informing broader discussions of psychedelic phenomenology
and the anthropology of consciousness.
