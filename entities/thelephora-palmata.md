## Overview

**Thelephora palmata** (commonly known as the **stinking earthfan**) is a species
of basidiomycete fungus in the family Thelephoraceae. It is an ectomycorrhizal
symbiont primarily associated with coniferous trees, particularly species of
*Pinus* and *Picea*.

The species is notable for its strong, unpleasant odor resembling fetid
radishes or gas, which is detectable at some distance from fresh basidiocarps.
Despite its off-putting smell, it is not toxic and plays an important
ecological role in boreal and temperate forest [[comparison-fruiting-body-vs-mycelium-extracts-medicinal-mushrooms]] is fan-shaped to spatulate, 2-8 cm across,
arising from a short lateral or eccentric stipe. The upper (sterile) surface is
finely tomentose to pubescent, radially fibrillose, and brown to dark
grayish-brown in color. Margins are often lobed and irregularly crenate.

**Hymenium:** The fertile undersurface is paler than the upper surface, ranging
from grayish to smoky brown. It is smooth to finely wrinkled or tuberculate in
age, but lacks the prominent teeth or pores seen in other Thelephorales genera.

**Flesh:** The context is thin, tough, and leathery-coriaceous. It is
non-edible due to texture rather than toxicity. The characteristic fetid odor
is most pronounced in fresh specimens and diminishes as the basidiocarp dries.

**Spores:** Basidiospores are verrucose-echinulate, broadly ellipsoid to
subglobose, measuring 7-10 × 6-8 μm. Spore ornamentation consists of prominent
conical warts connected by fine ridges, a characteristic feature of the genus.
[[spore-print]] is ochre-brown.

## Distribution and Habitat

*T. palmata* has a wide circumboreal distribution across Europe, Asia, and
[[dighton-pine-needle-litter-decomposition-fungal-succession]] and moss, typically from
late summer through autumn. In North America, it ranges from the boreal zone
south through montane coniferous forests.

## Ecology and Mycorrhizal Symbiosis

*Thelephora palmata* is an obligate ectomycorrhizal fungus. It forms mantle
and Hartig net structures on the fine roots of host trees, predominantly
Pinaceae. The symbiosis enhances host phosphorus and [[fungal-bacterial-predators-nitrogen-acquisition-soil-ecology]] while
the fungus receives carbon from photosynthesis.

The genus *Thelephora* is one of the most commonly encountered ectomycorrhizal
genera in forest nursery studies. *T. terrestris* is frequently documented as a
pioneer colonizer of seedling root systems, and *T. palmata* occupies a similar
though less cosmopolitan niche in natural forest regeneration.

## Chemical and Bioactive Properties

Members of the Thelephorales produce characteristic polyphenolic pigments,
including thelephoric acid and various terphenyl quinones. These compounds
contribute to the dark pigmentation of basidiocarps and possess documented
antimicrobial and antioxidant activities.

The fetid odor of *T. palmata* is attributed to volatile sulfur compounds and
other low-molecular-weight organics. While not formally characterized for this
species, related *Thelephora* species emit methyl mercaptan and dimethyl
disulfide derivatives responsible for their distinctive scent.
