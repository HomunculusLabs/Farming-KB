# Kamarampi

## Description

Kamarampi (also spelled kamdrampi) is the Campa (Asháninka) name for ayahuasca, derived from the Quechua word meaning "to vomit." The name reflects the brew's extremely bitter and sometimes emetic qualities. Kamarampi is the central sacrament in Campa shamanic ceremonies in the Peruvian Amazon.

## Classification

- **Type:** Hallucinogenic plant brew ([[ayahuasca-preparation]])
- **Linguistic origin:** Quechua, meaning "to vomit"
- **Cultural context:** Campa (Asháninka) people, Peruvian Amazon
- **Botanical components:** [[banisteriopsis-caapi]] vine + [[psychotria-viridis]] leaves

## Botanical Components

The brew is prepared from two primary plant ingredients:

- **Kamdriampi (ayahuasca vine):** Banisteriopsis caapi, which the Campa find growing wild and transplant to the vicinity of their settlements
- **Horeva:** Psychotria viridis, leaves from an uncultivated tree that provides the DMT-containing admixture

The combination of a beta-carboline-containing vine with a DMT-containing plant follows the classic ayahuasca preparation formula found throughout the Amazon basin.

## Ceremony Structure

The kamarampi ceremony follows a distinctive structure:

1. **Gathering:** At nightfall, participants convene sitting or lying on mats in the settlement clearing or under a house roof
2. **Separation:** Women and men sit apart in Campa fashion
3. **Distribution:** The shaman drinks first using a small gourd bowl, then gives each participant a drink
4. **Repeated dosing:** The drinking procedure is repeated at intervals until the supply is consumed
5. **Onset:** About half an hour after the first dose, the drug takes effect
6. **Singing:** The shaman begins singing one song after another as long as he is under the influence, potentially lasting until dawn

## Shamanic Performance

The ceremony has a distinctive quality characterized by:

- **Eerie, distant voice:** The shaman's singing has an unearthly quality; his jaw may quiver and his clothing may vibrate
- **Spirit visitation:** Good spirits come in human form, festively attired, singing and dancing before the assembled mortals
- **Shaman as medium:** Only the shaman perceives the spirits clearly; he sings by repeating what he hears the spirits sing
- **No possession:** Unlike some traditions, the Campa shaman is never possessed by spirits — he merely accompanies them

## Key Facts

- The ceremony exhibits an ambivalent quality, simultaneously functioning as a shamanistic seance and an act of worship
- Soul-flight by the shaman is an optional element — some shamans move away from the group and pretend to disappear bodily
- Songs extol the excellence and bounty of good spirits, marking the appearance of specific spirit beings like the hawk Kodkiti and hummingbird spirits
- The atmosphere is one of decorum without frenzy
- Communication flows both from spirits to mortals (through the shaman) and from mortals to spirits (through praise)

## Regional Variation

In one part of Campa territory, a special local variation exists where:

- Men take turns singing rather than only the shaman performing
- The shaman becomes director but not sole virtuoso
- Men and women separately and together dance and sing in praise of good spirits
- The communication becomes unambiguously from mortals to immortals (worship) rather than the reverse

This variation may reflect some missionary influence, but remains within the framework of the basic Campa kamarampi ceremony.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Ethnobotany

The Campa practice of transplanting wild Banisteriopsis caapi vines near their settlements represents a form of proto-cultivation that demonstrates practical ethnobotanical management. The identification and consistent use of Psychotria viridis as the admixture plant shows sophisticated knowledge of plant synergy across the Amazon.

## Theoretical Significance

The kamarampi ceremony is theoretically important because it may represent the behavioral link between generalized shamans and specialized priests. The ambivalent quality of the ceremony — simultaneously seance and worship — suggests that a slight shift in interpretation could transform shamanistic ritual into priestly ritual, potentially explaining the historical emergence of priesthood from shamanism.
