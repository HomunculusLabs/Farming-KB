# Inocybe haemacta
**Inocybe haemacta** is a mycorrhizal mushroom in the family Inocybaceae
reported to contain [[psilocybin]]. It is one of the less commonly encountered
psychoactive species within the large and taxonomically complex genus
Inocybe, which contains over 1,000 described species worldwide. The
presence of psilocybin in certain Inocybe species is particularly noteworthy
because the genus is primarily known for containing toxic species with muscarine
and other dangerous compounds, making accurate identification critically
important.
## Taxonomy and Classification
Inocybe haemacta is classified in the order Agaricales, family
Inocybaceae, genus Inocybe. The genus is one of the largest of the
Agaricales and is characterized by fibrous or scaly caps, brown spore
prints, mycorrhizal ecology, and frequent cortinate (cobwebby) partial veils.
The taxonomy of Inocybe is among the most challenging in agaric mycology, with
many species complexes requiring microscopic examination for accurate
identification.
I. haemacta is placed in the subgenus Inocybe (section Inocybe), which
contains species with smooth or only slightly ornamented spores, as opposed to
subgenus Inocybium (section Rimosae) with angular or stellate spores. The
species name "haemacta" derives from Greek, referring to its blood-red or
reddish coloration, unusual in the typically brown genus.
## Morphology
### Cap (Pileus)
The cap is small to medium, ranging from 10 to 40 mm in diameter. It is conical
to convex when young, becoming broadly umbonate with a distinct central umbo
at maturity. The cap surface is notably reddish to reddish-brown, sometimes
with purplish or vinaceous tints — unusual in the genus and a useful field
character. The surface is covered with radially arranged fibrils or small
scales (squamules), denser at the center. The margin is initially incurved,
often retaining fibrillose partial veil remnants.
### Gills (Lamellae)
The gills are adnexed to sinuate (notched), moderately close to crowded.
They are initially pale grayish-white to cream, developing a brownish to
grayish-brown color with reddish tints as spores mature. The gill edges are
paler and may be slightly fimbriate. The cortinate partial veil leaves
fibrillose remnants on the cap margin and stipe.
### Stipe (Stem)
The stipe is central, cylindrical, equal or slightly enlarged at the base,
ranging from 25 to 70 mm in length and 2 to 5 mm in thickness. The surface
is fibrillose to silky-silvery, whitish to pale reddish-brown, sometimes
developing reddish, purplish, or bluish tints with age or upon bruising. The
stipe is solid to stuffed when young, becoming hollow with maturity. A
cortinate partial veil may leave a fibrillose annular zone on the upper stipe.
### Spores and Microscopic Features
Basidiospores are ellipsoid to bean-shaped (phaseoliform), smooth or nearly
so, measuring approximately 8 to 11 um by 5 to 7 um. Spore print color is
dull brown to tobacco brown. Basidia are typically four-spored and clavate.
Cheilocystidia are numerous, varying from clavate to ventricose with slender
necks, sometimes mucronate or capitate. Pleurocystidia are typically present.
The pileipellis is a cutis or trichoderm composed of cylindrical to somewhat
encrusted hyphae.
## Distribution and Habitat
I. haemacta is mycorrhizal, forming ectomycorrhizal associations with various
trees in forested habitats. It has been reported from Europe and possibly other
temperate regions. The species typically grows on the ground in mixed or
coniferous forests, often associated with spruce, pine, or oak. It may also
occur in disturbed habitats such as forest edges and parks. Fruiting occurs
from late summer through autumn during cool, wet periods. It is considered
uncommon to rare across its range.
## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds
I. haemacta has been reported to contain [[psilocybin]], placing it among the
minority of Inocybe species known to be psychoactive. This is significant
because the genus is predominantly associated with toxic compounds, particularly
muscarine, which causes parasympathetic overstimulation including sweating,
salivation, gastrointestinal distress, bradycardia, and in severe cases,
respiratory depression.
Other psilocybin-containing Inocybe species include [[inocybe-aeruginascens]]
(noted for its bluing reaction), [[inocybe-corydalina-entity]], [[inocybe-tricolor]],
and [[inocybe-crocata]]. The co-occurrence of psilocybin and potentially
muscarine in Inocybe species raises serious safety concerns.
## Toxicity and Safety Concerns
Consumption of Inocybe haemacta carries significant risk due to the potential
presence of muscarine and other toxic compounds alongside [[psilocybin]]. The
possibility of mixed psychoactive and toxic alkaloid profiles means Inocybe
species should never be consumed without thorough chemical analysis.
Additionally, accurate field identification of Inocybe to species level is
extremely difficult even for experienced mycologists, requiring microscopic
examination and often DNA sequencing. For these reasons, I. haemacta and other
Inocybe species are not recommended for consumption under any circumstances.
## Similar Species
I. haemacta may be confused with numerous other reddish-brown Inocybe species,
many of which are toxic. [[inocybe-tricolor]] is another potentially
psychoactive species with similar coloration. Non-psychoactive but toxic
species in the genus may be virtually indistinguishable without microscopic
examination.
## Cultivation
I. haemacta has not been cultivated and is unlikely to be amenable to
cultivation due to its obligate mycorrhizal ecology. Mycorrhizal fungi
require living host plants and cannot be grown on standard agar or grain-based
substrates.
## Legal Status
As a psilocybin-containing species, I. haemacta is subject to legal
restrictions in jurisdictions where psilocybin is controlled. However, the
species is so rarely encountered and difficult to identify that legal
prosecution would be unusual. The practical concern relates more to its
potential toxicity than its psychoactive properties.
## Etymology
The specific epithet "haemacta" derives from Greek "haima" (blood), referring
to the distinctive reddish to blood-red coloration of the cap and stipe.
