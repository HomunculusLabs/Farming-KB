## Campanula garganica

## Overview

Campanula garganica, commonly called the Adriatic bellflower or Gargano harebell, is a low-growing evergreen perennial native to southern Italy. It forms dense mats of toothed, heart-shaped basal foliage topped by star-shaped lavender-blue flowers in summer.

The species is widely cultivated as a [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] and ornamental appeal has made it one of the most widely grown alpine bellflowers.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:**
- Family: Campanulaceae
- Genus: Campanula
- Species: C. garganica Guss.
- Section: Campanula (formerly subgenus Campanula)

Closely related to [[campanula-portenschlagiana]] within the "garganica group" of trailing bellflowers. The group is characterized by procumbent stems, reflexed corolla lobes, and a chromosome base number of x = 17.

Some botanists have treated Campanula garganica as part of a polymorphic complex that includes several southeastern European taxa. Chromosome studies by Kovanda (1970) clarified the distinctness of the Italian diploid populations (2n = 34) from related tetraploids in the Balkans.

**Morphology:**
- Height: 5–15 cm, spreading to 30–60 cm wide
- Leaves: Small, cordate to ovate, 1–3 cm long, coarsely crenate-toothed, bright green, mostly basal on long petioles
- Stems: Slender, trailing or ascending, often reddish at the base
- Flowers: Star-shaped, 1.5–2.5 cm across, lavender-blue to violet, corolla lobes reflexed and sharply pointed; borne in loose terminal racemes
- Blooming period: June through August, sometimes continuing into September
- Fruit: Erect, ovoid capsule dehiscing by three basal pores

The reflexed, sharply pointed corolla lobes distinguish it from the similar [[campanula-poscharskyana]], which has more open, funnel-shaped flowers with less reflexed lobes.

## Distribution and Habitat

Endemic to the Apennine Peninsula, primarily found in the Gargano promontory region of Puglia, southeastern Italy. Also occurs in scattered populations across the southern Apennines and on the island of Sicily.

Grows naturally on limestone cliffs, rock crevices, dry stone walls, and scrubby slopes at elevations from sea level to approximately 1,200 meters. Prefers well-drained, alkaline substrates in full sun to partial shade.

The species thrives in Mediterranean-mountain conditions with hot, dry summers and mild, moist winters. It is adapted to drought by its deep root system and evergreen foliage that can become semi-dormant during extended dry periods.

Populations in Sicily tend to grow at slightly higher elevations than those on the Gargano promontory, reflecting cooler and wetter montane conditions. Despite its limited native range, the species shows modest morphological [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]]. It is hardy to USDA zones 5–8 and RHS hardiness rating H5 (−10 to −15 °C).

**Growing requirements:**
- Soil: Well-drained, alkaline to neutral [[loam]] or gritty substrate
- Light: Full sun to light shade; benefits from afternoon shade in hot climates
- Water: Moderate during active growth; drought-tolerant once established
- Propagation: Division in spring or autumn; stem cuttings in late spring; seed sown in autumn

The species spreads by slender stolons and can form extensive mats over rocks and walls. It self-seeds freely in favorable conditions but is not considered invasive.

**Notable cultivars:**
- 'Dickson's Gold' — golden-yellow foliage with contrasting blue flowers
- 'W.H. Paine' — larger, deeper blue-violet flowers than the type

No serious pests or diseases affect this species. Slugs may feed on young growth in damp conditions.

In alpine house cultivation, plants benefit from a gritty top-dressing of crushed limestone to prevent crown rot. Established clumps can be divided every 3–4 years to maintain vigor and flowering quality.

Plants grown in deep shade produce fewer flowers and more elongated, sparse growth. Full-sun exposures yield the most compact, floriferous specimens with the deepest flower color.

## Ecological Role

The flowers attract a range of pollinators including bees, hoverflies, and small butterflies. The open corolla shape allows access to nectar by short-tongued insects.

Seed set is generally good in wild populations, with each capsule producing numerous minute seeds dispersed by wind through the basal pores. Germination rates are highest in freshly collected seed sown in autumn.
