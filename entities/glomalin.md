## Glomalin

## Description

Glomalin is a glycoprotein produced abundantly by arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi, specifically secreted by the hyphae of Glomales species. It was first identified by USDA scientist Sara Wright in 1996. This sticky, glue-like substance coats fungal hyphae and is deposited in soil as hyphae senesce and decompose, forming a critical component of soil organic matter.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil glycoprotein / biochemical compound
- **Producing organisms**: [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (phylum Glomeromycota), particularly species in the order Glomerales
- **Chemical nature**: Iron-containing glycoprotein, highly recalcitrant (resistant to microbial degradation)
- **Environment**: Found in virtually all soils that support mycorrhizal plant communities

## Key Facts

- Glomalin is extraordinarily stable in soil, with a reported half-life of 7 to 42 years, making it one of the most persistent forms of soil carbon.
- It contributes approximately 27% of total soil carbon in some soils and is a major component of soil organic matter pools.
- The protein acts as a biological "superglue," binding soil microaggregates into stable macroaggregates, which is fundamental to soil structure.
- Glomalin levels are used as an indirect indicator of [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal-diversity-patterns-distribution]] biomass and activity in soil ecosystems.
- Production is stimulated by active plant root colonization by AM fungi and declines under practices like intensive tillage, fallow periods, and chemical-intensive farming.
- Soils with high glomalin content exhibit improved water infiltration, reduced erosion, and greater resistance to compaction.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Soil Health Management

- Glomalin is a key indicator of healthy mycorrhizal associations. In compost tea applications (AACT), maintaining mycorrhizal fungal populations ensures ongoing glomalin production.
- Elaine Ingham's field guide highlights glomalin production as one of the primary benefits of maintaining robust mycorrhizal fungal communities in agricultural soil.

### Compost Tea Application

- [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT) that supports mycorrhizal fungal spore germination and hyphal growth promotes glomalin deposition in the rhizosphere.
- Foliar applications of compost tea do not directly produce glomalin, but soil drench applications that establish mycorrhizal colonies will lead to glomalin production over time.

### Soil Structure Benefits

- Improves soil tilth and aggregate stability, creating ideal growing conditions for both plants and [[forest-and-bamboo-beneficial-microorganisms]].
- Enhanced glomalin levels create pore spaces that support aerobic microbial communities, which in turn suppress plant pathogens.
- The glycoprotein contributes to [[cation-exchange-capacity]] (CEC), helping soils retain nutrients.

### Carbon Sequestration

- Glomalin represents a significant pathway for long-term carbon storage in terrestrial ecosystems.
- [[mycorrhizal-agricultural-practices]] that support mycorrhizal fungi (cover cropping, [[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]], compost tea applications) can increase glomalin-mediated carbon sequestration.

### Measurement and Assessment

- Glomalin is extracted from soil using citrate buffer and autoclaving, then quantified using the Bradford protein assay.
- Soil food web assessments by Elaine Ingham's lab include glomalin measurements as part of comprehensive soil biology evaluations.
- Typical values range from 2 to 15 mg per gram of soil, with higher values indicating healthier mycorrhizal activity.
