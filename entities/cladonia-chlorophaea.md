## Overview

**Cladonia chlorophaea**, commonly known as the mealy pixie-cup or green pixie-cup lichen, is a widespread fruticose lichen in the family Cladoniaceae. It forms distinctive cup-shaped podetia arising from a squamulose primary thallus, closely resembling the better-known *Cladonia pyxidata*.

The species is circumpolar in distribution and occurs across boreal and temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere. It is a common component of forest floor communities, particularly in coniferous and mixed woodlands.

*C. chlorophaea* is chemically variable, containing several depsidones and depsides that have historically been important in lichen taxonomy. These [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] include fumarprotocetraric acid and other lichen acids.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

The species was described by Flörke in 1828. The epithet *chlorophaea* refers to the greyish-green colour of the podetia.

The primary thallus consists of small, persistent squamules that are 2–5 mm wide and grey-green to olive in colour. These squamules form a mat from which the secondary thallus (podetia) arises.

Podetia are 10–30 mm tall, unbranched or sparingly branched, and terminate in open cups 2–5 mm in diameter. The cup margins are typically entire or slightly crenulate.

The surface of the podetia is finely squamulose near the base and more corticate towards the tips. The colour ranges from grey-green to brown-green, darkening with age.

Apothecia are brown, seated on the margins or inner surface of the cups, 0.5–1.5 mm in diameter. Ascospores are hyaline, ellipsoid, and measure 8–14 × 3–5 µm.

Pycnidia occur on the podetia margins, producing filiform conidia. Soredia are absent in typical morphs, which helps separate it from sorediate relatives.

The hypothallus is conspicuous and white to pale grey, forming a mat beneath the squamules. This feature is helpful for identifying the species in the field when podetia are absent or underdeveloped.

Vegetative propagation occurs primarily through fragmentation of podetia and squamules. Dispersal by animal movement and water splash contributes to local colonisation patterns.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Cladonia chlorophaea* has a circumpolar distribution across Europe, Asia, and North America. It extends south into mountainous regions of the Mediterranean and into higher elevations of tropical montane areas.

The species occurs most commonly on humus, mossy ground, and decaying wood in coniferous and mixed forests. It is frequently found along forest margins, in open clearings, and on road banks.

It tolerates a range of moisture regimes but is most abundant in humid, shaded microsites. It is less common in heavily polluted urban environments but persists in moderately polluted areas.

In the [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]], scattered populations occur in montane South America, New Zealand, and southeastern Australia. These disjunct populations may reflect ancient Gondwanan distributions.

The species is also recorded from rocky outcrops, peatland margins, and acidic soils. It occasionally colonises tree bases and rotting stumps in old-growth forests.

Altitudinal range extends from sea level to approximately 2000 m in mountainous regions. At higher elevations, it is often replaced by more cold-tolerant *Cladonia* species such as *C. rangiferina*.

## Chemistry

The species is known for chemical polymorphism, with several chemotypes recognised based on their secondary metabolite profiles.

The most common chemotype contains **fumarprotocetraric acid** as the major substance, sometimes accompanied by protocetraric acid. This chemotype reacts C+ red and P+ yellow-orange in spot tests.

A second chemotype contains **fumarprotocetraric acid** plus **cryptochlorophaeic acid** or **grayanic acid**. These depsidones are more reactive in spot tests and give stronger colour reactions.

Trace amounts of thamnolic acid, squamatic acid, and usnic acid have been reported from some populations. These minor constituents are useful for fine-scale chemotaxonomic discrimination.

[[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]] by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) or high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) is typically required to distinguish *C. chlorophaea* from morphologically similar species in the *C. pyxidata* complex.

The depsidones produced by *C. chlorophaea* have documented [[mycelium-running-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties-species-chart]]. Fumarprotocetraric acid shows moderate activity against Gram-positive bacteria and some [[bloomfield-fungal-pathogens-and-parasites]].

These lichen acids may also serve defensive functions against herbivory and microbial attack. The chemical variability within the species may reflect local adaptation to different environmental pressures.

## Ecology and Biomonitoring

*Cladonia chlorophaea* is an important ground-dwelling lichen in boreal and temperate forest ecosystems. It contributes to soil stabilisation and moisture retention on the forest floor.

The species is moderately sensitive to air pollution, particularly sulphur dioxide. Its presence in an area indicates relatively clean air, though it is more tolerant than many other *Cladonia* species.

Lichenologists have used *C. chlorophaea* as a bioindicator in air quality monitoring programmes across northern Europe. Its decline in industrial regions documented the impact of acid rain in the 1970s and 1980s.

The species provides microhabitat for various invertebrates, including tardigrades and mites. It is occasionally grazed by reindeer and caribou, though it is less preferred than *Cladonia rangiferina*.

Recovery of *C. chlorophaea* populations has been documented following reductions in industrial emissions. This recolonisation is slower in areas with severely depleted soil lichen communities.

The photobiont partner is typically a unicellular green alga of the genus *Asterochloris*. Photobiont identity can influence the growth rate and chemical profile of the lichen thallus.

In permafrost-affected soils of the boreal zone, *C. chlorophaea* contributes to the development of biological soil crusts. These crusts play a role in nitrogen and carbon cycling in cold ecosystems.

## Similar Species

*Cladonia pyxidata* is the most similar species, distinguished by its coarser squamules, broader cups, and the presence of farinose soredia on the podetia surface.

*Cladonia fimbriata* has podetia that are more extensively sorediate and typically form pointed tips rather than distinct cups. Its chemistry also differs, usually containing fumarprotocetraric and thamnolic acids.

*Cladonia deformis* has more robust podetia and a distinctively proliferating cup margin. It is less common and typically found in more northern or alpine habitats.

*Cladonia coniocraea* lacks well-developed cups and has more decumbent, strap-shaped podetia. It often grows intermixed with *C. chlorophaea* in similar habitats but is chemically distinct.

*Cladonia chlorophaea* sensu lato has been variously split by different authors. Some chemotypes have been elevated to species rank, including *C. grayi* (grayanic acid chemotype) in some treatments.

Field identification requires careful examination of podetia morphology, soredia presence, and chemistry. Beginners frequently confuse *C. chlorophaea* with several other cup-forming *Cladonia* species that co-occur in the same habitats.

Molecular phylogenetic studies have confirmed that *C. chlorophaea* is a distinct lineage within the *Cladonia* clade. However, relationships within the *C. pyxidata* species complex remain partially unresolved.
