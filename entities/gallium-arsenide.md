# Gallium Arsenide

## Overview
Gallium arsenide is a binary III-V compound semiconductor with the formula GaAs.
It is made from gallium and arsenic in a one-to-one atomic ratio.
Its [[silicon]].

## Physical and Electronic Identity
GaAs is not simply doped silicon with a different impurity package.
It is a distinct compound whose bonding, lattice constant, and band extrema differ from elemental group-IV semiconductors.
The zinc blende lattice gives each gallium atom four nearest arsenic neighbors.
Each arsenic atom likewise has four nearest gallium neighbors.
This tetrahedral coordination produces a [[coleman-louis-savier-twentieth-century-maraicher]].
Victor Goldschmidt is associated with early synthesis work in 1926.
Heinrich Welker and Siemens-Schuckert patents helped establish III-V compounds as semiconductors in the early 1950s.
Commercial monocrystal production began in the 1950s.
The first infrared LEDs followed in the early 1960s.
Those milestones placed GaAs near the beginning of practical compound-semiconductor electronics.
Its role later expanded through laser diodes, microwave integrated circuits, and space solar cells.

## Preparation and Crystal Growth
GaAs single crystals must control both stoichiometry and defects.
Industrial growth methods include vertical gradient freeze processes.
Bridgman-Stockbarger style growth can also be used with gallium and arsenic vapor transport.
Liquid encapsulated Czochralski growth is important for high-purity single-crystal wafers.
Epitaxial GaAs films are commonly grown by methods such as MOCVD or molecular beam epitaxy.
In MOCVD, organogallium precursors and arsine can supply the two elements.
In molecular beam epitaxy, beams of gallium and arsenic species build the crystal layer by layer.
Precise epitaxy is important for quantum wells, heterostructures, and laser diode stacks.

## Semi-Insulating Material
GaAs can be grown in semi-insulating form.
Semi-insulating GaAs has very high resistivity for a semiconductor.
This property is valuable because it isolates active microwave devices on the same substrate.
Arsenic antisite defects and compensation can pin the Fermi level near midgap.
The result is a low free-carrier concentration without requiring a perfect intrinsic crystal.
Semi-insulating substrates became a practical foundation for GaAs RF integrated circuits.
They reduce parasitic conduction through the wafer beneath transistors and interconnects.

## Device Uses
GaAs appears in [[microwave-frequency-2450-mhz-water-dipole-coupling-sterilization]] integrated circuits.
It is used in monolithic microwave integrated circuits, often abbreviated MMICs.
It supports MESFET, HEMT, JFET, HBT, and related high-frequency device families.
High-electron-mobility transistors exploit heterostructures such as AlGaAs/GaAs.
Heterojunction bipolar transistors use band engineering to improve speed and gain.
GaAs is also used for infrared light-emitting diodes.
It is used in laser diodes and optical communication components.
High-efficiency multi-junction solar cells may use GaAs or GaAs-related junctions.
Optical windows and specialized detectors are other application areas.

## Comparison With Silicon
GaAs is faster than silicon in some high-frequency contexts.
Its higher electron mobility allows short transit times in carefully designed transistors.
It is also naturally suited to light emission because of the direct band gap.
Silicon is cheaper, mechanically robust, and supported by an enormous oxide-based manufacturing ecosystem.
Silicon dioxide gives silicon process engineers an unusually useful native insulator.
GaAs lacks an equally convenient native oxide for mainstream MOS manufacturing.
GaAs wafers are also more expensive and often more fragile.
Thus GaAs complements silicon rather than replacing it across the electronics industry.
