## Overview

Nephrolepis cordifolia, commonly known as the tuberous sword fern, erect sword fern, or fishbone fern, is a species of fern in the family Nephrolepidaceae. It is native to tropical and subtropical regions worldwide, with a [[nephrolepis-exaltata]]) by its erect frond habit, smaller frond size, and the presence of underground tubers on its rhizomes. These tubers are a key identification feature.

Tuberous sword fern typically grows 30-70 cm tall, forming dense clumps of narrow, arching fronds. It is widely cultivated as an ornamental plant but is also recognized as an invasive species in several regions.

The underground tubers are edible and have been used as a food source by indigenous peoples in parts of its native range. This distinguishes it from N. exaltata, which lacks tubers entirely.

The tubers can be roasted, baked, or ground into flour. They contain approximately 30% starch by dry weight and have a mildly nutty flavor when cooked. Aboriginal Australians traditionally harvested tubers during the dry season.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Nephrolepis cordifolia was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 as Polypodium cordifolium and later transferred to Nephrolepis by Presl. The species epithet cordifolia means "heart-leaved," referring to the shape of the pinnae.

The species is placed in the family Nephrolepidaceae, order Polypodiales. Modern phylogenetic studies support the recognition of Nephrolepidaceae as distinct from Dryopteridaceae.

Fronds are 30-70 cm long and 3-6 cm wide, linear to lanceolate in outline, with a stipe that is 10-20 cm long. The stipe is pale brown, slightly scaly at the base, and grooved on the upper surface.

The frond texture is leathery to semi-leathery, with a medium green color that may darken with age. Fronds typically persist for 1-2 years before senescing.

Pinnae are 40-80 pairs per frond, overlapping, falcate to sickle-shaped, 1-3 cm long and 4-8 mm wide. The pinnae are attached to the rachis by a short auricle at the base, giving a characteristic herringbone pattern.

Sori are round, positioned near the pinna margin, and covered by a reniform indusium. Sori are borne on the undersurface of fertile pinnae toward the frond tips.

The rhizome is creeping, producing both fibrous roots and tuberous swellings at intervals. The tubers are 1-3 cm in diameter, brown-skinned, and contain starchy reserves. These tubers distinguish N. cordifolia from all other cultivated Nephrolepis species.

The species has a diploid chromosome count of 2n = 82 in most populations, though polyploid cytotypes have been reported from some tropical locations.

## Distribution and Habitat

Nephrolepis cordifolia has a pantropical distribution, occurring naturally in Africa, southern Asia, [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-hawaii-pacific-islands]]. Its exact native range is uncertain due to widespread naturalization.

The species grows in a wide range of habitats including forests, forest margins, disturbed areas, roadways, and along stream banks. It tolerates both shaded and partially sunny conditions.

It is particularly common in disturbed sites, colonizing cleared land, building sites, and abandoned agricultural areas. This ecological flexibility contributes to its success as an invasive species.

The species also establishes epiphytically on tree trunks and rock surfaces in humid tropical environments. Epiphytic colonies produce smaller fronds than terrestrial ones but can persist for years.

In Australia, N. cordifolia is native to the eastern coastal regions from northern Queensland to New South Wales. It has become a significant environmental weed in southeastern Australia, where it displaces native groundcover species.

The species has been introduced to the southern [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], and numerous Pacific and Indian Ocean islands. It is listed as invasive in Florida, Hawaii, and New Zealand.

Global dispersal has been facilitated by the horticultural trade. The species is commonly sold as an ornamental and escapes cultivation through rhizome fragments in discarded potting material.

## Cultivation

Nephrolepis cordifolia is widely cultivated as an ornamental fern, though it is less commonly grown indoors than the Boston fern (N. exaltata). It is frequently used in landscaping and as a groundcover in subtropical gardens.

The species is remarkably adaptable, tolerating a range of light conditions from full shade to partial sun. It prefers consistently moist soil but survives periods of drought through its tuber reserves.
