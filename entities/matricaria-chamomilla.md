# Matricaria chamomilla

German chamomile is an annual herbaceous plant in the Asteraceae
family, native to Europe and Western Asia, now naturalized across
temperate regions worldwide. It is one of the most extensively
used medicinal plants in history, with documented therapeutic use
spanning over 2,000 years from ancient Egyptian, Greek, and Roman
medical traditions through to modern phytotherapy.

The plant is distinguished from Roman chamomile (Chamaemelum
nobile) by its annual growth habit, hollow receptacle, and
presence of both ray and disk florets. It produces an essential
oil rich in (-)-α-bisabolol, chamazulene, and bisabolol oxides,
responsible for anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and spasmolytic
properties. The dried flower heads remain among the most widely
consumed herbal teas globally.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes, Angiosperms, Eudicots, Asterids
- **Order:** Asterales
- **Family:** Asteraceae
- **Tribe:** Anthemideae
- **Genus:** Matricaria
- **Species:** M. chamomilla

The genus name derives from Latin 'matrix' (womb), reflecting
traditional gynecological use. The epithet 'chamomilla' comes
from Greek 'chamaimelon' (ground apple), referring to the apple-
like fragrance. Formerly classified as Chamomilla recutita, still
referenced under that synonym in pharmacological literature.

## Morphology

German chamomile is an erect, branching annual reaching 15-60 cm.
Stems are glabrous to sparsely pubescent. Leaves are bipinnate or
tripinnate, 2-5 cm long, with filiform segments giving a feathery
appearance.

Capitula are solitary on long peduncles, 12-25 mm diameter, with
a conical, hollow receptacle lacking scales — a key diagnostic
feature. Ray florets are white, pistillate, 8-12 per head,
reflexed at maturity. Disk florets are tubular, yellow,
hermaphroditic. Fruit is an achene 1-1.5 mm long with small
coroniform pappus. Crushed flower heads release a sweet, apple-
like aroma from volatile oil constituents.

## Active Compounds

### Essential Oil Constituents

- **(-)-α-Bisabolol:** Primary sesquiterpene alcohol (up to 50%
  of oil), responsible for anti-inflammatory and wound-healing
  properties. Inhibits COX-2 and 5-LOX pathways.
- **Chamazulene:** Blue-colored bicyclic sesquiterpene formed
  during steam distillation from matricin. Not present in fresh
  plant. Inhibits leukotriene synthesis.
- **Bisabolol oxides A, B, C:** Oxidized derivatives comprising
  15-30% of oil. Bisabolol oxide A is predominant with significant
  anti-inflammatory activity.
- **Spathulenol:** Minor sesquiterpene contributing to the
  overall pharmacological profile.

### Flavonoids and Coumarins

- **Apigenin and apigenin-7-glucoside:** Major flavonoids with
  anxiolytic activity via central benzodiazepine receptors (GABA_A).
  Anxiolytic without sedation at dietary doses.
- **Luteolin and luteolin-7-glucoside:** Antioxidant, anti-
  inflammatory flavones. Luteolin inhibits NF-κB signaling and
  reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine release.
- **Herniarin and umbelliferone:** Coumarin derivatives with
  spasmolytic effects on smooth muscle tissue.
- **Matricin:** Sesquiterpene lactone precursor to chamazulene
  with anti-inflammatory and anti-edematous properties.

## Pharmacological Properties

1. **Anti-inflammatory:** α-Bisabolol and chamazulene inhibit
   COX-2, 5-LOX, and 12-LOX. Topical preparations show efficacy
   comparable to 0.25% hydrocortisone in atopic dermatitis models.
2. **Spasmolytic:** Apigenin and herniarin relax intestinal
   smooth muscle via calcium channel antagonism and cAMP-mediated
   mechanisms. Supported by clinical trials for dyspepsia and IBS.
3. **Anxiolytic:** Apigenin binds central benzodiazepine receptors
   without sedation or tolerance. Trials show significant anxiety
   reduction with standardized extract (500 mg TID).
4. **Wound healing:** α-Bisabolol stimulates fibroblast
   proliferation and collagen synthesis. Essential oil shows broad-
   spectrum activity against Gram-positive bacteria and Candida.
5. **Antioxidant:** Apigenin and luteolin scavenge reactive oxygen
   species and inhibit lipid peroxidation. Chamomile tea increases
   urinary hippurate and glycine, suggesting gut microbiota modulation.

## Traditional and Modern Uses

In European herbalism, chamomile flowers (Matricariae flos) are
indicated for digestive complaints, mild anxiety, insomnia,
topical inflammation, and menstrual cramping. The German
Commission E and EMA/HMPC recognize preparations for mild
gastrointestinal complaints and minor inflammatory conditions of
skin and mucous membranes. In pediatric medicine, chamomile is
considered one of the safest herbal medicines for infants and
children.

## Cultivation

Thrives in full sun to partial shade in well-drained sandy or
loamy soils (pH 6.0-7.5). Adaptable to poor soils; considered a
pioneer species. Seeds are extremely small (~2,000 per gram) and
require light for germination. Optimal germination at 15-21°C
with emergence in 7-14 days. Direct sowing in early spring is
preferred. Flowering begins 60-70 days after sowing. Major
producers: Egypt, Germany, Hungary, Argentina.

## Permaculture and Companion Planting

Valued as a dynamic accumulator of potassium, calcium, and sulfur
from subsoil layers. As a companion plant, traditionally
associated with improved growth in neighboring crops —
particularly cabbage, onions, and cucumbers. Flowers attract
hoverflies, parasitic wasps, and predatory bugs for biological
pest control.

When used as compost additive, accumulated minerals become
available to other plants. Chamomile tea foliar spray prevents
damping-off in seedlings — a well-known organic horticulture
technique. Self-seeds readily for persistent, low-maintenance
integration in polyculture systems.
