## Description

Alpha-aminoisobutyrate (AIB), also written as alpha-aminoisobutyric acid, is a non-metabolized amino acid analogue used extensively as a tracer compound in fungal nutrient transport research. Because AIB is taken up by fungal amino acid transport systems but is not incorporated into proteins or otherwise metabolized, it serves as an ideal marker for tracking amino acid translocation pathways and dynamics without the complication of metabolic conversion.

## Classification

- **Category**: Amino acid analogue (non-proteinogenic)
- **Chemical class**: alpha,alpha-dialkylated amino acid
- **Role**: Radiolabelled tracer for nutrient transport studies
- **Radiolabelled form**: 14C-AIB (carbon-14 labelled)

## Key Facts

- AIB is structurally similar to natural amino acids but resists metabolic breakdown, making it an excellent tool for visualizing pure transport phenomena in living mycelial systems.
- When labelled with 14C, AIB can be tracked non-invasively using photon-counting scintillation imaging (PCSI), enabling real-time observation of nutrient movement through mycelial networks.
- Research by Tlalka et al. (2002, 2003) demonstrated that 14C-AIB transport in Phanerochaete velutina exhibits a strong pulsatile component superimposed on net translocation patterns.
- AIB distribution patterns in growing colonies can be characterized by three quantitative parameters: centre of mass displacement, angular concentration, and alignment with resource vectors.
- In microcosm experiments, AIB was shown to travel up to 250 mm along major cord systems within 1 hour of loading, demonstrating rapid long-distance nutrient translocation in cord-forming basidiomycetes.
- The addition of new resources (such as damp cellulosic materials) triggered marked changes in AIB allocation, with tight focusing of nitrogen transport toward the new resource.
- AIB pulsatile transport frequencies could be mapped across entire colonies using Fourier analysis, revealing spatially organized phase domains with locally synchronized oscillatory behaviour.

## Transport Dynamics

The transport of AIB through mycelial networks operates at multiple scales:

1. **Microscopic (um to mm)**: Vacuolar diffusion through tubular and discrete vacuolar compartments
2. **Mesoscopic (mm to cm)**: Pulsatile translocation through corded hyphal systems, with oscillations observed in transport through individual cords
3. **Macroscopic (cm to m)**: Network-level routing with resource-directed allocation, route-switching between alternative cord pathways, and dynamic re-prioritization of transport routes

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

AIB tracer studies have fundamentally advanced our understanding of how cord-forming fungi redistribute nutrients across their mycelial networks. For cultivators, these findings illuminate several key principles:

- **Resource allocation**: Mycelial networks dynamically redirect nutrient flow toward newly encountered resources, a behaviour that can be leveraged by strategic placement of substrate additions.
- **Transport pulsatility**: Nutrient movement is not steady-state but occurs in pulses, suggesting that flush patterns in cultivation may partly reflect underlying transport oscillations.
- **Route-switching**: Networks can rapidly activate alternative transport pathways, explaining the resilience of mycelial colonies to physical damage.
- **Foraging strategy**: Different species exhibit different transport patterns reflecting their ecological foraging strategies, which has implications for strain selection in cultivation contexts.
