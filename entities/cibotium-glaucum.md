## Overview

**Cibotium glaucum** (Hook. & Arn.) Hook., commonly known as **hāpuʻu** or Hawaiian tree fern, is the most abundant tree fern species in the Hawaiian Islands. It is a prominent member of the Dicksoniaceae and a defining feature of Hawaiʻi's montane wet forests, where its trunk and spreading fronds create a lush, primeval atmosphere.

Hāpuʻu reaches heights of 3–5 meters, occasionally up to 8 meters in optimal conditions, with large arching fronds 2–4 meters long. The trunk is covered with a dense mantle of golden-brown hairs, though less dramatically golden than the closely related *Cibotium chamissoi*.

The species is **endemic to the Hawaiian Islands** and is a culturally significant plant in Native Hawaiian traditions. The mucilaginous trunk pith was used as a **famine food**, and the golden hairs served practical and medicinal purposes. Hāpuʻu remains a beloved symbol of Hawaiʻi's native forests and a popular ornamental in tropical gardens.

Conservation concerns center on habitat loss from **feral pig disturbance** and competition from invasive plant species. While not currently listed as threatened, populations in degraded lowland areas have declined substantially.

Hāpuʻu is one of the most photographed and culturally referenced plants in the Hawaiian Islands. Its image appears on interpretive signage, conservation materials, and tourism publications as an icon of native Hawaiian forest.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Dicksoniaceae → Cyatheales → Polypodiopsida. The genus *Cibotium* has a disjunct distribution across Hawaiʻi (4 endemic species), Southeast Asia, and Central America. *Cibotium glaucum* was described by Hooker and Arnott and later recombined by Hooker.

The Hawaiian *Cibotium* species are the result of a **single colonization event** from Asia, followed by adaptive radiation into four endemic species. *Cibotium glaucum* is the most widespread and abundant of these.

The trunk is erect, 3–5(–8) m tall and 10–20 cm in diameter, clothed in a dense mantle of **light brown to golden hairs** and persistent stipe bases. The hair covering is less luxuriant than that of *Cibotium barometz* but still provides a distinctive soft-textured appearance.

Fronds are bipinnate to tripinnate, 2–4 m long, with arching stipes 40–80 cm long. The stipe and rachis are **dark brown to blackish**, bearing narrow dark scales and sparse pale hairs.

Pinnules are oblong-lanceolate, 3–7 cm long, with serrate margins. The lower surface of pinnules may have a **glaucous (blue-gray) bloom**, particularly on young fronds, giving rise to the specific epithet *glaucum*.

Sori are borne on the margins of reduced pinnule segments, enclosed in the bivalved indusium characteristic of Dicksoniaceae. Spores are trilete, tetrahedral-globose, 45–60 μm in diameter.

The chromosome number is 2n = 136, consistent with other polyploid *Cibotium* species. The glaucous bloom on young frond undersurfaces fades with age, making it most visible on freshly expanded fronds in the growing season.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Cibotium glaucum* is **endemic to the main Hawaiian Islands**, occurring on **Kauaʻi**, **Oʻahu**, **Maui**, **Molokaʻi**, and **Hawaiʻi Island**. It is the most widespread of the four Hawaiian *Cibotium* species.

It grows in **montane wet forests** and **rainforests** between 300–1800 m elevation, preferring areas with annual rainfall exceeding 2500 mm. The species is a dominant component of the native forest understory on windward slopes.

Preferred habitats include **ʻōhiʻa (*Metrosideros polymorpha*) forests**, tree fern forests, and bog margins. It thrives on volcanic substrates including ash, cinder, and weathered basalt, often establishing on steep, well-drained slopes.

Hāpuʻu is less common in lowland areas due to historical clearing and ongoing feral pig disturbance. The healthiest populations persist in protected montane reserves and national parks on each island.

The species can form **near-monospecific stands** called "tree fern forests" where hāpuʻu dominates the midstory beneath an ʻōhiʻa canopy. These fern forests are among the most distinctive plant communities in the Hawaiian Islands.

On the windward slopes of **Mauna Loa** and **Haleakalā**, hāpuʻu forms extensive stands that define the character of the montane rainforest belt. These forests are critical watershed areas, with the tree fern understory playing a key role in fog interception and water retention.

## Ecology

Hāpuʻu plays a **keystone ecological role** in Hawaiian montane wet forests. The trunk surface and persistent stipe bases provide critical substrate for **epiphytic plants**, including native orchids, ferns, and bryophytes.

The fibrous root mantle and fallen fronds create a deep organic layer that retains moisture and provides **germination sites for native trees** such as koa (*Acacia koa*) and ʻōhiʻa. Without tree fern nurse substrates, recruitment of these canopy dominants is reduced.

The trunk's water-holding capacity provides an important **moisture reservoir** during dry periods. Epiphytes and invertebrates dependent on constant moisture find refuge in the fibrous trunk mantle even when rainfall is intermittent.

**Feral pigs** (*Sus scrofa*) represent the primary threat to hāpuʻu populations. Pigs root up trunks to consume the starchy pith, killing the fern and destroying the epiphyte communities it supports. Pig disturbance also creates seedbeds for **invasive plants** that outcompete native species.

Spore dispersal is wind-mediated, with peak release during drier periods. Recruitment from spore to established juvenile takes 3–5 years, making population recovery from disturbance slow.

Conservation management focuses on **feral pig exclusion fencing** and control of invasive plant species within protected areas. Where pigs have been excluded, hāpuʻu populations show significant recovery within 5–10 years, demonstrating the species' resilience when key threats are removed.

## Cultivation

*Cibotium glaucum* is cultivated as an ornamental tree fern in tropical and subtropical gardens. It requires **consistent moisture**, **high humidity**, and **shade to filtered light** for best growth.

The species is somewhat more tolerant of dry conditions than *Cibotium barometz* but still requires regular watering in cultivation. Well-drained, humus-rich soil with a slightly acidic pH provides optimal growing conditions.

Trunk growth averages **2–5 cm per year**, moderate for a tree fern. Plants grown in ideal conditions with consistent warmth and moisture may grow faster than those in the wild.

Propagation is by **spores**, which germinate on sterile peat at 20–25°C. The species can also be propagated from **trunk cuttings**, a method unique to some *Cibotium* species where sections of trunk placed horizontally in moist medium produce new shoots.

In the Hawaiian horticultural industry, hāpuʻu is a popular landscape plant for shaded gardens. However, state regulations restrict collection of wild plants, and nursery-propagated specimens are strongly preferred.

The species has been introduced to cultivation in other tropical regions, including Southeast Asia and Central America, where it performs well in montane tropical gardens above 500 m elevation.

## Ethnobotany and Cultural Significance

The **starchy trunk pith** (core) of hāpuʻu was a traditional **famine food** in Native Hawaiian culture. During periods of food scarcity, the pith was extracted, cleaned, and cooked to produce a starchy paste.

The pith has a bland, slightly sweet flavor and gelatinous texture when prepared. While not a preferred food, it provided reliable sustenance during droughts, warfare, and crop failures. Its use is documented in **Hawaiian oral traditions** and early European accounts.

The **golden hairs** (pulu) covering the trunk were harvested for several practical uses. When dried, the hairs become highly absorbent and were used as **wound dressings** and stuffing material. Hawaiian healers applied pulu to cuts and abrasions to stop bleeding.

In traditional Hawaiian medicine, preparations from the trunk and fronds were used to treat various ailments. The frond stalks were used as **splints** for broken bones, and trunk preparations were applied to skin conditions.

Hāpuʻu features in **Hawaiian moʻolelo** (stories and legends) as a symbol of forest abundance and resilience. The fern's ability to resprout after damage made it a metaphor for endurance and renewal in Hawaiian cultural expression.

The **crozier (unfurling frond)** of hāpuʻu is used in modern Hawaiian floral design and hula adornment. The dramatic spiral of the emerging frond represents new growth and is incorporated into lei and ceremonial displays.

Today, hāpuʻu remains a powerful symbol of Hawaiian forest conservation. The species features prominently in **educational programs** that teach residents and visitors about the unique biology and cultural significance of Hawaii's native flora.
