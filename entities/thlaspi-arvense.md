## Overview

Thlaspi arvense, commonly known as field penny-cress or fanweed, is an annual herb in the family Brassicaceae native to Eurasia and widely naturalized across [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]].

The species has attracted significant agronomic interest as a potential winter-hardy oilseed crop and as a phytoremediation tool for heavy-metal-contaminated soils.

Field penny-cress produces seeds with an oil composition well-suited for biodiesel production, and its winter annual growth habit allows double-cropping with summer commodities.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Thlaspi arvense was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The genus name derives from the Greek *thlapsion*, used by Dioscorides for a flat-leaved plant.

The specific epithet *arvense* means "of the fields," reflecting the species' common occurrence in cultivated and disturbed [[fukuoka-textdoc-agricultural-land-law-obstruction-return-farming]].

Plants grow 20–60 cm tall with an erect, typically unbranched or sparingly branched stem arising from a basal rosette.

Basal leaves are oblong to spatulate, 3–10 cm long, with entire or slightly wavy margins and a petiole that narrows at the base.

Stem leaves are arrowhead-shaped and clasping, with an auriculate base that wraps around the stem, a distinctive feature of the genus.

The inflorescence is a terminal raceme bearing numerous white flowers approximately 5–8 mm in diameter with four obovate petals.

Fruits are broadly ovate silicles 10–16 mm long and 8–14 mm wide, conspicuously winged and notched at the apex, giving a distinctive coin-like appearance.

Each silicle contains 4–12 seeds held within a membranous septum, with seeds approximately 1.5–2.0 mm long and reddish-brown at maturity.

The root system consists of a taproot with secondary lateral branching, reaching depths of 15–25 cm in favorable soil conditions.

Seedling emergence typically occurs in autumn or early spring, with the species exhibiting both winter annual and spring annual life histories depending on germination timing.

## Distribution and Habitat

Thlaspi arvense is native to Europe, western Asia, and Siberia, with a natural distribution ranging from the British Isles and Iberian Peninsula eastward to Mongolia and western China.

The species was introduced to North America in the eighteenth century and has since naturalized across most of the contiguous United States and southern Canada.

In North America, field penny-cress is most common in the Great Plains, Upper Midwest, and [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] regions, where it frequently occurs as an agricultural weed.

The plant inhabits cultivated fields, roadsides, railway embankments, waste areas, and disturbed ground with moderately fertile, well-drained soils.

It tolerates a broad pH range from slightly acidic to alkaline conditions and performs well in loamy and clay-loam agricultural soils.

Thlaspi arvense is found at elevations from sea level to approximately 2,000 meters in its native European range, including montane agricultural zones.

The species is a characteristic component of the *Caucalidion lappulae* weed community in [[psilocybe-bohemica-central-european]] cereal fields.

## Edibility and Uses

All above-ground parts of Thlaspi arvense are edible when young, with a mustard-like, slightly bitter flavor typical of Brassicaceae.

Young leaves from the basal rosette are the most palatable and can be consumed raw in salads or cooked as a potherb, similar to other wild mustard greens.

Seeds have been used historically as a mustard substitute when ground and mixed with vinegar, though their flavor is milder than that of commercial mustard species.

The species was consumed as a famine food in Europe during periods of crop failure, and the young shoots were occasionally gathered as a spring vegetable.

In traditional European herbalism, field penny-cress was attributed with antiscorbutic and diuretic properties, though it was never a major medicinal plant.

Modern interest in the species focuses on its seeds as a source of industrial oil rather than its culinary potential.

## Phytoremediation Potential

Thlaspi arvense has demonstrated capacity for accumulating heavy metals from contaminated soils, particularly zinc, cadmium, and nickel.

The species has been investigated as a candidate for phytoextraction of uranium from contaminated groundwater and soil environments.

Research has shown that field penny-cress can tolerate elevated concentrations of selenium and sulfur in soils associated with industrial contamination.

While not a hyperaccumulator in the strictest sense, the species combines reasonable metal uptake with high [[dighton-fungal-biomass-production-aquatic-ecosystems]] and rapid growth.

Integration of phytoremediation with biofuel production from harvested seed oil represents a potentially economically viable remediation strategy.

Field trials have demonstrated that Thlaspi arvense can be grown on marginally contaminated land without significant metal transfer to harvested seeds under certain soil conditions.
