## Description

Darwin's finches (also called Galápagos finches) are a group of approximately 18 species of passerine birds endemic to the Galápagos Islands. They are named after Charles Darwin, whose observation of their varied beak shapes and sizes contributed to the development of his theory of natural selection. Mollison references them as an example of species-swarm evolution: when many niches are empty (as on isolated islands), a single pioneer species can differentiate into many forms without competition, filling diverse ecological roles.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Animalia
- **Phylum**: Chordata
- **Class**: Aves
- **Order**: Passeriformes
- **Family**: Thraupidae (tanagers)
- **Subfamily**: Geospizinae (finches)
- **Genera**: *Geospiza*, *Certhidea*, *Platyspiza*, *Camarhynchus*, *Pinaroloxias*
- **Species count**: ~13 species on Galápagos + 1 on Cocos Island

## Key Facts

- All species evolved from a single ancestral species that colonized the Galápagos Islands
- Beak morphology varies dramatically among species, adapted to different food sources:
  - Large ground finch: thick beak for cracking hard seeds
  - Warbler finch: slender beak for catching insects
  - Woodpecker finch: uses tools (twigs, cactus spines) to extract insects from bark
  - Cactus finch: long pointed beak for probing cactus flowers and fruits
- Peter and Rosemary Grant's long-term studies on Daphne Major documented evolution in real-time
- Beak size can shift measurably within a single generation in response to drought or food availability changes
- The finches exemplify adaptive radiation — rapid diversification from a common ancestor into multiple ecological niches
- Mollison notes this as evidence that empty niches drive species differentiation

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- The principle of adaptive radiation applies to fungal species diversification in varied substrates and ecological niches
- Understanding how organisms specialize to fill empty niches informs [[fungal-community-assembly]] in cultivation substrates
- The concept that "only when many niches are empty is a species able to differentiate" parallels substrate colonization dynamics
