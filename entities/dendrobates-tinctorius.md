## Overview

**Dendrobates tinctorius**, the dyeing poison dart frog, is among the largest and most colorful members of the family Dendrobatidae. It is native to the Guiana Shield region of [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]]. It is most abundant in forested areas with high humidity and abundant [[fungal-diversity-leaf-litter-coarse-woody-debris]] and moisture are retained. However, extensive deforestation and conversion to agriculture eliminate suitable habitat.

Unlike many dendrobatids, *D. tinctorius* is primarily terrestrial, foraging on the [[fungal-ecology-forest-floor]]. It is also found climbing low vegetation and rocky outcrops near streams.

Breeding occurs in the wet season. Males establish territories and call from elevated perches to attract females. Calls are a series of short, buzzing notes.

Females lay 6–15 eggs in moist leaf litter or among root masses. Males guard the clutch and transport hatched tadpoles on their backs to small pools of water in bromeliads or tree holes.

Tadpoles develop over 10–14 weeks, feeding on unfertilized eggs periodically deposited by the female. This form of parental provisioning is known as trophic egg feeding.

The parental care system in *D. tinctorius* is among the most complex of any amphibian. Both sexes contribute: the male guards and transports tadpoles, while the female provides ongoing nutritional support through egg feeding.

## Bioactive Compounds

The skin of *D. tinctorius* contains over 30 alkaloid compounds, many of which are structurally unique to dendrobatid frogs. These are derived from dietary sources, primarily ants, mites, beetles, and millipedes.

Major alkaloid classes include pumiliotoxins, allopumiliotoxins, histrionicotoxins, and gephyrotoxins. Pumiliotoxin B (PTX-B) is one of the most abundant compounds in wild-caught specimens.

Pumiliotoxins act as potent myo- and neurotoxins, causing muscle contraction, locomotor difficulties, and convulsions in predators. They affect calcium channels and sodium channels in excitable tissues.

Histrionicotoxins block nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, interfering with neuromuscular transmission. These compounds have been studied as pharmacological tools for understanding cholinergic signaling.

Captive-bred specimens are significantly less toxic than wild-caught individuals. Without access to the specific arthropod prey that supply alkaloid precursors, captive frogs sequester few or no defensive compounds.

The specific arthropod sources of dendrobatid alkaloids were identified through decades of research by John W. Daly and colleagues at the National Institutes of Health. This work demonstrated that dietary specialization drives chemical defense in poison dart frogs.
