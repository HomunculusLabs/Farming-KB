## Overview

**Khaya senegalensis**, known as African mahogany or dry-zone mahogany, is a large evergreen to semi-deciduous tree in the family Meliaceae. It is native to the Sahelian and Sudanian savanna zones of sub-Saharan Africa.

The species is valued for its high-quality timber, which is traded as a substitute for true mahogany (*Swietenia* spp.). It also serves important roles in traditional medicine, agroforestry, and urban landscaping.

African mahogany has been widely planted across tropical Africa and beyond for timber, shade, and windbreak purposes. Its [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] makes it particularly valuable in arid and semi-arid regions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Khaya senegalensis* was first described by Augustin Pyramus de Candolle in 1830. The genus Khaya comprises five African species and is closely related to *Swietenia*.

Trees reach 20–35 meters in height with a crown diameter of 10–15 meters. The trunk is straight and cylindrical, often buttressed at the base.

Bark is dark grey to brown, rough and fissured, exuding a bitter reddish gum when cut. This gum has traditional medicinal applications.

Leaves are pinnately compound, 20–35 cm long, with 4–6 pairs of opposite leaflets. Leaflets are glossy dark green, elliptic to oblong, and 5–12 cm long.

Flowers are small, white to creamy-white, borne in loose axillary panicles. They are sweetly fragrant and appear during the dry season.

The fruit is a woody, four- or five-valved capsule measuring 5–8 cm in diameter. Each capsule contains numerous winged seeds that are wind-dispersed.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is distributed across a wide band of sub-Saharan Africa, from Senegal east to Sudan and Uganda, and south to northern Cameroon and the Democratic Republic of Congo.

It occurs naturally in savanna woodlands, dry forests, and riparian zones at elevations up to 1,500 meters. Annual rainfall in its native range varies from 500 to 1,500 mm.

Khaya senegalensis is notably drought-tolerant compared to other Khaya species. It survives extended dry seasons of 5–7 months in the Sahel zone.

Optimal growth occurs on deep, fertile, well-drained soils. The species also tolerates lateritic and sandy soils, though growth rates are reduced.

It has been introduced to many [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]] including Australia, Southeast Asia, and the Caribbean. Plantations in northern Australia have shown promising growth rates.

## Timber Properties and Uses

Heartwood is pinkish-brown to reddish-brown, moderately durable, and resistant to termites and borers. The wood is less durable than true mahogany when exposed to weather.

Timber density ranges from 560–680 kg/m³ at 12% moisture content. The grain is interlocked, producing a distinctive ribbon figure on quarter-sawn surfaces.

Wood is moderately easy to work, taking a good finish with proper techniques. The interlocked grain can cause tearing during planing if cutting angles are not adjusted.

Primary uses include furniture, cabinetry, flooring, boat building, and general construction. The timber is widely used in West Africa for door and window frames.

The bark is harvested for traditional medicine across its range. Decoctions treat fever, malaria, digestive ailments, and skin conditions.

Seed oil has been investigated for soap making and as a lubricant. The bitter bark extract shows demonstrated antimalarial and anti-inflammatory properties in laboratory studies.

## Cultivation and Silviculture

Propagation is primarily from seed, which requires no pretreatment. Germination occurs within 2–3 weeks at rates of 70–90%.

Seedlings are raised in nursery beds or polybags for 4–6 months before outplanting. Planting at the onset of rains significantly improves establishment success.

Spacing in plantations ranges from 3 × 3 to 4 × 4 meters depending on management objectives. Thinning begins at 8–10 years, targeting final stockings of 150–200 trees per hectare.

Rotation lengths in plantations are typically 35–50 years for [[honey-locust-pod-fodder-timber-production]]. Mean annual increment ranges from 8–15 m³/ha/year on suitable sites.

The species coppices vigorously, making it suitable for fuelwood production systems. Coppice rotations of 10–15 years can produce sustainable biomass yields.

In agroforestry systems, Khaya senegalensis is planted as a shade tree for crops including cocoa and coffee. Root competition can reduce crop yields if spacing is inadequate.

## Ecology and Conservation

In its native range, Khaya senegalensis provides food and habitat for numerous wildlife species. The seeds are eaten by birds and small mammals, facilitating natural regeneration.

The species is a significant component of riparian forest ecosystems, contributing to streambank stabilization and water cycle regulation. Its deep root system helps maintain groundwater levels.

Natural regeneration is often poor under heavy [[gadd-mycelial-network-resilience-grazing-pressure]]. Seedlings are palatable to livestock and frequently browsed before they can establish.
