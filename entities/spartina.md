# Spartina (Cordgrass)

## Description

Spartina, commonly known as cordgrass, is a genus of perennial grasses in the family Poaceae that dominate salt marsh ecosystems in temperate regions worldwide. Species such as [[spartina-alterniflora]] are foundational ecosystem engineers in coastal wetlands. Spartina marshes host specialized fungal communities that decompose above-ground leaves separately from below-ground roots and rhizomes, which constitute more than half of the plant's total biomass.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Division**: Tracheophyta
- **Class**: Liliopsida (monocots)
- **Order**: Poales
- **Family**: Poaceae
- **Genus**: Spartina

## Key Facts

- Roots and rhizomes constitute more than 50% of total plant biomass in Spartina-dominated marshes, representing a massive below-ground carbon reservoir.
- [[fungal-decomposition]] of Spartina involves a specialized community: leaves are decomposed primarily by ascomycetes (dominated by Phaeosphaeria spartinicola), while root and rhizome decomposition involves a different fungal assemblage.
- Live fungal biomass on decomposing Spartina is relatively low (less than 20 mg/g substrate), but fungi can immobilize 75-100% of the total nitrogen in decaying cordgrass leaves.
- Salt marsh sediments show a rapid transition from aerobic to anaerobic conditions with depth. Fungal biomass (measured as ergosterol) is negatively correlated with sediment redox potential.
- Despite anaerobic dominance at depth, fungi can conduct oxygen from aerobic zones through hyphae to advancing mycelial fronts, enabling decomposition in anaerobic sediments over distances of 5-10 mm within 15 days.

## Fungal Associates

- **Phaeosphaeria spartinicola**: Dominant ascomycete decomposer of Spartina leaves
- **Halophytophthora spp.**: Oomycetes common on decaying Spartina, though their ecological role is poorly characterized
- **[[nia-vibrissa]]**: The only basidiomycete species reported from salt marsh vegetation decomposition (Kohlmeyer and Kohlmeyer, 1979)
- Various ascomycete and mitosporic fungi form the primary decomposer community

## Ecological Significance

- Spartina marshes are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth, generating vast quantities of detritus that support estuarine food webs
- Fungal decomposition of Spartina releases dissolved and fine particulate organic matter that fuels near-shore marine productivity
- The cordgrass-fungal system demonstrates the highest decomposition rates among the ecosystems studied, with the greatest proportion of carbon flowing through fungal biomass
- Fungal-mediated decomposition produces more dissolved organic carbon (DOC) from Spartina than from freshwater leaf litter species
- The amphipod Ulorchestia spartinophila grows best and produces the most offspring when feeding on decaying Spartina leaves with high fungal biomass, demonstrating the importance of fungal conditioning for detritivore nutrition

## Carbon Budgets

Field carbon budgets comparing Spartina alterniflora in salt marshes to [[platanus-occidentalis]] (sycamore) in freshwater streams show:
- Spartina decomposes faster than Platanus
- A higher percentage of carbon is released as dissolved and fine particulate organic matter from Spartina
- Fungal biomass constitutes a significant proportion of total detrital mass (up to 15%) during early decomposition

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Spartina systems demonstrate how fungal communities specialize on specific plant tissue types (leaves vs. roots/rhizomes), relevant to substrate selection in cultivation
- The low fungal biomass but high nutrient immobilization efficiency of Spartina decomposers shows that fungal impact is not proportional to biomass alone
- Understanding fungal oxygen conduction through hyphae informs moisture management in cultivated substrates
- Salt-tolerance mechanisms of Spartina-associated fungi could inform cultivation in saline environments
- The system illustrates how fungal conditioning of plant material enhances its nutritional value for invertebrates, relevant to integrated fungal-invertebrate cultivation systems

## Fungal Oxygen Conduction

One of the most remarkable findings from Spartina marsh research:
- Fungi can conduct oxygen from aerobic zones through hyphae to advancing mycelial fronts in anaerobic sediments
- This enables active decomposition in anaerobic zones that would otherwise be dominated by bacteria
- Colonization distances of 5-10 mm from aerobic to anaerobic zones were achieved within 15 days
- Many colonizing species cannot grow in entirely anaerobic conditions but use this oxygen conduit strategy
- This mechanism significantly extends the functional depth range of fungal decomposition in sediments

## Comparison with Mangrove Systems

Salt marsh (Spartina) and mangrove decomposition systems show key differences:
- Salt marsh fungal biomass: 60-85 mg/g on decaying vegetation
- Mangrove fungal biomass: approximately 1 mg/g on decaying leaves
- Salt marsh decomposition is dominated by ascomycetes; mangrove by oomycetes initially
- Both systems are almost exclusively saprotrophic, with basidiomycetes poorly represented
- Only one basidiomycete (Nia vibrissa) reported from salt marsh vegetation decomposition
- Mangrove communities are compositionally distinct from salt marsh communities
