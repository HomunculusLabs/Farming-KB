# Entoloma hochstetteri

## Overview
Entoloma hochstetteri is a species of mushroom in the family Entolomataceae, notable for its striking all-blue coloration.
Native to New Zealand, it is one of the few entirely blue fungi known to science and an icon of the country's unique fungal biodiversity.
It was featured on the New Zealand $50 banknote in 2016 as part of a nature-themed redesign, the first fungus ever depicted on a national banknote.
Its edibility and psychoactive properties remain subjects of ongoing investigation by mycologists and natural products chemists.
The species represents a remarkable example of fungal pigment biochemistry and continues to attract interest from textile researchers worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Classification
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Entolomataceae
- **Genus:** Entoloma
- **Species:** E. hochstetteri (Reichardt) Singer 1962
The species was originally described by Ferdinand von Hochstetter in 1866 during an Austrian expedition to New Zealand.
It was later reclassified by Rolf Singer in 1962 into the modern Entoloma genus, characterized by pinkish spore prints caused by angular spores.
E. hochstetteri is one of approximately 30 described Entoloma species in New Zealand.
Molecular phylogenetic studies place it within the Rhodopolia section, a clade with pink spore prints and mycorrhizal associations.
DNA barcoding using the ITS region has confirmed its distinctiveness from related species and supports citizen science specimen verification.

## Morphology
The fruiting body is entirely blue, from cap to stem to gills, making it one of the most visually distinctive mushrooms in the world.
- **Cap:** 10–40 mm diameter, conic to convex when young, becoming broadly umbonate with age; smooth and silky, pale sky-blue to deep indigo.
- **Gills:** Adnate to sinuate, moderately spaced, initially blue but developing pinkish tones as spores mature.
- **Stem:** 30–80 mm long, 2–5 mm thick, central and equal, pale blue to blue, smooth or finely fibrillose.
- **Flesh:** Thin, blue throughout, with no distinctive odor or taste.
- **Spore Print:** Pinkish-brown to salmon, characteristic of the Entoloma genus.
- **Spores:** Angular (polygonal) in face view, measuring 8–11 × 7–9 µm.
- **Chemical Reactions:** KOH on cap surface may produce a greenish or olive discoloration.
The blue coloration persists even when the mushroom is dried or preserved in alcohol.

## Distribution and Habitat
Entoloma hochstetteri is endemic to New Zealand, found on both the North and South Islands.
It grows primarily in mixed podocarp-broadleaf forests, often associated with Nothofagus (southern beech) and podocarp species such as rimu and kahikatea.
The species appears to be at least facultatively mycorrhizal, associating with tree roots for nutrient exchange.
Fruiting occurs from autumn through early winter (March to June), typically after sustained rainfall when soil moisture is high.
It is found in both lowland and montane forest habitats, from sea level to approximately 1000 m elevation.
The species prefers well-drained soils with moderate organic matter and is rarely found in disturbed or agricultural habitats.

## Pigment Chemistry
The blue coloration is attributed to unique fungal pigments known as azulenes and related polyene compounds.
Research published in 2015 identified the primary blue pigments as novel azulene derivatives not found in other organisms.
These compounds absorb light in the orange-red spectrum (approximately 580–620 nm), producing the perceived blue color.
The pigments are stable across a range of pH values, unlike anthocyanin-based blue pigments in plants.
They are partially soluble in ethanol and methanol and resistant to heat degradation up to approximately 80°C.
The textile and food industries have expressed interest in these pigments as potential natural blue colorants.
Transcriptomic analysis revealed a unique polyketide synthase gene cluster responsible for azulene production, with no close homologs in other fungal genomes, suggesting convergent evolution.
Total azulene content has been estimated at 0.1–0.5% of dry weight.
Preliminary studies have explored extracts as sustainable alternatives to synthetic blue dyes, though commercial development remains early.

## Bioluminescence
A 2020 study examining New Zealand bioluminescent fungi detected weak bioluminescence in Entoloma hochstetteri.
This makes it one of the few bioluminescent Entoloma species known to science.
The bioluminescent mechanism appears distinct from the luciferin-luciferase system of Omphalotus species.
The ecological function of this faint glow remains speculative but may relate to spore dispersal enhancement or insect attraction.
The combination of bioluminescence and vivid blue pigmentation makes E. hochstetteri one of the most visually remarkable fungi in existence.

## Comparison with Other Blue Fungi
Few fungi exhibit true blue coloration, making E. hochstetteri part of an exclusive group.
- **Indigo milk cap (Lactarius indigo):** Blue latex and blue-staining flesh, but cap surface is grayish.
- **Blue staining boletes (Neoboletus, Suillus):** Develop blue discoloration when bruised due to oxidation reactions.
- **Cortinarius azureovelatus:** A rare blue Cortinarius from New Zealand with a distinct blue velvety cap.
- **Terana caerulea (cobalt crust):** A resupinate fungus forming bright blue crusts on dead wood.
- **Psilocybe cyanescens:** Bruises blue but is not inherently blue; contains psilocybin and psilocin.
E. hochstetteri is unique among these in being entirely blue across all structures rather than developing blue through bruising or latex production.

## Psychoactive and Edibility Status
Entoloma hochstetteri has not been confirmed to contain psilocybin or other classical tryptamine psychedelics.
Several Entoloma species have been reported to contain bioactive alkaloids, but none have been isolated from this species.
The blue pigmentation has led to speculation about potential tryptamine content, but current evidence is insufficient.
The species should not be consumed, as its edibility has not been established and the genus is generally considered dangerous for foraging.
Many Entoloma species are toxic, containing gastrointestinal irritants or sesquiterpenoid compounds.
No fatalities have been attributed to E. hochstetteri, but the lack of toxicological data warrants extreme caution.
Some confusion exists because the blue color superficially resembles psychoactive Psilocybe species, but there is no chemical relationship.

## Cultural Significance
The Māori name for the species is werewere-kōkako, referencing the blue wattle of the kōkako (Callaeas wilsoni), an endangered New Zealand wattlebird.
The resemblance between the mushroom's blue cap and the bird's blue wattles reflects deep Māori natural observation and ethnobiological knowledge.
In 2016, the Reserve Bank of New Zealand featured E. hochstetteri on the $50 banknote alongside the kōkako.
This was the first time a fungus appeared on a national banknote anywhere in the world, sparking international interest in New Zealand's fungal biodiversity.
The banknote design was developed in collaboration with Māori artists and mycologists, and the inclusion was widely praised as a landmark in public engagement with fungal biology.

## Ecological Role
As a likely mycorrhizal species, E. hochstetteri forms symbiotic associations with forest trees.
The fungal mycelium extends the effective root zone of host trees, improving phosphorus and nitrogen uptake in exchange for photosynthetically derived carbon.
This role is critical in the nutrient-poor soils characteristic of many New Zealand forest types.
The species also contributes to soil aggregation and organic matter decomposition, supporting forest floor health and microbial diversity.
The ectomycorrhizal network connects multiple host trees, potentially facilitating inter-tree nutrient transfer through common mycorrhizal networks.
Research on similar Entoloma species in European forests has demonstrated significant contributions to ectomycorrhizal community diversity, suggesting E. hochstetteri plays a comparable keystone role.

## Related Entoloma Species
Several other blue Entoloma species have been reported from the Asia-Pacific region:
- **E. virescens:** Found in southeastern Australia with smaller spores and a more restricted distribution.
- **E. lazulinum:** Native to Tasmania with deeper blue coloration and a more robust fruiting body.
- **E. bloxamii:** A rare blue species from European old grasslands, not closely related despite superficial similarity.
- **E. aeruginosum:** Found in New Caledonia and Papua New Guinea with greenish-blue coloration.
Phylogenetic analyses suggest blue pigmentation evolved independently multiple times within Entoloma, highlighting ecological contexts where blue coloration may provide selective advantages.

## Conservation Status
While not formally listed as threatened, E. hochstetteri is considered an indicator species for healthy, undisturbed native forest.
Its distribution is closely tied to old-growth podocarp forests, making it potentially vulnerable to deforestation and habitat fragmentation.
Climate change may also impact its distribution through altered autumn rainfall patterns essential for fruiting.
Conservation efforts for New Zealand's native fungi remain limited compared to plant and animal programs.
Increased awareness following the banknote feature has generated public interest in fungal conservation.
Landcare Research New Zealand maintains fungal specimen collections and distribution records supporting ongoing monitoring.
Citizen science platforms such as iNaturalist have contributed valuable distribution data for the species.
