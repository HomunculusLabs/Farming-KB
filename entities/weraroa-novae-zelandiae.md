# Weraroa novae-zelandiae

## Overview

**Weraroa novae-zelandiae** is a sequestrate (truffle-like) fungus in the family Hymenogastraceae, endemic to New Zealand. Originally described as a species of *Weraroa*, phylogenetic studies published in 2012 demonstrated that it is nested within the genus *Psilocybe*, making it one of the few sequebrate members of an otherwise agaricoid genus. It is known to contain psilocybin and psilocin, and has a long history of use as an entheogen by Māori and subsequent New Zealand residents. The species is commonly referred to as the "New Zealand pouch fungus" or "dung fungus," reflecting its distinctive fruiting body morphology and habitat preferences.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae
- **Genus:** *Psilocybe* (formerly *Weraroa*)
- **Species:** *P. novae-zelandiae* (G.W. Martín) Borovička, Noordel. & Redhead, 2012
- **Basionym:** *Weraroa novae-zelandiae* G.W. Martin, 1953

### Nomenclatural History

The species was first described by G.W. Martin in 1953 based on collections from New Zealand, placed in the genus *Weraroa* alongside other sequestrate agarics. The genus *Weraroa* was long considered a natural group of pouch-like fungi. However, molecular phylogenetic analysis by Borovička, Noordeloos, and Redhead (2012) using ITS and LSU rDNA sequences revealed that *W. novae-zelandiae* is sister to *Psilocybe subaeruginosa* and falls well within the *Psilocybe* clade. This finding has significant evolutionary implications, suggesting that the sequestrate habit evolved independently from agaricoid ancestors within the *Psilocybe* lineage — an adaptation likely driven by the damp forest floor conditions of New Zealand's native bush.

## Morphology

### Fruitbody

- **Type:** Secotioid (intermediate between agaric and truffle; the pileus does not fully expand and the gills remain enclosed within a partial veil)
- **Cap (pileus):** 10–35 mm diameter, hemispherical to convex, often irregularly shaped, surface smooth to slightly wrinkled, pale brown to reddish-brown or ochraceous when fresh, darkening with age
- **Peridial (outer) surface:** Often with adhering soil particles and debris, slightly viscid when moist
- **Stipe (stem):** 20–50 × 3–8 mm, central or slightly eccentric, cylindrical, solid, whitish to cream-colored, often curved due to subterranean development
- **Gleba (internal spore-bearing tissue):** Brownish, loculate (chambered), with irregular gill-like lamellae that are partially enclosed; gills do not radiate openly as in typical *Psilocybe* species
- **Partial veil:** Forms a rudimentary annulus or cortina-like structure that may be visible as a fibrillose zone on the upper stipe
- **Spore print:** Dark purple-brown to nearly black

### Microscopic Features

- **Basidia:** 4-spored, clavate, 14–20 × 4–6 μm
- **Basidiospores:** Ellipsoid to slightly amygdaliform (almond-shaped), 11–15 × 6–8.5 μm, thick-walled, smooth, with a broad germ pore, brownish in KOH, dextrinoid
- **Cheilocystidia:** Ventricose to lageniform, 15–25 × 5–8 μm
- **Pleurocystidia:** Similar to cheilocystidia but less common
- **Pileipellis:** A cutis of filamentous hyphae, 3–6 μm wide, with brown intracellular pigment

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Geographic range:** Endemic to New Zealand, found on both the North and South Islands
- **Habitat:** Grows on decaying wood, woody debris, and sometimes directly from buried wood in native mixed broadleaf-podocarp forests; also reported from exotic pine plantations (*Pinus radiata*)
- **Substrate:** Primarily lignicolous — fruits from decaying logs, branches, and stumps; occasionally found in association with dung-enriched soil
- **Season:** Autumn to early winter (April–July in the Southern Hemisphere), coinciding with cooler temperatures and increased rainfall
- **Elevation:** Lowland to montane forests, typically 0–800 m a.s.l.
- **Associations:** Often found in the same forest types as *Psilocybe subaeruginosa* sensu lato, though the latter is more commonly found on woodchips and mulched garden areas in New Zealand urban settings

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

### Active Compounds

*Weraroa novae-zelandiae* has been confirmed to contain the classic psilocybin mushroom alkaloids:

| Compound | Concentration (% dry weight) |
|----------|------------------------------|
| Psilocybin | 0.11–0.37% |
| Psilocin | Trace – 0.04% |
| Baeocystin | Present (trace amounts) |

The relatively modest psilocybin content compared to species like *Psilocybe azurescens* or *P. cyanescens* places it in the low-to-moderate potency range among psilocybin-containing fungi. However, the total effects can be significant due to the sometimes large size of individual fruitbodies and the tendency of foragers to collect multiple specimens.

### Pharmacological Activity

The psychoactive effects are consistent with 5-HT2A receptor agonism by psilocin (the dephosphorylated, active metabolite of psilocybin). Effects typically include visual hallucinations, altered perception of time and space, synesthesia, emotional amplification, and ego-dissolution at higher doses. The onset is typically 20–60 minutes after ingestion, with effects lasting 4–6 hours depending on dosage and individual metabolism.

## Ethnobotanical and Cultural Significance

*Weraroa novae-zelandiae* occupies a unique position in the ethnobotanical history of New Zealand. While the extent of traditional Māori use of psychoactive fungi is debated — some scholars argue that indigenous knowledge of psychoactive mushrooms was limited, while others point to oral traditions describing visionary experiences associated with forest fungi — the species has been well-known among the New Zealand psychedelic community since at least the 1970s. Its distinctive appearance (unlike any typical gilled mushroom) and subterranean fruiting habit made it both a curiosity and a valued find.

The species gained wider attention in the mycological community following the 2012 taxonomic revision that transferred it to *Psilocybe*, highlighting the evolutionary convergence between sequestrate and agaricoid fruiting body forms within psychedelic mushroom lineages. It has since become an important reference organism in studies of fungal morphology evolution and secotioid fungi.

## Cultivation

Cultivation of *Weraroa novae-zelandiae* is rarely attempted and considered challenging due to its specific ecological requirements and sequestrate growth habit. Unlike gilled *Psilocybe* species that readily fruit on sterilized substrates, the pouch fungus appears to require conditions that closely mimic its native forest floor environment:

- **Substrate:** Hardwood sawdust (native beech — *Nothofagus* — or mixed hardwoods) supplemented with bran
- **Inoculation:** Grain spawn (rye or millet) colonized with mycelium, mixed into bulk substrate
- **Fruiting conditions:** Requires sustained high humidity (90–95%), cool temperatures (10–15°C), and extended periods of darkness or very low light; some cultivators report that a thin casing layer of native forest soil improves pinning
- **Growth rate:** Slow to moderate; mycelial colonization of bulk substrate may take 4–8 weeks, with an additional 4–12 weeks before fruiting initiation
- **Yield:** Generally low compared to cultivated *P. cubensis*, which limits its appeal to commercial or research cultivation programs

## Legal Status

- **New Zealand:** The species is not specifically listed in Schedule 3 of the Misuse of Drugs Act 1975, which prohibits *Psilocybe* species and fungi containing psilocybin. However, the 2012 taxonomic transfer to *Psilocybe* may bring it under the provisions of the Act. Enforcement has been inconsistent.
- **International:** In most jurisdictions, the legal status follows from its psilocybin content. The species is controlled in Australia, the United States (Schedule I), the United Kingdom (Class A), and most European countries.

## Similar Species

- **Weraroa erythrocephala:** Another New Zealand sequestrate fungus; distinguished by its bright red to orange cap and association with native beech forests. Not known to contain psilocybin.
- **Weraroa crawfordiae:** A pouch fungus found in similar habitats, typically smaller and paler; chemistry unknown.
- **Thaxterogaster spp.:** Secotioid Cortinariaceae that may be confused with *W. novae-zelandiae* in the field; these lack psilocybin and have rusty-brown spore prints.
- **Psilocybe subaeruginosa:** The closest phylogenetic relative; an agaricoid species with a hygrophanous, caramel-colored cap that bruises blue, commonly found on woodchips in New Zealand. Much easier to identify in the field.
