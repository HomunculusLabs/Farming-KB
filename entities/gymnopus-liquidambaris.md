Originally described by [[rolf-singer]] in the genus *Marasmius*, it was transferred to *Gymnopus* following taxonomic revisions that redefined generic boundaries within the Marasmiaceae complex.

The species is notable for its ability to persist through dry periods, reviving rapidly when rehydrated — a characteristic "marasmioid" trait that allows it to fruit year-round in suitable habitats.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom** Fungi → **Phylum** [[basidiomycota]] → **Class** Agaricomycetes → **Order** [[agaricales]] → **Family** Omphalotaceae → **Genus** *Gymnopus* → **Species** *G. liquidambari* (Singer) Halling

The basidiocarp is small, with a pileus (cap) 5–25 mm diameter. Cap color ranges from pale orange-brown to reddish-brown, often with a slightly darker disc and paler margin.

The surface is smooth to slightly wrinkled, hygrophanous, and translucent-striate when moist. The lamellae (gills) are adnexed to subfree, white to cream, with lamellulae of varying lengths.

The stipe is 20–50 mm long × 1–2 mm thick, central, equal, glabrous, and tough-cartilaginous. Color matches the cap or is slightly paler. No annulus or volva is present.

Spores are ellipsoid, 6–9 × 3–4.5 μm, inamyloid, smooth, and thin-walled. Basidia are 4-spored, clavate, 16–20 × 5–6 μm. Cheilocystidia are cylindrical to ventricose.

## Distribution and Habitat

*G. liquidambari* is found in [[bolete-fungi-appalachian-region]] and south to the Gulf Coast states.

It fruits primarily on fallen leaves, twigs, and small branches of *[[liquidambar-styraciflua]]* (American sweetgum), especially in bottomland hardwood forests and riparian corridors.

Fruiting occurs from spring through late autumn, often after rain events. The species can persist through dry periods in a desiccated state, rehydrating and resuming spore production when conditions improve.

Collections have also been reported from mixed hardwood forests where sweetgum is not the dominant species, suggesting a broader host range or association with mixed leaf litter.

## Ecological Role

*Gymnopus liquidambari* is primarily **saprotrophic**, decomposing lignocellulosic material in sweetgum leaf litter and contributing to [[air-pollution-fungal-community-responses]], it plays a role in breaking down tough leaf cuticles and secondary compounds characteristic of *Liquidambar* foliage, which contains tannins and resinous compounds.

The species may also have weak **[[fungal-decomposition-processes]] throughout the [[gymnopus-dryophilus]]* is a common lookalike that fruits on oak litter; it differs in its strongly hygrophanous cap and broader spore size range (5–8 × 3.5–5 μm).

*Gymnopus confluens* grows in dense clusters on hardwood debris and has a distinctive pinkish-brown cap with a striate margin, more robust stature, and grows in cespitose clusters rather than scattered individuals.

*Marasmius rotula* (also found on woody debris) is readily distinguished by its well-developed collarium (a membrane connecting gills to the stipe) and smaller, more delicate habit.

*Collybia cirrhata* and *C. tuberosa* grow from sclerotia in decomposed wood and have distinctly reduced fruiting bodies compared to the more robust *G. liquidambari*.

## Taxonomic History

The species was originally described by Rolf Singer in 1948 as *Marasmius liquidambari* based on collections from Puerto Rico and the southeastern United States.

It was transferred to *Collybia* by some authors before the broad taxonomic revisions of the 1990s and 2000s that dismantled the traditional *Marasmius* and *Collybia* concepts.

Roy Halling formally placed it in *Gymnopus* in 1983, as part of his monographic work on Caribbean Marasmius. This placement has been supported by subsequent molecular phylogenetic analyses using ITS and LSU rDNA sequences.
