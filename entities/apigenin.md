## Overview

**Apigenin** (4′,5,7-trihydroxyflavone) is a naturally occurring flavone found abundantly in many edible plants, herbs, and fruits.

It is classified as a dietary flavonoid and is one of the most widely studied polyphenolic compounds for its anxiolytic, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties.

Apigenin gained significant attention in neuroscience for its ability to bind the GABA_A receptor at the benzodiazepine site, producing anxiolytic effects without sedation or motor impairment.

The compound is a yellow crystalline solid with low water solubility but good oral bioavailability relative to other flavonoids.

It is marketed as a dietary supplement for anxiety, sleep support, and general antioxidant protection.

## Chemical Properties

**Molecular formula:** C₁₅H₁₀O₅.

**Molecular weight:** 270.24 g/mol.

**IUPAC name:** 5,7-dihydroxy-2-(4-hydroxyphenyl)-4H-chromen-4-one.

**Chemical class:** Flavone (a subclass of flavonoids characterized by a double bond between C-2 and C-3 and a ketone at C-4).

**CAS number:** 520-36-5.

**Solubility:** Poorly soluble in water (< 1 mg/mL); soluble in DMSO, ethanol, and alkaline aqueous solutions.

**Melting point:** 347–348°C (decomposition).

The planar flavone structure allows intercalation with DNA and direct enzyme interaction, contributing to its diverse pharmacological activities.

The 4′-hydroxyl group on the B-ring and the 5,7-dihydroxyl groups on the A-ring are critical for [[tihkal-receptor-binding-serotonin]] and antioxidant activity.

## Dietary Sources and Bioavailability

Apigenin is widely distributed in the human diet, with estimated daily intake ranging from 0.5 to 5 mg depending on dietary habits.

**Rich sources (per 100 g fresh weight):**

- Chamomile flowers (*Matricaria chamomilla*): 3,000–8,000 mg (dried herb basis).
- Parsley (*Petroselinum crispum*): 200–450 mg, the richest common culinary source.
- Celery (*Apium graveolens*): 19–60 mg.
- Oregano (*Origanum vulgare*): 50–180 mg (dried).
- Onions (*Allium cepa*): 10–60 mg.
- Grapefruit, oranges, and chamomile tea: 1–15 mg per serving.

**Bioavailability:** Oral bioavailability is estimated at 2–30%, limited by extensive phase II metabolism (glucuronidation and sulfation) in intestinal epithelial cells and the liver.

Apigenin is primarily absorbed in the small intestine via passive diffusion and [[diffusion-osmosis-and-active-transport-in-plants]].

Plasma peak concentrations typically occur 1–8 hours after oral administration, with a half-life of 1.5–5 hours.

Metabolites (apigenin-7-O-glucuronide, apigenin-7-O-sulfate) retain partial biological activity and may contribute to systemic effects.

## Pharmacology and Mechanism of Action

**GABA_A receptor modulation:** Apigenin acts as a positive allosteric modulator at the benzodiazepine binding site of GABA_A receptors.

Unlike classical benzodiazepines, apigenin shows receptor subtype selectivity, producing anxiolysis without sedation, tolerance, or dependence.

**Anti-inflammatory activity:** Inhibits NF-κB signaling by blocking IKK phosphorylation, reducing expression of COX-2, iNOS, TNF-α, and IL-6.

Apigenin also suppresses MAPK pathway activation (ERK, JNK, p38) in inflammatory cell models.

**Antioxidant activity:** Scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) directly and upregulates endogenous antioxidant enzymes via Nrf2/ARE pathway activation.

The compound chelates transition metals (Fe²⁺, Cu²⁺), preventing Fenton reaction-mediated oxidative damage.

**Anticancer mechanisms:** Induces cell cycle arrest (G2/M phase) and apoptosis in multiple [[blesching-cannabis-apoptosis-and-cancer-cell-death]] lines through mitochondrial pathway activation.

Apigenin inhibits VEGF-mediated angiogenesis and downregulates PI3K/Akt/mTOR survival signaling in tumor models.

**CD38 inhibition:** Identified as a direct inhibitor of CD38, an enzyme involved in NAD+ metabolism, linking apigenin to potential anti-aging applications.

## Therapeutic Applications

**Anxiety and insomnia:** Multiple clinical and preclinical studies support apigenin's anxiolytic effect, particularly when delivered via chamomile extract.

A randomized controlled trial of chamomile extract (containing ~1.2% apigenin) showed significant reduction in Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) scores over 8 weeks.

**Cancer prevention and adjunct therapy:** Preclinical evidence supports apigenin as a chemopreventive agent for breast, prostate, colon, and skin cancers.

Human epidemiological data show inverse correlations between dietary apigenin intake and incidence of ovarian, breast, and digestive tract cancers.

**Neuroprotection:** Demonstrated protective effects in models of Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and ischemic stroke via anti-inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms.

Apigenin reduces amyloid-β aggregation and tau hyperphosphorylation in cellular and animal models of Alzheimer's pathology.

**Cardiovascular health:** Improves endothelial function, reduces LDL oxidation, and attenuates atherosclerotic lesion development in animal models.

**Diabetes:** Enhances insulin sensitivity and reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis in diabetic animal models through AMPK activation.

## Safety and Drug Interactions

Apigenin is classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the FDA when consumed in amounts naturally present in food.

**Supplement doses:** Clinical studies have used 50–1,500 mg/day without serious [[blesching-cannabis-safety-adverse-effects-evidence]], though long-term safety data at high doses is limited.

**Common side effects at high doses:** Mild gastrointestinal discomfort, headache, and drowsiness (rare).

**Drug interactions:**

- Moderate CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 inhibition may increase plasma levels of warfarin, statins, and certain chemotherapeutic agents.
- Potentiates sedative effects of benzodiazepines, barbiturates, and alcohol through additive GABA_A modulation.
- May enhance the effect of antihypertensive medications due to vasodilatory nitric oxide pathway activation.

Pregnant and breastfeeding women are advised to avoid high-dose apigenin supplements due to insufficient safety data.
