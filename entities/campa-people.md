## Campa People

## Description

The Campa (also known as Asháninka or Asháninca) are an indigenous people of the [[ayahuasca-tourism-economics-peruvian-amazon]] whose territory lies in proximity to the former Incan empire. They are notable for their distinctive kamdrampi (ayahuasca) ceremony, which exhibits a unique ambivalence between shamanistic séance and priestly ritual. The Campa have been studied by anthropologist [[gerald-weiss]], whose work illuminates the relationship between [[campa-ayahuasca-shamanism-and-priesthood]].

## Classification

- **Category**: Indigenous people / ethnic group
- **Language family**: Arawakan
- **Location**: Peruvian Amazon, near the eastern slopes of the Andes
- **Alternative names**: Asháninka, Asháninca, Campa
- **Population**: One of the largest indigenous groups in the Peruvian Amazon

## Geographic and Cultural Context

The Campa inhabit territory in the Peruvian Amazon that is geographically close to the former Incan empire. This proximity has led to more than three centuries of contact with both Andean civilizations and European missionary activity. The Campa have maintained their indigenous religious practices while being subject to varying degrees of external influence.

## Kamdrampi (Ayahuasca) Ceremony

The Campa kamdrampi ceremony is the centerpiece of their shamanistic religious practice:

### Key Features
- The brew is prepared from transplanted ayahuasca vine ([[banisteriopsis-caapi]]) and wild horéva leaves ([[chacruna-psychotria-viridis]])
- The ceremony takes place at nightfall with gender-segregated seating
- The shaman serves as the central figure, distributing drinks from a gourd bowl
- Participants remain passive spectators while the shaman communicates with good spirits

### Shamanic vs. Priestly Ambivalence
The ceremony exhibits a notable quality described as a "Necker illusion":
- **As shamanistic séance**: Spirits communicate through the shaman to spectators; the shaman puts on a show
- **As priestly ritual**: The shaman leads the group in worship and adoration of good spirits through song
- This ambivalence suggests a possible behavioral link between shamanism and priesthood

### Regional Variant
In one part of Campa territory, a local variation emphasizes the worship element more strongly: men take turns singing, and both men and women separately and together dance and sing in praise of the good spirits. This variant shifts the communication arrow unambiguously from mortals to immortals.

## Cosmology and Spirit World

The Campa cosmology features several spirit types:

- **Good spirits**: Visit the ceremony in human form, festively attired, singing and dancing
- **Kodkiti**: A hawk spirit who brings tobacco from "River's Beginning"
- **Hummingbird spirits**: Appear as "all our brothers" in groups without blemish
- The Campa explicitly do not believe in spirit possession — the shaman relays spirit songs but is never possessed
- Soul-flight is an optional [[palmer-mushroom-cooking-and-personal-experience]], not central to the ceremony

## Cultural Significance

The Campa are significant in anthropological studies because:

1. **Shamanism-priesthood transition**: Their ceremony potentially demonstrates the behavioral link between generalized shamans and specialized priests
2. **Communal harmony**: Unlike the hostile individualistic shamanism of the Jivaro, Campa ayahuasca use is characterized by communal harmony and decorum
3. **Gender organization**: The ceremony maintains traditional Campa gender separation while involving both men and women
4. **Plant cultivation**: The practice of transplanting wild ayahuasca vines shows deliberate cultivation of entheogenic plants

## Key Facts

- Campa territory is proximate to the former Incan empire with its full-blown priesthood
- More than three centuries of European missionary activity among the Campa
- The ceremony atmosphere is one of "decorum without frenzy"
- The shaman's singing has a distinctive "eerie, distant quality of voice" under the drug's influence
- The Campa represent one of several Amazonian groups (alongside Cashinahua, Sharanahua) characterized by harmonious communal ayahuasca use

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The Campa practice of transplanting ayahuasca vines and using Psychotria viridis demonstrates sophisticated ethnobotanical knowledge. Their cultivation of [[ayahuasca-and-banisteriopsis-caapi]] near settlements represents one of the documented traditional cultivation practices for this important entheogenic vine. The Campa example is frequently cited in discussions of the origins of religious institutions and the role of psychoactive plants in [[cultural-evolution-and-psychoactive-substances]].

## Contemporary Status

The Campa/Asháninka remain one of the largest indigenous groups in the Peruvian Amazon, with populations estimated in the tens of thousands. Their continued practice of the kamdrampi ceremony and other traditional customs makes them an important living example of Amazonian shamanistic religious practice.
