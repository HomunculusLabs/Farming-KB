# Interceptor Banks

## Description

Interceptor banks are deep, wide contour ditches combined with compacted downslope embankments, designed to halt overland water flow and subsoil seepage on agricultural land affected by dryland salinity and [[wisalt]] group, they represent one of the most effective mechanical interventions for reversing desertification in cleared dryland farmland.

## Classification

- **Category:** Land management technique / Earthwork
- **Domain:** Dryland permaculture, soil and [[dryland-salting-mollison]]

## Construction Method

1. Begin 1–3 m below ridgelines and plateaus
2. Strip and set aside topsoil
3. Excavate a ditch 2 m deep and 3–5 m wide using a bulldozer blade
4. Rip concreted subsoil if necessary (often to 1–2 m depth)
5. Compact the excavated clayey/stony material by ramming it firmly against the downhill bank
6. Ensure the downslope bank is effectively sealed (clay-compacted)

The combined ditch and embankment form the interceptor bank. Upper banks are built level as swales; lower banks slope at grades of 1:3000 toward natural stream beds, carrying both salted and fresh water to waterways.

## Spacing and Layout

- On slopes of 10–15° or more: continuous series of ditches from ridge to foothills, spaced every 3 m of vertical height
- On gentler slopes (5° or less): maximum 300 m between ditches
- Upper banks function as swales (level); lower banks grade to natural streams
- All water surplus to ditch capacity spills into natural waterways

## Key Functions

- **Stops overland flow and seepage** immediately upon construction
- **Holds fresh water** after rains in upper (swale) banks
- **Prevents downslope [[soil-collapse]]** by eliminating waterlogging
- **Blocks shallow aquifers** — seepage flows at <2 m depth are intercepted and diverted
- **Deep sand seams** (to 6 m) can be excavated by backhoe and sealed with vertical plastic barriers in clay backfill

## Ecological Benefits

Within 2–3 years of construction:

- Strips between banks regenerate grasses naturally
- Dying trees regain health
- New trees self-establish on the banks
- Wind erosion ceases immediately
- Crops regain vigor; fertilizers are no longer washed away
- Freshwater canals form downslope from sandy areas, attracting waterfowl, fish, and aquatic plants

Long-term soil recovery and gully elimination may take up to 10 years of careful management.

## Complementary Practices

- **Tree lines** (5–8 trees wide) below each bank to stop desert wind drying and wind erosion
- **Uphill side** of ditches kept free of trees for maintenance access
- **Backhoe pits** dug to diagnose whether water wells up from deep seepages or trickles down from shallow seepage above the "B" horizon

## Economic Return

Western Australian farmers estimate that increased livestock and crop yields pay for all earthwork within 5–7 years of construction.

## Comparison with Contour Banks

Traditional contour banks were shallow (0.5 m deep) and designed only to stop soil loss on slopes. They had no profound effect on wind erosion, gully formation, tree death, or soil collapse. Salt eventually causes contour banks to collapse, with runoff flooding over them in heavy rains. Interceptor banks address the root cause — subsurface water movement — rather than merely surface symptoms.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Interceptor banks are a critical tool for any permaculture designer working in dryland or seasonally arid regions where deforestation has led to rising water tables and soil salinization. They demonstrate the permaculture principle of addressing problems at their source (hilltops) rather than treating symptoms (valley salt pans). Mollison considered them of international importance for reversing desertification, urging both private investment and government co-investment in their construction.
