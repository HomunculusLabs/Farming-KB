## Overview

**Dicksonia squarrosa** (G.Forst.) Sw., commonly known as **wheki** or rough tree fern, is a slender tree fern endemic to New Zealand. It is one of the most abundant and widely distributed tree ferns in the country, forming dense thickets in lowland and montane forests throughout the main islands.

Wheki is distinguished from the related **wheki-ponga** (*Dicksonia fibrosa*) by its slender trunk (5–15 cm diameter), rough-textured stipe bases, and tendency to produce **suckers from the base**. This suckering habit allows it to form extensive clonal colonies, creating the dense fern groves that are a characteristic feature of many New Zealand forests.

The species reaches heights of 2–6 meters, though occasionally taller specimens are found in sheltered gullies. Its slender profile and colonial growth habit make it ecologically distinct from the solitary, massive-trunked mamaku (*Cyathea medullaris*).

Wheki is one of the most cold-tolerant tree ferns in the New Zealand flora, surviving freezing temperatures that would kill mamaku or the silver fern (*Cyathea dealbata*). This hardiness contributes to its broad geographic distribution and ecological success.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Dicksoniaceae → Cyatheales → Polypodiopsida. The genus *Dicksonia* contains approximately 25 species distributed across the Southern Hemisphere tropics and subtropics, with centers of diversity in New Zealand, Australia, and South America.

*Dicksonia squarrosa* was described by Georg Forster in 1786 from collections made during Cook's second voyage. The specific epithet *squarrosa* refers to the rough, spreading scales on the stipe bases.

The trunk is slender, erect, 2–6 m tall and 5–15 cm in diameter. It is covered with persistent, **rough, brown stipe bases** that give the trunk its characteristic textured appearance. These stipe bases are more coarse and spreading than those of *Dicksonia fibrosa*.

Fronds are bipinnate, 1.5–3 m long, forming a relatively open crown compared to the massive crown of mamaku. The stipe is dark brown, 30–60 cm long, densely covered with stiff brown hairs and narrow brown scales.

The frond crown typically holds 8–15 living fronds at any time, with new fronds produced throughout the growing season. Dead fronds persist briefly before dropping cleanly from the trunk, unlike *Dicksonia fibrosa* which retains a thick skirt of pendant dead fronds.

Pinnules are oblong, 3–6 cm long, with crenate to serrate margins and blunt tips. Sori are terminal on pinnule veins, enclosed in a **bivalved indusium** — an outer firm valve and inner delicate membrane typical of the Dicksoniaceae.

Spores are trilete, tetrahedral-globose, 35–50 μm in diameter. The chromosome number is 2n = 130.

The **suckering root system** is a key diagnostic feature. Unlike *Dicksonia antarctica* and most *Cyathea* species, *Dicksonia squarrosa* readily produces new shoots from its creeping rhizome, forming multi-stemmed clumps that can persist for centuries through continuous stem replacement.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Dicksonia squarrosa* is **endemic to New Zealand**, occurring throughout the **North Island**, **South Island**, **Stewart Island/Rakiura**, and the **Chatham Islands**. It is one of the most widely distributed tree ferns in the country.

It grows from sea level to approximately **900 m elevation**, occupying a broader elevational range than either mamaku or wheki-ponga. The species is absent only from the driest Central Otago intermontane basins and the highest alpine zones.

Preferred habitats include **lowland podocarp-broadleaf forests**, coastal forests, stream margins, regenerating scrub, and damp gullies. It is particularly abundant in disturbed forests and along forest margins where extra light penetrates the canopy.

Wheki tolerates a wider range of soil conditions than most New Zealand tree ferns. It grows on clay banks, rocky slopes, and alluvial terraces as well as the rich volcanic and sedimentary loams typical of lowland forest floors.

It is one of the first tree ferns to colonize **regenerating forest** after logging or land abandonment. Its ability to establish from wind-blown spores and spread vegetatively through suckering makes it a dominant component of secondary successional communities throughout New Zealand.

## Ecology

The **suckering habit** of *Dicksonia squarrosa* gives it a unique ecological role among New Zealand tree ferns. Clonal colonies can cover tens of square meters, creating dense thickets that exclude most other understory plants.

These fern thickets provide important habitat for **invertebrates**, including endemic beetles, wētā, and spiders that shelter among the persistent stipe bases. The dense growth also creates humid microclimates that favor bryophyte and filmy fern establishment.

Several native bird species use wheki thickets as **nesting and roosting habitat**, particularly in areas where mature forest has been cleared. The dense vertical structure provides protection from avian predators and shelter from adverse weather.

Fallen fronds contribute to a thick litter layer that suppresses seedling establishment of woody plants. This creates a self-reinforcing fern-dominated understory that persists for decades, slowing succession to angiosperm-dominated forest.

Wheki is relatively **tolerant of frost** and can regenerate after fire from its protected rootstock and suckering bases. This resilience makes it a persistent component of the New Zealand forest landscape even in disturbed sites.

The species is browsed by introduced **brushtail possums** (*Trichosurus vulpecula*), which consume the growing tips and young fronds. Heavy browsing can kill individual stems, though the clonal root system typically survives and produces new suckers.

Despite possum browsing pressure, *Dicksonia squarrosa* remains one of the most common and secure tree fern species in New Zealand. Its clonal growth strategy and broad habitat tolerance provide resilience that solitary tree fern species lack.

## Cultivation

*Dicksonia squarrosa* is cultivated as an ornamental tree fern in New Zealand gardens and, less commonly, in international horticulture. It is valued for its slender profile, colonial habit, and relatively compact size.

It prefers **partial shade to dappled light** and consistent soil moisture. Unlike mamaku, it tolerates moderate frost (to −5°C) and performs well in cooler, drier gardens provided the root zone remains moist.

The suckering habit means that a single planted specimen will gradually produce a **small colony** over 5–10 years. This makes it excellent for naturalistic planting schemes and fern gardens, where the clonal spread creates an authentic forest-floor effect.

Propagation is straightforward from **spores**, which germinate reliably on sterile peat at 15–20°C. Vegetative propagation by division of suckering stems is also possible and produces faster results than spore-grown plants.

The species is not commonly available in the international horticultural trade due to New Zealand's **strict export regulations** on native plant material. Most specimens in cultivation are propagated domestically within New Zealand.

Transplanted specimens establish readily if kept moist during the first growing season. The species responds well to mulching with leaf litter or composted bark, which mimics the forest floor conditions of its natural habitat.

In urban landscapes, wheki is valued for its compact size and ability to thrive in shaded courtyards and narrow garden spaces where larger tree ferns would overwhelm the setting. Its slow rate of spread can be managed by removing unwanted suckers.

## Ethnobotany and Cultural Significance

Māori used the **trunk of wheki** as a building material for storehouses (**pātaka**) and palisade posts. The slender, durable trunks were split and used as flooring slabs and wall linings in traditional structures.

The **pith** was occasionally eaten as a food source, though it was considered less palatable than mamaku pith. The starchy core was more commonly used as a **famine food** or emergency survival resource.

The rough stipe bases were used as **kindling and fire-starting material** due to their fibrous, dry texture. When dead stipe bases accumulate on standing trunks, they form a highly flammable mantle that can ignite readily.

The trunk's durability when dried made it useful for constructing **erections and frameworks** in traditional Māori material culture. Straight, slender trunks were selected for use as ridgepoles, fence posts, and tool handles.

In contemporary Māori arts and crafts, the dark trunk and distinctive stipe bases are sometimes incorporated into landscape design that references traditional forest ecology. Wheki thickets feature in **restoration plantings** on marae and culturally significant sites.

The species is not considered threatened and has a conservation status of **Not Threatened** in the New Zealand threat classification system. Its abundance and clonal resilience provide a degree of security uncommon among tree ferns globally.
