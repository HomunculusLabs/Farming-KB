## Taxonomy and Morphology

Lemna gibba was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The species epithet "gibba" means swollen or humped, referring to the distinctive convex lower surface of the frond.

The accepted authority is L. The genus Lemna contains approximately 13–15 species of small free-floating aquatic plants distributed globally.

Recent phylogenetic studies using molecular data have confirmed the monophyly of Lemna. Lemna gibba is placed within Lemna sect. Lemna, alongside species such as Lemna minor and Lemna turionifera.

The plant body consists of a single frond (thallus) that functions as both leaf and stem. Each frond is oval to obovate, measuring 3–8 mm long and 2–6 mm wide.

The upper surface of the frond is flat to slightly concave and green. The lower surface is prominently swollen or gibbous, appearing pale green to reddish, with a single central root.

The gibbous lower surface contains aerenchyma tissue that provides buoyancy. Air spaces within this tissue allow the frond to float at the water surface.

A single root arises from the ventral surface of each frond. The root is slender, 1–4 cm long, and lacks lateral roots, serving primarily for nutrient absorption and stability.

Reproduction is almost exclusively vegetative through daughter fronds that bud from two lateral pouches on the parent frond. Daughter fronds remain attached briefly before separating.

Flowering is rare and occurs under specific environmental stress conditions. Flowers are extremely reduced, consisting of a single pistillate and two staminate flowers within a small pouch on the frond margin.

The fruit is a tiny utricle containing one or two seeds. Seed production is uncommon in most populations, and genetic diversity is typically low within local populations.

Turions, or dormant vegetative buds, are formed in response to declining temperatures or nutrient scarcity. These dense, starch-filled structures sink to the substrate and overwinter.

The turions are smaller and denser than normal fronds, with reduced aerenchyma and increased starch reserves. They can survive freezing temperatures and extended periods of desiccation.
