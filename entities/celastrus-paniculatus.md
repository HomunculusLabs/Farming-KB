## Overview

Celastrus paniculatus is a large woody liana in the family Celastraceae, widely known as the intellect tree or black oil plant. It has been used for centuries in Ayurvedic medicine as a cognitive enhancer and memory tonic.

The species is native to the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia, where it is valued for its seeds that yield a medicinally important fixed oil. Traditional practitioners prescribe seed oil for improving intellect, memory, and mental acuity.

Modern pharmacological studies have confirmed neuroprotective, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory activities in seed extracts. The plant is classified as a Medhya Rasayana (brain tonic) in classical Ayurvedic texts.

In the Western herbal tradition, Celastrus paniculatus is sometimes called the "intelligence shrub" or "brain berry." Its rising popularity in the nootropic supplement market has increased demand for sustainably harvested seed material.

The species has been studied extensively in Indian pharmaceutical research since the 1960s. Over 120 peer-reviewed papers document its pharmacological activities as of 2025.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Angiosperms — Eudicots — Rosids
- **Order:** Celastrales
- **Family:** Celastraceae
- **Genus:** Celastrus
- **Species:** C. paniculatus Willd.

A dioecious, deciduous climbing shrub reaching 10–18 m in length. Stems are cylindrical with pale grayish bark and prominent lenticels.

Leaves are simple, alternate, ovate to elliptic, 5–12 cm long, with crenate margins and acuminate tips. Leaf surfaces are glabrous above and lightly pubescent beneath along the midrib.

Flowers are small, greenish-yellow, and borne in terminal panicles 5–15 cm long. Male and female flowers appear on separate plants, requiring both for fruit production.

The fruit is a globose, three-lobed capsule 8–12 mm in diameter that turns yellow-orange at maturity. Each capsule contains 2–6 seeds enclosed in a bright scarlet aril.

Seeds are ellipsoid, brown-black, approximately 4–5 mm long, and contain 25–40% fixed oil by weight. The aril is attractive to birds, which serve as the primary seed dispersal agent.

The root system consists of a deep taproot with lateral spreading roots. Root bark has a characteristic yellow coloration and bitter taste that indicates its alkaloid content.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Thailand, and Malaysia. In India, it occurs throughout the Deccan Peninsula, Western Ghats, and sub-Himalayan tracts up to 1,500 m elevation.

The species grows in dry deciduous and tropical moist forests, often colonizing forest edges and cleared areas. It prefers well-drained, sandy-loam soils in partial to full sun.

Celastrus paniculatus is drought-tolerant once established and requires minimal irrigation in suitable climates. It scrambles over surrounding vegetation, forming dense canopy tangles in favorable habitats.

The plant has been introduced to portions of East Africa and the Caribbean as an ornamental climber. In some regions it is considered potentially invasive due to its vigorous growth habit.

Conservation status in India is Near Threatened due to habitat loss and overharvesting of wild populations. Several Indian states have initiated cultivation programs to reduce pressure on natural stands.

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds

Seed oil contains a mixture of fatty acids including oleic (45–55%), palmitic (15–20%), linoleic (15–20%), and stearic acids (5–8%). The unsaponifiable fraction contains sesquiterpene alkaloids and phenolic compounds.

Key bioactive triterpenoids include celastrine, paniculatin, and malkangunin — quinone-methide-type compounds unique to the Celastraceae family. These exhibit anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective activities in vitro.

Alkaloidal fractions contain celapagine, celapanigine, and celapanine, which demonstrate acetylcholinesterase-inhibiting properties. These compounds are under investigation as leads for Alzheimer's disease therapeutics.

Polyphenolic fractions from the oil show significant free-radical scavenging activity in DPPH and ABTS assays. Total phenolic content correlates with antioxidant capacity across different extraction solvents.

The methanol extract of seeds demonstrates the highest in vitro antioxidant activity among tested solvents. Ethyl acetate fractions contain the most concentrated polyphenol profile.

The leaves contain quercetin, kaempferol, and rutin as major flavonoids. Stem bark yields beta-sitosterol and friedelin as principal triterpenes.

Essential oil from seeds contains copaene, humulene, and delta-cadinene as volatile constituents. These terpenes may contribute to the plant's insect-repellent and antimicrobial properties.

## Pharmacology and Uses

Cognitive enhancement is the primary traditional and research-supported application. Seed oil at 200–500 mg/kg in rodent models significantly improves acquisition and retention of learned tasks.

The mechanism involves acetylcholinesterase inhibition, enhanced cholinergic transmission, and antioxidant protection of hippocampal neurons. Chronic administration (30+ days) produces the most pronounced nootropic effects.

Anti-anxiety (anxiolytic) effects are observed at lower doses in elevated plus-maze and open-field tests. These effects are comparable to low-dose diazepam without the sedative side effects.

Anti-inflammatory activity is mediated through COX-2 and TNF-alpha suppression. Seed extracts reduce carrageenan-induced paw edema in rat models at 300 mg/kg doses.

Antioxidant protection extends to lipid peroxidation inhibition in brain tissue homogenates. This neuroprotective antioxidant mechanism contributes to the plant's cognitive-enhancing reputation.

Additional reported activities include hepatoprotective, antimalarial, and wound-healing properties. Seed paste is applied topically for skin conditions in rural Indian folk medicine.

Ayurvedic practitioners combine the seed oil with ghee or milk as a carrier to enhance bioavailability of the lipophilic constituents. The oil is also massaged into the scalp as a treatment for headaches and mental fatigue.

Traditional dosing in Ayurveda ranges from 1–3 g of seed powder or 5–15 drops of seed oil daily. Modern supplement formulations typically provide 250–500 mg of standardized extract per capsule.

## Cultivation

Seeds require scarification or warm water soaking for 12–24 hours before sowing to break dormancy. Germination rates are typically 40–60% under nursery conditions with bottom heat at 25–30°C.

The plant thrives in tropical to subtropical climates with 800–2,000 mm annual rainfall. It tolerates temperatures from 10°C to 42°C but is sensitive to frost and prolonged cold.

Propagation is primarily by seed, though semi-hardwood stem cuttings treated with IBA (2000 ppm) root successfully in 30–45 days. Field spacing of 3 × 3 m is standard for plantation cultivation.

[[soil-ph]] tolerance ranges from 6.0 to 8.0, with neutral to slightly alkaline conditions producing the best growth. Organic mulching improves moisture retention during the establishment phase.

First harvest of seeds occurs 3–4 years after planting from seed. Mature vines yield 2–5 kg of seed per plant per season, with oil extraction via cold pressing preferred over solvent extraction.

Pruning is recommended after the dormant season to maintain manageable vine length. Support structures such as trellises or live trees are necessary for optimal growth and fruit production.
