The species produces one of the world's most valuable and sought-after tropical hardwoods, prized for its rich reddish-brown color and workability.

Intensive exploitation over the past three centuries has led to severe population declines across much of its natural range.

It is listed on CITES Appendix II, regulating international trade in harvested timber and logs.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Swietenia macrophylla** King was described by George King in 1886.

The genus Swietenia contains three species: S. macrophylla, S. mahagoni (small-leaf mahogany), and S. humilis (Pacific mahogany).

All three belong to the family Meliaceae, which also includes the [[honey-locust-pod-fodder-timber-production]].

Bark is dark brown to grey, rough and scaly, with vertical fissures developing on older trees.

Leaves are pinnately compound, 30–60 cm long, with 4–6 pairs of opposite leaflets.

Each leaflet is large, 8–20 cm long and 3–8 cm wide, giving the species its common name "big-leaf" mahogany.

Leaflets are glossy dark green above, paler below, and lack the hairy undersurface characteristic of S. mahagoni.

Flowers are small, pale green to whitish, borne in axillary panicles approximately 10–20 cm long.

The fruit is a large, woody, erect capsule, 12–20 cm long, that splits open from the base to release 40–50 winged seeds.

Seeds are brown, approximately 1.5–2 cm long, with a distinctive papery wing facilitating wind dispersal.

## Timber Properties and Uses

Mahogany heartwood is reddish-brown to deep red, darkening with age to a rich, lustrous color.

The wood has a fine, even texture with a straight to interlocking grain that polishes to an exceptional finish.

Density ranges from 0.48–0.65 g/cm³ at 12% moisture content, making it moderately heavy but easy to work.

Heartwood is highly durable and naturally resistant to decay, termites, and marine borers.

These properties have made it the traditional wood of choice for fine furniture, cabinetry, musical instruments, boat building, and decorative veneers.

Historically, mahogany was the dominant timber for high-end European and American furniture from the 18th through mid-20th centuries.

Current uses include luxury flooring, architectural millwork, custom cabinetry, and aircraft interiors.

The wood machines, turns, glues, and finishes exceptionally well, contributing to its premium market position.
