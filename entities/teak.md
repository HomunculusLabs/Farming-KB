## Teak

## Description

Teak (*Tectona grandis*) is a large deciduous tropical hardwood tree in the family Lamiaceae (formerly Verbenaceae). Native to South and Southeast Asia, it is one of the world's most valuable timber species, prized for its durability, water resistance, and aesthetic golden-brown heartwood. Teak has been cultivated in plantations across the tropics for over 150 years and is a dominant species in the dry-deciduous tropical forests of India, Myanmar, Thailand, and Laos.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Lamiaceae
- **Genus**: Tectona
- **Species**: *T. grandis*
- **Related species**: *T. hamiltoniana* (Dahat teak), *T. philippinensis* (Philippine teak)

## Key Facts

- Large tree reaching 30–40 m in height with a straight, cylindrical trunk up to 1.5–2.0 m in diameter.
- Heartwood is extremely durable, resistant to termites, fungi, and weathering, due to natural oils and tectoquinone content.
- One of the most commercially valuable tropical timbers; global plantation area exceeds 6 million hectares.
- Native to the seasonal tropics with distinct wet and dry periods; requires a dry period of 3–5 months for optimal growth and wood quality.
- Leaves are large (30–60 cm) and papery, providing abundant leaf litter that decomposes to form rich humus.
- Natural range spans from India through Myanmar, Thailand, and Laos; widely planted in Africa, Central America, and Oceania.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Mollison mentions teak in the context of dry-deciduous tropical broadleaf forests:

- **Forest composition**: Identified as a key species in the dry-deciduous broadleaf forests that characterize the monsoon tropics, alongside bamboo. These forests once covered vast areas of India and Southeast Asia but have been devastated by population pressure, deforestation, and marginal agriculture.
- **High-value agroforestry component**: Teak represents one of the most economically productive long-term tree crops for tropical regions with appropriate climate, combining timber value with ecological function.
- **Soil improvement through leaf litter**: Large deciduous leaves create a thick mulch layer during the dry season, contributing to humus formation and nutrient cycling in tropical soils where organic matter is critical for maintaining [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs]]
