## John W. Allen

## Overview

John W. Allen is an [[cattle-dispersal-psychoactive-fungi-australia]] in Southeast Asia, Australia, New Zealand, and the Pacific region.
His name appears frequently in concept pages dealing with psilocybin [[bloomfield-taxonomic-authority-debate-lloyd-underwood-atkinson]] for that species is Guzmán, Bandala & J.W. Allen, 1993.
Allen also published the field guide *Magic Mushrooms of Australia & New Zealand* in 1999.
Erowid's psilocybin mushroom vault lists several of his identification and history articles.
Those include material on Australia and New Zealand, safe mushroom identification, and [[psilocybe-samuiensis-profile]] notes Allen's contribution of type specimens and field documentation.
That page also describes his photographs of freshly picked specimens from the rice-paddy habitat.
Allen's work therefore links taxonomy, field ecology, and photographic evidence in one case study.

## Thailand and Southeast Asia

Allen's Thailand work is important because it records both fungal occurrence and local human use.
The *Psilocybe samuiensis* literature includes Allen and Merlin's 1992 article on psychoactive fungi use in Koh Samui and Koh Pha-Ngan.
Later work with Sihanonth, Gartz, and Toro updated the ethnopharmacology and ethnomycology of neurotropic fungi from Thailand.
These studies combine occurrence records with cultural observations and chemical analysis.
The resulting evidence is more useful than a simple species list.
It documents where fungi grow, how they are encountered, who uses them, and how local commerce or tourism affects use.
The wiki's Samui profile draws on that tradition by treating habitat and cultural context together.
Allen's field documentation is therefore a reference point for Southeast Asian psilocybian fungi.

## Australia and New Zealand guide

Allen's *Magic Mushrooms of Australia & New Zealand* anchors several concept pages.
[[psilocybin-mushroom-cultural-artifacts]] notes that he photographed handmade silk mushroom batik from Bali.
Those photographs appear in the context of contemporary mushroom-inspired art.
This gives Allen's archive a cultural dimension as well as a taxonomic one.
Psychoactive mushroom history is often reconstructed from art, ritual accounts, commerce, and field biology together.
Allen's photographic work contributes to that mixed evidence base.
His images help preserve transient material culture that might otherwise remain undocumented.
They also show how mushroom imagery moved through travel, tourism, craft, and counterculture networks.
