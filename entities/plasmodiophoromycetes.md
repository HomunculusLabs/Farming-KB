# Plasmodiophoromycetes

## Description

The Plasmodiophoromycetes are a class of **obligate endoparasitic**
organisms in the kingdom Protoctista (not true fungi) that infect
plants, algae, aquatic protoctists, and fungi. They frequently cause
**gall-like symptoms** in their hosts and are found in soil and fresh
water environments. The group comprises **15 genera** of endoparasites,
including the economically important genus *Plasmodiophora* (cause of
clubroot disease).

Although traditionally studied by mycologists and included in mycological
literature, plasmodiophoromycetes are now classified as **Protoctista**
based on their unique cellular organization, which includes multinucleate
protoplasts lacking cell walls during their vegetative phase.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Protoctista (Protista)
- **Phylum:** Plasmodiophoromycota
- **Class:** Plasmodiophoromycetes

**Taxonomic note:** Plasmodiophoromycetes are sometimes called
**plasmodiophorids** or **endoconidia**. They are unrelated to true
fungi but share ecological similarities as plant pathogens with gall-
forming capabilities. Their study has historically fallen within
mycology due to their microscopic size, obligate biotrophy, and
inability to be cultured away from hosts.

## Key Facts

### Genera and Cystosorus Organization

Genera are distinguished by the **organization of cystosori** (aggregates
of thick-walled resting cysts) within host cells:

- **Plasmodiophora** — cysts **irregularly arranged**, lying free within
  the host cell; includes *P. brassicae* (clubroot of crucifers)
- **Sorosphaera** — cysts arranged in **hollow spheres**
- **Spongospora** — cysts in **loosely arranged, spongelike clusters**;
  includes *S. subterranea* (powdery scab of potato)
- **Woronina** — cysts arranged in a **single layer**
- **Sorodiscus** — cysts in **bilayered disks**
- **Octomyxa** — cysts **united in groups of eight**
- **Tetramyxa** — cysts **united in groups of two or four**
- **Membranosorus** — cysts of **irregular shape and size** in a single
  layer conforming to the host cell dimensions
- **Ligniera** — found in roots and [[root-hairs]]; do **not cause host
  cells to enlarge**, making them difficult to detect
- **Polymyxa** — also found in roots and root hairs without enlargement;
  important as **vectors of plant viruses**

### Life Cycle

1. **Resting cysts** in soil germinate to produce single zoospores
2. **Zoospores** — biflagellate, motile cells that swim through soil
   water to reach host roots
3. **Infection** — zoospores encyst on the host surface and penetrate
  cell walls
4. **Plasmodium** — the parasite develops as a multinucleate, unwalled
  **protoplast** within the host cell
5. **Sporangia** — some plasmodia develop into sporangia that produce
   secondary zoospores (reinfection within the same host)
6. **Cystosori** — other plasmodia develop into resting structures
  composed of aggregates of thick-walled cysts
7. **Cyst release** — cysts are liberated into soil with death and
  breakdown of host tissue
8. **Resting period** — after a dormant period, cysts germinate to
   produce new zoospores

### Symptoms

- **Above-ground:** Stunting, wilting, [[chlorosis]], and discoloration of
  leaves
- **Below-ground:** Striking symptoms including **galls, lesions,
  cankers, warts, and dry rot** of roots and underground stems
- *Ligniera* and *Polymyxa* species **do not cause host cell
  enlargement** and are therefore difficult to detect underground,
  though above-ground symptoms may be present

### Important Diseases

- **Clubroot** (*Plasmodiophora brassicae*) — causes swollen, distorted
  (clubbed) roots on cruciferous crops (cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower,
  canola); one of the most serious diseases of Brassica crops worldwide
- **Powdery scab** (*Spongospora subterranea*) — causes raised, scabby
  lesions on potato tubers; also transmits potato mop-top virus
- **Root infection** (*Polymyxa* species) — important not for direct
  damage but as **vectors of destructive plant viruses** including
  beet necrotic yellow vein virus (sugar beet), barley yellow mosaic
  virus, and wheat spindle streak mosaic virus

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Cultivation Impact

- **Clubroot** can persist in soil for **15–20 years** through resting
  spores, making crop rotation alone insufficient for control
- In mushroom [[composting]] operations, awareness of plasmodiophorid
  contamination is relevant when sourcing substrate materials from
  cruciferous crops
- *Polymyxa*-transmitted viruses affect cereal crops that may be used
  in [[grain-spawn-preparation]]; sourcing from virus-free fields is
  important

### Cannot Be Cultured

- Members of the Plasmodiophoromycetes **cannot be grown in culture**
  away from their hosts, making them exceptionally difficult to study
- Identification requires **microscopic examination of infected cells**
  (Karling 1968), observing the organization of cystosori and sporangia
- Molecular detection methods (PCR, qPCR) are increasingly used for
  soil testing and early diagnosis

### Virus Vector Role

- *Polymyxa* species are among the most important **fungal vectors of
  plant viruses**, transmitting viruses that cause billions of dollars
  in crop losses annually
- Viruses are carried both **externally on zoospore surfaces** and
  **internally within resting cysts**, ensuring survival through
  unfavorable conditions
- This vector-pathogen relationship is a major driver of soilborne
  viral diseases in cereals and sugar beet

### Ecological Significance

- In aquatic ecosystems, plasmodiophoromycetes parasitize **algae and
  water molds**, potentially regulating populations of these organisms
