## Taxonomy and Morphology

Physcia adscendens was described by Christian Hendrik Persoon and later validated by Friedrich Weber. It belongs to the order Teloschistales within the class Lecanoromycetes.

The genus Physcia contains approximately 80 species worldwide, many of which share the foliose growth form and preference for nutrient-enriched bark. Physcia adscendens is among the most widely distributed members of the genus.

The thallus is foliose, forming loose rosettes up to 5 cm in diameter. Individual lobes are narrow, 0.5 to 1.5 mm wide, and radiate from a central holdfast.

Lobe tips are frequently ascendant, curling upward from the bark surface, which gives the species its specific epithet. The ascendant habit is most pronounced in well-lit, exposed microhabitats.

The upper surface is gray to pale blue-gray, sometimes with a greenish tinge in shaded habitats. The lower surface is white to pale tan with simple rhizines.

A distinctive feature is the dense covering of black cilia along the lobe margins, especially concentrated at the lobe tips. These cilia are stiff, unbranched, and 0.3 to 1.0 mm long.

The cilia develop from the cortical hyphae at lobe margins and are composed of densely packed dark-pigmented fungal cells. Their density and length can vary with light exposure [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] observed in widespread populations.

## Distribution and Habitat

Physcia adscendens has a cosmopolitan distribution, occurring across Europe, North America, South America, Africa, Asia, and Oceania. It is one of the most widely distributed foliose lichens.

The species is strongly associated with nutrient-enriched bark, particularly on trees growing in agricultural or urban settings. It is commonly found on the trunks and branches of Fraxinus, Acer, Quercus, and Tilia.

In North America, Physcia adscendens is most abundant in the eastern deciduous forest region and along the Pacific coast. It is less frequent in the interior continental climate of the Great Plains.

Physcia adscendens shows a strong preference for well-lit conditions and is most abundant on exposed bark surfaces. It is less common in deeply shaded forest interiors.
