# Lysimachia nummularia

## Overview

**Lysimachia nummularia**, commonly known as creeping Jenny, moneywort, or herb twopence, is a low-growing perennial herb in the family Primulaceae. It is native to Europe and western Asia, where it forms extensive mats in damp habitats.

The species is widely cultivated as an ornamental ground cover, valued for its rounded leaves and long trailing stems. The golden-leaved cultivar 'Aurea' is particularly popular in hanging baskets and container plantings.

The specific epithet *nummularia* refers to the leaf shape, derived from the Latin *nummulus* (small coin), as the leaves resemble small coins. The common name "moneywort" also references this resemblance.

The species has become naturalized far beyond its native range, occurring across much of temperate North America, New Zealand, and parts of southern South America. It is considered mildly invasive in some regions.

Within the genus Lysimachia, L. nummularia is one of approximately 180 species distributed across temperate and subtropical regions worldwide. It is most closely related to other creeping species in section Lysimachia, including L. congestiflora and L. hemsleyana.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Lysimachia nummularia** L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753. It belongs to Lysimachia subgenus Lysimachia section Lysimachia, a group of creeping or procumbent species with solitary axillary flowers.

The plant forms prostrate stems 15 to 60 cm long that root at the nodes where they contact soil. Adventitious roots develop quickly at node contacts, enabling rapid colonization. The stems are slender, cylindrical, and green to reddish-green, producing dense mats of foliage.

Leaves are opposite, broadly ovate to nearly circular, 8 to 25 mm long and 8 to 22 mm wide. They are bright green in the wild type, with a slightly crenate margin and short petioles.

The leaf surface is glabrous and slightly fleshy, with inconspicuous pinnate venation. Leaf size varies considerably depending on growing conditions, with shaded plants producing larger, thinner leaves.

The golden cultivar 'Aurea' has bright yellow-green foliage that is more prominent in full sun. Leaf color intensity varies with light exposure, with shaded leaves reverting toward green.

Flowers are solitary in the leaf axils, 12 to 20 mm in diameter with a yellow corolla divided into five broadly ovate petals. The petals are slightly notched at the apex.

The calyx is deeply lobed with five triangular sepals. Flowering occurs from June to August in native populations, with individual flowers lasting two to three days.

Under favorable conditions, a single stem can produce 20 to 30 flowers over the course of a growing season. The flowers are self-compatible but produce more seed when cross-pollinated.

The fruit is a globose capsule 3 to 4 mm in diameter containing numerous small seeds. Seeds are dispersed by water and occasionally by ants. The species is diploid with 2n = 30 chromosomes.

## Distribution and Habitat

**Lysimachia nummularia** is native to most of Europe, from Ireland and the Iberian Peninsula eastward to western Russia and the Caucasus. Its range extends into western Asia including Turkey, northern Iran, and the western Himalayas.

In native habitats, the species grows in damp grassland, stream banks, marsh edges, damp woodland, and other moist, shaded or semi-shaded locations. It prefers nutrient-rich soils with consistent moisture.

The plant is intolerant of prolonged drought but tolerates temporary flooding and waterlogged soils. It frequently colonizes disturbed ground including roadside ditches and railway embankments.

Naturalized populations are widespread in eastern North America, the Pacific Northwest, New Zealand, and southeastern Australia. In North America, it is most common in the northeastern United States and the Great Lakes region.

Naturalized populations typically occur in similar habitats to those in the native range, favoring damp, disturbed sites. The species spreads vegetatively through stem fragmentation, making it difficult to eradicate once established.

Climate modeling suggests that the species' potential range may expand northward as temperatures warm, particularly in Scandinavia and Canada.

In North America, the species was likely introduced as an ornamental plant in the 19th century. It escaped cultivation and established self-sustaining populations by the early 20th century.

## Cultivation and Uses

**Lysimachia nummularia** is one of the most widely grown ground cover plants for moist, shaded gardens. The species forms dense, weed-suppressing mats that stabilize soil on slopes and stream banks.

The golden-leaved cultivar 'Aurea' is the form most commonly sold in nurseries. It provides bright color contrast in shade gardens and is extensively used in hanging baskets and window boxes.

Cultivation requires consistently moist soil in partial to full shade. The plant tolerates full sun only if soil moisture is maintained, and the golden cultivars develop better color in sunnier positions.

Propagation is straightforward from stem cuttings or by division of established mats. Stem fragments with nodes root readily when in contact with moist soil, which contributes to the species' invasive potential.

The plant has a long history of use in European folk medicine. Infusions of the aerial parts were traditionally used as a diuretic, astringent, and wound treatment, though these applications are not supported by modern pharmacological evidence.

The aerial parts contain flavonoids, saponins, and phenolic acids. The flavonoid content is similar to that of other Lysimachia species, though no major bioactive compounds specific to L. nummularia have been identified.

Chemical analysis has identified rutin, hyperoside, and quercetin derivatives among the flavonoid constituents. The saponin content contributes to the plant's traditional reputation as a diuretic, though clinical validation is lacking.

In traditional European herbalism, the plant was sometimes applied externally as a poultice for minor wounds and skin inflammations. These uses are documented in 16th and 17th century herbals but have largely fallen out of practice.

## Ethnobotany and Cultural Significance

The distinctive coin-shaped leaves of **Lysimachia nummularia** have given rise to numerous folk names across Europe. In England, "moneywort" and "herb twopence" reflect the leaf resemblance to coins.

In Irish folk tradition, the plant was associated with financial luck. Sprigs were sometimes carried as a charm or placed in purses, reflecting the belief that plants resembling money could attract wealth.

The golden cultivar 'Aurea' has been in cultivation since at least the Victorian era. It was popularized by the 19th-century garden designer Gertrude Jekyll, who used it extensively in her herbaceous border designs.

In parts of Scandinavia and central Europe, the plant was traditionally woven into garlands for midsummer festivals. The trailing habit made it useful for decorative purposes, and the bright green stems symbolized vitality and growth.

## Ecology and Conservation

In the wild, **Lysimachia nummularia** is pollinated by bees, particularly long-tongued species. The flowers produce nectar and are visited by various flying insects, though they are not a major nectar source.

The species spreads primarily through vegetative reproduction. Stem fragments transported by water or attached to animal fur establish new colonies readily. Seed production is relatively low compared to vegetative spread, and seedlings are uncommon in the wild.

The dense mats formed by creeping Jenny provide habitat for small invertebrates and help stabilize stream banks against erosion. Amphibians and small mammals may use the mats for cover in riparian habitats.

However, dense mats can also suppress native vegetation in naturalized areas by blocking light and competing for soil moisture. In severely infested areas, the species forms near-monocultures that reduce local plant diversity.

Invasive behavior has been documented in North America, particularly in wetland areas and riparian zones. The species is listed as invasive in several US states and Canadian provinces, where it can displace native wetland plants through rapid vegetative expansion.

In its native European range, the species is common and not considered threatened. It benefits from habitat disturbance and is one of the characteristic species of damp, nutrient-rich lowland habitats. Populations have declined somewhat in intensively farmed areas where wetlands have been drained.
