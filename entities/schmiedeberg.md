## Description

Oswald Schmiedeberg (1838-1921) was a pioneering German pharmacologist and chemist who, along with his colleague Koppe, isolated the alkaloid **[[muscarine]]** from *[[amanita-muscaria]]* (the [[fly-agaric]] mushroom). His work on fungal alkaloids and their physiological antagonists laid essential groundwork for modern toxicology and pharmacology. He is widely regarded as one of the founding figures of modern pharmacology as a scientific discipline.

## Classification

- **Field:** Pharmacology / Toxicology / Mycology
- **Nationality:** German (Baltic German, born in Gut Sassen, Livonia)
- **Era:** 19th-early 20th century
- **Known for:** Isolation of Muscarine; advocacy of atropine as antidote to mushroom poisoning; founding modern pharmacology
- **Related researchers:** Koppe (colleague), Letellier, Boudier
- **Source species:** *Amanita muscaria*

## Key Facts

- Schmiedeberg and Koppe extracted their alkaloid from *Amanita muscaria* specifically and gave it the name "Muscarine," after the source species (muscaria = fly agaric).
- They advocated the use of **atropine** (the active principle of *[[atropa-belladonna]]*) administered via subcutaneous injection as an antidote to Amanita poisoning. This recommendation was based on the observation that atropine could restore a frog's heart stopped by muscarine.
- Their research demonstrated that animals injected with atropine could survive otherwise fatal doses of muscarine -- a key finding in the history of antagonistic drug interactions and receptor pharmacology.
- Palmer noted that an American consulting physician independently arrived at the same treatment (belladonna/atropine) without knowledge of Schmiedeberg and Koppe's work, suggesting the pharmacological logic was sound and cross-culturally validated.
- The classic demonstration: "The throbbing heart of a frog is silenced by a drop of muscarine; action is restored by a drop of atropine." This experiment became a foundational demonstration in pharmacology education.
- Schmiedeberg's broader contributions to pharmacology were extensive; he established the first major pharmacological institute at the University of Strasbourg.

## Pharmacological Contributions

Schmiedeberg's work on muscarine-atropine antagonism established several key pharmacological principles that remain foundational:
- **Competitive antagonism:** One drug can block the effects of another at the receptor level, a concept central to modern pharmacology
- **Receptor specificity:** Different substances act on specific physiological receptors (muscarinic acetylcholine receptors in this case)
- **Dose-response relationships:** The amount of antagonist needed relates to the amount of agonist present
- **Subcutaneous drug delivery:** Demonstrated that direct injection could deliver antidotes more effectively than oral administration, especially when the digestive system is compromised

## The Frog Heart Experiment

The experiment cited by Palmer became one of the most famous demonstrations in pharmacological history:
- A frog's beating heart is exposed
- A drop of muscarine (Schmiedeberg's alkaloid from *A. muscaria*) is applied -- the heart stops
- A drop of atropine is then applied -- the heart resumes beating
- This reversible antagonism proved that the two substances act on the same physiological target in opposite ways

This experiment was cited as evidence that atropine could be an effective antidote to Amanita poisoning in humans, though the situation proved more complex than the simple frog model suggested.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Schmiedeberg's work has lasting significance for:

- **Antidote development:** His advocacy of atropine as a physiological antagonist to muscarine/Amanitine poisoning remains relevant to emergency treatment protocols for Amanita ingestion today.
- **Pharmacological principles:** The muscarine-atropine antagonism he documented became a classic example of competitive receptor antagonism taught in pharmacology courses worldwide.
- **Mushroom safety:** His research reinforced the extreme danger of *Amanita* species and the need for definitive identification before consumption.
- **Toxin classification:** The distinction between Muscarine (from *A. muscaria*) and the more deadly amatoxins (from *A. phalloides* and *A. verna*) was not yet clear in Schmiedeberg's era, but his work began the process of chemical differentiation among Amanita toxins.
- **Modern treatment:** While atropine is effective against muscarine poisoning, modern medicine recognizes its limitations against the amatoxins that cause most fatal Amanita poisonings.
- **Species specificity:** Schmiedeberg's choice of *A. muscaria* as his source species was significant, as this species contains primarily muscarine and [[ibotenic-acid]] rather than the amatoxins found in the more lethal *A. phalloides*.

The naming conflict between his "Muscarine" and Letellier's "Amanitine" reflects the incomplete state of alkaloid chemistry in the 19th century, when different researchers likely isolated different compounds or mixtures from different source species. The resolution came only with modern analytical chemistry, which identified the distinct toxin families within Amanita.
