# John Ramsbottom

British mycologist at the British Museum (Natural History), authority on [[ramsbottom-poisonous-fungi-identification-guide]], and author of the influential 1945 reference "Poisonous Fungi."

## Overview

John Ramsbottom (1885-1974) was a British mycologist who served as Keeper of Botany at the British Museum (Natural History). His 1945 publication "Poisonous Fungi" (King Penguin Books, with colour plates by Rose Ellenby) is a landmark reference in mycological safety literature, written during wartime when foraging had become newly important to the British public.

## Key Contributions

- **Systematic debunking of foraging myths**: Proved that every traditional rule for distinguishing edible from [[palmer-poisonous-mushrooms-deadly-species]] is worthless
- **Species-level toxicology**: Detailed descriptions of all British poisonous fungi with clinical symptom profiles
- **Historical poisoning documentation**: Compiled case studies from classical antiquity through 1944
- **Treatment assessment**: Evaluated historical and contemporary poisoning treatments, including the Institut Pasteur antiphalloidian serum

## Poisonous Fungi Classification (1945)

Ramsbottom classified poisonous fungi into three categories by mechanism:

### 1. Cell-Degenerating Toxins (Long Incubation, Often Fatal)
- **Amanita phalloides** (Death Cap) -- responsible for 90%+ of recorded deaths; 50-90% fatality rate
- **Amanita verna** (Fools' Mushroom) -- pure white, more dangerous due to resemblance to [[palmer-edible-mushrooms-north-america]]
- **[[bloomfield-destroying-angel-amanita-virosa-amatoxin-poisoning-clinical-course]]** (Destroying Angel) -- conical cap, floccosely scaly stem, rare in Britain
- **Lepiota helveola** (Poisonous Lepiota) -- mild Amanita phalloides-type symptoms, much less dangerous

### 2. Neurotoxic Toxins (Nervous System Paralysis)
- **[[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]]** (Fly Agaric) -- never causes death in healthy people; delirium, hallucinations, stupor, amnesia
- **Amanita pantherina** (False Blusher) -- similar but more serious symptoms than muscaria
- **Inocybe Patouillardi** -- caused deaths (Surrey 1937); Amanita muscaria-type poisoning

### 3. Hemolytic Toxins (Red Blood Cell Destruction)
- **Gyromitra esculenta** -- safe when cooked or dried, deadly raw; attributed to helvellic acid

## Ramsbottom's Myth-Busting

His most important contribution was systematically demolishing folk rules. See [[ramsbottom-poisonous-fungi-species]] for full species detail. Key debunked myths:

- **"Edible mushrooms peel"**: Both Field Mushroom and Death Cap peel. Deaths at Ipswich (1944) resulted from this belief.
- **"Silver spoon turns black"**: Silver blackens from sulphides; no fungus affects silver this way.
- **"Bright colors = poisonous"**: Color distinguishes species, not toxicity.
- **"Color change when cut = poisonous"**: Chromogens + oxidizing enzymes (lactase, tyrosinase) cause color change; unrelated to toxicity.
- **"Animals won't eat poisonous fungi"**: Slugs thrive on deadly Amanitas. Rabbits are immune due to stomach chemistry.
- **"Poisonous fungi grow in woods"**: Habitat is irrelevant to toxicity.
- **"Taste reveals poison"**: None of the truly dangerous fungi have sharp taste.

## Historical Treatment Methods

Ramsbottom documented and assessed historical approaches:
- **Gerard's method (1851)**: Macerate in water + vinegar + salt for 2 hours, wash, boil. "It is believed that he was finally the victim of his experiments."
- **Fabre's blanching** (Serignan): Boil in salted water, rinse in cold water. Unnecessary for known-edible species but makes fungi more digestible.
- **Antiphalloidian serum** (Institut Pasteur): Produced by immunizing sheep; good results when injected soon after ingestion (at least 40cc, hypodermic or intravenous).
- **Intravenous glucose** (20-25%): German treatment for hypoglycaemia caused by liver damage.
- **Salt solution** (20% oral or IV, 20cc doses): French treatment with reported success.

## Toxin Science

### Amanita phalloides Toxins
- **Phallin**: Glucoside attacking red blood corpuscles; readily destroyed by heat and digestive juices; NOT the active poison
- **Amanita-toxin (Amanitin)**: Complex chemical structure, resists heat, drying, and digestive juices; causes liver degeneration and the majority of lesions
- **Phalloidin**: Polypeptide, quickly active but destroyed by heat

### Amanita muscaria Toxins
- **Muscarine (mycomuscarine)**: Present in small amounts; effects don't match actual poisoning symptoms
- **Mycetoatropine (muscaridine)**: Effects similar to atropine on central nervous system; precise constitution unknown
- **Choline**: Present in fairly large amounts; probably responsible for gastrointestinal symptoms

### Gyromitra esculenta
- **Helvellic acid**: Acts on red blood corpuscles
- However, German poisonings showed action is "not simple haemolysis" -- true nature still undetermined at time of writing
- Factory workers suffered eye trouble from spores or finger-irritation by poison

## Etymology and Word History

Ramsbottom provided linguistic research:
- **Amanita**: Greek *Amanitai*, from Mount Amanon in Cilicia; originally the Greek name for Field Mushroom (*Amanites* or *Manitari*)
- **Mushroom**: 15th century *musseron*/*muscheron*, from Old French *moisson*/*mousseron*, derived from *mousse* (moss)
- **Toadstool**: 14th century *tadstole*/*tode stole*; variant *todyshatte*; from *paddocstol* (paddock-stool), recalling Dutch *paddestoel*
- **Mushroom/toadstool distinction**: Originally interchangeable terms for umbrella-shaped fungi. OED primary definition of toadstool: "A fungus having a round disk-like top and a slender stalk, a mushroom"

## Wartime Context

The 1945 book was published when wartime food shortages drove many Britons to reconsider eating wild fungi. Ramsbottom noted that "the majority still believe that only the Cultivated Mushroom is to be trusted" and quoted the Pauline injunction "Whatsoever is sold in the shambles, that eat, asking no question" as religiously adopted.

## Personal Allergy

Ramsbottom documented that some people react to perfectly harmless fungi: "Occasionally a drop of mushroom ketchup has an effect out of all proportion to the amount of fungus absorbed." This is no more frequent than allergies to milk, eggs, strawberries, or fish, but "its manifestation usually creates more alarm."
