# Ricinus communis
## Castor Bean
Fast-growing annual or perennial shrub in the Euphorbiaceae family, native
to eastern Africa and now pantropical in distribution. Castor bean is the
source of castor oil, one of the most important industrial vegetable oils,
as well as ricin — one of the most potent natural toxins known. This duality
of utility and extreme toxicity makes Ricinus communis one of the most
botanically significant plants in human history. Castor oil has been used
medicinally for millennia and remains critical in modern industry as a
lubricant, plasticizer, and biodiesel feedstock. The plant is widely grown
as an ornamental for its bold, palmate foliage and dramatic growth rate,
and has permaculture applications as a windbreak and soil accumulator.
## Taxonomy and Naming
- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Malpighiales
- **Family:** Euphorbiaceae
- **Genus:** Ricinus
- **Species:** R. communis L.
- **Authority:** Carl Linnaeus, 1753
- **Common names:** Castor bean, castor oil plant, palma christi,
  wonder tree, mole bean
- **Note:** Ricinus is a monotypic genus — R. communis is the sole
  species. The genus name derives from Latin "ricinus" (tick), referring
  to the tick-like appearance of the seeds.
- **Cultivar groups:** Ornamental cultivars selected for red, bronze,
  or dark purple foliage (e.g., 'Carmencita', 'Impala', 'Red Giant');
  industrial cultivars selected for high seed yield and oil content
## Description
### Growth Habit and Morphology
- **Habit:** Annual in temperate zones, perennial in tropical climates;
  1–5 m tall as an annual, up to 12 m as a perennial in favorable
  tropical conditions; growth rate among the fastest of all plants
  (up to 3 cm/day under optimal conditions)
- **Stem:** Erect, hollow, glabrous to slightly glaucous, green to
  reddish-purple depending on cultivar; succulent when young, becoming
  semi-woody with age; conspicuous leaf scars
- **Leaves:** Large, palmately compound, 30–80 cm across; 7–11
  (typically 8) lanceolate lobes with serrate margins; long petioles
  (15–30 cm) with pulvinus (joint) at base; glossy green, bronze, or
  dark red depending on cultivar
- **Inflorescence:** Terminal panicles, 15–50 cm long; monoecious
  (separate male and female flowers on same plant)
- **Male flowers:** Numerous, small, yellowish-green, borne on upper
  peduncle; stamens in branched clusters
- **Female flowers:** Fewer, larger, located at base of inflorescence;
  prominent red to green pistil with three-lobed stigma
- **Fruit:** Globose to ovoid capsule, 1.5–2.5 cm diameter, green
  turning brown at maturity; covered in soft, fleshy prickles
  (spineless varieties exist); dehiscent at maturity into three
  valves, each containing one seed
- **Seeds:** Elongated-ovoid, 1–1.5 cm long, mottled grey-brown with
  a prominent caruncle (fleshy appendage) at one end; superficially
  resembling engorged ticks; highly toxic
## Distribution and Habitat
- **Native range:** Eastern Africa (Ethiopia, Somalia, Kenya); possibly
  also anciently introduced to Egypt and India
- **Current distribution:** Pantropical and warm temperate; naturalized
  in much of the Americas, Africa, South Asia, and Australia; commonly
  cultivated in Brazil, India, and China (world's largest producers)
- **Habitat preference:** Disturbed sites, riverbanks, roadsides, and
  waste areas in tropical and subtropical regions; thrives in full sun,
  deep fertile soils, and warm temperatures (20–30°C optimal);
  drought tolerant but frost sensitive; tolerates a wide range of soil
  types
- **Invasiveness:** Aggressive invader in tropical riparian zones,
  scrublands, and disturbed areas; listed as a noxious weed in parts
  of Australia, South Africa, and the United States
## Chemistry and Active Compounds
### Castor Oil
- **Oil content:** 45–55% of seed weight; 50–60% of kernel weight
- **Fatty acid composition:** Ricinoleic acid (85–95%), oleic acid
  (2–6%), linoleic acid (1–5%), palmitic acid (0.5–1%), stearic acid
  (0.5–1%)
- **Ricinoleic acid (12-hydroxy-9-cis-octadecenoic acid):** An unusual
  hydroxylated fatty acid unique to castor oil among major seed oils;
  responsible for castor oil's unique properties — high viscosity,
  solubility in alcohols, and lubricity
- **Industrial properties:** Highest viscosity of any vegetable oil;
  remains liquid at extremely low temperatures (pour point -18 to -27°C);
  excellent lubricity and dielectric strength
### Ricin
- **Structure:** Type II ribosome-inactivating protein (RIP); heterodimer
  of A-chain (ribotoxic) and B-chain (galactose-binding lectin) linked
  by a disulfide bond; molecular weight ~64 kDa
- **Mechanism:** B-chain binds to galactose-containing receptors on
  cell surfaces, facilitating endocytosis; A-chain enters cytosol and
  catalytically depurinates a specific adenine residue (A4324) in 28S
  rRNA of the 60S ribosomal subunit, irreversibly halting protein
  synthesis; a single A-chain molecule can inactivate 1,500 ribosomes
  per minute
- **Toxicity:** LD50 (oral, rat) ~20 mg/kg; LD50 (inhalation, rat)
  ~3–5 µg/kg (extremely potent); estimated lethal dose for humans
  ~0.2–1.0 mg/kg by inhalation or injection; oral toxicity is
  significantly lower due to poor absorption and B-chain degradation
  in the gut, but ingestion of 3–20 seeds can be fatal to an adult
- **Treatment:** No specific antidote; supportive care including
  activated charcoal, intravenous fluids, and monitoring for
  gastrointestinal hemorrhage; experimental ricin-neutralizing
  antibodies under development
- **Biowarfare concern:** Classified as a Schedule 1 toxic agent under
  the Chemical Weapons Convention; investigated as a bioweapon agent;
  used in assassinations (notably the 1978 umbrella murder of Georgi
  Markov in London)
## Industrial Applications of Castor Oil
- **Lubricants:** High-performance lubricants for jet engines, racing
  engines, and precision machinery; the hydroxyl group provides
  superior film-forming and boundary lubrication properties
- **Polymers and plastics:** Polyamide 11 (Nylon 11, Rilsan) from
  11-aminoundecanoic acid derived from castor oil; used in automotive
  fuel lines, medical devices, and coatings; fully bio-based nylon
- **Biodiesel:** Castor oil methyl ester (COME) meets most biodiesel
  standards but has high viscosity; typically blended with other
  feedstocks
- **Cosmetics and pharmaceuticals:** Base for lipsticks, creams, and
  ointments; castor oil USP as a stimulant laxative; component of
  modern polyurethane foams
- **Coatings and inks:** Raw material for alkyd resins, varnishes,
  and printing inks; dehydrated castor oil used in fast-drying coatings
## Medicinal and Ethnobotanical Uses
- **Castor oil (purified, ricin-free):** Traditional laxative and
  purgative; ricinoleic acid activates EP3 prostanoid receptors in
  intestinal smooth muscle, stimulating fluid secretion and peristalsis;
  also used topically for wound healing, skin conditions, and as a
  hair treatment

## Permaculture Applications
- **Windbreak:** Rapid growth makes castor bean an effective temporary
  windbreak; establishes a 2–3 m barrier in a single growing season
- **Soil improvement:** Deep taproot breaks hardpan layers; large leaf
