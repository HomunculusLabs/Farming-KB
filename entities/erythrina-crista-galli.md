## Erythrina crista-galli

**Erythrina crista-galli** L., commonly known as the **cockspur coral tree**, **ceibo**, or **coral tree**, is a deciduous tree in the family Fabaceae. It is the national tree and flower of Argentina and Uruguay.

The species is renowned for its spectacular scarlet-red flowers, which appear in dense terminal racemes before the leaves emerge. The vibrant blooms make it one of the most recognizable [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]].

It belongs to a genus of approximately 130 species, many of which share the characteristic bright red, hummingbird-pollinated flowers. The genus name derives from Greek *erythros* (red).

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Fabales
- **Family:** Fabaceae
- **Subfamily:** Faboideae
- **Genus:** *Erythrina*
- **Species:** *E. crista-galli*

The species was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1767. The specific epithet *crista-galli* means "cock's comb," referring to the curved shape of the standard petal, which resembles a rooster's crest.

**Morphological features:**

The tree typically reaches 5–8 m in height, though specimens up to 12 m are recorded. The trunk is crooked and thorny, with gray, rough bark that becomes deeply fissured with age.

Branches are armed with scattered, conical prickles. Young branches are green and slightly pubescent, becoming gray and woody in the second year.

Leaves are trifoliate with long petioles (up to 30 cm). Leaflets are broadly ovate, 5–15 cm long, with entire margins. They are deciduous, falling before flowering in spring.

The flowers are the species' most striking feature. They are arranged in terminal racemes, each 20–30 cm long, bearing 10–30 flowers.

Individual flowers are papilionaceous, with a prominent, curved standard petal (banner) up to 5 cm long. The keel petals are fused and shorter, enclosing the stamens and pistil.

Flowers are brilliant scarlet to crimson, occasionally pink or orange-tinged. They produce copious nectar, with each flower secreting 10–30 µL per day.

Fruits are pods, 10–20 cm long, constricted between seeds, dark brown at maturity. Each pod contains 3–12 kidney-shaped seeds, brown and mottled with a hard seed coat.

Pods dehisce explosively when dry, scattering seeds several meters from the parent tree. This ballistic dispersal mechanism supplements water-mediated dispersal in riparian habitats.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Erythrina crista-galli* is native to southern [[mollison-designers-gallery-forests-and-valley-bottomland-systems]] and riparian woodland communities. It often forms pure stands on seasonally inundated river margins.

The species tolerates a range of conditions from subtropical to warm temperate. It can withstand brief freezes to approximately -7°C, making it suitable for cultivation in Mediterranean climates.

It prefers moist, well-drained soils but tolerates periodic waterlogging. It is commonly found in riparian zones, gallery forests, and wetland margins.

## Ecology and Applications

The tree forms **nitrogen-fixing nodules** on its roots, associating with *Bradyrhizobium* and *Rhizobium* species. This capacity enhances soil fertility in agroforestry and riparian restoration plantings.

The flowers are adapted for **hummingbird pollination** (ornithophily). The curved standard petal serves as a perch, and the copious nectar attracts multiple hummingbird species. In its native range, it is a key nectar resource.

Butterflies and bees also visit the flowers, though hummingbirds are the primary pollinators. The bright red color is specifically tuned to avian visual perception.

The seeds are toxic, containing alkaloids similar to those found in other *Erythrina* species. Despite this, they are dispersed by water (hydrochory) in riparian habitats.

In agroforestry, the species is planted as [[mollison-designers-legume-shade-tree-pasture-systems]], and windbreak. Its rapid growth and coppicing ability make it useful for [[soil-temperature-plant-growth-rate-doubling-rule]]:** Fast, with height increments of 1–2 m per year under favorable conditions. Trees flower from seed in 3–5 years.

**Ornamental use:** Widely planted as an ornamental tree in subtropical and warm temperate gardens, parks, and street plantings. The spectacular red flower display is its primary horticultural asset.
