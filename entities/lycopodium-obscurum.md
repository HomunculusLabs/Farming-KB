## Overview

**Lycopodium obscurum** (ground pine, rare clubmoss, princess pine) is an evergreen lycophyte in the family Lycopodiaceae.

It is native to temperate and boreal forests of North America, where it forms extensive colonies on moist forest floors.

The species is named for its resemblance to small pine seedlings, with dense radial clusters of sterile branches creating a miniature tree-like silhouette.

Its spores have been used historically as a drying powder and in traditional medicine, and the species contains bioactive lycopodium alkaloids.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Lycopodium obscurum** L. was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753.

The species has been placed in several genera, including *Dendrolycopodium* and *Lycopodiella*, though *Lycopodium* remains the widely accepted placement.

Plants grow 10–25 cm tall with upright, unbranched fertile stems and dense whorls of sterile branches.

The sterile branches are arranged in tight, overlapping clusters, each 3–8 cm long, with small, scale-like microphylls 3–5 mm long.

The overall appearance resembles a miniature conifer seedling, giving rise to the common name ground pine.

Fertile stems arise from the center of the sterile branch cluster and bear terminal strobili.

Strobili are cylindrical, 2–5 cm long and 4–6 mm wide, composed of sporophylls bearing kidney-shaped sporangia.

Spores are tetrahedral, approximately 30–40 micrometers in diameter, and yellow when released in mass.

The horizontal underground stems (rhizomes) creep extensively, producing new upright shoots at intervals and forming large clonal colonies.

A single genet may cover several square meters through clonal expansion over decades.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is distributed across much of temperate North America, from Newfoundland and Quebec west to Minnesota and south to Georgia and Arkansas.

Disjunct populations occur in the Pacific Northwest and in montane regions of the Appalachian chain.

It is most abundant in mixed hardwood-conifer forests, boreal spruce-fir forests, and northern hardwood stands.

Typical habitats include moist, acidic forest floors with deep organic duff, often on slopes and ravines.

The species is associated with mature forest conditions and is sensitive to heavy disturbance, canopy removal, and soil compaction.

Elevation ranges from near sea level in the north to approximately 1800 meters in southern Appalachian populations.

It is found across USDA zones 3–7.

## Ecology and Reproduction

Reproduction is entirely spore-based, with sexual reproduction requiring a separate subterranean gametophyte generation.

The gametophyte is mycoheterotrophic, obtaining nutrition from symbiotic fungi rather than photosynthesis, and may remain underground for several years before producing gametes.

Fertilization requires a film of water for motile sperm to reach the archegonia, limiting reproductive success to consistently moist microsites.

The long-lived rhizomatous growth habit allows extensive clonal spread, and many populations consist primarily or entirely of vegetatively propagated clones.

Spore dispersal occurs from July through September and depends on wind currents from the elevated strobili.

Spore banks may persist in soil for several years, providing a reservoir for population recovery after disturbance.

The dense mats of sterile branches provide microhabitat for soil invertebrates and retain moisture in the forest floor.

The species is an indicator of old-growth or late-successional forest conditions in parts of its range.

## Ethnobotanical and Medicinal Use

The dry, highly flammable spores of *Lycopodium obscurum* have been used historically as a flash powder for theatrical effects and early photography.

Indigenous peoples of eastern North America used the plant medicinally, including the Cherokee, who applied it as a poultice for wounds and skin conditions.

The Iroquois used decoctions of the plant to treat fevers and urinary complaints.

The spores were applied as a dusting powder to prevent skin irritation and to absorb moisture in wound dressings.

The species contains lycopodium alkaloids, including lycodine and obscurine, which have attracted pharmacological interest.

Modern commercial harvest of lycopodium spores primarily targets *Lycopodium clavatum*, but *L. obscurum* has been collected when available.

The distinctive appearance of the sterile branches has made the species popular in the commercial florist trade for wreath-making and holiday decoration.

Over-collection for ornamental use has contributed to population declines in some areas.

## Conservation

**Lycopodium obscurum** is considered threatened or of special concern in several states at the margins of its range.

Habitat loss from logging, development, and forest fragmentation has reduced populations in the southern Appalachian and Great Lakes regions.

The species' dependence on mature forest conditions and slow reproductive rate limit its ability to recolonize disturbed sites.

Commercial collection for the floral trade has been identified as a significant threat in some states, leading to harvest regulations.

Conservation recommendations include protection of mature forest habitat, restrictions on wild collection, and maintenance of forest floor integrity.

The species is listed as endangered in Ohio and New Jersey and as threatened in Indiana and Tennessee.

Climate change poses a long-term threat by shifting the distribution of suitable cool, moist forest habitats northward.
