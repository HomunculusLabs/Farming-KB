# Peronospora

## Description

*Peronospora* is the largest genus of **[[downy-mildew]]** fungi, comprising
approximately **260 species** of obligate biotrophic plant parasites in
the order Peronosporales (phylum Oomycota, [[kingdom-straminipila]]). These
pathogens produce characteristic **white to gray "down"** on the
undersides of infected leaves — microscopic treelike sporangiophores
that protrude through stomata and give infected tissues a fuzzy
appearance.

Downy mildews are among the most economically important groups of plant
pathogens, causing devastating diseases on vegetables, grapes, tobacco,
and numerous other crops. *Peronospora* is the most species-rich genus
in the group and is entirely restricted to **dicotyledonous** hosts.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Straminipila (Chromista/Stramenopiles)
- **Phylum:** Oomycota
- **Class:** Oomycetes
- **Order:** Peronosporales
- **Family:** Peronosporaceae
- **Genus:** *Peronospora*

**Taxonomic note:** Although traditionally studied by mycologists and
included in fungal textbooks, downy mildews are **not true fungi**. They
belong to the Oomycota (water molds), a group of funguslike organisms
that are phylogenetically related to brown algae and diatoms rather than
to true fungi. This distinction has important implications for
fungicide selection and disease management.

## Key Facts

### Morphology and Sporulation

- Sporangiophores are **microscopic, erect, treelike, and determinate**,
  protruding singly or in tufts through stomata on leaf undersides
- The genera of downy mildews are delimited by the **characteristic
  branching patterns** of sporangiophores and the morphology of
  sporangia
- *Peronospora* produces **conidia** (not true sporangia) that germinate
  by germ tube formation rather than by releasing zoospores
- **Oospores** (sexual resting spores) are produced infrequently but can
  persist in soil and plant debris for extended periods

### Disease Symptoms

- The **downlike appearance** on leaf undersides is frequently
  accompanied by **[[chlorosis]]** (yellowing) or **necrosis** (tissue
  death) on the upper leaf surface
- All species require **free moisture** for sporulation, which is
  restricted to periods of high relative humidity and darkness
- Downy mildews can **sporulate repeatedly** for several successive
  nights from the same lesions
- For systemic infections, the sporulation period can extend for
  **several months or more** under favorable conditions
- Systemic infections may cause **stunting, distortion, and general
  plant decline**

### Important Species

- **Peronospora tabacina** — causes **blue mold of tobacco**, one of the
  most destructive tobacco diseases; responsible for epidemics that
  devastated tobacco crops in North America and Europe
- **Peronospora parasitica** (now *Hyaloperonospora parasitica*) —
  causes downy mildew of **cruciferous crops** (Brassicaceae), including
  broccoli, cabbage, and cauliflower
- **Peronospora viciae** — downy mildew of **peas and vetch**
- **Peronospora sparsa** — downy mildew of **roses and caneberries**
- **Peronospora farinosa** — affects **spinach and beet** crops

### Host Range

- 54 plant families of Dicotyledoneae serve as hosts
- *Peronospora* species are **highly host-specific**, with most species
  restricted to one or a few related host genera
- Downy mildews on monocots are caused by related genera in the
  Sclerosporales (*Sclerospora*, *Peronosclerospora*, *Sclerophthora*)

### Cultivation Limitations

- With the exception of *Verrucalvus* species, downy mildews **cannot be
  grown in pure culture**, making them exceptionally difficult to study
  experimentally
- Identifications are based primarily on **sporangiophore morphology**
  produced on host tissue

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Agricultural Impact

- Downy mildews are among the **top five most economically damaging**
  groups of plant pathogens globally
- They affect high-value crops including grapes (see *Plasmopara
  viticola*), lettuce, onions, cucurbits, and tobacco
- The **Great French Wine Blight** of the 1870s (caused by downy mildew
  *Plasmopara viticola*) led to the discovery of Bordeaux mixture —
  the first widely used fungicide

### Disease Management

- **Cultural controls:** Improve air circulation, avoid overhead
  irrigation, use resistant cultivars, rotate crops
- **Chemical controls:** Systemic and contact fungicides (phosphonates,
  phenylamides, strobilurins); note that many fungicides effective
  against true fungi are **ineffective against oomycetes** due to their
  different cell wall composition (cellulose, not chitin)
- **Biological control:** Emerging approaches using antagonistic
  bacteria and fungi show promise

### Research Value

- Downy mildews are important models for studying **obligate biotrophy**
  — a nutritional strategy that parallels rust fungi in true fungi
- The independent evolution of biotrophy in both Oomycota and true
  fungi provides insights into **convergent evolution** of pathogenic
  strategies
- The inability to culture downy mildews has driven development of
  molecular identification techniques (PCR-based detection in plant
  tissue)

### Ecological Role

- In natural ecosystems, downy mildews influence plant community
  dynamics by selectively suppressing susceptible host species
- Their requirement for free moisture links them to specific
  **microclimate conditions**, making them indicators of humid habitats
- Downy mildew epidemics are predicted to increase in frequency with
  **climate change**, as warmer temperatures and altered precipitation
  patterns favor pathogen development
