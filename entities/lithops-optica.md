## Overview

Lithops optica is a small, stemless succulent in the family Aizoaceae, native to a restricted area around Lüderitz in southwestern Namibia. It is commonly known as the "optical living stone" for its remarkably stone-like appearance and translucent windowed leaf surfaces.

The species is one of the most distinctive Lithops, prized for the ruby-red form (var. rubra) that has become iconic in succulent collections worldwide. Plants consist of a single pair of thick, fused leaves with a fissure at the top from which the flower and new leaves emerge.

Like all Lithops species, Lithops optica is a master of camouflage, closely resembling the quartz gravel and rocks among which it grows. This mimicry protects it from herbivores in its harsh desert environment.

The species was first collected by Rudolf Marloth near Lüderitzbucht in the early 1900s. It remains one of the most sought-after species in the genus by specialist collectors.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Lithops optica was first described by Marloth in 1909 and later transferred to the genus Lithops by N.E. Brown. Two varieties are recognized: var. optica with grey-green to brownish bodies, and var. rubra with vivid ruby-red to magenta coloration.

The plant body consists of a single pair of thick, opposite leaves fused into a truncate cone shape. Bodies measure 20–35 mm tall and 15–25 mm wide, with a smooth to slightly roughened surface.

The upper surface features a distinct window pattern of translucent dots and channels that allow light to penetrate to the photosynthetic tissue inside. The window pattern is more pronounced in var. rubra, where the red pigmentation contrasts with the pale translucent areas.

Flowers appear in late autumn to early winter, emerging from the central fissure. They are yellow, daisy-like, and 20–30 mm in diameter, opening in the late afternoon.

## Ecological Role

In its native Namib Desert habitat, Lithops optica plays a role in the specialized gravel plain ecosystem. Its flowers provide nectar for small desert insects during the brief autumn flowering period.

The plant's camouflage is so effective that populations can be virtually invisible even to trained observers. This crypsis reduces predation by birds, lizards, and small mammals that patrol the gravel plains.

Leaf window adaptation allows the plant to perform nearly all photosynthesis below ground level. This strategy minimizes surface area exposed to desiccating winds while maximizing light capture through the translucent tips.

## Conservation

Lithops optica is considered vulnerable due to its extremely restricted natural range and the threat of illegal collection. The ruby-red form (var. rubra) is particularly targeted by poachers.

All Lithops species are listed on CITES Appendix II, regulating international trade. Namibian law prohibits collection from the wild without a permit.

Habitat degradation from diamond mining activities near Lüderitz has impacted some populations. Coastal development and off-road vehicle use pose additional threats to the fragile gravel plain ecosystems.

Ex situ conservation is maintained through cultivated collections at botanic gardens and specialist nurseries worldwide. Seed banking provides a further safeguard against loss of wild populations.
