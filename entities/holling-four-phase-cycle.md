# Holling's Four-Phase Cycle

## Description

The Holling Four-Phase Cycle (also known as the Adaptive Cycle) is a model of ecosystem change developed by ecologist C.S. "Buzz" Holling. It describes how ecosystems cycle through four distinct phases: Conservation, Release, Reorganisation, and Exploitation. David Holmgren presents this model as a more elaborate and instructive alternative to the simpler two-phase pulsing model, and uses it as a powerful framework for understanding ecological, economic, and social change — including the rise and potential decline of global capitalism and the role permaculture might play in a post-affluent world.

## The Four Phases

### 1. Conservation
The long-lived, steady-state climax phase characterised by a high degree of interconnection between system elements, a large amount of stored biological capital, and minimal leakage of nutrients. This is the phase of maximum biomass and organisation but also maximum rigidity and vulnerability to disturbance. The system is efficient but increasingly brittle.

### 2. Release
A brief, intense pulse of disturbance that disrupts the accumulated connections and stored capital of the conservation phase. This may be triggered by fire, flood, insect plague, drought, or other catastrophic events. The release phase is typically very short in duration but releases enormous amounts of energy and nutrients that were locked in the biomass of the conservation phase.

### 3. Reorganisation
The most unstable and uncertain phase, occurring after the release. Open niches, soluble nutrients, and energy are available to be tapped or lost. During this phase there is the potential for the system to flip to a state that is either less or more productive and organised than before. This phase carries:
- The high risk of ecological drift to lower productivity (generally due to loss of mineral nutrients)
- The lower risk of major degradation (often due to coincidence of severe weather events)
- The potential for ecological invasion and possible shift to more productive systems better able to use available resources

### 4. Exploitation
Fast-growing pioneer species colonise the opportunities created by the reorganisation phase. They catch and store energy quickly and establish the patterns for the gradual build-up of biomass and greater connectedness, leading toward a new conservation phase.

## Key Facts

- The model focuses attention on the conditions within the climax that make systems vulnerable to disturbance.
- The reorganisation phase is the most critical and uncertain, with the highest risk but also the greatest opportunity.
- Applied beyond ecology to understand economic cycles, social change, and cultural evolution.
- Holmgren uses it to frame global capitalism as a "release" phase, with permaculture representing the potential of the "reorganisation" phase.
- The cycle is not necessarily balanced — systems can spiral upward or downward through successive cycles depending on conditions during reorganisation.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Design framework:** Understanding where a garden, farm, or landscape sits within the four-phase cycle helps determine appropriate management interventions.
- **Managing disturbance:** The model suggests that controlled disturbance (grazing, pruning, cultivation) can be used strategically to prevent the rigidity and vulnerability of the conservation phase.
- **Resilience building:** By maintaining systems in earlier phases of the cycle, permaculture designers can increase resilience while maintaining productivity.
- **Energy descent context:** Holmgren applies the four-phase model to argue that the current pulse of fossil-fuelled affluence represents a "release" phase, and that permaculture offers pathways for the "reorganisation" that follows.
- **Soil management:** Understanding the cycle helps explain why both over-exploitation and over-protection can be detrimental to long-term system health.
- **Social and economic design:** The model applies to community development, organisational change, and cultural evolution — all domains relevant to permaculture design.

## Connections

- Builds on the earlier two-phase pulsing model of ecosystem succession
- Related to Chaos Theory concepts of attractors and bifurcation points
- Complements the concept of ecological succession in permaculture design
- Applied by Holmgren to understand socio-economic cycles including gentrification and the rise and fall of affluence
- Informs the understanding of both ecological and social "back-to-the-land" pioneer dynamics
