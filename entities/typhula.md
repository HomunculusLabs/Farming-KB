## Typhula

## Description

*Typhula* is a genus of fungi in the family Typhulaceae that causes [[gray-snow-mold]] (also known as speckled [[snow-mold]]), a disease of turf grasses and winter cereals that develops under snow cover. The disease is particularly problematic in regions with prolonged snow cover, where it can cause significant damage to lawns, golf courses, and winter wheat crops.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Typhulaceae
- **Genus:** *Typhula*
- **Notable species:** *T. incarnata*, *T. ishikariensis*, *T. phacorrhiza*

## Key Facts

- Causes gray snow mold, which develops under snow cover on unfrozen ground.
- Disease appears as circular, grayish-white patches in the lawn when snow melts in spring.
- Infects grass blades and crowns, killing tissue under the insulating blanket of snow.
- Favored by prolonged snow cover on unfrozen ground — the snow insulates the pathogen, keeping temperatures in the active range.
- Can be suppressed by fungally dominated [[compost]] teas applied in autumn before snowfall.
- *Typhula phacorrhiza* has been investigated as a biological control agent against other *Typhula* species through mycoparasitism.
- Cultural controls include avoiding late-season nitrogen applications and improving drainage.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

*Typhula* represents a class of cold-adapted fungal pathogens that exploit the unique microenvironment created by snow cover. The insulating effect of snow keeps soil surface temperatures in a range (around 32–36°F / 0–2°C) where *Typhula* remains metabolically active while most beneficial microbes are dormant or slowed.

For cultivators in cold climates, gray snow mold management requires a preventive approach: building a diverse [[soil-food-web]] in autumn through compost tea applications ensures that beneficial fungi are present to compete with *Typhula* before snow cover arrives. This aligns with [[the-soil-food-web]] principle of using biological diversity as a disease suppression strategy.

The genus also illustrates fungal parasitism within Basidiomycota — some *Typhula* species are mycoparasites of other *Typhula* species, suggesting potential for biological control development.
