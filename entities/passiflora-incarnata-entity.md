# Passiflora incarnata

Passiflora incarnata, commonly known as maypop, purple passionflower, or wild passion vine, is a perennial climbing vine in the family Passifloraceae native to the southeastern United States and extending into Central and South America. Revered for its striking, intricate flowers and edible fruit, P. incarnata has been used for centuries in traditional medicine as a mild anxiolytic, sedative, and antispasmodic. 
  Modern pharmacological research has identified several bioactive flavonoid compounds, particularly chrysin and vitexin, that modulate GABA-A receptor activity and contribute to the plant's calming effects. Its combination of ornamental beauty, edible fruit, rapid growth, and medicinal properties makes it a valuable species in both herbal medicine and permaculture design, where it serves as a productive perennial vine for food forests, trellis systems, and wildlife habitat corridors.

## Taxonomy and Naming

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Magnoliophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Malpighiales
- **Family:** Passifloraceae
- **Genus:** Passiflora
- **Species:** P. incarnata L. (1753)
- **Common names:** Maypop, purple passionflower, wild passion vine, apricot vine, wild apricot, ocoee (Cherokee)
- **Etymology:** "Passiflora" from Latin passio (suffering) + flos (flower), referencing the Passion of Christ — the flower's structure was seen by 16th-century missionaries as symbolizing the crucifixion; "incarnata" meaning flesh-colored, referring to the corona

The name "maypop" derives from the loud popping sound the ripe fruits make when stepped on. 
  The Cherokee name "ocoee" was applied to both the plant and the Ocoee River valley in Tennessee where it grows abundantly.

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Native range:** Southeastern United States from Virginia to Florida, west to Texas and Oklahoma, north to Missouri and Illinois; also native to parts of Central America
- **Introduced/naturalized:** Cultivated and naturalized in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide, including Mediterranean Europe, South Asia, and parts of Africa
- **Habitat:** Disturbed areas, old fields, roadsides, fence rows, forest edges, stream banks, and sandy open woods
- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 6–10; dies back to ground in winter in zones 6–7, evergreen or semi-evergreen in zones 9–10
- **Soil preference:** Adaptable to a wide range of soils but prefers well-drained sandy loams with pH 6.1–7.5
- **Light requirements:** Full sun to partial shade; best flowering and fruiting in full sun

## Traditional and Ethnobotanical Use

P. incarnata has a rich history of use among indigenous peoples of the Americas
and was rapidly adopted into European and American herbal medicine:

- **Cherokee and other Southeastern tribes:** Used the roots and leaves as a poultice for boils, wounds, and inflammations; dried leaves were smoked for insomnia; root tea used for earaches and liver ailments
- **Early European settlers (18th century):** Adopted Cherokee uses; the plant entered the United States Pharmacopeia in the late 19th century as a sedative and tranquilizer
- **Eclectic medicine (19th–early 20th century):** Widely prescribed for insomnia, anxiety, epilepsy, and hysteria; included in standard eclectic formularies as a nervine tonic
- **Modern herbalism:** Primarily used as a mild anxiolytic and sleep aid; standardized extracts are common in commercial herbal supplements throughout Europe, North America, and Australia

## Pharmacology and Clinical Evidence

The anxiolytic and sedative properties of P. incarnata are primarily attributed
to flavonoid modulation of the GABAergic system. Key mechanisms include:

- **GABA-A receptor positive allosteric modulation:** Chrysin and vitexin bind to the benzodiazepine site on GABA-A receptors, enhancing chloride conductance without producing the dependence or tolerance associated with benzodiazepine drugs
- **Monoamine oxidase inhibition:** Mild MAO inhibition by harmala alkaloid constituents (clinically insignificant at typical doses)
- **Anti-inflammatory activity:** Vitexin and orientin suppress NF-κB signaling and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine production
- **Antispasmodic effects:** Direct smooth muscle relaxation demonstrated in isolated tissue preparations

Clinical studies have shown modest but consistent benefits for generalized anxiety disorder and sleep quality, with a generally favorable safety profile. 
  A 2018 meta-analysis of 7 randomized controlled trials found that passionflower extract was comparable in efficacy to oxazepam for generalized anxiety, with significantly fewer side effects. The typical therapeutic dose is 250–500 mg of standardized extract (providing ~0.5% vitexin) taken 30–60 minutes before bedtime.

## Permaculture Applications

Passiflora incarnata is an exceptionally useful plant in permaculture systems
due to its multiple functions and rapid growth:

- **Food production:** Edible fruit can be eaten fresh, made into jelly, juice, or desserts; high in vitamin C and beneficial antioxidants
- **Medicinal crop:** Leaves can be harvested and dried for tea or tincture production; a valuable medicinal perennial for home herb gardens
- **Nitrogen accumulation:** Moderate ability to accumulate nutrients from subsoil through its deep root system
- **Wildlife support:** Flowers are important nectar sources for butterflies (especially Gulf fritillary), bees, and hummingbirds; fruit is eaten by songbirds and small mammals; serves as a larval host for several butterfly species
- **Ground cover and erosion control:** Dense trailing growth suppresses weeds on banks and slopes
- **Ornamental value:** Showy flowers make it a desirable climbing vine for fences, trellises, and pergolas in food forest zone 1–2 plantings
