# Myrica pensylvanica

## Overview

**Myrica pensylvanica** Mirb., the **Northern bayberry**, is a semi-evergreen dioecious shrub in the family Myricaceae native to eastern North America.

It is valued in permaculture and native landscaping for its ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen through root-associated actinorhizal symbiosis with *Frankia* bacteria, salt tolerance, and wildlife-supporting fruit.

The waxy coating on its berries was historically harvested for candle-making, giving rise to the common name "candleberry."

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Magnoliophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Fagales
- **Family:** Myricaceae
- **Genus:** Myrica
- **Species:** M. pensylvanica

**Authority:** Charles-François Brisseau de Mirbel (1815).

**Synonyms:** *Myrica carolinensis* Mill., *Myrica cerifera* var. *pensylvanica* (Mirb.) Fern., *Morella pensylvanica* (Mirb.) Kartesz.

Modern taxonomic treatments by Kartesz (1994) and others place the species in genus *Morella*, though *Myrica* remains in widespread horticultural use.

### Morphology

**Growth habit:** Dense, rounded, multi-stemmed shrub, 0.9–2.5 m tall and wide. Spreads slowly by rhizomes to form colonies.

**Leaves:** Alternate, simple, obovate to oblanceolate, 3–8 cm long, 1.5–3 cm wide. Dark green and leathery above, lighter with resinous dots beneath.

Leaves are aromatic when crushed, emitting a spicy-balsamic scent characteristic of the Myricaceae.

**Foliage** is semi-evergreen in southern portions of its range, with leaf retention through mild winters.

**Flowers:** Dioecious, appearing April–May before or with leaf emergence. Male catkins cylindrical, 1–2.5 cm long, yellowish. Female flowers are small, inconspicuous axillary clusters.

**Fruit:** Globose drupes 4–6.5 mm diameter, grayish-white with a thick waxy coating. The wax constitutes approximately 20% of fruit dry weight. Fruit persists through winter.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to the Atlantic coast of North America, from Newfoundland and Nova Scotia south to North Carolina, with inland populations around the Great Lakes.

Primarily found in coastal dunes, sandy barrens, pine barrens, and disturbed sites with poor, acidic, sandy soils.

Highly tolerant of salt spray, making it a key species in coastal stabilization and dune restoration plantings.

Adaptable to a range of soil conditions from dry sand to moist loam, with a preference for acidic soils (pH 4.5–6.5).

Hardy to USDA Zone 3, one of the most cold-hardy nitrogen-fixing shrubs available for northern permaculture systems.

## Nitrogen Fixation

Like other Myricaceae, *M. pensylvanica* forms actinorhizal root nodules in symbiosis with nitrogen-fixing actinomycetes of the genus *Frankia*.

Nitrogen fixation rates are moderate, estimated at 10–30 kg N/ha/year depending on site conditions and plant density.

The Frankia symbiosis enables growth on nitrogen-poor sandy soils where many other woody plants struggle.

Nodules form primarily on fine lateral roots in the upper 15 cm of soil and are most active during the growing season.

## Permaculture Applications

Northern bayberry serves multiple functions in permaculture design: nitrogen fixation, erosion control, wildlife habitat, windbreak, and edible fruit production.

It is an excellent choice for the outer edge of food forest systems, providing a hardy, low-maintenance nitrogen-fixing windbreak.

The aromatic, waxy berries attract migratory birds including cedar waxwings, robins, and yellow-rumped warblers, supporting biological pest control in adjacent plantings.

Dried leaves can be used as an insect-repelling mulch for garden beds, a traditional practice documented in eastern North American folk horticulture.

Companion planting associations include pairing with fruit-bearing shrubs (blueberry, serviceberry) where bayberry provides dappled shade, nitrogen input, and wind protection.

## Cultivation

Propagated from seed (requires 60–90 days cold stratification), softwood cuttings in early summer, or division of established colonies.

Transplants readily but establishes best when planted from container stock in early spring.

Male and female plants must both be present for fruit production; a ratio of one male per 6–8 females ensures adequate pollination.

Requires minimal maintenance once established — no fertilizer needed due to nitrogen fixation, and it is drought-tolerant after the first growing season.

Tolerates severe pruning and can be shaped as a hedge, though informal natural forms are most common in permaculture settings.
