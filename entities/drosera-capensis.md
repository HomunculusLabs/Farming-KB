## Overview

**Drosera capensis**, the Cape sundew, is a perennial carnivorous plant native to the Cape Floristic Region of South Africa. It is one of the most widely cultivated sundews and has become naturalized in several regions worldwide.

The species uses sticky glandular tentacles on its leaves to capture insects. When prey contacts the mucilaginous secretion, the tentacles slowly bend toward the prey over several minutes, entrapping it more firmly.

Flowers are borne on tall, slender scapes up to forty centimeters tall. Individual flowers are approximately one centimeter in diameter, typically pink or white with five petals. Flowering occurs primarily from December through January.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Drosera capensis* is native to the Western Cape province of South Africa, occurring in a roughly 300-kilometer radius around Cape Town. Its natural habitat includes marshes, streambanks, and seasonally damp sandy areas.

The Cape Floristic Region is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the world's biodiversity hotspots. The species grows at elevations from near sea level to approximately 500 meters.

Naturalized populations have been documented in California, Florida, New Zealand, and parts of Europe. In California, populations are established in coastal marshes and pond margins.

In New Zealand, *D. capensis* has been recorded in the North Island and is monitored by biosecurity agencies. Its presence in conservation areas has raised concerns about potential impacts on native invertebrate communities.

Invasive behavior is facilitated by prolific seed production, with each flower capsule releasing 50 to 200 seeds. Seeds germinate readily without stratification, allowing rapid colonization of suitable habitats.
