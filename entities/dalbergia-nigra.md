## Overview

**Dalbergia nigra** (Vell.) Fr. Allem., commonly known as Brazilian rosewood, bahia rosewood, or jacarandá-da-bahia, is a leguminous tree species in the family Fabaceae, subfamily Faboideae. It is endemic to the Atlantic Forest of eastern Brazil.

Brazilian rosewood is renowned for producing one of the world's most valued tonewoods, prized by luthiers for its acoustic properties in classical guitar construction. The species has been heavily exploited since the colonial era, leading to severe population declines.

It is listed as **Vulnerable** on the IUCN Red List and is protected under CITES Appendix I, which prohibits international trade in wild-harvested specimens. The species was a primary driver of the original 1975 CITES convention.

Mature trees reach 15–25 meters in height with trunk diameters of 40–80 cm. The heartwood is richly pigmented, ranging from dark chocolate-brown to purplish-black with distinctive black veining patterns.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Dalbergia nigra was originally described by José Mariano da Conceição Vellozo in 1825 as *Machærium nigrum* and later transferred to the genus *Dalbergia* by Frans Allemão. The genus Dalbergia contains approximately 270 species of tropical trees and shrubs.

The genus is distributed across tropical regions of Africa, Asia, and the Americas, with the greatest diversity in [[fukuoka-pine-forest-ecosystem-collapse-cascade-matsutake]] dynamics as a canopy and subcanopy tree. It contributes to [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]] fixation. This capability enhances soil fertility and supports the growth of neighboring plants.

Flowers are pollinated primarily by bees and other insects attracted to the nectar and pollen. The timing of flowering coincides with the rainy season, ensuring optimal conditions for seed development and dispersal.

Seed dispersal is facilitated by wind, with the flat, lightweight pods being carried considerable distances from parent trees. Birds and small mammals may also contribute to secondary dispersal.

The species supports epiphytic communities in its canopy, including bromeliads, orchids, and lichens. Its dense wood provides long-lasting dead wood habitat for cavity-nesting birds and invertebrates when trees die.

Loss of Dalbergia nigra populations reduces the structural and [[fungal-functional-diversity-decomposition]] of Atlantic Forest fragments. The species' ecological roles as a nitrogen fixer and canopy component are difficult to replace once eliminated.

Habitat fragmentation has isolated remaining populations, reducing genetic exchange and increasing inbreeding depression. Ex situ conservation efforts include seed banking and living collections in botanical gardens, though germination rates decline significantly after two years of storage.
