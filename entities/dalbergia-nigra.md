## Overview

**Dalbergia nigra** (Vell.) Fr. Allem., commonly known as Brazilian rosewood, bahia rosewood, or jacarandá-da-bahia, is a leguminous tree species in the family Fabaceae, subfamily Faboideae. It is endemic to the Atlantic Forest of eastern Brazil.

Brazilian rosewood is renowned for producing one of the world's most valued tonewoods, prized by luthiers for its acoustic properties in classical guitar construction. The species has been heavily exploited since the colonial era, leading to severe population declines.

It is listed as **Vulnerable** on the IUCN Red List and is protected under CITES Appendix I, which prohibits international trade in wild-harvested specimens. The species was a primary driver of the original 1975 CITES convention.

Mature trees reach 15–25 meters in height with trunk diameters of 40–80 cm. The heartwood is richly pigmented, ranging from dark chocolate-brown to purplish-black with distinctive black veining patterns.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Dalbergia nigra was originally described by José Mariano da Conceição Vellozo in 1825 as *Machærium nigrum* and later transferred to the genus *Dalbergia* by Frans Allemão. The genus Dalbergia contains approximately 270 species of tropical trees and shrubs.

The genus is distributed across tropical regions of Africa, Asia, and the Americas, with the greatest diversity in [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]] and the Neotropics. Many Dalbergia species produce commercially valuable rosewood timber.

Brazilian rosewood is a slow-growing, semi-deciduous tree with an irregular, spreading crown. Leaves are alternate, pinnately compound, with 9–19 alternate leaflets per leaf.

Each leaflet is ovate to oblong, 2–5 cm long and 1–2 cm wide, with a slightly leathery texture. The upper surface is dark green and glossy, while the lower surface is paler with fine pubescence along the midrib.

Flowers are small, white to pale violet, borne in axillary or terminal panicles 8–15 cm long. The papilionaceous flowers have the typical Faboideae morphology with a standard, two wings, and a keel.

The fruit is an oblong, flattened legume (pod), 5–8 cm long and 1.5–2 cm wide, containing 1–3 seeds. Pods are brown at maturity and dehisce to release buoyant seeds adapted for wind dispersal.

The bark is grayish-brown, rough, and fissured, often peeling in irregular plates on older trees. Inner bark is pinkish to reddish and exudes a characteristic rose-like fragrance when cut.

## Distribution and Habitat

Dalbergia nigra is endemic to the Brazilian Atlantic Forest (Mata Atlântica), historically occurring in the states of Bahia, Espírito Santo, Minas Gerais, Rio de Janeiro, and São Paulo. Its range has been severely reduced by deforestation.

The species occurs in lowland to submontane moist tropical forests at elevations of 0–800 meters. It is found on well-drained soils in both primary and secondary forest formations.

Optimal growing conditions include annual rainfall of 1,200–2,000 mm, mean annual temperatures of 22–26°C, and fertile, acidic to neutral soils. The species is shade-tolerant in its seedling stage but requires increased light for vigorous growth.

The Atlantic Forest has been reduced to approximately 12% of its original extent, with remaining fragments highly fragmented and degraded. This habitat loss is the primary driver of Dalbergia nigra population decline.

Remaining populations are concentrated in protected areas and forest reserves in southern Bahia and northern Espírito Santo, where some relatively intact forest patches persist. Regeneration in disturbed areas is limited by the species' slow growth rate.

## Cultivation and Uses

Cultivation of Dalbergia nigra is primarily limited to conservation plantings and experimental trials. The species is slow-growing, requiring 40–60 years to produce harvestable timber, which discourages commercial plantation establishment.

Seed germination is moderately high (60–80%) but is improved by mechanical scarification or hot water treatment to break seed coat dormancy. Seedlings grow slowly, averaging 30–50 cm per year in the first five years.

The heartwood is extremely dense (specific gravity 0.80–1.01), with a fine, uniform texture and interlocking grain. It is one of the heaviest and hardest commercial timbers, with exceptional dimensional stability.

The wood's primary use is in fine musical instrument construction, particularly for classical and acoustic guitar backs and sides, piano cases, and marimba bars. It produces a warm, resonant tone with excellent projection.

Historically, the wood was extensively used in luxury furniture, cabinetmaking, turnery, and decorative veneers. The distinctive fragrance of freshly cut wood has also made it valued for perfume and aromatherapy applications.

Brazilian rosewood essential oil, distilled from wood shavings and waste material, is used in high-end perfumery as a base note. The oil has a complex, sweet, woody-rose aroma with spicy undertones.

The species' decline has created a significant gap in the supply of this essential oil. Synthetic rosewood fragrance compounds have partially replaced natural oil in commercial perfumery, though natural material remains preferred by niche fragrance houses.

CITES regulations require strict documentation and permits for any international trade involving Dalbergia nigra products. Pre-convention specimens and certified plantation-grown material are subject to reduced restrictions under CITES Appendix I provisions.

## Ecology

Dalbergia nigra plays a role in Atlantic [[fukuoka-pine-forest-ecosystem-collapse-cascade-matsutake]] dynamics as a canopy and subcanopy tree. It contributes to [[mollison-wet-tropical-forest-structure-and-polyculture]] diversity and provides habitat and food resources for various fauna.

As a member of Faboideae, the species forms root nodules with rhizobial bacteria, enabling [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]] fixation. This capability enhances soil fertility and supports the growth of neighboring plants.

Flowers are pollinated primarily by bees and other insects attracted to the nectar and pollen. The timing of flowering coincides with the rainy season, ensuring optimal conditions for seed development and dispersal.

Seed dispersal is facilitated by wind, with the flat, lightweight pods being carried considerable distances from parent trees. Birds and small mammals may also contribute to secondary dispersal.

The species supports epiphytic communities in its canopy, including bromeliads, orchids, and lichens. Its dense wood provides long-lasting dead wood habitat for cavity-nesting birds and invertebrates when trees die.

Loss of Dalbergia nigra populations reduces the structural and [[fungal-functional-diversity-decomposition]] of Atlantic Forest fragments. The species' ecological roles as a nitrogen fixer and canopy component are difficult to replace once eliminated.

Habitat fragmentation has isolated remaining populations, reducing genetic exchange and increasing inbreeding depression. Ex situ conservation efforts include seed banking and living collections in botanical gardens, though germination rates decline significantly after two years of storage.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The heartwood of Dalbergia nigra contains a rich profile of [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]], including neoflavonoids, isoflavonoids, coumarins, and volatile aromatic compounds. The distinctive fragrance is attributed to a complex mixture of terpenoids and phenolic constituents.

Key neoflavonoids identified include dalbergin, dalberginol, and 4-methoxydalbergione. These compounds are characteristic of the Dalbergia genus and contribute to the wood's natural resistance to decay and insect attack.

The essential oil composition is dominated by linalool, nerol, geraniol, and various phenylpropanoid derivatives. The oil's complex aroma profile has made it a benchmark reference in perfumery.

Extracts have demonstrated antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and certain fungi. Heartwood extracts show inhibitory effects on wood-decay fungi, correlating with the timber's natural durability.

Anti-inflammatory activity has been reported for neoflavonoid fractions in in vitro models. Cytotoxic activity against certain cancer cell lines has also been observed at elevated concentrations.

The species' conservation status severely limits research opportunities, as wild collection of plant material is prohibited. Most chemical studies have been conducted on historical specimens, museum collections, or legally acquired cultivated material.

Comparative phytochemical analysis across Dalbergia species reveals that D. nigra has a unique volatile profile distinct from Asian rosewood species. This chemical distinctiveness supports its taxonomic separation and may have implications for identifying illegal timber trade samples.
