Creeping buttercup is considered a noxious weed in many agricultural settings, particularly in damp pastures and meadows. Like other Ranunculus species, it contains protoanemonin, a toxic compound that causes blistering and irritation.

Despite its weed status, Ranunculus repens provides valuable early-season nectar resources for pollinators in [[ranunculus-acris]] and [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]]. It is less common in the southeastern states and arid western regions.

The species thrives in damp, heavy soils including wet meadows, stream banks, ditch edges, poorly drained pastures, and disturbed ground. It tolerates a wide pH range but prefers slightly acidic to neutral conditions.

Unlike Ranunculus bulbosus, which dominates dry grasslands, Ranunculus repens is a plant of moist, fertile habitats.

It is highly tolerant of trampling and soil compaction, making it a common weed of heavily trafficked agricultural land and recreational areas.

## Ecology

Ranunculus repens flowers from May to September, producing flowers over an extended period compared to other buttercup species. This prolonged flowering season makes it an important sustained nectar source.

Pollinators include bees, hoverflies, butterflies, and beetles. The glossy yellow petals reflect UV light, creating visual signals that attract insect visitors.

The extended flowering period of Ranunculus repens is ecologically significant, providing nectar continuity in grassland systems when other spring-flowering species have finished blooming.

The species reproduces both sexually via seed and vegetatively through stolon production. Vegetative spread is the primary mode of local colonization, with individual clones potentially covering several square meters.

Seed production is prolific, with a single plant capable of producing 200–500 seeds per season. Seeds persist in the soil seed bank for at least 3–5 years, complicating eradication efforts.

Ranunculus repens is an early successional species that colonizes disturbed ground rapidly but declines as taller vegetation establishes.

It is characteristic of moderately grazed pastures and mown grassland, where disturbance prevents competitive exclusion by taller species.

The plant's protoanemonin content deters most mammalian herbivores, giving it a competitive advantage in grazed ecosystems. However, it is an important food plant for the larvae of several moth species.
