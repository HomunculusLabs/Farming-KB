## Overview

**Vaccinium caesariense** Mackenz., commonly known as the New Jersey blueberry or sandy-ridge blueberry, is a deciduous shrub in the family Ericaceae.

It is native to the eastern United States, primarily the Atlantic Coastal Plain from New Jersey south to the Carolinas and Georgia.

The species is a diploid member of the Cyanococcus section and contributed genetic material to the early development of cultivated highbush blueberry (*V. corymbosum*) through natural hybridization.

Its berries are smaller than commercial cultivars but are valued for their intense flavor and high anthocyanin content.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Vaccinium caesariense** was described by William Mackenzie in 1932.

It belongs to section *Cyanococcus* within genus *Vaccinium*, characterized by berries with a persistent calyx and a ten-loculed ovary.

The species is diploid (2n = 24), distinguishing it from the tetraploid *V. corymbosum* (2n = 48) and hexaploid *V. corymbosum* var. *angustifolium* complex.

**Growth habit:** The shrub typically reaches 0.5–1.5 meters in height with a spreading, multi-stemmed form.

**Leaves:** Elliptic to obovate, 2–5 cm long, with finely serrate margins and a glaucous underside.

**Flowers:** Urceolate, white to pinkish, borne in axillary racemes of 4–10 flowers in early to mid-spring (April–May).

**Fruit:** Round berries, 5–9 mm in diameter, blue to black with a waxy bloom, ripening mid-summer (June–July).

The calyx is prominently persistent on the ripe fruit, a key distinguishing feature from some co-occurring *Vaccinium* species.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Vaccinium caesariense* occurs along the Atlantic Coastal Plain from central New Jersey through the Delmarva Peninsula to [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] Carolina and northern South Carolina.

Disjunct populations have been reported from the Piedmont of New Jersey and southeastern Pennsylvania.

It grows in sandy, acidic soils of pine barrens, coastal heaths, and oak-pine woodlands at elevations below 200 meters.

The species is adapted to low-nutrient, well-drained sands with pH 4.0–5.5, often found alongside *[[vaccinium-angustifolium]]* and *[[gaylussacia-baccata]]*.

It is intolerant of heavy clay soils and prolonged flooding, restricting its natural range to coarse-textured substrates.

## Ecology

The flowers are pollinated by native bees, particularly bumblebees (*Bombus* spp.) and small solitary bees in the families Andrenidae and Halictidae.

Fruits are consumed by a variety of birds including American robin, eastern bluebird, gray catbird, and wild turkey.

Mammalian dispersers include black bear, white-tailed deer, red fox, and raccoon, which contribute to seed distribution across the landscape.

The species is an important component of the Pine Barrens understory, providing both cover and forage for wildlife.

It resprouts vigorously after fire, and periodic burning maintains open canopy conditions favorable to its growth and reproduction.

Leaf litter acidity from *V. caesariense* contributes to soil podzolization in sandy coastal plain ecosystems.

The species serves as a host for several eriophyid mites and leaf-mining Lepidoptera that are specialized on Ericaceae foliage.

It participates in [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] associations with *[[dighton-ericoid-mycorrhizal-nutrient-acquisition]] fungi*, which enhance [[aact-microbial-foliar-nutrient-uptake-co2-stomata-ingham]] in nutrient-poor sandy soils.

These mycorrhizal partnerships are critical for [[comparison-phosphorus-solubilizing-microorganisms-vs-mycorrhizal-phosphorus-acquisition]] and are shared across the Ericaceae family.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

The berries are edible raw and have a sweet-tart flavor that is often described as more concentrated and complex than commercial highbush blueberries.

They can be used in jams, jellies, pies, muffins, and other baked goods, though their small size makes commercial harvesting impractical.

[[fukuoka-textdoc-fallacy-infinite-nutritional-analysis]] indicates high levels of anthocyanins, particularly delphinidin and cyanidin glycosides, which contribute antioxidant capacity.

Vitamin C content is comparable to other wild blueberries at approximately 10–15 mg per 100 g of fresh fruit.

The species has limited commercial use but is of interest for breeding programs seeking to introduce novel flavor compounds and [[endophyte-mediated-plant-stress-tolerance]] into cultivated lines.

Indigenous peoples of the Mid-Atlantic coast historically harvested the berries as a seasonal food source, though specific ethnobotanical records are sparse.
