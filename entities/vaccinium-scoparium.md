## Overview

**Vaccinium scoparium** Leiberg ex Coville, commonly known as the grouseberry or littleleaf huckleberry, is a low-growing deciduous shrub in the family Ericaceae.

It is native to the mountains of western [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], forming extensive low mats in subalpine coniferous forests and meadows.

Its tiny red to orange berries are an important food for ruffed grouse, hence the common name, and are also consumed by bears, deer, and songbirds.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Vaccinium scoparium** was described by J.B. Leiberg and formally published by Frederick Vernon Coville in 1897.

The species epithet *scoparium* means "broom-like," referring to the dense, twiggy branching pattern of the plant.

It is a diploid species (2n = 24) within section *Cyanococcus*, closely related to *V. cespitosum* and *V. deliciosum*.

**Growth habit:** Extremely low and mat-forming, typically 3–10 cm tall, with dense interlaced stems creating a carpet-like cover.

**Leaves:** Small and oval to round, 0.5–1.5 cm long, with entire or finely toothed margins, bright green and often reddish-tinged in autumn.

**Flowers:** Small, urn-shaped, pink to red, borne singly or in pairs in the leaf axils, blooming in mid-summer (June–July).

**Fruit:** Very small round berries, 4–6 mm in diameter, red to orange-red when ripe, without the prominent waxy bloom of many blue-fruited *Vaccinium* species.

The fruit ripens from August through September, persisting on the plant into early autumn.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Vaccinium scoparium* occurs in the Cascade Range from southern British Columbia to northern California and in the Rocky Mountains from Montana to Colorado.

It is also found in the Sierra Nevada of California and in isolated mountain ranges of eastern Washington and Oregon.

The species grows in subalpine and montane coniferous forests, typically at elevations of 1,200–3,000 meters.

It colonizes open forest floors, rocky slopes, avalanche chutes, and recently burned areas where canopy openness provides sufficient light.

Preferred soils are acidic, well-drained sandy or gravelly loams derived from volcanic or granitic parent material.

The species is tolerant of shallow soils and exposed sites, frequently occupying gaps between rocks and on morainal deposits.
