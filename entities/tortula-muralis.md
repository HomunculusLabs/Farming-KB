## Overview

Tortula muralis, commonly known as wall screw-moss, is a saxicolous acrocarpous moss in the family Pottiaceae. It is one of the most frequently encountered mosses on walls, paving stones, and concrete surfaces throughout [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]].

The species is instantly recognizable by its distinctive capsule, which remains attached to the seta long after spore release. The empty capsule, with its elongated neck and contracted mouth, resembles a miniature urn or amphora.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Tortula muralis was described by Johann Hedwig in 1801. The genus Tortula in the broad sense has been subject to significant taxonomic revision, with many species transferred to Syntrichia, Hennediella, and other genera.

Recent molecular phylogenetic analyses place T. muralis within the Syntrichia clade. Some authors advocate merging Tortula muralis into Syntrichia, though the name Tortula muralis remains widely used in floristic works.

The gametophyte forms loose to dense turfs, 0.5–2 cm tall. Stems are erect and simple, arising from a branched protonemal system.

Leaves are ovate to obovate, 2–3 mm long, with a short, bluntly pointed apex. The costa is strong and extends to near the leaf tip, but unlike [[syntrichia-ruralis]], the leaves lack a well-developed hair point.

The leaf margins are recurved from near the base, and the lamina is constricted below the apex, giving the leaf a distinctive trumpet-shaped appearance when viewed from above.

Laminal cells are densely papillose with multiplex papillae. The papillosity is most pronounced in the upper leaf and decreases toward the base.

The leaf color ranges from yellowish-green when moist to dark grayish-green when dry. This color change, combined with the constricted leaf shape, makes the species readily identifiable in the field even without capsules.

The capsule is cylindrical to ellipsoidal, 2–3 mm long, on a yellowish seta of 8–15 mm. The capsule neck is distinctly narrowed below the mouth, creating the species' characteristic profile.

The peristome teeth are divided nearly to the base and spirally twisted when dry. The operculum is long-rostrate. Capsules persist on the plant for many months after spore release.

This persistent capsule is the origin of several of the species' common names, including "wall screw-moss." The empty capsule with its contracted mouth and elongated neck is one of the most recognizable moss structures in temperate urban environments.

## Distribution and Habitat

T. muralis has a widespread distribution across Europe, [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]], occurring in southeastern Australia, New Zealand, and parts of South America.

The species is considered native to Europe and western Asia but has been spread globally through human-mediated dispersal on building stone and horticultural materials. Its introduction history in the [[guzman-allen-gartz-northern-southern-hemisphere-mycobiota-convergence-mexico]] dates to the colonial period.

The species is overwhelmingly saxicolous, occurring on brick walls, concrete, mortar, limestone, and sandstone. It is among the most common mosses of [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].

The persistent capsule is a key ecological adaptation, functioning as a spore bank. Spores are released gradually over months, ensuring dispersal across multiple rain events rather than a single episode.

The species contributes to the biodeterioration of mortar and concrete through rhizoid penetration and organic acid secretion. This process is accelerated on older, weathered masonry with deteriorating mortar joints.

However, the moss cover also provides a protective microclimate that can reduce surface temperature fluctuations. In conservation contexts, moss removal from historic masonry is debated due to these competing effects.

Invertebrate communities within T. muralis turfs include tardigrades, collembolans, and mite species adapted to the xeric conditions of wall surfaces. These communities are less diverse than those of more mesic moss species.

[[fungal-life-cycles-sexual-reproduction-metamorphosis]] involves autoicous or cladautoicous inflorescences. Sporophyte production is abundant in favorable moisture conditions, and mature capsules are present for much of the year.

## Desiccation Tolerance

T. muralis exhibits considerable desiccation tolerance, though less extreme than its close relative Syntrichia ruralis. It can survive air-dry conditions for weeks to months and recover photosynthetic function within an hour of rehydration.

The species accumulates sucrose and trehalose during drying, which stabilize cellular membranes in the absence of liquid water. These sugar concentrations increase progressively as water content decreases.

Chloroplast structure is partially disassembled during drying but reconstituted rapidly upon rewatering. The speed of chloroplast recovery is correlated with the pre-desiccation carbohydrate reserves of the plant.

Respiratory metabolism resumes within minutes of rehydration, preceding the recovery of photosynthesis by 15–30 minutes. This sequential recovery pattern is consistent across populations from diverse climatic origins.
