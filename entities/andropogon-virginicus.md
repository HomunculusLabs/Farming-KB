## Overview

Andropogon virginicus, commonly known as broomsedge bluestem or broomsedge, is a perennial bunchgrass native to the eastern and central United States.

It is one of the most recognizable and widespread old-field indicator species in the southeastern United States, often dominating abandoned [[fukuoka-textdoc-agricultural-land-law-obstruction-return-farming]].

Despite its common name, broomsedge is not a sedge but a true grass belonging to the tribe Andropogoneae.

The species produces distinctive silvery-white, fluffy seed heads in autumn that persist through winter, giving it considerable ornamental value in naturalistic landscapes.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Andropogon virginicus L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753.

The specific epithet refers to the state of Virginia, within the species' native range.

Plants form loose to dense bunches of erect to slightly arching culms reaching 60 to 150 centimeters in height.

Leaf blades are flat to slightly involute, 3 to 8 millimeters wide, and 20 to 50 centimeters long, with a prominent midrib.

Basal leaves are typically shorter and denser than those on the flowering culms.

The inflorescence is a terminal panicle composed of several pairs of racemes, each bearing spikelets subtended by long, silky hairs.

At maturity, the entire panicle takes on a silvery, cottony appearance due to the elongated rachis hairs of the pedicellate spikelets.

Reproduction is primarily by seed, though plants can slowly increase in size through tillering at the base.

## Distribution and Habitat

Andropogon virginicus is native to the eastern and central United States, from New York and Massachusetts south to Florida and west to Texas, Oklahoma, and Kansas.

It also occurs in parts of Mexico, [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-caribbean-central-america]], and has been introduced in Hawaii, Japan, Australia, and parts of Africa.

The species is a classic old-field successional grass, aggressively colonizing disturbed sites including abandoned farmland, roadsides, logged areas, and burned sites.

It is adapted to a wide range of soil conditions, from acidic, nutrient-poor sands to heavier loams, but strongly prefers low-fertility sites.

Broomsedge reaches its greatest abundance on acidic soils with pH below 5.5, where it outcompetes more nutrient-demanding species.

It is tolerant of both drought and periodic flooding, reflecting its broad ecological amplitude.

In fire-prone ecosystems, broomsedge increases after burning due to its ability to resprout from the basal crown and its prolific [[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]].

## Ecological Role

Andropogon virginicus is a dominant species in old-field succession across the southeastern Piedmont and coastal plain.

It typically appears within one to three years after agricultural abandonment and can persist for decades in the absence of further disturbance.

The species is considered an indicator of low soil fertility and acidic conditions, and its presence often signals the need for lime and fertilizer in agricultural contexts.

Broomsedge tussocks provide nesting cover and shelter for a variety of wildlife, including quail, meadowlarks, sparrows, and small mammals.

The fluffy seed heads are used as nesting material by some bird species.

However, dense broomsedge stands can suppress the establishment of more palatable forage species and desirable native wildflowers.

In prescribed [[permaculture-fire-management]], broomsedge is an important fuel source that carries fire through old-field and pine savanna ecosystems.

The species serves as a larval host for several skipper butterflies and supports a modest diversity of grass-feeding insects.

## Forage and Agricultural Significance

Broomsedge is considered a low-quality forage grass because its nutritional value declines rapidly after maturity.

Crude protein content drops from approximately 8 to 10 percent during early growth to below 4 percent by the time seed heads appear.

Livestock will graze young shoots in spring but avoid mature plants, which become coarse and unpalatable.
