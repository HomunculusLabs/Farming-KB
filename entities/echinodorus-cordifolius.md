## Overview

Echinodorus cordifolius is a perennial aquatic plant in the family Alismataceae, commonly known as the creeping burhead or cordate burhead. It is native to the southeastern United States, Mexico, Central America, the Caribbean, and northern South America.

The species is widely cultivated in the aquarium and water garden trades for its large, cordate (heart-shaped) leaves and tall inflorescences. It is one of the most frequently encountered Echinodorus species in both ornamental and wild settings.

Echinodorus cordifolius exhibits significant morphological plasticity depending on growing conditions. Emergent forms produce large aerial leaves up to 30 cm long, while submerged forms develop narrower, more elongated foliage.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Angiosperms → Monocots → Alismatales → Alismataceae → Echinodorus cordifolius (L.) Griseb.

The species was originally described as Alisma cordifolium by Carl Linnaeus in 1759. It was transferred to the genus Echinodorus by August Heinrich Rudolf Grisebach in 1857.

Synonyms include Echinodorus radicans (Nuttall) Engelmann and Echinodorus rostratus (Nuttall) Engelmann ex Small. Several taxonomic revisions have proposed different infrageneric placements within Echinodorus.

**Emersed growth form** produces stout, creeping rhizomes with thick fibrous roots. Petioles are triangular in cross-section and can reach 40–100 cm in length.

Leaf blades are broadly ovate to cordate, measuring 10–30 cm long and 8–20 cm wide. The leaf base is deeply cordate with rounded to slightly overlapping basal lobes.

Leaf surfaces are medium green, smooth, and slightly glossy with 5–9 prominent longitudinal veins. Leaf margins are entire or slightly undulate, with no serration.

**Inflorescences** are tall, branching panicles rising 50–150 cm above the water surface. Peduncles are triangular, robust, and typically bear whorls of adventitious plantlets at the lower nodes.

Flowers are hermaphroditic, 1.5–2.5 cm in diameter, with three green sepals and three white to pale pink petals. Each flower has 15–24 stamens arranged in multiple whorls and a compound pistil of numerous free carpels.

Fruits are small achenes arranged in tight, sessile heads. Each achene is 2–3 mm long with a short, terminal beak and lateral ribs.

## Distribution and Habitat

Echinodorus cordifolius ranges from the southeastern United States (Texas, Louisiana, Florida, Georgia, and the Carolinas) south through Mexico to Panama. It also occurs throughout the Caribbean islands and in northern South America including Colombia, Venezuela, and Brazil.

The species has been introduced and naturalized in parts of Southeast Asia, particularly Thailand, where it colonizes rice paddies and irrigation canals. Naturalized populations also exist in parts of tropical Africa and the Indian subcontinent.

It typically grows in shallow, slow-moving or still freshwater habitats at elevations below 500 meters. Common sites include marshes, swamps, ditches, pond margins, and the edges of slow streams.

Echinodorus cordifolius tolerates a wide range of water conditions but prefers neutral to slightly acidic pH (6.0–7.5) and moderate hardness. It grows in full sun to partial shade.

The species is adapted to both permanently inundated and seasonally drawdown conditions. During dry periods, emersed plants persist via their rhizomes and quickly regenerate when water returns.

## Cultivation

Echinodorus cordifolius is one of the most undemanding aquarium plants and is recommended for beginners. It grows well in a wide range of aquarium conditions with moderate to high lighting.

Substrate should be nutrient-rich, as the species is a heavy root feeder. A layer of laterite or clay-based substrate beneath gravel is beneficial for sustained growth.

In the aquarium, temperatures between 20–28 °C are optimal. Growth accelerates noticeably in warmer conditions above 25 °C and slows below 18 °C.

Propagation occurs readily through the adventitious plantlets that form on the inflorescence. These plantlets develop roots while still attached and can be separated once they reach 5–8 cm in diameter.

Division of the rhizome is another effective propagation method. Each division should include at least one growing point and several roots.

Emersed cultivation is common in water gardens and bog filters. In these settings, the plant produces its characteristic large aerial leaves and tall flower spikes.

Growth rate is moderate to fast depending on nutrient availability. Under optimal conditions with CO₂ supplementation, new leaves emerge weekly and plantlets may form continuously.

Adequate iron supplementation is particularly important for maintaining vibrant green leaf coloration. Iron deficiency manifests as chlorosis between the leaf veins while the veins themselves remain dark green.

The species can become quite large in the aquarium, requiring tanks of at least 200 liters for mature specimens. Regular removal of older leaves and spent inflorescences maintains a tidy appearance.

## Ecological Role

Echinodorus cordifolius provides important habitat structure in shallow freshwater ecosystems. Its dense stands offer shelter for juvenile fish, amphibian larvae, and aquatic invertebrates.

The broad leaves create shaded microhabitats that help moderate water temperature in shallow ponds and marshes. This shading effect can reduce algal growth and improve conditions for other submerged species.

In its native range, the flowers are visited by various pollinators including bees, flies, and beetles. The seeds and plantlets serve as food sources for waterfowl and herbivorous fish.

The species contributes to nutrient cycling by absorbing dissolved nitrogen and phosphorus from the water column through its roots. Dense root systems also help stabilize soft sediments along pond margins.

Echinodorus cordifolius can form extensive monocultures that outcompete native vegetation in disturbed habitats. In Thailand and other introduced ranges, it is considered a minor agricultural weed in rice paddies.

The adventitious plantlets on flowering stems facilitate rapid vegetative spread across suitable habitat. This reproductive strategy allows single plants to colonize large areas within a single growing season.

Echinodorus cordifolius also reproduces sexually through seed production. Seeds germinate readily on moist substrate and seedlings establish quickly in open, muddy areas with full sun exposure.

## Related Species

Echinodorus cordifolius belongs to the subgenus Echinodorus within the genus. It is closely related to Echinodorus floribundus and Echinodorus palaefolius, with which it has sometimes been confused in herbarium collections.

Echinodorus amazonicus (commonly sold as Echinodorus bleheri) is a more strictly aquatic species with narrower, lanceolate leaves. It does not produce the tall emersed inflorescences characteristic of Echinodorus cordifolius.

Sagittaria latifolia is a member of the same family (Alismataceae) but belongs to a different genus. Sagittaria species typically have sagittate (arrow-shaped) rather than cordate leaves.

Echinodorus grisebachii (also known as Echinodorus amazonicus or Echinodorus parviflorus) is a popular aquarium species with smaller, more compact growth. It rarely exceeds 30 cm in height.

Echinodorus macrophyllus is a larger South American species with broader leaves and a more southern distribution. It is less common in cultivation than Echinodorus cordifolius.

Echinodorus uruguayensis is a distinctive species with narrow, undulate leaves that develops a reddish hue under intense lighting. It is sought after by planted aquarium enthusiasts for its unique texture and coloration.
