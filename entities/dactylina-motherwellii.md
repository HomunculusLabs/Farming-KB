## Overview

Dactylina motherwellii is a rare terricolous lichen in the family Parmeliaceae, known from Arctic and subarctic regions of [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], Greenland, and northern Europe. It is one of the less frequently collected macrolichens in the Arctic, owing to its cryptic growth habit and restricted habitat preferences.

The species was named in honor of a Scottish lichenologist and has been the subject of relatively few ecological or taxonomic studies compared to more common Arctic lichens.

The species forms a distinctive brown to dark brown, coral-like thallus composed of repeatedly branching, hollow lobes arising from a basal holdfast. Dactylina motherwellii grows primarily on sandy or silty soils in dry, exposed tundra habitats.

The coral-like branching pattern gives the species a distinctive appearance in the field, although it is easily overlooked when growing among other terricolous lichens in sparsely vegetated tundra.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Dactylina motherwellii was originally described from Arctic North America and has since been reported from scattered localities across the circumpolar Arctic. The genus Dactylina was historically placed in the family Stereocaulaceae but has been reclassified to Parmeliaceae based on molecular phylogenetic evidence.

This reclassification reflects the broader reshuffling of lichen family boundaries driven by DNA sequence data, which has significantly revised our understanding of relationships within the Lecanoromycetes.

The genus contains only a few species, with [[dactylina-arctica]] being the most widespread and commonly encountered. Dactylina motherwellii is distinguished from Dactylina arctica by differences in branching pattern, lobe width, and secondary chemistry.

Some authors have suggested that Dactylina motherwellii may represent an ecological variant or chemical race of Dactylina arctica, but molecular data support its recognition as a distinct species.

The thallus is fruticose to subfruticose, forming erect to ascending tufts 15 to 40 mm tall from a compact basal cushion. The branches are cylindrical to slightly flattened, 0.5 to 1.5 mm in diameter, and brown to dark brown in color.

When moist, the branches become somewhat pliable and darker in color, but in dry conditions they are brittle and can break easily during collection. This fragility contributes to the species being undercollected.

Branching is dichotomous to anisotomous, with the tips often becoming narrower and slightly curved. The branch surface is smooth to slightly wrinkled, lacking the dense covering of phyllocladia found in Stereocaulon species.

The absence of phyllocladia is one of the key field characters separating Dactylina from Stereocaulon, as the latter genus almost invariably produces these small, leaf-like propagules on its branches.

The medulla is hollow in mature branches, a characteristic feature of the genus Dactylina. The cortex is well-developed, approximately 50 to 80 micrometers thick.

The cortical structure consists of densely packed [[fungal-hyphae]] arranged in a prosoplectenchymatous pattern, providing [[comparison-silicon-vs-calcium-in-plant-defense-and-structural-support]] and protection against desiccation and UV radiation.

Apothecia are rare but when present occur terminally on branch tips, measuring 2 to 5 mm in diameter. The disc is brown to dark brown and convex. Ascospores are broadly ellipsoid to subglobose.

Mature ascospores measure approximately 12 to 18 micrometers in length and are hyaline, non-septate, and thin-walled. [[spore-production-geotropic-sensitivity-coupling-basidiomycete-badham-1982]] appears to be infrequent, which may contribute to the species' rarity.

The species does not produce soredia or isidia, relying entirely on ascospore dispersal for propagation across its scattered Arctic range.

This reliance on [[basidiomycete-mating-systems-sexual-reproduction]] may limit the species' ability to colonize new habitats, as ascospores must land on suitable substrate and establish a functional symbiosis with compatible Trebouxia photobionts.

## Distribution and Habitat

Dactylina motherwellii has a circumpolar Arctic distribution, with confirmed records from northern Alaska, the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, Greenland, Svalbard, and northern Scandinavia. It is considered rare throughout its range, with most populations consisting of only a few scattered individuals.

The rarity of Dactylina motherwellii may reflect genuine ecological scarcity or may be partly artifactual due to its cryptic appearance and the remoteness of its habitats, which are infrequently surveyed by lichenologists.

The species occurs on dry, well-drained sandy or silty soils in exposed tundra habitats. It is typically found on river terraces, sand eskers, morainic deposits, and stabilized dune systems.

These geomorphological features provide the well-drained, mineral-rich substrates that the species requires. Populations are often localized, reflecting the patchy distribution of suitable habitat across the Arctic landscape.

Elevation ranges from near sea level in coastal tundra to approximately 500 meters in inland Arctic locations. It avoids wet habitats and waterlogged substrates entirely.

Excessive moisture appears to be detrimental to the species, as it is never found in areas with prolonged snow cover or in water-saturated soils even within otherwise suitable Arctic habitats.

Dactylina motherwellii shows a preference for sites with minimal vascular plant cover, often growing in association with other terricolous lichens such as [[cetraria-islandica]] and Stereocaulon species.

These lichen associations form low-diversity but ecologically important communities on dry Arctic soils, contributing to soil stability and surface [[blesching-cannabis-fever-temperature-regulation]] through their insulating mat-like growth.

## Chemistry and Secondary Metabolites

Dactylina motherwellii contains a distinctive combination of secondary metabolites that separates it from other Dactylina species. The primary compounds include atranorin in the cortex and protolichesterinic acid and fumarprotocetraric acid in the medulla.

The combination of atranorin in the cortex with both protolichesterinic acid and fumarprotocetraric acid in the medulla is unusual among Arctic macrolichens and provides a reliable diagnostic profile for the species.

Spot tests yield K+ yellow on the cortex due to atranorin and P+ yellow to red on the medulla from fumarprotocetraric acid. The C test is negative.

The spot test profile provides a rapid [[palmer-mushroom-hunting-field-identification]] method, although chemical overlap with some Dactylina arctica populations requires [[reversed-phase-hplc-tlc-confirmation-psilocybin-psilocin-quantification-bigwood-beug]] for definitive identification.

The presence of protolichesterinic acid is a key diagnostic character that distinguishes Dactylina motherwellii from Dactylina arctica, which typically lacks this compound. Thin-layer chromatography is recommended for confirmation.

Protolichesterinic acid has been studied for its potential antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties, although the [[phytochrome-ecological-function-dusk-dawn-red-far-red-light-switch]] of this compound in Dactylina motherwellii remains speculative.
