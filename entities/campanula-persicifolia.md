## Campanula persicifolia

## Overview

Campanula persicifolia, the peach-leaved bellflower, is a clump-forming perennial in the family Campanulaceae. It is native to Europe and western Asia, growing in meadows, woodland margins, and rocky slopes.

The species is widely cultivated for its large, outward-facing blue or white bell-shaped flowers. It is considered a classic cottage garden plant and has been in cultivation since the 1500s.

Plants reach 60–90 cm in height with erect, slender stems arising from a basal rosette. The specific epithet "persicifolia" refers to the narrow, lance-shaped basal leaves that resemble those of a peach tree ([[mushroom-natural-habitats]] include semi-dry grasslands, open deciduous woodlands, and forest clearings. It also colonizes rocky limestone outcrops at elevations from sea level to 2000 m.

The species prefers neutral to slightly alkaline soils with good drainage. It tolerates light shade but flowers most prolifically in full sun.

It is classified as Least Concern on most European national red lists. Local declines have occurred where grassland management has been abandoned, allowing succession to scrub.

In the United Kingdom, it is a well-established garden escape in parts of southern England. Populations in North America are occasional garden escapes, primarily in the northeastern states and [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

## Ecological Role

The open bell-shaped flowers are accessible to a wide range of pollinators. Bumblebees (Bombus spp.) and leafcutter bees (Megachile spp.) are the most frequent visitors.

The flowers produce nectar at the base of the corolla tube, rewarding long-tongued pollinators most effectively. Short-tongued bees sometimes bite through the corolla base to access nectar, a behavior known as nectar robbing.

In European meadow communities, C. persicifolia contributes to structural diversity with its tall flowering stems and rosette of basal leaves. It is a minor but consistent component of species-rich calcareous grasslands.

The species is a host plant for the mirid bug Kalokoroscos convexus in parts of its native range. Seed dispersal is primarily by gravity and wind, with seeds shaken from the basal pores as the stem sways.
