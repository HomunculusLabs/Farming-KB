# alpha-Amanitin

**alpha-Amanitin** is a cyclic octapeptide toxin and the most potent of the amatoxins, a
family of bicyclic peptide toxins found in several species of Amanita, particularly the
death cap ([[amanita-verna]]), and the
destroying angel complex (amanita virosa, amanita bisporigera).

It is one of the most lethal natural toxins known to science, with a mechanism of action that
targets the fundamental process of gene transcription in all eukaryotic cells. Its discovery
and characterization have had profound implications for molecular biology, cell biology, and
toxicology.

## Chemical Structure

alpha-Amanitin is a bicyclic octapeptide with the molecular formula C39H54N10O14S and a
molecular weight of approximately 918.97 g/mol. It consists of eight amino acid residues
arranged in a unique structure containing both a macrocyclic ring and a cross-bridge formed
by a tryptophan-derived sulfoxide moiety.

The eight amino acids in alpha-amanitin are: L-isoleucine, D-isoleucine,
(2S,3R,4S)-4,5-dihydroxyisoleucine (a unique non-proteinogenic amino acid),
L-gamma-hydroxyasparagine, L-asparagine, L-proline, L-tryptathionine (a modified
tryptophan-cysteine bridge forming the bicyclic structure), and glycine.

The bicyclic structure, comprising a 25-membered macrocyclic ring and a 6-membered
sulfoxide-containing bridge, is essential for its biological activity. This rigid, compact
structure allows alpha-amanitin to bind tightly and selectively to its molecular target, RNA
polymerase II, with nanomolar affinity.

The absolute configuration of the sulfoxide bridge is critical: the natural (S)-sulfoxide is
the biologically active form, while the (R)-sulfoxide epimer has greatly reduced toxicity.

## Physicochemical Properties

alpha-Amanitin is a white to off-white crystalline solid at room temperature. It is highly
polar and water-soluble, with limited solubility in organic solvents such as ethanol and
methanol, and is essentially insoluble in non-polar solvents like hexane and chloroform.

It is notably resistant to heat, acid, and enzymatic degradation. These properties are
critically important to its toxicity: the toxin survives cooking temperatures (even prolonged
boiling), gastric acid (pH 1 to 3), and digestive enzymes (pepsin, trypsin, chymotrypsin)
intact, reaching the systemic circulation and target organs in its fully active form.

alpha-Amanitin is not destroyed by drying, freezing, or prolonged storage, meaning that even
dried specimens of [[amanita-phalloides]] typically contains 5 to 8 mg of total
amatoxins, of which alpha amanitin is the predominant component (typically 40 to 60
percent of the total). This means that a single mushroom contains more than enough toxin to
cause fatal poisoning in an adult human.

The LD50 in mice is approximately 0.3 to 0.7 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection, and
approximately 20 mg/kg by oral administration (the lower oral toxicity reflects reduced
gastrointestinal absorption in rodents compared to humans).

## Pharmacokinetics

After oral ingestion, alpha amanitin is absorbed through the intestinal epithelium and
enters the portal circulation. The toxin is selectively taken up by hepatocytes via the
organic anion transporting polypeptide (OATP) transporters, particularly OATP1B1 and
OATP1B3, which are highly expressed on the sinusoidal membrane of hepatocytes.

This selective hepatic uptake explains the liver's extreme susceptibility to the toxin and
the rapid development of hepatic necrosis. Once inside hepatocytes, alpha-amanitin is
concentrated in the nucleus where it binds to RNA polymerase II.

The toxin undergoes minimal metabolism and is primarily excreted by the kidneys, with some
biliary excretion also occurring. Its elimination half-life is approximately 24 to 48 hours
in humans, meaning that significant toxin levels persist in the body for several days after
ingestion.

## Treatment Considerations

Several therapeutic strategies have been developed to counteract alpha amanitin poisoning,
though none provide a complete antidote:

- **Silibinin (silybin):** A flavonolignan derived from milk thistle (silybum marianum),
  silibinin is the most effective specific therapy currently available. It competitively
  inhibits the OATP-mediated uptake of amatoxins into hepatocytes and may also stimulate
  RNA polymerase activity. Intravenous silibinin at doses of 20 to 50 mg/kg/day has
  significantly improved survival rates in clinical studies.
