## Cesium-137

## Description

Cesium-137 (¹³⁷Cs) is a radioactive isotope of cesium with a half-life of approximately 30.17 years. It is a major fission product of uranium and plutonium, released into the environment primarily through nuclear weapons testing and nuclear accidents such as Chernobyl (1986) and Fukushima (2011). In [[fungal-ecology]], ¹³⁷Cs is of particular interest because many fungal species actively accumulate and immobilize this radionuclide from contaminated soils.

## Classification

- **Category**: Radionuclide / Radioactive Isotope
- **Element**: Cesium (Cs), atomic number 55
- **Mass Number**: 137
- **Half-life**: 30.17 years
- **Decay Mode**: Beta decay to Barium-137m, then gamma emission
- **Chemical Analog**: Potassium (K⁺), which explains its biological uptake

## Key Facts

- Released globally via atmospheric nuclear weapons testing (1950s–1960s) and localized events such as the Chernobyl disaster.
- Fungi are major biological sinks for radiocesium in terrestrial ecosystems (Olsen et al., 1990).
- Uptake and accumulation of ¹³⁷Cs by upland grassland soil fungi documented as a potential pool of Cs immobilization (Dighton et al., 1991).
- Ectomycorrhizal and [[saprotrophic-fungi]] can concentrate ¹³⁷Cs to levels significantly exceeding surrounding soil concentrations.
- Chemical similarity to potassium (K⁺) facilitates uptake through potassium transport channels in fungal cell membranes.
- Persists in forest ecosystems for decades due to fungal immobilization and recycling through organic [[soil-horizons]].

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Radiocesium Accumulation**: Fungal fruiting bodies in contaminated regions accumulate ¹³⁷Cs at concentrations relevant to human health, making mushroom consumption a pathway for internal radiation exposure.
- **Bioremediation Potential**: Certain fungal species immobilize ¹³⁷Cs in their mycelial biomass, potentially reducing bioavailability in contaminated soils.
- **Ecological Tracing**: ¹³⁷Cs serves as an inadvertent tracer for studying fungal nutrient translocation, mycelial turnover rates, and below-ground carbon allocation patterns.
- **Food Safety**: Wild-harvested mushrooms from regions with residual Chernobyl contamination continue to exceed regulatory limits for ¹³⁷Cs in some European countries.
- **Mycorrhizal Dynamics**: [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] mediate the transfer of ¹³⁷Cs from soil to host plants, influencing the overall contamination pathway in forest food webs.

## Fungal Accumulation Mechanisms

The accumulation of ¹³⁷Cs by fungi occurs through multiple mechanisms. The isotope enters fungal cells via potassium transport systems, as cesium ions (Cs⁺) mimic potassium ions (K⁺) in biological systems. Once inside the hyphae, ¹³⁷Cs can be sequestered in vacuoles or bound to cellular components. Mycorrhizal mycelium, with its extensive network through forest soils, acts as a biological filter that concentrates radiocesium from large volumes of soil.

## Ecological Significance

Following the Chernobyl accident, research by Dighton, Clint, and Poskitt (1991) demonstrated that upland grassland soil fungi serve as a significant pool for cesium immobilization. The work by Olsen, Joner, and Bakken (1990) further established that fungi function as a Cs-sink in soil ecosystems, playing an underestimated role in the long-term fate of environmental radiocesium. This has important implications for understanding contamination persistence, food chain transfer, and the potential for fungal-based [[bioremediation-strategies]].
