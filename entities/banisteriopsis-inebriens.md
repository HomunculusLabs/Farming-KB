## Description

Banisteriopsis inebriens is one of several species in the Banisteriopsis genus (family Malpighiaceae) used in the preparation of ayahuasca in the Amazon basin. Identified by Friedberg (1965) as one of the four major Banisteriopsis species employed in the region alongside B. caapi, B. quitensis, and B. rusbyana. The species name "inebriens" directly references its intoxicating properties, reflecting its long history of use as a psychoactive plant in indigenous and mestizo Amazonian cultures.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Malpighiaceae
- **Genus**: Banisteriopsis
- **Species**: B. inebriens
- **Etymology**: Species name derived from Latin "inebriare" (to intoxicate)
- **Role in ayahuasca**: Source of beta-carboline MAO inhibitors

## Key Facts

- B. inebriens was delineated by Friedberg (1965) as one of the four major Banisteriopsis species in the Amazon region
- The four species are B. caapi, B. quitensis, B. inebriens, and B. rusbyana
- Like other Banisteriopsis species used in ayahuasca, it contains beta-carboline alkaloids
- The primary active compounds are harmine, harmaline, and tetrahydroharmine
- These beta-carbolines function as reversible monoamine oxidase inhibitors (RIMAs)
- The species is part of the traditional pharmacopoeia of Amazonian healers who prepare ayahuasca
- Its use in ayahuasca preparations dates back to ancient traditions of hallucinogen use in the Peruvian Amazon
- Healers in urban centers like Iquitos and Pucallpa may use different Banisteriopsis species interchangeably or in combination

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Banisteriopsis inebriens is an important member of the ayahuasca vine complex, representing the botanical diversity that traditional healers draw upon when preparing the psychoactive brew. For cultivators working with entheogenic plants, understanding the range of viable Banisteriopsis species is important for several reasons: different species may have varying alkaloid profiles, growth characteristics, and environmental requirements. The species epithet "inebriens" — reflecting the plant's intoxicating properties — underscores its significance in the ethnobotanical record. Traditional cultivation of Banisteriopsis vines involves propagation from cuttings, cultivation in tropical conditions with adequate rainfall, and provision of support structures for the climbing growth habit. The plants require several years to reach harvestable maturity, and experienced cultivators can identify optimal harvest times based on vine diameter and alkaloid content.

## Pharmacological Profile

- Contains beta-carboline alkaloids common to Banisteriopsis genus
- Harmine: the most abundant beta-carboline, a potent MAO-A inhibitor
- Harmaline: another MAO-A inhibitor with somewhat different pharmacokinetics
- Tetrahydroharmine: unique to Banisteriopsis, may act as a weak serotonin reuptake inhibitor
- The combination of these compounds creates the MAO-inhibiting base that makes oral DMT activity possible
- Individual species profiles may vary, contributing to nuanced differences in the ayahuasca experience when different vine species are used
