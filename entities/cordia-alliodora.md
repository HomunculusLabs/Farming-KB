## Overview

**Cordia alliodora** (Ruiz & Pav.) Oken, commonly known as Ecuador laurel, Spanish elm, or pau d'arco, is a fast-growing tropical hardwood tree in the family Bignoniaceae. It ranges from southern Mexico through [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]] and the Caribbean.

The species is one of the most commercially important timber trees in its native range, valued for its moderately durable, straight-grained wood. Ecuador laurel is also widely planted in agroforestry systems, particularly in [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-caribbean-central-america]] (Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, Panama) into northern South America (Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia).

It is also found throughout the Caribbean, including Cuba, Jamaica, Hispaniola, Puerto Rico, and the Lesser Antilles. The species has been introduced to several tropical regions for [[plantation-forestry-carbon-dynamics]].

The tree occurs naturally in lowland to premontane tropical moist and wet forests, typically at elevations of 0–1,500 meters. It grows on a wide range of soils but performs best on well-drained, fertile volcanic loams.

It is a light-demanding, gap-phase species that colonizes forest edges, secondary growth, and disturbed sites. Natural regeneration is abundant along roadsides, pastures, and riverbanks.

Optimal annual rainfall for growth is 1,500–3,000 mm with a mean annual temperature of 22–28°C. It is moderately drought-tolerant once established but is sensitive to prolonged waterlogging.

## Cultivation and Uses

Cordia alliodora is widely cultivated in agroforestry systems throughout Central and South America. It is one of the most common shade trees in coffee and cacao plantations, typically planted at densities of 100–250 trees per hectare.

In coffee systems, the tree provides 30–50% canopy shade, which improves coffee bean quality while generating supplemental timber income. Rotation lengths in coffee systems are typically 15–25 years.

Silvicultural practices include direct seeding, nursery-grown seedling transplants, and natural regeneration management. Seed viability is relatively short, declining significantly after 6–8 months of storage.

The wood is medium-weight (specific gravity 0.45–0.55), with a straight grain and fine to medium texture. Heartwood is pale brown to reddish-brown, distinct from the lighter sapwood.

Timber uses include furniture, cabinetry, interior paneling, veneer, boat building, and general construction. The wood is moderately durable against decay but requires treatment for ground contact applications.

In traditional medicine, bark decoctions are used as an anti-inflammatory and antirheumatic remedy in several Latin American countries. Leaf infusions are employed as a treatment for coughs and respiratory ailments.

Bark extracts have also been used topically to treat skin wounds and fungal infections in rural communities across [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]]. The resinous exudate from cut bark is applied as a poultice for insect bites and minor burns.

The species is also planted as an ornamental street tree and windbreak in tropical urban areas. Its rapid growth and attractive form make it popular in landscape architecture.

Growth rates in plantations are impressive, with mean annual increments of 2–4 cm in diameter and 1.5–2.5 m in height under favorable conditions. Trees can reach harvestable size for timber in 15–20 years.

Rotation management in agroforestry typically involves selective thinning, removing lower-quality stems while retaining the best individuals. Coppice regrowth after cutting is moderate, though not as vigorous as some other tropical species.
