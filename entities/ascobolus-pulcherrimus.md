## Description

Ascobolus pulcherrimus (also referred to as Lasiobolus pulcherrimus in some historical literature) is a species of coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) ascomycete fungus. It holds a special place in the history of mycology as the species in which [[woronin-bodies]] were first discovered by the Russian mycologist Mikhail Stepanovich Woronin in 1866. The species was central to the original description and characterization of these unique septal-pore-associated organelles that are diagnostic of the Euascomycete lineage.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Ascomycota
- **Subphylum**: Pezizomycotina
- **Class**: Pezizomycetes
- **Order**: Pezizales
- **Family**: Asbolaceae / Ascobolaceae
- **Genus**: Ascobolus (syn. Lasiobolus)
- **Species**: A. pulcherrimus
- **Habitat**: Coprophilous (found on herbivore dung)

## Key Facts

- Woronin discovered highly refractive particles in the cells of Ascobolus pulcherrimus in 1866, with several typically visible on one or both sides of each septum. These were the first observed Woronin bodies.
- The species was noted by A.H.R. Buller in his seminal work Researches on Fungi (1933) as the original source of Woronin body observations.
- Buller proposed the name Woronin bodies for these structures in recognition of their discoverer.
- Charlotte Ternetz (1900) used A. pulcherrimus and related species to further define basic features of Woronin body cell biology.
- As a Euascomycete, A. pulcherrimus possesses perforate septa at regular intervals with associated Woronin bodies, a defining feature of this lineage.
- The species is coprophilous, meaning it grows predominantly on herbivore dung, playing a role in decomposition and nutrient cycling in pasture ecosystems.

## Biological Features

- **Woronin bodies**: The species produces prominent Woronin bodies near septal pores, which can rapidly seal the pore to prevent cytoplasmic loss upon hyphal damage.
- **Septal structure**: Possesses the typical Euascomycete perforate septum, allowing protoplasmic streaming between cells while maintaining the ability for rapid cellular isolation.
- **Fruiting bodies**: Produces small cup-shaped apothecia (fruiting bodies) on dung substrates.
- **Life cycle**: Typical ascomycete life cycle with both sexual (ascospore-producing) and potentially asexual reproductive phases.

## Historical Significance

Ascobolus pulcherrimus occupies a unique position in the history of cell biology and mycology:

1. **1866**: Woronin first observes refractive particles near septa, the first description of the organelle that would bear his name.
2. **1900**: Ternetz uses this and related species to characterize Woronin body biology in greater detail.
3. **1933**: Buller formalizes the name Woronin bodies and describes their distribution across multiple fungal species.
4. **Modern era**: The hex-1 gene encoding the Woronin body core protein has been studied in Ascobolus and related genera, confirming the peroxisomal origin of these organelles.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

While Ascobolus pulcherrimus is not a cultivated species, its study has provided foundational knowledge relevant to all fungal cultivation:

- **Cellular integrity**: Understanding Woronin body function in A. pulcherrimus has illuminated how all septate fungi maintain colony integrity during damage and stress.
- **Evolutionary biology**: This species serves as a model for understanding the evolution of multicellular organization in fungi.
- **Comparative mycology**: The presence of Woronin bodies in Ascobolus but not in basidiomycetes highlights the independent evolutionary solutions to septal pore management in different fungal lineages.
- **Compost ecology**: As a coprophilous species, Ascobolus is part of the decomposer community that processes organic matter, analogous to the composting processes used in [[mushroom-cultivation]].
