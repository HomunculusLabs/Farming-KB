## Overview

**Oxydendrum arboreum**, commonly known as sourwood or sorrel tree, is a deciduous tree in the family Ericaceae.

It is the sole species in the genus *Oxydendrum*, making it a monotypic genus within the Ericaceae.

The species is native to the eastern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], where it is valued for its showy panicles of white flowers and brilliant autumn foliage.

Sourwood honey, produced from its nectar, is considered one of the finest honeys in [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]].

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Oxydendrum arboreum** (L.) DC. was originally described as *Andromeda arborea* by Linnaeus in 1753.

Augustin Pyramus de Candolle transferred it to the genus *Oxydendrum* in 1839.

The genus name derives from the Greek *oxys* (sour) and *dendron* (tree), referring to the sour-tasting foliage.

The tree typically reaches 9–15 m in height with a straight trunk 20–40 cm in diameter.

Its growth form is pyramidal in youth, becoming more rounded and irregular with age.

Bark is dark gray to reddish-brown, deeply furrowed into scaly ridges on mature trees.

Leaves are alternate, simple, elliptic to lanceolate, 10–20 cm long and 4–7 cm wide.

The margins are finely serrate, and the leaves are bright green and glossy above, paler below.

New leaves have a distinct sour or acid taste due to oxalic acid content.

Autumn color is exceptional, ranging from brilliant scarlet and crimson to orange and purple.

Flowering occurs in June through July, producing terminal, pendulous panicles 15–25 cm long.

Each panicle bears numerous small, white, urn-shaped flowers approximately 8 mm long.

The flowers resemble those of other Ericaceae and are highly attractive to bees.

Fruit is a small, dry, five-valved capsule approximately 5–8 mm in diameter, persisting into winter.

## Distribution and Habitat

**Oxydendrum arboreum** occurs from southern Pennsylvania and Ohio south to northern Florida and west to eastern Texas and Oklahoma.

It is also found in the Mississippi Valley north to southern Illinois and the extreme southwest of Indiana.

The species is most abundant in the [[bolete-fungi-appalachian-region]], particularly on dry, acidic ridges and slopes.

It grows on a variety of well-drained acidic soils derived from sandstone, shale, and granite parent materials.

Optimal growth occurs on loamy, acidic soils in partially shaded to full-sun positions.

The species is commonly found as an understory tree in oak-hickory and oak-pine forests.

It also occurs on rocky bluffs, forest margins, and open slopes at elevations up to 1500 m.

Sourwood is moderately shade-tolerant but produces the most vigorous growth and best flowering in partial sun.

It is considered an indicator species of acidic, nutrient-poor soils in the Appalachian region.

## Ecological Role

The flowers of **Oxydendrum arboreum** are a major midsummer nectar source for honeybees in the Appalachian region.

Sourwood blooms during a period when few other major nectar sources are available, making it exceptionally valuable.

The resulting sourwood honey is light-colored, slow to crystallize, with a distinctive spicy-sweet flavor.

Bees produce this premium honey commercially, particularly in the southern Appalachians of [[north-carolina-mycodiversity]], Tennessee, and Virginia.

Sourwood honey commands premium prices and has been designated the state honey of Alabama.
