he common name "woolly pipevine" refers to the dense pubescence covering all above-ground parts. "Dutchman"s-pipe" describes the shape of the flower, which resembles a meerschaum smoking pipe.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:** Kingdom Plantae → Order Piperales → Family Aristolochiaceae → Genus *Aristolochia* → Species *A. tomentosa*.

The species was first described by John Sims in 1819. It is closely related to *[[bolete-fungi-appalachian-region]], with which it shares the pipevine swallowtail as its primary herbivore.

Molecular phylogenies place *Aristolochia tomentosa* within the Aristolochia clade, distinct from the Asian *Aristolochia* species that are primary sources of aristolochic acid in traditional Chinese medicine.

The vine is deciduous, twining, and can reach nine to fifteen meters in length when supported. Stems are stout, covered with dense grayish-white tomentum that gives the plant a woolly appearance.

The twining habit is clockwise, distinguishing it from some other native vines that twine counter-clockwise. Stems develop a woody, fissured bark with age.

Leaves are cordate to reniform, ten to twenty centimeters long and wide, with a dense coating of fine pubescence on both surfaces. The leaf base is deeply cordate, often overlapping the petiole.

Petioles are two to five centimeters long, densely tomentose like the stems. The leaf margin is entire, and venation is palmate with five to seven prominent veins radiating from the base.

Autumn leaf color is yellow-green, and the vine drops its foliage with the first hard freeze. New growth emerges in early spring, often before the supporting trees have fully leafed out.

Flowers are borne in axillary clusters from April through June. Each flower is pipe-shaped, two to four centimeters long, with a curved tube expanding into a limb approximately two centimeters across.

The exterior of the calyx is densely tomentose, typically pale green to yellowish-brown with darker purple veining on the interior. The throat is constricted and lined with downward-pointing trichomes that trap pollinating insects.

The floral mechanism is protogynous, with the stigma receptive before anthers release pollen. This temporal separation prevents self-pollination and promotes outcrossing.

The fruit is a dehiscent capsule, four to six centimeters long, hanging pendulous on long stalks. Seeds are numerous, flat, and winged, dispersed by wind when the capsule opens at maturity.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Aristolochia tomentosa* is native to the central and southeastern United States, ranging from Missouri and Illinois south to Texas and east to [[wisteria-frutescens]]* and *[[cordyceps-cancer-bioactive-compounds]]. These nitrophenanthrene carboxylic acids are potent mutagens and carcinogens.

Aristolochic acids cause aristolochic acid nephropathy, a progressive [[legal-responses-to-hallucinogenic-mushrooms-in-the-european-union]], and Canada have issued warnings and bans on products containing the compound.

The pipevine swallowtail caterpillar tolerates aristolochic acids through specialized metabolic pathways, sequestering the compounds as a chemical defense. Birds and other predators learn to avoid both the caterpillars and the resulting adult butterflies.

The bright orange and black coloration of the pipevine swallowtail adult is a classic example of aposematism, warning predators of the sequestered aristolochic acids. Several other butterfly species mimic the pipevine swallowtail's appearance.

## Ecology

*Aristolochia tomentosa* is the primary larval host for the pipevine swallowtail (*Battus philenor*) throughout much of its range. The vine's distribution closely matches that of the butterfly, suggesting a coevolutionary relationship.
