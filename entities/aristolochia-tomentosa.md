## Overview

**Aristolochia tomentosa**, commonly known as woolly pipevine or Texas dutchman's-pipe, is a deciduous woody vine in the family Aristolochiaceae, native to the central and southeastern United States.

The species is named for the dense tomentose pubescence covering its leaves, stems, and flower calyx. It produces distinctive pipe-shaped flowers that trap pollinating flies temporarily, facilitating pollination through a one-way floral mechanism.

*Aristolochia tomentosa* is the primary larval host plant for the pipevine swallowtail butterfly (*Battus philenor*). The caterpillars sequester aristolochic acids from the foliage, making both larvae and adults unpalatable to predators.

The plant contains aristolochic acids, which are nephrotoxic and carcinogenic in mammals. Despite its toxicity, the species has a history of use in Native American and folk medicine, and it remains a popular ornamental vine for butterfly gardens.

The common name "woolly pipevine" refers to the dense pubescence covering all above-ground parts. "Dutchman's-pipe" describes the shape of the flower, which resembles a meerschaum smoking pipe.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:** Kingdom Plantae → Order Piperales → Family Aristolochiaceae → Genus *Aristolochia* → Species *A. tomentosa*.

The species was first described by John Sims in 1819. It is closely related to *Aristolochia macrophylla* (large-leaved pipevine) of the Appalachian region, with which it shares the pipevine swallowtail as its primary herbivore.

Molecular phylogenies place *Aristolochia tomentosa* within the Aristolochia clade, distinct from the Asian *Aristolochia* species that are primary sources of aristolochic acid in traditional Chinese medicine.

The vine is deciduous, twining, and can reach nine to fifteen meters in length when supported. Stems are stout, covered with dense grayish-white tomentum that gives the plant a woolly appearance.

The twining habit is clockwise, distinguishing it from some other native vines that twine counter-clockwise. Stems develop a woody, fissured bark with age.

Leaves are cordate to reniform, ten to twenty centimeters long and wide, with a dense coating of fine pubescence on both surfaces. The leaf base is deeply cordate, often overlapping the petiole.

Petioles are two to five centimeters long, densely tomentose like the stems. The leaf margin is entire, and venation is palmate with five to seven prominent veins radiating from the base.

Autumn leaf color is yellow-green, and the vine drops its foliage with the first hard freeze. New growth emerges in early spring, often before the supporting trees have fully leafed out.

Flowers are borne in axillary clusters from April through June. Each flower is pipe-shaped, two to four centimeters long, with a curved tube expanding into a limb approximately two centimeters across.

The exterior of the calyx is densely tomentose, typically pale green to yellowish-brown with darker purple veining on the interior. The throat is constricted and lined with downward-pointing trichomes that trap pollinating insects.

The floral mechanism is protogynous, with the stigma receptive before anthers release pollen. This temporal separation prevents self-pollination and promotes outcrossing.

The fruit is a dehiscent capsule, four to six centimeters long, hanging pendulous on long stalks. Seeds are numerous, flat, and winged, dispersed by wind when the capsule opens at maturity.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Aristolochia tomentosa* is native to the central and southeastern United States, ranging from Missouri and Illinois south to Texas and east to North Carolina and Florida.

*Aristolochia tomentosa* occurs in bottomland hardwood forests, floodplain woodlands, stream banks, and thickets. It is most abundant in rich, moist soils along waterways, where it climbs into the canopy of supporting trees.

The species is particularly common along river floodplains in the Mississippi River basin. It frequently co-occurs with other native vines including *Wisteria frutescens* and *Campsis radicans*.

Elevation range is typically from sea level to five hundred meters.

The species is tolerant of both acidic and alkaline soils, making it adaptable to a range of site conditions throughout its distribution.

The vine is shade-tolerant and thrives in partial to full shade beneath a forest canopy. In open sites, it grows more vigorously but may require artificial support to reach flowering size.

## Chemistry and Toxicology

*Aristolochia tomentosa* contains aristolochic acids I and II as its primary bioactive compounds. These nitrophenanthrene carboxylic acids are potent mutagens and carcinogens.

Aristolochic acids cause aristolochic acid nephropathy, a progressive kidney disease characterized by interstitial fibrosis and urothelial carcinoma. The compound is metabolized to aristolactam, which forms DNA adducts leading to characteristic A-to-T transversion mutations.

Despite these dangers, aristolochic acid-containing *Aristolochia* species have been used in traditional Chinese medicine under the name "Mu Tong." International health agencies have banned or severely restricted the sale of aristolochic acid-containing products.

The World Health Organization has classified aristolochic acid as a Group 1 carcinogen. Regulatory agencies in the United States, European Union, and Canada have issued warnings and bans on products containing the compound.

The pipevine swallowtail caterpillar tolerates aristolochic acids through specialized metabolic pathways, sequestering the compounds as a chemical defense. Birds and other predators learn to avoid both the caterpillars and the resulting adult butterflies.

The bright orange and black coloration of the pipevine swallowtail adult is a classic example of aposematism, warning predators of the sequestered aristolochic acids. Several other butterfly species mimic the pipevine swallowtail's appearance.

## Ecology

*Aristolochia tomentosa* is the primary larval host for the pipevine swallowtail (*Battus philenor*) throughout much of its range. The vine's distribution closely matches that of the butterfly, suggesting a coevolutionary relationship.

Flowers are pollinated by small flies and gnats attracted to the carrion-like odor produced by the floral tube. The constricted throat and downward-pointing trichomes temporarily trap insects, covering them with pollen before release.

The vine provides structural habitat in forest ecosystems, creating dense leafy cover that supports invertebrates and small birds. Its climbing habit allows it to exploit vertical space in the forest understory and canopy.

Seed dispersal is anemochorous, with winged seeds carried by wind from the pendulous capsules. Germination requires exposed mineral soil and adequate moisture, limiting establishment to disturbed sites and stream banks.

In areas where native *Aristolochia* species have been extirpated, pipevine swallowtail populations decline. Conservation of native pipevines is therefore directly linked to butterfly preservation efforts.

## Cultivation and Uses

*Aristolochia tomentosa* is widely cultivated in native plant gardens and butterfly habitats for its value as a pipevine swallowtail host. It is readily available from native plant nurseries throughout its range.

Propagation is by seed, stratified for sixty days, or by softwood cuttings taken in early summer. Seed-grown plants typically flower in their third or fourth year.

The vine requires a sturdy support structure, as mature plants become heavy. Trellises, fences, and large shrubs provide adequate support for ornamental plantings.

Cultural requirements are minimal once established. The vine tolerates a wide range of soil types but performs best in deep, moist, loamy soils with partial shade.

Historically, Native American peoples used *Aristolochia tomentosa* roots as a poultice for snakebites and swelling, and as a treatment for digestive complaints. These uses are strongly discouraged due to the nephrotoxic and carcinogenic properties of aristolochic acids.
