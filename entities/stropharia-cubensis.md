# Stropharia cubensis

**Stropharia cubensis** is a coprophilous (dung-inhabiting) basidiomycete fungus in the
family Hymenogastraceae, widely known for its production of the psychoactive compounds
[[psilocybin-compound]] and [[psilocin]]. It is the most widely distributed and commonly cultivated
psychoactive mushroom species in the world, and the species most frequently associated with
both traditional ceremonial use and modern recreational and therapeutic applications. Its
accessibility, rapid growth cycle, and moderate potency have made it the de facto gateway
species for mycophiles and psychonauts worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

The species was first described by Franklin Sumner Earle in 1906 from specimens collected in
Cuba. The original placement in the genus Stropharia has been retained in common usage
among cultivators and psychonauts, though mycological authorities including Gastón Guzmán
(1983) transferred it to the genus Psilocybe as [[psilocybe-cubensis]], a classification
now widely accepted in formal mycological literature. The specific epithet "cubensis" refers
to the type locality in Cuba.

Several synonyms exist in the historical literature. Singer (1949) placed it in
Naematoloma. Some older texts refer to it as Stropharia cyanescens (not to be confused
with the wood-inhabiting [[psilocybe-cyanescens]] of the Pacific Northwest). The current
accepted name is [[psilocybe-cubensis]] (Earle) Singer.

Guzmán's 1983 monograph "The Genus Psilocybe" established the modern taxonomic framework,
recognizing the species as the type species of Section Cubensisae. This section includes
several closely related Neotropical dung-inhabiting species sharing hygrophanous caps,
darkening gills, and spores with a distinct germ pore.

## Morphology

The basidiocarp features a convex to plane cap measuring 20–80 mm in diameter, though
specimens exceeding 100 mm are occasionally observed under optimal cultivation conditions.
The cap surface is caramel to golden brown when moist, distinctly hygrophanous (changing
color as it dries from the center outward), and often bears a prominent umbo (nipple-like
protrusion at the center). Young caps may be covered with a fine whitish bloom from
universal veil remnants.

The gills are adnate to adnexed in attachment, close to subdistant in spacing, and darken
progressively from pale grayish to deep purple-black at maturity. The stipe is 60–150 mm
long and 4–15 mm thick, hollow, and bruises bluish where damaged due to psilocin
oxidation. A membranous partial veil leaves a persistent annulus, often dual-layered with
a purplish inner surface from spore deposits.

Spore prints are dark purple-brown. Basidiospores are subellipsoid, 11.5–17 × 7.5–12 µm,
with thick walls and a distinct germ pore. Cheilocystidia are numerous, ventricose to
lageniform. Clamp connections are present at hyphal septa.

## Distribution and Habitat

stropharia cubensis is pantropical and subtropical, found throughout Central and South
America, the Caribbean, Southeast Asia, India, Oceania, and sub-Saharan Africa. It grows
primarily on bovine and equine dung in pastures, particularly in humid climates with
temperatures exceeding 24°C and regular rainfall.

The species has been introduced globally through cattle ranching over the past five
centuries. It is now reported from at least 60 countries, including the Gulf Coast US,
Hawaii, temperate Australia, and South Africa. Its ability to colonize diverse dung types
and climatic conditions has contributed to its remarkable cosmopolitan distribution.

## Chemistry

The principal psychoactive constituents are [[psilocybin-compound]] (typically 0.5–1.2% dry weight)
and [[psilocin]]. Baeocystin and norbaeocystin are present as minor alkaloids at roughly
10–30% of psilocybin content. Biosynthesis proceeds through the tryptamine pathway via
enzymes PsiD, PsiH, PsiM, and PsiK, whose genes form a clustered biosynthetic gene
cluster identified and characterized in 2017 by Fricke et al.

Potency varies significantly based on genetic strain, substrate composition, maturity at
harvest, and post-harvest handling. Bigwood and Beug documented up to 10-fold variation
between individual fruiting bodies from the same flush. Caps generally contain higher
alkaloid concentrations than stems.

## Cultural Significance

The species is central to Mesoamerican entheogenic mushroom traditions and a leading
candidate for the Aztec teonanácatl ("flesh of the gods"), documented by the 16th-century
chronicler Bernardino de Sahagún in the Florentine Codex. The Mazatec tradition of Oaxaca
continues ceremonial use today, referring to the mushrooms as "ndi xijo" (little ones that
spring forth).

Terence McKenna proposed in *Food of the Gods* (1992) that stropharia cubensis
catalyzed the emergence of human consciousness and culture during the Paleolithic — the
"Stoned Ape" hypothesis. While not accepted in mainstream anthropology, this theory
remains influential in psychedelic discourse and consciousness studies.

## Cultivation

stropharia cubensis is among the easiest psychoactive mushrooms to cultivate. It
readily colonizes sterilized grain spawn (rye, millet, brown rice) and fruits on
pasteurized straw, manure, coco coir, or vermiculite-based substrates.

Robert McPherson ("Psilocybe Fanaticus") popularized home cultivation through the PF Tek
method in the 1990s, using brown rice flour and vermiculite in half-pint jars. Optimal
fruiting requires 23–27°C, >90% relative humidity, 12+ hours of blue-spectrum light
(370–460 nm), and adequate fresh air exchange. A full crop cycle from inoculation to
harvest takes 4–6 weeks, with multiple flushes possible from a single substrate.

See the mushroom cultivator for comprehensive cultivation protocols and
working with agar for isolation and strain development techniques.

## Strains and Varieties

Numerous cultivated strains reflect the species' broad natural distribution and multiple
domestication events:

- **B+**: Robust, beginner-friendly Florida strain with large caramel-capped fruiting
  bodies.
- **Golden Teacher**: Widely available, reliable fruiting, moderate potency. Often
  recommended for first-time cultivators.
- **Ecuador**: Andean origin, dense thick-stemmed fruiting bodies with moderate to high
  potency.
- **Cambodian**: Southeast Asian, fast-colonizing, compact prolific fruiting.
- **Penis Envy**: Distinctive morphology (thick stipes, underdeveloped caps), reportedly
  higher potency, slower colonization.
- **Thai** (Koh Samui, Lipa Yai): Compact fruiting bodies, strong potency.

## Legal Status

The species is controlled in many jurisdictions due to its [[psilocybin-compound]] content. Schedule
I in the United States under the Controlled Substances Act. Legal status varies globally:
the Netherlands banned fresh mushrooms in 2008, while Brazil does not specifically schedule
the mushrooms themselves. Decriminalization measures have passed in multiple US
jurisdictions since 2019, with Oregon (2020) and Colorado (2022) establishing regulated
therapeutic access frameworks.
