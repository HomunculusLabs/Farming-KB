# Inula helenium

## Overview

**Inula helenium** L., elecampane or horse-heal, is a robust
perennial herb in the family Asteraceae.

Native to Europe and western Asia, it has been cultivated for
centuries as a medicinal herb, particularly valued for respiratory
conditions. Growing 1.5–2.5 m tall, it produces large leaves and
bright yellow flower heads. Its thick aromatic root contains inulin
(up to 44%), alantolactone, and isoalantolactone. Used in European
herbal medicine since ancient Greece and Rome.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Eudicots → Asterids
- **Order:** Asterales
- **Family:** Asteraceae
- **Tribe:** Inuleae
- **Genus:** Inula
- **Species:** I. helenium L.

**Etymology:** Genus from Greek *inein* (to cleanse); specific
epithet *helenium* commemorates Helen of Troy. Common name
"elecampane" derives from Latin *elecampanea*.

**Synonyms:** *Aster helenium* (L.) Scop.

## Morphology

**Growth habit:** Erect perennial, 1.5–2.5 m, forming dense clumps
from a thick branching rhizomatous rootstock.

**Root:** Thick fleshy taproot, 15–30 cm × 3–6 cm. Exterior brown,
interior white and fibrous. Warm, aromatic, slightly bitter taste.
Contains up to 44% inulin (fructan polysaccharide).

**Stem:** Stout, ribbed, 10–20 mm diameter, sparingly branched.
Green to purplish, pubescent below.

**Leaves:** Large, alternate, ovate to elliptic. Lower leaves 40–70
× 15–30 cm with winged petioles; upper smaller and sessile.
Margin dentate. Dark green above; gray-green tomentose below.

**Flowers:** Terminal capitula, 50–80 mm diameter. Ray florets
bright yellow, 15–25 mm; disk florets yellow, tubular. Blooms
July–September.

## Distribution and Habitat

**Native:** Central and eastern Europe, western and central Asia.

**Introduced:** Northeastern North America, South America, New Zealand.

Damp meadows, stream banks, forest edges, pastures. Prefers moist
deep loamy soils, partial shade to full sun, up to 1,500 m elevation.
Hardy USDA zones 4–8.

## Bioactive Compounds

**Sesquiterpene lactones (1.5–3% of dry root):**
- **Alantolactone** — major active; anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial,
  antitumor properties in vitro
- **Isoalantolactone** — structural isomer, similar bioactivity

**Polysaccharides (up to 44%):** Inulin — fructan with β(2→1)
linkages; prebiotic, supports gut microbiota.

**Essential oil (1–2.5%):** Alantolactone, isoalantolactone, bornyl
acetate, camphene. Warm, camphoraceous aroma.

**Other:** Phenolic acids, flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol
glycosides), triterpenes, mucilage, bitter principles.

## Medicinal Uses

**Respiratory (primary):** Chronic bronchitis, productive coughs,
upper respiratory infections. Approved by German Commission E and
EMA/HMPC as an expectorant.

**Digestive:** Appetite stimulant (bitter tonic), carminative,
traditional antiparasitic, prebiotic from inulin.

**Antimicrobial:** Broad-spectrum in vitro activity against
Gram-positive bacteria and Candida albicans.

**Anti-inflammatory/anticancer:** Alantolactone inhibits NF-κB,
induces apoptosis in cancer cell lines. Clinical trials lacking.

## Cultivation

**Propagation:** Seed (stratify 4–6 weeks at 4°C) or root division
in early spring/autumn. Division preferred. Space 60–90 cm.

**Soil:** Deep fertile loam, pH 5.5–7.5. Raised beds in heavy clay.

**Harvest:** Roots in autumn of year 2–3 (September–October). Slice
and dry below 40°C. Yield: 1–3 kg fresh per plant (~30% dry).

## Permaculture Applications

**Dynamic accumulator:** Deep taproot mines subsoil minerals for
surface nutrient cycling.

**Pollinator support:** Late summer flowers provide nectar for bees
and butterflies when many other species have finished blooming.

**Companion planting:** Aromatic root may deter soil pests; planted
at vegetable bed edges.

## Side Effects

- Contact dermatitis in Asteraceae-sensitive individuals
- Large doses of essential oil: nausea, vomiting, mucosal irritation
- Contraindicated in pregnancy (uterine stimulant in animal studies)
- Possible interaction with anticoagulants (coumarin content)
