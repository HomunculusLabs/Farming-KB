## Overview

**Drosera regia**, commonly known as the king sundew, is the largest species in the genus *Drosera* and one of the most morphologically distinct carnivorous plants in the family Droseraceae.

Endemic to a single mountain range in South Africa, it produces upright leaves reaching over 60 cm in length, covered with large glandular tentacles that secrete sticky mucilage to trap insects.

Phylogenetic studies place *D. regia* as sister to all other *Drosera* species, making it a critical taxon for understanding the evolution of carnivory within the genus.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Drosera regia* was first described by John Stephens in 1924 from specimens collected in the Baviaanskloof Mountains of the Eastern Cape Province.

It is assigned to the monotypic section *Regiae* within subgenus *Drosera*, reflecting its isolated phylogenetic position.

### Etymology

The specific epithet *regia* means "royal" in Latin, referencing both the species' imposing stature and its status as one of the most morphologically distinctive sundews.

The common name "king sundew" reinforces this association with regality and physical dominance within the genus.

### Root System

The root system is unusually well-developed for a sundew, consisting of a thick perennial rhizome that allows the plant to survive seasonal fires and dormancy periods.

Leaves are lanceolate, arranged in a dense rosette, and can exceed 60 cm in length at maturity.

Each leaf bears two types of trichomes: long stalked marginal tentacles and shorter sessile glands distributed across the lamina surface.

The plant supplements its nutrient intake primarily with nitrogen and phosphorus derived from captured arthropods, compensating for the impoverished substrate.

## Ecological Interactions

In its native habitat, *D. regia* competes with other carnivorous species including *Drosera trinervia* and *Genlisea hispidula* for insect prey in seepage zones.

The tall inflorescences are visited by various flying insects, which serve as pollinators while occasionally becoming trapped on the nearby leaves.

Fire plays a significant ecological role, with the rhizomatous root system allowing *D. regia* to resprout after periodic grassland burns.
