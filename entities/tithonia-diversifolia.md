# Tithonia diversifolia

## Overview

*Tithonia diversifolia* (Hemsl.) A.Gray, commonly known as Mexican sunflower, tree marigold,
or wild sunflower, is a fast-growing perennial shrub in the family Asteraceae. Native to
Mexico and Central America, it has become widely naturalized across tropical and subtropical
regions of Africa, Asia, and South America. In permaculture and agroforestry systems, it is
valued as a premier green manure crop, biomass producer, and compost activator. Its leaves
contain exceptionally high nitrogen concentrations — among the highest recorded for
non-leguminous plants — and its rapid growth rate enables multiple harvest cycles per year,
making it a cornerstone species for nutrient cycling in tropical food forests and alley
cropping systems.

## Taxonomy and Naming

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Asterales
- **Family:** Asteraceae
- **Tribe:** Heliantheae
- **Genus:** *Tithonia*
- **Species:** *T. diversifolia*
- **Authority:** (Hemsl.) A.Gray, 1883

The genus *Tithonia* comprises approximately 10–12 species native to Mexico and Central
America. The specific epithet *diversifolia* refers to the variability in leaf shape across
the plant — lower leaves are typically broader and more deeply lobed while upper leaves
become narrower and less divided. Common names include Mexican sunflower (English), bolillo
(Mexico), kembang bulan (Indonesian), and wild sunflower (multiple regions). It should not
be confused with the ornamental annual *Tithonia rotundifolia*, though the two share similar
floral morphology.

## Description

### Growth Habit

Erect, semi-woody perennial shrub reaching 3–4 m in height within a single growing season
under favorable conditions. Stems are hollow, green when young, becoming woody and slightly
pubescent at the base with maturity. Branching is prolific from the lower nodes, forming a
broad, bushy canopy 2–3 m in diameter. Growth rate exceeds 3 cm per day during peak
warm-season conditions. The plant can behave as an annual in cooler climates but persists
as a perennial above USDA zone 9. When cut back to ground level, regrowth from the root
crown is rapid, typically producing 1–2 m of new growth within 6–8 weeks.

### Leaves

Leaves are alternate, broadly ovate to cordate, 15–35 cm long and 12–25 cm wide, with 3–5 shallow to deep lobes. Leaf margins are serrate to dentate. Upper leaf surface is dark green and scabrous (rough to touch), lower surface is lighter green with prominent venation and sparse pubescence along the veins. Petioles are 5–15 cm long, slightly winged at the base. Leaf area index in dense stands can exceed 6, contributing to aggressive light interception and weed suppression capacity.

### Flowers

Inflorescences are terminal, solitary or in loose cymes, composed of bright orange-yellow ray florets (8–13 per head) surrounding a central disc of yellow tubular florets. Flower heads are 8–12 cm in diameter. Blooming is photoperiod-sensitive, typically triggered by shortening days; in equatorial regions flowering can be nearly year-round. Each flower head produces 80–120 achenes (seeds), each 5–7 mm long with a pappus of two deciduous scales. Seed viability persists for 2–3 years under dry storage.

### Root System

Extensive fibrous root system with a well-developed taproot reaching 40–60 cm depth in the
first year, extending to 1 m or more in mature plants. Lateral roots spread 1–1.5 m from
the crown, forming a dense network in the upper 30 cm of soil. Root biomass accounts for
approximately 20–25% of total plant biomass. The root system contributes significantly to
soil structure improvement through aggregate formation and the creation of continuous
macropores. Arbuscular mycorrhizal associations are common and facilitate phosphorus
acquisition from low-fertility soils.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to the montane and piedmont regions of Mexico (particularly Jalisco, Guerrero, and Oaxaca) and extending through Central America to Costa Rica. Introduced widely as an ornamental and subsequently naturalized across tropical Africa (East and West), South and Southeast Asia, Pacific Islands, and parts of South America. It thrives from sea level to 2,000 m elevation, preferring mean annual temperatures of 20–30 °C and rainfall of 800–2,500 mm. The species is remarkably tolerant of infertile and acidic soils (pH 4.5–7.0), showing strong performance on degraded lands, roadcuts, and disturbed sites. It does not tolerate waterlogging or prolonged drought but establishes quickly after the onset of rains. Classified as invasive in parts of Africa, Australia, and Pacific Islands where it can form dense monospecific stands.

## Propagation and Establishment

*Tithonia diversifolia* is most commonly propagated by direct seeding or stem cuttings. Seeds require no pretreatment and germinate within 5–10 days at 25–30 °C. Direct seeding at 2–3 cm depth, spaced 50–100 cm within rows and 1–2 m between rows, establishes effective hedgerows within 8–12 weeks. Stem cuttings of 20–30 cm length with 2–3 nodes root readily in moist soil during the rainy season, achieving 80–95% survival. In areas where the species carries invasive risk, vegetative propagation via cuttings is preferred as it eliminates seed spread. Weed competition during establishment is the primary constraint — heavy mulching or intercropping with a short-lived cover crop during the first 6 weeks improves survival rates significantly.

## Permaculture Applications

### Biomass Production

*Tithonia diversifolia* is among the highest-yielding non-leguminous biomass crops in the tropics. Fresh leaf biomass yields of 15–20 t/ha per harvest are routinely reported, with 3–4 harvests possible annually under adequate moisture, totaling 45–80 t/ha/year fresh weight (approximately 6–12 t/ha/year dry matter). In alley cropping systems with maize, hedgerows spaced at 4–5 m intervals can supply sufficient biomass for full soil surface mulch. Its productivity rivals or exceeds that of [[moringa-oleifera]] in similar environments, and its softer stems decompose more rapidly than [[acacia-confusa]] prunings. In comparison, [[comfrey-symphytum-officinale]] produces roughly 1/5 the annual dry biomass in temperate systems, highlighting the exceptional productivity of this species in its adapted range.

### Nutrient Cycling

The primary value of *T. diversifolia* in agroecological systems lies in its capacity to accumulate and recycle nutrients from deep soil layers and redistribute them through leaf litter and green manure incorporation. Leaf tissue concentrations of 3.0–3.5% N (dry weight basis) are exceptional for a non-legume and approach values typical of leguminous species such as [[alfalfa-medicago-sativa]] (2.5–3.0% N) and [[trifolium-pratense]] (2.0–2.8% N). Potassium accumulation is particularly notable, with leaf K concentrations of 3.0–4.5%, making the species an effective potassium pump. When incorporated as green manure, *T. diversifolia* has been shown to increase maize yields by 50–200% compared to unfertilized controls, with effects comparable to moderate applications of mineral NPK fertilizer in trials across East Africa.

### Pest Management

*Tithonia diversifolia* plays a dual role in pest management through its participation in push-pull strategies and its function as a reservoir for natural enemies. The flowers are highly attractive to a wide range of beneficial insects, including predatory wasps (Hymenoptera: Vespidae and Ichneumonidae), hoverflies (Syrphidae), ladybird beetles (Coccinellidae), and tachinid flies. In push-pull systems developed for stem borer management in cereal crops, *T. diversifolia* planted as a border trap crop attracts stem borers away from the main crop while also drawing in their natural parasitoids. This approach parallels systems using [[tagetes-erecta]] for nematode management and [[raphanus-sativus]] as a biofumigation trap crop, though *T. diversifolia* offers the added benefit of massive biomass production.

### Mulch and Compost

Fresh *Tithonia diversifolia* biomass decomposes rapidly, with 50–70% mass loss within 4–6 weeks when used as surface mulch, releasing nutrients quickly into the soil profile. This decomposition rate is faster than many woody mulches and comparable to [[comfrey-symphytum-officinale]] leaf mulch. When added to compost piles, *T. diversifolia* acts as a powerful activator due to its high nitrogen content, low C:N ratio (approximately 12:1 in fresh leaves), and diverse microbial populations carried on leaf surfaces. Compost containing 20–30% *T. diversifolia* by volume typically reaches thermophilic temperatures (55–65 °C) within 48 hours and matures 30–40% faster than composts relying solely on grasses or cereal straw. The resulting compost is rich in potassium and micronutrients, complementing nitrogen-rich inputs from legumes.

## Limitations and Risks

The primary concern with *T. diversifolia* is its invasive potential. In tropical regions outside its native range, particularly in Africa, the Philippines, and eastern Australia, it readily escapes cultivation and displaces native vegetation along waterways, roadsides, and disturbed areas. Seed production is prolific and seeds disperse readily by wind and water. Allelopathic compounds in leaf litter can suppress germination of some crop species, so fresh mulch should be composted or aged before direct application to seed beds. The species is also a known alternate host for certain cassava and tomato viruses, warranting careful placement relative to susceptible crops. In regions where it is invasive, vegetative propagation only and regular harvest management are strongly recommended.

## Relevance

*Tithonia diversifolia* is a keystone species for tropical and subtropical permaculture systems where rapid nutrient cycling and biomass production are priorities. Its combination of high nitrogen content, exceptional growth rate, broad environmental tolerance, and multiple ecological functions (biomass production, nutrient pumping, beneficial insect attraction, soil rehabilitation) make it one of the most versatile non-leguminous plants available for regenerative agriculture. It is particularly valuable in systems where access to mineral fertilizers is limited or where closed-loop nutrient management is a design goal. The species merits integration into food forests, agroforestry alleys, contour hedgerow systems, and dedicated biomass production banks in any climate within its adapted range. Its capacity to rehabilitate degraded soils while simultaneously producing harvestable biomass places it in a small class of multipurpose pioneer species that accelerate succession and build the biological capital needed for more diverse plantings to follow.
