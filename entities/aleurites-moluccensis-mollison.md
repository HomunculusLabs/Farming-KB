## Description

Aleurites moluccensis, commonly known as the kukui tree or candlenut tree, is a large tropical tree species in the family Euphorbiaceae. It is the state tree of Hawaii and holds significant cultural and agricultural importance throughout Polynesia and Southeast Asia. The tree produces nuts rich in oil that were traditionally used as candles (hence the common name), and the species plays a critical role in traditional Hawaiian agricultural systems as a source of mulch for taro terraces.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Euphorbiaceae
- **Genus**: Aleurites
- **Species**: A. moluccensis
- **Common names**: Kukui (Hawaiian), candlenut, kemiri (Indonesian), buah keras (Malay), lumbang (Filipino)
- **Growth form**: Large evergreen tree, 15-25 m tall with spreading crown
- **Native range**: Indo-Malayan region, widely distributed throughout tropical Pacific by Polynesian voyagers
- **Habitat**: Lowland to montane wet forests, riparian zones, disturbed sites; 0-800 m elevation

## Key Facts

- The kukui tree is central to traditional Hawaiian taro (kalo) cultivation systems as a source of organic matter and mulch.
- Logs of kukui are traditionally placed in shallow water of taro terraces to rot. As the logs decompose, an edible fungus appears on the wood, which is harvested as a secondary crop before the remaining log material is crushed and spread as terrace mulch.
- Leaves and branches of kukui and other forest trees were gathered specifically for taro terrace mulching, providing humus, nutrients, and weed suppression.
- The nuts contain 60-70% oil and were traditionally strung on palm midribs and burned as candles, providing light for evening activities.
- The tree produces large, pale green leaves that create substantial leaf litter, making it an effective mulch-producing species in polyculture systems.
- Kukui has largely been replaced by Azolla (water fern) as a taro mulch in modern Hawaiian agriculture, though the traditional system remains of great interest to permaculture designers.
- The oil from kukui nuts (kukui nut oil) is used traditionally for skin care, treating burns and skin ailments, and as a wood finish and waterproofing agent.

## Role in Tropical Agricultural Systems

In traditional Hawaiian agriculture, kukui was integrated into a sophisticated multi-species system:

- **Taro terrace mulching**: Rotting kukui logs in flooded taro terraces provided both an edible mushroom crop and decomposed organic matter that enriched the terrace soil.
- **Leaf litter production**: The large, soft leaves decompose readily, contributing to soil humus content and nutrient cycling.
- **Canopy integration**: Kukui trees were part of the upper canopy in agroforestry systems, providing shade for understory crops such as taro, kava, and ginger.
- **Oil production**: The nuts yield a drying oil used for lighting, waterproofing, wood finishing, and medicinal skin applications.
- **Timber**: The lightweight wood was used for canoes, fishing floats, and containers.

## Traditional Fungal Cultivation

The kukui-taro terrace system represents a sophisticated form of integrated fungal cultivation:

- Logs are placed in shallow water of flooded taro terraces and allowed to decompose over weeks to months.
- An edible fungus colonises the rotting wood, producing fruiting bodies that are harvested as food.
- Once fungal production declines, the remaining log material is crushed and incorporated into the terrace soil as organic amendment.
- This system demonstrates how tropical indigenous agriculture seamlessly integrated mycology, aquaculture, and crop production.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Mulch source**: Kukui exemplifies the permaculture principle of every element serving multiple functions: providing shade, mulch, edible fungi, oil, and timber from a single tree species.
- **Traditional knowledge**: The kukui-taro system demonstrates how indigenous agricultural knowledge integrated fungal cultivation, nutrient cycling, and crop production seamlessly.
- **Mycological interest**: The edible fungus that colonises rotting kukui logs in taro terraces represents an under-studied traditional mushroom cultivation technique.
- **Soil building**: Kukui leaf litter contributes to rapid humus formation in tropical soils where organic matter is rapidly depleted.
- **Agroforestry integration**: Suitable as an upper-canopy species in tropical polyculture designs, providing dappled shade for understory crops.

## Other Traditional Uses

- **Dye**: Kukui nut kernels produce a black dye used in traditional Hawaiian kapa (bark cloth) making.
- **Medicine**: The oil is used to treat skin conditions, burns, and wounds. Roasted kernels serve as a laxative.
- **Lei**: The polished nuts are strung into lei (garlands) of cultural significance in Hawaii.
- **Timber**: Lightweight wood used for canoes, fishing net floats, and food containers.
- **Soil stabiliser**: Fast growth and extensive root system make kukui useful for riparian stabilisation.

## Cultivation Notes


- Prefers wet tropical to sub-tropical climates with rainfall above 150 cm annually.
- Tolerates a range of soil types from volcanic to coral-derived soils.
- Fast-growing in suitable conditions, reaching productive size within 5-7 years.
- Propagation is by seed; nuts should be planted fresh as viability declines rapidly.
- The raw nuts are mildly toxic (containing saponins and phorbol) and must be roasted before consumption.
- Once established, trees are relatively drought-tolerant and can persist in seasonal climates.
