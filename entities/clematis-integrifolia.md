## Overview

*Clematis integrifolia* L., commonly known as entire-leaved clematis or solitary clematis, is a herbaceous perennial in the family Ranunculaceae. Unlike most clematis species, it is non-climbing and forms an upright, bushy clump.

The species is native to central and eastern Europe, extending eastward through Russia to western Siberia and Central Asia. It inhabits grasslands, meadows, and scrubby slopes at low to moderate elevations.

The nodding, bell-shaped flowers are a distinctive deep violet-blue, produced singly at the stem tips. Each flower is 3–5 cm across with four thick, leathery sepals.

After flowering, the plant develops highly ornamental silky seed heads that persist through autumn. These feathery plumes provide extended seasonal interest in the garden.

The species is valued as a low-maintenance perennial for borders and rock gardens. It does not require the support structures that climbing clematis species demand.

Several botanical authorities recognize a variety *C. integrifolia* var. *italica* from southern Europe with slightly larger flowers. This variety is not universally accepted as distinct.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Order Ranunculales → Family Ranunculaceae → Genus *Clematis* → Section *Campanella* → Species *C. integrifolia*. The species name *integrifolia* means "entire-leaved," referring to the unlobed leaf margins.

The plant grows 30–90 cm tall from a woody root crown. Stems are erect, unbranched or sparsely branched, ribbed, and sparsely pubescent.

Leaves are opposite, sessile or short-petiolate, ovate to lanceolate, and 5–12 cm long. The leaf margins are entire (unlobed), which is unusual in a genus known for divided foliage.

Flowers are nodding, solitary, terminal, bell-shaped, and 3–5 cm in diameter. The four sepals are thick, leathery, deep violet-blue, and slightly recurved at the tips.

True petals are absent, as in all clematis species. The showy sepals serve the visual function of petals in attracting pollinators.

The fruit is an achene with a persistent, feathery style 2–4 cm long. These silky plumes create the ornamental seed heads that are a hallmark of the species.

The root system consists of a woody crown with deep, fibrous roots. This root structure makes established plants difficult to transplant successfully.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Clematis integrifolia* is native to a broad swath of Eurasia from Hungary and the Balkans eastward through Ukraine, Russia, and Kazakhstan. Its range extends to the Altai Mountains and western Siberia.

The species grows in dry to mesic grasslands, meadow steppes, forest edges, and rocky slopes. It prefers calcareous to neutral soils with good drainage.

Elevational range is 100–1,800 meters, with populations at higher elevations occurring in more southern latitudes. The species is absent from Mediterranean climates, preferring continental temperature regimes.

Annual precipitation in native habitats ranges from 400–700 mm. The species tolerates summer drought well once established.

It is listed as Least Concern by most conservation authorities. Local populations in western Europe are stable, though agricultural intensification has reduced some meadow habitats.

The species has naturalized sporadically in parts of northern Europe and [[clematis-recta]]*. The hybrid × *Clematis* 'Arabella' is a popular garden selection from this cross.

Notable cultivars include 'Durandii' (a hybrid with *C. jackmanii*), 'Hakuree' (compact Japanese selection), and 'Rosea' (pink-flowered form). These extend the ornamental range of the species.

Pruning is simple: cut all stems to 15–20 cm in late winter before new growth emerges. This hard pruning encourages vigorous new growth and abundant flowering.

Plants typically die back completely to the ground in winter, even in mild climates. New shoots emerge from the crown in [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]], often later than many other perennials.

## Ecological Role

The nodding flowers are pollinated primarily by bumblebees, which can access the nectar by pushing into the bell-shaped blooms. Small bees and flies also visit but are less effective pollinators.

The silky seed heads are dispersed by wind, with the feathery styles acting as parachutes. Seeds can travel considerable distances across open grasslands.

The foliage contains ranunculin-derived compounds that deter most mammalian herbivores. Deer and rabbits typically avoid the plant in garden settings.

Several species of moths use *Clematis* as a larval food plant. The presence of these compounds in the foliage provides some protection from generalist insect herbivores.
