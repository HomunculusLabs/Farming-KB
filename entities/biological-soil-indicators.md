## Description

A framework for using plants, animals, and ecological patterns to assess soil and site conditions without laboratory equipment. Mollison presents this as a practical observational science where the composition, shape, size, and distribution of living organisms reveal hidden soil properties. Ten specific factors can be assessed through biological indicators: soil depth, water reserves, pH, mineral status, fire frequency, frost incidence, drainage quality, mineral deposits, overgrazing severity, and macrofaunal effects.

## Classification

- **Category:** Diagnostic technique / observational framework
- **Scale:** Site assessment from garden to landscape
- **Practitioner level:** Accessible to non-scientists
- **Section:** 8.15 in [[permaculture]]: A Designer's Manual

## Ten Indicators and Their Readings

### 1. Soil Depth
- **Method:** Compare height and spread of a single tree species (e.g., [[acacia]], Prosopis, honey locust) across a site
- **Reading:** Largest specimens mark deepest soils; dwarfish specimens on same rock type indicate shallow soils

### 2. Water Reserves
- **Method:** Observe deep-rooted water-demanding trees (large nut trees, candlenuts / Aleurites)
- **Reading:** These species mark springs or aquifer discharge areas; variety of deep-rooted shrubs in sands indicates clay base at 1-2 m

### 3. Soil pH
- **Acid indicators:** Sorrel, oxalis in pastures; absence of snails (pH less than 5.0)
- **Alkaline indicators:** Fen and limestone species; large, dense snail populations
- **Acid-intolerant crops (pH 7.0-8.5):** Cauliflower, cabbage, asparagus, celery, leek, beet, onion, chard, parsnip, spinach
- **Acid-loving crops (pH 4.5-6):** Blueberry, chicory, chestnut, endive, potato, fennel, tea, shallot, coffee, watermelon, rhubarb

### 4. Mineral Status
- Covered by the mineral deficiency diagnostic key

### 5. Fire Frequency
- **Method:** Observe species changes on east-west ridges; examine tree cross-sections for fire scars
- **Frequent fire:** Dry, scrabbly, summer-deciduous, thick-seeded species
- **No fire:** Broadleaf, winter-deciduous, small-seeded, thin capsules, deep litter

### 6. Frost
- **Frost-sensitive indicator species:** Tomatoes, bananas, potatoes
- **Method:** Plant frost-susceptible species down a hill profile to map frost intensity

### 7. Drainage
- **Poor drainage indicators:** Mosses, sundews, fine-leaved heaths
- **Good drainage indicators:** Chestnuts (require 2 m of well-drained soil)

### 8. Mineral Deposits
- **Plant-ore associations** (from Davidov, Sputnik 1979):
  - Violets indicate zinc
  - Catchfly indicates cobalt
  - Aster indicates selenium
  - Milk vetch indicates selenium and uranium
  - Russian thistle indicates boron
  - Alyssum indicates nickel
  - Honeysuckle indicates silver and gold
  - Horsetail indicates gold and silica

### 9. Overgrazing and Compaction
- **Indicators:** High grasshopper and pasture grub activity; patches of poisonous, thorny, unpalatable plants (Sodom apple, oxalis, capeweed)
- **Pioneer species:** Chicory colonises compacted areas

### 10. Macrofaunal Effects
- **Indicators:** Sea-bird rookeries, rabbit warrens, owl pellet piles, termite mounds, harvester-ant colonies
- **Reading:** Each creates distinctive vegetation patterns from specific nutrient inputs and soil disturbance

## Key Facts

- Earthworm abundance is a primary indicator of soil health
- Worms accumulate pollutants (DDT, lead, cadmium, dioxins) at 14-20 times soil levels
- Oysters concentrate zinc to 11% dry weight; abalone concentrate cadmium
- Some plants can be used for biological mining of gold and other metals
- Where birds, reptiles, and worms are absent, soil contamination is likely
