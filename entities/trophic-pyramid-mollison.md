# Trophic Pyramid

## Description

The trophic pyramid mollison is an ecological concept critically examined by [[bill-mollison]] in *[[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual*. While acknowledging its usefulness as a conceptual model, Mollison argues that the pyramid oversimplifies natural systems and that real-world food relationships are better understood as complex, cyclic food webs rather than linear hierarchies.

## Classification

- **Concept type**: Ecological theory / Energy transfer model
- **Related concepts**: Food web, trophic levels, energy flow, nutrient cycling
- **Discipline**: Ecology, systems theory

## Key Facts

- Mollison critiques the trophic pyramid as "very simplistic" and "unrelated to field reality" except in laboratory or feed-lot conditions.
- He identifies four key failings of the simple pyramid model:
  1. **Nature is more complex**: Field conditions involve food webs, not simple trophic levels, governed by management practices rather than food habits alone.
  2. **Pyramids ignore feedback**: Vegetation eventually "eats" grasshoppers, frogs, fish, and people through nutrient cycling. Animals return 90%+ of consumed food as manure, feeding vegetation growth.
  3. **Maturity changes the equation**: Mature organisms become "constant recyclers" rather than growers. Old fish/carp recycle food without growing. This is why permaculture harvests young, growing organisms.
  4. **Food chains are not simple**: Most species are omnivorous; even cows ingest insects with grass. People eat at multiple trophic levels simultaneously.

## Mollison's Critique in Detail

- The real system "resembles less of a one-way staircase (the pyramid) than a series of cyclic events; less of a ziggurat and more like a spider's web."
- The pyramid is sometimes used to argue for universal vegetarianism, which Mollison also critiques — noting that much of Earth's vegetation is inedible to humans and that animals "represent a valid method of storing inedible vegetation as food."
- However, the pyramid remains valid for understanding toxin bioaccumulation: "poisons at the base concentrate at the top" — the highest levels of radioactives and DDT are found in mothers' milk.

## Implications for Permaculture Design

- Design for cycles and routes rather than linear hierarchies
- Recognise that waste recycling to herbage is the main producer of that herbage
- Use mixed trophic strategies (omnivory) for resilience
- Crop organisms before maturity for maximum food conversion efficiency
- Integrate animals to convert inedible vegetation to human food

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The trophic pyramid critique directly applies to understanding soil food webs. In soil ecology, bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and microarthropods form complex cyclic food webs — not simple pyramids. Fungi occupy multiple trophic roles: decomposers breaking down organic matter, mutualists exchanging nutrients with plant roots, and predators trapping nematodes. The fungal-mediated decomposition cycle that Mollison describes (waste to decomposition to regeneration) is the core engine of soil fertility. Understanding soil as a web rather than a pyramid leads to better composting, mulching, and soil management practices.
