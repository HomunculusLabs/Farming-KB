## Overview

*Helleborus lividus* Aiton ex Curtis, the Majorcan hellebore or Balearic hellebore, is a compact evergreen perennial in the Ranunculaceae family. It is endemic to the Balearic Islands of Spain, occurring naturally only on Mallorca and nearby Cabrera.

The species is one of the smaller hellebores, typically reaching 30–45 cm in height. Its distinctive three-lobed leaves with bluish-green marbling make it one of the most ornamental members of the genus.

It produces nodding, cup-shaped flowers in late winter to early spring. Petals (actually modified sepals) are pale green to cream, often flushed with pink or purple on the exterior.

Like all hellebores, *H. lividus* contains toxic cardiac glycosides including hellebrin and helleborin. The species has a long history of use in folk medicine on Mallorca despite its toxicity.

In traditional Mallorcan folk medicine, a decoction of the roots was used as an emetic and to treat parasitic infections in livestock. Human use was rare due to the well-known toxicity.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Plantae · **Order:** Ranunculales · **Family:** Ranunculaceae · **Genus:** *Helleborus* · **Species:** *H. lividus*

Authority: Aiton ex Curtis (1789). The epithet "lividus" refers to the bluish-grey (livid) coloration of the foliage.

The plant forms a compact clump of leathery, evergreen leaves. Each leaf is divided into three broad, toothed leaflets, 5–12 cm long, with distinctive silver-grey veining and marbling on the upper surface.

Leaf undersides are paler green, sometimes tinged purple. The foliage is more prominently patterned than in any other *Helleborus* species, making it valuable for ornamental breeding.

Flowers are 4–6 cm across, composed of five petal-like sepals that persist for 6–8 weeks. True petals are reduced to small, tubular nectaries surrounding the stamens.

Flowering occurs from January to March, making it one of the earliest-flowering hellebore species. The pale flower color contrasts attractively against the dark, marbled foliage.

The rhizome is short, stout, and blackish, with dense fibrous roots. It grows slowly, forming a clump 20–30 cm across over several years.

*Helleborus lividus* is one of the few diploid species in the genus (2n = 2x = 16). This makes it genetically distinct from the polyploid *H. orientalis* complex, which has 2n = 4x = 32.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Helleborus lividus* is endemic to Mallorca and the island of Cabrera in the Balearic archipelago of the western Mediterranean. Its total native range is less than 500 km².

It grows in limestone crevices, on shady cliffs, and in humus-rich pockets in the Serra de Tramuntana mountain range of Mallorca. Elevations range from 200 to 1,000 meters.

The species is typically found in association with other Mediterranean calcicole plants including *Rhamnus alaternus*, *Arum italicum*, and various fern species (*Asplenium*, *Polypodium*).

It prefers north- and east-facing slopes that receive shade during the hottest part of the day. It is strictly calcicole, requiring alkaline soils (pH 7.5–8.5).

Mediterranean climate conditions prevail: mild, wet winters and hot, dry summers. Annual rainfall in its habitat is 600–900 mm, mostly falling October through April.

The plant enters partial summer dormancy, with reduced growth during the dry season. Evergreen leaves persist but become less turgid, recovering with autumn rains.

Old leaves are shed gradually as new growth emerges in autumn. The plant maintains 4–6 functional leaves year-round in favorable conditions.

Fire is not a significant ecological factor in its cliff-face habitat. The species shows no adaptation to post-fire regeneration, unlike many Mediterranean plants.

## Toxicology

All parts of *H. lividus* contain ranunculin-derived protoanemonin and cardiac glycosides of the bufadienolide type. Hellebrin is the most abundant cardiac glycoside, present in all tissues but concentrated in the rhizome.

Ingestion causes nausea, vomiting, dizziness, and in severe cases, cardiac arrhythmia due to the digitalis-like action of bufadienolides on the myocardium. Livestock poisoning has been documented on Mallorca.

The toxic threshold for hellebrin in humans is estimated at 0.1–0.3 mg/kg body weight. Even handling cut flowers and then touching the mouth can cause mild symptoms.

Skin contact with sap can cause contact dermatitis.

## Cultivation

*Helleborus lividus* is prized in hellebore breeding for its distinctive foliage and compact habit. It is one of the parent species of the popular hybrid *H.* × *sternii* (*H. lividus* × *H. argutifolius*).

In cultivation, it requires very well-drained, alkaline soil and protection from winter wet. It is hardy to approximately –8 °C (USDA zones 8–9), making it less cold-tolerant than most other hellebores.

Plants perform best in dappled shade with morning sun. They are susceptible to crown rot in heavy, poorly drained soils.

Container cultivation is popular in regions with cold winters, allowing plants to be moved under cover during freezing weather. Use a gritty, lime-rich compost mix.

Propagation is by seed or careful division. Seeds should be sown fresh in late spring, as viability declines rapidly with storage. Germination occurs the following winter after a warm-cold stratification cycle.

Division should be done in early autumn, taking care to avoid damage to the fragile root system. Established plants resent disturbance and may take a year to recover.

The species is susceptible to hellebore black death, a viral disease caused by HeNV (Helleborus net necrosis virus). Infected plants display black streaking on stems and leaves and should be destroyed to prevent spread.

## Conservation and Ecological Role

The species is classified as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List due to its extremely restricted range and small total population. Wild populations are estimated at fewer than 10,000 mature individuals.

Grazing pressure from feral goats on Mallorca and Cabrera poses a significant threat. Goats eat the new growth in spring, preventing seed production in accessible populations.

A goat exclusion fencing program has been implemented at three key sites in the Serra de Tramuntana since 2018. Early results show increased flowering and seed set within exclosures.

Climate change projections indicate increased drought frequency in the Balearic Islands. The species' reliance on winter rainfall for growth and reproduction makes it vulnerable to drying trends.

Ex situ conservation collections exist at the Botanical Garden of Sóller (Mallorca) and several European botanical gardens. Seed banking has been initiated but germination protocols need optimization.

Flowers are pollinated by early-emerging bees and hoverflies on Mallorca. The nectar-rich flowers provide an important early-season food source when few other plants are in bloom.
