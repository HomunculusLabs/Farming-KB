# Suillus bovinus

**Suillus bovinus** is an ectomycorrhizal basidiomycete fungus in the family Suillaceae, commonly known as the Jersey cow mushroom. It is a pine-associated ectomycorrhizal species notable for its [[bionutrients-lab-calcium-phosphate-calamansi]] using
plate-clearing assays. Suillus bovinus was one of only three species, alongside
Hysterangium setchellii and Rhizopogon vinicolor, that formed halos around
colonies, indicating a degree of mineral solubilization. In contrast,
Cenococcum geophilum, Hebeloma crustuliniforme, Laccaria laccata, and Piloderma

This mineral weathering capacity is ecologically significant because it enables Suillus bovinus to access nutrients bound in mineral matrices, potentially making phosphorus, calcium, and other essential elements available to its host trees. These values place Suillus bovinus as a moderate-quality food resource for mycophagous mammals. Its nutritional profile is comparable to other Suillus species and suggests that it contributes meaningful protein and mineral nutrition to animals that include it in their diet.

## Collembolan Feeding Preferences

Shaw (1988) evaluated the feeding preferences of the collembolan Onychiurus armatus among various fungal species grown in agar culture. Suillus bovinus was among the less preferred food sources, with only 18.2% of the colony area consumed and a mean fecal count of 16.6 per culture vessel. This relatively low palatability may provide some protection from grazing pressure by soil microarthropods compared to more palatable species like Marasmius androsaceus (72.2%) or Mycena galopus (68.2%).

The low palatability of Suillus bovinus to collembola is consistent across the
Suillus genus; Suillus luteus was more preferred at 50.7% consumption, but
Pisolithus tinctorius was even less preferred at 0.2%. This suggests that some
ectomycorrhizal fungi may have evolved chemical defenses or nutritional profiles
that deter microarthropod grazing, potentially protecting the mycorrhizal
interface from physical disruption.

## Decomposition Interactions

### Litter Decomposition Suppression

Colpaert and van Tichelen (1996) demonstrated that the decomposition of beech leaf litter was significantly less in the presence of Scots pine seedlings colonized by Suillus bovinus than in the presence of the saprotroph Lepista nuda. [[fungal-nitrogen-mineralization-pathways]] only occurred in the presence of Lepista nuda, not with Suillus bovinus or the other ectomycorrhizal species tested (Thelephora terrestris and Paxillus involutus).

This finding supports the Gadgil effect, where the presence of ectomycorrhizal tree roots and their associated fungal communities can suppress leaf litter decomposition rates. The authors suggest that while ectomycorrhizal fungi like Suillus bovinus are capable of some decomposition in the absence of competing saprotrophs, they are competitively inferior to specialist saprotrophs for organic resource utilization.

### Context-Dependent Decomposition Effects

Zhu and Ehrenfeld (1996) revisited the Gadgil effect in oligotrophic forest systems and found that in some conditions, the presence of roots actually increased saprotrophic fungal activity and decomposition rates. The nematode faunal population of rooted chambers was significantly enhanced. The difference in results among studies is probably related to the overall fertility of the soil and the relative dependence of the system on readily available versus unavailable sources of nutrients.

### Interaction with Paxillus involutus

Lindahl et al. (1999) investigated the interaction between Suillus luteus and Paxillus involutus with the wood-decomposing saprotroph Hypholoma fasciculare. These studies revealed complex competitive dynamics between ectomycorrhizal and saprotrophic communities that influence decomposition rates and nutrient cycling.

## Ecological Significance

Suillus bovinus occupies an important niche in pine forest ecosystems as both an ectomycorrhizal partner and a participant in mineral weathering processes. Its ability to solubilize minerals distinguishes it from many other ectomycorrhizal species and may contribute to the long-term sustainability of pine forests on nutrient-poor soils by slowly releasing mineral-bound nutrients. The species serves as a moderate food resource for both mycophagous mammals and soil invertebrates, contributing to belowground food web dynamics while its low palatability to collembola may help protect mycorrhizal function from grazing disruption.
