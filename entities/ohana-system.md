# Ohana System

## Description

The Ohana system is a traditional Hawaiian land division and resource management framework that integrated [[watershed-management]], agriculture, aquaculture, and community governance into a single indivisible unit. Described by [[bill-mollison]] as "a nice concept" and one of the most sophisticated indigenous permaculture systems ever developed, the Ohana division treated people as integral components of the landscape rather than separate from it.

## Classification

- **Category**: Indigenous land management system
- **Geographic origin**: Hawaiian Islands
- **Historical period**: Pre-contact Hawaii (estimated thousands of years of development)
- **Related systems**: [[keyline-design]], ahupua'a (similar Polynesian watershed divisions)

## Key Facts

### Watershed-Based Division

The Ohana divided each island from mountain ridge to surrounding reef, including the lagoon between shoreline and reef, extending over the reef itself. Each division controlled its entire watershed from origin to discharge into the sea and beyond. The boundaries followed natural features — ridges between valleys — creating logical, ecologically coherent districts.

### Keyline Water Management

The Hawaiians independently invented what modern permaculture calls the keyline system. They constructed massive stone walls at the keyline elevation, directing water from upper valleys out to the ridges and back to valleys at lower levels. These stone-lined channels carried water to taro paddy fields situated on ridge points. The keyline systems still exist and can be examined, some built of enormous cyclopean stones.

### Forest Protection

Forests were designated taboo areas because they served as the water source for the entire system. Forested from keyline upward, foot traffic was confined to ridge tops to protect the watershed integrity. Forests were used carefully for essential purposes only, reflecting a sophisticated understanding of the relationship between forest cover and water availability.

### Nutrient Cycling to Aquaculture

Where human and forest nutrients eventually reached the sea, the Hawaiians enclosed areas with rock walls. Within these enclosures, marine organisms (particularly algae) would capture nutrients within approximately three days. The Hawaiians raised millions of pounds of mullet in these enclosed fish ponds, converting runoff nutrients into protein. The nutrient cycle then returned upslope to the taro paddies.

### Agricultural Techniques

On exposed dry sites, they used intricate cross-wind patterns of tiny rock mulch walls, sometimes only ten feet apart, to grow dryland crops like sweet potato. They also cultivated a dryland fern for mulch material. These micro-catchment systems demonstrated sophisticated understanding of microclimate manipulation.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

### Integrated Design Model

The Ohana represents one of the finest historical examples of integrated [[permaculture-design]], combining forestry, agriculture, aquaculture, and human settlement into a closed-loop nutrient cycling system. It demonstrates that sophisticated design can support high population densities on limited island resources without degrading the ecosystem.

### Forest-Water Relationship

Mollison uses the Hawaiian example to illustrate a critical permaculture principle: the amount of water generated on high islands is directly relevant to the amount of forest present. Historical springs dried up or recommenced flowing depending on whether forest was being removed or extending. People alive in Hawaii can remember the descent of mists as forests have extended — fields that were dry grasslands are now wet and marshy forested areas.

### Degradation After Disruption

The arrival of missionaries and cattle disrupted the Ohana system. Condominium development, deforestation, and the breakdown of traditional governance led to all fish traps filling and becoming solid earth, the nutrient flow going directly into the sea, and overall productive capacity declining rapidly. Mollison notes that fewer people now live on the islands and the land is far less able to support them.

### Modern Applicability

Mollison suggests the Ohana system could be reinstituted as a model for modern permaculture design, particularly the concept that people are integral parts of their watershed division. The principle of managing complete watersheds from source to sea remains a cornerstone of permaculture design methodology.

### People as Integral to the System

The word Ohana itself carries the meaning that the people belong to the division — you belong to a valley, a set of fields, a fish pond, and a reef section. The Ohana is a totality: watershed, people, nutrient flow, animals, everything forming a single indivisible unit. This concept prefigures modern ecosystem management by millennia and offers a social-ecological model that modern permaculture aspires to replicate.

### Reef Management and Fish Shelters

Beyond the shoreline, the Hawaiians built underwater structures extending over the reef, creating additional fish shelter and habitat. They guarded right out to the reef and beyond, treating the marine environment as an integral extension of the terrestrial system. This demonstrates a holistic land-sea management approach that modern conservation is only now rediscovering.

### Technical Innovations

- Stone-lined keyline channels on steep gradients
- Ridge-top taro paddy fields at keyline elevation
- Enclosed reef fish ponds for nutrient capture
- Rock mulch micro-catchments for dryland cropping
- Foot traffic restricted to ridge tops to protect forest soils
- Underwater reef structures for fish habitat enhancement

### Volcanic Hazard Awareness

The Hawaiians also perched their homes up off the valley floor on the sides of valleys, avoiding areas subject to volcanic mud flows that could descend at speeds of 400 miles per hour. This awareness of natural hazards informed the entire settlement pattern within the Ohana, demonstrating that sustainable design must account for periodic catastrophic events.
