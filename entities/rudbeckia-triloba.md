# Rudbeckia triloba

## Overview

**Rudbeckia triloba** L., commonly known as brown-eyed Susan or three-lobed coneflower, is a short-lived perennial herbaceous plant in the family Asteraceae. It is native to eastern and central North America, from New York and Massachusetts west to Minnesota and south to Texas and Florida.

The species is distinguished from the more widely known black-eyed Susan (*Rudbeckia hirta*) by its taller stature, more numerous flower heads per plant, and distinctive three-lobed lower leaves. It typically reaches 0.9–1.5 meters in height and produces dozens of smaller flower heads (2.5–5 cm across) with warm orange-yellow rays and dark brown central cones.

Brown-eyed Susan is a popular subject for native plant gardens, pollinator meadows, and ecological landscaping projects. Its extended bloom period from mid-summer to hard frost provides continuous nectar resources for butterflies, native bees, and other beneficial insects.

Despite being short-lived (individual plants typically persist 2–4 years), *R. triloba* reliably self-seeds and can form persistent colonies in cultivated settings. Its adaptability to a range of soil and light conditions makes it one of the easiest native wildflowers to establish.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Plantae — **Order:** Asterales — **Family:** Asteraceae — **Subfamily:** Asteroideae — **Tribe:** Heliantheae — **Genus:** Rudbeckia

**Authority:** Carl Linnaeus, *Species Plantarum* (1753).

*R. triloba* is a short-lived perennial or biennial herb with an erect, branched stem covered in fine hairs (pubescence shorter and less coarse than *R. hirta*). The root system is fibrous without a taproot.

The most diagnostic feature is the lower stem leaves, which are typically 3–5 lobed (hence "triloba") with irregularly toothed margins. Upper leaves are smaller, lanceolate, and mostly unlobed. All leaves are alternate, 5–15 cm long, rough to the touch from short glandular hairs.

Flower heads (capitula) are borne in loose, branched corymbs. Each head has 8–16 ray florets (2–3 cm long) with warm yellow to orange-yellow ligules, surrounding a dome-shaped central disk 8–15 mm across, dark brown to black in color.

The pappus is absent or reduced to a short crown. Achenes are 2–3 mm, dark brown, oblong, with no wing or significant adaptation for wind dispersal. Seed dispersal is primarily by gravity and rain splash. Chromosome number: 2n = 38.

The species produces a bushy, multi-branched habit that can spread 60–90 cm in width at maturity. Under favorable conditions, a single plant may bear 50–100 flower heads simultaneously, creating a dramatic floral display.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is native to the eastern and central United States, from Massachusetts and New York west to Wisconsin and Minnesota, south to Oklahoma, Texas, and the Gulf Coast states. Isolated populations occur in southern Ontario, Canada.

It occupies open habitats including woodland edges, savannas, thickets, stream banks, meadows, pastures, and disturbed sites such as roadsides and abandoned fields. It is tolerant of partial shade, a trait that distinguishes it from most other *Rudbeckia* species that require full sun.

*R. triloba* is hardy to USDA Zone 4 (−34 °C) and tolerates a wide range of soil types including clay, loam, and sandy soils. It prefers moist, well-drained conditions but establishes readily on dry sites once the taproot-like root system develops.

The species is widely planted in gardens and restorations across its range and has naturalized in scattered locations outside its native distribution, particularly in the northeastern United States and Pacific Northwest. It is not considered ecologically invasive.

In ecological restoration, *R. triloba* is included in seed mixes for prairie reconstructions, riparian buffer plantings, and roadside beautification projects. Its tolerance of partial shade and compact growth form make it well-suited to understory plantings in open woodlands and savanna restorations.

## Ecology and Pollinator Value

*R. triloba* is a significant nectar and pollen resource for a wide range of pollinators. The small, accessible flower heads with abundant pollen are particularly attractive to native bees including small carpenter bees (*Ceratina* spp.), sweat bees (*Lasioglossum* spp.), and leafcutter bees (*Megachile* spp.).

Butterfly visitors include monarchs (*Danaus plexippus*), painted ladies (*Vanessa cardui*), various sulphurs (*Colias* spp.), and skippers (Hesperiidae). The prolonged bloom period (July–October in most areas) makes brown-eyed Susan one of the most reliable late-season nectar sources in eastern deciduous forest ecosystems.

Goldfinches, chickadees, and other small seed-eating birds consume the achenes in autumn. The dense, bushy growth form provides cover for ground-nesting birds and small mammals. The species serves as a minor host plant for the silvery checkerspot butterfly (*Chlosyne nycteis*).

In ecological landscaping, *R. triloba* is valued for its ability to naturalize in partially shaded areas where many other prairie forbs fail to persist. It performs well under the dappled shade of open-canopy trees and along woodland edges.

The species has moderate deer resistance due to the rough, hairy foliage, though young growth may be browsed in areas with heavy deer pressure. It is generally free of serious disease problems, though powdery mildew can affect plants in humid, poorly ventilated locations.

## Cultivation and Ornamental Use

Brown-eyed Susan is easily grown from seed, which requires no cold stratification but benefits from a 30-day cold-moist treatment to improve germination uniformity. Seeds germinate in 14–21 days at 20–22 °C. Transplants can be started indoors 6–8 weeks before the last frost date.

The species performs best in full sun to partial shade with average, well-drained soil. It is more shade-tolerant than *R. hirta* and *R. fulgida*, making it suitable for woodland-edge plantings and partially shaded meadows. Established plants are drought-tolerant and require minimal supplemental irrigation.

Deadheading spent flower heads can extend the bloom period but eliminates the seed source necessary for natural recruitment. For naturalizing, allow seed heads to mature and shatter in place. Self-seeded progeny will flower in the second year.

In mixed perennial borders, brown-eyed Susan combines effectively with native grasses (*Panicum virgatum*, *Schizachyrium scoparium*), asters (*Symphyotrichum* spp.), and goldenrods (*Solidago* spp.). The warm orange-yellow flowers create a strong visual contrast with purple asters and blue salvias.

The cultivar 'Prairie Glow' is a widely available selection noted for its deeper orange ray coloration and compact habit. Several seed strains are marketed by native plant nurseries, including strains selected for uniform height and extended bloom period.

## Related Species and Comparison

*R. triloba* is readily distinguished from other common *Rudbeckia* species by its three-lobed lower leaves, numerous small flower heads, and orange-yellow (vs. pure yellow) ray florets.

*R. hirta* (black-eyed Susan) is an annual or short-lived biennial with coarser stem pubescence, fewer but larger flower heads (5–10 cm), and unlobed leaves. *R. hirta* prefers full sun and drier conditions.

*R. fulgida* (orange coneflower) is a long-lived perennial with glossy, unlobed leaves and flower heads with more elongated rays. *R. fulgida* var. *sullivantii* 'Goldsturm' is one of the most widely planted native perennials worldwide.

*R. laciniata* (cutleaf coneflower) is a tall perennial with deeply incised pinnatifid leaves and greenish-yellow central disks. It tolerates partial shade similarly to *R. triloba* but reaches much greater height (1.5–3 meters).

Natural hybridization between *R. triloba* and *R. hirta* has been reported, producing intermediate forms with partially lobed leaves and variable ray coloration. These hybrids are uncommon in the wild but may occur in garden settings where both species are planted in proximity.
