# Terfezia claveryi

**Terfezia claveryi** (desert truffle, terfez, kama) is an ectomycorrhizal ascomycete fungus in the family Pezizaceae, native to the arid and semi-arid regions of the Mediterranean basin and the Middle East. One of the most prized edible fungi of the desert, it has been collected and consumed for millennia by indigenous peoples of North Africa, the Levant, and the Arabian Peninsula. Unlike the closely related [[tuber-melanosporum|European truffles]], desert truffles fruit underground in sandy soils and form obligate symbiotic associations with desert shrubs, primarily species of *Helianthemum* (rock rose).

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

- **Class:** Pezizomycetes (cup fungi and truffles)
- **Order:** Pezizales
- **Family:** Pezizaceae
- **Genus:** *Terfezia* Tul. & C. Tul., 1851
- **Species:** *T. claveryi* Chatin, 1892
- **Authority:** Chatin
- **Synonyms:** *Terfezia leonis* Tul.
- **Etymology:** Named after the French mycologist Clavery, who contributed to early studies of North African fungi
- **Arabic:** kama (Egypt), terfez (Levant), fagga (Saudi Arabia)
- **French:** truffe du désert
- **Spanish:** turma, pèbade

## Morphological Description

**Ascocarps** (fruiting bodies) are hypogeous (subterranean), roughly spherical to irregularly shaped, measuring 2-8 cm in diameter (occasionally up to 12 cm). The outer surface (peridium) is smooth to slightly verrucose, pale ochre to brownish, sometimes with a marbled appearance. The gleba (inner flesh) is firm, white to pale cream when young, becoming yellowish-tan with age, marbled with lighter veins that are the fertile tissue containing asci and spores.

**Microscopic features:** Asci are cylindrical to clavate, 180-280 × 14-22 μm, containing 8 spores each. Ascospores are globose to broadly ellipsoidal, 20-35 μm in diameter, ornamented with a prominent reticulate (net-like) wall pattern. The peridium consists of angular cells forming a textura angularis.

**Odor and taste:** Mildly aromatic, with a nutty, earthy, and slightly sweet fragrance — less pungent than *Tuber* species. The taste is mild and pleasant when cooked, developing richer umami notes. Raw specimens can be slightly astringent.

## Geographic Distribution and Habitat

*T. claveryi* is distributed across the Mediterranean's arid fringe and extends into the Middle East:

- **North Africa:** Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Egypt — the most productive collection areas
- **Southern Europe:** Spain (Almería, Murcia), Italy (Sicily, Sardinia), Portugal, Greece
- **Middle East:** Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Iraq, Iran, Syria, Jordan, Israel/Palestine
- **Horn of Africa:** Somalia (reported)

The fungus is adapted to semi-arid to arid climates with annual rainfall of 100-400 mm. It fruits in sandy, well-drained soils, typically in association with *Helianthemum* species (*H. almeriense*, *H. salicifolium*, *H. ledifolium*, *H. sessiliflorum*) growing in garrigue, steppe, and desert scrub communities. Fruiting occurs after heavy rains in autumn and winter (November-March in the Northern Hemisphere), typically 5-10 days after significant precipitation events when soil temperatures range from 10-20°C.

The symbiotic relationship is obligate ectomycorrhizal. *Terfezia* forms mantle structures around the fine roots of host *Helianthemum* species, exchanging soil minerals and water for plant-derived carbohydrates. This relationship is crucial for the fungus's survival in nutrient-poor desert soils and also benefits the host plant by enhancing drought tolerance and mineral uptake.

## Chemical Constituents and Nutritional Value

**Macronutrients:** Fresh *T. claveryi* contains approximately 85-90% water, 6-10% protein (dry weight 50-60%), 3-5% lipids, and 5-8% carbohydrates (including chitin). It is considered a good source of dietary protein for desert communities.

**Fatty acids:** Predominantly linoleic acid (C18:2, ω-6, ~40-50% of total), oleic acid (C18:1, ~20-30%), and palmitic acid (C16:0, ~15-20%). The fatty acid profile is favorable for human nutrition.

**Minerals:** Rich in potassium, phosphorus, magnesium, calcium, and iron. Also contains zinc, copper, manganese, and selenium.

**Bioactive compounds:** Phenolic compounds with antioxidant activity (phenolic acids and flavonoids), β-glucans with immunomodulatory properties, and ergosterol (vitamin D2 precursor). Total phenolic content is comparable to or exceeds that of many cultivated mushrooms.

**Volatile aroma compounds:** 1-octen-3-ol (mushroom alcohol), octanal, nonanal, and various aliphatic alcohols and ketones contribute to the characteristic aroma.

## Traditional and Culinary Uses

**North African cuisine:** Desert truffles are a traditional delicacy in Egypt, Libya, and Tunisia. In Egypt, kama is typically cleaned, sliced, and cooked in stews with onions, garlic, tomatoes, and cumin, or prepared with eggs and butter as a breakfast dish. Bedouin communities have collected them for centuries, locating fruiting bodies by observing cracking patterns in the soil surface.

**Levantine and Middle Eastern cuisine:** In the Levant, terfez is used in rice pilafs, stuffed vegetables, and slow-cooked meat dishes. In Saudi Arabia and the Gulf states, fagga is highly prized and commands premium prices during the winter season. Traditional preparation involves parboiling to remove any bitterness, followed by cooking in clarified butter (ghee) or olive oil.

**Spanish cuisine:** In southeastern Spain (Almería), turmas are prepared in revueltos (scrambled eggs), stews, and grilled dishes. Collection is regulated, and local festivals celebrate the truffle season.

**Historical records:** Desert truffles are mentioned in ancient texts including the works of Pliny the Elder (Naturalis Historia), Ibn al-Baytar's Kitab al-Jami' li-l-Adwiya al-Mufrada, and are referenced in the Quran. Theophrastus described fungi growing in sandy soils of Libya. Bedouin lore holds that desert truffles grow where lightning strikes the ground after rain.

## Modern Cultivation

Cultivation of *T. claveryi* has been researched since the 1990s, primarily in Spain and Morocco. The process involves:

1. **Host plant inoculation:** *Helianthemum* seedlings are inoculated with *T. claveryi* spore suspensions or mycelial cultures under controlled conditions
2. **Outplanting:** Inoculated seedlings are transplanted to sandy, well-drained field sites
3. **Management:** Minimal irrigation, weed control, and patience — 3-5 years to first fruiting
4. **Harvest:** Dogs or experienced collectors locate mature ascocarps during the fruiting season

Yields remain lower than for European truffle cultivation, but progress continues. Research groups at the University of Murcia (Spain) and institutions in Morocco and Tunisia are actively developing improved cultivation protocols. Challenges include the long time to first harvest, variable yields, and competition from native soil fungi.

## Ecological Significance

*Terfezia* species play important ecological roles in arid ecosystems. Their ectomycorrhizal associations improve host plant nutrition and water uptake, contributing to the stability of fragile desert plant communities. The fungi contribute to soil aggregation, carbon cycling, and the establishment of plant succession in disturbed sandy areas. They are sensitive to habitat destruction through overgrazing, urbanization, and agricultural expansion, making conservation of their habitat important for maintaining ecosystem function.

## Related Species

The genus *Terfezia* contains approximately 20-30 described species. *T. boudieri* (the "brown desert truffle") is the most commercially important after *T. claveryi*, with a darker peridium and stronger flavor. *T. leptoderma* is found in similar habitats. *Terfezia* is closely related to *Mattirolomyces* and *Choiromyces*. Desert truffles should not be confused with the hypogeous pezizalean genera *Picoa* and *Genea*, which also occur in Mediterranean arid habitats but are generally less palatable.

## Economic and Cultural Significance

Desert truffles constitute a significant non-timber forest product in North Africa and the Middle East. In Egypt, annual market value of kama reaches millions of Egyptian pounds during the winter season. In Saudi Arabia and the Gulf states, premium-quality fagga commands prices comparable to or exceeding those of European truffles during peak season.

The trade supports rural and Bedouin communities across the species' range. Traditional knowledge of collection sites, timing, and identification is passed down through generations. However, overharvesting, habitat destruction from urbanization and agriculture, and climate change (increasing drought frequency in the Mediterranean) threaten wild populations. Conservation efforts include habitat protection, regulated harvest seasons, and promotion of cultivation as an alternative to wild collection.
