## Overview

**Melissa officinalis** L. is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Lamiaceae, widely cultivated for its lemon-scented leaves and extensive medicinal applications.

Native to the Mediterranean basin and Central Asia, lemon balm has been used for over 2,000 years as a calming agent, digestive aid, and culinary herb.

The species name *Melissa* derives from the Greek word for honeybee (*melissa*), reflecting the plant's long association with beekeeping — bees are strongly attracted to its nectar-rich flowers.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom** Plantae → **Family** Lamiaceae → **Tribe** Nepetoideae → **Genus** *Melissa* → **Species** *M. officinalis* L.

Lemon balm grows 30–90 cm tall with a square stem characteristic of the mint family. Leaves are ovate to cordate, 3–8 cm long, with crenate margins and a pronounced lemon fragrance when crushed.

Flowers are small, white to pale pink, arranged in whorled clusters in the leaf axils. Blooming occurs from June to October in temperate regions.

The root system is fibrous and shallow, with stolons enabling vegetative spread. The plant is hardy to USDA zones 4–9 and tolerates a wide range of soil conditions.

## Distribution and Habitat

*M. officinalis* is native to southern Europe, the Mediterranean, western Asia, and Iran. It has been widely naturalized across Europe, North America, and parts of South America.

The species thrives in well-drained, loamy soils with partial shade to full sun. It is commonly found in woodland edges, roadsides, disturbed ground, and herb gardens.

Naturalized populations in eastern North America are often descendants of colonial-era introductions for medicinal and culinary use.

## Bioactive Compounds

The essential oil of lemon balm contains **citral** (geranial + neral, 30–40%), **geraniol** (10–15%), **linalool** (5–10%), **citronellal** (5–8%), and **β-caryophyllene** (3–5%).

Major phenolic constituents include **rosmarinic acid** (up to 5% dry weight), **caffeic acid**, and **chlorogenic acid** — all with documented antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity.

Triterpenes such as **ursolic acid** and **oleanolic acid** contribute to the plant's anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective properties.

## Pharmacology

The anxiolytic and sedative effects of lemon balm are primarily attributed to **GABA_A receptor modulation** by rosmarinic acid and specific terpenoid components.

Clinical trials demonstrate significant reductions in anxiety scores and improvements in calmness following acute administration of standardized extracts (300–600 mg).

Cognitive studies show that low-dose extracts improve memory performance and accuracy on cognitive tasks, while higher doses produce sedation — a characteristic biphasic dose-response curve.

Antiviral activity against HSV-1 and HSV-2 has been demonstrated in vitro, attributed to polyphenolic compounds that interfere with viral adsorption and replication.

Topical applications of lemon balm cream (1% lozenges or 1% extract cream) have shown reduced healing time and symptom duration in clinical trials for herpes labialis.

Animal studies indicate hepatoprotective effects through reduction of liver enzyme markers and antioxidant activity in models of chemically induced liver damage.

## Culinary and Nutritional Use

Fresh or dried lemon balm leaves are used as a flavoring agent in teas, salads, soups, and desserts. The lemon aroma pairs well with fish, poultry, and fruit dishes.

Melissa tea is a traditional European herbal infusion, often combined with chamomile, valerian, or mint for its calming properties. The tea is prepared by steeping 1.5–4.5 g of dried herb in 150 mL hot water for 10 minutes.

The essential oil is used in aromatherapy, perfumery, and as a flavoring in liqueurs such as Benedictine and Chartreuse. Oil yield from commercial cultivars ranges from 0.1–0.5% v/w.

Nutritional analysis shows dried lemon balm provides moderate levels of vitamin C, calcium, and flavonoids per 100 g serving, though typical culinary quantities contribute minimally to daily requirements.

## Cultivation and Uses

Lemon balm is propagated by seed, division, or stem cuttings. Seeds require light for germination and benefit from cold stratification at 4°C for 2–4 weeks.

Harvest occurs when the plant reaches 15–20 cm height. Leaves are typically dried at 35–40°C to preserve essential oil content. Fresh leaves contain 0.05–0.3% essential oil.

In permaculture systems, lemon balm serves as a beneficial insect attractant, ground cover, and companion plant for tomatoes and brassicas. Its spreading habit requires management in small gardens.

The plant's rapid vegetative growth and tolerance of repeated harvesting make it a productive perennial for herb spirals, polyculture beds, and edible landscape designs.

## Traditional and Historical Use

Dioscorides and Paracelsus both documented lemon balm for its healing properties. Medieval monastic gardens maintained extensive *Melissa* plantings for medicinal teas and tinctures.

In Persian traditional medicine, lemon balm (*Badranjboya*) was prescribed for heart palpitations, digestive complaints, and melancholy — uses that align with modern pharmacological findings.

The 16th-century herbalist John Gerard wrote that lemon balm "comforteth the heart and driveth away all sadnesse," reflecting its established reputation as a mood elevator.
