The species has a long history of use in traditional Chinese medicine
(TCM) and Japanese Kampo medicine, where the bark and wood are prepared
as decoctions for [[mollison-designers-neem-tree-as-natural-insecticide]] and
bittering agent.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

picrasma quassioides was first described by David Don in 1832 as Aeschrion
quassioides, later transferred to the genus Picrasma by Bentham in 1861.
The genus Picrasma contains approximately 6–9 species distributed across
tropical and temperate Asia.

The tree typically reaches 10–15 meters in height, with a straight trunk
up to 30 cm in diameter at maturity. Bark is grey-brown, smooth when
young, developing shallow fissures with age. Inner bark is intensely
bitter and bright yellow.

Leaves are odd-pinnately compound, 20–40 cm long, with 7–15 leaflets.
Each leaflet is 5–12 cm long, 2–4 cm wide, lanceolate to elliptic, with
serrate margins and acuminate tips. The foliage is dark green above and
lighter below, turning yellow in autumn.

Flowers are small, greenish-yellow, arranged in axillary panicles
appearing in May–June. The fruit is a drupe, 5–8 mm in diameter, blue-
black when ripe, containing a [[clay-pellet-seed-dispersal]]
and root suckering.

## Bioactive Compounds

The defining constituents of P. quassioides are quassinoids, a class of
degraded triterpenoid lactones exclusive to the Simaroubaceae family. Over
50 quassinoids have been isolated from the species, including quassin,
neoquassin, picrasin, and nigakilactone.
