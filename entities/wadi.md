## Description

A wadi (Arabic: وادي) is a dry riverbed, valley, or watercourse that contains water only during and immediately after heavy rain events. Wadis are characteristic landforms of arid and semi-arid regions across the Middle East, North Africa, and parts of Australia (where similar features are sometimes called "creek beds" or "outwash runnels"). They function as the primary drainage and water-transport networks of desert landscapes, carrying episodic flood flows from highlands to interior basins.

## Formation and Characteristics

- Formed by intermittent water erosion over geological timescales in arid climates.
- Typically broad, shallow, and braided channels with sandy or gravelly beds.
- Vegetation concentrates along wadi channels, creating linear oases of biological productivity.
- Subsurface water may persist in wadi sediments long after surface flow ceases.
- Wadis grade from narrow, steep upland channels to broad alluvial fans on desert flats.

## Key Facts

- Wadis are the primary conduits for water transport in desert landscapes, channelling run-off from highland areas.
- The term appears extensively in archaeological and historical literature; many abandoned settlements in the Saharan and Thar deserts were originally located along wadi systems.
- Wadi sediments often contain higher moisture, organic matter, and nutrient levels than surrounding desert pavement.
- Mollison references wadis as key elements in dryland water harvesting and reforestation strategies.

## Ecological Significance

- **Biological corridors**: Wadis concentrate water and nutrients, supporting vegetation bands that act as wildlife corridors across otherwise barren desert.
- **Alluvial soils**: Sediment deposited in wadis creates some of the most productive agricultural soils in arid regions.
- **Groundwater recharge**: Infiltration through wadi beds is a primary mechanism for groundwater recharge in arid basins.
- **Flood routing**: Wadis can carry devastating flash floods after rare rain events, making understanding their behaviour critical for settlement planning.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Water harvesting**: Mollison's dryland reforestation strategy involves grading wide hollows on contour from "outwash (wadi) runnels," intercepting water before it concentrates in destructive floods.
- **[[tree-establishment-in-deserts]].
- **Historical lesson**: Many wadi-based agricultural civilisations collapsed when sediment loads from upstream erosion silted their canals and fields — Mollison cites this pattern in discussing desertification.
- **Contour interception**: The strategy of intercepting wadi flow at intervals with swales and spreading structures converts destructive channelised flow into productive dispersed infiltration.
