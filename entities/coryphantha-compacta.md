# Coryphantha compacta

**Coryphantha compacta** (Engelm.) Britt. et Rose, commonly known as the **Pincushion Cactus**, is a small globular cactus native to the [[australian-magic-mushroom-common-names]]:** Pincushion Cactus, Bakana, Wichuri

The genus Coryphantha has undergone considerable taxonomic revision over the years. Some authorities have merged it with Mammillaria, while others maintain its distinct status based on differences in the structure of the axillary areoles and the presence of grooved tubercles. The genus name derives from the Greek "koryphe" (summit) and "anthos" (flower), referring to the flowers arising from the crown of the plant.

## Morphology

Coryphantha compacta is a small, solitary, globular but somewhat flattened, spiny cactus growing up to 3.5 inches (8 cm) in diameter. It grows in dry hilly and mountainous regions and is hardly visible in the sandy soil where it occurs due to its small size and cryptic appearance.

The radial spines are whitish, measuring 0.5 to 0.75 inches (1 to 2 cm) in length. Central spines are usually absent. The crowded tubercles are arranged in 13 rows. Arising from the center of the crown, either singly or in pairs, the yellow flowers measure up to 1 inch (2.5 cm) in length.

The root system is typically a thickened taproot that helps the plant survive in arid conditions. During dry periods, the cactus can shrink and retract into the soil, further enhancing its cryptic appearance and protecting it from herbivores and excessive sun exposure. The tubercles are prominently grooved on the upper surface, a characteristic feature of the genus Coryphantha.

## Ethnobotanical Uses

### Tarahumara Use

The Tarahumara Indians of northern Mexico consider Coryphantha compacta a kind of **Peyote** or **Hikuli**. The plant is known locally as **Bakana** (also referred to as Wichuri or Bakana-wa). It is taken by shamans and is both respected and feared within the community.

Bakana is used as a substitute for true Peyote ([[lophophora-williamsii]]), the most important visionary cactus
- **Bakana** or **Wichuri** — Coryphantha compacta, considered more dangerous
- **Matsawari** — ariocarpus-retusus or related species
- **Sunami** — pelecyphora-aselliformis

Each hikuli is believed to have a distinct spirit or personality, and shamans must develop relationships with each one individually. The Tarahumara believe that improper use of Bakana can lead to madness or permanent mental disturbance.

### Other Coryphantha Species

Coryphantha palmerii has likewise been reported as a hallucinogen in Mexico, suggesting that the psychoactive properties may extend across the genus. This broader pattern of psychoactivity among Coryphantha species supports the traditional Tarahumara classification of multiple cacti as hikuli.

## Pharmacology

The phenylethylamine alkaloids found in Coryphantha species are structurally related to mescaline and act primarily as serotonin 5-HT2A [[cannabinoid-receptor-agonists-antagonists-synthetic-reference-blesching]]. Macromerine, the most psychoactive of the Coryphantha alkaloids, has been shown to produce effects qualitatively similar to but generally milder than mescaline. The relative potencies and ratios of these alkaloids likely contribute to the distinct subjective experience reported from Bakana use compared to true Peyote.

## Historical Documentation

The use of Coryphantha species among the Tarahumara was first documented by the Norwegian ethnographer Carl Lumholtz in the early 20th century during his expeditions through the Sierra Madre of Chihuahua. Lumholtz provided detailed accounts of Tarahumara ceremonial life and the various hikuli cacti employed by their shamans. His work remains a foundational reference for the ethnobotany of the region.

## Cultural Context

Within the Tarahumara pharmacopoeia of psychoactive cacti, Coryphantha compacta occupies an important but subsidiary role to true Peyote. The recognition of multiple "hikuli" species demonstrates the sophisticated ethnobotanical knowledge of the Tarahumara, who distinguish between cacti based on their specific effects and ceremonial appropriateness.

The continued use of Bakana by Tarahumara shamans, despite the availability of true Peyote, suggests that each hikuli species serves a unique function within their ceremonial and healing practices. This nuanced understanding of plant pharmacology, developed over centuries of empirical observation, represents one of the most sophisticated systems of psychoactive plant classification in the world.
