## Description

**Samuel Kayman** is a farmer and landowner from **Wilton, New Hampshire, USA**,
whose property served as a site for Bill Mollison's field observations of natural
processes in early spring. Mollison assessed woodlands on Kayman's farm for
forages, microsites, snow melt effects, wildlife refuges, and leaf litter
distribution — leading to appropriate design strategies. The site visit is
cited as an example of **Method of Design No. 2: Observation** in permaculture
methodology.







## Classification

- **Category**: Person — Farmer / Landowner

## Key Facts

- Landowner at **Wilton, New Hampshire** in the northeastern USA.
- Mollison visited Kayman's farm in **early spring** to conduct site assessment.
- Observations made on the property included:
  - **Forages** — wild edible and useful plant species present in the woodland
  - **Microsites** — small-scale variations in topography, moisture, and exposure
  - **Snow melt effects** — how differential melting creates moisture gradients
  - **Wildlife refuges** — habitat features supporting animal populations
  - **Leaf litter distribution** — patterns of organic matter accumulation
- These observations led directly to **design strategies** for the site.
- The visit exemplifies **Method of Design No. 2 (Observation)** as one of
  Mollison's core permaculture design methods.
- New England climate: cold temperate, significant snowfall, acidic forest soils.







## Relevance to Cultivation / Mycology

- **Leaf litter distribution** assessment is directly relevant to understanding
  **fungal decomposition patterns** in temperate forest floors.
- New England forests are dominated by **ectomycorrhizal** tree species (oak,
  beech, birch, pine) whose root systems depend on fungal partnerships.
- **Microsite assessment** identifies locations where moisture, shade, and
  organic matter converge to create ideal conditions for **wild mushroom
  fruiting** — potential sites for cultivating *Morchella* (morel),
  *Pleurotus* (oyster), or *Grifola frondosa* (maitake).
- **Snow melt gradients** create seasonal moisture pulses that trigger fungal
  fruiting in spring — understanding these patterns is essential for both wild
  mushroom foraging and outdoor cultivation timing.
- **Wildlife refuges** on the farm support mammal species that disperse
  **fungal spores** (especially mycorrhizal truffle-like species) through
  their foraging and digested material.
