## Description

The Rothamsted Experimental Station (now Rothamsted Research) is the oldest agricultural research station in the world, located at Harpenden in Hertfordshire, United Kingdom. Established in 1843 by John Bennet Lawes and Joseph Henry Gilbert, it hosts long-term field experiments that have run continuously for over 180 years. Mollison references Rothamsted data extensively to illustrate the remarkable persistence of [[soil-biota]] even under continuous wheat monoculture, and the slow turnover of ancient humus derived from pre-agricultural forests.

## Key Facts

### Key Experiments
- **Broadbalk Wheat Experiment:** Continuous wheat cropping since 1843, one of the longest-running field experiments in history.
- Over 140 consecutive wheat cropping cycles have been conducted on the same plots.

### Soil Biomass Findings
- After 140 continuous wheat cycles, the plough layer still supports 0.5 tonnes per hectare of living microbial biomass.
- About 1.2 tonnes/ha of organic carbon is returned annually to the soil as root and stalk material from grain crop.
- **Biomass composition:** 50% fungi, 20% bacteria, 20% yeasts/algae/protozoa, 10% macrofauna (earthworms, nematodes, arthropods, molluscs).

### Humus Longevity
- Humus at Rothamsted has a mean age of 1,400 years.
- This ancient humus likely derives from forests that preceded the wheat monoculture by many centuries.
- It yields nutrients very slowly and is resistant to bacterial attack.
- This demonstrates the extraordinary persistence of soil organic matter when not destroyed by intensive cultivation.

### Soil Biota Survival Under Monoculture
- The massive soil biomass exists on a very meagre food supply — described by Mollison as "rather like an elephant eating a cabbage once a day."
- Periods of sudden food supply come from root masses at harvest and [[root-exudates]] during growth.
- 30% of plant energy may be released to soil via roots as sugars and other compounds.
- Even the destructive practice of continuous wheat monoculture cannot fully eliminate soil life.

### Broader Significance
- Rothamsted data provides some of the only long-term evidence about soil carbon dynamics, nutrient cycling, and biological persistence.
- The experiments demonstrate that soil biological communities are extraordinarily resilient, surviving even prolonged agricultural abuse.
- However, in Canada, similar continuous wheat cropping has reduced humus levels to just 1% of original levels, showing that Rothamsted's clay soils may be unusually resilient compared to other soil types.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Rothamsted is significant to permaculture practitioners because its long-term data proves two key points. First, soil biota are remarkably persistent — even 140 years of continuous wheat monoculture cannot eliminate them. Second, the ancient humus derived from pre-agricultural forests is still slowly releasing nutrients after 1,400 years, demonstrating the enormous long-term value of building soil organic matter. The implication is clear: if soil life persists even under the worst agricultural practices, imagine how productive it can be under permaculture management that actively feeds and protects it. The Rothamsted data underscores Mollison's argument that the soil biological component, not mineral fertiliser additions, is the true foundation of sustainable agriculture.

## Experimental Design

The Rothamsted experiments use replicated plots with controlled treatments
maintained continuously for over 180 years. Treatments include unfertilized
control, farmyard manure, and various inorganic fertilizer combinations.
This long-term approach reveals soil changes invisible in short-term studies.

## Key Discoveries

The experiments proved that soil organic matter declines under continuous
cropping without organic inputs. Mycorrhizal diversity decreases with high
phosphorus fertilization. Soil pH drift caused by ammonium sulfate applications
demonstrated the acidifying effect of nitrogen fertilizers.

## Modern Research

Contemporary Rothamsted research focuses on sustainable agriculture, plant
pathology, and soil microbiome dynamics. The North Wyke Farm Platform tracks
environmental outcomes of different farming systems, providing data on carbon
sequestration, water quality, and biodiversity under varied management regimes.

Continued research into rothamsted experiment expands the knowledge base for ecological design.
