## Overview

**Nymphaea alba** L. is a perennial aquatic plant in the family Nymphaeaceae, native to Europe, North Africa, and [[panax-ginseng]].

In permaculture and [[aquatic-plants-and-pond-ecosystems]], providing shade, habitat, and water filtration.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Order:** Nymphaeales
- **Family:** Nymphaeaceae
- **Genus:** *Nymphaea* L.
- **Species:** *N. alba* L.

The species was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in *Species Plantarum*.

Several subspecies have been recognized: *N. alba* subsp. *alba* (widespread European form), *N. alba* subsp. *occidentalis* (North African populations), and *N. alba* subsp. *transcaspica* (Central Asian populations).

The genus name derives from the Greek *nymphaia*, referring to water nymphs, reflecting the plant's association with springs and still waters.

## Morphology

**Roots:** Thick, fleshy rhizomes buried in the muddy substrate, producing numerous fibrous roots for nutrient absorption.

**Leaves:** Large, round to broadly ovate, 15–30 cm diameter, green on the upper surface and often purplish-red beneath. The **cleft (notch)** at the base of the leaf blade is a key diagnostic feature distinguishing *Nymphaea* from *Nuphar*.

**Flowers:** Solitary, floating on the water surface, 10–20 cm diameter. Petals are white, 20–30 per flower, arranged in overlapping whorls. Stamens are bright yellow. Flowers open in the morning and close in the evening over 3–4 days.

**Fruit:** A spongy, berry-like capsule that ripens underwater, eventually releasing seeds as it decomposes.

## Distribution and Habitat

*N. alba* is native across **Europe from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean**, extending eastward through Turkey, the Caucasus, Iran, and into Central Asia.

It also occurs in **North Africa** (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia) and has been naturalized in parts of [[ethnomycology-cultural-significance-sacred-fungi]] across European traditions.

In **[[solanaceous-hallucinogens-of-medieval-european-witchcraft]] herbalists including Dioscorides and later authors prescribed water lily rhizomes for **dysentery, leucorrhea, and as a cardiac sedative**.

The dried rhizomes were sometimes smoked or prepared as a tea for their mild sedative and **oneirogenic (dream-enhancing) properties**, a use paralleled by the related blue water lily (*[[nymphaea-caerulea]]*) in Egyptian tradition.
