    43|# Tulsi — Holy Basil (*Ocimum tenuiflorum*)
    44|Tulsi (Sanskrit: "the incomparable one") is a sacred aromatic herb in the mint family, revered for millennia across South Asia as both a medicinal plant and a manifestation of the divine. Known as Holy Basil in English, it occupies a central position in Ayurveda, Hindu ritual practice, and modern phytotherapy. Classified as an [[adaptogenic-herbs-guide]], it helps the body resist physiological and psychological stress through broad-spectrum normalization of homeostatic processes.

    56|The binomial *Ocimum tenuiflorum* is the currently accepted name; the synonym *Ocimum sanctum* L. remains common in pharmacological literature. Distinct from sweet basil (*Ocimum basilicum*).
    57|
    58|## Morphology
    59|
    60|Erect, much-branched annual or short-lived perennial, 30–90 cm tall. Square, hairy stems; simple, opposite, ovate leaves 2–5 cm with serrate margins and prominent venation. Densely glandular leaf surface releases a strong clove-like aroma when crushed. Terminal racemes of small, two-lipped white to purplish flowers. Fibrous, shallow root system.
    61|
    62|## Three Main Varieties
    63|
    64|### Krishna Tulsi (*Ocimum tenuiflorum* var. *purpurascens*)
    65|
    66|Dark green to deep purple leaves and stems with the most pungent, peppery aroma. Considered the most medicinally potent variety, with the highest concentrations of eugenol and anthocyanins.
    67|
    68|### Rama Tulsi (*Ocimum tenuiflorum* var. *longifolia*)
    69|
    70|The most widely cultivated variety, with bright green leaves and a sweeter, milder anise-clove aroma. Hardier and more disease-resistant; preferred for commercial cultivation and mass-market tulsi tea products.
    71|
### Vana Tulsi (*Ocimum gratissimum*)
Also called wild or forest tulsi — a taller, more robust plant with light green, hairy leaves and a strong lemon-clove scent. Botanically a separate species but traditionally grouped with tulsi. Rich in eugenol and thymol; often blended with Krishna and Rama in classical formulations.

## Phytochemistry
The pharmacological activity of tulsi arises from volatile terpenoids, phenolic acids, flavonoids, and triterpenoids:
    79|
### Volatile Oil Constituents
The essential oil (0.5–1.1% dry weight) is dominated by **eugenol** (up to 70%), with β-caryophyllene, α-humulene, linalool, methyl eugenol, and camphor. Eugenol provides the clove-like aroma and contributes anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties.

### Phenolic Acids
- **Rosmarinic acid** — caffeic acid ester with potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory activity. Inhibits COX-2, lipoxygenase, and TNF-α; major contributor to neuroprotective and stress-reduction effects. Caffeic and chlorogenic acids provide additional antioxidant and hepatoprotective activity.
    87|
### Triterpenoids
- **Ursolic acid** — the most significant triterpene (0.5–2.5% in dried leaf). Anti-inflammatory via NF-κB inhibition; anti-diabetic through enhanced glucose uptake and AMPK activation; hepatoprotective and anti-cancer in vitro. Oleanolic acid provides similar activity.

### Flavonoids and Other Compounds
Apigenin, luteolin, quercetin, kaempferol, and their glycosides; carotenoids (β-carotene, lutein); vitamins A and C; zinc, iron, and calcium.

## Adaptogenic Properties
Tulsi meets the classical definition of an [[adaptogenic-herbs-guide]]: nonspecific increase in resistance to diverse stressors, normalizing influence irrespective of pathological direction, and safety with minimal side effects. Mechanisms include HPA axis modulation (eugenol and rosmarinic acid reduce cortisol by regulating CRH/ACTH), antioxidant enzyme induction (SOD, catalase, glutathione peroxidase), anti-inflammatory cascade (NF-κB, COX-2, iNOS inhibition), and neurotransmitter support (GABA, serotonin, dopamine modulation; MAO inhibition).

## Traditional Ayurvedic Uses
   101|
   102|In Ayurvedic medicine, tulsi is classified as *rasayana* (rejuvenative), *kapha-vata hara* (reduces kapha and vata doshas), and *medhya* (enhances intellect). Classical indications:
   103|
   104|- **Jvara** (fever) — household remedy for intermittent and malarial fever
   105|- **Prameha** (diabetes and urinary disorders) — adjunct to metabolic management
   106|- **Kasa** (cough), **Shwasa** (dyspnea), and **Vishama jvara** (chronic fever) — expectorant and bronchodilatory
   107|- **Mood and cognition** — daily *tulsi panaka* or tea for mental clarity
   108|- **Skin disorders** — leaf paste applied topically for eczema, ringworm, and insect bites
   109|
   110|## Modern Clinical Research
   111|
   112|### Stress and Anxiety
   113|
   114|A double-blind RCT (n=158, 2017) using 500 mg tulsi extract twice daily for 6 weeks demonstrated significant reductions in perceived stress (PSS), anxiety (BAI), and depression (BDI) scores, with improved sleep quality. Multiple smaller trials corroborate anxiolytic and nootropic effects.
   115|
   116|### Blood Sugar Regulation
   117|
   118|Clinical studies show tulsi leaf powder (1–3 g/day, 4–12 weeks) significantly reduces fasting blood glucose (15–25%), postprandial glucose, HbA1c, and cholesterol. Ursolic acid enhances insulin signaling and peripheral glucose uptake via AMPK.
   119|
   120|### Immune Function
   121|
   122|Studies demonstrate enhanced phagocytic activity, NK cell cytotoxicity, antibody titers, and Th1 responses. Human trials report reduced incidence and severity of upper respiratory infections with regular consumption.
   123|
   124|### Anti-inflammatory and Analgesic
   125|
   126|Eugenol and ursolic acid inhibit COX-2 and lipoxygenase with NSAID-comparable potency in animal models, without gastric toxicity. Clinical reports show benefit in rheumatoid and osteoarthritis as adjunctive therapy.
   127|
   128|## Cultivation
   129|
   130|Native to the Indian subcontinent; thrives in tropical/subtropical climates (USDA 9–11). Prefers well-drained loamy soil, full sun to partial shade. Propagation by seed (spring) or cuttings; germination 1–2 weeks. Space 30–45 cm apart; flowers in 60–90 days. Pinch regularly for bushier growth. Frost-sensitive annual in temperate regions. Generally pest-resistant.
   131|
   132|## Culinary and Ritual Uses
   133|
   134|In Hindu tradition, tulsi is the earthly manifestation of goddess Lakshmi-Tulsi, worshipped daily in household shrines and integral to the *Tulsi Vivah* ceremony. Tulsi leaves and infused water are offered at temples. In cuisine, fresh leaves flavor chutneys, raita, stir-fries, and herbal teas across South and Southeast Asia. Thai holy basil (*kaphrao*) is a closely related species.

   146|## Dosage, Safety, and Drug Interactions
   147|
   148|**Dosage:** Dried leaf tea 2–4 g per cup, 2–3 cups daily; leaf powder 1–3 g/day; standardized extract 300–600 mg/day; tincture (1:5) 2–4 mL 2–3×/day.
   149|
   150|**Safety:** Generally well-tolerated. Mild GI upset possible at high doses. Avoid large medicinal doses in pregnancy (uterotonic in animals); discontinue 2 weeks before surgery; monitor thyroid at high doses if hypothyroid.
   151|
   152|**Drug Interactions:** Anticoagulants (additive effect; monitor INR); antidiabetics (enhanced hypoglycemia); antihypertensives (additive BP lowering); sedatives (additive CNS depression); CYP2E1/CYP1A2 substrates (eugenol inhibits metabolism); thyroid medications.
