The roots have not been found to contain significant tryptamine concentrations; the bark is the primary alkaloid-bearing tissue.

Total alkaloid content varies seasonally, with some reports suggesting higher concentrations during the Australian spring and summer months.

Tannins and flavonoids are also present in the bark, contributing to its astringent properties and traditional medicinal applications.

## Ethnobotanical Use

Aboriginal Australian peoples of southwestern Australia have used Acacia acuminata for various practical and medicinal purposes for thousands of years.

The wood was traditionally used for making spear throwers (woomera), boomerangs, and other tools due to its hardness and workability.

The aromatic wood and bark were burned as incense in ceremonial contexts, a practice documented by several anthropological sources.

Some Aboriginal groups reportedly prepared decoctions of the bark for treating skin ailments and wounds, exploiting its astringent tannin content.

The psychoactive properties of the bark have been discussed in ethnopharmacological literature, though direct evidence of traditional visionary use remains limited.

Contemporary interest in A. acuminata as a source of DTM for botanical extraction has developed largely outside of traditional Aboriginal practice.
