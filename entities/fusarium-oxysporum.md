# Fusarium oxysporum

**Type:** Species (Fungus)
**Category:** Plant Pathogenic Ascomycete
**Also known as:** Fusarium wilt pathogen

## Overview
Fusarium oxysporum is a widespread soil-borne fungal pathogen that causes
vascular wilt diseases in over 100 plant species. The species is divided
into numerous formae speciales (f. sp.), each specialized to infect a
particular host plant. It is one of the most economically damaging plant
pathogens worldwide, affecting crops from banana to tomato to cotton.

## Taxonomy and Classification
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Sordariomycetes
- **Order:** Hypocreales
- **Family:** Nectriaceae
- **Genus:** Fusarium
- **Species:** F. oxysporum

Note: The F. oxysporum species complex includes both pathogenic and
non-pathogenic strains. Formae speciales are defined by host
specificity and include f. sp. cubense (banana), f. sp. lycopersici
(tomato), f. sp. vasinfectum (cotton), and many others.

## Key Details

### Disease Mechanism
- Infects through roots, entering the vascular system (xylem)
- Colonizes water-conducting vessels, blocking water transport
- Causes wilting, yellowing, and eventual death of the plant
- Produces three types of asexual spores: macroconidia,
  microconidia, and chlamydospores
- Chlamydospores persist in soil for years without a host

### Formae Speciales (Host-Specific Groups)
- f. sp. cubense: Panama disease of banana (Tropical Race 4 is
  currently devastating global banana production)
- f. sp. lycopersici: Fusarium wilt of tomato
- f. sp. vasinfectum: Fusarium wilt of cotton
- f. sp. melonis: Fusarium wilt of melon
- f. sp. niveum: Fusarium wilt of watermelon
- f. sp. basilici: Fusarium wilt of basil
- Over 120 formae speciales have been described

### Cell Biology
- Woronin body development shows an early hexagonal form that
  matures into an oval form (Wergin, 1973)
- Woronin bodies plug septal pores to maintain cellular integrity
  after damage
- HEX-1 homologs are conserved across Fusarium species

### Genetic Diversity
- Capable of both clonal and recombining reproduction
  (O'Donnell et al., 1998)
- Mating populations have been used to study biological species
  recognition
- Phylogenetic analysis has revealed nine phylogenetically distinct
  species within the Fusarium grisea complex

### Interactions with Mycorrhizae
- [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] ([[glomus-mosseae]]) reduce Fusarium
  solani incidence on peanut plants (Abdalla and Abdel-Fattah, 2000)
- [[laccaria-laccata]] suppresses F. oxysporum on Douglas fir seedlings
  (Sylvia and Sinclair, 1983)
- Mycorrhizal protection works through both enhanced plant nutrition
  and direct antagonism

### Environmental Persistence
- Chlamydospores survive in soil for extended periods (10 to 15 years)
- Spread through contaminated soil, water, tools, and transplants
- Optimal disease development occurs at warm soil temperatures
  (25 to 30 degrees C) and moderate moisture

### Biocontrol Strategies
- Non-pathogenic F. oxysporum strains can be used as biocontrol agents
  through induced systemic resistance
- Trichoderma species are effective antagonists
- Soil solarization and crop rotation reduce inoculum levels
- Resistant cultivars are available for many crops

## Relevance
Fusarium oxysporum is a major threat to global agriculture, with
Tropical Race 4 currently threatening the Cavendish banana, the world's
most exported banana variety. Understanding its biology, genetics, and
interactions with beneficial soil fungi is critical for developing
sustainable disease management strategies. In permaculture, building
soil health through [[mycorrhizal-inoculation]] and biological diversity is
a key approach to managing Fusarium wilt without chemical inputs.
