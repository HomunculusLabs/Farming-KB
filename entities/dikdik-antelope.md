# Dikdik Antelope

## Description

The dikdik is a small species of antelope in the genus Madoqua, native to eastern and
southern Africa. Mollison uses the dikdik as a reference in the stock unit system of
animal equivalents for [[rangeland-management]], noting that approximately 125 dikdik
antelope equal one standard stock unit (one European cow and calf at approximately
450 kg liveweight). These diminutive antelopes represent the potential for micro-livestock
in integrated rangeland systems, converting browse to protein very efficiently.

Despite their tiny size, dikdik demonstrate the principle that diverse small species
can outproduce cattle per unit area when properly managed in multi-species systems.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Animalia
- **Class:** Mammalia
- **Order:** Artiodactyla
- **Family:** Bovidae
- **Genus:** Madoqua
- **Common names:** Dikdik, dik-dik
- **Notable species:** M. kirki (Kirk's dikdik), M. guentheri (Guenther's dikdik)
- **Size:** 30–40 cm shoulder height, 3–6 kg adult weight

## Key Facts

- One of the smallest antelope species, weighing only 3–6 kg at maturity
- 125 dikdik equivalent to one standard stock unit (450 kg cow + calf)
- Native to dry bush and scrub of eastern and southern Africa
- Monogamous pair-bonding with strong territorial behavior
- Browsers feeding on leaves, shoots, buds, and fruits of woody plants
- Can survive without free surface water, obtaining moisture from vegetation
- Selective feeders choosing high-quality browse items from diverse shrubs
- Standing crop of large herbivores on African savannah approximately 500 kg/ha
- Dikdik demonstrate extreme efficiency in converting browse to animal protein

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Micro-livestock potential:** Represents extreme end of scaled livestock for rangeland systems
- **Rangeland efficiency:** Diverse species mixes always exceed monoculture yields per Mollison
- **Browse utilization:** As browsers, dikdik use vegetation layers inaccessible to grazers
- **Dung dispersal:** Antelope dung contributes to soil fertility and fungal substrate
- **[[coprophilous-fungi]]:** Dung supports specialized fungi including Panaeolus and Coprinus
- **Ecological indicator:** Dikdik presence indicates healthy shrub and browse layer

## Ecological Relationships

- Browser feeding strategy complements grazers in multi-species rangeland systems
- Can exist without free surface water except in extreme drought conditions
- Territorial behavior creates even distribution across available range area
- Dung marking of territories contributes to nutrient cycling and distribution
- Prey base supports predator populations (eagles, wild cats, jackals, snakes)
- Seed dispersal through ingestion and deposition of fruit and browse seeds

## Stock Unit Context (from Mollison)

Mollison's stock unit system provides equivalents for rangeland planning. The standard
unit is a European cow and calf at 450 kg liveweight. This equals: 2 African cows;
5 European sheep; 20 African sheep; 25 Thompson gazelles; or 125 dikdik antelope.
Ten standard stock units equal 1 elephant; 4 black rhino; 15 zebras; or 27 [[hartebeest]].

## Design Implications

- Multi-species stocking using diverse animal sizes utilizes range resources efficiently
- Small species can be continuously harvested with minimal environmental impact
- Protein production per unit area can exceed cattle with well-managed small species
- Integration of browsers and grazers maximizes rangeland productivity per hectare
- Natural behaviors (territoriality, water independence) reduce management inputs
