# Tim Flannery

## Description

Tim Flannery is an Australian scientist, explorer, and author known for his
influential work on ecology, climate change, paleontology, and the deep
relationship between Australian environments and their human and non-human
inhabitants. Holmgren references Flannery's argument that Australian ecosystems
and people have a strong disposition towards cooperative rather than competitive
relationships as a consequence of the continent's uniquely harsh and infertile
environment.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Ecology, palaeontology, climate science, science communication
- **Nationality**: Australian
- **Notable Work**: *The Future Eaters* (Reed Books, 1994)
- **Field**: Ecological history, evolutionary biology, climate change
- **Recognition**: Australian of the Year (2007), climate change communicator

## Key Facts

- Author of *The Future Eaters* (1994), a landmark work discussing the
  metabolic efficiency of Australian animals and the deep coevolution of
  Australian ecosystems and human cultures over tens of thousands of years
- Argues that Australian ecosystems and people have a strong disposition towards
  cooperative rather than competitive relationships because of the continent's
  harsh and infertile environment — a thesis with profound implications for
  [[permaculture-design]] in Australian conditions
- This cooperativity hypothesis appears to hold true for indigenous ecosystems
  and Aboriginal culture, where resource scarcity favoured mutualistic strategies
  over competitive ones
- Holmgren notes that extending this hypothesis to post-settlement Australian
  culture is more difficult to accept, because fossil-fuel-based capitalism and
  individualism masked the development of cooperative rural culture almost before
  it could become established in the late 19th century
- His work provides scientific and historical backing for [[permaculture]]'s emphasis
  on cooperative and mutualistic relationships in system design
- Discusses the metabolic efficiency of Australian animals, which have evolved
  lower metabolic rates as an adaptation to nutrient-poor environments

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

1. **Cooperation in Harsh Environments**: Flannery's thesis that resource-poor
   environments favour cooperation over competition supports permaculture's
   design emphasis on mutualistic relationships. This is especially relevant for
   designing systems in marginal or degraded landscapes where resources are
   inherently limited

2. **Metabolic Efficiency**: His discussion of metabolic efficiency in Australian
   fauna parallels permaculture's focus on [[aquaculture-design-principles-and-energy-efficiency]] and catching/storing
   energy in biological systems. Australian species achieve more with less energy,
   providing a model for low-input design

3. **Ecological History**: Understanding the deep ecological history of a
   continent — the evolutionary pressures, extinction events, and adaptive
   strategies — informs bioregional design decisions about appropriate species,
   structures, and [[forest-management-strategies]]

4. **Competition vs. Cooperation Dynamics**: Provides scientific grounding for
   the [[permaculture-principle-1-observe-interact]] of "[[permaculture-integrate-rather-than-segregate]] segregate" by showing
   that mature, energy-limited ecosystems naturally trend toward mutualistic
   relationships rather than competitive ones

5. **Australian Bioregional Context**: Flannery's analysis helps permaculture
   designers understand why Australian conditions favour fundamentally different
   design approaches than those developed in more fertile continents like Europe
   or [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]]

## Key Arguments from The Future Eaters

- Harsh, infertile environments select for cooperative rather than competitive
  ecological strategies over evolutionary time
- Australian Aboriginal culture developed extreme cooperative mechanisms suited
  to the continent's low productivity — sharing, mutual obligation, and
  prohibition of competition were survival strategies
- Post-settlement Australia's cooperative tendencies were suppressed by
  fossil-fuel-driven competitive capitalism before they could take root
- Comparison with America's resource-rich pioneer history shows much more violent
  and competitive dynamics, supporting the thesis that resource abundance selects
  for competition
- Energy descent may reawaken cooperative tendencies in Australian land
  management and culture as fossil fuel subsidies diminish
