## Description

A hotbed is a traditional horticultural structure used for growing plants in cold weather by harnessing the heat generated by decomposing organic matter, typically fresh horse manure mixed with straw. Hotbeds consist of a sunken bed or raised frame filled with a thick layer of fermenting manure topped with growing medium, covered with glass frames (lights) or other transparent material. They were a cornerstone of the Parisian maraicher system in the 19th century, enabling year-round vegetable production in cold climates without fossil fuels.

## Classification

- **Category**: Protected cultivation technique / season extension
- **Type**: Passive heating system (biological heat source)
- **Historical period**: Widespread use 17th–early 20th century; origins traceable to 1670s Versailles
- **Related structures**: Cold frame, cloche, greenhouse

## Key Facts

- Hotbeds generate heat through the aerobic decomposition of fresh horse manure, which can maintain bed temperatures of 60-80°F (15-27°C) even when outside temperatures are well below freezing.
- The technique was pioneered at the royal potager (vegetable garden) at Versailles under head gardener La Quintinie in the 1670s and 1680s.
- Parisian maraichers (market gardeners) used hotbeds extensively between 1850 and 1900 to produce fresh salads in January and early cucumbers and melons in May and June.
- A typical Parisian market garden applied between 100 and 400 tons of horse manure per acre annually, depending on the number of hotbeds in use.
- Decomposing manure was placed not only under the growing beds but also filled into walkways between frames to provide additional ambient heat.
- Once the manure lost its heating capacity (after several weeks), it was removed and used as a mature compost soil amendment, creating a closed-loop fertility system.
- Horse manure from city stables was delivered daily by wagons returning from vegetable deliveries to urban markets—a sustainable recycling of "transportation waste."
- Hotbeds were covered with glass frames and further insulated with one-inch-thick rolled rye straw mats on cold nights.
- The system declined in the early 20th century as automobiles replaced horses (reducing manure supply) and chemical fertilizers and heated greenhouses became available.
- Eliot Coleman cites the hotbed system as historical inspiration for his low-input winter harvest techniques.

## Construction and Operation

- Dig a pit or build a frame 18-24 inches deep; fill with 12-18 inches of fresh horse manure mixed with straw.
- Top with 4-6 inches of quality growing medium (compost and loam).
- Cover with glass sash (lights) or modern transparent covers.
- Manure heats for 3-6 weeks, then gradually cools; timing plantings to match heat output is critical.
- Ventilate by propping covers on warm days to prevent overheating.
- After use, spent manure becomes compost for field application.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Hotbeds represent one of the most elegant examples of closed-loop, low-input food production in horticultural history. The system required no external energy source beyond the metabolic heat of decomposition and demonstrated that year-round vegetable production is achievable without fossil fuels. Modern growers can adapt hotbed principles using various organic materials for winter production in community gardens, small farms, and off-grid situations. The hotbed model directly inspired Eliot Coleman's unheated greenhouse winter harvest system, substituting solar energy captured by plastic covers for biological heat.
