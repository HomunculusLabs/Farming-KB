# Safrole

Safrole (4-allyl-1,2-methylenedioxybenzene) is a naturally occurring
phenylpropanoid compound and the principal component of sassafras oil.
It serves as the primary precursor in the biosynthesis of several
important natural and synthetic compounds, including myristicin,
elemicin, and the synthetic empathogen MDMA (3,4-methylenedioxy-
methamphetamine). Found in high concentrations in Sassafras albidum
(sassafras tree) bark oil and Ocotea pretiosa (Brazilian sassafras),
safrole has a long history of use as a flavoring agent and fragrance
ingredient. Its role as a precursor to controlled substances has led
to strict international regulation under the United Nations Convention
Against Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances,
making it one of the most heavily regulated plant-derived chemicals.

## Chemical Identity

- **IUPAC name:** 5-(2-propenyl)-1,3-benzodioxole
- **Molecular formula:** C10H10O2
- **Molecular weight:** 162.19 g/mol
- **CAS number:** 94-59-7
- **SMILES:** O1c2ccccc2C=C1CC=C
- **InChIKey:** YFOVMDLMPJUYKP-UHFFFAOYSA-N
- **Appearance:** Colorless to pale yellow oily liquid
- **Odor:** Sweet, spicy, warm; reminiscent of sassafras root,
  anise, and camphor
- **Boiling point:** 232-234 C at 760 mmHg
- **Density:** 1.096 g/mL at 20 C
- **Solubility:** Insoluble in water; soluble in ethanol, ether,
  chloroform, and fixed oils

## Natural Occurrence

Safrole is distributed across multiple plant families and is
biosynthesized via the phenylpropanoid pathway from cinnamic acid.
Major natural sources include:

| Plant Species | Part | Safrole Content |
|--------------|------|----------------|
| Sassafras albidum | Root bark oil | 75-90% |
| Ocotea pretiosa | Essential oil | 80-95% |
| Cinnamomum camphora | Root bark oil | 80-90% |
| Myristica fragrans | Nutmeg oil | 2-4% |
| Piper betle | Leaf oil | 1-3% |
| Dorema ammoniacum | Gum resin | Trace |
| Illicium verum | Fruit oil | Trace |

Sassafras albidum (North American sassafras) was historically the
primary commercial source of safrole, with root bark yields of
6-9% essential oil by weight. Following regulation, Ocotea pretiosa
from Brazil became the dominant source. Cinnamomum camphora
varieties from China and Taiwan also serve as significant sources.

## Biosynthesis and Metabolism

Safrole is biosynthesized in plants from phenylalanine via the
phenylpropanoid pathway. Phenylalanine undergoes deamination to
cinnamic acid, which is then hydroxylated and O-methylated. A
chain-shortening step produces the allyl side chain characteristic
of safrole. In mammals, safrole undergoes extensive hepatic
metabolism via cytochrome P450 enzymes, primarily CYP1A2 and
CYP2E1. The major metabolic pathway involves hydroxylation of
the allyl side chain at the 1'-position, followed by conjugation
with glucuronic acid or sulfate for excretion. A minor but
toxicologically significant pathway involves oxidation to
1'-hydroxysafrole, which can be further activated to form
DNA-reactive sulfuric acid esters.

## Pharmacology and Toxicology

Safrole itself has mild psychoactive properties when consumed
in substantial quantities. At doses of 5-10 mL of sassafras oil,
effects include euphoria, visual distortions, and mild sedation.
However, safrole's primary pharmacological significance lies in
its hepatotoxicity and carcinogenicity. The compound is classified
as a Group 2B carcinogen (possibly carcinogenic to humans) by IARC.
Chronic administration in rodent studies produces hepatic tumors
through a genotoxic mechanism involving the formation of
1'-sulfooxysafrole, which forms DNA adducts in the liver.
The LD50 in rats is approximately 1950 mg/kg (oral), indicating
moderate acute toxicity. The carcinogenic dose in rats is
significantly lower than what would be consumed through
traditional use of sassafras tea.

## Synthetic Chemistry

Safrole's principal industrial significance is as a precursor
to piperonal (heliotropin), used in perfumery and flavoring.
The oxidation of safrole to piperonal was historically performed
with chromium-based oxidants, though modern methods use ozonolysis
or potassium permanganate. Safrole is also the starting material
for the isomerization to isosafrole, which is then oxidized to
MDP2P (3,4-methylenedioxyphenyl-2-propanone), the key precursor
to MDMA and related substituted methylenedioxyamphetamines
(MDA, MDEA). This synthetic route is the primary reason for
international regulation of safrole and safrole-rich oils.

## Historical and Cultural Use

Sassafras root bark tea was a widely consumed beverage in North
America from pre-Columbian times through the early 20th century.
Indigenous peoples of eastern North America used sassafras for
medicinal purposes including treatment of fevers, skin conditions,
and digestive complaints. European settlers adopted sassafras tea
as a general tonic and spring blood purifier. The FDA banned
safrole as a food additive in 1960 based on the National Academy
of Sciences evaluation of its carcinogenic potential. Root beer
originally derived its characteristic flavor from sassafras root;
modern commercial root beers use artificial sassafras flavoring
without safrole. File powder (gumbo file), made from dried and
ground sassafras leaves, remains legal as the leaf material
contains only trace amounts of safrole.

## Legal Status

Safrole and safrole-rich essential oils are internationally
regulated as Category 1 precursor chemicals under the 1988
United Nations Convention Against Illicit Traffic in Narcotic
Drugs and Psychotropic Substances. In the United States, safrole
is listed as a List I chemical under the Controlled Substances
Act, requiring registration, record-keeping, and reporting for
all transactions. The European Union classifies it similarly under
Regulation (EC) No 273/2004. Many countries restrict or prohibit
the sale, import, and export of sassafras oil and safrole
concentrates. These regulations were enacted primarily to control
the diversion of safrole into MDMA production.
