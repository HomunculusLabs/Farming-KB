# Weston La Barre

## Description

Weston La Barre (1911-1996) was an American anthropologist best known for his comprehensive studies of the peyote religion among Native American peoples. His landmark work "The Peyote Cult" (1938) and its subsequent revised editions established him as the leading authority on the ritual use of peyote (Lophophora williamsii) in North American indigenous religious practice. He is cited extensively in the general bibliography of Harner's Hallucinogens and Shamanism with multiple publications spanning three decades of research on peyote and hallucinogenic plant use.

## Key Facts

- Published "The Peyote Cult" in 1938 as Yale University Publications in Anthropology, No. 19, establishing the definitive ethnographic study of peyote religion
- Published an enlarged edition of "The Peyote Cult" in 1969 through Schocken Books, incorporating three decades of additional research
- His 1960 article "Twenty Years of Peyote Studies" in Current Anthropology provided a comprehensive review of the field
- Cited in the Harner bibliography with publications spanning 1938, 1960, and 1969, demonstrating a sustained research program
- His work documented the Native American Church and its sacramental use of peyote as a legitimate religious practice
- La Barre's research provided the ethnographic foundation for the legal defense of peyote use as a religious right
- His work demonstrated the continuity between pre-Columbian plant use and contemporary Native American religious practice
- He argued that peyote use was not drug abuse but a sophisticated religious practice with deep historical roots
- His approach influenced the broader field of ethnopharmacological research by establishing rigorous ethnographic methods for documenting plant-based religious practices

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

While La Barre's primary focus was on peyote rather than mushrooms, his methodological approach to documenting the ritual use of psychoactive plants provided a model for ethnomycological research. His insistence on understanding peyote within its full cultural and religious context parallels the approach taken by Munn and others in documenting the Mazatec mushroom ceremony. The legal and academic framework that La Barre helped establish for peyote has informed subsequent efforts to protect the traditional use of psychoactive mushrooms and other sacred plants. His work also demonstrated the importance of long-term, sustained engagement with a single subject, as evidenced by his three decades of peyote research.

## Legacy

La Barre's work on the peyote cult remains a foundational text in the anthropology of religion and the study of psychoactive plant use. His combination of historical research, ethnographic fieldwork, and comparative analysis established a methodological standard that continues to influence the field. The legal protections for peyote use by members of the Native American Church owe much to the scholarly documentation that La Barre and his colleagues provided, demonstrating the practical importance of rigorous ethnographic research for protecting indigenous religious freedoms.

## Intellectual Context

La Barre's work was part of a broader movement in mid-twentieth century anthropology to take indigenous religious practices seriously as subjects of scholarly inquiry. Along with colleagues like Richard Evans Schultes (ethnobotany), R. Gordon Wasson (ethnomycology), and Michael Harner (shamanic studies), La Barre helped establish the academic legitimacy of studying psychoactive plant use as a cultural and religious phenomenon. His career at Duke University spanned decades during which the field evolved from dismissive curiosity to rigorous scholarly engagement, and his publications document this transformation in real time.

## The Peyote Religion

La Barre's documentation of the peyote religion describes a syncretic religious practice that combines elements of Native American spiritual traditions with Christian symbolism, centered on the sacramental consumption of peyote buttons. The peyote ceremony typically takes place in a tipi, lasts all night, and includes prayer, singing, and the communal ingestion of peyote. La Barre's detailed ethnographic accounts of these ceremonies provided Western readers with their first comprehensive understanding of how a psychoactive substance could serve as the central sacrament of an organized religion. His work demonstrated that peyote use was not recreational but deeply embedded in a complex religious system that served therapeutic and communal functions.

## Publications in the Harner Bibliography

The Harner volume cites three distinct publications by La Barre spanning three decades: "The Peyote Cult" (1938, Yale University Publications in Anthropology), "Twenty Years of Peyote Studies" (1960, Current Anthropology), and "The Peyote Cult" enlarged edition (1969, Schocken Books). This trajectory from initial fieldwork to comprehensive revision documents the maturation of peyote studies as a scholarly field and reflects La Barre's own evolving understanding of the peyote religion over a thirty-year career.

## Place in the Harner Volume

The prominence of La Barre in the Harner volume's bibliography reflects the central importance of peyote studies in the broader field of hallucinogenic plant research. Peyote was the first psychoactive plant to receive sustained scholarly attention in North America, and the methodological approaches developed for studying peyote use provided templates for subsequent research on mushrooms, ayahuasca, and other psychoactive substances. The fact that Harner cites La Barre's publications from 1938, 1960, and 1969 demonstrates the longitudinal depth of peyote scholarship and its foundational role in the field. La Barre's work thus serves as the historical backbone against which the newer research on Amazonian and Mexican hallucinogens presented in the Harner volume can be understood.

## Relationship to Harner's European Witchcraft Chapter

Harner's chapter on the role of hallucinogenic plants in European witchcraft draws on some of the same analytical principles that La Barre applied to peyote religion. Both scholars sought to understand how psychoactive plant use operates within a coherent religious system, and both resisted reductionist explanations that would dismiss the phenomena as mere "drug use." La Barre's demonstration that peyote use among the Native American Church constituted a legitimate religion provided a model for Harner's argument that European witchcraft may have similarly been grounded in the ritual use of psychoactive plants. The intellectual connection between these two bodies of research highlights the importance of the comparative method in understanding the relationship between hallucinogenic plants and religious practice.

## Critical Reception

La Barre's work received widespread recognition within the anthropological community for its thoroughness, sensitivity, and scholarly rigor. "The Peyote Cult" remains a standard reference in the field and has been continuously cited for over eight decades. His willingness to take indigenous religious practice seriously as a subject of scholarly inquiry was pioneering for its time and helped establish the academic legitimacy of the anthropological study of psychoactive plant use. Subsequent scholars including Omer Stewart, J.S. Slotkin, and David Aberle built directly on La Barre's foundation, and the Harner volume's extensive citation of his work demonstrates his continuing relevance to the field of hallucinogen studies.
