## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

The corm of H. hemerocallidea contains a distinctive profile of sterols and sterolins. The principal bioactive constituents include **hypoxoside** (a diglucoside of rooperol), **rooperol**, and sitosterol derivatives.

Rooperol, the aglycone of hypoxoside, is the compound most often associated with pharmacological activity. In vitro studies have demonstrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and weak cytotoxic properties for rooperol.

Sitosterol and sitosterolin (the glucoside form) are present at concentrations of approximately 0.1–0.6% of fresh corm mass. These compounds have been investigated for immunomodulatory effects, though clinical evidence in humans remains inconclusive.

Anti-diabetic activity has been demonstrated in rodent models, where aqueous corm extracts reduced blood glucose levels. Anti-cancer activity has been shown in vitro against several cell lines, but in vivo data are lacking.

## Traditional and Modern Uses

In Zulu traditional medicine, the corm is used to treat a wide range of conditions including urinary tract infections, prostate disorders, and sexually transmitted infections. Preparations typically involve boiling dried corm material in water or steeping it in milk.

The Sotho people use H. hemerocallidea as a tonic for dizziness, convulsions, and internal tumors. In Xhosa medicine, it is applied to burns and wounds and used as an emetic.

Since the late 1990s, the plant has been marketed commercially as an immune system booster for people living with HIV/AIDS. Plants are typically spaced 300–500 mm apart in well-prepared beds. The corms are planted 50–100 mm deep in sandy, well-drained soil. Full sun to partial shade is tolerated.

Under cultivation, the corm requires 2–4 years to reach harvestable size. Optimal growth occurs at temperatures of 18–28°C with regular irrigation during the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].

Commercial cultivation is expanding in South Africa and Zimbabwe to meet demand and reduce pressure on wild populations. Good Agricultural Practice (GAP) guidelines have been developed for the species.

## Nutritional and Chemical Profile

The corm contains approximately 60–70% carbohydrate (primarily starch), 8–12% protein, and 2–5% fat by dry weight. Mineral content includes notable levels of calcium, iron, zinc, and magnesium.

The characteristic yellow-orange color of the corm is due to carotenoid pigments, including beta-carotene and lutein. These contribute to the plant's antioxidant capacity alongside the sterol constituents.

Trace amounts of various alkaloids and flavonoids have been detected in phytochemical surveys, though these are not considered primary bioactive components. The total phenolic content is relatively low compared to other medicinal plants.

## Conservation Status

H. hemerocallidea is listed as Near Threatened on the South African National Biodiversity Institute's Red List. Wild harvesting for the traditional medicine trade is the primary threat to natural populations.

Demand for the corm has increased dramatically since the mid-1990s, driven by commercial promotion as an immune booster. Estimates suggest that several hundred tonnes of corm are harvested annually from wild populations.
