## Overview

Sisyrinchium angustifolium, commonly called narrow-leaf blue-eyed grass, is a rhizomatous perennial in the iris family. Despite its common name, it is not a true grass but produces grass-like foliage topped with small star-shaped violet-blue flowers.

The species is native to [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]], where it inhabits moist meadows, prairies, and open woodlands. It is valued in native plant gardens for its delicate flowers and tidy clumping habit.

Blue-eyed grass blooms from late spring through mid-summer, providing color when many spring bulbs have finished. The flowers open only in bright sunlight and close by afternoon.

The genus Sisyrinchium contains approximately 150 species distributed throughout the Americas. Sisyrinchium angustifolium is one of the most widespread species in eastern North America.

The species has received the RHS Award of Garden Merit for its ornamental reliability. Several cultivars have been selected for darker flower color or compact growth.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Family:** Iridaceae
- **Genus:** Sisyrinchium
- **Species:** Sisyrinchium angustifolium Mill.
- **Synonyms:** Sisyrinchium graminoides; Sisyrinchium bermudiana var. angustifolium

The genus name derives from the Greek for "pig snout," possibly referring to the rooting habit of corms. The species epithet "angustifolium" means "narrow-leaved."

Taxonomy of Sisyrinchium is notoriously difficult due to extensive hybridization and polyploidy. Sisyrinchium angustifolium has been confused with Sisyrinchium montanum and Sisyrinchium bellum in [[biodiversity-fungal-herbarium-collections]].

Chromosome numbers vary, with diploid (2n = 32) and tetraploid (2n = 64) populations documented. Tetraploid forms tend to be more robust with broader leaves.

Recent molecular phylogenetic studies place Sisyrinchium angustifolium in a clade with other eastern [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] species. The genus is most closely related to the [[chiric-caspi-brunfelsia-south-american-hallucinogen]] genus Orthrosanthus.

## Morphology

Plants form compact clumps 15–45 cm tall from short, knotted rhizomes. The root system consists of slender fibrous roots arising from the rhizome base.

Leaves are basal, linear, 2–4 mm wide, and grass-like with a slightly bluish-green cast. Leaf tips are bluntly rounded, distinguishing this species from some relatives.

Flowering stems (scapes) are flattened or slightly winged, 1–3 mm wide, bearing 2–4 reduced bracts. The wings on the scapes are a key diagnostic character for the genus.

Flowers are produced in terminal clusters of 2–7, subtended by two unequal spathe bracts. Each flower is approximately 1–1.5 cm across with six tepals in violet-blue.

The tepals are tipped with a small, sharp point and display a darker central vein. A yellow or white eye ring at the center creates a distinctive contrast.

Flowers open in the morning on sunny days and close by mid-afternoon. Individual blooms last only 1–2 days but are produced in succession over several weeks.

The fruit is a small, globose capsule 3–5 mm in diameter containing numerous dark brown seeds. Seeds are approximately 1 mm in diameter with a minutely wrinkled surface.

Plants are semi-evergreen in milder climates, retaining basal foliage through winter. In cold zones, foliage dies back and resprouts from the rhizome in early spring.

## Distribution and Habitat

Sisyrinchium angustifolium is native to eastern North America from Nova Scotia west to North Dakota and south to Florida and Texas. It is most abundant in the northeastern and mid-Atlantic states.

The species inhabits moist to wet meadows, prairies, stream banks, pond margins, and damp open woodlands. It tolerates brief seasonal flooding but requires well-drained conditions during [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

Populations occur on a wide range of soil types from sandy loams to clay. The species prefers neutral to slightly acidic pH but tolerates mildly alkaline conditions.

In the southeastern states, populations are often restricted to higher elevations in the Appalachian Mountains. Coastal plain populations occur in wet pine savannas and pitcher plant bogs.

The species is listed as endangered in some Midwestern states at the edge of its range. Conservation efforts focus on protecting remnant wet-meadow habitats.

## Cultivation

Blue-eyed grass is easily grown in average to moist garden soils in full sun to partial shade. It performs best in consistently moist but not waterlogged conditions.

Plants spread slowly by rhizome expansion and light self-seeding. In favorable sites, clumps may reach 20–30 cm in diameter over 3–5 years.

Division of established clumps in early spring or autumn is the easiest propagation method. Each division should include several growing points and a portion of the rhizome system.

Seed propagation is straightforward but slow. Seeds require 4–6 weeks of cold stratification and germinate irregularly over 30–60 days.

No serious insect or disease problems affect this species. Slugs may damage new growth in spring but rarely harm established plants.

The species combines well with other moisture-loving native perennials such as cardinal flower and blue flag iris. Its compact size suits it for the front of perennial borders.
