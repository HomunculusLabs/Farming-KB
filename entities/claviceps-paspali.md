# Claviceps paspali
## Overview
*Claviceps paspali* is a sclerotium-forming fungal pathogen in the family Clavicipitaceae that parasitizes grasses of the genus *Paspalum*. It is distinguished from the well-known cereal ergot *C. purpurea* by its host specificity, geographic range, and alkaloid profile. *C. paspali* is the causative agent of "paspalum staggers," a neurological syndrome in livestock that results from ingestion of sclerotia-contaminated forage. The fungus produces a characteristic suite of clavine alkaloids — notably paspaline, paspalicine, and paspalinine — that differ structurally from the peptide ergot alkaloids (ergotamine, [[serotonin]] and dopamine receptors, generating pharmaceutical interest. *C. paspali* is also used as a model organism in alkaloid biosynthesis research and experimental endophyte studies. Unlike *C. purpurea*, which has caused devastating ergotism epidemics in human populations through contaminated grain, *C. paspali* is primarily a veterinary concern affecting grazing animals on infested pastures.
## Taxonomy and Naming
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Ascomycota
- **Subdivision:** Pezizomycotina
- **Class:** Sordariomycetes
- **Order:** Hypocreales
- **Family:** Clavicipitaceae
- **Genus:** *Claviceps*
- **Species:** *C. paspali* (Stevens & J.G. Hall) Langdon, 1954
- **Synonyms:** *Sphacelia paspali* Stevens & J.G. Hall (anamorph)
The species epithet derives from its primary host genus *Paspalum*. The teleomorph (sexual stage) was described from specimens collected on *Paspalum dilatatum* in Australia. The anamorphic (asexual) *Sphacelia* stage is the form first observed infecting grass florets during the early phase of disease development.
Phylogenetic analyses using ITS, β-tubulin, and RPB1 sequence data place *C. paspali* in a distinct clade separate from *C. purpurea* and *C. fusiformis*, reflecting both host specialization and alkaloid pathway divergence. Molecular clock estimates suggest divergence of the *C. paspali* lineage from other *Claviceps* species during the Miocene, coinciding with the radiation of tropical grasses. Multilocus phylogenetic studies have consistently recovered *C. paspali* as sister to a clade containing *C. maximensis* and other tropical *Claviceps* species associated with wild grasses, rather than the cereal-infecting clade that includes *C. purpurea* and *C. purpurea* var. *spartinae*.
## Description and Morphology
*C. paspali* follows the typical *Claviceps* life cycle with three major morphological stages:
1. **Sphacelial (honeydew) stage:** Infected florets exude a sugary, sticky fluid (honeydew)
   containing numerous conidia (1–2 × 5–10 µm). The honeydew serves as the primary means
   of secondary spread via insects and rain splash. This stage typically lasts 7–14 days
   after initial infection.
2. **Sclerotial stage:** Following honeydew production, the fungus replaces the host ovary
   with a hard, dark purple to black sclerotium (ergot body). *C. paspali* sclerotia are
   elongated, cylindrical to slightly curved, typically 5–15 mm long, and protrude
   conspicuously from the grass spikelet. Internally, sclerotia have a white to pale
   purple medulla surrounded by a dark rind.
3. **Perithecial (sexual) stage:** After a dormancy period of several weeks to months,
   sclerotia germinate in moist conditions to produce stalked stromata (10–30 mm tall)
   bearing multiple perithecia near the tip. Ascospores (30–50 µm, filiform) are
   forcibly discharged and carried by wind to infect new host florets.
Sclerotia of *C. paspali* are generally smaller and more slender than those of *C. purpurea*. Microscopical fungal hyphae l hyphae within host tissue are intercellular and aseptate during early infection, becoming septate as the colony matures.
## Host Range and Distribution
*C. paspali* is a specialist pathogen of *Paspalum* species (Poaceae, subfamily Panicoideae). Major recorded hosts include:
- *Paspalum dilatatum* (dallisgrass) — primary host worldwide
- *Paspalum notatum* (bahiagrass) — important [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]], South America (Brazil, Argentina, Colombia, Venezuela)
- **Africa:** West Africa (Nigeria, Ghana), East Africa (Kenya, Tanzania), southern Africa (South Africa, Zimbabwe)
- **Asia:** India, Southeast Asia (Thailand, Philippines), southern China
- **Oceania:** Eastern Australia (Queensland, New South Wales), where it was among the first described
Warm, humid conditions favor infection. Outbreaks in livestock are most common in late summer and early autumn when sclerotia mature and fall to ground level in pastures. Infection rates can exceed 30% of spikelets in severely affected fields during favorable seasons.
## Chemistry and Active Compounds
*C. paspali* is notable for producing clavine alkaloids rather than the peptide ergot alkaloids characteristic of *C. purpurea*. The clavines are ergoline derivatives that serve as biosynthetic precursors to lysergic acid-type alkaloids in some *Claviceps* species but remain as end-products in *C. paspali*. The indole-diterpenoid clavines (paspaline-type) are structurally distinct from the simpler tetracyclic clavines found in other species.
