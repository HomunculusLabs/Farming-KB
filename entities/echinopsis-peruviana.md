## Overview

**Echinopsis peruviana** (syn. *[[echinopsis-pachanoi]]* ([[gottlieb-peyote-botany-and-mescaline-content]] compared to *E. pachanoi*.

Peruvian torch has been used for over 3,000 years in pre-Columbian Andean cultures, with archaeological evidence of its ritual use dating to the Chavín civilization (circa 900–200 BCE).

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Tracheophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Caryophyllales
- **Family:** Cactaceae
- **Subfamily:** Cactoideae
- **Tribe:** Trichocereeae
- **Genus:** *Echinopsis* Zucc. (formerly *Trichocereus* (A.Berger) Riccob.)
- **Species:** *E. peruviana* (Britton & Rose) Friedrich & G.D.Rowley

The taxonomic history is complex. The species was originally described as *Cereus peruvianus* by Britton and Rose in 1920, later transferred to *Trichocereus*, and most recently to *Echinopsis* following molecular phylogenetic studies.

The synonym *[[trichocereus-peruvianus]]* remains widely used in ethnobotanical and horticultural literature.

### Morphology

**Stem:** Columnar, erect to slightly procumbent, 3–6 m tall in mature specimens, 8–20 cm in diameter. Deep green to blue-green, sometimes glaucous.

Ribs number 6–8, broadly rounded, 15–25 mm high, separated by shallow furrows.

**Areoles:** Large, woolly, 1–2 cm apart, bearing 1–6 central spines and 8–14 radial spines.

**Spines:** Central spines are stout, 2–8 cm long, brownish-yellow to dark brown. Radial spines are shorter (1–3 cm), spreading, yellowish.

Young specimens often have notably longer, more prominent spines than mature plants, which may become nearly spineless with age.

**Flowers:** Large, showy, nocturnal to diurnal, 18–25 cm long, white with a greenish outer pericarpel tube. Blooming occurs in summer.

**Fruit:** Ovoid, green, 4–6 cm long, splitting longitudinally when ripe to reveal white pulp containing numerous small black seeds.

## Distribution and Habitat

*E. peruviana* is native to the western slopes of the Andes, occurring from approximately 2,000 to 3,000 meters elevation in Peru, with populations extending into southern Ecuador and northern Bolivia.

It grows in rocky, well-drained soils on steep hillsides, canyon walls, and river valleys in the inter-Andean dry forest and montane scrub ecosystems.

The species is tolerant of significant temperature fluctuations, withstanding daytime highs of 30°C and nighttime lows near freezing at high elevations.

Natural populations are concentrated in the departments of Cajamarca, Amazonas, Ancash, Lima, and Arequipa in Peru, often growing alongside *E. pachanoi* in overlapping ranges.

Wild populations have declined due to overharvesting for the entheogenic trade, habitat destruction from agriculture and urbanization, and illegal collection.

## Bioactive Compounds

The primary psychoactive constituent of *E. peruviana* is [[mescaline]], a phenethylamine alkaloid.

- **Mescaline:** Reported concentrations range from 0.1% to over 4% dry weight in the outer green tissue, with considerable variation between populations and individual specimens.
- **3-Methoxytyramine:** A phenolic amine present in trace amounts, possibly contributing to the overall pharmacological profile.
- **Anhalonidine, Anhalamine:** Minor tetrahydroisoquinoline alkaloids detected in analytical studies.

Mescaline content is concentrated in the photosynthetic green outer layer (cortical tissue) of the stem, with the white inner pith containing negligible alkaloids.

Reports of significantly higher mescaline content in *E. peruviana* compared to *E. pachanoi* have been inconsistent, with some analytical studies showing substantial overlap between the two species.
