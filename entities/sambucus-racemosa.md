## Overview

**Sambucus racemosa**, commonly known as red elderberry, is a deciduous shrub in the family Adoxaceae native to cool-temperate regions of [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] plants sometimes treated as *Sambucus racemosa* var. *pubens*.

Red elderberry grows in moist to mesic forests, forest openings, riparian corridors, subalpine meadows, and avalanche tracks. It is a common early-successional species along streambanks and roadcuts.

It prefers rich, moist, well-drained soils with pH 5.0–7.0 but tolerates a wide range of conditions. The species is cold-hardy and grows at higher elevations than other elderberries, reaching subalpine zones in western mountains.

Red elderberry is a valuable component of riparian restoration plantings. Its extensive root system stabilizes streambanks, and its early fruiting provides critical food for birds during the breeding season.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

Red elderberry fruits are edible when cooked thoroughly but contain cyanogenic glycosides (sambunigrin) in the raw state. Consumption of raw berries can cause nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, though serious poisoning is rare.

Cooking neutralizes the cyanogenic compounds and produces a tart, tangy fruit suitable for jellies, syrups, and wines. The flavor is more acidic and less sweet than that of black elderberry.

Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest harvested red elderberries extensively, cooking them in steaming pits or boiling them into sauces. The berries were also dried for winter storage and used as a fish preservative.

The flowers are edible and can be used for fritters, cordials, or tea, similar to those of other elderberry species. Flowering stems should be removed before processing.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

Red elderberry fruits contain anthocyanins, flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol), and phenolic acids with documented antioxidant activity. Cyanogenic glycoside content decreases significantly with cooking.

Traditional medicinal uses include treatment of colds, fevers, and respiratory infections. Indigenous peoples used inner bark preparations as an emetic, purgative, and contraceptive.

Modern research on elderberry pharmacology has focused primarily on *Sambucus nigra* and *S. canadensis*. Limited studies on *S. racemosa* suggest similar antiviral and immunomodulatory properties.

All parts of the plant except the ripe, cooked fruit contain toxic compounds. The stems, bark, leaves, roots, and unripe berries should not be consumed.

## Cultivation

Red elderberry is occasionally cultivated for ornamental and wildlife plantings. It is hardy to USDA Zone 2, making it one of the most cold-tolerant elderberries available.

Propagation is by seed (requires cold stratification), softwood cuttings, or division of root suckers. The species establishes readily and requires minimal maintenance once planted.
