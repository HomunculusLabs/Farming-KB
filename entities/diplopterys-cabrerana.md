# Diplopterys cabrerana

**Diplopterys cabrerana** (Cuatr.) B. Gates is a psychoactive plant species native to the Amazon basin, best known as one of the principal admixture plants added to **Ayahuasca** ([[banisteriopsis-caapi]]) preparations. Its leaves are rich in DMT and 5-MeO-DMT, making it essential to the visionary quality of the brew.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Family:** Malpighiaceae
- **Genus:** Diplopterys
- **Species:** D. cabrerana
- **Authority:** (Cuatr.) B. Gates
- **Common names:** Oco-yaje, Chaliponga, Chagropanga

The taxonomic history of this species reflects the complexity of classifying Ayahuasca admixture plants. It was originally described as Banisteriopsis rusbyana before being transferred to the genus Diplopterys. Some sources still refer to it under its earlier name. The genus Diplopterys is closely related to Banisteriopsis, both belonging to the Malpighiaceae family, and both containing species with psychoactive properties.

## Morphology

Diplopterys cabrerana is a shrubby climbing plant of the tropical Amazon rainforest. It is a woody vine that climbs into the forest canopy using tendrils characteristic of the Malpighiaceae family. The leaves are opposite, simple, and entire, with a glossy dark green upper surface.

The leaves are the primary plant part used in Ayahuasca preparations. Fresh leaves are preferred, though dried leaves are also effective. The plant produces small flowers and fruits typical of the family, though these reproductive structures are rarely collected or used ethnobotanically.

## Distribution

The species occurs in the western half of the Amazon Valley and is also used by isolated tribes on the Pacific slopes of the Colombian and Ecuadorean Andes. Its range includes parts of Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru. D. cabrerana is typically found in primary and secondary rainforest at low to medium elevations, where it grows as a canopy-climbing liana.

## Ethnobotanical Uses

### Ayahuasca Admixture

D. cabrerana is one of the most commonly used admixtures added to Ayahuasca ([[banisteriopsis-caapi]]). Among the western Tukanoan Siona of the Colombian Putumayo, it is known as **Oco-yaje**. The leaves are added to the Ayahuasca brew to provide the visionary DMT component.

### Role in the Brew

While Banisteriopsis caapi provides the beta-carboline alkaloids (harmine, harmaline) that inhibit monoamine oxidase and allow DMT to be orally active, it is the admixture plants like D. cabrerana that supply the DMT itself. Without these additives, Ayahuasca would lack its characteristic visionary effects.

The synergistic interaction between the MAO-inhibiting beta-carbolines of Banisteriopsis and the DMT of Diplopterys is one of the most remarkable examples of ethnopharmacological knowledge in the plant kingdom. Indigenous Amazonian peoples discovered this pharmacological synergy through centuries or millennia of empirical experimentation, a discovery that was not replicated in Western laboratories until the mid-20th century.

### Regional Variation in Admixture Choice

In the western Amazon, particularly among Ecuadorian Kofan and Siona peoples, D. cabrerana is often preferred over [[psychotria-viridis]] as the DMT source. This regional preference may relate to local availability of plant species, differences in tryptamine content and ratios, cultural tradition and shamanic lineage, or the subjective quality of the resulting visionary experience.

The use of D. cabrerana tends to produce a brew with higher 5-MeO-DMT content compared to preparations using Psychotria viridis, which may account for the distinct character attributed to western Amazonian Ayahuasca.

### Other Ayahuasca Admixtures

Other commonly used admixtures alongside D. cabrerana include:

- Leaves of **Psychotria viridis** (Chacruna)
- Leaves of **Psychotria carthaginensis**
- **Brugmansia suaveolens** (Toe)
- **Brunfelsia chiricaspi** and **Brunfelsia grandiflora**
- Tobacco (Nicotiana spp.)
- **Justicia pectoralis**

## Chemistry

Diplopterys cabrerana contains the following major psychoactive alkaloids:

- **N,N-Dimethyltryptamine (DMT)** — the primary visionary compound
- **5-Methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (5-MeO-DMT)** — a more potent but less visual tryptamine

The leaves are the plant part richest in these alkaloids, which is consistent with their use in Ayahuasca preparations. Analysis has shown DMT concentrations in the range of 0.17-1.0% dry weight and 5-MeO-DMT concentrations of approximately 0.05-0.2% dry weight, though significant variation exists depending on growing conditions, plant age, and chemotype.

### Comparison with Psychotria viridis

D. cabrerana is notable for containing significant quantities of 5-MeO-DMT in addition to DMT, while P. viridis typically contains primarily DMT with little to no 5-MeO-DMT. This difference in alkaloid profile may contribute to the distinct subjective effects reported between Ayahuasca preparations made with each admixture plant.

## Pharmacology

DMT is a potent agonist at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, the primary receptor responsible for classical psychedelic effects. 5-MeO-DMT acts as a non-selective serotonin receptor agonist with particular affinity for 5-HT1A and 5-HT2A receptors. The combination of these two tryptamines in a single plant creates a complex pharmacological profile that may account for the richness and variability of the Ayahuasca experience.

## Safety Considerations

When used within the traditional Ayahuasca context, D. cabrerana is generally considered safe. However, the brew can interact dangerously with certain medications, particularly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and other antidepressants, monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs), and certain antipsychotic medications. These interactions can lead to serotonin syndrome, a potentially life-threatening condition.

## Cultural Significance

D. cabrerana plays a critical role in Amazonian shamanism as the DMT source in Ayahuasca. The knowledge of which plants to combine and in what proportions represents centuries of indigenous botanical and pharmacological understanding. The growing global interest in Ayahuasca has led to increased cultivation of D. cabrerana, raising concerns about sustainable harvesting practices and the protection of indigenous intellectual property related to its use.

## Conservation and Sustainability

The growing global demand for Ayahuasca has led to increased harvesting of D. cabrerana from wild populations. In some areas, overharvesting has raised concerns about the sustainability of this practice. Sustainable cultivation programs have been established in several countries to reduce pressure on wild populations.

The protection of indigenous intellectual property related to Ayahuasca and its admixture plants remains an important ethical and legal issue. International frameworks for protecting traditional knowledge, such as the Nagoya Protocol, are increasingly relevant to the Ayahuasca complex of plants and their associated indigenous practices.

## Comparison with Other Ayahuasca Admixtures

D. cabrerana is one of several plants that serve as DMT sources in Ayahuasca preparations:

- [[psychotria-viridis]] — Chacruna, the most commonly used DMT admixture
- psychotria-carthaginensis — another Psychotria species used in Ayahuasca
- [[entheogen-jurema-mimosa-hostilis-ethnobotany]] — Jurema, used in Brazilian Ayahuasca analogs

The primary advantage of D. cabrerana over these alternatives is its content of both DMT and 5-MeO-DMT, which produces a qualitatively different experience compared to admixtures containing only DMT. This unique alkaloid profile has made it the preferred DMT source for many indigenous groups in the western Amazon.

Recent research has also explored the potential of D. cabrerana extracts for pharmacological applications beyond traditional use, including investigation of its antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties.

These studies suggest that the plant may have therapeutic value beyond its role as an Ayahuasca admixture.
