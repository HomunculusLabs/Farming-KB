## Overview

Adenophora triophylla (Thunb.) A.DC., commonly known as lady bell or sandao-shen, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Campanulaceae. It is native to East Asia, ranging across Japan, Korea, northeastern China, and the Russian Far East.

The species is valued in traditional East Asian medicine for its root, which contains triterpenoid saponins and polysaccharides. These compounds demonstrate expectorant and immunomodulatory properties in pharmacological studies.

It also serves as an ornamental garden plant due to its graceful sprays of pendulous bell-shaped flowers. The plant is hardy and adaptable, making it suitable for temperate gardens worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Adenophora triophylla was first described by Carl Peter Thunberg as Campanula triophylla in 1784. It was later transferred to the genus Adenophora by Augustin Pyramus de Candolle in 1830.

The genus name derives from the Greek "aden" (gland) and "phoros" (bearing), referring to the distinctive disc-shaped nectary at the base of the style. This nectary disc is the key character separating Adenophora from the closely related genus Campanula.

Erect stems reach 40–100 cm in height, typically unbranched below the inflorescence. Basal leaves are cordate to reniform, long-petiolate, and usually wither by flowering time.

Stem leaves are arranged in whorls of three, giving the specific epithet "triophylla." They are narrowly ovate to lanceolate with serrate margins and measure 3–8 cm long.

Flowers are nodding and campanulate, pale blue to violet-blue, measuring 1.5–2.5 cm long. The corolla is divided to about one-third of its length into five shallow lobes.

The style is exserted well beyond the corolla. A distinctive disc-shaped nectary gland at the style base distinguishes this species from similar Campanula taxa.

The fruit is a poricidal capsule that opens by lateral pores near the apex. Seeds are small, light brown, and ellipsoid, approximately 1–1.5 mm long, with a smooth testa surface.

Several infraspecific taxa have been described, including varieties differing in leaf shape and flower size. However, the taxonomic boundaries within the A. triophylla complex remain under study, and some varieties may represent distinct species.

Chromosome number is 2n = 34, consistent with the base chromosome number of x = 17 found across most Adenophora species. Hybridization with other East Asian Adenophora species has been reported where ranges overlap.

## Distribution and Habitat

Adenophora triophylla is distributed across temperate East Asia. Its range includes Japan (Hokkaido, Honshu, Shikuku, Kyushu), the Korean Peninsula, and northeastern China (Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning).

The species also occurs in the Russian Far East, including Primorsky Krai and Sakhalin Island. Populations are concentrated in cool-temperate zones between 100–1800 m elevation.

It inhabits mountain slopes, forest margins, grasslands, and disturbed areas within deciduous and mixed forests. The species prefers moist, well-drained soils in partial shade to full sun.

In Japan, it is one of the more common Adenophora species. It is frequently encountered along forest roads and in montane meadows throughout the main islands.

In Korea, it occurs throughout the peninsula. It is particularly associated with oak-pine woodlands and open grassy slopes in the central and northern regions.

The species shows some habitat flexibility, occurring in both primary forest remnants and disturbed secondary growth. This adaptability contributes to its relatively wide distribution compared to more specialized Adenophora species.

Population densities vary considerably across the range. The highest densities are found in mesic montane meadows of central Honshu and the Taebaek Mountains of Korea.

## Cultivation and Uses

Adenophora triophylla is hardy to USDA zones 4–8. It prefers humus-rich, slightly acidic to neutral soils with consistent moisture and good drainage.

Medicinal cultivation centers on root production, which requires 2–3 growing seasons before harvest. Roots are typically harvested in autumn after aerial parts have died back.

The dried roots, known as "nan-sha-shen" in Chinese herbalism, are used in traditional formulas for respiratory conditions. These include chronic cough, bronchitis, and dry throat syndromes.

In Korean traditional medicine, the roots are similarly employed under the name "sa-sam." They are often combined with other medicinal roots in decoctions for lung and throat ailments.

In ornamental horticulture, Adenophora triophylla is grown for its graceful sprays of pendant blue bells. It is suitable for rock gardens, woodland edges, and cottage garden schemes.

Propagation is by seed or division. Cold stratification at 4°C for 4–6 weeks improves germination rates. Division of established clumps is best performed in early spring before new growth emerges.

The species is relatively pest-free when grown in appropriate conditions. Crown rot can occur in poorly drained soils, and powdery mildew may appear in humid summers with poor air circulation.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

Pharmacological research has identified triterpenoid saponins as the major bioactive constituents of Adenophora triophylla roots. Key compounds include shashoroside A and B, lupeol, and beta-sitosterol.

Polysaccharides isolated from the roots demonstrate immunostimulatory activity. They enhance macrophage phagocytosis and promote cytokine production in vitro.

Studies confirm expectorant and antitussive activities in animal models. The saponin fraction increases respiratory tract fluid secretion, facilitating mucus clearance.

Anti-inflammatory effects have been documented in cell-based assays. Lupeol and its derivatives inhibit NF-kB signaling pathways in macrophage cell lines.

The roots also contain trace amounts of alkaloids and flavonoids. These contribute minor antioxidant activity but are not considered primary therapeutic agents.

Quality control of commercial Adenophora roots relies on saponin content as a marker. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) methods have been developed for standardization of herbal preparations.

The species is sometimes adulterated with other Adenophora roots in the herbal trade. Molecular authentication using ITS sequences has been developed to ensure correct species identification in commercial products.

## Ecological Role

Adenophora triophylla serves as a nectar and pollen source for various native bees. Bumblebees (Bombus spp.) are particularly effective pollinators of the pendant flowers.

The poricidal capsule dehiscence mechanism ensures seed dispersal by wind when stems sway in mountain breezes. This passive dispersal strategy is effective in the open habitats where the species commonly grows.

The species is a component of the herbaceous understory in East Asian deciduous forests. It contributes to plant community diversity in montane meadow and forest-edge habitats.

It forms associations with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi that enhance nutrient uptake. These associations are especially important for phosphorus acquisition in the nutrient-poor mountain soils it often inhabits.

Herbivory by slugs and snails can be significant in cultivation settings. In natural populations, the species shows moderate resistance to foliar herbivores due to its saponin content.

The roots penetrate moderately deep into subsoil layers, contributing to soil aeration and organic matter turnover. This makes the species a useful component in perennial polyculture systems.
