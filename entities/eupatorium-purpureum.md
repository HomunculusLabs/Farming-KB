## Eupatorium purpureum

## Overview

**Eupatorium purpureum**, commonly known as Joe-Pye weed, sweet Joe-Pye weed, or gravel root, is a herbaceous perennial plant in the family Asteraceae. It is native to eastern and central [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] herbalist who reportedly used the plant to treat typhus and other fevers in colonial New England. The species has a long history of use in traditional [[growing-gourmet-mushrooms-mycelium-mycopermaculture-ecological-landscaping]]. Its tall stature, attractive pink flower clusters, and value as a pollinator plant have made it a staple of perennial borders and rain gardens.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Plantae — **Family:** Asteraceae — **Tribe:** Eupatorieae — **Genus:** Eupatorium — **Species:** E. purpureum L.

The species was described by Linnaeus in 1753. Recent taxonomic revisions have moved many former Eupatorium species into other genera (e.g., *Eutrochium*), but E. purpureum remains within a narrower circumscription of Eupatorium in many treatments.

E. purpureum is a robust perennial, 1.5–2.5 meters tall, with erect, unbranched or sparingly branched stems. The stems are distinctive — pale green to purplish, with a glaucous bloom and characteristic purple speckling at the nodes.

Leaves are in whorls of 3–5 at each node, ovate to lanceolate, 10–20 cm long and 5–12 cm wide, with serrate margins and a rugose upper surface. The leaves emit a faint vanilla-like fragrance when crushed.

The inflorescence is a large, dome-shaped corymb of numerous flower heads, 15–30 cm across. Individual flower heads are composed of 5–8 pink to purplish disc florets, lacking ray florets entirely. Flowering occurs from July through September.

The fruit is an achene, 3–4 mm long, with a pappus of 20–30 white bristles that facilitate wind dispersal.

## Related Species

The genus Eupatorium sensu stricto contains approximately 40 species, with E. purpureum being one of the most widely recognized. It is closely related to E. perfoliatum (boneset), E. cannabinum (hemp agrimony), and E. rugosum (white snakeroot).

E. purpureum is distinguished from E. maculatum (spotted Joe-Pye weed) by its more rounded inflorescence, paler stem color, and leaf whorls of 3–5 (versus 3–4 in E. maculatum). E. fistulosum (hollow Joe-Pye weed) has hollow, glaucous stems and flat-topped flower clusters.

Some modern taxonomic treatments place E. purpureum in the genus *Eutrochium* alongside E. maculatum, E. fistulosum, and E. dubium. This classification is based on molecular phylogenetic data, though the traditional placement in Eupatorium remains common in horticultural literature.

## Medicinal Preparations and Dosage

The dried root is the primary medicinal part, typically harvested in autumn after flowering. It is sliced and dried at temperatures below 40°C to preserve volatile constituents. Commercial "gravel root" products include tinctures, capsules, and loose dried herb.
