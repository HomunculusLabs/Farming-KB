# Psilocybin

Psilocybin (4-PO-DMT; O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) is a
naturally occurring prodrug psychedelic compound of the tryptamine class. It is
dephosphorylated in the body to psilocin (4-HO-DMT), the pharmacologically active
metabolite responsible for its psychoactive effects.

## Chemical Profile

- **IUPAC name:** [3-(2-Dimethylaminoethyl)-1H-indol-4-yl] dihydrogen phosphate
- **Molecular formula:** C₁₂H₁₇N₂O₄P
- **Molecular weight:** 284.25 g/mol
- **Appearance:** White crystalline solid
- **Melting point:** 220–228 °C (decomposes)
- **Solubility:** Soluble in water; slightly soluble in ethanol

Psilocybin is a structural analog of serotonin (5-HT) and differs from DMT by
the presence of a phosphoryloxy group at the 4-position of the indole ring.

## Pharmacology

### Mechanism of Action

Psilocybin itself is biologically inactive until it undergoes rapid
dephosphorylation by alkaline phosphatase enzymes in the liver and intestinal
mucosa, converting to **psilocin**. Psilocin acts primarily as a:

- **5-HT₂A receptor agonist** — the primary mechanism driving hallucinogenic
  effects. Activation of cortical 5-HT₂A receptors in layer V pyramidal neurons
  increases glutamate release and alters thalamocortical signaling.
- **5-HT₁A receptor partial agonist** — contributes to anxiolytic and
  antidepressant effects.
- **5-HT₂C receptor agonist** — modulates mood and anxiety responses.

### Pharmacokinetics

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Onset of action | 20–60 minutes |
| Peak plasma concentration | 80–120 minutes |
| Duration of effects | 4–6 hours (oral) |
| Half-life (psilocin) | ~2–3 hours |
| Elimination | Renal (excreted as psilocin glucuronide) |

## Natural Occurrence

Psilocybin is produced biosynthetically by over 200 species of fungi in the
genera *Psilocybe*, *Panaeolus*, *Gymnopilus*, *Pluteus*, *Inocybe*, and
*Copelandia*. The most commonly encountered species include:

- **Psilocybe cubensis** — the most widely cultivated species; moderate potency
- **Psilocybe semilanceata** (liberty caps) — among the most potent naturally
  occurring species; found in temperate grasslands of Europe and North America
- **Psilocybe azurescens** — the most potent known species; native to the
  Pacific Northwest coast of North America
- **Psilocybe cyanescens** (wavy caps) — wood-loving species; high potency;
  common in urban mulched areas of the Pacific Northwest and Europe

Psilocybin content varies significantly between species and even within
specimens, typically ranging from 0.1% to 1.8% of dried weight.

## Historical and Cultural Context

### Indigenous Use

Archaeological evidence suggests ritual use of psilocybin mushrooms dates back
at least 6,000–9,000 years in the Sahara Desert region (Tassili n'Ajjer rock
paintings depicting mushroom-like figures). Indigenous Mesoamerican cultures
including the Mazatec, Mixtec, Nahuatl, and Zapotec peoples used psilocybin
mushrooms — known in Nahuatl as *teonanácatl* ("flesh of the gods") — in
healing ceremonies, divination, and religious rituals for millennia prior to
Spanish colonization.

### Modern Rediscovery

- **1957:** R. Gordon Wasson published "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" in *Life*
  magazine, introducing psilocybin mushrooms to Western audiences.
- **1958:** Albert Hofmann isolated and identified psilocybin and psilocin from
  *P. mexicana* specimens collected by Wasson in Mexico.
- **1959–1960s:** Sandoz marketed psilocybin as Indocybin® for psychiatric
  research; over 1,000 clinical papers published before prohibition.
- **1970:** U.S. Controlled Substances Act placed psilocybin in Schedule I,
  effectively halting research for decades.
- **2000s–present:** Resurgence of clinical research into psilocybin-assisted
  therapy for depression, anxiety, addiction, and end-of-life distress.

## Clinical Research

### Depression and Treatment-Resistant Depression

Phase II clinical trials at Imperial College London and Johns Hopkins University
have demonstrated rapid and sustained antidepressant effects of a single dose
of psilocybin (10–25 mg) in patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) and
treatment-resistant depression (TRD). The COMPASS Pathways Phase IIb trial
(2021) showed that 25 mg psilocybin produced significant reductions in
depressive symptoms measured by the MADRS scale at 3 weeks post-dose compared
to 1 mg control.

### End-of-Life Anxiety

Studies at NYU Langone and Johns Hopkins (2016) demonstrated that a single
dose of psilocybin produced substantial and enduring decreases in depression
and anxiety in patients with life-threatening cancer diagnoses, with effects
persisting at 6-month and 4.5-year follow-ups.

### Addiction

Pilot studies have shown promise for psilocybin-assisted therapy in treating
alcohol use disorder and tobacco/nicotine dependence, with abstinence rates
significantly exceeding those of standard treatments.

### Neuroplasticity

fMRI studies reveal that psilocybin decreases functional connectivity within
the default mode network (DMN) while increasing global brain connectivity.
This "reset" of rigid neural patterns is hypothesized to underlie therapeutic
effects. Psilocybin also promotes neuritogenesis and spinogenesis in cortical
neurons via 5-HT₂A-mediated pathways (Ly et al., 2018).
