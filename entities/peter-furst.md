## Peter T. Furst

## Description

Peter T. Furst is an anthropologist and editor cited in the general bibliography of Harner's Hallucinogens and Shamanism as the editor of "Flesh of the Gods: The Ritual Use of Hallucinogens" (1972), a major edited volume on the ritual and religious dimensions of psychoactive plant use. Furst's editorial work helped consolidate the emerging field of ethnographic hallucinogen studies and provided a companion volume to Harner's own collection, establishing the academic study of psychoactive plant use as a legitimate and important area of anthropological inquiry.

- His contribution helped shift academic discourse from a pharmacological model to a cultural-anthropological model of understanding psychoactive plant use

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Furst's emphasis on the ritual context of hallucinogen use is directly relevant to understanding how psychoactive mushrooms are cultivated and used in traditional settings. The Mazatec mushroom ceremony documented by Munn in the Harner volume exemplifies the kind of ritual context that Furst's editorial work was designed to highlight. Understanding the full ceremonial framework of traditional mushroom use, as advocated by Furst, is essential for anyone studying the cultivation, preparation, and therapeutic application of Psilocybe species. Modern clinical research on psilocybin has increasingly adopted Furst's emphasis on set and setting, recognizing that the context of use significantly affects outcomes.

## Connection to the Harner Volume

Furst's "Flesh of the Gods" and Harner's "Hallucinogens and Shamanism" were published within a year of each other and together represent the state of the art in ethnographic hallucinogen studies in the early 1970s. Both volumes brought together leading researchers to document the cultural contexts of psychoactive plant use across different indigenous traditions. The complementary nature of these two volumes established the field's core literature and provided the foundation for subsequent decades of research into the relationship between hallucinogenic plants, shamanism, and healing.

## Academic Legacy

The publication of "Flesh of the Gods" marked a turning point in the academic study of psychoactive plant use. Before the 1970s, much of the literature on hallucinogenic plants was either narrowly pharmacological or dismissively anthropological. Furst's volume, together with Harner's, helped establish the convention of treating indigenous plant use with the same scholarly seriousness afforded to other religious and therapeutic practices. This approach has endured and expanded, culminating in contemporary research on psychedelic-assisted therapy that explicitly acknowledges the importance of indigenous knowledge systems.

## The "Flesh of the Gods" Concept

The title "Flesh of the Gods" (carne de los dioses) is the Spanish translation of the Aztec Nahuatl word "teonanacatl," which was used to describe psychoactive mushrooms. This term, first recorded by Spanish chroniclers during the conquest of Mexico, encapsulates the indigenous understanding that these mushrooms are not mere plants but the physical embodiment of divine presence. Furst's adoption of this term as his volume title signaled his commitment to understanding hallucinogenic plants from within the indigenous frameworks of meaning rather than imposing Western pharmacological categories. The term has since become standard in ethnographic literature on psychoactive plant use.

## Contemporary Relevance

The research tradition that Furst helped establish continues to influence modern psychedelic science. Clinical trials of psilocybin-assisted therapy increasingly acknowledge the importance of indigenous knowledge and traditional ceremonial practices in informing treatment protocols. The concept that set, setting, and ritual context determine therapeutic outcomes, which Furst emphasized in his editorial work, is now widely accepted in the field. His legacy is visible in the growing movement to integrate traditional plant medicine practices with modern clinical approaches to psychedelic therapy.

## Network of Researchers

Furst's editorial work on "Flesh of the Gods" brought together a network of researchers who were collectively establishing the field of ethnographic hallucinogen studies. Contributors to and colleagues of Furst's volume included Richard Evans Schultes (botanical taxonomy of hallucinogenic plants), R. Gordon Wasson (ethnomycology), Michael Harner (shamanism and hallucinogens), Weston La Barre (peyote religion), and Marlene Dobkin de Rios (urban ayahuasca use). This network of researchers shared a commitment to documenting indigenous plant use in its full cultural context, and their collective work in the late 1960s and early 1970s defined the field for subsequent decades. Furst's role as editor and convener was essential in consolidating this research community and its shared intellectual commitments.

## Impact on Ethnobotanical Research

Furst's emphasis on the ritual context of hallucinogen use catalyzed a shift in ethnobotanical research methodology. Prior to the 1970s, much ethnobotanical research focused primarily on documenting plant species and their chemical constituents. Furst's editorial work helped establish the convention that documenting the cultural context of plant use was equally important. This expanded methodology requires researchers to engage not only with botanical taxonomy and phytochemistry but also with the religious, therapeutic, and social systems within which plants are used. The result has been a richer and more comprehensive ethnobotanical literature that preserves indigenous knowledge in its full cultural context.

## Documentation of Indigenous Knowledge Systems

Furst's editorial work contributed to the broader movement to document and preserve indigenous knowledge systems at a time when many traditional plant medicine practices were at risk of disappearing due to cultural assimilation and environmental destruction. The chapters in "Flesh of the Gods" serve as permanent records of cultural practices that may no longer exist in their original form. This preservation function of ethnographic research has become increasingly important as globalization accelerates the pace of cultural change in indigenous communities worldwide. The detailed documentation of plant species, preparation methods, ritual protocols, and therapeutic applications preserved in Furst's volume represents an irreplaceable resource for both indigenous communities and the broader scientific community.

## Theoretical Approach to Hallucinogenic Plant Use

Furst's editorial approach reflected a theoretical commitment to understanding hallucinogenic plants as agents of religious experience rather than mere pharmacological substances. This position, shared by many contributors to his volume, held that the cultural context of plant use — including the beliefs, expectations, ritual practices, and social relationships surrounding the experience — was at least as important as the chemical properties of the plants themselves in determining the nature and value of the experience. This theoretical position has been substantially confirmed by modern clinical research on psychedelics, which consistently demonstrates that set and setting are among the most powerful determinants of therapeutic outcome. Furst's early advocacy for this position demonstrated considerable theoretical sophistication and prescience.
