# Otzi the Iceman

## Description

Otzi (also spelled Oetzi), commonly known as the Iceman, is the naturally preserved mummy of a nomadic traveler who lived around 3300 BCE during the Chalcolithic (Copper Age) period. Discovered frozen in the Swiss and Austrian Alps in 1991, Otzi is one of the oldest and best-preserved natural human mummies ever found. Among his possessions were several species of mushrooms, including [[fomes-fomentarius]] (iceman [[polypore]]), providing profound historical evidence of early human use of fungi for food, fuel, medicine, and practical materials. The discovery of this mushroom in Otzi's possession has profound historical implications regarding the use of fungi dating back over five millennia.

## Classification

- **Type**: Historical person / archaeological discovery
- **Time Period**: circa 3300 BCE (Chalcolithic / Copper Age)
- **Discovery Location**: Otztal Alps, on the border between Austria and Italy
- **Discovery Date**: September 1991
- **Elevation of Discovery**: approximately 3,210 meters (10,530 feet)
- **Current Location**: South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology, Bolzano, Italy

## Key Facts

- Lived approximately 5,300 years ago during the Copper Age
- Discovered by German tourists in the Otztal Alps at an elevation of roughly 3,210 meters
- Found with Fomes fomentarius (iceman polypore / [[amadou]]) among his possessions, which is why the species is commonly called the "iceman polypore"
- The mushroom in his possession likely served multiple purposes: tinder for fire-starting, medicinal use, and possibly as a material for carrying or insulation
- Discovery of fungal remains among his belongings provides some of the earliest archaeological evidence of human-fungal interactions
- Otzi's possession of Fomes fomentarius implies sophisticated knowledge of useful fungi among prehistoric European peoples
- The find has profound historical implications regarding the use of fungi for food, fuel, and medicine stretching back millennia
- His body and artifacts are housed in the South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology in Bolzano, Italy
- The preservation quality allowed detailed analysis of his diet, health, clothing, and toolkit
- His equipment included a copper axe, flint knife, quiver with arrows, and birch bark containers
- The Fomes fomentarius he carried had been deliberately processed, not simply gathered raw

## Connection to Fomes fomentarius

The Fomes fomentarius found with Otzi represents one of the earliest documented uses of polypore fungi by humans. The iceman polypore can be processed into amadou, a soft felt-like material with multiple practical applications. Amadou was historically used as tinder for fire-starting due to its excellent spark-catching properties. It could also serve as a wound dressing material, clothing insulation, and even a material for making pouches or containers. The presence of this prepared fungal material in Otzi's kit demonstrates that prehistoric humans had developed sophisticated techniques for processing and utilizing fungal resources as essential survival equipment.

## The Fomes fomentarius Species

Fomes fomentarius is an easily identifiable polypore, hoof-shaped to beehive-like in appearance, with a grayish and banded, extremely tough to hard texture. It is a pored mushroom that lacks a stem and attaches to the sides of downed or standing trees or logs, primarily fruiting on dying birch trees at higher elevations. The fruitbodies persist year-round and sometimes grow in layers, with new layers added annually; a few known wild specimens have been growing for over fifty years. Though not considered edible as food, it can be used for making medicinals, textiles and fabric, and paper. The mushroom is rich in compounds similar to turkey tail (Trametes versicolor), including polysaccharide-K.

## Medicinal Properties of Fomes fomentarius

Studies have found that Fomes fomentarius can help boost and modulate immune system function, regulate blood pressure and sugar levels, lower cholesterol, and provide cardiovascular and digestive support. It contains antiviral and antibacterial properties as well as anti-inflammatory compounds, and has been shown to suppress many cancer cell lines. The antibiotic properties make it a great candidate for mycofiltration of bacteria in contaminated soil and water. Colonized biomasses of myceliated wood chips can be used in trenches downslope of livestock operations to absorb and filter surface runoff.

## Cultivation of Fomes fomentarius

The mycelium of Fomes is white, cottony, delicate, and slow growing, eventually oxidizing to dark chocolate brown as it ages. The smell is sweet and pleasant. Commercial spawn is rare but when available typically comes as wooden dowels for outdoor cultivation. These species prefer standing dead trees (snags) or large-diameter downed trees. Inoculation to fruiting time is one to three years. Yields are very low; these conks are extremely slow growing, making them increasingly rare in the wild. Indoor cultivation is not recommended. Fresh Fomes keeps for one month at 38 to 42 F; dried conks can be used to make powders, tinctures, and even fire starter.

## Historical Significance

Otzi's discovery revolutionized understanding of Copper Age life in Europe. The level of preservation provided unprecedented insight into prehistoric clothing, diet, toolmaking, and medicinal practices. The fungal components of his kit revealed that mushroom use was deliberate and knowledgeable, suggesting mycological knowledge was transmitted across generations. This finding pushed back the timeline of documented human-fungal interactions by millennia and provided a tangible link between prehistoric and modern uses of medicinal fungi.

## Cultural Significance

Otzi's discovery provides a rare window into prehistoric mycological knowledge. The presence of Fomes fomentarius in his kit connects modern [[mushroom-cultivation]] and medicinal use to an unbroken tradition spanning over five millennia. The "iceman polypore" common name for Fomes fomentarius is a direct tribute to this archaeological find, cementing Otzi's place in mycological history.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Otzi represents the historical foundation of human-fungal relationships. His possession of Fomes fomentarius demonstrates that the practical and medicinal value of polypore mushrooms was recognized and utilized by humans thousands of years before modern mycology. Creating wild patches of Fomes and its relatives such as agarikon will help keep these species perpetuating in the wild, since they are extremely slow growing and increasingly sought after as medicinal species.
