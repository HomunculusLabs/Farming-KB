## Red Clover

## Description

Red clover (*[[trifolium-pratense]]*) is a short-lived perennial legume widely used as a [[green-manure]] and cover crop in agricultural systems. In Eliot Coleman's [[winter-harvest-system]], red clover is specifically used as an undersown green manure in standing tomato crops, serving as a soil-building bridge between summer and winter production cycles in the movable greenhouse rotation.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Fabaceae (legume family)
- **Genus**: *Trifolium*
- **Species**: *Trifolium pratense*
- **Common names**: Red clover, purple clover, bee bread
- **Category**: Green manure / cover crop / forage legume

## Key Facts

### Botanical Characteristics

Red clover is a herbaceous perennial plant growing 8 to 20 inches tall with trifoliate leaves marked by a characteristic pale V-shaped chevron. The flower heads are rose-purple to magenta, composed of many small pea-like florets. Red clover has a taproot system that can extend several feet into the soil, though it is shorter-lived than some other clover species, typically persisting 2-3 years.

### Nitrogen Fixation

As a legume, red clover forms symbiotic associations with *Rhizobium* bacteria in root nodules, converting atmospheric nitrogen into plant-available forms. This nitrogen fixation capability makes it one of the most valuable [[green-manure-crops]], capable of adding 50-200 pounds of nitrogen per acre to the soil depending on growing conditions and duration.

### Undersowing in Standing Crops

Coleman's specific innovation is the practice of undersowing red clover into standing tomato crops on approximately September 1. This technique:
- Uses the remaining growing season after tomato establishment
- Allows the clover to establish under the tomato canopy before winter
- Provides living soil cover through fall and into the following season
- Eliminates the need for a separate cover-cropping window

### Role in Movable Greenhouse Rotation

In the greenhouse rotation cycle, red clover undersown in tomatoes serves as a transition crop:
1. Tomatoes are grown through summer, trellised to overhead supports
2. Red clover is undersown on September 1
3. Tomato vines are unclipped from overhead supports the day before the house is moved
4. The greenhouse is moved to cover a winter crop of carrots in late October
5. The red clover remains growing on the now-uncovered site
6. This rotation repeats a second year before being exchanged with a different rotation

## Relevance to Cultivation

### Soil Building

Red clover contributes to soil health through multiple mechanisms:
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Adds significant nitrogen to the soil
- **Organic matter**: Substantial above-ground and root biomass
- **Soil structure**: Deep taproot improves soil aeration and water infiltration
- **Erosion control**: Dense canopy protects soil from erosion during fall and winter
- **[[beneficial-insect-habitat]]**: Flowers attract pollinators and beneficial insects

### Integration with Four-Season System

The red clover practice exemplifies the principle that every moment and every square foot should be productive in a market garden. Rather than leaving soil bare between tomato harvest and the next planting, the undersown clover maintains biological activity and soil improvement through the transition period.

### Weed Suppression

The dense growth habit of established red clover effectively suppresses weeds, reducing the need for cultivation and keeping the soil covered and protected.

### Forage and Pollinator Value

Beyond its green manure role, red clover is one of the most important nectar plants for honeybees and other pollinators. In a diversified farm system, the clover growing on uncovered greenhouse sites provides forage for beneficial insects during the fall and the following spring.
