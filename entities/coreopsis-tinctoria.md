## Overview

Coreopsis tinctoria, commonly known as plains coreopsis, calliopsis, or golden tickseed, is an annual flowering plant in the family Asteraceae native to central and western [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] peoples. It is also valued for its [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America, Europe, Asia, and Australia. In some areas, it is considered a roadside weed.

The plant is highly adaptable and colonizes disturbed habitats rapidly. It is a common component of wildflower seed mixes for highway beautification and [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] such as hoverflies, soldier beetles, and parasitic wasps. These insects contribute to biological pest control in adjacent agricultural areas.

Goldfinches and other small seed-eating birds consume the achenes in late summer and autumn. The plant's prolific seed production supports bird populations through the winter.

The fine-textured foliage provides minimal cover value for wildlife. However, the plant's root system helps stabilize loose soils in disturbed areas.

C. tinctoria is sometimes included in pollinator habitat seed mixes alongside other native annuals and perennials. Its extended bloom period makes it particularly valuable for late-season pollinator support.

## Cultivation

C. tinctoria is one of the easiest wildflowers to grow from seed. Seeds germinate readily in 14–21 days without cold stratification and can be direct-sown in spring.

The species performs best in full sun with moderate moisture but is remarkably drought-tolerant once established. It self-seeds freely and may persist in gardens for years without replanting.

In ornamental plantings, plains coreopsis provides long-lasting color from early summer through frost. Deadheading extends bloom time and reduces unwanted self-seeding.

The species is widely available in commercial wildflower seed mixes and is recommended for meadow gardens, cottage gardens, and cut flower production.

Several cultivars have been developed, including 'Roulette' (with more pronounced red ray tips) and various dwarf forms suited to container cultivation.

## Ethnobotanical Uses

The Meskwaki (Fox) people of the Great Lakes region used C. tinctoria as a source of red and yellow dye. The flowers were boiled to produce colors for dyeing basket materials and textiles.

Other Native American tribes used the plant medicinally. The Navajo prepared an infusion of the plant for internal pain, while the Zuni used it as a topical treatment for skin conditions.

The dried flowers retain color well and have been used in potpourri and dried arrangements. Modern natural dyers continue to use coreopsis flowers to produce shades of gold, orange, and rust.
