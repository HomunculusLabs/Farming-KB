## Description

Composting is the controlled biological decomposition of organic materials into a stable, humus-like product. In permaculture systems, compost processes range from cold (anaerobic) to hot (aerobic) methods, each with distinct advantages. Material selection is critical to avoid contamination from heavy metals, biocides, and excessive nutrient concentrations that can harm soil life and plant health.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil amendment / organic waste management
- **Process types**: Hot (aerobic, 55-60 C), cold (anaerobic), hybrid
- **Temperature regimes**: Hot compost 50-60 C; biogas digestion 30-35 C

## Key Facts

### Hot vs Cold Compost

- **Hot compost**: Heats to 55-60 C, kills weed seeds and pathogens, but loses nitrogen as ammonia
- **Cold compost** (pitted or silage): Preserves nitrogen but does not kill weed seeds
- Adding 1% superphosphate to hot compost prevents ammonia escape
- **Chinese hybrid method**: Build aerated heap with bamboo pole air tunnels, cover with mud, heat to 55-60 C for days, then seal all holes for anaerobic finish

### Rapid Compost Methods

- UK method: Liquid manures sprinkled on straw in silos/tanks with forced air produce compost in about one week
- Open piles encased in straw need about 10 days
- Biogas digester sludge (still full of seeds) can be processed through liquid effluent + straw systems
- In severe winter areas, large piles of 10-50 cubic metres provide slow heat release for greenhouse and house heating over winter

### Compost Material Categories

- **(a) Dry carbonaceous**: Nut husks, shells, coffee/tea/cocoa residues, shredded paper, bark, woodchips, sawdust, natural fabrics
- **(b) Seeded materials**: Hay with seed heads, weeds in flower, weed bulbs or roots (use as hot compost)
- **(c) High-nitrogen wet materials**: Sewage, liquid manure, urine, meat/animal trimmings, household waste; add lime and 1% superphosphate
- **(d) Processed materials**: Digester sludge, weed-free manures
- **(e) Concentrated manures**: Chicken/bird manures, animal shed litter, blood, bone, feathers, seaweeds
- **(f) Liquid amendments**: Dissolved minerals, urine, seaweed and manure teas

### Contamination Risks (Handreck, Organic Growing, 1987)

- Galvanised, copper, or brass containers leach zinc and copper into manure teas; use clay, stainless steel, glass, or iron instead
- Pig, poultry, cattle manures and sewage sludges can add excessive copper, zinc, nickel, boron, lead, or cadmium
- Acid sandy soils are especially vulnerable to heavy metal accumulation
- Earthworm casts from contaminated wastes carry excess zinc
- Animals penned on galvanized surfaces produce zinc-contaminated wastes
- Limit worm casts to 10% of potting mix; supply nitrogen and potash via dilute urine
- Copper, arsenic, and other minerals are commonly added to stock feeds; buy organic feeds when possible

### Safe Practice

- Grow green manures from leguminous hedgerows, windbreaks, and intercrops
- Use foliage as mulch and compost material
- Monitor foliage for deficiency symptoms
- Compost improves sugar content and flavour of vegetables in taste tests

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Compost is the foundational substrate for many cultivated mushrooms, particularly Agaricus species. The hot compost process (Phase I and Phase II in commercial production) mirrors the hybrid approach described by Mollison: initial aerobic heating to kill competitors and condition the substrate, followed by controlled conditions favouring the target species. Understanding material contamination risks is directly applicable to [[mushroom-substrate-preparation]], where heavy metals can bioaccumulate in fruiting bodies. The safest approach for mushroom growers mirrors Mollison's recommendation: source clean organic materials and maintain closed-loop nutrient cycling.

## Carbon-to-Nitrogen Ratio

Mollison emphasized maintaining a C:N ratio of 25-30:1 in compost piles.
High-carbon materials (straw, leaves, sawdust) must be balanced with
nitrogen-rich materials (manure, food scraps, legume residues). Too much
carbon slows decomposition; excess nitrogen causes ammonia loss.

## Composting Methods

Mollison described several composting approaches suited to permaculture:
cold composting (passive piles), hot composting (turned piles reaching 60°C),
and vermicomposting (worm-driven decomposition). Each method produces humus
with different characteristics suited to different applications.
