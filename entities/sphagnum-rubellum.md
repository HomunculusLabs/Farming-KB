## Overview

**Sphagnum rubellum** Wils., commonly known as the **red bog moss**, is a widespread peat-forming moss in the family Sphagnaceae. It is one of the most characteristic species of ombrotrophic raised bogs across the Northern Hemisphere.

The species is readily recognized by its strong pink to deep red coloration, especially in exposed, sunlit positions. This pigmentation results from anthocyanin accumulation and serves as a photoprotective mechanism against UV radiation.

*S. rubellum* plays a central role in **peat formation** and bog hydrology. Its remarkable water-holding capacity (up to 20 times its dry weight) and slow [[coleman-sunlight-and-winter-crop-growth-rates]] of 5–15 mm under optimal conditions. Growth rates decline with increasing hummock height as capillary water supply decreases.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Sphagnum rubellum* was described by William M. Wilson in 1855. It belongs to *Sphagnum* section *Acutifolia*, a group characterized by stem leaves that are typically pointed and often colored red or pink.

Plants form dense cushions and hummocks 5–20 cm tall. Individual stems are slender, 1–2 mm in diameter, with a compact arrangement of fascicles. Branch fascicles contain 2–3 spreading branches and 2–4 pendant branches.

The **stem leaves** are small, triangular-lingulate, 1.0–1.5 mm long, with a broad, rounded apex. The border of stem leaves is entire or weakly serrulate at the tip.

**Branch leaves** are larger, 1.5–2.5 mm long, ovate-lanceolate, with strongly recurved margins when dry. The hyaline cells on the upper surface of branch leaves bear large, ringed pores.

Coloration varies from green in shaded or wet positions to deep crimson-red in exposed hummock tops. The intensity of red pigmentation increases with light exposure and desiccation stress.

The species is intolerant of shade and is outcompeted by *S. magellanicum* and *S. palustre* in wetter, more sheltered hollow microsites. It dominates the exposed hummock tops that receive maximum light exposure.

In oceanic regions of western Europe, *S. rubellum* extends to lower elevations and forms particularly extensive hummock systems in blanket bogs. In continental interiors, it is largely restricted to higher elevations and more northerly latitudes.

The moss can be propagated vegetatively by fragmentation. Small pieces placed on wet, acidic peat or coconut coir establish within 4–8 weeks under humid conditions, producing new shoots from stem nodes.
