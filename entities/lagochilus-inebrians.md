## Description

Lagochilus inebrians, commonly known as Turkestan Mint, is a small shrub native to the dry steppes of Turkestan in Central Asia. For centuries it has served as a source of intoxication among the Tajik, Tartar, Turkoman, and Uzbek tribesmen. It is valued both as a folk medicine and as a mild hallucinogen.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Lamiaceae (Labiatae) - Mint family
- **Genus**: Lagochilus
- **Species**: L. inebrians
- **Common names**: Turkestan Mint, intoxicating sage

## Key Facts

- Small shrub of the dry steppes of Turkestan (Central Asia)
- Leaves are gathered in October, toasted, and sometimes mixed with stems, fruits, and flowers
- [[psilocybin-mushroom-drying-and-storage-methods]] increase the aromatic fragrance of the prepared material
- Honey and sugar are often added to reduce the intense bitterness
- Some 34 other species in the genus Lagochilus
- Members of the mint family (Labiatae), native from central Asia to Iran and Afghanistan
- Included in the 8th edition of the Russian pharmacopoeia

## Traditional Uses

- **Intoxicant**: Leaves are toasted and consumed for psychoactive effects
- **Folk medicine**: Used to treat skin disease
- **Hemostatic**: Helps check hemorrhages
- **Sedative**: Provides sedation for nervous disorders
- **Culinary**: Added as condiment with sweeteners to mask bitterness

## Chemistry

- A crystalline compound named lagochiline has been isolated from the plant
- Lagochiline is a diterpene
- Whether lagochiline produces the psychoactive effects of the whole plant is unknown
- The full chemical profile responsible for intoxication has not been fully characterized
