## Overview

Verbascum blattaria, commonly known as moth mullein, is a biennial or short-lived perennial herb in the family Plantaginaceae. It is native to Eurasia and North Africa but has become widely naturalized in [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]] and other [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]].

The plant produces a low rosette of lobed basal leaves in its first year, followed by a tall flowering stem 50–150 cm tall in the second year. Flowers are white to pale yellow with a purple or pink center.

The common name "moth mullein" refers to the resemblance of the fuzzy stamens to moth antennae. It is one of the more graceful species in the genus, with slender stems and delicate flowers.

Moth mullein is less robust than its close relative Verbascum thapsus (common mullein) and occupies somewhat different habitats. It is often found in disturbed sites, meadows, and along roadsides.

The flowers open in the morning and close by late afternoon, a trait shared with several other Verbascum species. This diurnal rhythm concentrates pollinator visits during peak daylight hours.

It has a long history of use in traditional European herbal medicine, though it is less commonly used than common mullein.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Verbascum blattaria was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The genus Verbascum contains approximately 360 species, primarily distributed across Europe and western Asia.

The species is readily distinguished from other mulleins by its deeply lobed or pinnatifid basal leaves. Most other Verbascum species have entire or merely toothed leaves.

First-year rosettes produce leaves 10–25 cm long with irregular, deep lobes and rounded teeth. The foliage is bright green and sparsely hairy, lacking the dense woolly pubescence of Verbascum thapsus.

The flowering stem is slender, angular, and glandular-pubescent. It bears leaves that become progressively smaller and less lobed toward the apex.

Flowers are borne in loose, terminal racemes with 1–3 flowers per node. Each flower is 20–30 mm across with five petals, white to pale yellow, with a contrasting purple or pink center.

The five stamens are covered in conspicuous white or purple hairs, the upper three shorter and the lower two longer and bearded. This arrangement is diagnostic for the species.

The fruit is an ovoid capsule 5–8 mm long containing numerous small, brown seeds. Seeds are angular, roughly 0.5–0.8 mm long, with a ridged surface texture.

The root system consists of a slender taproot with few lateral branches. This simple root architecture is typical of biennial species that invest heavily in reproduction rather than perennial structure.

Hybridization with other Verbascum species occurs rarely in cultivation but is uncommon in the wild. Natural hybrids with Verbascum thapsus have been reported but are typically sterile.

## Distribution and Habitat

Verbascum blattaria is native to Europe, North Africa, and temperate Asia from Turkey east to the Himalayas. It has been introduced to North America, [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], Australia, and New Zealand.

In North America, it is naturalized across most of the eastern and central United States and southern Canada. It is less common in the arid West, where it is confined to irrigated areas.

The species grows at elevations from sea level to 2,000 m in pastures, meadows, roadsides, waste ground, and open woodlands. It prefers well-drained soils and full to partial sun.

Unlike Verbascum thapsus, which dominates dry, gravelly sites, moth mullein favors somewhat moister, more fertile soils. It often occurs in old fields undergoing [[comparison-primary-vs-secondary-succession]].

Populations fluctuate from year to year, with mass germination events following soil disturbance. The species forms a persistent seed bank that can remain viable for 50–100 years.

## Ecology

Moth mullein flowers from June through September, producing blooms that open sequentially from the bottom of the raceme upward. Individual flowers remain open for a single day.

The flowers are primarily visited by bees, flies, and small butterflies. The colored center serves as a nectar guide, directing pollinators to the nectar at the base of the ovary.

Self-pollination is possible but results in lower seed set than cross-pollination. The species is self-compatible but not autogamous — flowers require some mechanical disturbance to transfer pollen.

Verbascum blattaria is a strict biennial in most populations, though occasional short-lived perennial forms have been documented. After flowering and seed set, the entire plant dies.

Seed production is prolific, with a single plant producing 10,000–100,000 seeds. Seeds are dispersed by wind, water, and human activity, particularly along transportation corridors.

The species is attacked by the mullein moth (Cucullia verbasci), whose larvae feed on leaves and flowers. Several species of Capsid bugs also feed on developing seed heads.

Deer and rabbits generally avoid Verbascum blattaria due to its hairy foliage and bitter iridoid compounds. This unpalatability allows it to persist in heavily browsed areas.

Long-term [[mycorrhiza-bioassays-ecological-studies]] at the Kellogg Biological Station in Michigan have tracked moth mullein populations across decades of old-field succession.

## Cultivation and Uses

Moth mullein is occasionally grown in wildflower gardens and naturalized plantings for its airy flower spikes and long bloom period. It self-seeds freely and can become persistent.

Propagation is by seed, which requires no special treatment. Seeds germinate in 10–20 days at 15–20°C, typically in spring after winter dispersal.

The species has a history of use in European folk medicine. Leaves and flowers were used to make teas for respiratory complaints, coughs, and sore throats.
