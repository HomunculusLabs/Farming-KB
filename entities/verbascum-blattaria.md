In North America, it is naturalized across most of the eastern and central United States and southern Canada. It is less common in the arid West, where it is confined to irrigated areas.

The species grows at elevations from sea level to 2,000 m in pastures, meadows, roadsides, waste ground, and open woodlands. It prefers well-drained soils and full to partial sun.

Unlike Verbascum thapsus, which dominates dry, gravelly sites, moth mullein favors somewhat moister, more fertile soils. It often occurs in old fields undergoing [[mycorrhiza-bioassays-ecological-studies]] at the Kellogg Biological Station in Michigan have tracked moth mullein populations across decades of old-field succession.

## Cultivation and Uses

Moth mullein is occasionally grown in wildflower gardens and naturalized plantings for its airy flower spikes and long bloom period. It self-seeds freely and can become persistent.

Propagation is by seed, which requires no special treatment. Seeds germinate in 10–20 days at 15–20°C, typically in spring after winter dispersal.

The species has a history of use in European folk medicine. Leaves and flowers were used to make teas for respiratory complaints, coughs, and sore throats.
