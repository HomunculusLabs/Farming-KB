# Fusarium lateritium

## Taxonomy

| Rank | Name |
|------|------|
| Phylum | Ascomycota |
| Class | Sordariomycetes |
| Order | Hypocreales |
| Family | Nectriaceae |

Fusarium lateritium is an ascomycete fungus within the
Nectriaceae family, belonging to the order Hypocreales. The
genus Fusarium is one of the most economically important
groups of filamentous fungi, encompassing species that range
from beneficial saprotrophs and endophytes to destructive
plant pathogens. Fusarium lateritium is primarily recognized
as a saprotrophic species, although it has been reported to
exhibit weak pathogenicity toward certain host plants under
specific conditions. The species is cosmopolitan in
distribution and is commonly isolated from soil and decaying
plant material.

## Overview

Fusarium lateritium is a saprotrophic fungus that plays a
role in the decomposition of [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] in soil
ecosystems. The species has attracted particular research
attention for its interactions with soil fauna, especially
earthworms. Studies have revealed a complex and somewhat
paradoxical relationship between F. lateritium and
earthworms: while earthworms readily consume fungal-colonized
substrates containing this species, passage through the
earthworm gut completely destroys the viability of its spores.
This dynamic highlights the multifaceted nature of
fungus-fauna interactions in soil food webs and has
significant implications for understanding fungal dispersal
and survival strategies in terrestrial ecosystems.

## Earthworm Interactions

The interactions between Fusarium lateritium and earthworms
were investigated in detail by Moody et al. (1996), who
examined the feeding preferences of two earthworm species,
Lumbricus terrestris and Aporrectodea longa, toward straw
baits colonized by various fungal species. Their results
demonstrated that both earthworm species consumed straw baits
colonized by F. lateritium at relatively high rates, with
Lumbricus terrestris consuming at a rate of 12.8 and
Aporrectodea longa at a rate of 7.9. These consumption rates
were higher than those recorded for many other fungal species
included in the study, indicating that F. lateritium produces
colonized substrates that are palatable or attractive to
earthworms.

colonized material may relate to the [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]] of
the fungal mycelium, the degree of substrate modification by
fungal enzyme activity, or specific chemical cues produced
during the [[chaetomium-globosum]] exhibited even
more remarkable gut tolerance, with 33 out of 41 spores
remaining viable after passage through the earthworm
digestive system. These interspecific differences in spore
survival rates demonstrate that the impact of earthworm gut
passage on fungal propagules is highly species-dependent,
with F. lateritium being particularly vulnerable to the
conditions encountered during transit through the earthworm
alimentary canal.

The researchers determined that spore destruction in
F. lateritium was caused by the chemical action of
intestinal fluids rather than by the abrasive physical
effects of soil particles within the gut. This finding
suggests that specific enzymatic or chemical properties of
earthworm digestive fluids are selectively lethal to F.
lateritium spores while permitting the survival of spores
from other fungal species.

## Role in Decomposition

Fusarium lateritium contributes to soil decomposition processes
through its enzymatic modification of organic substrates,
particularly plant litter and straw material. The species
produces a range of extracellular enzymes that [[query-can-fungi-break-down-plastic-pollution]]

available to other soil organisms and plants. Its role as a
