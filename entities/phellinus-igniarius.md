## Overview

**Phellinus igniarius** (L.) Quél., commonly known as the **fire sponge** or **willow bracket**, is a perennial poroid fungus in the family Hymenochaetaceae. It is a pathogenic wood-decay species causing a white rot in broadleaf trees, particularly willow (*Salix*), poplar (*Populus*), and birch (*Betula*).

The species has a long history of use in traditional medicine across East Asia, where it is valued as an anti-inflammatory and immune-modulating agent. Modern research has identified polysaccharides, hispolon, and other bioactive phenolic compounds as the primary pharmacologically active constituents.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Fungi > Basidiomycota > Agaricomycetes > Hymenochaetales > Hymenochaetaceae > **Phellinus** > *P. igniarius*.

**Synonyms:** *Boletus igniarius* L. (basionym), *Fomes igniarius* (L.) Fr., *Polyporus igniarius* (L.) Fr., *Xanthochrous igniarius* (L.) Pat.

The basidiocarp is a perennial, hoof-shaped to semicircular conk, typically 5–20 cm wide and 2–8 cm thick. The upper surface is deeply zonate, dark grey to black, with a hard crust that often cracks with age.

The pore surface is brown to rust-brown, with 5–7 circular pores per mm. Tubes are stratified, each layer 2–6 mm thick, separated by thin brown context lines. The context is woody, cinnamon-brown, and distinctly hard.

Basidiospores are broadly ellipsoid, 5–7 × 4–5 µm, hyaline, smooth, and thin-walled. The hyphal system is dimitic, with generative hyphae bearing clamp connections and skeletal hyphae that are thick-walled and golden-brown.

Setal hyphae (characteristic of the genus) are present in the trama, typically 15–30 µm long with a sharply pointed apex.

These thick-walled, darkened hyphal tips are a key microscopic diagnostic feature that separates *Phellinus* from other polypore genera.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Phellinus igniarius* has a wide circumboreal distribution across temperate and boreal forests of Europe, Asia, and North America. It is particularly common in riparian zones and moist lowland forests where willow is abundant.

The species fruits exclusively on dead or dying hardwoods, most frequently *Salix* species but also recorded on *Populus*, *Betula*, *Alnus*, *Malus*, and *Quercus*. It is almost never found on conifers, distinguishing it from the closely related *Phellinus pomaceus*.

Fruiting occurs year-round in temperate regions, though basidiocarp development is most active during cool, moist conditions in autumn. The perennial conks can persist for many years, accumulating new pore layers annually.

## Bioactive Compounds

**Polysaccharides** constitute the major bioactive fraction. Water-soluble β-glucans isolated from the fruiting body exhibit significant immunomodulatory activity, stimulating macrophage proliferation and enhancing natural killer cell cytotoxicity in vitro.

**Hispolon** (6-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-4-hydroxy-2-hexen-1-one) is the most studied phenolic compound. It demonstrates potent anti-inflammatory activity through inhibition of NF-κB signaling, and has shown antiproliferative effects against several human cancer cell lines.

**Phelligridins** are unique styrylpyrone-class pigments isolated from *P. igniarius*. These compounds exhibit antioxidant activity, with IC₅₀ values comparable to ascorbic acid in DPPH radical scavenging assays.

**Inoscavin A** and other benzoquinone derivatives contribute to the antioxidant profile. The total phenolic content of fruiting bodies ranges from 20–45 mg GAE/g dry weight depending on substrate and geographic origin.

## Pharmacology

In traditional Chinese medicine, *P. igniarius* is prescribed for diarrhea, hemorrhoids, and menstrual irregularities. In Siberian folk medicine, a decoction of the conk was consumed for gastrointestinal ailments and as a general tonic.

Korean folk medicine employs it as a remedy for stomach disorders and general fatigue. The dried fruiting body is ground into a powder and brewed as a tea, often combined with other medicinal fungi.

Modern pharmacological studies have validated several traditional uses. Polysaccharide fractions demonstrate immunostimulatory effects at doses of 50–200 mg/kg in murine models, increasing splenocyte proliferation and cytokine production.

Hispolon shows selective cytotoxicity against human hepatoma (HepG2), breast cancer (MCF-7), and lung cancer (A549) cell lines with IC₅₀ values in the 10–50 µM range. In vivo studies in mice have shown tumor growth inhibition of 40–60% at oral doses of 20 mg/kg.

Anti-inflammatory effects are mediated through suppression of iNOS and COX-2 expression, reducing prostaglandin E₂ and nitric oxide production in LPS-stimulated macrophages.

Hepatoprotective activity has also been demonstrated in CCl₄-induced liver injury models. Oral administration of polysaccharide extracts at 200 mg/kg reduced serum ALT and AST levels by 40–55%.

## Ecological Role

As a white-rot decomposer, *P. igniarius* plays a significant role in nutrient cycling in forest ecosystems. It secretes lignin-modifying enzymes including laccase, manganese peroxidase, and lignin peroxidase, which break down the lignin component of wood.

The species is parasitic on weakened or stressed trees, establishing heart rot that progressively compromises the structural integrity of the host. Infected trees become susceptible to windthrow and branch failure.

In riparian ecosystems, *P. igniarius* contributes to the decomposition of fallen willows and poplars, facilitating nutrient return to aquatic systems. The conks provide microhabitat for invertebrates and nesting sites for birds and small mammals.

## Similar Species

*Phellinus linteus* (mesima) is a closely related species found in tropical and subtropical Asia. It has a smoother, less zonate upper surface and smaller pores (7–9 per mm). *P. linteus* is commercially cultivated for medicinal use.

*Fomes fomentarius* (tinder conk) occupies a similar ecological niche but has a distinctly grey pore surface that darkens to brown when bruised. Its context is softer and more fibrous, lacking the woody hardness of *P. igniarius*.

*Phellinus pomaceus* fruits primarily on Prunus species (especially plum and almond) and has a smaller, thinner conk with a more uniformly dark upper surface.

## Cultivation

Cultivation of *P. igniarius* is challenging and not commercially established at scale. The species has slow mycelial growth, with optimal rates on malt extract agar at 25–28°C and a preference for slightly acidic conditions (pH 5.5–6.0).

Substrate colonization typically requires 60–90 days on supplemented sawdust, significantly longer than most cultivated medicinal fungi. Fruiting induction requires prolonged cold shock (4–8°C for 2–4 weeks) followed by high humidity (85–95%) and diffuse light.

Yields on artificial substrates remain low compared to wild-harvested material. Most commercial supplies are still collected from wild populations, primarily in northeastern China and the Russian Far East. This has raised concerns about overharvesting in some regions.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology Summary

The pharmacological profile of *P. igniarius* is summarized by its three major compound classes. Polysaccharides (β-glucans) drive immunomodulation, phenolic compounds (hispolon, phelligridins) provide antioxidant and anticancer activity, and triterpenoids contribute anti-inflammatory effects.

Extraction methods significantly influence the bioactive profile. Hot water extraction favors polysaccharide recovery, while ethanol extraction is more efficient for hispolon and other phenolics. Dual extraction protocols combining both solvents are recommended for maximum therapeutic benefit.

Bioavailability of hispolon is moderate, with peak plasma concentrations reached within 30–60 minutes of oral administration in rodent models. Metabolism occurs primarily via hepatic glucuronidation, with a plasma half-life of approximately 2 hours.
