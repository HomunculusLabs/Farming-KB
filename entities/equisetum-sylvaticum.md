## Overview

Equisetum sylvaticum, commonly known as wood horsetail, is a perennial horsetail in the family Equisetaceae. It is one of the most ornamental species in the genus, recognized by its gracefully whorled, feathery branchlets.

The species grows 20–60 cm tall and forms extensive colonies in damp, shaded woodlands across the Northern Hemisphere. Its delicate, spreading branches give it a distinctive appearance unlike any other horsetail.

Equisetum sylvaticum is one of only a few horsetails that prefer shaded woodland habitats over open wetlands. It thrives in acidic, humus-rich soils along stream banks, forest margins, and damp clearings.

The plant has a long evolutionary history, belonging to an ancient lineage of sphenophytes that dominated terrestrial vegetation during the Carboniferous period over 300 million years ago.

Like all horsetails, Equisetum sylvaticum accumulates silica in its tissues, which has historically made the stems useful for abrasives and polishing. However, this species is not commercially harvested for that purpose.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Equisetum sylvaticum was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. It belongs to the subgenus Equisetum (the "articulated" horsetails), which includes species with regularly whorled branches and conical strobili.

The genus Equisetum is the sole surviving genus of the class Equisetopsida, with approximately 15 living species worldwide. Equisetum sylvaticum shows no close hybrid relationships, unlike many other horsetail species that readily form crosses.

Sterile stems are 20–60 cm tall, green, and bear 3–4 tiers of gracefully arching branchlets. Each branch whorl contains 10–20 slender branches that spread horizontally, then curve upward at their tips.

Fertile stems emerge in spring, initially unbranched and pale brownish, bearing a solitary terminal strobilus. After [[mushroom-veil-function-wind-light-protection-prior-to-spore-release]], the fertile stems develop green branches similar to sterile stems and persist through [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

The stem surface is rough due to silica deposits in the epidermal cells, a characteristic shared by all horsetails. The central cavity occupies approximately one-third of the stem diameter.

The stem is articulated with distinct nodes and internodes, each node bearing a sheath of fused microphylls. The teeth of the sheath are dark brown with pale margins, providing a useful identification character.

Strobili are 1–3 cm long, pointed, and consist of hexagonal sporangiophores bearing spores with elaters that aid dispersal. Spores are green, spherical, and approximately 25–35 µm in diameter.

Chromosome number is 2n = 216, consistent with a high polyploid level common in the subgenus Equisetum. The species does not form hybrids with other horsetail species, making it taxonomically distinct and easier to identify in the field.

## Distribution and Habitat

Equisetum sylvaticum has a circumboreal distribution across northern Europe, Asia, and North America. In Europe, it occurs from Iceland and the British Isles through Scandinavia to central Europe and eastward across Russia.

In North America, it ranges from Alaska across Canada to Newfoundland, extending south to the northern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]]. Asian populations span from the Ural Mountains to Japan and the Korean Peninsula.

The species is strongly associated with damp, acidic woodland habitats including boreal coniferous forests, birch woodlands, and alder carrs. It also grows along shaded stream banks, [[tahuya-forest-road-reclamation]] embankments, and damp clearings.

Soil preferences include acidic, peaty, or humus-rich substrates with high moisture content. It tolerates seasonal flooding but does not grow in permanently inundated soils. Elevation range extends from sea level to approximately 1,500 m.

The species has declined in parts of western Europe due to drainage of wet woodlands, clear-felling of native forests, and eutrophication of damp habitats. However, it remains common throughout most of its circumboreal range.

In the British Isles, Equisetum sylvaticum is widespread in Scotland, Wales, and northern England but local and declining in southern and eastern England. It is listed on the county Rare Plant Register in several English counties.

Global population trends are considered stable, though systematic monitoring is lacking across much of the Asian range.

## Ecology

Equisetum sylvaticum spreads primarily through extensive rhizome networks that can extend several meters from the parent plant. Clonal colonies are common, with individual genets potentially persisting for decades.

The species is an important component of the ground flora in damp boreal and temperate woodlands. It often forms dense stands that suppress herbaceous competitors through shading and rhizome competition.

[[spore-production-geotropic-sensitivity-coupling-basidiomycete-badham-1982]] occurs in spring, typically April to June in European populations. The green spores are short-lived and require moist conditions for germination within 48–72 hours of release.

Herbivory is limited due to the high silica content and toxic compounds in the stems. However, some Lepidoptera larvae feed on horsetail foliage, and the plant provides cover for small woodland invertebrates.

Equisetum sylvaticum accumulates silica and various minerals from the soil, contributing to [[nutrient-cycling-in-soil]] woodland ecosystems. Its persistent rhizome systems also help stabilize stream banks and prevent erosion on damp slopes.

The species can act as an indicator of acidic, nutrient-poor woodland conditions. Its presence often correlates with other acidophilic plants including bilberry, heath bedstraw, and various Sphagnum mosses.

Equisetum sylvaticum contains thiaminase and various alkaloids that make it toxic to livestock if consumed in large quantities. Horses are particularly susceptible to horsetail poisoning, which causes thiamine deficiency and neurological symptoms.

Despite its toxicity, some [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] groups used decoctions of Equisetum species as medicinal washes [[cannabis-for-skin-conditions-detailed]] and urinary complaints. However, specific ethnobotanical records for Equisetum sylvaticum are sparse compared to the well-documented use of [[equisetum-arvense]].

## Conservation

Equisetum sylvaticum is not considered threatened at the global level and is listed as Least Concern by most national conservation bodies. Its wide circumboreal distribution provides resilience against regional declines.

However, local populations have suffered from habitat loss, particularly the drainage of wet woodlands for agriculture and forestry in temperate Europe. Acidification of soils from atmospheric pollution has also negatively impacted some populations.

Conservation of this species depends primarily on protection of damp woodland habitats and maintenance of natural hydrological regimes. It benefits from broadleaved woodland management that retains moist, shady conditions.
