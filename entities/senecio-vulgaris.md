## Taxonomy and Morphology

Senecio vulgaris L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The genus name derives from Latin "senex" (old man), referring to the gray, pappus-bearing achenes that resemble an old man's white hair.

The species belongs to the tribe Senecioneae within Asteraceae. It is closely related to S. jacobaea (tansy ragwort), S. vulgaris var. denticulatus, and several other PA-producing Senecio species found worldwide.

Chromosome number is 2n = 40 for diploid populations, though polyploid forms occur. Molecular studies indicate that S. vulgaris is an allotetraploid derived from hybridization within the Senecio section.

Plants are typically 10–40 cm tall, though robust specimens may reach 60 cm. Stems are erect, hollow, and branched from the base, with a green to purplish-green coloration and sparse glandular pubescence.

Roots are fibrous and relatively shallow, forming a dense mat in the upper 10 cm of soil. This shallow root system makes the species susceptible to drought but allows rapid nutrient capture in disturbed, nutrient-rich substrates.

### Leaf and floral morphology

Leaves are alternate, oblong to spatulate, 3–10 cm long, and pinnately lobed or toothed. Upper leaves are sessile and clasping, while lower leaves have short petioles.

Leaf margins are irregularly dentate to pinnatifid, with terminal lobes often larger than lateral ones. The foliage exudes a characteristic, somewhat rank odor when crushed, due to the presence of sesquiterpene lactones and alkaloids.

The inflorescence is a terminal corymb of 20–40 small, cylindrical capitula. Each head is 6–10 mm long, consisting entirely of tubular disk florets — ray florets are absent. Florets are yellow, and the involucre is composed of a single row of black-tipped bracts.

Achenes are cylindrical, 2–3 mm long, with a white pappus of fine bristles that facilitates wind dispersal. A single plant produces 1,000–2,500 seeds, which remain viable in soil for 2–4 years.

Seed dispersal occurs primarily by wind, with pappus-bearing achenes traveling distances of 10–50 meters under normal conditions. Longer-distance dispersal is facilitated by human activities, particularly the movement of contaminated soil, compost, and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], southern Africa, Australia, and New Zealand.

The species reached North America sometime before 1700, likely as a contaminant in ship ballast or imported seed. By the early 19th century, it was widespread across the eastern United States and had begun spreading westward with agricultural expansion.

The species is highly adaptable, colonizing disturbed ground in agricultural fields, gardens, nurseries, roadsides, waste areas, and coastal habitats. It is particularly problematic in winter cereal crops and [[psilocin-biosynthetic-delay-hypothesis-successive-flushes]] following rainfall or irrigation. In container nurseries, it is one of the most common and difficult-to-control weed species.

## Toxicity

The primary toxic principle in S. vulgaris is pyrrolizidine alkaloids, predominantly senecionine, seneciphylline, and retrorsine. These compounds are hepatotoxic, genotoxic, and carcinogenic, affecting the liver through metabolic activation by cytochrome P450 enzymes.

Livestock poisoning occurs most commonly in cattle, horses, pigs, and poultry that consume contaminated hay or graze in infested pastures. Chronic PA exposure causes progressive liver fibrosis, hepatic veno-occlusive disease, and megalocytosis of hepatocytes.

Acute poisoning is rare because the alkaloids render the plant unpalatable to most livestock. However, when groundsel is dried and baled with hay, the bitterness diminishes while toxicity remains, leading to inadvertent chronic exposure.

Human health concerns have centered on PA contamination of herbal teas and [[bloomfield-fungal-pathogens-and-parasites]] including Puccinia lagenophorae (groundsel rust).

Green peach aphids (Myzus persicae) frequently colonize S. vulgaris before migrating to nearby vegetable crops, transmitting cucumber mosaic virus and other pathogens in the process. This vector role makes groundsel management important in [[fukuoka-textdoc-arrowhead-scale-ladybug-biological-control]] agents against related invasive Senecio species.
