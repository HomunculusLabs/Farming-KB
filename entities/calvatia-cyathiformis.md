## Overview

**Calvatia cyathiformis**, commonly known as the **purple-spored puffball** or **pestle puffball**, is a saprotrophic gasteroid fungus in the family Lycoperdaceae. It is distinguished from other puffballs by its prominent purple-brown spore mass at maturity, a feature that makes field identification straightforward once the gleba is exposed.

The species fruits singly or in small groups on soil in grasslands, lawns, and disturbed ground. It is widely distributed across eastern North America, parts of Central America, and has been reported in Australia.

Immature specimens are edible and considered choice by many foragers, though the species is less commonly collected than **[[calvatia-gigantea]]** or **[[lycoperdon-perlatum]]**. Mature specimens with purple spore powder are inedible.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Calvatia cyathiformis** was first described as *Bovista cyathiformis* by Louis Augustin Guillaume Bosc in 1791, and later transferred to *Calvatia* by Andrew Price Morgan in 1887.

The fruiting body is a sessile to short-stipitate puffball, typically 4–10 cm in diameter and 5–12 cm tall. The shape ranges from roughly spherical to pear-shaped or inverted-conical (pestle-shaped), giving rise to the common name.

The outer surface (peridium) is smooth to finely warted when young, white to cream-colored, becoming tan to brownish with age. It dehisces irregularly at maturity to release spores.

The gleba is firm and white when immature, gradually turning yellowish, then olive-gray, and finally a distinctive deep purple-brown as spores mature. This purple gleba is the key diagnostic feature.

Spores are spherical to broadly ellipsoidal, 5–8 µm in diameter, ornamented with fine spines or warts, and purple-brown in mass deposit. Capillitial threads are slender, branched, and septate.

## Distribution and Habitat

**C. cyathiformis** is found primarily in eastern and central North America, ranging from southern Canada through the eastern United States to Texas and Florida. It has also been documented in parts of Central America, the Caribbean, and Australia, where it may have been introduced.

The species is terrestrial and saprotrophic, fruiting on soil in open grasslands, pastures, lawns, parks, and along roadsides. It prefers warm, humid conditions and typically fruits from late summer through autumn (August to November in North America).

It occasionally appears in disturbed habitats including cultivated fields and garden beds. Unlike many puffballs, it is rarely found on wood or woody debris, distinguishing it from species like **[[coprinus-comatus-detailed]]**.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

Immature specimens with firm, white internal flesh are edible and considered good when properly prepared. The texture is firm and somewhat crisp, with a mild, pleasant flavor comparable to other puffballs.

Foraging guidelines require cutting specimens vertically to confirm the gleba is entirely white with no developing gills or chambers. Any specimen showing yellow, purple, or darkened gleba should be discarded.

Preparation methods include slicing and sautéing in butter, breading and frying, or adding to soups and stews. The flesh does not store well and should be cooked within a day or two of collection.

As with all puffballs, individuals with known allergies to mushroom spores should exercise caution, as inhaling mature spore dust from *Calvatia* species can cause respiratory irritation in sensitive individuals.

## Bioactive Compounds

Like other members of Lycoperdaceae, **C. cyathiformis** produces calvacin, a protein-bound polysaccharide fraction that has demonstrated antitumor activity in laboratory studies. Calvacin was first isolated from *Calvatia gigantea* but is present in related species.

The spores contain small quantities of sterols and fatty acids typical of Basidiomycota, including ergosterol (the fungal precursor to vitamin D2). UV exposure of sliced fruiting bodies can significantly increase vitamin D2 content.

Antimicrobial activity has been reported for crude extracts of several *Calvatia* species against gram-positive bacteria, though specific studies on *C. cyathiformis* are limited.

The purple pigmentation of the mature gleba is attributed to melanin-like compounds in the spore walls, which may serve a protective function against UV radiation and microbial degradation.

## Ecological Role

As a saprotroph, **C. cyathiformis** decomposes organic matter in grassland soils, contributing to nutrient cycling. The mycelium breaks down plant litter and thatch, releasing nitrogen, phosphorus, and carbon back into the soil.

The species may form weak ectomycorrhizal associations with grasses, though its primary ecological role is saprotrophic. It contributes to soil structure through the physical action of mycelial networks.

Mature puffballs release enormous quantities of spores that are dispersed by wind and rain splash. A single large specimen can produce billions of spores, which can remain viable in soil for years.

The fruiting bodies serve as food for various invertebrates including slug species and insect larvae. Small mammals may also consume immature puffballs, contributing to local spore dispersal.

## Related Species

Several other puffball species occur in similar habitats and may be confused with **C. cyathiformis**. The most important distinction is the purple gleba, which no other common North American puffball shares at such intensity.

**Calvatia gigantea** is much larger (15–70 cm), nearly spherical, lacks a stem, and has white to olive-brown spores at maturity. It fruits in similar grassland habitats but is unmistakable when mature due to its enormous size.

**Lycoperdon perlatum** is smaller (2–5 cm), pear-shaped with prominent conical warts on the upper surface, and produces olive-brown spore dust. It is more commonly found on decaying wood and stumps.

**Bovista plumbea**, the tumble puffball, is smaller (1–4 cm), grows on a fragile stem-less base, and breaks free to roll in the wind at maturity. Its spore mass is dark brown to olive, never purple.

**Calvatia craniformis**, the brain puffball, has a deeply wrinkled, brain-like surface and pinkish spore mass. It is less common and typically found in sandy soils.

## Foraging and Identification Notes

The purple gleba is the single most reliable diagnostic feature for confident identification. Beginners should always slice puffballs vertically to confirm the interior is homogeneous and white, with no trace of gills, a developing mushroom, or an outline visible within.

Specimens growing on buried wood may be confused with young *Amanita* "eggs," which can be fatal if consumed. The key difference is that amanita eggs contain a developing cap and gills when cut open, while true puffballs have featureless white flesh throughout.

The pestle shape (wider at the top, tapering to a narrow base) is characteristic but not universal. Some specimens are nearly spherical, especially when growing in sheltered locations with reduced wind exposure.

Foragers should avoid collecting from roadsides, lawns treated with herbicides or pesticides, or industrial sites, as puffballs readily accumulate heavy metals and chemical residues from contaminated soils.
