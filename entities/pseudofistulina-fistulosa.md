## Overview

**Pseudofistulina fistulosa** (syn. *[[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]] on dead or dying hardwood.

The genus name refers to the separately individual tubes (stipe-like structures) that distinguish it from typical fused-pore polypores.

It is a brown-rot fungus causing a characteristic cubical fracture in colonized wood, primarily on oaks and other Fagaceae.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Fungi — **Phylum:** Basidiomycota — **Class:** Agaricomycetes — **Order:** Agaricales — **Family:** Fistulinaceae

**Species:** *Pseudofistulina fistulosa* (Schwein.) G. Cunn. (1963)

The basidiocarp is effused-reflexed to pileate, forming semicircular brackets 5–20 cm across and 1–3 cm thick at the base.

The upper surface is tomentose to smooth, reddish-brown to dark brown, often with concentric zone lines.

The hymenophore consists of individually separate, cylindrical tubes that can be easily separated from one another with a finger, unlike fused-pore polypores.

Each tube is 5–15 mm long, with a pore surface that is pale pinkish to reddish when fresh, darkening with age.

Spores are cylindrical to allantoid, hyaline, smooth, measuring 6–9 × 2–3 µm. Basidia are clavate, four-spored.

The context is fleshy and reddish when cut, sometimes exuding reddish juice when fresh — a trait shared with the unrelated *Fistulina hepatica*.

## Chemistry

The chemistry of *Pseudofistulina fistulosa* has not been extensively studied compared to more commercially significant polypores.

The reddish pigmentation of both the context and hymenophore suggests the presence of anthraquinone or benzochromenone derivatives, which are common in brown-rot fungi.
