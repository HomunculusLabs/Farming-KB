## Overview

*Silene regia*, known as royal catchfly, is a taprooted herbaceous perennial in the family Caryophyllaceae. It is native to tallgrass prairies and glades in the central and eastern United States.

The species produces striking scarlet-red flowers in midsummer that are among the showiest of any North American Silene. These brilliant blooms make it a sought-after plant for prairie restoration and native gardens.

Royal catchfly is listed as threatened or endangered in several states across its range. Habitat loss from prairie conversion to agriculture has eliminated most historic populations.

The flowers are adapted for hummingbird pollination, making royal catchfly one of the few red-flowered prairie species. This specialized pollination strategy contributes to its ecological significance.

The specific epithet *regia* means "royal," referring to the stately appearance and rich crimson flower color. The common name "catchfly" refers to the sticky calyx that can trap small insects.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantate → Phylum Tracheophyta → Class Magnoliopsida → Order Caryophyllales → Family Caryophyllaceae → Genus *Silene* → Species *S. regia* Urb.

The species belongs to *Silene* section *Siphonomorpha*, characterized by tubular calyces and nocturnal to diurnal flowering. It is closely allied with the Eurasian *Silene italica* and *Silene nutans*.

Plants grow 60–120 cm tall with an erect, unbranched or sparingly branched stem. The stem is covered with short, sticky glandular hairs, especially on the upper portions.

Leaves are lanceolate to narrowly elliptic, 5–12 cm long and 1–3 cm wide. Basal leaves form a rosette that persists through early summer, while stem leaves are opposite and sessile.

Flowers are borne in a loose, terminal panicle with 5–15 flowers per inflorescence. Each flower is 2.5–4 cm across with five brilliant scarlet-red, deeply notched petals.

The calyx is tubular, 15–22 mm long, with ten prominent veins and sticky glandular surfaces. The sticky exudate traps small crawling insects, potentially reducing herbivore damage to developing seeds.

Blooming occurs from June through August. Individual flowers open in the morning and remain open for 2–3 days.

The fruit is a cylindrical capsule 12–18 mm long that opens by six teeth at the apex. Each capsule contains 50–100 small, dark gray seeds approximately 1 mm in diameter.

The sticky glandular hairs on the calyx and upper stem exude a viscous secretion. This trapping mechanism may reduce seed predation by ants and other crawling insects.

## Distribution and Habitat

Royal catchfly is native to the central and eastern United States, ranging from Ohio and Michigan west to Kansas and south to Arkansas and Tennessee. Populations are scattered and localized within this range.

The species occurs in mesic to dry-mesic tallgrass prairies, limestone glades, oak savannas, and open woodlands. It prefers well-drained soils, often on limestone-derived substrates.

Historically, *Silene regia* was more widespread across the tallgrass prairie belt. Conversion of prairie to cropland has reduced populations to less than 10% of their original extent.

The largest remaining populations occur in the Flint Hills of Kansas and the Osage Plains of Missouri. These areas retain intact prairie due to their rocky soils being unsuitable for cultivation.

Smaller disjunct populations persist in Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, and Wisconsin. Many of these peripheral populations are critically small, consisting of fewer than 50 individuals.

Ohio populations are restricted to a few remnant prairies in the west-central counties. The largest Ohio population occurs at the Travis Prairie Reserve in Adams County, managed by The Nature Conservancy.

Wisconsin populations occur on dry, limestone-influenced prairies in the southern part of the state. Intensive management including prescribed burning has stabilized these populations since the 1990s.

## Ecology

The bright red flowers are primarily pollinated by ruby-throated hummingbirds (*Archilochus colubris*). This hummingbird specialization is unusual among North American Caryophyllaceae, which are typically pollinated by moths or bees.

Sphinx moths also visit the flowers during evening hours, contributing to cross-pollination. The tubular calyx and narrow corolla tube exclude most bee species as effective pollinators.

Seeds are dispersed primarily by wind, shaken from the open capsule as it dries and splits at the apex. The small seeds (approximately 1 mm diameter) can travel short distances on prairie winds.

*Silene regia* is a poor competitor against aggressive prairie grasses such as *Andropogon gerardii* and *Sorghastrum nutans*. Fire suppression that allows woody encroachment further reduces available habitat.

The species forms a deep taproot that can extend 60–90 cm into the soil. This root system provides drought tolerance and allows plants to persist through dry summers on exposed prairie sites.

The deep taproot also makes established plants resistant to periodic grazing and mowing. Plants resprout readily from the crown after top growth is removed.

## Cultivation

Royal catchfly is cultivated in native plant gardens, prairie restorations, and wildlife borders. It requires full sun and well-drained, loamy to sandy soil with neutral to slightly alkaline pH.

Hardy to USDA zones 4–8. Plants are long-lived perennials that may take 2–3 years to reach flowering size from seed. First-year seedlings form only a basal rosette.

Propagation is primarily by seed, which requires 4–8 weeks of cold stratification for germination. Seeds germinate at relatively high rates (60–80%) after proper stratification.

In cultivation, plants benefit from companion planting with native grasses that provide physical support. The tall, slender stems may require staking in fertile garden soils.

The species has no serious insect pests or diseases. Deer tend to avoid the sticky-foliaged stems, making it a good choice for gardens with heavy deer pressure.

Plants transplant poorly due to the deep taproot, so direct seeding is preferred for establishment. Container-grown nursery stock should be planted in early spring before taproot development becomes extensive.

Royal catchfly combines well with other tallgrass prairie forbs such as *Echinacea purpurea*, *Ratibida pinnata*, and *Liatris pycnostachya*. This assemblage creates a long-blooming native perennial border.

## Conservation

*Silene regia* is listed as endangered in Ohio, Wisconsin, and Indiana, and threatened in several other states. The primary threat is habitat loss from agricultural conversion and urban development.

Fire suppression in remnant prairies allows woody species to encroach, shading out the light-demanding catchfly. Prescribed burning benefits populations by removing competing vegetation and exposing mineral soil for seedling establishment.

Genetic studies reveal low diversity in small, isolated populations. Conservation biologists recommend maintaining population connectivity to prevent further genetic erosion.

Several state native plant societies conduct annual monitoring and seed collection programs. Collected seed is used for ex-situ conservation and prairie restoration projects throughout the Midwest.

The species has proven adaptable to cultivation and is increasingly available from native plant nurseries. Garden cultivation serves as a genetic reservoir that may benefit future reintroduction efforts.
