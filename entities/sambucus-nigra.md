# Sambucus nigra

**Sambucus nigra**, commonly known as **European elderberry**, **black elder**, or simply **elder**, is a deciduous shrub or small tree in the family Adoxaceae (formerly Caprifoliaceae).
Native to Europe, western Asia, and North Africa, it has become naturalized in many temperate regions worldwide and is one of the most widely recognized medicinal plants in Western herbalism.
The dark purple-black berries and fragrant cream-white flowers of *Sambucus nigra* have been used for thousands of years in traditional European medicine, winemaking, and culinary applications.
Modern clinical research has validated many of its traditional uses, particularly its antiviral properties against influenza and common respiratory infections, making elderberry one of the best-studied and most commercially successful herbal medicines of the 21st century.

Several subspecies are recognized: the European type subsp. *nigra*, American elderberry subsp. *canadensis* (sometimes *S. canadensis*), and Blue elderberry subsp. *cerulea* of western North America.

## Botanical Description

### Growth Habit and Size

- Deciduous shrub or small tree, typically 3–8 meters tall, multi-stemmed with arching branches
- Fast-growing (60+ cm/year), spreading crown, lifespan 20–30+ years
- Forms dense thickets through root suckering

### Leaves

- Opposite, pinnately compound, 10–30 cm long, 5–7 leaflets
- Leaflets elliptic to lanceolate, 5–12 cm long, serrate margins, dark green above
- Distinctive odor when crushed

### Flowers

- Large flat-topped corymbs 10–25 cm across, individual flowers 5–6 mm, creamy-white
- Extremely fragrant with sweet honey-like scent; bloom period May–June
- Highly attractive to pollinators; hermaphroditic

### Fruit

- Berry-like drupe, 6–8 mm diameter, glossy black-purple when ripe
- Juicy, slightly tart pulp; 3–5 seeds per berry; ripens August–October
- Rich in anthocyanins (cyanidin-3-glucoside, cyanidin-3-sambubioside)

### Bark and Wood

- Young bark greenish-gray; older bark gray-brown, deeply furrowed
- Large white spongy pith in young branches — key identification feature
- Hard, fine-grained wood historically used for tools and instruments

## Distribution and Habitat

- Native to Europe, North Africa, and western/central Asia; naturalized in North America, Australia, and NZ
- USDA Zones 4–8; found in woodland edges, hedgerows, waste ground, riparian areas
- Highly adaptable — tolerates clay, loam, and sandy soils; prefers moist, nitrogen-rich sites
- Full sun to partial shade; often a pioneer species colonizing disturbed ground

## Chemistry and Active Compounds

### Berries

- **Anthocyanins:** Cyanidin-3-glucoside, cyanidin-3-sambubioside (primary pigments)
- **Flavonols:** Quercetin, kaempferol, rutin, isoquercitrin
- **Phenolic acids:** Chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, ferulic acid
- **Vitamins:** Vitamin C, vitamin A (beta-carotene); minerals: potassium, calcium, iron
- **Other:** Tannins, lectins, trace essential oils

### Flowers

- **Flavonoids:** Rutin, quercetin, isoquercitrin, hyperoside
- **Phenolic acids:** Chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid
- **Triterpenes:** Ursolic acid, oleanolic acid
- **Mucilage:** Polysaccharides with soothing properties; essential oil with terpenoids

> ⚠️ **TOXICITY WARNING:** Raw elderberries contain cyanogenic glycosides (sambunigrin) and lectins that can cause nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. Cooking (boiling for 15–20 minutes) destroys these compounds. The stems, bark, leaves, and roots contain higher concentrations and should not be consumed. Never consume raw elderberry products in quantity.

## Traditional and Modern Medicinal Uses

### Traditional Uses (European Herbalism)

- Treatment of colds, influenza, and respiratory infections — the most prominent traditional use
- Diaphoretic (sweat-inducing) to break fevers; anti-inflammatory for joint pain
- Laxative and diuretic properties; topical application for wounds and burns
- Flower preparations as mild relaxant and sleep aid; culinary preserves as food and preventive medicine

### Modern Clinical Evidence

| Condition | Evidence Level | Key Findings |
|-----------|---------------|--------------|
| Influenza | Strong (multiple RCTs) | Reduces duration and severity by 1–4 days |
| Common cold | Moderate | May reduce cold duration and symptom severity |
| Upper respiratory infections | Moderate | Symptomatic relief supported by several trials |
| Antioxidant support | Strong | High ORAC value, free radical scavenging |
| Anti-inflammatory | Moderate | Inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokines in vitro |

The most widely cited clinical trial (Zakay-Rones et al., 2004) found elderberry extract significantly reduced flu symptom duration by 3–4 days versus placebo.
A 2019 meta-analysis concluded elderberry supplementation substantially reduced upper respiratory symptoms.

## Cultivation and Permaculture Applications

- Easily propagated from hardwood cuttings (20–30 cm) in winter or softwood cuttings in summer
- Plant spacing: 2–3 meters; begins fruiting at 2–3 years, full production by year 4–5
- Annual yield: 10–15 kg of berries per mature bush
- Excellent [[food-forest]] species with minimal care; nitrogen-fixing through endophytic bacteria
- Root suckering creates wildlife thickets; responds well to [[fermented-plant-juice]] and [[comfrey]] mulch
