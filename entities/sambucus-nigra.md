# Sambucus nigra

**European black elderberry** is a deciduous shrub or small tree in the family Adoxaceae (formerly Caprifoliaceae), native to Europe, northern Africa, and western Asia. Renowned for its clusters of dark purple-black berries and creamy white flower umbels, S. nigra has been a cornerstone of European folk medicine for millennia and has gained modern scientific validation for its antiviral and immune-modulating properties.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Dipsacales
- **Family:** Adoxaceae
- **Genus:** Sambucus
- **Species:** S. nigra
- **Subspecies:** S. n. nigra (European), S. n. canadensis (American — sometimes treated as separate species S. canadensis)

## Botanical Description

Sambucus nigra typically grows 3–10 m tall, forming a multi-stemmed thicket with arching branches. The bark is grey-brown with prominent lenticels. Young shoots are green and contain a soft pith characteristic of the genus.

**Leaves** are opposite, pinnate, 10–30 cm long, with 5–7 serrated leaflets. Foliage emits a distinctive, somewhat unpleasant odor when crushed.

**Flowers** appear in May–June as large, flat-topped corymbs (umbel-like clusters) 10–25 cm across. Individual flowers are creamy-white, 5–6 mm diameter, with five petals and five stamens. The blossoms are intensely fragrant and attract a wide range of pollinators.

**Fruit** is a glossy black-purple drupe, 6–8 mm diameter, containing 3–5 small seeds. Berries ripen in late August–September in dense hanging clusters. Raw berries contain trace amounts of cyanogenic glycosides (sambunigrin) and should not be consumed uncooked.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to most of Europe (absent only from the far north), across North Africa, and into western and central Asia. Naturalized in [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], Australasia, and parts of South America. Grows in hedgerows, woodland edges, waste ground, and disturbed soils. Prefers moist, nitrogen-rich soils in partial shade to full sun. Hardiness zones 4–8.

## Cultivation

Elderberry is remarkably adaptable and low-maintenance:

- **Soil:** Tolerates a wide range including heavy clay, but prefers moist, fertile, well-drained loam. pH 5.5–7.5.
- **Light:** Full sun to partial shade. [[query-how-do-i-prune-brambles-for-maximum-fruit-production]] production in full sun.
- **Propagation:** Softwood cuttings in summer (ease of rooting is exceptional — cuttings pushed into the ground often succeed without hormone), hardwood cuttings in winter, or seed (requires cold stratification). Division of established clumps.
- **Pruning:** Fruit is borne on the previous year's growth. Remove dead and weak canes at ground level in early spring; retain vigorous 1- and 2-year-old canes for maximum yield. A "renewal pruning" approach removing oldest third of canes annually keeps plants productive.
- **Yield:** Established bushes yield 5–15 kg of fruit annually.
- **Pests/diseases:** Relatively pest-free. Elder shoot borer (Aphis fabae), elderberry leafroller, and [[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]] can affect plants. Birds are the primary fruit pest — netting is often necessary.

## Permaculture Applications

Elderberry excels in permaculture systems as a multi-functional pioneer and guild member:

- **Wildlife habitat:** Flowers provide nectar for over 50 insect species; berries feed birds (especially blackcaps, thrushes, and waxwings); dense growth shelters nesting birds and small mammals.
- **Soil improvement:** Leaf mulch is rich in potassium and decomposes rapidly. The root system helps stabilize riparian banks and disturbed slopes.
- **[[comparison-companion-planting-reference-vs-jadam-monoculture-critique]]:** Elderberry leaves buried in soil or used as mulch are said to deter rodent pests. The shrub's canopy provides dappled shade for understory herbs and berry shrubs.
- **Pioneer species:** Quick to colonize disturbed ground, elder fixes edges and creates microclimate for succession species.
- **Fodder:** Foliage is palatable to livestock (goats and cattle), though consumption of stems and roots should be avoided.
- **Hedgerow component:** Traditional European hedgerow staple providing both fruit and windbreak function.

## Medicinal Properties

### Active Compounds

- **Anthocyanins:** Cyanidin-3-glucoside and cyanidin-3-sambubioside are the primary pigments, contributing to deep purple color and significant antioxidant capacity.
- **Flavonoids:** Quercetin, rutin, kaempferol, and isorhamnetin. Quercetin content is among the highest of any temperate fruit.
- **Polyphenols:** Chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, ferulic acid.
- **Lectins:** Type 2 ribosome-inactivating proteins (nigrin b) present in seeds — destroyed by cooking.
- **Vitamins:** High in vitamin C (36 mg/100g fresh berries), vitamin A, and vitamin B6.

### Antiviral Activity

Elderberry extract has demonstrated antiviral activity against influenza A and B viruses, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and some herpesviruses. The mechanism involves inhibition of viral replication and entry into host cells, and stimulation of cytokine production. A 2019 meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found elderberry supplementation substantially reduced upper respiratory symptoms.

### Other Pharmacological Effects

- **Anti-inflammatory:** Inhibits cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and nitric oxide production in vitro.
- **Antioxidant:** ORAC values among the highest of any berry (ORAC 14,697 μmol TE/100g fresh weight).
- **[[cancer-immune-modulation-mechanisms]]:** Enhances cytokine release from monocytes and activates the innate immune response.
- **Cardiometabolic:** Animal studies suggest anti-diabetic and lipid-lowering effects via activation of AMPK pathway.

### Traditional Uses

- **Flower tea (tisane):** Diaphoretic for fevers, treatment of colds and influenza. European traditional remedy for "breaking a fever."
- **Berry syrup:** Winter tonic and immune prophylactic. Historically used for rheumatism, neuralgia, and sciatica.
- **Flower fritters:** Culinary use of elderflower clusters dipped in batter and fried.
- **External applications:** Infused oil from flowers for sunburn and skin inflammation. Berry dye as fabric colorant.
- **Folklore:** Widely regarded as protective against evil spirits in European tradition. "Elder mother" (Hyldemoer) was believed to inhabit the tree in Scandinavian folklore.

## Culinary Uses

- **Elderflower cordial:** Popular British soft drink concentrate made by macerating flower heads in sugar syrup with lemon zest.
- **Elderflower champagne:** Fermented sparkling drink from flower infusion.
- **Elderberry jelly and preserves:** Traditional fruit preserve, often combined with apple or blackberry.
- **Elderberry wine:** Fermented from ripe berries; historically common in rural Europe.
- **Elderberry vinegar:** Berry-infused vinegar as a tonic and culinary ingredient.

## Cautions

- **Raw berries:** Contain sambunigrin, a cyanogenic glycoside that can cause nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. Cooking (even brief heating) neutralizes this compound.
- **Stems, bark, leaves, and roots:** Contain higher concentrations of cyanogenic glycosides and lectins. Internal use of these parts should be avoided.
- **Pregnancy/lactation:** Insufficient safety data; caution advised for medicinal doses.
- **[[cannabis-autoimmune-conditions-detailed]]:** Immune-stimulating properties may theoretically exacerbate autoimmune disorders.
- **Drug interactions:** Possible interaction with immunosuppressants and diuretics.
