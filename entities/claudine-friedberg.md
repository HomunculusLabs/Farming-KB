## Claudine Friedberg

## Description

Claudine Friedberg is a French ethnobotanist and researcher cited in the reference literature of Harner's *Hallucinogens and Shamanism* for her foundational work on *Banisteriopsis* species used as drugs in South America. Her 1965 publication in the *Journal d'Agriculture Tropicale et de Botanique Appliquée* documented the diverse use of various *Banisteriopsis* species across South American indigenous and folk traditions, contributing significantly to the early botanical understanding of ayahuasca and its component plants.

## Key Facts

- Published a significant 1965 paper on *Banisteriopsis* species used as drugs in South America, one of the earliest systematic botanical surveys of these species
- Her work appeared in the *Journal d'Agriculture Tropicale et de Botanique Appliquée* (volume 12, pages 9-12), published in Paris
- Contributed to the botanical documentation of plants used in ayahuasca preparation across different South American cultures
- Her research helped establish the geographic range and diversity of *Banisteriopsis* use across the Amazon basin and beyond
- Cited alongside Der Marderosian, Pinkley, and Dobbins (1968) as a foundational reference on *Banisteriopsis* botany and chemistry
- Her work documented that multiple *Banisteriopsis* species — not just *B. caapi* — were used as hallucinogenic drugs across different cultural contexts
- Contributed to the taxonomic clarity needed for accurate identification of ayahuasca component plants
- Part of a generation of French ethnobotanists who documented the plant knowledge of indigenous Amazonian peoples

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

While Friedberg's primary contribution is to the understanding of *Banisteriopsis* (the vine component of ayahuasca) rather than mushrooms directly, her work is part of the broader ethnobotanical literature that documents the traditional use of psychoactive plants in indigenous South American cultures. The systematic botanical documentation of *Banisteriopsis* species — including *B. caapi*, *B. inebrians*, and *B. rusbyana* — is essential for understanding the pharmacology of ayahuasca and the beta-carboline alkaloids (harmine, harmaline, tetrahydroharmine) that make oral DMT active by inhibiting monoamine oxidase. Her taxonomic work contributes to the foundation that enables accurate identification and cultivation of these species.

## Connection to Ayahuasca Research

Friedberg's research was published in the same period as other foundational ayahuasca studies by Dobkin de Rios (1969, 1972), Der Marderosian et al. (1968), and Friedberg's French colleague whose work on *Banisteriopsis* taxonomy complemented chemical studies of beta-carboline alkaloids. Together, these researchers established the botany and chemistry of ayahuasca that made subsequent pharmacological research possible. Her work on species-level identification of *Banisteriopsis* used in different regions helped explain why ayahuasca preparations vary in potency and subjective effects.

## Importance of Taxonomic Clarity

The accurate identification of Banisteriopsis species is critical for both ethnobotanical research and practical preparation of ayahuasca. Different species contain different concentrations and ratios of beta-carboline alkaloids (harmine, harmaline, and tetrahydroharmine), which directly affect the potency and character of the ayahuasca brew. Friedberg's contribution to the taxonomic literature helped establish which species were actually being used by different cultural groups, correcting misidentifications and clarifying the geographic distribution of each species.

## French Ethnobotanical Tradition

Friedberg's work is part of a distinguished French tradition of ethnobotanical research in the tropics, including the work of Claude Levi-Strauss on indigenous classification systems and the broader French school of structural anthropology that took indigenous plant knowledge seriously as a subject of scientific inquiry. French researchers were often more willing than their Anglophone counterparts to engage with the subjective and experiential dimensions of plant use, producing ethnographically richer accounts.

## Legacy

Friedberg's 1965 paper remains a frequently cited reference in the ayahuasca literature, and her species-level documentation continues to inform contemporary ethnobotanical surveys and conservation efforts. As interest in ayahuasca cultivation and use has expanded globally, the foundational taxonomic work of researchers like Friedberg has become increasingly important for ensuring accurate plant identification and sustainable harvesting practices.

## Broader Ethnobotanical Context

Friedberg's work on Banisteriopsis is part of a larger ethnobotanical research program documenting the plant knowledge of indigenous South American peoples. Her research demonstrated that traditional communities possessed detailed botanical knowledge including species identification, preparation methods, dosage guidelines, and therapeutic applications for psychoactive plants. This knowledge, developed through centuries of empirical observation and use, represents a valuable scientific resource that complements and sometimes anticipates Western pharmacological discoveries.
