## Overview

Andreaea rupestris, commonly known as rock moss or granite moss, is a species of moss in the family Andreaeaceae.

It is one of the most widespread species within its genus, occurring across boreal, montane, and arctic regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

The species is characteristically found growing on exposed acidic rock surfaces, particularly granite and quartzite outcrops.

Andreaea rupestris belongs to the class Andreaeopsida, an early-diverging group of mosses that lack a peristome and possess unique spore capsule dehiscence mechanisms.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Andreaea rupestris was described by the Swedish botanist Johan Hedwig in 1801.

The genus Andreaea is the sole genus in the family Andreaeaceae and the order Andreaeales, representing one of the most ancient lineages of mosses.

The gametophyte forms dense, dark brown to nearly black cushions or tufts tightly appressed to the substrate.

Stems are typically 0.5 to 2.0 centimeters tall, erect, and irregularly branched.

Leaves are small, lanceolate to ovate-lanceolate, 1.0 to 1.8 millimeters long, with recurved margins and a strong midrib that extends to or near the leaf apex.

The leaf cells are thick-walled, rectangular to elongated, with prominent papillae in some forms.

A distinctive feature of Andreaeaceae is the absence of a columella in the sporophyte, unlike most other moss groups.

The spore capsule is spherical to ovoid, borne on a short seta, and dehisces irregularly through longitudinal slits rather than an operculum.

Spores are relatively large, ranging from 12 to 22 micrometers in diameter, and are produced in moderate quantities per capsule.

## Distribution and Habitat

Andreaea rupestris has a circumpolar distribution in the Northern Hemisphere.

It occurs across northern Europe, Scandinavia, the British Isles, Iceland, and extends eastward through Siberia.

In North America, the species ranges from Greenland and the Canadian Arctic southward through the Rocky Mountains and Appalachian Mountains.

The species is also reported from mountainous regions of central and southern Europe, including the Alps, Carpathians, and Pyrenees.

Its preferred habitat is exposed, acidic rock surfaces, particularly granite, gneiss, and quartzite.

It is commonly found at elevations between 500 and 2500 meters, though it can occur from sea level in arctic regions to alpine zones.

The moss tolerates extreme desiccation and temperature fluctuations, making it well-suited to exposed rock surfaces.

It typically grows in dry, well-drained microsites where competition from vascular plants is minimal.

## Ecology

Andreaea rupestris plays a significant role in the primary colonization of bare rock surfaces.

It contributes to the initial stages of soil formation through the slow accumulation of organic matter from dead gametophyte tissue.

The species is an important component of saxicolous (rock-dwelling) moss communities in boreal and alpine ecosystems.

It frequently grows alongside other acidophilous mosses, particularly species of Racomitrium and Grimmia.

In some arctic and alpine communities, Andreaea rupestris can form extensive mats covering large areas of exposed bedrock.

The dense, dark cushions provide microhabitat for various microarthropods, including mites and springtails.

Water retention within the moss cushion creates favorable conditions for microbial activity on otherwise dry rock surfaces.

The species is relatively slow-growing, with annual growth rates estimated at 1 to 3 millimeters per year under optimal conditions.

## Reproduction

Andreaea rupestris reproduces both sexually and asexually.

Sexual reproduction is dioicous, with male and female reproductive structures borne on separate plants.

Archegonia are borne on short lateral branches, while antheridia occur on separate male plants.

The sporophyte consists of a short seta and a spherical capsule that lacks both an operculum and a peristome.

Instead of opening through an operculum like most mosses, the capsule dehisces by splitting along four to eight longitudinal valves.

This unique dehiscence mechanism is a diagnostic feature of the Andreaeaceae and Andreaeobryaceae.

Spores are dispersed by wind and are released gradually as the capsule valves split over time.

Asexual reproduction occurs through fragmentation of gametophyte tissue and the production of specialized brood bodies in some populations.

## Conservation

Andreaea rupestris is not currently considered threatened on a global scale.

Its circumpolar distribution and broad ecological amplitude provide a degree of resilience to localized disturbances.

However, the species may be sensitive to acid rain deposition in some European mountain ranges.

Air pollution and atmospheric nitrogen deposition can alter the chemistry of rock surfaces, potentially affecting colonization success.

Climate change poses a long-term threat to alpine populations, as warming temperatures may allow vascular plant competitors to encroach on exposed rock habitats.

In several European countries, the species is listed as having stable populations and is considered of least concern.

The species is protected in some nature reserves and national parks where its saxicolous habitat is preserved.
