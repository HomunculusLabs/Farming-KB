## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Order Boraginales → Family Boraginaceae → Genus *Echium* → Species *E. candicans*. The species was described by Carl Linnaeus filius in 1782.

The genus *Echium* contains approximately 40–60 species distributed across Europe, North Africa, and Macaronesia. Many Macaronesian species evolved woody habits in island habitats free from large herbivores.

*Echium candicans* is closely related to *Echium nervosum* and *Echium giganteum*, other Canary Island and Madeiran endemics. The Macaronesian echiums represent an adaptive radiation from herbaceous continental ancestors.

The plant develops a woody base with multiple branching stems forming a rounded shrub 1–1.5 meters wide. All aerial parts are covered with dense white or grayish appressed hairs giving a felted appearance.

Leaves are lanceolate to oblanceolate, 10–25 cm long, with a prominent midrib and entire margins. The basal rosette of silvery leaves persists year-round in frost-free climates.

Inflorescences are dense, cylindrical panicles 30–60 cm long, held on stout erect peduncles. Individual flowers are funnel-shaped, 8–12 mm long, with five stamens that extend beyond the corolla tube.

Flower color ranges from deep blue-violet to lighter shades depending on growing conditions and genetic variation. The stamens bear pinkish-red anthers that contrast with the blue petals.

The fruit is a four-lobed schizocarp containing four small nutlets (mericarps). Seeds germinate readily without pretreatment when sown in warm conditions.

The root system is deep and taprooted, allowing the plant to access moisture at depth during summer drought. This makes transplanting established plants very difficult without careful root ball preservation.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Echium candicans* is endemic to the island of Madeira, an autonomous region of Portugal in the North Atlantic Ocean. It occurs primarily in the northern and central mountainous regions of the island.

The species grows on rocky cliffs, scree slopes, and dry stone walls at elevations of 200–1,400 meters. It colonizes disturbed sites and road cuttings where competition from woody vegetation is minimal.

The native climate is Mediterranean-oceanic, with mild wet winters and warm dry summers. Annual precipitation ranges from 600–1,200 mm, falling predominantly between October and March.

Soils in native habitats are volcanic in origin, well-drained, and slightly acidic to neutral. The species tolerates poor soils but requires excellent drainage to prevent root rot.

Wind exposure is common in native cliff habitats, and the sturdy stems are well-adapted to withstand salt-laden Atlantic gales. This wind tolerance makes the species reliable in coastal garden settings.

Naturalized populations occur in coastal California from the San Francisco Bay Area southward. Smaller naturalized stands exist in parts of southwestern Australia, New Zealand, and southern France.

In its native Madeira, the species is protected and not considered threatened. Tourism development and road construction have impacted some cliff-face populations, but the overall range remains stable.
