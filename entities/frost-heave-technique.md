# Frost Heave

## Description

Frost heave is a cold-climate soil phenomenon where freezing and thawing cycles cause soil to expand upward, disrupting the ground surface. In [[permaculture]] contexts, Mollison identifies frost heave as both a challenge to manage (particularly for bare or eroded soils and infrastructure) and a natural opportunity for pasture establishment — the disturbed surface left after thaw creates ideal seedbed conditions for clover and other covering species. Frost heave only occurs on bare soils, which are paradoxically the soils most in need of vegetative cover to prevent erosion.

## Classification

- **Category**: Natural process / soil phenomenon
- **Type**: Cryogenic soil disturbance
- **Cause**: Freeze-thaw cycling expanding soil volume through ice crystal formation
- **Occurrence**: Only on bare, partially eroded, or exposed soils

## Key Facts

- Frost heave occurs exclusively on bare, part-eroded, or exposed soils — vegetated soils are insulated and protected
- The freeze-thaw cycle creates ideal seedbed conditions during the thaw period
- Particularly beneficial for establishing clover and other soil-covering species on degraded land
- Creates a natural seedbed preparation that requires only seed to be spread to establish cover
- In Iceland, frost penetration to 1.2 m in pastures is common, requiring open ditches at 50 m spacing for spring thaw runoff
- Tile drains in cold areas remain frozen well into spring, making open drainage essential
- Frost heave on bare eroded soils needs levelling, re-sodding with peat, or rest from grazing
- Mollison recommends spreading manures in late winter on frosted and late snow areas — meltdown deposits seed and manure for spring growth
- Frost heave is a key limiting factor in cold-climate agriculture and must be accounted for in infrastructure design
- Pipes must be buried below frozen soil levels (typically 1 metre or deeper) to prevent bursting from frost heave

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Frost heave has several connections to fungal cultivation and soil biology:

1. **Natural substrate disturbance**: Frost heave creates disturbed soil surfaces that are ideal not only for seed establishment but also for colonization by pioneer fungal species
2. **Mycelium disruption**: Severe frost heave can break established [[mycorrhizal-networks]], reducing fungal connectivity in pasture systems
3. **Spring fruiting trigger**: The thaw period following frost heave coincides with spring moisture availability, potentially triggering fruiting of cold-adapted mushroom species
4. **Seedbed for mycorrhizal plants**: By creating ideal seedbeds for clover and other plants, frost heave indirectly establishes hosts for mycorrhizal fungal partnerships
5. **Peat protection**: Re-sodding eroded frost-heaved areas with peat protects underlying soil biology including fungal communities
6. **Infrastructure protection**: [[mushroom-cultivation]] beds and structures in cold climates must account for frost heave to avoid structural damage

## Management Strategies

- On eroded areas: level soil, re-sod with peat, or rest from grazing to allow recovery
- Spread manure in late winter on frosted areas so spring meltdown deposits nutrients for spring growth
- Use sawdust mixed with manure for visibility of spread on snow
- Open ditches at 50 m spacing for spring runoff in areas with deep frost penetration
- Bury all pipes below frost line (1 metre+) to prevent frost heave damage
- Maintain vegetative cover to prevent frost heave occurrence — bare soils are the problem
- Use mixed-livestock economy and rotational grazing to maintain pasture health and reduce bare patches

## Cold Climate Design Implications

- Water storage: Place within building structures below floor level to avoid frost
- Ponds: Account for ice expansion — leave unfilled, slope-sided, or open-topped
- Structures: Insulate foundations and avoid placing on frost-susceptible soils
- Pastures: Prioritize maintaining continuous vegetative cover to prevent frost heave initiation
