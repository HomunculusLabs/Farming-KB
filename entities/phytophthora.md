## Description

Phytophthora is a genus of plant-damaging oomycetes (water molds) responsible
for some of the most devastating blights and mildews in agriculture and
horticulture. The name literally means "plant destroyer" in Greek. Unlike true
fungi, Phytophthora species are oomycetes belonging to the class Oomycota,
though they share many ecological similarities with fungi and are commonly
studied alongside them in plant pathology.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Eukaryota
- **Kingdom**: Chromista (Stramenopiles)
- **Phylum**: Oomycota
- **Class**: Oomycetes
- **Order**: Peronosporales
- **Family**: Peronosporaceae
- **Genus**: Phytophthora

Notable species include [[bloomfield-potato-blight-phytophthora-infestans]] (cause of potato [[late-blight]]
and the Irish Potato Famine), Phytophthora ramorum (sudden oak death),
Phytophthora capsici (affects peppers and cucurbits), and Phytophthora
cinnamomi (affects thousands of plant species worldwide).

## Key Facts

- Phytophthora species cause blights, mildews, and root rots across a wide
  range of host plants including vegetables, fruits, ornamentals, and trees
- They thrive in moist, poorly drained soils and spread rapidly through water
  movement and splashing rain
- The genus is responsible for billions of dollars in crop losses worldwide
  annually across dozens of crop species
- Phytophthora infestans triggered the Great Irish Famine (1845-1852) by
  devastating potato crops across Europe
- They produce motile zoospores that swim through water films in soil to
  locate and infect host roots and foliage
- Unlike many true fungi, Phytophthora species are often favored by anaerobic
  or low-oxygen soil conditions where beneficial competitors are absent
- The genus contains over 200 described species, each with different host
  ranges and environmental preferences

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

In the context of compost tea and [[soil-food-web]] management, [[elaine-ingham]]
identifies Phytophthora as a key pathogen that can be suppressed through
aerobic [[soil-conditioning-chisel-plow-biological-methods]]. When beneficial fungi, protozoa, and nematodes
are present in healthy aerobic soil, they outcompete Phytophthora for
resources and infection sites on plant surfaces.

However, if soil becomes anaerobic with oxygen dropping below 5.5-6 mg per
liter, the beneficial aerobic organisms die back, creating conditions where
Phytophthora and similar pathogens can proliferate unchecked. Ingham
specifically notes that losing beneficial organisms through anaerobic
conditions causes a slow decline in soil health as organisms that combat
Phytophthora (causing blights and mildews), Pythium (wilts), and Rhizoctonia
(root rots) are killed off.

Maintaining aerobic conditions in compost tea (above 5.5-6 mg oxygen per
liter) is therefore essential for producing teas that can suppress
Phytophthora diseases on both foliar and root surfaces. The relationship
between Phytophthora and soil biology illustrates the core principle of the
soil food web approach: [[ingham-aact-disease-suppression-through-food-competition]] competitive exclusion
rather than chemical intervention.

When the full food web is restored with bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and
nematodes all present, Phytophthora populations are naturally managed through
[[gadd-mycelial-resource-competition-and-combat-fungi]] occupation of potential infection sites. This
represents a fundamentally different approach from chemical fungicide
applications, which kill both pathogens and beneficial organisms, often
making the problem worse over time.

For [[enterprise-development-mushroom-growers]] specifically, understanding Phytophthora management
through biological means is relevant because both [[emcdda-hallucinogenic-mushroom-cultivation-and-growkit-market]]
Phytophthora suppression depend on maintaining proper environmental conditions.
Just as mushroom cultivation requires careful management of humidity, substrate
moisture, and air exchange, managing Phytophthora through compost tea requires
maintaining aerobic conditions, proper fungal biomass (minimum 2 micrograms per
the SFI standard), and adequate organism diversity to ensure complete disease
suppression across all plant surfaces.
