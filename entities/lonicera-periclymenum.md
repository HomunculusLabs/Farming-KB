# Lonicera periclymenum

## Overview

**Lonicera periclymenum**, commonly known as common honeysuckle or woodbine, is a deciduous climbing vine in the family Caprifoliaceae. It is native to much of Europe, parts of North Africa, and western Asia.

The species is widely cultivated for its intensely fragrant tubular flowers, which bloom from late spring through autumn. It serves as an important nectar source for moths, bees, and other pollinators in both garden and natural settings.

Plants can reach 4–8 meters in height when supported by structures or neighboring vegetation. The twining stems wrap clockwise around supports, producing dense foliage that provides excellent screening and shelter for wildlife.

The common name "woodbine" has been used in English literature for centuries, appearing in the works of Shakespeare and other Elizabethan writers. The species epithet "periclymenum" derives from the Greek for "twining around."

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Family:** Caprifoliaceae
- **Genus:** Lonicera
- **Species:** Lonicera periclymenum L.
- **Synonyms:** Periclymenum vulgare (L.) Hill

Leaves are opposite, oval to elliptic, 4–10 cm long, and dark green above with a paler underside. The uppermost leaf pairs beneath flower clusters often fuse into a distinctive disk-like structure surrounding the inflorescence.

Flowers are tubular, 4–6 cm long, creamy white to yellowish on the outside, and flushed pink or purplish-red on the inside. They are arranged in whorled terminal heads and emit a strong sweet fragrance, especially in evening hours when moth pollinators are active.

Berries are bright red, ovoid, 6–8 mm in diameter, and mildly toxic to humans if ingested. They ripen from late summer into autumn and are readily consumed by birds, which disperse seeds widely through hedgerows and woodlands.

The stems are woody at the base, reaching 2–3 cm diameter on mature plants. Young growth is green and slightly pubescent, becoming smooth and grey-brown with age as the bark develops shallow longitudinal fissures.

The root system is fibrous and spreading, with a preference for moist, humus-rich upper soil layers. Established plants develop a robust root crown that supports vigorous annual regrowth even after hard pruning.

Two subspecies are recognized: subsp. periclymenum (widespread) and subsp. hispanica (confined to Iberia and North Africa). The latter has broader leaves and more compact flower clusters adapted to warmer, drier conditions.

## Distribution and Habitat

Lonicera periclymenum occurs naturally across the British Isles, continental Europe from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean, and eastward to the Caucasus and Turkey. It is one of the most widespread native honeysuckle species in Europe.

The species favors hedgerows, woodland edges, scrub, and open forests where light is filtered through a partial canopy. It frequently scrambles through shrubby vegetation, using other plants as natural trellises to reach better light conditions.

It thrives in partial shade to full sun on a range of soil types including loam, chalk, and sandy soils. It prefers calcareous to moderately acidic, well-drained conditions but tolerates clay if not persistently waterlogged.

Several cultivated varieties have naturalized beyond their native range in [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] worldwide, particularly in [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]] and New Zealand. In the British Isles, it is a quintessential hedgerow plant deeply embedded in rural culture and folklore.

Unlike its relative [[lonicera-japonica]], Lonicera periclymenum is not considered invasive in [[berry-growing]], though it can be vigorous in favorable sites.

## Cultivation

Common honeysuckle is hardy in USDA zones 5–9 and grows vigorously once established. It prefers a cool root zone with its top growth in sun or partial shade, mimicking its natural woodland-edge habitat.

Plant against fences, trellises, or arbors in moist but well-drained soil enriched with [[fukuoka-textdoc-composting-critique-futility-prepared-organic-matter]]. Apply a thick mulch around the base in spring to keep roots cool and conserve moisture during dry summer periods.

Prune after flowering by removing one-third of the oldest stems at ground level to encourage fresh basal shoots. This renovation pruning prevents the plant from becoming a tangled mass of bare, leafless stems at the base.

Propagate from semi-hardwood cuttings taken in midsummer, or from seed stratified over winter at 1–5°C for 3–4 months. Cultivars are propagated vegetatively to preserve flower color and fragrance traits faithfully.

Notable cultivars include 'Graham Thomas' (pale yellow flowers, prolonged bloom), 'Serotina' (late-flowering, deep purple buds, exceptional fragrance), and 'Belgica' (early Dutch honeysuckle with pink-tinged blooms).

The species has earned the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit, confirming its reliability and gardenworthiness in UK conditions.

## Ecological Role

The tubular flowers are adapted for pollination by long-tongued moths, particularly hawkmoths (Sphingidae). Bumblebees also visit flowers during daylight hours, sometimes chewing through the corolla base to rob nectar directly.

Berries provide an important autumn food source for thrushes, blackcaps, and other migrating songbirds preparing for winter. Dense foliage offers nesting sites and shelter for small birds and invertebrates throughout the [[extending-growing-season]].

Lonicera periclymenum supports a range of specialist insects, including the honeysuckle aphid (Hyadaphis foeniculi) and several Lepidoptera whose larvae feed on the foliage. The broad leaves are particularly important for early-season moth caterpillars.

In mixed hedgerows, it contributes to structural diversity by adding a climbing layer above shrubs. This multi-tiered structure supports significantly greater biodiversity than single-layer hedgerows of equivalent length.

Deer and rabbits occasionally browse young shoots, though established plants generally recover quickly from browsing pressure.
