## Description

The antibiotic agar sandwich is a laboratory technique for purifying mushroom [[mycelium]]
cultures that have been contaminated with bacteria. The method exploits the ability of
[[fungal-hyphae]] to grow through antibiotic-laced agar while the accompanying bacteria cannot
survive the passage through the treated medium.

The contaminated culture is placed on one side of an antibiotic-supplemented agar plate. A
large wedge of the same antibiotic agar is then cut and flipped over on top of the
contaminated culture, sandwiching the mycelium between two agar layers. The mycelium must
drill its way through the agar to reach the surface, and during this passage, the bacteria
are unable to follow because they cannot survive the antibiotic-laced medium.

This technique is particularly relevant when dealing with hitchhiking bacteria. In natural
soil and wood ecosystems, fungi purposefully cultivate certain bacteria on their hyphae to
help metabolize waste products, functioning much like a biological septic system. These
symbiotic bacteria are difficult to remove from wild isolates without specialized
techniques like the antibiotic agar sandwich.

## Key Facts

The antibiotic agar formula was recommended by Dr. Bob Blanchette from the University of
Minnesota for screening cultures through one or two transfers before switching them back
to antibiotic-free medium.

Base ingredients combined and autoclaved for 20 minutes: 1,000 ml distilled or deionized
water, 15 grams malt extract, 15 grams agar, 2 grams yeast extract, and 0.06 gram Benlate
(50 percent wettable powder, a fungicide). After autoclaving, cool to 113 to 122 degrees F
(45 to 50 degrees C), then add 0.01 gram [[streptomycin-sulfate]] and 2 ml lactic acid.

The antibiotics and lactic acid must be added after autoclaving because heat destroys
streptomycin sulfate. The cooled agar temperature prevents thermal degradation while
remaining liquid enough for plate pouring.

Streptomycin sulfate is a broad-spectrum antibiotic effective against many gram-negative
and gram-positive bacteria. Benlate at the very low concentration used suppresses mold
contaminants without harming the target mushroom mycelium. Lactic acid adjusts the pH to
favor fungal growth over bacterial growth. Yeast extract provides B-vitamins and growth
factors supporting mycelial recovery.

## Procedure

Pour antibiotic-supplemented agar plates using the formula above. Place the bacteria-
contaminated mycelium on one side of the plate. Cut a large wedge of antibiotic agar and
flip it over on top of the contaminated culture, sandwiching it between two layers.

The mycelium will drill through the agar over several days to emerge on the outside.
Bacteria cannot accompany the mycelium through the antibiotic-laced agar and are left
behind. Purified mycelium will appear as fresh fuzzy growth on the outside of the agar
layers. Transfer this purified mycelium immediately to a fresh antibiotic-free plate.

The technique is recommended for one or two transfer cycles only before switching back to
antibiotic-free medium, as extended use of antibiotics may stress the mycelium. The
technique specifically targets bacterial contamination and does not address mold
contamination, which requires still-air transfer rescue instead.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Wild-collected fungal specimens frequently harbor bacteria that contaminate tissue
cultures and spore germination plates. The antibiotic agar sandwich provides a reliable
method for cleaning these cultures without expensive equipment beyond standard laboratory
supplies.

Bacteria-contaminated plates can be safely opened in the lab, unlike mold-contaminated
plates which must never be opened near a [[laminar-flow-hood]]. For dual contamination with
both mold and bacteria, address the mold first via still-air rescue in a separate
workspace, then clean the bacteria using the sandwich technique.
