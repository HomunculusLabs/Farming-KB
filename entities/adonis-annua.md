## Overview

Adonis annua, commonly known as pheasant's eye or autumn adonis, is an annual herbaceous plant in the family Ranunculaceae. It is native to the Mediterranean region and western Asia but has spread widely across temperate Europe as an agricultural weed.

The species produces solitary, bright scarlet flowers with a distinctive dark center, each 2–3 cm in diameter. It is one of the few red-flowered members of Ranunculaceae, making it easily recognizable in the field.

Adonis annua contains potent cardiac glycosides of the cardenolide type, including adonitoxin and cymarin. These compounds have been investigated for potential cardiotonic applications similar to digitalis.

The plant was once a common cornfield weed across southern England and continental Europe. Modern [[mycorrhizal-agricultural-practices]] and herbicide use have caused significant population declines throughout its range.

The genus is named after Adonis of Greek mythology, the beautiful youth killed by a wild boar. Legend states that red anemones (or adonis flowers) sprang from his blood.

In France the species is known as "goutte-de-sang" (drop of blood), referencing both its color and mythological origin. German common names include "Blutstropfen" (blood drop) and "Herbst-Adonisröschen" (autumn adonis rose).

The distinctive red coloration is produced by anthocyanin pigments in the sepals. This pigment class is unusual in Ranunculaceae, where most species display yellow or white flowers.

## Taxonomy

Adonis annua was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The genus Adonis comprises approximately 30 species of annual and perennial herbs distributed across Eurasia and North Africa.

The specific epithet "annua" means "annual," distinguishing this species from the perennial [[adonis-vernalis]]. The plant belongs to the tribe Adonideae within the subfamily Ranunculoideae.

Chromosome number is 2n = 16. Molecular phylogenetic studies confirm placement of Adonis as sister to the genus Callianthemum within Ranunculaceae.

Key synonyms include Adonis automnalis and Adonis flammea. The species is sometimes treated as Adonis annua subsp. automnalis in eastern European floras.

Several related species with red flowers occur in the Mediterranean region. Adonis aestivalis (summer pheasant's eye) flowers earlier and has a less conspicuously darkened flower center.

## Morphology

Adonis annua is a slender annual reaching 15–40 cm in height. The plant has finely divided, feathery leaves arranged alternately along wiry, often branching stems.

Leaves are sessile, two to three times pinnately divided into narrow linear segments. The overall appearance is delicate and feathery, similar to some species of fennel.

Flowers are solitary, terminal, and lack true petals. The 5–8 scarlet sepals serve as the showy floral display, surrounding a dark central mass of numerous stamens and carpels.

Each flower is 2–3 cm in diameter and faces upward on a slender pedicel. The dark anthers create a distinctive "black eye" effect in the center of the red flower.

Fruiting heads are ovoid, composed of numerous small achenes with short beaks. Each achene is roughly 3–4 mm long with a wrinkled surface texture.

The root system consists of a thin taproot with minimal branching. As an annual, the entire root system is replaced each generation from seed.

Adonis annua is a winter annual in the Mediterranean, germinating in autumn and flowering the following spring. In cultivation at higher latitudes it is treated as a spring-sown annual.

The life cycle from germination to seed production is approximately four to five months. Individual plants produce 50–200 seeds, which persist in the soil seed bank for up to ten years.

## Distribution and Habitat

Adonis annua is native to the Mediterranean basin, from Portugal and Morocco eastward to Turkey, Syria, and Iran. It has been introduced to central and northern Europe, southern Scandinavia, and parts [[berry-growing]].

Historically, it was a characteristic component of arable weed communities in cereal fields across southern England. The species was well-known to Victorian botanists as a cornfield specialty.

It grows in cultivated fields, disturbed ground, roadsides, and waste places. The plant prefers calcareous soils and open, sunny positions.

Populations have declined dramatically since the mid-twentieth century due to improved seed cleaning and herbicide application. It is now rare or endangered in most northern European countries.

Conservation status varies by region but is generally assessed as threatened in central and northern Europe. The species benefits from conservation headlands and wildflower margin schemes in agricultural landscapes.

In the United Kingdom, Adonis annua is classified as a Priority Species under the UK Biodiversity Action Plan. Active reintroduction projects are underway at several nature reserves in southern England.

The species is listed as endangered in the German Red List and critically endangered in Switzerland. Seed from remaining populations is being conserved in European gene banks for potential future restoration efforts.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

Adonis annua contains a complex mixture of cardenolide glycosides, primarily adonitoxin, cymarin, and strophanthidin. These compounds belong to the same chemical class as the cardiac glycosides found in Digitalis and Strophanthus.

The cardenolides inhibit Na+/K+-ATPase in cardiac muscle cells, producing positive inotropic effects similar to digitalis. Research has explored their potential as alternatives to digoxin in heart failure management.

Traditional Chinese medicine uses related species ([[adonis-amurensis]] and Adonis vernalis) for cardiac conditions. Preparations called "adonis herb" were included in several European pharmacopoeias historically.

The plant also contains flavonoids, saponins, and small amounts of alkaloids. The flavonoid profile includes orientin and vitexin, compounds with documented antioxidant activity.
