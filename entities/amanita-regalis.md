# Amanita regalis

**Amanita regalis** (also known as the royal fly agaric or brown fly agaric) is a species
of amanita mushroom known to contain [[amanita-muscaria-ibotenic-acid-fungi]] and [[muscimol-and-amanita-muscaria]], placing it in
the second group of neurotropic fungi alongside [[amanita-muscaria]] and [[amanita-pantherina]].
It is one of the few Amanita species recognized for its psychoactive properties. The species
was first described by the Swedish mycologist Elias Magnus Fries. The epithet "regalis" means
"royal," reflecting its imposing and distinguished appearance among the Amanita species.

## Taxonomy and Classification

*Amanita regalis* belongs to section Amanita (formerly Stirps Muscaria) of the genus
Amanita, which includes the well-known fly agaric complex. Some mycologists have at times
considered it a variety or subspecies of [[amanita-muscaria]] (as *A. muscaria* var. *umbrina*
or *A. muscaria* var. *regalis*), but modern molecular phylogenetic studies generally support
its status as a distinct species. Molecular analyses have confirmed that while *A. regalis* is
closely related to the [[amanita-muscaria]] clade, it represents a separate evolutionary lineage
with consistent morphological and chemical differences.

The genus Amanita is one of the most recognizable and widely studied genera of Agaricales
mushrooms. It contains both deadly poisonous species (such as [[amanita-phalloides]], the death
cap) and psychoactive species (including *A. regalis*, [[amanita-muscaria]], and
[[amanita-pantherina]]). The genus is characterized by white spores, free gills, a volva at the
base of the stipe, and typically a membranous annulus (ring) on the stipe.

## Morphology

### Macroscopic Features

The cap (pileus) is 8 to 20 cm in diameter, initially convex to hemispherical when young,
becoming plano-convex to slightly depressed with age. The cap surface is brown to reddish-brown,
often with a distinctly darker center (umbonate region), and covered with conspicuous white to
pale yellowish warts (remnants of the universal veil). These patches (pyramidal warts) are
typically larger, thicker, and more prominent than those on [[amanita-muscaria]], which is one
of the key distinguishing features between the two species. The cap margin is typically smooth
and non-striate, or only faintly striate in mature specimens. The cuticle is separable from the
flesh.

The gills (lamellae) are white, free (not attached to the stipe), and crowded. They are broad
and may have a slightly floccose edge in young specimens. As the mushroom matures, the gills
may develop a slight cream or yellowish tint.

The stipe is white to pale cream, 8 to 20 cm tall and 1.5 to 3 cm thick, solid when young
but becoming hollow with age. It is cylindrical or slightly clavate (club-shaped) at the base.
A prominent membranous, hanging annulus (ring) is present on the upper stipe. The ring is white
to cream-colored, sometimes with a slight yellowish tinge on the lower surface, and is typically
thick and persistent. The base of the stipe bears a prominent volva consisting of several
concentric rings or zones of tissue (the remains of the universal veil). These concentric rings
are a characteristic feature distinguishing it from some other Amanita species.

The flesh is white and firm when young, becoming softer with age. It does not change color
when cut or bruised (unlike many Amanita species that stain or discolor). The odor is mild
to slightly radish-like, and the taste is not distinctive.

### Microscopic Features

The spore print is white. Basidiospores are broadly ellipsoid to globose, measuring approximately
8 to 10 by 6 to 8 μm, and are inamyloid (not staining with Melzer's reagent). Basidia are
clavate, typically 4-spored. The cap cuticle (pileipellis) consists of a filamentous ixocutis
with abundant gelatinous matrix. Clamp connections are present at the bases of basidia.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Amanita regalis* is found primarily in boreal and temperate regions of Europe and Asia. It is
especially common in Scandinavia (Norway, Sweden, Finland), northern and central Europe
(Germany, Poland, the Baltic states), and parts of Russia and Siberia. It has also been
reported from the Himalayan region (India, Nepal, Bhutan) and from East Asia (Japan, Korea).
Reports from North America are less certain and may represent misidentified specimens of
[[amanita-muscaria]] var. *formosa* or other taxa.

The species forms ectomycorrhizal associations with trees, primarily birch
(Betula spp.) but also with spruce (Picea spp.) and pine (Pinus spp.). It is most
commonly found in boreal and temperate coniferous and mixed forests,
particularly in association with betula pendula (silver birch) and betula pubescens
(downy birch). The mycorrhizal relationship is obligate, meaning the fungus cannot complete its
life cycle without a suitable host tree.

Fruiting occurs in late summer to autumn, typically from August through October, though the
exact timing depends on latitude and local climate conditions. In northern Scandinavia, fruiting
may extend into November. The mushrooms are found solitarily or in small groups on the forest
floor among mosses and leaf litter.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

Like other members of the Amanita section Amanita, *A. regalis* contains [[amanita-muscaria-ibotenic-acid-fungi]]
and its decarboxylation product [[muscimol-and-amanita-muscaria]] as the primary active compounds. These compounds
belong to a class of psychoactive molecules known as isoxazole derivatives. Ibotenic acid acts
as a potent glutamate receptor agonist, specifically at ionotropic
AMPA and kainate receptors, and to a lesser extent at NMDA receptors. Muscimol is a potent and
selective GABA-A receptor agonist, structurally related to GABA itself.

The concentrations of these compounds in *A. regalis* are reported to be comparable to or
slightly lower than those in [[amanita-muscaria]]. Some published analyses indicate that the
dried mushroom contains approximately 0.1 to 0.2% ibotenic acid and 0.02 to 0.1% muscimol
by dry weight. However, concentrations vary significantly depending on the specimen's age,
geographic origin, and environmental conditions.

The conversion of [[amanita-muscaria-ibotenic-acid-fungi]] to [[muscimol-and-amanita-muscaria]] occurs through decarboxylation, which is
accelerated by heat. This is why traditional preparation methods (drying over fire, parboiling)
enhance the ratio of muscimol to ibotenic acid. Muscimol is the principal compound responsible
for the characteristic psychoactive effects of the mushroom, while ibotenic acid is primarily
responsible for the unpleasant toxic effects (nausea, confusion, ataxia).

Additional compounds found in *A. regalis* include [[muscimol-and-amanita-muscaria]] derivatives and trace
amounts of other isoxazole alkaloids. The species does not contain [[psilocybin]] or other
indole alkaloids, placing its pharmacology in a fundamentally different category from the
psilocybian mushrooms.

## Ethnobotanical and Traditional Use

*A. regalis* has been used in some regions of Scandinavia and northern Europe in a manner
similar to [[amanita-muscaria]]. The specific role of *A. regalis* in these traditions is
less well documented, but the species was likely used interchangeably with
[[amanita-muscaria]] where both occur. Traditional preparation methods include air-drying,
drying near a fire, and parboiling in water, which reduce toxicity by converting
[[amanita-muscaria-ibotenic-acid-fungi]] to [[muscimol-and-amanita-muscaria]].

## Toxicity and Effects

Consumption of raw or improperly prepared *A. regalis* causes gastrointestinal distress
(nausea, vomiting, diarrhea), confusion, dizziness, visual distortions, muscle twitching,
and ataxia (loss of coordination). These effects are attributed primarily to [[amanita-muscaria-ibotenic-acid-fungi]],
which is neurotoxic and irritating to mucous membranes. The onset of effects is typically
30 to 90 minutes after ingestion, with peak effects at 2 to 3 hours and a total duration
of 4 to 8 hours.

When properly prepared (dried or parboiled), conversion of ibotenic acid to [[muscimol-and-amanita-muscaria]]
produces a qualitatively different experience: sedative-hypnotic, dissociative, and mildly
hallucinogenic effects. Users frequently report dreamlike states, a sense of heaviness
or floating, auditory distortions, vivid imagery with eyes closed, and altered perception
of body size and distance (micropsia and macropsia, the Alice in Wonderland effect). The
experience is characteristically more sedating and less visual than that of [[psilocybin]]-containing
mushrooms.
