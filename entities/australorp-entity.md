# Australorp Chicken

## Description

The [[rhode-island-red]], and Plymouth
  Rock. These heavy breeds trace their ancestry to the Chinese fowl, in
  particular the Langshan, which contributed size, meat quality, and winter
  hardiness.
- The Australorp was specifically developed in Australia to retain the
  Orpington's large body size for meat while dramatically improving egg
  production through selective breeding for utility rather than exhibition
  standards.
- The plumage is uniformly black with a characteristic greenish-purple sheen
  in good condition. The comb is single and upright, and the skin and shanks
  are white or slate-coloured.
- Roosters weigh 3.9–4.5 kg (8.5–10 lbs); hens weigh 3.0–3.5 kg (6.5–7.5
  lbs), placing the breed firmly in the heavy category.
- The breed holds the official world record for egg production from a
  dual-purpose heavy breed, with the famous 1920s Australian laying trials
  demonstrating sustained output exceeding 300 eggs per hen per year.
- Australorps are renowned for their calm, docile temperament, making them
  easy to handle and well-suited to small-scale and backyard poultry systems.
- They are good winter layers, continuing to produce through cold months when
  many lighter breeds reduce output.
- The breed is hardy across a wide range of climates, from cool temperate
  regions to hot, arid areas, reflecting its Australian development.

## Relevance to Permaculture and Cultivation

The Australorp is arguably one of the best chicken breeds for [[food-forest]] zones.
- **Climate resilience:** The breed's Australian origin means it handles heat
  well, while the heavy frame provides sufficient cold tolerance for temperate
  winters, making it versatile across diverse permaculture sites.

## Design Considerations

Deploy Australorps as the backbone breed in mixed poultry flocks, particularly
in systems where reliable egg production and moderate meat yield are both
priorities. Their docile temperament makes them suitable for family farms and
educational permaculture sites where handling by children or novices is
expected. Combine with lighter, more alert breeds (such as Leghorns or
Anconas) for predator awareness in free-range systems. Plan housing to
accommodate the heavier body weight, with lower roosts and wider nesting boxes
than would be needed for lighter breeds.
