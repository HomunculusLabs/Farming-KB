## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kalmia latifolia** L. was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753, with the genus named in honor of the Swedish botanist Pehr Kalm.

Flowering occurs from May through July, producing terminal corymbose clusters of 50–150 flowers.

Each flower is approximately 15–25 mm across with a shallow, five-lobed bowl-shaped corolla.

The corolla is white to deep pink, often with darker pink markings or a purple ring inside.

The distinctive anthers are lodged in small pockets in the corolla, releasing explosively when triggered by visiting bees.

This remarkable staminal mechanism ensures forceful pollen deposition onto the pollinator's body.

The fruit is a five-valved, dehiscent capsule approximately 4–6 mm in diameter, containing numerous tiny seeds.

## Distribution and Habitat

**Kalmia latifolia** ranges from southern Maine and New Brunswick south to northern Florida and west to Louisiana and Indiana.

It is most abundant in the Appalachian Mountains, where it forms extensive, nearly impenetrable thickets on mountain slopes.

The species thrives on acidic, well-drained soils derived from sandstone, shale, and other siliceous parent materials.

It is a dominant understory component of oak-heath and mixed hardwood-coniferous forests.

Elevation range extends from near sea level in the northern part of its range to over 1800 m in the southern Appalachians.

It tolerates shade but flowers most prolifically in partial to full sun at forest margins and openings.

The species is fire-adapted and resprouts vigorously from the root crown after top-kill by wildfire.

Mountain laurel thickets can become so dense that they inhibit forest regeneration and limit understory diversity.

## Toxicity

All tissues of **Kalmia latifolia** contain grayanotoxins (andromedotoxins), a group of diterpenoid neurotoxins.

The highest concentrations are found in the leaves, flowers, and young shoots.

Ingestion causes symptoms including salivation, vomiting, abdominal pain, dizziness, and bradycardia.

Severe poisoning can lead to cardiac arrhythmias, hypotension, convulsions, and respiratory failure.

Livestock poisoning is relatively common, particularly in sheep and goats that browse the foliage in winter.

Cases of human poisoning are rare but have been documented, primarily from honey made from mountain laurel nectar.

The so-called "mad honey" produced by bees foraging heavily on Kalmia and Rhododendron species contains grayanotoxins.

Consumption of as little as a few tablespoons of mad honey can produce symptoms within 30 minutes to 2 hours.
