## Overview

Pertusaria pertusa, commonly known as the wart lichen, is a widespread crustose lichen in the family Pertusariaceae. It forms thick, chalky-white thalli on tree bark and rock surfaces, characterized by prominent white pore-like fruiting bodies.

The species is one of the most common and recognizable Pertusaria species in temperate Europe. It is an important component of mature epiphytic lichen communities on old-growth trees in forested landscapes.

P. pertusa is chemically distinctive, containing thiophanic acid as its primary [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America.

In the British Isles, P. pertusa is widespread and locally common, particularly in areas with mature forest cover and good [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] may include stictic acid traces and occasionally norstictic acid in some populations. The cortical layer contains no distinctive secondary compounds, giving C– reactions.

The species is morphologically variable and [[challenges-neurotropic-fungi-chemical-analysis-taxonomic-identification]] is often necessary for reliable identification. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) is the standard method for confirming the presence of thiophanic acid.

Standard spot test reactions for P. pertusa are: cortex K–, C–, KC–, PD–; medulla K+ yellow, C–, PD–. The K+ yellow medullary reaction with large multi-celled spores is diagnostic.

## Ecology

Pertusaria pertusa is a slow-growing, long-lived lichen that requires stable substrate conditions for establishment. Growth rates are estimated at 1–3 mm per year in diameter under optimal conditions.

The species is a late-successional epiphyte, typically colonizing bark surfaces that have been available for decades. Its presence on a tree is often an indicator of ecological continuity and long-term habitat stability.

P. pertusa provides microhabitat for diverse invertebrate communities, including mites, springtails, and bark-dwelling insects. The thick thallus retains moisture and creates humid microconditions for these organisms.

The species is sensitive to acidification and nitrogen eutrophication. Decline has been documented near intensive agricultural operations where ammonia deposition elevates bark nitrogen content above the species' tolerance threshold.

Several lichenicolous fungi are associated with P. pertusa, including Arthonia pertusariae and Abrothallus pertusariae, which grow parasitically on the thallus surface.

## Similar Species

Pertusaria corallina is larger and more prominently warted, with pinkish-buff ostioles rather than white. It contains lecanoric acid (C+ red) rather than thiophanic acid, producing a different spot test profile.

Pertusaria coccodes produces bright red-orange ostioles containing rhodocladonic acid, making it immediately distinguishable from the white-ostioled P. pertusa.

Pertusaria amara contains picrolichenic acid, which gives the medulla an intensely bitter taste. This species is separated by its K+ yellow → crimson medullary reaction and different spore morphology.

Ochrolechia tartarea forms a similar chalky-white crustose thallus but produces prominent lecanorine apothecia with visible discs rather than immersed pore-like ostioles. Its chemistry (gyrophoric acid) also differs.

### Taxonomic Complexity

The Pertusaria pertusa species aggregate includes several morphologically similar taxa that have been variously treated as species, subspecies, or chemical races. Modern molecular studies have begun to resolve these relationships but the group remains taxonomically challenging.

Chemical variation within P. pertusa sensu lato includes populations containing stictic acid complex compounds in addition to thiophanic acid. Whether these represent distinct species or infraspecific variation remains unresolved.
