## Description

Humanure refers to human feces and urine that has been composted and converted into a safe, nutrient-rich soil amendment. The term was popularized by Joseph Jenkins in his 1995 book *The Humanure Handbook*. In gardening contexts, humanure is presented as a free, locally available alternative to purchased animal manures and [[teaming-with-nutrients-natural-vs-synthetic-fertilizers]].

## Classification

- **Category:** Soil amendment / fertilizer
- **Type:** Composted human waste
- **Related concepts:** Composting, nutrient cycling, organic farming, soil fertility

## Key Facts

- Humanure must be thoroughly composted at thermophilic temperatures (above 120°F / 49°C) to destroy pathogens including bacteria, viruses, and parasites.
- The composting process typically requires 6 months to 2 years depending on method and climate.
- Humanure is rich in nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and trace minerals — nutrients that are otherwise lost to sewage systems.
- [[steve-solomon]] endorses humanure as one of the most cost-effective soil fertility strategies, particularly for gardeners with limited budgets.
- When properly composted, humanure is indistinguishable from other finished compost in appearance, odor, and safety.
- The practice is legal in many jurisdictions but regulations vary; some areas restrict its use on food crops.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Humanure addresses a core challenge in self-sufficient gardening: obtaining sufficient [[savory-soil-organic-matter-and-decomposition-dynamics]] nutrients without purchased inputs. It is especially relevant in scenarios where:

- Commercial fertilizers are unavailable or unaffordable
- Animal manure sources are distant or expensive to transport
- The gardener seeks a closed-loop nutrient cycle

Solomon positions humanure as part of a [[soil-fertility-gradient-approach]] to soil fertility — from no inputs, to minimal amendments, to full organic fertility programs. It represents the most resource-efficient option in his fertility spectrum.

## Safety Considerations

- Raw human waste must never be applied directly to crops
- A minimum composting period with proper carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C:N of 20:1 to 30:1) is essential
- Cover material (sawdust, straw, leaves) must be added after each deposit
- The finished compost should be tested or aged sufficiently before garden use

## Nutrient Cycling
[[cervantes-organic-amendments-chart]] play a vital role in closing nutrient loops
on farms and in gardens. Manure and compost return nutrients
to the soil that were removed through harvest. The slow release
of nutrients from organic sources provides sustained feeding
for crops throughout [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

## Application Methods
Proper application ensures maximum benefit and minimizes
nutrient loss. Incorporate amendments into the soil surface
or apply as side-dressing during the growing season. Avoid
applying fresh manure directly to edible crops. Composting
or aging manure before application reduces pathogen risks.

## Safety and Best Practices
Always wear gloves when handling animal manures. Age or
compost manure for at least six months before garden use.
Keep amendments covered during storage to prevent nutrient
runoff. Follow local regulations regarding manure application
rates and timing near waterways.

## Integration with Garden Systems
Combine amendments with cover cropping and crop rotation for
optimal soil building. Monitor plant response to guide future
amendment schedules. Soil testing helps determine appropriate
[[ingham-compost-tea-application-rates-and-timing]] identifies nutrient imbalances that need
correction.

## Additional Notes
These principles apply across a range of growing conditions and
can be adapted to suit specific local requirements. Continued
observation and record-keeping help refine techniques over time.
