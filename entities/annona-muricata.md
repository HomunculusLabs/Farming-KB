## Overview

**Annona muricata** L., commonly known as soursop, graviola, guanábana, or sirsak, is a tropical fruit tree in the family Annonaceae.

It is native to the tropical regions of the Americas and the Caribbean but is now cultivated pantropically for its edible fruit and medicinal properties.

The species has attracted significant research interest due to its acetogenin content, which exhibits cytotoxic activity against cancer cell lines in vitro.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Magnoliales
- **Family:** Annonaceae
- **Genus:** Annona L.
- **Species:** A. muricata L.
- **Authority:** Carl Linnaeus (1753)

The genus *Annona* contains approximately 170 species of trees and shrubs, several of which produce commercially important fruits.

### Morphology

*A. muricata* is an evergreen tree reaching 5–8 m in height under cultivation, though wild specimens can grow to 10 m.

Leaves are oblong to elliptic, 8–16 cm long, glossy dark green above and paler beneath, with a characteristic pungent aroma when crushed.

Flowers are solitary or in pairs, borne on short peduncles from the trunk and older branches (cauliflory). They are yellow-green, with three fleshy outer petals and three smaller inner petals.

The fruit is an aggregate of fused berries forming an irregular ovoid to heart-shaped syncarp, 15–30 cm long and 10–15 cm in diameter.

The skin is green, covered with soft, curved spines. The white, fibrous pulp has a sweet-tart, custard-like flavor and contains numerous brown seeds.

## Distribution and Cultivation

The species is native to the Caribbean, Central America, and northern South America. It is now widely naturalized and cultivated throughout the tropics.

Major producing countries include Brazil, Mexico, Peru, the Philippines, Indonesia, Vietnam, and several West African nations.

*A. muricata* thrives in humid tropical lowlands below 1000 m elevation, with annual rainfall of 1000–2500 mm and mean temperatures of 25–28°C.

Trees begin bearing fruit 3–5 years after planting. A single mature tree can produce 50–100 fruits per year under favorable conditions.

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds

### Acetogenins

The most notable phytochemicals in *A. muricata* are annonaceous acetogenins, a class of long-chain fatty acid derivatives unique to the Annonaceae.

Over 100 acetogenins have been isolated from the species, including annonacin, annonacinone, muricin, and squamocin (annonacin-A-one).

These compounds are potent inhibitors of mitochondrial complex I (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase), which is the primary mechanism underlying their cytotoxic activity.

### Other Compounds

The leaves and bark contain alkaloids including anonaine, nornuciferine, and asimilobine.

Flavonoids, including quercetin, kaempferol, and luteolin glycosides, contribute to antioxidant activity.

Vitamins present in the fruit include vitamin C, vitamin B1, vitamin B2, and niacin. The fruit also contains significant potassium, magnesium, and dietary fiber.

## Pharmacology and Ethnobotany

### Traditional Medicine

In traditional medicine systems across the tropics, all parts of the tree are used medicinally.

Leaf infusions are consumed as a sedative, antispasmodic, and hypotensive agent. Decoctions of the bark are used to treat diabetes and fever.

The fruit pulp is eaten for its antipyretic and digestive properties. Crushed seeds are applied topically as parasiticides and insect repellents.

### Anticancer Research

Acetogenins from *A. muricata* have demonstrated selective cytotoxicity against multiple cancer cell lines in vitro, including breast, prostate, pancreatic, and colon cancer cells.

Annonacin, the most studied compound, shows IC50 values in the low micromolar to nanomolar range against various tumor lines.

These effects are attributed to inhibition of mitochondrial complex I and induction of apoptosis through the intrinsic pathway.

**Important caveat:** Despite promising in vitro data, no clinical trials have demonstrated efficacy of *A. muricata* extracts against cancer in humans. The consumption of soursop products as cancer treatment is not evidence-based.

### Neurotoxicity Concerns

Epidemiological studies in the French West Indies have linked chronic consumption of annonaceous fruits and herbal teas to atypical parkinsonism.

This association is attributed to annonacin, which can cross the blood-brain barrier and damage dopaminergic neurons at sufficient cumulative doses.

Regular long-term consumption of soursop leaves or teas may pose a risk for neurodegenerative disease, and caution is warranted.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

The ripe fruit pulp is consumed fresh, blended into beverages, and used in desserts, ice creams, and preserves.

It has a distinctive flavor described as a combination of strawberry, pineapple, and citrus with creamy, custard-like texture.

Soursop nectar and juice are commercially produced in many tropical countries. The pulp is also processed into concentrates and frozen products.
