## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

Leaves are oblong to elliptic, 8–16 cm long, glossy dark green above and paler beneath, with a characteristic pungent aroma when crushed.

Flowers are solitary or in pairs, borne on short peduncles from the trunk and older branches (cauliflory). They are yellow-green, with three fleshy outer petals and three smaller inner petals.

The fruit is an aggregate of fused berries forming an irregular ovoid to heart-shaped syncarp, 15–30 cm long and 10–15 cm in diameter.

The skin is green, covered with soft, curved spines. The white, fibrous pulp has a sweet-tart, custard-like flavor and contains numerous brown seeds.

## Distribution and Cultivation

The species is native to the Caribbean, [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]]. It is now widely naturalized and cultivated throughout the tropics.

Major producing countries include Brazil, Mexico, Peru, the Philippines, Indonesia, Vietnam, and several West African nations.

*A. muricata* thrives in humid tropical lowlands below 1000 m elevation, with annual rainfall of 1000–2500 mm and mean temperatures of 25–28°C.

Trees begin bearing fruit 3–5 years after planting. A single mature tree can produce 50–100 fruits per year under favorable conditions.

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds

### Acetogenins

The most notable phytochemicals in *A. muricata* are annonaceous acetogenins, a class of long-chain fatty acid derivatives unique to the Annonaceae.

Over 100 acetogenins have been isolated from the species, including annonacin, annonacinone, muricin, and squamocin (annonacin-A-one).

These compounds are potent inhibitors of mitochondrial complex I (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase), which is the primary mechanism underlying their cytotoxic activity.

### Other Compounds

The leaves and bark contain alkaloids including anonaine, nornuciferine, and asimilobine.

Flavonoids, including [[quercetin]], [[kaempferol]], and [[luteolin]] glycosides, contribute to antioxidant activity.

Vitamins present in the fruit include vitamin C, vitamin B1, vitamin B2, and niacin. The fruit also contains significant potassium, magnesium, and dietary fiber.

## Pharmacology and Ethnobotany

### Traditional Medicine

In traditional medicine systems across the tropics, all parts of the tree are used medicinally.

Leaf infusions are consumed as a sedative, antispasmodic, and hypotensive agent. Decoctions of the bark are used to treat diabetes and fever.

The fruit pulp is eaten for its antipyretic and digestive properties. Crushed seeds are applied topically as parasiticides and insect repellents.
