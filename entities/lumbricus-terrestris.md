# Lumbricus terrestris

## Description

Lumbricus terrestris, commonly known as the **night crawler** or **common earthworm**, is one of the most recognizable and widespread earthworm species in garden and agricultural soils across North America and Europe. It is a large, deep-burrowing anecic earthworm species that plays a foundational role in [[soil-food-web]] dynamics, organic matter decomposition, [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]].

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Animalia
- **Phylum:** Annelida
- **Class:** Clitellata
- **Order:** Opisthopora
- **Family:** Lumbricidae
- **Genus:** Lumbricus
- **Species:** L. terrestris

## Key Facts

- **Common name:** Night crawler, dew worm, common earthworm
- **Size:** Can grow up to 12 inches (30 cm) in length
- **Burrowing behavior:** Creates permanent vertical burrows that can extend as deep as 12 feet (3.6 meters) into the soil
- **Feeding:** Primarily bacterivorous but also consumes fungi, nematodes, protozoa, [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]]
- **Reproduction:** Hermaphroditic (carries both male and female sexual organs); requires two worms to produce offspring; cocoons contain 15 or more baby worms; maturity reached in 3-4 months; lifespan up to 15 years
- **Non-native to North America:** Arrived with European settlers in potted plants and ship ballast; now dominant in garden soils across the continent except in the warm desert Southwest
- **Population density:** An acre of good garden soil can contain 2-3 million earthworms (10-50 per square foot)

## Role in the Soil Food Web

### Shredding and Decomposition
L. terrestris is classified as a **shredder**. It breaks down leaf litter and organic matter, accelerating decomposition from 1-2 years to approximately 3 months. By opening up organic material, it grants bacteria and fungi better access to cellulose and lignin.

### Burrowing and Soil Structure
- Creates deep vertical burrows that serve as pathways for water drainage, air passage, and root growth
- Moves rocks up to six times its own body weight while burrowing
- Increases [[soil-porosity]] and water-holding capacity
- Deposits subsoil onto the surface, mixing horizons

### Vermicastings Production
Worms deposit 10-15 tons of castings per acre on the surface annually. Vermicastings from L. terrestris are significantly enriched compared to surrounding soil:
- **50% higher** in organic matter
- **7x richer** in phosphate
- **10x more** available potash
- **5x more** nitrogen
- **3x more** usable magnesium
- **1.5x higher** in calcium

### Nutrient Cycling
The worm's digestive system relies on intestinal bacteria to digest organic matter. [[peat-calcium-carbonate-perlite-casing-psilocybe-cubensis-miniculture]] is added during digestion. The grinding action of the gizzard (using sand and small rock particles) breaks food into fine particles accessible to bacteria, unlocking chemical bonds that tie up nutrients and converting them to plant-available forms.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Lumbricus terrestris is a keystone organism in soil health for cultivation:

1. **Bacterial dominance indicator:** Soils with large L. terrestris populations are typically bacterially dominated, making them ideal for vegetables, annuals, and grasses
2. **[[mycorrhizal-interactions-plant-pathogens]]:** Earthworm burrows create pathways for mycorrhizal [[fungal-hyphae]] to colonize new root zones
3. **[[composting-systems]]:** While not the primary composting worm (that role belongs to Eisenia fetida), L. terrestris contributes significantly to field-scale decomposition
4. **Soil food web health indicator:** Presence of L. terrestris indicates a functioning soil food web with adequate bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes
5. **Negative impacts:** In some North American and Canadian forests, introduced L. terrestris from fishermen have disrupted native litter layers, accelerating decomposition beyond healthy rates for forest ecosystems

## Threats and Management

- **Rototilling** destroys burrows and kills or injures worms
- **[[cannabis-chemical-fertilizers]]** (salt-based) irritate and repel earthworms
- **Pesticides and fungicides** reduce worm populations indirectly by eliminating their microbial food sources
- Maintaining organic matter, avoiding tillage, and using organic amendments supports healthy L. terrestris populations
