## Overview

Iris foetidissima, known as the stinking iris, gladdon, or roast-beef plant, is an evergreen rhizomatous perennial native to western Europe and the Mediterranean region. It typically grows 40–80 cm tall.

The species is notable for its persistent seed capsules that split open in autumn to reveal bright orange-red seeds. These remain showy through winter, making the plant valued for dried arrangements.

Despite the unflattering common name, the crushed leaves emit only a faint unpleasant odor. The flowers are relatively subdued, typically pale lavender to yellowish, but the seed display compensates.

It is one of the most shade-tolerant iris species, thriving in conditions where few other irises persist. This quality makes it a valuable plant for difficult dry-shade gardens.

The common name "roast-beef plant" comes from the scent of the crushed leaves, which some compare to the smell of cooked meat. The name "gladdon" derives from the Latin gladius, referring to the sword-shaped leaves.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Iris foetidissima was described by Linnaeus in 1753. It belongs to the subgenus Limniris, section Lophiris, sharing the crested falls characteristic with [[iris-pseudacorus]].

## Ecology

Iris foetidissima provides cover for ground-dwelling wildlife in hedgerow and woodland edge habitats. The dense evergreen fans offer year-round shelter.

The bright seeds are dispersed by birds, particularly thrushes and blackbirds, which eat the fleshy aril. Seeds pass through the digestive tract and germinate readily.
