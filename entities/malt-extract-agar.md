# Malt Extract Agar

Malt extract agar (MEA) is a standard microbiological growth medium widely used in mycology for the cultivation, isolation, and maintenance of fungal cultures. It provides a nutrient-rich, semi-solid surface that supports the growth of a broad range of fungi, making it the preferred medium for germinating spores and establishing pure mycelial cultures of species such as [[psilocybe-azurescens]] and related mushrooms. MEA has been a staple of mycological laboratories since the early 20th century and remains the most commonly recommended medium for general-purpose [[fungal-cultivation-methods]].

## Composition

Malt extract agar is composed of the following ingredients:

- **Malt extract**: 30 g per liter, providing a complex mixture of carbohydrates (maltose, glucose, dextrins), amino acids, peptides, vitamins (particularly B vitamins including thiamine, riboflavin, and niacin), and minerals (potassium, magnesium, phosphorus, [[teaming-with-nutrients-micronutrients-and-trace-elements]]) essential for fungal growth
- **Agar**: 15-20 g per liter, serving as a solidifying agent derived from red algae (Rhodophyceae, primarily Gelidium and Gracilaria species) that creates a stable, translucent gel matrix at room temperature. Agar melts at approximately 85 degrees C and solidifies at approximately 32-40 degrees C, exhibiting the useful property of hysteresis (solidifying at a lower temperature than it melts)
- **Distilled water**: to 1 liter total volume

The pH of the prepared medium is typically adjusted to approximately 5.6 to 6.0 using dilute sodium hydroxide (NaOH) or hydrochloric acid (HCl), which is near the optimal range for most fungal growth. Most fungi prefer slightly acidic conditions, and this pH range provides a favorable environment while inhibiting many bacterial contaminants.

Some formulations include additional components for specific purposes:

- **Peptone** (5 g/L): a partially digested protein source that provides additional nitrogen and amino acids, creating Malt Extract Peptone Agar (MEPA), which supports faster mycelial growth and more robust colony morphology
- **Yeast extract** (1-2 g/L): provides additional B vitamins and growth factors that can accelerate the growth of nutritionally demanding fungal species
- **Antibiotics** (chloramphenicol at 25-50 mg/L, or gentamicin): added to selective formulations to suppress bacterial growth when isolating fungi from environmental samples that contain mixed bacterial and fungal populations
- **Dextrose** (10-20 g/L): sometimes added as Malt Extract Dextrose Agar (MEDA) to provide an additional readily metabolizable carbon source

Malt extract is produced commercially from barley grains (Hordeum vulgare) that have been germinated (malted) to activate endogenous amylase and protease enzymes, then dried and kilned to produce the desired flavor and color profile. The malted barley is extracted with hot water in a process similar to brewing, and the resulting liquid (wort) is concentrated into a syrup or spray-dried into a powder. The extraction process yields a complex nutrient mixture that closely mirrors the types of carbohydrates and nitrogen sources fungi encounter when colonizing grain and plant materials in their natural environments.

## Preparation

Standard preparation of malt extract agar follows these steps:

1. **Weigh and dissolve**: Add 30 g of malt extract powder and 15-20 g of agar to approximately 800 mL of distilled water in a heat-resistant Erlenmeyer flask or beaker. Heat gently while stirring continuously to dissolve the components fully. The agar requires sustained heating above 90 degrees C to dissolve completely.
2. **Adjust pH**: Test the pH with a calibrated pH meter or pH indicator strips and adjust to 5.6-6.0 using dilute NaOH to raise pH or dilute HCl to lower it. Proper pH adjustment is important for consistent growth results.
3. **Bring to final volume**: Add distilled water to reach exactly 1 liter total volume, accounting for any water lost during heating.
4. **Sterilize**: Seal the flask with aluminum foil and sterilize by autoclaving at 121 degrees C (15 psi) for 15-20 minutes. Proper [[sterilization]] is essential to eliminate contaminant organisms that would otherwise outcompete the desired fungal species. The foil cover prevents the medium from boiling over while allowing steam penetration.
5. **Cool and pour**: After autoclaving, allow the medium to cool to approximately 45-55 degrees C (warm but not hot to the touch), then pour 20-25 mL into each sterile Petri dish in a [[laminar-flow-hood-construction-hepa-filter]] or still-air box. Pouring at this temperature minimizes condensation on the lid while keeping the agar liquid enough to flow evenly across the plate surface. Pouring at too high a temperature creates excessive condensation; pouring at too low a temperature causes premature solidification and uneven surfaces.
6. **Solidify and store**: Allow the poured plates to solidify undisturbed at room temperature for approximately 30 minutes, then store inverted (lid down) at 4 degrees C in sealed plastic bags. Inverting the plates prevents any condensation droplets from falling onto the agar surface. ### Spore Germination

MEA provides an ideal surface for mushroom spores to germinate. When spores from species like [[psilocybe-azurescens]] are deposited onto the agar surface (typically using a sterile spore syringe or spore print), the nutrients support hyphal emergence and initial growth. The moisture-retaining gel surface maintains high local humidity around the spores, which is critical for germination. Germination typically occurs within 3 to 14 days depending on species, spore viability, and incubation conditions. Fresh spores from recently collected specimens typically germinate faster than older spores from stored prints.

### Mycelium Isolation

When working with wild specimens, spore prints, or multi-spore cultures, mycologists use MEA to isolate individual mycelial strains with desired characteristics. Fast-growing, rhizomorphic (cord-like, organized) sectors of [[mycelium]] are cut from the agar using a sterile scalpel and transferred to fresh MEA plates. This process of serial transfer is repeated until a genetically uniform culture is obtained. The goal is to identify and preserve the most vigorous, fastest-growing, and most rhizomorphic sectors for use in subsequent cultivation steps.

### Mycelial Expansion

Once a pure dikaryotic culture is established, it can be expanded by transferring small pieces (agar wedges, approximately 5 mm square) of colonized agar to fresh MEA plates. This allows rapid multiplication of mycelial biomass before transfer to bulk substrates such as hardwood chips or grain spawn. Each plate-to-plate transfer is called a "transfer" or "passage," and most cultures can be maintained through 5-10 passages before senescence (gradual loss of vigor) becomes a concern.

### Strain Comparison and Selection

MEA plates allow mycologists to compare growth rates, morphological characteristics, and vigor of different strains side by side under identical conditions. This is essential for selecting the most robust or productive strains for cultivation purposes. Linear growth rate measurements (mm/day) taken on MEA are a standard metric for comparing fungal strains.

### Long-Term Storage

Fungal cultures can be stored on MEA slants (agar solidified in test tubes rather than Petri dishes) at refrigerator temperatures (2-4 degrees C) for several months to years, depending on the species. The slant format minimizes desiccation and contamination risk compared to plates. Periodic transfer to fresh medium (every 6-12 months) is necessary to maintain long-term viability. Alternative long-term storage methods include cryopreservation in liquid nitrogen (with cryoprotectants such as glycerol) and preservation in sterile distilled water.

## Advantages and Limitations

MEA is favored in mycology because it supports a wide range of fungi without being overly selective or inhibitory. The complex nutrient profile encourages vigorous growth and typical colony morphology. It is also inexpensive, straightforward to prepare from commercially available components, and the results are highly reproducible between laboratories and batches.

However, MEA's broad nutritional profile also means it supports many contaminant organisms, including bacteria and fast-growing mold species. For slow-growing species like [[psilocybe-azurescens]], contaminant organisms can easily outcompete the desired mycelium on MEA unless strict [[mushroom-cultivation-sterile-technique]] is maintained. Additionally, the relatively high sugar content of MEA can promote bacterial growth and encourage mucoid (slimy) colony morphologies in some fungal species.

## Alternative Mycological Media

- **Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA)**: uses potato extract and dextrose; slightly less nutrient-rich than MEA, producing slower but often denser mycelial growth; the most widely available commercial mycological medium
- **Dog Food Agar (DFA)**: made from canned dog food; used for fast-growing species that benefit from high protein content; inexpensive and accessible
- **Honey Agar**: uses honey (10-15 g/L) as a carbohydrate source; a simple, accessible alternative when malt extract is unavailable
- **Corn Meal Agar**: supports sporulation in some fungal species and can be used to differentiate morphological variants based on pigment production
- **Malt Yeast Agar (MYA)**: combines malt extract with yeast extract for enhanced growth of nutritionally demanding species
