## Cold Frame

## Description

A cold frame is a bottomless box structure covered with transparent glazing material, used to protect plants from cold weather and [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]] season. Cold frames are the simplest and least expensive climate moderator available to gardeners and small-scale growers. In Eliot Coleman's [[winter-harvest-system]], cold frames serve as the foundational technology that led to the development of the double-coverage principle and modern [[unheated-greenhouse-winter-harvest]] production.

## Classification

- **Category**: Agricultural infrastructure / season-extension technology
- **Type**: Passive solar plant protection structure
- **Complexity**: Low (simplest form of [[coleman-protected-cultivation]])
- **Energy input**: None (unheated, relies on solar gain)

## Key Facts

### Traditional Design

The standard old-time cold-frame design, validated by comparative investigations, consists of:
- A bottomless box made of 2-inch-thick planks
- 8 to 12 inches high at the back (north side)
- 6 to 8 inches high at the front (south side)
- Covered with glass panels or other transparent glazing

The sloped design (higher at back, lower at front) maximizes solar gain by presenting the glazing at an angle perpendicular to the low winter sun.

### Climate Modification

A cold frame provides the equivalent of moving plants approximately one and one-half USDA zones warmer, or roughly 500 miles south on the U.S. East Coast. A cold frame will typically prevent crops inside from freezing until outdoor temperatures drop below 25F (-4C).

### Historical Significance

Cold frames have been used in European market gardening for centuries. The French maraichers of 19th-century Paris used glass-covered frames extensively. In Holland, commercial cold-frame production of [[cold-hardy-winter-vegetables]] persisted into the 1970s using the Dutch lights design — approximately 30-by-60-inch glass panes in wooden frames.

### The Double-Layer Discovery

Coleman's critical insight came in the winter of 1980 when he erected cold frames inside an unheated greenhouse, creating a double layer of protection. This moved the effective growing climate an additional 500 miles south to USDA Zone 8, with the bonus of a snow-shedding roof. This discovery transformed the concept from simple frost protection to a viable winter production system.

## Relevance to Cultivation

### Winter Vegetable Production

Cold frames enable production of cold-hardy crops through winter without supplemental heat. Key crops include spinach, mache, claytonia, tatsoi, endive, escarole, carrots, radishes, turnips, leeks, scallions, pak choi, kale, parsley, celery, and fennel.

### Limitations for Commercial Use

While effective, cold frames have drawbacks for commercial-scale production:
- Costly to build in large quantities
- Require manual venting to prevent overheating on sunny days
- Labor-intensive to manage compared to row covers
- Limited growing space per unit

These limitations led Coleman to replace cold frames with floating row covers for the inner layer in commercial operations.

### Transition to Modern Systems

The cold frame's principles were preserved but its form evolved:
- Inner layer: Floating row covers on wire wickets replaced glass cold frames
- Outer layer: Hoop houses and greenhouses replaced individual cold frames
- The concept of passive solar protection remained central

## Management Considerations

- **Venting**: Critical on sunny days; temperatures inside can soar even in cold weather
- **Watering**: Reduced winter transpiration means less frequent watering needed
- **Snow removal**: Snow insulates but blocks light; must be cleared from glazing
- **Orientation**: Face south (in northern hemisphere) for maximum solar gain
