# Cinnamomum tamala

## Overview

Cinnamomum tamala, commonly known as Indian bay leaf or tejpat, is an evergreen tree in the family Lauraceae native to the Himalayan foothills and the Indian subcontinent. It is valued as a culinary spice, traditional medicine, and source of aromatic essential oil.

The dried leaves of Cinnamomum tamala are a key ingredient in many Indian and South Asian dishes, imparting a warm, cassia-like aroma distinct from the unrelated Mediterranean bay laurel, [[laurus-nobilis]].

Indian bay leaf is a fundamental component of garam masala blends and is used to flavor rice dishes, curries, and meat preparations. The leaves are typically removed before serving, as they remain tough and fibrous even after prolonged cooking.

Unlike true cinnamon species, Cinnamomum tamala is primarily harvested for its aromatic foliage rather than its bark. The tree plays a significant role in Ayurvedic medicine, where it is used to treat digestive, respiratory, and [[cannabis-for-inflammatory-conditions]].

In Unani traditional medicine, the leaves are classified as warm and dry in temperament, prescribed for cold conditions of the stomach and liver. This classification system continues to influence therapeutic use across northern India and Pakistan.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Cinnamomum tamala (Buch.-Ham.) T. Nees & Eberm. belongs to the family Lauraceae, subfamily Lauroideae, tribe Cinnamomeae. The species was first described by Francis Buchanan-Hamilton and later refined by Theodor Nees von Esenbeck and Johann Friedrich Ebermann.

The tree is a medium-sized evergreen reaching heights of 8 to 20 meters, with a straight trunk and a dense, spreading crown. Bark is grayish-brown, rough, and aromatic when cut, emitting a cinnamon-like fragrance.

Leaves are simple, alternate, elliptic to lanceolate, 10 to 25 centimeters long and 4 to 8 centimeters wide. The upper surface is dark glossy green, while the lower surface is paler with prominent reticulate venation.

Leaf margins are entire and slightly undulate. When crushed, fresh leaves release a strong spicy aroma due to their high essential oil content. The characteristic three-veined pattern from the leaf base is a diagnostic feature of the genus.

Inflorescences are axillary or terminal panicles bearing small, pale yellow to cream-colored flowers. Each flower is approximately 4 to 6 millimeters in diameter with six tepals arranged in two whorls.

The fruit is a small, ovoid drupe approximately 1 to 1.5 centimeters long, ripening from green to dark purple or black. The single seed is enclosed within a thin fleshy pericarp and is dispersed primarily by birds.

Fruiting occurs annually, with peak maturation during the winter months in Himalayan populations. Seed viability is relatively short, declining rapidly after 3 to 4 months of storage at ambient temperatures.

Wood is moderately hard and durable, with a cinnamon-scented heartwood. Growth rings are indistinct in tropical conditions, making age determination difficult without [[fungal-destructive-sampling-herbarium-dna-extraction]].

## Distribution and Habitat

Cinnamomum tamala is native to the Himalayan foothills from Nepal and northern India through Bhutan, Bangladesh, and Myanmar. It extends into the Western Ghats and the hills of peninsular India, where it is also cultivated.

The species occurs naturally at elevations between 500 and 2500 meters above sea level, thriving in subtropical to warm temperate montane forests. It is a component of mixed broadleaf forests, often growing alongside other Lauraceae species.

Optimal growth occurs in areas with annual rainfall of 1500 to 3000 millimeters and well-drained loamy soils. The tree tolerates light frost when mature but is sensitive to prolonged freezing temperatures during the seedling stage.

Soil pH preference ranges from slightly acidic to neutral, approximately 5.5 to 7.0. The species shows moderate tolerance to waterlogging during the monsoon season, a trait uncommon among many Lauraceae species.

Cultivation has spread the species to other tropical and subtropical regions, including parts of [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]], East Africa, and the Caribbean. However, commercial production remains concentrated in northern India and Nepal.

Natural regeneration in forest understories can be limited by dense canopy shade. Gap-phase dynamics and forest edges provide the light conditions necessary for successful seedling establishment in wild populations.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The essential oil of Cinnamomum tamala leaves is composed predominantly of monoterpenoids and phenylpropanoids. Major constituents include linalool (25 to 50 percent), eugenol (10 to 25 percent), and cinnamaldehyde (5 to 15 percent).

Variation in essential oil composition is significant across different populations and collection times. Himalayan populations tend to be richer in linalool, while lowland accessions often contain higher proportions of cinnamaldehyde and eugenol.

The bark contains tannins, mucilage, and a volatile oil profile distinct from that of the leaves. Bark oil is richer in cinnamaldehyde, resembling the profile of [[cinnamomum-cassia]] more closely than that of [[cinnamomum-verum]].

Pharmacological studies have demonstrated antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria, including [[staphylococcus-aureus]] and [[bacillus-subtilis]]. The essential oil shows moderate antifungal activity against Candida species and [[aspergillus-niger]].

Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties have been documented in rodent models. Methanolic leaf extracts reduce carrageenan-induced paw edema and show free radical scavenging activity comparable to standard antioxidants.

The antioxidant capacity is attributed primarily to [[aact-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds-wood-composting-ingham]], including proanthocyanidins and flavonoid glycosides. Total phenolic content in dried leaves ranges from 40 to 80 milligrams per gram of gallic acid equivalents.

Hepatoprotective effects have been observed in carbon tetrachloride-induced liver injury models. Pre-treatment with leaf extract significantly reduces serum transaminase levels and hepatic lipid peroxidation markers.

Hypoglycemic effects have been observed in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats administered leaf extract orally. Blood glucose reduction is attributed to enhanced insulin sensitivity rather than direct insulin stimulation.

## Cultivation and Uses

Cinnamomum tamala is propagated both from seed and through vegetative cuttings. Seed germination is erratic, often taking 30 to 60 days with highly variable success rates. Semi-hardwood cuttings treated with [[cervantes-rooting-hormones-cloning]] show more reliable establishment.

Plantations are typically established at spacings of 3 to 5 meters, with trees reaching harvestable size for leaf collection within 4 to 6 years. Leaves are harvested year-round in tropical conditions, with peak oil content occurring during the warm season.

Annual leaf yield per mature tree ranges from 2 to 5 kilograms of dried product. Sustainable harvesting practices recommend removing no more than one-third of the canopy foliage per year to maintain tree health and vigor.
