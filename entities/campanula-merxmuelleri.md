## Overview

Campanula merxmuelleri is a rare, perennial cushion-forming plant in the family Campanulaceae, endemic to the mountains of northern Greece. It produces striking, large, deep violet-blue flowers that contrast sharply with its compact, grey-green foliage.

The species was described in 1972 by Dimitris Phitos and Jürgen Damboldt, who named it in honor of the German botanist Friedrich Merxmüller. It is one of several Greek bellflowers with extremely restricted distributions.

Campanula merxmuelleri is classified as Endangered on the Greek Red List due to its very small area of occupancy and potential threats from climate change. Fewer than five populations are known from the wild.

The plant grows as a dense cushion, a growth form typical of high-altitude species that must withstand freezing temperatures and strong winds. It is one of the most visually striking of the Greek endemic campanulas.

The species is virtually unknown in cultivation and is of interest primarily to botanists and alpine plant specialists.

Its discoverer, Dimitris Phitos, considered it one of the most significant botanical finds of his career. The species exemplifies the extraordinary endemism of Greek mountain flora.

The Greek endemic campanulas number over 40 species, making the genus one of the most species-rich on the Greek mainland. Campanula merxmuelleri stands out for its large flower-to-plant ratio.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Campanula merxmuelleri belongs to the genus Campanula section Saxicolae, a group of rock-dwelling species adapted to mountain environments. Its closest relatives include other Balkan endemics such as Campanula orphanidea and Campanula secundiflora.

Plants form dense, hemispherical cushions 5–12 cm tall and 10–25 cm in diameter. The stems are very short and crowded, with overlapping leaf bases that create a cushion structure.

Leaves are small, obovate to spatulate, 5–12 mm long, grey-green, and covered with short, appressed white hairs. The leaf margins are entire or faintly crenulate.

Flowering stems arise from the cushion surface, each bearing a single terminal flower. The flowering stems are 3–8 cm tall and sparsely hairy.

Flowers are large relative to the plant, campanulate, 2.5–4 cm long, and deep violet-blue. The corolla is divided to about one-third of its length into five broad, slightly recurved lobes.

The calyx is tubular, with five linear-subulate lobes covered in short glandular hairs. The style is exserted and curved downward, with a three-lobed stigma.

Capsules are cylindrical, nodding, and dehisce by three basal pores. Seeds are small, brown, and ellipsoid.

The chromosome number is 2n = 34, which is typical for the section. Karyotype analysis shows it is a diploid species.

## Distribution and Habitat

Campanula merxmuelleri is endemic to the Pindus mountain range in northern Greece, specifically in the regions of Epirus and western Macedonia. It is known from fewer than five mountain summits.

The species grows at elevations of 1,800–2,400 m on limestone cliffs, rock crevices, and stable scree. It favors north- and east-facing exposures where some shade and moisture are available.

The plant is restricted to calcareous substrates and does not occur on siliceous rocks. It is a strict chasmophyte, growing almost exclusively in vertical or near-vertical rock faces.

Associated species include other high-altitude specialists such as Alyssum handelii, Silene parnassica, and various saxicolous ferns. The plant communities are endemic-rich and of high conservation value.

Annual precipitation at these elevations exceeds 1,500 mm, much of it falling as snow. The growing season is short, lasting from June to September.

The total area of occupancy is estimated at less than 20 km², making it one of the most range-restricted bellflowers in Europe.

Population sizes are small, with individual subpopulations consisting of 50–200 mature individuals. The total wild population is estimated at fewer than 1,000 plants.

The species' inability to colonize new sites is limited by its poor dispersal ability and specific substrate requirements. Seeds rarely travel more than a few meters from the parent plant.

## Conservation

Campanula merxmuelleri is assessed as Endangered (EN) on the Greek Red Data Book. Its small area of occupancy and few locations qualify it under IUCN criteria B2ab(iii).

The primary threat is climate change, which is causing upward shifts in vegetation zones on Greek mountains. As temperatures rise, suitable habitat for high-altitude specialists shrinks.

Grazing pressure from livestock is a secondary threat, as animals use the same cliff ledges for shade and shelter. Trampling can damage cushion plants that take decades to develop.

All known populations occur within the Northern Pindos National Park, which provides some legal protection. However, enforcement of grazing regulations is inconsistent.

The species is included in the Bern Convention Annex I as a strictly protected plant. Seed collections are maintained at the National Agricultural Research Foundation of Greece.

No in situ monitoring programs are currently active. Botanists have called for regular population censuses to track trends.

## Cultivation and Ecology

Campanula merxmuelleri is among the most challenging bellflowers to cultivate. It requires sharply drained, lime-rich substrates and cool, humid conditions that mimic its mountain habitat.

Plants must be protected from winter wet, which causes rapid rotting of the cushion. An alpine house or well-ventilated cold frame is essential in cultivation.

Propagation is by seed, which should be sown in late autumn on the surface of a gritty compost. Germination occurs in spring at cool temperatures.

Seedlings are extremely slow-growing and may take 3–5 years to reach flowering size. This discourages commercial propagation.

Cuttings are rarely successful due to the dense cushion growth form. Division is essentially impossible without destroying the plant.

The large, vivid flowers are adapted to bee pollination in their exposed cliff-face habitat. The deep violet-blue color is highly visible against grey limestone.

Pollinator observations are limited but bumblebees have been recorded visiting flowers during brief mountain expeditions.

The cushion growth form traps heat and moisture, allowing the plant to survive in an environment where temperatures fluctuate wildly between day and night.

Individual cushions may live for decades, slowly increasing in size. The oldest cushions are estimated at 30–50 years based on growth ring analysis.

The species has no known medicinal or culinary uses. Its value is entirely ecological and scientific, as a component of Greece's unique high-altitude biodiversity.

In botanical gardens, Campanula merxmuelleri is grown in only a handful of European alpine collections. Successful cultivation requires careful attention to drainage and ventilation.
