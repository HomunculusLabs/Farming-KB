# Lentinula edodes

**Lentinula edodes** (Berk.) Pegler, commonly known as **shiitake**, is an edible and medicinal agaric mushroom in the family Omphalotaceae. It is the second most cultivated mushroom worldwide and one of the most thoroughly researched medicinal fungi, with particular importance for its immunomodulatory polysaccharide **lentinan**.

## Taxonomy and Description

Lentinula edodes produces medium to large agaric basidiocarps with convex to plane caps 5-12 cm in diameter. The pileus surface is light to dark brown, often with cracked or scaly patterns at maturity. Gills are white to cream, adnexed to free. The stipe is central, cylindrical, and whitish with an annulus. Spore print is white. It is a saprotrophic white-rot fungus that decomposes hardwood logs, especially species of *Quercus* (oak), *Castanopsis*, and *Shii* (Castanopsis cuspidata, the origin of the Japanese name). Native to East and Southeast Asia, it is now cultivated globally.

## Traditional and Culinary Use

Shiitake has been cultivated in China for approximately 1,000 years and in Japan since the 17th century, making it one of the oldest deliberately [[medicinal-mushrooms-biological-response-modifiers]] modifier (BRM). It was first isolated and characterized by Chihara and colleagues in Japan in the late 1960s. Lentinan has been approved as an anticancer drug in Japan since 1985 and is administered intravenously as an adjunct to chemotherapy.

### Mechanism of Action

Lentinan does not directly kill tumor cells. Instead, it acts as an immunopotentiator by activating the host immune system. Key mechanisms include:
- Activation of macrophages and enhancement of phagocytic activity
- Augmentation of natural killer (NK) cell cytotoxicity
- Potentiation of T-cell mediated immune responses
- Enhancement of antibody production
- Induction of cytokine release including IL-1, TNF-alpha, and interferons
- Activation of the alternative complement pathway

Lentinan is thought to bind to specific receptors on immune cells, particularly dectin-1 and complement receptor 3 (CR3), triggering downstream signaling cascades that amplify immune surveillance against tumors.

## Anticancer Activity

In the standard Sarcoma 180/mice model, lentinan demonstrates strong antitumor activity with tumor inhibition rates exceeding 90% in optimal dosing regimens. Clinical studies, primarily conducted in Japan, have shown that lentinan as an adjunct to conventional chemotherapy (particularly with tegafur or 5-FU in gastric cancer) significantly improves survival rates and extends life expectancy. Benefits have been most consistently demonstrated in gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Lentinan appears most effective when host immune function is partially intact.

## Additional Bioactive Compounds

Beyond lentinan, L. edodes contains:
- **Lentinacin** (eritadenine): an adenosine analogue with cholesterol-lowering properties
- **Lentysine**: antimicrobial activity
- **Protease inhibitors**: anti-HIV and anti-inflammatory potential
- **Lentinula edodes mycelium extract (LEM)**: a hot-water extract with immunomodulatory and antiviral properties
- **KS-2**: an alpha-mannan peptide with immunostimulatory activity
- **Ergosterol** and other sterols: vitamin D2 precursor

## Nutritional Composition

Dried shiitake mushrooms are nutritionally dense, containing approximately 50-55% carbohydrate, 15-20% protein, 3-5% fat, and 8-10% dietary fiber by dry weight. They are rich in B vitamins (particularly B2, B3, B5, and B6), vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol, produced from ergosterol upon UV exposure), and minerals including potassium, phosphorus, selenium, and zinc. The umami flavor results from high levels of glutamic acid and guanylic acid (5'-GMP). Shiitake also contains significant amounts of the nucleotide ergothioneine, a potent antioxidant that may contribute to its health-promoting properties.

## Antiviral Activity

Beyond its anticancer applications, L. edodes extracts have demonstrated antiviral activity against several human pathogens. Lentinan and LEM show inhibitory effects against influenza virus, herpes simplex virus, and hepatitis B virus in preclinical studies. The antiviral mechanisms involve both direct virucidal effects and enhancement of host immune defenses. LEM has been investigated as a complementary therapy in chronic hepatitis B infections, with some clinical studies reporting improvements in liver enzyme levels and viral markers. These antiviral properties, combined with the immunomodulatory effects, have generated interest in shiitake-derived compounds as broad-spectrum antiviral agents.

## Safety

Shiitake is generally safe as a food. However, consumption of raw or undercooked shiitake can cause **shiitake dermatitis**, a flagellate erythema rash attributed to lentinan. The dermatitis typically develops 12–72 hours after consumption and presents as linear, whip-like erythematous streaks on the trunk and extremities. The condition is self-limiting and resolves within 1–3 weeks, but can be intensely pruritic. Antihistamines and topical corticosteroids may provide symptomatic relief. Cooking shiitake thoroughly before consumption eliminates the risk of dermatitis, as the causative thermolabile component is destroyed by heat.

Oral lentinan preparations are well-tolerated. Intravenous lentinan may cause mild adverse reactions including fever, chills, and nausea in some patients. Rare cases of anaphylactoid reactions to intravenous lentinan have been reported. Shiitake may interact with anticoagulant medications due to its lentinacin (eritadenine) content, which can affect platelet aggregation. Patients on warfarin or other blood-thinning medications should exercise caution with high dietary intake of shiitake.

## Seasonality

In nature, *L. edodes* fruits in spring and autumn, triggered by temperature drops and increased moisture. The spring fruiting (typically March to May in East Asia) produces higher-quality, thicker-fleshed mushrooms known as *donko*, while the autumn fruiting (September to November) yields thinner-capped specimens called *koshin*. Modern cultivation manipulates temperature and moisture to mimic these seasonal triggers, allowing year-round production on controlled schedules.
