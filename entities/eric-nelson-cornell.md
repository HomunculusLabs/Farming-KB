# Eric Nelson (Cornell University)

## Description

Dr. Eric Nelson is a soil microbiologist at Cornell University whose research focuses on biological approaches to plant disease management, particularly in turf grass systems. His work is cited by Dr. [[elaine-ingham]] as important evidence that plate count methods have fundamental limitations for assessing soil health and disease suppressiveness. Nelson's research demonstrates that direct microscopic assessment of soil biology provides more meaningful data for understanding plant-microbe interactions.

## Classification

- **Type**: Person / Researcher

- **Era**: Active early 2000s (referenced in Ingham correspondence)

## Key Facts

- Cornell University, under researchers like Nelson, was forging ahead on understanding what different kinds of biological data reveal about soil health.
- Nelson was in the process of publishing a paper demonstrating that plate count assays and pathogen inhibition tests have no relationship to disease suppressiveness or plant response.
- This finding is significant because it undermines the conventional laboratory approach of using plate counts to assess [[compost]] tea quality or soil health.
- Dr. Ingham hoped to document the relationship between Nelson's turf grass health approaches and direct microscopic measurements to establish a science-based index of soil biological quality.
- Cornell is highlighted as one of the institutions making progress on integrating multiple biological assessment methods (direct counts, plate counts, enzyme assays) rather than relying on a single approach.
- The collaborative approach at Cornell (using multiple assessment methods for different questions) mirrors Ingham's philosophy that no single assay is universally best.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Assessment methodology**: Nelson's work supports the argument that direct microscopy reveals more about soil biological function than plate count methods, which capture only approximately 0.01% of soil organisms.
- **Turf grass management**: His biological approaches to turf health demonstrate that [[building-soil]] biology can replace chemical disease management in lawns and sports fields.
- **Compost tea validation**: Nelson's research provides scientific backing for the claim that biological assessment must go beyond simple bacterial enumeration to evaluate disease suppressiveness.
- **Interdisciplinary collaboration**: The relationship between Nelson's work at Cornell and Ingham's [[soil-food-web]] approach illustrates how multiple assessment methods each reveal different aspects of soil biology.
- **Practical implication**: For compost tea producers, Nelson's findings suggest that product quality should be assessed by organism type and biomass rather than total plate count numbers.
