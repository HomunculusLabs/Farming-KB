# Hypholoma fasciculare

Hypholoma fasciculare, commonly known as the sulphur tuft, is a
basidiomycete fungus that plays a significant role in wood
decomposition and soil ecology. It is a cord-forming species studied
for its mycelial [[mycelial-foraging-strategies-nutrient-translocation]], fractal growth patterns, and
interactions with soil invertebrates.

Unlike some other cord-formers, H. fasciculare produces surface
fractal mycelia optimized for exploiting patchily distributed
resources. The species is also notable for being poisonous to humans,
containing toxins that cause gastrointestinal symptoms if ingested.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Strophariaceae (Hymenogastraceae)
- **Genus:** Hypholoma
- **Species:** H. fasciculare

The genus Hypholoma includes several species commonly found on dead
wood, including H. capnoides and H. sublateritium. H. fasciculare is
the most widespread and commonly encountered species, found across
[[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of the Northern Hemisphere on diverse woody
substrates.

## Mycelial Architecture

H. fasciculare produces surface fractal mycelia, a growth form
distinct from the mass fractal mycelia of [[phanerochaete-velutina]] and
Resinicium bicolor. Surface fractal mycelia are characterized by
dense, undifferentiated growth filling space more thoroughly, suited
for exploiting homogeneously distributed resources at nutrient-rich
sites.

Stropharia species share similar growth patterns and are commonly
found on compost heaps and plant rhizomes including [[urtica-dioica]]. The
relationship between mycelial architecture and ecological strategy
reflects fundamental trade-offs in fungal [[mycelial-foraging-resource-allocation]] between
exploration and exploitation.

## Resource Colonization

H. fasciculare shows a broad resource colonization strategy. It
colonizes large woody resources (tree stumps, logs), small woody
debris (leaves, twigs, cupules), non-woody litter, and mixed
substrates. This catholic preference distinguishes it from more
specialized cord-formers and reflects its surface fractal growth
strategy.

## Interactions with Soil Invertebrates

H. fasciculare mycelial systems respond dynamically to grazing by
Collembola (springtails). When subject to varying grazing intensities
by Folsomia candida, Hypogastrura cf. tullbergi, and Proisotoma
minuta, the extra-resource mycelium changes from slow, dense
exploitative growth to different patterns depending on grazing
pressure.

The response differs from Phanerochaete velutina and Resinicium
bicolor, highlighting species-specific ecological strategies and
differential vulnerabilities to faunal grazing. These differences may
reflect adaptations to different ecological niches and foraging
strategies in soil environments.

## Temporal Changes in Mycelial Patterns

Mycelial pattern characteristics change over time, including fractal
dimension (space-filling ability), resource status influence on
exploration versus exploitation balance, seasonal dynamics varying
with environmental conditions, and network remodeling in response to
changing resource distributions in the soil environment.
