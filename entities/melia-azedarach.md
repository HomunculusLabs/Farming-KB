## Overview

**Melia azedarach**, commonly known as **chinaberry**, **white cedar**, or **Persian lilac**, is a fast-growing deciduous tree in the family Meliaceae. Native to South and [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]], it has been widely planted as an ornamental and [[mollison-designers-legume-shade-tree-pasture-systems]] throughout tropical and subtropical regions worldwide.

The tree is notable for its insecticidal properties, particularly the limonoid compounds in its seeds and leaves. Aqueous extracts have been used traditionally as botanical insecticides, piscicides, and anthelmintics across Asia, Africa, and the Americas.

Despite its medicinal and agricultural uses, M. azedarach is classified as an invasive species in many parts of the world. Its prolific [[query-how-do-i-prune-brambles-for-maximum-fruit-production]] and shade tolerance allow it to outcompete native vegetation in disturbed habitats.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

M. azedarach was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 and is the type species of the genus *Melia*. The genus name derives from the Greek *melia*, referring to the ash tree, due to the similar appearance of the leaves.

The tree reaches 10–15 m in height with a spreading crown and a trunk diameter of 30–60 cm. The bark is dark gray-brown, fissured longitudinally with age, and slightly bitter to taste.

Leaves are bipinnately compound, 30–60 cm long, bearing 3–5 pairs of pinnae, each with 5–11 ovate-lanceolate leaflets 3–8 cm long. Leaflets are dark green above, paler below, with serrate margins.

The inflorescence is a large, axillary panicle bearing numerous small, fragrant, lilac to pale violet flowers 8–10 mm in diameter. Each flower has five fused petals forming a tube. Flowering occurs in spring.

Fruits are spherical drupes 1–1.5 cm in diameter, yellowish when ripe, persisting on the tree after leaf fall. Each fruit contains 1–5 seeds. The fruits are poisonous to humans and livestock.

## Distribution and Habitat

M. azedarach is native to the Indian subcontinent, southern China, Southeast Asia, and northern Australia. It has been introduced to Africa, the Americas, the Mediterranean, and [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-hawaii-pacific-islands]] as an ornamental and timber tree.

The species grows in a wide range of habitats from sea level to 1800 m elevation. It is found in dry deciduous forests, riparian zones, and disturbed areas. It tolerates drought, poor soils, and moderate salinity.

Chinaberry is considered invasive in the southeastern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], eastern Australia, South Africa, and several Pacific islands. It forms dense monospecific stands that shade out native understory species and reduce biodiversity.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The seeds and bark of M. azedarach contain limonoid triterpenoids, primarily **azedarachin**, **melianone**, **toosendanin**, and **meliatoxin**. These compounds are responsible for the plant's insecticidal and anthelmintic activities.

Toosendanin, isolated from the bark of closely related *Melia toosendan*, is a potent selective antagonist of the insect GABA receptor. It has been developed as a commercial botanical insecticide in China.

The fruits contain meliatoxins (tetranortriterpenoids) that are toxic to mammals. Ingestion causes gastrointestinal distress, vomiting, and in severe cases, neurological symptoms including seizures and respiratory paralysis.

Leaf and seed extracts have demonstrated antimicrobial activity against bacteria and fungi in vitro. Antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytotoxic activities have also been reported for various plant extracts.

## Traditional Uses and Ecology

In traditional Chinese medicine, the bark and fruits of M. azedarach (known as *ku-lian-pi*) are used to expel intestinal parasites, particularly roundworms and tapeworms. The preparation is typically a decoction of dried bark.

In Indian Ayurvedic practice, the leaves are applied externally for [[blesching-cannabis-skin-diseases]] and rheumatic pain. A leaf paste is used as a poultice [[cannabis-for-inflammatory-conditions]]. The bark is used as an antipyretic.

Throughout tropical Africa and the Americas, aqueous seed extracts are used as botanical insecticides in stored grain protection. The seeds are crushed and soaked in water, and the resulting extract is applied to crops or stored produce.

The tree provides shade and ornamental value in agroforestry systems. Its flowers are attractive to pollinators, and the dense canopy provides nesting habitat for birds.

The ripe fruits persist on bare branches through winter, providing a food source for birds that disperse the seeds widely. This bird-mediated dispersal contributes significantly to the species' invasive potential in non-native ranges.

## Cultivation

M. azedarach is propagated from seed, which requires scarification or soaking to improve germination. Germination typically occurs within 2–4 weeks. [[hamilton-vegetative-propagation]] by cuttings and root suckers is also practical.

The tree grows rapidly in its early years, often 1–2 m per year, and can begin flowering and fruiting within 3–5 years. It prefers well-drained soils and full sun but is highly adaptable to various conditions.

Pruning is recommended to maintain a strong structure, as the branches can be brittle. The tree coppices readily from the stump, making it suitable for fuelwood production in agroforestry systems.

In regions where it is invasive, planting M. azedarach is discouraged. Local authorities recommend removing existing trees and replacing them with non-invasive native alternatives.

## Timber and Economic Uses

The wood of M. azedarach is moderately hard, medium-textured, and pale yellow to reddish-brown. It works easily and takes a good finish, making it suitable for furniture, cabinetry, and decorative carving.

Heartwood has natural resistance to decay and termites, giving it utility for outdoor applications and fence posts. The wood is also used for fuel and [[mollison-designers-charcoal-production-biochar-soil-amendment]] in rural communities.

Despite these qualities, commercial [[honey-locust-pod-fodder-timber-production]] of M. azedarach is limited. The relatively small trunk diameter and tendency for branches to develop low on the trunk restrict its use to small-scale applications.
