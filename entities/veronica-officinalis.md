## Overview

**Veronica officinalis** L., commonly known as common speedwell, gypsyweed, or Paul's betony, is a low-growing perennial herb in the family Plantaginaceae. It is native to Europe and western Asia and is recognized for its traditional use as a mild [[aact-foliar-sticker-spreaders-leaf-surface-attachment-ingham]] is pubescent with short, appressed hairs, while the lower surface is glandular-pubescent.

The inflorescence is a terminal raceme that elongates as flowers open from the base upward. Each raceme bears 10–30 flowers on short pedicels. Flowers are approximately 6–8 mm in diameter with a pale blue to lilac corolla.

The corolla has four petals, with the upper petal slightly larger and the lower petal smallest. The two stamens extend slightly beyond the corolla tube. Flowering occurs from June to August across most of the native range.

The fruit is a compressed, rounded capsule, 3–4 mm wide, notched at the apex, containing numerous small brown seeds. Seeds are flattened, approximately 1 mm long, and are dispersed by wind, water, and animal movement.

Seed viability is relatively short, with most seeds losing germinability within one to two years under field conditions. A persistent soil seed bank is not a significant feature of this species, relying instead on vegetative spread for long-term persistence.

The root system consists of fibrous, shallow roots arising from the creeping stems at nodes. This adventitious rooting pattern enables rapid colonization of suitable habitat from even small stem fragments.

## Ethnobotany

Common speedwell has been used in European folk medicine since antiquity. The Latin name *Veronica* may derive from *vera icon* (true image), referencing the herb's perceived healing power or the resemblance of the flower markings to a human face.

In traditional British herbalism, speedwell tea was consumed as a spring tonic and blood purifier. It was used for respiratory ailments including coughs, bronchitis, and asthma, and was sometimes combined with lungwort and coltsfoot in compound formulations.

The herb was also applied externally as a poultice for wounds, ulcers, and skin eruptions. Gypsies reportedly used the plant as a treatment for gout and rheumatic complaints, contributing to the common name gypsyweed.

In Scandinavian folk medicine, speedwell was used as a diuretic and treatment for kidney stones. The herb was also employed as a bitter tonic to stimulate appetite and aid digestion, particularly in spring when fresh greens were scarce.

The dried herb was historically traded in European apothecary shops under the name *Herba Veronicae*. It appeared in the London Pharmacopoeia of 1650 and remained in official drug listings until the early 20th century in several European countries.
