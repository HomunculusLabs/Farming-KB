## Overview

**Pimenta dioica** (L.) Merr., commonly known as allspice, pimento, or Jamaica
pepper, is a tropical evergreen tree in the family Myrtaceae. Native to the
Greater Antilles (particularly Jamaica) and parts of [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]], it is
cultivated for its dried unripe berries, which possess a unique flavor profile
combining the aromas of cinnamon, nutmeg, clove, and [[comparison-black-pepper-vs-cardamom-tropical-spice-cultivation]] — hence the
name "allspice."

The species is the source of one of the most important spice commodities in
the Americas, with Jamaica producing approximately 60–70% of the global supply.
Allspice oil, rich in eugenol, is also used in perfumery, pharmaceutical
preparations, and traditional medicine.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Plantae — **Family:** Myrtaceae — **Genus:** Pimenta — **Species:** P. dioica (L.) Merr.

Pimenta dioica was originally described as *Myrtus dioica* by Linnaeus in
1759 and transferred to *Pimenta* by Merrill in 1947. The genus name derives
from the Spanish "pimienta" (pepper), reflecting early European confusion
with [[comparison-cacao-vs-black-pepper-tropical-crop-cultivation]]. The specific epithet "dioica" refers to the species'
functionally dioecious reproductive system, though the [[cannabis-sex-determination-floral-biology]] is more
complex than true dioecy.

P. dioica is a slow-growing evergreen tree reaching 7–12 meters in height
under cultivation, with [[strain-isolation-from-wild-specimens]] occasionally reaching 18 meters. The
trunk is short and often twisted, with bark that is smooth and grey when
young, becoming rough and fissured with age.

Leaves are opposite, simple, elliptic to oblong, 8–15 cm long and 3–6 cm
wide, leathery, glossy dark green above and paler below. When crushed, the
leaves release a strong aromatic fragrance due to their essential oil content.

Flowers are small (1–1.5 cm diameter), white, borne in axillary panicles.
The species exhibits a complex sexual system: some trees produce only
functionally staminate flowers, others produce hermaphroditic flowers with
reduced fertility, and some populations include functionally pistillate
individuals.

The fruit is a globose berry 5–8 mm in diameter, green when unripe, turning
purple-black at maturity. The commercially harvested "berries" are the
dried unripe green fruits, which retain the characteristic allspice aroma
upon drying.

## Distribution and Habitat

Pimenta dioica is native to the Caribbean islands of Jamaica, Cuba, Hispaniola,
and Puerto Rico, as well as southern Mexico (Veracruz, Tabasco), Belize,
Guatemala, and Honduras. The species grows naturally in moist limestone
forests and woodlands at elevations of 100–800 meters.

Jamaican allspice is considered the world's finest, particularly from the
parishes of St. Ann, St. Mary, and Portland on the island's north coast. The
unique terroir of Jamaican pimento — volcanic-derived limestone soils,
consistent humidity, and specific temperature regimes — produces berries with
higher eugenol content and more complex flavor than material from other origins.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The dried berries contain 3–5% essential oil, with the major constituents
being eugenol (65–85%), methyleugenol (5–12%), 1,8-cineole (2–5%), and
α-terpineol (1–3%). Jamaican allspice oil is characteristically high in
eugenol (>80%), distinguishing it from Central American material which
contains more methyleugenol.
