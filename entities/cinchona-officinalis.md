# Cinchona officinalis (*Quinquina*)

## Overview

Cinchona officinalis is a species of evergreen tree in the family Rubiaceae, native to the Andean montane forests of [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]].

It is the original and most historically significant source of quinine, the first effective antimalarial compound, which revolutionized the treatment of malaria worldwide.

The bark of Cinchona species has been used medicinally for over 400 years and remains commercially important as a source of quinine, quinidine, and other cinchona alkaloids.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Angiosperms, Eudicots, Asterids
- **Order:** Gentianales
- **Family:** Rubiaceae
- **Genus:** *Cinchona* L.
- **Species:** *C. officinalis* L.

**Authority:** Carl Linnaeus, 1753.

The genus name honors the Countess of Chinchón, wife of the Viceroy of Peru, who according to legend was cured of malaria with cinchona bark in the 1630s.

This account is now considered apocryphal but persisted in botanical literature for centuries.

**Synonyms:** *Cinchona calisaya* Wedd., *Cinchona ledgeriana* (Moens) Trimen (high-quinine variety), *[[cinchona-succirubra]]* Pav.

Taxonomic boundaries within the genus remain contested, with 23–40 species recognized depending on authority.

## Morphology and Distribution

Cinchona officinalis is a medium-sized evergreen tree reaching 10–15 m in cultivation, occasionally 25 m in the wild.

**Leaves:** Opposite, elliptic to lanceolate, 10–25 × 4–12 cm, glossy dark green above, paler below. Margin entire, petiole 1–3 cm with interpetiolar stipules.

**Flowers:** Terminal panicles, 10–30 cm, with fragrant pink to salmon tubular corollas ~1.5–2 cm long. Flowering occurs during the rainy season.

**Fruit:** Ovoid capsule 1–2 cm with numerous flat-winged wind-dispersed seeds.

**Bark:** Thick, rough, deeply fissured, bitter-tasting, reddish-brown to yellowish. The medicinal bark is harvested from trunk and major branches.

**Distribution:** Native to eastern Andean slopes of Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia at 1,500–3,000 m elevation.

Widely planted in tropical highlands of India, Sri Lanka, Java, and Africa.

## Phytochemistry

The bark contains a complex mixture of quinoline alkaloids known as cinchona alkaloids.

The four major alkaloids and their approximate ranges in dried bark:

- **Quinine:** 2–8% of dry bark weight (primary antimalarial)
- **Quinidine:** 0.5–3% (antiarrhythmic drug)
- **Cinchonine:** 0.5–4% (bittering agent, limited pharmacology)
- **Cinchonidine:** 0.5–3% (synthetic precursor)

Additional constituents include cinchonamine, quinicine, and tannins (5–10%). The alkaloid profile varies significantly with species, provenance, tree age, and bark position.

*Cinchona ledgeriana* varieties produce the highest quinine content, up to 13% in selected clones.

Quinine (C₂₀H₂₄N₂O₂) has a molecular weight of 324.4 g/mol and is a diastereomer of quinidine.

## Pharmacology and Medicinal Use

**Antimalarial activity:** Quinine binds to heme within the malaria parasite's food vacuole, preventing heme polymerization into hemozoin.

This accumulation of free heme is lethal to *Plasmodium falciparum* trophozoites.
