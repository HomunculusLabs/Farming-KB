## Overview

**Heroin** (diacetylmorphine, DAM) is a semi-synthetic opioid derived from [[morphine]] by acetylation of the 3-hydroxyl and 6-hydroxyl groups. It was first synthesized in 1874 by Charles Romley Alder Wright at St. Mary's Hospital Medical School in London, though its analgesic properties were not recognized until Heinrich Dreser at Bayer Pharmaceuticals began commercial marketing in 1898 under the trade name Heroin (from the German *heroisch*, meaning heroic).

Heroin is one of the most potent and widely abused opioid drugs globally. It acts as a prodrug, being rapidly metabolized in the brain to 6-monoacetylmorphine (6-MAM) and then to [[morphine]], which binds to mu-opioid receptors (MOR) to produce profound analgesia, euphoria, and central nervous system depression. Its high lipid solubility compared to morphine enables rapid blood-brain barrier penetration, accounting for its characteristic intense rush.

The substance is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under the United Nations Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs (1961). Despite its legal status, heroin remains a major contributor to the global opioid crisis, with an estimated 16 million users worldwide. Recent decades have seen a shift from heroin to synthetic opioids such as [[fentanyl]] in many markets, though heroin remains the dominant illicit opioid in parts of Europe, Asia, and Africa.

## Chemical Properties

**IUPAC name:** (5α,6α)-7,8-didehydro-4,5-epoxy-17-methylmorphinan-3,6-diol diacetate

**Molecular formula:** C21H23NO5

**Molecular weight:** 369.41 g/mol

**SMILES:** CC(=O)O[C@H]1[C@@H]2C[C@H]3CC4=C(C=C(C=C4[C@H]2[NH]C(=O)[C@H]1OC(=O)C)O)O

**CAS number:** 561-27-3

Heroin base is a white crystalline powder at room temperature, though street samples range from white to brown depending on purity [[mushroom-storage-and-processing-methods]]. It is soluble in water (as the hydrochloride salt), ethanol, chloroform, and slightly soluble in ether. The freebase form is volatile enough to be vaporized for inhalation, which distinguishes it pharmacokinetically from [[morphine]].

The diacetylation of morphine dramatically increases lipophilicity, which is the key structural feature responsible for heroin's rapid onset of action. This modification increases the compound's ability to cross the blood-brain barrier by approximately 100-fold compared to morphine, resulting in brain concentrations 10–100 times higher at equivalent doses. Once in the CNS, heroin undergoes rapid deacetylation by brain esterases.

Heroin hydrochloride decomposes upon exposure to heat, light, and moisture, with the acetate groups hydrolyzing to yield 6-MAM and ultimately morphine. This degradation pathway complicates [[shirota-japan-2002-magic-mushroom-regulation-forensic-analysis]] of seized samples, as the relative proportions of heroin, 6-MAM, and morphine serve as indicators of sample age and [[spawn-storage-conditions]].

Street heroin is rarely pure, typically containing a mixture of active opioid, cutting agents (caffeine, paracetamol, lactose, mannitol), and occasionally adulterants with pharmacological activity (fentanyl, [[cocaine]], clenbuterol). The choice of cutting agents can alter the drug's appearance, melting point, and bioavailability, further complicating harm reduction efforts and forensic characterization.

## Pharmacology

Heroin functions primarily as a **prodrug** that is rapidly deacetylated to its active metabolites. The pharmacological cascade proceeds through three sequential steps:

- **Heroin to 6-MAM:** First-pass deacetylation occurs rapidly in the brain, liver, and blood, mediated by butyrylcholinesterase and carboxylesterases. 6-MAM retains the 6-acetyl group and has higher MOR affinity than morphine itself.
- **6-MAM to morphine:** Subsequent deacetylation at the 6-position yields [[morphine]], the ultimate active metabolite responsible for most of heroin's pharmacological effects. This conversion occurs more slowly than the first step.
- **Morphine to M6G and M3G:** Morphine is further metabolized via glucuronidation to morphine-6-glucuronide (M6G, a potent MOR agonist) and morphine-3-glucuronide (M3G, which lacks analgesic activity and may antagonize MOR effects).

Heroin and 6-MAM bind to mu-opioid receptors with higher affinity and faster kinetics than morphine, producing the characteristic "rush" experienced by users. The rush is mediated by rapid MOR activation in the ventral tegmental area, causing a massive phasic dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens. This mechanism underlies heroin's exceptional reinforcing properties and addiction liability.

Heroin also activates delta-opioid receptors (DOR) and kappa-opioid receptors (KOR) at higher concentrations, contributing to dysphoric side effects at excessive doses. The elimination half-life of heroin itself is only 2–6 minutes, while 6-MAM has a half-life of 6–25 minutes and morphine persists for 2–4 hours, determining the total duration of effects.

Tolerance to heroin develops rapidly, with users requiring dose escalation of 2–3 times the initial dose within weeks of daily use. Cross-tolerance extends to all mu-opioid agonists, complicating opioid rotation strategies in pain management [[psilocybin-and-addiction-treatment]]. The neurobiological basis of tolerance involves MOR desensitization, beta-arrestin recruitment, and compensatory upregulation of adenylyl cyclase activity.

## Effects

**Typical dose range:** 5–20 mg (insufflated); 5–15 mg (intravenous); varies enormously with tolerance.

**Duration:** 3–5 hours, with rush at 10–30 seconds (IV), onset at 5–10 minutes (insufflated), peak at 30–60 minutes.

**Subjective effects profile:**

- **Euphoria and rush:** An intense, warm, pleasurable sensation concentrated in the abdomen and chest, described as a full-body orgasm. The rush is uniquely intense and rapid with intravenous administration, producing a brief but overwhelming wave of physical and emotional gratification.
- **Analgesia:** Profound suppression of physical pain, mediated by MOR activation in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord and descending pain inhibitory pathways. Pain relief exceeds that of most other opioids at equivalent doses.
- **Sedation:** Progressive drowsiness and transition between wakefulness and sleep ("nodding"), characteristic of opioid intoxication. Users may alternate between conscious and semi-conscious states for extended periods.

**Common side effects:** Respiratory depression (the primary cause of fatal overdose), constipation, nausea and vomiting, miosis (pinpoint pupils), pruritus (itching), hypotension, bradycardia, urinary retention, and immunosuppression. Chronic use produces severe physical dependence with a protracted and painful withdrawal syndrome.

**Withdrawal syndrome:** Onset within 6–12 hours of last dose, peaking at 48–72 hours. Symptoms include lacrimation, rhinorrhea, piloerection ("gooseflesh"), hyperalgesia, gastrointestinal cramping, diarrhea, mydriasis, tachycardia, hypertension, anxiety, insomnia, and intense drug craving. Acute withdrawal typically resolves within 7–10 days, though protracted abstinence syndrome (dysphoria, anhedonia, insomnia) may persist for months.

The severity of heroin withdrawal, while intensely unpleasant, is generally not life-threatening in otherwise healthy adults. However, withdrawal management in clinical settings often employs methadone, buprenorphine, or clonidine to mitigate symptoms and improve treatment retention. Relapse rates following acute detoxification remain extremely high without concurrent psychosocial support and long-term pharmacotherapy.

## Toxicology

The primary mechanism of fatal heroin overdose is **respiratory depression** through MOR-mediated suppression of the brainstem respiratory centers (pre-Bötzinger complex). At lethal doses, heroin-induced respiratory depression progresses to apnea, hypoxia, and cardiac arrest. The narrow [[cannabis-therapeutic-index-overview]], combined with unpredictable street purity (typically 10–60%), makes accidental overdose a constant risk.

**Naloxone reversal:** The opioid antagonist naloxone is the standard treatment for heroin overdose, competitively displacing heroin and its metabolites from MOR. Due to heroin's short half-life, repeated naloxone administration may be required as 6-MAM and morphine continue to be formed from tissue stores. The increasing prevalence of [[fentanyl]]-adulterated heroin has complicated overdose reversal, as fentanyl requires higher and more prolonged naloxone dosing.
