# Psilocybe caerulescens

**Psilocybe caerulescens** is a psilocybin-containing mushroom species notable for its association with Mesoamerican indigenous ceremonies and its preference for disturbed, clay-rich soils. It is one of the species believed to be among the original **teonanácatl** ("flesh of the gods") used by Aztec and Mazatec peoples. It was one of three species featured in F.C. Ghouled's 1972 *Field Guide to the Psilocybin Mushroom*, selected for its historical and cultural significance.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae (formerly Strophariaceae)
- **Genus:** Psilocybe
- **Species:** P. caerulescens

The species epithet *caerulescens* means "becoming blue," referring to the prominent blue bruising reaction of the flesh. The species has several synonyms and has been variously classified. Some mycologists treat it as part of a species complex with *Psilocybe mexicana* and *Psilocybe weilii*.

Common names include **"tierra azul"** (blue earth) in Mexico, referring to both its habitat in dark clay soils and its bluing reaction.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Psilocybe caerulescens* has a more restricted distribution compared to many other psilocybin mushrooms:

- **Mexico:** The primary range, found in the central Mexican highlands, particularly the states of Oaxaca, Puebla, Morelos, and Mexico State. It is especially associated with the Mazatec region around Huautla de Jiménez.
- **United States:** Scattered reports from the southeastern states, particularly Mississippi, Georgia, and Alabama, where it fruits in red clay soils along riverbanks.
- **Central and South America:** Rare reports from Guatemala and Colombia.
- **Venezuela:** Reported from Andean cloud forest regions.

### Habitat Preferences

*P. caerulescens* is unusual among psilocybin mushrooms for its habitat preferences:

- **Clay soils:** Strongly associated with disturbed, compacted clay-rich earth — roadsides, trail edges, riverbanks, and landslide areas
- **Not coprophilous:** Unlike *P. cubensis* and *Panaeolus subbalteatus*, it does not grow directly on dung, though it may fruit in manured pastures where clay soils are present
- **Seasonality:** In Mexico, it fruits during the rainy season (June through September) following heavy rains
- **Altitude:** Found from lowland valleys up to approximately 2,000 meters elevation in the Mexican highlands

## Morphology

The fruiting body is medium-sized with distinctive coloration:

- **Cap (Pileus):** 20–70 mm diameter, convex to broadly convex, sometimes with a slight umbo. The cap color is a distinctive **silvery-gray to blue-gray** with a brownish or ochraceous center, setting it apart from the golden-brown tones of *P. cubensis*. Surface is smooth, slightly viscid when wet, and distinctly hygrophanous — fading to a pale straw or nearly white color when dry. The cap margin is often slightly translucent-striate at maturity.
- **Gills (Lamellae):** Adnate to sinuate (notched), moderately close to subdistant. Gray when young, darkening to deep purple-black with spore maturity. Gill edges are whitish and fringed.
- **Stem (Stipe):** 40–100 mm long, 4–10 mm thick, cylindrical, equal or slightly thickened at the base. Color is pale brown to whitish, often with a bluish or bluish-green tint at the base. **Bruises blue prominently**, especially at the base and where damaged — the most intense bluing among the three species covered in Ghouled's guide. The stem is solid to partially hollow.
- **Veil:** Partial veil is thin and evanescent; may leave a fibrillose annular zone on the upper stem that is easily washed away by rain.
- **Spore Print:** Dark purple-brown to nearly black.
- **Spores:** Ellipsoidal to broadly ellipsoidal, 8–12 × 6–8 µm, smooth, with a distinct germ pore.
- **Flesh:** Thin, whitish, staining intensely blue when cut or bruised.
- **Odor:** Mild, farinaceous or slightly aromatic.

## Chemical Composition

*Psilocybe caerulescens* contains **psilocybin** and **psilocin** in moderate concentrations. Reported alkaloid content is approximately 0.2–0.6% psilocybin and 0.01–0.05% psilocin in dried material, placing it in the moderate potency range — stronger than *Panaeolus subbalteatus* but generally less potent than *Psilocybe cubensis* or *Psilocybe azurescens*.

Baeocystin and norbaeocystin have also been detected in varying quantities. The intense blue bruising suggests significant psilocin content in fresh tissue.

## Cultural and Ethnobotanical Significance

*P. caerulescens* holds a central place in the history of psychedelic mushroom research due to its probable identification as one of the original **Aztec teonanácatl**:

- **Pre-Columbian use:** Archaeological evidence — mushroom stones, ceramics, and codices — suggests ceremonial use of psilocybin mushrooms in central Mexico dating back at least 3,000 years
- **Mazatec tradition:** The Mazatec people of Oaxaca, Mexico, maintained an unbroken tradition of using psilocybin mushrooms in healing and divination ceremonies. María Sabina, the Mazatec curandera who became famous after R. Gordon Wasson's 1955 visit, worked with multiple species including *P. caerulescens*
- **Wasson expedition:** R. Gordon Wasson and his wife Valentina Pavlovna Wasson traveled to Huautla de Jiménez in 1955, becoming the first Westerners to participate in a Mazatec velada (night ceremony). Their subsequent *Life* magazine article ("Seeking the Magic Mushroom," 1957) introduced psilocybin mushrooms to the Western world
- **Albert Hofmann:** Following the Wasson expedition, Albert Hofmann (the discoverer of LSD) received specimens from Wasson and isolated **psilocybin** and **psilocin** from them in 1958. While the original specimens are often attributed to *P. mexicana*, *P. caerulescens* was likely among the species used
- **Gordon Wasson's identification:** Wasson himself considered *P. caerulescens* a strong candidate for the original teonanácatl, noting its abundance in the Mazatec region and its powerful effects

## Similar Species and Identification

Key distinguishing features of *P. caerulescens*:

- **Blue-gray cap coloration** is unique among common psilocybin mushrooms
- **Clay soil habitat** is unusual; most psilocybin species prefer dung or wood
- **Intense blue bruising** at the stem base

Potentially confusing species include:

- **Psilocybe mexicana:** Smaller, with a more conical cap and preference for grasslands rather than clay. Found in similar Mexican habitats but less robust.
- **Psilocybe stuntzii:** Pacific Northwest species with similar bluing but different habitat (wood chips, mulch).
- **Deconica species:** Inactive lookalikes in the *Deconica* genus (formerly *Psilocybe*) that share similar cap colors but lack blue bruising and psychoactive compounds.

## Cultivation

*P. caerulescens* is rarely cultivated due to its specific habitat requirements and relatively slow growth. Unlike *P. cubensis*, which adapts readily to artificial substrates, *P. caerulescens* appears to require the mineral composition and microbial ecology of clay-rich soils. A few successful cultivation attempts have used sterilized clay-loam mixtures supplemented with organic matter.
