## Mushroom Absorption Toxicity

## Description

Mushroom absorption toxicity is a concept documented by Julius Auboineau Palmer describing the ability of toxic alkaloids from poisonous mushrooms to transfer to and affect other organisms through multiple pathways beyond direct digestion. Palmer demonstrated through personal experimentation that the poisonous principle of *Amanita* mushrooms could be absorbed through skin contact, olfactory exposure, and cross-contamination of edible species stored in proximity to toxic ones.

## Classification

- **Category**: Toxicological phenomenon
- **First documented by**: Julius Auboineau Palmer (1870s)
- **Primary alkaloid involved**: Amanitine (Palmer's umbrella term for *Amanita* toxins)
- **Significance**: Challenged the prevailing assumption that mushroom poisoning occurs only through ingestion

## Pathways of Toxic Exposure

### 1. Digestive Absorption

The primary and most dangerous route. The alkaloid passes through the stomach mixed with food, is absorbed through the intestinal villi, and enters the systemic circulation. Palmer noted that even after the physical mushroom debris is expelled (via emetics or purgatives), the absorbed poison continues to act on the body.

Key characteristics:
- **Delayed onset**: 8–15 hours between ingestion and first symptoms in fatal cases
- **Complete absorption**: The system may remain "so charged with poison as to refuse to rally" even after the fungus is removed
- **Cholera-like progression**: Symptoms resemble Asiatic cholera from attack to post-mortem

### 2. Cross-Contamination Absorption

Palmer documented that edible mushrooms stored alongside poisonous ones absorb the toxic alkaloid:

> "About four years ago, a number of poisonous mushrooms (not Amanitas but of a totally different family), were sent me with edible fungus. The two varieties had lain twelve hours in the same box. The noxious ones were rejected, the esculent washed and eaten."

**Result**: Within 30 minutes, Palmer experienced violent perspiration, vertigo, trembling, chills, nausea, purging, and tenesmus — all from eating mushrooms that were themselves perfectly safe but had absorbed poison from their neighbors.

Mechanism: "The virus exercises the same power of permeation through and absorption by the human system that the yeast (a true fungus) has over the dough."

### 3. Dermal (Skin) Absorption

Palmer demonstrated that the toxin can be absorbed through the skin:

> "An Amanita held in the closed hand will produce all the symptoms of poisoning, even to convulsions."

**Incident**: While perspiring from a long walk, Palmer carried a large bunch of *Amanita* specimens for an artist. Seated in a close car, holding them in his warm hand through a paper wrapper, he experienced "fearful nausea" and all the symptoms of seasickness, relieved only by distance from the mushrooms.

### 4. Olfactory (Smell) Absorption

Perhaps the most striking pathway. Palmer documented two incidents:

**First incident**: A friend sent two *Amanita* specimens in a confined box. Palmer smelled them several times and left the box near his desk. Three hours later, he suffered vomiting and stomach oppression. The whites of his eyes became livid, and the "leaden color" of his face was still noticeable the following day.

**Second incident**: Palmer chewed a piece of *Amanita* the size of a half-dime, ejecting the substance but swallowing a little saliva. Symptoms included throat constriction, vertigo, disgust with employment (similar to seasickness), and delayed intestinal effects 17 hours later.

### 5. Inoculation Between Fungi

Palmer observed that fungi "inoculate each other, either with or without contact, so that if edible and noxious toadstools be gathered together, the former will absorb the properties of the latter."

## The Yeast Analogy

Palmer drew an extended analogy between fungal toxin permeation and the action of yeast on dough:

- Yeast (a true fungus) permeates dough completely, transforming it throughout
- Poisonous mushroom alkaloids similarly permeate the entire human system through absorption
- Both processes demonstrate the fundamental biological property of fungal compounds to spread through and transform their substrate
- This analogy was used to explain the once-current error that mushrooms "were dangerous from their power to indefinitely multiply themselves in the blood"

## Implications for Foraging Safety

Palmer's observations have profound practical implications:

1. **Never mix species in collection**: Edible and unknown/poisonous mushrooms must be kept in separate containers
2. **Handle with care**: Poisonous species should not be handled with bare, warm, or perspiring hands
3. **Avoid smelling**: Deliberately inhaling near poisonous species can cause symptoms
4. **Wash thoroughly**: Even edible species that have been stored near unknown mushrooms should be treated with caution
5. **Physical separation in transport**: Use separate bags, boxes, or baskets for different species

## Animal Resistance

Palmer noted that animals show varying resistance to mushroom toxins:

- **Swine**: Eat *Amanita* stew and *A. muscaria* without ill effects
- **Rabbits**: Unharmed by some non-esculent fungi that sicken humans
- **Dogs**: Can be sickened by perfectly edible species (*Coprinus comatus*)
- **Cats**: Said to have died in convulsions from *Marasmius oreades* (an edible species)

This animal-human discrepancy reinforces that mushroom edibility is species-specific and cannot be inferred from animal feeding tests.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Palmer's absorption toxicity concept was remarkably prescient:

- **Modern toxicology** confirms that amatoxins are absorbed through the gastrointestinal tract and affect the liver systemically
- **Cross-contamination** remains a concern in wild mushroom foraging
- **Dermal sensitivity** to fungal compounds is well-documented in modern allergology
- **Individual sensitization**: Palmer's observation that "constant use develops an extreme sensitiveness" to fungal toxins aligns with modern understanding of allergic sensitization
