## Description

The Soil Food Web is the interconnected community of organisms living in soil — bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, earthworms, and larger fauna — that cycle nutrients, build soil structure, and suppress plant diseases. Dr. Elaine Ingham popularized the concept as a practical framework for understanding soil health and guiding compost tea applications.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Soil ecology / Soil microbiology
- **Category**: Ecological concept
- **Related frameworks**: Soil foodweb analysis (SFI), direct-count microscopy

## Key Facts

- The **complete food web** includes bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, and earthworms working in concert
- Each trophic level plays a distinct role: bacteria initiate decomposition of simple compounds; fungi process complex materials like lignin and cellulose; protozoa and nematodes graze on bacteria and fungi, releasing plant-available nutrients
- Dr. Ingham emphasizes that **all components** are needed — adding only bacteria back to degraded soil provides temporary improvement but "stalls" because the full food web must be restored for lasting results
- The food web directly controls nutrient retention, nutrient availability to plants, disease suppression, and soil structure development
- Compost tea aims to deliver the **whole food web** — not just bacteria — to restore degraded soils
- Healthy soils contain approximately 25,000 unique DNA strands per gram as measured by molecular assays

## Role in Nutrient Cycling

Bacteria start decomposition by consuming simple sugars and readily available carbon compounds. Fungi follow by breaking down complex materials — lignin, cellulose, and other resistant compounds — producing humic acids as metabolic byproducts. Protozoa and nematodes consume bacteria and fungi, mineralizing nitrogen and other nutrients into plant-available forms. Earthworms and microarthropods physically fragment organic matter and help maintain fungal dominance in soil structure.

## Disease Suppression Mechanism

The soil food web suppresses disease through:
1. **Competition** — beneficial organisms occupy all infection sites and consume available food resources before pathogens can establish
2. **Predation** — protozoa and nematodes consume pathogenic organisms
3. **Antibiosis** — some beneficial fungi and bacteria produce compounds that inhibit pathogens
4. **Nutrient immobilization** — beneficial organisms tie up nutrients that pathogens would otherwise use

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Restoring the soil food web is presented as the **primary strategy** for eliminating the need for pesticides and reducing inorganic fertilizer use
- In mycology, the fungal component of the food web is particularly critical — beneficial fungi (including mycorrhizal species) require aerobic conditions and are harmed by anaerobic byproducts
- Compost tea quality is measured by whether it contains the full complement of food web organisms, not just bacterial counts
- The food web framework explains why monoculture approaches (e.g., single-species bacterial inoculants) provide incomplete results compared to diverse biological amendments

## Measurement and Assessment

- Soil Foodweb Inc. (SFI) developed direct-count microscopy methods to assess food web composition
- Morphological assessment of bacterial and fungal diversity provides rapid, cost-effective evaluation
- Molecular DNA assays offer higher resolution but remain expensive (equipment costs ~$190,000 for probe replication systems as of early 2000s)
- Fungal biomass minimum of 2 micrograms per gram of compost tea is the threshold for reliable disease prevention (mildew and blight)
