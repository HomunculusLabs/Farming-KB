## Allium sativum

Allium sativum, commonly known as garlic, is a bulbous perennial plant in the family Amaryllidaceae (traditionally Alliaceae). Native to Central Asia, it has been cultivated for over 5,000 years and is now one of the most widely used culinary and medicinal plants globally. The bulb is composed of multiple cloves, each enclosed in a papery tunic, and contains a rich array of sulfur-containing organosulfur compounds — principally alliin, allicin, and their derivatives — responsible for garlic's characteristic pungency and most of its documented pharmacological activities. Garlic has demonstrated antimicrobial, cardiovascular-protective, antithrombotic, hypolipidemic, and potential anticancer properties in extensive preclinical and [[query-how-to-grow-rare-tropical-fruits-in-marginal-and-subtropical-climates]]; does not occur in the wild (domesticated species)
- **Soil requirements:** Well-drained, fertile loam with pH 6.0–7.5; sensitive to waterlogging
- **Climate:** Vernalization requirement of 1–2 months at 0–10°C for proper bulb formation; hardneck types require colder winters than softneck types
- **Global production:** Approximately 30+ million tonnes annually; China dominates with over 20 million tonnes per year

## Chemistry and Active Compounds

Garlic's bioactivity centers on organosulfur compounds formed through enzymatic reactions:

- **Alliin (S-allyl-L-cysteine sulfoxide):** Primary odorless precursor constituting 0.6–1.3% of fresh clove weight; stored in intact cloves in cytoplasmic vacuoles, physically separated from alliinase
- **Allicin (diallyl thiosulfinate):** Formed instantaneously when alliin contacts alliinase (released upon cell damage); responsible for the characteristic garlic odor and most acute bioactivity; highly unstable, degrades to ajoene, dithiins, and diallyl polysulfides within minutes to hours
- **Ajoene (E,Z-4,5,9-trithiadeca-1,6,11-triene-9-oxide):** Formed from allicin degradation; significant antithrombotic and antifungal properties; exists as both cis- and trans-isomers
- **S-allyl cysteine (SAC):** Major water-soluble compound in aged garlic extract; stable, bioavailable, antioxidant; one of the few garlic-derived compounds with well-characterized oral bioavailability (~50–70% in rats)
- **Diallyl sulfide (DAS), diallyl disulfide (DADS), diallyl trisulfide (DATS):** Oil-soluble polysulfides formed during cooking and processing; demonstrate dose-dependent anticancer activity in vitro
- **Other constituents:** Fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS) constituting 10–25% of dry weight (prebiotic fiber), saponins, flavonoids (primarily kaempferol derivatives), selenium (in selenium-fertilized garlic), and vitamins C and B6

## Ethnobotanical and Cultural Significance

Garlic has been used medicinally since at least the time of the ancient Egyptians, who provided it to laborers building the Great Pyramid of Giza. Hippocrates prescribed garlic for infections and digestive disorders. In traditional Chinese medicine, garlic is classified as warming and pungent, used to expel cold and resolve food stagnation. Ayurvedic medicine employs garlic (lahsun) for respiratory infections, digestive parasites, and rheumatic conditions. During World War I, garlic poultices were applied to wounds as an antiseptic when conventional antiseptics were unavailable. Garlic occupies a central role in the cuisines of the Mediterranean, Middle East, South Asia, East Asia, and Latin America. The global garlic market exceeds 30 million tonnes annually, with per capita consumption highest in Central Asian, Mediterranean, and East Asian countries.

- **Religious and cultural taboos:** Garlic is avoided in certain Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain traditions due to its classification as a tamasic (energy-dulling) food; some Hare Krishna and Buddhist meditation practitioners exclude garlic from their diets
- **Folk medicine traditions:** European folk medicine used garlic poultices for chest infections and insect bites; African traditional medicine employs garlic for malaria prevention; Latin American curanderismo uses garlic in cleansing rituals

## Cultivation

- **Planting:** Individual cloves planted 3–5 cm deep, pointed end up, in autumn (hardneck) or early spring (softneck). Cloves should be separated from the bulb no more than 48 hours before planting to prevent desiccation and premature sprouting.
- **Spacing:** 10–15 cm within rows, 30–45 cm between rows; closer spacing produces smaller bulbs but higher total yield per area
- **Fertilization:** Moderate nitrogen requirement; excessive nitrogen promotes foliage at the expense of bulb size. Phosphorus and potassium should be applied pre-plant based on [[query-how-to-manage-pests-and-diseases-in-mushroom-cultivation]]:** Susceptible to onion maggot (Delia antiqua), thrips (Thrips tabaci), nematodes, white rot (Sclerotium cepivorum), downy mildew (Peronospora destructor), and rust (Puccinia allii); crop rotation with non-Allium species is essential for [[comparison-companion-planting-reference-vs-jadam-monoculture-critique]]:** Traditional companion plant for roses (repels aphids), tomatoes, and fruit trees; allium compounds deter certain insect pests through volatile sulfur release
- **Storage:** Hardneck types store 4–6 months; softneck types 8–12 months at 0°C, 65% relative humidity; sprouting and desiccation are primary storage losses
