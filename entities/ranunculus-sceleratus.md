## Overview

*Ranunculus sceleratus* L., commonly known as celery-leaved buttercup or cursed crowfoot, is an annual to biennial herb in the family Ranunculaceae. It is one of the most toxic buttercup species native to [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of the Northern Hemisphere.

The species epithet *sceleratus* means "wicked" or "cursed" in Latin, a direct reference to its potent toxicity. This name has been in use since Linnaeus first described the species in 1753.

The plant grows in shallow water, mud, and damp ground at the margins of ponds, ditches, and marshes. Its distinctive palmately lobed basal leaves resemble those of celery, giving rise to its most widely used common name.

All parts contain the glycoside ranunculin, which hydrolyzes to the vesicant protoanemonin upon tissue damage. Contact with crushed foliage can cause severe blistering of the skin and mucous membranes.

The species is sometimes confused with other wetland buttercups, particularly *[[ranunculus-flammula]]* and *[[ranunculus-lingua]]*. However, its cylindrical fruiting head and celery-like leaves distinguish it clearly.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Order Ranunculales → Family Ranunculaceae → Genus *Ranunculus* → Species *R. sceleratus*. It belongs to section *Ranunculus*, a group of mostly semi-aquatic buttercups.

The plant reaches 10–70 cm in height with hollow, glabrous, succulent stems. Stems are erect to ascending, often branched above the middle, and root at the lower nodes when in contact with wet soil.

Basal leaves are long-petiolate with three palmately divided segments, each further lobed or toothed. The leaf blades are 1.5–5 cm broad and glossy green on the upper surface.

Stem leaves are smaller, sessile, and progressively less divided toward the apex. Upper stem leaves may be merely linear or narrowly oblong with a few teeth.

The root system is fibrous and shallow, adapted to the saturated soils of its wetland habitat. Plants growing in deeper water may develop partially submerged stems with adventitious roots at the nodes.

Flowers are small (6–10 mm diameter) with 3–5 pale yellow petals and reflexed sepals. Each flower bears 10–30 stamens surrounding a conical receptacle.

The head of achenes is elongated and cylindrical (5–15 mm long), a key diagnostic feature separating it from other buttercups. Each achene is approximately 1.5–2 mm long with a short, curved beak.

The receptacle elongates conspicuously at fruiting, giving the fruiting head a distinctive cylindrical or ellipsoidal shape. This feature is visible even at a distance in the field.

Synonyms include *R. sceleratus* var. *reptabundus* and *R. sceleratus* var. *facialis*, reflecting minor morphological [[psilocybin-psilocin-variation-across-flushes]] its range. These varieties are not consistently recognized in modern taxonomic treatments.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Ranunculus sceleratus* has a circumboreal distribution across Europe, Asia, and North America. It occurs throughout the British Isles, continental Europe, Scandinavia, and eastward to Siberia, China, and Japan.

In North America it ranges from Newfoundland to British Columbia and south to Virginia, Missouri, and California. It is also naturalized in parts of [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], Australia, and New Zealand.

The species occurs in pond margins, ditch edges, riverbanks, marshes, and seasonally flooded ground. It tolerates mildly saline conditions and is occasionally found in brackish coastal marshes.

It prefers full sun and nutrient-rich substrates but can persist in partially shaded sites. Soils are typically clay, silt, or organic muck with pH ranging from neutral to mildly alkaline.

The plant functions as an annual in habitats that dry completely in summer and as a biennial in permanently wet sites. Populations fluctuate dramatically year to year depending on water levels.

It is classified as Least Concern by most conservation authorities due to its extremely broad distribution. However, local declines have been noted where wetland drainage has eliminated suitable habitat.

In the United Kingdom, the species is widespread but has undergone local declines in intensively farmed landscapes. Drainage of agricultural ponds and ditching of wet meadows are the primary threats.

## Toxicology

The primary toxic principle is protoanemonin, formed from the glycoside ranunculin when plant tissues are crushed or damaged. Protoanemonin is a volatile, unsaturated lactone that causes vesiculation and inflammation on contact.

Ingestion of fresh plant material can cause severe gastroenteritis, oral blistering, abdominal pain, and diarrhea. Livestock poisoning has been reported in cattle and horses grazing wet pastures.

Drying and heating degrade protoanemonin to the less toxic anemonin, reducing the hazard in hay. This conversion is the reason dried buttercup-containing hay is generally safe for livestock.

Fatalities are rare but documented in cases of large ingestions, particularly in cattle. The lethal dose in mammals has been estimated at approximately 1–2% of body weight of fresh plant material.

Traditional Chinese medicine references the species under the name *shí lóng rúi* (石龙芮) for external applications only. Historical use as a counter-irritant and blistering agent in folk medicine has been recorded across Europe.

The plant is among the most toxic of the Ranunculaceae due to exceptionally high ranunculin concentrations. Laboratory assays have measured protoanemonin yields up to 0.4% of fresh leaf weight.

Skin contact with the sap can cause phytophotodermatitis in sensitive individuals, with symptoms worsening upon sun exposure. Field workers and foragers should wear gloves when handling the plant.

## Ecological Role

The flowers attract small flies, syrphid flies, and bees that serve as pollinators. The inconspicuous flowers produce modest amounts of pollen compared to other wetland forbs.

The achenes are dispersed primarily by water, floating for several days before sinking. Birds feeding in wetland margins occasionally consume and disperse the achenes without ill effects.

*Ranunculus sceleratus* contributes to early successional vegetation on exposed mudflats and drawdown zones. Its dense, shallow root system helps stabilize wetland sediments during seasonal flooding.
