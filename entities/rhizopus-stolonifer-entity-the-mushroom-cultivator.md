# Rhizopus stolonifer

## Taxonomy

Rhizopus stolonifer is a zygomycete fungus classified within the class
Zygomycetes, order Mucorales, and family Mucoraceae. It is one of the most
widely recognized contaminant molds in both mushroom cultivation and general
microbiology, commonly known as the Black Bread Mold.

The genus name Rhizopus is derived from the Greek prefix "rhizo" pertaining to
roots, and the suffix "pus" meaning foot, referring to the rhizoids at the base
of the sporangiophore that are characteristic of species in this genus. The
species epithet stolonifer refers to the stolons, which are long interconnecting
mycelial veins that join individual sporangiophores.

Rhizopus stolonifer is also known by the synonym Rhizopus nigricans. The
species is placed in the same family (Mucoraceae) and order (Mucorales) as
Mucor, and the two genera share many morphological and ecological
characteristics, though they can be distinguished by differences in their
sporangium structure and the presence of rhizoids in Rhizopus.

## Morphology

Rhizopus stolonifer is similar in overall appearance to Mucor. When
sporulating, it appears as a dense mat of tall, aerial, vertically oriented
hyphae upon which sit dark grey to grey black heads, resembling a forest of
pins. This characteristic appearance is the origin of the common name Pin Mold.

Under the microscope, Rhizopus displays a distinctive creeping hyphal network
that gives rise to individual, vertically oriented stalks that are unbranched.
At the base of each sporangiophore, distinct rhizoids can be observed, which
serve as anchoring structures. The apex of the sporangiophore swells into a
vesicle upon which a dark spherical body, the sporangium, rests.

A key distinguishing feature is that the sporangium does not fully envelope
the sporangiophore. The sporangiophore swells before contacting the
sporangium, creating a visible columella. The sporangium itself is a mass of
asexual spores within a thin envelope of tissue that soon disintegrates and
frees the spores.

Joining individual sporangiophores are long interconnecting mycelial veins
called stolons. Sexual reproduction can occur between two sexually complementary
hyphae, resulting in the formation of a globose reproductive body called a
zygosporium. The mycelia lack distinct cell walls, being non septate as is
typical of the Zygomycetes.

## Habitat and Ecology

Rhizopus stolonifer is a saprophyte that naturally inhabits dung and soils and
is a decomposer of dead plant and animal matter. It is one of the most common
fungi encountered in everyday environments. Within the home, this contaminant
is most often seen on old bread or on poorly stored grain and fruits, which
accounts for its common name, the Black Bread Mold.

In mushroom cultivation, Rhizopus is commonly seen in both agar and grain
culture. The spores are readily airborne and can quickly settle onto exposed
media surfaces. The rapid growth rate of Rhizopus allows it to colonize
substrates before slower growing mushroom mycelium can establish itself.

The species thrives at warm temperatures and can significantly elevate the
temperature of contaminated substrates through its own metabolic activity,
creating conditions that favor further microbial growth.

## Role in Mushroom Cultivation

Rhizopus stolonifer is one of the primary contaminants of grain spawn, along
with Aspergillus and Penicillium. Its very rapid growth rate means it can
quickly overtake spawn jars and render them useless for cultivation purposes.

One of the most significant dangers of Rhizopus stolonifer contamination is
its ability to generate heat. The species can elevate the substrate temperature
from room temperature to the 95 to 104 degrees F range through metabolic
activity. At this elevated temperature, the populations of true thermophilic
organisms increase dramatically, further heating the host substrate to
temperatures that are lethal to mushroom mycelium. This cascade effect means
that even a localized Rhizopus contamination can potentially destroy an entire
cultivation setup.

Because Rhizopus stolonifer is so rapidly growing, early detection and removal
of contaminated materials is critical. Once established, the mold can spread
quickly through a facility via airborne spores.

## Contamination Control

Measures of control for Rhizopus stolonifer include:

- Air filtration to reduce airborne spore load in cultivation areas
- Strict adherence to general hygienic practices throughout the cultivation
  process
- Steam sterilization of grain and agar media at appropriate temperatures and
  durations
- Prompt identification and removal of contaminated materials before sporulation
  occurs
- Proper storage of grains and substrates to prevent pre contamination

The primary medium through which contamination is spread is air, making
air filtration and clean room procedures essential for prevention. Because the
spores are ubiquitous in the environment, complete exclusion is difficult, but
rigorous sterile technique can keep contamination rates manageable.

## Interactions

Rhizopus stolonifer is closely related to Mucor, differing primarily in that
the Mucor sporangium completely covers the apex of the sporangiophore, whereas
in Rhizopus the sporangium does not fully envelope it. Additionally, Rhizopus
produces distinct rhizoids at the base of its sporangiophores and stolons
connecting individual fruiting structures, features not present in Mucor.

In the medical literature, Rhizopus has sometimes been blamed for zygomycosis,
when in fact other related genera such as Absidia and Mucor were responsible.
Rhizopus stolonifer itself is not considered a primary pathogen to humans.

Despite its role as a contaminant in mushroom cultivation, Rhizopus stolonifer
has beneficial industrial applications. The species is utilized in the
commercial production of fumaric acid and cortisone. Other species in the genus
secrete assorted alcohols and acids as metabolic waste products, some of which
have commercial value.
