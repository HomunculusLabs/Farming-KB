# Black Alder (*Alnus glutinosa* L.)

## Overview

*Alnus glutinosa*, the black alder or European alder, is a deciduous tree in the family Betulaceae native to most of Europe, western Siberia, and parts of North Africa.

It is one of the most ecologically significant temperate hardwoods due to its symbiotic nitrogen-fixing association with the actinomycete *Frankia alni*, which enables it to thrive on nitrogen-poor soils and contribute substantial nitrogen inputs to surrounding ecosystems.

Black alder is valued in permaculture and agroforestry for its rapid growth, nitrogen fixation, water tolerance, and versatile wood, making it a foundational species for riparian buffers, shelterbelts, and soil-rehabilitation projects.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Angiospermae
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Fagales
- **Family:** Betulaceae
- **Genus:** Alnus
- **Species:** *A. glutinosa* (L.) Gaertn. (1790)
- **Synonyms:** *Betula alnus* L. (basionym), *Alnus vulgaris* Spach
- **Etymology:** *Alnus* from the Celtic word for "near water"; *glutinosa* = "sticky," referring to the glutinous resin on young leaves and buds
- **Subspecies:** Two recognized: *A. glutinosa* subsp. *glutinosa* (European) and subsp. *barbata* (Caucasus/Western Asia)

## Morphology

### Growth Habit

- **Life form:** Medium-sized deciduous tree
- **Lifespan:** 60–100 years; occasionally up to 150 years
- **Height:** 15–25 m (occasionally 30 m)
- **Crown:** Conical when young, becoming broadly ovate with age; often multi-stemmed
- **Growth rate:** Rapid; 60–90 cm/year in youth, slowing after 20 years
- **Root system:** Shallow, spreading lateral roots with prominent nitrogen-fixing root nodules; often develops adventitious roots in waterlogged conditions

### Aerial Parts

- **Bark:** Dark grey-brown, rough and fissured with age; young bark is smooth and greenish-brown
- **Twigs:** Purplish-brown to olive-green, slightly sticky, with prominent lenticels
- **Buds:** Purplish, stalked, 6–8 mm long; glutinous with sticky resin
- **Leaves:** Alternate, simple, broadly ovate to orbicular, 5–10 cm long, 4–8 cm wide; doubly serrate margin; 5–8 pairs of lateral veins; dark green and glossy above, paler below; sticky when young, becoming glabrous; leaf base rounded to slightly cordate; petiole 1–3 cm
- **Flowers:** Monoecious; catkins appear before leaves in February–March
  - Male catkins: Pendulous, 5–10 cm long, purplish-brown, in groups of 2–4
  - Female catkins: Erect, ovoid, 1–1.5 cm long, dark red to purple, developing into woody cones
- **Fruit:** Small woody cone-like strobile, 1.5–2.5 cm long, composed of persistent bract scales; each scale bears a small winged nutlet; cones persist through winter

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Native range:** Europe (from Ireland and northern Spain to the Urals), western Siberia, North Africa (Morocco, Tunisia), western Asia Minor
- **Introduced range:** Widely naturalized in North America (northeastern US, eastern Canada), New Zealand, parts of South America
- **Altitude:** 0–1,500 m; most common in lowland and riparian zones
- **Habitat:** Riparian corridors, wet woodlands, marsh edges, floodplains; also colonizes disturbed sites, gravel bars, and nutrient-poor soils
- **Soil tolerance:** Extremely broad; thrives in waterlogged, poorly drained soils that kill most other tree species; also tolerates acidic, nutrient-poor, and compacted soils
- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 3–7; tolerates winter temperatures to −35°C
- **Light:** Full sun to partial shade; shade-intolerant as seedling, becoming moderately shade-tolerant

## Nitrogen Fixation Symbiosis

### *Frankia alni* Association

- **Symbiont:** *Frankia alni* (actinomycete bacterium), forming actinorhizal root nodules
- **Nodule structure:** Coralloid, multi-lobed, 1–3 cm diameter, appearing on lateral roots; beige to reddish-brown when active
- **Nitrogen fixation rate:** 40–100 kg N/ha/year, depending on site conditions and tree density
- **Mechanism:** Oxygen-limited compartment within nodules protects nitrogenase; hemoglobin (similar to leghemoglobin in legumes) regulates oxygen diffusion
- **Seasonality:** Active during growing season; nodules senesce in autumn and reactivate in spring
- **Transfer to ecosystem:** Fixed nitrogen is transferred to surrounding plants via [[mycorrhizal-networks]], leaf litter decomposition, and [[root-exudates]]
- **Comparison to legumes:** Fixes less nitrogen per unit area than soybeans (~200 kg N/ha) but provides year-round fixation over a much longer lifespan and across a broader range of soil conditions

### Ectomycorrhizal Associations

- Black alder simultaneously hosts both *Frankia* (nitrogen-fixing) and [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]], a dual symbiosis relatively uncommon among temperate trees
- Common ectomycorrhizal partners include *[[laccaria-laccata]]*, *[[paxillus-involutus]]*, *Alpova diplophloeus*, and several *Tomentella* species
- The tripartite association (alder–Frankia–ectomycorrhiza) significantly enhances phosphorus uptake and overall nutrient cycling efficiency

## Phytochemistry and Medicinal Properties

- **Bark constituents:** Tannins (10–20%; ellagitannins and gallotannins), betulin/betulinic acid (anti-inflammatory, in vitro), phenolic acids (gallic, ellagic, p-coumaric — antioxidant)
- **Leaf constituents:** Flavonoids (hyperoside, quercitrin), phenolic acids
- **Traditional uses:** Bark decoctions as astringent gargle for sore throat; topical applications for wounds and burns; anti-diarrheal preparations
- **Evidence level:** Traditional use supported by moderate in vitro evidence; limited clinical data

## Wood Properties and Uses

- **Wood characteristics:** Pale reddish-brown when fresh, darkening to deep orange-red on exposure; fine, even texture; Janka hardness ~600 N
- **Durability:** Low above ground; highly resistant to underwater decay — historically used for pilings, lock gates, and water pipes (Venicewood, Amsterdam foundations)
- **Working properties:** Easy to work, glues and finishes well; turns and carves readily
- **Major applications:** Underwater construction, furniture/cabinetry, veneer, plywood, charcoal (historically for gunpowder), wood pulp
- **Fuel value:** High calorific value when dried; excellent firewood

## Permaculture and Agroforestry Applications

- **Riparian buffers:** Premier species for temperate riparian buffer establishment; roots stabilize stream banks; canopy shades waterways and reduces temperature; nitrogen fixation fertilizes adjacent crops
- **Shelterbelts:** Effective nurse tree in shelterbelt systems; provides wind protection while nitrogen accumulates in soil; coppiced on 10–15 year rotation for wood production
- **Soil rehabilitation:** Pioneers on degraded, compacted soils; nitrogen inputs accelerate succession; roots break up subsoil; tolerates heavy metals (phytoremediation of Pb, Zn, Cd)
- **Silvopastoral systems:** Browse for cattle and deer; shade for livestock; leaf litter improves soil organic matter
- **Coppice:** Regrows vigorously from cut stumps; 8–15 tonnes/ha firewood per rotation; stools productive for 40–60 years

## Ecological Role

- **Pioneer species:** Colonizes open, wet, and disturbed sites rapidly
- **Nitrogen cycling:** Major contributor of biologically fixed nitrogen in riparian and wetland ecosystems
- **Wildlife habitat:** Seeds feed siskins, redpolls, and finches; catkins provide early pollen for bees; bark supports epiphytic mosses and lichens
- **Mycorrhizal hub:** Serves as a mycorrhizal inoculum source for establishing trees in riparian plantings
