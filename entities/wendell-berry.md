## Wendell Berry

## Description

Wendell Berry is an American poet, novelist, essayist, and organic farmer whose writings on the values of margins — both physical and conceptual — have been highly influential in the [[permaculture]] and sustainable agriculture movements. Holmgren draws directly on Berry"s critique of industrial agriculture and his articulation of the "widening margin of divergent possibilities" as intellectual foundation for permaculture"s emergence from the margins of conventional thinking.

## Classification

- **Type:** Person — poet, novelist, farmer, cultural critic
- **Nationality:** American
- **Born:** 1934, Henry County, Kentucky, USA
- **Fields:** Literature, sustainable agriculture, rural life, environmental philosophy

## Key Facts

- **Orthodoxy critique:** Berry characterised modern industrial agriculture as controlled by orthodoxy — both ignorant and arrogant — characterised by its progressive contraction around increasingly limited notions of what is productive and economic. This contraction narrows the range of acceptable practices and eliminates alternatives.

- **Widening margins of possibility:** Despite the narrowing orthodoxy, Berry identified a "widening margin of divergent possibilities" existing around the edges of conventional agriculture — unexplored approaches, alternative methods, and different ways of thinking about food production and land management.

- **Geographic and practical margins narrowed:** While conceptual margins were expanding, Berry observed that "the margins of geography and practice" had narrowed drastically — the places and people who know how to farm outside the orthodoxy were becoming rare.

- **Permaculture connection:** Holmgren notes that as Berry was writing these words, permaculture was being conceived in Tasmania as another divergent possibility at the geographic margins. Berry's framework helps explain why permaculture emerged where and when it did.

- **Organic farming advocacy:** Berry has been a lifelong advocate for organic farming, family-scale agriculture, and the preservation of rural communities and their knowledge.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Wendell Berry's work connects to permaculture theory and practice in several key ways:

1. **Margins as source of innovation (Principle 11):** Berry's articulation of the value of margins — both physical and intellectual — provides philosophical grounding for the permaculture principle of using edges and valuing the marginal. Innovation comes not from the centre of orthodoxy but from the edges where different approaches meet.

2. **Critique of agricultural simplification:** His analysis of how industrial agriculture progressively eliminates alternatives mirrors the permaculture critique of monoculture and supports the case for diverse, integrated, site-specific farming systems.

3. **Rural knowledge preservation:** Berry's concern about the loss of people and places that know how to farm outside the orthodoxy parallels permaculture's emphasis on preserving and transmitting traditional and alternative land management knowledge.

4. **Hope from the margins:** His observation that margins of geography and practice may be beginning to expand suggests that alternative approaches like permaculture, organic agriculture, and regenerative farming are gaining ground — literally and figuratively.

5. **Integration of culture and agriculture:** Berry's insistence that farming is not merely an economic activity but a cultural and spiritual one aligns with permaculture's ethical foundations and its recognition that sustainable land use requires cultural change.
