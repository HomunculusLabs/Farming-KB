## Overview

**Aloe aristata**, commonly known as **lace aloe** or **guinea-fowl aloe**, is a small stemless succulent in the family Asphodelaceae. Endemic to South Africa and Lesotho, it forms compact rosettes of fleshy, white-spotted leaves and is prized as a windowsill and rock-garden specimen.

The species produces dense rosettes 15–20 cm across with triangular leaves covered in white tubercles and soft white marginal teeth. In late spring to midsummer, it sends up slender racemes of orange-red to coral-pink tubular flowers.

It is one of the most cold-hardy aloes, tolerating brief exposure to −10 °C when kept dry. This hardiness, combined with its compact size, has made it a popular houseplant in [[dighton-fungal-decomposition-leaf-litter-dynamics]] or soil, with only the leaf tips exposed. This burial behavior protects the growing point from frost and desiccation.

## Cultivation

Lace aloe is easy to grow in containers or well-drained garden positions. It requires full sun to partial shade and prefers a gritty, free-draining potting medium.

It is hardy to approximately USDA zone 8a when planted in sharply drained sites. In colder regions it performs well as a houseplant on bright windowsills.

Propagation is most commonly by offsets, which the plant produces freely around the base. These can be separated and potted individually in spring or early summer.

A period of cool, dry rest in winter promotes flowering the following season. Plants kept continuously warm and wet tend to produce foliage at the expense of blooms.

Repotting is needed only every two to three years. A terracotta pot with drainage holes is ideal, as it allows the root zone to dry between waterings.

Outdoor plantings benefit from a gravel mulch. This prevents soil splash onto the rosette and reduces the risk of crown rot during wet periods.

The species tolerates coastal conditions well, including salt spray and wind exposure. It is a reliable choice for seaside gardens in frost-free climates.

## Ecological Role

In its native grasslands, *Aloe aristata* provides nectar for sunbirds and other nectar-feeding birds during its flowering season. Bees and butterflies also visit the flowers regularly.

The dense rosettes offer shelter for small invertebrates. In rocky habitats, the plants contribute to soil stabilization in crevices and on thin substrate layers.

Its CAM photosynthetic pathway enables efficient water use in [[fungal-diversity-arid-environments]]. This adaptation allows it to persist in habitats where rainfall is sparse and unpredictable.

The species is frequently found growing alongside grasses and other geophytes in high-altitude grasslands. It benefits from the shelter of surrounding vegetation while competing effectively for limited moisture.

Pollination in the wild is primarily by sunbirds, particularly the malachite sunbird and southern double-collared sunbird. The tubular flower shape and nectar volume are adapted to these avian pollinators.
