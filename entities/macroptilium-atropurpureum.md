## Macroptilium atropurpureum (Siratro)

## Description

Macroptilium atropurpureum, commonly known as siratro, is a perennial tropical forage legume valued for its drought tolerance, nitrogen-fixing ability, and persistence under grazing. In Bill Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual*, it is listed as "Siratro, Macroptilium atropurpureum" among the key plant species referenced for use in designed agricultural systems.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Fabaceae (Legume family)
- **Subfamily**: Faboideae
- **Genus**: Macroptilium
- **Species**: M. atropurpureum
- **Common names**: Siratro, purple bush-bean, atro

## Key Facts

- **Growth habit**: Twining, climbing perennial legume with stems reaching 2–3 meters in length when supported by companion grasses or structures. Forms a deep taproot that can extend to 2+ meters, providing exceptional drought resistance.
- **Native range**: Mexico and Central America. Now widely naturalized and cultivated across tropical regions worldwide, particularly in Australia, South America, and Southeast Asia.
- **Climate adaptation**: Adapted to tropical and subtropical regions with annual rainfall from 600–2500 mm. Notable for its ability to survive extended dry periods through its deep taproot system. Sensitive to frost but can regrow from the crown after mild cold events.
- **Soil requirements**: Grows on a wide range of well-drained soils from sands to medium clays. Tolerates moderately acid soils (pH 5.0–7.0) with moderate aluminum levels. Does not tolerate waterlogging or poorly drained sites.
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Forms effective nodules with Bradyrhizobium spp., contributing significant nitrogen to associated grasses and the broader soil ecosystem. Nitrogen fixation rates of 100–200 kg N/ha/year have been recorded under favorable conditions.
- **Leaf characteristics**: Distinctive dark green trifoliate leaves with a purple tinge on the undersides. Leaves fold under moisture stress, reducing transpiration.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

### Tropical Pasture Legume

Siratro was one of the first tropical legumes commercially developed for pasture improvement. It is typically sown in combination with tall tropical grasses such as Panicum maximum (guinea grass), Pennisetum purpureum (Napier grass), or Brachiaria spp. The climbing growth habit allows it to grow up through the grass canopy, accessing light while the grass provides structural support. This synergistic relationship produces pastures with higher protein content, improved digestibility, and greater year-round productivity compared to grass monocultures.

### Drought-Resilient Forage

The deep taproot system of siratro makes it one of the most drought-tolerant [[tropical-forage-legumes]]. In permaculture systems in seasonally dry tropical areas, it provides reliable forage and ground cover during dry periods when shallow-rooted species have died back. This resilience is particularly valuable in climate-adaptive design for smallholder systems.

### Soil Building and Nitrogen Contribution

As a vigorous nitrogen fixer, siratro is an excellent green manure and soil-building species. When slashed and left as mulch, it returns both nitrogen-rich organic matter and mineral nutrients to the soil surface. In food forest establishment phases, siratro can be sown as a pioneer ground cover to build fertility before planting more demanding crop species.

### Erosion Control on Slopes

The combination of a deep taproot and twining surface growth makes siratro effective for erosion control on sloping land. The taproot anchors the soil profile while the surface growth protects against rain splash erosion and sheet flow. It can be included in contour strip plantings and buffer zones along waterways.

### Integration with Livestock

Siratro is highly palatable to cattle and other ruminants, with crude protein levels of 14–20% in leaf material during the growing season. It maintains reasonable nutritional quality into the dry season, providing a valuable protein supplement when grass quality declines. In silvopastoral systems, it serves as an understory forage layer beneath scattered shade trees.

### Companion Planting in Permaculture Guilds

In diverse permaculture plantings, siratro can serve multiple functions simultaneously: nitrogen fixation, ground cover, mulch production, forage, and habitat for beneficial insects. Its flowers attract bees and other pollinators. It pairs well with nitrogen-demanding crops and can be managed by periodic slashing to feed mulch to adjacent plants.

## Management Considerations

Siratro is susceptible to overgrazing when young — rotational grazing with adequate recovery periods is essential for persistence. The species can be affected by Rhizoctonia foliar blight in humid conditions, though this is rarely fatal in well-managed systems. Regular slashing or grazing prevents the vine from smothering companion species. For seed establishment, scarification of hard-coated seeds improves germination rates.

## Related Species

Macroptilium lathyroides (phasey bean) is a related annual species mentioned alongside siratro in the permaculture plant lists, used as a shorter-season forage legume in similar contexts.

### Seed Production and Natural Reseeding

Siratro produces hard-coated seeds that build a persistent soil seed bank. Under managed grazing or cutting regimes, the species naturally reseeds and maintains itself in the pasture without regular re-sowing. This self-perpetuating characteristic aligns well with permaculture principles of designing for self-maintenance. In some systems, a single establishment event can provide decades of productive service.

### Integration with Agroforestry

In multi-strata agroforestry systems, siratro occupies the herbaceous/shrub layer between ground covers and canopy trees. Its climbing habit allows it to utilize vertical space efficiently without competing directly with tree seedlings for light at the canopy level. When managed within tree rows, it can be allowed to climb low branches during establishment, providing additional biomass that can be cut and applied as mulch around the trees.

## Disease and Pest Considerations

Siratro can be affected by peanut stripe virus and various fungal leaf spots in humid conditions. However, these rarely cause significant production losses in diverse mixed plantings where disease pressure is diluted across multiple species. Rhizoctonia foliar blight is the most significant disease concern, particularly in high-rainfall tropical lowlands. Selecting adapted cultivars and maintaining species diversity in pastures are the primary management strategies.
