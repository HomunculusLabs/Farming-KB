# Masonhalea richardsonii

## Description

*Masonhalea richardsonii* is a fruticose (shrubby) terricolous [[lichen]] commonly known as the arctic tumbleweed lichen. It is found in arctic and subarctic tundra ecosystems across North America, where it grows on the ground surface in open, exposed habitats. The species is ecologically significant as winter forage for reindeer and caribou (*Rangifer tarandus*), and its growth dynamics are an important component of arctic primary productivity budgets. It is sometimes placed in the genus *Cetraria* in older taxonomic treatments.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi

- **Class**: Lecanoromycetes

- **Species**: *M. richardsonii*

## Key Facts

- Reported to increase in biomass by approximately 10% per year in Alaska (Peck et al., 2000), a growth rate comparable to other arctic fruticose [[lichens]] measured by Karenlampi (1971).
- Functions as a "tumbleweed" in arctic environments: wind can detach and roll thalli across the tundra, enabling vegetative dispersal.
- Grows at rates of approximately 2.4-10.6 mg g-1 per week, consistent with other fruticose species like *Cetraria* spp. and *Cladonia* spp. measured by Cooper and Wookey (2001).
- Critical winter food source for reindeer (*Rangifer tarandus*) and caribou in arctic ecosystems.
- Growth is limited by the short arctic growing season (approximately 10 weeks of active photosynthesis).
- Tolerates extreme cold and desiccation, metabolically reactivating rapidly during brief periods of warmth and moisture.

## Growth and Productivity

Arctic lichens including *M. richardsonii* exhibit some of the slowest growth rates among terrestrial photosynthetic organisms:

- Biomass accumulation is strongly controlled by temperature, light availability, and hydration status.
- The growing season is limited to approximately 10 weeks per year, constraining annual production.
- Despite slow individual growth, the cumulative standing biomass of fruticose lichens in arctic tundra can be enormous, forming the dominant ground cover in many plant communities.
- Regeneration after heavy grazing can take decades due to slow growth rates, making lichen pastures vulnerable to overgrazing.

## Ecological Role

1. **Primary production**: Contributes significantly to net primary production in arctic tundra where vascular plant diversity is low.
2. **Reindeer forage**: Essential winter food source; heavy grazing can deplete lichen cover, with long recovery times.
3. **Soil stabilization**: Dense mats of fruticose lichens help insulate [[permafrost]] and reduce soil erosion from wind.
4. **Nutrient cycling**: Traps atmospheric deposition of nutrients, particularly nitrogen, and slowly releases them to the soil upon [[decomposition]].
5. **Carbon cycling**: Arctic lichens represent a significant carbon store in tundra ecosystems.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Sustainable harvesting**: Understanding growth rates is essential for managing reindeer pastures sustainably; lichen regeneration can take 20-50 years after depletion.
- **Climate change vulnerability**: Arctic lichens are sensitive to warming, changes in snow cover duration, and increased shrub encroachment that reduces light availability.
- **Stress physiology**: The extreme desiccation and freezing tolerance of *M. richardsonii* makes it a model for studying cryptobiosis and stress-adaptation mechanisms.
- **Cultural importance**: Indigenous peoples of the Arctic have relied on lichen-rich pastures for reindeer herding for millennia.
