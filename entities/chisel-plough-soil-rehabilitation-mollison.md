# Chisel Plough Soil Rehabilitation

## Description

Chisel plough soil rehabilitation is a non-inversion tillage technique central to [[permaculture]]'s approach to restoring compacted and degraded soils. Unlike conventional mouldboard ploughing, which turns the soil over and destroys its structure, chisel ploughing uses narrow, axe-edged shanks to fracture and aerate the soil profile while leaving the surface largely intact. Mollison describes this as a foundational technique for broadacre soil restoration.

## Classification

- **Type**: Soil rehabilitation technique / agricultural practice
- **Category**: Non-inversion tillage, minimum tillage, soil conditioning
- **Equipment**: Chisel plough (rectangular steel frame with forward-curved vertical flat bars and slip-on steel shoes)
- **Scale**: Broadacre agricultural to small farm

## Key Facts

- The chisel plough consists of a rectangular steel frame (tool bar) towed by tractor or draught animals, with narrow-edge (axe-edged) forward-curved vertical flat bars (shanks) attached. Slip-on steel shoes attach to the points of the shanks.
- Even a small implement of 5 shanks (25-50 b.h.p. tractor) covers significant ground. Multiple manufacturers in the USA, Europe, and Australia produce soil-loosening machines.
- The technique does not turn soil over — it lifts and fractures the soil profile, creating air pockets and channels without destroying surface vegetation or soil structure.
- Standard treatment depth is 18 cm (often sufficient); heroic depths of 30-80 cm are used only for extreme compaction or caliche layers.
- The process follows a sequential cycle over approximately one year: chisel shallow, graze or cut, chisel deeper, graze or cut again. Each cycle adds organic matter from dead roots and cut foliage.
- P.A. Yeomans recommended ripping to 90 cm or 1.5 m for disposal of massive sewage waste-water, using deep-rooted trees or legumes to take up wastes.
- Mollison states: "I have scarcely seen a property that would not benefit by soil conditioning as a first step before any further input."

## Relevance to Cultivation

- **Compaction relief**: Addresses the most common form of soil degradation — compaction from overgrazing, machinery traffic, and poor management.
- **Water infiltration**: Chisel lines prevent fast runoff and absorb overland flow, leading water to ridges through subsurface channels.
- **Biological activation**: Air pockets stimulate aerobic soil biology — [[earthworms]], bacteria, and fungi multiply in the aerated, organic-matter-rich environment.
- **Root penetration**: Loose, aerated soil allows plant roots to penetrate 30-50 cm in pasture and deeper in forests, accessing water and nutrients previously unavailable.
- **Soil temperature regulation**: Aerated soil stays warmer in winter and cooler in summer, extending growing seasons.
- **Erosion prevention**: Because the surface is little disturbed, roots hold against erosion even after fresh chisel ploughing.

## Pattern and Implementation

- The fundamental pattern rule: drive slightly downhill from valleys to ridges, creating herring-bone patterns. Spines are the valleys; ribs slope outward and downhill.
- This pattern allows water to move subsurface from valleys to ridges, reversing the typical erosion flow pattern.
- Ideal timing is early spring at the end of a gentle rainy period, when soil is neither too dry (producing clods) nor too wet (collapsing back).
- Follow up with permanent deep-rooted plants (forests or prairies) and minimum tillage management to prevent re-compaction.
- Not suitable for coarse sandy soils, cinder soils, dunes, very stony or boulder-soil mixtures, or steep slopes — use biological (root penetration) methods instead.

## Practical Applications

Chisel Plough Soil Rehabilitation Mollison plays a critical role in sustainable land management and regenerative agriculture.
Understanding these processes enables practitioners to design systems that work with natural
soil biology rather than against it, reducing inputs while improving long-term productivity.

## Management Considerations

Effective management requires monitoring soil indicators and responding to changes in
soil structure, organic matter content, and biological activity. Key strategies include
minimizing soil disturbance, maintaining ground cover, and incorporating diverse plantings.
