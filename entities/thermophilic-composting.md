## Description

The thermophilic composting phase is the second and most biologically intense stage of the composting process, occurring when internal pile temperatures reach 104-150F (40-65C) and beyond. During this phase, heat-adapted (thermophilic) microorganisms dominate and rapidly decompose complex carbohydrates, proteins, and hemicelluloses. The thermophilic phase serves the critical functions of maximum decomposition rate and pathogen destruction. A properly constructed compost pile should reach 135F (57C) within 24-72 hours and peak at 150F (65C) within approximately three days. Managing this phase -- through turning, moisture control, and monitoring -- is the primary skill of successful hot composting.

## Classification

- **Type:** Composting process phase
- **Temperature range:** 104-150F (40-65C)
- **Duration:** Typically 3 days to several weeks
- **Position in cycle:** Second phase, between mesophilic (phase 1) and maturation (phase 3)
- **Key organisms:** Thermophilic bacteria, actinomycetes, heat-tolerant fungi

## Key Facts

- **Rapid heating:** A properly made compost pile with the correct C:N ratio (25:1 to 30:1) will heat up to 135F (57C) in 24-72 hours and reach 150F (65C) in approximately three days. The center of the pile heats first and fastest.

- **Pathogen destruction:** Maintaining temperatures of 140-150F (60-65C) for at least a few days kills pathogenic microbes that may be present in the compost ingredients. This is one of the most important functions of the thermophilic phase.

- **Weed seed destruction:** At 150F (65C), weed seeds in the compost are also destroyed, preventing unwanted germination when the compost is later applied to gardens.

- **Key organisms:** Many more bacteria become active during this phase, including Arthrobacter spp., Pseudomonas spp., and Streptomyces (and other actinomycetes). Certain fungi also join or play more prominent roles in decomposition.

- **Complex compound breakdown:** During the thermophilic phase, complex carbohydrates are fully broken down, proteins are decomposed, and hemicelluloses (the more resistant structural components of plant cell walls) are decayed. These are materials too complex for mesophilic organisms to handle efficiently.

- **Temperature ceiling:** Compost piles should never exceed 155F (68C) because at this point carbon begins to burn off, reducing the organic matter content of the finished compost. Overheating is controlled by turning the pile, adding water, or adding more brown (carbon-rich) materials.

- **Self-heating mechanism:** The heat in compost comes entirely from microbial metabolic activity (primarily bacterial), not from external sources. The sun does not heat compost piles; bacteria do.

## Managing the Thermophilic Phase

**Monitoring:**
- Use a soil thermometer, oven thermometer, or metal probe (rebar/gutter nail) inserted into the pile center
- Hand insertion can also gauge heat -- the pile should feel distinctly hot to the touch
- Target range: 140-150F (60-65C) for pathogen and weed seed kill

**If the pile won't heat up:**
1. Turn the pile to introduce oxygen (switch inside/bottom with outside/top)
2. Add fresh green material (rich in easy-to-digest sugars for bacteria)
3. Add newspaper, fruit pulps, or commercial compost inoculants
4. Ensure adequate moisture

**If the pile overheats (above 155F / 68C):**
1. Turn the pile -- paradoxically, turning both heats (by adding oxygen) and cools (by opening the pile to air)
2. Add water to cool the pile down
3. Add more brown materials to shift the green-brown ratio and reduce bacterial food supply

**If the pile drops below 104F (40C) before maturity:**
- Add more green, high-nitrogen material to restart bacterial activity

**If the pile stays above 131F (55C) too long:**
- Add more brown, carbon-containing material to moderate bacterial activity

## Microbial Succession During the Phase

As temperatures rise through the thermophilic range, the microbial community shifts:

- **Early thermophilic (104-122F / 40-50C):** Bacillus spp. and other endospore-forming bacteria that survived from the mesophilic phase become dominant. Simple proteins and sugars are rapidly consumed.

- **Peak thermophilic (122-150F / 50-65C):** Arthrobacter, Pseudomonas, and Streptomyces species become most active. Complex carbohydrates and hemicelluloses are broken down. Most pathogens and weed seeds are destroyed.

- **Late thermophilic (cooling from 150F / 65C):** As readily available substrates diminish, metabolic activity slows and the pile begins to cool. Heat-tolerant organisms begin to decline.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Mushroom substrate preparation:** Agaricus (button mushroom) composting relies on a carefully managed thermophilic phase to condition the substrate. The temperature profile directly affects the nutritional quality of the final substrate for mycelial growth.

- **Pathogen-free compost:** The thermophilic phase ensures that compost used as casing material or substrate amendment is free of competing organisms and pathogens that could contaminate mushroom cultures.

- **Nutrient mineralization:** The thermophilic phase converts organic nitrogen and phosphorus into forms more available to subsequent fungal colonization. This is critical for substrate that will support mycelial growth.

- **Compost quality control:** Mushroom cultivators can assess compost quality by whether it achieved proper thermophilic temperatures. Insufficient heating indicates incomplete decomposition and potential contamination risk.

- **Soil food web restoration:** Gardeners restoring depleted soils rely on thermophilic composting to produce pathogen-free, biologically rich compost that inoculates soils with beneficial organisms without introducing diseases or weeds.
