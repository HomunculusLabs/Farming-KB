## Overview

Anemone hortensis, commonly called broad-leaved anemone or garden anemone, is a tuberous perennial in the family Ranunculaceae. It is native to the Mediterranean region and produces solitary flowers in shades of violet-blue, pink, or white.

The species is one of several Mediterranean anemones cultivated as spring-flowering ornamentals. It is closely related to Anemone coronaria but distinguished by its broader leaf segments and fewer floral bracts.

The species has been cultivated since at least the sixteenth century in European botanical gardens. Early herbals depict forms similar to modern garden selections.

Unlike the widely available Anemone coronaria cultivars, Anemone hortensis remains a specialist plant in horticultural commerce.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Clade: Tracheophytes
- Clade: Angiosperms
- Clade: Eudicots
- Order: Ranunculales
- Family: Ranunculaceae
- Genus: Anemone
- Species: Anemone hortensis L.

Synonyms include Anemone stellata and Anemone pavoniana. The species has historically been confused with Anemone coronaria in horticultural literature.

The specific epithet "hortensis" means "of gardens," reflecting its long history of cultivation.

Plants grow 15–30 cm tall from a irregularly shaped tuber. Basal leaves are palmately divided into three broad, deeply toothed segments. A whorl of three sessile floral bracts appears just below the flower.

Flowers are solitary, 4–7 cm across, with 6–10 petaloid sepals. Color varies from violet-blue to mauve-pink to white, often with a paler reverse. The center contains numerous yellow stamens surrounding a conical cluster of carpels.

Chromosome studies report a diploid number of 2n = 16 for Anemone hortensis. This differs from the tetraploid Anemone coronaria (2n = 32), supporting their status as distinct species.

The tuber is dark brown to black, roughly ovoid, and 2–4 cm in diameter. Multiple growing points may emerge from a single tuber in mature specimens.

Roots emerge from the basal plate of the tuber and extend 10–15 cm into the surrounding soil.

## Distribution and Habitat

Anemone hortensis is native to the northern Mediterranean basin. Its range extends from Spain and southern France eastward through Italy, the Balkans, and Greece to Turkey.

The species grows in garigue, open woodland margins, rocky hillsides, and disturbed ground. It favors calcareous soils at elevations from sea level to 1,200 meters.

Isolated populations occur on the Balearic Islands and in parts of North Africa. The Sicilian populations show particularly rich flower color variation.

The species is not considered threatened across most of its range. Localized habitat loss from agricultural intensification affects some lowland populations.

Populations are concentrated in areas with mild, wet winters and hot, dry summers. The tuber survives summer drought in a dormant state, re-sprouting with autumn rains.

## Cultivation

In cultivation, Anemone hortensis requires well-drained soil in full sun to partial shade. It is hardy to approximately USDA zone 7, tolerating brief freezes to −12°C.

Tubers should be planted in autumn, 5–8 cm deep and 10–15 cm apart. Soaking tubers in water for 12 hours before planting improves emergence rates.

A well-drained site is critical; tubers rot readily in waterlogged winter soils. Raised beds or sloping ground are ideal planting locations.

Flowering occurs in early to mid-spring, typically March through April in Mediterranean climates. After blooming, foliage persists for 4–6 weeks before dying back completely.

Summer irrigation during dormancy can cause tuber rot. Keep plantings dry from June through September in Mediterranean-style gardens.

The species adapts well to container culture and rock garden plantings. It naturalizes freely where conditions suit it, forming colorful colonies over several years.

Slugs and snails may damage emerging shoots in spring. Providing gritty top-dressing around the tuber helps deter mollusk damage.

## Ecological Role

Mediterranean anemones provide early nectar and pollen resources for emerging insects. Solitary bees and hoverflies are frequent visitors during the brief flowering period.

The tuber serves as a geophyte storage organ, enabling survival through the extended summer drought. This life strategy is characteristic of many Mediterranean bulbous and tuberous species.

Grazing pressure from wild herbivores such as rabbits and deer typically does not affect established tuber populations. The toxic protoanemonin content deters most browsing.

Seed dispersal occurs via wind, with each achene bearing a short, feathery style that aids aerial distribution. Seeds require a warm-cold stratification sequence for germination.

In some Mediterranean regions, dense patches of Anemone hortensis indicate areas of moderate grazing pressure. The species persists where competitive grasses are kept in check by herbivory.

## Horticultural Significance

Anemone hortensis is less commonly cultivated than Anemone coronaria but valued by collectors of Mediterranean flora. It is available from specialist bulb nurseries, particularly those sourcing from Mediterranean seed collections.

The species hybridizes readily with Anemone coronaria where their ranges overlap. Some cultivated forms may represent ancient garden hybrids between these two species.

In southern European gardens, it is used for spring color in dry borders and gravel gardens. The compact habit and vivid flower colors make it suitable for foreground plantings.

Dried specimens retain some color and are used in pressed flower arrangements.

It has received limited attention from plant breeders compared to Anemone coronaria. The de Caen and St. Brigid groups of garden anemones are derived from Anemone coronaria, not Anemone hortensis.

Collectors value the species for its genetic distinctness and potential as a breeding parent for improved heat tolerance in garden anemones.
