# Arthur Koestler

## Description

Arthur Koestler (1905–1983) was a Hungarian-British author, journalist, and philosopher. In Mollison's *[[permaculture]]: A Designer's Manual*, Koestler is quoted from his 1967 work *The Ghost in the Machine* to illustrate the concept of open systems in living organisms — that life maintains complex forms and functions through continuous exchanges of energy and materials with the environment, constantly building up more complex substances, energies, and informational patterns rather than running down like mechanical clocks.

## Classification

- **Category**: Person — Author, Philosopher, Journalist
- **Domain**: Philosophy of Science, Systems Theory, Psychology
- **Era**: 20th Century

## Key Facts

- Born in Budapest, Hungary; became a British citizen and wrote extensively in English and German.
- Best known for *Darkness at Noon* (1940), a novel critiquing totalitarianism and the Moscow show trials.
- Coined the term "holon" to describe entities that are simultaneously wholes and parts of larger wholes — a concept foundational to systems hierarchy theory.
- In *The Ghost in the Machine* (1967), explored the relationship between mind and brain, arguing against reductionist mechanistic explanations of consciousness.
- His description of living organisms as "open systems" that continuously exchange energy and materials with their environment is used by Mollison to ground permaculture theory in biological reality.
- Developed a theory of creativity involving bisociation — the intersection of two independent frames of reference.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Koestler's open-systems framework is directly applicable to ecological design and cultivation:

- **Open vs. closed systems**: Permaculture explicitly designs open systems that exchange nutrients, energy, and organisms with their surroundings, contrasting with industrial monocultures that attempt to control and close off biological processes.
- **Holon concept**: The holon idea maps directly onto ecological hierarchies — a mycelial network is simultaneously a whole organism and part of a larger forest ecosystem. Understanding these nested relationships is essential for guild design.
- **Complexity and self-organization**: Koestler's insight that living systems build complexity rather than degrade toward entropy underpins the permaculture principle that designed ecosystems can become more productive and resilient over time without external energy inputs.
