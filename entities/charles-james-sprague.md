# Charles James Sprague

## Description

Charles James Sprague was a Boston-based mycologist active in the 1870s

and 1880s, described by [[julius-auboineau-palmer]] as a 'mycologist of

rare attainments.' He made a significant contribution to the Boston

Library of the Natural History Society through the donation of original

correspondence and foreign works on [[mycology]].

Sprague was known for his cautious and conservative approach to fungal

identification, particularly his belief that intermediate forms existed

among the Agarics (gilled mushrooms) that no expert could reliably

identify with certainty.

## Classification

- **Nationality:** American
- **Location:** Boston, Massachusetts
- **Era:** 1870s-1880s
- **Field:** Mycology
- **Role:** Mycologist, library contributor, consultant

## Key Facts

- Donated original correspondence and foreign works to Boston Natural History Society
- Considered by Palmer to possess 'rare attainments' in mycological science
- Refused to allow any fungi except the common mushroom eaten in his household
- Believed intermediate forms among Agarics confounded even expert identification
- His caution exemplified the conservative approach Palmer generally advocated
- His library donation provided access to European works scarce in America
- Recognized by Palmer as one of the few American authorities on fungi
- His donation helped establish Boston as a center of [[palmer-mushroom-charts-american-mycological-education]] study

## Views on Fungal Identification

Sprague's position was notably conservative. He never allowed any fungi

eaten in his house except the common mushroom (*[[agaricus-campestris]]*).

He believed that among the Agarics, the largest family of gilled

mushrooms, intermediate forms existed that confounded even expert

identification. This view supported the broader consensus that no

general rule could distinguish edible from [[identifying-poisonous-mushrooms]].

Palmer cited Sprague's caution as additional evidence against popular

'rules' for identifying [[palmer-edible-mushrooms-north-america]], particularly the rule

proposed by Mr. Garlick in Forest and Stream.

## Contribution to Boston Mycology

Sprague's donation to the Natural History Society library was

significant scientific philanthropy. At a time when American

mycological literature was scarce, access to foreign works and

correspondence with European experts was invaluable for researchers.

This helped establish Boston as a center of American mycological

study in the late 19th century, alongside the collections at

Horticultural Hall.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Sprague's cautionary approach remains relevant to modern mycology and

foraging practice. His warning about intermediate forms among Agarics

highlights the difficulty of identifying species within large genera.

For cultivators, his emphasis on the limits of identification

underscores the importance of purchasing spawn from reliable sources

and verifying species identity through multiple characteristics.
