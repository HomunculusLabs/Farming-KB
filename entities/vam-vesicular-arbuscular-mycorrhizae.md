## VAM (Vesicular-Arbuscular Mycorrhizae)

## Description

Vesicular-Arbuscular Mycorrhizae (VAM), also called [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (AMF), are a type of endomycorrhizal symbiosis where [[fungal-hyphae]] penetrate the cortical cells of plant roots, forming specialized structures called arbuscules (for nutrient exchange) and vesicles (for storage). VAM is the most widespread mycorrhizal type, associating with approximately 80% of terrestrial plant species, including most agricultural crops, grasses, and many ornamentals. Dr. Elaine Ingham identifies VAM colonization levels as [[ingham-dissolved-oxygen-critical-thresholds-compost-tea]] for plant health and disease resistance.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Glomeromycota
- **Type**: Endomycorrhiza (arbuscular mycorrhiza)
- **Distinguished from**: Ectomycorrhizae (Hartig net outside root cells), [[comparison-orchid-mycorrhizae-vs-ericoid-mycorrhizae]] (Ericaceae-specific)
- **Key structures**: Arbuscules (nutrient exchange), vesicles (lipid storage), hyphae (soil exploration), spores (reproduction)

## Key Facts

- VAM colonization below 40% correlates with root disease problems, root-feeding nematodes, and other root pests.
- Once colonization exceeds 30-40%, root disease problems begin to diminish significantly.
- Colonization below 10-15% indicates that [[mycorrhizal-inoculation-guide]] is needed to improve plant health.
- Between 15-40% colonization, adding compost, compost tea, or humic acids to the root zone accelerates colonization.
- Compost and compost tea serve as food resources for VAM fungi, supplementing plant-derived carbon when conditions are suboptimal.
- Humic acids in compost and compost tea act as fungal foods that stimulate mycorrhizal growth and macroaggregate formation.
- VAM fungi are essential for soil structure: their hyphae bind soil particles into macroaggregates, improving water infiltration, aeration, and erosion resistance.
- Dr. Jim Trappe (Oregon State University) and Dr. Efren Cazares provided expertise on VAM identification and assessment protocols.
- SFI technicians trained by Cazares can differentiate VAM from ectomycorrhizal and ericoid [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] in root samples.
- VAM fungi are sometimes classified as parasites (they take carbon from the plant), but because both organisms benefit, the preferred term is mutualist.
- The original proof of concept for [[soil-food-web]] approach traces to Ingham et al. 1985, published in Ecological Monograph.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Crop production**: Most vegetable crops, grains, and fruit trees form VAM associations. Maintaining colonization above 40% is essential for optimal nutrient uptake and disease resistance.
- **[[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]]**: Understanding VAM dynamics is important for cultivators establishing outdoor mushroom beds or working with plants in integrated systems.
- **Soil restoration**: Degraded soils often have VAM colonization well below the 15% threshold. Inoculation combined with [[compost-tea-application-methods]] can accelerate recovery.
- **Disease management**: VAM fungi compete with root pathogens for space and resources, provide physical barriers to infection, and can trigger plant defense responses.
- **Nutrient cycling**: VAM hyphae extend far beyond root zones, accessing phosphorus, micronutrients, and water that roots alone cannot reach.
- **Tillage impact**: Excessive tillage severs VAM hyphal networks, reducing colonization and the benefits they provide. No-till or minimal-till practices preserve VAM function.
