## Overview

Lemna trisulca is a free-floating aquatic plant in the family Araceae (subfamily Lemnoideae, formerly Lemnaceae), commonly known as ivyleaf duckweed or star duckweed. Unlike most duckweed species, it grows primarily as a submerged plant rather than on the water surface.

The species is native to a broad range across Europe, Asia, and [[fungal-biosorption-of-heavy-metals-by-filamentous-fungi]] and nutrients makes it useful for water quality management.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Angiosperms → Monocots → Alismatales → Araceae → Lemnoideae → Lemna → Lemna trisulca L.

The species was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum. The specific epithet trisulca refers to the three-pronged branching pattern characteristic of the species.

Lemna trisulca has occasionally been placed in the separate genus Staurogeton by some authorities, though modern molecular phylogenetic studies support its placement within Lemna.

**Fronds** are elongated, narrowly lanceolate, and measure 5–12 mm long and 1–3 mm wide. They are typically green to dark green and translucent, especially when viewed against light.

Unlike surface-floating duckweeds, Lemna trisulca fronds are connected in branching chains. New fronds bud from a basal pocket and remain attached, forming Y-shaped or star-shaped colonies.

Each frond has a single root emerging from the ventral surface. Roots are relatively short (5–15 mm) compared to other Lemna species and lack a root cap.

**Reproduction** is primarily vegetative through budding from the basal meristem. Under favorable conditions, new fronds are produced every 2–4 days.

Flowers are rarely produced and consist of a single stamen and pistil within a dorsal pouch of the frond. Fruiting is uncommon in wild populations, and seed set is rare.

The species overwinters as dormant turions (dense, starch-filled buds) that sink to the substrate. These turions resume growth in spring when water temperatures rise above approximately 10 °C.

## Distribution and Habitat

Lemna trisulca has a circumboreal distribution across the Northern Hemisphere. It occurs throughout Europe, northern and central Asia, and most [[lemna-minor]], Spirodela polyrrhiza, [[aact-nitrogen-forms-microbial-nutrient-cycling-ingham]] through rapid uptake of dissolved nitrogen and phosphorus from the water column. Its subsurface growth position allows it to access nutrients throughout the water column.

Dense colonies can reduce underwater light penetration, potentially competing with submerged macrophytes. In oligotrophic (nutrient-poor) waters, this competition can be a limiting factor for other plant species.

Lemna trisulca serves as an indicator species for moderate trophic conditions. Its presence generally indicates mesotrophic to eutrophic waters with adequate light penetration.

Seasonal biomass dynamics play an important role in freshwater nutrient budgets. Autumn dieback releases stored nutrients back into the water column and sediment, fueling winter microbial activity.

The submerged growth habit provides microhabitat for periphyton (attached algae) and biofilm communities. These [[ingham-chelation-microbial-nutrient-processing]] within the water body.

## Phytoremediation and Water Quality

Lemna trisulca has demonstrated significant capacity for removing nutrients, heavy metals, and organic contaminants from polluted water. Its phytoremediation potential has been studied in both laboratory and field settings.

The species effectively removes ammonium and nitrate from wastewater, with reported nitrogen removal rates of 0.5–2.0 g per square meter per day. Phosphorus uptake rates are similarly high.

Lemna trisulca accumulates heavy metals including copper, zinc, lead, cadmium, and chromium in its frond tissues. Metal bioaccumulation factors are generally higher than in surface-floating duckweed species.
