## Overview

Lemna trisulca is a free-floating aquatic plant in the family Araceae (subfamily Lemnoideae, formerly Lemnaceae), commonly known as ivyleaf duckweed or star duckweed. Unlike most duckweed species, it grows primarily as a submerged plant rather than on the water surface.

The species is native to a broad range across Europe, Asia, and North America, where it inhabits still and slow-moving freshwater bodies. It is one of the most widely distributed duckweed species globally.

Lemna trisulca is distinguished from other Lemna species by its elongated, branching fronds that grow submerged beneath the water surface. Small colonies may occasionally rise to the surface during calm conditions.

The species has attracted interest for its phytoremediation capabilities and its role in freshwater ecosystem functioning. Its ability to accumulate heavy metals and nutrients makes it useful for water quality management.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Angiosperms → Monocots → Alismatales → Araceae → Lemnoideae → Lemna → Lemna trisulca L.

The species was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum. The specific epithet trisulca refers to the three-pronged branching pattern characteristic of the species.

Lemna trisulca has occasionally been placed in the separate genus Staurogeton by some authorities, though modern molecular phylogenetic studies support its placement within Lemna.

**Fronds** are elongated, narrowly lanceolate, and measure 5–12 mm long and 1–3 mm wide. They are typically green to dark green and translucent, especially when viewed against light.

Unlike surface-floating duckweeds, Lemna trisulca fronds are connected in branching chains. New fronds bud from a basal pocket and remain attached, forming Y-shaped or star-shaped colonies.

Each frond has a single root emerging from the ventral surface. Roots are relatively short (5–15 mm) compared to other Lemna species and lack a root cap.

**Reproduction** is primarily vegetative through budding from the basal meristem. Under favorable conditions, new fronds are produced every 2–4 days.

Flowers are rarely produced and consist of a single stamen and pistil within a dorsal pouch of the frond. Fruiting is uncommon in wild populations, and seed set is rare.

The species overwinters as dormant turions (dense, starch-filled buds) that sink to the substrate. These turions resume growth in spring when water temperatures rise above approximately 10 °C.

## Distribution and Habitat

Lemna trisulca has a circumboreal distribution across the Northern Hemisphere. It occurs throughout Europe, northern and central Asia, and most of North America south to approximately 35°N latitude.

In Europe, the species is widespread from the British Isles and Scandinavia to the Mediterranean and eastward to Siberia. It is considered common to abundant throughout most of its range.

The species grows in still or very slow-moving freshwater. Typical habitats include ponds, lakes, ditches, canals, and the backwaters of slow rivers. It tolerates a wide range of water chemistry.

Lemna trisulca prefers cool to temperate conditions and grows optimally at water temperatures between 15–25 °C. It is more cold-tolerant than many other duckweed species and can persist under ice cover.

The species typically occupies the subsurface zone, growing at depths of 0.5–2 meters below the water surface. It may occasionally reach the surface in sheltered areas with minimal water movement.

It tolerates moderate eutrophication and can persist in waters with elevated nutrient levels. However, excessive turbidity that reduces light penetration limits its growth since it depends on photosynthesis at depth.

Lemna trisulca frequently grows alongside Lemna minor, Spirodela polyrrhiza, Ceratophyllum demersum, and various submerged macrophytes such as Elodea and Potamogeton species.

## Ecological Role

Lemna trisulca provides important habitat structure in the subsurface zone of freshwater bodies. Its branching colonies create a three-dimensional matrix that shelters small invertebrates and fish larvae.

Waterfowl including teal, wigeon, and diving ducks consume Lemna trisulca as a food source, particularly during autumn migration. The plant's nutritional value is comparable to other duckweed species.

The species contributes to nutrient cycling through rapid uptake of dissolved nitrogen and phosphorus from the water column. Its subsurface growth position allows it to access nutrients throughout the water column.

Dense colonies can reduce underwater light penetration, potentially competing with submerged macrophytes. In oligotrophic (nutrient-poor) waters, this competition can be a limiting factor for other plant species.

Lemna trisulca serves as an indicator species for moderate trophic conditions. Its presence generally indicates mesotrophic to eutrophic waters with adequate light penetration.

Seasonal biomass dynamics play an important role in freshwater nutrient budgets. Autumn dieback releases stored nutrients back into the water column and sediment, fueling winter microbial activity.

The submerged growth habit provides microhabitat for periphyton (attached algae) and biofilm communities. These microbial communities contribute to decomposition and nutrient processing within the water body.

## Phytoremediation and Water Quality

Lemna trisulca has demonstrated significant capacity for removing nutrients, heavy metals, and organic contaminants from polluted water. Its phytoremediation potential has been studied in both laboratory and field settings.

The species effectively removes ammonium and nitrate from wastewater, with reported nitrogen removal rates of 0.5–2.0 g per square meter per day. Phosphorus uptake rates are similarly high.

Lemna trisulca accumulates heavy metals including copper, zinc, lead, cadmium, and chromium in its frond tissues. Metal bioaccumulation factors are generally higher than in surface-floating duckweed species.

The subsurface growth habit provides an advantage for phytoremediation because fronds have greater contact with the water column than surface-floating species. This increases the surface area available for contaminant uptake.

Constructed wetland systems incorporating Lemna trisulca have shown effective treatment of municipal wastewater, agricultural runoff, and aquaculture effluent. Removal efficiencies of 80–90% for nitrogen and 70–85% for phosphorus have been reported.

The species tolerates moderate levels of organic pollutants including phenols and some pesticides. Metabolic breakdown of these compounds occurs within plant tissues through enzymatic detoxification pathways.

Regular harvesting is necessary for sustained phytoremediation performance. Harvested biomass can be composted, used as animal feed (if contaminant levels are low), or processed for biogas production.

## Related Species

Lemna minor (common duckweed) is the most widespread surface-floating duckweed species. It is smaller and more rounded than Lemna trisulca and forms continuous mats on the water surface.

Lemna gibba (gibbous duckweed) is similar to Lemna minor but with a swollen, gibbous upper surface that traps air for buoyancy. It is more tolerant of nutrient-poor conditions than Lemna minor.

Spirodela polyrrhiza (greater duckweed) is the largest duckweed species, with fronds reaching 5–10 mm across and multiple roots per frond. It is more common in eutrophic waters.

Lemna turionifera (turion-forming duckweed) is a North American species that produces prominent turions for overwintering. It closely resembles Lemna minor but can be distinguished by its turion production.

Wolffia arrhiza is the smallest known flowering plant, consisting of tiny rootless fronds less than 1.5 mm long. It often grows in mixed duckweed communities alongside Lemna species.
