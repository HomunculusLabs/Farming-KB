## Overview

Actaea spicata, commonly known as herb bennet or baneberry, is a rhizomatous perennial herb in the family Ranunculaceae. It is native to the woodlands of Europe and western Asia, where it grows in deep shade on calcareous soils.

The species is easily recognized by its compound leaves, terminal racemes of small white flowers, and distinctive black berries. The berries are highly poisonous, containing cardiotoxic compounds that can cause serious illness if ingested.

Actaea spicata has a long history in European folk medicine, though its use has declined due to toxicity concerns. The plant was traditionally used to treat rheumatism, snake bites, and various [[permaculture-plants-herbaceous-perennials]] in the European flora, capable of photosynthesizing efficiently at very [[psilocybe-bohemica-central-european]] mountains).

The species is closely related to [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]]. Both species contain similar triterpene glycosides but differ in their pharmacological profiles.

## Morphology

Actaea spicata is a glabrous perennial with erect stems 30–60 cm tall. The stems arise from a short, thick rhizome with numerous fibrous roots.

Leaves are biternate to triternate, with leaflets 3–8 cm long, ovate to lanceolate, sharply toothed, and dark green. The lower leaves have long petioles, while upper leaves are nearly sessile.

The inflorescence is a short, oblong raceme 3–8 cm long at the stem apex. Flowers are small, 6–10 mm in diameter, with 3–5 white petaloid sepals and numerous stamens.

True petals are absent. The stamens are the showy part of the flower, with white filaments that give the raceme a fluffy appearance.

Flowering occurs from May through July. Pollination is primarily by small flies and beetles attracted by a faint unpleasant odor.

The fruit is a fleshy, ellipsoid berry 8–12 mm long, turning from green to glossy black at maturity. Each berry contains 6–12 small, triangular brown seeds approximately 3 mm long.

## Distribution and Habitat

Actaea spicata occurs throughout temperate Europe, from Britain and Scandinavia southward to the Mediterranean mountains. Its range extends eastward through central Europe to the Caucasus and western Siberia.

The species is most abundant in central European beech forests, where it is a characteristic species of the Fagetalia alliance. In southern Europe, populations are restricted to montane forests and moist ravines.

It grows in deep to partial shade on calcareous to neutral soils rich in humus. Typical habitats include limestone beechwoods, ash-alder ravine forests, and rocky woodland slopes.

Elevational range extends from near sea level in Scandinavia to 2,000 m in the Alps and Pyrenees. The species requires consistent soil moisture and does not tolerate drought.

Associated species include [[aconitum-napellus]].

Historical poisonings involved children attracted to the glossy black berries. No modern fatalities have been documented, though hospitalization is typically required after ingestion.

Skin contact with crushed plant material can cause contact dermatitis in sensitive individuals due to the presence of ranunculin-derived compounds.
