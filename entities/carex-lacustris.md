# Carex lacustris

## Overview

Carex lacustris, commonly known as lake sedge or hairy sedge, is a large perennial sedge in the family Cyperaceae.

It is one of the tallest and most robust native sedges in [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]] in lacustrine and riverine systems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- Kingdom: Plantae
- Order: Poales
- Family: Cyperaceae
- Genus: Carex
- Species: Carex lacustris Willd.

The species was described by Carl Ludwig Willdenow in 1805.

Stems are stout, sharply triangular, and reach 60–150 cm, among the tallest of North American Carex species.

Leaves are broad, 5–12 mm wide, firm, and typically exceed the stem height, giving plants a coarse, grass-like appearance.

Leaf sheaths are thick and the basal sheaths are conspicuously red-brown to purple, a useful identification feature.

The inflorescence is large and compound, with 3–5 lateral spikes on long peduncles arching below the terminal spike.

Perigynia are 5–8 mm long, ovoid to broadly ellipsoid, and prominently veined with a short bidentate beak.

The perigynia surface is densely hairy, particularly on the margins, distinguishing it from similar species.

Achenes are approximately 2.5 mm long, lenticular, and tightly enclosed within the persistent perigynium.

## Distribution and Habitat

Carex lacustris is native to eastern and central North America.

Its range extends from Manitoba and Ontario south through the Great Lakes states to Iowa, Missouri, and the [[typha-latifolia]], and [[pontederia-cordata]] in Great Lakes coastal wetlands.

## Ecology and Ecological Role

Carex lacustris is a foundational species in Great Lakes coastal marsh ecosystems.

Its robust root system provides effective shoreline stabilization, reducing wave erosion and protecting upland areas.

The tall, dense stands create important habitat structure for fish, amphibians, and aquatic invertebrates.

Northern pike and other fish species use lake sedge beds as spawning habitat and nursery areas.

The species provides nesting cover and foraging habitat for marsh birds, including least bittern, Virginia rail, and common gallinule.

Muskrats extensively use Carex lacustris for both food and lodge construction in northern marshes.

Its dense growth helps filter pollutants and trap sediments from agricultural and urban runoff entering lake systems.

The species can become aggressive in disturbed sites, occasionally colonizing ditches and artificial waterways.
