## Overview

**Atractylodes macrocephala** Koidz. (Compositae/Asteraceae) is a perennial herbaceous plant native to eastern and central China. Its dried rhizome, known as *Bai Zhu* (白术), is one of the most frequently prescribed herbs in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM).

The rhizome is valued for its tonifying effects on the spleen and digestive system, its diuretic properties, and its ability to stabilize pregnancy. Modern pharmacological research has confirmed immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and gastroprotective activities.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Atractylodes macrocephala** Koidz., Bot. Mag. (Tokyo) 38: 315 (1924). Family Asteraceae, tribe Cynareae. The genus Atractylodes contains approximately 7–10 species distributed across East Asia.

The plant is an erect perennial herb reaching 30–80 cm in height. The rhizome is thick, fleshy, irregularly lobed, 3–13 cm long, and brownish-gray externally with a yellowish-white interior. It is the primary organ of commercial and medicinal interest.

Stems are solitary or few, unbranched below the inflorescence, with a distinct node at the base. Leaves are alternate, the lower ones petiolate with deeply lobed blades, and the upper ones sessile and smaller. Leaf surfaces are green above and grayish-tomentose beneath.

The inflorescence is a terminal head (capitulum) 3–5 cm wide, surrounded by leaf-like bracts. Flowers are all tubular (disc florets), white to purplish. The fruit is an achene with a pappus of feathery bristles facilitating wind dispersal.

## Distribution and Habitat

A. macrocephala is endemic to China, occurring in Zhejiang, Anhui, Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi, Fujian, and Sichuan provinces. It grows on mountain slopes, forest edges, grassy hillsides, and cultivated fields at elevations of 500–1,800 m.

The plant prefers a cool, moist climate with well-drained, [[humus]]-rich soils. Harvesting occurs 2–3 years after planting when the aerial parts senesce in late autumn. Rhizomes are washed, sliced, and dried in the sun or by artificial heat. Processing methods (raw, stir-fried, bran-fried, soil-fried) produce different therapeutic properties.

## Processing Methods

TCM processing of Bai Zhu significantly alters its chemical composition and therapeutic effects. Raw Bai Zhu (Sheng Bai Zhu) retains the strongest drying and diuretic properties for resolving dampness.

Stir-frying with wheat bran (Fu Chao Bai Zhu) reduces the drying properties and enhances the spleen-tonifying effect. The bran absorbs some volatile oils while the mild heat promotes Maillard reactions that modify polysaccharide structures.

Soil-frying (Tu Chao Bai Zhu) involves wrapping the herb in yellow earth before roasting. This process further reduces the drying nature and is preferred for formulas targeting chronic diarrhea and loose stools. It adds a mild astringent quality.

Dry-frying (Chao Bai Zhu) without any medium is the simplest processing method. It removes excess moisture and slightly reduces volatile oil content, producing a milder preparation suitable for general tonification.

Charred Bai Zhu (Bai Zhu Tan) is prepared by stir-frying until the surface blackens. It is used specifically for stopping bleeding, particularly in cases of bloody stool associated with spleen deficiency.

## Related Species

Atractylodes lancea (Cang Zhu, 苍术) is the closest relative and most commonly confused species. Its rhizome is used for drying dampness and dispelling wind-cold, but lacks the spleen-tonifying properties of Bai Zhu.
