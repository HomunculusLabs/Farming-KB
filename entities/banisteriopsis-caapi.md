## Taxonomy and Botany

**Kingdom:** Plantae → **Division:** Tracheophyta → **Class:** Magnoliopsida → **Order:** Malpighiales → **Family:** Malpighiaceae → **Genus:** *Banisteriopsis* → **Species:** *B. caapi*

**Synonyms:** *Banisteriopsis inebrians*, *Banisteriopsis quitensis*, *Banisteriopsis candolleana*, *Premna caapi*

The vine is a massive, woody climber that can grow to 30 meters or more, ascending into the forest canopy. Key morphological features:

- **Stem:** Cylindrical, woody, 2–10 cm in diameter, with characteristic longitudinal fissures and a brown to reddish-brown bark. The inner wood is pale yellow to white. Cross-sections show a distinct pattern of concentric rings and vascular bundles.
- **Leaves:** Opposite, ovate to elliptical, 8–18 cm long, with entire margins and a short petiole. The upper surface is glossy dark green, the lower surface lighter with prominent venation. Two small glands (extrafloral nectaries) are present at the leaf base — a diagnostic feature of the Malpighiaceae.
- **Flowers:** Small, pink to magenta, borne in dense axillary and terminal panicles. Each flower has five petals fringed with fine hairs at the margins — a characteristic feature of the Malpighiaceae that facilitates pollination by oil-collecting bees.
- **Fruit:** A samara (winged achene) with three wings, brown when mature, facilitating wind dispersal. The fruit is approximately 1–2 cm in diameter.

The vine is difficult to distinguish in the field from related *Banisteriopsis* species, particularly *B. rusbyana* and *B. marginata*, which may also be used in ayahuasca preparations. Indigenous healers often distinguish between multiple "types" of ayahuasca vine based on subtle bark characteristics, growth habit, and the qualitative nature of the visionary experience each produces.

## Phytochemistry

*B. caapi* contains a distinctive profile of β-carboline alkaloids concentrated primarily in the bark:

**Primary alkaloids:**

| Alkaloid | Typical concentration | Pharmacological profile |
|---|---|---|
| **Harmine** | 0.18–1.36% dry weight | Potent reversible MAO-A inhibitor, also binds 5-HT₂A and benzodiazepine receptors |
| **Harmaline** | 0.05–0.78% dry weight | Reversible MAO-A inhibitor, more sedating than harmine |
| **Tetrahydroharmine (THH)** | 0.05–0.68% dry weight | Weak MAO-A inhibitor, serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SRI), may contribute to ayahuasca's antidepressant effects |

The total β-carboline content varies significantly based on genetics, growing conditions, and the specific chemotype. Four main chemotypes have been identified based on the relative ratios of harmine, harmaline, and THH, and indigenous healers (ayahuasqueros) reportedly select specific vine varieties for different ceremonial purposes.

**Other constituents:** The vine contains proanthocyanidins, catechin, epicatechin, and various flavonoid glycosides. These polyphenolic compounds may contribute to the vine's antioxidant and neuroprotective properties observed in modern research. Shikimic acid has also been isolated from the vine.

## Traditional Use and Cultural Significance

Ayahuasca is deeply embedded in the cultural and spiritual practices of at least 72 indigenous ethnic groups across the Amazon basin, spanning Brazil, Peru, Ecuador, Colombia, Bolivia, and the Guiana Shield.

**Key cultural contexts:**

- **Shipibo-Conibo people (Peru):** Among the most extensively documented ayahuasca traditions. The vine is central to healing ceremonies led by *onányá* (healers), who receive *ícaros* (medicine songs) during [[mckenna-shamanic-tobaccos-and-nicotiana-rustica]]*) mapacho smoke for diagnostic and cleansing purposes.

**Syncretic religious movements:**
- **Santo Daime:** Founded in the Brazilian Amazon in the 1930s by Raimundo Irineu Serra, this syncretic religion incorporates ayahuasca (called *daime*) as a central sacrament in ritual ceremonies featuring hymns and communal dancing.
- **União do Vegetal (UDV):** Founded in 1961 by José Gabriel da Costa, the UDV uses ayahuasca (called *hoasca*) in structured ceremonial sessions focused on spiritual development and mental concentration. The UDV has achieved legal recognition in Brazil, the United States, and several other countries.

## Pharmacology of Ayahuasca

The synergistic interaction between *B. caapi* (MAO inhibition) and DMT-containing admixture plants (*Psychotria viridis*, *Diplopterys cabrerana*) is one of the most remarkable examples of ethnopharmacological knowledge:

1. **MAO-A inhibition:** Harmine and harmaline reversibly inhibit MAO-A enzymes in the gut wall and liver, preventing the oxidative deamination of orally ingested DMT
2. **DMT activation:** With MAO-A inhibited, DMT passes through the gut wall, enters the hepatic portal system, and reaches systemic circulation at sufficient concentrations to cross the blood-brain barrier
3. **Central effects:** DMT acts as a 5-HT₂A receptor agonist in the cerebral cortex, producing the characteristic visionary and introspective effects
