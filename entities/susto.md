## Description

Susto (from Spanish "fright" or "soul-loss") is a folk illness recognized throughout Peru and Latin America, characterized by profound alteration of metabolism or nervous disorder following a traumatic emotional event. It is one of the most frequently treated conditions by Peruvian folk healers, including ayahuasqueros in the Amazon region. The illness is caused by the loss of the sick person's soul, which departs the body following a terrifying experience.

## Classification

- **Type:** Folk illness / culture-bound syndrome
- **Geographic distribution:** Throughout Latin America, especially Peru, Amazon Basin, and Mesoamerica
- **Medical category:** Psychosomatic / psychocultural disorder
- **Alternative names:** Soul-loss, espanto, pasmo, perdicion del alma
- **Primary symptom cluster:** Metabolic disruption, nervous disorder, loss of appetite and energy

## Key Facts

- Susto is an intense psychic trauma provoked by an emotion of fear
- Primary symptoms include loss of appetite, lack of energy, and profound alteration of metabolism
- The illness is attributed to the loss of the sick person's soul -- the soul is believed to depart the body following a terrifying experience
- It is one of the most frequent types of illness treated by Peruvian folk healers
- Susto can be triggered by sudden fright, accidents, encounters with supernatural beings, or other traumatic events
- The condition represents a culture-specific framework for understanding post-traumatic stress and dissociative symptoms
- Treatment typically involves ritual healing aimed at recalling the lost soul to the body
- In the Belen district of Iquitos, susto is commonly treated by ayahuasqueros using ayahuasca alongside traditional curing techniques
- Susto is distinguished from other folk illnesses like dano (magical harm) and mal de ojo (evil eye) by its specific causal mechanism of soul-loss through fright
- The condition is found throughout Peru and across Latin America, making it one of the most widely recognized culture-bound syndromes in the Americas

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Susto represents one of the primary categories of illness that Amazonian folk healers treat using plant medicines including ayahuasca. Understanding susto is essential for comprehending the therapeutic context in which psychedelic plants are used in Amazonian cultures. The condition illustrates how plant hallucinogens are employed not as direct curative agents but as diagnostic and revelatory tools -- ayahuasca visions help the healer identify the traumatic event that caused soul-loss and guide the soul-recovery ritual. For cultivators and researchers interested in ethnopharmacology, susto demonstrates the deeply embedded cultural framework that gives meaning to plant medicine use in Amazonian healing traditions.

## Treatment Context

- Healers use ayahuasca visions to identify the cause and nature of the soul-loss
- Traditional curing techniques include whistling, singing, recitation of orations, sucking at afflicted body regions, and blowing tobacco smoke
- Herbal remedies may supplement the primary ritual treatment
- The healer's diagnostic process involves analyzing visionary content experienced during ayahuasca intoxication
- Recovery depends on successful recall of the departed soul through ritual intervention
- Simple illnesses are rarely treated with ayahuasca; herbs, plants, and store-bought medicines are prescribed for those types of afflictions

## Differential Diagnosis

- Susto: soul-loss from fright, causing metabolic and nervous disruption
- Dano: magical harm from envy or vengeance, causing varied physical symptoms
- Pulsario: repressed emotions forming a painful ball at the stomach, mainly in women
- Mal de ojo: illness from the evil eye, especially in children

## Cross-Cultural Significance

Susto is not unique to the Peruvian Amazon. It is found throughout Mexico, Central America, and South America, where it goes by various names including espanto, pasmo, and perdicion del alma. The condition has been the subject of extensive anthropological and psychiatric study, with researchers attempting to bridge indigenous explanatory models with Western diagnostic categories. Some researchers have compared susto to post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and dissociative disorders, noting parallels in symptom presentation while emphasizing the culturally specific causal explanation of soul-loss.

## Treatment Protocol

When treating susto with ayahuasca, the healer follows a structured protocol:
1. The patient describes the triggering event and their symptoms during an initial consultation
2. The healer assesses whether ayahuasca is appropriate (simple illnesses are treated with herbs and store-bought medicines instead)
3. During the ceremony, the healer uses ayahuasca visions to identify the moment of soul-loss
4. The healer performs ritual techniques (whistling, singing, sucking, tobacco smoke) to recall the departed soul
5. The patient receives counseling and is ritually exorcised throughout the session
6. Dietary restrictions are imposed before and after the session (no salt, lard, or sweets for 24 hours)
7. Follow-up consultations determine whether the soul has been successfully recovered

## Comparative Folk Illness Framework

The four major magical illnesses in the Iquitos area can be distinguished by their causal mechanisms:
- Susto: caused by soul-loss through fright (environmental trigger)
- Dano: caused by intentional magical harm from another person (social trigger)
- Pulsario: caused by repressed emotional content becoming somatized (internal trigger)
- Mal de ojo: caused by the power of another's gaze (interpersonal trigger)
