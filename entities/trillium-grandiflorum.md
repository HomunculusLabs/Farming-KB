The species is the provincial flower of Ontario and the state wildflower of Ohio. It serves as an important indicator of high-quality, undisturbed forest habitat.

Large natural colonies can contain thousands of individuals, forming spectacular white carpets on the forest floor in peak bloom years. Flowering typically lasts 2–3 weeks before petals senesce.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*T. grandiflorum* belongs to the family Melanthiaceae (previously Trilliaceae or Liliaceae). It was described by André Michaux in 1803. The specific epithet *grandiflorum* means "large-flowered," referring to its prominent blooms.

Phylogenetic studies confirm that *T. grandiflorum* forms a well-supported clade with other pedicellate trilliums. Molecular data suggest the genus diverged from its closest relatives during the late Eocene, approximately 35–40 million years ago.

The plant grows 20–40 cm tall from a thick, fleshy rhizome. A single unbranched stem bears a whorl of three sessile, ovate leaves 8–18 cm long at the apex.

Mature rhizomes can reach 2–5 cm in diameter and produce annual growth rings visible in cross-section. The rhizome stores starch reserves that power both spring emergence and subsequent flowering.

Flowers are borne above the leaves on a short pedicel. Each flower has three white petals 3–7 cm long, three green sepals, six stamens with pale yellow anthers, and a three-lobed ovary.

The fruit is a fleshy, greenish-white berry 1–1.5 cm across, ripening to purplish-red. Each berry contains 15–60 seeds with an oily elaiosome attractive to ants.

*T. grandiflorum* is part of the *Trillium* subgenus *Trillium*. It is most closely related to *T. erectum* (red trillium) and *T. cernuum* (nodding trillium), with which it occasionally hybridizes in areas of range overlap.

Natural hybrids with *T. erectum*, known as *T.* × *helodes*, produce flowers intermediate in color between white and maroon.
