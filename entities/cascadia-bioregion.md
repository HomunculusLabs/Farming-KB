## Cascadia

## Description

Cascadia is a bioregion in the Pacific Northwest of North America, encompassing the area west of the Cascade Mountains. It extends from southern British Columbia through western Washington and Oregon to northern California, including nearby islands. The region is characterized by its distinct maritime climate with cool, frequently cloudy summers and mild, wet winters. These climatic conditions significantly influence gardening and agricultural practices in the area. The term "Cascadia" is used both as a geographic descriptor and as a cultural-environmental identity that reflects the shared ecology of the Pacific Northwest coastal zone.

## Classification

- **Type:** Bioregion / geographic area
- **Location:** Pacific Northwest, North America
- **Boundaries:** West of the Cascade Mountains, from British Columbia to northern California
- **Climate:** Maritime, Mediterranean-like with dry summers and wet winters
- **Major cities:** Seattle, Portland, Vancouver (BC), Eugene, Olympia
- **Elevation range:** Sea level to mountainous terrain within short distances

## Key Facts

- The region's cool, frequently cloudy summers mean most vegetables grow more slowly than in warmer areas of the United States or Canada.
- Mild winters allow gardeners to harvest frost-hardy crops year-round without glass protection or heated greenhouses.
- The climate is especially well-suited to cool-season crops including brassicas, root vegetables, and salad greens throughout the year.
- Steve Solomon wrote *Waterwise Vegetables* specifically about dry gardening in Cascadia, focusing on methods that reduce or eliminate irrigation by spacing plants widely apart.
- Rainfall patterns in Cascadia are highly seasonal, with most precipitation falling between October and May, creating summer drought conditions that make water-wise gardening essential.
- The bioregion's unique climate made it an important seed-growing area; the Territorial Seed Company was founded here partly because of the favorable conditions for seed production.
- Summer drought typically runs from June through September, requiring gardeners to either irrigate, use dry-gardening techniques, or accept reduced yields.
- The Cascade Mountains create a rain shadow effect: areas west of the crest receive abundant rainfall (40-60+ inches annually), while areas east are significantly drier.
- Soil types in the lowlands are often heavy clay or glacial till, requiring significant amendment with organic matter for productive gardening.
- The region's native vegetation includes dense coniferous forests (Douglas fir, western red cedar, hemlock) which contribute to acidic soil conditions.
- The mild, moist climate is favorable for a wide range of fungi, both mycorrhizal and saprophytic, making it one of the most mushroom-rich regions in North America.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- The maritime climate of Cascadia is relevant to outdoor mushroom cultivation, particularly for species adapted to cool, moist conditions such as oyster mushrooms and wine caps.
- The region's abundant woody debris, consistent moisture, and temperate forests create excellent natural habitat for many fungi species including morels, chanterelles, and matsutake.
- Understanding the Cascadia bioregion's rainfall patterns and soil types is essential for planning both vegetable gardens and outdoor mushroom beds or logs.
- The seasonal drought cycle (dry summers) means supplemental watering or shade may be needed for mushroom cultivation during summer months.
- The mild winters allow extended growing seasons and year-round outdoor cultivation possibilities not available in continental climates.
- Hardwood trees common in the region (alder, maple, oak) provide excellent substrate material for mushroom log cultivation.
- The region's reputation as a mushroom foraging destination reflects the natural suitability of the climate for fungal growth and cultivation.
- Cascadia's climate is closely analogous to parts of Britain and Tasmania, allowing cross-application of techniques from all three regions.

## Geographic Boundaries

The Cascadia bioregion encompasses the watersheds of rivers flowing
into the Pacific Ocean through the Cascade Range, from northern
California through British Columbia. It spans approximately
550,000 square kilometers.

## Ecological Characteristics

Cascadia features temperate rainforests, alpine meadows, coastal
estuaries, and dry interior plateaus. The region contains some of
Earth's largest remaining old-growth forests and most productive
salmon streams.

## Agricultural Significance

The mild maritime climate supports diverse agriculture including
berry production, specialty mushrooms, wine grapes, and year-round
vegetable growing in the Willamette and Fraser Valleys.

## Keystone Species

Cascadia's ecosystems depend on keystone species including Pacific
salmon, which transport marine nutrients upstream, Douglas fir forests
that regulate water cycles, and beavers that create wetland habitats.

## Conservation Challenges

Urban expansion, logging, and climate change threaten Cascadia's
biodiversity. Conservation efforts focus on wildlife corridors,
watershed protection, and sustainable forestry certification.
