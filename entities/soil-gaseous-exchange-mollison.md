## Description

Soil gaseous exchange refers to the continuous movement of gases between the atmosphere and soil pore spaces, driven by physical forces (earth tides, barometric pressure changes, wind) and biological processes (plant transpiration, animal burrowing, microbial metabolism). This exchange is essential for root respiration, nutrient cycling, and maintaining the aerobic-anaerobic mosaic that supports healthy soil function.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil physics / soil atmosphere
- **Key processes**: Diffusion, mass flow, biological exchange, earth tides
- **Key gases**: Nitrogen (80%), Oxygen (18%), CO2, ammonia, methane, ethylene, radon

## Key Facts

### Physical Exchange Mechanisms

- **Earth tides**: Lunar tides affect groundwaters and continental masses (about 25 cm rise and fall across the continental USA), causing air to be drawn in and expelled from soils
- **Barometric pressure**: Low and high pressure cells and turbulent wind flow create pressure differentials that draw out or inject air via crevices, fissures, and burrows
- **Wind effects**: Fast surface airflow draws gases from soil and dries surface soils
- Estuary and mud flat soils show visible air bubbling as tides rise

### Biological Exchange

- A single large broadleaf tree can increase the transpiration area of one acre of soil by a factor of 40
- A forest can increase transpiration area by hundreds of times
- Plants transpire oxygen, carbon dioxide, water vapour, metallic vapours, ammonia, hydrogen, and chlorine gases
- Many non-woody plant species consume more oxygen than they produce
- Animals create burrows that function as pump pistons (like a train in a tunnel) to draw in and exhaust gases
- Many burrowers (ants, crabs, termites, prairie dogs, worms, land crayfish) build mounds or chimneys that act as Pitot tubes for airflow
- Termite mounds are large permeable surface structures across which waste gases diffuse

### Gases Manufactured in Soils

- **Ammonia**: Produced via titanium or rutile (TiFe) in sands under sunlight; released from actively nodulating legumes
- **Ethylene**: Produced via ferrous iron in anaerobic soil micro-sites
- **Biogas/marsh gas**: CO2, methane, sulphuretted hydrogen, ethylene, and sulphur dioxide from anaerobic soils and waters
- Much atmospheric ammonia, carbon dioxide, and at least 16% of atmospheric methane comes from soil
- Molybdenum, vanadium, and zinc assist root bacteria in creating available soil nitrogen as catalysts

## Gas Exchange Processes

Soil respiration produces CO2 through microbial and root metabolism. Oxygen
diffuses from the atmosphere into soil pores to support aerobic processes.
When water fills pore spaces, anaerobic conditions develop, producing methane,
nitrous oxide, and hydrogen sulfide instead.

## Management Implications

Mollison emphasized that soil aeration directly affects root health and
microbial community composition. Compacted soils restrict gas exchange,
limiting root respiration and favoring anaerobic pathogens. Cover crops,
minimal tillage, and organic matter additions maintain soil porosity.

## Monitoring Soil Aeration

Mollison recommended observing soil color as an indicator of aeration status.
Well-aerated soils display uniform brown or reddish colors from iron oxidation.
Gray or mottled patterns indicate periodic waterlogging and reduced gas exchange.
Earthworm activity at the surface signals healthy below-ground aeration.

## Design Applications

Permaculture designers use earthworks like swales and raised beds to improve
soil aeration in wet areas. Aggressive deep-rooting cover crops like daikon
radish and chicory create bio-drainage channels that persist after decomposition.

## Plant Root Adaptations

Plants adapt to low-oxygen soils through aerenchyma tissue development, which
forms air channels connecting roots to above-ground atmosphere. Wetland species
like rushes and sedges excel at this adaptation. Mollison noted that permaculture
pond edges benefit from these natural oxygen-transporting root systems.
