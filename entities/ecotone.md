## Ecotone

## Description

An ecotone is the transitional edge or zone between two bioregions or ecological communities where the distribution of species from both regions overlaps, creating greater biodiversity than exists in either of the respective regions alone. Ecotones are fundamental to understanding landscape-scale patterns of diversity and are a key concept underpinning permaculture Principle 11: Use Edges and Value the Marginal.

## Classification

- **Type:** Ecological concept / landscape feature
- **Discipline:** Biogeography, ecology
- **Related terms:** [[edge-effect]], transitional zone, tension zone

## Key Facts

- **Biodiversity amplification:** Ecotones support species from both adjacent bioregions plus species unique to the transition zone itself, resulting in greater total biodiversity than either bordering region.

- **Scale varies by generating factor:**
  - Altitude-generated ecotones may be narrow (less than 1 kilometre wide)
  - Latitude-generated ecotones may span tens of kilometres
  - Distance-from-sea (continental vs. maritime) ecotones may also be very broad

- **[[towamba-valley]] example:** The Towamba Valley on the far south coast of New South Wales represents one of the most important ecotones on the Australian continent — a three-way edge between the warm temperate east coast, the cool temperate south coast, and the cold dry plateaus of the Monaro region. A single 75-hectare bush property in this valley supported 13 species of eucalypts from all three bioregions, compared to the typical 2-8 species found on similar-sized properties elsewhere in southern Australia.

- **Settlement patterns:** Geographers recognise that older cities and towns were typically located at ecotones — edges between at least two major resource regions or land systems. Cities are commonly built on harbours along coasts, adjacent to great rivers, at river confluences, or on foot slopes below mountains and above adjacent plains.

- **Analogous to Venn diagrams:** Ecotones can be conceptualised as Venn diagram overlaps, where the intersection of two biogeographical sets creates a zone of combined and unique elements.

## Types of Ecotones

1. **Altitudinal:** Transitions driven by elevation changes (e.g., lowland forest to montane vegetation)
2. **Latitudinal:** Transitions driven by climate gradients (e.g., temperate to subtropical)
3. **Continental-maritime:** Transitions driven by distance from ocean influence
4. **Edaphic:** Transitions driven by soil type changes
5. **Hydrological:** Transitions along waterways and wetland margins
6. **Cultural:** Transitions between different land use or management regimes

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

The ecotone concept is central to [[permaculture-design]]:

1. **Principle 11 — Use Edges and Value the Marginal:** Ecotones are nature's premier example of productive edges. Understanding how they function provides a template for designing productive edges in gardens, farms, and settlements.

2. **Design implication:** Rather than seeking uniform conditions, permaculture designers can deliberately create and exploit ecotones — zones where different microclimates, soil types, or management regimes meet — to maximise diversity and productivity.

3. **Site selection:** Properties located at ecological edges (e.g., where two soil types meet, or at altitude transitions) may offer greater natural diversity and productive potential than sites in the middle of homogeneous regions.

4. **Riparian zones:** Vegetation adjacent to streams and waterways (riparian vegetation) is often more diverse and dense than vegetation further from water — a common ecotone type that permaculture design seeks to protect and enhance.

5. **Property design:** Within a single property, designers can create micro-ecotones through strategic placement of trees, water bodies, structures, and different land uses, mimicking the diversity-amplifying effects of natural ecotones at a manageable scale.
