The plant produces upright flower spikes bearing tubular lavender-purple flowers with white throats. Blooms appear in late spring to early summer, lasting 3–4 weeks.

Hairy beardtongue reaches 30–75 cm in height with opposite, lance-shaped leaves that are distinctly pubescent (hairy), giving the plant its common and scientific names.

The species is valued for its adaptability to poor, dry soils and its usefulness in native plant gardens, rock gardens, and pollinator plantings. It is among the easiest penstemons to grow in eastern North America.

Penstemon hirsutus is the most shade-tolerant of the eastern penstemon species. It grows well in woodland edges and light shade where other penstemons fail to thrive.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Penstemon hirsutus was described by Carl Ludwig Willdenow in 1800. The species belongs to Penstemon section Penstemon, the largest section of the genus in eastern North America.

Basal leaves form a rosette of oblong to lance-shaped leaves 5–15 cm long. Stem leaves are narrower, opposite, and sessile, becoming smaller toward the apex.

The stems and leaves are covered with fine, spreading hairs (hispid pubescence). This hairiness distinguishes P. hirsutus from the similar but glabrous P. digitalis.

Flowers are arranged in a loose panicle (thyrsus) with 10–30 blooms per stem. Each flower is 2–2.5 cm long, tubular with a two-lipped corolla in lavender to pale purple.

The sterile stamen (staminode) bears golden-yellow hairs at the tip, a characteristic feature of the genus. The species name "hirsutus" refers to the hairy foliage and stems.

Flowers open sequentially from bottom to top on each stem, extending the effective bloom period to 3–4 weeks per stem. Multiple stems per plant further extend the display.

## Distribution and Habitat

Penstemon hirsutus occurs across eastern North America from southern Ontario and Maine west to Minnesota, and south to Georgia, Alabama, and Arkansas. It is absent from the coastal plain of the deep South.

The species inhabits dry open woods, woodland edges, rocky slopes, prairie remnants, and roadsides. It prefers well-drained soils in full sun to partial shade.

Unlike most penstemons, P. hirsutus tolerates considerable shade. It is often found in dappled woodland clearings and along forest trails where it receives filtered sunlight.

Soil preferences range from sandy loams to rocky, shallow soils over bedrock. The plant tolerates acidic to mildly alkaline conditions (pH 5.0–7.5).

Populations are stable across most of the range. The species is not considered threatened or endangered in any state within its native distribution.

Hairy beardtongue shows considerable phenotypic [[penstemon-digitalis]] where their ranges overlap. Natural hybrids display intermediate characteristics in flower color and leaf shape.

## Cultivation

Hairy beardtongue is among the easiest penstemons to grow in eastern [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] root readily without hormone treatment.

Division is possible but not often necessary. Plants are longer-lived than many penstemons, persisting 5–8 years in favorable sites.

Penstemon hirsutus has no serious pest problems. The fuzzy foliage deters most herbivorous insects, and deer rarely browse the plants.

Cutting flower stems after bloom encourages a second, smaller flush of flowers. Leave some stems to self-seed if naturalizing is desired.

## Ecological Role

Penstemon hirsutus is a valuable nectar plant for long-tongued bees, particularly bumblebees (Bombus spp.) and large carpenter bees (Xylocopa spp.). The tubular flowers restrict access to shorter-tongued visitors.

The plant serves as a larval host for the penstemon borer moth (Papaipema approximata). This specialist herbivore feeds internally on the stems of Penstemon species.

Hummingbirds visit the flowers regularly, though the lavender coloration is less attractive to them than red-flowered penstemon species. Ruby-throated hummingbirds are the most frequent avian visitors.

Seed capsules provide food for goldfinches and other small songbirds in late summer. The small seeds are rich in lipids and proteins.

The species integrates well with native grasses in meadow and prairie plantings. Its vertical flower spikes provide structural contrast to mounded grasses and forbs.
