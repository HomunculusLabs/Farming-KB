## Overview

**Metarhizium anisopliae** is a cosmopolitan entomopathogenic fungus widely used as a [[query-how-do-green-roofs-and-living-walls-benefit-urban-environments]] rol in crops, pastures, and urban environments.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Ascomycota
- **Subphylum:** Pezizomycotina
- **Class:** Sordariomycetes
- **Order:** Hypocreales
- **Family:** Clavicipitaceae
- **Genus:** Metarhizium
- **Species:** M. anisopliae (Metschn.) Sorokīn

Molecular phylogenetics has revealed that *M. anisopliae* is a species complex containing multiple cryptic species.

Bischoff et al. (2009) reclassified the complex, recognizing *M. anisopliae* sensu stricto alongside species such as *M. robertsii* and *M. brunneum*.

## Morphology

Colonies on PDA are olive-green to dark green, growing moderately fast (5–8 mm/day at 25°C) with a velvety to powdery surface texture.

Conidiophores are branched, bearing whorls of phialides that produce cylindrical to slightly falcate conidia in chains.

Conidia (spores) measure 5–9 × 2–3 μm, are hyaline when young and become green en masse at maturity, serving as the primary infective propagule.

The fungus also produces blastospores (yeast-like cells) during in vivo growth within the insect hemocoel.

A distinct feature is the formation of appressoria (infection structures) on the host cuticle surface, which generate mechanical pressure for penetration.

In advanced infection, the fungus erupts through the insect integument and sporulates on the cadaver surface, producing green spore mats visible to the naked eye.

## Infection Process

Infection begins when a conidium lands on the host cuticle, adheres via hydrophobic interactions and specialized adhesion proteins.

The conidi [[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]] nation of tissue destruction, nutrient depletion, and toxic metabolite production.

## Bioactive Compounds

*M. anisopliae* produces a diverse array of [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] that contribute to pathogenicity and host mortality.

**Destruxins** (cyclodepsipeptides) are the most studied toxins, with destruxin A, B, and E being the most prevalent.

Destruxins disrupt insect immune function by inhibiting phagocytosis, suppressing phenoloxidase activity, and damaging hemocyte membranes.

The fungus also produces swainsonine, an indolizidine alkaloid that inhibits α-mannosidase, though this compound is more prominently associated with *Metarhizium* spp. from plant endophytic contexts.

Cyanide production has been observed in some strains, contributing to rapid host mortality during the late stages of infection.

Additional metabolites include cytochalasins, helvolic acid, and various small peptides with insecticidal activity.

## Agricultural and Permaculture Applications

*M. anisopliae* is widely deployed in [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] management (IPM) programs as an alternative to synthetic chemical insecticides.

Target pests include termites (Isoptera), grasshoppers and locusts (Orthoptera), whiteflies (Hemiptera), and various beetle larvae (Coleoptera).

In permaculture systems, the fungus is valued for its specificity — most strains show narrow host ranges, preserving [[comparison-pheromone-based-pest-control-vs-beneficial-insect-biocontrol]] populations.

The fungus can be applied as conidial sprays, granular formulations, or bait stations, depending on the target pest and crop system.

[[blesching-cannabis-strain-selection-receptor-targeting]] is critical, as virulence varies significantly among isolates; commercial products typically use proprietary high-virulence strains.

The fungus persists in soil for months to years, providing residual pest control and reducing the need for repeated applications.
