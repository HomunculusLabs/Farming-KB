# Armillaria mellea

**Type:** Species (Fungus)
**Category:** Forest Pathogen / Wood-Decay Basidiomycete
**Also known as:** Honey fungus, Honey mushroom, Armillaria mellea
  (Vahl) P. Kumm.

## Overview
Armillaria mellea is a basidiomycete fungus that functions both as a
significant forest pathogen and a wood-decay saprotroph. It is one of the
largest living organisms on Earth, with individual clones (genets) spanning
hectares. The species complex includes A. ostoyae, A. bulbosa, and others,
some of which form the largest known terrestrial organisms by biomass.

## Taxonomy and Classification
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Physalacriaceae
- **Genus:** Armillaria
- **Species:** A. mellea (species complex includes A. ostoyae, A.
  bulbosa, A. gallica, A. tabescens, and others)

## Key Details

### Morphology
- Fruiting bodies are honey-colored mushrooms with clustered growth
  on wood or at the base of trees
- Cap is 3 to 10 cm, convex to flat, honey-brown with darker scales
- Gills are white, becoming pinkish with age, running down the stem
- Stem has a prominent annulus (ring) and is fibrous
- Spore print is white
- Forms distinctive black, shoestring-like rhizomorphs that
  transport nutrients over long distances

### Pathogenic Behavior
- Causes root rot and white rot of wood in a wide range of tree
  species, both conifers and hardwoods
- Rhizomorphs spread through soil to infect healthy trees
- Can kill trees by girdling the root collar and decaying root and
  trunk tissue
- Particularly damaging to orchard trees (oak, apple, walnut) and
  forest plantations

### Remarkable Size and Longevity
- A. ostoyae specimen in Oregon covers approximately 9.6 square
  kilometers, making it one of the largest organisms by area
- Estimated age of large genets is 2,400 to 8,650 years
  (Smith, Bruhn, and Anderson, 1992)
- Genet size is determined by somatic incompatibility testing

### Heavy Metal Accumulation
- Rhizomorphs with melanized outer cortex accumulate heavy metals
  from the environment (Rizzo et al., 1992)
- Some elements concentrated 50 to 100 times more in fungus than in
  surrounding soil
- Concentrations in rhizomorphs: Al 3,440, Zn 1,930, Fe 1,890, Cu
  15, Pb 680 mg/g
- Metal ions accumulate in the outer rhizomorph cortex, not the
  interior
- Metal coating may protect rhizomorphs from microbial degradation
  and grazing fauna

### Rhizomorph Function
- Melanized rhizomorphs serve as exploratory organs, growing through
  soil to locate new woody hosts
- Function in long-distance transport of water and nutrients
- Can translocate radiocesium and other elements through the thallus
- The melanized cortex provides structural integrity and protection

### Ecological Role
- Functions as both pathogen and saprotroph, killing weakened trees
  and then decomposing the dead wood
- Creates canopy gaps that promote forest regeneration and diversity
- Plays a role in carbon cycling and nutrient redistribution in forest
  ecosystems
- Dead Armillaria wood serves as habitat for insects and other fungi

## Relevance
Armillaria mellea is a critical forest pathogen that can devastate orchards
and timber plantations. Understanding its biology is essential for forest
management and disease control. In permaculture, it highlights the
complexity of fungal roles in ecosystems, where a single organism acts as
both pathogen and decomposer. Management strategies include removing
infected stumps, improving tree vigor, and selecting resistant rootstocks.
