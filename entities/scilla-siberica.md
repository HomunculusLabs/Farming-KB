## Overview

Scilla siberica, commonly known as Siberian squill or wood squill, is a small bulbous perennial in the family Asparagaceae, subfamily Scilloideae.

The species has been cultivated in European gardens since the 1790s and remains one of the most popular small bulbs for spring displays. Its intense blue color is rarely matched in the temperate bulb palette.

Despite its common name, the species is native to southern Russia, the Caucasus, and Turkey rather than Siberia proper. It produces intense blue, bell-shaped flowers in early spring that are among the most vivid blues in the temperate garden.

The species has become widely naturalized in Europe and North America, where it is commonly planted in lawns and woodland gardens for spring color. It is one of the most familiar and commercially available spring-blooming bulbs.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Monocots
- **Order:** Asparagales
- **Family:** Asparagaceae
- **Subfamily:** Scilloideae
- **Genus:** Scilla
- **Species:** S. siberica

The genus Scilla has been subject to significant taxonomic revision. Many former Scilla species have been transferred to other genera including Prospero, Othocallis, and Nectaroscilla based on molecular data.

Chromosome counts report 2n = 24 for the diploid species, with some populations showing higher ploidy levels. Karyotype analysis reveals a bimodal chromosome size distribution typical of Scilloideae.

Scilla siberica sensu stricto belongs to the Scilla bifolia group, characterized by two broad basal leaves and few-flowered inflorescences. Two subspecies are recognized: subsp. siberica and subsp. caucasica.

The species name "siberica" was applied by early botanists who mistakenly believed the type specimen originated from Siberia. The actual native range lies considerably further south and west.

## Morphology

The bulb is small, ovoid, 15–25 mm in diameter, with dark brown to purplish tunics. Each bulb typically produces two (rarely three) broad, lanceolate basal leaves.

Leaves are 10–20 cm long and 15–30 mm wide, glossy dark green, and emerge shortly before or with the flowers. They are semi-erect at first, becoming more spreading as they elongate.

The leaves emerge from the bulb tip and are initially erect, becoming recurved as they mature. A distinctive midrib runs the length of each leaf, visible as a slightly raised ridge on the upper surface.

The flowering stem (scape) is 10–20 cm tall, slender, and erect, bearing 1–3 (rarely up to 5) nodding, bell-shaped flowers. Each flower is held on a slender pedicel 15–30 mm long.

Individual flowers are 15–25 mm across, with six tepals fused at the base forming a shallow campanulate tube. The color is an intense, luminous cobalt blue, occasionally white in garden selections.

The stamens are six in number, inserted at the base of the perianth tube, with dark blue anthers that complement the tepal color. The style is slender with a small, capitate stigma.

The fruit is a rounded capsule, 6–8 mm in diameter, containing several small, dark brown to black seeds. Flowering occurs from February to April in the Northern Hemisphere.

## Distribution and Habitat

The native range of Scilla siberica extends from the Caucasus Mountains through southern European Russia into northwestern Turkey. It grows in deciduous woodlands, forest margins, and damp meadows.

In the wild, the species favors partially shaded sites with rich, moist but well-drained soils. It occurs from lowland elevations up to approximately 1,500 meters in the Caucasus.

Climate in the native range features cold winters with reliable snow cover and warm, relatively dry summers. The species is remarkably cold-hardy, surviving temperatures below −30 °C when insulated by snow.

As a widely naturalized species, S. siberica has established persistent populations across northern and central Europe, the United Kingdom, eastern North America, and parts of the Pacific Northwest.

In some regions, particularly the upper Midwest of the United States and parts of Scandinavia, the species has become invasive in woodland habitats. It can form dense mats that exclude native spring ephemerals.

## Cultivation

Scilla siberica is among the easiest spring bulbs to grow, thriving in USDA zones 2–8. Plant bulbs 8–10 cm deep in autumn, 5–8 cm apart, in any reasonable garden soil.

The species prefers partial shade but tolerates full sun in cooler climates. It naturalizes freely in lawns, under deciduous trees, and along woodland edges, where it forms expanding colonies over time.

Once established, the bulbs require no maintenance and will multiply freely. The foliage dies back by early summer, allowing the ground to be used for later-emerging plants.

In containers, Scilla siberica combines effectively with other early bulbs such as Galanthus, Eranthis, and Crocus. The contrast of intense blue with white and yellow creates striking spring displays.

For naturalized lawn plantings, scatter bulbs randomly and plant where they land for a naturalistic effect. Avoid mowing until the foliage has yellowed completely, typically 6–8 weeks after flowering.

Several cultivars are available, including 'Spring Beauty' (larger flowers), 'Alba' (white-flowered), and 'Atrocaerulea' (deeper blue). The species has received the RHS Award of Garden Merit.

Under ideal conditions, established clumps can persist for decades without any intervention, making this one of the most sustainable spring bulbs for low-maintenance landscapes.

Scilla siberica was introduced to Western European horticulture in the late 18th century and has been in continuous cultivation since the 1790s. Its reliability and vivid color made it a staple of Victorian spring gardens.

Bulbs are inexpensive and widely available from autumn through early winter. They store poorly if kept warm and should be planted promptly after purchase for best results.

## Ecological Role

The early spring flowers provide an important nectar and pollen source for emerging bees and other pollinators. Honeybees, mining bees, and hoverflies are frequent visitors during warm late-winter days.

In native habitats, the species is primarily bee-pollinated, with the bright blue flower color acting as a long-distance attractant. The pendant flower shape protects pollen from rain and dew.

The foliage and bulbs contain cardiac glycosides and saponins that deter herbivory. All parts of the plant are toxic if ingested, which contributes to its persistence in gardens with deer and rabbit pressure.

Ants serve as secondary seed dispersers, attracted by elaiosomes on the seeds. This myrmecochorous dispersal mechanism contributes to the gradual expansion of naturalized colonies.

The toxicity of all plant parts to mammals and the ability to grow in lawn settings make this species nearly immune to common garden pests. Squirrel and vole damage to bulbs is rare.

Naturalized populations in North America raise conservation concerns due to competition with native spring ephemerals such as Claytonia virginica and Erythronium americanum. Control efforts focus on preventing spread into natural areas.
