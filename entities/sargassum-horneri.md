It is native to the northwest Pacific, occurring along the coasts of Japan, Korea, and China, where it forms extensive subtidal beds.

The species has become an aggressive invader along the Pacific coast of [[berry-growing]] in 2003, in Long Beach Harbor, California.

By the 2010s, it had spread along the coast from Baja California to British Columbia.

Established populations have also been reported in European waters, including the Mediterranean Sea and the Atlantic coast of France.

The primary vector for introduction is believed to be hull fouling on commercial shipping vessels.

## Ecology and Invasiveness

**Sargassum horneri** forms dense floating canopies and attached beds that can outcompete native kelp and seagrass species.

In introduced regions, it displaces native macroalgal assemblages by monopolizing substrate space and reducing light penetration.

The annual [[bloomfield-fungal-life-cycle-spiral-versus-circle]] produces massive quantities of drifting biomass that washes ashore, creating nuisance accumulations on beaches.

Drifting rafts of thalli transport attached epifauna and can facilitate the spread of non-native invertebrate species.

In its native range, the species provides critical habitat and nursery structure for fish, crustaceans, and mollusks.

Over 100 species of invertebrates and 50 fish species have been documented associating with *S. horneri* beds in Japanese waters.

The phenology differs between native and introduced ranges: in California, growth peaks in winter and spring, whereas in Japan it peaks in summer.

This shifted phenology may allow the invader to exploit seasonal niches with reduced competition from native species.
