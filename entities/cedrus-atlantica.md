## Overview

**Cedrus atlantica** (Endl.) Manetti ex Carrière, commonly known as the **Atlas cedar**, is a large evergreen coniferous tree native to the Atlas Mountains of Morocco and Algeria. It is one of four species in the genus *Cedrus* and the only cedar native to Africa.

The species forms majestic, broadly conical trees reaching 30–40 m in height in its native habitat. Its silvery-blue to blue-green foliage and spreading branch architecture make it one of the most visually striking conifers in cultivation.

Atlas cedar is ecologically significant as a dominant component of montane coniferous forests in the Mediterranean region. It provides critical watershed protection in the Atlas Mountains and supports unique plant and animal communities.

The species has been harvested for timber and other products for millennia. Ancient Egyptian and Roman civilizations used Atlas cedar wood for construction, shipbuilding, and religious purposes. The wood's natural durability and aromatic properties made it highly valued.

The species is closely related to [[pinus-sylvestris]] and [[mollison-valley-and-mountain-climate-effects]] characterized by cool, moist winters and warm, dry summers. Annual precipitation ranges from 600 to 1,800 mm, with significant winter snowfall at higher elevations.

Soils are typically acidic to neutral, well-drained, and developed on limestone or siliceous substrates. The species shows moderate tolerance of calcareous soils but performs best on deep, moist, well-drained loams.

The native range of Atlas cedar has contracted significantly over the past century due to overexploitation, fire, and [[query-best-mediterranean-herbs-for-beginner-herb-garden]] and shrubs. Bird species associated with Atlas cedar forests include the Atlas flycatcher, Levaillant's woodpecker, and various raptors.

The extensive root systems and dense canopy cover make Atlas cedar forests critical for watershed protection in the Atlas Mountains. They regulate streamflow, reduce erosion, and maintain water quality for downstream communities.

The species is relatively fire-sensitive compared to associated pines. Mature trees have thick bark that provides moderate fire resistance, but crown fires are typically lethal. Fire exclusion policies have contributed to cedar forest recovery in some areas.

Mycorrhizal associations are critical for Atlas cedar seedling establishment. [[query-what-are-ectomycorrhizal-fungi-and-which-plants-do-they-associate-with]] including species of *Lactarius*, *Russula*, and *Amanita* form mutualistic relationships with cedar roots. These fungi enhance nutrient uptake, particularly phosphorus, in the nutrient-poor mountain soils.

Climate change poses the most significant long-term threat to Atlas cedar populations. Rising temperatures and declining precipitation are causing drought stress, increased mortality, and upward range contraction. Recent drought years have triggered extensive dieback events.

Genetic studies reveal significant population structure across the species' range. Moroccan and Algerian populations show distinct genetic lineages, suggesting long-term isolation. This genetic diversity is important for conservation planning and provenance selection for reforestation.

## Cultivation

Atlas cedar has been widely planted as an ornamental tree in temperate regions worldwide. The silvery-blue cultivar 'Glauca' is among the most popular ornamental conifers, valued for its distinctive foliage color and graceful form.

The species performs best in full sun with well-drained soil. It tolerates a wide range of soil types including clay, loam, and sandy substrates, provided drainage is adequate. It is moderately drought-tolerant once established.

Cold hardiness varies with provenance, but most selections are hardy to USDA Zone 6 (−23 °C). Young trees are more cold-tender than mature specimens. The species performs well in coastal conditions and tolerates urban pollution.
