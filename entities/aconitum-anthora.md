## Overview

Aconitum anthora L., commonly called yellow monkshood or healing wolfsbane, is a perennial herb in the family Ranunculaceae. It is native to the mountains of central and southern Europe, where it inhabits subalpine meadows and rocky slopes at 1,000–2,800 m elevation.

Unlike most Aconitum species, which bear blue or purple flowers, A. anthora produces pale yellow to cream-colored blooms. This unusual flower color has made it a point of taxonomic interest and horticultural curiosity.

The species contains diterpenoid alkaloids, though in lower concentrations than the notorious A. napellus. Traditional European herbalism ascribed healing properties to the plant, reflected in its species epithet *anthora* — derived from the Greek "against harm."

It is considered vulnerable in parts of its range due to overcollection and habitat loss from alpine tourism development.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Class Magnoliopsida → Order Ranunculales → Family Ranunculaceae → Tribe Delphinieae → Genus *Aconitum* → Species *A. anthora* L.

Linnaeus described the species in 1753. The name *anthora* is traditionally interpreted as a corruption of "antithora," referring to its historical use as an antidote to toxic *Aconitum* species.

The plant grows 30–80 cm tall from a tuberous rootstock consisting of two conical daughter tubers. The stem is erect, robust, and covered with soft, spreading hairs, especially near the base.

Leaves are palmately divided into five to seven deeply cut segments, each 3–8 cm wide. Lower leaves are long-petiolate while upper leaves become sessile with narrower lobes.

The inflorescence is a dense terminal raceme, sometimes with additional lateral branches. Bracts are linear and shorter than the pedicels.

Flowers are 15–25 mm long with the characteristic hooded (cucullate) upper sepal that defines the genus. The helmet is broadly rounded and pale yellow to creamy white.

Lateral sepals are elliptical, pale yellow, and downy on the outer surface. The two nectary-bearing petals are concealed within the hood, each with a coiled spur.

Follicles are 3–5 in number, 12–18 mm long, sparsely hairy, and contain numerous small, dark brown seeds with angular ridges.

The species is protected under national legislation in Austria, Switzerland, and Italy. Collection from the wild is restricted in most of its range.

## Chemistry and Toxicology

The principal alkaloids in Aconitum anthora include anthorine, aconitine, and mesaconitine, though total alkaloid content is lower than in A. napellus. Roots contain 0.2–0.8% total alkaloids by dry weight.

Anthorine is a C19-diterpenoid alkaloid considered characteristic of the species. It exhibits neurotoxic activity through voltage-gated sodium channel modulation.

The plant also contains flavonoids, phenolic acids, and small amounts of atisine-type diterpenes. These [[panax-ginseng]].

Toxicity is primarily due to aconitine-type alkaloids that cause cardiac arrhythmias and neurotoxicity. Symptoms of poisoning include paresthesia, nausea, bradycardia, and in severe cases, ventricular fibrillation.

Unlike A. napellus, which has been responsible for numerous fatal poisonings, A. anthora is considered less dangerous. This lower toxicity may explain its historical reputation as a safer "healing" aconite.

## Cultivation

Aconitum anthora is cultivated as an ornamental for its unusual yellow flowers and architectural form. It is hardy to USDA zones 4–7 and thrives in cool, moist conditions.

Propagation is by seed or division of tuberous roots. Seeds require cold stratification at 4°C for 8–12 weeks followed by gradual warming to germinate reliably.

The plant prefers partial shade to full sun in cool-summer climates. Soils should be humus-rich, consistently moist, and alkaline to neutral in pH.
