## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Liliopsida → Arecales → Arecaceae → Arecoideae → Roystoneeae → Roystonea → R. regia.

The species was described by O.F. Cook in 1901. It was previously classified under several different genera, including Oreodoxa and Palma. The current genus name honors General Roy Stone, a 19th-century American engineer.

The trunk is tall, straight, smooth, and columnar, colored in distinctive gray-white to cream bands. It reaches 25-40 centimeters in diameter and can extend 20-30 meters before the crown begins.

The smooth trunk surface results from the clean shedding of dead leaf bases. This gives the trunk its characteristic polished appearance, which distinguishes it from most other large palms.

The crownshaft is prominent, smooth, and glossy green, forming a distinct bulbous swelling at the top of the trunk. The crownshaft measures 1-1.5 meters in length and contrasts dramatically with the pale trunk.

This dramatic contrast between the green crownshaft and white trunk makes Roystonea regia one of the most visually striking palms in tropical landscapes. The effect is most pronounced when backlit against the sky.

Leaves are pinnate, 3-5 meters long, with approximately 100 leaflets per side. The leaflets are arranged in several planes, giving the crown a full, feathery appearance characteristic of the species.

Inflorescences emerge from the base of the crownshaft and are branched, creamy-white, and approximately 1 meter long. The species is monoecious, bearing both male and female flowers on the same inflorescence.

Fruits are small, spherical drupes approximately 1-1.3 centimeters in diameter, ripening from green to purple-black. Each fruit contains a single round seed surrounded by a thin, sweetish pulp.

## Distribution and Habitat

Roystonea regia is native to Cuba, where it is abundant in savannas, wetlands, and forest margins throughout the island. It is also native to the Isle of Youth (Isla de la Juventud) and parts of Central America, including southern Florida, Honduras, and Nicaragua.

The species has been widely introduced and naturalized throughout the Caribbean, Central America, northern [[holzer-permaculture-landscape-design]].

In Cuba, the palm heart (cabbage) of Roystonea regia has been traditionally harvested as a vegetable. The trunk has been used for construction, fencing, and water pipes. The leaves provide thatching material.

The timber from royal palm trunks is lightweight yet surprisingly durable when kept dry. It has been used for everything from house posts to furniture and tool handles in rural Cuban communities.

The species is fast-growing and relatively low-maintenance once established. It requires adequate moisture and full sun but can tolerate brief periods of drought. [[knf-potassium-deficiency-and-excess]], are common in cultivated specimens.

Regular fertilization with palm-specialty fertilizers is recommended to prevent frond yellowing and premature leaf drop. Well-maintained specimens can retain 15-20 leaves in their crown at any time.

## Ecology and Conservation

In its native habitat, Roystonea regia is an important component of palm savanna ecosystems. These savannas are maintained by seasonal flooding and periodic fire, both of which the species tolerates.

The fruit is consumed by a variety of birds and mammals, including parrots, toucans, and bats, which serve as seed dispersers. The dense crown provides nesting sites and foraging habitat for numerous bird species.

Pollination is primarily carried out by insects, particularly bees and beetles attracted to the fragrant inflorescences. The massive flowering displays produce large quantities of nectar and pollen, supporting diverse pollinator communities.

Royal palm savannas in Cuba are among the most extensive palm-dominated ecosystems in the Caribbean. These habitats support specialized plant communities including endemic grasses, sedges, and herbaceous species.
