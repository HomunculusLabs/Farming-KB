# Soil Horizons

## Description

Soil horizons are distinct, roughly parallel layers of soil material that differ from adjacent layers in physical, chemical, and biological properties. They form over time through the combined effects of weathering, leaching, organic matter accumulation, and biological activity. A vertical cross-section revealing all horizons is called a soil profile. Soil scientists assign letter designations (O, A, B, C, R) to each horizon, sometimes with subscript modifiers, to classify and communicate about soil layers.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Soil science / Pedology
- **Category:** Soil structure
- **Subcategory:** Vertical stratification

## Key Facts

- **O Horizon (Organic):** The uppermost layer composed of organic matter in varying states of decomposition. It is subdivided into:
  - **Oi (fibric):** Organic material still identifiable as specific plant matter.
  - **Oe (humic):** More decomposed; identifiable as plant matter but specific plants cannot be determined.
  - **Oa (sapric):** Fully decomposed; origin (plant or animal) cannot be determined.
- **A Horizon:** Lies beneath the O horizon. Humus particles accumulate here as water carries organic material downward from the O horizon above. This horizon has the highest organic matter content and the highest biological activity of any soil layer. This is where the majority of roots grow.
- **B Horizon (Subsoil):** Zone of accumulation where materials leached from the A horizon (clays, iron, aluminum, organic compounds) accumulate.
- **C Horizon:** Partially weathered parent material, transitional between soil and bedrock.
- **R Horizon:** Unweathered bedrock.
- Horizons develop through continuous processes of weathering, leaching, biological mixing, and organic matter decomposition operating over hundreds to thousands of years.
- One or more horizons may be missing in any given location due to erosion, weathering, or geological history.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

For gardeners and cultivators, the O and A horizons are the most critical. The O horizon provides the raw organic material that feeds the soil food web, while the A horizon is where root growth, nutrient cycling, and microbial activity are concentrated. Understanding horizons helps diagnose soil problems: a thin or missing A horizon indicates depleted organic matter, while compaction in the B horizon can impede drainage and root penetration. In mycology, the organic-rich O horizon is the primary substrate for saprophytic fungi, while mycorrhizal fungi operate predominantly in the A horizon where plant roots are concentrated.
