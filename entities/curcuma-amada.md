# Curcuma amada

## Overview

Curcuma amada, commonly known as mango ginger, is a rhizomatous herbaceous
perennial in the family Zingiberaceae. Despite belonging to the turmeric genus,
its raw rhizome possesses a distinct mango aroma and flavor that has earned it
widespread culinary use across South and [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]].

The species is native to the Indian subcontinent and has been used in Ayurvedic
medicine for centuries, primarily for digestive and [[cannabis-for-inflammatory-conditions]]. It
is closely related to turmeric ([[curcuma-longa]]) but is distinguished by its
characteristic fragrance and lower curcuminoid content.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Curcuma amada was described by William Roxburgh in 1810. The species epithet
"amada" references the mango-like aroma of the rhizome. It belongs to
Curcuma subgenus Curcuma, section Curcuma.

Plants reach 60–90 cm in height with lanceolate leaves 15–30 cm long and 5–8 cm
wide. The leaf sheaths form a distinct pseudostem. Inflorescences arise from
the rhizome separately from the leaf shoot, typical of the genus.

The rhizome is the primary economic organ, producing ovoid to ellipsoid tubers
that are pale yellow internally with a thin brown skin. Fresh rhizomes emit a
strong, sweet mango odor when cut or crushed, attributable to specific volatile
sulfur compounds.

Flowers are pale yellow with a pinkish labellum, arranged in a dense spike.
The bracts are green with pink tips. Flowering occurs during the monsoon season
(July–September) in native habitats.

## Distribution and Habitat

C. amada is native to India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, and Myanmar, with
naturalized populations in parts of Southeast Asia and tropical Africa. In
India, it is widely distributed across the Western Ghats, Eastern Ghats, and
northeastern hill states.

The species thrives in tropical [[query-how-to-grow-rare-tropical-fruits-in-marginal-and-subtropical-climates]] with annual rainfall
of 1500–3000 mm. It prefers well-drained loamy to sandy-loam soils with a pH
of 5.5–7.0 and partial shade conditions, often growing as an understory herb
in moist deciduous forests.

Wild populations are found at elevations up to 1200 m. The species has been
domesticated for centuries and is commonly cultivated in home gardens and
smallholder plots throughout its native range.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The essential oil of C. amada rhizome contains major volatiles including
α-pinene, β-pinene, camphor, and cis-ocimene, along with unique mango-flavor
compounds such as 2-carene and several monoterpenoid esters. The characteristic
mango aroma is attributed to cis-β-ocimene and 3-carene.

Curcuminoid content is significantly lower than in C. longa, with curcumin
present at 0.1–0.3% of dry rhizome weight. However, the species contains
bioactive diarylheptanoids and labdane diterpenes unique to the species.

Pharmacological studies have demonstrated anti-inflammatory activity comparable
to C. longa in carrageenan-induced edema models. Extracts show moderate
antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and antifungal activity
against Candida species. Antioxidant capacity is significant, primarily
attributable to [[aact-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds-wood-composting-ingham]] and volatile constituents.

In Ayurvedic practice, C. amada is prescribed for anorexia, dyspepsia, and
[[blesching-cannabis-skin-diseases]]. The rhizome paste is applied topically for inflammatory skin
conditions and wounds.
