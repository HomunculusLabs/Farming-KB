## Overview

Magnolia virginiana, commonly called sweetbay magnolia, is a deciduous to semi-evergreen tree in the family Magnoliaceae native to the eastern and southeastern [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], named by Mark Catesby and later validated by Carl Linnaeus.

The specific epithet virginiana refers to the Colony of Virginia, where early botanical specimens were collected.

Two varieties are generally recognized: var. virginiana (evergreen to semi-evergreen, coastal plain) and var. australis (more consistently evergreen, Gulf Coast).

**Bark** is smooth and gray on young stems, becoming dark gray and slightly fissured with age.

**Leaves** are simple, alternate, 8 to 15 centimeters long, with dark green upper surfaces and distinctive silvery-white pubescence beneath. They create an attractive bicolor effect when stirred by wind.

**Flowers** are cup-shaped, 5 to 10 centimeters in diameter, with 9 to 12 creamy-white petals. They are intensely fragrant with a lemon-scented aroma.

Flowering occurs from May through July, with individual blooms lasting 2 to 3 days but new flowers opening sequentially over several weeks.

**Fruit** is a cylindrical aggregate of follicles, 3 to 5 centimeters long, containing bright red seeds exposed at maturity in late summer and fall.

## Distribution and Habitat

Sweetbay magnolia occurs along the Atlantic coastal plain from Long Island, New York, south to central Florida, and west to eastern Texas.

Disjunct populations exist in Massachusetts and in the Florida panhandle, with isolated stands in the Appalachian foothills of Alabama and Georgia.

The species is most abundant in acidic wetland habitats including swamps, bays, pocosins, and the margins of ponds and streams.

It tolerates both permanently saturated soils and seasonally flooded conditions, making it more adaptable to wet sites than southern magnolia.

Northern populations are typically found in boggy or peaty soils associated with Atlantic white cedar and red maple swamps.

The species is hardy in USDA zones 5 through 9, with northern populations demonstrating significant [[hamilton-ornamental-trees]] thrive.

The wood is soft, light, and straight-grained but has limited commercial use due to the tree's relatively small size.
