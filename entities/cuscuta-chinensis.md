## Overview

Cuscuta chinensis, commonly known as Chinese dodder or tu si zi, is a parasitic
annual vine in the family Convolvulaceae. It is one of the most important
medicinal plants in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), where the dried seeds
have been used for centuries as a kidney tonic and reproductive health remedy.

Unlike most plants, C. chinensis lacks functional roots and chlorophyll,
obtaining all nutrients and water from host plants through specialized
haustoria that penetrate host vascular tissue.

The species is widespread across East and Southeast Asia and parasitizes a
broad range of host plants, including economically important crops and
medicinal herbs.

The genus Cuscuta comprises approximately 200 species of obligate parasitic
plants. C. chinensis is closely related to C. japonica and C. europaea, which
are also used medicinally in various traditions.

## Morphology

C. chinensis is a leafless, rootless, twining parasitic vine with slender,
yellow to orange stems measuring 0.5–1.5 mm in diameter.

Stems are filamentous and form dense tangled mats over host plants, extending
several meters. The plant produces no true leaves, only minute scales.

Flowers are small (2–3 mm diameter), white to pale yellow, arranged in dense
glomerules. The calyx is 5-lobed, corolla campanulate with 5 lobes equal to
or shorter than the calyx tube. Flowering occurs from July to September.

The fruit is a globose capsule 2–3 mm in diameter containing 2–4 seeds.
Seeds are ovoid, brownish, approximately 1 mm long, with a rough surface.

The most distinctive feature is the haustorium — a specialized organ that
penetrates host tissue to establish vascular connections for nutrient transfer.

## Distribution and Habitat

C. chinensis is native to East Asia, found throughout China, Korea, Japan,
Vietnam, and India. It has been introduced to parts of Africa, Australia, and
the Americas.

The species occurs in lowland to mid-elevation habitats (up to 2,000 m) in
fields, meadows, roadsides, and disturbed areas. It requires host plants to
complete its life cycle.

Common hosts include alfalfa ([[medicago-sativa]]), clover (Trifolium spp.),
soybean, various Asteraceae, and numerous herbaceous species.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The seeds contain a diverse array of secondary metabolites, with flavonoids
and lignans being the primary bioactive constituents.

Major flavonoids include **quercetin**, **kaempferol**, **hyperoside**, and
**isorhamnetin** glycosides. Key lignans include **cuscutamine**, **cuscutin**,
and **cuscutalin**.

These compounds contribute to the observed antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and
estrogen-modulating activities. Quercetin glycosides demonstrate significant
free radical scavenging capacity.

In TCM pharmacological models, C. chinensis seed extracts show protective
effects on renal function, reducing oxidative stress markers and improving
glomerular filtration in animal models of kidney disease.

Anti-osteoporotic effects have been demonstrated through stimulation of
osteoblast proliferation and inhibition of osteoclast activity.

Immunomodulatory effects include enhanced phagocytic activity of macrophages
and regulation of cytokine production.

## Traditional Uses

In TCM, C. chinensis seeds (tu si zi) are classified as sweet and neutral,
entering the liver, kidney, and spleen meridians. They are primarily used to
tonify kidney yang, nourish liver yin, and stabilize the fetus.

Classical indications include lower back pain, knee weakness, frequent
urination, impotence, premature ejaculation, infertility, threatened
miscarriage, and blurred vision.

The seeds are typically decocted or prepared as a powder. Common
combinations include pairing with other kidney tonics in formulas like
Tu Si Zi Wan and Jin Gui Shen Qi Wan.

In Korean traditional medicine (Hanyak), the seeds are used similarly for
reproductive and musculoskeletal conditions.

## Agricultural Impact

C. chinensis is a significant agricultural pest in some regions, reducing
crop yields through parasitism. Alfalfa and clover crops can be heavily
infested, requiring management strategies.

Seed contamination of crop seed stocks is a primary dispersal mechanism.
Clean seed practices and crop rotation help manage infestations.

Despite its pest status, the increasing commercial value of cultivated
medicinal C. chinensis seeds has created economic incentives for controlled
cultivation on host plants.
