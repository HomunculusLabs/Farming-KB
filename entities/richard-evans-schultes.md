# Richard Evans Schultes

**Richard Evans Schultes** (1915–2001) was an American ethnobotanist widely regarded as the father of modern ethnobotany. Over a career spanning six decades, he conducted pioneering fieldwork in the Amazon rainforest, documenting the uses of thousands of plants by indigenous peoples. His work on psychoactive plants—particularly the identification of the botanical sources of ayahuasca, ololiuqui, and teonanácatl—fundamentally shaped the scientific understanding of entheogenic flora in the Americas. Schultes's blend of rigorous botanical science with deep respect for indigenous knowledge set the standard for all subsequent ethnobotanical research.

## Early Life and Education

Schultes was born on January 12, 1915, in Boston, Massachusetts, to working-class parents. His father was a plumber and his mother was a homemaker. He attended Harvard University on scholarship, initially intending to study medicine but shifting to botany after taking an introductory course with Oakes Ames. He was particularly influenced by a lecture on the ceremonial use of peyote, which ignited his fascination with psychoactive plants.

He studied under the noted orchidologist Oakes Ames, who became his doctoral advisor and mentor. His undergraduate thesis on the ethnobotany of teonanácatl (the Aztec "flesh of the gods," identified as psilocybin mushrooms) established his lifelong fascination with psychoactive plants and indigenous plant knowledge. This thesis was based on careful analysis of historical Spanish colonial sources alongside botanical detective work.

He earned his A.B. (1937), A.M. (1938), and Ph.D. (1941) from Harvard, all in biology/botany. His doctoral dissertation examined the ethnobotany of the mushrooms used in Mesoamerican rituals, drawing on both botanical specimens and historical Spanish colonial sources including the writings of Bernardino de Sahagún and Francisco Hernández.

## Fieldwork in the Amazon

Schultes's most significant fieldwork occurred during extended expeditions to the northwest Amazon (primarily Colombia) between 1941 and 1954. His initial trip was funded by a grant from the National Research Council to study rubber (Hevea braziliensis) and its disease problems, as the United States sought alternative rubber sources during World War II after the Japanese occupation of Southeast Asian rubber plantations. This practical mission became a decades-long immersion in Amazonian ethnobotany.

Spending over a decade in the field—much of it in extremely remote regions accessible only by river—Schultes lived among and learned from indigenous groups including:

- The Kofán (Colombia/Ecuador) — from whom he learned about ayahuasca preparations and the use of *Virola* species as snuff. The Kofán became his primary cultural hosts and teachers.
- The Siona and Secoya of the Putumayo region
- The Witoto (Huitoto) and Bora of the Caquetá and Putumayo drainages
- Various Tukanoan-speaking peoples of the Vaupés and Río Negro regions
- The Yukuna, Makuna, and Barasana

During this period he collected over **24,000 botanical specimens**, representing more than 2,000 species. Many of these were new to science. He documented indigenous uses of plants for medicine, ritual, construction, hunting, fishing, and daily life with meticulous detail. His field notes and photographs remain invaluable primary sources for ethnobotanists and anthropologists. Many of his specimens are housed at the Harvard University Herbaria and at the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew.

His work was conducted in conditions of extraordinary physical hardship: tropical diseases (repeated bouts of malaria, dysentery, beriberi, and other tropical illnesses), dangerous river travel in dugout canoes through rapids and waterfalls, encounters with venomous snakes and insects, and the profound isolation of the deep rainforest, weeks or months from the nearest colonial settlement. He was once presumed dead for over a year when his mail failed to reach the outside world.

## Key Contributions to Psychoactive Plant Research

Schultes made several landmark identifications in the field of psychoactive plants, each of which opened new areas of scientific inquiry:

### 1. Ayahuasca Source Identification
Schultes was among the first Western scientists to clearly identify *Banisteriopsis caapi* as the primary ayahuasca vine and document the range of DMT-containing admixture plants (*Psychotria viridis*, *Diplopterys cabrerana*) used in its preparation. His fieldwork established that ayahuasca was not a single preparation but a complex pharmacological system involving numerous botanical combinations across different indigenous cultures. He documented dozens of distinct ayahuasca recipes used by different ethnic groups.

### 2. Ololiuqui Identification
In 1939, while still a graduate student, Schultes traveled to Oaxaca, Mexico, and identified ololiuqui—the ancient Aztec divinatory entheogen mentioned in colonial chronicles—as the seeds of *Rivea corymbosa* (morning glory). The active compounds (ergoline alkaloids including lysergic acid amide and lysergic acid hydroxyethylamide) were later identified by Albert Hofmann in 1960. This was a significant discovery because the ergoline alkaloids of ololiuqui are chemically closely related to LSD, suggesting that indigenous Mesoamerican peoples had independently discovered a psychoactive compound class closely related to one of the most potent psychedelics known to modern science.

### 3. Teonanácatl Identification
His undergraduate and graduate research linked the Aztec teonanácatl to psychoactive mushrooms of the genus *Psilocybe*, work later expanded by R. Gordon Wasson (who participated in a Mazatec velada ceremony with María Sabina in 1955) and Albert Hofmann (who isolated and identified psilocybin and psilocin in 1958–1959).

### 4. Virola Snuff Documentation
Schultes documented the use of *Virola* species resin as an intoxicating snuff (epená/yopo) by numerous Amazonian peoples, providing the first detailed ethnographic and botanical accounts of this practice. The active compound was identified as 5-MeO-DMT, one of the most potent naturally occurring psychedelics.

## Academic Career and Legacy

Schultes served on the Harvard faculty from 1954 until his retirement in 1985. He held several notable positions including:

- Curator of the Oakes Ames Orchid Herbarium at Harvard
- Director of the Harvard Botanical Museum
- Edward C. Jeffrey Professor of Biology

His most influential publications include:

- **"The Botany and Chemistry of Hallucinogens"** (1973, with Albert Hofmann) — a comprehensive reference work cataloguing psychoactive plants and their chemistry worldwide; considered the definitive scholarly reference in the field
- **"Plants of the Gods: Origins of Hallucinogenic Use"** (1979, with Albert Hofmann and later editions with Christian Rätsch) — a seminal popular work on entheogenic plants across cultures, widely regarded as the foundational text in the field
- **"Where the Gods Reign: Plants and Peoples of the Colombian Amazon"** (1988) — a richly illustrated account of his Amazonian expeditions
- **"Vine of the Soul: Medicine Men, Their Plants and Rituals in the Colombian Amazonia"** (with Robert F. Raffauf, 1992) — detailed ethnographic documentation of Amazonian shamanic plant use

He trained and mentored numerous students who became leading figures in ethnobotany, pharmacognosy, and conservation biology, including **Timothy Plowman** (cocina taxonomy and ethnobotany), **Mark Plotkin** (Amazonian ethnobotany and conservation), and **Wade Davis** (ethnobotany and cultural anthropology). His influence extended beyond academia through the popular impact of his students' books and films.

Schultes died on April 10, 2001, in Boston at the age of 86. He is remembered as one of the last great plant explorers—a scientist whose deep respect for indigenous knowledge, rigorous botanical methodology, and relentless fieldwork in some of Earth's most challenging environments created the foundation for modern ethnobotany and the scientific study of psychoactive plants.
