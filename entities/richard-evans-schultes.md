## Richard Evans Schultes

## Description

Richard Evans Schultes (1915–2001) was an American botanist and **founder of modern ethnobotany**, widely regarded as the preeminent authority on hallucinogenic plants of the Americas. He spent decades at **Harvard University's Botanical Museum** and conducted extensive fieldwork in the Amazon Basin, documenting indigenous use of psychoactive plants including *Banisteriopsis*, peyote, and numerous other species.

## Classification

- **Profession:** Botanist, ethnobotanist
- **Institution:** Botanical Museum, Harvard University
- **Specialty:** Hallucinogenic plants of the Americas, especially the Amazon
- **Legacy:** "Father of ethnobotany"

## Key Facts

- Extensively referenced throughout Harner's *[[hallucinogens-and-shamanism]]* (1973) as a primary botanical authority.
- Harner notes that some of the most significant contributions to understanding hallucinogens came not from anthropologists but from **botanists such as Schultes** (e.g., 1940, 1955).
- Made the original botanical identifications and surveys of *Banisteriopsis* species used in [[ayahuasca]] preparations (1957, 1963).
- Documented the use of *Prestonia* (formerly *Haemadictyon*) as the second plant in ayahuasca brews among Indians of the Rio Negro region in Brazil.
- Found that among the Cofán, Inga, and Sione Indians of the Rio Putumayo area (eastern Colombia), the second plant was simply another *Banisteriopsis* species.
- Observed that the biochemistry of *Banisteriopsis* drinks is **not well known** due to the variety of admixtures involved (1960: 177–79; 1970: 576–77).
- Pointed out that the **New World is unusually rich in hallucinogenic plants** (1963: 147), facilitating their use by indigenous peoples.
- Provided photographs of *[[banisteriopsis-caapi]]* and peyote used in the Harner volume.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Schultes's work is foundational to the entire field of psychoactive plant research, including [[mycology]]. His documentation of *Banisteriopsis* species and their admixtures provides the taxonomic and ethnobotanical framework upon which all subsequent ayahuasca research is built. His broader contributions to identifying and cataloging hallucinogenic plants of the Americas — including psychoactive mushrooms, peyote, morning glories, and DMT-containing species — make him a central figure in the study of all naturally occurring psychoactive substances.
