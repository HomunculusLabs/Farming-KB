# Richard Evans Schultes (1915-2001)

Harvard ethnobotanist, widely regarded as the father of modern ethnobotany. Spent over a decade living among indigenous peoples of the Amazon, documenting their use of psychoactive and medicinal plants. Curator of economic botany and director of the Harvard Botanical Museum, he identified over 300 new plant species during his Amazon expeditions.

## Career

Schultes entered Harvard in 1933 to study medicine but shifted to botany after a lecture by Oakes Ames on the economic uses of plants. His doctoral research on the ethnobotany of teonanacatl (the "flesh of the gods" — psilocybin mushrooms used by Mazatec peoples of Oaxaca) was interrupted by World War II, during which the U.S. government sent him to the Amazon to search for rubber-producing plants to support the war effort. He remained in the Amazon for over twelve years, living among indigenous groups and documenting their plant knowledge.

## Key Works

- **"The Golden Guide to Hallucinogenic Plants" (1976):** Co-authored with [[albert-hofmann]], this remains the most accessible nontechnical survey of hallucinogenic plants worldwide. A compact, richly illustrated volume covering psychoactive plants from every continent, it introduced generations of readers to the global diversity of mind-altering flora.
- **"Plants of the Gods" (1979):** Co-authored with [[albert-hofmann]] and Albert Hofmann's wife Anita, this expanded treatment of psychoactive plants provided detailed botanical, chemical, and ethnographic information on sacred and medicinal plants used across cultures.
- **"Plants of the Gods" established the pharmacological baseline** for understanding psilocybin content in mushrooms: dried mushrooms contain 0.2 to 0.4 percent psilocybin by weight, with a full effective dose of approximately 10-12 milligrams (about 5g dry weight), and an LD50 in mice of 280 mg/kg — making psilocybin one of the least toxic of all hallucinogens.

## Contributions to Psychoactive Plant Science

- Documented ayahuasca (Banisteriopsis caapi) use among Amazonian peoples, along with DMT-containing admixture plants (Psychotria viridis, Diplopterys cabrerana)
- Studied peyote (Lophophora williamsii) and its role in Native American Church ceremonies — work later built upon by [[john-halpern]] at Harvard
- Researched Ololiuqui (morning glory seeds) and other Mesoamerican psychoactive plants
- His classification of naturally occurring hallucinogens — indole alkaloids (psilocybin, DMT), lysergic acid amides, mescaline-containing cacti, and anticholinergic plants — provided the taxonomic framework still used by researchers

## Legacy

Schultes maintained that indigenous knowledge of plants represented thousands of years of empirical pharmacological research that deserved serious scientific attention. His students and collaborators include many major figures in ethnobotany and psychopharmacology. His work laid the foundation for the entire field of entheogenic plant scholarship and the recognition that psychoactive plants have been central to human culture, religion, and medicine for millennia.
