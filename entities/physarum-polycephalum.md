# Physarum polycephalum

Physarum polycephalum is a plasmodial slime mold (myxomycete) that has become one of the most important model organisms in cell biology, protistology, and biologically inspired computing. It is easily recognized by its bright yellow, multinucleate plasmodium that can reach impressive sizes and exhibits remarkable information-processing capabilities that have attracted attention from fields ranging from molecular biology to computer science and urban planning.

## Taxonomy and Classification

P. polycephalum (Schweinitz) belongs to the Myxogastria (Myxomycetes) within the Amoebozoa. Phylogenetic analyses using EF-1 alpha amino acid sequences indicate that plasmodial and cellular slime molds comprise a monophyletic group (Baldauf and Doolittle 1997; Baldauf et al. 2000). While historically studied by mycologists due to their spore-producing fruiting bodies, molecular evidence has conclusively demonstrated that slime molds are not true fungi but belong to a separate eukaryotic lineage, the Amoebozoa, which is more closely related to animals and fungi than to plants.

## Morphology and Life Cycle

The life cycle of P. polycephalum is complex and involves several distinct morphological stages, each adapted to different environmental conditions:

### Plasmodium

The dominant, conspicuous feeding stage is a bright yellow, fan-shaped phaneroplasmodium that migrates across surfaces in search of food. It has a feeding anterior edge and a posterior trailing network of veins (Fig. 25.9 in the source). The plasmodium is coenocytic, containing thousands of nuclei within a single cytoplasmic mass that is not divided by cell walls. This multinucleate condition allows for rapid cytoplasmic streaming and efficient distribution of nutrients throughout the organism. Plasmodia can reach sizes of up to a square meter or more under laboratory conditions.

### Sporulation

Under appropriate conditions of light, moisture, and nutrient depletion, the plasmodium converts into sporangia, which are stalked fruiting structures containing spores. The sporangia are typically small (1 to 3 mm) and contain dark spores borne on capillitium threads. This is the stage traditionally recognized by mycologists and field biologists.

### Germination

Spores germinate to release myxamoebae, which are small, unicellular, amoeboid cells that can move by pseudopodia. Under certain conditions, the myxamoebae can transform into flagellated swarm cells that swim in films of water, providing a dispersal mechanism.

### Zygote Formation

Myxamoebae of compatible mating types fuse to form a zygote, which then undergoes repeated nuclear divisions without cytokinesis to develop into a new multinucleate plasmodium. This cycle can repeat indefinitely under favorable conditions.

## Habitat and Distribution

P. polycephalum is commonly found on rotting wood, leaf litter, and other decaying organic matter in temperate forests worldwide. In the field, large plasmodia are often seen on rotting wood or feeding on wood-rotting basidiomycetes, where they exploit the partially decayed wood as a nutrient source. Plasmodia can also be located by the distinctive slime trails they leave behind as they migrate across surfaces.

### Moist Chamber Cultivation

The moist chamber technique works well for culturing P. polycephalum when harsh environmental conditions prevent fruiting in the field (Braun and Keller 1993). Moist chambers consist of Petri dishes or fingerbowls in which substrata are placed onto an appropriate source of moisture, either moistened paper or agar. This technique is a standard method for collecting and studying myxomycetes from field samples.

## Biological Problem-Solving

P. polycephalum has attracted extraordinary attention for its remarkable information-processing capabilities, which challenge conventional understandings of intelligence and computation in non-neural organisms:

### Maze-Solving

The plasmodium can find the shortest path through complex mazes, demonstrating primitive spatial problem-solving. When food sources are placed at the entrance and exit of a maze, the plasmodium expands to fill the maze and then thins out its cytoplasm to maintain only the most efficient route between the food sources, effectively "solving" the maze.

### Network Optimization

P. polycephalum constructs efficient transport networks that mimic optimal transportation infrastructure. When food sources are arranged to approximate the geographic distribution of cities around Tokyo, the plasmodium creates a network that closely resembles the actual Tokyo rail system, demonstrating an ability to optimize network topology for efficiency and resilience.

### Decision-Making and Memory

The organism exhibits habituation and can "remember" periodic environmental stimuli. It can also make trade-off decisions between multiple food sources based on their quality and accessibility, suggesting a capacity for cost-benefit analysis without any nervous system.

### Computational Applications

Researchers have used P. polycephalum to model biological computing and unconventional computation. The concept of "physarum computing" has emerged as a field of research exploring how the organism's distributed information processing can inspire new computational algorithms and approaches.

## Mitochondrial Genetics

P. polycephalum has been an important model for studying mitochondrial genetics and evolution. Key findings include:

- Mitochondrial genes for cytochrome B, ATPases 6 and 9, cox I and cox II from [[saccharomyces-cerevisiae]] and Zea mays did not hybridize with mtDNA from P. polycephalum, whereas rRNA probes did (Jones et al. 1990), consistent with ancient divergence from the fungal and animal lineages
- The order of mitochondrial genes in each fungal genus so far examined is unique, although some blocks of genes are conserved among some genera (Wolf and Del Giudice 1988)
- P. polycephalum provides a phylogenetically informative outgroup for comparative mitochondrial genomics studies

## Phylogenetic Significance

P. polycephalum occupies an important position in eukaryotic phylogeny. Its EF-1 alpha sequence has been used to investigate deep branching patterns among eukaryotes, including the relationship between slime molds, fungi, and animals. The organism's genetic and cellular features provide clues about the evolution of multicellularity, motility, and cytoskeletal organization in early eukaryotes.

## Cytoplasmic Streaming and Movement

One of the most distinctive features of P. polycephalum is its vigorous cytoplasmic streaming, also called shuttle streaming, which alternates direction approximately every 30–120 seconds. This rhythmic flow is driven by the contraction and relaxation of actin-myosin networks within the cytoplasm and serves to distribute nutrients, organelles, and chemical signals throughout the plasmodium. The streaming can achieve velocities of up to 1 mm per second, making it one of the fastest documented examples of intracellular transport. This streaming mechanism is central to the organism's ability to coordinate activities across its extensive body and to respond to environmental stimuli in a coherent manner despite lacking a nervous system.

## Feeding Behavior and Nutrition

P. polycephalum is a phagotrophic organism that feeds by engulfing bacteria, fungal spores, and other microorganisms found on decaying organic matter. The plasmodium extends over food sources and secretes digestive enzymes extracellularly before absorbing the resulting nutrient molecules. Under laboratory conditions, P. polycephalum is commonly maintained on oat flakes or other rolled grain preparations placed on agar or moist filter paper. The organism shows clear nutritional preferences and can detect and move toward preferred food sources over considerable distances, demonstrating chemotactic sensing capabilities. Its diet in nature includes the spores and hyphae of wood-decay fungi, contributing to the decomposition of fungal fruiting bodies on decaying wood.

## Reproductive Biology

The reproductive biology of P. polycephalum involves a heterothallic mating system with multiple mating types. Compatible myxamoebae must be of different mating types to fuse and form a zygote, which develops into the diploid plasmodium. The plasmodium itself can be propagated vegetatively indefinitely by transferring small pieces to fresh substrate, allowing long-term maintenance of laboratory strains. Under starvation or other stress conditions, the plasmodium undergoes sclerotization, forming hardened, resistant sclerotia that can survive desiccation and other adverse conditions for extended periods. Upon rehydration and the return of favorable conditions, the sclerotia germinate to produce a new active plasmodium, providing a survival mechanism in fluctuating natural environments.

## Impact on Biological Computing Research

The study of P. polycephalum has given rise to the field of "physarum computing," which explores the use of biological systems for solving computational problems. Research in this area has demonstrated that P. polycephalum can:

- **Solve shortest path problems**: By connecting food sources with the minimum total length of protoplasmic tubes
- **Construct efficient networks**: Creating redundant yet efficient transport networks that balance cost and resilience
- **Recall past events**: Exhibiting a form of externalized memory through changes in the thickness of existing tubes
- **Make risk-consistent decisions**: Choosing between food sources of different quality and reliability in a manner consistent with optimal foraging theory

These capabilities have inspired new algorithms in computer science and operations research, and the organism has been proposed as a model for unconventional and biological computing paradigms that do not rely on silicon-based processors or conventional algorithmic approaches.

## Ecological Role in Decomposition

In forest ecosystems, P. polycephalum contributes to decomposition by consuming bacteria and fungal spores on decaying wood and leaf litter. Its plasmodium can cover large areas of dead wood surface, ingesting microbial colonizers and potentially influencing the composition of the microbial decomposer community. The organism's role in decomposition is distinct from that of the wood-decay basidiomycetes that it feeds upon and alongside; rather than directly degrading lignocellulose, P. polycephalum occupies a bacterial-grazing niche that may accelerate decomposition indirectly by stimulating microbial turnover on decaying substrates. The slime trails left by migrating plasmodia may also influence the movement and colonization patterns of other microorganisms on the same substrate.

## Comparison with Cellular Slime Molds

P. polycephalum, as a plasmodial slime mold, is frequently compared with the cellular slime molds (Dictyostelida) such as dictyostelium-discoideum. While both groups were historically studied by mycologists and share the general designation "slime mold," they represent fundamentally different biological strategies. Plasmodial slime molds like P. polycephalum form a true multinucleate coenocyte, whereas cellular slime molds maintain individual cell membranes and aggregate into multicellular structures only during fruiting. Despite these differences, both groups provide valuable models for studying cell motility, chemotaxis, differentiation, and the evolution of multicellularity, and their comparison has yielded important insights into the diversity of eukaryotic developmental strategies.
