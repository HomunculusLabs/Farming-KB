This compound causes blistering upon contact with skin or mucous membranes and makes the plant strongly unpalatable to livestock.

Despite its toxicity, the species has a long history of use in traditional European folk medicine as a vesicant and counter-irritant. It is one of the three most common buttercup species encountered in temperate grasslands worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Scientific classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Family: Ranunculaceae
- Genus: Ranunculus
- Species: R. bulbosus
- Authority: Linnaeus (1753)

**Key synonyms:** Ranunculus bulbosus var. valdepubens Borbás; Ranunculus bulbosus subsp. adscendens (Brot.) Nyman.

The plant grows 20–60 cm tall from a characteristic bulbous corm-like stem base, typically 1–2 cm in diameter. This swollen base is the most reliable diagnostic feature in the field and persists through winter as a perennating organ.

Basal leaves are three-lobed and deeply divided on long petioles (5–15 cm), with each lobe further subdivided into rounded or obtuse segments.

Stem leaves are narrower, more finely divided, and sessile or short-stalked. Upper stem leaves are bract-like and much reduced in size compared to the basal rosette.

The foliage is moderately hairy, especially on the lower leaf surfaces and stem bases. Hairs are appressed and silky, giving the leaves a slightly greyish-green cast.

Flowers are glossy yellow, 2–3.5 cm in diameter, with five broadly overlapping petals and five reflexed sepals. The reflexed sepals are the key diagnostic feature separating this species from Ranunculus acris and Ranunculus repens.

Each flower produces numerous achenes (dry one-seeded fruits) arranged in a globose head. The achenes are flattened, 2.5–3.5 mm long, with a short curved beak approximately 0.5–1.0 mm long.

Seed dispersal is primarily by wind and gravity, with seeds falling near the parent plant. Germination typically occurs in autumn, with seedlings establishing a small corm before winter dormancy.

The root system is fibrous, arising from the base of the swollen corm. Unlike Ranunculus repens, this species produces neither stolons nor runners.

Chromosome number is 2n = 16, consistent with most diploid Ranunculus species in section Ranunculus. The species is self-incompatible, requiring cross-pollination for seed set.

## Distribution and Habitat

Ranunculus bulbosus is native across most of Europe, from the British Isles and southern Scandinavia south to the Mediterranean, and eastward into western Asia and northwestern Africa.

In North America, it is now naturalized in the northeastern United States, southeastern Canada, and parts of the [[mycorrhizal-agricultural-practices]] and pasture seed contamination.

The species thrives in dry, well-drained grasslands, pastures, meadows, roadsides, and sandy or gravelly soils. It strongly prefers calcareous or neutral pH conditions.

It is considerably more drought-tolerant than other common buttercups. This tolerance allows it to dominate dry pastures and south-facing slopes where Ranunculus acris and Ranunculus repens cannot compete effectively.

Unlike Ranunculus repens, which favors damp ground and wet meadows, Ranunculus bulbosus characteristically occupies drier, more open habitats.

It is a poor competitor in tall, rank vegetation but persists under heavy grazing or mowing regimes that suppress taller competitors.

## Ecology

Ranunculus bulbosus flowers from May to August in its native range, producing abundant nectar and pollen that attract diverse insect pollinators.

Bees (particularly solitary species), hoverflies (Syrphidae), and various beetles are the primary flower visitors. The glossy petal surface reflects UV light, creating a "mirrored" landing signal visible to insect compound eyes.

The plant is strongly unpalatable to grazing livestock due to its protoanemonin content, which causes immediate oral irritation upon tasting. This chemical defense gives it a significant competitive advantage in heavily grazed pastures.

Despite toxicity to mammals, several insect species specialize on Ranunculus foliage as larval host plants. The bulbous corm base allows the plant to survive drought, fire, and moderate soil disturbance.
