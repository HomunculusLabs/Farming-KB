## Overview

**Mangifera indica** L., the mango, is an evergreen tropical tree in the family
Anacardiaceae. Originally domesticated in South Asia over 4,000 years ago, it
is now cultivated throughout the tropics and subtropics and is one of the most
important fruit crops globally, with annual production exceeding 55 million
tonnes. India alone accounts for approximately 45% of world production.

Beyond its economic importance as a fruit, virtually every part of the mango
tree — bark, leaves, fruit, seed, and kernel — has documented medicinal use
in Ayurveda and other traditional systems. The fruit is rich in mangiferin,
a xanthone glycoside with significant anti-inflammatory and antioxidant
properties.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Magnoliopsida → Sapindales →
**Anacardiaceae → *Mangifera → *M. indica***.

Mango is a large evergreen tree reaching 30–45 m in height with a dense,
symmetrical, dome-shaped canopy. The trunk is straight, 60–100 cm in diameter,
with dark gray, fissured bark. Roots are long taproots with extensive lateral
surface feeder roots.

Leaves are simple, alternate, lanceolate to oblong, 15–40 cm long and 5–10 cm
wide, with a leathery texture. Young leaves are copper-red to purple, turning
glossy dark green at maturity. Inflorescences are terminal panicles, 20–40 cm
long, bearing hundreds of small pinkish-white to yellowish-green flowers.

Fruit is a large, fleshy drupe, 5–25 cm long, varying widely in shape
(ovate, oblong, round), size (150 g to over 2 kg), and skin color (green,
yellow, orange, red, or purple). The mesocarp is juicy and aromatic; the
endocarp contains a single large, woody seed.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia, where wild populations
still occur in the forests of Assam, Myanmar, and the Western Ghats. The tree
is now naturalized and cultivated throughout tropical and subtropical regions
between 25°N and 25°S.

Optimal growth occurs at temperatures of 24–30°C, annual rainfall of
1,000–2,500 mm, and elevations below 600 m. Mango tolerates a wide range of
soils but performs best in deep, well-drained loams with pH 5.5–7.5. It is
moderately drought-tolerant once established but sensitive to waterlogging
and frost.

## Bioactive Compounds

Mango contains a diverse array of phytochemicals distributed across all plant
parts:

- **Mangiferin** — a xanthone C-glucoside, the most studied bioactive
  compound; concentrated in bark (up to 8.5%), leaves, and kernel.
- **Gallotannins** — polyphenolic tannins with anti-diabetic and
  hepatoprotective activity, abundant in the fruit peel and kernel.
- **Carotenoids** — β-carotene, lutein, violaxanthin; the deep yellow-orange
  varieties contain 3,000–6,000 μg β-carotene per 100 g fresh weight.
- **Vitamin C** — 30–40 mg per 100 g fresh fruit, higher in unripe mango.
- **Polyphenols** — quercetin, kaempferol, and ellagic acid derivatives.
- **Enzymes** — mango latex contains mangiferin aglycone and the allergen
  protein profilin, responsible for contact dermatitis in sensitized
  individuals.

## Pharmacology and Health Effects

Mango-derived compounds exhibit broad pharmacological activity:

- **Anti-inflammatory** — mangiferin inhibits COX-2, iNOS, and NF-κB
  signaling; comparable activity to NSAIDs in animal models of arthritis.
- **Anti-diabetic** — kernel extract improves glucose tolerance, stimulates
  insulin secretion, and inhibits α-glucosidase and α-amylase.
- **Anti-oxidant** — mangiferin and gallotannins scavenge free radicals and
  upregulate endogenous antioxidant enzymes (SOD, catalase, GPx).
- **Anti-microbial** — leaf and bark extracts show activity against
  *Staphylococcus aureus*, *E. coli*, and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.
- **Immunomodulatory** — enhances phagocytic activity and modulates cytokine
  production.

In Ayurveda, mango bark is used for diarrhea, hemorrhages, and respiratory
complaints. The kernel treats diarrhea and dysentery. Dried flowers are used
for dysentery and urinary complaints.

## Cultivation and Permaculture Applications

Mango is propagated by grafting ( veneer or epicotyl grafting) onto
seedling rootstocks. Trees begin bearing 3–5 years after planting and
reach full production at 10–15 years, with productive lifespans exceeding
100 years. Yields range from 5–15 tonnes/ha for bearing trees.

In permaculture design, mango serves as a high-canopy food forest tree
providing shade, fruit, mulch, and microclimate regulation. It functions
effectively as an overstory species above banana, papaya, turmeric,
ginger, and understory legumes. The deep taproot accesses subsoil nutrients
and the dense canopy moderates ground-level temperatures.

Mango intercropping with legumes (*Gliricidia sepium*, *Flemingia
macrophylla*) improves soil nitrogen and provides pruning material for
mulch. The tree's litter decomposes readily and contributes significant
organic matter to the soil surface.
