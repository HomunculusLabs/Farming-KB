# Psilocybe pseudoaztecorum

**Psilocybe pseudoaztecorum** is a bluing psilocybin mushroom in the family Hymenogastraceae, native to the highlands of central Mexico. It was first described by Gastón Guzmán in 1995, who noted its close morphological resemblance to [[psilocybe-aztecorum-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]] but distinguished it by differences in spore size, cheilocystidia shape, and habitat preference. The species belongs to Psilocybe section Aztecorum, a group of high-elevation Mexican species that share a preference for montane grasslands and forest edges.

## Taxonomy and Etymology

Guzmán erected the species to accommodate collections from the states of Mexico, Puebla, and Tlaxcala that were morphologically similar to [[psilocybe-aztecorum-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]] but consistently smaller in all macroscopic and microscopic features. The epithet *pseudoaztecorum* (false Aztec) reflects this close similarity. Molecular phylogenetic studies have confirmed its placement in section Aztecorum alongside [[psilocybe-aztecorum-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]] and [[psilocybe-weilii]]-complex species. Some authorities have suggested it may warrant placement in the genus Deconica based on weak bluing reactions, but the presence of [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]] in chemical analyses supports its retention in Psilocybe sensu stricto.

## Morphology

The cap (pileus) is 10–25 mm in diameter, convex to subcampanulate when young, becoming broadly convex or plane with age. The surface is hygrophanous, brownish to ochraceous when moist, fading to a pale straw or nearly white color as it dries. It is smooth to slightly viscid when wet, with a silky sheen from innate fibrils. The margin is appendiculate with remnants of the partial veil in young specimens, and often develops a slight undulating or wavy character with maturity. The flesh is thin, whitish, and bruises slowly to a bluish or blue-green color, though the bluing reaction is reportedly weaker than in [[psilocybe-aztecorum-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]]. The hygrophanous nature of the cap means that specimens can appear dramatically different depending on moisture conditions, with fresh, wet caps showing rich brown coloration that fades rapidly as the mushroom dries.

The gills are adnate to slightly adnexed, moderately crowded, and narrow. They are grayish with a lilaceous tint when young, becoming dark purplish brown to nearly black with spore maturity. The gill edges are paler than the faces and remain whitish even in mature specimens. The stipe is 40–70 mm long and 2–3 mm thick, equal or slightly enlarged at the base. It is hollow, brittle, and covered with fine white fibrillose scales that are more conspicuous toward the base. The partial veil is membranous, leaving a transient annular zone that often disappears with age. Like the cap flesh, the stipe tissue bruises blue, though again the reaction is subdued.

## Spores and Microscopic Features

Basidiospores are ellipsoid to slightly rhomboid in face view, 6.5–8.5 × 4–5.5 μm, with a thick wall (approximately 0.5–1 μm) and a distinct germ pore. They are brownish in deposit, smooth under light microscopy, and show no pleurocystidia. Cheilocystidia are abundant, forming a sterile band on the gill edge, ventricose to lageniform with narrow necks, 15–25 × 4–7 μm. Pleurocystidia are absent. The pileipellis is a cutis of hyaline, thin-walled hyphae 2–4 μm wide.

## Distribution and Habitat

psilocybe pseudoaztecorum is endemic to the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt, occurring at elevations of approximately 2,000–4,000 meters above sea level. It fruits singly or in small scattered groups on soils rich in organic matter, particularly in subalpine grasslands (zacatonal), along forest edges, and in meadows adjacent to pine-oak forests (Bosque de Pinus-Quercus). It is most commonly found in the states of Mexico, Puebla, and Tlaxcala. Fruiting occurs during the rainy season, typically from June through September, following periods of sustained precipitation. It has been reported growing in association with grass roots and decomposing herbaceous plant material.

## Chemistry

Chemical analyses by gastón-guzmán and colleagues detected [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]] in fruiting body tissue. The total tryptamine content is moderate compared to other Mexican Psilocybe species, with [[psilocybin]] as the primary alkaloid. Baeocystin has also been detected in trace amounts. The relatively low alkaloid content is consistent with the species' weak bluing reaction, as psilocin (the oxidative product responsible for blue coloration) is present in lower concentrations than in more potent relatives.

## Ethnomycological Significance

While [[psilocybe-aztecorum-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]] has documented use among indigenous communities in the central Mexican highlands, the ethnomycological history of psilocybe pseudoaztecorum is less clear. Its morphological similarity to [[psilocybe-aztecorum-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]] means it was likely included under the same indigenous names (variously reported as *teonanacatl*, *apipiltzin*, or *hongos que emborrachan*) and may have been collected interchangeably. Guzmán noted that local mushroom gatherers in the Sierra Nevada region did not consistently distinguish between the two species. The species has no significant modern recreational or therapeutic use outside of its natural range, owing to its restricted distribution, modest potency, and the difficulty of distinguishing it from numerous non-psychoactive grassland Mycena and Marasmius species.

## Cultivation

There are no established cultivation protocols for psilocybe pseudoaztecorum, and the species has not been cultivated outside of research settings. If fresh specimens were available, cultivation methods adapted from other section Aztecorum species would likely be applicable. The species' high-elevation habitat preferences suggest it may require cooler temperatures than lowland tropical Psilocybe species for successful fruiting. The practical challenges of collecting fresh material from remote high-elevation sites, combined with the modest potency of the species, have limited cultivation interest.

## Molecular Phylogeny

Molecular phylogenetic studies using ITS, LSU, and other genetic markers have confirmed the placement of psilocybe pseudoaztecorum within section Aztecorum. It forms a well-supported clade with [[psilocybe-aztecorum-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]] and psilocybe urdapilletae, all of which are endemic to high-elevation Mexican habitats. The genetic divergence between psilocybe pseudoaztecorum and [[psilocybe-aztecorum-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]] is consistent with their recognition as distinct species, despite their morphological similarity. Molecular data have also helped resolve the relationship between section Aztecorum and other Psilocybe sections, showing that the Aztecorum lineage is part of a larger clade of predominantly Mesoamerican and North American species that includes section Mexicanae and the [[psilocybe-weilii]] complex. The phylogenetic distinctiveness of the Aztecorum species supports their conservation as elements of Mexico's unique fungal biodiversity.

## Ecology

psilocybe pseudoaztecorum occupies a specialized ecological niche in the subalpine grasslands and forest margins of the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt. These high-elevation zacatonal grasslands are characterized by dense tussock grasses, volcanic soils, and significant diurnal temperature variation. The species fruits during the summer rainy season when consistent precipitation creates conditions suitable for fruiting body development. It appears to be associated with the root zones of grasses and herbaceous plants, though whether it forms any symbiotic relationship with plant roots or is purely saprotrophic on decomposing plant material remains unclear. The species is typically found in open, sun-exposed situations rather than under closed forest canopy, distinguishing its microhabitat preferences from those of many forest-dwelling Psilocybe species.

## Conservation

The species has not been formally assessed by the IUCN but is considered potentially vulnerable due to its restricted high-elevation range in central Mexico. The zacatonal grasslands it inhabits are threatened by overgrazing, agricultural expansion, and climate change. As elevations warm, the suitable habitat for this cold-adapted species may contract upward, eventually eliminating suitable habitat altogether on the volcanic peaks where it currently occurs. The Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt is also subject to significant land-use pressure from expanding urban areas, particularly around Mexico City and Puebla. Several of the volcanoes where the species occurs are within protected areas (such as Iztaccíhuatl-Popocatépetl National Park), which provides some degree of habitat protection.

## Similar Species

psilocybe pseudoaztecorum is most easily confused with [[psilocybe-aztecorum-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]], from which it differs by its smaller stature, narrower spores, and more elongated cheilocystidia. [[psilocybe-serbica]] and [[psilocybe-bohemica]] are superficially similar in cap coloration but are European species of temperate forests and can be separated by habitat, spore morphology, and molecular data. Small, non-psychoactive Mycena species that share the same grassland habitat can be distinguished by their lack of bluing reaction, different spore print color (white rather than dark purplish brown), and the absence of a germ pore on the spores.

## Identification Notes

Field identification of psilocybe pseudoaztecorum is complicated by its small size, the abundance of morphologically similar grassland mushrooms in its habitat, and its relatively weak bluing reaction. The most useful field characters are:
- Small stature with a hygrophanous cap that fades dramatically from brown to nearly white as it dries
- Adnate gills that are grayish with lilaceous tint when young, darkening with maturity
- Slender, hollow stipe with fine white fibrillose scales
- Slow, pale blue bruising that is weaker than in [[psilocybe-aztecorum-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]]
- High-elevation habitat (2,000–4,000 meters) in zacatonal grasslands
- Dark purplish brown spore print

The weak bluing reaction is a particular challenge for identification, as it may be barely perceptible in dried or older specimens and can be easily confused with the brownish or greenish discoloration that sometimes occurs in non-psychoactive species upon handling. Foragers should not rely on bluing alone as a diagnostic character for this species. The high-elevation grassland habitat provides useful context — psilocybin mushrooms found in the zacatonal of the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt during the rainy season are most likely to be either this species or [[psilocybe-aztecorum-entity-the-mushroom-cultivator]]. Microscopic examination, particularly of spore dimensions (6.5–8.5 × 4–5.5 µm) and cheilocystidia morphology (ventricose to lageniform with narrow necks), is necessary for definitive identification.
## Seasonality

Psilocybe pseudoaztecorum fruits during the rainy season from June through September, with peak fruiting typically in July and August when consistent precipitation maintains high soil moisture in the high-elevation grasslands. The zacatonal grasslands experience significant diurnal temperature variation, with warm days and cool to cold nights during the fruiting season. Frost is possible at the highest elevations where the species occurs, particularly toward the end of the fruiting season in September. The species fruits singly to scattered, rarely in large groups, and individual fruiting bodies are often partially hidden among dense tussock grasses.
