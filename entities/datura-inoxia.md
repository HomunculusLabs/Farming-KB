## Overview

**Datura innoxia**, commonly known as downy thorn-apple, pricklyburr, or tolguacha, is an annual or short-lived perennial herbaceous plant in the family Solanaceae. It is native to Mexico and the southwestern United States and is widely naturalized in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide.

The species is a potent source of tropane alkaloids, particularly scopolamine, and has been used both medicinally and ceremonially in indigenous cultures. All parts of the plant are highly toxic, and accidental poisonings are well documented.

Datura innoxia is also cultivated as an ornamental for its large, fragrant, trumpet-shaped flowers that open in the evening. The plant's dramatic floral display and nocturnal blooming habit have made it popular in xeriscaping and night gardens.

Taxonomic confusion has surrounded Datura innoxia for centuries. The species was frequently treated as a variety or synonym of Datura metel, and the correct spelling of the epithet has been debated between "innoxia" (harmless) and "inoxia."

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Datura innoxia Mill. was described by Philip Miller in 1768. The genus Datura contains approximately 10 to 14 species distributed across the Americas, with a secondary center of diversity in Asia. The species epithet "innoxia" (meaning harmless) is ironic given the plant's extreme toxicity.

The plant belongs to the tribe Datureae within Solanaceae, closely related to Brugmansia (angel's trumpets). Datura species are distinguished from Brugmansia by their erect (rather than pendulous) flowers and annual or herbaceous perennial habit. This distinction has taxonomic significance, though the two genera have sometimes been merged in classifications.

The genus name Datura derives from the Hindi word "dhatura," reflecting the plant's long history of use in South Asian traditional medicine following its introduction from the Americas.

Datura innoxia grows 60 to 150 centimeters tall with a bushy, branching habit. The entire plant is covered in a dense layer of soft, velvety pubescence (tomentose), which distinguishes it from the less pubescent Datura stramonium.

Stems are stout, green to purplish-green, and covered with fine glandular and nonglandular trichomes. The plant produces a strong, somewhat unpleasant odor when the foliage is crushed.

Leaves are large, ovate to triangular, 10 to 25 centimeters long, with irregularly toothed to lobed margins. The leaf surface is densely pubescent on both adaxial and abaxial surfaces, giving a grayish-green appearance.

Flowers are large, trumpet-shaped, 15 to 20 centimeters long, opening in the evening and remaining open until the following morning. The corolla is white to pale lavender with a five-toothed limb. Flowers are borne on short pedicels in the leaf axils.

The fruit is a globular capsule, 4 to 6 centimeters in diameter, densely covered with slender, sharp spines. The spines are approximately 5 to 10 millimeters long and serve as a deterrent to herbivores.

Each capsule contains numerous kidney-shaped seeds, approximately 3 to 4 millimeters long, which are released when the capsule dehisces at maturity. A single plant can produce 1000 to 5000 seeds, contributing to the species' persistent soil seed bank.

Seeds can remain viable in the soil for 10 to 20 years or longer, creating management challenges in agricultural settings where eradication is desired. The seed bank persists even after adult plants are eliminated.

## Distribution and Habitat

Datura innoxia is native to the highland regions of central and southern Mexico and the adjacent southwestern United States, including Arizona, New Mexico, and Texas. It is most commonly found at elevations of 1000 to 2500 meters in its native range.

The species has been widely introduced and naturalized in tropical, subtropical, and warm temperate regions worldwide. Naturalized populations are established in parts of South America, Africa, southern Europe, South Asia, and Australia.

It grows in disturbed habitats including roadsides, abandoned fields, riverbanks, waste ground, and livestock corrals. The plant thrives in sandy or gravelly, well-drained soils and is highly drought-tolerant once established.

Datura innoxia is remarkably tolerant of poor, nutrient-deficient soils and can colonize areas where few other plants establish. This adaptability contributes to its success as a weedy species in anthropogenic landscapes.

In agricultural settings, Datura innoxia can become a problematic weed, particularly in row crops. The seeds can persist in soil for many years, creating a persistent seed bank that complicates weed management.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

All parts of Datura innoxia contain tropane alkaloids, with the highest concentrations found in the seeds and leaves. Total alkaloid content ranges from 0.3 to 0.8 percent of dry weight, with scopolamine as the dominant alkaloid.

Scopolamine (hyoscine) is the primary tropane alkaloid, typically comprising 50 to 80 percent of the total alkaloid fraction. Hyoscyamine, atropine, and norhyoscyamine are present in smaller amounts along with trace quantities of apoatropine and tigloidine.

The high scopolamine content distinguishes D. innoxia from Datura stramonium, which is predominantly hyoscyamine-rich. This difference has pharmacological significance, as scopolamine has greater central nervous system activity than hyoscyamine.

Scopolamine acts as a competitive antagonist at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, producing anticholinergic effects including mydriasis (pupil dilation), tachycardia, dry mouth, and at higher doses, hallucinations and delirium. The anticholinergic syndrome caused by Datura poisoning is sometimes referred to as "hot as a hare, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet."

Despite its toxicity, D. innoxia has been investigated as a commercial source of scopolamine for pharmaceutical use. Tissue culture and fermentation techniques have been developed to produce the alkaloids without field cultivation.

Hairy root cultures induced by Agrobacterium rhizogenes transformation have shown particular promise for scopolamine production. These cultures can be maintained in bioreactors and produce alkaloid profiles comparable to or exceeding those of intact plants.

The species has also been investigated for its potential in phytoremediation of heavy metal-contaminated soils. Datura species have demonstrated the ability to accumulate cadmium and lead in their tissues.

## Traditional Uses and Toxicity

Datura innoxia has a long history of use in traditional medicine and ritual practices among indigenous peoples of Mexico and the southwestern United States. The Zuni people used preparations of the plant for analgesic purposes, and the Aztecs reportedly used it in ceremonial contexts.

In Mexican traditional medicine, leaf preparations have been applied externally for joint pain, arthritis, and skin infections. The anticholinergic properties that make the plant toxic also provide genuine analgesic and antispasmodic effects when applied topically at appropriate doses.

Internal use of Datura innoxia is extremely dangerous and has resulted in numerous hospitalizations and deaths. Symptoms of poisoning include dry mouth, blurred vision, rapid heartbeat, hyperthermia, hallucinations, aggressive behavior, and potentially fatal respiratory paralysis.

The recreational use of Datura species for their hallucinogenic properties is well documented but highly dangerous. The dose-response curve for tropane alkaloids is steep, and the difference between a psychoactive dose and a lethal dose is narrow and unpredictable.

Users frequently report terrifying hallucinations, complete amnesia of the experience, and prolonged anticholinergic effects lasting 24 to 48 hours. Emergency department visits for Datura intoxication are common in regions where the plant grows.

Children and adolescents are at particular risk due to their lower body weight and curiosity about the attractive flowers and seed pods. Poison control centers in regions where Datura grows commonly receive calls regarding pediatric exposures.

Antidotal treatment for severe poisoning involves administration of physostigmine, a reversible cholinesterase inhibitor. Supportive care including intravenous fluids, cooling measures for hyperthermia, and sedation may also be required.

## Ornamental Use

Datura innoxia is cultivated as an ornamental plant for its large, showy, fragrant flowers. The nocturnal blooming habit makes it particularly suited for moon gardens and evening landscape designs where pale flowers are illuminated by moonlight.

The plant is drought-tolerant and requires minimal irrigation once established, making it suitable for xeriscaping in arid and semi-arid climates. It readily self-seeds and can persist in gardens for many years through its persistent soil seed bank.

Caution is advised when planting D. innoxia in gardens accessible to children or pets. Several horticultural societies and poison control centers recommend against planting the species in residential landscapes due to the toxicity risk.
