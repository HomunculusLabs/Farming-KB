## Description

Amoebae are a group of protozoa that move and feed by extending pseudopodia (false feet). They are important bacterial grazers in the soil food web and play a central role in nutrient mineralization — converting nitrogen locked in bacterial biomass into plant-available forms. Along with flagellates, healthy amoebae populations indicate aerobic, well-structured soil conditions.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Eukarya
- **Kingdom**: Protista (Protozoa)
- **Type**: Amoeboid protozoa
- **Ecological Role**: Bacterial grazer, nutrient cycler
- **Group**: One of three major protozoan groups (alongside flagellates and ciliates)

## Key Facts

- Amoebae move through soil by extending pseudopodia, allowing them to navigate soil pores and bacterial colonies.
- They are major contributors to the microbial loop — consuming bacteria and excreting nitrogen in plant-available ammonium form.
- Testate amoebae (those with shells) can be important indicators of soil maturity and organic matter quality.
- Amoebae are killed by anaerobic conditions; their absence in compost tea indicates the brew went anaerobic.
- Inocula of amoebae can be obtained from quality compost, especially worm compost, or from commercial products.
- Amoebae populations that are too low result in reduced nutrient cycling, meaning bacteria immobilize nutrients without releasing them to plants.
- Combined flagellate and amoeba populations need to be above minimum levels on a Soil Foodweb Inc. report for healthy nutrient cycling.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- In the soil foodweb restoration protocol, amoebae are addressed in Step Seven — if populations are too low, protozoan inoculum is recommended.
- Amoebae in compost tea contribute to the tea's ability to cycle nutrients when applied to soil or leaf surfaces.
- Anaerobic conditions during compost tea brewing kill amoebae, which is why anaerobic teas cannot provide the nutrient cycling benefits of aerobic teas.
- In mushroom cultivation, healthy amoeba populations in casing soil contribute to competitive exclusion of contaminants.
- Amoebae help regulate bacterial populations, preventing any single bacterial species from dominating and potentially becoming pathogenic.
- The ratio of amoebae to flagellates to ciliates provides diagnostic information about soil aeration status and compaction levels.
