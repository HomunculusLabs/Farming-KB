# Gymnopilus aeruginosus

Gymnopilus aeruginosus, commonly known as the magic blue gym, is a large psychoactive mushroom in the family Cortinariaceae (or Hymenogastraceae under modern classifications). It is one of several Gymnopilus species confirmed to contain [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]], distinguished by its imposing size, rusty orange spore print, and characteristic greenish to blue-green coloration that develops with age. Despite its striking appearance and confirmed psychoactive properties, it is seldom sought due to its intensely bitter taste and variable potency. The species represents an important example of the independent evolution of psilocybin biosynthesis outside the core Psilocybe lineage, making it of particular interest for evolutionary mycology.

## Taxonomy and Naming

The species was originally described as *Pholiota aeruginosa* by Charles Horton Peck and later transferred to Gymnopilus by Rolf Singer. The epithet "aeruginosus" is derived from Latin, meaning "verdigris" or "copper-rust green," referring to the distinctive greenish coloration that develops on the cap and stipe as the mushroom matures. The genus name *Gymnopilus* derives from Greek *gymnos* (naked) and *pilus* (cap), referring to the bare cap surface characteristic of many species in the genus. The current placement in Gymnopilus reflects its rusty-brown spores, partial veil, and lignicolous habit. Recent phylogenetic analyses have placed Gymnopilus within the family Hymenogastraceae, alongside Psilocybe and related genera, though the traditional placement in Cortinariaceae remains in widespread use.

Jochen-Gartz|Gartz, along with Allen (1992), confirmed the presence of [[psilocybin]] in this species through analytical chemistry, establishing it among the neurotropic Gymnopilus species (listed as species #177 in Guzmán, Allen & Gartz's worldwide catalogue of neurotropic fungi). The genus Gymnopilus contains over 200 described species worldwide, but only approximately 14 have been confirmed to contain psychoactive compounds, making Gymnopilus the second-largest genus of neurotropic fungi after Psilocybe in terms of species count. Along with [[gymnopilus-spectabilis-profile]], [[gymnopilus-purpuratus]], [[gymnopilus-luteofolius]], and [[gymnopilus-validipes]], G. aeruginosus represents one of the major psychoactive species in this genus. The phylogenetic distribution of psilocybin within Gymnopilus suggests independent acquisition of the biosynthetic pathway, supporting hypotheses of horizontal gene transfer or convergent evolution.

## Description

### Cap (Pileus)
The pileus is large, measuring 20 to 230 mm in diameter — among the largest of the psychoactive Gymnopilus species. It is convex with an incurved margin when young, expanding to broadly convex or nearly plane with age. The surface is dry, covered with fine fibrils or tawny to dark brown scales (squamules), especially toward the center, becoming more prominent and darker brown at maturity. Color is variable and hygrophanous: dull bluish gray-green to variegated green and yellow when fresh, becoming drab as the specimen dries. Flesh is pallid to whitish with greenish to dull bluish tones, yellowish in drying. The margin may be slightly appendiculate with fibrillose veil remnants. This green coloration is one of the most diagnostic field features and is unusual among macrofungi.

### Gills (Lamellae)
Gill attachment is adnexed to adnate, occasionally slightly decurrent, and the gills often tear away from the stem at maturity. They are close to crowded, broad, with edges that are frequently irregular and slightly paler than the faces. Color is initially cream buff to pale orangish, maturing to the characteristic rusty brown to ferruginous tone of the genus as spores develop. The rusty spore color is diagnostic for the entire Gymnopilus genus and distinguishes it from Psilocybe species, which produce dark purplish-brown spore prints. Gill edges may be slightly fringed and can develop a mottled appearance as spores mature unevenly.

### Stipe (Stem)
The stipe measures 30 to 120 mm long by 4 to 40 mm thick, similar in color to the cap. The surface is covered with appressed fibrils that soon disappear, becoming smooth and slightly striate. It is solid when young but becomes hollow with age. The partial veil is cortinate, yellowish, and fragile, soon disappearing to leave a fibrillose annular zone in the superior region of the stem. Below the annular zone, longitudinal fibrillose striations may be visible. The stem base often has whitish to yellowish mycelial threads attached to the wood substrate.

### Spores and Microscopic Features
Spore print is rusty brown to rusty orange to reddish cinnamon. Basidiospores are 6 to 9 × 3.5 to 4.5 µm, ellipsoid in face view, inequilateral in side view, with rough to warty (echinulate) ornamentation when mature. Basidia are 4-spored, clavate. Pleurocystidia are rare, measuring 23 to 35 × 5 to 7 µm. Cheilocystidia are abundant, 20 to 38 × 5 to 9 µm, flask-shaped to ventricose, and mostly capitate. The echinulate spore ornamentation is a key microscopic feature distinguishing Gymnopilus from smooth-spored genera and is essential for definitive identification to species level.

### Odor and Taste
Odor is slight, oily farinaceous, with hints of anise reported from specimens collected in the Pacific Northwest. The taste is distinctly and intensely bitter — a hallmark characteristic of psychoactive Gymnopilus species that helps distinguish them from non-psychoactive lookalikes. This bitterness persists even after cooking and is attributable to sesquiterpenes and related secondary metabolites unrelated to psilocybin content.

## Distribution and Habitat

Gymnopilus aeruginosus has a wide distribution across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere. In North America, it is reported from the Pacific Northwest (California, Oregon, Washington, Idaho), the Great Lakes region (Michigan, Ohio), and the eastern states (Tennessee, Pennsylvania). It also occurs in central to northern Europe and Japan, reflecting a broadly circumboreal temperate distribution pattern.

It is a saprotrophic wood-decay fungus that grows gregarious to cespitose on woody debris of both hardwoods and conifers, including stumps, fallen logs, sawdust piles, and wood chips used in landscaping and nurseries. Common hardwood substrates include oak, maple, alder, and beech. It may also fruit on buried wood or large woody debris not immediately visible. Fruiting occurs from May through September, depending on regional climate conditions. The mushrooms often reappear at the same locations year after year as the woody substrate decomposes progressively, sometimes forming extensive fruiting sheets on heavily decayed logs.

## Chemistry and Active Compounds

Gymnopilus aeruginosus is confirmed to contain [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]], established by analytical studies including TLC and HPLC (Hatfield et al., 1978; Allen & Gartz, 1992). The species is rated as moderately active:

| Compound | Typical Concentration (% dry weight) |
|----------|--------------------------------------|
| Psilocybin | 0.03 – 0.15 (variable) |
| Psilocin | trace – 0.05 |
| Baeocystin | trace |
| Norbaeocystin | not specifically quantified |

These concentrations are modest compared to potent Psilocybe species such as [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-azurescens-species-guide]] (up to 1.8% psilocybin) or [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-cyanescens-species-guide]], but are consistent with the general Gymnopilus range of 0.01–0.2% psilocybin by dry weight. Potency varies considerably between individual collections and geographic populations. For comparison, the related [[gymnopilus-purpuratus]] contains 0.23–0.25% psilocybin and 0.21–0.33% psilocin, making it one of the more potent species in the genus.

The greenish-blue coloration is partly attributable to oxidation of psilocin upon exposure to air, though other pigments including quinone derivatives may also contribute. Paul-Stamets|Stamets (1996) noted that there may be compounds other than psilocybin, but closely related, that potentiate the psychoactive experience in this species. The pronounced bitterness is due to sesquiterpenes and other secondary metabolites and is not correlated with psilocybin content.

The bluing reaction in G. aeruginosus is variable — some collections exhibit distinct blue or greenish-blue staining on the stem and cap when bruised or handled, while others show minimal reaction. This inconsistency means that absence of bluing should not be taken as evidence that psychoactive compounds are absent, a caution that applies broadly across the Gymnopilus genus.

## Edibility and Taste

Gymnopilus aeruginosus is not considered edible in any culinary sense. The intensely bitter taste makes it wholly unpalatable, and this bitterness is not diminished by cooking, drying, or any other preparation method. The bitter compounds, likely sesquiterpenes, may cause gastrointestinal distress (nausea, vomiting) at the large quantities that would need to be consumed for psychoactive effect. There are no well-documented traditional or indigenous uses of this species as food or entheogen — the extreme bitterness alone would have discouraged any intentional consumption. Modern recreational use is exceedingly rare due to the low-to-moderate potency and profoundly unpleasant flavor profile. The mushroom should be regarded as inedible and potentially problematic if consumed in quantity.

It is worth noting that the bitter taste, while off-putting, serves as a useful chemotaxonomic marker. In the Gymnopilus genus, intense bitterness is more strongly associated with psychoactive species than with non-psychoactive ones. However, this correlation is imperfect and should not be relied upon as a definitive indicator of psilocybin content. Some non-psychoactive Gymnopilus species are also bitter, and the converse — a non-bitter Gymnopilus being guaranteed safe — is certainly false given the presence of other toxic compounds in some species.

## Similar Species

Accurate identification of Gymnopilus aeruginosus requires care, as several other Gymnopilus species can appear similar and their psychoactive status varies:

- **[[gymnopilus-spectabilis-profile]]** — Much larger (cap up to 40 cm), bright yellowish orange to rusty orange without the characteristic green tones. Known as the "big laughing gym." Its potency varies dramatically by geography — inactive in Europe but active in Japan and parts of the US (Ohio, Michigan, Massachusetts). Many North American collections identified as G. spectabilis are actually the inactive lookalike G. ventricosus. Has a well-formed membranous annulus unlike the fragile fibrillose zone of G. aeruginosus.

- **[[gymnopilus-luteofolius]]** — Smaller (cap 2–6 cm), with dark red to reddish brown fibrillose scales that fade to pinkish red or yellowish, sometimes bruising bluish green in cold weather. Primarily found on wood chips and woody debris from California to British Columbia, and widely scattered across the US. Mildly active per anecdotal reports. The purplish-red coloration when fresh is a key differentiator from the green tones of G. aeruginosus.

- **[[gymnopilus-junonius]]** — Large and orange, superficially similar habitat on hardwood stumps and logs. Not reliably psychoactive and considered potentially toxic — responsible for gastrointestinal poisonings in Japan and Korea where it is confused with edible species. This is the most commonly encountered Gymnopilus and the most important non-psychoactive lookalike to exclude. Typically lacks the greenish tones of G. aeruginosus.

- **[[gymnopilus-viridans]]** — Develops greenish coloration on the gills and flesh with age or bruising, but the cap lacks the blue-green tones of G. aeruginosus from the start. Primarily distributed in eastern North America from the Great Lakes to the Gulf Coast. Contains 0.02–0.10% psilocybin.

- **[[gymnopilus-purpuratus]]** — Smaller (cap 1–5 cm), reddish purplish brown with tinges of yellow and green. South American, European, and Australasian distribution. Among the more potent Gymnopilus species: 0.23–0.25% psilocybin, 0.21–0.33% psilocin, 0.03–0.05% baeocystin.

- **Galerina species** — Share rusty spore prints and some species have rings. Some Galerina species contain deadly amatoxins (the same toxins found in *Amanita phalloides*). This is the single most dangerous potential confusion and the primary reason Stamets warns that amateurs should avoid consuming any Gymnopilus specimen without expert verification. Microscopic examination of the cap cuticle (filamentous in Galerina vs. different in Gymnopilus) can help distinguish them.

## Field Identification Tips

Several field characters help narrow identification to G. aeruginosus, though microscopic confirmation is always recommended:

1. **Greenish-blue cap coloration** — The single most distinctive macroscopic feature. When fresh, the cap shows dull bluish gray-green to variegated green and yellow tones that are highly unusual among macrofungi. This color is most apparent in young, moist specimens and fades as the mushroom dries. No other common wood-inhabiting mushroom in temperate regions shows this combination of size and green cap coloration.

2. **Tawny scales on the cap surface** — Dark brown scales overlay the greenish background, becoming more prominent with age. This combination of green ground color with brown scales is characteristic and helps separate it from uniformly orange Gymnopilus species.

3. **Extremely bitter taste** — A tiny nibble (then spit out) producing an intense, lingering bitterness strongly suggests a psychoactive Gymnopilus. Non-psychoactive orange Gymnopilus species (e.g., G. junonius) tend to be moderately bitter at most. While not a definitive identifier, extreme bitterness is a useful screening character.

4. **Rusty spore print** — Essential for confirming the genus. Take a spore print on white paper; a rusty brown to reddish cinnamon deposit rules out Psilocybe (dark purplish-brown) and most other genera. This simple test should be performed for every collection.

5. **Woody substrate and clustered growth** — Growth on dead hardwood stumps, logs, sawdust, or wood chips, often in dense clusters (cespitose), distinguishes it from terrestrial species. The cespitose habit is characteristic.

6. **Season and size** — Large stature (cap commonly 5–15 cm, up to 23 cm) fruiting May through September on woody debris narrows the field considerably within the Gymnopilus genus.

Despite these clues, definitive identification to species level requires microscopic examination of spore ornamentation and cystidia morphology, particularly the capitate cheilocystidia and the rare pleurocystidia.

## Historical Reports

The species was first documented by Peck as *Pholiota aeruginosa* and later transferred to Gymnopilus by Singer. Hesler (1969) provided early North American distribution records in his monographic treatment of the genus. Hatfield, Valdes, and Smith (1978) published the landmark paper "The occurrence of psilocybin in Gymnopilus species" in *Lloydia* (41: 140–144), confirming psilocybin in multiple Gymnopilus species including G. aeruginosus. Allen and Gartz (1992) surveyed the scientific literature and listed G. aeruginosus among ten confirmed psilocybin-active Gymnopilus species. Ammirati et al. (1985) included it in their treatment of North American neurotropic fungi. Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz (1998) listed it as species #177 in their comprehensive worldwide catalogue of neurotropic fungi published in *Mycotaxon*. Despite this scientific documentation spanning several decades, very few people have actually experimented with this mushroom — likely because it was not widely known to be active and the extreme bitterness deters consumption even among experienced foragers. The species has no known history of ethnobotanical use, unlike [[gymnopilus-spectabilis-profile]] which has a documented history as "O-waraitake" (the laughing mushroom) in Japan.

## Toxicity Concerns

Some Gymnopilus species contain toxins unrelated to psilocybin, and confusion between psychoactive and non-psychoactive species carries real risk. [[gymnopilus-junonius]] has been associated with gastrointestinal distress and is a common cause of mushroom poisoning in East Asia, where it is frequently confused with edible species. The most dangerous potential confusion, however, is with *Galerina* species — some of which contain lethal amatoxins identical to those found in death cap mushrooms (*Amanita phalloides*). Both genera produce rusty spore prints and can share similar statures and habitats. Foragers should always verify identification through microscopic features — particularly spore ornamentation, cap cuticle structure, and cystidia morphology — before consuming any Gymnopilus specimen. The bitter taste, while a useful field indicator, is not a reliable marker of psychoactive content or safety.

## Ecological Role

As a saprotrophic wood-decay fungus, Gymnopilus aeruginosus plays an important role in forest ecosystems by decomposing dead and dying hardwood trees. It contributes to carbon cycling and nutrient release, making nutrients locked in woody biomass available to other organisms. The species is part of the successional fungal community that colonizes dead wood, typically appearing after primary decomposers have begun the breakdown process. Its preference for large stumps and fallen logs means it often dominates the later stages of wood decomposition, facilitating the transition of woody debris into soil organic matter. The cespitose growth habit means that individual fruiting events can involve dozens of mushrooms arising from a single colonized log, representing a significant investment of decompositional biomass. Gymnopilus species, including G. aeruginosus, may also act as weak parasites on stressed or dying trees, blurring the line between saprotrophic and parasitic lifestyles. This ecological versatility allows the species to persist across a range of forest conditions and wood decay stages.
