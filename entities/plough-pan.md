# Plough Pan

## Description

A plough pan (also called a plow pan or hardpan) is a clay-based compacted layer that develops below cropland, typically caused by the repeated passage of mouldboard ploughs through wet soil. The smearing action of the plough share compresses soil particles at the base of the cultivated layer into a dense, impermeable barrier that restricts root growth, water infiltration, and gas exchange. Plough pans are one of the most widespread forms of human-induced soil degradation in conventional agriculture.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil compaction / anthropogenic hardpan
- **Composition**: Clay-based compacted layer
- **Cause**: Mouldboard ploughing in wet conditions
- **Position**: At the base of the ploughed layer (typically 15–30 cm depth)
- **Distribution**: Widespread in conventionally ploughed cropland worldwide

## Key Facts

- Caused specifically by mouldboard ploughs operating in wet periods
- Clay particles are smeared and compressed into a dense, impermeable layer
- Restricts root penetration — crops develop shallow root systems
- Impedes water infiltration — increases surface runoff and erosion
- Limits gas exchange — roots and soil organisms suffer from poor aeration
- One of the most common forms of soil degradation in conventional agriculture
- Unlike geological pans (calcrete, ferricrete), plough pans are entirely human-caused

## Management in Permaculture

- **Ripping** with deep-tined implements breaks the compacted layer
- **Deep mulch pits** can be used to establish trees through the pan
- **Keyline ploughing** (Yeomans plough) can shatter the pan without inverting the soil
- **Deep-rooted [[cover-crops]]** (chicory, radish, comfrey) can biologically fracture plough pans over time
- Transition to minimum tillage or no-till methods prevents reformation
- Avoid working soil when wet — the primary cause of pan formation
- Raised beds can bypass shallow plough pans in garden-scale systems

## Relevance to Cultivation

Plough pans are a direct consequence of conventional agriculture and one of the primary soil problems that [[permaculture]] seeks to address. They exemplify how short-term farming practices can create long-term soil damage. Permaculture approaches — minimum soil disturbance, deep-rooted perennials, [[keyline-design]] — directly address both the prevention and remediation of plough pans. Mollison groups plough pans with geological concretions (calcrete, ferricrete, silcrete) because they all require similar interventions: ripping, explosive shattering, or deep mulch pits to establish trees. However, unlike geological pans, plough pans can be prevented entirely through changed management practices.

## Soil Rehabilitation Context

Mechanical soil conditioning addresses compaction layers and improves water infiltration
without inverting the soil profile. This approach preserves soil structure and biological
communities while restoring physical soil properties essential for plant growth.
