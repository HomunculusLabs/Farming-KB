## Overview

Argyreia nervosa is a perennial climbing vine in the family Convolvulaceae, commonly known as Hawaiian baby woodrose or elephant creeper. The species is native to the Indian subcontinent and has been widely introduced throughout tropical and subtropical regions worldwide.

The seeds of Argyreia nervosa contain ergoline alkaloids, principally lysergic acid amide (LSA, ergine), at concentrations significantly higher than those found in morning glory species. This has made the plant one of the most widely used botanical sources of naturally occurring lysergic acid derivatives.

In traditional Indian medicine (Ayurveda), the plant has been used for centuries as a treatment for various ailments. The roots, leaves, and seeds each hold distinct positions in the Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia.

The plant's large, heart-shaped leaves with prominent silvery-white venation on the undersurface make it easily recognizable in cultivation. It has become one of the most well-known entheogenic plants in the Convolvulaceae family, alongside morning glories and ololiuqui.

## Taxonomy

**Kingdom:** Plantae

**Division:** Angiospermae

**Class:** Magnoliopsida

**Order:** Solanales

**Family:** Convolvulaceae

**Genus:** Argyreia

**Species:** A. nervosa

**Authority:** Burman f. (1775)

The genus name Argyreia derives from the Greek word "argyros" meaning silver, referring to the silvery pubescence on the leaf undersides characteristic of many species in the genus. The specific epithet "nervosa" refers to the prominent leaf veins.

The genus Argyreia contains approximately 90 species distributed across tropical and subtropical Asia, Australia, and the Pacific islands. Argyreia nervosa is the most widely known species in the genus due to its psychoactive seeds.

Synonyms include Convolvulus nervosus (Burman f.) and Argyreia speciosa (Sweet). Some authorities distinguish Argyreia nervosa from the closely related Argyreia speciosa, which is also used in Ayurvedic medicine, though the two are frequently conflated in the horticultural and ethnobotanical literature.

## Morphology

Argyreia nervosa is a vigorous perennial woody vine that can climb to heights of 10 to 15 meters when supported by trees or structures. The stems are thick, twining, and covered with a dense rusty-tomentose pubescence when young, becoming somewhat smoother with age.

The leaves are large, broadly cordate, 15 to 30 centimeters long and 10 to 25 centimeters wide, with a deeply cordate base and acuminate apex. The upper leaf surface is dark green and sparsely hairy, while the lower surface is covered with a dense layer of silvery-white pubescence that gives the leaves their distinctive silvery appearance.

The inflorescences are axillary or terminal cymes bearing several to many flowers. The flowers are funnel-shaped (campanulate), approximately 5 to 8 centimeters long, with a pale pink to rose-purple corolla and a darker throat. The calyx is covered with rusty-brown tomentose hairs.

The fruit is a globose capsule approximately 1 to 1.5 centimeters in diameter, containing 1 to 4 seeds. The seeds are large (8 to 12 millimeters long), ovoid to broadly triangular, dark brown to black, and covered with a short, dense pubescence. The seed coat is extremely hard and durable.

The root system consists of a thick, fleshy taproot that can reach considerable size in mature plants. The roots are used extensively in Ayurvedic medicine.

## Distribution and Habitat

Argyreia nervosa is native to the Indian subcontinent, where it occurs in tropical and subtropical forests from the Himalayan foothills through peninsular India, Sri Lanka, and extending into Bangladesh and Myanmar. The species grows in a variety of habitats including forest edges, thickets, disturbed areas, and along stream banks, typically at elevations below 1,500 meters.

The plant has been widely introduced and naturalized throughout the tropics. It is particularly well-established in Hawaii, where it was introduced as an ornamental and has become naturalized in lowland wet forests and disturbed areas. Other regions of naturalization include the Caribbean, Central and South America, tropical Africa, Southeast Asia, and northern Australia.

In Hawaii, the species is considered an invasive weed in some areas due to its vigorous growth habit and ability to smother native vegetation. It is commonly found climbing on fences, utility poles, and native trees in lowland disturbed habitats.

The species is tolerant of a wide range of soil types but performs best in moist, well-drained soils with partial to full sun exposure. It is drought-tolerant once established but grows most vigorously with regular moisture.

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds

The seeds of Argyreia nervosa contain ergoline (lysergic acid derivative) alkaloids as their principal active constituents. These compounds are structurally related to the ergot alkaloids and to LSD.

The dominant alkaloid is lysergic acid amide (LSA, ergine), typically present at concentrations of 0.3% to 0.9% of seed dry weight. This is substantially higher than the LSA content of morning glory seeds (0.02% to 0.1%), making Argyreia nervosa seeds among the most potent botanical sources of ergoline alkaloids known.

Other ergoline alkaloids identified in the seeds include isoergine (the C-8 stereoisomer of ergine), lysergic acid hydroxyethylamide (LSH), lysergic acid methylcarbinolamide (ergonovine), elymoclavine, chanoclavine-I, and setoclavine. The total ergoline alkaloid content is typically 0.5% to 1.2% of seed dry weight.

As with morning glory species, the ergoline alkaloids in Argyreia nervosa are believed to be produced by a fungal endophyte of the genus Periglandula (family Clavicipitaceae) rather than by the plant itself. These fungi grow within the leaf and seed tissues and synthesize the alkaloids that accumulate in the seeds.

The seeds also contain a cyanogenic glycoside and various fixed oils. The seed coat coating can cause significant gastrointestinal discomfort when ingested, and many preparation methods involve removal of this outer coating.

The roots contain different constituents from the seeds, including triterpenoids, flavonoids, and glycosides. The root chemistry is the basis for the plant's traditional medicinal applications in Ayurveda, which are entirely separate from the psychoactive properties of the seeds.

## Ethnobotanical and Traditional Use

In the Ayurvedic medical tradition of India, Argyreia nervosa has been used for centuries under the Sanskrit name "Vidhara" or "Samudrasoka." The plant is classified as a rasayana (rejuvenating tonic) and is used to treat a wide range of conditions.

The roots are the most commonly used plant part in Ayurvedic medicine. They are prescribed as a general tonic, aphrodisiac, and anti-inflammatory agent. Traditional applications include treatment of rheumatism, arthritis, gout, diabetes, nervous disorders, and respiratory complaints. The root is typically prepared as a decoction or powder and is often combined with other Ayurvedic herbs in compound formulations.

The leaves are applied externally as a poultice for wounds, burns, and skin diseases. The leaf juice is used in traditional medicine for its anti-inflammatory properties.

In modern recreational contexts, the seeds of Argyreia nervosa have gained notoriety as a readily available source of LSA. Typical doses range from 3 to 12 seeds, depending on the potency of the batch and individual sensitivity. The effects are similar to those of morning glory seeds but significantly more potent due to the higher alkaloid concentration.

The psychoactive experience is generally described as sedating and dreamlike, with visual alterations, altered time perception, and mild euphoria. Physical side effects including nausea, drowsiness, lethargy, and abdominal discomfort are commonly reported and are considered more pronounced than with other ergoline-containing botanicals.

Commercial seed suppliers frequently treat Argyreia nervosa seeds with fungicides and other chemicals. Seeds sold for horticultural purposes should never be ingested without thorough washing and verification that they are untreated.
