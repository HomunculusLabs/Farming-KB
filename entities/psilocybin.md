## Overview

Psilocybin (O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, abbreviated as 4-PO-DMT) is a naturally occurring psychedelic prodrug and the principal psychoactive compound found in over 200 species of mushrooms, primarily in the genus Psilocybe. Upon ingestion, psilocybin is rapidly dephosphorylated by alkaline phosphatase enzymes to psilocin (4-HO-DMT), the pharmacologically active metabolite responsible for the characteristic psychedelic effects.

Psilocybin acts primarily as a partial agonist at the serotonin 5-HT₂A receptor, a mechanism shared with other classical psychedelics such as LSD, mescaline, and DMT. This receptor activation leads to profound alterations in consciousness, perception, cognition, and emotional processing. The subjective experience — often described as a "trip" — typically lasts 4-6 hours and can include visual and auditory hallucinations, synesthesia, altered sense of time, mystical-type experiences, and deeply emotional introspective states.

After decades of research suppression following the Controlled Substances Act of 1970, psilocybin has experienced a dramatic scientific renaissance since the early 2000s. Modern clinical trials at institutions including Johns Hopkins University, Imperial College London, and Yale University have demonstrated significant therapeutic potential for treatment-resistant depression, end-of-life anxiety, substance use disorders, and obsessive-compulsive disorder. As of 2026, psilocybin has been decriminalized in several jurisdictions and approved for regulated therapeutic use in others, with ongoing clinical development toward full FDA approval.

## Chemical Profile

- **IUPAC Name:** [3-(2-Dimethylaminoethyl)-1H-indol-4-yl] dihydrogen phosphate
- **Chemical Formula:** C₁₂H₁₇N₂O₄P
- **Molecular Weight:** 284.25 g/mol
- **CAS Number:** 520-53-6
- **SMILES:** CN(C)CCc1c[nH]c2ccc(OP(=O)(O)O)cc12
- **Appearance:** White to off-white crystalline powder
- **Solubility:** Soluble in water; moderately soluble in methanol and ethanol
- **Melting Point:** 220-228°C (decomposes)

### Biosynthesis

Psilocybin is biosynthesized in fungal tissue through a pathway that begins with the amino acid L-tryptophan. The pathway proceeds through four enzymatic steps: decarboxylation to tryptamine, N,N-dimethylation to 4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (psilocin), and finally phosphorylation to psilocybin. The biosynthetic gene cluster (PsiD, PsiK, PsiM, PsiH) was fully characterized in 2017, enabling potential heterologous production in yeast and bacterial systems.

## History

### Indigenous Use

Archaeological evidence suggests that psilocybin mushrooms have been used by indigenous peoples of Mesoamerica for at least 3,000 years. The most well-documented tradition is that of the Mazatec people of Oaxaca, Mexico, who refer to the mushrooms as "nti xi tho" (land's flesh) or, following Spanish colonization, "los niños" (the little ones). Mazatec curanderos (healers), most famously María Sabina, used psilocybin mushrooms in healing ceremonies called "veladas" to diagnose illness, communicate with spirits, and guide communal rituals.

Stone mushroom-shaped artifacts dating to approximately 1000 BCE have been discovered in Guatemala, and the Aztec/Nahua people referred to psychoactive mushrooms as "teonanácatl" — "flesh of the gods." The use of these mushrooms was suppressed during the Spanish colonial period, and the traditions survived primarily in remote highland communities.

### Modern Discovery

The Western discovery of psilocybin mushrooms is attributed to R. Gordon Wasson, a J.P. Morgan vice president and amateur mycologist who, in 1955, participated in a Mazatec velada ceremony led by María Sabina. Wasson's account, published in Life magazine in 1957 under the title "Seeking the Magic Mushroom," introduced psilocybin mushrooms to Western popular culture and scientific attention.

In 1958, Swiss chemist Albert Hofmann — the same scientist who first synthesized LSD — isolated and identified psilocybin and psilocin from Psilocybe mexicana specimens collected by Wasson. Hofmann subsequently synthesized both compounds, enabling controlled clinical research throughout the 1960s.

### Research Suppression and Renaissance

The Controlled Substances Act of 1970 classified psilocybin as a Schedule I substance, effectively halting all legal research for nearly three decades. Research slowly resumed in the 1990s with studies at the University of Zurich, and the modern renaissance began in 2000 when Roland Griffiths at Johns Hopkins received regulatory approval to conduct the first human psilocybin study in decades. The results, published in 2006, demonstrated that psilocybin could produce sustained positive changes in attitudes, mood, and behavior, catalyzing a wave of new research.

## Pharmacology

### Mechanism of Action

Psilocybin's primary mechanism is partial agonism at the serotonin 5-HT₂A receptor (Ki ≈ 6-20 nM), located predominantly in the cerebral cortex, particularly in layer V pyramidal neurons. This activation leads to:

- **Increased cortical entropy:** Reduced default mode network (DMN) connectivity, associated with the dissolution of the ego and increased cognitive flexibility
- **Glutamate release:** 5-HT₂A activation on cortical pyramidal neurons triggers glutamatergic signaling cascades that promote neuroplasticity
- **BDNF upregulation:** Brain-derived neurotrophic factor expression increases, facilitating synaptic growth and remodeling
- **Amygdala modulation:** Reduced amygdala reactivity to negative emotional stimuli, contributing to antidepressant and anxiolytic effects

### Pharmacokinetics

- **Absorption:** Orally absorbed; onset of effects in 20-60 minutes
- **Metabolism:** Rapidly dephosphorylated to psilocin by alkaline phosphatase; psilocin is further metabolized by monoamine oxidase (MAO) to 4-hydroxyindole-3-acetic acid (4-HIAA)
- **Distribution:** Crosses the blood-brain barrier readily; peak plasma concentrations at 80-100 minutes
- **Elimination:** Renal excretion; half-life of psilocin is approximately 2-3 hours; total duration of subjective effects is 4-6 hours
- **Active doses:** Microdose (0.1-0.3g dried mushrooms), moderate (1-2.5g), strong (2.5-5g), heroic (5g+)

## Uses and Applications

### Clinical Therapeutics

Modern clinical trials have demonstrated efficacy for:

- **Treatment-resistant depression:** A 2021 randomized controlled trial (COMPASS Pathways) showed that a single 25mg dose of synthetic psilocybin produced rapid and sustained antidepressant effects, with 37% of patients in remission at 3 weeks
- **End-of-life anxiety:** Studies in patients with terminal cancer diagnoses showed significant reductions in depression and anxiety lasting 6+ months after a single session
- **Substance use disorders:** Pilot studies have shown promise for alcohol use disorder, tobacco smoking cessation, and cocaine dependence
- **Obsessive-compulsive disorder:** Early trials indicate rapid reduction in OCD symptoms following psilocybin administration

### Microdosing

A popular but scientifically debated practice involving the regular consumption of sub-perceptual doses (0.1-0.3g dried mushrooms or 10-20 micrograms of synthetic psilocybin) every 3-4 days. Anecdotal reports include improved mood, focus, creativity, and energy, though rigorous placebo-controlled trials have produced mixed results.

### Neuroscience Research

Psilocybin has become an important tool for studying consciousness, brain connectivity, and the neurobiology of psychiatric disorders. Neuroimaging studies have revealed that the psychedelic state is associated with increased global brain connectivity and decreased DMN integrity, providing a neurobiological model for understanding altered states of consciousness.

## Safety and Precautions

- **Psychological risks:** Psilocybin can induce challenging psychological experiences ("bad trips") characterized by anxiety, paranoia, and confusion. These are most common in unprepared individuals, those with personal or family history of psychosis, or in uncontrolled settings
- **Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder (HPPD):** Rare condition involving persistent visual disturbances following psychedelic use; prevalence estimated at 1-4% of regular users
- **Contraindications:** Individuals with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or other psychotic spectrum conditions should avoid psilocybin due to risk of precipitating psychotic episodes
- **Serotonin syndrome risk:** Theoretical risk when combined with serotonergic medications (SSRIs, MAOIs, triptans); current clinical guidance typically requires a washout period from SSRIs before psilocybin administration
- **Cardiovascular effects:** Mild increases in heart rate and blood pressure; caution in individuals with cardiovascular disease
- **Legal status:** Schedule I in the United States; Class A in the UK; illegal in most countries, though decriminalization and regulated therapeutic use frameworks are expanding
- **Set and setting:** Therapeutic outcomes are heavily dependent on the psychological preparation (set) and physical/social environment (setting); clinical protocols include extensive screening, preparation sessions, and integration support
