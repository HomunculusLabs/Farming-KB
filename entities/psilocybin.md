# Psilocybin

## Overview

**Psilocybin** (4-PO-DMT, O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) is a naturally occurring psychedelic prodrug and the principal psychoactive compound found in over 200 species of mushrooms in the genus *Psilocybe* and related genera. After ingestion, psilocybin is rapidly dephosphorylated by alkaline phosphatase enzymes into **psilocin** (4-HO-DMT), the pharmacologically active metabolite. Psilocybin acts primarily as a serotonin 5-HT₂A receptor agonist, producing profound alterations in perception, cognition, and emotion. After decades of research suppression, it has re-emerged as one of the most promising compounds in modern psychiatric medicine.

## Pharmacology

### Mechanism of Action

Psilocybin itself is pharmacologically inert; it serves as a prodrug that is converted to psilocin by esterases and alkaline phosphatases in the digestive tract, liver, and blood. Psilocin is the active compound:

- **5-HT₂A receptor agonism:** Primary mechanism. Partial agonist at cortical 5-HT₂A receptors, particularly in layer V pyramidal neurons of the prefrontal cortex. This activation is responsible for the characteristic psychedelic effects.
- **5-HT₁A receptor agonism:** Contributes to anxiolytic and prosocial effects.
- **5-HT₂C receptor agonism:** Modulates mood and anxiety responses.
- **Serotonin transporter (SERT) inhibition:** Psilocin is a weak SERT inhibitor, modestly increasing extracellular serotonin levels.

### Pharmacokinetics

- **Onset:** 20–60 minutes after oral ingestion
- **Peak plasma concentration:** 80–120 minutes
- **Half-life:** 2–5 hours (psilocin); 50 minutes (psilocybin)
- **Duration of effects:** 4–6 hours (typical); up to 8 hours at high doses
- **Metabolism:** De-phosphorylation to psilocin, then hepatic glucuronidation to psilocin-O-glucuronide
- **Excretion:** Primarily renal (urine); ~65% excreted within 24 hours

### Dose Ranges

- **Microdose:** 0.1–0.3 g dried mushroom equivalent (sub-perceptual)
- **Low:** 0.5–1.5 g (~2–10 mg psilocybin)
- **Moderate:** 1.5–3.5 g (~10–25 mg psilocybin)
- **High:** 3.5–5+ g (~25–35+ mg psilocybin)

## Natural Occurrence

Psilocybin is produced as a secondary metabolite by fungi in at least 14 genera:

### Primary Genera

- ***Psilocybe:*** Over 180 species; *P. cubensis*, *P. semilanceata*, *P. cyanescens*, *P. azurescens*, *P. mexicana*, *P. tampanensis* (truffle-forming)
- ***Panaeolus:*** *P. cyanescens* (copelandia), *P. cinctulus*
- ***Gymnopilus:*** *G. spectabilis* (big laughing gym)
- ***Inocybe:*** Several species (difficult to identify — dangerous genus)
- ***Pluteus:*** *P. salicinus*
- ***Conocybe:*** Several small, fragile species

### Biosynthesis

Psilocybin biosynthesis follows the tryptamine pathway:
1. L-Tryptophan → Tryptamine (via decarboxylation)
2. Tryptamine → 4-Hydroxytryptamine (via hydroxylation, PsiH enzyme)
3. 4-Hydroxytryptamine → 4-Hydroxytryptamine dimethyltransferase → Norbaeocystin → Baeocystin → Psilocybin

The biosynthetic gene cluster was fully characterized by Fricke et al. (2017), identifying four key enzymes: PsiD, PsiH, PsiK, and PsiM.

## History

### Indigenous Use

Archaeological evidence suggests psilocybin mushroom use dates back at least 6,000–9,000 years in Mesoamerica. The Aztecs called them **teonanácatl** ("flesh of the gods") and used them in religious and divinatory ceremonies. The Mazatec people of Oaxaca, Mexico, maintain an unbroken tradition of using *Psilocybe* species in healing rituals, a practice continued into the present by curanderos like María Sabina.

### Western Discovery and Research

- **1939:** Richard Schultes identifies *P. caerulescens* in Oaxaca and sends specimens to Harvard.
- **1957:** R. Gordon Wasson publishes "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" in *LIFE* magazine, sparking Western interest.
- **1958:** Albert Hofmann isolates and synthesizes psilocybin and psilocin at Sandoz Laboratories.
- **1959–1965:** Sandoz distributes psilocybin as "Indocybin" for clinical research worldwide.
- **1960s:** Over 1,000 clinical papers published on psilocybin; used in psychotherapy, alcoholism treatment, and end-of-life anxiety.
- **1970:** Controlled Substances Act places psilocybin in Schedule I in the United States; research effectively halted.
- **2000s:** Research renaissance begins at Johns Hopkins, Imperial College London, and NYU.

## Modern Clinical Research

### Treatment-Resistant Depression

The most advanced area of clinical investigation. Key studies:

- **Carhart-Harris et al. (2016, 2017, 2021):** Imperial College London demonstrated rapid and sustained antidepressant effects. A 2021 Phase II trial showed 70% of patients achieved remission at 3 weeks after two doses of 25 mg psilocybin with psychological support.

- **Davis et al. (2021):** Johns Hopkins study showed psilocybin was 4x more effective than escitalopram in a randomized trial for major depressive disorder (though the study design has been debated).

- **COMPASS Pathways Phase IIb (2022):** Largest psilocybin trial to date (n=233). Single 25 mg dose showed significant reduction in depression scores at week 3, with effects sustained at week 12.

### End-of-Life Anxiety

Griffiths et al. (2016) at Johns Hopkins demonstrated that a single high-dose psilocybin session produced significant and sustained decreases in depression and anxiety in patients with life-threatening cancer diagnoses, with effects lasting 6+ months.

### Substance Use Disorders

- **Tobacco addiction:** A Johns Hopkins open-label study (2014) showed 80% abstinence at 6 months — dramatically higher than conventional treatments (~35%).
- **Alcohol use disorder:** Bogenschutz et al. (2022) Phase II trial showed significant reduction in heavy drinking days following psilocybin treatment.

### Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder

An early pilot study (Moreno et al., 2006) showed acute reductions in OCD symptoms. Larger trials are underway.

## Neuroscience of Psychedelic States

### Default Mode Network (DMN) Disruption

Psilocybin decreases connectivity within the default mode network — the brain system associated with self-referential thought, rumination, and the sense of ego. This "ego dissolution" is correlated with therapeutic outcomes. Increased global brain connectivity and cross-network communication during the psychedelic state are hypothesized to enable rigid mental patterns to be reorganized.

### Neuroplasticity

Psilocybin promotes structural and functional neuroplasticity:
- Increases dendritic spine density in the prefrontal cortex (animal studies)
- Upregulates BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) expression
- Activates the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway
- These effects may underlie the compound's ability to produce lasting psychological changes from a single or few administrations

## Legal Status

- **United States:** Schedule I (DEA); Oregon and Colorado have legalized therapeutic use; multiple cities have deprioritized enforcement
- **Canada:** Allowed for end-of-life use under special access (2022); expanded to treatment-resistant depression (2024)
- **Netherlands:** Fresh mushrooms technically legal (truffles are sold openly in smart shops)
- **Brazil:** Not scheduled; legal for religious use (Santo Daime, União do Vegetal)
- **Australia:** Rescheduled as controlled drug for psychiatric use by authorized psychiatrists (2023)
- **Jamaica:** Unregulated; several retreat centers operate legally
- **EU:** Generally Schedule I; clinical trials ongoing in UK, Germany, Netherlands, Switzerland

## Risk Profile

- **Toxicity:** Very low physiological toxicity; no confirmed human fatalities from psilocybin alone. LD50 in mice: ~280 mg/kg (intravenous).
- **Psychological risks:** Acute anxiety, panic, paranoia possible, especially in unsupported settings. Rare persistent perceptual changes (HPPD). Contraindicated in schizophrenia spectrum disorders and bipolar I disorder.
- **Physiological risks:** Mild nausea (common), transient hypertension, pupil dilation. No evidence of addiction or dependence.
- **Drug interactions:** Dangerous when combined with MAOIs, lithium, or tramadol. Serotonin syndrome risk with SSRIs (theoretical, rarely reported).
