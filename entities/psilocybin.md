# Psilocybin

Psilocybin (4-PO-DMT; O-phosphoryl-4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) is a naturally occurring prodrug psychedelic compound of the tryptamine class. It is the primary psychoactive constituent found in over 200 species of fungi, predominantly within the genus *Psilocybe*. Upon ingestion, psilocybin is rapidly dephosphorylated by alkaline phosphatase enzymes into its pharmacologically active metabolite, **psilocin** (4-HO-DMT), which is responsible for the characteristic psychedelic effects.

## Chemical Structure

Psilocybin is a tryptamine derivative with the molecular formula C₁₂H₁₇N₂O₄P. Structurally, it consists of a dimethylated ethylamine side chain attached to an indole ring, with a phosphate ester group at the 4-position of the indole — distinguishing it from its close relative DMT (N,N-dimethyltryptamine). This phosphate group renders psilocybin more water-soluble but pharmacologically inactive until enzymatic cleavage. The molecule is relatively unstable in the presence of heat, light, and alkaline conditions.

## Pharmacology

### Receptor Binding Profile

Psilocybin (via psilocin) acts primarily as a **5-HT₂A receptor agonist** — the mechanism considered central to its psychedelic effects. However, its receptor pharmacology is notably broader:

| Receptor | Affinity (Ki) | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 5-HT₂A | ~6–12 nM | Full agonist — primary psychedelic mechanism |
| 5-HT₂C | ~15–25 nM | Agonist — modulates mood and anxiety |
| 5-HT₁A | ~50–100 nM | Partial agonist — anxiolytic contribution |
| 5-HT₂B | ~30–50 nM | Agonist |
| Sigma-1 | ~50 nM | Agonist — neuroprotective effects |

Activation of cortical 5-HT₂A receptors, particularly in layer V pyramidal neurons of the prefrontal cortex, leads to increased cortical excitability, disruption of default mode network (DMN) connectivity, and global increases in entropy in brain activity patterns measured by fMRI. The temporary dissolution of DMN dominance is strongly correlated with the subjective "ego dissolution" or "mystical-type" experiences reported by users.

## Metabolism

Psilocybin is not active in its ingested form. After oral administration, it undergoes rapid **first-pass dephosphorylation** by alkaline phosphatases in the intestinal mucosa and hepatic tissue, converting to psilocin within 20–40 minutes. Psilocin is further metabolized primarily by monoamine oxidase (MAO-A) to yield 4-hydroxyindole-3-acetaldehyde, which is oxidized to 4-hydroxyindole-3-acetic acid (4-HIAA) — the primary urinary metabolite. A smaller fraction undergoes glucuronidation. The elimination half-life of psilocin is approximately 2–3 hours, with subjective effects lasting 4–7 hours depending on dosage.

## Natural Sources

Psilocybin is produced biosynthetically by a wide range of gilled mushrooms. The most well-known genera include:

- ***Psilocybe*** — the largest and most widely distributed genus, including *P. cubensis*, *P. semilanceata* (liberty caps), *P. cyanescens*, and *P. azurescens* (among the most potent)
- ***Panaeolus*** — *P. cyanescens* (copelandia)
- ***Gymnopilus***, ***Pluteus***, ***Inocybe*** — less commonly consumed species

Over **200 species** are currently known to contain psilocybin and/or psilocin, found across every inhabited continent. The biosynthetic pathway involves the enzymes PsiD, PsiK, and PsiM, which convert tryptophan → tryptamine → 4-hydroxytryptamine → baeocystin → norbaeocystin → psilocybin. Concentrations vary dramatically by species, strain, growing conditions, and developmental stage, typically ranging from 0.1% to 2.0% dry weight.

## Effects

### Psychological & Perceptual

- Visual distortions: enhanced colors, geometric patterns (form constants), halos, synesthesia
- Altered sense of time — time dilation is one of the most reliably reported effects
- Ego dissolution and mystical-type experiences (highly dose-dependent)
- Enhanced introspection, emotional catharsis, and novel philosophical insights
- Increased suggestibility and openness to experience

### Emotional

Psilocybin commonly produces profound emotional responses — both positive (awe, gratitude, connection, joy) and challenging (anxiety, grief, existential confrontation). The "challenging experience" is increasingly understood as therapeutically valuable when integrated with appropriate psychological support. Set (mindset) and setting (environment) are the strongest predictors of experience quality.

## Therapeutic Potential

Clinical research into psilocybin-assisted therapy has accelerated significantly since 2010, with multiple Phase II trials completed and Phase III trials underway as of 2026.

### Treatment-Resistant Depression

The most extensively studied application. Carhart-Harris et al. (2016) demonstrated significant antidepressant effects persisting 3–6 months post-treatment in TRD patients. Goodwin et al. (2022) in a Phase IIb trial (COMPASS Pathways) found that a single 25 mg dose produced rapid, sustained reduction in depressive symptoms compared to 1 mg control. COMPASS has advanced to Phase III trials for TRD under the compound "COMP360."

### Anxiety & Depression in Life-Threatening Illness

Ross et al. (2016) at NYU Langone showed that a single psilocybin session produced rapid and sustained reductions in anxiety and depression in patients with life-threatening cancer diagnoses, with effects persisting at 6-month and 4.5-year follow-ups.

### Addiction

- **Alcohol use disorder**: Bogenschutz et al. (2022) demonstrated that psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy significantly reduced heavy drinking days compared to placebo in a randomized controlled trial.
- **Tobacco smoking**: Johnson et al. (2014) found that 80% of participants achieved abstinence at 6-month follow-up — a remarkably high success rate compared to conventional treatments.

### PTSD & Cluster Headaches

Early-phase research and anecdotal reports suggest potential efficacy for PTSD (emotional processing facilitated by reduced DMN activity and enhanced neuroplasticity) and cluster headache interruption, though controlled trials are still needed as of 2026.

## Dosage Ranges

| Dose Level | Dry Mushroom Equivalent | Psilocybin (mg) | Effect Profile |
|---|---|---|---|
| Microdose | 0.1–0.3 g | 0.1–0.3 | Sub-perceptual; no overt psychedelic effects |
| Low | 0.5–1.5 g | 1–5 | Mild perceptual enhancement, mood elevation |
| Moderate | 1.5–3.5 g | 5–15 | Full psychedelic experience; moderate visual effects |
| High | 3.5–5.0 g | 15–25 | Intense psychedelic experience; potential ego dissolution |
| Heroic | 5.0 g+ | 25 mg+ | Overwhelming; mystical-type experiences; high risk without supervision |

**Microdosing** — the practice of consuming sub-threshold doses on a regular schedule (typically every 3 days) — has gained popularity since ~2015. While user self-reports cite benefits for mood, focus, and creativity, controlled clinical evidence remains mixed and inconclusive. A 2021 double-blind study by Szigeti et al. found no significant difference between microdose and placebo on psychological outcomes, though methodological limitations were noted.

## Safety Profile

### Toxicity

Psilocybin has an exceptionally high **therapeutic index** — the effective dose is far below the toxic dose. The estimated LD₅₀ in rats is ~280 mg/kg (intravenous), translating to an impossibly high human lethal dose. No confirmed human fatalities have been attributed to psilocybin toxicity alone. Deaths associated with psilocybin mushrooms are exclusively due to accidents, misidentification (toxic species confusion), or dangerous behavior while impaired.

### Adverse Effects

- Acute: anxiety, paranoia, nausea, confusion, tachycardia, dilated pupils
- **Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder (HPPD)**: rare but documented; involves ongoing visual disturbances (flashbacks, trailing, geometric imagery) persisting well beyond the acute intoxication period. Prevalence estimated at 1–4% among regular psychedelic users, though true HPPD is rarer than transient perceptual disturbances.

### Contraindications

- Personal or family history of psychosis or schizophrenia (may trigger psychotic episodes)
- Active manic episodes (bipolar I disorder)
- Cardiovascular conditions (5-HT₂B agonism carries theoretical cardiac valve risk)
- Pregnancy and lactation
- Concurrent use of MAOIs, lithium, or other serotonergic agents (serotonin syndrome risk)
- Antipsychotic medications (will block effects)

## Legal Status

Psilocybin is classified as a **Schedule I** controlled substance under the United Nations Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971), restricting it to research use only in most jurisdictions.

| Region | Status (2026) |
|---|---|
| United States | Schedule I (federal); decriminalized in several cities/states; Oregon and Colorado have regulated therapeutic use frameworks |
| Canada | Schedule III (as of 2023 regulatory amendment for therapeutic use); available via Special Access Programme |
| Netherlands | *Truffles* containing psilocybin are legal (fresh only); dried mushrooms banned since 2002 |
| Brazil | Not scheduled; possession and use not criminalized |
| Portugal | Decriminalized for personal use |
| Australia | Reclassified as Schedule 8 (controlled medicine) for psychiatric treatment in 2023 |
| UK | Class A |
| Germany | Schedule I (Betäubungsmittelgesetz Anlage I); pilot therapy programs underway |

## History of Use

### Indigenous & Pre-Columbian

Archaeological evidence — including mushroom-shaped stone artifacts ("mushroom stones") dating to ~500 BCE in Mesoamerica — suggests ritual use of psilocybin mushrooms among the Maya, Aztec, and other Mesoamerican cultures. The Aztec term *teonanácatl* ("flesh of the gods") referred to psilocybin mushrooms used in divinatory and healing ceremonies. The Mazatec people of Oaxaca, Mexico, maintain an unbroken tradition of ritual use to the present day, most famously through the practice of María Sabina.

### Modern Discovery

In 1957, **R. Albert Hofmann** — the Swiss chemist who first synthesized LSD — isolated and identified psilocybin and psilocin from *Psilocybe mexicana* specimens collected by R. Gordon Wasson in Mexico. Hofmann synthesized psilocybin in the laboratory and self-administered it, confirming its psychedelic effects. The Sandoz corporation subsequently marketed synthetic psilocybin as "Indocybin" for psychotherapeutic research.

### Modern Resurgence

Following decades of suppression after the Controlled Substances Act (1970), psilocybin research experienced a dramatic revival beginning ~2006 with the Johns Hopkins psilocybin research program led by Roland Griffiths. As of 2026, psilocybin is at the forefront of a psychedelic renaissance in psychiatry, with multiple organizations (COMPASS Pathways, Usona Institute, MAPS) conducting clinical trials, and several jurisdictions moving toward regulated access for therapeutic purposes.
