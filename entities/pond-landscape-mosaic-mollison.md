## Description

The Pond Landscape Mosaic is a permaculture design pattern described by Mollison for integrating aquaculture ponds into the broader landscape. It prescribes a specific proportional mix of pond, marsh, forest, and meadow components arranged to maximise mutual benefits between water and land systems. The concept emphasises that ponds should never exist in isolation but as part of a functional mosaic where each component supports the others.

## Classification

- **Type**: Landscape design pattern
- **Domain**: Permaculture, landscape architecture, integrated land management
- **Components**: Open water (pond), marsh, forest, meadow/pasture/crop
- **Scale**: Applicable from smallholdings to large properties

## Key Facts

- Recommended proportional composition for sub-humid to humid areas:
  - **15% pond** (open water)
  - **15% marsh** (contiguous with ponds)
  - **30-60% forest** (upstream, downstream, and between ponds)
  - **10-40% meadow, crop, or pasture** (remainder)
- Forest must be positioned upstream of, downstream from, and between ponds for maximum benefit.
- Marshes should be positioned upstream of and within ponds to capture nutrients and support aquatic food webs.
- Specific tree recommendations for pond-side planting:
  - **River red gums** (*Eucalyptus camaldulensis*) and some leguminous trees enhance phosphate drip through rain throughfall — belong close to waterways.
  - **Willows** (*Salix* spp.) and **Casuarina** species fix phosphates in downstream zones.
  - **Freshwater mussels** confine phosphate cycling within the pond system.
- Forest functions in the mosaic include: clean water production, waste absorption from fish systems, direct food provision (fruit), and indirect food supply (insect bodies and frass falling into ponds).
- Marsh functions include: rich habitat for birds and crustaceans, phosphate collection and recycling.
- The entire system should be linked by permanent or intermittent drains, streams, canals, and swales.
- Historical precedent: Chinese carp pond complexes integrating mulberries, ducks, and silkworms demonstrate centuries of successful mosaic design.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The pond landscape mosaic pattern has significant implications for integrated fungal cultivation:

- **Forest component and fungal habitat**: The 30-60% forest component provides ideal habitat for woodland mushroom cultivation — shiitake on logs, lion's mane on standing timber, and ground-dwelling species in the forest floor.
- **Nutrient cycling through fungi**: Fungal decomposers in the forest component break down leaf litter and woody material, releasing nutrients that wash into ponds and support aquatic food webs.
- **Mycoremediation at pond margins**: The marsh component can be enhanced with mycoremediation filters — mushroom mycelium beds that process excess nutrients and potential contaminants before water enters or exits pond systems.
- **Integrated substrate production**: Forest management for the mosaic produces prunings, chips, and sawdust that serve as mushroom substrate, closing the loop between pond landscape maintenance and fungal cultivation.
- **Casuarina and mycorrhizae**: Casuarina species form both ectomycorrhizal and actinorhizal associations, supporting fungal diversity in the pond landscape mosaic.
- **Riparian fungal communities**: The drain and canal network connecting mosaic components supports unique riparian fungal communities that process organic matter in the transitional zone between land and water.
