# Piper methysticum

**Piper methysticum** G. Forst. is a perennial shrub in the family Piperaceae, native to the western Pacific islands. It is commonly known as **kava** (also *kava-kava*, *awa* in Hawaii, *yaqona* or *yangona* in Fiji, *sakau* in Pohnpei, and *malok* or *malogu* in Vanuatu). The plant is cultivated for its rhizomes, which contain a group of psychoactive compounds called **kavalactones** that produce sedative, anxiolytic, and mild euphoric effects. Kava has been used for centuries in Pacific Island cultures as a ceremonial beverage, social drink, and traditional medicine, making it one of the oldest continuously used psychoactive plant preparations in human history.

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

*Piper methysticum* belongs to the genus *Piper*, which comprises approximately 2,000 species of tropical plants distributed worldwide. The family Piperaceae also includes black pepper (*Piper nigrum*) and betel leaf (*Piper betle*). The species name *methysticum* derives from the Greek *methustikos* (intoxicating), referring to the plant's psychoactive properties.

The word "kava" or "kawa" has cognates across Austronesian languages and is believed to derive from Proto-Oceanic *kawaR, which may itself be related to the Proto-Austronesian root for "bitter." The same root gives rise to the word for *Piper methysticum* in most Pacific Island languages, as well as the word "kava" used in Sanskrit and Avestan for a different bitter plant preparation, suggesting deep linguistic connections.

## Botany

*Piper methysticum* is a slow-growing, bushy shrub that typically reaches 2 to 4 meters in height, though some cultivars can grow taller under ideal conditions. The plant has thick, knotty, woody stems arising from a large underground rhizome system. The stems are green when young, becoming brown and woody with age, and are slightly swollen at the nodes.

The leaves are large, heart-shaped (cordate), 10 to 25 cm long and 8 to 20 cm wide, with a long petiole (5 to 15 cm). They are dark green, glossy on the upper surface, and lighter green beneath, with 5 to 7 prominent basal veins radiating from the point of petiole attachment. The leaf margins are entire (smooth). The leaves are arranged alternately along the stems and are slightly leathery in texture. The inflorescence is a cylindrical spike 3 to 8 cm long, bearing small, densely packed, greenish-white flowers.

The most important part of the plant is the underground rhizome (commonly referred to as the "root" or "kava root" in commercial contexts, though botanically it is a rhizome). The rhizome system consists of multiple thick, fleshy, tuberous segments that can reach 10 to 25 cm in diameter. Fresh kava rhizome has a pungent, earthy, somewhat peppery aroma. When dried, the rhizome is hard, fibrous, and yellowish to brownish in color. The interior of the rhizome is white to cream-colored when fresh.

*Piper methysticum* is a sterile cultivar — it does not produce viable seeds and is propagated exclusively through vegetative means (stem cuttings, typically 15 to 25 cm sections planted at a 45-degree angle). It is believed to have been domesticated from the wild species *Piper wichmannii* (also known as *P. subbullatum*) through centuries of selection by Pacific Island peoples. The sterility of cultivated kava is considered evidence of its long history of domestication, as sterility typically arises from polyploidy induced during cultivation.

## Distribution and Habitat

Kava is cultivated throughout the western Pacific, with significant cultivation in the following regions:

- **Vanuatu**: Considered the center of kava genetic diversity, with over 80 recognized cultivars. Kava is deeply embedded in Vanuatu's cultural, social, and economic life.
- **Fiji**: Kava (*yaqona*) is the national drink and a cornerstone of Fijian social and ceremonial life.
- **Samoa**: Kava (*'ava*) plays important roles in chiefly ceremonies and village governance.
- **Tonga**: Kava (*kava*) is used in both ceremonial and informal social contexts.
- **Pohnpei** (Federated States of Micronesia): Kava (*sakau*) is central to traditional ceremonial life and is prepared using distinctive methods involving stone pounding.
- **Hawaii**: Kava (*'awa*) was used in religious ceremonies and healing practices in pre-contact Hawaii. Cultivation declined after European contact but has experienced a revival.
- **Papua New Guinea**: Kava is cultivated and used in some coastal and island communities.

The plant requires tropical conditions with temperatures between 20°C and 30°C, high humidity, and rainfall of 1,500 to 3,000 mm annually. It grows best in well-draining, fertile volcanic soils at elevations up to 600 meters. Kava is typically intercropped with food plants such as taro, sweet potato, and bananas.

## Chemical Composition

The psychoactive constituents of kava are concentrated in the rhizome and consist of a group of lipid-soluble compounds called **kavalactones** (also known as kavapyrones). At least 18 kavalactones have been identified, with six constituting approximately 95% of the total lactone content:

### The Six Major Kavalactones

- **Kavain** — the most abundant kavalactone in noble cultivars, typically 15% to 30% of total lactones. Considered primarily responsible for the anxiolytic and mild euphoric effects. Has been shown to modulate GABA-A receptor activity and voltage-gated sodium and calcium channels.
- **Dihydrokavain (DHK)** — a reduced form of kavain, typically 10% to 20% of total lactones. Contributes sedative and muscle-relaxant properties.
- **Methysticin** — typically 10% to 20% of total lactones. Contributes to the overall psychoactive profile and may have anti-convulsant properties.
- **Dihydromethysticin (DHM)** — typically 5% to 15% of total lactones. May contribute to hepatotoxicity concerns when present at high levels.
- **Yangonin** — typically 5% to 15% of total lactones. Notable for mild cannabinoid receptor (CB1) binding activity, which may contribute to the subjective effects.
- **Desmethoxyyangonin (DMY)** — typically 10% to 20% of total lactones. The most potent MAO-B inhibitor among the kavalactones, potentially contributing to mood-elevating effects.

Total kavalactone content in dried rhizomes typically ranges from 3% to 20%, depending on the cultivar, growing conditions, age of the plant (older plants generally have higher lactone content), and preparation method. Plants harvested at 3 to 5 years of age generally produce the highest quality rhizomes.

### Chemotype Classification

Different kava cultivars are classified by their **chemotype**, which describes the relative proportions of the six major kavalactones using a numerical coding system (e.g., "425631" indicating decreasing proportions of kavain, dihydrokavain, yangonin, desmethoxyyangonin, methysticin, and dihydromethysticin). The "noble" chemotypes are characterized by high kavain content and low dihydromethysticin content. "Tudei" (two-day) cultivars, which produce prolonged and sometimes unpleasant effects, have higher proportions of dihydromethysticin and dihydrokavain.

## Psychoactive Effects

Kava produces a characteristic psychoactive profile that is distinct from other sedative substances and contributes to its unique cultural role:

- **Onset**: 15 to 30 minutes after consumption of a traditional preparation
- **Duration**: 1 to 3 hours, depending on dose, preparation, and individual sensitivity
- **Physical effects**: Relaxation of skeletal muscles (particularly neck and shoulder tension), mild numbness or tingling of the mouth, tongue, and throat (the traditional indicator of potency, called *kava* in some languages, meaning "bitter"), pupil dilation at higher doses, and a sense of physical heaviness, warmth, or floating
- **Psychological effects**: Anxiety reduction (the most consistently documented effect), mild euphoria, increased sociability and talkativeness, mental calmness and clarity, and a state described as "relaxed but alert" or "peaceful but awake." Unlike many sedatives, kava typically does not impair cognitive function or motor coordination at moderate doses.

The subjective experience is often described as a pleasant, peaceful state of relaxation without significant impairment of mental clarity, judgment, or motor function. This profile distinguishes kava from alcohol, benzodiazepines, and other CNS depressants. Users remain conversant and aware, which is essential to its role as a social and ceremonial beverage.

## Traditional and Cultural Use

### Ceremonial Context

Kava occupies a central position in the social, political, and religious life of Pacific Island cultures across the western Pacific:

- **Fiji**: The *yaqona* ceremony (*sevusevu*) is performed to welcome guests, mark important occasions, settle disputes, and open formal gatherings. The ceremony follows strict protocols for preparation, serving order, and speech-making. Kava is consumed in virtually every social context in Fiji.
- **Vanuatu**: Kava bars (*nakamals*) serve as community gathering places where kava is consumed daily. Traditional *kastom* ceremonies involving kava mark life events, resolve conflicts, and maintain social cohesion. Vanuatu recognizes kava as a national cultural treasure.
- **Samoa**: Kava (*'ava*) ceremonies accompany chiefly councils (*fono*), formal receptions, and important village decisions. The *ava* ceremony follows elaborate protocols with designated roles for preparation and serving.
- **Pohnpei**: The *sakau* ceremony is among the most elaborate kava rituals in the Pacific, involving stone pounding of fresh kava roots, ritual serving, and formal oratory. The ceremony can last several hours and is performed to honor guests, mark achievements, and conduct village business.

### Preparation Methods

Traditional preparation involves grinding or pounding fresh or dried kava root, mixing it with cold water, and kneading or squeezing the mixture to extract the kavalactones. The resulting beverage is opaque, brownish-gray, and has a distinctly earthy, peppery, slightly bitter taste. In Hawaii, fresh kava was traditionally chewed and spat into a bowl to begin extraction (the saliva enzymes were believed to enhance potency). In Pohnpei, fresh kava is pounded on a basalt stone and mixed with water from hand-squeezed hibiscus bark. Modern preparations often use dried, powdered root mixed with water or coconut milk.

### Historical Spread

Kava cultivation spread across the Pacific with Austronesian migrations beginning approximately 3,000 years ago. It is absent from Polynesia east of Hawaii, likely because the voyaging canoes carrying it did not reach those islands (New Zealand, Rapa Nui, and the Society Islands). Captain James Cook was among the first Europeans to document kava use during his voyages to the Pacific in the 1760s and 1770s. His naturalists, Joseph Banks and Daniel Solander, provided early botanical descriptions of the plant.

## Modern Use and Research

Kava has gained significant popularity worldwide as a natural anxiolytic and sleep aid since the 1990s. Clinical studies, including several randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses, have demonstrated efficacy for generalized anxiety disorder comparable to benzodiazepines and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), without the dependency risk, significant cognitive impairment, or tolerance development associated with those medications.

Kava extracts are sold as dietary supplements in many countries, standardized to 30% or 70% kavalactone content. Products include capsules, tablets, tinctures, and dried root powder. The German Commission E (a regulatory body for herbal medicines) approved kava preparations for anxiety, stress, and restlessness before the hepatotoxicity concerns emerged.

## Hepatotoxicity Concerns

Since the early 2000s, kava has been associated with rare but serious cases of hepatotoxicity (liver damage), including several cases requiring liver transplantation and a small number of fatalities. This led to bans or restrictions in several European countries, Australia, and Canada between 2002 and 2005.

Subsequent research and investigation have identified several likely contributing factors:

- **Non-noble cultivars**: "Tudei" (two-day) cultivars with high dihydromethysticin content are more likely to cause adverse effects
- **Plant part quality**: Use of aerial parts (stems and leaves) instead of root material; stems and leaves contain different alkaloid profiles with higher concentrations of potentially hepatotoxic pipermethystine
- **Extraction methods**: Acetone or ethanol extraction of aerial parts may concentrate harmful compounds not present in traditional water-based root preparations
- **Pre-existing conditions**: Users with pre-existing liver conditions or concurrent use of hepatotoxic medications may be at increased risk
- **Adulteration**: Some commercial products may have been adulterated with non-kava plant material or improperly processed

Noble kava cultivars prepared traditionally from root material only have not been convincingly linked to liver damage in the extensive ethnobotanical literature covering centuries of use. Several countries, including Germany (2015), have subsequently lifted or relaxed their restrictions on noble kava preparations.

## Legal Status

Kava's legal status varies significantly by country:

- **Pacific Islands**: Legal and culturally integral. Fiji, Vanuatu, Samoa, and other Pacific nations actively promote kava cultivation and export as part of their cultural heritage and economic development.
- **United States**: Legal as a dietary supplement. The FDA issued a consumer advisory in 2002 regarding potential hepatotoxicity but did not ban the product. Kava remains widely available.
- **European Union**: Varies by member state. Germany lifted its ban in 2015, allowing noble kava preparations. The UK, France, and some other nations maintain restrictions.
- **Australia**: Reclassified in 2019 from prescription-only (Schedule 4, imposed in 2007) to a permitted import category, allowing personal use. Commercial sale restrictions were eased.
- **Canada**: Restricted following safety advisories but available through some natural health product channels.
