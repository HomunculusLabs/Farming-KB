# Quercetin

**Quercetin** (3,3',4',5,7-pentahydroxyflavone) is a plant-derived flavonoid polyphenol and one of the most abundant and extensively studied bioactive compounds in the human diet. A potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory agent, quercetin is found in high concentrations in capers, red onions, kale, berries, apples, tea, and red wine. Beyond its dietary presence, quercetin has attracted intense research interest for its senolytic properties (ability to selectively kill senescent cells), exercise performance benefits, antiviral activity, and potential in preventing and treating chronic diseases including cardiovascular disease, neurodegenerative conditions, and cancer.

## Chemical Identity

- **IUPAC Name:** 2-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-3,5,7-trihydroxy-4H-chromen-4-one
- **Molecular Formula:** C15H10O7
- **Molecular Weight:** 302.24 g/mol
- **CAS Number:** 117-39-5
- **Appearance:** Yellow-green crystalline powder
- **Solubility:** Poorly soluble in water (< 0.01 mg/mL); soluble in ethanol, DMSO, and alkaline aqueous solutions
- **Melting Point:** 314-316°C (decomposes)
- **Structure:** Flavonol subclass of flavonoids, characterized by a 3-hydroxyflavone backbone with hydroxyl groups at the 3, 5, 7, 3', and 4' positions. The catechol group (3',4'-dihydroxy) on the B-ring and the 2,3-double bond conjugated with the 4-oxo group are critical for biological activity.

## Natural Sources

Quercetin is ubiquitously distributed in the plant kingdom. Major dietary sources (per 100 g fresh weight):

- **Capers:** ~234 mg (highest known natural source)
- **Red onion:** ~32-45 mg (outer rings contain more than inner)
- **Kale:** ~23 mg
- **Cherry tomato:** ~22 mg
- **Blueberry:** ~8-15 mg
- **Black plum:** ~12 mg
- **Apple (with skin):** ~4-5 mg (concentrated in the peel)
- **Green tea:** ~2-5 mg per cup
- **Red wine:** ~2-5 mg per glass

In plants, quercetin rarely occurs as the free aglycone. It is predominantly found as glycosides conjugated to sugars: quercetin-3-O-glucoside (isoquercitrin), quercetin-3-O-rutinoside (rutin), quercetin-3-O-galactoside (hyperoside), and quercetin-4'-O-glucoside (spiraeoside). onions are unusual in containing predominantly the glucoside rather than the rutinoside, contributing to their higher bioavailability. The glycoside form affects absorption, metabolism, and bioactivity.

## Pharmacokinetics and Bioavailability

Quercetin's bioavailability is a major research focus due to its generally low oral absorption. Key pharmacokinetic characteristics:

**Absorption:** Quercetin glycosides are absorbed in the small intestine via sodium-dependent glucose transporters (SGLT1). The aglycone is absorbed via passive diffusion in the colon after microbial deglycosylation. Quercetin glucosides (as in onions) show ~2-fold higher bioavailability than rutinosides (as in tea), because the latter require initial hydrolysis by intestinal β-glucosidases.

**Metabolism:** Extensive first-pass metabolism in the intestine and liver via glucuronidation, sulfation, and methylation. Major circulating metabolites include quercetin-3-O-glucuronide, quercetin-3'-O-sulfate, and isorhamnetin-3-O-glucuronide (3'-O-methylquercetin). Plasma concentrations after a 500 mg oral dose of quercetin aglycone reach approximately 1-5 μM Cmax within 2-6 hours.

**Distribution:** Quercetin and metabolites distribute to most tissues, with highest concentrations in the lungs, liver, and kidneys. Brain penetration is limited but detectable, suggesting potential neuroprotective effects.

**Excretion:** Primarily via feces (60-80%) and urine (5-10%) within 24-72 hours. Elimination half-life is approximately 11-28 hours.

**Formulation strategies** to improve bioavailability include phytosomal complexes (quercetin phosphatidylcholine), liposomal delivery, nanoemulsions, and co-administration with bromelain or [[vitamin C]] (ascorbic acid). Phytosomal formulations show 5-20× improved absorption.

## Pharmacological Activities

**Antioxidant activity:** Quercetin is a potent direct free radical scavenger (ORAC value ~5,800 μmol TE/g) and indirect antioxidant through Nrf2/ARE pathway activation, upregulation of endogenous antioxidant enzymes (SOD, catalase, glutathione peroxidase, heme oxygenase-1), and chelation of transition metal ions (iron, copper) that catalyze Fenton reactions.

**Anti-inflammatory activity:** Inhibits NF-κB activation, reduces TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and COX-2 expression. Suppresses MAPK and JAK/STAT signaling cascades. Inhibits phospholipase A2 and lipoxygenase, reducing leukotriene and prostaglandin production.

**Senolytic activity:** Quercetin was one of the first identified senolytic compounds. In combination with dasatinib (the "D+Q" protocol), it selectively induces apoptosis in senescent cells by inhibiting anti-apoptotic pathways (PI3K/Akt, Bcl-xL, p21). The landmark 2015 Nature Medicine paper by Xu et al. demonstrated that D+Q treatment extended healthspan and lifespan in progeroid mice. Human trials are ongoing for age-related conditions.

**Antiviral activity:** Quercetin inhibits viral entry, replication, and protease activity for multiple viruses including influenza, SARS-CoV-2, rhinovirus, hepatitis C, and dengue. Mechanisms include inhibition of viral polymerases, blocking of viral attachment to host cell receptors, and modulation of host antiviral responses. A 2021 clinical trial showed reduced severity and duration of COVID-19 with quercetin supplementation.

**Cardiovascular protection:** Improves endothelial function via increased eNOS activity and NO bioavailability; reduces LDL oxidation; inhibits platelet aggregation; lowers blood pressure in hypertensive individuals (meta-analysis: -3.04 mmHg systolic, -2.63 mmHg diastolic); reduces atherosclerotic lesion formation in animal models.

**Exercise performance and recovery:** Reduces exercise-induced oxidative stress and inflammation; decreases post-exercise muscle damage markers (CK, LDH); attenuates illness incidence in athletes undergoing heavy training; may improve VO2max and endurance performance. The 2009 Davis study showed significantly reduced upper respiratory tract infection incidence in cyclists supplementing with 1,000 mg/day quercetin.

**Neuroprotection:** Reduces neuroinflammation; inhibits amyloid-β aggregation and tau hyperphosphorylation; protects against glutamate excitotoxicity; enhances hippocampal neurogenesis; improves cognitive performance in animal models of Alzheimer's disease.

## Dosing and Supplementation

- **Typical supplemental dose:** 500-1,000 mg/day (divided doses with meals)
- **D+Q senolytic protocol:** Dasatinib 100 mg + Quercetin 1,000 mg, taken intermittently (e.g., 3 days per month)
- **Food additive:** GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) status in the United States; permitted as a food supplement in the EU (with daily intake limits)
- **Common forms:** Quercetin dihydrate (aglycone), quercetin phytosome, isoquercitrin (enzymatically modified to improve solubility), and quercetin-3-glucoside

## Safety and Interactions

Quercetin is well-tolerated at doses up to 1,000 mg/day for up to 12 weeks in clinical trials. Occasional side effects include headache, nausea, and tingling at high doses (>1,000 mg/day). The NOAEL in animal studies is approximately 500-1,000 mg/kg/day.

Concerning interactions include: inhibition of CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and P-glycoprotein, potentially increasing blood levels of drugs metabolized by these pathways; additive effects with anticoagulants (warfarin) due to antiplatelet activity; potential enhanced nephrotoxicity with certain antibiotics (aminoglycosides) at very high doses; and theoretical interference with quinolone antibiotics. Individuals with kidney disease should exercise caution at high doses due to reported cases of nephrotoxicity with IV quercetin (not oral).

## Food Synergies and Dietary Considerations

Quercetin's bioavailability and efficacy are enhanced through several dietary synergies:

- **[[vitamin C]] (ascorbic acid):** Co-consumption prevents quercetin oxidation in the gastrointestinal tract and may improve absorption by 2-3×
- **bromelain (from pineapple):** Frequently combined in supplement formulations; bromelain may enhance quercetin absorption and has complementary anti-inflammatory action
- **[[piperine]] (from black pepper):** Inhibits hepatic glucuronidation, potentially increasing quercetin bioavailability
- **Fats:** Quercetin absorption is enhanced when consumed with dietary fat due to improved micelle formation in the intestine
- **Fermentation:** Lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium) can metabolize quercetin glycosides to aglycone and smaller phenolic metabolites with enhanced bioactivity, suggesting potential synergy with fermented foods
- **Onions vs. tea:** Quercetin from onions (primarily glucoside form) shows ~2× higher bioavailability than from tea (primarily rutinoside/rutin form), illustrating the importance of food matrix
