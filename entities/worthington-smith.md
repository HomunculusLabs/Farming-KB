# Worthington G. Smith

## Description

Worthington George Smith (1835–1917) was an English architect, illustrator, archaeologist, and
mycologist. He is best known in mycological circles for his illustrated works on British fungi,
particularly his work on toadstools with large colored plates. Smith was one of the four leading
British mycologists referenced by Palmer, alongside Berkeley, Cooke, and Badham. His publications
served as primary reference material for American students of fungi in the late 19th century.

## Classification

- **Field:** [[mycology]] / Illustration / Archaeology
- **Role:** Mycologist and scientific illustrator
- **Nationality:** English
- **Active Years:** 1860s–1910s
- **City:** London

## Key Facts

- One of the four leading British mycologists of the 19th century, alongside M.J. Berkeley,
  M.C. Cooke, and Charles Badham.
- Authored *Toadstools and How to Find Them* (illustrated with large colored charts), published
  by Robert Hardwicke, London, 1870s.
- Palmer purchased Smith's works from London as primary references when beginning his fungal
  studies.
- Smith reported that all species of Hydnum (tooth fungi) of any size "enjoy a good character"
  (are edible), a claim Palmer relayed to readers.
- Experienced illness from fungal experimentation himself — Palmer noted that Smith was "a victim
  in some of his experiments," suffering purging and vomiting similar to Palmer's own experience
  with fermented Boletus sub-tomentosus.
- United States government pamphlets on fungi drew almost entirely from Smith's earlier and
  incomplete labors, which Palmer criticized as inadequate.

## Published Works

- *Toadstools and How to Find Them*. London: Robert Hardwicke. Illustrated with large colored
  charts, used as a primary [[edible-mushroom-species-identification-guide]].
- Contributions to periodicals and scientific journals on British fungal species.
- His illustrations were considered among the best available for [[amanita-muscaria-physical-description-field-identification]] in an era
  before widespread photography.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Smith's works were among the few accessible references available to American students of fungi
  in the 1870s-1880s, making his classification system highly influential.
- His position that all large Hydnum species were edible encouraged experimentation with tooth
  fungi.
- Smith's experimental approach — personally testing fungi and documenting illness — exemplified
  the empirical tradition in mycology that Palmer also followed.
- Palmer's criticism that US government pamphlets merely reprinted Smith's incomplete work
  highlights the gap between British and [[palmer-mushroom-charts-american-mycological-education]] knowledge at the time.
- His colored plates made [[dna-barcoding-fungal-identification]] accessible to amateurs without microscopic
  training.

## Historical Context

Smith worked during the formative period of British mycology (1860s–1890s). Alongside Berkeley
and Cooke, he helped establish the classification of British fungi that served as the baseline
for American studies. Palmer noted that discrepancies and obscurities in these British works
confronted the American student, as descriptions applied to foreign species and classification
was not harmonious across authorities.

## Legacy

- His illustrated works remain historically significant for understanding 19th-century mycological
  taxonomy.
- Represented the empirical tradition of self-experimentation in determining [[fungal-edibility-classification-key]].
- His publications are accessible through biodiversity heritage libraries and historical
  collections.
