# Robinia pseudoacacia

## Overview

*Robinia pseudoacacia*, commonly known as **black locust**, is a fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing deciduous tree native to the Appalachian Mountains and Ozark Plateau of the eastern United States. Widely planted across temperate regions worldwide for its exceptionally durable wood, rapid growth, nitrogen fixation capability, and value as bee forage, black locust has become one of the most important **multi-functional agroforestry and permaculture trees** in the world. Despite its ecological invasiveness in some regions — particularly Europe, Asia, and parts of the Southern Hemisphere — its economic and ecological utility in appropriate contexts is unmatched by few other temperate tree species. The wood of black locust is among the most rot-resistant of all temperate hardwoods, rivaling tropical hardwoods in durability, making it highly valued for fence posts, outdoor construction, and biomass energy. Its nitrogen-fixing symbiosis with *Rhizobium* bacteria, prolific flowering providing major nectar flows for honeybees, and rapid early growth make it an excellent pioneer, nurse, and companion tree in diverse permaculture systems.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae → **Division:** Angiosperms → **Class:** Eudicots
- **Order:** Fabales → **Family:** Fabaceae (subfamily: Faboideae)
- **Genus:** *Robinia* (monotypic section) → **Species:** *R. pseudoacacia* L.
- **Authority:** Carl Linnaeus (1753)
- **Etymology:** Named for Jean Robin (1550–1629), royal gardener to Henry IV of France, and his son Vespasien Robin, who first cultivated the species in Europe. "Pseudoacacia" means "false acacia," reflecting its resemblance to *Acacia* species.
- **Common names:** Black locust, false acacia, yellow locust, post locust, shipmast locust

## Description

### Tree Form
- **Height:** 12–30 meters (40–100 feet); occasionally to 35 m in optimal conditions
- **DBH:** Typically 30–60 cm; can reach 100+ cm in mature specimens
- **Crown:** Open, irregular, upright-oval; often leaning or with crooked trunk
- **Lifespan:** 60–100 years in cultivation; up to 200+ years in native range
- **Growth rate:** Very fast — 1–2 meters per year in youth, slowing with maturity

### Bark
- Young branches: smooth, olive-green to brown, with paired stipular thorns at nodes
- Mature bark: Dark brown to grey, deeply furrowed and ridged, thick (1–3 cm)
- Thorns: Paired, sharp stipular spines at branch nodes; 5–20 mm long; an important identification feature distinguishing from other leguminous trees

### Leaves
- **Type:** Compound, odd-pinnate, 15–35 cm long
- **Leaflets:** 7–19 per leaf, ovate to elliptic, 2–5 cm long, dark green above, pale below
- **Margin:** Entire (smooth); no serration
- **Autumn color:** Yellow, often dropping early

### Flowers

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| Type | Pendulous racemes, 10–20 cm long |
| Color | White, fragrant (similar to orange blossom) |
| Structure | Papilionaceous (typical pea family form) |
| Bloom period | May–June (Northern Hemisphere) |
| Pollination | Bees (primary); also visited by butterflies and moths |
| Nectar value | Exceptionally high — one of the premier honey-producing trees |

### Fruit and Seed
- **Type:** Legume (pod), 5–10 cm long, 1–1.5 cm wide, flat
- **Color:** Green maturing to dark brown
- **Seeds:** 4–10 per pod, kidney-shaped, dark brown, 3–5 mm; hard seed coat requiring scarification
- **Dispersal:** Explosive dehiscence of dry pods; limited natural spread

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Native range:** Appalachian region and Ozark Plateau, from Pennsylvania south to Georgia and Alabama, west to Iowa and Oklahoma
- **Introduced/naturalized:** Widely planted in Europe (since 1601), East Asia, South America, southern Africa, Australia, and New Zealand
- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 4–9; tolerates temperatures to -30°C
- **Habitat:** Thrives on disturbed, nutrient-poor sites; prefers well-drained soils (sandy loam to clay loam); tolerant of drought, acid soils (pH 4.5–7.5), and moderate salinity
- **Successional role:** Early to mid-successional pioneer; colonizes degraded sites rapidly via root suckering

## Wood Properties

Black locust produces one of the most durable and valuable timbers of any temperate tree:

| Property | Value | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Specific gravity | 0.66–0.73 | Very dense; comparable to white oak |
| Janka hardness | 1,700 lbf | Among hardest North American hardwoods |
| Heartwood durability | Class 1 (very durable) | Resists ground contact for 25–50+ years |
| Compression strength | ~6,900 psi | Excellent structural properties |
| Shrinkage | Moderate (4.5% radial, 7.2% tangential) | Relatively stable in use |
| Fuel value | ~20.5 MJ/kg (dry) | Excellent firewood; high BTU content |
| Workability | Difficult; hard on tools | Pre-drilling recommended for fasteners |

## Permaculture and Agroforestry Applications

### Nitrogen Fixation
Black locust forms a symbiotic relationship with nitrogen-fixing *Rhizobium* bacteria in root nodules, fixing 50–150 kg N/ha/year. This makes it invaluable as a nurse tree, intercrop, and soil builder. In alley cropping and silvopastoral systems, black locust provides a nitrogen subsidy to adjacent crops and pastures.

### Bee Forage
Black locust is one of the world's premier honey-producing trees. A single large tree can produce enough nectar for 10–20 kg of honey. The resulting honey is light-colored, mild, and slow to granulate. In many regions, the "locust flow" is the most significant single-source nectar event of the year.

### Erosion Control and Land Reclamation
- Rapid root establishment on degraded, steep, or contaminated sites
- Tolerant of mine spoils, industrial wastelands, and compacted soils
- Root sucker networks stabilize slopes and stream banks
- Used extensively in mine reclamation and brownfield restoration

### Coppice and Pollard Systems
Black locust coppices vigorously, producing 3–6 meter shoots within 3–5 years of cutting. This makes it suitable for:
- Sustainable fence post production (25–50 year ground-contact lifespan)
- Biomass energy (high heat value, fast rotation)
- Tool handles, garden stakes, and small-diameter timber

### Fodder
Leaves and young shoots contain 20–24% crude protein and are palatable to livestock (cattle, goats, sheep) despite the presence of robinin (a lectin that is neutralized by drying or ensiling). Black locust leaf fodder has been used historically during drought periods.

### Windbreaks and Shelterbelts
Fast growth, drought tolerance, and upright form make black locust an effective windbreak species, particularly in continental climates.

## Medicinal Properties

- **Flowers:** Traditionally used as antispasmodic, diuretic, and anti-inflammatory; tea from flowers used for colds, headaches, and digestive complaints
- **Bark:** Contains robinin (a lectin) and toxic lectins (robin and phasin); bark extracts have shown antimicrobial and cytotoxic properties in research
- **Leaves:** Used in traditional medicine for wound healing; contain flavonoids with antioxidant activity
- **Toxicity warning:** Bark, seeds, and leaves contain toxalbumins (robin, phasin) that are toxic if ingested raw — causing nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and in severe cases, liver damage. Proper preparation (drying, cooking) reduces toxicity

## Invasiveness and Ecological Concerns

Black locust is classified as **invasive** in many parts of its introduced range:
- **Europe:** One of the most widespread invasive trees; forms dense monocultures that reduce native plant diversity
- **Japan:** Listed as a top-100 invasive alien species
- **New Zealand:** Restricted or prohibited in some regions
- **Management:** Root suckering makes eradication difficult; cut stumps resprout aggressively unless treated with herbicide

In its native range, black locust is not invasive and plays a valuable ecological role as a pioneer and wildlife habitat tree.
