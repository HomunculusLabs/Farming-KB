## Description

Amphinema byssoides is a corticioid (crust-like) basidiomycete fungus that forms thin, web-like fruiting bodies on decaying wood and woody debris. It is classified as a lignicolous saprotroph, playing a role in the decomposition of woody material in forest ecosystems. The species has been studied in the context of liming experiments in coniferous forests, where it showed increased abundance following soil pH amendment — providing insight into how soil chemistry management affects wood-decay fungal communities.

- **Ecological role:** Lignicolous saprotroph
- **Habitat:** Dead wood in coniferous and deciduous forests

## Key Facts

- Forms thin, arachnoid (cobweb-like) basidiocarps on the undersides of dead wood and woody debris.
- Veerkamp et al. (1997) documented that A. byssoides increased in abundance on woody substrates in limed Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris) forests on acidic soils.
- The liming-induced community shift made the lignicolous fungal assemblage more similar to that found in deciduous woodlands than in typical coniferous forests.
- This shift was attributed to elevated soil pH increasing nitrogen availability, which stimulated decomposition of high C:N ratio coniferous wood.
- Other species that co-increased with liming included Hyphodontia breviseta, Hypochnicium geogenium, and Sitotrema octosporum, while Trechispora farinacea decreased.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Amphinema byssoides illustrates how [[soil-ph-management]] cascades through decomposer food webs:

1. The species serves as a bioindicator for soil pH recovery in acid-damaged forests — its increased abundance signals improved conditions for wood decomposition.
2. In mycoremediation contexts, liming combined with fungal inoculation could accelerate decomposition of woody debris in acidified soils.
3. The community shift from coniferous-type to deciduous-type [[wood-decay-fungi]] under liming suggests that pH management can fundamentally alter decomposition pathways and nutrient cycling rates.
4. For mushroom cultivators working with wood substrate, this research suggests that substrate pH adjustment can influence which decomposer species dominate, with implications for both mycelial growth rates and fruiting.
5. The species demonstrates the ecological principle that even apparently soil-distant organisms (wood-decay fungi) respond to soil chemistry changes mediated through nutrient availability and substrate quality.
