## Description

Edward "Teddy" Goldsmith (1928–2009) was a British environmentalist, philosopher, and founding editor of *The Ecologist* magazine. Mollison cites Goldsmith's 1981 essay "Thermodynamics or Ecodynamics" (published in *The Ecologist*) multiple times as a key intellectual influence on permaculture theory.

Goldsmith argued that Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis shows the biosphere "actually created those conditions that are required for its support" and "systematically builds up the stock of materials that it requires to move towards increasing complexity, diversity, and stability." He criticized mainstream scientists for refusing to consider the function of life in thermodynamic systems, and warned that the destruction of natural self-regulating systems leads to their replacement by artificial, energy-dependent systems managed by the State.

Mollison draws on Goldsmith to argue that "only by returning self-regulating function and responsibility to living things (such as people)" can stable life systems evolve.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Environmental philosophy, systems ecology, publishing
- **Era**: Mid to late 20th century

## Key Facts

- Founded *The Ecologist* magazine in 1970, one of the world's first environmental periodicals
- Authored *The Way: An Ecological World-View* (1992), a comprehensive critique of industrial society from an ecological perspective
- Argued that quantifiable scientific concepts often "correspond to nothing whatsoever in the world of living things"
- Contended that natural systems disintegrate when their self-regulating functions are removed, producing "more and more helpless plants, animals, and people"
- His "Ecodynamics" essay argued that biological systems build complexity in apparent defiance of the second law of thermodynamics
- Influenced the deep ecology and appropriate technology movements

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Goldsmith's insights are directly applicable to cultivation system design:

1. **Self-regulating systems**: The principle that living systems should manage their own regulation rather than depending on external control is fundamental to designing low-maintenance cultivation systems. Fungal mycelial networks are natural self-regulators.
2. **Ecodynamics vs. thermodynamics**: Goldsmith's argument that living systems build complexity rather than degrade toward entropy explains why well-designed cultivation systems improve over time, building soil, increasing biodiversity, and becoming more productive.
3. **Critique of reductionism**: His warning against reducing complex biological systems to quantifiable metrics is relevant to cultivation, where overly simplified growing protocols often fail because they ignore the complexity of living systems.
4. **Function of life**: Goldsmith's insistence that life itself is a functional force in thermodynamic systems supports the permaculture principle of using biological solutions (fungal decomposition, mycorrhizal associations, living mulches) rather than mechanical or chemical ones.
