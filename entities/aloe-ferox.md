Inflorescences are multi-branched racemes reaching 1.2 m tall. Flowers are bright orange-red to scarlet, occasionally yellow, appearing May–July in the [[portulacaria-afra]] and Euphorbia species in thicket vegetation. Bird pollination by sunbirds (Nectarinia spp.) is the primary pollination mechanism.

## Cultivation

Aloe ferox is cultivated as both an ornamental and a medicinal crop. It requires full sun, excellent drainage, and minimal supplemental water once established.

Propagation is primarily from seed, though stem cuttings can root if allowed to callus.

Seeds germinate in 3–4 weeks at 20–25 °C in well-drained, sandy medium. Fresh seeds have higher germination rates than stored seeds.

Commercial plantations in the Eastern Cape harvest wild and cultivated plants for bitter sap. Harvesting involves making transverse cuts on lower leaves and collecting the dark exudate that flows from the inner leaf skin.

Plants begin flowering at 4–5 years from seed. Mature specimens produce 20–30 harvestable leaves per year.

Ornamental cultivation benefits from a frost-protected position with good [[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]] the exudate include aloe-emodin, aloin (barbaloin), and aloesin.

These anthraquinone glycosides exhibit laxative, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activities. Aloin concentration in Cape aloes ranges from 8–25% of the dried exudate.

The inner leaf gel, distinct from the bitter exudate, is used similarly to Aloe vera gel in cosmetics and skin preparations.

It contains polysaccharides (acemannan) with moisturizing and wound-healing properties. The gel also contains amino acids, vitamins, and minerals.
