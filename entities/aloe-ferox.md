## Overview

Aloe ferox, commonly known as bitter aloe or Cape aloe, is a tall, single-stemmed succulent native to southern Africa. It is one of the most commercially important Aloe species, harvested extensively for its bitter sap and bitter leaf gel.

The species has been used in traditional medicine for centuries across South Africa.

It differs from Aloe vera in producing a dark, bitter exudate rich in anthraquinone compounds.

Cape aloes, the dried bitter sap, has been a major export commodity from South Africa since the 18th century. The Eastern Cape remains the center of commercial harvesting activity today.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Plantae
**Family:** Asphodelaceae
**Genus:** Aloe
**Species:** Aloe ferox Mill.

Synonyms include Aloe supralaevis Haw. and Aloe perfoliata var. ferox (Mill.) Aiton. The specific epithet "ferox" means "fierce" or "warlike," referring to the sharp spines along the leaf margins.

Aloe ferox forms a robust, erect stem reaching 2–3 m tall. The leaves are thick, fleshy, and dull green to bluish-green, armed with reddish-brown teeth along the margins.

Leaf surfaces may reach 1 m in length and 15–20 cm wide at the base.

Dried leaf tips persist along the stem, forming a characteristic skirt. The rosette typically contains 20–30 leaves arranged in a dense, upright crown.

Roots are shallow and spreading, adapted to capture surface moisture in [[fungal-diversity-arid-environments]]. The stem surface is smooth and gray, marked by leaf scars from shed foliage.

Inflorescences are multi-branched racemes reaching 1.2 m tall. Flowers are bright orange-red to scarlet, occasionally yellow, appearing May–July in the [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]].

Individual flowers are tubular, 3–4 cm long, and produce copious nectar that attracts sunbirds and bees. Fruit is a loculicidal capsule containing numerous winged seeds.

## Distribution and Habitat

Aloe ferox is endemic to southern Africa, ranging from the Western Cape through the Eastern Cape to southern KwaZulu-Natal, Lesotho, and the Orange Free State. It is one of the most widely distributed Aloe species in South Africa.

The species occupies a range of habitats including rocky hillsides, grassland, thicket, and fynbos margins.

It thrives in well-drained soils and tolerates both summer and winter rainfall regimes. Soils are typically clay-loam to sandy, often on shale or dolerite-derived substrates.

Populations are particularly dense in the Albany Thicket Biome of the Eastern Cape. Here, Aloe ferox forms conspicuous stands visible on hilltops and ridges.

Altitudinal range extends from near sea level to approximately 1,500 m. The species is drought-tolerant but is sensitive to prolonged frost.

Aloe ferox co-occurs with other succulent species such as [[portulacaria-afra]] and Euphorbia species in thicket vegetation. Bird pollination by sunbirds (Nectarinia spp.) is the primary pollination mechanism.

## Cultivation

Aloe ferox is cultivated as both an ornamental and a medicinal crop. It requires full sun, excellent drainage, and minimal supplemental water once established.

Propagation is primarily from seed, though stem cuttings can root if allowed to callus.

Seeds germinate in 3–4 weeks at 20–25 °C in well-drained, sandy medium. Fresh seeds have higher germination rates than stored seeds.

Commercial plantations in the Eastern Cape harvest wild and cultivated plants for bitter sap. Harvesting involves making transverse cuts on lower leaves and collecting the dark exudate that flows from the inner leaf skin.

Plants begin flowering at 4–5 years from seed. Mature specimens produce 20–30 harvestable leaves per year.

Ornamental cultivation benefits from a frost-protected position with good [[stamets-growing-room-air-circulation-co2-management]]. Container-grown specimens require a large, heavy pot to prevent toppling.

In landscaping, Aloe ferox serves as a dramatic architectural specimen. It attracts pollinators and provides winter nectar for birds and insects.

## Uses

The bitter exudate (Cape aloes) is one of the oldest and most widely used purgative substances in pharmacopoeias worldwide. It is listed in the British Pharmacopoeia and has been traded internationally since the 1700s.

Key [[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]] the exudate include aloe-emodin, aloin (barbaloin), and aloesin.

These anthraquinone glycosides exhibit laxative, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activities. Aloin concentration in Cape aloes ranges from 8–25% of the dried exudate.

The inner leaf gel, distinct from the bitter exudate, is used similarly to Aloe vera gel in cosmetics and skin preparations.

It contains polysaccharides (acemannan) with moisturizing and wound-healing properties. The gel also contains amino acids, vitamins, and minerals.
