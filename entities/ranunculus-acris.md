## Overview

Ranunculus acris, commonly known as meadow buttercup, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Ranunculaceae. It is one of the most widespread and recognizable wildflowers across temperate Eurasia and naturalized regions worldwide.

The species is characterized by its glossy, golden-yellow flowers that blanket meadows and pastures from May through September. It is the tallest common buttercup in Europe, reaching 30–100 cm in height.

All parts of the plant contain ranunculin, which converts to the toxic compound protoanemonin when plant tissue is damaged. Despite this, meadow buttercup is an ecologically important species in temperate grasslands.

It serves as a food plant for numerous invertebrate species and provides nectar for a wide range of pollinators. The species is considered a weed in improved pastures but a valued component of species-rich hay meadows.

Ranunculus acris is one of the most phenotypically variable species in the European flora. Leaf shape, plant height, and flower size vary dramatically across latitudinal and altitudinal gradients.

The species is a classic example of a generalist grassland plant with broad ecological amplitude. It occurs in over 80% of European grassland community types surveyed in continental-scale studies.

## Taxonomy

Ranunculus acris was described by Carl Linnaeus in his 1753 Species Plantarum. The genus Ranunculus is the largest in Ranunculaceae, comprising approximately 600 species distributed on all continents except Antarctica.

The specific epithet "acris" means "sharp" or "acrid," referring to the pungent taste of the fresh foliage. The plant belongs to the section Ranunculus within the subgenus Ranunculus.

Chromosome number is 2n = 14 (diploid), though polyploid cytotypes are known from northern populations. Molecular phylogenetics place Ranunculus acris in a clade with [[ranunculus-bulbosus]].

Several subspecies are recognized, including subsp. acris, subsp. friesianus, and subsp. strigulosus. The species exhibits considerable morphological variation across its broad geographic range.

Hybridization with Ranunculus repens occurs occasionally where the two species co-occur. These hybrids (Ranunculus × levenensis) are typically sterile and intermediate in morphology.

Genetic differentiation between northern and southern European populations suggests isolation during the last glacial maximum. Post-glacial recolonization routes have been reconstructed using chloroplast DNA haplotypes.

Ranunculus acris exhibits clinal variation in flowering time across latitude. Southern populations flower up to six weeks earlier than Scandinavian populations under common garden conditions.

## Morphology

Meadow buttercup forms an erect, hairy plant reaching 30–100 cm in height. Multiple flowering stems arise from a short, stout rootstock with numerous fibrous roots.

Basal leaves are palmately lobed with three to seven deep segments, each further divided into narrow toothed lobes. Stem leaves are smaller, sessile, and become progressively simpler upward.

Flowers are solitary or in loose cymes at stem tips, each 2–3 cm in diameter. Five glossy yellow petals surround a prominent cluster of stamens and green carpels in the center.

The glossy appearance of the petals results from a specialized epidermal structure that reflects light efficiently. This optical adaptation makes the flowers particularly visible to insect pollinators.

Fruiting heads are globose, composed of numerous flattened achenes with short curved beaks. Each achene contains a single seed dispersed by wind or water.

The root system consists of a short vertical rhizome with fibrous roots. Unlike Ranunculus repens, meadow buttercup does not produce stolons.

The plant overwinters as a basal rosette of lobed leaves close to the ground. This rosette stage allows it to survive mowing and moderate [[hamilton-wildflower-meadow]] plantings. Several double-flowered horticultural selections exist, including the popular cultivar 'Citrinus' with pale lemon flowers.

The species has limited traditional medicinal use due to its toxicity. Fresh herb was applied externally as a vesicant and counterirritant in folk medicine, but such practices are discouraged.

The plant is avoided by grazing livestock due to the acrid taste caused by protoanemonin. Heavy grazing reduces competition and can paradoxically increase buttercup abundance in pastures.
