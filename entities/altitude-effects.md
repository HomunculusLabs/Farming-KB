# Altitude Effects on Climate and Agriculture

## Description

Altitude effects describe the systematic changes in temperature, atmospheric pressure, solar radiation, humidity, and growing conditions that occur with increasing elevation above sea level. Mollison treats altitude as a fundamental design variable, equivalent to latitude change, and critical for understanding the climatic potential of any site with significant topographic relief. The interaction of altitude with slope, aspect, and continentality creates the most complex microclimatic conditions that permaculture designers must analyse and work with.

## Classification

- **Type**: Climatic concept / environmental gradient
- **Domain**: Climatology, agriculture, [[permaculture-design]]
- **Key variable**: Elevation above sea level (metres or feet)
- **Equivalence rule**: Approximately 100 m altitude is equivalent to 1 degree of latitude

## Key Facts

- **Temperature lapse rate**: Average temperature decrease with altitude is 9.8 degrees C per km (5.4 degrees F per 1000 feet) in dry air, and 4-9 degrees C per km (2.2-5 degrees F per 1000 feet) in humid saturated conditions.
- **Altitude-latitude equivalence**: Every 100 m (330 feet) of altitude is roughly equivalent to 1 degree of latitude. At 1000 m on the equator, temperatures approximate a climate 10 degrees off the equator with the same humidity.
- At 10 degrees off the equator, a plateau at 1850 m (6000 feet) has a climate similar to 30 degrees latitude, with probability of wind chill to below freezing during cold snaps.
- **Atmospheric effects at high altitude**: Air is more rarefied, air pressure is lower, and solar radiation is stronger (particularly ultraviolet), creating conditions that are not strictly equivalent to latitude despite similar temperatures.
- On mountains above 4000 m (13,000 feet), people may experience oxygen deficiency (mountain sickness), snow or radiation blindness, and extreme day-night temperature fluctuations.
- **Peruvian Andes example**: Day temperatures remain 16-19 degrees C all year at high altitude, but night temperatures fall rapidly to -10 degrees C. Shade temperatures are lower than at sea level due to less effective heat transfer in the rarefied air.
- Water boils at lower temperatures at altitude (approximately 93 degrees C at 2000 m), affecting cooking and sterilisation procedures. Snow may sublime directly to water vapour rather than melting under certain conditions.
- **Snow cover** serves as an insulating blanket, preventing early spring thawing or preserving unfrozen ground beneath if it falls before deep freezing occurs.
- Snow also causes intense reflection by day (raising air temperatures just above the snow surface) and extreme cold ground-air layer at night through radiative cooling, creating extreme diurnal temperature swings for any plants protruding above snow.

## Slope Effects (Often Greater Than Altitude Alone)

- Daubenmire (1974) records that slopes of just 5 degrees towards the poles "reduce soil temperatures as much as 168 km distance" towards the poles, demonstrating that aspect can be more powerful than altitude in determining local climate.
- Cold ravines in near-permanent shadow create extreme conditions where only the hardiest life forms exist, with ice permanently covering rocks near waterfalls and spray zones.
- The effect of slope orientation is described as "even more pronounced" than the effect of altitude itself, making aspect analysis a critical first step in site evaluation.
- One may stand in hot sunlight in the Himalayas and gaze into icy depths mere metres away where a different microclimate supports an entirely different community of organisms.

## Valley Wind Systems at Altitude

- Upslope and up-valley winds by day, downslope and down-valley winds by night, operating on a daily cycle that strongly influences temperature, humidity, and growing conditions.
- These daily wind cycles are used by bird species for daily migration: downhill at dawn on cold downslope winds, returning to ridge forests at evening on warm upslope winds.
- In large cool-moist valleys, upslope winds generate chains of cumulus clouds at valley heads, creating localised rainfall patterns.
- In tropical humid climates, cloud may be permanently held on mountain tops, forming the standing cloud of high islands and supporting unique cloud-forest ecosystems.
- Glacial winds flowing down cold valleys reach maximum speed just before dawn, coinciding with the time of greatest cold exposure for living organisms.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Species range by altitude**: High islands and mountain ranges enable growing a wide range of plant and fungal species from ocean to mountain-top within a single property, making altitudinal diversity one of the greatest assets in permaculture design.
- **Mycorrhizal zonation**: Ectomycorrhizal fungal communities shift dramatically with altitude, tracking their host trees along elevational gradients. Understanding altitude effects helps predict which mycorrhizal species will be present and productive at any given elevation.
- **[[mushroom-cultivation]] at altitude**: Reduced atmospheric pressure and lower boiling points affect sterilisation procedures for substrate preparation. Longer sterilisation times or pressure cookers calibrated for altitude are needed to achieve proper Pasteurisation.
- **Snow management for fungal beds**: Snow cover insulating over-wintering mushroom beds (e.g., outdoor shiitake logs) protects mycelium from lethal freezing while maintaining adequate moisture levels through the dormant season.
- **UV radiation effects**: Higher UV at altitude may affect the viability of spore prints, liquid cultures, and surface-inoculated substrates. Indoor or covered cultivation becomes more important at higher elevations to protect sensitive biological materials.

## Design Implications

- Use altitude to grow temperate crops in tropical countries by selecting appropriate elevations that match the climate requirements of target species.
- Place frost-sensitive crops on mid-slope thermal belt positions, not in valley floors or on exposed ridges.
- Design water storages at higher elevations for gravity-fed irrigation to lower growing areas, capturing the energy of altitude in the distribution system.
- Account for increased solar radiation with shade structures at high altitude, especially for crops and mushrooms that require moderated light.
- Use slope orientation to create warm (equator-facing) and cool (pole-facing) microclimates on the same property, effectively multiplying the range of growing conditions available.
