## Overview

Cydonia oblonga, commonly known as the quince, is a deciduous tree in the family Rosaceae and the sole species in the genus Cydonia. Native to the Caucasus region and Central Asia, it has been cultivated for over 4,000 years across the Mediterranean, Middle East, and [[backyard-food-forest-design]], quince serves as a hardy, disease-resistant fruit tree that requires minimal maintenance.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Magnoliophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Rosales
- **Family:** Rosaceae
- **Subfamily:** Amygdaloideae
- **Tribe:** Maleae
- **Genus:** Cydonia
- **Species:** C. oblonga

**Authority:** Philip Miller (1768).

The genus name Cydonia derives from Kydonia, an ancient city in Crete where the fruit was widely cultivated. The species epithet oblonga refers to the oblong shape of the fruit. The taxonomic position has shifted over time — some older classifications placed quince within Pyrus (pears).

## Morphology

The quince is a small-to-medium deciduous tree, typically reaching 3–8 meters in height with a spreading, somewhat irregular crown. Young branches are covered in a dense, grayish tomentum; older bark is smooth, dark gray, and may flake in patches.

**Leaves:** Simple, alternate, ovate to elliptic, 5–10 cm long and 3–5 cm wide. The upper surface is dark green and glabrous; the underside is densely pubescent with white-gray hairs. Leaf margins are entire or crenate.

**Flowers:** Solitary or in small cymes, produced on short spur shoots. Flowers are 4–5 cm in diameter with five white to pale pink petals. The calyx is five-lobed and persistent. Flowering occurs in late spring (May–June), later than most other Rosaceae fruit trees, which reduces frost damage risk.

**Fruit:** A large pome, 5–12 cm in diameter, variable in shape from oblong to pyriform or apple-shaped. The skin is smooth, golden-yellow when ripe, covered with a fine pubescence that rubs off. The flesh is firm, aromatic, and contains numerous small seeds (carpels) arranged in a papery, leathery core. Raw flesh is extremely astringent due to high tannin content.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cydonia oblonga is native to the region spanning the Caucasus Mountains, northern Iran, Turkmenistan, and possibly southwestern Turkey. It has been cultivated in the Mediterranean since antiquity — the fruit was sacred to Aphrodite [[query-how-to-manage-pests-and-diseases-in-mushroom-cultivation]]:** Quince is generally more disease-resistant than apple or pear. Fire blight (Erwinia amylovora) is the most significant disease threat. Quince leaf blight (Diplocarpon mespili) can cause premature defoliation in humid conditions. Codling moth and various aphids are the primary insect pests.

## Nutritional and Bioactive Profile

Quince is notable for its exceptionally high pectin content — approximately 1.0–1.5% of fresh weight, making it one of the richest fruit sources of this soluble fiber.

**Key nutrients (per 100 g raw):**

- Calories: 57 kcal
- Dietary fiber: 1.9 g (mostly pectin)
- Vitamin C: 15 mg (25% DV)
- Potassium: 197 mg
- Copper: 0.13 mg

**[[aact-tannins-terpenes-phenolic-compounds-wood-composting-ingham]], including hydroxycinnamic acids (chlorogenic, caffeic, ferulic acids), flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol glycosides), and organic acids (citric, malic, ascorbic). The peel contains significantly higher concentrations than the flesh.

Antioxidant capacity of quince extracts is high, comparable to or exceeding that of many common fruits. Anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, and antidiabetic activities have been demonstrated in animal and in vitro studies.
