## Description

A classification system for community wealth and resources developed by Turnbull (1975) and adopted by [[bill-mollison]] in [[permaculture-design]]. The system categorises all assets into five types based on their ecological and economic behaviour: degenerative, generative, procreative, informational, and conservative. This framework guides financial and [[mycelial-foraging-resource-allocation]] decisions in [[permaculture-community-design]].

## The Five Asset Categories

### 1. Degenerative Assets
Assets that decay, rust, or wear out: buildings, roads, cars, furnishings, and appliances. Mollison warns that too many degenerative assets in any region will impoverish the region in the long term. These require constant energy and resource input for maintenance and eventual replacement.

### 2. Generative Assets
The tools of society that manufacture or process raw materials into useful products: huskers, grinders, blenders, lathes, furnaces, and workshop equipment. These wear out but can be used to repair each other in workshops. All groups need some processing and repair tools; wise farmers hire out or share such tools.

### 3. Procreative Assets
Living systems: trees, wildlife, fish, invertebrates, mammals, and domestic livestock. People who maximise a procreative asset base can support the use of some tools and modest degenerative assets. Those who maximise degenerative assets eventually fail to maintain them — hence ruined castles and stately homes.

### 4. Informational Assets
Information, education, and data, plus applied intelligence. These make the best use of all assets, decide balances in the asset base, assess future trends, and foresee needs and changes. Seeds have a high information content, as do books and databases.

### 5. Conservative Assets
Insulation, dams, money recycling systems, good storage areas, and strategic forests guarding against erosion or desertification. All these conserve resources for future use and are essential to sustainable systems.

## Application to Wealth Creation

Mollison states that expenditure on categories 3-5 (procreative, informational, conservative) conserves and creates wealth in any society. If many wealth-producing assets are available, some degenerative assets can be supported. Any society spending only on categories 1 and 2 will first pollute and then eventually extinguish its resource base.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

The asset classification directly applies to farm and garden design:

- **Procreative assets**: Food forests, perennial plantings, livestock, mushroom cultures, seed banks, [[composting-systems]] — all self-replicating biological wealth
- **Generative assets**: Processing equipment (dehydrators, grain mills, oil presses, fermentation vessels) that add value to raw biological production
- **Conservative assets**: Water tanks, swales, windbreaks, mulch layers, cold frames — infrastructure that conserves energy and resources
- **Informational assets**: Seed collections with genetic information, soil test data, [[hamilton-seasonal-planting-calendars]], observation journals
- **Degenerative assets**: Minimise these — buildings, vehicles, synthetic tools — and ensure they are paid for by procreative surplus

## The Law of Return

Mollison connects asset categories to the principle "everything must pay" — more specifically, "proposers pay capital" and "users pay costs." In natural systems, this is the law of return. Soils, crops, and forests cannot be used without costing total upkeep and replacement, or the common wealth is impoverished.

## Financial Institution Design

The classification guides the design of ethical financial institutions in permaculture communities. Such institutions should:
- Prevent leakages of essential resources
- Export local surplus to bring scarce resources into the region
- Never base surplus on loss of irreplaceable resources (soil, humus)
- Operate under a published ethical charter
- Restrict operations to appropriate resource development

## Classification Framework

Mollison categorizes community assets into five primary
types that form the foundation of permaculture village
economics:

1. **Natural assets** — land, water, forests, mineral
   resources, and biodiversity
2. **Built assets** — infrastructure, buildings, tools,
   and processing equipment
3. **Financial assets** — local currency, credit pools,
   and cooperative savings
4. **Human assets** — skills, knowledge, labor capacity,
   and organizational abilities
5. **Social assets** — trust networks, governance
   structures, and cultural practices

## Design Implications

Asset categorization directly informs zoning and village
layout. Productive assets (workshops, processing
facilities) cluster in zones 2-3, while social assets
(gathering spaces, markets) anchor the village center.
Natural assets require protective buffers and
connectivity corridors in the overall design.
