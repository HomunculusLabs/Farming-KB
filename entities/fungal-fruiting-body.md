## Description

A fruiting body (also called a sporocarp or fruit body) is a specialized multicellular structure produced by fungi for the purpose of sexual or asexual spore production and dispersal. In higher fungi (Basidiomycota and Ascomycota), these structures are macroscopic and conspicuous — including mushrooms, puffballs, truffles, cups, and brackets. Fruiting bodies represent a significant energetic investment, serving as sinks for internally translocated carbon and nutrients.

## Key Facts

### Structure and Function
- Fruiting bodies bear spores on specialized structures — basidia in Basidiomycota and asci in Ascomycota.
- They function as dispersal agents, launching or releasing spores for colonization of new substrates.
- Production demands significant energy and nutrients, making fruiting bodies strong sinks for translocated carbon and minerals.
- Due to high nutritional content (proteins, vitamins, minerals), fruiting bodies are prime food sources for grazing animals.

### Ecological Role
- Critical link in food webs, supporting secondary production in ecosystems.
- Mushroom-forming species and lichens are staple food for reindeer and moose in boreal ecosystems (Cooper and Wookey, 2001; Kumpula, 2001).
- Hypogeous fruiting bodies (truffles) are food for rodents and wild boar, with spore dispersal dependent on animal vectors (Trappe and Maser, 1976; Trappe, 1988).
- Fungal hyphae and spores are food for fungivorous mites, collembola, and nematodes (Shaw, 1992; McGonigle, 1997).

### Nutritional Significance
- Fungi provide highly nutritious food with high protein and vitamin content (Fogel, 1979; Gronwall and Pehrson, 1984).
- Commercial mushroom production in the US was approximately 430,000 mg per year at time of publication.
- Fruiting bodies concentrate nutrients from the mycelial network, mining substrates for minerals and bioactive compounds.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Fruiting body production is the primary economic output of mushroom cultivation. Cultivators manipulate environmental triggers (temperature drop, increased humidity, fresh air to lower CO2, light exposure) to simulate natural fruiting conditions. Many medicinal compounds (beta-glucans, triterpenes) are concentrated differently in mycelium versus fruiting bodies, informing extraction and supplementation practices.
