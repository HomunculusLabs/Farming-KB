## Mineral Indicator Plants

## Description

Mineral indicator plants are species that accumulate or are associated with specific mineral deposits in the underlying soil or rock. Their presence, vigour, or chemical composition can reveal subsurface ore bodies, trace element concentrations, or soil mineral status. Mollison draws on research from Davidov (published in *Sputnik*, 12 December 1979) documenting plant-ore associations in Russia, and extends the concept to practical applications in [[compost]] amendment

## Key Facts

- Plant ash analysis reveals mineral concentrations from underlying ore bodies
- Some plants can concentrate minerals to extraordinary levels in their tissues
- Patents have been granted for mining gold deposits using banana or citrus plants deprived of common elements (potash, phosphate) — their leaves then concentrate sparse gold deposits
- Oysters concentrate zinc to 11% dry weight (an emetic dose)
- Abalone concentrate cadmium
- Large fish concentrate mercury and biological poisons from corals to inedible levels
- The concept has implications for both mineral exploration and public health monitoring

## Applications in Permaculture

1. **Site Assessment**: Indicator plants reveal subsurface mineral conditions during site analysis
2. **Compost Amendment**: Concentrator plants can be grown and harvested to add trace minerals to compost
3. **Bioremediation**: Plants that concentrate toxic metals (lead, uranium, cadmium) can be used to clean contaminated soils through repeated harvesting
4. **[[soil-testing]]. In permaculture, they offer a low-cost, long-term method for understanding subsurface mineral conditions. The concept of biological concentration — using plants to accumulate trace minerals for compost — is particularly valuable for organic growers seeking to address micronutrient deficiencies without synthetic inputs. Conversely, understanding which plants concentrate toxic metals (lead, cadmium, mercury) is essential for food safety on potentially contaminated sites.
