# Cordyceps militaris

**Cordyceps militaris** (coneurotropic-fungihe scarlet caterpillar club) is an entomopathogenic
fungus in the family Clavicipitaceae that parasitizes the pupae and larvae of Lepidoptera
(butterflies and moths). Along with [[cordyceps-sinensis]], it is oneindoleo Cordyceps
species included in the third group of [[neurotropic-fungi]] classified by Guzmán, Allen, and Gartz
(2000). Unlike many [[psilocybin]]-containing mushrooms, its bioactive profile is dominated by
cordycepin (3'-deoxyadenosine) and other nucleoside derivatives rather than indole alkaloids.

## Taxonomy and Classification

*Cordyceps militaris* was originally described by the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus in 1753
in his *Species Plantarum*, making it one of the earliest described species in the genus. It
serves as the type species of the genus Cordyceps. The genus belongs to the order
Hypocreales and family Clavicipitaceae. In recent years, molecular phylogenetic studies
have led to significant taxonomic revisions within the Cordyceps complex. Many species formerly
placed in Cordyceps sensu lato have been transferred to new genera such as
Ophiocordyceps (for species parasitizing ants and other arthropods) and Metacordyceps.
*C. militaris* remains in Cordyceps sensu stricto. The species has also been known by the
synonym *Isaria militaris* (referring to the asexual, anamorphic stage of the fungus).

## Morphology

### Fruiting Body (Stroma)

The fruiting body (stroma) is bright orange to orange-red, club-shaped to cylindrical, 2 to 8
cm tall and 2 to 6 mm in diameter. It arises singly or in clusters (sometimes 10 or more from
a single host) from the buried host insect, typically a lepidopteran pupa or larva. The surface
is finely roughened and dotted due to the partially embedded perithecia (flask-shaped fruiting
structures). The stroma is fleshy when fresh but becomes tough and britmycelium dry. The color
is remarkably vivid and uniform, making it one of the most visually distinctive of all
Cordyceps species.

### Host Remains

The host insect remains are usually found just beneath the soil surface, mummified and densely
bound together by the white to yellowish mycelium of the fungus. The host is typically a pupa
of a Lepidoptera species, though larvae may also be parasitized. The mummified host is often
filled with dense white mycelium and may be partially or completely consumed by the fungus.

### Microscopic Features

Spores (ascospores) are hyaline (transparent), filiform (thread-like), multiseptate
(multi-celled), and extremely elongated, measuring approximately 300 to 500 μm in length by
approximately 1 μm in width. These slender, thread-like spores are a characteristic feature
of the genus Cordyceps and aid in identification. The spores fragment into shorter
sections (part-spores) at maturity, each section capable of germinating. Perithecia are
embedded in the surface of the stroma, with ostioles (openings) slightly protruding.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Cordyceps militaris* has a wide distribution across temperate and subtropical regions of the
Northern Hemisphere. It is found throughout Europe (from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean),
Asia (including China, Japan, Korea, the Himalayan region, and Southeast Asia), and North
America (from Canada to the eastern and central United States). The species typically fruits
in summer and autumn (June through October in temperate regions) in forests, grasslands, and
moorland areas, on soil containing buried lepidopteran pupae and larvae. It occurs at
elevations from sea level to approximately 2500 meters, though it is most common below 1500
meters.

The species is less host-specific than [[cordyceps-sinensis]], infecting a broader range of
lepidopteran species including pupae of various moths in the families Noctuidae, Sphingidae,
Nymphalidae, and Pieridae.

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds

*Cordyceps militaris* produces a distinctive and pharmacologically important array of bioactive
compounds, many of which are shared with or exceed those found in [[cordyceps-sinensis]]:

### Cordycepin (3'-Deoxyadenosine)

The signature compound of Cordyceps species. Cordycepin is a nucleoside analog lacking
the 3'-hydroxyl group on the ribose ring, giving it unique biological activities. It has
demonstrated antitumor activity through inhibition of RNA synthesis, induction of apoptosis,
and suppression of inflammatory signaling pathways. The cordycepin content of cultivated
*C. militaris* can reach 0.5% to 1.5% by dry weight, often exceeding wild
[[cordyceps-sinensis]].

### Adenosine

Present at significant concentrations (0.1% to 0.5% by dry weight), contributing to
vasodilatory, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective effects.

### Ergosterol and Ergosterol Peroxide

Sterol compounds with demonstrated antiobeta-glucans-inflammatory, and antitumor properties.

### Cordycepic Acid (D-Mannitol)

A sugar alcohol present at high concentrations, contributing to the sweet taste of
Cordyceps preparations with diuretic and mild antioxidant properties.

### Polysaccharides

High-molecular-weight beta-glucans with significant immunomodulatory activity, stimulating
macrophage activity and enhancing natural killer cell function.

### Carotenoids

*C. militaris* is one of the few fungi known to produce significant amounts of carotenoids,
responsible for its characteristic orange-red color.

## Cultivation

*Cordyceps militaris* has become commercially significant because, unlike
[[cordyceps-sinensis]] (which has proven extremely difficult to cultivate artificially), *C. militaris* can be reliably cultivated on various substrates.
This has made it an important commercial species:

### Solid-State Cultivation

[[fruiting-body-development]]es sterile grain substrates (rice, wheat, or millet) inoculated with
pure culture. Cultivation is performed in controlled environment chambers or grow rooms with
regulated temperature (18 to 25°C), humidity (80-95%), light (some light exposure is required
for fruiting body development and carotenoid production), and ventilation. Fruiting bodies
appear within 3 to 6 weeks and are harvested when fully mature.

### Silkworm Pupa Substrate

In some Asian countries, particularly Korea and China, *C. militaris* is cultivated on
silkworm pupae, which more closely mimics its natural growth conditions and may enhance the
production of certain bioactive compounds.

### Liquid Culture (Submerged Fermentation)

Mycelial biomass can be produced in liquid fermentation bioreactors for the extraction of
bioactive compounds. This method is fast, scalable, and suitable for industrial production of
cordycepin and other metabolites.

## Traditional and Modern Use

In traditional Chinese medicine, Cordyceps species have been valued for centuries as
tonic remedies. *C. militaris* has been increasingly adopted as a sustainable, affordable
alternative to [[cordyceps-sinensis]]. Modern research has investigated its applications
in oncology, immunology, neuroprotection, and respiratory health.
