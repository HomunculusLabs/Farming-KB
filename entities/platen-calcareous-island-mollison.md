# Platen (Calcareous Island Hardpan)

## Description

The platen is a hardened layer of calcium triphosphate that forms in the soils of low coral islands (atolls and cays), typically occurring 9 inches to 3 feet below the surface. This cement-like layer is created by the chemical reaction between phosphate accumulations (derived from centuries of seabird guano) and the calcareous coral sand substrate. The platen is the defining agricultural challenge of atoll [[permaculture]] — it blocks rain infiltration, prevents deep root penetration, and severely limits the fresh water lens available to plants.

## Classification

- **Type**: Pedological feature (soil horizon)
- **Composition**: Calcium triphosphate (Ca3(PO4)2)
- **Formation**: Chemical precipitation from phosphate + calcium carbonate reaction
- **Depth**: 9 inches to 36 inches below surface
- **Thickness**: Variable, typically 6-18 inches
- **Geographic occurrence**: Low coral islands, atolls, calcareous cays worldwide

## Key Facts

- Forms from the combination of seabird guano (phosphate source) and coral sand (calcium carbonate source) over geological time.
- Acts as an impermeable barrier to both water infiltration and root penetration, creating conditions of extreme aridity despite tropical rainfall.
- Rain hitting the platen layer follows it laterally and runs off the island, lost to storage — making atolls effectively deserts with fresh water constraints.
- Only a very shallow-rooted agriculture is possible where platen is intact.
- Must be physically broken through (smashed with tools) to establish productive trees on atolls.
- Regenerates within approximately 12 months if broken and left — meaning platen-breaking must be followed immediately with organic matter placement and tree planting.
- Coconut palms can only establish on low islands where the platen has been breached, either naturally or by human intervention.
- Both ancient and modern Polynesian sagas document the deliberate transport and planting of coconut palms on previously barren atolls.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

The platen is the central constraint and design challenge for atoll agriculture:

1. **Platen smashing**: The primary technique for establishing productive gardens on atolls. Sand is shoveled away, the platen broken with picks or bars, [[humus]] added to the hole, and a tree (typically coconut) planted. The tree's roots then keep the platen cracked open permanently.

2. **Ring garden technique**: Mollison's circular garden design specifically addresses platen constraints. Circles of broken platen are filled with mulch and connected to a central drip point, creating efficient wick-watered gardens.

3. **Water table access**: Breaking platen allows the tree to reach the brackish groundwater below, establishing a self-sustaining freshwater zone around the root system.

4. **Progressive reclamation**: Each tree planted creates a zone of permeable platen that expands over time. Within 20 years of systematic tree planting, productive forests can be established on previously barren atoll surfaces.

5. **Concrete potential**: The broken platen material is literally calcium triphosphate concrete and can be used for construction — stone walls, windbreak structures, and tank bases.

## Management Strategies

- **Break and plant**: Smash platen, add organic matter, plant immediately
- **Tree maintenance**: Once established, tree roots prevent platen reformation
- **Avoid removal without replacement**: Removing sand and platen without replanting causes rapid reformation
- **Concentrate effort on key trees**: Focus platen-breaking on strategically important pioneer trees
- **Ring gardens**: Small circular breaches with mulch fill for vegetable production

## Ecological Significance

- The platen is a product of the unique biogeochemistry of seabird colonies on coral substrates
- Represents thousands of years of nutrient accumulation locked in an inaccessible form
- Breaking the platen releases this phosphate for plant uptake
- The fresh water lens perched above salt water on atolls is directly controlled by platen permeability
