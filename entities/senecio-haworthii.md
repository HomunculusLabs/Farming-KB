## Overview

**Senecio haworthii** (Sweet) Sch.Bip. is a small succulent shrublet in the family Asteraceae. It is endemic to the Western and Eastern Cape of South Africa, where it grows on dry, rocky slopes.

The species is immediately recognizable by its pure white, felted stems and leaves. This dense white tomentum gives the entire plant a striking silvery-white appearance unlike any other succulent Senecio.

It is commonly known as "cocoon plant" in the horticultural trade. The name references the way the white felt wraps each leaf like a protective cocoon.

The species was introduced to European horticulture in the early 1800s. It remains a popular collectors' plant prized for its unique foliage texture and color.

It was named in honor of John Haworth, an English botanist and entomologist. Haworth was a specialist in succulent plants and described numerous species from South African collections.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Senecio is one of the largest genera of flowering plants, with over 1,200 species worldwide. Approximately 300 succulent species occur in southern Africa, many adapted to arid conditions.

Senecio haworthii forms an erect to sprawling shrublet 15–30 cm tall. The stems are succulent, cylindrical, and densely covered in white felt-like hairs.

Leaves are alternate, cylindrical to club-shaped, 2–4 cm long and 0.5–1 cm thick. They are completely enveloped in a dense white tomentum that masks the green tissue beneath.

The white felt consists of tangled trichomes that reflect sunlight and reduce transpiration. This adaptation helps the plant survive in exposed, arid habitats with intense solar radiation.

Leaves are brittle and break easily from the stem. In habitat, fallen leaves may root and establish new plants, serving as a vegetative reproduction strategy.

Flower heads are terminal, small, and yellow, typical of the Asteraceae. Each capitulum is about 8–10 mm across with 5–8 ray florets surrounding a disc of tubular florets.

Flowering occurs in late winter to early spring. The yellow flowers emerge in stark contrast against the white foliage, creating a striking display.

After flowering, the main stem typically ceases growth and lateral branches develop. This branching creates the shrubby habit of mature plants.

The flowerheads produce abundant seed in favorable conditions. In cultivation, however, seed set is often poor due to lack of appropriate pollinators.

## Distribution and Habitat

This species is endemic to the Western and Eastern Cape provinces of South Africa. It occurs from the Little Karoo eastward through the Sundays River Valley.

Plants grow on dry, rocky slopes and hillside outcrops in semi-arid vegetation. The habitat receives 200–400 mm of annual rainfall, predominantly in autumn and spring.

The white tomentum is an adaptation to the high light intensity and low humidity of these inland valleys. It reflects excess radiation while trapping a boundary layer of humid air against the leaf surface.

Associated species include other succulent Senecio, *Crassula*, *Aloe*, and *Euphorbia* taxa. The vegetation type is Spekboomveld and Valley Thicket.

Populations are scattered and localized, typically restricted to specific rock formations. Individual colonies may consist of several hundred plants on a single hillside.

The species is not currently listed as threatened. However, habitat degradation from overgrazing and agricultural expansion affects portions of its range.

## Cultivation

Senecio haworthii requires well-drained, gritty potting soil similar to other succulent species. A mix of coarse sand, perlite, and a small proportion of organic material works well.

Water moderately during the active growing period from autumn through spring. Reduce watering significantly during summer when the plant enters a semi-dormant state.

Full sun to bright indirect light is essential for maintaining the compact white growth. Plants grown in shade develop sparse, greenish growth with reduced tomentum density.

The species is sensitive to overwatering and cold, damp conditions. Root and stem rot are the most common causes of death in cultivation, particularly during winter.

Propagation is easy from stem cuttings. Allow cut surfaces to callus for 2–3 days before placing on moist substrate. Roots develop within 1–2 weeks at temperatures above 18 °C.

Leaf propagation is also possible but slower than stem cuttings. Individual leaves root in 3–4 weeks but may take several months to produce a new shoot.

The species is relatively pest-free when grown in appropriate conditions. Mealybugs occasionally colonize stem axils but are easily controlled with targeted treatment.

## Ecology

The dense white tomentum serves multiple ecological functions. It reflects excess solar radiation, reduces water loss through transpiration, and may deter herbivores by making the leaves unpalatable.

CAM photosynthesis is employed for efficient water use. This allows the plant to maintain turgor during extended dry periods between rainfall events.

Flowers are pollinated by small bees, flies, and beetles. The composite flower head provides an accessible platform for a range of generalist pollinators.

Achenes (seeds) are small, lightweight, and equipped with a pappus for wind dispersal. This enables colonization of suitable rock outcrops at some distance from the parent plant.

The species contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids, a class of compounds common in Senecio. These alkaloids are hepatotoxic to mammals and serve as a chemical defense against herbivory.

Despite the toxicity, some specialist insects feed on Senecio species. These insects often sequester the alkaloids for their own defense against predators.

## Horticultural Significance

The unique white foliage makes Senecio haworthii one of the most distinctive succulents in cultivation. It provides strong contrast in mixed succulent arrangements and container plantings.

The species has received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit. This reflects its reliability and ornamental appeal for general horticulture.

It is frequently used in succulent dish gardens and living picture frames. The white stems create dramatic contrast when paired with dark-leaved succulents like *Aeonium* 'Zwartkop'.

Plants remain compact for several years but may become leggy with age. Regular pruning maintains shape and provides material for propagation.

Individual plants have a lifespan of approximately 5–8 years in cultivation. Older plants tend to lose lower leaves and develop bare stems.

The species has been grown in European collections since the early nineteenth century. Illustrations appear in Curtis's Botanical Magazine from 1823, testifying to its long horticultural history.

It is one of the most cold-tolerant succulent Senecio species. Plants survive brief exposure to −4 °C if kept completely dry.
