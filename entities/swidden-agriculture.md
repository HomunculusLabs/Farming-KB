## Swidden Agriculture (Slash-and-Burn)

## Description

Swidden agriculture, also known as slash-and-burn or shifting cultivation, is an agricultural system where small areas of forest are cleared, burned, and planted with diverse perennial crops forming food forests. After several years, the garden is abandoned to regenerate to forest. David Holmgren describes the New Guinea highland version as "the most energy-efficient form of agriculture documented by scientists."

## Background and Classification

- **Type:** Agricultural system / Cultivation technique
- **Alternative names:** Slash-and-burn, shifting cultivation, rotational bush fallow
- **Geographic distribution:** New Guinea, Southeast Asia, Amazon basin, sub-Saharan Africa
- **Historical depth:** Thousands of years
- **Ecological basis:** Pulsing ecosystem model — alternating production and accumulation phases

## Key Facts

- Small areas of rainforest are felled, burned, and planted to diverse perennial crops
- Food gardens mimic rainforest structure with multiple canopy layers: tree, shrub, vine, and root crops
- Multi-layered planting minimises nutrient leaching
- Retained forest trees called "mother of the gardens" in recognition of their role in regeneration
- Pre-contact New Guinea highlands: sustainably supported dense populations
- Critical rotation time: approximately 20 years to fully rebuild fertility
- Garden-to-forest ratio always small (roughly 1:5 to 1:10)
- Now threatened as population pressure shortens fallow periods below sustainable thresholds

## Relevance to Permaculture and Cultivation

- **Pulsing ecosystem model:** The most evolved sustainable example of alternating production (exploitation) and rebuilding (conservation) phases
- **Food forest design:** Multi-layered food garden structure directly anticipates Permaculture food forests
- **Successional dynamics:** Practitioners use natural successional processes rather than fighting them
- **Nutrient conservation:** Layered canopy prevents leaching in high-rainfall tropical environments
- **Energy efficiency:** Benchmark for low-input Permaculture systems
- **Critical lesson:** "The benefits from the pulse are dependent on the slow accumulation of the building phase; pulsing too frequently leads to a downward spiral of land degradation"
