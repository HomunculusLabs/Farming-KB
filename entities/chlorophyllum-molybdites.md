## Taxonomy

Basidiomycota → Agaricomycetes → Agaricales → **Agaricaceae → *Chlorophyllum → *C.
molybdites***

Described as *Agaricus molybdites* by G. Meyer in 1818, transferred to
*Lepiota* by Quélet, and placed in *Chlorophyllum* by Massee in 1898. The genus
name refers to the distinctive green spore color (Greek: *chloros* = green,
*phyllon* = leaf). The specific epithet *molybdites* derives from Latin for
lead-colored, referring to the grayish-green gill color at maturity. Molecular
phylogenetics confirms *C. molybdites* is distinct from the type species *C.
rhacodes* and its close relatives.

## Morphology

- **Cap**: 8–30 cm diameter, oval when young expanding to broadly convex or
  nearly flat with age. Surface dry, white to cream, covered with
  concentrically-arranged brown to tawny scales that are more densely packed
  toward the center. The scales are easily removable. Margin often appendiculate
  with remnants of the partial veil.
- **Gills**: White when young, gradually turning grayish-green to dark green
  (the critical diagnostic feature) as spores mature. Free from the stipe, close
  to crowded. This green color distinguishes it from all edible lookalikes.
- **Stipe**: 10–25 cm long, 1–3 cm thick, with a bulbous base. Surface white,
  smooth above the ring, sometimes scaly below. The ring is large, movable

- **[[macrolepiota-procera]]*** (parasol mushroom): Edible and highly prized.
  White spore print, snakeskin pattern on stipe below the ring, smaller
  scales on cap. Generally found in temperate woodlands rather than tropical
  grasslands.
- ***Chlorophyllum rhacodes*** (shaggy parasol): Edible for some but causes
  gastrointestinal upset in others. White spore print, more scaly cap with
  more upright scales.
- ***[[lepiota-brunneoincarnata]]*** (deadly dapperling): Contains amatoxins;
  potentially fatal. Much smaller (cap 2–5 cm), white spore print. Causes
  delayed-onset liver failure rather than rapid GI symptoms.

## Ecological Role

As a saprotrophic decomposer, *C. molybdites* breaks down organic matter in
grassland soils, contributing to nutrient cycling. The species thrives in
disturbed habitats — lawns, agricultural fields, and managed grass areas —
suggesting it benefits from the nutrient-rich, frequently irrigated conditions
of human-maintained landscapes. Despite its toxicity to humans, the mushroom
is consumed without apparent harm by some animals including slugs and certain
insects.
