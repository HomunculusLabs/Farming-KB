## Chlorophyllum molybdites

## Description

Chlorophyllum molybdites, commonly known as the green-spored parasol, is a poisonous mushroom that is considered the most common cause of mushroom poisoning in North America. It closely resembles the edible parasol mushroom ([[macrolepiota-procera]]) and other [[lepiota]] species, making it a dangerous lookalike that cultivators and foragers must be able to identify. The key distinguishing feature is its distinctive green [[spore-print]], which is rare among gilled mushrooms and serves as a definitive identification characteristic.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Agaricaceae
- **Genus:** Chlorophyllum
- **Species:** C. molybdites

## Key Facts

- The most common cause of mushroom poisoning in North America
- Poisonous — causes severe gastrointestinal distress
- Closely resembles the edible parasol mushroom (Macrolepiota procera)
- Distinguished by its green [[spore]] print — a rare and definitive identifier
- Found in temperate to tropical regions, particularly in grassy areas and lawns
- Often fruits in fairy rings or scattered groups in fertilized yards and fields
- Cap is mostly white with larger scales compared to the true parasol
- Commonly encountered by amateur foragers who mistake it for edible Lepiota species

## Identification — Distinguishing from Edible Parasol

The green-spored parasol can look similar to the true parasol (Macrolepiota procera), but key differences include:

| Feature | C. molybdites (Poisonous) | M. procera (Edible) |
|---------|--------------------------|---------------------|
| Spore print | **Green** (definitive) | Creamy white |
| Cap color | Mostly white | Light brown, darker in middle |
| Cap scales | Larger scales | Finer scales and fibrils |
| Habitat | Lawns, grassy areas | Woodland edges, composting grass |
| Stem ring | Present | Double-edged annulus, movable |

### Critical Identification Steps

1. **Always take a spore print** — the green spore print of C. molybdites is unmistakable
2. Check cap coloration — mostly white suggests C. molybdites
3. Examine scale size — larger scales point toward C. molybdites
4. Note habitat — lawn fruitings in suburban areas are suspicious

## Toxicity

- Causes severe gastrointestinal poisoning
- Symptoms typically include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal cramps
- Onset usually within 1–3 hours of consumption
- Poisoning is rarely fatal but can be severely debilitating
- Most commonly implicated species in North American mushroom poisoning cases
- Children and pets are particularly at risk due to fruiting in residential lawns

## Ecology

- Saprobic fungus decomposing organic matter in soil
- Fruits in grassy areas, lawns, parks, and pastures
- Often appears after heavy rains in warm weather
- Can form fairy rings
- Found across North America, especially in temperate to subtropical regions
- Common in the southeastern United States

## Importance for Cultivators

Anyone cultivating Lepiota or Macrolepiota species must be able to distinguish their crop from C. molybdites:

- When collecting wild specimens for cloning, always verify identity with a spore print
- Wild parasol collections should be double-checked with expert mycologists
- NAMA-sponsored mushroom hunts (namyco.org) provide expert identification
- Never culture or distribute specimens without confirming they are not C. molybdites
- If cultivating outdoors near grassy areas, monitor for C. molybdites contamination
- The importance of proper identification cannot be overstated for species in the Lepiota/Macrolepiota complex

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

C. molybdites serves as a critical cautionary species in [[mushroom-cultivation]] and foraging. Its close resemblance to edible parasol mushrooms makes it one of the most important toxic lookalikes that any cultivator working with Lepiota or Macrolepiota species must recognize. The green spore print is the definitive diagnostic feature and should always be checked when working with any parasol-type mushroom. Understanding this species is essential for safe wild specimen collection, outdoor cultivation near grassy habitats, and responsible mushroom education.
