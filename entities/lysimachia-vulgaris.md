# Lysimachia vulgaris

## Overview

Lysimachia vulgaris, commonly known as yellow loosestrife or garden loosestrife, is a herbaceous perennial plant in the family Primulaceae. It is native to Europe and western Asia, where it inhabits wetlands, stream margins, and damp meadows.

The species is a tall, robust plant that produces conspicuous spikes of bright yellow flowers in midsummer. It is one of the most visually prominent plants of European wetland margins.

Despite its common name, yellow loosestrife is unrelated to purple loosestrife, Lythrum salicaria. The shared name arises from historical medicinal use rather than botanical relationship.

Lysimachia vulgaris has been introduced to [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], where it is considered invasive in some wetland habitats. It can form dense monotypic stands that displace native vegetation.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Lysimachia vulgaris was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum. The genus Lysimachia contains approximately 180 species distributed primarily across temperate and subtropical regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

The generic name honors King Lysimachus of Thrace, who is said to have discovered the plant's [[panax-ginseng]]. The specific epithet vulgaris means common, reflecting the plant's widespread distribution.

The plant grows from a creeping rhizome that produces multiple erect stems. Stems are unbranched, 60 to 150 centimeters tall, and prominently angled or winged.

Leaves are arranged in whorls of three to four at each node along the upper stem. Lower stem leaves may be opposite and are broadly lanceolate to ovate.

Each leaf is 5 to 12 centimeters long with a pointed tip and finely toothed margin. The leaf surface is dotted with translucent or reddish glands visible under magnification.

The inflorescence is a terminal panicle of leafy cymes bearing numerous star-shaped yellow flowers. Flowers are 12 to 18 millimeters in diameter with five broadly ovate petals.

Petals are bright yellow and often marked with reddish veins at the base. The calyx is deeply five-lobed with reddish margins on the lobes.

Stamens are conspicuously exserted beyond the corolla, forming a prominent tuft. This feature distinguishes Lysimachia vulgaris from several related species with shorter stamens.

The fruit is a globose capsule approximately 5 millimeters in diameter containing numerous small seeds. Seeds are reddish-brown and angular in shape.

## Distribution and Habitat

Lysimachia vulgaris is native to Europe and temperate Asia, occurring from the British Isles and France eastward to Siberia and Japan. It is widespread across central and northern Europe.

In the British Isles, the species is common in lowland England and eastern Scotland. It becomes more scattered in western Scotland, Wales, and Ireland.

The plant is strongly associated with wetland habitats including stream banks, pond margins, marshes, and damp woodland. It tolerates standing water in spring and prefers nutrient-rich soils.

Lysimachia vulgaris is also found in damp meadows, ditches, and railway embankments. It is tolerant of moderate disturbance and can colonize recently cleared wet ground.

The species was introduced to North America in the early 19th century as an ornamental garden plant. It has since naturalized in wetlands across the northeastern United States and the [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]].

In North America, Lysimachia vulgaris can form extensive clonal colonies along waterways. Its competitive ability is enhanced by its vigorous rhizomatous growth habit.

The plant thrives in full sun to partial shade and is winter-hardy to USDA zone 3. It tolerates a range of soil pH conditions from slightly acidic to moderately alkaline.

## Ecology

Lysimachia vulgaris is pollinated by medium-tongued bees, hoverflies, and other generalist pollinators. The bright yellow flowers and exserted stamens make them highly visible to insect visitors.

The flowers produce modest quantities of nectar as a pollinator reward. The prominent anthers produce pollen that adheres to the bodies of visiting insects.

The species plays a role in riparian stabilization due to its extensive rhizome network. The root mass helps bind soil along stream banks and prevents erosion during high-flow events.

Several insect herbivores feed on Lysimachia vulgaris. The chrysomelid beetle Galerucella calmariensis, known primarily as a biocontrol agent for purple loosestrife, also feeds on this species.

The foliage is a food source for larvae of several moth species. Leaf-mining flies of the genus Phytomyza have been recorded creating mines in the leaves.

In [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] wetlands, dense stands of Lysimachia vulgaris can reduce plant diversity. The species shades out smaller native plants and may alter soil chemistry through allelopathic root exudates.

Fungal pathogens recorded on the species include rust fungi of the genus Uromyces and [[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]] caused by Erysiphe species. These pathogens rarely cause significant damage.

## Cultivation

Lysimachia vulgaris is cultivated as an ornamental plant for wetland and wildlife gardens. Its tall stature and bright summer flowers make it effective at the back of herbaceous borders.

The plant prefers consistently moist soil and tolerates shallow standing water. It performs best in full sun but tolerates partial shade in hotter climates.

Propagation is straightforward from seed sown in spring or autumn. Division of established clumps in early spring is the most common [[hamilton-vegetative-propagation]] method.
