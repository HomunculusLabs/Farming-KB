The species produces edible nuts that are smaller but similarly flavored to commercially grown European hazelnuts from [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of the Northern Hemisphere.

American hazelnut typically grows as a multi-stemmed shrub 2 to 4 meters tall, occasionally reaching 5 meters under favorable conditions.

The plant forms dense clonal thickets through extensive rhizomatous root systems that can spread several meters from the parent.

Stems are slender, gray-brown, and covered with glandular hairs when young, becoming smoother with age.

Leaves are alternate, broadly ovate to cordate, 6 to 14 centimeters long, with doubly serrate margins and a rounded to cordate base.

The leaf surface is dark green and rugose above, paler beneath, with prominent lateral veins.

American hazelnut is monoecious, bearing separate male and female flowers on the same plant during late winter to early spring.

Male catkins are pendulous, cylindrical, 5 to 10 centimeters long, and develop in the previous growing season before opening in late winter.

Female flowers are tiny red stigmas barely visible at the tips of swollen buds, appearing before leaf emergence.

The fruit is a hard-shelled nut 1 to 1.5 centimeters in diameter, enclosed in a tubular involucre that extends 1 to 2 centimeters beyond the nut.

Nuts ripen in late summer to early autumn, turning brown as the involucres dry and split open.

## Distribution and Habitat

Corylus americana ranges from Maine and Nova Scotia west to Minnesota, [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] forest ecosystems.

The nuts are consumed by numerous bird species including wild turkey, ruffed grouse, woodpeckers, blue jays, and red-bellied woodpeckers.

Mammalian consumers include white-tailed deer, black bear, fox squirrel, gray squirrel, eastern chipmunk, red fox, and raccoon.

Deer also browse twigs and foliage during winter months when other food sources are scarce.

The dense thicket-forming habit provides cover and nesting habitat for songbirds, rabbits, and other small wildlife.

American hazelnut is a larval host plant for several moth species, including the hazel sphinx moth and the American dagger moth.

The catkins provide early-season pollen for bees and other pollinating insects emerging from winter dormancy.

Root systems contribute to soil stabilization on slopes and stream banks, reducing erosion in riparian areas.
