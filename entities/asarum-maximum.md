## Overview

**Asarum maximum** is a species of flowering plant in the family Aristolochiaceae, native to central and southeastern China. It is commonly known as giant wild ginger or panda face ginger, referring to the distinctive panda-like patterning on its flowers.

The species is among the largest-flowered members of the genus Asarum, with calyx tubes reaching 3 to 5 centimeters in diameter. Its dramatic, boldly marked flowers have made it one of the most sought-after species for shade gardens.

Asarum maximum was first described by William Botting Hemsley in 1887 based on specimens collected from Hubei Province. The specific epithet "maximum" refers to the unusually large floral size relative to other Asarum species.

Unlike many Asarum species that produce small, cryptic flowers at ground level, the flowers of Asarum maximum are prominently displayed above the foliage on short peduncles. This makes the species particularly attractive to horticulturists.

The species is evergreen in mild climates, forming low mounds of glossy, heart-shaped leaves that persist through winter. It is closely related to Asarum delavayi and Asarum crispulatum of southwestern China.

In cultivation, the species has received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit in the United Kingdom. It is considered one of the most cold-tolerant of the large-flowered Chinese Asarum species.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Asarum maximum belongs to the subgenus Asarum, section Asarum. It is placed within the Asarum splendens complex of large-flowered Chinese species that share bold floral markings.

The rhizome is stout, creeping, and slightly aromatic. It produces numerous fibrous roots and forms dense clumps over time, reaching a spread of 30 to 45 centimeters after several years of growth.

Leaf blades are broadly ovate to cordate, 6 to 12 centimeters long and 7 to 14 centimeters wide. The leaf surface is dark green and glossy above, often with paler green marbling or variegation along the veins.

The flowers are the most striking feature of this species. The calyx tube is broadly campanulate to urceolate, 2.5 to 4 centimeters in diameter and 2 to 3 centimeters deep.

The exterior of the calyx is greenish to purplish with white flecking. The interior is white with bold purple-black markings that create a pattern reminiscent of a panda face, giving rise to the common name.

The three sepals are broadly triangular with rounded tips and short, blunt tails extending 5 to 15 millimeters. This contrasts sharply with the long-tailed sepals of species like Asarum caudatum.

The ovary is inferior, six-celled, with numerous ovules per cell. The fruit is a globose capsule approximately 1.2 centimeters in diameter that splits open at maturity to release seeds.

Seeds are relatively large for the genus, measuring 3 to 4 millimeters in length. The elaiosome is conspicuous and white, constituting approximately one-third of the seed mass.

## Distribution and Habitat

Asarum maximum is endemic to China, occurring in the provinces of Hubei, Hunan, Sichuan, Guizhou, and Guangxi. It is found in mountainous regions at elevations between 500 and 1,800 meters.

The species grows in the understory of broadleaf and mixed forests, often on shaded slopes, ravines, and stream banks. It prefers sites with deep, well-drained, humus-rich soils derived from limestone or sandstone parent material.

The natural climate features warm, humid summers with abundant rainfall and cool, moist winters. Annual precipitation in the native range typically exceeds 1,200 millimeters.

Asarum maximum is adapted to the warm-temperate to subtropical climate of central-southern China. It is less cold-hardy than northern species such as Asarum canadense or Asarum europaeum.

Populations are generally scattered rather than forming extensive colonies. The species is most common in areas of old-growth forest with well-developed canopy cover and deep litter layers.

Deforestation and habitat fragmentation have reduced available habitat for Asarum maximum in parts of its range. The species is listed as near-threatened in some provincial red lists due to collection pressure from horticultural trade.

## Cultivation

Asarum maximum has become one of the most popular wild ginger species in temperate horticulture. It is prized for its bold floral display, attractive foliage, and relative ease of cultivation compared to other Chinese species.

The plant performs best in partial to full shade in consistently moist, well-drained soil rich in organic matter. A slightly acidic to neutral pH of 5.5 to 7.0 is preferred.

Hardiness is rated at USDA Zone 7, tolerating temperatures to approximately -15 degrees Celsius. In colder zones, a thick winter mulch of leaf litter or pine needles provides adequate protection.

Propagation is primarily through rhizome division in spring or early autumn. Divisions should include at least one growth bud and a section of healthy rhizome with attached roots.

Seed germination requires a warm-cold-warm stratification cycle simulating natural conditions. Fresh seeds sown in autumn and kept moist may germinate the following spring after a cold period.

In cultivation, the plant forms an attractive ground cover reaching 15 to 20 centimeters in height. It combines well with ferns, hostas, hellebores, and other shade-tolerant perennials in woodland garden settings.

The species is relatively pest-free, though slugs and snails may damage new growth in damp conditions. Crown rot can develop in poorly drained soils, making adequate drainage essential for long-term cultivation success.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

Like other Aristolochiaceae species, Asarum maximum contains aristolochic acids, which are characteristic secondary metabolites of the family. Aristolochic acids I and II have been detected in rhizome extracts.

The essential oil of the rhizome contains a complex mixture of monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes. Major constituents include elemicin, asarone, linalool, and various pinene derivatives.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis has identified over 50 volatile compounds in the rhizome oil. The unique chemical profile distinguishes Asarum maximum from related species and may have chemotaxonomic value.

Aristolochic acids are known to be nephrotoxic and have been classified as Group 1 carcinogens by the International Agency for Research on Cancer. Consumption of plant material containing these compounds is associated with aristolochic acid nephropathy and urothelial carcinoma.

Traditional Chinese medicine has historically used related Asarum species, but modern pharmacopoeias have largely removed aristolochic acid-containing herbs from approved formulations. Extracts of Asarum maximum are not used in contemporary herbal practice.

Research has investigated the anti-inflammatory and antifungal properties of Asarum essential oils. However, the toxicity of aristolochic acids limits any potential therapeutic applications of crude extracts.

## Ecological Role

In its native habitat, Asarum maximum contributes to the diversity of the forest floor herb layer. The dense leaf canopy suppresses weed growth and helps maintain soil moisture in the shaded understory.

The flowers are pollinated by small flies attracted to the musty scent and dark coloration of the calyx interior. The pollination mechanism is similar to other Asarum species, with insects entering the calyx tube to reach nectar glands.

Flowering typically occurs from March to May in the native range, with individual flowers lasting 2 to 3 weeks. The flowering period may extend longer in cultivation under favorable conditions.

Seed dispersal is facilitated by ants, which are attracted to the elaiosome attached to each seed. This myrmecochorous dispersal strategy helps distribute seeds to favorable microsites away from the parent plant.

The species provides minimal direct forage value for wildlife due to the presence of toxic aristolochic acids. However, it serves as a potential larval host for pipevine swallowtail butterflies in regions where these insects occur.

The dense leaf litter created by senescing Asarum maximum leaves contributes to soil organic matter accumulation. This improves water retention and provides a substrate for decomposer organisms essential to forest nutrient cycling.
