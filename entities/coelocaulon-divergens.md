## Description

**Coelocaulon divergens** (syn. *Cetraria divergens*) is a fruticose terricolous lichen found in arctic and boreal tundra ecosystems. It was identified during post-Chernobyl monitoring in Norway as one of the soil-dwelling lichen species with significant **radiocesium (137Cs) accumulation**, contributing to the transfer of radioactive contamination through the lichen–reindeer food chain in Scandinavian ecosystems.

## Key Facts

- **Radiocesium accumulation:** Following Chernobyl, C. divergens collected from soil in Norway in 1986 contained 16,683 Bq/kg of 137Cs (Gaare, 1990), making it one of the more heavily contaminated terricolous lichen species in the survey.
- **Reindeer forage component:** As a terricolous (ground-dwelling) lichen in tundra ecosystems, C. divergens forms part of the winter diet of reindeer, contributing to radionuclide transfer to grazing animals and subsequently to human populations dependent on reindeer products.
- **Tundra ecosystem biomonitoring:** Its presence and contamination levels serve as indicators of radioactive fallout patterns in northern Scandinavian ecosystems.
- **Intermediate contamination level:** Its 137Cs content of 16,683 Bq/kg placed it in the mid-range of terricolous lichens surveyed, between Cladina mitis (12,389 Bq/kg) and Alectoria ochroleuca (24,005 Bq/kg).

## Ecological Role

C. divergens is a component of [[biological-soil-indicators]] and lichen mats in arctic and subarctic tundra. It contributes to soil stabilization, nutrient cycling, and provides winter forage for reindeer and caribou populations. As a slow-growing organism in harsh environments, it integrates long-term atmospheric deposition signals, making it an effective biomonitor for persistent environmental contaminants.

The species is typically found in open, exposed tundra habitats where it forms part of the diverse lichen community that covers the ground between vascular plants. These lichen mats serve multiple ecological functions: they fix carbon through photosynthesis, retain moisture, protect soil from erosion, and provide food for herbivores.

## Chernobyl and the Lichen–Reindeer–Human Chain

The contamination of C. divergens and other terricolous lichens following Chernobyl illustrates a critical pathway for radionuclide transfer in northern ecosystems:

1. **Atmospheric deposition** deposited 137Cs across northern Scandinavia
2. **Lichen accumulation** concentrated the radionuclide in lichen biomass (mean activities of 6,700–24,000 Bq/kg across sites)
3. **Reindeer consumption** transferred contamination to grazing animals, with lichens forming about 40% of annual food intake
4. **Human exposure** occurred through consumption of reindeer meat, particularly affecting Sami herding communities

## Relevance to Mycology and Cultivation

- **Radioecology:** Illustrates the role of terricolous lichens as intermediaries in the transfer of atmospheric radionuclide contamination to higher trophic levels.
- **Sami pastoralism impact:** The contamination of lichen species including C. divergens after Chernobyl had direct impacts on indigenous reindeer herding cultures and food safety in Scandinavia.
- **Ecosystem monitoring:** Serves as a long-term integrator of atmospheric radioactive contamination in remote northern environments where instrumental monitoring is sparse.
- **Soil crust ecology:** Important for understanding biological soil crust dynamics in tundra ecosystems.

## Distinguishing Features

- Fruticose, branching thallus growing from a basal attachment point on soil
- Terricolous habitat — grows on soil in open tundra and heathland
- Dark brown to olive-brown coloration with divergent branching pattern
- Forms mats intermixed with other tundra lichen species including Cladina and Cetraria species
- Robust branches compared to more delicate Cladina species

## Habitat and Distribution

C. divergens is distributed across arctic and subarctic regions of the Northern Hemisphere, including northern Scandinavia, Siberia, Canada, and Alaska. It is typically found on acid soils in open tundra, heathland, and exposed ridges where it forms part of the ground-covering lichen mat. The species prefers well-drained, nutrient-poor substrates and is characteristic of mature, stable lichen heath communities.

## Comparison with Related Species

Within the Norwegian Chernobyl monitoring survey, C. divergens occupied an intermediate position in radiocesium accumulation among terricolous lichens. Its 16,683 Bq/kg was higher than Cladina mitis (12,389 Bq/kg) but lower than Alectoria ochroleuca (24,005 Bq/kg) and Cetraria nivalis (24,286 Bq/kg). These differences may reflect variations in growth form, surface area, moisture retention, and thallus chemistry that affect radionuclide interception and retention.
