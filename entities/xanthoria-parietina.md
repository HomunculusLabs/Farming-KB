# Xanthoria parietina

**Xanthoria parietina** is a golden-yellow, almost ubiquitous lichen-forming ascomycete.

It is one of the most recognizable and widespread [[lichens]] in the world, occurring on tree bark, stone, and other substrates from boreal to temperate climates.

The species has served as an important model organism for understanding lichen water relations, hydrophobin biology, and environmental stress tolerance.

## Taxonomy and Classification

X. parietina belongs to the class Lecanoromycetes within the Ascomycota.

The genus *Xanthoria* comprises species characterized by their bright yellow to orange pigmentation, derived from the secondary metabolite parietin.

Phylogenetic studies of hydrophobin (H1) gene sequences among Xanthoria species have helped clarify species relationships within the genus.

Molecular markers have proven valuable for resolving species boundaries in lichenized fungi.

Key taxonomic details include:

- **Phylum:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Lecanoromycetes
- **Order:** Teloschistales
- **Family:** Teloschistaceae

## Morphology and Identification

Xanthoria parietina forms large, foliose (leaf-like) thalli that are typically golden yellow to orange in colour.

The colour is owing to the parietin (a secondary metabolite, also known as physcion) deposited in the cortex.

The thallus has an internally stratified structure with *Trebouxia* species as the green algal photobiont.

The fungal partner usually amounts to more than 80 percent of thalline biomass, reflecting the dominant role of the mycobiont in the symbiosis.

The lobes are typically 3 to 8 mm wide with crenulate margins.

Apothecia are common, orange to red, with a thalline margin.

## Wall Surface Hydrophobicity and Hydrophobins

X. parietina has been central to understanding water-repellency mechanisms in lichens.

Unlike many lichens that rely on crystallized secondary metabolites in the medullary layer for hydrophobicity, X. parietina lacks medullary lichen compounds yet maintains water-repellent surfaces.

In freeze-etch preparations, a semicrystalline rodlet layer was resolved on wall surfaces of medullary hyphae of vegetative thalli.

These rodlet layers are formed by class 1 hydrophobins, small cysteine-rich proteins that self-assemble at hydrophobic-hydrophilic interfaces to form amphipathic films.

The hydrophobin of X. parietina (XPH1) is one per species in haploid mycelia.

Hydrophobins have limited sequence similarity and are defined primarily by the presence of eight conserved cysteine residues and their ability to self-assemble into amphipathic monolayers.

Hydrophobin-based wall surface hydrophobicity at the mycobiont-photobiont interface allows the lichen to control water movement between the symbiotic partners.

This ensures that the photobiont receives adequate hydration while preventing waterlogging.

## Environmental Stress Tolerance

The impressive environmental stress tolerance of lichen-forming fungi lies in their poikilohydric water relations.

Desiccated cells can survive temperature extremes unharmed in a state of dormancy.

Desiccated thalli of X. parietina grew normally after cryoimmobilization in sub-cooled liquid nitrogen (approximately -200 degrees C).

They also survived freeze-fracturing, sputter coating, and examination in the low-temperature scanning electron microscope under high vacuum conditions at 60 kV.

However, hydrated thalli did not survive this treatment.

Most lichen-forming fungi and their photobionts are fully adapted to continuous wetting and drying cycles during diurnal and seasonal fluctuations in water availability.

They die off under continuously moist conditions or when prevented from drying at elevated temperatures.

## Distribution and Ecology

Abundant epiphytic lichen growth, including X. parietina, is typically found in boreal to temperate climates.

In temperate rainforests, bryophytes tend to dominate epiphytic communities.

In tropical rainforests, tracheophytes (ferns, bromeliads, orchids) are the primary epiphytes.

Approximately 85 percent of lichen-forming ascomycetes have green algal photobionts (chlorolichens) like X. parietina.

X. parietina is notably tolerant of nitrogen enrichment and is often found in areas with elevated nutrient deposition.

It is a common sight on trees near farms and along roadsides.

## Photobiont Interface

At the mycobiont-photobiont interface, X. parietina creates a complex wall structure with multiple layers.

The photobiont has access to water and dissolved nutrients only via passive apoplastic fluxes within the quantitatively dominant fungal exhabitant.

The mycobiont-derived hydrophobin layer overlays the interface, with additional compounds in some but not all contact areas.

This asymmetric arrangement gives the fungal partner control over the movement of water and solutes to the photobiont.

## Secondary Metabolites

Parietin, the characteristic orange pigment of X. parietina, belongs to the anthraquinone class of secondary metabolites.

It serves a protective function, absorbing ultraviolet radiation and potentially deterring herbivory.

The concentration of parietin can vary with light exposure, being higher in sun-exposed thalli.

This phenotypic plasticity in secondary metabolite production is a feature shared with many other lichen species.
