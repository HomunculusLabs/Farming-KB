## Cortinarius armillatus

## Description

*Cortinarius armillatus* (commonly known as the red-banded cortinarius or bracelet cort) is a medium to large basidiomycete fungus in the family Cortinariaceae, order Agaricales. It is an **ectomycorrhizal** species, forming mutualistic associations primarily with birch (*Betula*) and sometimes spruce (*Picea*) in boreal and temperate forests. The cap is reddish-brown to ochre-brown (4–12 cm), and the stem is distinctive for its bright orange-red to brick-red bands (armillate zones) — the feature that gives the species its epithet. Like all *Cortinarius* species, it has a rusty-brown [[spore-print]] and a cortina (cobweb-like partial veil).

## Key Facts

- Recorded as having extremely high **radiocesium** (¹³⁴,¹³⁷Cs) activity levels of **43–44 kBq/kg dry weight** in fruiting bodies after the Chernobyl disaster (Byrne, 1988).
- This level is up to **80 times** the European Community safety limit of 600 Bq/kg for foodstuffs.
- Members of the Cortinariaceae are known to be **Cs (cesium) accumulators** — Byrne (1988) specifically highlighted this family for their radiocesium accumulation.
- Ectomycorrhizal associate primarily of birch, contributing to nutrient exchange in boreal forest ecosystems.
- In Belgian ecosystems post-Chernobyl, *C. armillatus* showed radiocesium content of 8,200–24,300 Bq/kg, among the highest of all organisms measured (Fraiture et al., 1990).
- A significant portion (25–92%) of ¹³⁷Cs in fungal fruiting bodies originated from **pre-Chernobyl sources** (nuclear weapons testing), indicating long-term retention by fungi (Dighton and Horrill, 1988).

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

*Cortinarius armillatus* is one of the most significant species in radioecology research:

- **Radiocesium biomonitoring**: As a top-tier radiocesium accumulator, it serves as one of the most sensitive biological indicators of radioactive contamination in forest ecosystems.
- **Food chain transfer**: The extremely high radiocesium levels make this species a critical node in understanding how radioactive contamination moves from soil through fungi to animals and humans.
- **Long-term radionuclide retention**: Data showing pre-Chernobyl radiocesium accumulation demonstrates that fungi can retain radionuclides for decades, acting as long-term environmental reservoirs.
- **Cortinariaceae physiology**: The family's exceptional Cs accumulation appears related to their ectomycorrhizal lifestyle and specific ion transport mechanisms that favor cesium uptake, sometimes even preferentially over potassium.
- **Mycorrhizal ecology**: Understanding how [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] process radionuclides is essential for predicting contamination patterns in forests following nuclear accidents.
- **Public health**: In countries where wild mushroom foraging is culturally important (Scandinavia, Eastern Europe), species like *C. armillatus* represent significant dietary sources of radiation exposure.

This species exemplifies how mycorrhizal fungi can dramatically concentrate environmental contaminants, creating hotspots of radioactivity in food webs that persist for decades after initial contamination events.
