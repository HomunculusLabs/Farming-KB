# Scleroderma citrinum

## Overview

Scleroderma citrinum, commonly known as the common earthball or pigskin
puffball, is a widespread ectomycorrhizal fungus in the order Boletales. It is
one of the most frequently encountered gasteroid (puffball-like) fungi in
temperate forests of the Northern Hemisphere, recognizable by its tough, warty,
yellowish-brown outer surface and dark purple-black internal spore mass at
maturity. Despite superficial resemblance to edible puffballs (Lycoperdon spp.),
Scleroderma citrinum is toxic and has caused gastrointestinal poisoning in
cases of mistaken consumption.

The genus name derives from Greek "skleros" (hard) and "derma" (skin),
accurately describing the firm, thick peridium that distinguishes earthballs
from true puffballs. The specific epithet "citrinum" refers to the lemon-yellow
color of young specimens.

## Taxonomy

**Kingdom:** Fungi → **Phylum:** Basidiomycota → **Class:** Agaricomycetes →
**Order:** Boletales → **Family:** Sclerodermataceae → **Genus:** *Scleroderma*
→ **Species:** *S. citrinum* Pers.

Synonyms include *Scleroderma aurantium* (Vaill.) Pers., *S. vulgare*
Hornem., and *S. geaster* Fr. Molecular phylogenetics confirm Scleroderma as
a member of Boletales rather than Agaricales, placing it closer to boletes
than to true puffballs, supported by bolete-type spores and shared
ectomycorrhizal ecology.

## Morphology

The fruiting body is a gasteroid basidiocarp with a subglobose to irregular
shape, 2–8 cm diameter, attached to the substrate by a short, stout mycelial
cord (rhizomorph). The peridium is thick, tough, and leathery when young; the
surface is covered with coarse, irregular warts or scales giving a pigskin-like
texture. Color ranges from pale yellowish-buff to ochre-yellow in young
specimens, darkening to brown with age.

The gleba (inner spore mass) is firm, white, and marbled with lighter veins
when young; it darkens through olive-brown to deep purple-black at maturity.
Spores are spherical to broadly ellipsoid, 8–14 μm diameter, dark brown,
thick-walled, covered with fine to coarse spines or reticulations. Columella
is absent or reduced to a small sterile base, distinguishing
Sclerodermataceae from Lycoperdaceae. Odor is indistinct when young, developing
a strong, unpleasant, somewhat fetid smell at maturity.

## Distribution and Habitat

Scleroderma citrinum has a broad distribution across temperate and boreal
regions of the Northern Hemisphere — throughout Europe, North America (Alaska to
Mexico), and temperate Asia (China, Japan, Korea), with reports from Australia
and South America as introduced. It occurs on the ground in deciduous and
coniferous forests, woodland edges, heathlands, and occasionally grassy areas,
strongly associated with ectomycorrhizal hosts: oaks (*Quercus*), pines
(*Pinus*), birches (*Betula*), and beech (*Fagus*). It prefers acidic to
neutral soils rich in organic matter, often sandy or gravelly forest soils.
Fruiting occurs from late summer through autumn (July–November). It is very
common and often abundant.

## Toxicity

Scleroderma citrinum is poisonous. Primary toxins are believed to be
sclerocitrin-type compounds and sesquiterpene metabolites. Symptoms include
severe nausea, vomiting, and abdominal cramping within 30 minutes to 3 hours of
ingestion, progressing to diarrhea, dehydration, and headache. Symptoms
typically resolve within 24–48 hours with supportive care; fatalities are
extremely rare.

Poisoning most commonly results from confusion with edible puffballs. Key
distinguishing features: true puffballs have thin, smooth peridia and pure
white gleba when young, while earthballs have thick, warty peridia and the
gleba is never entirely white, showing dark marbling even in immature
specimens. Earthballs have a firm, unyielding consistency unlike the soft,
spongy texture of true puffballs.

## Ecological Role

As an ectomycorrhizal fungus, Scleroderma citrinum forms mutualistic
symbioses with forest tree roots. The fungal mycelium envelops root tips,
facilitating bidirectional nutrient exchange: the fungus transfers water and
mineral nutrients (particularly phosphorus) to the host tree, while receiving
carbohydrates from host photosynthesis.

This relationship enhances phosphorus and micronutrient uptake on nutrient-poor
or acidic soils. Extraradical mycelium and rhizomorphs bind soil particles,
improving aggregation and water-holding capacity. Seedlings of mycorrhizal
host trees establish more successfully in soils where Scleroderma networks are
present. The species is early to mid-successional, often appearing in
disturbed or young forest sites before being replaced by more competitive
boletes and agarics.

## Permaculture and Soil Applications

In permaculture and ecological restoration, Scleroderma citrinum has niche
value as a mycorrhizal inoculant for tree establishment. Inoculation of
seedling root systems with Scleroderma spores or mycelial slurry improves
survival and growth rates of oak, pine, and birch seedlings on degraded sites.
The species has been observed colonizing heavy-metal-contaminated soils and may
contribute to phytostabilization with tolerant host trees. Decomposing
fruiting bodies feed soil invertebrates, and the extensive mycelial network
supports diverse microbial communities.

## Related Species

*S. verrucosum* — larger (up to 12 cm), darker, more pronounced warts;
found under conifers. *S. areolatum* — smaller (2–4 cm), smoother peridium
with cracked pattern; prefers grassy habitats. *S. cepa* — pear-shaped,
smoother peridium with powdery coating; less common. *Pisolithus arhizus*
(dead man's foot) — columnar, club-shaped; peridium disintegrates releasing
pea-like peridioles.
