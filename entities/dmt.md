## Overview

**N,N-Dimethyltryptamine (DMT)** is a naturally occurring tryptamine alkaloid and one of the most potent classical psychedelics known. It is found in a wide range of plant species, mammalian tissues (including the human brain), and is a major psychoactive component of ayahuasca, the traditional Amazonian entheogenic brew.

DMT produces intense visionary and mystical experiences at doses as low as 0.2 mg/kg when administered intravenously. Its effects are characterized by rapid onset (seconds), brief duration (15–60 minutes depending on route), and remarkably deep alterations in consciousness comparable to near-death experiences.

The compound was first synthesized in 1931 by the Canadian chemist Richard Manske, and its psychoactivity was confirmed in 1956 by the Hungarian psychiatrist Stephen Szára. It is structurally related to serotonin (5-HT), melatonin, and psilocybin.

## Chemical Properties

**Molecular formula:** C₁₂H₁₆N₂

**Molecular weight:** 188.27 g/mol

**IUPAC name:** N,N-dimethyl-2-(1H-indol-3-yl)ethan-1-amine

**Structure:** DMT is a substituted tryptamine consisting of an indole ring linked to a dimethylaminoethyl side chain. The indole ring system is shared with serotonin and psilocin.

**Appearance:** White to off-white crystalline solid when isolated as the freebase. The freebase is volatile and can be vaporized. As a salt (typically fumarate or hydrochloride), it forms stable white crystals soluble in water.

**Solubility:** Freebase is soluble in organic solvents (chloroform, DCM, acetone) and insoluble in water. Salt forms are water-soluble. The compound degrades at temperatures above 200°C.

**Stability:** DMT freebase is relatively stable when stored in a cool, dark, airtight container. It oxidizes slowly when exposed to air and light, leading to yellowing and reduced potency.

## Pharmacology

DMT is a non-selective serotonin receptor agonist with highest affinity for the **5-HT2A receptor**, the primary site of action for classical psychedelics. Its receptor binding profile includes:

- **5-HT2A** (Ki ≈ 0.12 µM) — primary site responsible for psychedelic effects
- **5-HT2C** (Ki ≈ 0.25 µM) — contributes to visual and somatic effects
- **5-HT1A** (Ki ≈ 0.46 µM) — modulates anxiety and mood components
- **Sigma-1 receptor** (Ki ≈ 1.5 µM) — neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects
- **TAAR1** — trace amine-associated receptor, possible role in endogenous signaling

DMT is also a substrate for the serotonin transporter (SERT) and the vesicular monoamine transporter (VMAT2). It undergoes rapid metabolism by **monoamine oxidase A (MAO-A)**, which deaminates it to 3-IAA (indole-3-acetic acid). This rapid metabolism is why oral DMT is inactive without an MAO inhibitor.

## Effects

The DMT experience varies significantly by dose, route of administration, and set-and-setting factors. Common elements include:

**Visual effects:** Geometric patterns, kaleidoscopic imagery, vivid colors, entity encounters, and environments that appear more real than ordinary perception. At breakthrough doses, users report complete immersion in alternate realities or contact with apparently autonomous beings.

**Cognitive effects:** Ego dissolution, time distortion, profound alterations in thought processes, encounters with seemingly sentient entities, and experiences of cosmic consciousness or divine unity. Many users describe the experience as among the most significant of their lives.

**Somatic effects:** Rapid heart rate, elevated blood pressure, pupil dilation, increased body temperature, nausea (particularly with oral administration), and tremor. These effects are generally transient and subside as the experience progresses.

**Duration by route:**
- Intravenous/intramuscular: 5–15 minutes peak, 30–60 minutes total
- Vaporized (freebase): 3–5 minutes onset, 15–30 minutes total
- Oral (with MAOI): 30–60 minutes onset, 3–6 hours total (ayahuasca)
- Intranasal: 5–10 minutes onset, 45–90 minutes total

**Tolerance and safety:** Tolerance to DMT develops rapidly and diminishes within hours. Cross-tolerance with LSD and psilocybin is documented. The compound has very low physiological toxicity — no deaths attributable solely to DMT have been recorded in clinical literature. The primary risks are psychological (acute anxiety, trauma from overwhelming experiences) and interaction hazards, particularly serotonin syndrome when combined with MAOIs and serotonergic medications.

## Biosynthesis

DMT is biosynthesized in plants and animals via two distinct but convergent pathways.

In plants, the primary pathway proceeds from **L-tryptophan** through decarboxylation to tryptamine, followed by two successive N-methylations catalyzed by **tryptamine N-methyltransferase**. This enzyme uses S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) as the methyl donor. In *Psychotria viridis*, DMT concentrations peak in young leaves and decline with leaf age.

In mammals, endogenous DMT synthesis follows the same basic route: L-tryptophan → tryptamine → N-methyltryptamine (NMT) → DMT. The key enzyme is **indolethylamine N-methyltransferase (INMT)**, which is expressed in the human brain, particularly in the cerebral cortex and pineal gland.

The discovery of the DMT biosynthetic pathway in mammalian tissue has fueled research into its potential role as an endogenous neurotransmitter. Studies have identified DMT in human cerebrospinal fluid, blood plasma, and urine, with levels varying by physiological state.

## Natural Occurrence

DMT is one of the most widely distributed psychedelic alkaloids in nature, occurring in over 65 plant species across at least 8 families:

**Plant sources:** *Banisteriopsis caapi* (ayahuasca vine, trace amounts), *Psychotria viridis* (chacruna, 0.1–0.6% DMT), *Diplopterys cabrerana* (chaliponga), *Mimosa tenuiflora* (jurema), *Acacia spp.* (numerous Australian species), *Anadenanthera peregrina* (yopo), and *Virola spp.* (Amazonian snuff sources).

**Animal occurrence:** DMT has been detected in mammalian brain tissue, cerebrospinal fluid, and blood. It is synthesized endogenously from tryptamine via the enzyme indolethylamine N-methyltransferase (INMT). The pineal gland hypothesis (popularized by Rick Strassman) remains debated, but INMT expression has been confirmed in the human brain.

**Endogenous function:** The biological role of endogenous DMT is not fully understood. Proposed functions include serving as a neurotransmitter or neuromodulator, acting as a neuroprotectant against hypoxia, and modulating sigma-1 receptor-mediated cellular stress responses.

## Legal Status

DMT is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under the United Nations Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971). It is listed as a Schedule I drug in the United States, a Class A drug in the United Kingdom, and is similarly controlled in most jurisdictions worldwide.

Notable exceptions and legal complexities exist: ayahuasca use is protected for religious purposes in several countries. In Brazil, the União do Vegetal (UDV) and Santo Daime churches have won legal recognition for ayahuasca ceremonies. The U.S. Supreme Court ruled in 2006 (Gonzales v. O Centro Espírita Beneficente União do Vegetal) that the Religious Freedom Restoration Act protects ayahuasca use in U.S. UDV ceremonies.

## History and Research

DMT was first synthesized by Richard Manske in 1931 during alkaloid chemistry research. Its psychoactive properties remained unknown until Stephen Szára administered it to himself in 1956 after obtaining it from a chemical supply catalog.

The 1960s saw significant research interest, with Szára and others studying DMT's effects in clinical settings. The compound became a controlled substance in 1971, effectively halting human research for over two decades.

Modern research has revived clinical interest in DMT and ayahuasca. Ongoing clinical trials are investigating ayahuasca for treatment-resistant depression, substance use disorders, and end-of-life anxiety. The development of extended-duration DMT formulations (e.g., SPL026 by Small Pharma) aims to allow DMT-assisted psychotherapy within standard therapeutic session lengths.
