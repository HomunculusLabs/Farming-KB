## Overview

**Lindera obtusiloba** (Japanese spicebush, blunt-lobed spicebush) is a deciduous shrub or small tree in the family Lauraceae, native to East Asia. The species is widely cultivated as an ornamental for its showy yellow spring flowers, attractive fall foliage, and winter branch structure.

In Korean traditional medicine (KKM), the bark and leaves of *L. obtusiloba* have been used to treat inflammatory conditions, digestive disorders, and circulatory ailments.

Modern pharmacological research has identified numerous bioactive lignans, flavonoids, and [[permaculture-designers-manual-essential-oil-plants]] components with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and hepatoprotective properties.

The species is one of several *Lindera* species used medicinally across East Asia, alongside *L. aggregata* (wu-yao) and *L. strychnifolia* (wu-zhu-yu).

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Lindera obtusiloba* Blume was described by Carl Ludwig von Blume in 1826 in *Bijdragen tot de Flora van Nederlandsch Indië*.

The genus name honors Johann Linder (1676–1723), a Swedish botanist and physician.

The specific epithet *obtusiloba* means "blunt-lobed," referring to the shape of the leaf apex.

Growth form is a deciduous shrub or small tree reaching 3–8 m in height, occasionally to 10 m.

The bark is smooth, grayish-brown, becoming slightly fissured with age.

Branches are slender, gray-brown, and develop an attractive framework in winter.

Leaves are alternate, broadly ovate to orbicular, 5–15 cm long and 4–12 cm wide, with three to five blunt lobes.

The leaf base is cordate to truncate, and the margins are entire or shallowly toothed.

Young leaves are covered with fine pubescence, becoming glabrous at maturity.

The upper [[bloomfield-rust-germ-tube-tactile-sensing-leaf-surface-navigation]] is medium green, the lower surface paler with prominent venation.

Autumn foliage color is excellent, ranging from bright yellow to orange and deep red.

Flowers appear in [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] (March–April) before leaf emergence, in small umbel-like clusters.

The flowers are yellow, dioecious, with perianth segments approximately 5–6 mm long.

Fruit is a small, ellipsoid drupe, approximately 8 mm in diameter, ripening from green to red then dark purple in autumn.

Each fruit contains a [[cervantes-selfing-single-seed-descent]] and is borne on a slender pedicel.

## Distribution and Habitat

*L. obtusiloba* is native to East Asia, with a distribution spanning Korea, Japan, and eastern China.

In Korea, it is found throughout the peninsula from lowlands to montane forests.

In Japan, the species occurs on Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu islands.

In China, it is distributed in provinces including Shandong, Anhui, Zhejiang, Jiangxi, and Hubei.

The species grows in deciduous and mixed forests, forest margins, and along mountain stream corridors.

Preferred elevations range from 100 to 2,000 meters, with optimal growth in mid-elevation montane forests.

It tolerates a wide range of soil types, from acidic forest soils to slightly calcareous substrates.

The plant prefers partial shade to full sun and moderate soil moisture.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The bark, leaves, and twigs of *L. obtusiloba* contain a diverse array of [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]].

**Lignans** are the most extensively studied compounds, including (-)-syringaresinol, (+)-pinoresinol, lirioresinol B, and various furofuran-type lignans.

These lignans have demonstrated significant anti-inflammatory activity in both in vitro and in vivo models.

Flavonoids identified in the species include quercetin, kaempferol, and their glycoside derivatives.

The essential oil of leaves contains **linalool**, **geraniol**, **1,8-cineole**, and **alpha-terpineol** as major components.

Methanolic bark extracts have shown hepatoprotective effects in animal models of chemically induced liver injury.

Antioxidant activity of leaf extracts is attributed primarily to flavonoid and phenolic acid content.

Anti-inflammatory effects are mediated through inhibition of NF-kappaB signaling and reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

Cytotoxic lignans isolated from the bark have shown activity against several human [[blesching-cannabis-apoptosis-and-cancer-cell-death]] lines in vitro.

The species does not contain aristolochic acids, distinguishing it pharmacologically from its Aristolochiaceae relatives.

## Cultivation

*L. obtusiloba* is widely grown as an ornamental landscape plant in temperate regions.

It is valued for its early spring yellow flowers, three-season interest (flowers, foliage, fruit), and attractive bark.

The species is hardy to USDA Zone 5 (approximately -29°C), making it suitable for cold-climate gardens.

Propagation is primarily by seed, which requires cold stratification for 60–90 days before germination.

Softwood cuttings taken in early summer can also be used for vegetative propagation.

The plant prefers moist, well-drained, slightly acidic soil in partial shade to full sun.

Established plants are moderately drought-tolerant and have few serious pest or disease problems.

Pruning is generally minimal, limited to removal of dead wood and shaping of the natural framework.

Several named cultivars exist, including variegated-leaf and compact-growing selections.

The species is relatively slow-growing in cultivation, adding 15–30 cm of height per year under favorable conditions.

## Ethnobotanical Use

In Korean traditional medicine, the bark of *L. obtusiloba* is used to treat abdominal pain, digestive disorders, and cold-related conditions.

A decoction of the bark is traditionally consumed as a digestive tonic and circulatory stimulant.

The leaves are sometimes applied topically as a poultice for bruises and joint pain.

In traditional Japanese herbalism, related *Lindera* species are classified as "warming" herbs that dispel cold and promote circulation.

The aromatic wood and bark have been used as incense and in traditional fumigation practices.

Young leaves have occasionally been used as a tea substitute in rural Korean communities.

Modern commercial products include dietary supplements and herbal extracts marketed for anti-inflammatory and liver-protective purposes.
