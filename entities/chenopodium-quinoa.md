Quinoa is notable for its exceptional nutritional profile. The seeds contain
all nine essential amino acids, making them a complete protein source rare
among plant foods.

The species was domesticated approximately 5,000–7,000 years ago in the
vicinity of Lake Titicaca, straddling modern-day Peru and Bolivia. It
remained a staple crop of Andean civilizations through the pre-Columbian
era.

The United Nations declared 2013 the International Year of Quinoa in
recognition of its nutritional value and potential contribution to global
food security.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- Kingdom: Plantae
- Division: Angiosperms
- Class: Eudicots
- Order: Caryophyllales
- Family: Amaranthaceae
- Genus: *Chenopodium*
- Species: *C. quinoa*

Authority: Willd. (1790).

Quinoa is an annual plant reaching 1–2 m in height under cultivated
conditions. The stems are erect, striate, and may be green, red, or purple
depending on the variety.

Leaves are broadly triangular to hastate, 3–15 cm long, with irregularly
dentate margins. The [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]].

Optimal growing conditions include cool temperatures (15–20°C during
growth), well-drained soils, and moderate precipitation. Quinoa exhibits
exceptional tolerance to drought, frost, salinity, and poor soils.

It is classified as a facultative C3 plant with strong drought avoidance
mechanisms. Some cultivars can tolerate soil salinity levels up to 40 dS/m.

## Nutritional Profile

Quinoa seeds contain approximately 14% protein by dry weight, with a
balanced amino acid profile particularly rich in lysine and isoleucine.
This lysine content distinguishes it from most cereal grains.
