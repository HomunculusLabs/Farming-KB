# Psilocybe wassoniorum

**Psilocybe wassoniorum** is a species of psychoactive mushroom in the family Strophariaceae, named in honor of R. Gordon Wasson and his wife Valentina Pavlovna Wasson, the pioneering ethnomycologists who brought the knowledge of Mexican sacred mushrooms to the attention of the Western world. It is one of the psilocybin-containing species cataloged in Guzmán, Allen & Gartz's (1998) comprehensive worldwide survey of 216 neurotropic fungi.

## Taxonomy and Etymology

The species was described by Gastón Guzmán and is named after R. Gordon Wasson (1898-1986), the American banker, author, and amateur ethnomycologist who, along with his Russian-born wife Valentina Pavlovna Wasson (1901-1958), became the first known Westerners to participate in a Mazatec mushroom ceremony. Their historic experience took place in Huautla de Jiménez, Oaxaca, Mexico, in 1955, under the guidance of the Mazatec curandera María Sabina.

The Wassons' subsequent publications — including the famous 1957 *Life* magazine article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" — sparked worldwide interest in psilocybin mushrooms and catalyzed decades of scientific research into their chemistry, effects, and cultural significance. Their earlier collaborative work, *Mushrooms, Russia and History* (1957), was a monumental two-volume exploration of the cultural significance of mushrooms across civilizations. The dual honorific "wassoniorum" (Latin genitive plural) recognizes both Gordon and Valentina Wasson as a couple, acknowledging their joint contributions to ethnomycology.

## Distribution

*Psilocybe wassoniorum* is found in Mexico, which possesses the highest diversity of neurotropic fungi in the world — 76 species in total, of which 44 belong to the genus Psilocybe. The species is associated with the humid subtropical forests of southern Mexico, particularly in the states of Oaxaca, Puebla, and surrounding regions where Mazatec, Chinantec, Zapotec, Mixtec, and other indigenous cultures have traditionally used psychoactive mushrooms for healing, divination, and religious ceremonies.

The Sierra Mazateca in northern Oaxaca is of particular significance as the heartland of Mazatec mushroom use and the region where the Wassons conducted their groundbreaking fieldwork. This mountainous area, with its cloud forests, coffee plantations, and rich biodiversity, provides ideal conditions for the growth of numerous Psilocybe species.

## Description

Fruiting bodies of *P. wassoniorum* are characterized by a conic to broadly convex pileus that may develop a slight umbo (a small central bump) with age. The cap surface is typically smooth and hygrophanous, shifting from a chestnut brown or reddish brown when moist to a lighter tan, buff, or straw color as it dries. The margin is often translucent-striate when fresh, a feature common to many Psilocybe species caused by the gills showing through the thin, moist cap tissue.

The gills are adnate to adnexed (attached to the stipe but not running down it), initially pale grayish and maturing to dark purplish brown as the spores develop and mature. The stipe is central, equal or slightly tapering upward, and typically whitish to pale brown. A partial veil is often present in young specimens, connecting the cap margin to the stipe, which ruptures as the cap expands and may leave a fragile annular zone or fibrillose ring on the upper stipe. The stipe often exhibits blue-green staining where bruised, handled, or damaged, a hallmark of psilocybin-containing species caused by the enzymatic oxidation of psilocin. Spore prints are dark purplish brown to nearly black, typical of the genus.

## Habitat

Like many Mexican Psilocybe species, *P. wassoniorum* grows in rich, humic soils of subtropical and tropical forests. It is often found in areas with well-decomposed leaf litter and woody debris, in cloud forests, coffee plantations, and along forest trails. The species typically fruits during the rainy season (approximately June to September in Oaxaca) when moisture conditions are optimal. Elevation ranges for Mexican Psilocybe species vary considerably, but many are found between 1,000 and 2,500 meters above sea level in the mountainous terrain of southern Mexico.

## Chemistry

The species contains psilocybin (4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) and psilocin (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine), the characteristic indole alkaloids of the genus Psilocybe. These compounds are structural analogs of the neurotransmitter serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine) and act as agonists primarily at the 5-HT2A receptor subtype, though they interact with multiple serotonin receptor subtypes. The resulting psychoactive effects include alterations in perception, visual hallucinations, changes in the perception of time and self, synesthesia, emotional intensification, and mystical-type experiences.

Chemical confirmation places *P. wassoniorum* firmly in Group 1 of Guzmán's classification of neurotropic fungi — species confirmed to contain psilocybin and related indole compounds. The baeocystin and norbaeocystin content, if any, has not been specifically quantified for this species.

## Historical Significance

The naming of this species honors what is arguably the most important partnership in the history of ethnomycology. Gordon Wasson's 1957 *Life* article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom" was read by millions and introduced the English word "psilocybin" into popular culture (the term had been coined by Albert Hofmann in 1958). The Wassons' collaboration with French mycologist Roger Heim led to the successful cultivation of several Psilocybe species at the Muséum National d'Histoire Naturelle in Paris, and ultimately to the isolation and synthesis of psilocybin by Albert Hofmann at Sandoz Laboratories in Basel, Switzerland.

The story of the Wassons' discovery is also one of the most consequential events in the history of psychedelic science, as it directly led to the identification and study of psilocybin and influenced the subsequent discovery of LSD's psychoactive properties, the founding of the Heffter Research Institute, and eventually the modern psychedelic renaissance in psychiatry and neuroscience.
