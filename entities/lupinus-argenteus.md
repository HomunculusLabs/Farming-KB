## Overview

**Lupinus argenteus** is a perennial herbaceous legume in the family Fabaceae, commonly known as silvery lupine.

It is widespread across western [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], from British Columbia and Alberta south through the Rocky Mountains to New Mexico and Arizona.

The species is recognized by its silvery-pubescent foliage, palmately compound leaves, and showy racemes of blue to purple flowers.

Flower color is highly variable across the species' range, with populations in the southern Rockies tending toward lighter blue and white forms.

Silvery lupine is an important component of montane and subalpine plant communities and provides significant forage and habitat value for wildlife.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Lupinus argenteus** Pursh belongs to the family Fabaceae, subfamily Faboideae, tribe Genisteae.

The specific epithet *argenteus* refers to the silvery sheen of the leaf surfaces, caused by a dense covering of appressed hairs.

It is a perennial forb growing 20–80 cm tall from a thick caudex. Multiple stems arise from the base, forming an open to rounded crown.

Leaves are palmately compound with 5–9 leaflets per leaf. Leaflets are 3–7 cm long, narrowly oblanceolate, with silky-silvery pubescence on both surfaces.

Inflorescences are terminal racemes 5–20 cm long, densely flowered and often secund. Flowers are papilionaceous, 10–14 mm long, varying from deep blue and violet to white or pinkish.

The banner petal is reflexed and typically bears a white or yellowish spot. Flowering occurs from June through August, depending on elevation.

The fruit is a legume pod 2–3 cm long, densely silky-pubescent, containing 4–8 seeds. Pods dehisce explosively at maturity, scattering seeds several meters.

Seeds are mottled brown and gray, approximately 3–4 mm in diameter. They can remain viable in the soil seed bank for 5–10 years.

## Distribution and Habitat

Silvery lupine ranges from southern British Columbia and southwestern Alberta south through the Rocky Mountain states to eastern California, Nevada, Arizona, and New Mexico.

It occurs at elevations from 1,200 to 3,500 meters, making it one of the most widely distributed lupine species in the [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] cordillera.

The species is common in open coniferous forests, mountain meadows, sagebrush steppe, subalpine parklands, and riparian corridors.

It prefers well-drained sandy or gravelly soils derived from granitic or volcanic parent material. It is frequently found on disturbed sites including roadsides, burned areas, and mine spoils.

Silvery lupine tolerates full sun to partial shade and is moderately drought-tolerant. It is one of the first forbs to colonize burned areas in montane forests.

## Ecology and Wildlife Value

As a legume, **Lupinus argenteus** fixes atmospheric nitrogen through symbiotic Bradyrhizobium bacteria in root nodules, enriching nutrient-poor montane soils.

The foliage is browsed by elk, mule deer, bighorn sheep, and mountain goats, particularly in spring and early summer when leaves are young and tender.

Silky lupine seeds are an important food source for several ground-foraging birds, including Columbian sharp-tailed grouse and dark-eyed junco.

It is the primary larval host for the mission blue butterfly (Icaricia icarioides missionensis), a federally endangered subspecies in the [[san-francisco-shipibo-ayahuasca-ceremonial-center]] Bay Area.

The alkaloid-rich foliage is toxic to livestock, particularly sheep, causing crooked calf disease when pregnant cows graze lupine during mid-gestation.

This teratogenic effect is caused by the quinolizidine alkaloid anagyrine, which interferes with fetal skeletal development during a narrow window of gestation (days 40–70).

In burned forest communities, silvery lupine serves as a nurse plant, facilitating the establishment of tree seedlings by providing shade and improved soil conditions.

Its rapid colonization of disturbed sites helps stabilize soils and reduce erosion on steep mountain slopes following wildfire or road construction.

## Cultivation and Uses

Silvery lupine is occasionally cultivated for native wildflower gardens and [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]] projects in montane regions.

Seed requires scarification, either mechanical abrasion or hot water treatment, to break physical dormancy. Cold stratification for 30–60 days further improves germination rates.

Plants establish readily from seed in well-drained soils and full sun. They are relatively short-lived for a perennial, typically persisting 3–7 years.

In ecological restoration, silvery lupine is included in seed mixes for post-fire rehabilitation, mine reclamation, and roadside revegetation at high elevations.
