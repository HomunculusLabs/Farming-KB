# Quercus velutina

## Overview

**Quercus velutina** Lam., commonly known as black oak, is a medium to large deciduous tree native to eastern and central North America. It is a member of the red oak section (Lobatae) and is one of the most widespread and ecologically important oaks of the eastern deciduous forest.

Black oak is recognized for its distinctive dark, furrowed bark, its yellow-orange inner bark (which yields a valuable yellow dye — hence the specific epithet *velutina*, meaning velvety), and its role as a fire-adapted component of upland oak-hickory forests. The species was historically a major source of tannins and quercitron dye.

- **Order:** Fagales
- **Family:** Fagaceae
- **Genus:** Quercus, section Lobatae (red oaks)
- **Species:** Q. velutina Lam. (authority: Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, 1785)

Key synonyms include Q. tinctoria Bartram. The common name "black oak" refers to the dark outer bark, distinguishing it from [[quercus-alba]] (white oak). It should not be confused with the unrelated blackjack oak or the European holm oak ([[quercus-ilex]]).

## Morphology

Black oak typically reaches 20–25 m in height and 60–90 cm in diameter at maturity, with exceptional specimens exceeding 30 m and 1.2 m DBH. The trunk is straight with a well-developed crown.

**Bark** on mature trees is thick (2.5–5 cm), dark grey to nearly black, with deep, irregular furrows broken into thick, scaly ridges. The inner bark is bright yellow to orange — a diagnostic feature unique among [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] American oaks.

**Twigs** are stout, reddish-brown to grey-brown, with star-shaped pith (a character shared with other red oaks). Terminal buds are angular, 5–8 mm long, with grey-brown, pubescent scales.

**Leaves** are alternate, simple, broadly obovate, 10–25 cm long and 8–15 cm wide, with 5–7 bristle-tipped lobes per side. The upper surface is dark shiny green; the lower surface is pale green with rusty pubescence, especially along veins.

The leaf base is characteristically wedge-shaped to rounded (not symmetrically cordate as in some white oaks). Autumn coloration is typically deep red to orange-brown, often among the most vivid of the eastern oaks.

**Flowers** are monoecious. Male catkins appear in April–May with the leaves, pendulous, 7–10 cm long. Female flowers are small, solitary or paired, in leaf axils near shoot tips.

**Acorns** are 1.3–2 cm long and 1–1.5 cm wide, ovoid, borne singly or in pairs on a stout peduncle 2–5 cm long. The cupule encloses roughly one-half of the nut, with thin, reddish-brown scales that are appressed at the base and loosely spreading at the tip. Acorns mature in two seasons (biennial), characteristic of red oaks.

## Distribution and Habitat

Quercus velutina is native to the eastern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], from southern Maine and New York westward to southern Wisconsin and eastern Oklahoma, and southward to northern Florida and eastern Texas. It is absent from the Gulf Coastal Plain and the lower Mississippi Valley alluvial soils.

The species is most abundant in the **Central Hardwood Region** — the Ozark Plateau, the Ouachita Mountains, the Appalachian Plateau, and the Piedmont. It is a dominant or codominant species in oak-hickory forests across this region.

In the Ozark Highlands, black oak is a primary component of the oak-pine and oak-hickory forest types, often growing alongside post oak, blackjack oak, and shortleaf pine ([[pinus-echinata]]). In the Appalachians, it occupies intermediate positions on slopes and ridges between the more mesic northern red oak and the xeric scarlet oak.

### Site Preferences

Black oak occupies a broad range of sites but is most characteristic of **dry-mesic to dry upland soils** — well-drained, acidic to neutral loams and sandy loams on ridges, slopes, and upper terraces. It is more drought-tolerant than [[quercus-rubra]] and [[quercus-alba]], frequently occupying warmer, drier, and more exposed sites.

It is intolerant of poorly drained soils, flooding, and deep shade. Elevational range extends from near sea level to approximately 1,500 m in the southern Appalachians.

## Ecological Role

Black oak acorns are an important food resource for wildlife including wild turkey, white-tailed deer, black bear, gray squirrel, fox squirrel, ruffed grouse, blue jay, and numerous small mammals. Acorn production is irregular, with heavy mast years occurring at 2–5 year intervals.

The species is a **fire-adapted mid-successional tree**. Before fire suppression, frequent low-intensity surface fires maintained oak dominance by killing competing shade-tolerant species (maples, beeches) while adult black oaks survived due to their thick bark. Modern fire suppression has led to mesophication — the gradual replacement of oak forests by shade-tolerant, fire-sensitive species.

**Advance reproduction** (seedling pools) of black oak can persist in the understory for decades, remaining suppressed until canopy disturbance releases them. This "oak bottleneck" — where seedlings survive but saplings fail to reach the canopy — is a central challenge for oak [[mollison-pdc-forest-management-products]] in the eastern United States.

Black oak provides nesting habitat, cavity sites, and forage for a wide range of birds and mammals. Its foliage supports larvae of hundreds of Lepidoptera species, making it a critical host plant in eastern forest food webs.

## Cultivation and Uses

### Timber

Black oak wood is hard, heavy (density 0.61–0.71 g/cm³), strong, and coarse-grained. Heartwood is reddish-brown with a paler sapwood band. It is marketed together with other red oaks as "red oak lumber."

Primary uses include flooring, furniture, cabinetry, railroad ties, mine timbers, and fuelwood. The wood is less durable than white oak when exposed to weather and is not used for cooperage.

### Historical Dye and Tannin Production

The inner bark of black oak was historically one of the most important sources of **quercitron** — a yellow dye used extensively in the [[staycare-fungal-treatment-of-textile-industry-wastewater]] from the 18th through early 20th centuries. The active compound is quercitrin, a glycoside of the flavonoid quercetin. Quercitron bark was harvested commercially from wild trees across the Appalachian region and exported to European textile mills.

The bark was also a major source of **tannins** for leather tanning. Quercitron extract was a significant commercial product in the eastern United States through the 1800s, with processing centers in Pennsylvania and the Carolinas.
