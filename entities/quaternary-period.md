## Quaternary Period

## Description

The Quaternary Period is the most recent geological time period, spanning approximately the last 2.6 million years to the present. David Holmgren discusses the Quaternary in *Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability* as a key example of how understanding deep geological time is essential for developing the long-term thinking necessary for sustainable culture. The Quaternary is characterised by dramatic climatic oscillations between ice ages and interglacial warm periods, making it one of the most dynamic periods in Earth"s history and the geological stage upon which the genus *Homo*, including *Homo sapiens*, evolved.

## Classification

- **Type:** Geological Time Period
- **Era:** Cenozoic Era
- **Period:** Quaternary (2.6 million years ago – present)
- **Subdivisions:** Pleistocene (2.6 Ma – 11,700 years ago), Holocene (11,700 years ago – present)

## Key Facts

- The Quaternary has been characterised by tectonic uplift, volcanism, global and montane ice caps, vastly varied climates, ice ages and warmer interglacials, massive extinctions, and prolific speciation.
- Ice ages lasting hundreds of thousands of years represent the stable norm of the Quaternary, while interglacials lasting roughly 10,000 years represent explosive pulses of life based on new fertility, benign climates, and biodiversity growth.
- Much of Earth's biological and geographic diversity exists specifically because of these periods of great change — it is unlikely that *Homo sapiens* could have emerged or survived the long, slow, stable periods that dominate Earth's geological history.
- Sea levels rose dramatically at the end of the last ice age (12,000–8,000 years ago), flooding vast areas including the Bassian Plain between Tasmania and mainland Australia.
- Evidence suggests continental glaciers shed icebergs of such magnitude that sea levels may have risen rapidly to flood vast areas, as in the Atlantis story.
- Mammoths were found frozen with grass in their mouths, suggesting sudden onset of severe winters precipitating ice ages in the northern hemisphere — evidence that far-reaching change can occur with great rapidity.

## Pulsing Pattern

The Quaternary exemplifies Holmgren's pulsing model of ecosystem and planetary change:

- **Stability phase:** Glacial periods lasting hundreds of thousands of years
- **Rapid transition:** Temporal "edge" between glacial and interglacial states
- **Exploitation phase:** Interglacial periods with abundant fertility and biodiversity
- **Current status:** Humanity exists in a remarkably stable interglacial that has enabled agriculture, civilisation, and industrial culture

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

The Quaternary context is essential for permaculture thinking for several reasons:

1. **Fragility of the current climate:** All of human agriculture and civilisation depends on the fragile stability of the interglacial period. Any serious consideration of the next ice age (due in the next thousand or so years) would demand designing a modest, generalist, flexible culture carried by a small global population.
2. **Nested cyclical thinking:** Understanding that human history occupies a tiny fraction of geological time helps designers break free from the "delusion of apparently linear acceleration" and think in cycles, waves, and pulses.
3. **Rapid change is normal:** The geological record shows that dramatic, far-reaching environmental change can be precipitated with great rapidity — mammoths frozen mid-meal, sea levels flooding continents. Permaculture designs must account for such potential rapid transitions.
4. **Biodiversity and disturbance:** The Quaternary demonstrates that much of the diversity we value exists because of, not despite, dramatic environmental oscillation — supporting the permaculture principle that edge and disturbance drive productivity.

Holmgren uses this geological perspective to argue that permaculture designers must think simultaneously on multiple time scales and rebuild the long-term thinking that was natural to indigenous cultures of place.
