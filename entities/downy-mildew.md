# Downy Mildew

## Description

Downy mildew is a group of plant diseases caused by oomycete pathogens in several genera including *Sclerophthora*, *Peronospora*, *Plasmopara*, and *Bremia*. On turfgrass, downy mildew is primarily caused by *Sclerophthora* species. In the soil food web framework, downy mildew is among the diseases that can be suppressed by fungally dominated compost teas through competitive exclusion on leaf and root surfaces.

## Classification

- **Causal organisms:** Multiple oomycete genera (*Sclerophthora*, *Peronospora*, *Plasmopara*, *Bremia*, *Pseudoperonospora*)
- **Type:** Foliar and systemic plant disease
- **Pathogen group:** Oomycetes (water molds) — not true fungi
- **Symptoms:** Yellow patches on upper leaf surface, grayish-white downy growth on lower surface
- **Conditions favored by:** Cool temperatures, high humidity, prolonged leaf wetness

## Key Facts

- Caused by oomycete pathogens — biologically distinct from true fungi but with similar pathogenic behavior
- Characterized by angular yellow patches on upper leaf surfaces and fuzzy, grayish growth on lower surfaces
- Spread by wind-blown sporangia and water-borne zoospores
- Favored by cool, wet conditions — dew, fog, and overhead irrigation promote disease development
- Suppressed by fungally dominated compost teas applied as foliar sprays
- Fungal tea mechanism of action:
  - Competitive fungi colonize leaf surfaces (phyllosphere), occupying space that downy mildew would colonize
  - Fungi compete for nutrients and exudates on leaf surfaces
  - Some compost tea fungi may produce antifungal/anti-oomycete compounds
- Requires 70% leaf surface coverage for effective foliar compost tea protection
- Application timing critical: apply in early morning or late afternoon to avoid UV damage to tea microbes

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Downy mildew management through compost tea illustrates biological disease control in the phyllosphere:

- **Phyllosphere ecology:** Leaf surfaces support diverse microbial communities; compost tea introduces beneficial organisms that outcompete pathogens
- **Foliar application technique:** Effective biological control requires thorough coverage (70% of leaf surface) and proper timing (avoiding UV exposure)
- **Oomycete management:** Despite being non-fungal pathogens, oomycetes can be suppressed by fungal competitors — competitive exclusion transcends taxonomic boundaries
- **Prevention over cure:** Regular compost tea applications before disease onset are more effective than treating established infections
