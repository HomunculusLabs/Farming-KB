## Overview

Echeveria lilacina, commonly known as the "Ghost Echeveria" or "Plata," is a striking silver-white succulent in the family Crassulaceae. It is native to the high-altitude desert regions of northeastern Mexico.

The species is one of the most distinctive Echeveria, recognized by its powdery silver-white rosettes and pink-edged coral flowers. Its ethereal, ghostly appearance has made it a highly sought-after specimen among succulent collectors.

Despite its popularity in cultivation, Echeveria lilacina is relatively slow-growing compared to other Echeveria species. This slow growth, combined with its dramatic appearance, contributes to its premium pricing in the horticultural trade.

The species has received the Royal Horticultural Society"s Award of Garden Merit in the United Kingdom. This recognition confirms its reliability as a garden plant in suitable climates.

In the Japanese succulent market, Echeveria lilacina is known as "Gosuto Ekubeeria" (Ghost Echeveria) and commands premium prices for specimens with dense, unblemished farina. Japanese breeders have produced several selected forms with enhanced coloration.

## Taxonomy and Description

Echeveria lilacina was described by John Thomas Howell in 1946 from specimens collected in Nuevo León, Mexico. The specific epithet "lilacina" refers to the lilac-pink flower color.

The plant forms solitary, stemless rosettes 15–25 cm in diameter. Leaves are broad, spatulate, and covered in a dense white farinose coating that gives the plant its characteristic silvery appearance.

Each leaf measures 5–10 cm long and 3–5 cm wide, with a rounded to slightly notched apex. The farinose bloom is a natural wax that protects against intense UV radiation at high altitudes.

Touching the leaves removes the powdery coating permanently from the contacted area, leaving visible fingerprints. For this reason, collectors handle the plants with care to preserve their aesthetic quality.

Inflorescences are short, arching cymes 10–20 cm tall, bearing lantern-shaped flowers. Petals are coral-pink on the exterior and pale yellow within, creating a two-toned display.

Flowering occurs from late winter to early spring. The species is relatively shy to bloom compared to other Echeveria, and some mature plants may skip flowering in suboptimal conditions.

Chromosome number is 2n = ca. 120, making it a high-polyploid species within series Echeveria.

The high chromosome count likely contributes to the species' genetic stability and morphological uniformity across its limited range. Polyploidy in Crassulaceae is associated with enhanced drought tolerance and altitudinal adaptation.

Roots are thin and fibrous, forming a shallow network adapted to absorbing moisture from brief rainfall events. In cultivation, root development is concentrated in the upper 5 cm of the growing medium.

## Distribution and Habitat

Echeveria lilacina is endemic to the Sierra Madre Oriental in the Mexican states of Nuevo León and Coahuila. It grows at elevations of 1,800–2,400 meters on steep, exposed limestone cliffs.

The habitat is characterized by semi-arid conditions with hot, dry summers and cool winters. Annual precipitation averages 300–500 mm, primarily from summer thunderstorms.

Plants grow in vertical rock crevices and on cliff faces where competition from other vegetation is minimal. The limestone substrate provides calcium-rich, alkaline conditions with excellent drainage.

Winter temperatures frequently drop below freezing, and the species tolerates brief exposure to −5°C. This cold hardiness is exceptional among Echeveria and reflects its high-altitude origin.

The species has a limited natural range and is considered vulnerable due to habitat loss and illegal collection. Several populations occur within protected areas of the Sierra Madre Oriental.

Field surveys in the 2010s documented population declines of 30–40 percent at accessible sites near Monterrey. Road construction and limestone quarrying are the primary threats to remaining habitat.

## Cultivation

Echeveria lilacina requires exceptionally well-drained soil, ideally a mineral mix of pumice, perlite, and coarse sand with minimal organic content. Standard cactus mixes often retain too much moisture for this species.

Watering should be infrequent but thorough, allowing the soil to dry completely between applications. During summer dormancy, watering should be reduced to once every three to four weeks.

Bright light is essential for maintaining the compact silver rosette form. Insufficient light causes stretching and loss of the characteristic white farina, revealing the green leaf surface beneath.

The species is notably slow-growing, producing only a few new leaves per growing season. Rosettes typically take three to five years to reach mature size from seedling stage.

Propagation is primarily by seed, as the species rarely produces offsets. Leaf cuttings have a low success rate compared to other Echeveria, typically below 30 percent.

Seed-grown plants maintain the species' characteristic vigor and form more reliably than cutting-propagated specimens. Fresh seed should be sown on the surface of sterile, sandy medium and kept moist at 20–25°C.

The species benefits from a pronounced winter rest period with cool temperatures around 5–10°C and minimal water. This dormancy appears to trigger flowering the following spring.

Mealybugs can hide in the tight leaf axils and are difficult to detect under the white farina. Systemic insecticides applied as soil drenches are the most effective treatment.

## Varieties and Similar Species

No formally named subspecies or varieties exist, but collectors recognize slight variations in leaf shape and farina density among wild populations. Northern populations tend to have broader, more powdery leaves.

Echeveria lilacina is sometimes confused with Echeveria laui, another white-farinose species from Oaxaca. Echeveria laui has narrower, more pointed leaves and larger flowers on taller inflorescences.

The species also resembles Echeveria cante, which has broader leaves with a pinkish hue beneath the white farina. Echeveria cante is from Zacatecas and was only described as a distinct species in 1998.

Hybrids between Echeveria lilacina and other white-farinose species exist in cultivation, often sold under cultivar names. These may display intermediate characteristics in leaf shape and farina density.

Some cultivated specimens labeled as Echeveria lilacina are actually hybrids or selected forms of Echeveria cante. Distinguishing pure species requires careful examination of flower morphology and leaf proportions.

## Uses

Echeveria lilacina is grown exclusively as an ornamental plant, valued for its striking silver-white rosettes. It is a favorite among succulent collectors and frequently featured in succulent shows and competitions.

The species is well-suited to container culture and rock garden plantings in Mediterranean and desert climates. Its compact form and silver coloration make it an excellent accent plant in xeriscape designs.

Specimens grown in mineral-rich, alkaline soil develop denser farina than those in peat-based media. This observation aligns with the limestone substrate conditions of the plant's native habitat.

Like other Crassulaceae, the species uses CAM photosynthesis for water efficiency. This metabolic adaptation makes it one of the most drought-tolerant Echeveria in cultivation.

The farinose coating has been studied for its UV-protective properties. Research on the wax composition may have applications in developing synthetic UV-protective coatings.

The farina consists primarily of flavonoid compounds and long-chain aldehydes that scatter UV radiation. Removing this coating experimentally causes rapid leaf damage under full sun exposure within hours.

Plants in cultivation gradually lose the farina density on older leaves, which take on a duller, more gray-green appearance. Only newly produced leaves display the full silvery-white effect that makes the species so distinctive.
