# Ceratonia siliqua

Ceratonia siliqua, commonly known as the [[carob]] tree, St. John's bread, or locust bean,
is an evergreen leguminous tree in the family Fabaceae, subfamily Caesalpinioideae. Native
to the eastern Mediterranean basin, it has been cultivated for over 4,000 years for its
edible pods, which serve as a nutritious food, animal fodder, and source of carob bean gum
(locust bean gum), a widely used industrial thickener and stabilizer.

## Taxonomy and Etymology

The genus name Ceratonia derives from the Greek keras, meaning horn, referring to the
curved, horn-like shape of the pods. The species epithet siliqua is Latin for pod. The
common name St. John's bread refers to the tradition that John the Baptist sustained
himself on carob pods in the wilderness. The alternative name locust bean has led to
confusion with the true locust (an insect), and it has been suggested that the locusts
eaten by John the Baptist were actually carob pods. The word carat, the unit of weight
used for precious stones, is derived from the Arabic qirat, which in turn comes from the
Greek keration (small horn), referencing the uniform weight of carob seeds that were
historically used as balance weights in gemstone trade.

## Botany and Identification

Ceratonia siliqua is a medium-sized evergreen tree, typically growing 8 to 15 meters
tall with a broad, hemispherical canopy and a thick, gnarled trunk that can reach 1 meter
or more in diameter on very old specimens. The tree develops a deep taproot (extending to
15 meters or more) supplemented by an extensive lateral root system. The bark is dark
brown to nearly black, rough, and deeply fissured into longitudinal and transverse ridges.
The crown is dense and spreading, providing excellent shade.

Leaves are pinnate, 10 to 20 cm long, with 4 to 10 pairs of glossy, leathery, dark
green leaflets. Each leaflet is 2 to 6 cm long, rounded to oblong, with entire margins and
a slightly revolute (rolled under) edge. Leaflets are alternate along the rachis, with a
small mucronate (pointed) tip. The foliage is persistent, with individual leaves lasting 2
to 3 years before being shed, giving the tree a year-round dense appearance. New leaf
flush occurs in spring alongside flowering.

Carob is dioecious, meaning individual trees are either male or female, although some
hermaphroditic forms exist in certain populations. Approximately 10 percent of trees in
natural populations are polygamous-monoecious, bearing both male and female flowers.
Flowers are small, inconspicuous, and reddish-green, borne in dense clusters along the
older branches and trunk (cauliflory), a distinctive feature of the species. This
cauliflorous flowering pattern means that flowers and subsequent pods develop directly
from the bark of mature branches and the main trunk. Each flower has five sepals but no
petals, with the sepals providing the reddish-green coloration. Male flowers are borne in
condensed catkin-like racemes and have prominent stamens, while female flowers have a
superior ovary and short style.

Female flowers develop into the characteristic carob pods: thick, leathery, straight or
slightly curved legumes, 10 to 25 cm long and 2 to 4 cm wide, dark brown to almost black
when ripe. The pod wall is thick (2 to 6 mm) and tough. Each pod contains 5 to 15 hard,
oval seeds (each approximately 8 to 10 mm long and 6 to 8 mm wide) embedded in a sweet,
sticky, brown pulp. Pods persist on the tree for several months after ripening and are
typically harvested by knocking them from branches with long poles.

## Ecology and Climate Requirements

Carob is supremely adapted to the Mediterranean climate, tolerating hot, dry summers
with temperatures exceeding 40 degrees Celsius and mild, wet winters. It is one of the
most drought-tolerant cultivated trees, capable of surviving and producing fruit in areas
with as little as 250 to 300 mm of annual rainfall once established. Deep roots enable
access to groundwater far below the soil surface. It grows well on poor, rocky,
calcareous, and saline soils, though it performs best on deep, well-drained loams.

The tree is frost-sensitive when young, with damage occurring below approximately -5
degrees Celsius, but mature trees can withstand brief dips to -7 to -9 degrees Celsius
with some dieback. Optimal growth occurs at temperatures between 20 and 30 degrees
Celsius. It thrives in full sun and does not tolerate waterlogging, though it can
withstand periodic waterlogging better than many other Mediterranean trees. The species is
also tolerant of coastal conditions including salt spray and wind.

## Edible Uses

The ripe carob pod pulp is naturally sweet, containing approximately 40 to 50 percent
sugars (mainly sucrose, fructose, and glucose), 5 to 10 percent protein, 1 to 2 percent
fat, and significant dietary fiber content. The pods also contain B vitamins, calcium,
iron, magnesium, [[plant-macronutrient-functions-nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium]], and zinc. It has been used as a food since
antiquity, referenced in the Bible (Genesis 30:37, where Jacob places carob rods before
Laban's flocks) and classical Greek and Roman texts.

The dried, ground pod powder (carob powder or carob flour) is used as a cocoa
substitute in baking and confectionery, valued for its natural sweetness, lack of caffeine
and theobromine (stimulants present in chocolate), and low glycemic index. Carob powder
has a milder, sweeter flavor than cocoa powder and does not contain oxalates. Fresh pods
can be chewed directly as a sweet snack. In Mediterranean and Middle Eastern cuisines,
carob is used in cakes, biscuits, beverages, and traditional sweets such as carob molasses
(dibs el kharrub) and carob jam. The pods are also fermented into alcoholic beverages in
several regions.

Carob pod meal is widely used as livestock feed, particularly for cattle, horses,
sheep, goats, and poultry, providing energy, fiber, and protein. The pods can constitute
up to 20 percent of ruminant feed rations without adverse effects. In times of scarcity,
carob pods have served as a famine food for human populations, as they store well for
years when kept dry.

## Industrial and Commercial Uses

The seeds of Ceratonia siliqua are the commercial source of locust bean gum (LBG), also
known as carob gum or carob bean gum. This galactomannan polysaccharide constitutes
approximately 35 to 42 percent of the seed weight and is extracted from the endosperm. LBG
is used as a thickening, stabilizing, and gelling agent in food products (ice cream, cream
cheese, baked goods, sauces, salad dressings), pharmaceuticals (laxatives, controlled-
release formulations), cosmetics (creams, lotions), and [[fungal-ligninolytic-enzymes-in-industrial-applications]] (textile
sizing, paper coating, oil well drilling fluids).

LBG has one of the highest molecular weights among commercial gums and forms strong
synergistic gels with xanthan gum, making it indispensable in modern food technology for
creating stable gels, controlling ice crystal formation in frozen products, and improving
texture in processed foods. Global production of locust bean gum is approximately 15,000
to 20,000 metric tons annually, with Spain, Italy, Portugal, Morocco, and Turkey being the
major producing countries.

The wood is hard, dense, and reddish-brown, used for woodworkinpermacultureand fuel. It
produces a hot, even burn and is valued as firewood. The tree produces a valuable honeydew
honey when tended by scale insects, which bees collect enthusiastically in Mediterranean
regions.

## Nitrogen Fixation and Permaculture Role

As a legume, Ceratonia siliqua forms symbiotic associations with rhizobial bacteria
(Bradyrhizobium spp.) in its root nodules, enabling atmospheric nitrogen fixation. For more on this, see [[nitrogen-fixing-trees]]. While
the nitrogen fixation rates are moderate compared to many other leguminous trees, the
carob contributes [[query-how-do-legumes-add-nitrogen-to-soil]] nitrogen cycling in agroforestry systems, particularly in the
dryland environments where it excels.

[[mollison-lawn-reform-and-alternatives-in-permaculture-design]], carob serves as a canopy-layer food tree in Mediterranean [[food-forest-design]]. Its deep taproot brings minerals from subsoil layers to the surface through [[leaf-litter-microfungal-diversity-tropical-temperate]]. The dense evergreen canopy provides year-round shade, wind protection, and mulch
from fallen leaves and pods. It functions effectively as a living fence and windbreak when
planted in hedgerow configurations. When intercropped with olives, grapes, citrus, or
pasture, it provides shade without severely competing for surface water due to its deep
root architecture. The fallen pods provide supplementary fodder for grazing animals in
agroforestry-pastoral silvopastoral systems.
