# Meruliporia incrassata

**Meruliporia incrassata** is a basidiomycete fungus and the primary cause of dry rot in buildings in California and other parts of North America.

It is one of the most destructive wood-decay fungi affecting residential structures, capable of reducing homes to rubble and sawdust within remarkably short periods.

Together with [[serpula-lacrymans]], these fungi share vivid Latin names that evoke their glistening, yellowish colonies and serpentine taproots.

## Taxonomy and Etymology

The species was first described in 1849 (Verrall, 1968).

The name decomposes as follows:

- *Meruli* refers to the yellow colour of the mycelium, comparable to the beak of a male blackbird (*Turdus merula*)
- *poria* refers to the porous nature of the fruiting bodies
- *incrassata* refers to their thickness

Mordecai Cooke (1871) wrote of related dry rot fungi with evocative descriptions.

The names of these organisms paint a vivid picture of their destructive biology.

## Damage to Buildings

Meruliporia incrassata has been called the "house-eating fungus" for good reason.

In documented cases in Santa Barbara, California, a mysterious foam was observed peeking out of laundry room walls.

After dry rot was initially diagnosed and treated, residents returned from vacation to find that nearly a quarter of their home had been consumed in a mere week.

Christina Kastner remarked that "it came back with a vengeance, like it got mad."

The fungus can transform springy lumber into parched brown cubes that crumble when touched.

Contractors refer to this condition as brown cubical rot.

Mycologist [[david-arora]] (1996) compared dry rot to "a horde of army ants in search of food," noting it will "overrun everything in its way: bricks, stones, tiles, plaster, drainpipes, wires, leather boots, cement floors, books, tea kettles, even corpses."

## Growth and Spread

The fungus enters buildings through several pathways:

- Wooden posts making direct contact with soil
- Soil accumulating under the bottom edge of stucco exteriors
- Contact with exterior particle board underneath vinyl siding
- Cracks in concrete slabs and holes for pipes or electrical work
- Plumbing leaks providing moisture

Once established in a basement, hyphae of dry rot fungi forge through the microscopic cells of hardwood beams, digesting cellulose walls.

The brown colour of the decayed wood is due to the progressive concentration of dark-pigmented lignin.

Unlike white rot fungi that extract lignin, brown rot fungi like M. incrassata selectively remove cellulose.

## Rhizomorphs and Water Transport

A critical feature of M. incrassata is its ability to form strands and rhizomorphs that can conduct water indoors.

Unlike [[serpula-lacrymans]], which forms strands but does not seem to produce rhizomorphs, Meruliporia generates both kinds of structure.

Rhizomorphs can be much larger than strands, have a waterproofed surface, and enclose a central pipe for the transmission of water and dissolved nutrients.

In one case, a "taproot with the diameter of a grapefruit" was found extending from the rear of a house.

A cut rhizomorph exudes a distinctive mushroomy smell.

The fungus can colonize an entire home without forming a single spore, relying instead on its rhizomorph network for both water transport and territorial expansion.

Advanced cases are obvious: white mats of mycelium cling to beams, strands and rhizomorphs dangle between areas of decay, and revolting fruiting bodies develop.

One homeowner described her infestation as looking like Camembert cheese, an apt comparison since the Camembert rind is itself a living mat of hyphae.

## Prevention and Control

Many cases of dry rot could be avoided if builders ensured that a few inches of bare concrete showed all the way around a home exterior.

Piling soil against a house is a critical mistake, as ants and termites can use the same pathways into homes as fungi.

Control involves cutting the rhizomorph connections, digging ditches around foundations filled with concrete, and replacing damaged timber.

Insurance policies frequently do not cover the extensive repair costs, which in documented cases have exceeded US$100,000.

Detection has even been explored using trained dogs: a Finnish study evaluated Labrador retrievers for detecting dry rot, finding them effective at locating contaminated materials.

## Comparison with Serpula lacrymans

M. incrassata is the North American ecological equivalent of the European [[serpula-lacrymans]].

Both produce brown rot, form extensive rhizomorph networks, and can devastate buildings.

The search for wild Serpula lacrymans has been conducted in the Himalayas, where its natural habitat has been studied.

Both fungi are ironically very sensitive to dehydration despite their common name referring to dry conditions.

They thrive in the wet interior of a beam but only emerge on surfaces if they remain damp and shaded from sunlight.

## Comparison with Other Wood Decay Fungi

Unlike indoor moulds such as [[stachybotrys-chartarum]] that get their water from the dampness of their surroundings, dry rot fungi operate through an active water transport system via rhizomorphs.

Wet rot fungi like [[coniophora-puteana]] and [[gloeophyllum-trabeum]] cause damage in pre-soaked wood but lack the long-range water-conducting structures that make dry rot so destructive in otherwise dry buildings.

Terry Amburgey, Professor of Forest Products at Mississippi State University, says that the dry rot fungus "will infiltrate a foundation, wood, or concrete, and pretty soon the entire house goes."
