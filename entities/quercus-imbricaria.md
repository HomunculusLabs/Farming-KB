## Taxonomy and Morphology

Quercus imbricaria was described by André Michaux in 1801 based on specimens collected in the southeastern United States. The specific epithet imbricaria refers to the historical use of its wood for shingles, derived from the Latin *imbrex* meaning a tile or roofing piece.

The species belongs to Quercus section Lobatae (the red oaks), characterized by bristle-tipped leaf lobes or teeth and acorns requiring two years to mature. Despite this placement, shingle [[jeavons-climate-adaptation-growing-seasons]] and are dispersed in autumn of the second year.

Germination is hypogeal, with the radicle emerging first and the cotyledons remaining below the soil surface. Seedling establishment is best on disturbed sites with partial canopy opening, where competition from herbaceous vegetation is reduced.

Male catkins appear in spring as leaves expand, dangling in pendulous clusters 5–8 cm long. Female flowers are inconspicuous, borne in the axils of new leaves on short peduncles. Pollination is primarily by wind.

The root system of shingle oak develops a deep taproot in well-drained soils, with widespread lateral roots. This deep rooting habit contributes to its [[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]].

The species sometimes hybridizes with other red oaks where ranges overlap, particularly with northern red oak and black oak. These hybrids can exhibit intermediate [[palmer-mushroom-hunting-field-identification]] challenging in contact zones.

Elevation range is from near sea level to approximately 600 meters in the southern Appalachians and Ozarks. The species is hardy to USDA Zone 4 in the northern portions of its range.

## Ecology and Applications

Shingle oak acorns are an important food source for wildlife including white-tailed deer, wild turkeys, fox squirrels, gray squirrels, and various songbirds. Acorns are relatively low in tannins compared to many other red oaks, making them more palatable to wildlife.

Acorn production begins at approximately 25–30 years of age, with good crops occurring at irregular intervals of 2–5 years. Individual trees can produce 2,000–5,000 acorns in a heavy mast year, supporting significant wildlife populations during fall and winter months.

The tree provides cover and nesting habitat for birds and mammals in mixed forest stands. Cavities in mature trees are used by woodpeckers, flying squirrels, and bats. The dense canopy offers shade for understory forbs and grasses.
