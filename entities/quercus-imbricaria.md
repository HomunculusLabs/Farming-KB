# Quercus imbricaria

## Overview

Quercus imbricaria, commonly known as shingle oak, is a deciduous tree in the family Fagaceae native to the central and eastern United States. It is one of the few oaks in the red oak group (section Lobatae) with entire, unlobed leaf margins, making it readily distinguishable from most of its relatives.

The species was historically valued for its straight-grained, split-resistant wood, which was widely used to produce wooden shingles for roofing. [[palmer-early-american-mycology-nineteenth-century]] settlers preferred shingle oak for this purpose because the wood splits cleanly along the grain into thin, durable pieces.

The common name "shingle oak" derives directly from this traditional use, and many historic structures in the Midwest were originally roofed with shingle oak shakes. The wood was also used for fence rails, barrel staves, and agricultural implements before the advent of modern synthetic materials.

Today the tree is planted as a [[mollison-designers-legume-shade-tree-pasture-systems]] and street tree across much of its range and beyond. Its uniform crown shape and tolerance of urban conditions make it a reliable landscape choice.

Shingle oak typically reaches 18–25 meters in height with a trunk diameter of 60–90 cm at maturity. Exceptional specimens can exceed 30 meters in height on optimal sites.

The tree forms a rounded to oblong crown with ascending branches. Under favorable conditions, shingle oak can live 200–300 years, with some documented individuals exceeding 350 years. [[soil-temperature-plant-growth-rate-doubling-rule]] is moderate, averaging 30–60 cm per year on good sites.

Quercus imbricaria is not considered globally threatened. It has a NatureServe rank of G5 (globally secure) and is widespread throughout its native range, though local populations have declined in areas of intensive agricultural conversion.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Quercus imbricaria was described by André Michaux in 1801 based on specimens collected in the southeastern United States. The specific epithet imbricaria refers to the historical use of its wood for shingles, derived from the Latin *imbrex* meaning a tile or roofing piece.

The species belongs to Quercus section Lobatae (the red oaks), characterized by bristle-tipped leaf lobes or teeth and acorns requiring two years to mature. Despite this placement, shingle [[calcium-accumulators-and-oak-leaves]] are entire or have shallow rounded teeth without bristle tips.

Leaves are 8–15 cm long and 2.5–5 cm wide, oblong to elliptical in outline. The upper surface is glossy dark green, while the lower surface is pale green to yellow-green with scattered stellate hairs along the midrib.

Leaf margins are entire or have 3–6 shallow, rounded teeth per side. The base is cuneate to rounded and the tip is acute to acuminate. This entire-margined condition is shared only with a few other red oaks, notably swamp chestnut oak and cherrybark oak.

Fall color is variable, ranging from yellow-brown to reddish-brown. It is generally less vivid than the deep reds and scarlets displayed by many other red oaks. Leaves may persist on the tree through much of the winter in a condition known as marcescence.

Twigs are slender, reddish-brown to gray-brown, with small, rounded lenticels. Terminal buds are ovoid, 3–5 mm long, with reddish-brown scales that are slightly pubescent along the margins. The pith is star-shaped in cross-section, typical of oaks.

Bark is dark gray to brown, developing shallow fissures and narrow ridges with age. On young stems the bark is smooth and gray, becoming rougher and more platy on mature trunks. Inner bark is reddish, consistent with the red oak group.

Acorns are 12–18 mm long, ovoid, and enclosed one-third to one-half within a shallow, thin-walled cap. The cap scales are reddish-brown and appressed. Acorns mature in two [[jeavons-climate-adaptation-growing-seasons]] and are dispersed in autumn of the second year.

Germination is hypogeal, with the radicle emerging first and the cotyledons remaining below the soil surface. Seedling establishment is best on disturbed sites with partial canopy opening, where competition from herbaceous vegetation is reduced.

Male catkins appear in spring as leaves expand, dangling in pendulous clusters 5–8 cm long. Female flowers are inconspicuous, borne in the axils of new leaves on short peduncles. Pollination is primarily by wind.

The root system of shingle oak develops a deep taproot in well-drained soils, with widespread lateral roots. This deep rooting habit contributes to its [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] but makes large specimen trees difficult to transplant. Nursery production typically relies on container-grown stock or root-pruned field-grown trees.

## Distribution and Habitat

Shingle oak occurs naturally from southern Pennsylvania and New Jersey west to southeastern Iowa and eastern Kansas. The range extends south to northern Georgia, northern Alabama, northeastern Texas, and the Ozark Highlands of Missouri and Arkansas.

The species is most abundant in the Ohio River Valley and the central Mississippi River Valley. It is generally absent from the Appalachian Mountains and the Gulf Coastal Plain, though scattered populations occur in the Arkansas Valley and Boston Mountains.

Historical records indicate that shingle oak was more common in presettlement forests than it is today. Selective logging of the largest, most accessible trees and conversion of forest land to agriculture reduced its abundance across much of its range during the 19th and early 20th centuries.

Shingle oak is relatively shade-tolerant in youth but becomes moderately shade-intolerant with age. It is often found as a scattered component of mixed hardwood forests rather than forming pure stands.

Typical habitat includes well-drained upland slopes, ridges, stream terraces, and bottomland edges. The species grows on a range of soil types including loams, clay loams, and alluvial soils, generally preferring slightly acidic to neutral pH.

Optimal growth occurs on moist, fertile, well-drained sites in full sun. Shingle oak is moderately drought-tolerant once established and can persist on drier sites where competition from more aggressive species is limited.

Annual precipitation across the species range varies from approximately 800 to 1,400 mm. Mean annual temperature ranges from 7°C in the northern part of the range to 18°C in the southern extent. The species requires a minimum of 160 frost-free days for reliable [[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]].

Shingle oak has been planted successfully as far north as southern Ontario and New England, well beyond its native range. It has also been introduced in parts of Europe as an ornamental tree, though it remains uncommon in cultivation there compared to native European oaks.

The species sometimes hybridizes with other red oaks where ranges overlap, particularly with northern red oak and black oak. These hybrids can exhibit intermediate [[cannabis-leaf-morphology-and-phyllotaxy]], making [[palmer-mushroom-hunting-field-identification]] challenging in contact zones.

Elevation range is from near sea level to approximately 600 meters in the southern Appalachians and Ozarks. The species is hardy to USDA Zone 4 in the northern portions of its range.

## Ecology and Applications

Shingle oak acorns are an important food source for wildlife including white-tailed deer, wild turkeys, fox squirrels, gray squirrels, and various songbirds. Acorns are relatively low in tannins compared to many other red oaks, making them more palatable to wildlife.

Acorn production begins at approximately 25–30 years of age, with good crops occurring at irregular intervals of 2–5 years. Individual trees can produce 2,000–5,000 acorns in a heavy mast year, supporting significant wildlife populations during fall and winter months.

The tree provides cover and nesting habitat for birds and mammals in mixed forest stands. Cavities in mature trees are used by woodpeckers, flying squirrels, and bats. The dense canopy offers shade for understory forbs and grasses.
