# Solanaceae (Nightshade Family)

## Description

The Solanaceae, commonly known as the nightshade family, is a family of flowering plants that has proven to be the primary source of pharmacological antidotes to Amanita mushroom poisoning. This botanical family produces a range of powerful alkaloids — including atropine, scopolamine, hyoscyamine, daturine, and nicotine — that act as physiological antagonists to the muscarine and amanitine alkaloids found in poisonous mushrooms. The convergence of toxic alkaloids from fungi and their antidotes from the nightshade family represents one of the most remarkable relationships in natural pharmacology.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Eudicots
- **Clade:** Asterids

## Key Fact

Palmer writes: "The conclusion is therefore unavoidable that, as all these plants belong to the same order, the antidotes to mushroom poisoning must be sought among the night-shades, botanically from the Solanaceae."

## Key Genera and Species Used as Antidotes

### Atropa belladonna (Deadly Nightshade)

- Primary source of **atropine**, the most well-established antidote for muscarine poisoning
- Atropine is a competitive antagonist at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors
- Administered hypodermically (subcutaneously) in cases of Amanita poisoning
- Dr. Isaac Ott demonstrated its efficacy against muscarine in frog and toad experiments (1870s)

### Datura species (Jimsonweed / Thorn Apple)

- Source of **daturine** (primarily scopolamine/hyoscine)
- Dr. T. F. Wood of North Carolina reported successful administration in mushroom poisoning cases
- Australian native species of Datura also cited as valuable remedy

### Hyoscyamus niger (Henbane)

- Source of **hyoscyamine**
- Also reported by Dr. Wood as successfully used in treating mushroom poisoning

### Nicotiana tabacum (Tobacco)

- Source of **nicotine**
- Palmer himself suggested the trial of nicotine or tobacco for non-smokers poisoned by mushrooms
- Acts on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, complementing muscarinic antagonism

### Datura stramonium (Stramonium)

- Professor Schiff of Italy advocated treatment using stramonium preparations
- Cited in Scientific American (October 1885) as a remedy for mushroom poisoning

## Pharmacological Mechanism

The Solanaceae alkaloids function as antidotes through a mechanism of receptor antagonism. Muscarine and related Amanita alkaloids overstimulate the parasympathetic nervous system by binding to muscarinic acetylcholine receptors. Atropine and related tropane alkaloids from Solanaceae are competitive antagonists at these same receptors — they bind without activating the receptor, thereby blocking the toxic alkaloid from exerting its effects.

This antagonism was experimentally demonstrated by Dr. Isaac Ott in the 1870s:

- A frog heart stopped by muscarine injection resumed beating after atropine administration
- The recovery was rapid and complete in experimental animals

## Historical Context

Palmer identified the Solanaceae connection as early as 1877, when a New Jersey physician suggested belladonna treatment after observing mushroom poisoning symptoms. Palmer published his analysis in the Moniteur Scientifique of Paris (January 1879), and noted the remarkable convergence of multiple independent researchers arriving at the same conclusion — that nightshade alkaloids were the antidote to Amanita poisoning — at roughly the same time.

Dr. S. E. Shadle's treatment of the Farris family poisoning in Shenandoah, Pennsylvania (August 1885) provided the first successful clinical application of atropine in human Amanita poisoning, saving three of five patients.

## Clinical Application

The standard approach derived from historical sources:

1. Administer atropine sulfate subcutaneously in repeated doses
2. Doses ranging from 1/120 to 1/60 grain historically reported
3. Monitor for improvement in heart action, pulse quality, and respiration
4. Continue supportive measures (warmth, stimulants, gastrointestinal protectants)
5. Begin treatment as early as possible — delay increases mortality

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Understanding the Solanaceae-antidote relationship is critical for anyone involved in mushroom foraging or cultivation because:

- Amanita species are among the most commonly encountered deadly mushrooms
- The latency period (6-18 hours) between ingestion and symptom onset means diagnosis is often delayed
- Atropine must be administered by medical professionals but the knowledge of its availability guides treatment decisions
- The pharmacological relationship illustrates the broader principle that natural products can serve as both poisons and medicines depending on context
