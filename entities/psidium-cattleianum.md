## Overview

**Psidium cattleianum**, commonly known as **strawberry guava** or **cattley guava**, is a small evergreen tree or shrub in the family Myrtaceae. Native to the Atlantic Forest of Brazil, it is valued for its edible fruit but has become one of the most damaging invasive species in tropical island ecosystems.

The species typically reaches 2–6 meters in height, forming dense thickets in its invasive range. The fruit is small (2–4 cm diameter), round to ovoid, with red or yellow skin and sweet, aromatic pulp.

P. cattleianum was introduced widely as an ornamental [[fukuoka-natural-farming-tropical-climates]], with peaks corresponding to seasonal rainfall patterns. A single mature tree can produce several thousand fruits per year.

## Distribution and Habitat

P. cattleianum is native to the Atlantic Forest biome of southeastern and southern Brazil, extending into eastern Paraguay and northern Uruguay. Its native habitat is subtropical moist forest and forest margins.

The species has been introduced to tropical and subtropical regions worldwide. Major invasive populations exist in Hawaii (where it threatens over 50% of remaining native forest), Réunion, Mauritius, Seychelles, New Caledonia, Fiji, and the Galápagos Islands.

In its invasive range, P. cattleianum colonizes disturbed forest, pasture margins, and intact understory. It tolerates a wide range of light conditions, from full sun to deep shade, giving it a competitive advantage over native species.

The species thrives in elevations from sea level to over 1,300 m in tropical islands. In Hawaii, it invades both wet and mesic forests, from lowland sites to montane cloud forests above 1,000 m elevation.

The species fruits prolifically and is dispersed by feral pigs, birds, and mammals. In Hawaii, feral pigs create disturbed sites that facilitate invasion, and their consumption and deposition of seeds accelerates spread into native forest. Seed viability in the soil can persist for over two years, creating a persistent seed bank that complicates eradication efforts.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

The fruit of P. cattleianum is edible raw and has a sweet, mildly tart flavor reminiscent of strawberry (hence the common name). The pulp is aromatic and juicy, though the numerous small seeds can be a textural inconvenience.

Culinary uses include jams, jellies, preserves, beverages, and desserts. The fruit makes an excellent jelly with a characteristic pink color and delicate guava flavor. It is also used in fruit salads, smoothies, and [[query-how-to-grow-rare-tropical-fruits-in-marginal-and-subtropical-climates]] international markets.

[[psidium-guajava]]**. It also contains significant levels of anthocyanins (red-fruited form), fiber, and potassium.

Traditional medicine in Brazil uses the leaves and bark of P. cattleianum as an astringent and antidiarrheal remedy. Pharmacological studies have confirmed antibacterial and anti-inflammatory activity in leaf extracts.

The yellow-fruited variety is generally sweeter and less astringent than the red form. Both varieties are commercially important in Brazil and are sold in local markets during the fruiting season. The red-fruited form commands a higher price due to its richer anthocyanin content and more intense flavor.

## Cultivation and Ecology

P. cattleianum is easily propagated from seed, which germinates within 2–6 weeks. [[hamilton-vegetative-propagation]] by cuttings and air layering is also successful and produces fruiting plants faster than seed-grown specimens.

The species is hardier than common guava, tolerating temperatures as low as -4°C. It adapts to a wide range of soils, from sandy to clay, and tolerates both moderate drought and periodic waterlogging. Optimal growth occurs in well-drained, slightly acidic soils with annual rainfall of 1,000–3,000 mm.
