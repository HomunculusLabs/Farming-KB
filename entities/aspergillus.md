## Aspergillus

## Description

Aspergillus is a large and ubiquitous genus of filamentous fungi (molds) and one of the most common contaminants in [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] laboratories. In culture, Aspergillus species appear as green, black, yellow, or brown circular colonies with a powdery texture from profuse conidiospore production. Aspergillus contamination frequently competes with mushroom [[mycelium]] on agar plates and [[grain-spawn]], appearing as green and powdery circular colonies.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Ascomycota
- **Class**: Eurotiomycetes
- **Order**: Eurotiales
- **Family**: Aspergillaceae
- **Genus**: Aspergillus

## Key Facts

- One of the most common mold contaminants in mushroom cultivation
- Appears as green (most common), black, yellow, or brown circular powdery colonies
- Spores ubiquitous in soil, air, and on organic matter worldwide
- Some species (A. flavus, A. parasiticus) produce carcinogenic mycotoxins including aflatoxins
- [[aspergillus-niger]] is used industrially for citric acid production
- Thrives in warm, humid conditions on nutritive substrates
- Easily distinguished from white, cottony mushroom mycelium by its colored, powdery colonies

## Relevance to Cultivation

Aspergillus contamination is a major concern in [[spawn-production]]. Green, powdery colonies are easily distinguished from white, cottony mushroom mycelium. Contamination typically originates from airborne spores during transfers, compromised HEPA filters, or insufficient sterilization. Contaminated containers must be immediately removed from the lab and sanitized with diluted bleach. The culture should never be expanded from contaminated material.

## Relevance to Mycology

One of the most studied fungal genera with significant roles in industrial mycology (enzyme production, organic acid fermentation, pharmaceuticals), medical mycology (aspergillosis), and food safety (aflatoxin contamination). A model system for studying fungal secondary metabolism, gene regulation, and eukaryotic cell biology.

## Identification in Culture

- Green, black, yellow, or brown circular colonies
- Powdery texture from conidiospore production
- Colonies expand radially with defined borders
- Does not form clamp connections
