# Crataegus monogyna

## Overview

**Crataegus monogyna**, the common hawthorn, is a deciduous shrub or small tree
in the family Rosaceae. Native to Europe, northwest Africa, and western Asia,
it is one of the most ecologically and medicinally significant hedgerow plants
in temperate regions. The species produces clusters of white spring blossoms
followed by deep red pomes (haws) in autumn. Its leaves, flowers, and fruits
have been used in European traditional medicine for centuries, primarily as a
cardiotonic. In permaculture, hawthorn is valued as a multifunctional hedge
component providing windbreak, wildlife habitat, and edible fruit.

## Taxonomy

**Plantae → Rosales → Rosaceae → Amygdaloideae → Maleae → Crataegus → C. monogyna** Jacq.

Key synonyms include *Mespilus monogyna* (Jacq.) Kuntze and *Crataegus
oxyacantha* var. *monogyna* (Jacq.) W.D.J. Koch. The genus *Crataegus* contains
approximately 200 species with significant hybridization between *C. monogyna*
and *C. laevigata* in Europe. *C. monogyna* is distinguished by its deeper-lobed
leaves (5–7 lobes vs. 3–5), single styles (vs. two), and later flowering time.

## Morphology

Hawthorn is a dense, thorny shrub reaching 5–14 m height with a rounded to
irregularly spreading crown. **Bark** is grey-brown, developing fissures with age;
young twigs are smooth and reddish-brown with sharp thorns up to 2.5 cm.
**Leaves** are alternate, deeply 5–7 lobed, 2–6 cm long, bright green above and
paler beneath with irregularly toothed margins. **Flowers** are hermaphroditic,
10–15 mm diameter, in dense corymbs of 5–25 with five white petals, 5–20 stamens
with pink anthers, and a single style — the key diagnostic feature.
**Fruit** (haw) is a pome 6–10 mm, ovoid, bright red to crimson, containing one
stone, ripening September–October with mealy but edible flesh.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to Europe (Britain to the Mediterranean), northwest Africa, and western
Asia (Turkey, Caucasus, northern Iran). Widely naturalized in North America,
Australasia, and temperate South America. Habitats include woodland margins,
hedgerows, scrubland, and rocky slopes from sea level to 1,800 m. Tolerates a
wide range of soils (chalk, clay, loam, sand) at pH 5.0–8.0. Moderately
shade-tolerant when young, fruits best in full sun. Hardy to USDA zones 4–8.
Withstands exposed sites and coastal salt spray.

## Bioactive Compounds

Primary pharmacological activity resides in the leaves and flowers (European
pharmacopeial drug "folium cum flore"). Key constituents include **oligomeric
procyanidins (OPC)** — catechin and epicatechin oligomers (dimers through
decamers) — the most therapeutically relevant compounds, contributing
cardioprotective, antioxidant, and vasodilatory effects. **Flavonoids** include
hyperoside (quercetin-3-O-galactoside, the marker compound for standardization),
vitexin, orientin, vitexin-2″-O-rhamnoside, and rutin. **Triterpenoid acids**
(oleanolic, ursolic, crataegolic) and **phenolic acids** (chlorogenic, caffeic)
are also present. Fruit is higher in sugars, pectin, organic acids, and vitamin
C (20–60 mg/100 g fresh weight).

## Pharmacology

Hawthorn preparations are among the best-studied herbal cardiotonics. Clinical
evidence supports use as an adjunct in mild chronic heart failure (NYHA class II).
Mechanisms include **positive inotropic effect** (increased myocardial
contractility via cAMP-mediated calcium modulation), **coronary vasodilation**
(procyanidins increase coronary blood flow), **antiarrhythmic activity**
(prolonged refractory period), **antioxidant activity** (procyanidin and
flavonoid free-radical scavenging with endothelial protection), and weak
**ACE inhibition** by certain procyanidin fractions.

Meta-analyses (Pittler et al. 2008; Guo et al. 2013) demonstrate significant
improvement in exercise tolerance, dyspnea, and fatigue versus placebo.
Standardized extracts (WS 1442, Crataemon) containing 18.75% OPC are the most
rigorously studied. German Commission E, ESCOP, and EMA have all issued positive
monographs. Typical dosage: 160–900 mg/day of standardized leaf+flower extract.

## Traditional and Modern Use

European traditions spanning back to Dioscorides used hawthorn for heart ailments,
diarrhea, and menstrual disorders. Modern phytotherapy applications include heart
failure (adjunct, NYHA II), mild hypertension, and traditional nervine use for
anxiety. Culinary uses include hawthorn jelly, fruit leather, wine, and syrups.
Young spring leaves are edible raw in salads.

## Ecological Role

Hawthorn is a keystone hedgerow species: haws feed blackbirds, fieldfares,
waxwings, and small mammals through winter. Dense thorny branches provide nesting
for 20+ bird species. Flowers attract bees, hoverflies, and moths as an important
early-summer nectar source. Larvae of numerous Lepidoptera feed on the foliage.
The species forms arbuscular mycorrhizal partnerships (Glomus spp., Rhizophagus
spp.), improving soil structure in mixed hedges.

## Permaculture Applications

Hawthorn is one of the most valuable multi-function plants in temperate
permaculture design. As a **hedgerow component**, it provides stock-proof barriers,
windbreaks (effective at 8–10× downwind distance), and wildlife corridors.
Combined with blackthorn (*Prunus spinosa*), hazel (*Corylus avellana*), field
maple (*Acer campestre*), and dog rose (*Rosa canina*) in mixed native hedges.
**Edible yield** reaches 2–5 kg per mature tree annually. Leaves and young shoots
are browsed by livestock in traditional hedge-laying systems. Propagation by cold
stratification (3–5 months), semi-hard cuttings in late summer, or whip-and-tongue
grafting for named cultivars.

## Related Species

- ***Crataegus laevigata*** (Midland Hawthorn) — Closest European relative; 2
  styles, 3–5-lobed leaves, earlier flowering; similar medicinal use.
- ***Crataegus pinnatifida*** (Chinese Hawthorn) — East Asian species; fruit used
  in TCM for digestive stagnation; source of commercial haw flakes.
- ***Crataegus phaenopyrum*** (Washington Hawthorn) — North American ornamental
  with distinctive thorns and excellent fall color.
