## Overview

**Agaricus xanthodermus**, commonly known as the yellow-stainer, is a basidiomycete
fungus in the family Agaricaceae. It is one of the most commonly encountered toxic
mushrooms in the genus *Agaricus*, frequently mistaken for the edible field mushroom
(*A. campestris*) or cultivated button mushroom (*A. bisporus*).

The species is responsible for the majority of mushroom poisoning incidents involving
the *Agaricus* genus, though its effects are generally gastrointestinal rather than
life-threatening. Rapid yellowing of the flesh upon cutting is the key diagnostic
character separating it from edible relatives.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Fungi
- Division: Basidiomycota
- Class: Agaricomycetes
- Order: Agaricales
- Family: Agaricaceae
- Genus: *Agaricus*
- Species: *A. xanthodermus*

The species was originally described by Joseph-Henri Léveillé in 1884. The epithet
*xanthodermus* derives from Greek *xanthos* (yellow) and *derma* (skin), referring to
the distinctive yellowing reaction. The lectotype was designated by Pegler in 1977.

**Cap:** 5–15 cm diameter, convex to plane-convex, dry, white to cream, sometimes
with a pale brown disc. Surface is smooth or finely fibrillose. The cap does not
typically stain yellow on its own but may show faint yellow tones in age.

**Gills:** Free, crowded, initially white then progressing through pink to
chocolate-brown at maturity. This pink stage is characteristic of the genus and
helps distinguish *Agaricus* from amanitas (which always have white gills).

**Stipe:** 5–12 cm tall, 1–3 cm thick, cylindrical with a slight bulbous base.
Ring is membranous, single, white, often with a yellowish tinge on the underside.
The stipe flesh turns vivid chrome-yellow when cut or bruised, especially at the
base.

**Flesh:** White, thick, turning bright chrome-yellow almost immediately when cut
or bruised. This reaction is most intense at the base of the stipe. The yellowing
fades gradually over 30–60 minutes.

**Spores:** 5.5–7.5 × 4–4.5 µm, ellipsoid, smooth, with a small germ pore. Spore
print is dark chocolate-brown.

**Odor:** Characteristic and distinctive — described as phenolic, inky, or like
library paste or iodine. This phenolic odor is considered the second most important
identification feature after the yellowing reaction.

## Distribution and Habitat

*A. xanthodermus* has a cosmopolitan distribution and is found across temperate and
subtropical regions worldwide. It is particularly common in Europe, North America,
Australia, and parts of Asia. Originally described from Europe, the species complex
has since been recognized as containing multiple cryptic taxa.

The mushroom fruits in disturbed habitats, particularly gardens, parks, lawns, and
along roadsides. It grows solitary to gregarious on the ground, often in enriched
soils containing compost, mulch, or manure. Fruiting occurs from late spring through
autumn, with peak abundance in summer and early fall.

The species has a strong association with human-altered landscapes and is rarely
found in old-growth forests or undisturbed prairie ecosystems. This anthropophilic
tendency contributes to its frequency as a cause of poisoning, as it commonly
appears in suburban yards and urban parks where foragers may encounter it.

## Toxicity and Pharmacology

The toxic principle of *A. xanthodermus* has been the subject of ongoing research.
Several compounds have been identified:

- **Phenol and phenolic derivatives** are present in high concentrations and are
  responsible for the characteristic odor. Free phenol content ranges from 0.1–0.5%
  of fresh weight, significantly higher than edible *Agaricus* species.

- **Agaricus derivatives** including various hydroquinone and catechol compounds
  have been isolated. The specific toxins responsible for the gastrointestinal
  effects have not been definitively identified.

- **Aromatic compounds** including p-anisaldehyde and benzaldehyde derivatives
  contribute to the off-putting odor. Symptoms of poisoning typically begin 15
  minutes to 2 hours after ingestion and include nausea, vomiting, abdominal
  cramps, and diarrhea. Symptoms are usually self-limiting and resolve within
  4–24 hours. No fatalities have been documented from *A. xanthodermus* alone,
  though dehydration from severe vomiting and diarrhea may require medical
  attention.

Some individuals appear able to consume the mushroom without ill effects, suggesting
variation in individual sensitivity or possible differences in toxin concentration
between populations. However, consumption is never recommended.

## Similar Species

Accurate identification is critical because *A. xanthodermus* closely resembles
several edible *Agaricus* species:

- ***Agaricus campestris*** (field mushroom) — the most commonly confused species.
  It does NOT stain yellow when cut, lacks the phenolic odor, and has a more
  pleasant anise-like fragrance.

- ***Agaricus bisporus*** (button mushroom) — the cultivated supermarket mushroom.
  Rarely shows any yellowing and has no phenolic odor.

- ***Agaricus arvensis*** (horse mushroom) — larger species with an almond-like
  odor. May show very slight yellowing that is much less intense than
  *A. xanthodermus*.

- ***Agaricus silvicola*** — woodland species with an anise odor. May show
  faint yellowing at the stipe base but the phenolic odor is absent.

The key diagnostic test is to cut the stipe base and observe whether the flesh
turns bright chrome-yellow within 10–20 seconds, combined with a strong phenolic
or iodine-like smell.

## Ecological Role

*A. xanthodermus* is a saprotrophic species, decomposing organic matter in soil.
It plays a role in nutrient cycling in disturbed habitats, breaking down compost
and decomposing plant material. The species is effective at colonizing
anthropogenic substrates including woodchip mulch and compost heaps.
