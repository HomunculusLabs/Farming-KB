## Overview

**Cibotium regale** (Schltdl. & Cham.) J. Sm. is a large tree fern in the family Cibotiaceae, native to the montane cloud forests of southern Mexico and Central America. It is one of the most ornamental species in the genus, prized for its golden-brown trunk scales and gracefully arching fronds.

The species is commonly called the Mexican royal tree fern or "helecho real" in Spanish-speaking regions. It grows at elevations between 1,500 and 2,800 meters in moist, shaded ravines.

Like other Cibotium species, it produces distinctive golden hairs on its trunk and stipe bases. These hairs have been used traditionally in some regions for wound dressing and as a styptic.

Cibotium regale belongs to an ancient lineage of tree ferns with a fossil record extending to the Jurassic period. The genus Cibotium once had a much wider distribution across Laurasia before continental drift isolated surviving species in Mesoamerica, Hawaii, and Southeast Asia.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Plantae · **Division:** Polypodiophyta · **Class:** Polypodiopsida · **Order:** Cyatheales · **Family:** Cibotiaceae · **Genus:** Cibotium · **Species:** C. regale

The species was first described by Schlechtendal and Chamisso and later transferred to Cibotium by John Smith. Synonyms include *Dicksonia regalis* (Schltdl. & Cham.) Hook.

The trunk reaches 2–4 meters tall and 10–15 cm in diameter, densely covered with soft golden-brown hairs and persistent stipe bases. Fronds are tripinnate to quadripinnate, reaching 2–3 meters in length.

Stipes are dark brown at the base, transitioning to greenish higher up, and bear the same golden indument. Pinnae are broadly lanceolate with finely toothed pinnules.

Sori are borne on the margins of the ultimate segments, protected by two-valved indusia characteristic of the genus. Spores are trilete, pale yellow, and approximately 40–50 µm in diameter.

The golden trunk indumentum is composed of multicellular uniseriate hairs up to 5 mm long. These hairs are soft and silky to the touch, distinguishing Cibotium from Dicksonia which has stiffer, bristly scales.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cibotium regale is endemic to the montane regions of southern Mexico, with populations concentrated in Oaxaca, Chiapas, and Veracruz. Small disjunct populations extend into Guatemala and Honduras.

The species inhabits cloud forests and moist montane ravines at elevations of 1,500–2,800 meters. It favors permanently damp, shaded sites along stream banks and on mossy rock walls.

Annual precipitation in its native range exceeds 2,000 mm, with frequent fog and mist providing additional moisture inputs. Temperatures range from 10°C to 22°C seasonally.

The fern grows on acidic volcanic soils rich in organic matter. It rarely colonizes disturbed sites, preferring mature forest interiors with intact canopy cover.

Cloud forest habitat in southern Mexico has declined by an estimated 50% since the mid-twentieth century. This loss directly impacts Cibotium regale populations, which depend on the stable microclimate these forests provide.

## Ecology

Cibotium regale functions as a substrate and microhabitat for epiphytic mosses, liverworts, and small orchids that colonize its fibrous trunk. The persistent stipe bases create a humid microenvironment favorable to invertebrate communities.

The golden trunk hairs may deter some herbivores and provide insulation against occasional frost at higher elevations. Spore dispersal is wind-mediated, with peak spore release during the dry season.

The species is slow-growing, adding approximately 3–5 cm of trunk height per year under optimal conditions. Individual plants may live for several decades.

It co-occurs with other montane ferns including species of Dryopteris, Polystichum, and Hymenophyllum. Mycorrhizal associations have been observed on the root mantle.

Decaying fronds form a nutrient-rich litter layer at the base of the trunk, supporting a detritivore food web. Amphibians such as plethodontid salamanders use the moist trunk crevices as refugia.

## Cultivation and Uses

Cibotium regale is cultivated as an ornamental specimen in botanical gardens and private collections worldwide. It requires consistently moist, well-draining soil with high organic content and partial to deep shade.

The species is cold-hardy to approximately –2°C for brief periods, making it suitable for frost-free Mediterranean and subtropical climates. Trunk humidity must be maintained through regular misting or drip irrigation.

Propagation is exclusively by spores, which germinate in 4–8 weeks on sterile peat at 18–22°C. Sporophyte development is slow, requiring 2–3 years before transplanting.

In traditional Mexican medicine, the golden hairs (called "pelo de helecho") have been applied as a hemostatic agent on wounds. This use parallels the ethnobotanical applications of other Cibotium species across Asia and the Pacific.

The species performs well in container culture when provided with a large, well-draining pot and consistent moisture. It is generally free of serious pest problems, though scale insects may colonize fronds in greenhouse settings.

## Conservation

Cibotium regale is threatened by habitat loss due to cloud forest clearance for agriculture and cattle ranching in southern Mexico. Climate change is projected to reduce suitable habitat by shifting cloud base elevations upward.

The species is included in the Mexican Official Standard NOM-059-SEMARNAT as a species subject to special protection. International trade is regulated under CITES Appendix II, which covers all Cyatheales.

Several populations occur within protected areas including the El Triunfo Biosphere Reserve in Chiapas and the Sierra Norte de Oaxaca conservation zones. Ex situ conservation collections are maintained at the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew.

Habitat fragmentation and small population sizes raise concerns about genetic diversity. Ongoing reforestation efforts in Chiapas include cloud forest restoration with native fern propagation.

Community-based conservation programs in Oaxaca have integrated Cibotium regale into sustainable shade-grown coffee plantations. This agroforestry approach maintains forest structure while providing economic incentives for habitat preservation.
