## Overview

Hyophorbe lagenicaulis, commonly known as the bottle palm or champagne palm, is a small, distinctive palm species endemic to Round Island, Mauritius, in the Indian Ocean.

It is renowned for its swollen, bottle-shaped trunk, which gives the species its common name. The trunk bulge is most pronounced in younger specimens and becomes less obvious with age.

The species is critically endangered in the wild due to its extremely limited natural distribution. Fewer than 50 mature individuals remain on Round Island, though it is widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical gardens worldwide. The species is one of the most commonly sold ornamental palms despite its critical [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] and ecological role of wild populations.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Hyophorbe lagenicaulis belongs to the family Arecaceae, subfamily Coryphoideae, tribe Chuniophoeniceae. The species was described by H. E. Moore and J. Dransfield in 1979.

The genus name Hyophorbe derives from the Greek words "hyos" (pig) and "phorbe" (fodder). The species epithet "lagenicaulis" means bottle-stemmed, referring to the distinctive trunk shape. Earlier taxonomic treatments sometimes placed Hyophorbe within the genus Areca before its recognition as a distinct genus.

The palm is relatively small, reaching 3 to 5 meters in total height. The trunk is solitary, swollen at the base and middle, tapering toward the crown, creating the characteristic bottle silhouette.

Trunk diameter at the widest point is 40 to 60 centimeters, narrowing to 15 to 25 centimeters at the apex. The swollen portion is most pronounced in cultivated specimens that receive adequate water and nutrients. In severely drought-stressed plants, the trunk may appear nearly cylindrical.

The trunk surface is smooth and light grey, with faint leaf scar rings. The crown is small relative to trunk size, consisting of 4 to 8 arching, pinnate leaves up to 3 meters long.

Leaflets are numerous, narrow, and arranged in a V-shape along the rachis, giving the crown a graceful, feathery appearance. Emerging leaves are red to bronze before maturing to glossy green. This coloration provides ornamental interest and is most vivid in actively growing specimens.

The leaf crown has a distinctive upward-angled habit, with leaves arching outward from a central point. This creates a shuttlecock or fountain-like silhouette that is highly valued in ornamental horticulture.

The inflorescence is short, arising from among the leaf bases, and bears cream-colored flowers. Male and female flowers are borne on the same inflorescence in a tightly branched structure.

The fruit is a small ovoid drupe, approximately 1.2 centimeters in diameter, ripening from green through orange to black. Each fruit contains a single rounded seed enclosed in a thin fleshy pulp. The seeds are dispersed by fruit-eating birds and possibly by ocean currents in coastal populations.

The root system is relatively compact for a palm, making the species suitable for container culture. Roots are sensitive to disturbance and the palm does not transplant well once established.

## Distribution and Habitat

Hyophorbe lagenicaulis is endemic to Round Island, a small islet of approximately 151 hectares located 22 kilometers northeast of Mauritius. It is one of the most geographically restricted palm species in the world.

Round Island is a nature reserve that supports several endemic plant and reptile species. The palm grows on rocky, well-drained slopes at elevations below 200 meters above sea level. It is often found on exposed ridgelines where competing vegetation is sparse.

Round Island has never been permanently inhabited by humans, which has helped preserve its endemic flora. However, introduced species caused severe degradation before conservation interventions began in the 20th century.

The native habitat consists of dry, tropical littoral forest and open scrub on coral limestone substrate. The island experiences a semi-arid climate with annual rainfall of approximately 800 to 1,000 millimeters. Vegetation on Round Island is dominated by drought-tolerant scrub and palm savanna communities.

Several other endemic plant species share the Round Island habitat with Hyophorbe lagenicaulis. These include the Round Island screw pine and the endemic bottle palm relative Hyophorbe vaughanii, though the latter is now extinct in the wild.

Historical records suggest the species may have once occurred on the main island of Mauritius. Habitat destruction and introduced herbivores eliminated all mainland populations by the early 19th century. The species is now considered extinct in its historical range outside of Round Island.

Fossil palm pollen from Mauritius dating to the Holocene suggests that Hyophorbe was once more widespread across the Mascarene archipelago. Sea-level changes and human colonization led to the progressive restriction of its range.

The species has been widely planted in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide. It is common in cultivation in Florida, Hawaii, southern California, and throughout the Caribbean and [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]].

In cultivation, the species has proven adaptable to a range of tropical and subtropical conditions. It is one of the most commonly available Mascarene endemic palms in the international nursery trade.

## Cultivation

Bottle palms are widely cultivated as ornamental plants valued for their compact size and dramatic trunk form. They are popular in residential landscapes, resort gardens, and as container specimens for patios and pool areas.

The species requires full sun for optimal growth and trunk development. Shade-grown specimens produce elongated, less swollen trunks and sparser crowns. The trunk swelling is partly a response to water availability, and irrigated specimens develop the most dramatic bottle shape.

Warm temperatures above 5 degrees Celsius are essential for survival. The palm is not frost-tolerant and suffers significant damage at temperatures below 2 degrees Celsius. Brief exposure to freezing temperatures can kill the growing point and result in the death of the palm.

It is moderately salt-tolerant and performs well in coastal landscapes. Established palms can tolerate brief exposure to salt spray and wind, making them suitable for beachfront plantings. Container-grown specimens require regular fertilization with a balanced palm-specific formula.

Irrigation needs are moderate once established, though consistent moisture promotes faster growth and more pronounced trunk swelling. [[mycorrhizal-biodiversity-drought-stress-response]] causes leaf tip burn and slows growth significantly. In extreme drought, the palm may abort its newest leaf and enter a state of dormancy until conditions improve.

Propagation is by seed, which germinates within 2 to 6 months under warm, humid conditions. Seedlings grow slowly, requiring 3 to 5 years to develop visible trunk swelling in cultivation. Plants begin flowering at approximately 10 to 15 years of age.

The palm is relatively slow-growing in general, adding approximately 15 to 30 centimeters of trunk height per year under ideal conditions. Maximum lifespan in cultivation is estimated at 50 to 80 years. Older specimens often lose the pronounced bottle shape as the trunk elongates and the swelling becomes less apparent.
