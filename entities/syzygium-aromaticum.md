## Overview

**Syzygium aromaticum** (L.) Merr. & L.M.Perry, commonly known as clove, is a medium-sized evergreen tree in the family Myrtaceae. It is native to the Maluku Islands (the Moluccas or Spice Islands) of Indonesia.

The tree is cultivated for its aromatic flower buds, which are harvested before opening and dried to produce the familiar culinary spice. Clove buds contain approximately 15–20% essential oil by weight, dominated by eugenol at 70–90% of the oil fraction.

Cloves rank among the most valuable spice crops globally by weight, with Indonesia, Madagascar, India, and Sri Lanka as the leading producers. Beyond culinary use, the buds and their extracted oil serve widespread roles in traditional medicine, dentistry, and pharmaceutical manufacturing.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:** Kingdom Plantae, order Myrtales, family Myrtaceae, genus *Syzygium*, species *S. aromaticum*. The basionym is *Eugenia caryophyllata* Thunb., and *Caryophyllus aromaticus* L. is a widely used synonym.

**Tree habit:** Clove trees reach 8–12 meters in cultivation and up to 20 meters in the wild. The crown is dense and conical when young, becoming rounded with age. Bark is rough, grayish-brown, and fissured longitudinally.

**Leaves:** Evergreen, opposite, coriaceous, simple, elliptic to oblong, 5–15 cm long and 2–6 cm wide. The upper surface is glossy dark green; the lower surface is paler with prominent oil glands visible as translucent dots.

**Flowers:** Bisexual, terminal or subterminal in cymes of 3–10. Calyx is thick, fleshy, and strongly aromatic, 1.5–2 cm long — this is the commercially harvested "bud." Petals are four, pale pink to red, 6–8 mm, shed shortly after anthesis.

**Fruit:** A fleshy, ovoid drupe, 2–3 cm long, purplish-red at maturity, containing a single seed. The fruit is known as "mother clove" and has limited commercial value.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cloves are native to the Bacan, Halmahera, Moti, Ternate, and Tidore islands in the North Maluku province of Indonesia. The species has been naturalized in Zanzibar, Madagascar, Tanzania, Sri Lanka, India, Malaysia, and Brazil.

The tree thrives in humid tropical climates with annual rainfall of 1,500–2,500 mm distributed throughout the year. Optimal temperature range is 20–30°C, with elevation typically 0–500 meters above sea level.

Soil preference is for deep, loamy, well-drained volcanic or alluvial soils with a pH of 5.5–6.5. Clove trees are sensitive to waterlogging and prolonged drought. They grow best as understory trees in partial shade during early establishment.

## Cultivation

Propagation is primarily by seed, though vegetative methods (grafting, air-layering, and cuttings) are used for selected cultivars. Seeds lose viability rapidly and must be sown within 7–10 days of collection.

Trees begin bearing at 5–7 years from seed, reaching full production at 15–20 years. Economic lifespan extends to 50–80 years. Yield ranges from 1.5–4.5 kg of dried buds per tree annually.

Harvest occurs when buds reach full size but before petals open, typically twice per year. Buds are hand-picked and sun-dried for 5–7 days until they turn reddish-brown and comprise roughly one-third of their fresh weight.

Major production constraints include sudden death syndrome (vascular wilt), root rot (*Pythium* spp.), powdery mildew, and infestation by clove bud moth (*Cryptophlebia illepida*). Nutrient deficiencies, especially potassium and magnesium, also limit yields.

## Essential Oil Composition

Clove bud essential oil is dominated by **eugenol** (70–90%), with eugenyl acetate (5–15%), β-caryophyllene (5–12%), α-humulene (1–3%), and minor quantities of α-pinene, limonene, and farnesol.

Eugenol is a phenylpropanoid responsible for the characteristic warm, pungent, aromatic flavor. It possesses significant antimicrobial, antifungal, antiviral, and antioxidant properties documented in extensive pharmacological literature.

Leaf oil composition differs substantially from bud oil, containing 60–80% eugenol but with higher proportions of β-caryophyllene and lower eugenyl acetate. Stem oil is intermediate. Bud oil commands the highest market price due to superior aroma and eugenol content.

Indonesia is the world's largest clove oil producer, with the kretek (clove cigarette) industry consuming approximately 90% of domestic production. Pharmaceutical and dental applications represent the primary export markets.

## Pharmacology and Traditional Medicine

Clove has a long history in Ayurvedic, Chinese, and Western herbal medicine. Traditional applications include toothache relief (clove oil applied topically), digestive aid, antimicrobial mouthwash, and respiratory decongestant.

The primary pharmacological activities are attributed to eugenol. It exhibits broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, including *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Escherichia coli*, and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.

Antifungal activity has been demonstrated against *Candida albicans*, *Aspergillus niger*, and dermatophyte species. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for eugenol ranges from 50–500 µg/mL depending on the organism.

Analgesic effects are mediated through inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis and direct interaction with TRPV1 (capsaicin) receptors. Eugenol has been widely used in dentistry as an analgesic and antiseptic in dental cements and temporary fillings.

Antioxidant capacity of clove extract is among the highest of all culinary spices, with ORAC values exceeding those of cinnamon, oregano, and turmeric. Hepatoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects have been confirmed in rodent models.

## Ecological Role

Clove trees provide habitat and food for various tropical bird species, including sunbirds and flowerpeckers that feed on the nectar. The dense canopy offers nesting sites for arboreal birds and supports epiphyte growth.

As a component of agroforestry systems in the Maluku Islands and Zanzibar, clove trees contribute to soil stabilization on sloping terrain. The leaf litter decomposes into nutrient-rich organic matter, benefiting understory crops.

The species is a significant nectar source for honey bees (*Apis cerana* and *A. mellifera*) in tropical apiaries. Clove honey is a specialty product with a distinct spicy flavor profile, commanding premium prices in niche markets.

## History and Trade

Cloves were among the most precious spices in ancient trade, valued in Han Dynasty China (c. 200 BCE) for breath freshening and court rituals. Archaeological evidence shows cloves in a burned-down kitchen in Syria dating to c. 1700 BCE, indicating early Indo-Pacific trade networks.

The Portuguese seized control of the Maluku clove trade in the early 16th century under Afonso de Albuquerque. The Dutch East India Company (VOC) later monopolized production through the "extirpation policy," destroying trees on islands outside their control to maintain scarcity and high prices.

French colonial administrator Pierre Poivre successfully smuggled clove seedlings to Mauritius and Réunion in the 1770s, breaking the Dutch monopoly. From these introductions, cultivation spread to Zanzibar, Madagascar, and eventually the East African coast, which became the world's leading producer by the mid-19th century.

## Culinary and Commercial Uses

Whole cloves are used in pickling, mulled beverages, baked goods, and spice blends such as garam masala, Chinese five-spice powder, and quatre-épices. Ground clove is used in baked goods, fruit pies, and meat marinades.

Indonesia's kretek industry is the single largest commercial application, blending clove with tobacco to produce aromatic cigarettes that account for approximately 5% of national GDP. The industry consumes over 50,000 tonnes of cloves annually.

Clove oil is used as a flavoring agent in food products, a fragrance component in perfumery, and a raw material for vanillin synthesis. Industrial applications include use as a metal corrosion inhibitor and as a biopesticide.
