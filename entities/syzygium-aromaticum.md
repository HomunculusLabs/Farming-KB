## Overview

**Syzygium aromaticum** (L.) Merr. & L.M.Perry, commonly known as clove, is a medium-sized evergreen tree in the family Myrtaceae. It is native to the Maluku Islands (the Moluccas or Spice Islands) of Indonesia.

The tree is cultivated for its aromatic flower buds, which are harvested before opening and dried to produce the familiar culinary spice. Clove buds contain approximately 15–20% essential oil by weight, dominated by eugenol at 70–90% of the oil fraction.

Cloves rank among the most valuable spice crops globally by weight, with Indonesia, Madagascar, India, and Sri Lanka as the leading producers. Beyond culinary use, the buds and their extracted oil serve widespread roles in traditional medicine, dentistry, and pharmaceutical manufacturing.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:** Kingdom Plantae, order Myrtales, family Myrtaceae, genus *Syzygium*, species *S. aromaticum*. The basionym is *[[eugenia-caryophyllata]]* Thunb., and *Caryophyllus aromaticus* L. is a widely used synonym.

**Tree habit:** Clove trees reach 8–12 meters in cultivation and up to 20 meters in the wild. The crown is dense and conical when young, becoming rounded with age. Bark is rough, grayish-brown, and fissured longitudinally.

**Leaves:** Evergreen, opposite, coriaceous, simple, elliptic to oblong, 5–15 cm long and 2–6 cm wide. The upper surface is glossy dark green; the lower surface is paler with prominent oil glands visible as translucent dots.

**Flowers:** Bisexual, terminal or subterminal in cymes of 3–10. Calyx is thick, fleshy, and strongly aromatic, 1.5–2 cm long — this is the commercially harvested "bud." Petals are four, pale pink to red, 6–8 mm, shed shortly after anthesis.

**Fruit:** A fleshy, ovoid drupe, 2–3 cm long, purplish-red at maturity, containing a single seed. The fruit is known as "mother clove" and has limited commercial value.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cloves are native to the Bacan, Halmahera, Moti, Ternate, and Tidore islands in the North Maluku province of Indonesia. The species has been naturalized in Zanzibar, Madagascar, Tanzania, Sri Lanka, India, Malaysia, and Brazil.

The tree thrives in humid tropical climates with annual rainfall of 1,500–2,500 mm distributed throughout the year. ## Essential Oil Composition

Clove bud essential oil is dominated by **eugenol** (70–90%), with eugenyl acetate (5–15%), β-caryophyllene (5–12%), α-humulene (1–3%), and minor quantities of α-pinene, limonene, and farnesol.

Eugenol is a phenylpropanoid responsible for the characteristic warm, pungent, aromatic flavor. It possesses significant antimicrobial, antifungal, antiviral, and antioxidant properties documented in extensive pharmacological literature.

Leaf oil composition differs substantially from bud oil, containing 60–80% eugenol but with higher proportions of β-caryophyllene and lower eugenyl acetate. Stem oil is intermediate. Bud oil commands the highest market price due to superior aroma and eugenol content.

Indonesia is the world's largest clove oil producer, with the kretek (clove cigarette) industry consuming approximately 90% of domestic production. Pharmaceutical and dental applications represent the primary export markets.

## Pharmacology and Traditional Medicine

Clove has a long history in Ayurvedic, Chinese, and Western herbal medicine. Traditional applications include toothache relief (clove oil applied topically), digestive aid, antimicrobial mouthwash, and respiratory decongestant.

The primary pharmacological activities are attributed to eugenol. It exhibits broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, including *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Escherichia coli*, and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.

Antifungal activity has been demonstrated against *[[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]], benefiting understory crops.

The species is a significant nectar source for honey bees (*Apis cerana* and *A. mellifera*) in tropical apiaries. Clove honey is a specialty product with a distinct spicy flavor profile, commanding premium prices in niche markets.

## History and Trade

Cloves were among the most precious spices in ancient trade, valued in Han Dynasty China (c. 200 BCE) for breath freshening and court rituals. Archaeological evidence shows cloves in a burned-down kitchen in Syria dating to c. 1700 BCE, indicating early Indo-Pacific trade networks.
