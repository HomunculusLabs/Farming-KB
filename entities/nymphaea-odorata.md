## Overview

Nymphaea odorata, the fragrant water lily or American white water lily, is a
perennial aquatic plant in the family Nymphaeaceae native to eastern North
America.

It is among the most recognizable aquatic plants, with showy white flowers
that float on the water surface from June through September.

The species is ecologically important as habitat and food for aquatic
wildlife, and has been used medicinally by Indigenous peoples throughout its
range.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Nymphaeaceae → Nymphaeoideae → Nymphaea → N.
odorata.

Nymphaea odorata was described by William Aiton in 1789.

Two subspecies are recognized: ssp. odorata (fragrant water lily) and ssp.
tuberosa (tuberous water lily), differing in rhizome morphology and flower
scent.

The plant grows from thick, fleshy rhizomes anchored in the substrate.

Leaves are floating, circular to oval, 10–25 cm in diameter, with a narrow
sinus extending to the center of the blade.

The upper [[bloomfield-rust-germ-tube-tactile-sensing-leaf-surface-navigation]] is bright green; the underside is often reddish or
purplish.

Flowers are fragrant, white to pinkish, 7–15 cm in diameter, with 20–25
petals arranged in overlapping whorls.

Blooms open in the morning and close in the evening over 3–4 consecutive
days before submerging.

Fruit is a globose capsule 2–3 cm in diameter containing numerous small
seeds that are dispersed by water.

## Distribution and Habitat

Nymphaea odorata is native to eastern North America, from Newfoundland west
to Manitoba and south to Florida and Texas.

It is absent from most of the Great Plains and arid western regions.

The species grows in quiet freshwater habitats including ponds, lakes,
slow-moving streams, and marshes at water depths of 0.5–2.5 m.

Water lily beds are most extensive in mesotrophic to eutrophic waters with
moderate nutrient levels.

The species tolerates a wide pH range but grows best in slightly acidic to
neutral conditions.

## Ecology

Water lily beds provide critical habitat for fish, amphibians, and aquatic
invertebrates.

The broad floating leaves create shade that moderates water temperature and
reduces algal growth beneath the canopy.

Aquatic insects including dragonflies, damselflies, and beetles use the
leaves and flowers as perching and mating sites.

Frogs and turtles rest on the leaves, and fish shelter beneath them.

Bees are the primary pollinators, with flowers opening during daylight
hours. Seeds are dispersed by water currents and possibly by waterfowl.

The rhizomes store energy reserves that allow the plant to survive winter
dormancy and regrow in spring.

Dense water lily colonies can impede water flow and limit recreational use,
and the species is sometimes managed as a nuisance in artificial waterways.

## Ethnobotany and Uses

Indigenous peoples throughout eastern North America used Nymphaea odorata
for food and medicine.

Rhizomes were roasted, boiled, or dried and ground into flour. Seeds were
harvested and eaten raw or parched, and young leaves were consumed as
vegetables.

Medicinally, poultices of crushed roots were applied to wounds, swellings,
and skin ailments.

Leaf infusions were used to treat digestive complaints, sore throats, and
coughs.

The flowers are widely cultivated as ornamentals for water gardens and
ornamental ponds, valued for their showy blooms and fragrance.

## Cultivation

Fragrant water lily is readily cultivated in ornamental ponds and water
gardens within its hardiness range (USDA zones 3–11).

Propagation is primarily by division of rhizomes in spring, which is faster
and more reliable than [[jeavons-seed-propagation-techniques]].

Rhizomes are planted in containers of heavy loam soil submerged 30–60 cm
below the water surface.

Full sun is required for prolific flowering; plants in shade produce
primarily foliage.

Fertilizer tablets placed in the root zone promote vigorous growth and
blooming. Pests include aphids, leaf miners, and the water lily beetle.

Overwintering in cold climates requires rhizome protection below the frost
line in ponds that do not freeze solid to the bottom.
