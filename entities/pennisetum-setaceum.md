## Overview

**Pennisetum setaceum**, commonly known as crimson fountaingrass or fountain grass, is a perennial bunchgrass native to Africa and the Middle East. It is widely cultivated as an ornamental landscape plant for its arching flower plumes and [[cannabis-sativa-flowering-cycle-management]].

Leaf blades are involute or convolute when dry, a morphological adaptation that reduces transpiration water loss during drought. The ligule is a short ciliate membrane, and the auricles are absent.

Growth habit varies with environmental conditions: plants in arid environments produce smaller tussocks with shorter flowering stems, while well-watered ornamental specimens reach maximum size. This phenotypic plasticity contributes to the species' success across diverse habitats.

Polyploid cytotypes occur across the native range, with diploid (2n = 2x = 18) and tetraploid (2n = 4x = 36) populations both documented. Most invasive populations are tetraploid, which may confer enhanced ecological amplitude through heterosis.

## Distribution and Habitat

The native range spans northeastern Africa (Egypt, Sudan, Ethiopia, Somalia), the Arabian Peninsula, and parts of the Middle East eastward to Pakistan. It occupies arid grasslands, rocky slopes, and disturbed sites within its native range.

Introduced globally as an ornamental, the species is now naturalized across the southern United States (Arizona, California, Nevada, Texas, Florida), the Hawaiian Islands, Mexico, South America, southern Africa, and Australia. It thrives in Mediterranean, arid, and semi-arid climates.

Preferred habitats include roadsides, urban open spaces, riparian zones, lava fields in Hawai'i, and degraded rangelands. It tolerates a wide range of soil types from sandy to clay, provided drainage is adequate.

Elevation range in its introduced habitats spans from sea level to over 2,000 meters in tropical and subtropical mountains. The species is less common in continuously humid tropical lowlands, where faster-growing perennial grasses outcompete it.

Seed bank persistence has been documented for several years in arid soils, with germination triggered by rainfall events. This persistent seed bank complicates eradication efforts even after mature plants are removed.

## Cultivation and Ornamental Use

Fountain grass is one of the most widely planted ornamental grasses worldwide, valued for its graceful arching form and showy flower spikes. Numerous cultivars exist, with the purple-leaved form *P. setaceum* 'Rubrum' (often listed as *P. advena* 'Rubrum') being the most popular.

Cultivation requirements are minimal: full sun, well-drained soil, and low to moderate water once established. It is exceptionally heat-tolerant and moderately cold-hardy, surviving temperatures to approximately -7°C.

In xeriscaping and water-wise garden design, fountain grass serves as a specimen plant, mass planting, or border accent. Its dried flower heads provide winter interest and are commonly used in floral arrangements.

The popularity of ornamental cultivars has contributed directly to the species' spread as a weed, particularly in regions where sterile cultivars are not reliably used. Nursery sales generate significant revenue in warm-climate regions, with the 'Rubrum' cultivar alone representing a substantial market share.

Breeding efforts have focused on developing sterile triploid cultivars to reduce the risk of escape and naturalization. The cultivar 'Fireworks' represents one such effort, though sterility in triploids can be incomplete under favorable environmental conditions.

## Invasive Species Impact

**Pennisetum setaceum** is classified as a noxious weed or invasive species by multiple regulatory agencies, including the Arizona Department of Agriculture, the Hawai'i Invasive Species Council, and the Australian government. The purple cultivar 'Rubrum' is considered sterile in many climates but has been documented setting viable seed in warm regions.

In Hawai'i, fountain grass has invaded thousands of hectares of volcanic substrates on the islands of Hawai'i, Maui, and O'ahu. It creates continuous fuel beds that increase fire frequency and intensity, converting fire-resistant native shrublands to fire-prone grasslands.

In the southwestern United States, fountain grass displaces native Sonoran Desert species along washes, roadsides, and urban interfaces. Its dense thatch accumulates and carries fire into desert plant communities that did not evolve with frequent fire.

Economic costs of invasion include reduced rangeland productivity, increased wildfire suppression expenditures, and ongoing control program expenses. In Hawai'i, fountain grass management costs are estimated at millions of dollars annually.

Control methods include manual removal, herbicide application (glyphosate, imazapyr), prescribed burning followed by herbicide treatment, and [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]] declines rapidly after flowering as the plant allocates resources to [[cervantes-seed-production-storage]] rather than leaf maintenance.

The dense thatch layer inhibits native seedling establishment by creating a physical barrier and altering soil surface microclimate. Allelopathic compounds have not been conclusively demonstrated, but competitive exclusion through resource preemption is well documented.

Several insect herbivores have been recorded on the species in its introduced range, but none provide effective biological control. Pathogens including rust fungi (*Puccinia* spp.) occasionally affect ornamental plantings.
