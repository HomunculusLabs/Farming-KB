## Solanum nigrum

## Overview

Solanum nigrum, commonly known as black nightshade, is a widespread annual or short-lived perennial herbaceous plant in the family Solanaceae. The species is native to Eurasia but has become naturalized across temperate and subtropical regions worldwide.

Black nightshade produces small black berries that are consumed as food in many cultures when fully ripe, while unripe berries and green plant parts contain toxic glycoalkaloids. The plant also has a long history of use in traditional medicine systems across Asia, Africa, and Europe.

Solanum nigrum is frequently confused with the more toxic [[plants-of-the-gods-atropa-belladonna-deadly-nightshade]] ([[atropa-belladonna]]), though the two belong to different genera and differ markedly in toxicity, growth habit, and berry morphology.

## Distribution and Habitat

Solanum nigrum is native to Europe, Asia, and North Africa. It has been introduced and naturalized in the Americas, Australia, and many [[psilocybe-semilanceata]].

The species thrives in disturbed habitats including agricultural fields, waste grounds, roadsides, forest edges, and riverbanks. It grows from sea level to approximately 2000 m elevation in tropical and temperate zones.

Black nightshade demonstrates remarkable adaptability to diverse climatic conditions. It germinates throughout [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] and can behave as a perennial in frost-free environments.

The plant is classified as an agricultural weed in many cropping systems but is also deliberately cultivated for its edible berries in parts of Africa, India, and China.

## Bioactive Compounds

The primary bioactive constituents of Solanum nigrum are steroidal glycoalkaloids, flavonoids, and phenolic acids. The glycoalkaloid profile varies significantly with plant part, maturity, and growing conditions.

Solasodine and its glycosides (solasonine and solamargine) are the major steroidal alkaloids. These compounds serve as chemical defense agents and are concentrated in unripe berries and green foliage.

Ripe berries show dramatically reduced glycoalkaloid levels, typically below 10 mg per 100 g fresh weight, compared to 100–400 mg per 100 g in green berries. This dramatic decline is the basis for the species' edibility when fully ripe.

Flavonoid constituents include quercetin, kaempferol, and their glycosides. These contribute to the documented antioxidant activity of leaf and berry extracts.

Phenolic acids identified include chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, and ferulic acid. The total phenolic content of ripe berries ranges from 30 to 60 mg gallic acid equivalents per gram of dry extract.

Polysaccharide fractions from the plant have demonstrated immunomodulatory activity in vitro, stimulating macrophage proliferation and cytokine production.

Trace amounts of vitamin C, iron, and calcium are present in ripe berries. The nutritional profile, while modest, contributes to the plant's value as a supplementary food source in subsistence agricultural systems.

## Edibility and Toxicity

The edibility of Solanum nigrum is closely tied to fruit maturity. Fully ripe black berries are widely consumed as food in Africa, South Asia, and parts of Europe. They are eaten fresh, cooked into preserves, or dried for later use.

Unripe green berries and all green plant parts contain significant concentrations of solanine-type glycoalkaloids. Ingestion causes gastrointestinal distress including nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea. Severe poisoning is rare but has been documented, particularly in children and livestock.

Cases of human poisoning attributed to S. nigrum often involve misidentification with more toxic Solanaceae species. Proper identification and consumption of only fully ripe berries eliminates virtually all risk.

Livestock poisoning occurs occasionally when animals graze on large quantities of green plant material. Sheep and cattle are the most commonly affected species, presenting with ataxia, salivation, and gastrointestinal distress.
