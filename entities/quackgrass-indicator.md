# Quackgrass (Lawn Indicator Weed)

## Description

Quackgrass (*Elymus repens*, syn. *Agropyron repens*) is a perennial grass weed that serves as a biological indicator of insufficient nitrogen cycling in lawn soils. Its presence in lawns signals that the soil food web is not adequately cycling nitrogen — specifically, that protozoa and nematode populations are too low to release nitrogen from bacterial and fungal biomass through predation.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Phylum:** Tracheophyta
- **Class:** Liliopsida
- **Order:** Poales
- **Family:** Poaceae
- **Genus:** *Elymus*
- **Species:** *E. repens*
- **Common names:** Quackgrass, couch grass, twitch grass, witchgrass, dog grass

## Key Facts

- Aggressive perennial grass that spreads by both seed and an extensive rhizome system.
- Indicator weed: its presence alongside clover in lawns indicates inadequate nitrogen cycling.
- The soil food web response is to add nematodes and protozoa via compost, compost tea, or protozoa soup to increase nitrogen cycling.
- Rhizomes can penetrate deep into soil and are extremely difficult to eradicate mechanically.
- Historically used in herbal medicine as a diuretic; rhizomes are also edible.
- Competitive advantage over lawn grasses in soils with poor biological activity because it can access nutrients at deeper soil levels via its extensive rhizome network.
- Reduces when biological nitrogen cycling is restored because lawn grasses with mycorrhizal partnerships can then outcompete it.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Quackgrass and clover appearing together in a lawn is a diagnostic signal that the soil food web's nitrogen cycling is impaired. Without sufficient protozoa and nematodes to consume bacteria and release plant-available nitrogen, lawn grasses — which depend on this biological nitrogen supply — cannot compete with deep-rooted weeds like quackgrass that can access nutrients from deeper soil layers.

The biological fix — adding protozoa and beneficial nematodes — restores the predator-prey interactions that drive the nitrogen cycle. As protozoa consume bacteria, they excrete ammonium, which becomes available to grass roots (especially those with mycorrhizal partnerships). With adequate biological nitrogen cycling restored, lawn grasses regain competitive advantage.

This approach contrasts sharply with the conventional response of applying herbicides, which further damages the soil food web and perpetuates the conditions that favor quackgrass.
