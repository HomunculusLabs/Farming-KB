## Fireblight

## Description
Fireblight is a devastating bacterial disease caused by *Erwinia amylovora* that affects members of the Rosaceae family, particularly apples, pears, quince, crabapples, and related ornamental plants. The disease gets its name from the scorched, blackened appearance of infected leaves, blossoms, and twigs, which appear to have been burned by fire. It is one of the most serious diseases of pome fruit worldwide and can kill entire trees in a single growing season. Elaine Ingham reports anecdotal evidence of fireblight suppression using fungal-dominated compost tea on several estates in New York.

## Classification
- **Causal organism**: Erwinia amylovora
- **Type**: Gram-negative bacterium
- **Family**: Enterobacteriaceae
- **Host range**: Rosaceae family — primarily pome fruits (apple, pear, quince) and ornamental relatives (crabapple, cotoneaster, hawthorn, pyracantha, mountain ash)
- **Disease type**: Systemic bacterial infection

## Key Facts

### Symptoms and Disease Progression
- **Blossom blight**: Blossoms appear water-soaked, then wilt, turn brown to black
- **Shoot blight**: Tips of shoots wilt and curl into a characteristic "shepherd's crook" shape
- **Canker formation**: Sunken, darkened areas on branches and trunk where bacteria overwinter
- **Fruit infection**: Fruit becomes water-soaked, then dark, ooze (bacterial exudate) may appear
- **Bark symptoms**: Blistered, sunken bark with amber/brown bacterial ooze in warm humid weather
- Progression can be extremely rapid in warm, wet conditions (75-85F, 24-29C)
- Infection can spread through vascular system to roots, killing the entire tree

### Infection Conditions
- Primary infection occurs through blossoms during warm (65-86F), wet weather
- Bacteria are spread by rain splash, insects (pollinators), wind-driven rain, and pruning tools
- Natural openings (stomata, nectaries, wounds) serve as entry points
- Hail storms can cause epidemic-level spread by creating numerous wound sites
- Bacteria multiply rapidly in blossom nectar before invading tissue

### Biological Control via AACT
- Elaine Ingham reports that fungi are needed to suppress fireblight
- Anecdotal evidence from estates in New York shows fireblight was controlled using compost tea
- Paul Wagner and James Sottilo (SFI New York lab) have documented results with fireblight using biological methods
- Key mechanism: beneficial fungal coverage on blossoms and leaf surfaces prevents Erwinia from establishing
- Strategy requires high fungal biomass in tea — bacterial tea alone is insufficient
- Applications should begin before blossom opening and continue through the susceptible period

### Conventional Management
- Antibiotic sprays: streptomycin and oxytetracycline (increasing resistance concerns)
- Copper-based sprays during dormancy and bloom
- Removal and destruction of infected wood (cutting 8-12 inches below visible symptoms)
- Resistant cultivar selection
- Weather-based predictive models (MARYBLYT, Cougarblight) for timing spray applications
- Dormant-season copper applications to reduce overwintering inoculum

### Challenges in Biological Management
- Fireblight can spread extremely rapidly under favorable conditions
- Blossom period is the critical infection window — timing of AACT applications is essential
- Need high fungal coverage on blossoms where infection initiates
- Complementary approach may include beneficial bacteria that compete with Erwinia for blossom resources
- Monitoring and early detection remain critical regardless of management approach

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology
- **Pome fruit production**: Most serious bacterial disease of apple and pear worldwide
- **Biological control frontier**: Represents one of the more challenging diseases for biological management due to rapid progression
- **Compost tea efficacy testing**: Useful test case for demonstrating fungal-dominated AACT against bacterial pathogens
- **Integrated management**: Most realistic approach combines biological methods with cultural practices and careful monitoring
- **Research opportunity**: More documented, replicated trials needed to validate anecdotal successes reported by Ingham and colleagues
