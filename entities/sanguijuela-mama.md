# Sanguijuela Mama

## Description

The sanguijuela mama (mother leech) is a colossal supernatural leech described in Amazonian folklore as approximately twelve meters long, with four antennae and no mouth.
It inhabits isolated jungle lakes (cochas bravas) and kills by draining all the blood from its victims using electromagnetic waves emanating from its head.
In Amaringo's account, the being belongs to a class of dangerous aquatic or forest entities whose reality is confirmed through visionary experience rather than ordinary zoological observation.
The story presents remote lakes as places where the boundary between animal, spirit, and cryptid becomes unstable.

## Key Facts

- The sanguijuela mama is described as a giant leech approximately twelve meters in length.
- It has four antennae on its head but no mouth, feeding instead through electromagnetic waves.
- It inhabits cochas bravas, isolated lakes deep in the jungle where enormous beasts are said to live.
- The creature kills by drawing all the blood from victims' bodies using electromagnetic waves emanating from its head.
- In the story recounted by Amaringo, eight hunters camped near a lake.
- One man awoke at midnight under a full moon to see the sanguijuela mama attacking a companion in his hammock.
- The creature had the power to make everyone sleep deeply, similar to the yana-puma (black jaguar) and [[amaringo-ayahuasca-visions-shipibo-iconography]].
- The sanguijuela mama is associated with the same class of sleep-inducing predators as the yana-puma and Sachamama.
- The story originates from Amaringo's grandfather, who lived near a brook called Quincha, a tributary of the Fanacha River.

## Narrative Pattern

The account begins as inherited oral knowledge rather than direct experience.
A grandfather's story describes a dangerous place, a group of hunters, a night camp, and a survivor who witnesses what others cannot.
The full moon heightens visibility while also making the scene uncanny.
The predator does not attack like an ordinary animal.
It immobilizes the group through sleep and drains life at a distance.
The survivor's inability to wake his companions reinforces the creature's supernatural power.
By morning, the evidence is physical: dead bodies without blood.
The story therefore joins visionary, ecological, and forensic modes of truth.

## Interpretive Notes

The absence of a mouth is crucial because it marks the sanguijuela mama as more than an enlarged natural leech.
Its feeding is energetic, distant, and invisible except through its effects.
The four antennae emphasize sensing and projection rather than biting.
Its lake habitat suggests depth, isolation, and the danger of places that are not part of everyday human movement.
The sleep power connects it to other Amazonian predators that overcome humans by altering awareness before killing the body.
In practical terms, the tale teaches caution around remote waters, night camps, and places known by elders to be dangerous.
## Place-Based Caution

The story also functions as a map of risk, warning listeners that some lakes should not be treated as ordinary campsites.
Its power depends on attention to place: the danger is tied to specific waters, night conditions, and inherited knowledge.
Respecting such warnings is part of moving safely through an Amazonian landscape understood as alive and inhabited.
