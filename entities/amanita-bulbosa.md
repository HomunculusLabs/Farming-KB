## Description

Amanita bulbosa is a species of mushroom in the [[amanita-genus]], classified among the most dangerous fungi known to mycology. It is one of the primary sources of the deadly alkaloid amanitine (also called muscarine or bulbsosine by different researchers). The French include A. bulbosa along with A. verna, A. phalloides, and A. mappa under a collective grouping due to their similar toxicological profiles.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Amanitaceae
- **Genus:** Amanita
- **Species:** A. bulbosa

## Morphology and Identification

Amanita bulbosa shares the universal characteristics of the Amanita genus:

- **Volva:** A wrapper enclosing the young plant before it leaves the soil; found at the base of the stem in mature specimens
- **Pileus (cap):** Remains of the volva appear as irregularly scattered, sub-persistent warts or scurf that can be rubbed off without damaging the skin beneath
- **Gills:** Pure white underneath the cap
- **[[spore-print]]:** White — visible as delicate shading when the mushroom is placed on blue paper
- **Color:** Highly variable — red, orange, green, yellow, white, and intermediate shades
- **Stem:** Characteristically bulbous at the base (reflected in the species epithet)

## Toxicology

A. bulbosa is extremely poisonous. Key toxicological facts:

- Contains amanitine/bulbosine — a potent alkaloid with no taste, smell, or visual indication
- A single meal can be fatal within three days
- Symptoms are delayed 8–15 hours after ingestion
- Produces symptoms resembling Asiatic cholera: dizziness, nausea, purging, fainting, delirium, and death
- Dr. Boudier specifically isolated his named alkaloid "Bulbosine" from this species

## Documented Poisoning Cases

### Sautry, near Corbeil, France
Two persons made a meal from A. bulbosa. Beyond initial vertigo in one case, no ill effects were felt until 3 AM and 8 AM the following morning. Both patients died within three days. The attending physician tried coffee, tea, antimony, ether, egg whites, laudanum, milk, leeches, ice, and emollient fomentations — none produced improvement except ice internally and external emollients as simple palliatives.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

A. bulbosa exemplifies why the entire Amanita genus should be avoided by amateur foragers. Its key lessons:

- **No reliable field test** can distinguish it from edible species by taste, appearance, or chemical reaction
- **Cross-contamination** is a real danger — storing A. bulbosa with edible mushrooms can poison the edible specimens
- **Delayed symptom onset** is the hallmark — the 8–15 hour window means patients may not connect their illness to the meal
- **Species grouping:** The French convention of grouping A. bulbosa with A. verna, A. phalloides, and A. mappa reflects their practical equivalence in danger level
- Several Amanita species are safely edible, but the risks of misidentification make the whole group dangerous for non-experts
