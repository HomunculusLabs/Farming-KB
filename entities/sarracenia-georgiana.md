# Sarracenia georgiana

## Overview

Sarracenia georgiana, commonly known as the **Georgia pitcher plant** or **Okefenokee pitcher plant**, is a perennial carnivorous plant in the family Sarraceniaceae. It is endemic to the southeastern [[fungal-biodiversity-conservation-status]] reflects ongoing habitat loss from wetland drainage, agriculture, and fire suppression.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Sarracenia georgensis (sic) was first described by Thomas Walter in 1788, though the currently accepted orthography is S. georgiana. The species name refers to the state of Georgia, where the type locality is located.

The classification places it within Sarracenia sect. Sarracenia, alongside S. purpurea and S. minor. Some authorities have treated it as a variety of S. purpurea, but molecular and morphological evidence supports its recognition as a distinct species.

Pitchers are 15–35 cm tall, shorter and more stocky than those of S. flava or S. leucophylla. The pitcher tube is green at the base grading to bright red or purple at the hood.

The hood is broad and rounded, projecting forward over the pitcher opening. A pronounced wing (ala) runs along the front of the tube, often with undulating margins.

The phyllodia (non-carnivorous winter leaves) are flattened, sword-shaped, and persist through the dormant season. Flowers are deep red to maroon, 4–7 cm in diameter, and appear on tall scapes before new pitcher growth in spring.

## Distribution and Habitat

S. georgiana is restricted to the coastal plain of the southeastern United States. The core of its range is in southern Georgia, with disjunct populations extending into northern Florida and southeastern South Carolina.

The largest known populations occur within the Okefenokee Swamp watershed in Georgia. Additional populations exist in the Apalachicola National Forest region of the Florida panhandle.

Habitat is limited to open, acidic wetlands including seepage bogs, pitcher-plant bogs, and sandy wet savannas. These communities depend on frequent fire to maintain the open canopy and herbaceous ground layer.

Associated plant species include sphagnum mosses, sundews (Drosera spp.), bladderworts (Utricularia spp.), and a diversity of carnivorous and non-carnivorous wetland herbs. The herbaceous layer is typically species-rich.

Elevation ranges from near sea level to approximately 50 meters. The plant absorbs released nitrogen and phosphorus, supplementing the nutrient-poor bog environment.
