# Panaeolus campanulatus

**Panaeolus campanulatus**, commonly known as the **bell-shaped Panaeolus**, is a mushroom species in the genus Panaeolus that has been investigated for psychoactive properties. The epithet "campanulatus" derives from Latin "campanula" (bell), referring to the distinctive bell-shaped cap. Some authorities place certain collections in the genus Panaeolina or treat them as conspecific with [[panaeolus-retirugis]].

## Taxonomy

- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Bolbitiaceae (or Panaeolaceae)
- **Genus:** Panaeolus (sometimes Panaeolina)
- **Synonyms:** Panaeolus retirugis, [[copelandia-panaeolus-genera]] remain imperfectly defined. The debate over placing some species in a separate genus Panaeolina continues, with modern DNA sequencing beginning to clarify relationships but a fully resolved taxonomy not yet achieved.

The genus Panaeolus is defined by its distinctive mottled gills — a pattern resulting from asynchronous spore maturation that is one of the most reliable field identification characters for the genus.

## Description

### Cap (Pileus)

- **Size:** 1 to 3 cm diameter
- **Shape:** Characteristically campanulate (bell-shaped) when young; may expand to convex but often retains umbo
- **Surface:** Smooth to slightly wrinkled or rugose, especially when dry
- **Color:** Hygrophanous; brownish or grayish-brown when moist, becoming tan or buff when dry
- **Margin:** Paler than center; slightly translucent-striate when moist
- **Dry pattern:** May develop radial cracks or fissures creating areolate pattern

### Gills (Lamellae)

- **Attachment:** Adnate to adnexate
- **Spacing:** Moderately spaced to subdistant
- **Mottling:** Characteristic spotted/checkered pattern from asynchronous spore maturation
- **Young color:** Pale grayish
- **Mature color:** Darkened patches develop progressively; uniformly dark at full maturity

The mottled gill pattern is the single most important field identification feature for the genus Panaeolus and is visible to the naked eye.

### Stipe (Stipe)

- **Dimensions:** 3 to 8 cm long; 2 to 4 mm thick
- **Shape:** Cylindrical; equal or slightly thickened at base
- **Surface:** Smooth or finely pruinose; pale to brownish
- **Texture:** Somewhat fragile; breaks easily
- **Base:** May be embedded in substrate; covered with fine white mycelial threads
- **Veil:** No partial veil or annulus present

### Spores and Microscopy

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| [[spore-print]] | Black |
| Spore shape | Ellipsoid to lemon-shaped (citriniform) |
| Spore size | ~11-17 x 7-10 µm |
| Spore wall | Smooth; moderately thick with distinct germ pore |
| Cheilocystidia | Present but often inconspicuous |
| Cap cuticle | Structure provides characters for species-level ID |

The black spore print is a key identification feature distinguishing Panaeolus from genera with brown or pink spore prints.

## Distribution and Habitat

Panaeolus campanulatus is widely distributed across temperate and subtropical regions of Europe, [[aquatic-fungi-spore-dispersal-ingold]] in animal dung.

The species is primarily coprophilous:

- **Primary substrate:** Herbivore dung, particularly horse and cow manure
- **Secondary habitats:** Manured soil, compost, enriched grasslands
- **Ecological role:** Among the first fungal colonizers of fresh dung
- **Season:** Spring through autumn; year-round in tropics
- **Peak:** Warm, moist periods; often after rain

As a coprophilous decomposer, P. campanulatus plays an important ecological role in breaking down complex organic materials in dung and releasing nutrients available to plants and soil organisms.

## Chemistry

The species has been reported to contain [[psilocin]], though typically at lower concentrations than more potent Panaeolus species. Psychoactive content varies significantly depending on:

- Geographic origin of the collection
- Specific substrate composition
- Stage of development at harvest
- Environmental conditions during growth
