---# Taxonomic position

**Kingdom:** Fungi.
**Phylum:** Mucoromycota in many modern treatments; historically grouped with Zygomycota.
**Order:** Mucorales.
**Family:** Rhizopodaceae.
**Genus:** Rhizopus.
**Species:** Rhizopus stolonifer.
The name Rhizopus nigricans was used after Christian Gottfried Ehrenberg described the fungus in 1818.
The currently used combination Rhizopus stolonifer was established by J. P. Vuillemin in 1902.
Older cultivation manuals may use broad labels such as Mucor, Rhizopus, or black pin mold rather than a precise species name.

## General description

The organism grows as hyphae that spread rapidly across exposed substrate.
It produces aerial stolons that bridge surfaces and allow the colony to occupy new areas quickly.
Rhizoids anchor the colony to the substrate at nodes.
Unbranched sporangiophores rise from the [[four-groups-neurotropic-fungi-global-distribution-guzman-1998]].
It is common in air, soil, decaying plant material, compost, kitchens, and storage rooms.
It appears readily on stale bread and damaged fruit because those substrates provide accessible sugars and moisture.
The species is especially common in warm, humid environments.
It tolerates a broad range of nutrient conditions and can use many carbon and nitrogen sources.
In laboratory culture it grows on many media, although nitrate as the sole nitrogen source can be limiting.

## Growth and physiology

The species is famous for speed.
Under favorable conditions it can cover a plate or exposed food surface in a short time.
Reported optimal growth is commonly near 25 to 30 degrees Celsius.
It can grow in acidic environments and tolerate a broad pH range.
Spores germinate when moisture, temperature, and nutrients are suitable.
Ultraviolet exposure can delay germination, but normal room exposure is not a reliable control method.
Heat sufficient to kill cells is far above ordinary incubation temperatures.
The practical lesson is that prevention and early disposal matter more than trying to suppress an established colony.

## Reproduction

[[conidia-and-asexual-reproduction-in-filamentous-fungi]] occurs by production of sporangiospores inside sporangia.
Sporangia are borne at the tips of sporangiophores and release many airborne spores when disturbed.
Sexual reproduction is possible in compatible mating types through formation of zygospores.
In practice, cultivators usually observe the asexual spore-bearing stage.
The abundant dry spores explain why a contaminated culture can seed an entire work area.
Containers showing mature black sporangia should be handled gently and sealed before removal.
