## Overview

**Bouteloua gracilis** (Kunth) Lag. ex Griffiths, commonly known as **blue grama**, is a perennial warm-season bunchgrass native to the [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] Great Plains and southwestern grasslands. It is one of the most ecologically dominant and economically important native grasses of the shortgrass prairie.

The species is recognized by its distinctive inflorescences, which resemble eyelashes or tiny combs. The one-sided, comb-like spikes are borne on slender, curving stems that give the plant a characteristic appearance when in bloom.

These ornamental flower spikes have made blue grama increasingly popular in ornamental horticulture. The golden-tan inflorescences persist through autumn and winter, adding texture and movement to naturalistic plantings and meadow gardens.

Blue grama is exceptionally drought-tolerant and maintains green foliage through the hottest summer months when many associated grasses go dormant. This summer-green growth habit makes it a critical forage species across arid and semi-arid rangelands.

The genus name honors Spanish botanists Claudio and Esteban Boutelou (1774–1862, 1776–1813). The specific epithet *gracilis* means "slender" or "graceful," describing the delicate flowering stems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Poales
- **Family:** Poaceae
- **Subfamily:** Chloridoideae
- **Genus:** Bouteloua Lag.
- **Species:** B. gracilis (Kunth) Lag. ex Griffiths

**Synonyms:** *Chondrosum gracile* Kunth, *Bouteloua oligostachya* (Nutt.) Torr., *Dinebra gracilis* (Kunth) P.M. Peterson & N.W. Snow.

Blue grama forms dense tussocks 15–60 cm tall. Under favorable conditions, it develops short rhizomes and can form a sod. The basal leaves are fine, 1–2 mm wide and 10–30 cm long, typically rolled or involute.

Leaf color is grayish-green to blue-green, giving rise to the common name. The ligule is a short membrane approximately 0.5 mm long, fringed with short hairs. Sheaths are glabrous to sparsely hairy at the throat.

Inflorescence culms are 20–45 cm tall, bearing 1–3 one-sided spikes. Each spike is 1–3 cm long and resembles a tiny comb, with 20–60 short branchlets arranged along one side of a flattened rachis.

Spikelets are 5–7 mm long, with one floret. The glumes are unequal and papery. Lemma bears a short awn or is awnless. Flowering occurs from July through September across most of its range.

Wind pollination is the primary mechanism. Anthesis occurs in the early morning when humidity is highest and temperatures are moderate. Each spikelet produces a single caryopsis (grain) enclosed within the persistent lemma and palea.

Seed dispersal is facilitated by the comb-like inflorescence structure, which catches wind and animal fur. The barbed awns and lemma hairs aid in epizoochory. Seeds may also be dispersed by water flow in low-lying grassland swales.

## Distribution and Habitat

**Bouteloua gracilis** is native to the Great Plains of North America, ranging from southern Canada (Alberta, Saskatchewan) through the central United States to northern Mexico. Its range extends westward to Arizona and New Mexico.

The species is the dominant or codominant grass of the shortgrass prairie, where it grows alongside buffalo grass (*Bouteloua dactyloides*). It also occurs in mixed-grass prairie, desert grasslands, and piñon-juniper woodlands.

Blue grama is adapted to a wide range of soil types but is most abundant on well-drained sandy loams and clay loams. It tolerates strongly alkaline and saline soils, making it important on gypsum-derived and calcareous substrates.

Elevation ranges from near sea level in the southern plains to approximately 2,400 m in the Rocky Mountain foothills. It occurs in areas receiving 250–500 mm of annual precipitation.

The species is increasingly dominant on rangelands that have experienced long-term heavy grazing pressure. Its low growth form and basal meristems make it tolerant of repeated defoliation, allowing it to increase relative to taller, less grazing-tolerant species.

## Ecology

Blue grama is a keystone species of the North American shortgrass prairie ecosystem. It provides the primary forage base for both native and domestic herbivores across millions of hectares of rangeland.

The species is a C4 grass, utilizing the efficient C4 photosynthetic pathway that confers superior water-use efficiency under high temperatures and drought. This physiological adaptation allows it to maintain productivity when C3 grasses become dormant.

Root systems are extensive, with fibrous roots extending 0.9–1.8 m deep. A dense network of fine roots in the upper 30 cm of soil contributes significantly to soil organic [[holmgren-soil-humus-carbon-storage-rebuilding]]. Blue grama grasslands store an estimated 25–30 tonnes of carbon per hectare.

The species provides habitat and food for numerous wildlife species. Prairie dogs, bison, pronghorn antelope, and elk all graze on blue grama. Ground-nesting birds including the mountain plover and long-billed curlew nest in blue grama-dominated grasslands.

Blue grama exhibits a dual reproductive strategy. It reproduces primarily by seed but also spreads vegetatively through short rhizomes and stolons. Seed viability in soil banks can persist for several years, facilitating recovery after drought.

Mycorrhizal associations are common and ecologically significant. Blue grama forms [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] relationships that enhance nutrient and water uptake in the nutrient-poor soils typical of arid grasslands. These fungal partnerships may contribute to its drought resilience.

The species responds favorably to moderate grazing intensity, which can increase tillering and overall productivity. However, continuous heavy grazing without rest periods depletes root carbohydrate reserves and reduces plant vigor over time.

## Cultivation

Blue grama is widely cultivated for range seeding, [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]], and low-maintenance turf. Its [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]], low fertility requirements, and grazing tolerance make it an excellent species for sustainable land management.

Seed should be planted at 2–4 kg per hectare in early summer when soil temperatures exceed 18 °C. Germination is typically slow and irregular, taking 14–30 days. Seedling establishment benefits from a season of reduced competition from weeds.

No irrigation or fertilizer is needed for range plantings once established. In turf applications, minimal supplemental irrigation (25–50 mm per month) maintains green cover through summer. Mowing at 5–8 cm height produces an acceptable low-input lawn.

The species is cold-hardy to USDA Zone 3 and heat-tolerant to Zone 9. It has no significant pest or disease problems in most settings. Seed production in cultivated stands is reliable, with yields of 50–100 kg per hectare.

## Uses and Ethnobotany

Blue grama has been a critically important forage grass since the bison-dominated prairie era. It remains one of the most valuable native forage species for cattle production on the Great Plains, providing nutritious summer forage with crude protein levels of 8–12% in the [[comparison-cannabis-vegetative-stage-vs-flowering-stage]].

The species is increasingly used in low-input and xeriscape landscaping. Blue grama lawns require 50–75% less water than conventional turfgrass and eliminate the need for regular fertilization and pesticide applications.

In native landscaping and ecological restoration, blue grama is a foundational species for recreating shortgrass prairie communities. It pairs effectively with other native forbs and grasses to establish self-sustaining plant communities.

Traditional uses among Indigenous Plains peoples include weaving the dried flower spikes into small decorative items and using the grass as bedding material. The Lakota and other groups used blue grama in purification ceremonies.

Modern conservation applications include its use in reclamation of disturbed rangelands, mine sites, and roadside verges. Its drought tolerance and ease of establishment from seed make it a reliable species for restoring degraded arid and semi-arid ecosystems.

Several commercial cultivars have been developed for improved turf quality and seed production. Notable selections include 'Hachita' (selected for turf use), 'Lovington' (improved forage type), and 'Native' (source-identified for conservation plantings).
