## Description

Evapotranspiration is the combined process of water evaporation from surfaces and transpiration through plant stomata, representing the primary mechanism by which trees return water to the atmosphere. Mollison devotes extensive attention to evapotranspiration as the critical link between forests and rainfall, citing USDA data and Amazon research to demonstrate that trees recycle the majority of received water back to the atmosphere, where it forms new clouds and rain.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Hydrology / Plant physiology / Atmospheric science
- **Components**: Evaporation (surface water loss) + Transpiration (stomatal water release)
- **Measurement**: mm/year or litres/tree/day
- **Relevance**: Primary driver of the forest-rainfall feedback cycle

## Key Facts

- An ordinary elm of medium size transpires approximately 15,000 pounds (6,800 kg) of water on a clear, dry, hot day (USDA Yearbook of Agriculture, 1949)
- Evapotranspiration in a 40-inch rainfall area is generally not less than 15 inches per year
- *Eucalyptus globulus* transpires 3,600–4,500 litres per tree per day
- A forest can return 75% of received water to the atmosphere (compared to the sea, which does not efficiently recycle)
- Of the 75% returned by trees: 25% evaporated from leaf surfaces, 50% transpired through stomata
- Only 25% of rainfall infiltrates soil and reaches streams
- Forested areas return ten times as much moisture as bare ground
- Forested areas return twice as much moisture as grasslands
- The release of water from trees and other plants accounts for half or more of all moisture returned to air globally
- Evaporation by day cools air; night condensation warms surrounding air
- Leaf surfaces are typically cooler than other objects at evening due to daytime stomatal evaporation

## The Two Components

### Evaporation (25% of forest water return)
- Water evaporates from wet leaf and stem surfaces after rain
- Light rain may not penetrate beyond the canopy ("total interception")
- Surface tension traps films of water across leaves and stems
- Cells absorb what is needed; remainder evaporates

### Transpiration (50% of forest water return)
- Water is released through stomata as part of photosynthetic gas exchange
- Sap rises in young oaks at approximately 1 cm/sec (Vogel, 1981)
- Only about 7% of trunk area needed as conductive tissue
- Only about one-fiftieth of xylem actively conducting at any moment
- Water may move in pulsed stages rather than continuous flow

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Evapotranspiration is arguably the most important process linking permaculture tree strategies to climate:

1. **Rainfall creation**: Forest-generated evapotranspiration produces 50% of the rain falling on the Amazon — the forest creates its own weather
2. **Cooling effect**: Daytime evapotranspiration cools air in hot weather; nighttime condensation warms it — natural air conditioning
3. **Humidity management**: Trees can reduce humidity by direct absorption in hot, humid tropical areas (dehumidifying effect)
4. **Isotope evidence**: Oxygen isotope analysis can distinguish forest-derived from oceanic water vapor, proving forests' contribution
5. **Design imperative**: Removing trees breaks the evapotranspiration-rainfall feedback loop, causing drought — planting trees restores it
6. **Water budgeting**: Understanding evapotranspiration rates allows designers to calculate how many trees are needed to modify local climate

## Temperature Effects

Evapotranspiration creates local temperature moderation:
- Day: evaporation causes local cooling (latent heat absorption)
- Night: condensation causes local warming (latent heat release)
- Leaf surfaces cooler than surroundings at evening → initiate condensation
- Plants may be 15°C warmer than surrounding air temperature
- Dense air from evapotranspiration can be used for induced cross-ventilation in buildings
