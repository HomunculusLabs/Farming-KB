## Overview

Canarina canariensis, the Canary Island bellflower, is a scandent suffrutescent
perennial endemic to the Canary Islands. It is the sole representative of its
monotypic genus within the Campanulaceae family.

The species produces large, pendulous, orange-campanulate flowers up to 5 cm
long that make it one of the most visually striking members of the Macaronesian
flora. Its twining stems can reach 2–3 m in length.

It holds cultural significance in the Canary Islands, where traditional
herbalists have used its latex and leaf preparations for wound healing and
gastrointestinal complaints. Habitat fragmentation has elevated its conservation
concern.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Canarina canariensis (L.) Vatke belongs to the monotypic genus Canarina within
the tribe Campanuleae of Campanulaceae. The genus is one of several
paleoendemic lineages restricted to Macaronesia.

The species is a deciduous, twining perennial herb arising from a fleshy,
tuberous rootstock. Stems are glabrous to sparsely pubescent, scrambling over
adjacent vegetation for support.

Leaves are opposite, ovate to triangular-cordate, 3–8 cm long, with crenate
margins and pubescent surfaces. The lamina is slightly succulent, an adaptation
to the seasonally dry environments of the laurel forest understory.

Flowers are solitary, axillary, and pendulous with a campanulate corolla
colored deep orange to reddish-orange. The calyx has six lanceolate lobes, and
the corolla tube is 3–5 cm long.

The fruit is a fleshy, ovoid berry, 1.5–2 cm in diameter, turning orange-red
at maturity. Seeds are small, numerous, and ellipsoid.

## Distribution and Habitat

Canarina canariensis is endemic to the western Canary Islands, occurring on
Tenerife, La Gomera, La Palma, El Hierro, and Gran Canaria. It is absent from
the eastern, drier islands of Lanzarote and Fuerteventura.

The species inhabits the remnants of laurisilva (laurel forest) and
fayal-brezal heath, primarily between 400–1,200 m elevation. It favors shaded,
humid ravines and north-facing slopes with persistent cloud cover.

Populations are fragmented, with the largest concentrations in the Anaga and
Teno massifs of Tenerife and in Garajonay National Park on La Gomera. Smaller
populations persist in scattered refugia across Gran Canaria"s northern slopes.

## Ecology

As a scandent understory plant, Canarina canariensis relies on surrounding
shrubs and small trees for physical support. Its twining growth habit allows it
to access light gaps in the dense laurel forest canopy.

Flowering occurs from November through February, coinciding with the winter
rainy season. The bright orange, pendulous flowers are adapted for bird
pollination, primarily by the Canary Island chiffchaff (Phylloscopus canariensis)
and the endemic subspecies of great tit.

Berries mature from February through April. Avian frugivores, particularly
endemic passerines, disperse the seeds through gut passage.

The tuberous rootstock allows the plant to survive the summer dry season
(June–September) in a dormant state. Above-ground growth resumes with autumn
rains in October.

## Cultivation and Uses

Canarina canariensis is cultivated as an ornamental in mild-climate gardens for
its dramatic bell-shaped flowers. It requires dappled shade, humus-rich soil,
and protection from frost.

Propagation is by seed or division of the tuberous rootstock. Seeds require
cold stratification at 4°C for 4–6 weeks and germinate irregularly over
2–4 months.

In Canarian traditional medicine, the plant's latex has been applied topically
to wounds and skin infections. Leaf infusions have been used as a digestive
aid and mild purgative, though no clinical studies validate these uses.

The species is rare in cultivation outside the Canary Islands and southern
Europe. Specialist nurseries occasionally offer it under the common name
"Canary bellflower."

## Conservation

The IUCN has not formally assessed Canarina canariensis, but regional Red Lists
classify it as Near Threatened. Habitat loss from agricultural expansion and
urbanization is the primary threat.

Invasive species, particularly Hedychium gardnerianum and Rubus ulmifolius,
outcompete native understory plants in remaining laurel forest fragments.
Grazing pressure from feral goats further degrades habitat quality.

Garajonay National Park (La Gomera) and the Anaga Rural Park (Tenerife)
provide the strongest in-situ protection. Ex-situ collections are maintained at
the Jardín Botánico Canario Viera y Clavijo on Gran Canaria.
