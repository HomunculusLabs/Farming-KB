# Bacterial Compost

## Description

Bacterial [[compost]] is compost that has been intentionally managed to promote bacterial dominance, resulting in a material teeming with bacterial biomass, flagellates, amoebae, and other bacteria-feeding organisms. It contrasts with fungally dominated compost. Bacterial compost is the primary soil amendment used when growing annuals, vegetables, and grasses—plants that prefer bacterially dominated soils and nitrate nitrogen. Along with mulches and compost teas, it is one of the three foundational tools of [[soil-food-web]] gardening.

## Classification

- **Category**: Organic soil amendment
- **Type**: Bacterially dominated compost
- **Role**: Inoculant and soil food web modifier

## Key Facts

- Produced using fresh, green (nitrogen-rich) organic materials as primary feedstock
- Grass clippings, green plant material, kitchen scraps, and manures promote bacterial growth
- The composting process proceeds rapidly with frequent turning and adequate moisture
- Rich in diverse bacterial populations including decomposers, nitrogen fixers, and nutrient cyclers
- Supports high populations of bacteria-feeding protozoa and nematodes
- Produces nitrate nitrogen through the bacterial decomposition pathway
- Typically has a more neutral to slightly alkaline pH compared to [[fungal-compost]]
- Contains readily available nutrients that annual plants can quickly access
- Attracts and supports earthworm populations

## Production Methods

To create bacterially dominated compost:

1. Use a higher ratio of green (nitrogen-rich) to brown (carbon-rich) materials
2. Include fresh grass clippings, green plant debris, and manures in the feedstock
3. Turn the pile frequently to introduce oxygen and promote bacterial activity
4. Maintain adequate moisture—wetter (but not anaerobic) conditions favor bacteria
5. Grind or shred materials finely to increase surface area for bacterial colonization
6. Monitor temperature: bacterial compost heats up quickly to 130–160°F (54–71°C)
7. Compost is ready sooner than fungal compost due to faster bacterial processing

## Applications in the Garden

Bacterial compost is applied where bacterial soil dominance is desired:

- **Vegetable gardens**: Apply 1–2 inches before planting annuals and vegetables
- **Annual flower beds**: Top-dress and side-dress throughout the growing season
- **Lawns**: Broadcast thin layers to introduce biology without smothering grass
- **Compost tea production**: Used as the inoculum for brewing bacterially dominated compost tea
- **Soil restoration**: Applied to damaged soils to restart the bacterial soil food web
- **Container plants**: Mixed into potting soil for annual container plantings

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Bacterial compost is the key amendment for vegetable gardeners and annual flower growers practicing soil food web methods. It provides the bacterial dominance that annuals and vegetables require while supporting the protozoa and nematode populations that cycle nutrients from bacterial biomass into plant-available nitrate nitrogen.

In the succession model, bacterial compost represents early successional soil conditions—those found in recently disturbed soils where bacteria thrive and nitrates are the dominant nitrogen form. This is why rototilling traditionally "worked" for vegetable gardens: it destroyed fungal networks and promoted bacterial dominance. Bacterial compost achieves the same result biologically, without the destructive effects of mechanical soil disturbance.

For mycologists, understanding bacterial compost is important because it represents the soil conditions that are unfavorable for most fungal fruiting—useful knowledge when trying to create or avoid fungal dominance in specific areas.

## Relationship to Other Entities

- **Fungal compost**: The complementary compost type used for trees, shrubs, and perennials
- **[[green-mulch]]**: Applied in conjunction with bacterial compost to maintain bacterial food supply
- **Compost tea**: Bacterial compost serves as the inoculum for brewing bacterially dominated tea
- **Earthworms**: Attracted to bacterial compost; their presence indicates bacterial soil dominance
- **[[protozoa-soup]]**: Protozoa in bacterial compost cycle nutrients by consuming bacteria
- **Annuals and vegetables**: The primary plant groups that benefit from bacterial compost

## Practical Applications
Understanding these compost dynamics enables targeted amendment strategies
for specific crop needs and soil conditions. Different composting methods
produce distinct microbial communities that influence plant health outcomes.
Thermal composting favors bacterial dominance while fungal composting methods
promote mycorrhizal associations essential for perennial crop systems.
Application rates and timing depend on existing soil biology and crop stage.
