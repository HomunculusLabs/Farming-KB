## Overview

**Crocosmia 'Lucifer'** is a vigorous, cormous perennial hybrid in the family Iridaceae, celebrated for its fiery red, arching sprays of funnel-shaped flowers. It is one of the most widely planted summer-flowering bulbs in temperate gardens worldwide.

The cultivar was raised by Alan Bloom at Bressingham Gardens in Norfolk, England, and introduced in 1966. It resulted from a cross between *Crocosmia masoniorum* and *Crocosmia paniculata*, combining the large flowers of the former with the hardiness of the latter.

Crocosmia 'Lucifer' produces bold, pleated, sword-shaped leaves that form substantial clumps 60–90 cm tall. The flowering stems arch gracefully above the foliage, reaching 100–120 cm, and bear 10–20 vivid scarlet flowers along each spike.

The plant is exceptionally attractive to hummingbirds in [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]] due to its exceptional winter hardiness. It survives temperatures to −20 °C with minimal protection, far outperforming most other Crocosmia hybrids in northern gardens.

The genus Crocosmia comprises approximately 8 species, all native to southern and eastern Africa. Several species, notably *Crocosmia × crocosmiiflora* (montbretia), have become invasive weeds in mild-climate regions worldwide.

Crocosmia 'Lucifer' is a named hybrid cultivar, not a botanical species. It does not produce viable seed and is propagated vegetatively by corm division. The cultivar name 'Lucifer' references the brilliant flame-red flower color.

The parent species *Crocosmia masoniorum* is native to the Drakensberg Mountains of South Africa, contributing large flower size and bold form. *Crocosmia paniculata*, also South African, adds winter hardiness and vigorous clump-forming habit.

Alan Bloom reportedly selected the name 'Lucifer' from over 200 seedlings of this cross, choosing this particular specimen for its exceptional flower size and color intensity. The cultivar quickly became the best-selling Crocosmia in the world after its introduction.

## Morphology

Crocosmia 'Lucifer' produces flattened, concentric corms that build up in layers over successive [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of Europe, North America, Japan, Australia, and New Zealand.

It thrives in USDA hardiness zones 5–9, making it one of the hardiest Crocosmia cultivars available. In zones 5–6, a winter mulch is recommended to protect corms from freeze-thaw cycles.

The parent species originate from montane grasslands and stream margins in the eastern Cape and Drakensberg regions of South Africa. These habitats experience warm, wet summers and cool, dry winters.

Crocosmia 'Lucifer' performs best in moist, well-drained soil in full sun to partial shade. Unlike some Crocosmia species that have become invasive, 'Lucifer' is relatively well-behaved, though clumps will expand steadily over time.

The plant's preference for consistent moisture reflects the streamside habitats of its South African parent species. In dry conditions, flowering is reduced and foliage may yellow prematurely.

## Cultivation

Plant corms 8–10 cm deep and 10–15 cm apart in spring, after the danger of frost has passed. Crocosmia 'Lucifer' prefers humus-rich, moisture-retentive soil that does not become waterlogged in winter.

Established clumps benefit from division every 3–4 years to maintain flowering vigor. Congested clumps produce smaller blooms and may cease flowering if overcrowded. Division is best done in early spring as new growth emerges.

When dividing, separate the chain of corms carefully, retaining the current season's flowering corm and discarding older, exhausted corms. Replant the largest corms immediately to prevent drying out.

The plant requires consistent moisture during [[query-how-do-cover-crops-suppress-weeds]] around the clump.

In colder regions, the foliage should be left in place after frost to provide natural insulation for the corms. Cut back the dead foliage in spring before new growth begins.
