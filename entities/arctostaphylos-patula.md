## Overview

**Arctostaphylos patula** is an evergreen shrub in the family Ericaceae, commonly known as greenleaf manzanita or pine mat manzanita. It is native to the western United States, occurring in montane coniferous forests from Washington and Oregon through the Sierra Nevada and Cascade ranges into northern Baja California.

The species is a prominent component of montane chaparral and mixed-conifer understory communities. It is fire-adapted, relying on both seed banking and basal burl resprouting for post-fire regeneration.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Arctostaphylos patula was described by Edward Lee Greene in 1887. The genus name derives from Greek *arktos* (bear) and *staphyle* (cluster of grapes), referencing the bear-favored berries. The specific epithet *patula* means spreading or open, referring to the branching habit.

## Distribution and Habitat

Arctostaphylos patula ranges from southwestern Washington through the Oregon Cascades and Sierra Nevada of California, extending south through the Transverse and Peninsular Ranges to northern Baja California. It occurs at elevations of 600–3,000 meters.

The species is characteristic of montane coniferous forests, mixed-evergreen forests, and montane chaparral. It is common in the understory of ponderosa pine (*Pinus ponderosa*), Jeffrey pine (*P. jeffreyi*), and red fir (*Abies magnifica*) forests.

In the Oregon Cascades, *A. patula* often co-occurs with *Ceanothus velutinus*, *Holodiscus discolor*, and *Symphoricarpos albus* in post-fire montane chaparral. These mixed shrub communities provide important habitat structure during the decades-long fire-free interval.

It frequently occurs on dry, rocky, well-drained slopes, ridges, and exposed outcrops. Soils are typically acidic to neutral sandy loams or rocky colluvium derived from granitic or volcanic parent material.

In the Sierra Nevada, *A. patula* is a dominant shrub in the montane chaparral belt between 1,500 and 2,500 meters elevation, forming dense stands after fire that may persist for decades before conifer canopy closure.

Successional dynamics are strongly fire-driven. Post-fire, *A. patula* establishes rapidly from seed and resprouting, forming dense monotypic stands that inhibit conifer regeneration for 20–40 years. As conifers eventually overtop the shrub canopy, light limitation reduces manzanita density.

Hybridization with other *Arctostaphylos* species occurs in contact zones, particularly with *A. viscida* in the northern Sierra Nevada and *A. glandulosa* in southern California. Hybrid individuals show intermediate morphology and may complicate species identification.

## Ecology

Arctostaphylos patula exhibits a dual post-fire regeneration strategy: obligate seeding from a persistent soil seed bank and facultative resprouting from the basal burl. This dual strategy provides reproductive insurance across varying fire intensities.

Seeds require fire-related cues for germination, primarily heat scarification of the hard seed coat. Charcoal leachates and smoke-derived chemicals (karrikinolide) further enhance germination rates. Seed longevity in the soil is estimated at 50–100+ years.

The burl stores carbohydrate reserves that fuel rapid post-fire resprouting. Burls may be massive, weighing up to 20 kg in mature plants, and contain hundreds of dormant buds.

Age determination is challenging due to the burl-based growth form. Radiocarbon dating of burl tissue suggests individual plants may persist for centuries, with some genets estimated at over 200 years old.

Birds including robins, thrushes, jays, and grouse consume the fruit and disperse seeds. American black bears are significant consumers, with manzanita berries forming a substantial portion of the bear diet in late summer and fall.

Mule deer browse the foliage and twigs moderately, particularly in winter when other forage is limited. However, the tough, leathery leaves and woody stems provide some resistance to heavy browsing pressure.

The evergreen foliage provides year-round cover for wildlife, and the dense shrub canopy offers thermal refugia and nesting sites for birds and small mammals. Flowers are visited by hummingbirds, bees, and butterflies.
