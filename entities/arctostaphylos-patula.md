## Overview

**Arctostaphylos patula** is an evergreen shrub in the family Ericaceae, commonly known as greenleaf manzanita or pine mat manzanita. It is native to the western United States, occurring in montane coniferous forests from Washington and Oregon through the Sierra Nevada and Cascade ranges into northern Baja California.

The species is a prominent component of montane chaparral and mixed-conifer understory communities. It is fire-adapted, relying on both seed banking and basal burl resprouting for post-fire regeneration.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Arctostaphylos patula was described by Edward Lee Greene in 1887. The genus name derives from Greek *arktos* (bear) and *staphyle* (cluster of grapes), referencing the bear-favored berries. The specific epithet *patula* means spreading or open, referring to the branching habit.

The genus *Arctostaphylos* contains approximately 60 species, the vast majority endemic to western [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]]. California alone hosts over 50 species, making it one of the most speciose genera in the California flora.

The species is placed in section *Arctostaphylos* within the genus, a group characterized by smooth, exfoliating bark and well-developed basal burls (lignotubers). It is most closely related to *A. nevadensis* and *A. viscida*.

The shrub is evergreen, typically 1–2.5 meters tall with a rounded to spreading crown. The distinctive smooth, reddish-brown bark peels in thin papery sheets, exposing lighter bark underneath — a signature manzanita characteristic.

Growth form varies with habitat: open, exposed sites produce compact, rounded plants, while shaded understory conditions produce taller, more open forms with fewer branches. This phenotypic plasticity enables the species to occupy a broad ecological niche.

Twigs are round, hairless, and reddish when young, becoming gray-brown with age. The plant possesses a well-developed basal burl (lignotuber) that enables vigorous resprouting after fire or mechanical damage.

Leaves are bright green, ovate to elliptic, 2–5 cm long and 1.5–3 cm wide, with entire margins that may be slightly revolute. The upper surface is glossy and glabrous, while the lower surface is paler green with a prominent midrib.

Leaf arrangement is alternate, with petioles 3–6 mm long. The bright green foliage, which gives the species its common name, contrasts with the gray-green leaves of many related manzanitas.

Inflorescences are terminal panicles or racemes, 3–7 cm long, bearing 8–20 small urn-shaped flowers. Flowers are white to pale pink, 5–7 mm long, with a constricted throat and five short, reflexed petals.

Flowering occurs from April through July, depending on elevation and latitude. Lower-elevation populations bloom earlier than montane populations, which may not flower until mid-summer.

Fruit is a drupe-like berry, 6–10 mm in diameter, reddish-brown when mature, with a mealy texture and several hard seeds. The fruit is edible but largely insipid, consumed by bears, birds, and small mammals.

Each fruit typically contains 3–5 seeds enclosed in a hard, bony endocarp. [[cervantes-seed-production-storage]] is abundant in favorable years, with mature shrubs yielding thousands of fruits.

Nutritional analysis shows moderate sugar content (5–8%) with low fat. The mealy texture and mild flavor limit human culinary interest, though indigenous peoples of the Sierra Nevada region gathered and processed the berries into dried cakes and beverages.

## Distribution and Habitat

Arctostaphylos patula ranges from southwestern Washington through the Oregon Cascades and Sierra Nevada of California, extending south through the Transverse and Peninsular Ranges to northern Baja California. It occurs at elevations of 600–3,000 meters.

The species is characteristic of montane coniferous forests, mixed-evergreen forests, and montane chaparral. It is common in the understory of ponderosa pine (*Pinus ponderosa*), Jeffrey pine (*P. jeffreyi*), and red fir (*Abies magnifica*) forests.

In the Oregon Cascades, *A. patula* often co-occurs with *Ceanothus velutinus*, *Holodiscus discolor*, and *Symphoricarpos albus* in post-fire montane chaparral. These mixed shrub communities provide important habitat structure during the decades-long fire-free interval.

It frequently occurs on dry, rocky, well-drained slopes, ridges, and exposed outcrops. Soils are typically acidic to neutral sandy loams or rocky colluvium derived from granitic or volcanic parent material.

In the Sierra Nevada, *A. patula* is a dominant shrub in the montane chaparral belt between 1,500 and 2,500 meters elevation, forming dense stands after fire that may persist for decades before conifer canopy closure.

Successional dynamics are strongly fire-driven. Post-fire, *A. patula* establishes rapidly from seed and resprouting, forming dense monotypic stands that inhibit conifer regeneration for 20–40 years. As conifers eventually overtop the shrub canopy, light limitation reduces manzanita density.

Hybridization with other *Arctostaphylos* species occurs in contact zones, particularly with *A. viscida* in the northern Sierra Nevada and *A. glandulosa* in southern California. Hybrid individuals show intermediate morphology and may complicate species identification.

## Ecology

Arctostaphylos patula exhibits a dual post-fire regeneration strategy: obligate seeding from a persistent soil seed bank and facultative resprouting from the basal burl. This dual strategy provides reproductive insurance across varying fire intensities.

Seeds require fire-related cues for germination, primarily heat scarification of the hard seed coat. Charcoal leachates and smoke-derived chemicals (karrikinolide) further enhance germination rates. Seed longevity in the soil is estimated at 50–100+ years.

The burl stores carbohydrate reserves that fuel rapid post-fire resprouting. Burls may be massive, weighing up to 20 kg in mature plants, and contain hundreds of dormant buds.

Age determination is challenging due to the burl-based growth form. Radiocarbon dating of burl tissue suggests individual plants may persist for centuries, with some genets estimated at over 200 years old.

Birds including robins, thrushes, jays, and grouse consume the fruit and disperse seeds. American black bears are significant consumers, with manzanita berries forming a substantial portion of the bear diet in late summer and fall.

Mule deer browse the foliage and twigs moderately, particularly in winter when other forage is limited. However, the tough, leathery leaves and woody stems provide some resistance to heavy browsing pressure.

The evergreen foliage provides year-round cover for wildlife, and the dense shrub canopy offers thermal refugia and nesting sites for birds and small mammals. Flowers are visited by hummingbirds, bees, and butterflies.

[[dighton-ericoid-mycorrhizal-nutrient-acquisition]] associations are critical for nutrient acquisition in the nutrient-poor soils typical of montane chaparral. These fungal symbionts enable the plant to access organic nitrogen and phosphorus that would otherwise be unavailable.

## Cultivation

Greenleaf manzanita is valued in western native plant landscaping for its attractive bark, glossy foliage, and [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]]. It is used in fire-resistant landscaping, habitat restoration, and [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]] on disturbed slopes.

Propagation from seed requires fire treatment (boiling water soak or charred wood leachate) followed by cold stratification for 60–90 days. Softwood cuttings under intermittent mist yield 30–50% rooting success.

[[cannabis-seed-germination-methods]] without fire treatment is typically below 5%. The most effective laboratory treatment involves a 5-minute soak in 95°C water followed by 90 days at 2–4°C, which can raise germination to 40–60%.

Plants require full sun, excellent drainage, and acidic to neutral soils. Irrigation should be minimal after establishment — the species is highly drought-adapted and susceptible to root rot (*Phytophthora* spp.) in poorly drained soils.

Spacing for landscape plantings is 1.5–2.5 meters. Growth rate is moderate (15–30 cm per year), with plants reaching full size in 5–10 years. No serious pests or diseases in cultivation.

Pruning is generally unnecessary and can damage the natural form. Selective removal of dead branches maintains a tidy appearance without compromising the characteristic branching pattern.

The species is classified as fire-resistant rather than fire-retardant — while the foliage contains volatile oils, the dense woody stems and burl-based architecture enable survival of low-to-moderate intensity fires.
