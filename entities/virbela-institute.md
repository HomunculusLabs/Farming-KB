## Description

The Virbela Institute is a small research and design organisation based at Emerson College, Forest Row, East Sussex, UK. It is known for developing **Flowform** water aeration systems — artificial turbulence basins modelled on natural rock formations carved by flowing streams. The institute's work bridges art, hydrology, and ecological engineering, creating sculptural water features that serve practical functions in water purification, sewage treatment, and aquaculture oxygenation.

## Flowform Design System

The Virbela Institute's primary contribution is the **Flowform** — an artificial basin that replicates the forms carved naturally by turbulent streams in sandstone and mudstone:

**Basin configurations:**
- Elongate or truncated forms
- Symmetrical or asymmetrical
- Stepped in line, stacked like ladders, or spiralled to conserve space

**Three turbulence effects produced:**
1. **Plunge / vertical overturn**: Fluid drops from one basin to the next
2. **Figure-8 lateral flow**: Water circulates around the basins themselves
3. **Interaction effect**: The lateral flow deflects the vertical drop, switching it from side to side in a regular rhythm

**Within these major patterns** are distinct vortices, counterflows, overfolds, and cusps that further mix air and water at basin edges.

## Practical Applications

Flowform systems developed by the Virbela Institute are used for:

- **Primary sewage treatment**: Stacked below sewage pipe outfalls to aerate and oxidise pollutants
- **Aquaculture**: Placed above fish ponds at pipe inlets to oxygenate water for aquatic species production
- **Water purification**: Turbulence-induced aeration oxidises organic pollutants
- **Aesthetic water features**: Sculptural forms that are visually pleasing and educational
- **Ecological engineering**: Demonstrates applied pattern in everyday hydrological use

## Historical Precedents

The Virbela Institute's work builds on a lineage of water engineering:

- **Natural occurrence**: Basin forms carved by turbulent streams in massive sandstones and mudstones
- **Roman hydrologists**: Modelled similar turbulence basins in pozzolanic cement in antiquity
- **Leonardo da Vinci**: Portrayed the fundamental turbulence patterns (documented in Popham, A.E., *The Drawings of Leonardo da Vinci*, Jonathan Cape, London, 1946)
- **Catastrophe theory**: Analysed by Chappell (*Landform Evolution in Australia*, ANU Press, 1978) for coastal uprush and backwash turbulence

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

The Virbela Institute's Flowform system is cited by Bill Mollison as a prime example of **applied pattern** in permaculture design. Its relevance includes:

1. **Pattern application**: Natural hydrological patterns are identified, studied, and replicated for human benefit
2. **Multiple functions**: Each installation serves aesthetic, educational, and practical water treatment purposes simultaneously
3. **Appropriate technology**: Low-energy, gravity-fed systems that work with natural water behaviour rather than against it
4. **Integration of art and science**: Demonstrates Mollison's principle that meaningful art serves practical ecological functions

## Design Philosophy

The Virbela Institute exemplifies the permaculture principle of designing with natural patterns. Their work follows a clear evolution:

1. **Wonder and observation** of natural water forms
2. **Sketches and analysis** of turbulence patterns
3. **Constructed basins** that replicate natural forms
4. **Practical applications** across diverse sites and functions

This mirrors the permaculture design process: observe, analyse, design, implement — with pattern understanding as the unifying thread.
