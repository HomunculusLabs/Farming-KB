# Scleroderma sp.

## Description

*Scleroderma* is a genus of puffball-like [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] commonly known as earthballs. Unlike true puffballs, earthballs have a firm, thick peridium (outer wall) that is often dark and hard, and the gleba (internal spore mass) matures from white to dark purple-brown or black. Species in this genus form [[ectomycorrhizal-associations]] with a wide range of trees, including pines, oaks, and eucalypts.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Boletales
- **Family:** Sclerodermataceae
- **Genus:** *Scleroderma*

## Key Facts

- Exhibits high heavy metal tolerance, with 50% inhibition concentrations of 200 mg/L for Al, 100 mg/L for Fe, 100 mg/L for Cu, 100 mg/L for Zn, and 200 mg/L for Pb.
- Among the most tolerant ectomycorrhizal genera to aluminum, iron, and lead, alongside *Pisolithus* and *Hymenogaster*.
- *Scleroderma flavidum* has been documented to protect host plants from nickel toxicity through non-metabolic exclusion mechanisms (Jones and Hutchinson, 1986).
- Widespread in both temperate and tropical forest ecosystems.
- Some species are considered edible when young but can cause gastrointestinal distress if misidentified.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

*Scleroderma* species are valuable in forestry and land reclamation for their dual capacity to form functional mycorrhizae and tolerate heavy metals. Their nickel exclusion mechanism distinguishes them from other ectomycorrhizal fungi that rely on metabolic uptake. This makes them particularly useful for restoration of nickel-contaminated sites. The genus is also studied for its role in carbon cycling in forest soils.

## Ecological Role

- Ectomycorrhizal partner with broad host range including conifers and hardwoods
- Protects host plants from heavy metal toxicity through exclusion mechanisms
- Contributes to soil structure through extensive extraradical mycelial networks
- Produces long-lived sclerotia-like fruiting bodies that persist in soil
