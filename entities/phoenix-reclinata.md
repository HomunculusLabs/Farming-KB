## Overview

Phoenix reclinata, commonly known as the wild date palm or Senegal date palm, is a species of flowering plant in the family Arecaceae. It is native to tropical and southern Africa, Madagascar, and the Comoro Islands.

The species is a multi-stemmed clustering palm, typically growing 6–12 meters tall with multiple stems arising from a single base. It is one of the most widespread and ecologically important palms in sub-Saharan Africa.

Senegal date palm is valued both for its edible fruit and for its ornamental qualities in tropical and subtropical landscapes. Its graceful, arching fronds and clustering habit make it a popular specimen for large gardens and public spaces.

The species is among the most commonly planted ornamental palms in tropical Africa. It is also widely used in [[fukuoka-natural-farming-tropical-climates]], with peak production typically in the dry season. A mature clump with both male and female plants can produce thousands of fruits annually.

The fruit, while edible, is much smaller and less fleshy than that of the commercial date palm ([[dighton-fungi-acidifying-pollutants-forest-ecosystems]] across tropical Africa.

In southern Africa, the species marks the presence of groundwater and is used as an indicator plant by local communities and geologists. Its presence often suggests permanent or seasonal water at relatively shallow depths.

## Ecology and Uses

Phoenix reclinata is one of the most ecologically significant palms in Africa. Its fruits are consumed by a wide variety of birds, including hornbills, parrots, and starlings, as well as by mammals such as baboons, monkeys, elephants, and bushpigs.

The dense clumps provide important nesting and roosting sites for birds. The palm is a favored nesting tree for several bird of prey species, including African fish eagles and Verreaux's eagles.

In many African communities, the palm is a multi-purpose resource. The sweet fruit is eaten fresh or fermented into palm wine. The sap is tapped for toddy production, a practice widespread across tropical Africa.

Leaves are used for thatching, basket weaving, and mat making. The fibrous leaf sheaths provide material for cordage, and the trunk wood is used as [[query-can-bamboo-be-used-as-a-building-material-and-how-do-i-get-started]] and for making tool handles.

The palm heart is edible and was historically harvested as a food source. However, harvesting the terminal bud kills that particular stem, making sustainable harvest possible only in multi-stemmed clumps.
