## Overview

**Fraxinus pennsylvanica** (green ash) is a medium to large deciduous tree in the family Oleaceae, native to eastern and central North America. It is one of the most widely distributed and ecologically important ash species on the continent.

The species ranges from Nova Scotia west to southeastern Alberta and south to northern Florida and eastern Texas. It is a dominant component of bottomland hardwood forests, riparian corridors, and floodplain ecosystems across its range.

Green ash is commercially significant for timber, landscape plantings, and windbreaks. The species has been devastated by the invasive emerald ash borer (Agrilus planipennis) since its introduction to North America in the 1990s.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Family Oleaceae → Genus Fraxinus → Section Melioides → Species F. pennsylvanica.

Authority: Humphry Marshall (1785). The species epithet references Pennsylvania, where the type specimen was collected. Taxonomic treatment has varied, with some authorities recognizing varieties based on twig pubescence.

Green ash typically reaches 15–24 meters in height with a straight trunk 40–60 cm in diameter at maturity. The crown is broadly oval to rounded, becoming irregular with age in open-grown specimens.

Bark is gray-brown, developing interlacing ridges and furrows with age. Young bark is relatively smooth with prominent lenticels, distinguishing it from the corky-barked F. quadrangulata.

Leaves are opposite, pinnately compound, 20–35 cm long, with 5–9 (typically 7) leaflets. Leaflets are 5–12 cm long, lanceolate to elliptic, with serrate margins and green to yellow-green upper surfaces.

Twigs are slender, gray-brown, with semicircular leaf scars and flat or slightly raised bud scars. Unlike F. americana (white ash), green ash twigs are typically pubescent in the first year.

Flowers are dioecious, appearing before the leaves in [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]]. Male flowers are in compact clusters, female flowers in loose panicles; both are purple and inconspicuous.

Fruit is a samara 2.5–5 cm long, with a flattened wing extending from the base to near the seed apex. Samaras ripen from late summer to fall and are dispersed primarily by wind.

## Distribution and Habitat

Fraxinus pennsylvanica occurs across a vast range in eastern and central North America. It extends from the Atlantic coast to the Great Plains and from southern Canada to the Gulf Coast.

The species is most abundant in bottomland hardwood forests, riparian corridors, and alluvial floodplains. It tolerates seasonal flooding and is a characteristic species of the silver maple-green ash association in midwestern floodplains.

Green ash is moderately shade-intolerant, requiring canopy gaps for establishment and rapid [[cervantes-seedling-care-early-growth]]. In bottomland forests, it typically establishes after disturbance events that open the canopy.

[[coleman-sunlight-and-winter-crop-growth-rates]] are relatively fast for a hardwood species, with young trees capable of 60–90 cm of height growth per year on favorable sites. Maximum age is approximately 100–150 years, though exceptional individuals may exceed 200 years.

The species reproduces prolifically from seed, with heavy seed crops occurring at 2–3 year intervals. Seed viability is high, and seeds can persist in the soil [[query-how-do-i-create-a-seed-bank-and-start-seed-saving]] for 2–4 years.

Soil preferences range from silty loams to sandy alluvium, with optimal growth on moist, well-drained bottomland soils. The species tolerates a broad pH range from slightly acidic to moderately alkaline.

## Ecological Role

Green ash provides important habitat and food resources across its range. The seeds are consumed by numerous bird species including finches, grosbeaks, cardinals, and woodpeckers during fall and winter.

The foliage serves as a larval food plant for several moth species, including the ash sphinx (Sphinx chersis) and various geometrid moths. Deer and rabbits browse young shoots, particularly in winter.

In riparian systems, green ash contributes to stream bank stabilization and flood attenuation. The extensive root system helps prevent erosion on alluvial soils subject to periodic flooding.

Cavity-nesting birds including woodpeckers, nuthatches, and chickadees use mature ash trees for nesting sites. The loss of ash to emerald ash borer has created significant canopy gaps in riparian forests.

Fall color in green ash is yellow to golden, providing late-season visual interest. The color display is more reliable than in white ash, which often turns brown before yellow.

The species is an important nectar source for specialist bees in the genus Andrena that are oligolectic on Fraxinus pollen. These relationships are at risk as ash populations decline due to emerald ash borer.

## Uses and Economic Importance

Green ash wood is hard, heavy, and relatively shock-resistant, with a Janka hardness rating of approximately 1,200 lbf. It is used for tool handles, baseball bats, furniture, flooring, and crates.

The species has been one of the most widely planted shade trees in the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] and Canada. Its tolerance of urban conditions, including drought, compacted soils, and road salt, made it a standard street and park tree.

In the Plains states, green ash was extensively planted in shelterbelts and windbreaks during the Prairie States Forestry Project of the 1930s. Millions of ash trees were established across the Great Plains for soil conservation.

Green ash is also used in [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]] and mine [[fukuoka-desert-reclamation-projects]]. Its rapid growth, flood tolerance, and adaptability to disturbed sites make it valuable for stabilizing degraded riparian corridors.

## Threats and Conservation

The emerald ash borer (EAB) has caused catastrophic mortality of F. pennsylvanica across its range since its detection near Detroit, Michigan in 2002. Mortality approaches 99% in infested stands, making EAB one of the most destructive forest pests in [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] history.

Biocontrol programs have released three species of [[comparison-parasitoid-wasps-vs-chemical-pesticides]] (Tetrastichus planipennisi, Oobius agrili, Spathius agrili) targeting EAB. Results have been mixed, with parasitism rates varying significantly by region and stand conditions.

Some individual green ash trees have shown apparent tolerance or resistance to EAB, exhibiting reduced canopy dieback and sustained growth in heavily infested areas. These "lingering ash" are the focus of breeding programs aimed at developing EAB-resistant cultivars.
