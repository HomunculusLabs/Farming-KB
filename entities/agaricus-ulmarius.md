## Agaricus ulmarius (Elm-tree Mushroom)

## Description

[[agaricus]] ulmarius, now more commonly classified as [[hypsizygus-ulmarius]] or Pleurotus ulmarius,
is a large, fleshy agaric that grows exclusively on elm trees, typically in the fall. The
mushroom has a thick, solid stem (sometimes eccentric or lateral), broad white gills, firm white
flesh, and a light-yellow cap that is smooth at first, later becoming spotted and cracked.
Older specimens develop yellow gills and a leathery cap surface. It frequently reappears yearly
in the same spot, especially in clefts or where limbs have been sawn off.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Lyophyllaceae (modern) / Agaricaceae (historical)
- **Genus:** Hypsizygus (modern) / Agaricus (historical, Palmer"s usage)
- **Species:** Hypsizygus ulmarius

## Key Facts

- Grows only in the fall on elm trees (Ulmus spp.), typically from wounds, clefts, or sawn
  branch stubs.
- One of the first species Palmer experimented with personally.
- Characterized by eccentric stem (growing from the side rather than the center of the cap),
  which contradicted popular advice to avoid all fungi with lateral stems.
- Palmer stated he knew of no other large fungus with gills and white flesh growing from the elm,
  making identification relatively straightforward.
- Reappears annually in the same location, providing reliable foraging sites.
- Described in Palmer's Plate I as the "Elm-tree Mushroom" — rated esculent.

## Edibility

Edible and highly regarded. Palmer listed it among the species best cooked by broiling on a
fine-wire gridiron with salt and butter, noting that varieties tasting "raw of grass or trees"
are best prepared this way rather than stewed. The firm flesh and substantial size make it a
satisfying edible. Should be collected when young and firm before the cap becomes leathery.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- An accessible species for beginners due to its restricted habitat (elm trees only) and
  distinctive appearance.
- Palmer used it to debunk the myth that mushrooms growing from wood are unsafe — a widespread
  but incorrect belief.
- Its annual reappearance at the same site makes it useful for studying [[fungal-life-cycles]] and
  wood decay ecology.
- The eccentric stem placement illustrates the diversity of agaric morphology.
- Modern taxonomy has reclassified it from Agaricus to Hypsizygus, reflecting advances in
  phylogenetic understanding.
- Closely related to the commercially cultivated shimeji ([[hypsizygus-tessulatus]]).

## Identification Notes

- Cap: large (8-20 cm), convex to flat; light yellow, smooth when young, spotted and cracked
  with age.
- Gills: broad, white when young, turning yellow in age; adnate to sinuate.
- Stem: thick, solid, often eccentric or lateral; white.
- Flesh: firm, white, unchanging on cutting.
- Habitat: exclusively on living or dead elm trees, fall season.
- Smell: pleasant, mild.
- [[spore-print]]: white to cream.

## Ecological Notes

A wood-decay fungus that acts as a weak parasite and saprobe on elm. Its preference for wound
sites (pruning cuts, broken limbs) suggests it colonizes through bark injuries. The annual
reappearance indicates persistent mycelium within the host wood.

## Historical Notes

Palmer featured this species prominently as one of his "first experiments" in toadstool eating.
He cited it as a counterexample to the then-common rule that fungi growing from wood should be
avoided. He also noted that its eccentric stem violated another popular "rule" about avoiding
fungi with stems growing from the side.
