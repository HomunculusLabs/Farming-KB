## Overview

**Litsea cubeba** is a small evergreen tree in the family Lauraceae, native to East and Southeast Asia. It is commonly known as May Chang, mountain pepper, or tropical verbena, names that reflect its aromatic, citrus-scented foliage and fruits.

The species is commercially important as a source of essential oil, which is rich in citral (a mixture of geranial and neral). The oil is widely used in perfumery, aromatherapy, food flavoring, and traditional medicine throughout Asia.

May Chang oil has a fresh, lemony fragrance and serves as a natural source of citral, an important precursor in the synthesis of vitamin A, ionones, and various aroma chemicals. Global production is centered in China, Vietnam, and Indonesia.

The tree is also used in traditional Chinese medicine for treating respiratory ailments, digestive complaints, and inflammatory conditions. The fruits, leaves, and bark all have documented medicinal applications.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Litsea cubeba** (Lour.) Pers. was originally described by João de Loureiro in 1790 as *Laurus cubeba* and later transferred to *Litsea* by Christiaan Hendrik Persoon in 1805. The genus *Litsea* contains approximately 200–400 species distributed across tropical and subtropical Asia, Australia, and the Pacific.

The species epithet *cubeba* refers to the cube-shaped fruits, which resemble those of *Piper cubeba* (tailed pepper), though the two species are not related.

*L. cubeba* is a small to medium evergreen tree, typically 5–12 m tall, with a slender trunk and spreading crown. In cultivation, it is often managed as a coppiced shrub to facilitate harvesting.

Leaves are alternate, lanceolate to elliptic, 6–14 cm long and 2–5 cm wide, with entire margins. The upper surface is dark green and glabrous, while the lower surface is paler with prominent lateral veins.

Crushed leaves emit a strong, fresh lemon-citrus fragrance due to high concentrations of citral and other volatile terpenoids. The fragrance is most intense during the warm months.

Flowers are small, yellowish-white, arranged in axillary umbels. The species is dioecious, with male and female flowers on separate trees. Flowering occurs from February to April.

The fruit is a small, globose drupe 4–6 mm in diameter, green when immature, turning dark purple-black at maturity. Each fruit contains a single seed. Fruits are aromatic and rich in essential oil.

In high-yielding cultivars, individual trees can produce 5–15 kg of fresh fruit per harvest season. Essential oil yield from fruit averages 30–50 kg per hectare, depending on management intensity.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Litsea cubeba* is native to southern China, Taiwan, Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia, Thailand, Myanmar, and the eastern Himalayas. It has been introduced and naturalized in parts of Indonesia, Malaysia, and the Philippines.

The species occurs in evergreen and mixed deciduous forests, forest margins, and secondary growth at elevations of 100–2400 m. It is most abundant in subtropical montane forests with well-distributed rainfall.

Optimal growth conditions include mean annual temperatures of 15–25 °C, annual rainfall of 1200–2000 mm, and well-drained, acidic to neutral soils (pH 5.0–6.5). The tree tolerates light frost but is damaged by temperatures below −5 °C.

In its native range, *L. cubeba* is a pioneer species that colonizes disturbed sites, forest clearings, and abandoned agricultural land. It is shade-intolerant as a seedling but tolerates partial shade as a mature tree.

The species is fire-adapted and resprouts vigorously from the root crown after burning. This trait makes it useful for reforestation of fire-prone areas in subtropical mountainous regions.

## Cultivation and Harvest

*L. cubeba* is propagated from seed, which germinates readily without stratification. Seeds should be sown fresh, as viability declines rapidly after 3–4 months of storage. Germination typically occurs within 2–4 weeks.

Vegetative propagation by softwood cuttings under mist is also practiced, particularly for selecting high-yielding clones with superior essential oil profiles. Grafting onto seedling rootstocks is used in commercial orchards.

In commercial essential oil production, trees are planted at spacings of 2–3 m on center and managed through coppicing on a 3–5 year rotation. The entire above-ground biomass is harvested and steam-distilled for oil extraction.

Fruits are harvested when they begin turning from green to dark purple, typically from July to September. Fruit oil content ranges from 3–5% by fresh weight and contains up to 80% citral.

Leaf and branch distillation provides a lower-yielding but more sustainably harvested oil source. Leaf oil contains 60–70% citral with a different terpenoid profile than fruit oil.

## Essential Oil and Chemistry

The essential oil of *L. cubeba* is dominated by citral, a mixture of two isomeric monoterpene aldehydes: geranial (citral A, ~40–50%) and neral (citral B, ~25–35%). Total citral content ranges from 70–85% depending on plant part, harvest timing, and geographic origin.

Other significant constituents include limonene (5–10%), linalool (2–5%), 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one (2–4%), and various minor monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes. The oil also contains trace amounts of α-pinene, β-pinene, and myrcene.

Citral is a key industrial chemical used as a precursor for synthesizing ionones (fragrance compounds), vitamin A, and carotenoids. It also serves as an antimicrobial agent in food preservation.

The essential oil exhibits broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against bacteria (including *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Escherichia coli*), fungi, and yeast. This activity is primarily attributed to the citral content.

Quality grading of commercial May Chang oil is based on citral content, with premium grades exceeding 80% total citral. Adulteration with synthetic citral or cheaper citral-rich oils (lemongrass) is a known quality concern.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis is the standard method for verifying oil authenticity and profiling the complete volatile composition of commercial May Chang oil samples.

## Pharmacology and Uses

In traditional Chinese medicine, *L. cubeba* fruits are used to treat cough, asthma, bronchitis, and other respiratory conditions. The dried fruits are decocted or powdered for internal use.

Modern pharmacological studies have confirmed anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, and insecticidal activities for the essential oil and its constituents. Citral has demonstrated anti-tumor activity in several cell line studies.

The oil is widely used in aromatherapy as a calming and uplifting agent. It is considered a natural antidepressant and is commonly blended with other citrus and floral essential oils for emotional wellness applications.

Topical applications include use in massage oils for muscle tension, in skincare formulations for oily and acne-prone skin, and in insect repellent products. The oil should be diluted before topical use due to potential skin sensitization.

Citral is classified as a skin sensitizer by the International Fragrance Association (IFRA), with recommended maximum use levels of 0.6% in leave-on cosmetic products. Proper dilution is essential for safe topical application.

*L. cubeba* fruit extracts have been investigated as natural food preservatives due to their antimicrobial properties. They show particular promise against foodborne pathogens in meat and dairy products.

The species is also planted for soil conservation and reforestation in degraded mountainous areas of southern China. Its rapid growth and dense root system help prevent erosion on steep slopes.
