# Psychotria viridis

**Psychotria viridis** is a perennial shrub of the coffee family (Rubiaceae) native to the Amazon basin and other tropical regions of the Americas. It is best known as the principal **DMT-containing admixture plant** in the ayahuasca brew, where its leaves are combined with *Banisteriopsis caapi* vine to produce the entheogenic preparation used in indigenous and contemporary shamanic ceremonies. Without P. viridis (or an equivalent DMT source), the ayahuasca vine alone produces only mild psychoactive effects from its beta-carboline alkaloids. P. viridis thus provides the visionary and perceptual dimension of the ayahuasca experience, complementing the purgative and grounding qualities of the caapi vine.

## Taxonomy and Distribution

- **Family:** Rubiaceae (the coffee family — the fourth largest family of flowering plants, with approximately 620 genera and 13,000 species)
- **Genus:** Psychotria (one of the largest genera of flowering plants, with over 1,500 species distributed throughout tropical and subtropical regions worldwide)
- **Distribution:** Lowland tropical rainforests from Colombia and Ecuador through Peru and Bolivia to Brazil. Also found in Central America and parts of the Caribbean. Altitudinal range from sea level to approximately 1,500 m.
- **Common names:** Chacruna (Peru, Ecuador), Chacrona (Brazil), Amiruca panga (Shipibo), Chacruna negra (some Amazonian groups, referring to darker-leafed varieties), Rami (Asháninka), Lady of the Night (English, rarely used).

Taxonomic uncertainty persists within the P. viridis complex. Some botanists recognize multiple varieties or closely related species that are used interchangeably in ayahuasca preparations, including P. carthaginensis (found from Mexico to Bolivia, also containing DMT) and P. nepalensis (sometimes used as an adulterant or substitute in Brazil). Molecular phylogenetic studies suggest that the Psychotria species used in ayahuasca preparations form a natural group, though formal taxonomic revision is still needed.

## Botanical Description

P. viridis is an evergreen shrub typically growing 1–3 meters tall, though it can reach 5 meters in ideal conditions. Key morphological features include:

- **Leaves:** Opposite, simple, elliptic to obovate, 5–15 cm long and 2–6 cm wide, with a glossy dark green upper surface and lighter underside. The petioles are short (5–15 mm). Leaf venation is pinnate with a prominent midrib and 5–8 pairs of lateral veins. Leaf margins are entire.
- **Stems:** Green when young, becoming woody and brown with age. Branching is opposite (decussate), characteristic of the Rubiaceae. The bark on older stems is smooth and grayish.
- **Flowers:** Small, greenish-white, borne in terminal or axillary cymes. Inflorescences are pedunculate with multiple flowers. Flowers are hermaphroditic, approximately 4–6 mm in diameter.
- **Fruit:** Small drupe that turns from green to red then dark purple or black at maturity. Each fruit contains two small seeds approximately 3–4 mm long. Fruits are dispersed by birds.

The species is adapted to the understory of tropical rainforest, where it receives filtered light. In cultivation, plants grown in deeper shade tend to have larger, darker leaves, while those in more sun are more compact with lighter foliage. The leaves have a characteristic slightly mucilaginous texture when crushed.

## Phytochemistry

The primary psychoactive compound in P. viridis is **N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT)**, found predominantly in the leaf tissue. Key phytochemical details:

- **DMT concentration:** Typically ranges from 0.1% to 0.6% by dry weight, though this varies significantly with genetics, growing conditions, age of leaf, and chemotype. Some exceptional specimens have been reported with concentrations exceeding 1%. Young, expanding leaves often have higher alkaloid content than mature, fully expanded leaves.
- **N-Methyltryptamine (NMT):** Present in smaller quantities alongside DMT in most populations, typically at 10–30% of the DMT concentration. NMT is itself a psychoactive tryptamine but considerably less potent than DMT.
- **Other alkaloids:** Trace amounts of 5-MeO-DMT have been reported in some populations, though this is not typical for P. viridis. Other minor tryptamine derivatives may also be present.

The tryptamine alkaloids in P. viridis are **orally inactive on their own** because monoamine oxidase (MAO) enzymes in the gastrointestinal tract and liver rapidly deaminate them via oxidative deamination before they can cross the blood-brain barrier. The half-life of orally administered DMT without MAO inhibition is estimated at less than 10 minutes. When combined with the MAO-inhibiting beta-carboline alkaloids of *Banisteriopsis caapi*, DMT becomes orally bioavailable with effective plasma concentrations lasting 2–6 hours—this is the fundamental pharmacological basis of ayahuasca.

The biosynthesis of DMT in P. viridis follows the tryptamine pathway: L-tryptophan → tryptamine (via tryptophan decarboxylase) → N-methyltryptamine (via indolethylamine N-methyltransferase, INMT) → N,N-dimethyltryptamine (via a second N-methylation by INMT). Environmental stress, leaf age, and time of harvest all influence alkaloid accumulation. Some evidence suggests that alkaloid production follows a diurnal cycle, with peak concentrations occurring during certain times of day.

## Ethnobotanical Use

P. viridis is one of the two most commonly used DMT admixture plants in ayahuasca preparations (the other being *Diplopterys cabrerana*). Among indigenous Amazonian groups:

- The Shipibo-Conibo of Peru call it *chacruna* and consider it the "light" or "vision" component of ayahuasca, while the caapi vine provides the "force" or "spirit." In Shipibo cosmology, chacruna is associated with the anaconda and with the feminine principle. The visions induced by chacruna are interpreted by the curandero through the lens of Shipibo visual language (quene).
- Leaves are typically harvested from mature plants, dried in shade (to preserve alkaloid content), and added to the caapi brew during the extended boiling process. Some traditions prefer fresh leaves; others use dried material stored for months.
- The ratio of chacruna to caapi varies widely between traditions and practitioners, producing brews of different potency and subjective character. More chacruna generally produces stronger visual effects, while more caapi produces a deeper, more "bodily" experience.
- In some regions, the specific leaves used are selected based on the desired therapeutic or spiritual outcome—different leaf sizes, positions on the plant, or harvest times are believed to produce different effects.

In Brazilian ayahuasca traditions (Santo Daime, UDV, Barquinha), P. viridis is typically the preferred DMT source, though P. carthaginensis is sometimes used as a substitute. In Colombian Amazonian traditions, *Diplopterys cabrerana* (chagropanga) is more commonly used than P. viridis.

## Cultivation

P. viridis is cultivated both within its native range and internationally by ayahuasca practitioners, botanical gardens, and ethnobotanical enthusiasts. Propagation is primarily by:

- **Softwood or semi-hardwood cuttings** (most common and reliable method; rooting hormone improves success rates; cuttings typically root in 4–8 weeks under humid conditions with bottom heat)
- **Seed** (less commonly used; seeds have variable germination rates and require fresh, viable seed; germination may take 4–12 weeks)

The plant prefers warm (20–30°C), humid (60–90% relative humidity), shaded conditions similar to its native understory habitat. It is sensitive to frost, direct intense sun, and drought. Soil should be well-draining, slightly acidic (pH 5.5–6.5), and rich in organic matter. In non-tropical climates it is grown in greenhouses or indoors under grow lights with high humidity enclosures. Pests include spider mites, scale insects, and mealybugs, particularly when grown indoors.

## Modern Context

P. viridis has become a globally traded botanical due to the spread of ayahuasca ceremonies. DMT is a Schedule I controlled substance in many countries, which creates legal complexity around the plant despite it being relatively easy to cultivate. Research into ayahuasca's therapeutic potential (depression, addiction, PTSD) necessarily involves P. viridis as the DMT source in traditional preparations. The species has also attracted broader phytochemical interest in the Psychotria genus, which contains numerous species producing bioactive alkaloids, including compounds with antimicrobial, antiviral, and anticancer properties.
