## Native American Church

## Description

The Native American Church is a syncretic religious movement among American Indians in the United States that combines Christian beliefs with the ceremonial use of the [[mescaline]]-containing peyote cactus ([[lophophora-williamsii]]). Practitioners believe that with the aid of peyote they can both "talk" with Jesus and cure illness. It is described as "probably the most famous combination of hallucinogenic drug use and Christianity."

## Classification

- **Type:** Syncretic religious movement
- **Sacrament:** Peyote (Lophophora williamsii), containing mescaline
- **Geographic scope:** United States, primarily among Native American tribes
- **Theological basis:** Syncretism of indigenous peyote ceremonialism and Christian beliefs
- **Historical period:** Adopted largely since the latter part of the nineteenth century

## History

The Native American Church emerged from the widespread adoption of peyote by North American tribes beginning in the late nineteenth century. The peyote cult spread northward from its origin in central Mexico through a network of intertribal transmission routes involving groups such as the Carrizo, [[lipan-apache]], Tonkawa, and others.

By the twentieth century, the Native American Church had become the largest intertribal religious organization in the United States, providing a pan-Indian spiritual framework that united diverse tribal traditions through the shared sacrament of peyote.

## Ceremonial Practice

The Church's ceremonies are characterized by:

- **Communal harmony:** Group peyote sessions that foster social cohesion rather than conflict
- **Christian syncretism:** Prayers and invocations addressed to Christian figures, particularly Jesus
- **Healing focus:** Use of peyote for both spiritual communion and therapeutic purposes
- **Night-long meetings:** Ceremonies typically extending from evening until dawn
- **Structured ritual:** Formal procedures that contrast with the more individualistic shamanistic peyote use of some tribes

## Contrast with Mescalero Apache Practice

The harmonious communal use of peyote in the Native American Church contrasts sharply with the experience of the [[mescalero-apache]]:

- The Mescaleros never became involved in the Peyote Religion as described by Slotkin (1956)
- Mescalero peyote use was intended to affirm traditional religious practices, not to adopt Christian elements
- Peyote meetings among the Mescalero led to disruption, bloodshed, and feuds rather than communal harmony
- The Mescaleros ultimately proscribed peyote use, defining it as an evil substance

This contrast illustrates how the same psychoactive substance can produce vastly different social outcomes depending on cultural context and personality structure.

## Key Facts

- The Church represents the most widely known instance of hallucinogenic drug use combined with Christianity
- Peyote use in the Church is distinguished by its amiable, harmonious group character
- The Church has legal protections for peyote use as a religious sacrament in the United States
- Not all tribes that adopted peyote joined the Native American Church — some maintained distinct ceremonial traditions

## Relevance to Ethnobotany

The Native American Church demonstrates the role of botanical sacraments in maintaining cultural identity and facilitating intertribal unity. The spread of peyote from its Mesoamerican origin to tribes across North America represents one of the most significant ethnobotanical diffusion events in indigenous American history.

## Legal Status

The Church's peyote use has been the subject of significant legal battles in the United States, establishing important precedents for religious freedom and the protection of indigenous spiritual practices involving controlled substances.

## References in Source

Harner discusses the Native American Church in the context of cultures undergoing Westernization, contrasting its harmonious peyote use with the hostile experience of the Mescalero Apache.
