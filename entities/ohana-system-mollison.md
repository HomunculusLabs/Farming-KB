# Ohana System (Hawaiian Land Division)

## Description

The [[ohana-system]] was the indigenous Hawaiian [[integrated-land-management-and-polyculture-design]] social organization system that integrated entire watersheds — from mountain ridges to surrounding reefs — as indivisible ecological and social units. [[bill-mollison]] presents the Ohana as an exemplary model of integrated land stewardship, calling it "a nice concept" and "a totality" that unified watershed, people, nutrient flow, animals, and reef systems into a single, indivisible unit.

## Classification

- **System type:** Indigenous integrated [[watershed-management]]
- **Geographic origin:** Hawaiian Islands (pre-contact, pre-missionary)
- **Scale:** Watershed-level (mountain ridge to reef)
- **Social structure:** Communal, kinship-based land stewardship

## Key Facts

- Each Ohana controlled an entire watershed from its **mountain origins to its discharge into the sea and beyond the reef**
- Land divisions followed **natural ridge lines between valleys** — ecologically logical boundaries
- The system was developed over **thousands of years** through successive iterations of settlement, extinction, and reinvasion
- Hawaiians independently invented the **keyline system** — massive stone walls at the keyline directing water from upper valleys to ridge points
- Stone-lined channels carried water to taro paddy fields on ridge points, at steeper gradients than modern keyline practice
- **Forests were taboo areas** (kapu) — protected as the source of water, used only for essential purposes
- **Fish ponds** at the nutrient discharge point captured run-off and converted it to fish protein (mullet)
- The nutrient cycle was: upland forest → paddy fields → fish ponds → reef → food → people → repeat
- This system supported **thousands of people** on a single island in a stable, sustainable ecology

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Watershed Integration

The Ohana model demonstrates the [[permaculture-principle-1-observe-interact]] of **whole-system design** at a landscape scale:

1. **Mountain forest (watershed source):** Protected, tabu. Generates water through condensation and rainfall interception
2. **Keyline channels:** Stone-lined water distribution from valleys to ridge-tops
3. **Taro paddies (ridge points):** Primary [[solomon-garden-planning-calorie-production]] using gravity-fed irrigation
4. **Dryland crops (exposed ridges):** [[tropical-root-crops-cassava-taro-sweet-potato-and-yam]] dryland fern mulch behind cross-wind rock walls
5. **Fish ponds (coastal):** Converted nutrient run-off into protein via algae → mullet food chain
6. **Reef structures:** Underwater construction created fish shelter and extended the productive zone

### Nutrient Cycling

The Ohana system perfected **closed-loop [[cannabis-nutrient-management]]** centuries before modern permaculture named the concept:

- Nutrients from forest → agricultural land → coastal zone → fish → people → back to land
- No nutrient waste: everything flowed downstream and was captured at each stage
- Fish ponds acted as **nutrient interceptors** — in three days, sea organisms fixed all incoming nutrients
- The enclosed reef system raised "millions of pounds of mullet" from what would otherwise be lost nutrients

### Design Lessons for Modern Permaculture

Mollison suggests the Ohana model could be **re-instituted** on degraded [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-hawaii-pacific-islands]]:

- Seize headwaters and begin "Ohana-ing" downhill
- Restore the forest-keyline-paddy-pond-reef sequence
- Protect upper forests absolutely (no logging, no clearing)
- Use rock mulch walls for dryland crop protection on exposed sites
- Integrate people as an **essential component** of the system, not external managers

### Cultural Dimension

The word "Ohana" means family in Hawaiian, and this is deliberate — the people were an **integral part** of the land division. Belonging to an Ohana meant belonging to a specific valley, a set of fields, a fish pond, and a reef section. This stands in contrast to Western property models where people are separate from and owners of land.

### Collapse and Potential Revival

Mollison notes that missionaries, cattle, and disease dismantled the Ohana system. The result:

- Forests cleared → clouds pushed up ridges → drying cycle began
- Nutrient flow now dumps into the sea without capture
- Fish traps filled and became solid earth
- Fewer people live on the islands, and the islands are less able to support them
- Production declining rapidly

However, Hawaiian people are **buying back the islands**, creating potential for Ohana restoration.
