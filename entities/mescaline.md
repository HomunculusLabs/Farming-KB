## Overview

Mescaline (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine) is a naturally occurring protoalkaloid
and classical psychedelic compound. It is the primary psychoactive constituent of
peyote (*Lophophora williamsii*), San Pedro cactus (*Echinopsis pachanoi*), and
the Peruvian torch (*Echinopsis peruviana*). Mescaline has been used in ceremonial
contexts by Indigenous peoples of the Americas for at least 5,700 years, making it
one of the oldest known psychedelic substances.

As a phenethylamine derivative, mescaline is structurally distinct from the
tryptamine-based psychedelics (psilocybin, DMT) and the ergoline-based LSD.
Its effects include visual hallucinations, altered perception of time and space,
synesthesia, and profound introspective experiences lasting 8–12 hours.

## Chemical Properties

Mescaline has the molecular formula C₁₁H₁₇NO₃ and a molecular weight of
211.26 g/mol. The IUPAC name is 2-(3,4,5-trimethoxyphenyl)ethan-1-amine.
It belongs to the phenethylamine class and is specifically a trimethoxyphenethylamine.

The compound presents as a white crystalline powder at room temperature with a
melting point of 35–36 °C (hydrochloride salt: 181–184 °C). Mescaline is soluble
in water, ethanol, and slightly soluble in chloroform. The hydrochloride salt is
the most common stable form encountered in laboratory and clinical settings.

Mescaline's three methoxy groups at the 3, 4, and 5 positions on the phenyl ring
are critical for its psychedelic activity. Removal or repositioning of these
substituents dramatically reduces 5-HT₂A receptor affinity and subjective potency.

## Pharmacology

Mescaline acts primarily as a serotonin 5-HT₂A receptor agonist, the same
mechanism shared by other classical psychedelics. Its binding affinity (Kᵢ) at
5-HT₂A is approximately 500 nM, making it less potent than either psilocybin
(~6 nM) or LSD (~1 nM) on a molar basis.

Additional receptor interactions include:

- **5-HT₂C**: Moderate agonism contributing to anxiety and stimulant-like effects
- **5-HT₁A**: Weak partial agonism
- **Alpha-2 adrenergic**: Weak antagonism, possibly contributing to cardiovascular effects
- **TAAR1**: Trace agonist activity

Mescaline is metabolized primarily by hepatic monoamine oxidase (MAO-A) via
oxidative deamination, yielding 3,4,5-trimethoxyphenylacetic acid as the major
urinary metabolite. The plasma half-life is approximately 6 hours, with subjective
effects persisting longer due to active metabolites and receptor dynamics.

## Natural Sources

Mescaline occurs naturally in several cactus species within the Cactaceae family:

- **Peyote** (*Lophophora williamsii*): 1–6% mescaline by dry weight; the most
  culturally significant source, used by the Native American Church
- **San Pedro** (*Echinopsis pachanoi*): 0.1–2% by dry weight; widely cultivated
  and used in Andean traditional medicine
- **Peruvian torch** (*Echinopsis peruviana*): 0.5–2% by dry weight
- **Bolivian torch** (*Echinopsis lageniformis*): Variable alkaloid content

Mescaline is also found in trace amounts in the bean pods of *Anadenanthera peregrina*
and *Anadenanthera colubrina*, though bufotenin (5-HO-DMT) is the dominant
psychoactive alkaloid in these species.

## Effects and Dosage

The typical effective dose of mescaline hydrochloride ranges from 200–500 mg,
corresponding to approximately 2.3–5.8 mg/kg for a 70 kg individual. Onset
occurs within 45–90 minutes, with peak effects at 2–4 hours and a total
duration of 8–12 hours.

Characteristic effects include:

- Intense geometric and organic visual hallucinations with eyes open or closed
- Enhanced color perception and pattern recognition
- Altered body schema and proprioception
- Synesthesia (particularly sound-to-color)
- Euphoria and emotional lability
- Time distortion
- Mystical or ego-dissolution experiences at higher doses

Common aftereffects include fatigue, mild headache, and an extended period of
emotional sensitivity or introspection lasting 24–48 hours post-experience.

## Legal Status

Mescaline is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under the United
States Controlled Substances Act. It is listed under the UN Convention on
Psychotropic Substances (1971) in Schedule I, restricting international trade
and manufacture.

Notable legal exemptions exist for religious use: the Native American Church
(NAC) secured federal protection for peyote use in religious ceremonies through
the American Indian Religious Freedom Act Amendments of 1994. Similar protections
exist in Canada under the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act.

In the European Union, mescaline is controlled under national legislation in
most member states. Peru and Bolivia do not criminalize San Pedro cactus
cultivation, reflecting its long history of traditional use in Andean culture.
