## Description

The oxygen-ethylene cycle is a continuous alternation between aerobic (oxygen-dominated) and anaerobic (ethylene-dominated) conditions at soil micro-sites. This natural cycling, driven by microbial metabolism and gaseous diffusion, is essential for plant nutrient availability and soil health. The process was elucidated by A. Smith (1981) and represents a key argument against soil cultivation.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil biochemistry / soil gaseous dynamics
- **Key gases**: Oxygen (O2), Ethylene (C2H4), Carbon dioxide (CO2), Ammonia (NH3)
- **Catalyst**: Ferrous iron (Fe2+) required for ethylene production from decaying leaf material

## Key Facts

### The Cycle

- Natural soils are always a mosaic of aerobic and anaerobic patches called micro-sites
- At aerobic sites, oxygen dominates and microbial activity proceeds normally
- As oxygen is exhausted at a site, ethylene builds up (typically 1-2 ppm in soils)
- Ethylene inhibits (suspends) microbial activity at the site
- As ethylene diffuses outward, oxygen floods back and re-activates the site
- This cycle is continuous under natural forests and grasslands

### Why Cultivation Disrupts the Cycle

- Ploughing and aeration convert ammonia to nitrates and nitrites
- Nitrates inhibit ethylene production
- Aeration converts ferrous iron (Fe2+) to ferric iron (Fe3+)
- Ethylene formation requires ferrous iron as a catalyst
- Without ethylene, the oxygen-ethylene cycle breaks down
- Plant nutrients become tightly bound to ferric iron and unavailable to roots
- Humus oxidises to carbon dioxide and is lost

### Consequences of Disruption

- Nutrients become locked in unavailable oxide forms
- Ferric iron sequesters plant nutrients
- Humus is lost to atmospheric carbon dioxide
- Most plant root pathogens require aerobic conditions (which cultivation creates)
- Nitrate nitrogen is highly mobile and leaches from bare soil
- Net result: atmospheric pollution, stream pollution, and nutrient-depleted soil

### Recommendations for Restoration

- Minimise soil disturbance
- Use surface mulch (not incorporated) as ethylene precursor (old leaves are best)
- Apply small but frequent ammonia fertiliser until balances recover
- Maintain permanent pasture, forests, or orchards with permanent green crop
- Use no-dig or mulched garden systems
- Include legumes in proportion to natural plant associations

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The oxygen-ethylene cycle directly influences fungal activity in soils. Many beneficial fungi, including mycorrhizal species, depend on the micro-site mosaic for optimal function. Anaerobic micro-sites produce ethylene which regulates not only microbial activity but also root growth patterns and fungal colonization dynamics. In [[substrate-preparation]] aims to create appropriate aerobic-anaerobic gradients: composting creates anaerobic zones within the pile while maintaining aerobic surfaces, and the transition between these states drives the succession of microbial communities that produce a productive mushroom substrate.

## The Oxygen-Ethylene Cycle

Mollison described how soil oxygen and ethylene concentrations oscillate in a
cyclical pattern. Aerobic conditions promote ethylene production by soil
microorganisms. Rising ethylene then inhibits aerobic activity, reducing oxygen
consumption. This self-regulating cycle maintains balanced soil atmosphere.

## Implications for Soil Health

Disruption of the oxygen-ethylene cycle through compaction or waterlogging
shifts soil toward permanently anaerobic conditions. This favors pathogenic
organisms and reduces nutrient cycling efficiency. [[permaculture-earthworks]]
and mulching strategies preserve this natural regulatory mechanism.

## Detection and Measurement

Soil ethylene can be measured using gas chromatography, though most practitioners
rely on indirect indicators. Healthy soils with active oxygen-ethylene cycling
show vigorous root growth and diverse microbial populations. Stagnant, compacted
soils often smell sour from anaerobic metabolites.
