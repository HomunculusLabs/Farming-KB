# Erythroxylum coca

**Erythroxylum coca** Lam. is a tropical shrub in the family Erythroxylaceae, native to the eastern slopes of the Andes in South America. It is the primary source of **cocaine** (benzoylmethylecgonine), one of the most widely known and historically significant psychoactive alkaloids. The plant has been cultivated and used by indigenous peoples of the Andes for over 4,000 years, primarily for its stimulant and appetite-suppressing properties when the leaves are chewed with an alkaline substance. Coca occupies a central role in Andean indigenous culture, religion, medicine, and daily life.

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

The genus *Erythroxylum* comprises approximately 230 species of tropical shrubs and small trees distributed across the Americas, Africa, Madagascar, and Southeast Asia. The family Erythroxylaceae contains four genera and approximately 240 species. Within *Erythroxylum*, only a handful of species contain significant quantities of cocaine alkaloids.

Two varieties of *E. coca* are generally recognized:

- ***Erythroxylum coca* var. *coca*** (Lam.) — the "Huanuco coca" or "Bolivian coca," cultivated in the Yungas region of Bolivia and in Peru. This variety typically has broader, elliptical leaves and contains moderate to high cocaine levels.
- ***Erythroxylum coca* var. *ipadu* (Plowman)** — the "Amazonian coca," cultivated in the lowland Amazon basin by indigenous groups in Colombia, Peru, and Brazil. This variety has narrower leaves and is typically lower in cocaine content. It is adapted to cultivation under partial forest canopy.

The closely related ***Erythroxylum novogranatense*** (D. Morris) Hieron. is the source of "Colombian coca" and includes two varieties: var. *novogranatense* (cultivated primarily in Colombia) and var. *truxillense* (the "Trujillo coca" of Peru). This species is more drought-tolerant than *E. coca* and contains generally lower cocaine levels.

The word "coca" derives from the Aymara language and was adopted into Spanish during the colonial period. The genus name *Erythroxylum* is from the Greek *erythros* (red) and *xylon* (wood), referring to the reddish wood of some species in the genus.

## Botany

*Erythroxylum coca* is an evergreen shrub that typically grows 1 to 3 meters in height under cultivation, though wild specimens and older plants can reach 5 meters. The plant has a bushy growth habit with multiple slender, greenish-brown to reddish branches and smooth bark on younger growth.

The leaves are alternate, elliptical to obovate, 3 to 7 cm long and 1.5 to 4 cm wide, with an entire (smooth) margin. They are bright green and glossy on the upper surface, paler and slightly matte beneath, with a characteristic slightly incurved midrib that gives the leaf a subtle concave appearance. The leaves are leathery but pliable and emit a faint, tea-like aroma when crushed. Each leaf has two small, brown stipules at the base of the petiole.

The flowers are small, white to pale yellow, approximately 5 to 8 mm in diameter, borne in small clusters of 3 to 5 in the leaf axils. They have 5 petals and 5 sepals and are mildly fragrant. Flowering can occur year-round in suitable climates.

The fruit is a small, ovoid to ellipsoidal red drupe, approximately 5 to 8 mm long and 4 to 6 mm in diameter, containing a single seed. The fruit turns from green to bright red as it matures. The seed is approximately 3 to 4 mm in diameter, with a hard seed coat.

Coca plants can be harvested 2 to 4 times per year, with individual leaves remaining suitable for harvest for 2 to 3 years before declining in alkaloid content. A well-maintained coca field can remain productive for 20 to 40 years.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is native to the eastern Andean slopes (the montane cloud forest zone) from western Venezuela through Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia, at elevations between 300 and 2,000 meters. It thrives in humid, subtropical to tropical montane environments with temperatures between 15°C and 25°C, abundant rainfall (1,500 to 3,000 mm annually), high humidity, and rich, well-draining volcanic or alluvial soils.

The Amazonian variety (*var. ipadu*) is adapted to lowland tropical conditions below 600 meters elevation, where it is cultivated in forest garden systems by indigenous Amazonian peoples. This variety tolerates lower light conditions and is often grown under partial forest canopy.

## Chemical Composition

Coca leaves contain a complex mixture of tropane alkaloids, with cocaine being the most significant:

### Major Alkaloids

- **Cocaine** (benzoylmethylecgonine) — the primary tropane alkaloid, comprising 0.3% to 1.5% of dry leaf weight in traditional cultivars. Modern high-cocaine cultivars selected for illicit production may contain up to 2% or more. Cocaine is a powerful local anesthetic and central nervous system stimulant that blocks the reuptake of dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin.
- **Benzoylecgonine** — the primary inactive metabolite of cocaine, formed by hydrolysis of the methyl ester group. It is the target compound in most drug tests.
- **Ecgonine** — a tropane base alkaloid and the core structure of cocaine.
- **Methylecgonine** — also known as tropine or ecgonine methyl ester, a minor alkaloid.

### Minor Alkaloids

- **Cinnamoylcocaine** (cis and trans isomers) — typically 0.01% to 0.1%
- **Truxilline** (cocamine) — 0.01% to 0.05%
- **Tropacocaine** — trace amounts, structurally related to cocaine with a benzoyl group
- **Hygrine** — a pyrrolidine alkaloid found in both coca and some *Erythroxylum* species
- **Cucahygrine** — a related pyrrolidine alkaloid

### Nutritional Composition

Coca leaves are nutritionally significant, containing:

- **Vitamins**: A, B1 (thiamine), B2 (riboflavin), B3 (niacin), C (ascorbic acid), and E
- **Minerals**: Calcium, iron, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, sodium, and trace elements
- **Protein**: Approximately 15% to 20% by dry weight
- **Fiber**: Significant dietary fiber content

The nutritional value of coca leaves has been cited by indigenous advocates as evidence that coca chewing is a beneficial traditional practice distinct from cocaine abuse.

## Traditional and Cultural Use

### Pre-Columbian Use

The chewing of coca leaves (*acullico* or *coqueo*) is an ancient practice dating back at least 4,000 to 5,000 years in the Andean region. Archaeological evidence from coastal Peru shows coca chewing among pre-Columbian cultures including the Chavín (circa 1000 BCE), Moche (100-800 CE), Chimú (900-1470 CE), and Inca (1438-1533 CE) civilizations. Mummies from northern Chile dating to approximately 1000 BCE have been found with dental wear patterns consistent with coca chewing, and chemical analysis has confirmed the presence of cocaine metabolites in hair and tissue samples from pre-Columbian mummies.

Coca held sacred status in Inca society, where it was known as *khoka* (the plant originating from the divine). It was used in religious ceremonies as an offering to the gods (Inti the sun god, Pachamama the earth goddess, and the mountain spirits or *apus*), in divination rituals, in burial ceremonies (leaves were placed in the mouths of the dead to provision them for the afterlife), and as a form of currency or tribute payment.

### Traditional Methods

The traditional method of coca consumption involves placing a wad of leaves (*acullico* or *bollo*) in the cheek alongside an alkaline substance (*llipta*, *toasta*, or *cal*), typically prepared from the ash of quinoa plants, burnt limestone, or other alkaline plant materials. The alkaline agent raises the pH in the mouth from approximately 6.5 to approximately 8.5, facilitating the release of cocaine from the leaf matrix and absorption through the buccal mucosa. This method produces mild stimulant effects without the intense psychoactive rush, cardiovascular stress, or addiction potential associated with purified cocaine.

A typical coca chewing session involves maintaining the leaf wad in the cheek for 30 to 60 minutes, periodically adding fresh leaves and small amounts of the alkaline preparation. The wad is eventually discarded.

### Medicinal Uses

Indigenous Andean peoples use coca leaves to treat a wide range of ailments including altitude sickness (*soroche*), fatigue, hunger, thirst, headache, gastrointestinal disorders, toothache, and respiratory complaints. Coca tea (*mate de coca* or *cocada*) is commonly consumed in Peru and Bolivia as a digestive aid and for altitude adjustment. Coca is also used topically as a poultice for wounds, fractures, and insect bites.

## Psychoactive Effects

The effects of coca leaf chewing differ dramatically from those of purified cocaine, reflecting both the lower dose and the slower absorption kinetics:

- **Coca leaf**: Mild stimulant effects including increased alertness, reduced fatigue, appetite suppression, mild elevation of mood, and a general sense of well-being. Onset is gradual (10 to 20 minutes) and effects last 1 to 2 hours. Blood cocaine levels from traditional coca chewing are typically 10 to 50 nanograms per milliliter, compared to 100 to 500 ng/mL from intranasal cocaine use.
- **Purified cocaine hydrochloride** (snorted): Intense euphoria, increased energy, heightened confidence, talkativeness, dilated pupils, and elevated heart rate and blood pressure. Onset 1 to 5 minutes, duration 30 to 60 minutes.
- **Crack cocaine** (smoked): Extremely rapid onset (seconds), intense but short-lived euphoria (5 to 15 minutes), high abuse potential and addiction risk.

## History of Cocaine

Cocaine was first isolated from coca leaves in 1855 by German chemist Friedrich Gaedcke, who named it "erythroxyline." In 1860, Albert Niemann obtained a purer preparation and described its anesthetic properties on the tongue. Karl Koller, an Austrian ophthalmologist and colleague of Sigmund Freud, demonstrated cocaine's value as a local anesthetic for eye surgery in 1884, launching its widespread medical use.

In the 1880s, cocaine gained enormous popularity in Europe and North America. Sigmund Freud published his influential essay "Über Coca" (1884), advocating its use for treating morphine addiction, depression, and fatigue. Cocaine was widely used in patent medicines, tonics, and elixirs, and was an ingredient in the original formulation of Coca-Cola (introduced in 1886 by John Pemberton). The cocaine was removed from Coca-Cola in 1903.

The Harrison Narcotics Tax Act of 1914 effectively prohibited cocaine in the United States by requiring registration and taxation of all persons dealing in the drug. International control followed with the 1961 UN Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs, which classified coca leaf alongside cocaine as a Schedule I controlled substance. This classification was controversial: Andean nations argued that coca leaf chewing was a benign traditional practice fundamentally different from cocaine abuse, and Bolivia and Peru filed formal objections. Bolivia ultimately withdrew from the convention in 2011 and re-acceded in 2013 with a reservation protecting traditional coca chewing.

## Legal Status

- **Coca leaf**: Legal for traditional use and cultivation in Bolivia, Peru, and parts of Colombia. Bolivia's constitution recognizes coca as a cultural heritage and its cultivation is regulated and limited to specified areas. The Bolivian government maintains a system of community-controlled coca markets (*mercados de la coca*) where registered growers sell to licensed buyers for traditional and industrial purposes.
- **Cocaine**: Schedule I (US Controlled Substances Act), Schedule I (UN Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs). It is one of the most strictly controlled substances globally, with production, distribution, and possession carrying severe criminal penalties in virtually all jurisdictions.
- **Coca tea and food products**: Legal and widely available in Peru and Bolivia, where they are sold in markets, pharmacies, and grocery stores. Restricted or banned in most other countries, though enforcement varies. In the United States, coca tea is technically illegal but is occasionally available through importers. Several US Customs seizures of coca tea shipments have been reported. In the EU, coca leaf products are generally prohibited.

## Coca in the Illicit Drug Trade

The vast majority of globally produced coca is diverted into the illicit cocaine trade. The process involves extracting cocaine base from coca leaves through a series of chemical steps (typically using sulfuric acid, gasoline or kerosene, ammonia or sodium bicarbonate, and potassium permanganate), followed by conversion to cocaine hydrochloride for distribution. Colombia is the world's largest producer of illicit cocaine, followed by Peru and Bolivia. The global illicit cocaine market is estimated at over $100 billion annually.
