## Description

Banisteriopsis inebrians is a species of woody vine in the Malpighiaceae family, native to the Amazon basin. It is one of several Banisteriopsis species used in the preparation of ayahuasca, the psychoactive brew employed in Amazonian folk healing and shamanic practice. The species was identified by Friedberg (1965) as one of the major Banisteriopsis species in the Amazon region used for hallucinogenic preparations.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Family:** Malpighiaceae
- **Genus:** Banisteriopsis
- **Species:** B. inebrians
- **Common names:** Ayahuasca vine (general), one of several species used in ayahuasca preparation
- **Related species:** B. caapi, B. quitensis, B. rusbyana
- **Etymology:** The species epithet "inebrians" directly references the intoxicating properties of the plant

## Key Facts

- One of four major Banisteriopsis species delineated by Friedberg (1965) for the Amazon region
- The four species are B. caapi, B. quitensis, B. inebrians, and B. rusbyana
- Used in the preparation of ayahuasca brew, which is made by cutting and boiling the woody vine several hours
- Like other Banisteriopsis species, contains beta-carboline alkaloids (harmine, harmaline, tetrahydroharmine) that function as monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs)
- The MAOI action of the vine is essential for the oral activity of DMT-containing admixture plants
- Often combined with other plants in ayahuasca preparations, including Psychotria viridis (chacruna) or Diplopterys cabrerana (chaliponga)
- Indigenous and mestizo healers select specific Banisteriopsis species based on tradition, availability, and intended therapeutic effect
- The ayahuasca potion often contains additives besides various Banisteriopsis species
- Additional plants added to the brew may include toé (identified by Schultes as Datura suaveolens) and chacruna (B. rusbyana leaves, which contain dimethyltryptamine)

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Banisteriopsis inebrians is a candidate species for cultivation alongside the more commonly grown B. caapi. For cultivators, understanding the diversity of Banisteriopsis species used in ayahuasca preparations is important because different species may contain different ratios of beta-carboline alkaloids, potentially producing qualitatively different effects in the brewed medicine. The species is part of the Malpighiaceae family, which includes several other psychoactive vines. Cultivation requirements are likely similar to B. caapi -- tropical conditions, support structures for climbing growth, and consistent moisture. The identification of multiple Banisteriopsis species in traditional use suggests that cultivators should be precise about species identification when sourcing and growing vines for research or traditional use.

## Chemical Profile

- Contains beta-carboline alkaloids (harmine, harmaline, tetrahydroharmine)
- Functions as a reversible MAO-A inhibitor when ingested
- Enables oral activity of DMT from admixture plants by preventing degradation in the gut
- Specific alkaloid profile may differ from B. caapi and other congeneric species
- The cutting and boiling process extracts the active alkaloids into the aqueous preparation
- Friedberg (1965) delineated the major Banisteriopsis species based on their distribution and traditional use in the Amazon

## Traditional Preparation

- The vine is cut and boiled for several hours to extract active compounds
- May be combined with other Banisteriopsis species in the same preparation
- Additive plants may include Datura suaveolens (toé) and chacruna leaves
- The resulting brew is distributed in a communal cup during healing ceremonies
- Healers vary the amount of potion based on patient body weight and strength

## Botanical Description

As a member of the Malpighiaceae family, Banisteriopsis inebrians is a woody climbing vine native to the Amazon basin. The genus Banisteriopsis includes several species that are morphologically similar but may differ in their alkaloid profiles and traditional uses. The plants typically grow as lianas in tropical forest environments, climbing up host trees and other support structures. The vine is harvested by cutting sections that are then boiled for several hours to extract the active beta-carboline alkaloids.

## Preparation and Traditional Use

The preparation of ayahuasca using B. inebrians follows traditional methods documented across the Amazon:
1. The vine is cut into sections from the forest
2. The woody material is boiled for several hours (often with multiple boilings)
3. Additive plants may be included in the brew (Psychotria viridis, Diplopterys cabrerana, Datura suaveolens)
4. The resulting liquid is reduced to a concentrated brew
5. The potion is distributed in a communal cup during healing ceremonies
6. Healers vary the dose based on patient body weight (optimal: 7 mg/kg per Rios Reategui, 1962)

## Pharmacological Significance

The beta-carboline alkaloids in B. inebrians represent a sophisticated pharmacological system that Amazonian peoples discovered through empirical experimentation:
- Harmine and harmaline are reversible inhibitors of monoamine oxidase A (RIMA)
- This inhibition prevents the degradation of DMT in the gut and liver
- Without the MAOI action, orally consumed DMT would be inactive
- The combination of MAOI-containing vine with DMT-containing leaves represents one of the most pharmacologically complex traditional drug preparations known
- Tetrahydroharmine may also act as a weak serotonin reuptake inhibitor

## Conservation Concerns

As demand for ayahuasca increases globally, wild populations of all Banisteriopsis species face pressure from overharvesting. Cultivation of B. inebrians and related species is essential for sustainable supply. The species" specific ecological requirements and population status require further research.
