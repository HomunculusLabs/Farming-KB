## LET System (Local Employment Trading System)

## Description

The [[let-system]] (Local Employment Trading System) is a community-based local currency and exchange network described by [[bill-mollison]] in *Permaculture: A Designers" Manual* (1988). It is a form of community money or credit system designed to keep wealth circulating within a local community rather than leaking out to external financial centres, banks, or multinational corporations. The system allows members to trade goods and services using a local unit of account referred to as "green dollars" rather than conventional national currency, thereby decoupling local economic activity from the volatility of national and global financial systems. Mollison presents the LET System as one of the most powerful tools available for building community self-reliance and economic resilience.

## Key Facts

- Local green dollars carry **no interest**, and administration costs are charged on a "cost of service" basis only
- Every joining member must be willing to consider trading in local green dollars as a condition of membership
- Any member can know the turnover or balance of any other member, ensuring full transparency and accountability
- Every member receives periodic statements of accounts, keeping the system honest and open to scrutiny
- The currency, although equivalent to legal tender in purchasing power, is not issued and cannot be cashed in — it circulates only within the community
- Green dollars are "earned" by providing goods or services to others, and "lost" by using services or goods from other members
- All trade or credit standing is a public act and refers to the community as a whole, not just between two individuals
- Unlike simple barter, a member in credit can spend across the entire range of services or goods offered by all members
- Production (time spent by members in service to others) is never limited by the lack of conventional money
- Businesses can charge federal currency for spare parts and green dollars for labour simultaneously, blending both systems
- "Foreign" goods become more expensive under the system, naturally encouraging local sourcing and production
- Local businesses thrive because money stays in the community; charities and local farms benefit greatly from circulating credits
- Anyone who wants work can offer services immediately without needing to wait for formal "jobs" to become available
- As only members trade with each other, the community account is always balanced at all times with no net deficit
- Any member (by working or selling) effectively issues their own currency and could return any community to full employment

## Historical and Theoretical Context

Mollison places the LET System within a broader critique of conventional money. He argues that conventional money derives from many agencies external to a community and circulates throughout all communities, tending to be accumulated in cities, multinational coffers, and banks supporting large investors. Community money (or credit), however, is not usable or necessarily wanted outside that community, hence circulates indefinitely within it, providing a constantly available resource. This parallels the way water cycles through a well-designed landscape — money is to the social fabric as water is to the landscape, acting as the agent of transport and the shaper of trade.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The LET System is directly relevant to permaculture-based cultivation and mycology operations:

- Small-scale growers and mushroom cultivators can trade produce for services such as labour, equipment repair, or substrate materials without needing cash
- Community-supported agriculture (CSA) models can incorporate LET-style credits for member participation in work days and harvest events
- Mycology cooperatives could use LET systems to exchange cultures, substrate, spawn, and consulting services among members
- The system reduces dependency on external capital for community-scale composting operations, mushroom cultivation facilities, or [[food-forest-establishment]]
- Enables mutual aid networks for sharing expensive equipment such as sterilizers, laminar flow hoods, and fruiting chambers
- Encourages local sourcing of cultivation inputs, which reduces supply chain vulnerability and builds regional self-reliance
- An experienced mycologist could earn green dollars by offering identification services, cultivation workshops, or strain consulting

## Design Principles

The LET System embodies several core permaculture principles as defined by Mollison and Holmgren:

1. **[[holmgren-produce-no-waste-waste-hierarchy-refuse-reduce-reuse-repair-recycle]]** — money circulates within the community rather than accumulating in distant banks
2. **Use small and slow solutions** — builds local economic resilience incrementally from the ground up
3. **[[permaculture-integrate-rather-than-segregate]] segregate** — connects producers, consumers, and service providers within a unified system
4. **Use and value diversity** — supports a wide range of goods and services, not just market commodities
5. **Use edges and [[permaculture-principle-use-edges-and-value-the-marginal]]** — leverages [[informal-economy-and-community-currencies]] spaces that conventional banking ignores

## Implementation Notes

Mollison emphasises that LET systems work best in communities of 30-600 people where members know each other by name. The system requires a central accounting mechanism (originally paper-based, now typically computerised), a willing community with diverse skills and goods to offer, transparent record-keeping accessible to all members, and clear rules about taxation responsibilities falling on individual members. An ideal member has many transactions but accumulates modest debits and credits over time rather than hoarding or sinking deeply into debt.

## Critical Assessment

Mollison acknowledges potential limitations of the LET System. The model depends on a critical mass of diverse skills and goods being available within the community — a community of only mushroom growers would have limited exchange potential. Additionally, the system works best when the community has some access to conventional currency for truly external purchases (raw materials, specialised equipment). The green dollars complement but do not entirely replace conventional money. Tax obligations remain the responsibility of individual members, which requires honesty and clear guidelines from tax authorities. Despite these caveats, Mollison argues the system is one of the most democratic economic tools available, as it returns the power of money creation to individual community members.

## Relationship to Bioregional Economics

Mollison envisions LET systems as building blocks of bioregional economic networks. Individual community LET systems can federate through agreed exchange rates, allowing coastal communities to trade with inland or mountain communities. This creates access to a wider world of resources while maintaining local accountability and the core principle that money serves the community rather than the community serving money. The ultimate vision is a network of interdependent but autonomous local economies that collectively achieve sophisticated trade and travel organisation.
