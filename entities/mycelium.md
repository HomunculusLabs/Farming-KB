# Mycelium

Mycelium is the vegetative body of a fungus, consisting of a mass of branching, thread-like filaments called hyphae. It constitutes the active growing and feeding phase of fungal life cycles, responsible for nutrient acquisition, substrate colonization, and ultimately the formation of reproductive structures (fruiting bodies or mushrooms). In many ways, mycelium is analogous to the roots and root systems of plants, though it serves additional ecological roles that have no direct plant equivalent.

## Structure

Individual hyphae are tubular filaments typically 2-10 micrometers in diameter, though some specialized hyphae (such as rhizomorphs) can reach several millimeters in diameter. Each hypha is surrounded by a rigid cell wall composed primarily of chitin (a polymer of N-acetylglucosamine), glucans (beta-1,3 and beta-1,6 glucans), and structural proteins. The chitin content of fungal cell walls typically ranges from 1-15% by dry weight, providing mechanical strength and protection against osmotic stress.

Hyphae grow exclusively at their tips through a process called polarized extension or tip growth. This involves the targeted exocytosis of cell wall-synthesizing enzymes and precursors (vesicles containing chitin synthases, glucan synthases, and wall matrix materials) to the hyphal apex. A structure called the Spitzenkorper (apical body), visible as a dense cluster of vesicles at the growing tip, orchestrates this directed growth. - **Moisture**: substrate moisture content of 50-65% by weight; below 40% growth stalls, above 75% the risk of anaerobic contamination increases
- **pH**: slightly acidic conditions, typically pH 5.0-6.5 for most cultivated fungi; some species tolerate broader pH ranges
- **Oxygen**: strictly aerobic conditions; mycelium requires continuous gas exchange for respiratory metabolism; sealed or anaerobic conditions lead to stunted growth and death

- **Carbon dioxide**: elevated CO2 levels (0.5-2%) promote mycelial growth and suppress fruiting; this is why cultivation containers are often partially sealed during the colonization phase

## Types of Mycelium

### Monokaryotic Mycelium

Monokaryotic (haploid) mycelium results from the germination of a single basidiospore. Each cell contains a single haploid nucleus (n). Monokaryotic mycelium is generally slower-growing and less robust than dikaryotic mycelium. It cannot form fruiting bodies on its own because mushroom formation requires the dikaryotic genetic state.

Monokaryotic mycelium can be isolated in the laboratory by germinating single spores on [[malt-extract-agar]] to establish initial mycelial growth

## Ecological Significance

Mycelium plays critical and often underappreciated roles in ecosystem processes:

- **Decomposition**: fungi are the primary decomposers of lignin and cellulose in terrestrial ecosystems, recycling an estimated 1-2 billion tons of carbon annually
- **Mycorrhizal associations**: approximately 80-90% of land plants form symbiotic mycorrhizal relationships with fungal mycelium, which enhances plant nutrient uptake (particularly phosphorus) in exchange for plant-derived carbon compounds
- **Soil structure**: mycelial networks bind soil particles together, improving water retention, aeration, and resistance to erosion
- **Biological networks**: mycorrhizal networks (sometimes called "common mycorrhizal networks" or "wood wide webs") connect multiple plants and trees, facilitating resource sharing, chemical signaling, and possibly even information transfer between individuals
- **Bioaccumulation**: mycelium can concentrate heavy metals and organic pollutants from soil and water, making it useful for bioremediation applications
