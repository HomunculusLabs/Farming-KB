# Mycelium

Mycelium is the vegetative body of a fungus, consisting of a mass of branching, thread-like filaments called hyphae. It constitutes the active growing and feeding phase of fungal life cycles, responsible for nutrient acquisition, substrate colonization, and ultimately the formation of reproductive structures (fruiting bodies or mushrooms). In many ways, mycelium is analogous to the roots and root systems of plants, though it serves additional ecological roles that have no direct plant equivalent.

## Structure

Individual hyphae are tubular filaments typically 2-10 micrometers in diameter, though some specialized hyphae (such as rhizomorphs) can reach several millimeters in diameter. Each hypha is surrounded by a rigid cell wall composed primarily of chitin (a polymer of N-acetylglucosamine), glucans (beta-1,3 and beta-1,6 glucans), and structural proteins. The chitin content of fungal cell walls typically ranges from 1-15% by dry weight, providing mechanical strength and protection against osmotic stress.

Hyphae grow exclusively at their tips through a process called polarized extension or tip growth. This involves the targeted exocytosis of cell wall-synthesizing enzymes and precursors (vesicles containing chitin synthases, glucan synthases, and wall matrix materials) to the hyphal apex. A structure called the Spitzenkorper (apical body), visible as a dense cluster of vesicles at the growing tip, orchestrates this directed growth. Hyphal extension rates vary from less than 1 micrometer per minute in slow-growing species to over 40 micrometers per minute in fast-growing species like [[neurospora-crassa]].

Hyphae branch repeatedly as they extend, forming a dense, interconnected network called a mycelial mat or thallus. The branching pattern is regulated by both internal genetic programs and external environmental cues such as nutrient gradients, pH, and the presence of competing organisms.

### Septate Hyphae

In higher fungi (Basidiomycota and Ascomycota), hyphae are partitioned by cross-walls called septa (singular: septum) that divide the hypha into discrete cellular compartments. Each septum contains a central pore (the septal pore) that allows cytoplasmic streaming and the movement of organelles, nuclei, and signaling molecules between adjacent cells. In Basidiomycetes, the septal pore is surrounded by a specialized structure called the parenthesome (or septal pore cap), which can be rapidly sealed by Woronin bodies or other occlusion structures to prevent damage from spreading if a hyphal compartment is breached.

This interconnected cellular architecture allows for rapid distribution of nutrients, water, and chemical signals throughout the entire mycelial network, even over considerable distances. Individual mycelial networks of some fungal species can extend for hundreds of meters or even kilometers in natural settings.

### Coenocytic Hyphae

In lower fungi (Zygomycota and some Oomycota), hyphae lack septa and are coenocytic (multinucleate), meaning the cytoplasm is continuous throughout the hyphal mass without cross-walls. These non-septate hyphae can grow faster but are more vulnerable to damage.

## Growth and Colonization

### Substrate Colonization

Mycelium colonizes organic substrates by secreting extracellular enzymes that break down complex polymeric molecules into simpler, soluble molecules that can be absorbed through the hyphal cell wall and membrane:

- **Cellulases** (endo- and exo-acting): hydrolyze cellulose from plant cell walls into glucose and cellobiose units
- **Lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, and laccases**: collectively break down lignin, the complex aromatic polymer that, along with cellulose, forms the structural material of wood; these enzymes are particularly important in white-rot fungi
- **Proteases**: hydrolyze proteins into amino acids and short peptides
- **Amylases**: break down starch into maltose and glucose
- **Lipases**: hydrolyze fats and oils into fatty acids and glycerol
- **Pectinases and xylanases**: degrade hemicellulose and pectin components of plant cell walls

Species like [[psilocybe-azurescens]] are wood-decomposing (lignicolous) fungi that produce powerful lignocellulolytic enzymes, enabling them to colonize and metabolize hardwood chips, sawdust, and other woody substrates. The mycelium of wood-decomposing fungi can eventually break down the structural integrity of wood, playing a crucial role in carbon cycling in forest ecosystems. See [[malt-extract-agar]] for methods of initiating mycelial growth in the laboratory.

### Growth Parameters

Optimal mycelial growth varies significantly by species, but generally requires the following conditions:

- **Temperature**: 20-28 degrees C for most cultivated species; [[psilocybe-azurescens]] prefers cooler ranges around 18-24 degrees C, reflecting its adaptation to the cool, foggy Pacific Northwest climate
- **Moisture**: substrate moisture content of 50-65% by weight; below 40% growth stalls, above 75% the risk of anaerobic contamination increases
- **pH**: slightly acidic conditions, typically pH 5.0-6.5 for most cultivated fungi; some species tolerate broader pH ranges
- **Oxygen**: strictly aerobic conditions; mycelium requires continuous gas exchange for respiratory metabolism; sealed or anaerobic conditions lead to stunted growth and death
- **Light**: most mycelial growth occurs optimally in complete darkness; however, some ambient light is tolerated and may be beneficial for primordia formation in later stages
- **Carbon dioxide**: elevated CO2 levels (0.5-2%) promote mycelial growth and suppress fruiting; this is why cultivation containers are often partially sealed during the colonization phase

## Types of Mycelium

### Monokaryotic Mycelium

Monokaryotic (haploid) mycelium results from the germination of a single basidiospore. Each cell contains a single haploid nucleus (n). Monokaryotic mycelium is generally slower-growing and less robust than dikaryotic mycelium. It cannot form fruiting bodies on its own because mushroom formation requires the dikaryotic genetic state.

Monokaryotic mycelium can be isolated in the laboratory by germinating single spores on [[malt-extract-agar]] under a dissecting microscope or by using micromanipulation techniques. Mating studies between different monokaryotic isolates are used to determine fungal breeding systems and compatibility groups.

### Dikaryotic Mycelium

Dikaryotic mycelium contains two genetically distinct nuclei per cell (n+n), formed when two compatible monokaryotic hyphae undergo plasmogamy (fusion of their cytoplasm without nuclear fusion). The dikaryotic state is maintained through a specialized cell division process called clamp connection formation, which ensures that each new cell receives one copy of each parental nucleus.

Dikaryotic mycelium is typically more vigorous, faster-growing, and more environmentally resilient than monokaryotic mycelium. Only dikaryotic mycelium is capable of forming mushrooms (fruiting bodies) in most Basidiomycete species, including all cultivated psilocybin mushrooms.

### Rhizomorphic vs. Tomentose Growth

Mycelium can exhibit different growth morphologies on agar media:

- **Rhizomorphic** (strandy, rope-like): organized, directional growth that indicates vigorous, healthy mycelium; preferred for cultivation as it typically correlates with faster substrate colonization and more reliable fruiting
- **Tomentose** (cottony, fluffy): slower, less organized growth that may indicate suboptimal conditions, lower vigor, or an immature monokaryotic culture

## Cultivation Applications

In mushroom cultivation, mycelium serves as the inoculum ("spawn") for transferring fungal cultures from laboratory media to bulk substrates. The standard cultivation process follows this sequence:

1. Spore germination on [[malt-extract-agar]] to establish initial mycelial growth
2. Isolation of pure dikaryotic mycelium through serial transfer
3. Expansion of mycelium on sterilized grain (rye, millet, wheat) or wood substrate to produce spawn
4. Inoculation of a fruiting substrate with the colonized spawn
5. Environmental triggering (temperature drop, humidity increase, light exposure) to initiate the transition from vegetative mycelial growth to reproductive fruiting

During substrate colonization, the mycelium of [[psilocybe-azurescens]] produces the [[tryptamine]] alkaloids psilocybin and psilocin as secondary metabolites. Alkaloid biosynthesis begins during active mycelial growth and increases during fruiting.

## Ecological Significance

Mycelium plays critical and often underappreciated roles in ecosystem processes:

- **Decomposition**: fungi are the primary decomposers of lignin and cellulose in terrestrial ecosystems, recycling an estimated 1-2 billion tons of carbon annually
- **Mycorrhizal associations**: approximately 80-90% of land plants form symbiotic mycorrhizal relationships with fungal mycelium, which enhances plant nutrient uptake (particularly phosphorus) in exchange for plant-derived carbon compounds
- **Soil structure**: mycelial networks bind soil particles together, improving water retention, aeration, and resistance to erosion
- **Biological networks**: mycorrhizal networks (sometimes called "common mycorrhizal networks" or "wood wide webs") connect multiple plants and trees, facilitating resource sharing, chemical signaling, and possibly even information transfer between individuals
- **Bioaccumulation**: mycelium can concentrate heavy metals and organic pollutants from soil and water, making it useful for bioremediation applications

The largest known organism on Earth is a mycelial network of the honey fungus [[armillaria-ostoyae]] in the Malheur National Forest in Oregon, which covers an estimated 2,385 acres (965 hectares) and is between 2,400 and 8,650 years old.
