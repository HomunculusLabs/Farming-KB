The plant is characterized by its tall, woolly flowering spike and large, fuzzy basal leaves that form a distinctive rosette in the first year of growth.

Mullein has been used for over 2,000 years in European folk medicine. The Greek physician Dioscorides documented its use for pulmonary complaints around 70 CE, and it remained a staple in European pharmacopeias through the medieval period. Indigenous peoples of North America adopted the plant shortly after its introduction, incorporating it into their own healing traditions for coughs, colds, and wound care. Modern herbalists continue to value mullein as a safe, effective respiratory tonic.

## Description

### Growth Habit

Verbascum thapsus is a biennial plant with a distinctive two-year life cycle. In the first year, it produces a large basal rosette of woolly, gray-green leaves. In the second year, it sends up a tall, unbranched flowering stalk that can reach 1.5 to 2.5 meters (5–8 feet) in height. The plant is monocarpic, dying after seed production in its second year.

### Leaves

The basal rosette leaves are large, ovate to oblong, measuring 15–50 cm long and 5–15 cm wide. They are densely covered with a soft, woolly pubescence (star-shaped trichomes) on both surfaces, giving them a grayish-green appearance and a velvety texture. Stem leaves are alternate, progressively smaller upward, and clasp the stem at their bases. The dense pubescence serves as a protective adaptation against herbivory and water loss.

### Flowers

The flowering spike is dense and cylindrical, 20–80 cm long, bearing numerous five-petaled yellow flowers. Individual flowers are approximately 15–25 mm in diameter, with five fused petals forming a slightly irregular corolla. Flowers open sequentially from the bottom of the spike upward over a period of several weeks. The stamens are distinctive, with five filaments: three long and two short, all covered in purple to white woolly hairs. The anthers are kidney-shaped and connivent (converging around the style).

### Fruits and Seeds

The fruit is a two-valved, ovoid capsule approximately 6–8 mm long, containing numerous small brownish seeds (0.5–1.0 mm). A single mullein plant can produce 100,000 to 240,000 seeds, which remain viable in the soil for decades. Seeds are dispersed by wind and gravity, and the species relies on this massive seed bank for persistence.

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Native Range:** Europe (widespread), North Africa, temperate Asia (Turkey, the Caucasus, China)
- **Naturalized Range:** North America (all continental US states and most Canadian provinces), [[cotter-mushroom-nutrition-medicinal-compounds]] in Verbascum thapsus are concentrated in the leaves and flowers:

- **Mucilage (polysaccharides):** 2–8% of leaf dry weight; forms a soothing, gel-like layer on mucous membranes when infused in water. This is the primary basis for mullein's respiratory applications.
- **Saponins:** Present in leaves and flowers; contribute expectorant activity by irritating mucous membranes enough to stimulate mucus secretion, facilitating productive coughs.
- **Flavonoids:** Including luteolin, apigenin, and their glycosides; contribute anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity.
- **Iridoid glycosides:** Including aucubin; found in leaves and flowers with anti-inflammatory properties.
- **Volatile oils:** Present in small quantities in flowers; contribute mild antiseptic properties.
- **Coumarins:** Herniarin and other coumarin derivatives present in the flowers.
- **Tannins:** Present in low concentrations; contribute astringent properties to leaf preparations.
- **Minerals:** Notably calcium, magnesium, and potassium in leaf tissue.
