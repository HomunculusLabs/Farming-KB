## Overview

Scutellaria indica is a perennial or annual herbaceous plant in the family Lamiaceae, widely distributed across East and South Asia. Commonly known as Indian skullcap, it has been used in traditional medicine systems across China, Japan, Korea, and India for its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties.

The species is closely related to the commercially important [[scutellaria-baicalensis]] but is distinguished by its lower stature, broader leaf base, and preference for warmer, more humid habitats. Despite its widespread traditional use, it has received less scientific attention than its Chinese relative.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Order: Lamiales
- Family: Lamiaceae
- Genus: Scutellaria
- Species: Scutellaria indica L.

The species was first described by Linnaeus in 1753. Several infraspecific taxa have been recognized, including S. indica var. parvifolia and S. indica var. elongata, though taxonomic boundaries remain debated.

Plants are annual to short-lived perennial, growing 10–30 cm tall with prostrate to ascending stems. The stems are slender, quadrangular, and typically purple-tinged, with sparse glandular pubescence.

Leaves are opposite, broadly ovate to cordate, 1–3 cm long and 0.8–2.5 cm wide, with crenate-serrate margins and rounded to cordate bases. The petiole is short, approximately 2–5 mm.

Flowers are borne singly in upper leaf axils on short pedicels. The corolla is pale blue to violet, 10–15 mm long, with a characteristic two-lipped structure and a prominent hooded upper lip.

The calyx is two-lipped with the distinctive Scutellaria appendage on the upper lip. Four nutlets are produced per flower, each approximately 1 mm long, brown and smooth at maturity.

Flowering typically occurs from April to July, with fruiting continuing through September. The plant reproduces primarily by seed, with limited vegetative spread through rooting at stem nodes.

## Distribution and Habitat

Scutellaria indica is native to East and South Asia, with a range extending from eastern China and Japan through the Korean Peninsula to Taiwan, the Philippines, and the Indian subcontinent. It has also been recorded in Nepal, Thailand, and Myanmar.

The species occupies open, disturbed habitats including forest margins, grassy slopes, road embankments, rocky outcrops, and cultivated field margins. It occurs from lowland elevations to approximately 2,500 meters in mountainous regions.

It prefers well-drained, sandy to loamy soils in partial shade to full sun. Unlike S. baicalensis, which favors dry steppe conditions, S. indica tolerates higher humidity and warmer temperatures.

The plant is relatively common within its range and is not considered threatened. It is sometimes collected from the wild for local medicinal use but is not commercially cultivated on a significant scale.

In some Chinese provinces, S. indica is harvested opportunistically alongside S. baicalensis, leading to occasional adulteration of commercial Huang Qin material. DNA barcoding has been proposed as a method to distinguish the two species in trade.

## Bioactive Compounds

The aerial parts of Scutellaria indica contain a rich complement of flavonoids, phenolic acids, and terpenoids. The flavonoid profile overlaps with but is distinct from that of [[scutellaria-baicalensis]].

Major flavonoids include apigenin, luteolin, scutellarein, baicalein, wogonin, and their glycosidic derivatives. Apigenin-7-O-glucuronide and luteolin-7-O-glucuronide are among the most abundant individual compounds.

Baicalin (baicalein-7-O-glucuronide), the signature compound of S. baicalensis, is present in S. indica but at significantly lower concentrations. Wogonin-7-O-glucuronide and scutellarein-7-O-glucuronide are more prominent in S. indica by comparison.

Phenolic acid derivatives include rosmarinic acid, caffeic acid, and chlorogenic acid. These contribute significantly to the antioxidant capacity of the extracts.

Diterpenoid compounds related to the scutebarbatine series found in S. barbata have also been isolated, though in smaller quantities. Essential oil content is low, with β-caryophyllene and germacrene D as major volatiles.

Total flavonoid content of dried aerial parts ranges from 1.5–4.0%, depending on harvest time and geographic origin. Peak flavonoid accumulation occurs during the flowering period.

## Pharmacology

Research on Scutellaria indica has demonstrated several pharmacological activities of interest, though the evidence base is less extensive than for related species.

**Anti-inflammatory activity** is among the best-documented properties. Ethanolic and aqueous extracts suppress production of pro-inflammatory mediators including NO, TNF-α, IL-6, and PGE2 in LPS-stimulated macrophage cell lines.

The flavonoids apigenin and luteolin are primarily responsible for this activity, acting through inhibition of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways. Wogonin contributes additional anti-inflammatory effects through COX-2 suppression.

**Antioxidant activity** has been consistently demonstrated across multiple assay systems. S. indica extracts show potent free radical scavenging activity against DPPH, ABTS, and hydroxyl radicals, with activity comparable to standard antioxidants.

**Neuroprotective effects** have been observed in neuronal cell culture models. Extracts protect against glutamate-induced excitotoxicity, reduce oxidative stress markers, and inhibit amyloid-β aggregation in vitro.

Hepatoprotective activity has been reported in rodent models of carbon tetrachloride-induced liver injury. Pretreatment with S. indica extract significantly reduced serum transaminase levels and hepatic lipid peroxidation.

Antimicrobial activity against selected Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis) has been documented, though the extract shows limited activity against Gram-negative organisms and fungi.

Preliminary antitumor screening has shown moderate cytotoxicity against several human cancer cell lines. However, the anticancer activity is significantly weaker than that of S. barbata, and this application has not been pursued in clinical settings.

## Traditional Medicine

In Chinese traditional medicine, Scutellaria indica is sometimes used as a substitute or regional alternative to S. baicalensis (Huang Qin), though it is considered less potent. It is classified as bitter and cold, entering the Lung and Liver meridians.

Traditional applications include treatment of sore throat, cough, fever, inflammatory skin conditions, and digestive complaints. In some regions it is applied topically for boils, eczema, and minor wounds.

In Japanese Kampo medicine, the species has limited formal recognition compared to S. baicalensis, which is a component of the well-known Sho-saiko-to formula. However, folk use of S. indica persists in rural communities.

In Indian Ayurvedic and Siddha traditions, the plant is used as a febrifuge and anti-inflammatory agent. It is sometimes combined with other Lamiaceae herbs including [[ocimum-sanctum]] (holy basil) in traditional formulations.

No significant toxicity has been reported in traditional use. The herb is generally regarded as safe at standard doses, though comprehensive toxicological studies are lacking.

Modern interest in S. indica has focused primarily on its flavonoid constituents as potential anti-inflammatory drug leads. Standardized extracts are under investigation for cosmetic and nutraceutical applications.
