## Overview

**Adromischus maculatus** (Salm-Dyck) Lem. is a small succulent in the family Crassulaceae, native to the Western and Eastern Cape provinces of South Africa. The specific epithet "maculatus" refers to the distinctive spotted markings on its leaves.

It is one of the more easily grown species in the genus, making it a good entry point for collectors of South African succulents. The plant forms attractive rosettes of flattened, spotted leaves that blush purple-red under stress.

The species has a long history in cultivation, having been grown in European collections since the early 19th century. It was originally described as *Cotyledon maculata* by Salm-Dyck before Lemaire transferred it to *Adromischus*.

Plants are compact and well-suited to container culture. They produce small, tubular flowers on tall, slender inflorescences in summer.

The species has received the Royal Horticultural Society"s Award of Garden Merit in the United Kingdom. This recognition reflects its reliability and ornamental value in cultivation.

In its native South Africa, the plant is sometimes called "splaaiplakkie" (spreading flat-leaf) in Afrikaans, referring to the flattened, spreading leaf arrangement.

The species readily hybridizes with *Adromischus cristatus* in cultivation, producing intermediate forms with partially crinkled margins and spotted leaves. These hybrids are often sold unnamed in the horticultural trade.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Family: Crassulaceae
- Genus: *Adromischus*
- Species: *A. maculatus* (Salm-Dyck) Lem.

The species is closely related to *Adromischus cristatus* and was once considered a variety of that species. It is distinguished by its spotted, flat leaves and the absence of the crinkled margins seen in *A. cristatus*.

Plants are dwarf succulents forming rosettes 5–10 cm in diameter. The stems are short, producing aerial roots along their length.

Leaves are thick, fleshy, flattened, and broadly ovate to almost circular, 20–50 mm long. They are pale grey-green to blue-green, prominently marked with dark red-brown spots or blotches.

The spots vary considerably between individuals and populations. Some plants are heavily marked while others show only faint speckling.

Flowers are tubular, pale greenish-white with pinkish lobes, approximately 10 mm long. They are borne on erect, branched inflorescences up to 30 cm tall from December to February.

## Distribution and Habitat

Adromischus maculatus is found in the Western and Eastern Cape provinces of South Africa, from the Little Karoo eastward to Graaff-Reinet. Its range extends across several vegetation types.

It grows on rocky outcrops, in crevices of sandstone and quartzite, and on stony slopes. The species favors partially shaded positions, often growing under the protection of larger shrubs.

Rainfall in its habitat ranges from 200–400 mm annually, occurring predominantly in summer in the eastern part of its range and in winter in the west. This bimodal pattern makes the species adaptable to different watering regimes.

Elevations range from 400–1,200 m. Plants endure summer temperatures regularly exceeding 35°C and light winter frost in inland locations.

The species grows in association with other succulents including *Crassula*, *Haworthia*, and *Gasteria* species in the transition zone between succulent Karoo and fynbos vegetation.

Unlike many Adromischus species that are confined to winter-rainfall areas, *A. maculatus* tolerates both winter and summer rainfall. This adaptability contributes to its wider distribution and easier cultivation.

Plants growing on south-facing slopes tend to have broader, flatter leaves with more prominent spotting. Those on exposed, north-facing sites develop narrower leaves with less conspicuous markings.

## Cultivation

Adromischus maculatus is one of the easiest species in the genus to grow, tolerating a wider range of conditions than most of its relatives. A standard succulent mix with added grit provides adequate drainage.

Watering should be regular during the growing season but restrained during the summer rest period. The species tolerates brief periods of overwatering better than most Adromischus.

It prefers bright, indirect light to partial shade. Plants grown in strong light develop more pronounced spotting and reddish leaf margins, while shade-grown plants are greener with less distinct markings.

Propagation by leaf cuttings is simple and reliable. Leaves should be removed cleanly, allowed to callus for 2–3 days, then placed on moist sandy medium.

Seed propagation is straightforward, with germination typically occurring within 7–14 days at moderate temperatures. Seedlings grow relatively quickly and may reach flowering size in 2 years.

Plants benefit from a resting period in summer when watering is reduced. This mimics the natural dormancy cycle and promotes better flowering the following season.

The species is relatively pest-resistant but may attract mealybugs in crowded conditions. Good air circulation and regular inspection prevent most pest problems.

Older plants may develop elongated, woody stems that can be rejuvenated by beheading and re-rooting the rosette. The remaining stem will often produce new offsets.

## Ecology and Conservation

Like other Crassulaceae, the species uses CAM photosynthesis to conserve water in its semi-arid habitat. The flattened leaf shape maximizes light capture while minimizing water loss.

The spotted leaf pattern may serve as camouflage against herbivores, breaking up the leaf outline against the dappled light of its rocky habitat. This patterning is more pronounced in plants from more exposed sites.

Pollination is carried out by small insects, primarily bees and flies. The flowers produce small quantities of nectar that attract these pollinators during the summer flowering period.

The species is not considered threatened, as it has a relatively wide distribution and occurs in several protected areas. Populations are generally stable where habitat is intact.

Urban expansion and agricultural development have caused local declines in some areas of the Western Cape. However, the species' ability to colonize rocky outcrops provides some refuge from habitat conversion.

## Related Species

*Adromischus cristatus* is the closest relative, sharing a similar growth habit but distinguished by its undulating leaf margins. The two species are sometimes confused in cultivation.

*Adromischus marianiae* has heavily textured, warty leaves and a much more restricted range in the Richtersveld. It is significantly more difficult to cultivate.

*Adromischus filicaulis* has slender, elongated leaves and a trailing growth habit. It occurs in the central and eastern Karoo.

*Adromischus subdistinctus* bears spotted leaves similar to *A. maculatus* but has a more upright habit and longer, narrower foliage. It is found in the western Little Karoo.

*Adromischus roaneanus* has compact rosettes of short, rounded leaves with dark markings. It is restricted to a small area near Laingsburg in the Western Cape.
