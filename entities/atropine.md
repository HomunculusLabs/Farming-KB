# Atropine

## Overview

Atropine is a tropane alkaloid and competitive antagonist of muscarinic
acetylcholine receptors.

It occurs naturally in plants of the Solanaceae family, particularly
*Atropa belladonna* (deadly nightshade), *Datura stramonium* (jimsonweed),
and *Hyoscyamus niger* (henbane).

As one of the oldest drugs in continuous medical use, atropine remains on
the WHO Essential Medicines List for its critical roles in ophthalmology,
cardiology, anesthesia, and emergency medicine.

Its name derives from *Atropos*, one of the three Fates in Greek mythology
who cuts the thread of life, reflecting its toxicity at high doses.

## Chemical Properties

- **Chemical class:** Tropane alkaloid (secondary amine)
- **Molecular formula:** C₁₇H₂₃NO₃
- **Molecular weight:** 289.37 g/mol
- **Structure:** Ester of tropine and tropic acid; racemic mixture
- **Appearance:** White crystalline powder, odorless
- **Solubility:** Freely soluble in water, alcohol, glycerol

Natural atropine is the racemate of optically active hyoscyamine;
the (-)-enantiomer (hyoscyamine) carries pharmacological activity,
while the (+)-enantiomer is comparatively inactive. Racemization
occurs during extraction.

## Pharmacology

Atropine is a **competitive antagonist** at all five muscarinic
acetylcholine receptor subtypes (M1–M5), binding reversibly to
prevent acetylcholine activation.

Key pharmacological effects:

- **Heart:** Low doses cause paradoxical bradycardia; therapeutic doses
  produce tachycardia via M2 SA node blockade.
- **Eye:** Mydriasis via M3 sphincter pupillae blockade; cycloplegia via
  ciliary muscle paralysis.
- **Respiratory:** Bronchodilation and reduced secretions via M3 blockade.
- **GI tract:** Decreased motility, reduced gastric acid secretion.
- **Glands:** Anhidrosis, reduced salivation and lacrimation.

The mnemonic "blind as a bat, mad as a hatter, red as a beet,
hot as a hare, dry as a bone" describes anticholinergic toxidrome.

## Medical Applications

**Ophthalmology:** Produces sustained mydriasis and cycloplegia (7–14 days),
used for uveitis, pediatric amblyopia, and funduscopy.

**Cardiology:** IV atropine (0.5–1 mg) is first-line for symptomatic
bradycardia and hemodynamically unstable AV block.

**Anesthesia:** Preoperative use reduces secretions and prevents reflex
bradycardia during intubation.

**Organophosphate poisoning:** Primary antidote for organophosphate and
carbamate insecticide poisoning. Doses far exceed normal therapeutic
ranges (2–10 mg, repeated as needed).

**GI spasm:** Antispasmodic for biliary and renal colic (declining use).

## Dose and Administration

- **Ophthalmic:** 1% solution, 1 drop per eye
- **Bradycardia (IV):** 0.5 mg, repeat q3-5 min to max 3 mg
- **Organophosphate:** 2–5 mg IV initially, doubling doses q5-10 min
- **Preoperative:** 0.4–0.6 mg IM/IV, 30–60 min before procedure

## Toxicity and Side Effects

**Common side effects:** Dry mouth, blurred vision, photophobia,
tachycardia, constipation, urinary retention.

**Anticholinergic toxidrome** (overdose): Hallucinations, delirium,
hyperthermia, tachycardia, mydriasis, dry flushed skin, urinary
retention. Severe cases may progress to seizures, coma, and
cardiovascular collapse.

Treatment is primarily supportive, with physostigmine as a
specific antidote for severe anticholinergic delirium. Children
are especially susceptible; belladonna berry ingestion is a
documented pediatric emergency.

## History

The pharmacological properties of *Atropa belladonna* were known to
the ancient Romans. The name "belladonna" ("beautiful lady") refers to
its cosmetic use by Italian women to dilate pupils.

Atropine was first isolated in pure form by the German pharmacist
Mein in 1831. It entered mainstream medical practice during the 19th
century and has remained in continuous use since.

## Biosynthesis

Atropine is synthesized in *Atropa*, *Datura*, and *Hyoscyamus*
species via the tropane alkaloid pathway.

The pathway begins with ornithine-derived putrescine condensing with
acetoacetyl-CoA to form N-methyl-Δ¹-pyrrolinium, which condenses with
acetoacetate to form tropinone, then reduced to tropine.

Tropine is esterified with phenylalanine-derived tropic acid to form
hyoscyamine, which racemizes to atropine.

## Related Compounds

- **Hyoscyamine** — the (-)-enantiomer; more potent than atropine
- **Scopolamine (hyoscine)** — related tropane alkaloid with additional
  CNS depressant effects
- **Ipratropium bromide** — synthetic quaternary derivative for
  inhalation, minimal systemic absorption
- **Tiotropium** — long-acting anticholinergic for COPD
- **Oxybutynin** — anticholinergic for overactive bladder
- **Homatropine** — synthetic analog, shorter duration
