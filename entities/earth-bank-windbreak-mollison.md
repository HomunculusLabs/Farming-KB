## Earth Bank Windbreak

## Description

The earth bank windbreak is a [[permaculture]] technique for flat and low-relief landscapes where natural windbreaks are absent. It involves grading up soil into a raised bank (typically 4-6 feet high) combined with a [[swale]] on the windward side, then planting the crest with fast-growing species. Mollison describes it as "the fastest way to run a windbreak" and a fundamental tool for creating livable microclimates on exposed flatlands.

## Classification

- **Type**: Landscape engineering technique — wind protection
- **Components**: Raised earth bank + swale + plantings
- **Height**: 4-6 feet (bank alone); taller with mature plantings
- **Applications**: Flatlands, plains, arid margins, fire-prone landscapes
- **Construction method**: Grading, earth-moving, backhoe work

## Key Facts

- The fastest method for establishing functional windbreaks in treeless landscapes.
- Provides two functions simultaneously: wind deflection and livestock shelter.
- Earth banks act as radiation shields from fire — a critical function in fire-prone landscapes.
- Banks reduce noise (particularly traffic noise) more effectively than vegetation alone — vegetation requires approximately 100 meters of depth for significant noise reduction.
- A well-insulated house behind an earth bank can be "quite quiet" even near noisy infrastructure.
- The swale on the windward side captures and infiltrates runoff, supporting the plantings on the bank crest.
- Fast-growing species recommended for the crest include pampas grass and bamboo for rapid establishment.
- On flatlands, the technique creates the slopes needed for gravity water systems where no natural elevation exists.
- Combined with a below-grade water tank or cave, earth banks create complete microclimate systems — the house stays low and sheltered while the barn and water tank sit elevated.

## Design and Construction

### Bank Construction
1. Grade earth from the site into a continuous raised bank 4-6 feet high
2. Create a swale on the windward side to capture water
3. Compact the bank to prevent erosion
4. Shape the crown to deflect wind upward

### Planting Strategy
- **Crest**: Fast-growing species — pampas grass, bamboo, tamarisk, [[casuarina]]
- **Windward face**: Hardy ground covers to stabilize soil
- **Lee side**: Garden and dwelling placement in the wind shadow
- **Integration**: Use the bank as the backbone of a multi-species hedgerow

### Water Integration
- Swale captures and infiltrates rainfall
- Overflow directed to ponds or dams
- Runoff from the bank surface itself adds to swale catchment
- In flatlands, the bank creates the elevation differential needed for gravity-fed water systems

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

The earth bank windbreak is one of the first interventions Mollison recommends for flatland sites:

1. **Immediate protection**: Unlike tree windbreaks that take years to mature, earth banks provide wind protection from day one.

2. **Living system foundation**: The bank creates the microclimate needed to establish plants that would otherwise fail on exposed sites. Once plants mature, the combined bank-vegetation system provides layered protection.

3. **Privacy and noise control**: In flat landscapes where "achieving privacy and insulation against noise can really be a problem," earth banks provide immediate visual and acoustic barriers.

4. **Fire defense**: Earth banks are classified as fire barriers because they contain no combustible material and deflect radiant heat upward.

5. **Water system integration**: Banks and swales together create the catch-and-store water infrastructure that makes flatland settlement viable.

## Case Studies (from Mollison)

### New England Flatland Site
- 300 acres with only 2 feet of elevation drop
- Single-story house surrounded by earth bank with tall barn and water tank
- Water diversion from roadway around pond and through the system
- Annual garden partially converted to water garden
- High fire frequency site protected by water and earth bank system

### Gully Site (Melbourne Area)
- Flat eroded gully with steep little banks
- Backhoe-dug cave under the house site
- House built over cave with glass-roofed underground greenhouse
- Cave provided cold storage, heat ducts, and a cool retreat
- Opened onto deck overlooking private lake, entered through the cave

### Peninsula Site
- Excavated dirt formed into peninsula 9 feet above grade
- Two-story house on high peninsula surrounded by water on three sides
- Earth banks deflected flood waters away from garden
- Banks accumulated silt and leaf mulch transferred to gardens
