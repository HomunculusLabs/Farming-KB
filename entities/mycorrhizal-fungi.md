## Description

Mycorrhizal fungi are a diverse group of fungi that form **mutually beneficial
(symbiotic) associations** with plant roots. The term "mycorrhiza" literally
means "fungus root." In these partnerships, the fungus colonizes the plant's
root system and extends its hyphal network far into the surrounding soil,
dramatically increasing the plant's access to water and nutrients (particularly
phosphorus, micronutrients, and calcium). In return, the plant supplies the
fungus with carbohydrates produced through photosynthesis. Mycorrhizal
associations are among the most important symbioses in terrestrial ecosystems —
an estimated 80–90% of all plant species form mycorrhizal relationships.

## Classification

Mycorrhizal fungi are not a single taxonomic group but a functional category
spanning several fungal lineages:

### Major Types

- **Arbuscular Mycorrhizal (AM / VAM)**: Formed by fungi in the phylum
  Glomeromycota. These penetrate root cortical cells forming arbuscules (tree-
  like structures for nutrient exchange) and vesicles (storage organs). The most
  common type, associated with most crops, vegetables, and annuals.
- **Ectomycorrhizal (ECM)**: Formed primarily by Basidiomycota and some
  Ascomycota. They form a sheath (mantle) around root tips and a Hartig net
  between root cells, but do not penetrate cells. Common in forest trees,
  especially conifers and oaks.
- **Ericoid Mycorrhizal**: Associated with plants in the Ericaceae (heath
  family) — blueberries, rhododendrons, cranberries. Adapted to acidic,
  nutrient-poor soils.

## Key Facts

### Nutrient Exchange and Plant Health

- Mycorrhizal hyphae extend 10–100x beyond the root zone, accessing water and
  nutrients that roots alone cannot reach.
- Critical for phosphorus uptake in most soils, especially those with low P
  availability.
- Facilitate calcium delivery to plants — without adequate mycorrhizal
  colonization, calcium may remain bound in fungal biomass or soil particles
  rather than reaching the plant.

### Soil Structure Building

- Mycorrhizal fungi produce **glomalin**, a glycoprotein that acts as a
  biological glue, binding soil particles into stable macroaggregates.
- Macroaggregate formation is critical for soil structure, water infiltration,
  aeration, and erosion resistance.
- Compost and compost tea provide the building blocks (including humic acids)
  that mycorrhizal fungi use to build these macroaggregates.

### Disease and Pest Suppression

- **Colonization threshold**: Data from Soil Foodweb Inc. shows that when VAM
  colonization falls below **40%**, root diseases, root-feeding nematodes, and
  other root pests become problematic.
- Colonization above **30–40%** is associated with significant reduction in root
  disease problems.
- Colonization below **10–15%** indicates the need for mycorrhizal inoculation.

### Relationship with Compost Tea

- Compost and compost tea serve as **food resources** for mycorrhizal fungi,
  providing nutrients when the host plant is not supplying sufficient
  carbohydrates.
- AACT maintains soil moisture, provides an evaporation barrier, and supplies
  humic acids (fungal foods) that support mycorrhizal colonization.
- Humic acids in the root zone accelerate colonization when inoculum is applied.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Practical Management

- **Inoculation**: When colonization is below 15%, apply mycorrhizal inoculum
  alongside compost, compost tea, or humic acids for best results.
- **Maintenance**: For colonization between 15–40%, regular compost tea
  applications and humic acid amendments sustain and improve colonization.
- **Monitoring**: Microscopic assessment of root colonization percentage is the
  most reliable diagnostic tool.

### Threats to Mycorrhizal Fungi

- **Actinobacteria/Actinomycetes**: Can suppress both VAM and ectomycorrhizal
  fungi. Ingham advises against adding actinobacteria to compost or tea.
- **Excessive tillage**: Physically severs hyphal networks.
- **High-soluble-phosphorus fertilizers**: Reduce plant's dependence on
  mycorrhizal partners, causing the symbiosis to weaken.
- **Fungicides**: Many broad-spectrum fungicides damage mycorrhizal
  associations.
- **Compaction and waterlogging**: Reduce oxygen availability needed by
  mycorrhizal fungi.

### Special Considerations

- Not all plants form mycorrhizal associations (e.g., brassicas, beets,
  spinach are typically non-mycorrhizal).
- The type of mycorrhiza must match the plant — ericoid for blueberries,
  ectomycorrhizal for pines, VAM for most vegetables.
- Some plants (orchids) have specialized mycorrhizal requirements involving
  specific fungal partners.
