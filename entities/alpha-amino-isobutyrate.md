## Description

alpha-Amino-isobutyrate (AIB) is a non-metabolized amino acid analogue widely used as a tracer compound in fungal nutrient transport studies. When labelled with 14C, it enables non-invasive tracking of amino acid translocation through intact mycelial networks using photon-counting scintillation imaging (PCSI).

## Classification

- **Type**: Chemical compound (amino acid analogue)
- **Chemical formula**: C4H9NO2
- **Key property**: Non-metabolized by fungi, taken up but not incorporated into proteins or further metabolized
- **Radiolabel**: 14C-AIB used for tracking

## Key Facts

- Non-metabolizable amino acid analogue that is taken up by fungal amino acid transporters but cannot be incorporated into proteins, making it an ideal tracer for studying transport without confounding metabolic effects
- Used in 14C-labelled form with photon-counting scintillation imaging (PCSI) to map real-time amino acid distribution through intact mycelial networks
- Primary model system: Phanerochaete velutina grown from wood-block or agar inocula in sand/soil microcosms
- AIB distribution characterized by three parameters: centre of mass displacement (CMDAIB), angular concentration (ConcDAIB), and alignment with new resources
- Pulsatile transport of AIB discovered superimposed on net translocation patterns, with oscillations visible in assimilatory hyphae, foraging hyphae, and cord systems
- Pulsatile component analyzed using Fourier techniques, revealing phase domains of locally synchronized behavior
- In larger microcosms, 14C-AIB travelled 250 mm along major cords within 1 hour of loading
- Demonstrated route-switching behavior: pre-existing cords could become transiently activated as AIB transport routes
- Added damp cellulosic resources induced marked AIB accumulation toward the new resource, with tightly focused asymmetric distribution
- AIB transport showed synchronous pulsation in regions along main cords between inoculum and new resource, with oscillations continuing for 5-7 days

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

AIB tracer studies have revealed the remarkable dynamics of nutrient transport in fungal networks, including pulsatile oscillations, route-switching, and directed resource allocation. These findings are directly relevant to understanding how cultivated fungi colonize substrates and prioritize nutrient distribution. The discovery that fungi rapidly redirect amino acid transport toward new resources (within hours) has implications for understanding substrate supplementation timing and placement in mushroom cultivation. The pulsatile nature of transport also suggests that fungal nutrient allocation is a more active and regulated process than previously appreciated.
