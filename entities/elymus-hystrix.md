## Taxonomy and Morphology

Elymus hystrix belongs to the tribe Triticeae within the subfamily Pooideae.

The species was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753, originally placed in the genus *Bromus* and later transferred to *Elymus*.

The specific epithet *hystrix* derives from the Latin word for hedgehog, referring to the bristly appearance of the inflorescence.

It is a hexaploid species with 2n = 6x = 42 chromosomes, reflecting its complex hybridogenic origin within the Triticeae.

The plant forms loose clumps of flat, wide leaf blades 20 to 50 centimeters long and 5 to 15 millimeters wide.

Leaves are light to medium green, hairless or with scattered hairs along the upper surface, and have a prominent midrib.

The sheaths are rounded and smooth, and the ligule is a short, membranous collar approximately 1 millimeter long.

Flowering occurs from May through July, producing erect culms 60 to 120 centimeters tall.

The inflorescence is a dense, cylindrical spike 5 to 15 centimeters long, with spikelets arranged in two rows along the rachis.

Each spikelet bears a single floret with a long, stout awn 15 to 35 millimeters in length.

The awns spread outward at maturity, creating the characteristic bottlebrush appearance.

Glumes are broad and keeled, and the lemma is firm and leathery with several prominent veins.

## Distribution and Habitat

Elymus hystrix is widely distributed in eastern North America.

Its range extends from southern Ontario and southwestern Quebec west to Minnesota and Iowa, and south through the [[ornamental-grasses-guide]] suited to shaded conditions beneath deciduous trees.

The distinctive bottlebrush inflorescences provide textural interest from late spring through midsummer and can be used in dried arrangements.

The species combines effectively with other shade-tolerant natives including *Maianthemum racemosum*, *[[polygonatum-biflorum]]*, *Trillium* species, and woodland ferns.

Propagation is primarily by seed, which should be stratified for 60 days or sown directly in fall.

Seeds germinate readily under cool, moist conditions in spring.

Germination rates are typically high, and seedlings establish quickly when competition is minimal.
