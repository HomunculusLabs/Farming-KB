# Chamomilla recutita

Chamomilla recutita (syn. [[matricaria-chamomilla]]), commonly known as German chamomile, is an annual herbaceous plant in the family Asteraceae. Native to Europe and Western Asia, it has been cultivated for centuries as a medicinal herb and is one of the most widely used botanical medicines in the world. The dried flower heads contain a complex mixture of volatile oils, flavonoids, and sesquiterpene lactones that confer anti-inflammatory, spasmolytic, and mild sedative properties. Chamomile preparations — primarily teas, extracts, and essential oils — are employed in traditional medicine systems across Europe, Asia, and the Americas for gastrointestinal complaints, anxiety, wound healing, and topical inflammation.

## Taxonomy and Naming

- **Order:** Asterales
- **Family:** Asteraceae
- **Tribe:** Anthemideae
- **Genus:** Chamomilla
- **Species:** C. recutita (L.) Rauschert
- **Synonyms:** Matricaria chamomilla L., Matricaria recutita L.
- **Common names:** German chamomile, wild chamomile, scented mayweed
- **Nomenclature note:** The taxonomic history is confused by the synonym Matricaria chamomilla, which remains widely used in pharmacopoeias and herbal monographs. The genus was revised to Chamomilla by Rauschert in 1974, though many references retain the older Matricaria placement.

## Description

### Growth Habit
German chamomile is an erect, branching annual herb reaching 15–60 cm in height. The stems are glabrous to sparsely pubescent, often with a characteristic apple-like fragrance when crushed. The root system is fibrous and relatively shallow, making the plant adaptable to a range of soil types but susceptible to drought stress. Plants produce multiple branching stems from the base, forming a bushy, open canopy.

### Leaves and Stems
Leaves are alternate, 2–5 cm long, bipinnately or tripinnately dissected into narrow filiform segments 0.5–1.5 mm wide. The green segments give the foliage a feathery appearance. Stem leaves are sessile; upper leaves become progressively smaller and less divided.

### Flowers
The inflorescence is a solitary terminal flower head (capitulum) approximately 10–25 mm in diameter. The involucre consists of multiple rows of bracts with scarious margins. Ray florets are white, 6–12 mm long, pistillate, and reflexed at maturity. Disk florets are tubular, yellow, and hermaphroditic. Flower heads are conical and hollow at the center, a key diagnostic feature distinguishing Chamomilla from similar genera. Blooming occurs from late spring through early autumn. Individual flowers open in sequence, allowing for extended harvest periods from the same plant stand.

### Fruit
Achenes are obovoid, approximately 1–1.5 mm long, pale brown, with a slightly ribbed surface and no pappus (or a rudimentary coroniform pappus).

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Native range:** Europe, Western Asia (from the Iberian Peninsula eastward through the Balkans, Turkey, and into Central Asia)
- **Introduced/naturalized:** North America, South America, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, and much of temperate Asia
- **Habitat preferences:** Fields, roadsides, disturbed ground, cultivated land, and waste areas; prefers well-drained sandy or loamy soils
- **Altitude range:** Sea level to approximately 1,500 m
- **Climate:** Temperate; hardy to USDA zones 3–9; self-seeds readily in favorable conditions, often persisting as a casual weed in disturbed ground

## Chemistry and Active Compounds

The pharmacological activity of German chamomile derives from several compound classes:

- **Essential oil (0.3–1.5% dry weight):** Alpha-bisabolol (levomenol, the primary anti-inflammatory constituent), alpha-bisabolol oxides A and B, chamazulene (blue-colored sesquiterpene formed during steam distillation from matricin), farnesene, spiroether
- **Flavonoids:** [[luteolin]], luteolin-7-glu  uercetin, patuletin, and various acetylated and methoxylated derivatives. Total flavonoid content ranges from 0.25–1.0% in dried flower heads.
- **Coumarins:** Umbelliferone, herniarin (7-methoxycoumarin)
- **Sesquiterpene lactones:** Matricin (precursor to chamazulene), which possesses significant anti-inflammatory activity
- **Mucilage and polysaccharides:** Contribute to the soothing gastrointestinal effects of chamomile tea; water-soluble polysaccharide fraction constitutes 5–10% of flower dry weight
- **Phenolic acids:** Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, ferulic acid
- **Volatile oil variation:** Chemotypes exist with different bisabolol oxide ratios; the bisabolol oxide A-rich chemotype is most common in commercial cultivation

## Pharmacological Properties

1. **Anti-inflammatory:** Apigenin and alpha-bisabolol inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) and lipoxygenase (LOX) pathways, reducing prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis. Topical chamomile creams reduce eczema symptoms in controlled trials.
2. **Spasmolytic:** Flavonoid fractions relax smooth muscle in the gastrointestinal tract, supporting traditional use for colic and dyspepsia. In vitro studies demonstrate dose-dependent inhibition of acetylcholine-induced contractions in ileal tissue.
3. **Anxiolytic and mild sedative:** Apigenin binds to central benzodiazepine receptors; clinical trials show modest anxiolytic effects compared to placebo. A double-blind study found chamomile extract significantly reduced generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) scores over 8 weeks.
