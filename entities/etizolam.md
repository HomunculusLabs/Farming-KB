# Etizolam

## Overview

Etizolam is a thienodiazepine compound structurally related to classical benzodiazepines, with the benzene ring replaced by a thiophene ring. It was developed in the 1980s by a Japanese pharmaceutical company and has been marketed as an anxiolytic, sedative, and muscle relaxant in several countries.

Etizolam has gained significant attention as a novel psychoactive substance (NPS) in recreational drug markets, where it is sold as a "research chemical" or "designer benzodiazepine."

## Chemical Properties

- **IUPAC Name:** 4-(2-chlorophenyl)-2-ethyl-9-methyl-6H-thieno[3,2-f][1,2,4]triazolo[4,3-a][1,4]diazepine
- **Molecular Formula:** C₁₇H₁₅ClN₄S
- **Molecular Weight:** 342.85 g/mol
- **CAS Number:** 40054-69-1
- **Appearance:** White to off-white crystalline powder

The thiophene ring substitution (replacing benzene with a 5-membered sulfur-containing heterocycle) confers different metabolic and pharmacokinetic properties compared to classical benzodiazepines.

Etizolam is moderately lipophilic (logP ~2.2), facilitating blood-brain barrier penetration.

## Pharmacology

Etizolam acts as a positive allosteric modulator at the GABAₐ receptor, binding to the benzodiazepine site on the receptor complex.

This enhances the inhibitory effect of GABA on neuronal excitability, producing anxiolytic, sedative-hypnotic, muscle relaxant, and anticonvulsant effects.

Relative to diazepam, etizolam is approximately 6–10× more potent by weight.

It displays full agonist activity at the benzodiazepine site with comparable efficacy to classical benzodiazepines.

## Pharmacokinetics

- **Onset of action:** 30–60 minutes (oral)
- **Peak plasma concentration:** 1–2 hours
- **Half-life:** 3–6 hours (parent compound)
- **Active metabolites:** None clinically significant (α-hydroxyetizolam is minor)
- **Duration of effects:** 4–6 hours

The short half-life and lack of active metabolites distinguish etizolam from many longer-acting benzodiazepines.

Metabolism occurs primarily via hepatic CYP3A4-mediated hydroxylation and glucuronidation.

## Dose Ranges

- **Anxiolytic (therapeutic):** 0.25–1 mg
- **Hypnotic (therapeutic):** 0.5–2 mg
- **Recreational threshold:** 0.5–1 mg
- **Common recreational dose:** 1–2 mg
- **Heavy recreational dose:** 2–4 mg

⚠️ Tolerance develops rapidly with daily use. Doses above 4 mg significantly increase risk of blackout and respiratory depression.

## Effects Profile

**Desirable effects:** Anxiolysis, euphoria (mild), sedation, muscle relaxation, disinhibition, sleep induction.

**[[blesching-cannabis-safety-adverse-effects-evidence]]:** Drowsiness, cognitive impairment, memory loss (anterograde amnesia), ataxia, slurred speech, emotional blunting.

**Hangover effects:** Residual sedation ("benzo hangover"), brain fog, and rebound anxiety are common the day after use.

## Dependence and Withdrawal

Etizolam carries a high risk of physical and psychological dependence, comparable to classical benzodiazepines.

Withdrawal symptoms include anxiety, insomnia, tremors, sweating, nausea, perceptual disturbances, and in severe cases, seizures and delirium.

Withdrawal onset typically begins within 12–24 hours of the last dose, peaking at 2–4 days due to the short half-life.

Gradual tapering over 4–12 weeks is recommended for discontinuation after prolonged use.

## Legal Status

Etizolam is a prescription medication in Japan, India, and Italy, where it is marketed under [[emcdda-brand-names-user-terms-smartshop-marketing]] including Depas, Etizest, and Sedekopan.

It is unscheduled or uncontrolled in many countries, contributing to its availability as a research chemical.

The UK classified etizolam as a Class C drug under the Psychoactive [[controlled-substances-act-and-psychedelic-research]] (2017).

In the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], etizolam is not federally scheduled but is controlled in several states (e.g., Virginia, Georgia, Alabama).

## Toxicology

Fatalities involving etizolam are increasingly reported, typically in combination with opioids or alcohol.

Postmortem blood concentrations in fatal cases range from 0.01 to 4.0 mg/L.

The primary mechanism of death in overdose is respiratory depression, potentiated by CNS depressant polydrug use.
