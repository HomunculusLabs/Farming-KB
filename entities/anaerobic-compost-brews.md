## Anaerobic Compost Brews

## Description

Anaerobic compost brews are liquid preparations made by steeping compost or organic materials in water without adequate aeration. Unlike [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] (AACT), anaerobic brews develop in low-oxygen conditions that promote the growth of fermentative and putrefactive microorganisms. While these brews can show inhibition of some disease organisms in lab plate cultures, Dr. Elaine Ingham strongly discourages their use because they produce toxic compounds (alcohols, organic acids, phenols) that are harmful to plants, humans, animals, and beneficial soil organisms.

## Classification

- **Type**: Agricultural preparation / technique
- **Category**: Compost-derived liquid amendment
- **Oxygen status**: Anaerobic (no forced aeration)
- **Contrasted with**: [[actively-aerated-compost-tea]] Tea (AACT)
- **Risk level**: High (produces phytotoxic and zootoxic compounds)

## Key Facts

- Anaerobic brews ADD toxins to the soil, including alcohols, short-chain organic acids, and putrefactive compounds that are harmful to plants and soil life.
- While anaerobic brews can show disease inhibition on plate cultures in the laboratory, there is no evidence that these effects operate in the real world of soil ecosystems.
- The toxins produced in [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]] are comparable to pesticides and antibiotics in their potential harm: their concentrations must be carefully controlled and monitored.
- Careful studies of the specific toxins being produced in anaerobic brews have not been performed, meaning users are applying unknown toxic compounds to their soil.
- Dr. Ingham explicitly states: "Use of anaerobic brews should be discouraged."
- [[lactic-acid-bacteria]] used in anaerobic fermentations are "not really conducive to growth of fungi," limiting the fungal component that is essential for soil health.
- Fermentative bacteria used to START compost may prevent putrefactive loss of nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus through volatilization, but once the hot phase is over, promoting the right fungi becomes critical.
- Indigenous fungal species and indigenous fermentative species are important for locally adapted compost. Buying commercial inocula like EM ([[bokashi-composting-and-effective-microorganisms]]) may help start the process, but developing your own locally adapted inoculum over time is preferred.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Avoid anaerobic conditions**: Any compost tea system must maintain adequate dissolved oxygen (above 5-6 mg/L) throughout the brewing process to avoid anaerobic toxin production.
- **Toxin recognition**: Brew that smells putrid, sulfurous, or like ammonia indicates anaerobic conditions have developed and the tea should be discarded, not applied.
- **Fungal suppression**: Anaerobic conditions kill or suppress beneficial fungi, which are the most important organisms for building soil structure and suppressing disease in most soils.
- **Compost starter strategy**: Fermentative bacteria can be used to initiate composting and prevent nitrogen loss, but the process must transition to aerobic, fungal-dominant conditions for quality compost.
- **Local adaptation**: The best inocula come from local healthy soils and compost, containing species already adapted to local conditions. Commercial products like EM are starting points, not long-term solutions.
