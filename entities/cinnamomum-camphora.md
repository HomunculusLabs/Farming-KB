# Cinnamomum camphora

## Overview

Cinnamomum camphora is a large evergreen tree in the family Lauraceae, native to [[neurotropic-fungi-japan-east-asia-species-cultural-context]] (China, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam). It is the primary natural source of camphor, a bicyclic monoterpene ketone with centuries of use in medicine, perfumery, and industry.

Reaching heights of 20–30 m with a broad, spreading crown, the camphor tree is one of the most culturally significant aromatic trees in East Asian civilization. It has been planted as a temple and street tree across subtropical regions worldwide.

The species has become an aggressive invasive in parts of Australia, the southeastern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], and some Pacific islands, where it displaces native vegetation through allelopathic [[dighton-fungal-decomposition-leaf-litter-dynamics]] and dense shading.

The genus *Cinnamomum* contains approximately 250 species of aromatic evergreen trees and shrubs, including true cinnamon (*C. verum*) and cassia (*C. cassia*).

## Morphology

The trunk is straight and massive, reaching 1–2 m in diameter on mature specimens. Bark is rough, fissured, and dark gray-brown, becoming thick and corky with age.

Leaves are alternate, simple, 5–10 cm long and 2–5 cm wide, with three prominent yellowish veins arising from the base. They are leathery, glossy green above and paler below, releasing a strong camphor scent when crushed.

Flowers are small, yellowish-white, arranged in axillary panicles 4–8 cm long. They appear in spring (April–May) and have a pleasant fragrance.

Fruit is a small, globose drupe, 7–10 mm in diameter, green ripening to black or dark purple. Each fruit contains a [[cervantes-selfing-single-seed-descent]]. The fleshy pericarp is attractive to birds, which serve as primary dispersal agents.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native range spans southeastern China (Fujian, Jiangxi, Hunan provinces), southern Japan (Kyushu, Shikoku, Ryukyu Islands), Taiwan, and northern Vietnam.

It grows in warm-temperate to subtropical broadleaf forests at elevations from sea level to 2000 m. The species prefers well-drained, acidic to neutral soils with good moisture retention.

Outside its native range, C. camphora is widely naturalized in eastern Australia (declared a noxious weed in NSW and Queensland), the southeastern United States (Florida, Georgia, Alabama), California, southern Europe, and parts of South America.

## Chemical Composition

Camphor content varies significantly among chemotypes. Five main chemotypes have been identified based on dominant leaf oil constituents: camphor, linalool, cineole, nerolidol, and borneol.

The camphor chemotype (the most commercially important) contains 60–80% (+)-camphor in its essential oil. Linalool chemotypes yield oil with 70–90% linalool, valued in perfumery and aromatherapy.

Other constituents include safrole (in some chemotypes), eucalyptol (1,8-cineole), α-terpineol, α-pinene, limonene, and various sesquiterpenes. Bark oil differs from leaf oil in composition, generally containing higher camphor concentrations.

## Pharmacology and Traditional Medicine

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), camphor wood and oil are classified as acrid, warm, and toxic. They are used externally to relieve pain, kill parasites, and reduce swelling.

Camphor exerts its pharmacological effects primarily through TRPV1 and TRPA1 receptor modulation, producing a cooling/warming sensation followed by local analgesia. It also demonstrates antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and some fungi.

Linalool-rich chemotypes show anxiolytic, sedative, and anti-inflammatory properties in preclinical studies. Linalool modulates GABAergic neurotransmission and suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6).

**Safety note:** Camphor is neurotoxic in high doses. Oral ingestion of as little as 2 g can cause seizures in children. Topical use should avoid application to broken skin or near the faces of infants.

## Cultivation

C. camphora is hardy to approximately -10°C when mature, though young trees are more frost-sensitive. It grows best in full sun to partial shade with consistent moisture.

Propagation is primarily by seed, which requires cold stratification for 2–3 months. Semi-hardwood cuttings under mist also succeed but with lower strike rates than seed.

Growth rate is moderate to fast: 30–60 cm per year in favorable conditions. Trees begin flowering at 8–10 years of age and can live for several centuries.

## Ecological Impact as Invasive Species

C. camphora is classified as a Category 1 invasive weed in parts of Australia and a severe invasion threat in the southeastern United States. It forms dense monocultures that suppress native understory regeneration.

Allelopathic compounds in leaf litter (primarily camphor and other volatile terpenoids) inhibit germination and growth of competing plant species. This [[solomon-root-exudates-and-allelopathy]], combined with dense shading, allows camphor to dominate forest canopies rapidly.

Birds disperse seeds extensively, facilitating spread into intact native ecosystems. In Florida, camphor has invaded hardwood hammocks, cypress swamps, and coastal strand communities.

Control methods include mechanical removal of saplings, basal bark herbicide application, and prescribed burning where fire-adapted native communities are present.

## Timber and Economic Uses

Camphor wood is highly valued for fine woodworking and cabinetry in East Asia. Heartwood is light yellowish-brown to reddish-brown, moderately hard, with a straight to interlocked grain and distinctive camphor aroma.

The wood resists insect attack due to its natural camphor content, making it historically important for storage chests, shipbuilding, and furniture in Japan and China. It is also used for carving religious statuary and traditional musical instruments.

Camphor oil extraction was a major industry in Taiwan and Japan during the 19th and early 20th centuries, before synthetic camphor production displaced natural sources. At peak production, Taiwan exported over 10,000 tonnes of crude camphor annually.
