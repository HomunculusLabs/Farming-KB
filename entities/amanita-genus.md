## Description

[[amanita]] is a genus of mushrooms containing some of the most toxic fungi known to science. [[julius-auboineau-palmer]] identified the Amanita family as responsible for all or nearly all fatal cases of mushroom poisoning, based on his observations and reports of poisoning cases in the Boston area during the 1870s-1880s.

Palmer found ten varieties of Amanita in the vicinity of Boston alone. The genus includes some of the most recognizable and dangerous mushrooms in the world, including the death cap ([[amanita-phalloides]]) and the destroying angel ([[amanita-virosa]]).

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Amanitaceae
- **Genus:** Amanita

## Key Identification Features (from Palmer"s Description)

Palmer provided a detailed popular description of Amanita identification markers:

### The Three Marks

1. **Volva (basal wrapper):** A white membranous cup around the base of the stem, close to the soil. The mushroom appears to grow from within this wrapper.

2. **Annulus (stem ring):** A veil-like or ragged ring-shaped membrane encircling the stem approximately two-thirds of the way up from the ground.

3. **Cap warts:** An irregular collection of scurfy warts on the cap surface. These are quite defined in young plants but apt to be worn off in older specimens.

Palmer emphasized that all three marks must exist together to denote the sub-genus Amanita.

### Additional Features

- **Gills and stem:** Always pure white
- **Cap colors:** Variable — white, drab, golden, red, or greenish, soft and sticky when moist
- **Most common form:** Yellow spot in the center, shading gradually to pure white
- **Overall appearance:** Often very beautiful, making them particularly dangerous

## Toxicity

Palmer described the Amanita poison in vivid terms:

- Contains a subtle alkaloid not detectable by smell or taste
- Fatal consequences "surer than arsenic"
- No folk test (silver spoon, onion, bulrush pith) can detect the toxin
- The poison"s effect resembles hashish or opium in its action

### Historical Treatment

The only known treatment Palmer mentioned was hypodermic injection of atropia (atropine) or other alkaloids from the nightshade family (Solanaceae). He described this as "an antidotal measure yet in its infancy" and noted that physicians were otherwise "absolutely powerless to save life, however prompt and energetic the treatment."

## Look-Alikes: The Russula Problem

Palmer described a particularly dangerous confusion between Amanitas and Russulas:

- Both families have pure white gills and stems
- Members of both can be found in corresponding shades from scarlet to pure white
- They grow side by side in the same habitats
- Russulas are crisp, brittle mushrooms that grow in or near woodland
- Amanitas are nearly tasteless yet noxious, while non-acrid Russulas are safely eaten
- The visual similarity is so great that Palmer worried constantly about his young companion accidentally tasting an Amanita while sampling Russulas

## Ecological Notes

Palmer observed that Amanitas grow in the same locations year after year. The underground [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]] persist and produce fruiting bodies in predictable patterns, much like perennial plants. This regularity means that once a location is identified as harboring dangerous Amanitas, foragers can be forewarned in future seasons.

## Relevance to Mycology and Foraging Safety

1. **Most important genus for safety:** Amanita identification is the single most critical skill for any mushroom forager
2. **Folk test failure:** Amanitas pass every traditional folk safety test, making species-level knowledge essential
3. **Beautiful but deadly:** Their attractive appearance contradicts the folk belief that dangerous mushrooms look dangerous
4. **Look-alike confusion:** The similarity with edible Russulas represents one of the most dangerous identification challenges
5. **Treatment history:** The 19th-century struggle with Amanita poisoning parallels modern challenges in treating amatoxin poisoning

## Historical Context

Palmer's detailed description of Amanita was motivated by multiple poisoning deaths in the Boston area and New Jersey. He cited cases where entire families were poisoned, with fatalities including young children. His writings served as one of the earliest American popular guides to recognizing this deadly genus.
