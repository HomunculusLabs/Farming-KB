## Methcathinone

## Overview

Methcathinone (2-(methylamino)-1-phenylpropan-1-one) is a synthetic stimulant belonging to the cathinone class. It is the beta-ketone analogue of methamphetamine, sharing the same carbon skeleton with a carbonyl group at the beta position. The compound was first synthesized in 1928 but remained largely obscure until it emerged as a recreational drug in the former Soviet Union during the 1980s and 1990s.

In the Soviet Union and later Russia, methcathinone became widely known by the street name "ephedrone." It was prepared illicitly from readily available ephedrine or pseudoephedrine through a simple oxidation process using potassium permanganate. The drug gained notoriety for its high abuse potential and the severe neurological damage observed in long-term users, particularly a debilitating Parkinsonian syndrome. This neurotoxic profile distinguished it from other stimulants of the era and made it a subject of particular concern among public health officials.

Epidemiological data from the former Soviet Union suggest that methcathinone use peaked in the late 1980s and early 1990s before declining in the face of legal restrictions. However, sporadic outbreaks of use have continued to be documented, and the compound's simple synthesis ensures it remains a recurring concern.

The drug was encountered relatively rarely in Western markets compared to other cathinones, but its pharmacological profile established the template for understanding the entire cathinone class. Its effects and risks closely mirror those of methamphetamine, making it a textbook example of beta-ketone stimulant pharmacology.

Methcathinone is commonly administered intranasally or orally, though intravenous use has been documented particularly in Russian harm-reduction literature. The hydrochloride salt appears as a white crystalline powder with high water solubility, facilitating both nasal insufflation and injection. The freebase form is an oily liquid at room temperature and is seldom encountered outside of laboratory settings.

The compound exists as a chiral molecule, with the (S)-enantiomer being the more pharmacologically active form. Illicit production typically yields a racemic mixture. Resolution of enantiomers is not commonly performed in clandestine settings, though the (S)-enantiomer is considered responsible for the majority of central stimulant effects based on analogy with methamphetamine stereochemistry.

The hydrochloride salt of methcathinone is a white crystalline solid that is highly soluble in water and ethanol. The freebase is an oily, colorless to pale yellow liquid. The compound is sensitive to oxidation and should be stored under inert atmosphere to prevent degradation, though illicit samples are typically handled without such precautions.

Methcathinone has a pKa of approximately 8.9 for the conjugate acid of the amine group, making it predominantly protonated at physiological pH. The partition coefficient (log P) of the freebase is estimated at approximately 2.0, indicating moderate lipophilicity that supports crossing of the blood-brain barrier. The ketone group imparts slightly greater polarity compared to methamphetamine, marginally affecting its pharmacokinetic properties.

Analytical identification of methcathinone in forensic samples is typically accomplished using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). The mass spectrum shows characteristic fragment ions at m/z 58 (base peak, corresponding to the iminium ion), 72, 117, and 132. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) offers improved sensitivity for biological matrix analysis including blood, urine, and oral fluid. The compound is detectable in urine for approximately 2 to 4 days following use.

Synthesis from ephedrine or pseudoephedrine involves oxidation of the corresponding beta-hydroxy group using potassium permanganate, chromium trioxide, or sodium dichromate in acidic medium. This is a straightforward one-step reaction that contributed to the drug's popularity in clandestine settings. The reaction byproducts, particularly manganese dioxide from the permanganate route, have been implicated in some of the neurological toxicity observed in Russian users.

Alternative synthetic routes have been described in the patent and forensic literature, including the bromination of propiophenone followed by amination. However, the ephedrine oxidation route remains the most common in clandestine production due to the ready availability of starting materials and the simplicity of the procedure. Yields from the oxidation route typically range from 50 to 70 percent depending on conditions and the skill of the operator.

## Pharmacology

Methcathinone acts primarily as a releasing agent and reuptake inhibitor at monoamine transporters, with the strongest effects observed at the dopamine and norepinephrine transporters (DAT and NET). It functions as a substrate for the vesicular monoamine transporter (VMAT2), disrupting normal vesicular storage and promoting cytosolic accumulation of monoamines.

The compound promotes dopamine release through reversal of the dopamine transporter, similar to the mechanism of action of methamphetamine. Norepinephrine release is comparably robust, contributing to peripheral sympathomimetic effects including tachycardia, hypertension, and hyperthermia. Serotonin release is minimal, which distinguishes methcathinone from entactogenic cathinones such as methylone.

The beta-ketone group reduces lipophilicity relative to methamphetamine, resulting in slightly reduced central nervous system penetration. However, this structural difference does not significantly diminish the compound's overall potency as a CNS stimulant. The ketone moiety may confer greater susceptibility to metabolic degradation, contributing to a somewhat shorter duration of action compared to methamphetamine.

Its pharmacological activity is overwhelmingly mediated through transporter-mediated monoamine release rather than direct receptor interactions. This transporter selectivity profile categorizes it as a classical stimulant with minimal hallucinogenic or entactogenic properties.

The (S)-enantiomer of methcathinone is approximately 3-4 times more potent than the (R)-enantiomer at dopamine and norepinephrine transporters, consistent with the stereoselectivity observed across the amphetamine and cathinone classes. This enantiomeric preference has implications for both the subjective effects and the toxicity profile of the racemic mixture produced in clandestine synthesis.

Animal studies using rodent models have confirmed that methcathinone produces robust locomotor stimulation and conditioned place preference, consistent with significant abuse liability. Microdialysis experiments in rats have demonstrated dose-dependent increases in extracellular dopamine and norepinephrine in the nucleus accumbens and striatum. These neurochemical changes parallel the behavioral profile observed in human users and support the classification of methcathinone as a high-abuse-potential stimulant.

## Effects

The onset of effects following intranasal administration occurs within 5 to 15 minutes, while oral ingestion produces effects in 20 to 45 minutes. The duration of action ranges from 2 to 6 hours depending on the [[adverse-effects-and-harm-reduction-psilocybin]].

Desired effects include euphoria, increased alertness, enhanced sociability, elevated mood, and increased energy. Users typically report stimulation comparable to methamphetamine but with a somewhat shorter duration. Cognitive effects include improved focus and concentration at low to moderate doses, though higher doses produce scattered thinking and impaired judgment.

Physical effects include dilated pupils, dry mouth, increased heart rate, elevated blood pressure, increased body temperature, and appetite suppression. Bruxism (teeth grinding) and jaw tension are commonly reported. Peripheral vasoconstriction may lead to cold extremities. Sweating and restlessness are typical during the peak effects.

Sexual stimulation is commonly reported with methcathinone use, consistent with its dopaminergic and noradrenergic pharmacology. However, this effect diminishes with chronic use as tolerance develops. Some users report difficulty achieving orgasm despite increased libido, a paradoxical effect also observed with other potent stimulants including methamphetamine.

The typical recreational dose ranges from 50 to 150 mg when taken intranasally, and 100 to 250 mg when taken orally. Intravenous doses are substantially lower, ranging from 15 to 50 mg. The margin between a recreational dose and an overdose is narrower than with many other stimulants, contributing to its reputation as a particularly risky substance.

Side effects at common recreational doses include anxiety, insomnia, tremors, and headaches. At higher doses or with chronic use, users may experience paranoia, visual and auditory hallucinations, delusions, and full-blown stimulant psychosis resembling paranoid schizophrenia. The comedown is characterized by fatigue, depression, anhedonia, and intense cravings that reinforce compulsive redosing patterns.

Intravenous methcathinone use has been associated with additional complications including injection-site infections, thrombophlebitis, and the transmission of blood-borne pathogens through needle sharing. Track marks and soft-tissue infections are common among chronic intravenous users. The most significant acute risks include cardiovascular events (tachyarrhythmias, hypertensive crisis, myocardial infarction), hyperthermia, and serotonin syndrome when combined with serotonergic substances. Seizures have been reported at high doses, particularly following intravenous administration.

Chronic methcathinone use has been associated with a distinctive neurological syndrome documented extensively in Russian medical literature. This syndrome presents as a Parkinsonian disorder with bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, and postural instability. Post-mortem studies of individuals with methcathinone-related Parkinsonism have revealed severe loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, similar to the pathology seen in idiopathic Parkinson's disease. However, the age of onset is significantly younger, and the progression of symptoms may differ from the classical disease. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy studies have shown elevated manganese levels in the basal ganglia of some affected individuals, lending support to the contamination hypothesis.

Hepatotoxicity has been observed in chronic users, likely related to both the direct effects of the drug and exposure to toxic synthesis byproducts. Renal impairment can occur secondary to rhabdomyolysis triggered by hyperthermia and excessive muscular activity. Dental problems similar to "meth mouth" have been reported, driven by bruxism, dry mouth, and poor nutrition during periods of heavy use.

**Legal status:** Methcathinone is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance in the United States under the Controlled Substances Act. It is listed in Schedule I of the United Nations Convention on Psychotropic Substances (1971). Many countries have scheduled methcathinone explicitly, and in jurisdictions where it is not individually listed, it is frequently captured under analog or generic cathinone legislation. Russia criminalized the drug in the 1990s following the epidemic of neurological damage among users.
