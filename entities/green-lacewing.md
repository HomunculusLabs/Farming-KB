## Description

Green lacewings (family Chrysopidae) are delicate, bright green insects with large, translucent, net-veined wings. They are among the most important beneficial insects in the garden ecosystem and a key predator in [[the-soil-food-web]] gardening approach. Both the adult and larval stages contribute to pest control, though the larvae — often called "aphid lions" — are the most voracious predators.

## Classification

- **Order**: Neuroptera (net-winged insects)
- **Family**: Chrysopidae
- **Common genera**: *Chrysopa*, *Chrysoperla*
- **Type**: Beneficial predatory insect

## Key Facts

- Larvae consume aphids, spider mites, whiteflies, thrips, and caterpillars at remarkable rates
- A single lacewing larva can consume hundreds of aphids during its development
- Adults feed on pollen, nectar, and honeydew; some species are also predatory
- Eggs are laid on characteristic fine stalks attached to leaves, protecting them from predators including other lacewing larvae
- Multiple generations per year in warm climates; overwinter as pupae or adults depending on species
- Commercially available for release in gardens and agricultural settings
- Attracted to gardens with diverse plantings including pollen and nectar sources (umbellifers, composites)

## Role in the Soil Food Web

Green lacewings occupy a critical predator niche in the above-ground portion of the [[soil-food-web]]. By controlling populations of sap-sucking pests like aphids and spider mites, they help maintain the balance between pests and predators that characterizes a healthy garden ecosystem. Their presence is often an indicator that the soil food web is functioning properly.

In Lowenfels' soil food web framework, lacewings represent the kind of beneficial insect that naturally appears when chemical pesticides are eliminated and biological diversity is encouraged. They work in concert with other predators — ladybird beetles, assassin bugs, ground beetles, and parasitic wasps — to keep pest populations below damaging thresholds.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Pest management**: Primary biological control agent for aphids, mites, and whiteflies — common pests on vegetables, annuals, and perennials
- **Indicator species**: Presence in the garden suggests a healthy, balanced ecosystem without heavy chemical inputs
- **Soil food web gardening**: Thrives when [[compost]] teas and organic practices replace synthetic pesticides
- **[[companion-planting]]**: Attracted by dill, fennel, cosmos, coreopsis, and other pollen/nectar-rich flowers
- **Integrated approach**: Most effective when combined with healthy soil biology that produces unstressed, resilient plants

## Practical Notes

- Avoid broad-spectrum pesticides entirely — even organic options like pyrethrin can kill lacewings
- Provide overwintering habitat by leaving some leaf litter and perennial plant stems standing through winter
- Purchase lacewing eggs or larvae for targeted pest outbreaks
- Maintain flowering plants throughout the season to support adult populations
- Compost tea applications on foliage support the microbe layer on leaves that works synergistically with lacewing predation

## Relationships

- **Prey**: Aphids, spider mites, whiteflies, caterpillars, thrips, mealybugs, insect eggs
- **Predators**: Birds, spiders, other lacewing larvae (cannibalistic when food is scarce)
- **Allies**: Ladybird beetles, braconid wasps, assassin bugs, ground beetles
- **Attracted by**: Pollen/nectar plants, diverse plantings, absence of chemical sprays
