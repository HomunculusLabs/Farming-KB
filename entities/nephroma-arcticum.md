## Overview

**Nephroma arcticum** is a large foliose lichen in the family Nephromataceae, commonly known as the Arctic kidney lichen or starburst lichen. It is a bipartite lichen (ascomycete fungus associated with a green algal photobiont) found across boreal and arctic regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

The species is notable for its large, leafy thallus with distinctive marginal apothecia and its association with old-growth forest habitats. It has a long history of use in traditional medicine and as a food source by indigenous peoples of northern regions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** [[ascomycota]]
- **Class:** Lecanoromycetes
- **Order:** Peltigerales
- **Family:** Nephromataceae
- **Genus:** Nephroma
- **Species:** N. arcticum

The genus name **Nephroma** derives from the Greek *nephros* (kidney), referring to the kidney-shaped apothecia characteristic of the genus.

The species epithet **arcticum** refers to its northern distribution and prevalence in arctic and subarctic ecosystems.

### Morphology

Thallus is large and foliose, 10–20 cm in diameter, with broad, rounded lobes 1–3 cm wide. Upper surface is gray-green to brown-green, smooth to slightly wrinkled, with a matte finish when dry. Lower surface is pale tan to brown with a dense network of rhizines.

Apothecia are marginal, kidney-shaped to circular, 3–8 mm in diameter, with a reddish-brown disc and a prominent thalline margin. They develop at the tips and margins of lobes, giving the thallus a characteristic ruffled appearance.

The photobiont is the green alga **Coccomyxa**, a genus commonly associated with Peltigerales lichens. This differs from many Nephroma species that associate with cyanobacteria, making N. arcticum somewhat unusual within its genus.

Medulla is white and loosely woven. Cortex is well-developed on the upper surface, composed of densely packed hyphae forming a protective tissue layer approximately 40–60 µm thick.

## Ethnobotanical Uses

Indigenous peoples of northern regions have utilized **N. arcticum** for food and medicine. The lichen was boiled to remove bitter compounds and eaten as a survival food during times of famine, particularly by Sámi and Inuit communities.

In Scandinavian traditional medicine, N. arcticum preparations were used to treat respiratory ailments, wounds, and digestive disorders. The lichen was decocted in water or milk and consumed as a tea or poultice.

The species was also used as a dye lichen, producing brown to olive-green colors when processed with different mordants. Wool and other fibers treated with N. arcticum extracts yielded durable, earth-toned colors valued in traditional textile crafts.
