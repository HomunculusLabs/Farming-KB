## Overview

*Glechoma hederacea*, commonly known as ground ivy, alehoof, or gill-over-the-
ground, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the mint family (Lamiaceae). Native
to Europe and Asia, it has become naturalized across North America and other
temperate regions, where it often grows as a vigorous ground cover in shaded,
moist habitats.

The plant has a long history in traditional European herbal medicine, brewing,
and culinary practice. Its aromatic leaves were historically used to flavor and
clarify beer before the widespread adoption of hops (*Humulus lupulus*), and the
genus name *Glechoma* reflects this brewing association.

In modern herbalism, ground ivy is valued for its expectorant, anti-
inflammatory, and astringent properties. It contains bioactive terpenoids and
phenolic compounds, though clinical validation of traditional uses remains
limited.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- Kingdom: Plantae
- Order: Lamiales
- Family: Lamiaceae
- Subfamily: Nepetoideae
- Tribe: Mentheae
- Genus: *Glechoma* L.
- Species: *G. hederacea* L. (1753)

**Growth habit:** Perennial creeper with prostrate to ascending stems 10–60 cm
long. Stems are square (characteristic of Lamiaceae), green to purplish, rooting
at nodes where they contact soil.

**Leaves:** Opposite, reniform to broadly cordate, 1–3 cm diameter, with
crenate to shallowly lobed margins. Surfaces are pubescent with short curved
hairs. Leaf shape resembles common ivy (*Hedera helix*), reflected in the
specific epithet *hederacea* and the common name.

**Flowers:** Solitary or paired in leaf axils, 10–15 mm long, bilabiate with
a purple-blue to violet corolla. Upper lip notched, lower lip three-lobed.
Blooms March through May. Pollinated by long-tongued bees.

**Fruit:** Four nutlets (mericarps), dark brown, oblong-ovoid, ~1.5 mm long,
enclosed by persistent calyx. Dispersed by rain splash and animal adhesion.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to Europe and temperate Asia, from the British Isles to the Caucasus,
Siberia, and Japan. Widely introduced and now naturalized across North America,
New Zealand, and parts of South America.

Thrives in damp, shaded to semi-shaded environments including deciduous
woodlands, stream banks, hedgerows, meadows, and roadsides. Prefers moist,
nitrogen-rich loamy or clay soils with pH 5.5–7.0.

Ground ivy is a common lawn and garden weed, where creeping stolons enable
rapid colonization of bare soil. Considered invasive in some North American
contexts due to aggressive growth and allelopathic properties that suppress
competing vegetation.

## Phytochemistry and Pharmacology

The essential oil contains pulegone (up to 30–50% in some populations),
menthone, isomenthone, menthol, camphor, and limonene. Pulegone content is
variable and has raised toxicity concerns as it is a known hepatotoxin.

Flavonoids include luteolin-7-glucoside, apigenin-7-glucoside, and hyperoside,
contributing to documented antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity. Rosmarinic
acid, a caffeic acid ester common in Lamiaceae, is present in significant
quantities and supports astringent and anti-inflammatory properties.

**Traditional applications:** European folk medicine for respiratory conditions
(coughs, bronchitis), digestive complaints, kidney disorders, and wound healing.
Culpeper's Complete Herbal (1653) recommended it for tinnitus and as a spring
tonic.

**Respiratory effects:** Functions as an expectorant and mild bronchodilator.
Tea infusions used traditionally for catarrhal conditions and sinus congestion.
Anti-inflammatory activity demonstrated in vitro via COX enzyme inhibition.

**Safety:** Pulegone is classified by the FDA as "generally recognized as
unsafe" in high concentrations. Prolonged or excessive consumption is not
recommended. Pregnant women should avoid the herb due to potential emmenagogue
and abortifacient effects.

Listed in the German Commission E monographs for minor respiratory complaints,
though well-controlled clinical trials remain lacking.

## Permaculture Applications

Ground ivy provides effective living mulch, suppressing weeds and reducing soil
moisture loss through evaporation. Its dense, low-growing mat covers bare soil
rapidly.

The species functions as a dynamic accumulator, drawing nutrients from deeper
soil layers and cycling them through decomposing biomass. It is particularly
effective at accumulating potassium and phosphorus.

As an early-season nectar source, ground ivy supports bumblebees (*Bombus* spp.)
and other long-tongued bees during a period when few other plants bloom. This
aids colony establishment in spring.

Tolerance of shade and foot traffic makes it suitable for underplanting beneath
fruit trees and along garden paths. Its ability to establish on disturbed soil
aids erosion control on banks and slopes.
