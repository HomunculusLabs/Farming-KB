## Description

Saddle dams are the highest available water storages in the landscape, constructed on saddles or hollows in the skyline profile of hills. Named for their placement at topographic saddles—low points between two higher areas—these dams occupy positions that enable gravity-fed distribution to the maximum area below. They are a key element in Mollison's hierarchical dam placement strategy and are especially valuable for fire control and wildlife habitat.

## Classification

- **Category**: Earthworks / Dam type
- **Domain**: Permaculture, farm water management, landscape design
- **Landscape position**: Skyline saddles and hilltop hollows (highest position)
- **Primary uses**: Wildlife, stock water, high-level storage
- **Climate applicability**: Humid landscapes primarily

## Key Facts

### Construction Forms

Saddle dams can be constructed in several configurations:

- **Fully excavated below ground (grade)**: The entire storage is dug into the saddle
- **Walled on one or both sides**: Earth banks built to contain water within the saddle
- **Combined excavation and walling**: Partial cut-and-fill construction

### Shapes

- Circular
- Oblong
- "Shark egg" shaped with horns or extensions at either end

### Landscape Position

Saddle dams sit at the top of the dam hierarchy:

1. **Saddle dam** — highest storage, on skyline saddles
2. Ridgepoint dam — on sub-plateaus below saddles
3. Keypoint dam — at valley convex-to-concave transition
4. Contour dam — on slopes 8% or less
5. Barrier dam — across flowing streams

### Functions

- **Fire control**: Highest elevation water available for gravity-fed fire suppression
- **Wildlife habitat**: Creates upland water bodies for wildlife
- **Stock water**: Provides drinking water for grazing animals at elevation
- **Pumped storage**: Can receive water pumped from lower sources for energy generation
- **Runoff capture**: Fills from hill runoff and diversion drains

### Integration with Diversion Drains

Diversion drains cut at 1:250–500 rising grade catch runoff from hilltops and direct it to saddle dams. This fills the dam with water that would otherwise flow overland to lower elevations, making efficient use of the landscape's highest collection points.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

While saddle dams serve primarily as high-level storage rather than direct irrigation, they contribute to whole-farm water security. The consistent water availability at elevation supports wildlife corridors and biodiversity that maintain healthy soil ecosystems including fungal networks. The fire protection function is critical for protecting established cultivation areas and mushroom yards from wildfire damage.

The moist microclimate created around saddle dams supports diverse plant communities that can include mushroom-host tree species. In integrated permaculture systems, saddle dam catchments can support agroforestry zones with mycorrhizal tree species.
