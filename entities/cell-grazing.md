## Cell Grazing

## Description

Cell grazing (also known as rotational grazing, managed intensive rotational grazing, or mob grazing) is a pastoral management technique based on the observation that roaming herds of grazing animals maintained the health and vigour of natural rangelands by grazing intensely and then moving on. The technique was formalised and promoted by Allan Savory and others, and involves dividing pasture into small cells through which livestock are rotated in a controlled pattern, mimicking the natural behaviour of wild herbivore herds that evolved alongside grassland ecosystems.

## Classification

- **Category:** Technique / Land Management Practice
- **Domain:** Pastoral farming, rangeland management, permaculture
- **Originator:** Allan Savory (formalised the approach)
- **Related Practices:** Rotational grazing, mob grazing, holistic grazing management, pasture ley cropping

## Key Principles

1. **Short, intense grazing periods** — Animals graze a cell hard for a brief period (hours to days) before being moved to the next cell. This mimics the behaviour of wild herds that moved frequently under predation pressure.

2. **Long recovery periods** — Each cell is given extended time to recover fully before being grazed again, allowing perennial grasses to regrow, rebuild root reserves, and restore leaf area for photosynthesis.

3. **High stock density** — Concentrating animals in small areas ensures uniform grazing, trampling of rank growth, hoof impact on soil surface, and even manure distribution across the paddock.

4. **Pulsing production and consumption** — Separating the production phase (plant growth) from the consumption phase (animal grazing) increases total energy processing and system resilience, following the pulsing ecosystem model.

## Key Facts

- Pastures grow better without the constant nibbling of animals; a short burst of grazing maintains vegetative growth and recycles nutrients more effectively than continuous stocking.
- An occasional hard graze exposes and cuts the soil surface, providing favourable conditions for regeneration of short-lived legumes and other useful species that would otherwise be outcompeted.
- The technique separates the production aspect of the system (plant growth) from consumption (animal grazing), which increases total energy processing and resilience.
- Key management questions are how brief and how intense each grazing period should be, and how long the recovery period needs to be.
- Can be applied at scales ranging from small hobby farms to extensive rangeland operations.
- The approach draws on observations of natural herd behaviour in ecosystems such as the African savanna and the North American prairie.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Pulsing ecosystem model:** Cell grazing is a practical application of the pulsing model of ecosystem succession, where periods of intense consumption alternate with longer periods of biological accumulation.

- **Nutrient cycling:** The concentrated deposition of manure and urine during grazing pulses accelerates nutrient cycling and improves soil fertility without synthetic inputs.

- **Pasture diversity:** The disturbance created by intense grazing promotes species diversity by preventing dominant species from excluding others and creating niches for legumes and forbs.

- **Soil health:** The combination of root die-back during grazing and regrowth during recovery builds soil organic matter and improves soil structure over time.

- **Resilience:** By mimicking natural herbivore patterns, cell grazing builds resilience into pastoral systems, reducing vulnerability to drought, degradation, and weed invasion.

- **Integration with other systems:** Can be combined with fodder trees, alley farming, and other permaculture techniques for integrated land management at multiple scales.

## Connections

- Developed and promoted by Allan Savory as part of Holistic Resource Management
- Related to the permaculture principle of applying self-regulation and accepting feedback
- Demonstrates the pulsing ecosystem model discussed by Eugene Odum
- Compatible with permaculture approaches to integrated animal and plant systems
- Can be integrated with pasture ley cropping for broader farm system design
