The species is a robust, spiny, scrambling palm that climbs into the forest canopy using whiplike flagella armed with recurved hooks. Mature stems reach 15 to 30 meters in length, with canes approximately 2 to 5 centimeters in diameter. The plant produces its characteristic red resin from the fruit scales, which exude a blood-like substance when crushed.

Dragon's blood from Daemonorops draco has been traded across Asia and Europe for over a thousand years. It was valued in traditional Chinese, Indian, and Arabic medicine, and was imported into Europe during the medieval period as a exotic pharmaceutical commodity. The resin continues to be harvested commercially and used in various applications today.

The name "dragon's blood" refers to the deep red color of the resin, which resembles coagulated blood. Similar resins are produced by several unrelated plant species, including [[croton-lechleri]] (Amazonian dragon's blood), each with distinct chemical compositions and traditional uses.

Historical trade records document dragon's blood as a commodity in the spice and incense trade routes connecting Southeast Asia to China, India, the Middle East, and eventually medieval Europe. The resin was among the exotic goods traded through ports in Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Daemonorops draco belongs to the subfamily Calamoideae and the tribe Calameae, which includes all rattan palms. The genus Daemonorops contains approximately 115 species distributed from India and southern China through Southeast Asia to New Guinea. It is one of the largest genera in the palm family.

The stems are slender, cylindrical, and covered in sheaths bearing fierce, whorled spines that can exceed 5 centimeters in length. The spines are arranged in dense rings around the stem sheath and provide defense against herbivores. The stems are solid rather than hollow, unlike bamboo.

Leaves are pinnate, measuring 1.5 to 3 meters in length, with a spiny petiole and rachis. The leaf bears 30 to 50 pairs of linear-lanceolate leaflets on each side, each 20 to 40 centimeters long. The leaflets are dark green above and pale green beneath, with prominent midribs and small bristles along the margins.

The plant produces long, whip-like extensions of the leaf sheath called flagella, which are armed with reflexed, hook-like spines. These flagella enable the palm to scramble over and anchor to supporting trees and vegetation in the forest canopy.

Inflorescences are produced from the leaf axils and are pendulous, reaching 1 to 2 meters in length. Flowers are small and borne in dense clusters along the rachillae, with separate male and female inflorescences on the same plant.

Fruits are approximately 1.5 to 2 centimeters in diameter, covered in shiny, overlapping scales. Each scale is coated with a thin layer of bright red resinous material that constitutes the commercially harvested dragon's blood. The fruit contains a single rounded seed.

## Distribution and Habitat

Daemonorops draco is native to lowland and hill [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]]. It thrives in humid, warm environments with annual rainfall exceeding 2,000 millimeters.

Soils in its native habitat range from well-drained sandy loams to clay-rich alluvial deposits. The species tolerates periodic flooding in riparian habitats and shows moderate tolerance of seasonal moisture fluctuations.

Wild populations have declined due to deforestation and over-harvesting of the resin-producing fruits. Commercial demand for dragon's blood has led to intensive collection in some areas, reducing fruit set and natural regeneration. Habitat loss through logging and conversion of lowland forest to agriculture and plantation crops is the most significant long-term threat.

The species is part of a larger rattan palm community that has experienced severe population declines across Southeast Asia due to over-exploitation. Rattan canes are harvested for furniture and basketry, placing additional pressure on Daemonorops populations beyond the specific demand for dragon's blood resin.

Conservation efforts are complicated by the palm's economic value, which creates incentives for both sustainable management and unsustainable over-harvesting. The development of cultivated sources of dragon's blood resin could reduce pressure on wild populations, though cultivated material currently represents only a small fraction of total supply.

## Cultivation

Daemonorops draco is cultivated primarily for dragon's blood [[query-how-to-maximize-terpene-and-resin-production-in-cannabis]]. Harvesting typically begins five to eight years after planting. Yields are relatively low compared to other resin-producing species, which limits commercial viability to small-scale and traditional production systems.

In addition to its native range, Daemonorops draco has been introduced to tropical botanical collections and ethnobotanical gardens worldwide. It is rarely cultivated as an ornamental due to its formidable spines and aggressive climbing habit.

The cane of Daemonorops draco, like other rattan species, has been used for furniture making, basketry, and construction of lightweight structures. However, the cane quality is generally considered inferior to that of commercial rattan species such as [[blesching-cannabis-wound-care]].

Beyond medicine, dragon's blood has been used as a red dye for textiles, lacquer for wooden objects, pigment in painting and illumination of manuscripts, and as an ingredient in incense and spiritual rituals across multiple cultures. In medieval Europe, it was imported through trade routes and valued as a pharmaceutical ingredient.

Modern applications include use as a colorant in varnishes, as a component in traditional wood finishes, and as an ingredient in some folk medicine preparations. It is also used in the production of certain specialized inks and as a mordant in textile dyeing.

In recent years, scientific interest in dragon's blood has increased due to studies investigating its potential anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and antioxidant properties.
