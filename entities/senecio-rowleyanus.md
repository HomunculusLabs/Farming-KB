## Overview

Senecio rowleyanus, commonly known as string of pearls, is a trailing succulent vine in the family Asteraceae. It is native to southwestern Africa, where it grows as an epiphyte or lithophyte in semi-arid regions.

The species is instantly recognizable by its spherical, pea-sized leaves strung along thin, wiry stems. Each leaf is a perfect globe approximately 6–8 mm in diameter, adapted to minimize water loss.

String of pearls is among the most popular hanging basket succulents worldwide. Its cascading stems can reach 60–90 cm in length, creating a dramatic curtain effect.

The plant produces small, white, brush-like flowers with a sweet, cinnamon-like fragrance. Blooming occurs primarily in summer, though flowers are often sparse in indoor cultivation.

The species was named in honor of Gordon Rowley, the British botanist who first described it formally in 1958. Rowley was a prominent succulent specialist and author.

In its native habitat, Senecio rowleyanus grows in the dappled shade of trees and shrubs. It drapes over branches and rock surfaces, forming mats of trailing stems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Senecio rowleyanus belongs to the family Asteraceae, tribe Senecioneae. It was described by Gordon Rowley in 1958 from cultivated material of southern African origin.

The species is closely related to Senecio herreianus and Senecio radicans. These three species form the "string" group of trailing South African senecios.

Leaves are spherical, 6–8 mm in diameter, with a small pointed tip and a translucent "window" stripe on one side. The window allows light to penetrate deep into the leaf interior for photosynthesis.

Each leaf has a thin, crescent-shaped translucent area on its inner surface. This leaf window is an adaptation to low-light conditions, allowing the plant to photosynthesize efficiently in shaded habitats.

Stems are thin, wiry, and flexible, 1–2 mm in diameter. They root at nodes where they contact moist substrate, facilitating vegetative spread.

Roots are fibrous and shallow, adapted to growing in crevices and epiphytic situations. The root system spreads laterally rather than deeply.

Flower heads are small capitula approximately 5 mm across. The disc florets are surrounded by narrow, white ray florets that give a brush-like appearance.

Chromosome number is 2n = 20. The species is diploid and shows moderate variation in leaf size across its range.

## Distribution and Habitat

Senecio rowleyanus is native to Namibia and the Northern Cape Province of South Africa. It occurs in semi-arid regions with winter rainfall.

Plants grow as epiphytes on the branches of shrubs and small trees. They also colonize rock crevices and cliff faces in partially shaded positions.

The native climate features hot, dry summers and cool, moist winters. Annual rainfall is 100–250 mm, mostly falling between May and August.

Temperatures in the native range remain mild year-round. Summer maxima reach 30–35 °C, while winter minima rarely drop below 5 °C.

The species is locally common within its range but has a restricted overall distribution. It is not considered threatened due to its occurrence in remote, inaccessible habitats.

Associated species include other succulents adapted to semi-arid, partially shaded niches. These include various Crassula, Pelargonium, and Ceropegia species.

The species has adapted to grow both terrestrially and epiphytically, a flexibility that contributes to its success across varied microsites.

## Cultivation

Senecio rowleyanus is widely grown as a houseplant and hanging basket specimen. It requires bright, indirect light and protection from hot afternoon sun.

The potting medium should be well-draining with high inorganic content. A mix of standard potting soil with 40–50% perlite or pumice provides suitable drainage.

Water thoroughly when the soil has dried to a depth of 2–3 cm. Overwatering causes root rot and stem collapse, the most common cause of plant death.

The species prefers moderate humidity and good air circulation. Stagnant, humid conditions promote fungal diseases and stem rot.

Propagation is by stem cuttings rooted in moist perlite or water. Cuttings root within 2–3 weeks at 20–25 °C.

Individual leaves can also be propagated by pressing the pointed tip into moist substrate. Success rates are lower than stem cuttings but still viable.

The plant is hardy to approximately 5 °C. It is grown exclusively as an indoor plant in temperate climates.

Mealybugs and aphids are the most common pests. Infestations often start in the leaf axils and stem nodes where pests are protected from sprays.

The plant benefits from occasional flushing with water to remove dust from the spherical leaves. Clean leaves photosynthesize more efficiently and are less prone to pest problems.

## Ecological Role

In habitat, the trailing stems provide shelter for small insects and arachnids. The dense mats of foliage create microclimates that moderate temperature and humidity.

Flowers are visited by small flies and bees attracted to the sweet fragrance. Pollination biology has not been extensively studied in this species.

The leaf window adaptation allows the plant to grow in low-light environments where few other succulents survive. This niche partitioning reduces competition for resources.

The spherical leaf shape minimizes surface area relative to volume. This reduces transpiration and allows the plant to store maximum water per leaf.

## Similar Species

Senecio herreianus (string of beads) has elongated, oval-shaped leaves rather than perfectly spherical ones. It is less common in cultivation but equally easy to grow.

Senecio radicans (string of bananas or fishhooks) has crescent-shaped, pointed leaves. It is more tolerant of direct sun and drought than S. rowleyanus.

Ceropegia woodii (string of hearts) is an unrelated trailing plant with heart-shaped, silver-veined leaves. It occupies a similar niche in the hanging basket market but belongs to the Apocynaceae.

The three Senecio "string" species hybridize readily in cultivation. Intergeneric hybrids are not reported.
