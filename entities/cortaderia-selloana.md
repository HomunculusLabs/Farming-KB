## Overview

**Cortaderia selloana** (Schult. & Schult.f.) Asch. & Graebn., commonly known as **pampas grass**, is a large [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]]. It is widely cultivated as an ornamental landscape plant but has become a problematic invasive species in many temperate and subtropical regions worldwide.

The species is renowned for its dramatic, feathery flower plumes that can reach 1–3 m in height. These showy inflorescences have made it one of the most recognizable [[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]] that drives invasive spread. Many commercial cultivars are propagated vegetatively from female stock, contributing to the spread of seed-producing individuals.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Poales
- **Family:** Poaceae
- **Subfamily:** Danthonioideae
- **Genus:** Cortaderia Stapf
- **Species:** C. selloana (Schult. & Schult.f.) Asch. & Graebn.

**Synonyms:** *Arundo selloana* Schult. & Schult.f., *Gynerium selloanum* (Schult. & Schult.f.) Nees, *Cortaderia argentea* (Nees) Stapf.

Plants form large, fountain-like tussocks 1.5–3.0 m tall and 1–3 m wide. Leaves are evergreen, narrow (3–8 mm wide), and up to 2 m long. Leaf margins are sharply serrated with silica-reinforced teeth.

Leaf color ranges from blue-green to gray-green, with a prominent central midrib. The sheath is broad and overlapping, forming a dense basal crown. New leaves emerge from the center of the tussock in spring.

Flowering culms are stout and erect, 2–4 m tall. The panicle is large, dense, and plumose, 30–100 cm long and 10–25 cm wide, composed of numerous white or silvery spikelets.

Spikelets are 10–18 mm long, with one to three florets. Glumes are long-ciliate along the keel. Lemma is acute, with a short awn at the tip. Female florets are larger and more showy than male florets.

## Distribution and Habitat

**Cortaderia selloana** is native to southern South America, including the pampas grasslands of Argentina, Uruguay, southern Brazil, and Chile. It grows naturally in open grasslands, along watercourses, and on disturbed sites.

The species has been widely introduced as an ornamental plant and has naturalized in many regions including California, the [[fukuoka-textdoc-agricultural-land-law-obstruction-return-farming]]. It tolerates a broad range of soil types and moisture conditions.

Native habitats in South America experience periodic fire, and the species is fire-adapted. It resprouts vigorously from the crown after burning. This fire tolerance contributes to its invasiveness in fire-prone Mediterranean climates.

The species tolerates coastal conditions including salt spray and sandy soils. It is cold-hardy to approximately −12 °C, limiting its spread in colder continental climates but allowing persistence in maritime and Mediterranean zones.

## Ecology

Pampas grass produces enormous quantities of wind-dispersed seed. A single large female plant can produce up to one million seeds per year. The lightweight, plumed seeds travel distances of 20 km or more on wind currents.

This prolific seed production and long-distance dispersal capacity are key factors in its invasive success. Seeds germinate readily on disturbed, bare soil with minimal competition. Seedling establishment rates are highest on recently burned or cleared sites.

The dense tussocks suppress native vegetation through competition for light, water, and nutrients. In its native range, pampas grass provides habitat for various bird species that nest in the dense tussocks. The seeds are eaten by several bird species. However, in introduced ranges, these ecological benefits are outweighed by its negative impacts on native plant communities.

## Cultivation

Pampas grass has been a popular ornamental plant since its introduction to European horticulture in the late 18th century. Its dramatic form, showy plumes, and tolerance of poor soils make it attractive for large-scale landscape plantings.

Plants prefer full sun and well-drained soils but tolerate clay, sand, and coastal conditions. They are extremely drought-tolerant once established. No irrigation or fertilizer is needed in most settings.

Propagation is primarily by division in spring, though seed is also used. Female plants are preferred for ornamental use due to their larger, showier plumes. Male-only cultivars have been developed to reduce invasive seed spread.

Maintenance involves cutting back old foliage to near ground level in late winter before new growth emerges. This is a hazardous task requiring heavy gloves due to the sharp leaf margins. Burning is an effective alternative on appropriate sites.
