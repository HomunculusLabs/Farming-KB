## Overview

**Cotyledon undulata** Haw., commonly called the silver ruffle or silver crown, is a compact succulent shrublet in the family Crassulaceae. It is native to the Western and Eastern Cape provinces of South Africa.

The species is distinguished by its heavily scalloped, silvery-white leaves arranged in opposing pairs along short stems. The undulating leaf margins give rise to the specific epithet "undulata."

Cotyledon undulata is widely grown as an ornamental container plant and in rock gardens. Its silvery appearance and compact growth habit make it a popular accent plant in drought-tolerant landscapes.

The species produces clusters of nodding, orange-red flowers on tall stems in late winter to spring. These showy blooms add to its ornamental appeal during the flowering season.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Cotyledon undulata was described by Adrian Hardy Haworth in 1821 from cultivated material of South African origin. The species belongs to the genus Cotyledon, which comprises approximately 10–12 species of succulent shrubs.

The plant grows as a low, much-branched shrublet reaching 20–30 cm tall and up to 40 cm wide. Branches are woody at the base with peeling bark, becoming succulent toward the tips.

Leaves are arranged in opposite pairs, each leaf 3–6 cm long and 2–4 cm wide. The blade is broadly ovate to nearly circular with strongly undulate (wavy) margins.

Both leaf surfaces are covered with a dense white powdery bloom (epicuticular wax). This farinose coating gives the plant its characteristic silver appearance and provides protection against intense sunlight.

The leaf margins are prominently scalloped with rounded lobes. In strong light, the margins may develop a pinkish or reddish tinge.

Roots are fibrous and moderately deep. The root system supports the plant in rocky crevices and shallow soils of its native habitat.

Inflorescences are terminal, producing 1–3 flowering stems per branch. Each peduncle reaches 15–30 cm tall and bears 2–6 nodding flowers at the apex.

Flowers are tubular to urn-shaped, 1.5–2 cm long, with petals fused into a distinct tube. Petal color is orange-red to salmon, with a yellowish interior visible at the mouth.

Flowering occurs from late winter to early spring (July–September in the southern hemisphere). Individual flowers last 5–7 days, with buds opening sequentially along the inflorescence.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cotyledon undulata is endemic to South Africa, occurring in a narrow band along the southern coast from the Western Cape to the eastern parts of the Eastern Cape. The species is found primarily in the Succulent Karoo and Albany Thicket biomes.

Plants grow on rocky hillsides, cliff faces, and among boulders in well-drained situations. They are found from near sea level to approximately 500 m elevation.

The species favors north-facing slopes that receive full sun for much of the day. Plants often colonize crevices in shale, quartzite, and sandstone outcrops.

Annual rainfall in the native range is 200–400 mm, falling predominantly in winter and spring. Summers are hot and dry, and plants rely on stored moisture during extended drought.

Cotyledon undulata frequently grows in association with other succulent species. Common associates include species of Crassula, Euphorbia, Aloe, and Pelargonium.

The species shows limited tolerance of frost in its native range. Coastal populations experience mild winter temperatures, while inland populations at higher elevations may encounter light frost.

Populations near the western edge of the range tend to have more pronounced leaf undulation. Eastern populations are often more robust with slightly larger leaves.

The species hybridizes naturally with Cotyledon orbiculata where their ranges overlap. These hybrids show intermediate leaf morphology and are occasionally found in the horticultural trade.

## Cultivation

Cotyledon undulata is a rewarding plant for cultivation, requiring minimal care once established. It thrives in full sun to light shade, with brighter light producing more compact growth and stronger leaf coloration.

The growing medium should be well-draining with a high proportion of grit. A mix of 70% pumice or gravel with 30% loam-based potting soil provides excellent drainage.

Watering should follow a thorough soak-and-dry cycle. Allow the soil to dry completely between waterings during the growing season. Reduce watering in summer when the plant may enter a brief dormant period.

Overwatering is the primary cause of loss in cultivation. The species is adapted to arid conditions and rots quickly in persistently moist soil.

Temperature range is 5–35 °C during active growth. The species tolerates brief frost to -2 °C if kept dry but suffers damage from prolonged freezing.

Propagation is by stem cuttings, leaf cuttings, or seed. Stem cuttings root readily within 2–3 weeks when allowed to callus before planting.

Leaf cuttings are slower but viable. Remove a healthy leaf, allow it to callus for 3–5 days, and place on moist gritty medium.

Seed germinates within 1–2 weeks at 20–25 °C without pretreatment. Seedlings grow slowly and benefit from protection from strong sun during their first year.

The white farinose coating on leaves can be damaged by handling or water droplets. Handle plants by the stem or pot rather than touching the leaves to preserve their ornamental appearance.

## Ecology and Conservation

Cotyledon undulata is pollinated by sunbirds and bees in its native habitat. The tubular flowers with their orange-red coloration are adapted for bird pollination.

All parts of the plant contain cardiac glycosides (cotyledontoxin) that are toxic if ingested. Livestock poisoning has been documented in South Africa when animals graze on wild populations during drought.

The species is classified as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. Its relatively wide distribution and occurrence in protected areas provide some conservation security.

Illegal collection from wild populations occurs but is not considered a major threat. Most plants in cultivation are nursery-propagated.

Cotyledon undulata is not known to be invasive outside its native range. Its specific habitat requirements and slow growth prevent naturalization in most cultivated regions.

The silvery leaf coating is thought to reflect excess solar radiation and reduce water loss. This adaptation is common among succulents from exposed, arid habitats.

The species has been cultivated in European collections since the early 19th century. It remains one of the most popular Cotyledon species in the horticultural trade.

Cotyledon undulata received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit. This recognition confirms its reliability and ornamental value for garden cultivation.

Pruning is rarely necessary but can be used to maintain a compact shape. Removed branches root easily and can be used to start new plants.

Plants grown in containers may become root-bound after 2–3 years and benefit from repotting. Spring is the ideal time for repotting to allow new root growth during the active season.
