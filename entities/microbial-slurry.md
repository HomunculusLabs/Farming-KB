# Microbial Slurry

## Description
A microbial slurry is a liquid suspension of beneficial soil microbes prepared for application to [[casing-soil]] in order to induce or enhance fruitbody formation in mushroom species that require microbial partnerships. The slurry is typically derived from soil samples collected at the stem base of wild or cultivated mushrooms.

## Classification
- **Category**: Cultivation material / Biological inoculant
- **Domain**: [[mycology]], microbiology
- **Function**: Introducing beneficial microbes to trigger mushroom fruiting

## Key Facts

### Source Material
- Soil from the stem base of wild or recently harvested cultivated mushrooms
- Must include mycelium-bound soil with binding rhizomorphs
- Each gram of native soil can harbor more microbial cells than there are species of life on the planet

### Preparation Process
1. Identify the targeted dilution level through a dilution-to-extinction assay
2. Harvest casing soil from the jars at the effective dilution as soon as mycelial knotting appears
3. Blend the harvested casing into a larger volume of sterilized water and casing soil
4. Mix thoroughly and allow for drainage and gas exchange
5. Determine optimal proportion of slurry to casing soil through controlled experimentation

### Application
- Applied to sterilized casing soil at experimentally determined concentrations
- Start with small-scale tests before full operation use
- Can be perpetuated through additional stem base harvesting and dilutions
- Compatible with pasteurized but not sterilized casing soils

### Safety Considerations
- Test on small batches first — slurry may contain competing microbes
- Slurries from untested sources may harbor pathogens
- Proper labeling and record-keeping essential

### Species That Benefit
- Agaricus spp. (commercial button mushrooms)
- Stropharia rugoso-annulata ([[king-stropharia]])
- Morchella spp. (morels)

## Relevance to Cultivation
Microbial slurries represent a critical breakthrough in cultivating mushroom species that have historically been difficult or impossible to fruit in controlled environments. The technique acknowledges that successful cultivation often depends on managing entire microbial ecosystems.
