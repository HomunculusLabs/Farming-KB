# Cuminum cyminum

## Overview

Cuminum cyminum, commonly known as cumin, is an annual herbaceous plant in the family Apiaceae. It is one of the oldest and most widely used spice crops in human history, with evidence of cultivation dating back over 5,000 years to the ancient Levant and Egypt.

The plant is valued primarily for its dried fruits (commonly called cumin seeds), which are used extensively in cuisines across South Asia, the Middle East, North Africa, Latin America, and [[herb-growing-guide]] 15–50 cm tall. The stems are branching from the base, glabrous or sparsely pubescent, and green to greyish-green in color.

Leaves are 2–3 pinnately divided, with filiform segments 5–20 mm long. The upper leaves have reduced segments and a more linear profile compared to the basal leaves.

The inflorescence is a compound umbel with 3–5 primary rays, each 1–2.5 cm long. Individual flowers are small, white to pale pink, with petals approximately 1.5 mm in length. Flowering occurs 40–50 days after sowing.

The fruit is an ellipsoidal cremocarp, 4–6 mm long and 1.5–2 mm wide.

It is brownish-yellow to dark brown, with prominent longitudinal ridges. Each mericarp contains one seed and has nine vittae (oil ducts). The seeds adhere strongly to the pericarp, which is unusual among Apiaceae and influences processing efficiency.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

**Essential oil composition:** Cumin seeds yield 2.5–5.0% essential oil. The major constituents are **cuminaldehyde** (22–38%), **γ-terpinene** (15–25%), **p-cymene** (10–20%), **β-pinene** (5–15%), and **p-mentha-1,4-dien-7-al** (5–12%).

Minor constituents include α-pinene, limonene, 1,8-cineole, linalool, and carveol. The essential oil composition varies significantly with geographic origin, with Indian cumin typically higher in cuminaldehyde than Middle Eastern varieties.

## Culinary and Nutritional Use

Cumin seeds are a fundamental spice in the cuisines of India, Pakistan, Iran, Turkey, Morocco, Mexico, and the Caribbean. They are used both whole and ground, with dry-roasting before grinding being the standard preparation technique to enhance flavor.

In Indian cooking, cumin is the first spice added to hot oil or ghee in most preparations. It is a core component of garam masala, panch phoron, and chai masala. In Mexican cuisine, it defines the flavor profile of chili con carne, tacos al pastor, and various salsas.

The seeds can be ground into a paste with water or yogurt as a marinade base for meats and vegetables. Cumin is also used in pickles, breads, cheeses, and liqueurs (such as kummel and aquavit).

Black cumin (Kala jeera, Bunium persicum) is a closely related but botanically distinct species often confused with C. cyminum. It has a sweeter, more complex flavor and commands a significantly higher market price.

## Cultivation

Cumin is primarily cultivated in India (Rajasthan and Gujarat), Iran, Turkey, Syria, China, Egypt, and Mexico. India produces approximately 700,000–800,000 tonnes annually, making it the dominant global producer.

The crop requires a warm, dry climate with well-drained sandy loam soil. Optimal growing temperatures are 20–30°C. Cumin is highly sensitive to waterlogging and frost, which are the primary causes of crop failure.

Sowing occurs from October to November in India (rabi season). Seeds are broadcast or drilled at 8–12 kg/ha with 25–30 cm [[wide-row-spacing-intensive-gardening-comparison]]. Germination takes 7–12 days, and the crop matures in 100–120 days.

Irrigation is applied at flowering and pod-filling stages. Harvest is done by cutting plants at ground level when fruits turn brown, followed by sun-drying and threshing. Average yield is 500–700 kg/ha under rainfed conditions.
