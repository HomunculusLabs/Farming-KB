# Ranunculus pennsylvanicus

## Overview

Ranunculus pennsylvanicus (Pennsylvania buttercup or bristly buttercup) is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family [[ranunculus-acris]], native to North America. It is distinguished from other North American buttercups by its densely hispid stems and finely dissected leaves.

The species occurs in wet meadows, marshes, streambanks, and roadside ditches across much of eastern and central North America. It is one of the most widespread wetland buttercups on the continent, ranging from Newfoundland to Saskatchewan and south to Georgia.

Despite its common name, the species is not restricted to Pennsylvania and is actually most abundant in the upper Midwest and northeastern states. The name reflects the history of early botanical exploration in the mid-Atlantic colonies rather than the species' geographic center of abundance.

First described by the French botanist Louis-Marie Aubert du Petit-Thouars, the species epithet refers to Pennsylvania, where early collections were made. It has also been known historically as R. hispidus var. nitidus in some regional treatments.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Ranunculus pennsylvanicus belongs to section Epirotes within the genus Ranunculus, a group of primarily North American species characterized by densely hairy stems and finely divided leaves. Its closest relatives include several endemic North American buttercup species.

The plant grows 30–90 cm tall from a fibrous root system. Stems are erect, branched above the middle, and covered with stiff, spreading hairs that give the plant a distinctive bristly texture. This pubescence distinguishes it from the mostly glabrous [[ranunculus-acris]].

Basal leaves are long-petioled, palmately divided into three primary segments, each further dissected into narrow, acute lobes. Stem leaves become progressively smaller and less divided toward the apex. All leaves are sparsely to moderately hairy.

Flowers are 8–15 mm in diameter with five glossy yellow petals and reflexed hairy sepals. The receptacle is conspicuously elongated and pilose at fruiting, and achenes are flattened with a short, curved beak approximately 0.5 mm long.

Chromosome number is 2n = 32, a tetraploid count that suggests an ancient allopolyploid origin. The species reproduces exclusively by seed and does not produce stolons or tubers.

The hispid stem indumentum is one of the most reliable diagnostic characters, visible even on pressed [[herbarium-specimens-fungal-collections]]. Hairs are multicellular, uniseriate, and spreading, giving the stems a rough texture that can be felt when rubbed.

## Distribution and Habitat

R. pennsylvanicus is native to eastern and central North America, occurring from Newfoundland and Labrador west to Alberta and Saskatchewan, and south through the Great Lakes region to Georgia, Alabama, and Mississippi. It is most abundant in the northeastern United States and southeastern Canada.

The species occupies wet meadows, marsh margins, streambanks, spring seeps, roadside ditches, and other seasonally saturated habitats. It is classified as a facultative wetland plant, occurring in wetlands approximately 70% of the time.

Soil preferences include loamy to clay-rich substrates with neutral to slightly acidic pH. The species tolerates brief seasonal inundation but does not persist in permanently standing water, unlike the truly aquatic [[ranunculus-aquatilis]].

Population density is highest in disturbed wetland margins where competing vegetation is sparse. Roadside ditches and powerline corridors often support robust populations, reflecting the species' tolerance of anthropogenic disturbance in wetland settings.

In the southern part of its range, populations become scattered and confined to higher elevations in the Appalachian Mountains. These disjunct populations may represent Pleistocene refugia from a formerly more continuous distribution.

The species co-occurs with a characteristic suite of wetland plants, including Impatiens capensis, [[verbesina-alternifolia]], and various Carex species. In high-quality wetland communities, it serves as an indicator of intermediate hydrology and mineral-rich soils.

## Ecology

Flowering occurs from June to August, with peak bloom in early July across most of the range. Flowers are insect-pollinated, with small bees (Halictidae) and syrphid flies being the most frequent visitors.

The species contains ranunculin, which converts to the toxic protoanemonin upon tissue damage. This compound deters most mammalian herbivores, though white-tailed deer will occasionally browse young shoots in spring when other forage is scarce.

Seed production is moderate, with individual plants producing 50–150 achenes per season. Achenes are dispersed primarily by water, floating for short distances in runoff and streamflow before becoming waterlogged and sinking.

Germination requires cold stratification, with optimal emergence following 8–12 weeks of moist chilling at 1–5°C. Seedlings establish in bare mud along receding waterlines in late spring, taking advantage of reduced competition.

Vegetative spread is limited; the species does not produce stolons or rhizomes. Clumped growth forms observed in the field represent multiple seedlings establishing in close proximity rather than vegetative expansion.

Arbuscular [[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] are present but less developed than in upland Ranunculus species, likely due to the periodically [[lowenfels-aerobic-anaerobic-soil-conditions]] in wetland habitats. The species relies heavily on fine roots for nutrient uptake during the drier portions of [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

Climate change projections suggest potential northward range expansion as growing seasons lengthen, though the species' dependence on wetland habitats may limit its ability to track shifting climate zones. Drainage of wetlands for agriculture remains the primary conservation concern.

## Uses and Cultivation

Ranunculus pennsylvanicus has no significant horticultural or agricultural use. Its bristly stems and small flowers make it unattractive compared to ornamental species such as [[ranunculus-bulbosus]] or cultivated Persian buttercup.

The species is sometimes included in native wetland seed mixes for restoration projects in eastern North America, where it contributes to plant diversity in created wetlands and stormwater detention basins. Establishment from seed is reliable under moist conditions.

Germination rates in commercial seed lots typically reach 60–80% after proper stratification, making the species practical for large-scale restoration work. However, seed is not widely available from commercial suppliers and is usually collected from wild populations.

Its primary value is ecological, providing nectar and pollen for native pollinators during the midsummer flowering period. Several native bees specialize on Ranunculus pollen, making the genus an important resource in wetland plant communities.

The plant has been used in Native American traditional medicine as a topical treatment for skin ailments, though documentation is sparse and the practice is not widespread. Modern herbalism does not employ the species due to toxicity concerns.

The species is not considered threatened or endangered across most of its range, though some peripheral populations in the southeastern states are locally rare. It receives no special legal protection under federal or state endangered species legislation.
