## Pleurotus subareolatus

## Description

*Pleurotus subareolatus* is a species of [[oyster-mushroom]] identified by Barron and Thorn (1987) as one of four *Pleurotus* species capable of killing nematodes using specialized toxin-producing secretory cells. Like *P. ostreatus*, *P. strigosus*, and *P. cornucopiae*, this species produces minute spathulate secretory cells on its mycelium that secrete droplets of a potent toxin. Contact with these droplets kills nematodes within approximately 30 seconds, making *Pleurotus* one of the most effective nematode-killing fungal genera known.

## Key Facts

- **Nematode-killing ability**: Demonstrated by Barron and Thorn (1987) alongside *P. ostreatus*, *P. strigosus*, and *P. cornucopiae*. Nematodes are killed within ~30 seconds of contacting the toxin droplets secreted by specialized mycelial cells.
- **Spathulate secretory cells**: The toxin-producing structures are microscopic, spoon-shaped cells that project from the hyphae and continuously exude toxic droplets into the surrounding environment.
- **Rapid mechanism**: The speed of nematode killing (seconds) is far faster than other nematophagous mechanisms such as constricting rings (seconds to minutes) or adhesive traps (minutes to hours).
- **Post-mortem colonization**: Following nematode death, hyphae penetrate the nematode body through natural openings and digest the contents for nutrition.
- **Nitrogen acquisition**: Nematophagy in *Pleurotus* is interpreted as a strategy for supplementing nitrogen nutrition on wood substrates that are rich in carbon but poor in nitrogen.

## Ecological Role

- Natural regulator of soil and wood-associated nematode populations.
- Contributes to the nitrogen cycling in dead wood ecosystems by converting nematode nitrogen into fungal biomass.
- Part of the diverse community of organisms that colonize decomposing wood in forest ecosystems.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Cultivation potential**: Like other *Pleurotus* species, *P. subareolatus* can likely be cultivated on lignocellulosic substrates. Its nematophagy may provide ancillary benefits in agricultural settings.
- **Toxin research**: The toxin produced by *Pleurotus* species has been identified as a fatty acid derivative. Understanding the toxin's mechanism of action may inform the development of novel nematicides.
- **Evolutionary significance**: The independent evolution of nematophagy in basidiomycetes (*Pleurotus*) and ascomycetes (*Arthrobotrys*, *Dactylella*) represents convergent evolution toward carnivory in fungi.
