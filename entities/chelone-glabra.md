## Overview

Chelone glabra, commonly known as white turtlehead or balmony, is an erect perennial herb in the family Plantaginaceae native to [[childrens-herbal-medicine]], particularly as a bitter tonic, laxative, and vermifuge. The species was an important remedy in both [[north-carolina-mycodiversity]]. Conservation efforts focus on wetland protection and restoration.

## Cultivation and Uses

Chelone glabra is cultivated as an ornamental perennial for moist garden sites, rain gardens, and native plant landscapes. The late bloom period fills an important niche in the perennial border.

Propagation by seed requires cold stratification (60 days at 4°C) for reliable germination. Division of established clumps in early spring is the preferred method.

Seeds are very small and should be surface-sown, as they require light for germination. Germination rates are typically 40–60% with proper stratification.

The plant spreads slowly by short rhizomes, forming tidy clumps rather than aggressive colonies. Space plants 45–60 cm apart for best effect.

Few serious pests affect Chelone glabra in cultivation. Occasional problems include [[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]] in humid conditions and slug damage on young shoots. Good air circulation minimizes disease issues.

In herbal medicine, the aerial parts are used as a bitter tonic to stimulate digestion and appetite. The plant was official in the United States Pharmacopeia from 1820–1890.

Traditional uses include treatment of jaundice, liver complaints, gallbladder dysfunction, and intestinal parasites. The Cherokee used a decoction as a tonic and laxative.

The bitter principle stimulates bile flow and digestive secretions. Modern herbalists continue to use it for sluggish digestion and hepatic support.

A tincture of the fresh plant is the most common preparation, typically dosed at 1–3 mL three times daily before meals. The herb combines well with other bitters such as gentian and dandelion root.

## Ecological Role

Chelone glabra is the primary larval host plant for the Baltimore checkerspot butterfly (Euphydryas phaeton). Caterpillars feed on the leaves and overwinter in leaf nests at the base of the plant.

Flowers are pollinated almost exclusively by bumblebees (Bombus spp.), which are large enough to force open the corolla tube. The late bloom period provides critical pre-hibernation nectar resources.

Honeybees occasionally visit the flowers but are less effective pollinators due to their smaller size. The flower structure effectively excludes smaller visitors, ensuring pollination by robust bumblebee species.
