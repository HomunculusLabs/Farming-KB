# Capsicum frutescens

## Overview

**Capsicum frutescens** is a species of chili pepper native to Central and South America, characterized by its small, erect fruits and intense pungency.

The species is best known for producing **tabasco peppers**, the raw material for the iconic Tabasco brand hot sauce.

Plants typically grow as compact, bushy perennials in tropical climates and are among the most widely cultivated hot peppers in equatorial regions.

*C. frutescens* is closely related to *C. chinense* and the two species share many morphological features, leading to frequent taxonomic confusion.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Family:** Solanaceae
- **Genus:** Capsicum
- **Species:** *C. frutescens* L.

**Botanical authority:** Carl Linnaeus, 1753.

The taxonomic boundary between *C. frutescens* and *C. chinense* has been debated extensively, with some authorities treating them as a single species complex.

Molecular phylogenetic studies confirm them as distinct but sister species within the *Capsicum annuum* complex.

**Morphology:**

Plants are perennial shrubs reaching 60–120 cm in height, with a compact, highly branched growth habit.

Leaves are small, ovate to elliptic, 3–8 cm long, with a smooth to slightly pubescent surface.

Flowers are white to greenish-white, borne in clusters of two or more at each node — a key distinguishing feature from *C. annuum*.

Fruits are small, erect, and conical or ellipsoidal, typically 2–5 cm long and 0.5–1.5 cm in diameter.

The erect fruit orientation is one of the most reliable field identification characters for the species.

 immature fruits are green or yellowish-green, ripening to bright red, orange, or yellow depending on the cultivar.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is believed to have originated in the Amazon basin region of South America, with a secondary center of diversity in Central America.

Wild populations are distributed from northern South America through Central America and into the Caribbean islands.

It has been introduced pantropically and is now naturalized throughout Southeast Asia, Africa, and the Pacific Islands.

The species thrives in hot, humid tropical lowlands from sea level to approximately 1,500 meters elevation.

Naturalized populations are particularly common in West Africa, where the species has been cultivated for centuries.

The plant prefers well-drained soils in open, sunny locations but tolerates partial shade in forest margins.

## Culinary Use and Cultural Significance

*C. frutescens* is the primary pepper species used in many tropical cuisines, particularly in Southeast Asia and West Africa.

The **Tabasco pepper** cultivar is the most commercially significant, grown extensively on Avery Island, Louisiana, and used exclusively in Tabasco sauce production.

Tabasco peppers rate 30,000–50,000 SHU and are valued for their clean, forward heat without excessive fruitiness.

In Thai cuisine, *C. frutescens* (locally known as *prik khee nu*) is the standard everyday chili, used in curries, sauces, and condiments.

African bird's eye pepper (sometimes confused with *C. annuum* varieties) is used extensively in West African soups and stews.

In Brazil, the species is known as *malagueta* and is a fundamental ingredient in the national cuisine, especially in the state of Bahia.

The peppers are commonly preserved in vinegar or oil, or dried and ground into powders for long-term storage.

## Cultivation

*C. frutescens* is cultivated as a perennial in tropical zones and as an annual in subtropical and temperate regions.

Seeds germinate readily at 25–30°C, with seedling emergence typically within 7–10 days.

Plants require warm conditions throughout the growing season, with optimal temperatures of 25–35°C for vegetative growth and fruit set.

The species is particularly sensitive to cold and suffers chilling injury below 10°C.

Fruit production begins 80–100 days after transplanting and continues for several months under tropical conditions.

Yields range from 10–20 tonnes per hectare depending on cultivar, climate, and management practices.

The species is relatively pest-resistant compared to *C. annuum* but can be affected by anthracnose fruit rot and viral diseases.

## Bioactive Compounds and Nutrition

Capsaicinoid content is high, with total capsaicinoids ranging from 0.3% to 1.0% dry weight.

The primary capsaicinoids are capsaicin and dihydrocapsaicin, with nordihydrocapsaicin present in smaller quantities.

Carotenoid content includes capsanthin, β-carotene, and zeaxanthin, contributing to the bright red coloration of ripe fruits.

Vitamin C content is moderately high at 100–200 mg per 100 g fresh weight.

The species contains notable levels of flavonoids, including luteolin and apigenin derivatives.

Essential oil composition includes limonene, linalool, and various short-chain fatty acid esters contributing to the characteristic aroma.
