The species is recognized by its purple flowers with reflexed petals and bright red, ovoid berries that are toxic to humans and livestock. The common name "bittersweet" refers to the distinctive taste of the stem, which is bitter at first but becomes sweet when chewed.

Solanum dulcamara contains glycoalkaloids including solanine, solasodine, and dulcamarine. These compounds have been investigated for antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antineoplastic properties, though clinical evidence remains limited. All parts of the plant are considered toxic if ingested in sufficient quantity.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Solanum dulcamara L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum. The specific epithet "dulcamara" derives from Latin "dulcis" (sweet) and "amarus" (bitter), describing the characteristic taste of the woody stem base.

The species belongs to the large genus Solanum, which contains approximately 1,500 species distributed worldwide. It is placed in the subgenus Leptostemonum or section Dulcamara, depending on the taxonomic treatment used.

The plant is a perennial, deciduous climbing vine that typically reaches 1–3 meters in length. The stem becomes woody at the base, with younger growth remaining herbaceous and flexible. It scrambles over supporting vegetation using twining petioles.

Leaves are alternate, simple, 4–10 cm long, with a hastate or cordate base and an acuminate tip. The leaf margin is typically entire or slightly lobed at the base. The upper surface is dark green and glabrous, while the lower surface may have sparse pubescence along the veins.

Flowers are produced in pendant cymes of 3–20 flowers. Individual flowers are 10–15 mm in diameter, with five purple petals that are strongly reflexed backward. The prominent yellow anthers form a cone in the center of the flower.

The fruit is an ovoid to ellipsoid berry, 8–12 mm long, ripening from green to bright glossy red. Each berry contains 20–60 flat, disc-shaped seeds approximately 2–3 mm in diameter. The berries remain on the plant through winter in mild climates.

## Cultivation and Uses

Solanum dulcamara has a long history of use in European traditional medicine dating back to Dioscorides and Pliny the Elder. Preparations from the aerial parts were used to treat [[blesching-cannabis-apoptosis-and-cancer-cell-death]] lines in vitro.

The species is occasionally grown as an ornamental vine for its attractive flowers and berries. However, the bright red fruit poses a poisoning risk to children, and the plant is generally discouraged in gardens frequented by young children or pets.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

The primary toxic constituents of Solanum dulcamara are the steroidal glycoalkaloids solanine and solasodine. These compounds are structurally related to the glycoalkaloids found in potatoes and other Solanaceae crops.

Solasodine is the predominant glycoalkaloid in Solanum dulcamara, accompanied by smaller amounts of solamargine and solasonine. Total glycoalkaloid content ranges from 0.1% to 0.4% in dried aerial parts, with higher concentrations in unripe berries and leaves.

Glycoalkaloids exert their toxicity by disrupting cell membranes. They intercalate into cholesterol-containing membranes, compromising membrane integrity and causing cell lysis. This mechanism underlies both their antimicrobial activity and their toxicity to mammalian cells.

Symptoms of poisoning include gastrointestinal distress (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain), headache, dizziness, and in severe cases, neurological symptoms including hallucinations and paralysis. Fatalities are rare in adults but have been reported in children who consumed the attractive red berries.

Antimicrobial studies have demonstrated activity against various bacteria and fungi, including [[bloomfield-candida-albicans-yeast-hyphal-dimorphism]]. Anti-inflammatory effects have been observed in animal models, though these have not translated to validated clinical applications.

## Ecological Role

Solanum dulcamara serves as a food source for several insect herbivores that have adapted to tolerate its glycoalkaloid defenses. The potato aphid (Macrosiphum euphorbiae) and the three-lined potato beetle (Lema trilineata) both feed on the foliage.

The berries are consumed by some bird species, particularly thrushes and blackbirds, which disperse the seeds through their droppings. Mammalian herbivores generally avoid the plant due to its bitter taste, though livestock poisoning has been documented when other forage is scarce.

The species plays a notable ecological role as a reservoir host for the potato late blight pathogen [[aact-banana-cultivation-tropical-crop-disease-suppression-ingham]].
