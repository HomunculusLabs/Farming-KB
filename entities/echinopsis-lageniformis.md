## Overview

**Echinopsis lageniformis** (Forst.) H. Friedrich & G.D. Rowley, commonly known as the **Bolivian torch** cactus, is a fast-growing columnar cactus native to the Bolivian Andes. It is recognized as one of the four primary mescaline-containing *Echinopsis* (formerly *Trichocereus*) species, alongside *E. pachanoi* (San Pedro), *E. peruviana* (Peruvian torch), and *E. bridgesii* (Achuma).

The species has been used traditionally in Andean shamanic ceremonies for centuries and remains important in both indigenous spiritual practice and modern ethnobotanical contexts. It is valued by cultivators for its rapid growth, cold tolerance, and high mescaline content.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Division: Angiospermae
- Class: Magnoliopsida
- Order: Caryophyllales
- Family: Cactaceae
- Subfamily: Cactoideae
- Tribe: Trichocereeae
- Genus: *Echinopsis*
- Species: *E. lageniformis* (Forst.) H.Friedrich & G.D.Rowley

**Synonyms:** *Cereus lageniformis* Forst., *Trichocereus lageniformis* (Forst.) Britton & Rose, *Trichocereus bridgesii* Salm-Dyck (contested — see notes).

**Note on nomenclature:** There is ongoing [[neurotropic-fungi-taxonomic-confusion-and-identification]] between *E. lageniformis* and *E. bridgesii*. Some authorities treat them as conspecific, while others maintain them as separate species. The cactus sold commercially as "Bolivian torch" is generally *E. lageniformis*.

**Stem:** Columnar, upright, 3–5 m tall in habitat (smaller in cultivation), 8–12 cm diameter. Surface is green to blue-green with 4–8 ribs (typically 4–6). Ribs are broad and rounded, with areoles spaced 1.5–2.5 cm apart.

**Spines:** Variable; some specimens are nearly spineless, while others bear 1–4 yellowish-brown spines per areole, 1–4 cm long. Spination tends to decrease with age.

**Flowers:** Large, white, nocturnal, 18–22 cm long, borne near the stem apex. Flowers are fragrant and funnel-shaped, typical of the *Trichocereeae*. Fruit is ovoid, green, 3–5 cm, splitting open when ripe.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to the Bolivian departments of La Paz, Cochabamba, and Santa Cruz, where it grows at elevations of 2000–3000 m in dry inter-Andean valleys. It is found on rocky slopes, cliff faces, and disturbed ground along river valleys.

The species tolerates temperatures to -10°C when dry, making it one of the most cold-hardy psychoactive cacti. In habitat, it receives strong ultraviolet exposure, intense daily temperature fluctuations, and seasonal dry periods. It is adapted to well-drained, mineral-rich volcanic soils with low organic content.

## Bioactive Compounds

The primary psychoactive alkaloid is **mescaline** (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine). Reported mescaline concentrations vary significantly by specimen, population, and growing conditions:

- **Total mescaline:** 0.3–2.0% dry weight (typically 0.5–1.2%)
- **Fresh tissue:** Approximately 0.05–0.1% mescaline

Additional alkaloids detected include 3-methoxytyramine, tyramine, hordenine, and trace amounts of anhalonidine and anhalinine. These minor alkaloids may modulate the subjective experience but do not contribute significant psychoactivity at typical doses.

Alkaloid content is highest in the outer green cortical layer (chlorenchyma) and lowest in the central pith. Specimens grown in full sun with moderate water stress tend to produce higher alkaloid concentrations than shade-grown, heavily watered plants.

## Cultivation

*E. lageniformis* is among the easiest psychoactive cacti to cultivate. Growth rates of 30–60 cm per year are achievable under optimal conditions.

**Propagation:** Primarily from seed, though cuttings root readily. Cuttings should be allowed to callus for 2–4 weeks before planting in well-drained substrate. Seed germination requires warmth (25–30°C) and light; germination occurs in 1–4 weeks.

**Growing conditions:** Full sun to partial shade. Standard cactus soil mix (50% mineral grit, 50% organic). Water thoroughly during active growth (spring–autumn), withhold water during winter dormancy. Fertilize monthly during [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] with low-nitrogen cactus fertilizer.

**Pests:** Susceptible to mealybugs, scale insects, and spider mites. Root rot is the most common problem, caused by overwatering or poor drainage.

## Ethnobotanical and Cultural Significance

The Bolivian torch has documented use in indigenous Andean ceremonial contexts, particularly among mestizo curanderos (healers) in Bolivia. It is used in healing ceremonies (*mesadas*) that typically involve nighttime ritual, music, and purification practices.

The species is less well-documented ethnographically than *E. pachanoi*, partly due to Bolivia's more restrictive access for foreign researchers during much of the 20th century. Nevertheless, its ceremonial use is confirmed by multiple ethnographic sources and continues in contemporary Bolivian traditional medicine.

In the [[shamanic-vs-western-psychedelic-healing]] community, *E. lageniformis* is valued as a mescaline source due to its potency and lower incidence of nausea compared to *E. pachanoi* preparations. The [[kava-traditional-preparation-ceremonial-context]] method involves boiling sections of the cactus for several hours to produce a bitter tea.

## Comparison with Related Mescaline Cacti

*E. lageniformis* occupies a distinct niche among the four major psychoactive *Echinopsis* species:

- **Versus E. pachanoi (San Pedro):** Bolivian torch typically contains higher mescaline concentrations and produces a more intense experience per gram of material. It has fewer ribs (4–6 vs. 6–8) and is more cold-tolerant. However, San Pedro grows faster in cultivation and is more widely available as a nursery plant.
- **Versus E. peruviana (Peruvian torch):** Similar mescaline content, but *E. lageniformis* has a more slender profile and tends to be nearly spineless where *E. peruviana* often bears prominent spines. Peruvian torch is considered slightly easier to source from seed vendors.
- **Versus E. bridgesii:** The taxonomic distinction between these two species remains contested. In practice, cacti sold as "bridgesii" tend to be shorter, more heavily spined, and may have slightly different alkaloid ratios. Both are Bolivian in origin.

## Legal Status

- **United States:** Legal to cultivate; mescaline is Schedule I under the Controlled [[controlled-substances-act-and-psychedelic-research]]. The cactus itself is not scheduled, but extraction of mescaline constitutes a federal offense.
- **United Kingdom:** Legal to grow and possess; [[gottlieb-peyote-extraction-and-preparation-methods]] for consumption is controlled under the Psychoactive Substances Act 2016.
- **Australia:** *Echinopsis* species are not specifically scheduled, though some states restrict cultivation. Mescaline is a Schedule 9 prohibited substance.
- **Bolivia:** Protected under national biodiversity law. Wild collection is restricted; cultivated specimens are legal.
