## Overview

**Echinopsis lageniformis** (Forst.) H. Friedrich & G.D. Rowley, commonly known as the **Bolivian torch** cactus, is a fast-growing columnar cactus native to the Bolivian Andes. It is recognized as one of the four primary mescaline-containing *Echinopsis* (formerly *Trichocereus*) species, alongside *E. pachanoi* (San Pedro), *E. peruviana* ([[trichocereus-bridgesii]]* Salm-Dyck (contested — see notes).

**Note on nomenclature:** There is ongoing [[peyote-and-psychoactive-cacti]]. In habitat, it receives strong ultraviolet exposure, intense daily temperature fluctuations, and seasonal dry periods. It is adapted to well-drained, mineral-rich volcanic soils with low organic content.

## Bioactive Compounds

The primary psychoactive alkaloid is **mescaline** (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine). Reported mescaline concentrations vary significantly by specimen, population, and growing conditions:

- **Total mescaline:** 0.3–2.0% dry weight (typically 0.5–1.2%)
- **Fresh tissue:** Approximately 0.05–0.1% mescaline

Additional alkaloids detected include 3-methoxytyramine, tyramine, hordenine, and trace amounts of anhalonidine and anhalinine. These minor alkaloids may modulate the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] with low-nitrogen cactus fertilizer.

**Pests:** Susceptible to mealybugs, scale insects, and spider mites. Root rot is the most common problem, caused by overwatering or poor drainage.

## Ethnobotanical and Cultural Significance

The Bolivian torch has documented use in indigenous Andean ceremonial contexts, particularly among mestizo curanderos (healers) in Bolivia. It is used in healing ceremonies (*mesadas*) that typically involve nighttime ritual, music, and purification practices.

The species is less well-documented ethnographically than *E. pachanoi*, partly due to Bolivia's more restrictive access for foreign researchers during much of the 20th century. Nevertheless, its ceremonial use is confirmed by multiple ethnographic sources and continues in contemporary Bolivian traditional medicine.

In the [[kava-traditional-preparation-ceremonial-context]] method involves boiling sections of the cactus for several hours to produce a bitter tea.
