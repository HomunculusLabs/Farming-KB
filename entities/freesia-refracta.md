## Overview

*Freesia refracta* (Jacquin) Klatt is a cormous perennial in the family Iridaceae, native to the winter-rainfall region of the Western Cape of [[table-mountain-south-africa-mollison]]. It is one of the parent species of the cultivated freesias widely sold as cut flowers and garden ornamentals.

The species is renowned for its intensely fragrant, tubular flowers that bloom in one-sided racemes along slender, arching stems. The scent is sweet, citrus-like, and among the most recognizable of all floral fragrances in the horticultural trade.

Wild-type flowers are white to pale yellow with golden-yellow markings on the lower tepal. Modern cultivated freesias, derived largely from hybrids of *Freesia refracta* and *Freesia leichtlinii*, come in white, yellow, pink, red, purple, and blue.

The plant grows 15–30 cm tall with narrow, sword-shaped leaves. It is winter-growing and summer-dormant, adapted to the Mediterranean climate of its native range.

Unlike many Iridaceae, freesias produce a characteristic branched stem that gives the inflorescence its distinctive arching form. The branching pattern is a key diagnostic feature that distinguishes *Freesia* from related genera such as *Lapeirousia* and *Anomatheca*.

The genus name honors Friedrich Heinrich Theodor Freese (1795–1876), a German physician who studied southern African flora.

The species was among the first [[cannabis-strain-swazi-landrace-south-african-sativa-oner]] Iridaceae to reach European gardens, arriving via the plant trade routes that connected Cape Town to botanical gardens in London, Paris, and Leiden during the 1780s.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Freesia refracta* belongs to the tribe Freesieae within Iridaceae, a small tribe endemic to southern Africa. The genus *Freesia* comprises approximately 16 species, all native to South Africa, with the center of diversity in the Western Cape.

The species was first described by Nikolaus Joseph von Jacquin as *Gladiolus refractus* in 1804 and transferred to *Freesia* by Friedrich Klatt in 1866.

The transfer was part of a broader taxonomic revision that recognized *Freesia* as distinct from *Gladiolus* based on the characteristic one-sided raceme and zygomorphic flower structure. The epithet *refracta* refers to the sharply bent angle of the flowering stem.

Corms are ovoid, 1–2 cm in diameter, covered by a fibrous brown tunic. Each corm produces a fan of 4–7 narrow, equitant leaves 10–25 cm long and 3–6 mm wide.

The inflorescence is a strongly inclined to horizontal raceme bearing 5–8 flowers. Each flower is tubular, 3–4 cm long, with a slender perianth tube that flares into six tepal lobes. The lower three tepals bear prominent yellow or orange markings.

Flowers are strongly zygomorphic, with the upper tepals curving forward to form a hood. Three stamens cluster asymmetrically on the lower side.

Capsules are ovoid, 8–10 mm long, containing 6–10 angular, light brown seeds. The chromosome number is 2n = 22, consistent across the genus.

The species can be distinguished from *Freesia leichtlinii* by its smaller flowers (3–4 cm vs. 4–5 cm) and more strongly inclined inflorescence. Hybrids between the two show intermediate characteristics and increased vigor.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Freesia refracta* is endemic to the Western Cape Province of South Africa, from the Cape Peninsula eastward to Caledon and Bredasdorp. It grows in the fynbos biome, one of the world's most botanically diverse vegetation types.

Populations occur on damp, sandy flats and slopes at low elevations (10–300 m), often in seasonally wet sites near streams and seeps. The species prefers acidic, nutrient-poor soils derived from [[table-mountain]] Sandstone.

The climate is Mediterranean with cool, wet winters and warm, dry summers. Corms sprout with the first autumn rains (April–May), grow through winter, flower in August–September, and enter dormancy by November.

The species has naturalized widely outside South Africa, particularly in coastal California, the Mediterranean basin, Australia, and New Zealand. It is considered a minor environmental weed in parts of Australia where it forms dense colonies along roadsides and in coastal heathlands. In New Zealand, naturalized populations compete with native geophytes for pollinator attention during spring flowering.

It was first introduced to European cultivation in the late 18th century and became a fashionable glasshouse subject in Victorian England.

Despite its wide cultivation, wild populations of *Freesia refracta* face pressure from urban expansion and agriculture in the Western Cape. Several subpopulations near Cape Town have been extirpated by housing development. The species is not currently listed as threatened. By the early 1900s, Dutch hybridizers had produced the large-flowered cultivars that dominate the modern market.

## Cultivation

Freesias require well-drained, sandy soil with a slightly acidic pH (6.0–6.5). Corms are planted in autumn, 4–5 cm deep and 5–8 cm apart, in full sun to light afternoon shade.

Regular watering is essential during active growth from autumn through spring. The soil should be kept consistently moist but never waterlogged. After flowering, reduce watering gradually to allow the corms to enter summer dormancy.

Flowering occurs in late winter to spring, approximately 120–140 days after planting. Each corm produces one main flowering stem with strongly fragrant flowers that last 7–10 days individually.

For cut flower production, freesias are grown in controlled environments at 12–18°C. Lower night temperatures promote stronger stems and more intense flower color. Commercial growers use [[query-how-do-i-grow-figs-in-cold-climates]], corms can be started indoors in pots in late summer for winter flowering. Container-grown freesias benefit from a loose, well-aerated [[solomon-potting-mix-and-seedling-soil-recipe]] of equal parts peat, perlite, and coarse sand.

Propagation is by corm offsets or seed. Named cultivars are propagated vegetatively to maintain flower color and form. Seed-grown plants are variable and take two to three years to flower.

Fresh seeds germinate more reliably than stored seeds, with germination rates exceeding 80% when sown within six months of collection.

Commercial freesia production is centered in the Netherlands, Japan, and California. Global annual production exceeds 200 million stems, making freesias one of the most economically important flower crops.

Common problems include botrytis during humid, cool weather and aphids on emerging flower buds. Thrips can damage flowers in greenhouse environments.

## Ecology and Uses

In its native fynbos habitat, *Freesia refracta* is pollinated by long-proboscid flies in the family Nemestrinidae and by sunbirds. The tubular flower shape and intense fragrance are adaptations for these specialized pollinators.
