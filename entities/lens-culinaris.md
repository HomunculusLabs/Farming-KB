Lentils are a dietary staple in South Asia, the Middle East, North Africa,
and the Mediterranean. Global production exceeds 6 million tonnes annually,
with Canada, India, Australia, and Turkey as leading producers.

The crop is exceptionally adapted to dryland farming. Its ability to fix
nitrogen and produce protein-rich seed on minimal rainfall makes it a
cornerstone of sustainable food systems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Tracheophyta
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Fabales
- **Family:** Fabaceae
- **Subfamily:** Faboideae
- **Genus:** Lens
- **Species:** L. culinaris Medik.

The genus *Lens* comprises 6–7 species. The wild progenitor is *L.
orientalis*, still found in the eastern Mediterranean. Four market classes
exist: large green, small green, red (split), and brown/charcoal.

### Physical Description

Lentil is a slender, semi-erect annual [[allegro-plants-and-drugs-in-the-ancient-world]], consumed in Egypt, Greece,
and Rome, and referenced in the Bible and Quran. Today, lentils are grown on
every inhabited continent, primarily between latitudes 30°N and 50°N.

Canada is the world's largest exporter, India the largest consumer. Major
production also occurs in Turkey, Australia, the US, and Ethiopia.

## Cultivation

Lentils perform best at 15–25°C with 250–400 mm annual rainfall. Sowing
occurs in autumn (Mediterranean climates) or [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] (temperate zones)
at seed rates of 40–80 kg/ha.

The crop matures in 80–120 days. Average yields range from 800–1,500 kg/ha,
with experimental plots reaching 3,000 kg/ha. Harvest is typically by
swathing and combining due to uneven indeterminate maturity.
