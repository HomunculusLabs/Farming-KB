# Oxalic Acid (Fungal)

## Description

[[oxalic-acid]] is a simple dicarboxylic acid (C₂H₂O₄) secreted by many fungi as a
metabolite and weathering agent. It plays a crucial role in mineral weathering
and nutrient mobilization within soil ecosystems. As one of the strongest
organic acids produced by fungi, it serves as a primary mechanism by which
fungal organisms interact with and transform the mineral components of their
environment.

Fungi secrete oxalic acid through their hyphal tips into the surrounding
substrate, where it acts on mineral surfaces and soil particles. This process
is fundamental to biogeochemical cycling and the long-term development of soil
from parent rock material.

## Classification

- **Category:** Chemical
- **Subcategory:** Organic acid
- **Type:** Fungal metabolite
- **Chemical formula:** C₂H₂O₄
- **Functional group:** Dicarboxylic acid
- **pKa values:** pKa₁ ≈ 1.25, pKa₂ ≈ 4.14
- **Molecular weight:** 90.03 g/mol

## Key Facts

### Mineral Weathering

- Secreted by [[fungal-hyphae]] to assist in the weathering of calcareous bedrock,
  directly dissolving calcium carbonate and releasing embedded nutrients
- Facilitates dissolution of calcium carbonate and other minerals through
  acid-mediated breakdown of crystalline structures
- Acts as a key agent in biological weathering processes, complementing
  physical and chemical weathering in natural ecosystems
- Contributes to soil formation from parent rock material over geological
  and ecological timescales

### Nutrient Mobilization

- Chelates metal ions (Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺, Al³⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺), making them
  available for fungal and plant uptake
- Produces soluble metal-oxalate complexes that can be transported through
  hyphal networks over considerable distances
- Enhances [[phosphorus-availability]] by dissolving phosphate-bearing minerals
  that would otherwise remain inaccessible

### Fungal Producers

- Produced by **[[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]]**, which use it to access mineral nutrients
  for both themselves and their plant hosts
- Secreted by **[[saprotrophic-fungi]]** during decomposition of organic matter
- Produced by **wood-rotting fungi** (brown rot and white rot) during
  lignocellulose degradation, assisting in the breakdown of recalcitrant
  plant cell wall components

### Crystal Formation and Metal Interactions

- Involved in **calcium oxalate crystal** formation on and within fungal hyphae;
  these crystals are among the most common biominerals in soils
- Plays a significant role in **heavy metal tolerance** by immobilizing toxic
  metals (Pb, Cd, Cu, Zn) as insoluble oxalate salts, reducing their
  bioavailability and toxicity
- Lowers pH in microenvironments around hyphae to optimize enzyme activity,
  creating localized acidic zones that enhance nutrient solubilization

### Ecological Significance

- The secretion of oxalic acids represents one of the primary mechanisms by
  which fungi make nutrients available from mineral sources in soil
- Oxalic acid production interacts synergistically with other fungal metabolites
  and enzymes to drive ecosystem-level nutrient cycling

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Substrate pH modulation:** Oxalic acid production directly affects substrate
  pH, which in turn influences mushroom growth rates, fruiting body formation,
  and overall culture health
- **Mineral-supplemented substrates:** Understanding fungal weathering capacity
  helps in formulating mineral-supplemented substrates that leverage the fungus's
  natural ability to solubilize and absorb mineral nutrients
- **Heavy metal management:** Oxalic acid's metal chelation properties are
  relevant to managing heavy metal contamination in growing media; fungi can
  immobilize toxic metals as oxalate crystals, potentially improving substrate
  safety
- **Mycelial health indicator:** Calcium oxalate crystals visible on hyphae
  under microscopy can serve as indicators of healthy, metabolically active
  mycelial growth
- **Species competitiveness:** Acid production capacity varies significantly
  between fungal species and strains, affecting their competitiveness on
  substrates and their success in mixed-culture or outdoor cultivation
  environments
- **Brown rot cultivation:** Species such as *Laetiporus sulphureus* and
  *Fomitopsis betulina* rely heavily on oxalic acid for wood decay strategies;
  substrate preparation should account for their acid production requirements
- **Mycorrhizal inoculum:** For ectomycorrhizal cultivation, oxalic acid
  production influences the ability of inoculated fungi to establish nutrient-
  exchanging relationships with host plant root systems
