## Humus

## Description

Humus is the dark, organic material formed in soil from the decayed, compressed, and fossil remains of once-living organisms. It is one of the five fundamental components of soil identified by Mollison, alongside minerals, soil waters, gases, and life forms. Humus is the cornerstone of soil fertility, water retention, nutrient cycling, and overall soil health. It is the product of decomposition that has reached a relatively stable state resistant to further rapid breakdown.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil component / Organic matter
- **Type**: Complex mixture of organic compounds
- **Composition**: Humic acids, fulvic acids, humins, and associated minerals
- **Color**: Dark brown to black
- **Location**: Concentrated in the upper 6-12 cm of most soils (the living zone)

## Key Facts

- One of the five fundamental components of soil: minerals, soil waters, gases, life forms, and once-living remains (humus)
- The dark color of productive topsoil is primarily due to humus content
- Tribal and traditional soil classifications use color as an indicator of humus content
- Soils with high humus content have superior [[cation-exchange-capacity]] (CEC) far exceeding that of clay minerals alone
- Estimated topsoil builds at 2-4 tonnes per hectare per year under natural conditions
- Cropping and tillage removes 40-500 tonnes per year of topsoil
- The only places where soils are conserved or increased: uncut forests, quiet water, permanent plant meadows, and mulched non-tillage systems

## Formation Process

1. **Fresh organic matter**: Plant and animal residues added to soil surface and root zone
2. **Decomposition**: Microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, actinomycetes) break down fresh material
3. **Partial decomposition**: Some resistant compounds remain (lignin, waxes, chitin)
4. **Humification**: Further microbial processing creates stable, complex organic compounds
5. **Humus**: The end product is a stable, dark, colloidal material resistant to rapid decomposition

## Functions in Soil

### Physical Functions
- Binds soil particles into stable aggregates ([[crumb-structure]])
- Creates and maintains pore space for air and water movement
- Reduces soil compaction and improves tilth
- Acts as a glue holding soil structure open during rain events
- Reduces erosion by improving aggregate stability

### Chemical Functions
- Provides high cation exchange capacity (CEC) for nutrient retention
- Buffers [[soil-ph]] against rapid changes
- Chelates (binds) micronutrients, making them available to plants
- Stores nutrients in organic forms resistant to leaching
- Decomposes to release nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and trace elements slowly

### Biological Functions
- Provides energy source for soil microorganisms and soil fauna
- Supports diverse communities of bacteria, fungi, and soil fauna
- Contains natural antibiotics that may protect plants from some pathogens
- Supports mycorrhizal networks essential for nutrient uptake
- Earthworms and soil biota thrive in humus-rich environments

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Mushroom Cultivation
- Humus-rich substrates support diverse microbial communities beneficial for mushroom growth
- The organic component of compost for mushroom production is essentially managed humification
- Understanding humus formation is essential for preparing quality mushroom substrates
- Mycelial networks contribute directly to humus formation through wood and litter decomposition

### Permaculture Soil Building
- Building humus is the primary goal of permaculture soil management
- Achieved through mulching, composting, cover cropping, and minimum tillage
- Gardeners who develop sustainable soil systems allow 3-4 years for building a garden
- 5-15 years may be needed to restore a devastated soil landscape
- Organic materials and gels hold the soil structure open where plant nutrients become soluble

### Composting for Humus Production
- Composting is the controlled acceleration of humification
- Well-made compost is essentially stabilized humus with active microbial populations
- Vermicomposting (worm composting) produces especially high-quality humus
- Sheet composting and green manuring build humus directly in the soil profile
