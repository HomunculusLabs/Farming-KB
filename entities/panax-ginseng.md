# Panax ginseng

**Panax ginseng** C.A. Meyer is a slow-growing perennial herbaceous plant in the family Araliaceae, widely cultivated for its fleshy root, which has been used as a medicinal substance for over two millennia in East Asia. Commonly known as Korean ginseng or Asian ginseng, it is one of the most extensively studied adaptogenic botanicals in modern pharmacology. The genus name *Panax* derives from the Greek word *panakos* ("all-healing"), reflecting its historical reputation as a panacea.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes, Angiosperms, Eudicots, Asterids
- **Order:** Apiales
- **Family:** Araliaceae
- **Genus:** *Panax*
- **Species:** *P. ginseng*
- **Authority:** Carl Anton von Meyer, 1842

The genus *Panax* comprises approximately 12 species, including *P. quinquefolius* (American ginseng) and *P. notoginseng* (Notoginseng or Tienchi ginseng). *Panax ginseng* is distinct from *Eleutherococcus senticosus* (Siberian ginseng), which belongs to a different genus entirely despite the shared common name.

## Morphology

**Root:** The root is the primary organ of commercial and medicinal interest. Mature roots are fleshy, spindle-shaped or branched (often anthropomorphically described), and typically weigh 30–100 g after six years of growth. The outer surface is light yellowish-brown with distinct annular ridges and fine longitudinal wrinkles. Fresh roots are crisp and white internally; processed roots vary in color depending on the preparation method. The characteristic forked or "human-shaped" root form commands premium market prices, particularly in East Asian markets.

**Stem and Leaves:** Plants reach 30–70 cm in height at maturity. The palmately compound leaves are arranged in a single whorl at the apex of the stem. Each leaf typically bears five (occasionally three to seven) serrated leaflets, each 5–15 cm long and 2–6 cm wide, with an elliptic-lanceolate shape. Leaf color is dark green during the growing season, turning yellow before senescence in autumn. The number of leaf prongs increases with plant age: one-prong plants at year one, increasing to a typical maximum of five or six prongs by years five and six.

**Flowers and Berries:** A single terminal umbel of small, greenish-white flowers blooms in June and July. The umbel typically bears 30–50 pedicellate flowers, each with five petals and five stamens. Pollination is primarily entomophilous. The fruit is a small (5–8 mm diameter) drupe that matures from green to bright crimson in August and September. Each berry contains two to three reniform seeds, approximately 4–5 mm in length. Seeds exhibit deep morphophysiological dormancy and require 18–22 months of stratification for reliable germination.

## Growth Requirements

Panax ginseng is an understory plant with narrow ecological tolerances. Successful cultivation demands precise environmental management:

- **Climate:** Temperate zones (30–48°N latitude). Optimal annual mean temperature of 5–12 °C with cold winters (below -10 °C for required vernalization) and warm, moist summers. The plant requires approximately 1,400–1,800 growing degree days.
- **Soil:** Well-drained, loamy soils rich in organic matter (3–8% humus content) with a slightly acidic to neutral pH of 5.5–6.5. Soils must be deep (≥ 30 cm), aerated, and free of waterlogging. Wild-simulated ginseng thrives in forest soils dominated by deciduous hardwood species such as *Quercus* spp. and *Acer* spp.
- **Shade:** The plant requires approximately 70–80% shade from direct sunlight, typically provided by natural forest canopy or artificial shade structures in commercial cultivation. Excessive light causes leaf scorch and reduced root quality.
- **Moisture:** Consistent soil moisture (40–60% field capacity) is critical. Drought stress during the growing season significantly reduces root biomass and ginsenoside accumulation.

## Active Compounds

The pharmacological activity of *Panax ginseng* is primarily attributed to a class of steroidal saponins known as **ginsenosides** (also called ginseng saponins or panaxosides). Over 180 ginsenosides have been identified to date. They are classified into two major structural groups based on their aglycone skeletons:

**Protopanaxadiol (PPD) group:** Includes Rb1, Rb2, Rc, Rd, Rg3, Rh2, and compound K. PPD-type ginsenosides tend to exhibit sedative, anti-inflammatory, and anti-tumor activities. Rb1 is the most abundant ginsenoside in fresh ginseng root, constituting 0.3–0.6% of dry root weight.

**Protopanaxatriol (PPT) group:** Includes Re, Rg1, Rg2, Rh1, and F1. PPT-type ginsenosides tend to exhibit stimulatory effects on the central nervous system and cardioprotective properties. Rg1 is considered the primary mediator of cognitive-enhancing effects.

Other notable bioactive constituents include polysaccharides (with demonstrated immunomodulatory activity), polyacetylenes (e.g., panaxynol, panaxydol with anti-inflammatory properties), flavonoids, essential oils, and various amino acids and minerals.

## Traditional Use

**Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM):** Ginseng has been a cornerstone of TCM for over 2,000 years, first documented in *Shennong Ben Cao Jing* (Divine Farmer's Materia Medica, circa 200 CE). Classified as a superior tonic (上品) that tonifies *qi* (vital energy), strengthens the spleen, nourishes the lungs, generates fluids, and calms the spirit. It is prescribed for fatigue, poor appetite, shortness of breath, chronic diarrhea, and generalized weakness. TCM emphasizes the importance of proper diagnosis before use, as ginseng is considered inappropriate for individuals with "heat" or "excess" conditions.

**Korean Medicine:** Ginseng is the single most important medicinal plant in Korean traditional medicine (韩医学). It is prescribed for restoring vitality (기/氣), improving stamina, and supporting recovery from illness. The Korean Peninsula has been the center of ginseng cultivation and trade for centuries, with Geumsan in South Chungcheong Province serving as the primary historical cultivation and trading center.

## Modern Clinical Evidence

**Cognitive Function:** Multiple randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses support modest cognitive-enhancing effects, particularly in middle-aged and older adults. A 2023 systematic review by Geng et al. found significant improvements in working memory, attention, and processing speed with standardized ginseng extract (200–400 mg/day, standardized to 4–7% ginsenosides) over 8–12 weeks of supplementation. Effects appear most pronounced during periods of mental fatigue.

**Immune Modulation:** Clinical and preclinical evidence indicates that ginseng polysaccharides and ginsenosides enhance both innate and adaptive immune responses. Studies have demonstrated increased NK cell activity, enhanced macrophage phagocytosis, and modulation of cytokine production (increased IL-2, IFN-γ). Epidemiological data from Korea suggest reduced incidence and duration of upper respiratory infections with regular ginseng consumption.

**Fatigue and Physical Performance:** Ginseng extracts have demonstrated anti-fatigue effects in controlled trials, with improvements in subjective fatigue scores, aerobic endurance, and recovery from exhaustive exercise. The proposed mechanisms include modulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, antioxidant activity, and improved mitochondrial function.

**Other Areas:** Evidence for glycemic regulation (modest reductions in fasting blood glucose in type 2 diabetes), erectile dysfunction, and menopausal symptom relief remains preliminary but promising.

## Cultivation and Harvesting

Commercial ginseng cultivation follows a rigid multi-year cycle. Seeds are typically sown in prepared beds in autumn at a density of 200–300 seeds per m². Germination occurs in the second spring after sowing due to embryo underdevelopment. Cultivation proceeds through the following stages:

- **Year 1:** Seedling establishment under heavy shade (80–85%). Roots are small (< 5 g).
- **Years 2–3:** Transplanting to wider spacing (20 × 10 cm). Progressive shade reduction to 70–75%. Routine fungicide applications are critical to prevent soil-borne diseases.
- **Years 4–6:** Final growth phase. Optimal root biomass and ginsenoside accumulation occur at year 6, which is the standard harvest age for premium ginseng in Korea.

Harvest typically occurs in September through October. Roots are carefully excavated by hand or mechanical diggers to avoid damage. Yields average 1.0–1.5 kg/m² of fresh root at year six.

## Processing Methods

**White Ginseng (백삼, Baeksam):** Fresh roots are washed, peeled (to remove the outer cortex, which contains oxidase enzymes), and dried in the sun or in low-temperature dehydrators (30–40 °C) over 2–4 weeks. The resulting product is pale yellowish-white with a moisture content below 14%. White ginseng retains a ginsenoside profile similar to fresh root, with higher proportions of Rb1, Rc, and Rd.

**Red Ginseng (홍삼, Hongsam):** Fresh roots (typically ≥ 6 years old) are steamed at 95–100 °C for 2–3 hours, then dried at 50–60 °C until moisture drops below 15%. The Maillard reaction during steaming converts color to reddish-brown and chemically transforms the ginsenoside profile: significant portions of Rb1, Rb2, and Rc are hydrolyzed into Rg3, Rh2, and compound K (rare ginsenosides with enhanced bioavailability and pharmacological activity). Red ginseng also contains unique malonyl-ginsenosides and additional antioxidant compounds generated during thermal processing.

## Pharmacokinetics

Ginsenosides exhibit generally poor oral bioavailability (1–20% depending on the compound). After ingestion, they undergo extensive biotransformation by intestinal microbiota: deglycosylation by bacterial β-glucosidases converts parent ginsenosides (e.g., Rb1) into more bioactive metabolites (e.g., compound K). Peak plasma concentrations of major ginsenosides occur 1–8 hours post-dose. Elimination is primarily via biliary and renal excretion, with half-lives ranging from 4 to 22 hours depending on the specific ginsenoside and its metabolic pathway. The high inter-individual variability in ginsenoside pharmacokinetics is largely attributable to differences in gut microbiome composition.

## Drug Interactions

Panax ginseng interacts with several classes of pharmaceuticals:

- **Anticoagulants/Antiplatelets:** May potentiate the effects of warfarin, aspirin, and clopidogrel, increasing bleeding risk. INR monitoring is recommended.
- **Antidiabetic agents:** May have additive hypoglycemic effects, necessitating blood glucose monitoring when used concurrently with insulin or oral hypoglycemics.
- **Immunosuppressants:** Potentially antagonistic with cyclosporine and other immunosuppressive drugs due to ginseng's immunostimulatory properties.
- **MAO inhibitors:** Possible additive stimulatory effects on the central nervous system.
- **Caffeine:** Concurrent use may increase stimulatory effects, potentially causing insomnia, tachycardia, or anxiety.

## Sustainability Concerns

Wild *Panax ginseng* (산삼, Sansam) is critically endangered throughout its native range in Northeast Asia (Korean Peninsula, Manchuria, Russian Far East). Overharvesting, habitat destruction, and the species' slow reproductive cycle have driven wild populations to near extinction. Wild ginseng is listed in Appendix II of CITES, restricting international trade.

Commercial cultivation in Korea (primarily in the provinces of Chungcheongbuk-do, Gangwon-do, and Jeolla) is managed under strict government regulations. However, long-term monoculture depletes soil nutrients and promotes soil-borne pathogens (particularly *Cylindrocarpon destructans*, the causal agent of root rot), necessitating extended fallow periods (10–15 years) between ginseng crops. This land-use pressure has driven interest in wild-simulated and forest-cultivated ginseng as more ecologically sustainable alternatives. The Korean government has also invested in *in vitro* tissue culture and hydroponic cultivation research to reduce dependence on field cultivation.

Demand for high-quality cultivated ginseng continues to grow globally, driven by rising consumer interest in botanical supplements and functional foods. The global ginseng market was valued at approximately USD 2.1 billion in 2024 and is projected to reach USD 3.5 billion by 2032. South Korea is the largest producer and exporter, followed by China and Canada (primarily producing *P. quinquefolius*). Quality grading systems, such as the Korean government's Geumsan standard, classify roots by shape, internal texture, ginsenoside content, and absence of defects, with top-grade roots selling for thousands of dollars per kilogram.

## Geographic Distribution and Conservation Status

The native range of *Panax ginseng* encompasses the Korean Peninsula, northeastern China (Jilin, Heilongjiang, and Liaoning provinces), and the Primorsky Krai region of the Russian Far East. The species prefers north-facing mountain slopes between 200 and 1,000 meters elevation in mixed deciduous-coniferous forests.

The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) classifies wild *Panax ginseng* as **Endangered** (EN A2acd) on the Red List, citing an estimated population decline of over 50% across three generations due to collection for medicinal trade and habitat degradation. In South Korea, wild ginseng is designated a protected species under the Wildlife Protection Act, and unauthorized harvesting carries severe legal penalties. Conservation efforts include habitat protection in designated national parks, germplasm banking, and controlled reintroduction programs.

## History and Cultural Significance

Ginseng has occupied a central position in East Asian cultural and economic life for centuries. The earliest known Chinese reference appears in the *Shennong Ben Cao Jing* (~200 BCE–200 CE), where it is ranked as the highest class of herbs. Korean historical records, including the *Samguk Sagi* (History of the Three Kingdoms, 1145 CE), document tribute payments of ginseng to Chinese imperial courts. During the Joseon Dynasty (1392–1897), the Korean government established a state monopoly on ginseng trade, and the plant became one of the kingdom's most valuable exports.

In the 18th century, Korean ginseng was introduced to Europe by Jesuit missionaries and became one of the first East Asian medicinal plants to gain widespread attention in Western pharmacopoeias. The species was formally described by the Russian botanist Carl Anton von Meyer in 1842 based on specimens collected in the Ussuri River basin of the Russian Far East.
