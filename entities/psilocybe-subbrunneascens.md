# Psilocybe subbrunneascens

**Psilocybe subbrunneascens** is a psilocybin mushroom in the family Hymenogastraceae, described from the highlands of Guatemala and subsequently reported from southern Mexico and Colombia. It was first formally described by gastón-guzmán in the 1980s during his extensive fieldwork on neurotropic fungi of Mesoamerica. The species is placed in Psilocybe section Mexicanae, a group noted for sclerotia production and preference for subtropical grassland habitats.

## Taxonomy

The species epithet *subbrunneascens* refers to the subbrowning coloration of the cap surface, a character that distinguishes it from the darker-capped [[psilocybe-mexicana]]. Guzmán treated it as a distinct species based on differences in cap coloration, spore size, and the intensity of the bluing reaction. Some subsequent authors have suggested it may represent a subspecies or variant of [[psilocybe-mexicana]], particularly given the broad morphological variability within that species complex. Molecular phylogenetic studies have yielded mixed results, with some analyses placing psilocybe subbrunneascens within the [[psilocybe-mexicana]] clade and others suggesting it is a distinct lineage. The question remains unresolved, and both names continue to be used in the mycological literature.

## Morphology

The pileus is 5–15(–20) mm in diameter, hemispherical when young, becoming convex to broadly convex with age, occasionally with a slight umbo. The surface is hygrophanous, pale brown to dark brown when moist, fading to a pale straw yellow or nearly white when dry. It is smooth, slightly viscid when wet, and often slightly translucent-striate at the margin. The flesh is thin, membranous, and whitish, bruising slowly to a pale blue or blue-green color. The bluing reaction is typically less intense than in [[psilocybe-mexicana]].

The lamellae are adnexed to adnate, close to subdistant, narrow, and initially grayish with a lilaceous or purplish tint, darkening to purplish brown or dark brown with spore maturity. The stipe is 25–50 mm long and 1–2 mm thick, cylindrical, equal, and hollow. The surface is whitish, covered with fine appressed fibrils that give it a silky sheen. An annular zone from the partial veil may be present near the apex but is often evanescent and quickly lost. The stipe bruises blue, sometimes slowly and weakly.

## Microscopic Features

Basidiospores are broadly ellipsoid to ovoid, 6–8 × 4–5 μm, with a wall thickness of approximately 0.5 μm. The apical germ pore is present and distinct, approximately 1 μm in diameter. The spore print is dark purplish brown. Basidia are two- or four-spored, clavate, 12–16 × 4–5 μm. Cheilocystidia are abundant, forming a sterile band on the gill edges, predominantly lageniform to subcapitate, 14–22 × 4–6 μm. Pleurocystidia are absent. The pileipellis consists of a cutis of narrow (2–3 μm), hyaline, gelatinized hyphae. Clamp connections are absent at the base of the basidia.

## Molecular Phylogeny

The phylogenetic placement of psilocybe subbrunneascens has been examined using ITS ribosomal DNA sequences, with results that have contributed to the ongoing taxonomic debate about its status. Some analyses have placed it within the [[psilocybe-mexicana]] clade with minimal genetic divergence, supporting the hypothesis that it represents a morphological variant or subspecies rather than a fully distinct species. Other studies, using a broader set of genetic markers, have identified sufficient sequence divergence to warrant continued recognition at the species level. The question is complicated by the high degree of genetic and morphological variability within [[psilocybe-mexicana]] itself, which some researchers have suggested may represent a species complex encompassing several currently recognized taxa. Resolution of this question will likely require population-level genomic studies across the geographic range of both species.

## Ecology and Distribution

psilocybe subbrunneascens was originally described from the highlands of Guatemala, occurring in subtropical grasslands and pastures at elevations of 1,500–2,500 meters. It has subsequently been reported from southern Mexico (Chiapas, Oaxaca) and the Colombian Andes, suggesting a broader Central and South American distribution in montane regions. The species fruits during the rainy season, typically from May through October. It grows in rich soils, particularly those enriched with decomposing herbivore dung, along trails, in pastures, and at forest margins. Its habitat preferences closely parallel those of [[psilocybe-mexicana]] and other section Mexicanae members, and the two species sometimes occur sympatrically. The disjunct distribution pattern — Guatemala, southern Mexico, and Colombia — suggests that suitable montane grassland habitats in Central America may harbor additional populations that have not yet been documented.

## Cultivation

Although not widely cultivated, psilocybe subbrunneascens has been grown successfully using methods similar to those employed for [[psilocybe-mexicana]]. The species fruits on a variety of substrates including pasteurized straw, composted dung, and enriched grain substrates. Its sclerotia-producing tendency makes it potentially suitable for cultivation techniques that bypass the fruiting stage entirely, harvesting the sclerotia directly from grain jars after an extended colonization period. The relatively modest potency of both the fruiting bodies and sclerotia compared to more popular cultivated species has limited interest in commercial or hobbyist cultivation of this taxon.

## Chemistry

Chemical analysis has confirmed the presence of [[psilocybin-compound]] and [[psilocin]] in fruiting bodies. The tryptamine alkaloid profile is similar to that of [[psilocybe-mexicana]], though the total alkaloid content tends to be lower, consistent with the weaker bluing reaction. Baeocystin has been detected but at lower concentrations than in some other section Mexicanae species. Sclerotia produced in culture contain the same active compounds, with [[psilocybin-compound]] predominant over [[psilocin]] in the hardened mycelial masses. The alkaloid content of sclerotia is typically 30–60% of that found in mature fruiting bodies, depending on the substrate and colonization duration.

## Sclerotia Production

Like other members of section Mexicanae, psilocybe subbrunneascens has demonstrated the ability to form sclerotia under laboratory conditions. Sclerotia are dense, hard, rounded masses of mycelium that form in the substrate, typically on grain-based media after extended colonization periods of 8–12 weeks. The sclerotia serve as survival structures in nature, allowing the mycelium to persist through dry or unfavorable conditions. In cultivation, they can be harvested directly as a convenient alternative to fruiting, containing psychoactive alkaloids at concentrations roughly 30–60% of those found in mature fruiting bodies.

## Ethnomycology

The ethnomycological significance of psilocybe subbrunneascens is poorly documented. In Guatemala, indigenous Mayan communities in the highlands have a long history of mushroom use, though the specific species employed in traditional practices have not always been identified with certainty. It is possible that psilocybe subbrunneascens was included among the mushrooms used in the region, given its distribution and habitat in areas of known indigenous mushroom use. However, no specific traditional names or ritual applications have been definitively attributed to this species. The highland Maya regions of Guatemala, where the species was first documented, have their own distinct mushroom traditions that are less well studied than those of Oaxaca in Mexico.

## Similar Species

[[psilocybe-mexicana]] is the most similar and frequently confused species, from which psilocybe subbrunneascens differs in its paler cap coloration when moist, slightly smaller spores, and generally weaker bluing reaction. psilocybe mulgewii from Jamaica shares similar dimensions but has a distinctive odor and different cheilocystidia. [[psilocybe-martinezii]] from Oaxaca is closely related but has slightly larger spores. Deconica species of pastures can be ruled out by the absence of bluing and the presence of a germ pore. Definitive identification typically requires microscopic examination of spore and cystidia dimensions.

## Identification Notes

Field identification of psilocybe subbrunneascens is challenging due to its small size and the abundance of similar-looking species in its habitat. The most reliable field characters are:
- Hygrophanous cap that is pale brown when moist, fading to nearly white when dry — paler than [[psilocybe-mexicana]]
- Slow, weak blue bruising on the cap flesh and stipe when damaged
- Occurrence in montane grasslands and pastures at 1,500–2,500 meters elevation
- Dark purplish brown spore print on white paper

The weak bluing reaction can be a particular challenge, as it may take several minutes to develop and may be easily overlooked. In fresh specimens collected during peak rainy season, the bruising is typically more pronounced than in older or drier specimens. The farinaceous odor, when present, provides a supporting character. Definitive identification requires microscopic examination, particularly of spore dimensions (6–8 × 4–5 µm), which are slightly smaller than those of [[psilocybe-mexicana]] (7–9 × 4.5–5.5 µm). The absence of pleurocystidia and the lageniform cheilocystidia further distinguish it from similar species in the section.
## Conservation Status

Psilocybe subbrunneascens has not been formally assessed for conservation status by the IUCN or other conservation bodies. Its montane grassland habitats in Guatemala and southern Mexico are subject to pressure from agricultural expansion, particularly cattle ranching and slash-and-burn agriculture. Climate change may also threaten the species by altering precipitation patterns and temperature regimes in the highland areas where it occurs. The cloud forest margins and montane pastures of Central America are among the most threatened ecosystems in the region, with significant losses documented over recent decades. Additional field surveys are needed to assess the species' true distribution and population status, particularly in undersurveyed regions of Guatemala, Honduras, and El Salvador where suitable habitat may exist.
## Seasonality

The species fruits during the rainy season, typically from May through October, with peak fruiting coinciding with the wettest months of June through August. Fruiting is triggered by sustained rainfall events that raise soil moisture to levels sufficient for fruiting body development. In the highland Guatemalan and Mexican habitats where the species occurs, the rainy season is characterized by daily afternoon and evening thunderstorms that maintain consistently high humidity levels. These conditions can persist for weeks, supporting extended fruiting periods. The species may produce multiple flushes within a single rainy season, depending on the pattern and intensity of precipitation events.
