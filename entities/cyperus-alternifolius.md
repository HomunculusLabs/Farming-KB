## Overview

**Cyperus alternifolius**, commonly known as umbrella papyrus, umbrella sedge, or umbrella grass, is a perennial sedge in the family Cyperaceae. It is native to Madagascar, eastern Africa, and the Arabian Peninsula, and is widely cultivated as an ornamental plant in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide.

The species is best known for its distinctive umbrella-like inflorescence, in which numerous slender spikelets radiate outward from the tip of a leafless stem. This striking form has made it one of the most commercially popular ornamental sedges in the global horticultural trade.

The genus Cyperus is one of the largest genera of monocotyledonous plants, with approximately 700 species distributed across tropical, subtropical, and warm-temperate regions of the world.

In warm climates, Cyperus alternifolius can become naturalized and is classified as an invasive species in parts of Florida, southeastern Australia, and several [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-hawaii-pacific-islands]]. It forms dense monotypic stands in disturbed wetlands and drainage ditches.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Cyperus alternifolius** was described by the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum. It is the type species of Cyperus subgenus Pycreus, though some modern treatments place it in Cyperus subgenus Chlorocyperus.

The specific epithet *alternifolius* refers to the alternate arrangement of the spikelets around the inflorescence axis, which creates the characteristic whorled, umbrella-like appearance.

Plants grow 60–150 cm tall from a short, stout rhizome. Culms are solid, triangular to subcylindrical, and smooth, arising in dense tufts from the base.

The rhizome is short, thick, and covered with dark brown scale-like sheaths from previous seasons. Under favorable conditions, a single clump may produce 50–100 culms per [[extending-growing-season]].

Each culm terminates in a single involucral bract that extends horizontally, beneath which 10–30 slender spikelets radiate outward in an umbel-like arrangement. Spikelets are 10–25 mm long, compressed, and green to brownish.

The involucral bract is the most conspicuous feature of the inflorescence, often exceeding the spikelets in length and creating the characteristic parasol-like silhouette that gives the plant its common name.

The glumes are 2–3 mm long, broadly ovate, and keeled. Each spikelet contains 8–20 florets arranged distichously along the rachilla.

Achenes are oblong-triangular, approximately 1.2–1.5 mm long, brown to dark brown, and finely punctate. The plant flowers and fruits year-round in [[fukuoka-natural-farming-tropical-climates]], with peak flowering during warm, wet months.

Two subspecies are generally recognized: subsp. *alternifolius* (the typical form) and subsp. *flabelliformis*, which has a more [[fukuoka-ideal-rice-plant-morphology-compact-growth-high-yield]] habit and is the form most commonly cultivated. The dwarf cultivar 'Gracilis' is also widely grown.

Subspecies *flabelliformis* differs in its shorter stature (typically 45–90 cm) and fewer spikelets per umbel. Intermediate forms occur in areas where the subspecies ranges overlap, particularly in cultivated populations.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cyperus alternifolius is native to Madagascar, coastal East Africa (Kenya, Tanzania, Mozambique), and the southwestern Arabian Peninsula (Yemen). Some authorities also report native populations in Mauritius and the Comoros Islands.

In its native range, it occurs along riverbanks, lake margins, swamp edges, and in seasonally flooded grasslands. It prefers still or slow-moving freshwater habitats with muddy or sandy substrates.

The species has been widely introduced outside its native range through the ornamental plant trade. It is naturalized in Florida, Louisiana, California, Hawaii, eastern Australia, southeastern Brazil, and numerous Pacific islands.

Naturalized populations typically establish in disturbed wetland habitats including drainage ditches, canal margins, retention ponds, and artificial water features. The species thrives in nutrient-enriched waters associated with urban and [[mycofiltration-of-agricultural-runoff]].

In its introduced range in Florida, Cyperus alternifolius is most commonly encountered in the southern and central peninsula counties. Populations are concentrated in the Everglades Agricultural Area and suburban canal systems of the southeast coast.

It is frost-sensitive and does not persist in areas with regular freezing temperatures unless cultivated as an indoor or greenhouse plant. In temperate regions, it is grown exclusively as a container or houseplant.

Plants overwinter successfully indoors with bright indirect light and consistently moist soil. Growth resumes rapidly when temperatures exceed 18°C in spring.

## Cultivation and Uses

Cyperus alternifolius is one of the most widely cultivated ornamental sedges globally. It is grown as a houseplant, patio plant, and water garden feature [[query-how-to-grow-and-use-oca-in-temperate-climates]], and as a landscape accent in tropical and subtropical regions.

Propagation is straightforward and can be achieved by seed, division, or a unique method in which the entire mature inflorescence is inverted and placed in water, where new plantlets emerge from the spikelet bases within 2–4 weeks.

The species grows rapidly in full sun to partial shade and requires consistently moist to saturated soil. It tolerates standing water up to 10 cm deep, making it suitable for bog gardens and shallow margins of water features.

Fertilization with a balanced [[comfrey-tea-liquid-fertilizer]] during [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]] promotes vigorous growth and abundant flowering. Pests are generally minor but can include spider mites, mealybugs, and scale insects under indoor conditions.

The dwarf cultivar 'Nanus' is particularly popular for indoor use, reaching only 30–45 cm in height. It retains the characteristic umbrella inflorescence but in a compact form suitable for tabletop and windowsill cultivation.

In traditional African medicine, infusions of the rhizome have been used to treat digestive complaints, fever, and snakebite, though these applications lack modern pharmacological validation.

The stems have been used locally for weaving mats and baskets in parts of East Africa, though the stems are less durable than those of the related [[cyperus-papyrus]].

The species is increasingly used in green roof and bioswale installations in tropical urban areas, where its tolerance of saturated substrates and rapid growth provide effective stormwater retention and filtration.

## Ecology

In naturalized range, Cyperus alternifolius can form dense stands that exclude native wetland vegetation. The thick rhizomatous mat creates a physical barrier to seedling establishment of other species.

The dense root system contributes to [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]] along waterway margins but can also obstruct water flow in narrow drainage channels and ditches, creating maintenance challenges in managed waterways.
