# Gram Stain Testing

## Description

Gram stain testing is a fundamental microbiological technique used to identify and classify bacteria based on differences in their cell [[fungal-cell-wall-composition-and-structural-organization]]. The test sorts bacteria into two major groups — Gram-positive and Gram-negative — by applying a series of dyes that stain cells differently depending on the structure of their cell membranes. In [[mycoremediation]], gram staining is used to validate the identity and purity of bacterial cultures before, during, and after treatment to monitor the ongoing efficiency of [[mycofiltration]] systems.

## Classification

- **Category**: Microbiological testing technique
- **Type**: Differential staining method
- **Skill level**: Beginner to intermediate
- **Equipment required**: Gram stain kit, microscope, bacterial sample
- **Cost**: Under $30 for a gram stain kit from science supply stores

## Key Facts

- **Developed by**: Hans Christian Gram (1884)
- **Principle**: Bacteria with thick peptidoglycan [[alpha-1-3-glucan-fungal-pathogen-cell-walls]] retain crystal violet stain (Gram-positive, purple), while those with thinner walls and outer lipid membranes do not (Gram-negative, pink/red)
- **Information provided**: Cell membrane composition, cell shape (cocci, bacilli, spirilla), arrangement (chains, clusters, pairs), and metabolic function classification
- **Cost**: Gram stain kits are relatively inexpensive, typically under $30
- **Identification value**: Helps identify unknown bacteria by sorting them into classes, which is essential for tracking specific pathogens through a mycoremediation experiment
- **Time to results**: 15 to 30 minutes for the staining procedure; immediate results under microscope

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Mycoremediation Monitoring Protocol

Gram staining is used at multiple stages of a mycoremediation project:

1. **Initial characterization**: Perform a gram stain on a small sample of your bacteria to validate the identity and purity of the culture before beginning the experiment
2. **Mid-process monitoring**: Pull samples at regular intervals (30 min, 1 hr, 3 hr, 6 hr, 12 hr, then every 6 hr), plate them, incubate overnight, and gram stain the resulting colonies
3. **Post-treatment verification**: After incubating overnight, perform another gram stain and examine morphology under microscope to confirm you are counting and tracking the pathogen of interest

### Integration with Cell Counting

Gram staining works hand-in-hand with cell counting using the [[dilution-to-extinction]] method:
1. Perform gram stain to validate culture identity
2. Prepare serial dilutions of the bacterial culture
3. Spread across [[query-how-to-make-agar-plates-for-mushroom-cultivation]] and incubate overnight
4. Count individual colonies on plates with 30 or fewer colonies
5. Calculate average across triplicate plates
6. Multiply by inverse of dilution to calculate bacteria per unit volume

### Pathogen Tracking

The test is critical for ensuring that the bacteria being counted and tracked through a mycoremediation experiment are actually the pathogen of interest and not contaminant species. Without gram stain validation, cell count data may be unreliable.

### E. coli Considerations

Since E. coli can double in population every 20 minutes at optimum incubation temperatures, frequent sampling and gram stain verification are necessary to maintain accurate tracking. The rapid [[soil-temperature-plant-growth-rate-doubling-rule]] means sampling intervals must be carefully designed to capture meaningful data.

## Procedure Summary

1. Prepare a heat-fixed smear of the bacterial sample on a glass slide
2. Flood with crystal violet (primary stain) for 1 minute, then rinse
3. Flood with Gram's iodine (mordant) for 1 minute, then rinse
4. Decolorize with alcohol or acetone briefly (2 to 3 seconds), then rinse immediately
5. Counterstain with safranin for 1 minute, then rinse and blot dry
6. Examine under microscope at 1000x magnification (oil immersion)

## Interpretation

- **Gram-positive**: Purple/blue cells with thick peptidoglycan layer (e.g., Streptococcus, Bacillus, Staphylococcus)
- **Gram-negative**: Pink/red cells with thin peptidoglycan and outer lipid membrane (e.g., E. coli, Salmonella, [[pseudomonas]])
- **Morphology**: Note cell shape (cocci, bacilli, spirilla) and arrangement (chains, clusters, pairs) for species-level identification guidance

## Limitations

- Does not provide species-level identification on its own
- Some bacteria are Gram-variable and may stain inconsistently
- Must be combined with colony morphology, growth characteristics, and other tests for definitive identification
- Requires a functional microscope with oil immersion capability for best results
