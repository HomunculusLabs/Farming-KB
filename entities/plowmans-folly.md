## Description

*Plowman's Folly* is a landmark 1943 book by Edward H. Faulkner that challenged the universal use of the moldboard plow in agriculture. Faulkner argued that plowing — turning the soil over to bury crop residues — was fundamentally destructive to soil health and was the root cause of soil erosion, loss of fertility, and declining crop yields. The book was controversial upon publication but became one of the foundational texts of the conservation tillage and no-till farming movements.

## Key Facts

- Faulkner was a county agricultural agent in Kentucky who observed that unplowed fields outperformed plowed ones in moisture retention [[mollison-permaculture-two-arid-water-harvesting-and-erosion-control]].
- The book's central thesis: the moldboard plow creates a hardpan (plow pan) at plowing depth, buries beneficial surface organic matter too deep for [[decomposition]], and exposes bare soil to erosion.
- *Plowman's Folly* sold over 500,000 copies and sparked a national debate about tillage practices.
- Faulkner advocated for surface incorporation of organic matter using disk harrows rather than inversion by moldboard plows.
- The book influenced the development of minimum tillage, strip tillage, and eventually no-till farming systems.
- Solomon cites *Plowman's Folly* to support his argument against rototilling and in favor of hand-digging with a spade or shovel.

## Relevance to Cultivation

For gardeners and small-scale cultivators, the lessons of *Plowman's Folly* translate to:

- Avoid inversion tillage (turning soil upside down) whenever possible
- Keep organic matter near the soil surface where it feeds soil biology most effectively
- Be aware that any tool that compresses soil at a consistent depth (plow, [[rototiller]]) will create a compaction layer
- Prefer gentle soil loosening to aggressive soil inversion
- Garden-scale hand digging with a spade or fork naturally avoids the worst compaction effects of mechanized tillage

## Legacy

Faulkner's ideas were initially rejected by the agricultural establishment but have been largely vindicated. Modern no-till farming, which avoids all mechanical soil disturbance, is practiced on over 180 million acres in the United States alone and is considered a best practice for soil conservation.
