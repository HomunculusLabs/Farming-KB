## Overview

Eleocharis acicularis, commonly called needle spikerush or least spikerush,
is a perennial sedge in the family Cyperaceae.

It forms dense tufts of fine, hair-like stems in shallow water and moist
soil, creating a turf-like groundcover along shorelines and in wetlands.

The species has a circumboreal distribution and is one of the most widespread
spikerush species in [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]].

Its dense root systems stabilize substrates in littoral zones, making it
valuable for [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]] and [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]].

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Cyperaceae → Eleocharis → E. acicularis.

Eleocharis acicularis was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 as Scirpus
acicularis and later transferred to Eleocharis by Roemer and Schultes in
1817.

The species is part of a taxonomically complex group, and several varieties
have been recognized, though their status remains debated.

Stems are filiform, erect or ascending, 5–35 cm tall and 0.3–0.8 mm in
diameter, appearing as fine green threads growing from a dense basal tuft.

Leaves are reduced to basal sheaths; the green photosynthetic tissue is the
stem itself.

The inflorescence is a single terminal spikelet 3–8 mm long and 1.5–3 mm
wide, ovoid to ellipsoid, brownish at maturity.

Glumes are arranged in a spiral, each 1.5–2 mm long, with a green midrib
and reddish-brown margins.

The fruit is a small achene 0.8–1.2 mm long, whitish to yellowish, with a
persistent style base forming a short tubercle.

## Distribution and Habitat

Needle spikerush has a circumboreal distribution across [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]],
Europe, and Asia.

In North America, it occurs from Alaska to Newfoundland, south to
California, Texas, and Florida.

It is also found in [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]], the Caribbean, and northern South
America.

The species grows in shallow water and saturated soils along lake margins,
pond edges, stream banks, marshes, and wet meadows.

It tolerates water depths from 0 to 30 cm and colonizes a range of
substrates including sand, gravel, mud, and organic muck.

It is common in both pristine and disturbed wetlands and frequently
pioneers newly exposed shorelines.

## Ecology

Eleocharis acicularis provides important habitat and food for wetland
wildlife.

Its dense stands offer cover for aquatic invertebrates, amphibian larvae,
and small fish in the shallow littoral zone.
