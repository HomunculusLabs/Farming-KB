# Solcrete

## Description

Solcrete is a collapsed, cemented, greyish, gravelly soil pan formed in sodium-rich soils. It is impermeable to water and represents one of the most difficult soil pans to manage in permaculture because it combines physical cementation with chemical salinity. Solcrete develops when high sodium levels cause soil particles to disperse and collapse into a dense, impervious layer.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil concretion / hardpan
- **Composition**: Sodium-cemented soil particles, gravelly matrix
- **Colour**: Greyish
- **Formation context**: Sodium-rich (sodic) soils, saline-affected landscapes
- **Permeability**: Impermeable to water
- **Related conditions**: Sodicity, salinity, soil dispersion

## Key Facts

- Forms in sodium-rich soils as a collapsed, cemented layer
- Greyish, gravelly appearance
- Completely impermeable to water — creates severe drainage problems
- Combines physical hardness with chemical salinity
- Part of the broader family of soil concretions (calcrete, silcrete, ferricrete, solcrete)

## Management in Permaculture

- **Deep drainage of 1–2 m is essential** for salted soils affected by solcrete
- Only deep-rooted trees can help break through and manage solcrete over time
- Reduction of salt is necessary — this may involve flushing with fresh water
- Adding humus (organic matter) helps improve soil structure and reduce sodium effects
- Gypsum application (2–3 handfuls per square metre) helps displace sodium with calcium
- Flushing with fresh water removes sodium as sulphate
- Sand layering over the surface (4 cm) can help manage surface sealing

## Relevance to Cultivation

Solcrete is particularly challenging because it combines two problems: physical impermeability and chemical salinity. Simply breaking the pan is insufficient — the underlying sodium must also be addressed through drainage, gypsum application, and fresh water flushing. In permaculture design, solcrete-affected sites require a comprehensive approach that addresses both the physical barrier and the chemical imbalance. Mollison emphasises that deep drainage (1–2 m) is the non-negotiable first step for any productive use of salted soils with solcrete.
