## Description

Dr. F. S. Cordier was a French physician and mycologist, described as a member of many scientific societies, who published a landmark work on [[classification-of-edible-and-poisonous-mushroom-families]] mushrooms at Paris in 1876. [[julius-auboineau-palmer]] regarded Cordier's book as "by far the most complete work on the subject in the world" and translated extensive passages from it for his American readership.

## Major Work

Cordier's 1876 publication was illustrated with chromo-lithographs and provided the most systematic examination of [[mushroom-identification]] rules then available. Palmer translated key passages for publication in the Boston Evening Transcript, introducing Cordier's findings to an American audience.

The work was notable for its methodical approach: rather than proposing new rules for identification, Cordier took every existing folk rule and tested it against specific botanical examples, demonstrating contradictions in each case.

## Systematic Debunking of Folk Tests

Cordier's most significant contribution was his comprehensive demolition of popular [[bloomfield-angel-of-death-poisonous-mushroom-identification-toxinology]] methods. Palmer translated his findings in detail.

### Rules for Suspicious Mushrooms

Cordier noted that writers warned against fungi that:
- Grow in shady, dark, humid places
- Have soft, watery flesh
- Taste acrid, astringent, acid, or saline
- Show brilliant red, green, or blue colors
- Lack odor or smell different from [[fungi-cultivated-mushrooms-pathogens-competitors]]
- Secrete milky fluid
- Change color when broken
- Turn brown or black with age
- Have viscous surfaces
- Have flabby, fibrous flesh
- Are not attacked by snails, insects, or worms

### Rules for Safe Mushrooms

Conversely, writers claimed safe mushrooms:
- Grow in open meadows and cultivated fields
- Taste neither nauseous nor acid
- Smell of new flour or cultivated mushroom
- Have firm, fleshy, brittle consistency
- Have flesh that does not change color on exposure
- Have dry surfaces
- Are rosy, wine-tinted, or violet
- Are attacked by worms, insects, and snails
- Are sought by mammiferous animals

### Cordier's Conclusion

After cataloging these rules, Cordier demonstrated that "no one of these characteristics is exclusively the property either of esculent mushrooms or of poisonous mushrooms." He then systematically cited approximately fifty specific varieties that contradicted each rule.

## The Silver Spoon Test

Cordier specifically tested the widely-believed method of cooking mushrooms with silver implements. The folk belief held that if silver or gold turned black during cooking, the mushrooms were poisonous; if the metal remained bright, they were safe.

Cordier's experiment with [[agaricus-bulbosus]] (a deadly Amanita) proved that neither silver nor gold blackened. He stated: "Whether silver or gold grow black or remain brilliant in contact with mushrooms, the conclusion is equally of no value."

## Other Debunked Tests

### Necker's Onion Test

Swiss botanist Necker proposed that a white onion cooked with mushrooms would turn bluish or brown if they were poisonous. Cordier found this unconfirmed by experience.

### Bulrush Pith Test

Certain observers claimed the pith of the bulrush would grow black in the presence of poisonous mushrooms. Cordier rejected this as well.

## Cordier's Sole Recommendation

After dismantling every folk method, Cordier offered only one recommendation: "The true, perhaps the sole means of avoiding mistakes, when we wish to use mushrooms as food, is to learn to know the characteristics which are peculiar to each species by itself, or, in other terms, to have in the memory or under the eye the exact description of the species."

## Historical Significance

Cordier's work was foundational for several reasons:

1. **Scientific rigor:** He applied systematic empirical testing rather than repeating folk traditions
2. **Comprehensive scope:** He examined dozens of species as counterexamples to each popular rule
3. **Chromolithograph illustration:** His book set a standard for [[mushroom-spore-visual-identification]] aids
4. **Transatlantic influence:** Palmer's translations brought European mycological science to American audiences

## Relevance to Modern Mycology

Cordier's central insight — that each species must be learned individually — remains the fundamental principle of safe [[bloomfield-mushroom-foraging-immigrant-poisonings-paddy-straw-confusion]]. Every modern [[field-guide-psilocybin-bluing-reaction-identification]] and foraging instructor teaches essentially the same message that Cordier articulated in 1876.
