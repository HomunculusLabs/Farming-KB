## Description

Fungal specimen preservation encompasses the methods and protocols for collecting, drying, storing, and maintaining fungal specimens in herbaria for scientific study. Proper preservation is essential for documenting fungal biodiversity, supporting taxonomic research, and providing voucher specimens for ecological, morphological, phylogenetic, and molecular studies.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Mycological technique
- **Category**: Specimen management / Herbarium science
- **Applications**: Taxonomy, phylogenetics, biodiversity inventory, molecular studies
- **Primary method**: Warm-air drying (40–50°C)

## Key Facts

### Drying Methods

#### Warm-Air Drying (Preferred)

- Most common method for sporocarpic specimens
- Optimal temperature: 40–50°C (preferably 42°C)
- Electric fruit dryers with wire mesh shelves work best (Singer 1986a)
- Large or watery sporocarps sliced longitudinally to hasten drying
- In areas without electricity: shelves/screens over heat source (fire, gas burner, oil lamp, stove); avoid burning specimens
- Small specimens on dry substrata: air-dried indoors, in sun, or under high-wattage (>75W) incandescent bulb
- Preferred for molecular studies because chemical fixation alters DNA (Haines and Cooper 1993)

#### Silica-Gel Drying

- Specimens and field labels placed in sealed glass tubes or plastic bags with silica crystals
- Suitable only for small, easily desiccated specimens
- Preserves DNA satisfactorily

#### Freeze-Drying (Lyophilization)

- Specimen frozen first, then frozen moisture vaporized under vacuum
- Equipment expensive
- Specimens maintain shapes—good for displays
- Become brittle and fragile; absorb moisture readily
- After 5–6 years in storage, cells of Ascomycete and Basidiomycete specimens often collapse
- Not ideal for long-term preservation

### Specimen Types and Protocols

#### Sporocarps (Mushrooms, Shelf Fungi)

- Entire sporocarps placed loosely on drying shelves
- Large/watery specimens sliced longitudinally
- Small tag with collection number accompanies each specimen
- Dry specimens wrapped in wax paper or reclosable plastic bags for transport
- Silica gels used to remove moisture from storage containers

#### Plant Pathogens (Rusts, Smuts)

- Host-plant tissue with fungi spread on drying paper/newspaper with collection-number tag
- Blotters between specimens absorb moisture
- Corrugated aluminum/cardboard sheets enhance air circulation
- Stack compressed between wooden frames with straps
- Presses placed in sun or over heat source (35–45°C)
- Checked at least twice daily; wet papers and blotters changed

#### Lichens

- Air-dried on open shelves over gentle heat (42°C) or in sun
- May be left in paper bags if collected dry
- Leaflike/branched lichens: flattened and pressed
- Brittle lichens on soil: dried with substrata; soil glued and mounted on card
- Lichens on rocks: wrapped separately in tissue paper after drying

#### Myxomycetes (Slime Molds)

- Air-dried with substrata; collections kept upright during drying
- Stored separately in small tins or boxes to prevent damage
- Substratum glued to herbarium paper insert fitted into box
- Folded paper ends allow specimen removal without direct handling

#### Fluid Preservation

- Fresh material submerged in fixative for 2–7 days, then transferred to storage solution
- Fixatives: 70% ethanol, Kew Mixture, Copenhagen Solution (contain glycerol to prevent hardening)
- Stored in glass jars/vials with neoprene caps; sealed with wax or Parafilm
- Ethanol-stored specimens monitored for evaporation
- Safety: gloves, goggles, masks, chemical fume hood required

### Collection Acquisition Policies

Herbaria selectively accept specimens based on six criteria:
1. Specimen adequately documents the taxon (properly dried, sufficient material, uncontaminated)
2. Accompanying data adequately documents the specimen (full locality information)
3. Taxon is not already well-represented in the collection
4. Specimen identified by a specialist
5. Specimen cited in a publication or designated as a voucher
6. Appropriate authorizing permits accompany the specimen (collection, export, import)

### Record Keeping

- Specimens accessioned and collection data recorded
- Color notes taken on fresh material before processing (fungi lose natural color during drying)
- Note indicating preservation method included with non-air-dried specimens
- Field labels and field notes accompany all collections

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Proper specimen preservation is essential for documenting cultivated species, verifying identities of production strains, and maintaining reference collections. Cultivators who collect wild specimens for identification or breeding programs must follow correct drying protocols to ensure specimens remain useful for scientific study and molecular analysis. The emphasis on warm-air drying (avoiding chemical fixation) is particularly relevant for cultivators seeking to preserve specimens for future DNA analysis, which is increasingly important for strain identification and patent documentation.
