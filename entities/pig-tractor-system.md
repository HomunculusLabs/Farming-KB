# Pig Tractor System

## Description

The "pig tractor" system is a permaculture technique where pigs are confined to specific areas using temporary or electric fencing to clear, till, and manure land through their natural rooting behaviour. The method replaces mechanized ploughing with biological cultivation, simultaneously preparing seedbeds, removing unwanted vegetation, and fertilizing the soil.

Mollison describes the system in detail: 20 pigs per 4,000 square metres (1 acre) will effectively "plow" an area by scratching and rooting, preparing it for planting comfrey, sunroot (Jerusalem artichoke), lucerne, chicory, and clover. One hundred pigs in 2-hectare (5-acre) pens will plow 40 hectares (100 acres) in 18 months, removing gorse, blackberries, and small shrubs in the process.

## Classification

- **Category**: Agricultural technique — biological soil cultivation
- **System type**: Rotational livestock management
- **Key organism**: Domestic pig (*Sus scrofa domesticus*)
- **Function**: Land clearing, soil tillage, manuring, pest removal

## Key Facts

- Stocking rate: 20 pigs per 4,000 m² (1 acre) for effective ploughing
- Scale example: 100 pigs in 2-ha pens clear 40 ha in 18 months
- Eliminates gorse, blackberries, and small shrubs through rooting
- Prepares ground for forage crops: comfrey, sunroot, lucerne, chicory, clover
- After pig removal, land can be sown and followed by cattle, then pigs again in rotation
- Requires 3-5 years to develop a full complement of forage foods on range
- Can be followed by sowings then cattle then pigs again in repeating cycles

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

The pig tractor system embodies multiple permaculture principles:

1. **Biological resource replaces fossil fuel**: Pigs replace tractor power for ploughing, eliminating fuel costs, machinery depreciation, and soil compaction from heavy equipment. The system "obviates the use of mechanised tractors and improves soils."

2. **Multiple yields from single process**: While ploughing, pigs simultaneously produce meat, clear weeds, incorporate organic matter, distribute manure, and control pests (through consumption of grubs, roots, and small vermin).

3. **Sequential rotation design**: After pigs clear and manure an area, it is sown to forage crops, then grazed by cattle, then returned to pigs — a multi-year rotation that builds soil fertility with each cycle. Each animal species prepares conditions for the next.

4. **Edge effect and zone design**: Pig pens can be positioned to clear land adjacent to existing orchards or food forests. In the tropics, banana, papaya, and similar crops must be kept outside pig pens (as pigs root them out), but in temperate zones, fruit trees outside fenced areas can receive thrown surplus crop.

5. **J. Savage case study**: Mollison cites a Victorian farmer who successfully used pigs followed by multiple species in rotation over mixed forage plantings, with comfrey and lucerne as key persistent forage elements.

## Practical Implementation

- Use electric fencing for flexible pen management
- Allow 1 ha (2.5 acres) per breeding sow for the full system
- Plant tree forage areas outside and at corners initially
- Plan 15-18 pens for full rotational system with 4-8 animal species
- Move pigs before they completely denude an area to allow forage recovery
- Protect young trees from pigs with electric fencing until established

## Limitations

- Pigs will root out bananas, papayas, and shallow-rooted crops in tropical systems
- Requires 3-5 years to fully develop forage resources
- Some surplus crop must be "thrown over the fence" to pigs in established areas
- Careful planning and siting essential if pig-raising is a primary occupation
