# Saiga Antelope

## Description

The saiga antelope (*Saiga tatarica*) is a critically endangered antelope species native to the Eurasian steppes, particularly the semi-arid grasslands of Central Asia. Recognizable by its distinctive proboscis-like nose, the saiga is one of the most ancient ungulate species, having roamed the earth since the Pleistocene epoch alongside woolly mammoths and sabre-toothed cats.

Mollison references the saiga as an exemplar of wildlife-based [[rangeland-management]], noting that Russia preserved and encouraged the species from near extinction in the 1940s to over a million individuals, enabling an annual sustainable cull of 40% for food. This stands as one of the most successful examples of wildlife conservation integrated with productive land use.

## Key Facts

- Near extinction in the 1940s, recovered to over one million through Russian conservation efforts
- Can sustain a 40% annual cull rate, demonstrating high reproductive capacity
- Best-adapted species for tundra and steppe environments
- Migratory behaviour covering hundreds of kilometres seasonally
- The distinctive inflatable nose filters dust and warms [[hemenway-cold-air-drainage-and-frost-management]] in winter

## Relevance to Cultivation and Land Management

Mollison uses the saiga to illustrate a core [[permaculture-principle-1-observe-interact]]: that well-managed indigenous wildlife on natural grasslands can far exceed the productivity and sustainability of introduced domestic livestock. The saiga case demonstrates that:

1. **Wildlife yields exceed domestic livestock** on marginal rangelands when measured by total biomass, [[aquaculture-design-principles-and-energy-efficiency]], and environmental cost
2. **Conservation and production are not opposed** — the Russian model showed that protective management enabling population growth allows sustainable harvesting
3. **Indigenous species are better adapted** to local climate, forage, and disease pressures than imported cattle or goats
4. **Low-input systems outperform high-input systems** on semi-arid lands, requiring no supplemental feed, shelter, or veterinary intervention

This example reinforces Mollison's broader argument that 4% of cattle production on semi-arid and montane lands causes 70-80% of rangeland damage, while careful wildlife harvesting would exceed cattle products in value many times over.

## Ecological Role

Saiga grazing maintains steppe grassland structure and prevents woody encroachment. Their migration patterns distribute nutrients across vast landscapes. As a keystone herbivore, their presence supports predator populations and scavenger communities.

## Modern Conservation Concerns

Despite the historical recovery Mollison documents, saiga populations have since suffered catastrophic collapses from poaching (for horn trade), disease outbreaks, and habitat fragmentation. The species remains critically endangered, validating Mollison's implicit point that sustained political will and land-use policy are essential for wildlife-based rangeland systems.
