## Description

S. Dana Hayes was a prominent American assayer and chemist of the 19th century, described by Julius A. Palmer Jr. as "one of our best known assayers." Palmer recounts bringing Hayes a vial containing a black fluid resulting from the deliquescence (auto-digestion) of a fungus. Hayes initially dismissed it as impossible, stating that "nothing was known in nature which would thus liquify." Upon witnessing the process firsthand, Hayes "cheerfully acknowledged his error." This anecdote illustrates the gap in scientific knowledge about [[bloomfield-fungal-biology-and-hyphal-growth]] even among accomplished chemists of the era.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Hayes' ignorance of fungal deliquescence illustrates the historical neglect of [[mycology]] as a [[bloomfield-history-of-mycology-scientific-discipline]]. Even skilled analytical chemists were unfamiliar with basic fungal processes like autodigestion (deliquescence), which is a well-known feature of genera like *Coprinus* (inky [[query-how-to-grow-wine-cap-mushrooms]]). This knowledge gap — which Palmer made it his mission to address — parallels modern challenges in [[palmer-mushroom-charts-american-mycological-education]]. Understanding this [[ghouled-field-guide-historical-context]] helps explain why [[mushroom-cultivation]] remained underdeveloped for so long and why basic mycological knowledge was so hard-won.

## Historical Context

The exchange between Palmer and Hayes likely occurred in the 1870s, during Palmer's early mycological investigations in Boston. At that time, mycology was a neglected branch [[fukuoka-illusions-of-natural-science]] in America, with even basic fungal biology poorly understood by mainstream scientists. The fact that a leading analytical chemist was unaware of fungal deliquescence underscores the isolation of mycological knowledge from mainstream science in 19th-century America.
