## Overview

Symphoricarpos albus, commonly known as common snowberry, is a deciduous shrub in the family Caprifoliaceae native to [[aact-foliar-sticker-spreaders-leaf-surface-attachment-ingham]] is pale green to gray-green above, paler and often pubescent beneath, with prominent venation.

Flowers are small, bell-shaped, 5 to 8 millimeters long, borne in axillary clusters of 1 to 5 flowers during late spring to midsummer.

The corolla is pink to white with a five-lobed margin and a short tube surrounding the inserted stamens.

The fruit is a distinctive spherical drupe 1 to 1.5 centimeters in diameter, pure white, and containing two nutlets.

White berries persist on the branches from late summer through winter, often remaining after leaf drop.

## Distribution and Habitat

Symphoricarpos albus is native to most of North America, from southern Alaska and Canada south to California, New Mexico, and the [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] and var. laevigatus of western North America.

The western variety typically has larger, smoother leaves and occurs from British Columbia to California and east to the Rocky Mountains.

The eastern variety ranges from Quebec and Newfoundland west to Minnesota and south to Georgia and Arkansas.

Snowberry occupies a broad range of habitats including open woods, forest edges, stream banks, rocky slopes, and disturbed sites.

The species tolerates full sun to deep shade and is found from sea level to over 2,500 meters elevation in mountainous areas.

It grows on a wide variety of soil types from sandy loams to heavy clays, with moderate tolerance of both acidic and alkaline conditions.

Snowberry is a common early-successional species, colonizing disturbed sites and persisting into later successional stages as an understory component.

## Ecology and Wildlife Value

Common snowberry is an important food source for wildlife throughout its range, particularly during autumn and winter months.

The white berries are consumed by numerous bird species including grouse, quail, robins, thrushes, waxwings, and chickadees.

Ring-necked pheasants and wild turkeys rely heavily on snowberry fruits as a winter food resource in parts of their range.

Mammalian consumers include deer, elk, bighorn sheep, bears, coyotes, foxes, raccoons, and skunks.

White-tailed deer and mule deer browse both the foliage and stems, though snowberry is generally considered a low-preference forage.

The dense thicket-forming habit provides valuable cover and nesting habitat for songbirds, small mammals, and upland game birds.

Snowberry serves as a larval host plant for several moth species including the snowberry clearwing moth.

The flowers attract pollinators including native bees, bumblebees, and butterflies during the early summer flowering period.
