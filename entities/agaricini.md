## Description

The Agaricini are a taxonomic grouping of fungi referring to gilled mushrooms -- those that bear their spores on thin, blade-like radiating structures (lamellae or gills) underneath the cap. The term was widely used in 19th-century mycological literature to describe the largest and most diverse class of macroscopic fungi. In modern taxonomy, this group largely corresponds to the order **Agaricales**, though the historical usage was broader and more informal. The Agaricini represent the most economically important and taxonomically challenging group of macrofungi.

## Classification

- **Taxonomic rank:** Tribe / informal group (historical); modern equivalent: Order Agaricales
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales (modern equivalent)
- **Defining feature:** Presence of gills (lamellae) under the cap, radiating from a common center like spokes of a wheel
- **Distinguishing features:** Gill attachment, spacing, color, and [[spore-print]] color vary by genus and species

## Key Facts

- The Agaricini encompass the vast majority of commonly known mushrooms, including the commercial button mushroom (*[[agaricus-bisporus]]*), the deadly *Amanita* species, and many popular edible genera such as *Pleurotus*, *Lentinula*, and *Agaricus*.
- Palmer described them as having "a series of thin lines running into a common centre, like the spokes of a wheel" under the cap -- an accessible description of gill structure that remains valid and useful for beginners.
- This group "defies generalization" regarding edibility -- unlike puff-balls (generally safe when young and white inside) and Boleti (generally safe if mild-tasting), the Agaricini include both the most prized edible species and the most deadly poisonous ones.
- Among the Agaricini, Palmer highlighted the *Amanita* genus as containing the most dangerous species, while the *Russula* genus was noted as relatively safe to test by taste, since noxious species have an unmistakably acrid, biting fluid.
- The mushroom of commerce (*[[agaricus-campestris]]* in its wild form, *[[agaricus-arvensis]]* as the cultivated horse-mushroom) belongs to this group.
- Of approximately 1,000 known species of "good-sized fungi" (in Palmer"s era), the Agaricini constituted the largest class by far.

## Identification Methodology

Palmer emphasized that identifying Agaricini requires the same common-sense approach used for any other natural product:
- **Visual recognition:** Learn the habits, color, size, and general appearance of each species, as you would learn to recognize kitchen vegetables
- **Naming system:** Each species has distinct characteristics that allow it to be called by name, like identifying friends
- **No shortcuts:** No single test (peeling, silver spoon, color change) can distinguish edible from poisonous species
- **Individual study:** Each of the thousand kinds of mushrooms must be tested separately -- no rule from one applies to the next
- **Spore prints:** The color of the spore deposit (white, pink, brown, purple-brown, black) is a key diagnostic feature

## Notable Genera Within Agaricini

- **Amanita:** The most dangerous genus, containing species with deadly amatoxins. Characterized by volva at base, white gills, warts on cap, and white spore print.
- **Agaricus:** Includes the [[history-of-commercial-mushroom-cultivation]] and many edible species. Pink gills darkening to chocolate-brown with age. Brown spore print.
- **Russula:** Generally safe genus where toxic species are detected by extremely acrid taste. Brittle flesh that breaks like chalk. Various spore print colors.
- **Coprinus:** Inky cap mushrooms. Some species (e.g., *C. comatus*, shaggy mane) are edible. Deliquesce into black ink at maturity.
- **Marasmius:** Includes *M. oreades* (fairy ring mushroom). White spore print. Some species reported toxic to certain animals.

## Palmer's Three-Class System

The Agaricini were one of three major groups in Palmer's practical classification:
1. **Agaricini** (gilled): Largest group, requires species-level identification, contains both deadly and delicious species
2. **Polyporei** (pored): Generally safer, Boleti can be generalized as safe if mild-tasting
3. **Puff-balls** (globular): Safest group, all species edible when young and white inside

This three-way division remains a useful starting point for [[bloomfield-angel-of-death-poisonous-mushroom-identification-toxinology]] today.

## Edibility Principles

Palmer established several key principles about Agaricini edibility:
- The edible quality, once proven, never deserts that mushroom -- the same species cannot be safe in one location and poisonous in another
- Tradition about seasonal or geographic variation in toxicity is "sadly at fault"
- The mushroom either contains a poison or it does not -- there is no intermediate state
- Proper preparation matters: boiling mushrooms like cabbage and discarding the water (as one chef did) is ignorant but does not extract any non-existent poison from safe species

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The Agaricini/Agaricales remain the most economically important group of fungi:

- **Commercial cultivation:** The global mushroom industry is dominated by Agaricini -- *[[agaricus-bisporus-cultivation-biology]]* (button/cremini/portobello), *Pleurotus* species (oyster mushrooms), and *Lentinula edodes* (shiitake) are all members.
- **[[magic-mushroom-forensic-identification-challenges-shirota]]:** The Agaricini require the most careful species-level identification because the group contains both delectable edibles and lethal toxic species, sometimes within the same genus.
- **Taxonomic framework:** Understanding gill structure remains one of the first steps in mushroom identification. Gill attachment, spacing, and color are key diagnostic features used in all modern field guides.
- **Safety education:** Palmer"s warning that this group "cannot be safely used except on the recognition of each species" remains valid and is a cornerstone of modern foraging safety education.
- **Nutritional value:** Palmer noted that mushrooms occupy "a place between animal and vegetable food, approximating to the former more than to the latter" -- an observation that aligns with modern [[fukuoka-textdoc-fallacy-infinite-nutritional-analysis]] showing relatively high protein content in many Agaricini.
