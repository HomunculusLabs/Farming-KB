## Chicory (Cichorium intybus)

## Description

Chicory (*[[cichorium-intybus]]*) is a deep-rooted perennial herbaceous plant in the
Asteraceae (daisy) family, native to Europe but now naturalised worldwide. It is
widely recognised for its bright blue flowers, tough upright stems that can reach
1-1.5 metres in height, and its remarkably deep taproot that can extend several
metres into the soil profile. The plant has a long history of use as a coffee
substitute, forage crop, salad green, and medicinal herb.

In permaculture and sustainable agriculture, chicory is valued primarily as a
deep-rooted pasture plant that extracts nutrients from deep soil layers and opens
up poor clay subsoils. This biological "mining" function makes it an important
species in biological services approaches to soil improvement, where plants
perform the work that would otherwise require mechanical intervention or external
inputs.

Chicory is also well known for its cultivation as a forced vegetable (witloof
chicory or Belgian endive), where the roots are dug up and forced in darkness to
produce tight, pale heads of leaves prized as a bitter salad green.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Asteraceae
- **Genus**: Cichorium
- **Species**: C. intybus
- **Common Names**: Chicory, common chicory, blue sailors, coffeeweed, wild
  chicory, succory, Belgian endive (forced form), witloof
- **Origin**: Europe, Western Asia, North Africa
- **Growth Habit**: Deep-rooted perennial herb

## Key Facts

- The deep taproot of chicory can penetrate 2-3 metres or more into the soil,
  accessing nutrients and moisture unavailable to shallow-rooted plants.
- As a pasture plant, chicory is capable of extracting minerals from deep clay
  subsoils and bringing them to the surface through leaf litter and root turnover,
  slowly improving topsoil fertility.
- The plant contains inulin, a polysaccharide used as a prebiotic fibre in food
  products and traditionally as a coffee additive or substitute.
- Chicory is relatively drought-tolerant once established, making it valuable in
  dryland pasture systems.
- Roasted chicory root has been used as a coffee substitute since the 18th century,
  particularly in France and New Orleans.
- In New Zealand and Australia, chicory cultivars (particularly 'Puna' chicory)
  have been developed as high-quality forage for livestock.
- The plant supports a range of pollinators with its long flowering period through
  summer and autumn.
- Chicory grows well in poor soils and can thrive where many other forage species
  struggle, making it ideal for land rehabilitation.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Soil Structure Improvement**: The deep taproot creates channels in compacted
  clay subsoils, improving aeration and water infiltration — conditions that
  favour mycorrhizal fungal colonisation and general soil biological activity.
- **Nutrient Cycling**: By drawing up minerals from deep soil layers, chicory
  makes nutrients available to surface-dwelling soil organisms, including
  decomposer fungi, supporting active nutrient cycling.
- **Mycorrhizal Associations**: Chicory forms vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal
  (VAM) associations, particularly with Glomus species, enhancing phosphorus
  uptake and contributing to soil fungal networks.
- **[[companion-planting]]**: In permaculture systems, chicory's deep roots complement
  shallow-rooted crops and can be integrated into food forests as part of the
  ground layer or pasture beneath fruit trees.
- **Organic Matter Contribution**: Leaf litter and [[root-exudates]] from chicory feed
  soil microorganisms, building humus and supporting the decomposer food web that
  includes [[saprophytic-fungi]].
- **Forage System Integration**: When used in rotational grazing systems, chicory
  contributes to building the soil biological community, including fungal
  populations, through root turnover and manure deposition.
- **Forest Garden Role**: Can serve as a mineral-accumulating groundcover in forest
  garden systems, performing a similar function to comfrey in bringing nutrients
  up from deep in the soil profile.
