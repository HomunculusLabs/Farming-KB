# N,N-Dimethyltryptamine N-Oxide

N,N-Dimethyltryptamine N-oxide (DMT N-oxide) is an oxidative metabolite of [[dimethyltryptamine]] (DMT) formed by the action of flavin-containing monooxygenases (FMOs) in the liver and other tissues. It has been identified as a naturally occurring compound in several plant and animal species, and its presence has been investigated in the context of psychedelic tryptamine metabolism.

## Chemical Structure and Properties

DMT N-oxide has the molecular formula C₁₂H₁₆N₂O and a molecular weight of 204.27 g/mol. The compound is structurally identical to [[dimethyltryptamine]] except for the addition of an oxygen atom bonded to the tertiary amine nitrogen, forming an N-oxide functional group. The IUPAC name is N,N-dimethyl-2-(1H-indol-3-yl)ethanamine N-oxide.

The N-oxide group is a polar, zwitterionic functionality that significantly alters the physicochemical properties of the molecule compared to [[dimethyltryptamine]]. DMT N-oxide is more water-soluble than DMT and exists as a stable, crystalline solid rather than an oily liquid. It is more polar and less lipophilic, which affects its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier.

## Biosynthesis and Metabolism

DMT N-oxide is produced in vivo through the enzymatic oxidation of [[dimethyltryptamine]] by flavin-containing monooxygenase enzymes, particularly FMO3 in the human liver. This is a phase I metabolic reaction that converts the lipophilic tertiary amine into a more polar N-oxide for potential excretion.

The metabolic pathway can be summarized as:

1. [[tryptamine]] is N,N-dimethylated (by indolethylamine N-methyltransferase, INMT) to produce [[dimethyltryptamine]].
2. [[dimethyltryptamine]] undergoes N-oxidation by FMO enzymes to yield DMT N-oxide.
3. DMT N-oxide may be further metabolized, excreted, or potentially reduced back to DMT under certain conditions.

There is evidence that DMT N-oxide can be reduced back to [[dimethyltryptamine]] in the body, particularly in the gastrointestinal tract and possibly in the brain. This reduction has been proposed as a potential mechanism for the slow release or prolonged activity of orally ingested DMT preparations.

## Occurrence in Nature

DMT N-oxide has been detected in several natural sources, contributing to the complex alkaloid profiles of these organisms:

- **Psychotria viridis and Diplopterys cabrerana**: The two primary DMT-containing admixture plants used in ayahuasca preparations have been found to contain DMT N-oxide alongside [[dimethyltryptamine]]. The N-oxide may constitute 5 to 15% of the total DMT-related alkaloids in fresh leaf material.
- **Phalaris species**: Grasses of the genus Phalaris, known to contain tryptamine alkaloids, have been reported to contain DMT N-oxide.
- **Animal tissues**: Endogenous DMT N-oxide has been detected in mammalian brain tissue, cerebrospinal fluid, and urine, consistent with its role as a metabolic product of endogenous DMT.

The presence of DMT N-oxide in plant materials is notable because it may be partially converted back to [[dimethyltryptamine]] during the preparation of ayahuasca or other plant-based preparations, potentially contributing to the overall DMT content of the brew.

## Pharmacology

The pharmacological activity of DMT N-oxide is less well characterized than that of [[dimethyltryptamine]]:

- **Receptor affinity**: DMT N-oxide has been reported to retain some affinity for [[serotonin]] receptors, particularly 5-HT₁A and 5-HT₂A, though generally at lower potency than DMT itself. The N-oxide group reduces the basicity of the amine nitrogen, which is an important feature for 5-HT receptor binding.
- **Blood-brain barrier penetration**: Due to its increased polarity, DMT N-oxide crosses the blood-brain barrier less efficiently than [[dimethyltryptamine]], which may limit its central nervous system effects.
- **Potential prodrug activity**: The possibility that DMT N-oxide may be reduced to DMT in vivo has led to speculation that it could serve as a slow-release prodrug, though this hypothesis requires further investigation.

## Analytical Detection

DMT N-oxide can be detected and distinguished from [[dimethyltryptamine]] using:

- **LC-MS/MS**: The most reliable method, as the N-oxide has a distinct mass (+16 Da compared to DMT) and fragmentation pattern.
- **HPLC**: With appropriate chromatographic conditions, DMT N-oxide can be separated from DMT based on its greater polarity and shorter retention time on reverse-phase columns.
- **NMR spectroscopy**: The chemical shift of the N-methyl protons is diagnostic for distinguishing the N-oxide from the parent amine.

Care must be taken during sample preparation, as DMT N-oxide can be partially reduced to DMT under certain analytical conditions (e.g., exposure to reducing agents, elevated temperatures, or certain stationary phases).

## Reduction Back to DMT

A particularly interesting aspect of DMT N-oxide pharmacology is its potential for enzymatic reduction back to [[dimethyltryptamine]] in vivo. This retroconversion has several implications:

- **Gastrointestinal reduction**: The gut microbiome contains organisms capable of reducing N-oxide functional groups. This means that orally ingested DMT N-oxide may be partially converted to active [[dimethyltryptamine]] before absorption, potentially contributing to the overall psychoactive effect.
- **Hepatic cycling**: Liver enzymes may interconvert DMT and DMT N-oxide, creating a metabolic cycle that could extend the duration of detectable [[dimethyltryptamine]] levels in the body beyond what would be expected from a single dose.
- **Blood-brain barrier**: While DMT N-oxide itself may have limited ability to cross the blood-brain barrier due to its polarity, any reduction to [[dimethyltryptamine]] in brain tissue or at the capillary endothelium could produce centrally active DMT locally.
- **Analytical implications**: The presence of DMT N-oxide in biological samples may confound estimates of [[dimethyltryptamine]] exposure, as the N-oxide can be reduced during sample preparation or analysis, artificially inflating measured DMT levels.

## Quantitative Analysis

Accurate quantification of DMT N-oxide requires specialized analytical approaches:

- **Stability during sample collection**: Blood and urine samples for DMT N-oxide analysis should be collected with antioxidant additives and stored frozen immediately to prevent enzymatic interconversion between DMT and its N-oxide.
- **Chromatographic separation**: Reverse-phase HPLC with a polar-embedded stationary phase provides adequate separation of DMT N-oxide from [[dimethyltryptamine]]. The N-oxide elutes earlier than DMT due to its greater polarity.
- **Mass spectrometric detection**: Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions specific to DMT N-oxide (e.g., m/z 205 to 188, corresponding to loss of NH₃) and to [[dimethyltryptamine]] (m/z 189 to 172) allow simultaneous quantification of both species.
- **Reference standards**: Authentic DMT N-oxide reference material is commercially available from several chemical suppliers, enabling calibration and method validation.

Typical concentrations of DMT N-oxide in human urine following [[dimethyltryptamine]] administration are on the order of 10 to 50% of the parent DMT concentration, though this ratio varies with dose, route of administration, and individual metabolic factors.

## Endogenous DMT and the Trace Amine Hypothesis

The discovery of DMT N-oxide alongside endogenous [[dimethyltryptamine]] in mammalian tissues has fueled speculation about a possible physiological role for these compounds:

- **Sigma-1 receptor**: Both [[dimethyltryptamine]] and DMT N-oxide have been reported to interact with sigma-1 receptors, which are involved in cellular stress response and neuroprotection.
- **Neuroplasticity**: Recent research has demonstrated that sub-threshold doses of [[dimethyltryptamine]] can promote neurite outgrowth and synaptic plasticity in vitro.
- **Peripheral signaling**: The presence of DMT N-oxide in peripheral tissues suggests potential roles beyond the central nervous system.

These hypotheses remain speculative and require further investigation with more sensitive detection methods.

## Occurrence in Plant Materials

The detection of DMT N-oxide in plant materials used in traditional preparations has practical significance:

- **Ayahuasca**: [[psychotria-viridis]] leaves have been found to contain both [[dimethyltryptamine]] and DMT N-oxide. During the brewing process, which involves prolonged heating in acidic conditions, some DMT N-oxide may be reduced or hydrolyzed, contributing to the total DMT content of the finished brew.
- **Storage and degradation**: Plant materials containing [[dimethyltryptamine]] may gradually oxidize to form DMT N-oxide during storage, particularly if not properly dried or if stored under conditions that favor oxidation.
- **Extraction considerations**: Standard extraction protocols for [[dimethyltryptamine]] from plant materials may not efficiently recover DMT N-oxide, potentially leading to underestimation of total alkaloid content. Basic extraction conditions followed by acid-base partitioning can improve recovery of both species.

## Endogenous Occurrence and the DMT N-Oxide Hypothesis

The detection of DMT N-oxide in mammalian tissues has contributed to discussions about endogenous [[dimethyltryptamine]] and its possible physiological roles:

- **Trace levels in brain**: Both [[dimethyltryptamine]] and DMT N-oxide have been detected in rat and human brain tissue at trace concentrations (low nanomolar range), though the functional significance of these levels remains debated.
- **Enzymatic cycling**: The interconversion between DMT and DMT N-oxide by FMO and reductase enzymes suggests the possibility of a metabolic cycle that could modulate local concentrations of free [[dimethyltryptamine]] in tissues.
- **Physiological relevance**: Some researchers have proposed that endogenous [[dimethyltryptamine]] and its metabolites, including DMT N-oxide, may function as trace amines or neuromodulators, though this hypothesis lacks strong experimental support.
- **Biomarker potential**: DMT N-oxide levels in urine or blood have been investigated as potential biomarkers for [[dimethyltryptamine]] exposure in clinical and forensic settings, where the longer detection window of the N-oxide compared to the rapidly cleared parent compound is advantageous.

## Comparison with Other Tryptamine N-Oxides

DMT N-oxide is one of several tryptamine N-oxides that occur in nature or have been synthesized:

- **Bufotenine N-oxide**: The N-oxide derivative of [[bufotenin-entity-tihkal]] (5-hydroxy-DMT), which may contribute to the complex pharmacology of toad venom preparations.
- **Psilocin N-oxide**: A potential metabolite of [[psilocin]], though it has not been extensively characterized.
- **Serotonin N-oxide**: An oxidative metabolite of [[serotonin]] that has been detected in brain tissue and may have unique pharmacological properties distinct from the parent compound.

The N-oxide functional group adds polarity, reduces basicity, and alters receptor binding compared to the parent amine. These modifications generally reduce central nervous system activity but may confer unique pharmacological properties that warrant further investigation.

The systematic study of tryptamine N-oxides is an area of growing interest in psychopharmacology, as these metabolites may contribute to the overall pharmacological profile of psychedelic tryptamines in ways that are not fully captured by studying the parent compounds alone. Advances in analytical sensitivity have enabled the detection of N-oxide metabolites at increasingly low concentrations, revealing their presence in biological systems where they were previously undetectable.
