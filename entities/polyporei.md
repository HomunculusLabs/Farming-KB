## Description

The Polyporei are a historical taxonomic grouping of fungi characterized by having a sponge-like mass of pores or tubes beneath the cap, rather than the thin gills (lamellae) seen in the Agaricini. In Palmer's 19th-century classification, the Polyporei included both the Boleti (with well-defined stems and distinct, separable pores) and the shelf-like polypores (often stemless, growing on wood). The modern taxonomic equivalents span multiple orders, primarily the Polyporales and parts of the Boletales. This group contains some of the most important edible and medicinal fungi known to humanity.

## Classification

- **Taxonomic rank:** Informal group (historical)
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Modern equivalents:** Order Polyporales, Order Boletales, and related groups
- **Defining feature:** Pores or tubes (not gills) beneath the cap surface
- **Sub-groups:** Boleti (with stem and separable pore layer) vs. polypores proper (often stemless, bracket/shelf form)

## Key Facts

- Palmer described the Polyporei as having "in place of these lines [gills], a sponge or mass of tubes" under the cap -- a clear distinguishing feature from the gilled Agaricini that even a beginner can recognize.
- The subgroup known as **Boleti** (modern family Boletaceae) was identified as having a well-defined stem and pores that are distinct and separable from the cap flesh.
- *[[polyporus-squamosus]]* was specifically mentioned as a member with bitter taste, tasting "of leaves and grass" -- representative of the non-Boletus polypores that are generally not considered choice edibles.
- Palmer considered the Boleti "probably free from danger to human life," noting that no student would likely eat one of the nauseous varieties, and that mild-tasting, modest-colored Boleti are "delicious eating."
- The highly colored Boleti had "the reputation of being poisonous," though Palmer noted this was a generalization.
- Many polypore species are bitter, slimy, or tough -- qualities that naturally discourage consumption.
- The Polyporei were classified as the second of three major groups in Palmer's identification framework.

## Sub-groups

### Boleti
- Well-defined central stem supporting a cap
- Pore layer clearly separable from cap flesh
- Generally safe if mild-tasting and modest-colored
- Highly colored species (red, blue-staining) have reputation for toxicity
- Many delicious edible species including *[[boletus-edulis-and-pore-fungi-polyporaceae]]* (porcini/cep)
- Palmer's rule: "The mild tasting, modest-colored Boleti are delicious eating"

### Polypores Proper
- Often stemless, growing as brackets or shelves on living or dead trees
- Many species bitter, tough, or woody in texture
- Generally not eaten directly due to texture, but some have significant medicinal value
- *Polyporus squamosus* (dryad's saddle) mentioned as bitter-tasting
- Major decomposers of dead wood in forest ecosystems

## Comparison with Other Groups

Palmer's practical three-way classification system for [[mushroom-identification]]:
1. **Agaricini** (gilled): Largest group, requires species-level identification, contains both deadly and delicious species -- the most challenging group
2. **Polyporei** (pored): Generally safer than Agaricini, Boleti can be generalized as safe if mild-tasting -- moderate difficulty
3. **Puff-balls** (globular): Safest group, all species edible when young and white inside -- easiest for beginners

This three-way division remains a useful starting point for [[bloomfield-angel-of-death-poisonous-mushroom-identification-toxinology]] today and is the basis for most beginner field guides.

## Safety Profile

The Polyporei have a notably different safety profile from the Agaricini:
- **No confirmed human fatalities** from Boleti (though some cause severe gastrointestinal distress)
- **Natural deterrents:** Toxic or unpalatable species are bitter, acrid, slimy, or otherwise obviously unpleasant
- **Visual cues:** Highly colored species (especially those that bruise blue or red) are more likely to cause problems
- **Palmer's assessment:** "I do not believe a student would ever eat one of the family containing nauseous elements"

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The Polyporei remain significant in both taxonomy and practical mycology:

- **Boletales cultivation:** While most Boleti are mycorrhizal and difficult to cultivate commercially, they are highly prized as wild-foraged foods. *Boletus edulis* (porcini/cep) is among the most valuable wild mushrooms globally, with annual trade worth billions of dollars.
- **Medicinal polypores:** Many Polyporei have significant medicinal value. Species like *[[ganoderma-lucidum]]* (reishi/lingzhi), *[[growing-gourmet-turkey-tail-trametes-versicolor-cultivation]]* (turkey tail), and *[[inonotus-obliquus]]* (chaga) are widely used in traditional and modern medicine. Some are now cultivated specifically for medicinal extraction.
- **Identification framework:** The pore/tube vs. gill distinction remains one of the most fundamental divisions in mushroom identification. Beginners are taught to check for gills, pores, teeth, or smooth surfaces as a first step.
- **Ecological role:** Polyporei are major decomposers of wood in forest ecosystems, playing critical roles in [[ectomycorrhizal-nutrient-cycling-and-forest-dynamics]] carbon sequestration. Bracket fungi can live for decades and produce enormous quantities of spores from their pore surfaces.
- **Safety generalization:** Palmer's observation that Boleti are generally safe (if mild-tasting) remains a useful guideline for foragers, though modern guides caution about a few genuinely toxic species (e.g., *[[rubroboletus-satanas]]*, the Devil's bolete).
