# Thorazine (Chlorpromazine)

## Description

Thorazine, known generically as chlorpromazine, is a phenothiazine-class antipsychotic and major tranquilizer. Developed in the early 1950s by the French pharmaceutical company Rhône-Poulenc, it revolutionized psychiatric treatment and is widely regarded as the first modern antipsychotic medication. Its introduction in 1954 is often cited as a turning point in psychopharmacology, enabling the deinstitutionalization of psychiatric patients and fundamentally altering the landscape of mental health care.

In *The Psychedelic Experience*, Leary references Thorazine specifically as a pharmacological emergency tool — a means of terminating the effects of psychedelic drugs ([[lsd]], [[mescaline]], [[dmt]]) in crisis situations. Critically, Leary restricts its use to genuine external emergencies only and explicitly counsels against administering it for the subjective fear or distress of either the guide or the voyager.

## Classification

- **Chemical class:** Phenothiazine
- **Pharmacological class:** Typical (first-generation) antipsychotic; dopamine D2 receptor antagonist; major tranquilizer
- **Legal status:** Prescription medication; not a controlled substance but regulated as a pharmaceutical
- **Therapeutic use:** Schizophrenia, bipolar disorder (manic phase), severe behavioral disturbances, nausea/vomiting, intractable hiccups

## Dosage Information (per Leary)

The Psychedelic Experience specifies Thorazine as an emergency termination agent:

| Context | Dosage | Route | Purpose |
|---------|--------|-------|---------|
| Emergency psychedelic termination | 100–200 mg | Intramuscular (IM) injection | Rapidly abort the psychedelic experience |

This dosage range is consistent with the acute psychiatric use of chlorpromazine for managing agitated or psychotic states in clinical settings.

## Pharmacokinetics

- **Route of administration:** Oral, intramuscular (IM), or intravenous (IV)
- **Onset (IM):** 15–30 minutes for sedative effects
- **Duration of action:** 4–6 hours (biological effects persist considerably longer due to extensive tissue binding)
- **Metabolism:** Hepatic, via cytochrome P450 enzymes
- **Half-life:** 16–30 hours (highly variable)

## Key Facts

1. Chlorpromazine was synthesized in 1950 by Paul Charpentier at Rhône-Poulenc and first used psychiatrically by Henri Laborit in 1952.
2. Its mechanism of action — antagonism of dopamine D2 receptors — provided the foundation for the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia.
3. Thorazine was approved by the FDA in 1954 and rapidly became the most prescribed psychiatric medication in the United States.
4. It effectively terminates the effects of LSD and other serotonergic psychedelics by antagonizing the dopamine and [[serotonin]] receptors through which these compounds exert their effects.
5. Side effects include significant sedation, orthostatic hypotension, extrapyramidal symptoms (movement disorders), and long-term risk of tardive dyskinesia.
6. The drug's ability to abort psychedelic experiences made it a standard tool in clinical psychedelic research protocols of the 1950s and 1960s.

## Relevance to Psychedelic Studies

In the framework of *The Psychedelic Experience*, Thorazine occupies a deliberately circumscribed and cautious role. Leary's position reflects a sophisticated understanding of the dynamics of psychedelic sessions:

**Permitted use:** Genuine external emergencies — situations involving physical danger, medical crises, or threats that require the voyager to return to ordinary consciousness immediately. For example, fire, medical emergency, or physical threat.

**Prohibited use:** Internal psychological distress. Leary explicitly warns against administering Thorazine to terminate the experience simply because the guide is frightened, the subject is distressed, or the session has entered difficult psychological territory. Such premature termination, he argues, denies the voyager the opportunity to work through the challenging material and risks leaving them stranded in an unresolved psychological state — arguably worse than continuing through the difficulty.

This distinction reflects a core principle of psychedelic therapy: that the difficult passages of a session often contain the most valuable therapeutic material. Aborting the experience pharmacologically treats the symptom (distress) while preventing the resolution (integration and insight) that would occur naturally as the drug's effects subside.

The guide's role, in Leary's framework, is to maintain calm and confidence, helping the voyager navigate difficulty without retreating to chemical intervention. Thorazine is reserved as a last resort — the pharmacological equivalent of pulling the fire alarm.

## Historical Context

During the 1950s and 1960s, Thorazine was routinely available in clinical psychedelic research settings. Researchers including [[humphry-osmond]] and Abram Hoffer used chlorpromazine as a safety net in their LSD studies with alcoholics. The drug's ready availability and established safety profile made it a standard component of clinical psychedelic research protocols.

## Safety Considerations

- Sedation and cognitive impairment may persist well beyond the termination of the psychedelic experience.
- Orthostatic hypotension can cause dizziness or fainting upon standing.
- Extrapyramidal side effects (muscle rigidity, restlessness, tremor) may occur, particularly at higher doses.
- Long-term use carries risk of tardive dyskinesia, but this is not a concern in single-dose emergency use.
- Should only be administered by trained medical personnel when possible.

## The Ethics of Trip Termination

Leary's restriction on Thorazine use illuminates a broader ethical stance in psychedelic therapy: the guide's obligation is to the voyager's process, not to the guide's comfort. A frightened guide who administers Thorazine to calm their own anxiety betrays the fundamental trust of the guide-voyager relationship. The psychedelic experience, even in its most challenging moments, is understood as a natural and potentially transformative psychological process. Interruption should occur only when genuine external circumstances demand it.

## Modern Alternatives

In contemporary psychedelic therapy, benzodiazepines (such as lorazepam or diazepam) are generally preferred over antipsychotics for managing acute distress. Benzodiazepines reduce anxiety without fully terminating the psychedelic experience, allowing the session to continue at a more manageable intensity. This represents an evolution beyond Leary's binary approach (full experience vs. full termination) toward a more nuanced pharmacological toolkit.

## Contraindications

- Should not be used as a routine or preemptive measure during psychedelic sessions.
- Not recommended for managing the guide's own anxiety or discomfort.
- Individuals with Parkinson's disease or Lewy body dementia may experience severe adverse reactions.
