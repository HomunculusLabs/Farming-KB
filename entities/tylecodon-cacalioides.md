## Overview

**Tylecodon cacalioides** (L. f.) Tölken, commonly called the butter candle or candle bush, is a succulent shrub in the family Crassulaceae. It is native to the Western and Northern Cape provinces of South Africa.

The species is notable for its thick, waxy, yellow-green stems and its winter-growing habit. It produces dense clusters of tubular yellow flowers that are among the showiest in the genus.

Tylecodon cacalioides grows as a small, sparingly branched shrub reaching 30–60 cm tall. It is a winter-grower that is leafless during the hot summer months, a characteristic shared by all Tylecodon species.

The species is highly toxic to livestock and contains bufadienolide cardiac glycosides. Despite its toxicity, it is cultivated by succulent enthusiasts for its unusual growth form and attractive flowers.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Tylecodon cacalioides was originally described by Carl Linnaeus the Younger as Cotyledon cacalioides in 1782. Helmut Tölken transferred it to the genus Tylecodon in 1978 as part of his revision of the Crassulaceae.

The genus Tylecodon was separated from Cotyledon based on differences in growth habit and flowering time. Tylecodon species are winter-growing and flower in summer when leafless, while Cotyledon species are summer-growing.

The plant develops a thick, succulent caudiciform stem base that stores water and nutrients. The main stem is 3–8 cm in diameter, with bark that is smooth, pale gray-green, and peeling in papery strips.

Branches are few, erect to spreading, and covered with a waxy yellow-green cuticle. The waxy coating gives the branches a distinctive glossy appearance.

Leaves are produced in terminal clusters during the winter growing season. Each leaf is 2–5 cm long, oblong to obovate, with a rounded tip and slightly undulate margins.

Leaf surfaces are bright green, glabrous, and slightly fleshy. Leaves are shed in late spring as the plant enters summer dormancy.

Inflorescences are tall, unbranched or sparingly branched racemes reaching 20–40 cm. They emerge from the branch tips in summer when the plant is leafless.

Flowers are tubular, 1.5–2 cm long, bright yellow to yellowish-green. The petals are fused into a narrow tube with recurved lobes at the mouth.

Flowering occurs from December through February in the southern hemisphere. The showy yellow blooms are pollinated by moths and other nocturnal insects.

## Distribution and Habitat

Tylecodon cacalioides is endemic to the winter-rainfall region of South Africa. Its range extends from the Richtersveld in the Northern Cape southward through Namaqualand to the western parts of the Western Cape.

Plants grow on rocky slopes, hilltops, and among granite boulders in arid environments. They are found from 200 to 1,200 m elevation.

The species occurs in the Succulent Karoo biome, one of the world"s most biodiverse arid regions. Annual rainfall averages 100–250 mm, falling predominantly in winter.

Plants occupy well-drained positions in full sun. The rocky substrate provides root anchorage and protection from excessive soil moisture.

Tylecodon cacalioides is frequently found growing alongside other succulent species. Common associates include species of Aloe, Euphorbia, Lithops, and other Crassulaceae.

The species is well-adapted to extreme heat and prolonged drought. During summer dormancy, the leafless stems rely on stored water while the root system remains largely inactive.

Fog is an important moisture source in the Namaqualand portion of the range. Coastal fog rolls inland during summer mornings, providing humidity that supplements the minimal winter rainfall.

The Succulent Karoo where T. cacalioides grows is recognized as a global biodiversity hotspot. The region contains over 6,000 plant species, approximately 40% of which are endemic.

Plants can live for several decades in favorable conditions. Older specimens develop characteristically gnarled stems with multiple branching points and a thickened caudex.

## Cultivation

Tylecodon cacalioides requires careful cultivation practices that match its natural growth cycle. It is a winter grower that should be watered from autumn through spring and kept dry during summer.

The growing medium must be extremely well-draining. A mix of 80% pumice or coarse sand with 20% loam-based compost is recommended.

Watering during the winter growing season should be moderate, allowing the soil to dry between applications. Any watering during the summer dormant period risks fatal root rot.

Plants prefer full sun during the growing season. In cultivation at higher latitudes, a south-facing position provides the intense light needed for compact growth.

Temperature requirements range from 5–28 °C during active growth. The species tolerates light frost if kept dry but is damaged by prolonged freezing.

Propagation is primarily by seed, as stem cuttings are unreliable. Seed should be sown in autumn on the surface of moist, sterile, gritty medium.

Germination occurs within 1–3 weeks at 15–20 °C. Seedlings are tiny and grow slowly, requiring careful attention to watering during their first year.

The species is not recommended for indoor cultivation except in a greenhouse. It requires the bright light and temperature cycling that only outdoor or greenhouse conditions can provide.

## Ecology and Conservation

Tylecodon cacalioides contains potent bufadienolide cardiac glycosides that are toxic to mammals. The toxins are concentrated in the leaves and stems, providing effective defense against most herbivores.

Livestock poisoning is a documented problem in Namaqualand, particularly during drought when other forage is scarce. Sheep and goats that consume the plant develop cardiac symptoms that can be fatal.

The species is classified as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. Its relatively wide distribution across the Succulent Karoo provides conservation security.

Pollination is performed by nocturnal moths attracted to the pale yellow flowers and their nectar reward. The tubular flower shape is characteristic of moth-pollinated plants in the Crassulaceae.

The species is not known to be invasive outside its native range. Its specific climatic requirements and toxicity prevent naturalization in most cultivated environments.

Tylecodon cacalioides is protected under South African biodiversity legislation. Collection from wild populations requires permits, and most plants in cultivation are nursery-propagated.

The genus name Tylecodon is derived from the Greek "tylos" (knob) and "kodion" (small head), referring to the knobby stem tubercles found on many species. The species epithet "cacalioides" means "resembling Cacalia," a genus in the Asteraceae.

The plant has no known traditional medicinal use due to its high toxicity. Indigenous knowledge systems in Namaqualand recognize it as a plant to be avoided by livestock.

Research on Tylecodon cardiac glycosides has contributed to understanding of bufadienolide pharmacology. These compounds have been investigated for potential anticancer properties, though no commercial applications have been developed.
