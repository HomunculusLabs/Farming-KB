# Coprinus comatus

Coprinus comatus, commonly known as the Shaggy Mane, is a favorite edible mushroom among foragers in North America and Europe. Easy to identify, often growing in massive quantities, this brilliant white mushroom is hard to miss and difficult to confuse with poisonous species. Its fragile constitution and unique method of self-destruction (deliquescence), combined with its mild but excellent flavor, has made it a popular mushroom among hikers and hunters. See calvatia-gigantea for another large distinctive mushroom and [[marasmius-oreades]] for another lawn-inhabiting species.

## Taxonomy and Description

- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Coprinaceae
- **Synonyms:** None commonly used
- **Common names:** Shaggy Mane, Lawyer's Wig, Maotou-Guisafl (Chinese)

### Morphology

- **Cap:** 4-10 (15) cm high by 3-4 (5) cm thick, vertically oblong, dingy brown at first, soon white, and decorated with ascending scales.
- **Gills:** Crowded, white to pale, long, broad, and slightly attached or free to the stem.
- **Stem:** 6-12 (15) cm long by 1-2 cm thick, equal, hollow, bulbous at the base, adorned with a movable, membranous collar-like ring that separates from the cap margin as the mushrooms enlarge.
- **Deliquescence:** As mushrooms mature, the gills blacken and transform into a black, spore-laden fluid which drips from the rapidly receding cap margin. The cap eventually totally recedes, leaving only the stem.
- **Spores:** Black, 11-15 x 6.0-8.5 µm, ellipsoid, with a germ pore at one end. Subhymenium cellular.

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Global range:** Late summer and fall throughout the temperate regions of the world.
- **Habitat:** In lawns, meadows, around barnyards, in wood chips, along roadsides, and in enriched soils.
- **Growth pattern:** Cosmopolitan; Shaggy Manes are extremely adaptive and tend to wander, often fruiting meters away from the original site of inoculation.

## Growth Parameters

### Spawn Run
- **Incubation Temperature:** 70-80°F (21-27°C)
- **Relative Humidity:** 95-100%
- **Duration:** 12-14 days
- **CO2:** 5,000-20,000 ppm
- **Fresh Air Exchanges:** 0-1 per hour
- **Light Requirements:** n/a

### Primordia Formation
- **Initiation Temperature:** 60-70°F (16-21°C)
- **Relative Humidity:** 95-100%
- **Duration:** 12-15 days after casing
- **CO2:** 500-1,000 ppm
- **Fresh Air Exchanges:** 4-8 per hour
- **Light Requirements:** 500-1,000 lux for 8 hours per day

### Fruitbody Development
- **Incubation Temperature:** 65-75°F (18-24°C)
- **Relative Humidity:** 80-90%
- **Duration:** 5-7 days
- **CO2:** 500-1,000 ppm
- **Fresh Air Exchanges:** 4-8 per hour
- **Light Requirements:** 500-1,000 lux for 8 hours per day

### Cropping Cycle
- Two to three flushes, four to ten days apart.

## Cultivation

- **Mycelial characteristics:** Usually white, cottony, aerial, often develops tufts (hyphal aggregates) with maturity. Most strains form mycelial mats asymmetrically shaped along the outer edge.
- **Agar media:** PDA, PDYA, MEA, MYPA, DFA, or OMYA.
- **Spawn media:** Grain (rye, wheat, sorghum) throughout all generations. Adaptive to liquid fermentation.
- **Fruiting substrates:** Straw/manure compost (formulas described by Stamets and Chilton support substantial fruitings). Manure and urea-enriched sawdust also supports fruitings. Paper and pulp waste discharged from horse stables also support fruitings. Adding soya flour at 2% (giving 0.5% nitrogen content) yields approach 80% biological efficiency. Manure compost often exceeds 100% biological efficiency.
- **Casing:** This mushroom greatly benefits from a peat moss and gypsum-based casing soil. Not inclined to grow from anything but horizontal surfaces.
- **Containers:** Trays and bags.

## Primordia Formation

Primordia form as circular "dials," between the size of a dime to a quarter. These primordia are unique in that they are wide and flat. An inner collar forms within the dial and arises to form a dome. This dome soon shoots up to form a recognizable mushroom. The circular zone visible at the primordial dial stage becomes the movable ring resting on the stem of the mature mushroom. Yields can be substantially increased if the casing layer is vigorously raked just as the mycelium begins to show on the surface.

## Harvest and Storage

Mushrooms should be picked before the slightest hint of the gills turning black. If picked when no basidia have matured, mushrooms can be kept in cold storage for 4-5 days. Any mushrooms that begin to deliquesce should be removed from the fresher fruitbodies since the enzymes secreted by one deliquescing mushroom will decompose adjacent mushrooms, regardless of age. Mushrooms can be preserved by submerging in cold water under refrigeration. Packing in refrigerated, nitrogen gas-filled containers also extends shelf life. Young shaggy manes can be thinly sliced and quickly dried for storage. Freeze drying is also an option.

## Nutritional Content

- **Protein:** 25-29% (N x 4.38)
- **Fat:** 3%
- **Carbohydrates:** 59%
- **Fiber:** 3-7%
- **Ash:** 1-18%

## Medicinal Properties

A novel antibiotic has been isolated from this species and is currently being characterized by American researchers. Ying (1987) reports that "inhibition rates against sarcoma 180 and Ehrlich carcinoma are 100% and 90% respectively." No other research on the anti-tumor properties of this mushroom is widely known.

## Flavor, Preparation, and Cooking

Shaggy Manes have considerably more moisture than [[lentinula-edodes]] for instance, so the water should be cooked off before other ingredients are added. Preparation methods include frying thinly cut slices (stem included) with onions and light oil for omelets, or frying in butter at medium heat and serving on whole wheat toast. Many recipes can incorporate Shaggy Manes.

## Comments

This is a great mushroom to grow in your yard and in compost piles. Once an outdoor patch is established, Shaggy Manes can fruit for many years. Newly laid or fertilized lawns that are frequently watered are perfect habitats. Cow or horse manure, mixed with straw or sawdust, are also ideal substrates for establishing outdoor patches. A sporeless strain has been isolated that delays deliquescence, which is needed before commercial cultivation becomes practical.

## Historical and Cultural Significance

Coprinus comatus has a rich history as both a culinary mushroom and a scientific curiosity:

- **Scientific name meaning:** The genus name Coprinus comes from the Greek "kopros" (dung), referring to the habitat of many species in the genus. The specific epithet comatus means "hairy," describing the shaggy cap. Together: "the hairy dung-inhabiting mushroom."
- **Scientific importance:** C. comatus was one of the first mushrooms in which the phenomenon of autodigestion (deliquescence) was studied in detail. The rapid breakdown of the gills into an inky fluid fascinated early mycologists and contributed to understanding fungal enzymatic processes.
- **Culinary reputation:** Historically considered one of the finest edible mushrooms in Europe and North America. Many mycologists consider it superior in flavor to the commercial button mushroom.
- **Foraging tradition:** A favorite among mushroom foragers due to its distinctive appearance, which makes misidentification unlikely when specimens are in good condition.
- **Timing sensitivity:** The short window between ideal edibility and inky deliquescence has made C. comatus a symbol of the ephemeral nature of wild mushrooms.

## Seasonal Patterns and Distribution

C. comatus has a predictable seasonal pattern and wide distribution:

- **Spring fruiting:** Often appears in May-June, one of the first large edible mushrooms of the season in many regions.
- **Autumn fruiting:** A second, often more abundant fruiting occurs in September-October.
- **Multi-season:** Can fruit in any month during warm, wet conditions, but spring and autumn are most reliable.
- **Habitat range:** Extremely adaptable. Found in lawns, gardens, pastures, roadsides, compost piles, disturbed soils, and agricultural fields.
- **Geographic range:** Widespread across the Northern Hemisphere. Found throughout North America, Europe, and Asia. Also reported from Australia and New Zealand where it has been introduced.
- **Growth pattern:** Often appears in clusters or troops, sometimes in large numbers. Can return to the same location for multiple years.

## Medicinal Research

Beyond the novel antibiotic and anti-tumor properties mentioned above, C. comatus has shown additional bioactivity:

- **Antioxidant activity:** Extracts show significant free radical scavenging activity, comparable to some commercial antioxidants.
- **Anti-diabetic potential:** Some studies suggest hypoglycemic effects, potentially useful for diabetes management.
- **Anti-inflammatory:** Polysaccharide extracts have demonstrated anti-inflammatory activity in laboratory studies.
- **Heavy metal accumulation:** Like many saprotrophic mushrooms, C. comatus can bioaccumulate heavy metals from contaminated soils. Collection should be avoided in polluted areas.
