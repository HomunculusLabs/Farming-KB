## Description

A windbreak (also called a shelterbelt) is a barrier of trees, shrubs, earthworks, fencing, or constructed materials designed to reduce wind speed and provide shelter for crops, livestock, structures, and soil. In [[permaculture]], windbreaks are often the first infrastructure installed on exposed sites, preceding garden establishment by several years.

## Classification

- **Category:** Landscape design element
- **Domain:** Permaculture, forestry, agriculture, architecture
- **Types:** Living windbreak (trees/shrubs), structural (fences, walls, earth banks), combined systems
- **Also known as:** Shelterbelt, hedgerow, wind fence

## Key Facts

### Wind Effects on Crops and Livestock

- **8 km/h:** Harmless to crops.
- **24 km/h:** Crop production reduced; animal weight loss.
- **32-40 km/h:** Mechanical damage exceeds all other effects. Plants may uproot.
- **Salt-laden coastal winds:** Kill susceptible species through salt burn, even at moderate speeds.
- **Wind-chill factor:** Removes heat from surfaces and evaporates fluids, retarding plant growth.

### Essential Windbreak Design Principles

1. **Species selection:** Choose easily grown pioneer species for the windward row.
2. **Initial protection:** Use bagging, fencing, or earth mounds to shield young plantings.
3. **Irrigation:** Provide periodic or trickle irrigation to reduce desiccation during establishment.
4. **Anchoring:** Use stones or mulch to prevent uprooting of young plants.
5. **Penetrability:** Front-line species should allow 40-50% wind penetration (not solid barriers, which create turbulence).

### Wind-Resistant Plant Characteristics

Plants that resist desiccation and sandblast share common features:

- **Fibrous stems:** Palms.
- **Fleshy leaves:** Aloes, agaves, Euphorbias.
- **Hard needle-like leaves:** Pines, tamarisks, Casuarinas, some Acacias.
- **Hairy or waxy leaves:** Coprosma, eucalypts, some pines, some Acacias.

Many fire-resistant plants are also wind-resistant, making dual-purpose selections possible.

### Multi-Row Windbreak Progression

On severe coastal or desert sites:

- **Rows 1-3:** Hard, wind-flagged pioneer species bear the brunt.
- **Rows 4-5:** Taller, progressively less wind-tolerant species in the lee.
- **Interior:** Wind-sensitive fruit and nut trees can finally produce after the windbreak matures.

### Structural Alternatives

- **Earth mounds:** Streamlined, less sensitive to windthrow. Borrow pit can hold water.
- **Brush or wire-mesh fences:** Must have 40% penetrability.
- **Tyre walls:** Cheap and warm; removable once living windbreak is effective.
- **Trellis:** Sinuous (brick/mud) or zigzagged (timber) to withstand forces.
- **Fedge (fence-hedge):** Mesh fences grown over with semi-succulents such as [[rhagodia]], Tetragonia, Carpobrotus, or Mesembryanthemum.

### Shelterbelt Benefits

- **House heating:** Wind chill removes 60% of heat from glazed areas. Shelterbelt achieves 20-30% fuel savings in moderate to severe winters.
- **Desert cooling:** Shelterbelt trees reduce ground temperatures by up to 15 C.
- **Livestock survival:** Blizzards kill livestock; adapted animals can lose 30% bodyweight in 3 days. Shelterbelt access is critical.

## Relevance to Cultivation

1. **Yield protection:** The difference between some crop and a good crop; in severe areas, between any crop and none.
2. **Microclimate creation:** Moderate temperature, reduce evaporation, increase humidity.
3. **Integrated design:** Windbreaks provide food, fodder, bee forage, timber, and wildlife habitat simultaneously.
4. **Erosion control:** Essential for preventing wind erosion of topsoil.
5. **Coastal agriculture:** Without windbreaks, most coastal food production is impossible.
