## Cortinarius collinitus

## Description

Cortinarius collinitus, commonly known as the smeared cortinarius or the blue-girdled webcap, is a medium-sized agaric mushroom in the family Cortinariaceae. It features a viscid to glutinous cap surface, a cortina (web-like partial veil) that covers the gills in young specimens, and a distinctive slimy stem often with bluish or violet tones near the apex. The genus Cortinarius is the largest genus of mushrooms globally, containing over 2,000 species, but also includes some of the most toxic species known to mycology, making identification to species level absolutely critical before any consideration of consumption.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Cortinariaceae
- **Genus:** Cortinarius
- **Species:** C. collinitus
- **Authority:** (Pers.) Fr.

## Morphology

- **Cap:** 4-10 cm diameter, convex to broadly convex; surface smooth and viscid to slimy when moist, brownish to ochre-brown; often with a paler margin
- **Cortina:** A cobweb-like partial veil covers the gills of young specimens; ruptures as the cap expands, leaving fibrillose remnants on the stem
- **Gills:** Attached, close; initially pale violet-blue, becoming rusty cinnamon-brown as spores mature
- **Stem:** 6-12 cm tall, cylindrical, often covered with a gelatinous sheath; frequently with bluish-violet zones, especially near the apex; sometimes with banded appearance from cortina remnants
- **Flesh:** Pale to slightly bluish; firm
- **[[spore-print]]:** Rusty brown (diagnostic for the genus)
- **Odor/Taste:** Slight, not distinctive

## Key Facts

- Cap is smooth and viscid to slimy when moist, typically brownish to ochre-brown
- Young specimens feature a cortina (cobweb-like veil) covering the gills, which ruptures leaving traces on the stem
- Gills are initially violet-blue, becoming rusty brown as spores mature — a distinctive color transition
- Stem is often covered with a gelatinous layer, sometimes with bluish or violet zones
- Mycorrhizal with conifers, especially spruce (Picea), in temperate and boreal forests
- Considered edible by some European traditions, but extreme caution is warranted due to toxic lookalikes
- The genus Cortinarius contains deadly species with orellanine (e.g., C. rubellus, C. orellanus) that cause delayed irreversible renal failure
- Spore print is rusty brown, which helps distinguish Cortinarius from other genera
- The viscid/gelatinous stem is a useful diagnostic feature within the genus

## Historical Context

Julius A. Palmer mentioned C. collinitus as a species that acquaintances of his consumed without any ill effects, despite not being able to positively identify the varieties they were eating. He classified it after the fact as the "smeared cortinarius." These individuals reportedly experienced nothing more than "an intense desire to hunt for more" mushrooms.

However, Palmer explicitly cautioned against this approach to foraging, noting that the inability to identify any one variety is doubtless the cause of all cases of fatal mushroom poisoning. He used this anecdote not to endorse casual consumption of Cortinarius species, but to illustrate the spectrum of edibility within the fungal kingdom and the importance of proper identification.

## Ecology and Distribution

- Ectomycorrhizal, primarily with conifers (especially spruce)
- Found in coniferous and mixed forests across temperate and boreal regions
- Prefers acidic, moist soils
- Fruits in late summer to autumn
- Widespread across Europe and North America
- Often found in the same habitats as lethally toxic Cortinarius species

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Genus caution:** Cortinarius is the largest mushroom genus on Earth and contains both edible and deadly species; identification to species level is essential and should be confirmed by experts
- **Cortina feature:** The web-like partial veil (cortina) is the defining characteristic of the entire genus and one of the first features to check when identifying an unknown agaric
- **Spore print importance:** The rusty brown spore print helps distinguish Cortinarius from similar-looking genera with white, pink, or dark brown spore prints
- **Toxicology:** The genus contains orellanine-producing species that cause delayed (2-17 days) irreversible renal failure; this underscores why casual consumption of any unidentified Cortinarius is extremely dangerous
- **Mycorrhizal ecology:** Like many Cortinarius species, C. collinitus plays important ecological roles in forest nutrient cycling through its mycorrhizal associations
- **Research:** The genus is actively studied for novel secondary metabolites and toxins

## Warning

This species is documented here for educational and historical purposes. The genus Cortinarius contains multiple lethally toxic species that are extremely difficult to distinguish from edible members. Positive identification requires expert knowledge. Never consume any Cortinarius species unless identified with absolute certainty by an expert mycologist.
