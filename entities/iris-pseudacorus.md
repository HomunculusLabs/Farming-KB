## Overview

**Iris pseudacorus** L., commonly known as yellow flag iris, is a rhizomatous perennial herb of the family Iridaceae. It is native to Europe, western Asia, and North Africa, where it grows along the margins of ponds, lakes, rivers, and marshes.

The genus *Iris* comprises approximately 300 species distributed across temperate Northern Hemisphere. *I. pseudacorus* belongs to the section *Iris* (also called *Limniris* or *Limnogiris*), which includes beardless iris species typically found in moist habitats.

The species is widely cultivated as an ornamental water garden plant but has become a problematic invasive in parts of North America, particularly the Pacific Northwest and eastern Canada. Its dense clonal stands can displace native wetland vegetation and alter hydrology.

Yellow flag iris is one of the tallest and most robust members of the genus *Iris*, exceeded in height only by a few Asian species. It is the only predominantly yellow-flowered iris native to Europe, making it readily identifiable in the field.

The plant forms extensive clonal colonies via stout, horizontally creeping rhizomes. Individual shoots reach 100–150 cm in height, though robust specimens may exceed 200 cm in nutrient-rich waters.

Mature colonies can cover several square meters, with rhizome networks persisting for decades. The rhizomes are thick, fleshy, and dark brown externally with a yellowish interior, producing new fans of leaves annually from apical meristems.

Leaves are equitant, sword-shaped, glaucous-green, and 20–60 mm wide, arising from the rhizome in dense fan-like arrangements. They are prominently ribbed along the midrib and can be longer than the flowering stem.

Flowers are bright golden-yellow, 8–10 cm in diameter, borne on branched stems typically holding 4–12 blooms. Each flower has three large, reflexed falls with brown-purple veining at the base and three upright, smaller standards.

The fruit is a cylindrical capsule, 4–7 cm long, containing numerous flattened, disc-shaped seeds arranged in two rows. Seeds are buoyant and can disperse via water currents for several weeks. Each seed has a conspicuous aril that aids hydrochorous dispersal.

Root systems are fibrous and arise from the upper surface of the rhizome, forming dense mats that can extend 50 cm or more into the substrate. This extensive root network contributes to the species' effectiveness in bank stabilization and water filtration.

## Distribution and Habitat

Yellow flag iris is native to temperate Europe, the British Isles, North Africa (Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia), and western Asia through the Caucasus and into Siberia. Its native range extends from sea level to approximately 1,600 m elevation.

It occupies a broad [[ecological-niche]] within freshwater wetland systems. Typical habitats include shallow lake margins, slow-moving riverbanks, marshes, fens, ditches, and seasonally flooded meadows. It tolerates water depths from 0 to 50 cm but thrives in 5–25 cm of standing water.

The species exhibits considerable phenotypic plasticity, with dwarf forms occurring in oligotrophic montane lakes and robust, tall forms in nutrient-rich lowland waters. Flowering period extends from May to July across most of its range, with seed maturation occurring from August through October.

The species is adaptable to a wide range of soil types including clay, loam, and organic muck. It tolerates moderately acidic to alkaline conditions (pH 5.0–8.5) and grows in full sun to partial shade, though flowering is reduced in heavy shade.

**I. pseudacorus** has been widely introduced beyond its native range through horticultural trade. It is now naturalized across much of temperate North America, southern South America, southern Africa, eastern Asia, and southeastern Australia.

In North America, it is classified as invasive in Washington, Oregon, California, Connecticut, Massachusetts, and several Canadian provinces. Dense monocultures along waterways can impede drainage, crowd out native sedges and rushes, and reduce habitat quality for waterfowl.

Management of invasive populations relies primarily on mechanical removal of rhizomes, which must be conducted thoroughly as even small rhizome fragments can regenerate. The plant is valued for its ability to thrive in marginal soils where few ornamental species survive. It tolerates periodic flooding and drought equally well, making it suitable for rain gardens and bioswales in urban landscaping.

Propagation is primarily by division of rhizomes in late summer or early autumn. Seeds require cold stratification for 2–3 months and germinate readily in moist conditions. The plant is hardy to USDA Zone 3 and requires minimal maintenance once established.

Established clumps may be divided every 3–5 years to maintain vigor and prevent overcrowding. Division also serves as a population control measure in gardens where the species shows invasive tendencies.

In traditional European [[herbal-medicine]], the rhizome was used as a diuretic, cathartic, and emetic. Dried and powdered rhizome was applied externally to wounds and skin eruptions. However, the fresh rhizome is toxic, containing iridin and other irritating glycosides that cause severe gastrointestinal distress.

The rhizome yields a dark blue-black dye historically used in bookbinding, ink manufacture, and textile dyeing. The roots produce a black dye, while the flowers yield a yellow pigment suitable for watercolor painting.

Several cultivars have been selected for horticultural use. 'Variegata' has striped green-and-cream foliage, while 'Roy Davidson' is a compact form with paler yellow flowers. 'Golden Fleece' produces particularly abundant blooms on shorter stems.
