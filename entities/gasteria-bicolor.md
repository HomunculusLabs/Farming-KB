## Overview

**Gasteria bicolor** Haw., commonly called the lawyer's tongue or ox-tongue, is a small succulent in the family Asphodelaceae. It is native to the Eastern Cape and Western Cape provinces of South Africa.

The species is distinguished by its two-toned leaf coloration, with dark green surfaces marked by lighter spots or bands. This bicolor pattern gives the plant its specific epithet and makes it easy to identify.

Gasteria bicolor forms a compact rosette of thick, fleshy leaves that reaches 15–25 cm in diameter. It is one of the most widely cultivated Gasteria species, valued for its architectural form and ease of care.

In cultivation, mature plants readily produce offsets, forming attractive clumps over time. The species adapts well to container growing and is popular in succulent dish gardens and windowsill collections.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Gasteria bicolor was described by Adrian Hardy Haworth in 1819 from material collected in the Eastern Cape. The name "bicolor" refers to the two-toned leaf pattern that characterizes the species.

Two recognized varieties exist: var. bicolor with smooth leaf surfaces and var. liliputiana, a diminutive form reaching only 5–8 cm in diameter. Variety liliputiana was formerly treated as a separate species.

The plant forms a distichous rosette in juvenile growth, with leaves arranged in two opposing rows. Mature plants develop a more spiral leaf arrangement, though some specimens retain the distichous habit.

The transition from distichous to spiral leaf arrangement typically occurs when plants reach 3–5 years of age. This growth habit shift is a reliable indicator of plant maturity in cultivation.

Leaves are dark green with irregular white or pale green spots scattered across both surfaces. Leaf shape is tongue-like (linear-lanceolate), 6–15 cm long and 2–4 cm wide, with a bluntly rounded tip.

The leaf surface is smooth to slightly rough, with a faint waxy bloom. Margins are entire, without teeth, cilia, or spines.

Roots are thick, fleshy, and contractile, capable of pulling the plant deeper into the soil. This root contraction helps protect the stem apex from desiccation and temperature extremes.

The inflorescence is an unbranched or sparingly branched raceme reaching 30–60 cm tall. Flowers are tubular, 2–3 cm long, with a swollen base (gasteriform), giving the genus its name from the Latin "gaster" (stomach).

Flower color ranges from pink to reddish-orange with green-tipped petals. Flowering occurs in late winter to spring in the southern hemisphere, typically July through September.

## Distribution and Habitat

Gasteria bicolor is endemic to South Africa, with a distribution centered in the Eastern Cape province extending into the western parts of the Western Cape. The species occurs in the Albany Thicket and Succulent Karoo biomes.

Plants grow in rocky crevices, on cliff faces, and among boulders in well-drained situations. They are found from near sea level to approximately 800 m elevation.

The species favors partially shaded microhabitats, typically growing under shrubs or in rock overhangs. This sheltered positioning protects plants from intense afternoon sun and reduces water loss.

Annual rainfall in the native range is 300–600 mm, falling predominantly in spring and autumn. Summers are hot and dry, and plants survive by drawing on stored moisture in their thick leaves.

Gasteria bicolor often grows in association with other succulent species including Crassula, Cotyledon, and Bulbine. These mixed succulent communities are characteristic of the Albany Thicket vegetation type.

The species shows some habitat specificity, preferring quartzite or sandstone substrates. Populations on different geological formations may exhibit subtle morphological variation.

Some isolated populations in the western part of the range display more pronounced spotting patterns. These variants are sometimes sought by specialist collectors for their ornamental value.

The species can tolerate mild salt spray, allowing it to occupy coastal sites near the Indian Ocean. Inland populations occur in drier, more continental conditions.

Climate change modeling suggests the species may lose suitable habitat at the warmer margins of its range. However, its occurrence on rocky outcrops provides microrefugia that may buffer against regional temperature increases.

## Cultivation

Gasteria bicolor is one of the easiest succulents to grow and makes an excellent beginner plant. It thrives in bright indirect light but tolerates lower light conditions better than most succulents.

The growing medium should be well-draining with a high proportion of inorganic material. A recommended mix is 60% pumice or perlite with 40% standard potting soil.

Water thoroughly during the growing season, allowing the soil to dry between waterings. Reduce watering in winter when the plant enters a semi-dormant rest period.

Overwatering causes root rot, the most common cause of death in cultivation. The fleshy roots quickly succumb to fungal decay in persistently wet conditions.

Temperature preferences range from 10–30 °C during active growth. The species tolerates brief cold spells to -3 °C if kept dry, but prolonged freezing damages foliage.

Propagation is by offset division, leaf cuttings, or seed. Offsets root readily when separated and planted in moist, well-draining medium.

Leaf cuttings should be allowed to callus for 2–3 days before planting. New plantlets emerge from the base of the leaf within 4–8 weeks.

Seed requires no special treatment but germinates best at 20–25 °C. Fresh seed germinates within 1–3 weeks on the surface of moist, sterile medium.

Plants benefit from occasional flushing with plain water to remove accumulated mineral salts. This practice prevents leaf tip burn and maintains healthy root function over time.

Mealybugs and scale insects are the most common pests. Treatment with systemic insecticide or manual removal with isopropyl alcohol-soaked cotton swabs controls infestations effectively.

## Ecology and Conservation

Gasteria bicolor is pollinated by sunbirds in its native habitat, which are attracted to the nectar-rich flowers. The tubular flower shape is adapted for bird pollination, unlike many succulents that rely on insect vectors.

The species is classified as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. However, some localized populations are threatened by habitat loss from agricultural expansion and urban development.

Illegal collection from wild populations has been documented, particularly for unusual variegated forms. The majority of plants in cultivation are nursery-propagated from established stock.

Gasteria bicolor has been used extensively in hybridizing programs. It crosses readily with other Gasteria species and occasionally with related genera such as Aloe and Haworthia.

The species is not known to be invasive anywhere in its cultivated range. Its slow growth and specific habitat requirements prevent naturalization outside of southern Africa.

Gasteria bicolor contains anthraquinone compounds similar to those found in Aloe species. These compounds may provide herbivore deterrence but have no significant commercial or medicinal application.

The leaves store water in thick, gelatinous parenchyma tissue. This adaptation allows the plant to survive months without rainfall in its native habitat.

The genus name Gasteria derives from the Greek "gaster" meaning stomach, referencing the swollen base of the flowers.
