## Overview

*Ixia viridiflora* Lam., commonly called the green ixia or groenkalossie, is a cormous perennial in the family Iridaceae. It is native to the Western Cape of South Africa and is notable for its unusual turquoise-green flowers.

The species produces star-shaped blooms 3–4 cm in diameter that are among the few truly green flowers in the Iridaceae family. The central eye of each flower is dark purple-black, creating a striking contrast.

It is one of approximately 50 species in the genus *Ixia*, many of which are prized as ornamental spring bulbs. The green ixia is considered Endangered in the wild due to extensive habitat loss.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Order: Asparagales
- Family: Iridaceae
- Subfamily: Ixioideae
- Tribe: Ixieae
- Genus: *Ixia*
- Species: *I. viridiflora*

The species was described by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck in 1789. The specific epithet *viridiflora* refers to the green flower color, derived from Latin *viridis* (green) and *flos* (flower).

Plants grow 25–50 cm tall from ovoid corms 12–18 mm in diameter. Corms have a fibrous, brown tunic and produce 4–6 narrow, grass-like leaves.

The foliage is dark green, linear, and reaches 15–25 cm in length. Leaves emerge with the autumn rains and persist through the growing season.

Flowers are borne in spiraling, lax spikes of 6–12 blooms. Each flower is stellate with six tepals displaying the distinctive turquoise to sea-green coloration.

The dark central eye is surrounded by a narrow ring of white or pale yellow. This color pattern acts as a pollinator landing platform for hopliine monkey beetles.

Stamens are symmetrically arranged with erect, parallel filaments. The anthers are yellow and produce abundant pollen that is visible against the green tepal background.

Capsules are obovoid, 5–8 mm long, containing small angular seeds. Seeds lack elaiosomes and are dispersed by wind when capsules dehisce.

The chromosome number is 2n = 20, which is the base number for the genus. No polyploid races have been reported in *I. viridiflora*.

The species is most closely related to *I. maculata* and *I. scillaris*, which share similar corm and flower morphology. It is distinguished by the unique turquoise-green tepal coloration not found in any other *Ixia* species.

The green flower color is produced by a combination of flavonoid pigments rather than chlorophyll. This pigment combination is rare among flowering plants and contributes to the species' horticultural appeal.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Ixia viridiflora* is endemic to the Western Cape Province, restricted to a narrow band between Tulbagh and Malmesbury. Its total range covers less than 500 km².

The species grows in seasonally moist, clay-rich renosterveld soils at elevations of 50–300 m. It prefers sites that receive winter rainfall of 400–600 mm annually.

Like other Cape geophytes, it is winter-growing and summer-dormant. Corms remain dormant underground from November through March, shielded from the dry summer heat.

Less than 10% of the original renosterveld vegetation in its range remains. Agricultural expansion, urban development, and invasive alien plants have eliminated most historical populations.

The species is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List. Several subpopulations have been extirpated, and remaining populations are small and fragmented.

## Conservation

*Ixia viridiflora* faces severe conservation challenges due to its restricted distribution and habitat specialization. The species is classified as Endangered B2ab(iii) on the IUCN Red List.

Primary threats include ploughing of renosterveld for cereal crops, overgrazing by livestock, and competition from invasive grasses. Urban expansion around Malmesbury has eliminated several known sites.

Ex situ conservation is maintained through cultivated collections in botanical gardens. Kirstenbosch National Botanical Garden holds living accessions propagated from wild-collected seed.

Seed banking at the Millennium Seed Bank (Kew) provides an additional safeguard. However, in situ protection of remaining habitat fragments is the most critical conservation priority.

Several populations occur on privately owned land where voluntary stewardship agreements are the primary protection mechanism. Formal nature reserves protect only a minority of known sites.

Post-fire surveys have documented enhanced flowering in *I. viridiflora* following autumn burns. Smoke-derived compounds (butenolides) appear to stimulate corm sprouting and flower production.

The species has been featured in several conservation awareness campaigns highlighting the plight of Cape renosterveld endemics. Its unusual flower color makes it an effective flagship species for renosterveld conservation.

## Cultivation

The green ixia is cultivated as an ornamental bulb in USDA zones 8–10 and in Mediterranean-climate gardens worldwide. Its unusual flower color makes it a collector's plant among bulb enthusiasts.

Corms should be planted in autumn at 5–7 cm depth in well-drained, sandy loam. Full sun produces the best flowering, though plants tolerate light afternoon shade.

Water moderately during the growing season from autumn through spring. Withhold water completely once foliage yellows to prevent corm rot during summer dormancy.

Propagation is by seed or corm offsets. Seed requires no special pretreatment and germinates in 3–4 weeks at 15–18°C.

Plants flower in mid-spring (September–October in the southern hemisphere). The green flowers are long-lasting on the plant and make interesting cut stems for floral arrangements.

The species is somewhat susceptible to botrytis in humid conditions. Good air circulation and avoidance of overhead watering reduce disease risk.

Pests are minimal but thrips may feed on flower buds in warm weather. Insecticidal soap applied at the first sign of damage provides effective control.

The species hybridizes readily with other *Ixia* species in cultivation. Several cultivars with enhanced vigor and flower size have been selected by specialty bulb nurseries.

The green ixia has no known traditional medicinal or food uses. Its value is entirely ornamental, where its unique color makes it sought after by collectors and specialty cut-flower growers.

The species was introduced to European horticulture in the late 18th century. It appeared in the Curtis's Botanical Magazine illustration in 1792, one of the first Cape bulbs to be illustrated for a European audience.
