## Overview

**Scleroderma areolatum**, commonly known as the **earthball** or **starry earthball**, is a puffball-like basidiomycete fungus in the family Sclerodermataceae. Unlike true puffballs (Lycoperdales), Scleroderma species are ectomycorrhizal associates of trees rather than saprotrophs.

The species epithet *areolatum* refers to the areolate (polygonal-cracked) pattern that develops on the spore case surface at maturity. It is one of the smaller members of the genus Scleroderma and is found widely across temperate and subtropical regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Scleroderma areolatum was first described by Christian Hendrik Persoon in 1801. It belongs to the order Boletales, making it a distant relative of boletes despite its puffball-like appearance.

The fruiting body is 1–4 cm in diameter, roughly spherical to somewhat flattened. The outer surface (peridium) is initially whitish to pale brown, developing a distinctive pattern of small polygonal cracks that reveal darker brown tissue beneath as the spore case matures.

At full maturity, the entire upper surface may become deeply cracked, giving a reticulate or mosaic-like appearance. This areolate pattern is the most reliable field character distinguishing S. areolatum from the smoother-surfaced S. bovista.

The gleba (interior spore mass) is initially firm and white in young specimens, becoming dark purple-black to olive-brown and powdery at maturity. Spores are spherical, 8–12 µm in diameter, covered in short spines or warts, and are released when the peridium ruptures irregularly.

A rudimentary stem (sterile base) may be present, but it is short and inconspicuous compared to species like Scleroderma citrinum. The peridium is thin and papery, often rupturing irregularly rather than forming a defined apical pore.

## Distribution and Habitat

S. areolatum has a broad distribution across Europe, [[arbuscule-isolation-and-nutrient-exchange-mechanisms]] in a mutualistic symbiosis.

The fungus provides its host with enhanced access to soil minerals, particularly phosphorus and nitrogen, while receiving photosynthetically fixed carbon in return. This relationship is especially important in nutrient-poor or disturbed soils.

Like other Scleroderma species, S. areolatum plays a role in soil structure maintenance through its extensive [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]], though S. areolatum has been less studied than its larger congeners. The genus is known to contain sclerocitrin pigments, which contribute to the brown coloration of the peridium.

Some Scleroderma species have shown antimicrobial activity in vitro against Gram-positive bacteria and various [[cultivator-sterile-technique-and-agar-culture]] due to the frequent contamination of gleba by bacteria and other fungi. Germination rates from single-spore isolates are generally low, consistent with the outcrossing mating systems typical of many ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes.

## Similar Species and Identification Notes

Beginning foragers sometimes confuse Scleroderma areolatum with edible puffballs of the genera Lycoperdon and Bovista. The critical distinction is the gleba color: Scleroderma species have a dark purple to olive-brown gleba visible even in very young specimens, while edible puffballs remain pure white until spore maturity.

The areolate cracking pattern on the peridium of S. areolatum is a useful diagnostic feature, though this character develops primarily at maturity. Immature specimens may require sectioning to distinguish from true puffballs.

Unlike [[calvatia-gigantea]], which can reach impressive sizes, S. areolatum rarely exceeds 4 cm in diameter. Its small stature combined with its dark gleba makes it one of the easier Scleroderma species to recognize with practice.
