# Panaeolus venenosus

**Panaeolus venenosus** is a psychoactive mushroom species in the genus Panaeolus, reported from Australia. The species epithet "venenosus" (meaning poisonous or venomous) reflects its bioactive properties. It has historical significance in the study of Australian [[cattle-dispersal-psychoactive-fungi-australia]], having been used in early clinical investigations of [[emcdda-psilocybin-non-addictive-profile-tolerance-dynamics]] effects.

## Taxonomy and Classification

*Panaeolus venenosus* belongs to the family Bolbitiaceae, order Agaricales. The genus Panaeolus is characterized by its predominantly dung-inhabiting lifestyle, black spore print, and mottled gill appearance caused by the asynchronous maturation of spores (some basidia mature and release spores while others remain immature, creating a spotted pattern).

The species has been referenced in the scientific literature on Australian psychoactive fungi, notably in the work of Dr. Sam Stein, who conducted clinical studies using mushroom extracts obtained from dried specimens of *Panaeolus venenosus* in the late 1950s. These studies were among the earliest investigations of the subjective effects of Australian psychoactive mushrooms.

## Morphology

*Panaeolus venenosus* shares the general morphological features of the Panaeolus genus. The pileus (cap) is small to medium, typically 15-40 mm in diameter, conic to broadly convex or bell-shaped (campanulate) when young, expanding to hemispheric with age. The cap surface is smooth and often slightly viscid when moist, with a color ranging from grayish-brown to dark brown.

The lamellae (gills) are adnate to adnexed, close, and display the characteristic mottled or spotted pattern of the genus due to uneven spore maturation. The gills are initially pale gray, becoming black as spores mature. The stipe is slender, cylindrical, equal, and typically 40-80 mm long and 2-4 mm thick. It is generally whitish to pale gray and may exhibit slight bluing at the base when handled.

Spores are ellipsoid, smooth, and black in deposit, approximately 12-16 x 7-9 micrometers.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Panaeolus venenosus* has been reported from Australia, where it grows on herbivore dung, particularly in pastures and grasslands. Like other Panaeolus species, it is a coprophilous (dung-loving) fungus that fruits directly on or near cow, horse, and other livestock manure.

The species favors warm, humid conditions and is most commonly found during the warmer months in tropical and subtropical regions of Australia, particularly in Queensland and northern New South Wales. It may also occur in temperate regions during periods of warm, wet weather.

## Chemistry and Historical Research

*Panaeolus venenosus* contains [[psilocin]], though the specific alkaloid profile and concentrations have not been as thoroughly documented as those of more widely studied species. It is generally considered to be of low to moderate potency.

*Panaeolus venenosus* holds particular historical interest because of its use by Dr. Sam Stein in [[hofmann-lsd-clinical-research-early-studies]] conducted in Australia in 1958-1959. Stein prepared extracts from dried specimens and administered them to volunteer subjects in controlled settings, documenting the subjective effects. His colleagues found that the subjective effects produced by the ingestion of Panaeolus species were "more tranquil and less" intense compared to those produced by Psilocybe species, suggesting a qualitatively different experiential profile.

This distinction between the effects of Panaeolus and Psilocybe species was noted in multiple early studies and remains an interesting though under-explored aspect of comparative [[mckenna-food-gods-tryptamine-pharmacology-indoles]]. One possible explanation for the reported qualitative differences is that Panaeolus species may contain a different ratio of [[psilocin]] compared to Psilocybe species, or may contain additional minor alkaloids that modulate the overall psychoactive profile. Another possibility is that the presence of other naturally occurring compounds in Panaeolus species — such as urea derivatives, phenethylamine-related compounds, or other indole alkaloids not found in Psilocybe species — could contribute to the qualitative differences in subjective experience. This hypothesis has not been systematically tested with modern analytical or pharmacological methods.

## Microscopic Features

[[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] is essential for reliable identification of *Panaeolus venenosus*. Basidia are four-spored, clavate, measuring 20–28 × 6–8 µm. Cheilocystidia are ventricose to cylindrical, sometimes with a slightly capitate apex, 18–30 × 6–10 µm. Pleurocystidia are absent. The pileipellis consists of a thin layer of hyaline, cylindrical hyphae 3–5 µm wide. The spores are thick-walled, smooth, and possess a germ pore — a feature that distinguishes Panaeolus from the closely related genus Panaeolina, whose spores lack a germ pore. Spore size and shape, combined with cheilocystidia morphology, are the primary microscopic characters used to separate *P. venenosus* from other Panaeolus species.
