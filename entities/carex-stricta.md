## Overview

Carex stricta, commonly known as tussock sedge, is a perennial sedge native to wetlands, marshes, and moist meadows across much of eastern and central North America.

It forms distinctive dense tussocks or hummocks that can reach 60 to 90 cm in height, creating important microtopographic variation in wetland habitats.

The species is a keystone component of many sedge meadow and calcareous fen communities throughout the northeastern and midwestern United States.

It is widely used in [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]] projects for [[mollison-designers-cloudburst-and-stormwater-management]], riparian buffer stabilization, and [[comparison-constructed-wetlands-vs-bioswales]].

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Carex stricta Lamarck belongs to the family Cyperaceae, subfamily Cyperoideae, tribe Cariceae.

The species was described by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck in 1791, with the epithet "stricta" referring to the rigid, upright culms.

Carex stricta is part of the large and taxonomically complex section Acrocystis, which contains approximately 60 species worldwide.

Plants are cespitose, forming dense tussocks consisting of accumulated leaf bases and root material from successive [[jeavons-climate-adaptation-growing-seasons]].

Mature tussocks can be 30 to 60 cm tall and 30 to 50 cm in diameter, with living green foliage emerging from the upper portion.

Culms are sharply triangular in cross-section, 40 to 90 cm tall, stout, and strictly erect, as the species name suggests.

Leaf blades are 2 to 5 mm wide, M-shaped in cross-section due to the prominent midrib, and shorter than the culms.

The inflorescence consists of 3 to 7 spikelets arranged in a loose, nodding cluster at the culm apex.

Terminal spikelet is staminate or androgynous, 15 to 30 mm long, while lateral spikelets are pistillate and shorter.

The perigynium is 3 to 5 mm long, broadly ovate, greenish-brown, prominently nerved, and tapers to a short bidentate beak.

Root systems are extensive and fibrous, anchoring plants firmly within the tussock structure.

## Distribution and Habitat

Carex stricta ranges from Nova Scotia and Quebec west to Minnesota and Iowa, and south to Virginia, Kentucky, and Missouri.

It is most abundant in the glaciated regions of the northeastern and midwestern United States and the Great Lakes basin.

The species occurs in sedge meadows, fens, marshes, wet prairies, riparian zones, and the margins of ponds and streams.

It prefers saturated or seasonally flooded soils, often growing in standing water up to 15 cm deep during spring.

Optimal habitats include calcareous fens with circumneutral to slightly alkaline pH and groundwater-fed hydrology.

Carex stricta tolerates moderate shade but is most productive in full-sun conditions with minimal competition from woody vegetation.

It is found from sea level to approximately 800 meters elevation in the Appalachian region.

The species is characteristic of wetland communities with high water tables and seasonal water-level fluctuations.

## Ecology

Carex stricta is a dominant or codominant species in sedge meadow and prairie fen communities across its range.

Its tussock growth form creates heterogeneous microtopography that supports a diverse assemblage of plant and animal species.

The elevated tussocks provide nesting sites for wetland birds including sedge wrens, marsh wrens, and swamp sparrows.

Amphibians and aquatic invertebrates use the spaces between tussocks for foraging, refuge, and overwintering habitat.

The dense root mat stabilizes streambanks and shorelines, reducing erosion and trapping suspended sediments.

Carex stricta is an important component of stormwater [[mollison-designers-wastewater-treatment-wetlands]], where its root zone filters nutrients and pollutants.

It greens up early in spring, providing forage for migrating waterfowl and newly emerged insects.

The species is moderately fire-tolerant, resprouting from tussock bases after prescribed burns in sedge meadow communities.

Its litter production contributes to peat accumulation in fen and bog ecosystems over long time scales.
