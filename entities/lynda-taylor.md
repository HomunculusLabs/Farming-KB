## Description

Lynda Taylor is a cofounder of [[designing-sustainable-communities]]/ZERI-New Mexico
(SCZ-NM) and initiator of the New Mexico native fungal culture bank
project. Her work focuses on collecting, maintaining, and testing native
fungi for their ability to grow on locally available woody substrates as
part of forest restoration efforts. She is featured in [[ron-spinosa]]'s
article on [[fungi-and-sustainability]] published in Fungi Magazine.

## Key Facts

- Cofounded SCZ-NM (www.scizerinm.org), a creative enterprise working
  under the auspices of the U.S. Forest Service Collaborative Forest
  Restoration Program in New Mexico.
- Initiated the establishment of a long-term New Mexico native fungal
  culture bank.
- The culture bank collects and maintains native fungi on agar plates
  for long-term study and preservation.
- Various fungi in the bank are tested for growth on small-diameter tree
  species including ponderosa pine, piñon, and juniper.
- Also tested on invasive species like salt cedar and Russian olive being
  thinned during forest restoration.
- Her work connects forest management with practical [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]]

## The Native Fungal Culture Bank

The native fungal culture bank is Taylor's most significant contribution
to applied mycology. Unlike commercial spawn banks that focus on a handful
of cultivated species, Taylor's initiative systematically collects wild
fungi native to New Mexico's unique ecosystems. Each isolate is cataloged,
maintained on agar media, and tested for its ability to decompose specific
woody substrates. This creates a living library of fungal biodiversity
tied to specific ecological niches. The culture bank preserves genetic
resources that might otherwise be lost to habitat degradation and climate
change, while simultaneously building practical knowledge for forest-based
mushroom cultivation.

## Forest Restoration and Mycoforestry

New Mexico's forests face a dual crisis: overgrowth of small-diameter
trees creating severe wildfire hazard, and invasion by non-native species
like salt cedar (Tamarix) and Russian olive ([[elaeagnus-angustifolia]]).
Thinning operations produce enormous volumes of woody biomass with limited
economic value. Taylor's insight was to test whether native fungi could
convert this low-value thinned material into high-value mushroom crops.
By matching specific fungal isolates to specific tree species, she created
a framework for region-specific mycoforestry that supports both ecological
restoration and economic development in rural communities.

## Substrate Testing Protocols

The SCZ-NM substrate testing program follows a rigorous methodology.
Fungal isolates from the culture bank are inoculated onto prepared
substrates made from chipped or milled woody material from target tree
species. Growth rates, mycelial density, and fruiting potential are
recorded systematically. Key substrate species tested include ponderosa
pine (Pinus ponderosa), piñon pine (Pinus edulis), and one-seed juniper
(Juniperus monosperma). Invasive species like salt cedar and Russian olive
are also evaluated, as their removal generates significant biomass. This
practical research directly supports [[mushroom-cultivation-for-sustainable-agriculture]]
by identifying viable local substrate and species combinations.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Taylor's fungal culture bank represents an important conservation and
applied mycology initiative. By systematically collecting and
characterizing native fungi — and testing their ability to fruit on locally
available substrates — she is building a resource for sustainable forest
management. Matching native fungi to native tree species follows Paul
Stamets's [[mycoforestry-principles-stamets]] and ensures that mushroom cultivation
efforts use locally adapted organisms. Her approach to cultivating
[[rhizomorph-mycelium]] and other growth forms helps identify which
species are most productive on specific wood types.

## Significance

The native fungal culture bank concept could serve as a model for other
regions seeking to develop locally adapted mushroom cultivation industries
while simultaneously addressing forest fire hazard reduction. Taylor's
work demonstrates that conservation and economic development need not be
in conflict when fungi are integrated into land [[livestock-grassland-management-strategies]].
