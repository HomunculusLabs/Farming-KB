# Psilocybe verrucosa

**Psilocybe verrucosa** is a psilocybin mushroom in the family Hymenogastraceae, described from subtropical forests of Mexico. The species epithet *verrucosa* (warty) refers to the distinctive roughened or warty ornamentation of the pileus surface, a character that helps distinguish it from other members of Psilocybe section Mexicanae with which it shares its habitat and general morphology.

## Taxonomy

psilocybe verrucosa was described by mycologists working with the Mexican psilocybin flora, with the species placed in Psilocybe section Mexicanae based on its microscopic features, habitat preferences, and molecular phylogenetic placement. The section is characterized by small to medium-sized mushrooms that fruit on dung or enriched soils in tropical and subtropical regions. Within the section, psilocybe verrucosa is distinguished by the unusual verrucose (warty) texture of the cap surface, a trait uncommon among otherwise smooth-capped Mexican Psilocybe species. Molecular phylogenetic analyses have confirmed its placement as a distinct lineage within section Mexicanae, most closely related to [[psilocybe-mexicana]] and psilocybe subtropicalis.

## Morphology

The pileus is 8–20 mm in diameter, convex when young, becoming broadly convex to plano-convex with age, occasionally with a low umbo. The surface is the diagnostic feature: it is distinctly verrucose (covered with small warts or raised granules), especially at the disc and center, becoming smoother toward the margin. When moist, the cap is brown to dark brown, often with a reddish tinge; it fades to a pale tan or straw color when dry. The surface is slightly viscid when wet. The flesh is thin and whitish, bruising slowly to a pale blue or blue-green.

The lamellae are adnexed to adnate, close, and narrow. They are grayish when young, becoming dark purplish brown as spores mature. The stipe is 25–55 mm long and 1.5–2.5 mm thick, cylindrical, equal, and hollow. The surface is whitish to pale brown, with fine appressed fibrils and occasionally minute scales near the base. A transient annular zone may be present but is often absent in mature specimens. The stipe bruises blue when handled, with the reaction typically more pronounced at the base.

## Microscopic Features

Basidiospores are ellipsoid to slightly ovoid, 6.5–8.5 × 4–5.5 μm, with a wall thickness of approximately 0.5 μm and a distinct apical germ pore 1–1.5 μm in diameter. The spore print is dark purplish brown. Basidia are two- or four-spored, clavate, 12–18 × 4–5 μm. Cheilocystidia are abundant on the gill edges, forming a sterile band, predominantly lageniform with narrow necks, sometimes subcapitate, 14–24 × 4–7 μm. Pleurocystidia are absent. The pileipellis is an ixocutis (gelatinized cutis) of narrow (2–4 μm), hyaline hyphae, with the verrucose surface texture arising from clusters of erect hyphal elements protruding through the gelatinous layer. Clamp connections are absent at the basidial bases.

## Distribution and Habitat

psilocybe verrucosa is known from several Mexican states, including Veracruz, Oaxaca, Puebla, and Chiapas, occurring at elevations of 500–1,800 meters in subtropical and tropical forests. It fruits during the rainy season from June through October, typically appearing singly or in small groups on well-decomposed herbivore dung (especially bovine), on dung-enriched soils in pastures, and along forest trails in areas where livestock graze. It has also been collected from coffee plantations and disturbed areas within otherwise intact tropical forest. The species appears to have a preference for recently deposited or partially decomposed dung in open, sun-exposed situations, as opposed to the deeper shade preferences of some other section Mexicanae members. The verrucose cap texture may be an adaptation to its preferred microhabitat, possibly providing protection against desiccation in more exposed situations compared to smooth-capped relatives that fruit in shadier, more humid environments.

## Chemistry

Chemical analyses have confirmed the presence of [[psilocybin-compound]] and [[psilocin]] in psilocybe verrucosa. The total tryptamine content places it in the low-to-moderate potency range, comparable to [[psilocybe-mexicana]]. Baeocystin and norbaeocystin have been detected in minor amounts. The moderate bluing reaction in fresh specimens is consistent with the measured alkaloid concentrations, suggesting significant but not exceptional levels of [[psilocin]] and its oxidative products.

## Ethnomycology

While the ethnomycological history of psilocybe verrucosa specifically has not been documented, the species occurs in regions of southern Mexico with well-established traditions of ceremonial mushroom use. It is possible that the species was collected alongside more commonly used species such as [[psilocybe-caerulescens-entity]] and [[psilocybe-mexicana]] by indigenous communities in Oaxaca and Veracruz. The distinctive warty cap surface might have made it recognizable to experienced collectors, though it is small and could easily be overlooked among more abundant species in mixed fruitings.

## Cultivation

There are no documented cultivation protocols specifically developed for psilocybe verrucosa, and the species has not been cultivated commercially. As a member of section Mexicanae, it would likely respond to cultivation methods similar to those used for [[psilocybe-mexicana]], including pasteurized straw, composted dung, or enriched grain substrates. The species may also have sclerotia-producing potential, as this trait is shared by many section Mexicanae members. However, the relative rarity of the species in nature, combined with the availability of more easily cultivated and more potent alternatives, has limited interest in developing dedicated cultivation techniques for this taxon.

## Molecular Phylogeny

Molecular phylogenetic analyses have confirmed the placement of psilocybe verrucosa within Psilocybe section Mexicanae, as a distinct lineage closely related to [[psilocybe-mexicana]] and psilocybe subtropicalis. The genetic divergence between psilocybe verrucosa and its nearest relatives is sufficient to support its recognition as a distinct species, and the distinctive verrucose cap texture — which arises from a unique pileipellis structure — provides a morphological character consistent with the molecular data. The verrucose trait appears to be an autapomorphy (unique derived character) of this species within section Mexicanae, as no other members of the section display this cap surface ornamentation.

## Conservation Status

psilocybe verrucosa has not been formally assessed for conservation status by the IUCN or other conservation bodies. However, its dependence on subtropical forest habitats that are under increasing pressure from deforestation, agricultural expansion, and urbanization in southern Mexico suggests that it may be vulnerable to habitat loss. Coffee plantations, which represent one of the species' documented habitats, are themselves threatened by land-use conversion and climate change, potentially affecting the microhabitats where psilocybe verrucosa occurs. The species' apparent preference for partially sun-exposed, dung-enriched microhabitats within forested areas may make it somewhat adaptable to anthropogenically modified landscapes, provided that basic moisture and substrate requirements are met.

## Similar Species

[[psilocybe-mexicana]] is the most commonly encountered lookalike in the same habitats but has a smooth cap surface rather than verrucose. psilocybe subtropicalis shares the subtropical distribution but has larger spores and a non-warty pileus. deconica coprophila and other coprophilous Deconica species share the dung habitat but lack bluing reactions and have smooth spores without germ pores. The verrucose cap texture is the most reliable field character for distinguishing psilocybe verrucosa from all similar species.

## Identification Notes

The distinctive verrucose (warty) cap surface is the most reliable field character for identifying psilocybe verrucosa, as no other member of section Mexicanae displays this ornamentation. Additional field characters include:
- Cap with small, raised granules or warts, especially at the center, smoother toward the margin
- Hygrophanous cap: brown to dark brown when moist, fading to pale tan when dry
- Slow, pale blue to blue-green bruising on cap flesh and stipe
- Occurrence on or near herbivore dung in subtropical forests at 500–1,800 meters
- Dark purplish brown spore print

The warty texture is most pronounced in fresh, moist specimens and may become less conspicuous as the cap dries. In older specimens, the warts can wear down or become obscured by debris, making identification more challenging. The slow and pale bluing reaction means that patience is required when assessing this character — the blue coloration may take several minutes to develop and is never as intense as in strongly bluing species like [[psilocybe-cyanescens]]. The combination of the verrucose cap and the dung habitat is diagnostic; no other psilocybin mushroom in the region shares both characters.
## Seasonality

Psilocybe verrucosa fruits during the rainy season from June through October in its Mexican range. Peak fruiting typically occurs during the wettest months of July through September, when consistent precipitation maintains the moisture levels necessary for fruiting body development. The species may produce multiple fruiting flushes within a single rainy season, particularly in years with extended or heavy precipitation. Its preference for recently deposited dung means that it often appears on fresh substrate that has been exposed to rain for at least several days, suggesting that some degree of dung decomposition is required before the substrate becomes suitable for colonization.
## Cultivation Details

While no formal cultivation protocols exist for psilocybe verrucosa, its membership in section Mexicanae provides guidance for potential cultivation approaches. Based on methods developed for [[psilocybe-mexicana]]:
- **Substrate**: Pasteurized horse or cow manure, composted dung/straw mixtures, or enriched grain spawn
- **Temperature**: Fruiting likely occurs at 20–28°C, consistent with its subtropical habitat
- **Humidity**: High relative humidity (90–95%) required for primordia formation and fruiting body development
- **Light**: Ambient indirect light sufficient for normal fruiting
- **Sclerotia**: The species may produce sclerotia on grain substrates after extended colonization (8–12 weeks), a trait shared by many section Mexicanae members

The species' preference for dung substrates in nature suggests that composted or pasteurized manure would be the most effective fruiting substrate, though sterile cultivation on supplemented grain followed by casing with dung-enriched soil may also be productive.
