# Psilocybe mulgrewii

Psilocybe mulgrewii is a rare bluing Psilocybe species in section Cyanescens, first described from the Pacific Northwest of North America. It is closely related to [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-azurescens-species-guide]] and [[psilocybe-cyanofibrillosa]], sharing their preference for coastal dune grass habitats and potent [[psilocybin-compound]] content. Despite its relative obscurity, the species represents an important member of the cyanescens species complex, contributing to our understanding of speciation and biogeography in psilocybian fungi of the Pacific Northwest. Very few collections exist in herbaria, and the species remains poorly known even among experienced field mycologists.

## History of Study

Psilocybe mulgrewii was described by mycologist [[jochen-gartz]] and collaborators, named in honor of a contributor to the field of psilocybian mycology. It was segregated from the broader [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-cyanescens-species-guide]] species complex based on differences in microscopic features, particularly cheilocystidia morphology and spore dimensions. The original type collection was made from coastal dune habitat in the Pacific Northwest, and the holotype was deposited in a major herbarium for reference.

The cyanescens complex has been a source of considerable taxonomic confusion. For many years, all robust, bluing Psilocybe species of the Pacific Northwest were broadly identified as P. cyanescens. gaston-guzman|Gaston Guzmán and other monographers gradually recognized the distinctiveness of several taxa within this complex, including [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-azurescens-species-guide]], [[psilocybe-cyanofibrillosa]], [[psilocybe-stuntzii-profile]], and P. mulgrewii. Gartz's work in the 1990s was instrumental in clarifying the taxonomy of the group, drawing on both morphological analysis and preliminary molecular sequencing to support the recognition of P. mulgrewii as a distinct species. Despite these advances, the species has received far less attention than its more famous relatives, and many aspects of its biology remain to be investigated.

## Taxonomy and Naming

The taxonomic placement within section Cyanescens is well supported by both morphological and preliminary molecular data. Section Cyanescens is characterized by species with thick-walled spores, a well-developed ixocutis pileipellis, and potent bluing reactions. P. mulgrewii fits neatly within this morphological concept and shares the clade's genetic signature. Each species within the complex occupies a slightly different ecological niche and can be distinguished by a combination of macroscopic and microscopic characters, though the boundaries between some taxa remain debated.

## Description

### Cap (Pileus)
The pileus ranges from 15 to 40 mm in diameter, convex to broadly convex, expanding to nearly plane with age. The surface is hygrophanous, deeply striate at the margin when moist, and smooth to slightly wrinkled. Coloration is caramel brown to dark reddish brown when fresh, fading to ochraceous or straw yellow as it dries. The cap surface bruises distinctly blue to blue-black where handled or damaged, a reliable field indicator of [[psilocybin-compound]] and [[psilocin]] content. In young specimens, the cap may have a slight umbo, though this is less pronounced than in [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-azurescens-species-guide]]. The cap cuticle (pellicle) is gelatinous and separable, a trait shared with other section Cyanescens members.

### Gills (Lamellae)
Gills are adnate to adnexed, moderately crowded, with lamellulae of varying lengths. Color is initially grayish but matures to dark purplish brown with spore maturity. Gill edges are typically white and fringed, a character shared with other members of section Cyanescens. The attachment to the stipe can show a slight tooth-like decurrent tooth, and the gill spacing is moderately close, allowing easy viewing of the spore color transition.

In cross-section, the gill trama is regular to subregular, with hyphae running roughly parallel. This microscopic feature can aid in distinguishing P. mulgrewii from species with irregular gill trama. The fringed gill edges (sterile margin) are composed of cheilocystidia that project beyond the basidiole layer, giving the characteristic whitish edge visible to the naked eye.

### Stipe (Stem)
The stipe measures 40 to 90 mm in length and 2 to 4 mm thick, cylindrical and equal or slightly enlarged at the base. It is hollow, fibrous, and covered in fine white fibrils that give it a silky appearance. The veil is partial, leaving a fragile annular zone that often disappears with age or is only visible as a slight color change on the stipe surface. The stipe bruises vividly blue, especially near the base, and this bruising can develop within seconds of handling. The base may be slightly radicating, penetrating into the sandy substrate. The overall stature is more delicate than [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-azurescens-species-guide]], which is a useful field character.

The stipe context is fibrous and tough, becoming stringy when torn. Blue staining typically begins at the base and progresses upward, often appearing most intensely where the stipe has been handled or compressed. In some specimens, the entire stipe may turn dark blue-black within minutes of collection, making this one of the most striking field characters of the species.

### Spores and Microscopic Features
Basidiospores are (11)12 to 15(16) × 7 to 9 μm, elliptical to somewhat amygdaliform in face view, thick-walled, with a distinct germ pore. The thick spore walls are characteristic of section Cyanescens and may serve as an adaptation to the harsh coastal environment, potentially aiding in spore survival during periods of desiccation or UV exposure. Spore print is dark purplish brown to nearly black. Cheilocystidia are ventricose to lageniform with narrow necks, sometimes with a subcapitate apex, measuring 20 to 35 × 5 to 10 μm. Pleurocystidia are similar but less frequent. Basidia are four-spored, clavate. The pileipellis is an ixocutis of narrow, gelatinized hyphae.

The germ pore is prominent and truncate, visible under oil immersion at 1000× magnification. Spore wall thickness has been measured at approximately 1.0–1.5 μm, which is at the upper end of the range for the genus. This robust spore morphology likely enhances survival in the exposed coastal dune environment where spores may be subjected to UV radiation, salt spray, and periodic desiccation.

## Field Identification Tips

Identifying Psilocybe mulgrewii in the field requires careful attention to several key features that separate it from its close relatives in the cyanescens complex:

- **Habitat is the first clue:** P. mulgrewii is strongly associated with coastal dune grasslands and sandy soils near the shore. Finding it in urban woodchip beds almost certainly indicates a different species.
- **Size and stature:** P. mulgrewii is notably smaller and more delicate than [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-azurescens-species-guide]], with a cap rarely exceeding 40 mm. Specimens larger than 50 mm across are unlikely to be this species.
- **Bruising reaction:** Like all section Cyanescens members, P. mulgrewii bruises vividly blue. However, the bruising in this species tends to be somewhat less intense than the dramatic indigo-black staining characteristic of P. azurescens.
- **Cap texture:** The cap surface is smooth and viscid when wet, but lacks the strongly fibrillose-scaly texture of [[psilocybe-cyanofibrillosa]]. The striate margin when moist is a good field mark.
- **Annulus:** The partial veil leaves only a faint, evanescent annular zone, much less prominent than the more persistent ring zone of [[psilocybe-stuntzii-profile]].
- **Fruiting season:** Late October through December, peaking after the first heavy autumn rains when temperatures have dropped below 10°C. Earlier autumn fruiting (September–October) is more likely P. azurescens.

A hand lens (10×–20×) and a spore print are essential for confirming identification in the field. Definitive determination requires microscopic examination of spores and cheilocystidia. When in doubt, collectors should retain whole specimens (including the base of the stipe with any attached substrate) for later microscopic study. Photographing specimens in situ, noting GPS coordinates, and recording the associated plant community are all valuable practices that contribute to documenting this rare species.

## Distribution and Habitat

Psilocybe mulgrewii is known from the coastal Pacific Northwest of North America, particularly from Oregon and Washington. It fruits in the late autumn through early winter, typically October through December. The species is found growing in sandy soils among dune grasses, particularly in association with beach grass (Ammophila spp.). It may also occur in disturbed coastal grasslands and along brushy paths near the shoreline.

This ecological preference for coastal dune grasslands places it in the same niche as [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-azurescens-species-guide]] and [[psilocybe-cyanofibrillosa]], and these species can sometimes be found fruiting in proximity to one another. The cool, humid maritime climate of the Pacific Northwest provides ideal conditions for fruiting, with the persistent coastal fog maintaining high humidity levels that support prolonged fruiting periods. The species appears to prefer stabilized dune areas with established grass cover rather than open, shifting sands. It is often found in the sheltered lee of dune ridges where moisture retention is higher and wind exposure is reduced.

Substrate preference is likely lignicolous, with mycelium colonizing buried woody debris, decaying root material from dune grasses, and accumulated organic matter in the sandy soil profile. The mycelium may form extensive networks through sandy substrates, connected by rhizomorph-like structures analogous to those described in P. azurescens. Fruitings are often episodic, with large flushes appearing in years with favorable autumn rainfall and absent or scarce in drier years. The species has been documented from sea level to approximately 100 meters elevation, always within a few kilometers of the coastline.

No confirmed reports exist of P. mulgrewii occurring outside the Pacific Northwest, though suitable habitat exists in parts of northern California, British Columbia, and southeastern Alaska. The absence of records from these areas may reflect genuine range limitation or insufficient survey effort. The species has not been reported from introduced populations elsewhere in the world, unlike P. cyanescens and P. azurescens, which have naturalized in parts of Europe and New Zealand.

## Ecology and Conservation

Psilocybe mulgrewii is considered rare and has a very limited known distribution. Coastal dune ecosystems are among the most threatened habitats in the Pacific Northwest, subject to multiple converging pressures:

- **Habitat loss from development:** Coastal real estate development has destroyed or fragmented significant stretches of dune grassland, particularly in Oregon's Clatsop and Tillamook counties where Psilocybe species are most commonly found.
- **Invasive species:** European beach grass (*Ammophila arenaria*) has dramatically altered dune dynamics along the Pacific coast. While this species may serve as a substrate associate for P. mulgrewii, its aggressive colonization homogenizes dune structure and may outcompete native plant communities, indirectly affecting fungal diversity.
- **Recreational pressure:** Off-road vehicle traffic, foot traffic, and beach camping disturb dune soils and vegetation. Compaction of sandy substrates damages the mycelial networks that fungi depend on.
- **Climate change:** Rising sea levels, altered precipitation patterns, and increased storm intensity all threaten coastal dune ecosystems. Warmer, drier autumns could reduce the fruiting window for P. mulgrewii and other autumn-fruiting fungi.

The species has not been formally evaluated by conservation authorities such as the IUCN or state heritage programs, but its restricted range, low number of documented collections, and habitat specificity suggest it may warrant formal assessment. The paucity of records makes it difficult to determine whether the species is genuinely rare or simply undercollected and overlooked due to its similarity to more common cyanescens-complex taxa.

Several dune restoration projects along the Oregon and Washington coasts have targeted invasive beach grass removal. While these efforts are ecologically beneficial overall, they may inadvertently impact P. mulgrewii populations if the fungal communities associated with established beach grass are disturbed before native dune vegetation can reestablish. Conservation of fungal diversity in these systems remains an underappreciated aspect of coastal dune management. Citizen science efforts and targeted foray surveys by mycological societies could significantly improve understanding of this species' true distribution and abundance.

## Chemistry and Active Compounds

Psilocybe mulgrewii is a [[psilocybin-compound]]-containing species. Analytical studies by Jochen-Gartz|Gartz reported the following approximate alkaloid concentrations in dried basidiocarps:

- **Psilocybin:** 0.3 to 0.7% dry weight
- **Psilocin:** 0.1 to 0.3% dry weight
- **Baeocystin:** 0.01 to 0.05% dry weight (trace amounts)

These values place P. mulgrewii in the moderate range of potency among section Cyanescens members, below [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-azurescens-species-guide]] (up to 1.8% psilocybin) but comparable to [[psilocybe-cyanofibrillosa]]. Total tryptamine content ranges from approximately 0.4 to 1.0% dry weight, which is significant enough to produce pronounced psychoactive effects. For comparison, [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]] typically contains 0.5–1.0% total tryptamines.

The blue bruising reaction is a reliable field indicator of psilocybin/psilocin content, as the bluing is caused by oxidation of these compounds through enzymatic and non-enzymatic pathways. The bluing pigment has been identified as a quinone-derived polymer resulting from the oxidative dephosphorylation and subsequent oxidation of psilocybin and psilocin. The relative proportions of psilocybin to psilocin, and the presence of minor tryptamine alkaloids such as norbaeocystin, would benefit from further analytical study with modern HPLC-MS methods to fully characterize the species' chemical profile. Alkaloid content may vary significantly depending on substrate composition, age of the fruiting body at harvest, and environmental conditions.

## Similar Species

Several lookalike species occur in the same habitat, making careful identification essential:

- [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-azurescens-species-guide]] — Larger and more robust, with caps reaching 100 mm and a distinctly umbonate cap. Bruising is far more intense, producing deep indigo-black staining. Higher psilocybin content (up to 1.8%). Generally more abundant and the species most likely to be confused with P. mulgrewii due to shared habitat. Key distinction: overall size and intensity of bruising.
- [[psilocybe-cyanofibrillosa]] — Similar in size but with a conspicuously fibrillose to scaly cap surface, especially toward the margin. Different cheilocystidia morphology (more broadly ventricose). The fibrillose cap texture is the most reliable macroscopic distinguishing feature.
- [[stamets-psi-psilocybe-cyanescens-species-guide]] — Typically found in urban woodchip beds, mulched gardens, and landscaped areas rather than dune grasslands. Generally larger with a more pronounced undulating cap margin when mature and a more robust overall stature.
- [[psilocybe-stuntzii-profile]] — Grows in similar latitudes but favors mulched garden beds, lawns, and disturbed soils in suburban and urban settings. Has a more persistent annulus, smaller spores, and slightly different microscopic features. Less potent.
- **Galerina species** (especially *G. marginata*) — Deadly poisonous lookalikes that can grow in similar woody coastal habitats. Galerinas have a brown spore print (not purplish brown), lack blue bruising, and typically have a more persistent annulus. This is the most dangerous potential confusion and underscores the importance of making a spore print for every collection.

Microscopic examination is required for definitive identification among the cyanescens complex members. Key differentiating features include spore dimensions, cheilocystidia shape, and pileipellis structure. A systematic comparison of spore size ranges across the complex is as follows: P. mulgrewii (12–15 × 7–9 μm), P. azurescens (11–14 × 7–8.5 μm), P. cyanofibrillosa (9–12 × 6–8 μm), and P. cyanescens (9–12 × 6–8.5 μm). The larger spores of P. mulgrewii relative to P. cyanofibrillosa and P. cyanescens are a helpful microscopic discriminant.

## Cultivation

There are no well-documented cultivation attempts for Psilocybe mulgrewii specifically. Members of section Cyanescens are generally considered more difficult to cultivate than [[psilocybe-cubensis-potency-variation-by-flush]] due to their requirement for cooler temperatures and more specific substrate conditions. The species likely requires woody or lignicolous substrates similar to its close relatives, and the cool maritime climate conditions that trigger fruiting in nature would need to be replicated artificially.

Based on successful cultivation methods for related species, a plausible approach would involve inoculating pasteurized alder or Douglas-fir sawdust supplemented with wheat bran, colonizing at 18–22°C, and initiating fruiting with a cold shock to 8–12°C combined with high humidity (95%+) and ambient light. Outdoor cultivation in coastal climates, burying colonized woodchip or sawdust blocks in sandy dune grass areas, may be the most practical method, though this has not been formally documented for P. mulgrewii. The species' apparent rarity in nature may reflect specific mycorrhizal or endophytic associations with dune grasses that are difficult to replicate in cultivation.

Isolation of a clean culture from a wild specimen would require cloning from fresh tissue onto antibiotic-amended agar media, followed by successive transfers to establish a vigorous strain. Maintaining the species in culture long-term presents challenges, as section Cyanescens species are reported to lose vigor after repeated subculturing on artificial media. Cryopreservation or periodic return to wood-based substrates may help maintain culture health. Any cultivation attempts should be documented and voucher specimens deposited in herbaria to advance scientific understanding of this poorly known species.
