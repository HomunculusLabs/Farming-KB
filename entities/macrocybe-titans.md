## Macrocybe titans

## Description

Macrocybe titans, commonly known as the giant macrocybe, is one of the largest gilled mushrooms in the world. Found in subtropical to tropical regions of the Caribbean, Central America, Mexico, and occasionally the Gulf States of the US, this impressive fungus can produce caps up to 4 feet across with thick, leathery stems. The fruiting bodies are extraordinarily durable, persisting aboveground for up to two months. The species remains somewhat mysterious, with its edibility and full cultivation parameters still uncertain.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Tricholomataceae
- **Genus:** Macrocybe
- **Species:** M. titans

## Key Facts

- One of the largest gilled mushrooms in the world, with caps reaching 4 feet across
- Found in subtropical to tropical regions: Caribbean, Central America, Mexico, Gulf States
- Can persist aboveground for up to two months — exceptionally long-lived for a gilled mushroom
- Stem is extremely tough with a thick rind, soft and marshmallow-like center
- Heat-loving species preferring fruiting temperatures above 80°F (27°C)
- Edibility remains uncertain — further research needed
- Stem base often covered with powdery fungus suggesting possible symbiotic relationships
- Fruitbodies are notably insect-free, unusual for gilled mushrooms
- Mycelium smells like cherries
- Difficulty level: Outdoor cultivation 2/5, Indoor cultivation 3/5

## Morphology

- Stemmed mushroom with cap centered on the stem
- Attached gills, [[spore-print]] white
- Stem rough, covered with granules or powdery speckled molds
- Thick rind on stem exterior (presumably to support massive cap weight)
- Soft, cottony interior tissue
- Cap wavy along margin when mature
- Extremely tough exterior with marshmallow-soft interior

## Ecology and Observations

- Terrestrial mushroom, not a typical wood inhabiter
- Found in landscaped beds and open areas in subtropical/tropical regions
- Giant stems often covered with thick, powdery layer of microbes and other fungi
- This microbial coating may suggest a complex of symbionts needed for fruiting
- Absence of insect pressure is unusual and may indicate natural insecticidal properties
- A specimen from the South Carolina coast had a [[blue-cheese]]-like fungus colonizing the stem base
- Possible interspecies symbiotic relationship under investigation

## Cultivation Notes

### Substrate Preferences

- Prefers composted mixtures of grasses, cereal straws, wood mulch, spent coffee grounds
- Agricultural by-products: bean hulls, cornstalks, cotton waste, beet pulp, sugarcane bagasse
- Prefers complex growing media over basic formulations
- Warm-weather fruiter requiring diverse nutrient sources

### Casing Soil

- Microbial [[casing-soil]] is required for fruiting
- Apply when entire substrate surface is a continuous white mat
- Dress beds with 2–3 inches of hydrated microbial casing soil to stimulate primordia formation

### Outdoor and Indoor Cultivation

- Shred agricultural by-products into fluffy consistency
- Pasteurize in hot water bath for one to two hours
- Fill beds or containers 8–10 inches deep
- Once colonized, dress with 2–3 inches of microbial casing soil
- Water generously after casing
- Inoculation to fruiting time: three to five weeks
- [[grain-spawn]] fruits sooner and produces larger yields than sawdust spawn

### Preferred Spawn Types

- Grain spawn (recommended for faster fruiting and larger yields)
- Supplemented sawdust spawn

### Laboratory Culture

- Easily cloned from soft, cottony interior tissue of the stem
- Score thick outer rind and snap stem in half to reveal sterile interior
- Best cloning from extreme upper and lower stem portions
- Middle stem can be fibrous, hollow, and more differentiated

### Culture Storage

- Innovative liquid culture storage method: blend agar cultures with sterile water
- Transfer to storage tubes as oxygen-deprived slurries at room temperature
- Mycelium enters hibernation state when oxygen depletes
- Better recovery than dehydrated agar cultures or refrigerated grain cultures in sterile water
- This method may be applicable to other tropical mushroom species

## Harvesting

- Grasp the massive stalk and twist free from growing substrate
- Trim base to remove adhering substrate

## Storage

- Remarkably resistant to decay and rot
- Fresh storage: four to six weeks at 38–42°F (3–6°C)
- Much longer shelf life than most cultivated mushrooms
- Interior soft as marshmallow; cap cuticle and stem rind extremely tough

## Nutritional and Medicinal Properties

- Nutritional data not yet available for this species
- Mycelium is highly antibiotic — active against many strains of E. coli and Staphylococcus
- Natural insecticidal properties suggested by insect-free fruiting bodies
- Potential for mycelial extract as natural foliar spray for pest control in fruiting rooms

## Mycoremediation Potential

- Strong antibiotic action makes mycelium suitable for mycofiltration modules
- Natural insecticidal properties could yield extracts for pest management
- Possible use as foliar sprays to reduce pest pressure in mushroom fruiting rooms
- Adaptability to complex [[agricultural-waste-substrates]] suggests bioremediation applications

## Identification

- Massive size — caps up to 4 feet across
- White spore print
- Rough stem with powdery coating
- Thick leathery rind on stem
- Wavy cap margin when mature
- Heat-loving, fruiting above 80°F (27°C)
- Terrestrial habitat in subtropical/tropical regions

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Macrocybe titans represents one of the most intriguing frontiers in mushroom cultivation. Its extraordinary size, durability, and natural antibiotic and insecticidal properties suggest significant commercial and ecological potential. The innovative liquid culture storage method developed for this species may prove valuable for preserving other tropical mushroom cultures. While full cultivation parameters and edibility remain uncertain, the species' ability to grow on diverse agricultural waste substrates in just three to five weeks makes it promising for tropical cultivation operations.
