## Wombat Forest

## Description

The Wombat Forest is a 64,000-hectare (approximately 158,000-acre) native forest
located in central Victoria, Australia, near the towns of Daylesford, Trentham, and
Woodend. The forest is situated within the traditional lands of the Dja Dja Wurrung,
Taungurung, and Wathaurong peoples. It is one of the largest remaining blocks of
native forest in central Victoria and plays a significant role in the region"s water
catchment, biodiversity conservation, and landscape character.

The forest has a dramatic ecological history. Completely devastated during the
Victorian gold rush between 1860 and 1890 — when trees were felled for mining
timber, fuel, and to expose gold-bearing substrates — the forest underwent a
remarkable recovery through a combination of protection policies and active
management. However, this recovery has been threatened by subsequent mismanagement,
creating a complex case study in forest restoration and degradation.

Holmgren, who lives adjacent to the Wombat Forest at his Fryers Forest property,
has been a vocal advocate for improved forest management and has written extensively
about the forest's history and management challenges.

## Classification

- **Category**: Forest / Ecological Site
- **Location**: Central Victoria, Australia (near Daylesford, Trentham, Woodend)
- **Area**: 64,000 hectares
- **Forest Type**: Mixed eucalypt forest with Box and Stringybark species
- **Traditional Owners**: Dja Dja Wurrung, Taungurung, Wathaurung peoples
- **Management**: State government (Department of Energy, Environment and Climate
  Action)
- **Current Status**: State Forest with areas of National Park

## Key Facts

- The forest covers approximately 64,000 hectares in central Victoria, making
  it one of the largest remaining native forest blocks in the region.
- Completely devastated during the gold rush between 1860 and 1890, with
  virtually all timber removed for mining operations, fuelwood, and land
  clearing.
- Recovery began with a complete ban on cutting sawlogs from the 1930s, allowing
  the regrowth forest to mature.
- Continuous thinning for firewood and later for short lengths for hardboard
  manufacture helped create a valuable and diverse regrowth forest.
- Bad state government policies and management since the 1970s resulted in
  over-cutting for sawlogs and pulpwood from mature forest, undermining the
  recovery.
- Large stands of dense, unthinned regrowth have developed, representing both
  a fire hazard and a lost productive opportunity.
- The forest is ecologically significant, supporting diverse flora and fauna
  including threatened species.
- Major regional water catchment, contributing to the headwaters of several
  rivers including the Loddon, Coliban, and Werribee.
- Holmgren has been actively involved in advocacy for the forest, including
  making a formal submission (the "Wombat Forest Submission") regarding public
  forest management.
- In 2022, approximately 25,000 hectares was declared as the Wombat-Lerderderg
  National Park, providing increased protection for a significant portion of
  the forest.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **[[mycorrhizal-networks]]**: The Wombat Forest supports extensive underground
  mycorrhizal networks associated with its eucalypt overstorey. The forest"s
  history of devastation and recovery demonstrates the resilience (and
  vulnerability) of these fungal networks to disturbance.
- **Wild Mushroom Habitat**: The mixed eucalypt forest provides habitat for a
  range of wild fungi, including edible species. The forest's management
  history directly affects the availability and sustainability of wild
  [[mushroom-harvesting]].
- **Forest Thinning and Fungal Diversity**: The documented effects of thinning
  on forest structure are relevant to understanding how forest management
  practices affect fungal community composition and fruiting patterns.
- **Disturbance and Recovery**: The complete devastation and subsequent recovery
  of the forest provides insights into how fungal communities respond to
  catastrophic disturbance and the timeframes involved in soil biological
  recovery.
- **Decomposer Ecology**: The large volumes of woody debris created by historical
  clearing and subsequent thinning operations have fed decomposer fungi for
  over a century, [[building-soil]] organic matter and humus in the forest floor.
- **Integrated Forest Management**: Holmgren's advocacy for the Wombat Forest
  demonstrates how [[permaculture-principles]] can inform public land management,
  including the integration of sustainable mushroom harvesting with other
  forest uses.
- **Fire Ecology**: Dense, unthinned regrowth stands represent a fire risk that
  could devastate fungal communities — the management of fire hazard through
  thinning has direct implications for soil and fungal conservation.
