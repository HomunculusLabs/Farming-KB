## Ifugao People

## Description

The Ifugao people are an indigenous group of the Philippines renowned for constructing and maintaining one of the world"s oldest and most sustainable wet terrace agricultural systems. Their rice terraces in the mountains of northern Luzon have been in continuous use for at least 3,000 years, representing one of humanity's most enduring examples of sustainable land-water integration. Mollison draws extensively on the Ifugao as a model of self-governance and community-managed hydraulic infrastructure.

## Classification

- **Region:** Mountain Province, northern Luzon, Philippines
- **Language:** Ifugao (Austronesian language family)
- **Agricultural system:** Wet rice terraces with integrated polyculture
- **Cultural designation:** Indigenous highland people of the Philippines

## Key Facts

- Wet terraces have been in continuous use for at least 3,000 years
- [[harold-conklin]] estimates that 1,000 years of toil created the terrace systems
- A typical Ifugao family of 5 manages 0.5 ha of padi rice and 0.25 ha of sweet potato as staples, plus 1 ha of woodlot and grove crop
- Annual productive labor: 400 person-days per family, or 80 days per able individual
- Rainfall is 3–6 m per year, necessitating constant terrace maintenance
- Terrace soil depth is approximately 2 cm; half the mass of bunds is stone
- Canal and ditch maintenance requires annual earth and stone repairs
- Economic equivalence: one day of rice for one hour of work
- Domestic pigs and chickens are fed from gleanings and crop residues

## Governance and Social Organization

Mollison highlights a profound insight about the Ifugao, citing Fox (1977) on Indonesian palm cultures and Conklin on Ifugao: these peoples recognize no external authority. The construction of common canal systems and shared irrigation necessitates community organization and self-control. Mollison argues that the creators of such self-sustained systems have developed a form of self-governance never achieved by any central authority in history.

This stands in contrast to what Mollison terms "hydraulic despotism" found in colonized areas, which predisposes societies to exploitation and centralized government control.

## Agricultural System Details

The Ifugao system integrates:
- **Padi rice** (0.5 ha) as the primary carbohydrate staple
- **Sweet potato** (0.25 ha) as supplementary staple
- **Woodlot and grove crop** (1 ha) for fuel, construction, and forage
- **Domestic pigs and chickens** fed from gleanings and crop residues
- Constant terrace, canal, and ditch maintenance under extreme rainfall conditions
- Stone and earth bund engineering to retain 2 cm soil depth on steep terrain

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

The Ifugao represent a cornerstone example in [[permaculture]] philosophy of how small-scale, community-managed water systems can achieve extraordinary longevity and sustainability. Their 3,000-year track record demonstrates that:

1. **Community self-governance** of shared water infrastructure is more durable than centralized control
2. **Integrated polyculture** (rice, sweet potato, pigs, chickens, woodlot) provides complete nutrition from modest land area
3. **Continuous maintenance culture** — annual repairs to earth and stone infrastructure — is the key to permanence
4. **Low input efficiency** — the system runs on human labor and biological inputs without external fertilizers or machinery

Mollison uses the Ifugao as proof that self-reliant, decentralized food systems can outlast civilizations, and as a philosophical argument against centralized control of water and food production.
