# Dendroaspis polylepis

## Overview

Dendroaspis polylepis, commonly known as the black mamba, is the longest venomous snake in Africa and one of the fastest-moving snakes in the world. It is widely regarded as the most dangerous snake on the African continent due to the combination of its highly toxic neurotoxic venom, large venom yield, aggressive defensive temperament, and speed.

The species is native to sub-Saharan Africa, ranging from northeastern [[table-mountain-south-africa-mollison]] through eastern Africa to southern Somalia and Ethiopia. It is a diurnal, predominantly terrestrial snake that inhabits a variety of savanna and woodland habitats.

Despite its fearsome reputation, the black mamba typically avoids human confrontation and will flee when possible. Most bites occur when the snake is cornered, surprised, or deliberately provoked. Without antivenom treatment, mortality from black mamba envenomation approaches 100 percent.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Dendroaspis polylepis belongs to the family Elapidae. It was first described by Albert Günther in 1864. The genus name Dendroaspis is derived from Greek words meaning tree and asp, though the species is primarily terrestrial.

The specific epithet polylepis means many scales, referring to the species' high dorsal scale count compared to other African mamba species. Four species are recognized within the genus: the black mamba and three green mamba species.

Adult black mambas are among the longest venomous snakes in the world. Average total length is 2.0 to 2.5 meters, with specimens regularly exceeding 3.0 meters. The maximum reliably recorded length is 4.3 meters.

The body is slender and cylindrical, colored gunmetal grey to olive-brown dorsally with a lighter ventral surface. Juveniles are lighter in color and tend to be greyish-green. Despite the common name, the interior of the mouth is inky black, which is displayed during threat displays.

The head is coffin-shaped and distinct from the neck. The eyes are medium-sized with round pupils and a bronze to grey iris. The body scales are smooth and arranged in 23 to 25 rows at midbody.

The tail is long and thin, comprising approximately 17 to 25 percent of total body length. The species can raise up to one-third of its body off the ground when threatened, a behavior that enhances its already imposing appearance.

## Distribution and Habitat

Dendroaspis polylepis is distributed across eastern and southern sub-Saharan Africa. Its range extends from northeastern South Africa through Mozambique, Zimbabwe, Zambia, Malawi, Tanzania, Kenya, and Uganda to southern Somalia and Ethiopia.

The species is absent from dense tropical rainforests of the Congo Basin and the hyper-arid regions of the Namib and Kalahari deserts. It also does not occur in West Africa or Madagascar.

Preferred habitats include savanna, open woodland, rocky outcrops, and riverine forests. The species is frequently found in termite mounds, abandoned burrows, hollow trees, and rock crevices that serve as retreats.

Black mambas show a strong preference for areas with some tree cover, though they are primarily ground-dwelling. They readily adapt to anthropogenic environments including agricultural areas, plantations, and the outskirts of human settlements.

Activity is strictly diurnal. The species is most active during the warmer hours of the day, particularly in the early morning and late afternoon. During the hottest midday hours, black mambas typically retreat to shelter.

Home range sizes vary with habitat productivity, with estimates ranging from 2 to 15 hectares. Individuals may use multiple retreat sites within their range, moving between them based on prey availability and thermal conditions.

## Venom Composition

Dendroaspis polylepis venom is predominantly neurotoxic, with a murine LD50 of approximately 0.32 mg/kg by intravenous injection. This makes it among the most toxic of all elapid venoms, though several other species have lower LD50 values.

The venom contains a complex mixture of dendrotoxins, three-finger toxins, phospholipase A2 enzymes, and other protein components. Dendrotoxins, which are unique to the genus Dendroaspis, are potassium channel blockers.

Dendrotoxins facilitate excessive acetylcholine release at presynaptic nerve terminals by blocking voltage-gated potassium channels. This prolonged depolarization leads to rapid exhaustion of neurotransmitter stores and subsequent neuromuscular blockade.

Three-finger toxins in black mamba venom include both alpha-neurotoxins that block postsynaptic nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and fasciculins that inhibit acetylcholinesterase. The combined pre- and postsynaptic action produces rapid and profound paralysis.

Calciseptine, a specific toxin isolated from Dendroaspis polylepis venom, blocks L-type calcium channels in cardiac and smooth muscle. This may contribute to the cardiac arrhythmias and hypotension observed in severe envenomation.

The average venom yield per bite is 50 to 120 mg, with maximum yields exceeding 400 mg. A dose of 10 to 15 mg is sufficient to kill a human adult, meaning a single bite delivers many times the lethal dose.

Venom composition varies between populations across the species range. Eastern African specimens may have proportionally more dendrotoxins compared to southern African populations. This geographic variation has implications for antivenom cross-neutralization and clinical management.

## Clinical Significance

Dendroaspis polylepis envenomation is a medical emergency requiring immediate antivenom therapy. Without treatment, death can occur within 30 minutes to several hours of the bite, depending on the amount of venom injected and the location of the bite.

Early symptoms include a metallic taste, ptosis, diplopia, bulbar palsy, and progressive limb weakness. These rapidly progress to complete flaccid paralysis, respiratory failure, and cardiovascular collapse.

The onset of neurotoxic symptoms is notably rapid compared to many other elapid species. Respiratory paralysis may develop within 20 to 30 minutes of a severe bite. This rapid progression underscores the critical importance of early antivenom administration.

Local effects at the bite site are typically minimal, with little swelling or pain. This contrasts sharply with viper envenomations and may lead patients to underestimate the severity of the bite.
