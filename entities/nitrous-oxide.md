## Chemical Properties

Nitrous oxide has the molecular formula N₂O, a molecular weight of 44.01 g/mol,
and a density approximately 1.5 times that of air. It is a colorless,
slightly sweet-smelling gas at room temperature with a boiling point of −88.5°C.

The molecule is linear (N–N–O) and supports combustion in the same manner as
oxygen when mixed with fuel gases. This property makes it valuable as an
oxidizer in rocketry and automotive engines. It is stored as a compressed
liquefied gas in steel or aluminum cylinders.

Nitrous oxide is relatively stable under normal conditions but decomposes to
nitrogen and oxygen at temperatures above 570°C. It is moderately soluble in
water (0.15 g/L at 25°C) and more soluble in blood, which facilitates its
rapid uptake and elimination through the lungs.

## Pharmacology

Nitrous oxide acts primarily as a non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist,
blocking glutamate-mediated excitatory neurotransmission. This mechanism
underlies both its anesthetic and dissociative effects, shared with other
dissociatives like [[dextromethorphan]].

The gas also activates two-pore-domain potassium channels (TREK-1), which
contributes to its analgesic properties. Additionally, nitrous oxide interacts
with endogenous opioid systems, potentiating opioid receptor-mediated
analgesia through release of endorphins and dynorphins.

Its high blood-gas partition coefficient (0.47) allows rapid equilibration
between alveolar gas and blood, producing onset of effects within 20–30 seconds
of inhalation and recovery within 2–5 minutes after cessation. The MAC
(minimum alveolar concentration) for anesthesia is approximately 104%, meaning
nitrous alone cannot produce surgical anesthesia at atmospheric pressure.

## Effects

Medical doses (25–70% in oxygen) produce dose-dependent analgesia and light
sedation with preserved consciousness at lower concentrations and loss of
consciousness approaching 70%. Dental patients commonly experience calmness and
reduced anxiety.

Recreational doses typically involve inhaling 1–4 balloons (approximately
8–16 L of gas). Effects peak within 10–20 seconds and last 1–3 minutes per
inhalation. Common subjective effects include euphoria, laughter, distorted
body perception, mild visual and auditory hallucinations, and derealization.

Physical effects include tingling in the extremities, mild euphoria, reduced
motor coordination, and temporary changes in voice pitch (the "squeaky voice"
effect from altered vocal cord resonance in a denser gas atmosphere).

The effects profile is notably brief compared to other dissociatives, which
contributes to a pattern of repeated dosing in recreational contexts.
