## Overview

**Hydrilla verticillata** (L.f.) Royle is a submersed perennial aquatic plant in the family Hydrocharitaceae, commonly known as hydrilla or water thyme. It is widely regarded as one of the most problematic invasive aquatic plants worldwide.

Native to parts of Asia, Africa, and Australia, hydrilla has spread to every continent except Antarctica. In the United States, it infests water bodies across at least 19 states and causes estimated economic damages exceeding $100 million annually through management costs and ecological impacts.

The species earned the epithet "the perfect aquatic weed" due to its remarkable combination of growth rate, reproductive versatility, habitat tolerance, and resistance to control measures. It can grow up to 2.5 cm per day under optimal conditions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*H. verticillata* belongs to the order Alismatales, family Hydrocharitaceae, genus *Hydrilla*. The genus is monotypic, containing only this species, though two biotypes (monoecious and dioecious) are recognized based on flower arrangement.

Stems are slender, heavily branched, and can reach lengths of 7–8 meters in deep water. They root in substrate through a network of fleshy tubers and slender roots. The plant produces specialized overwintering structures called turions (dense buds) in leaf axils.

Leaves are sessile, arranged in whorls of four to eight (most commonly five) at each node. Each leaf is 6–20 mm long, 2–4 mm wide, with conspicuously serrulate margins visible under magnification. Leaf midrib is often reddish, and a small spinose cell is present at the leaf base.

Flowers are small, unisexual, and emergent. In dioecious populations (predominant in the southeastern U.S.), male and female flowers occur on separate plants. In monoecious populations (predominant in the northern U.S.), both sexes occur on the same plant.

Subterranean turions (tubers) are white to yellowish, 5–15 mm long, and produced in the sediment on rhizome tips. These tubers can remain viable in sediments for 4–10 years, making eradication extremely difficult once established.

## Distribution and Habitat

Hydrilla is native to warmer regions of Asia (India, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, China, Japan), eastern and central Africa, and northern Australia. The precise center of origin is debated, but the greatest genetic diversity occurs in South and Southeast Asia.

The dioecious biotype was first discovered in the United States in 1960 in the Crystal River system of Florida, likely introduced through the aquarium trade. It spread rapidly through interconnected waterways and boat traffic. The monoecious biotype was first detected in the Potomac River near Washington, D.C., in the early 1980s.

The species inhabits freshwater lakes, reservoirs, rivers, streams, canals, ponds, and marshes. It tolerates a remarkably wide range of conditions including depths from 0.1 to 15 meters, temperatures from 10–35°C, pH from 5–10, and both oligotrophic and hypereutrophic nutrient regimes.

Growth is optimal in warm (20–30°C), shallow, nutrient-rich waters with fine sediment substrates. However, the species persists in marginal habitats including brackish water (up to 10 ppt salinity) and turbid conditions where most submersed macrophytes cannot survive.

## Ecology and Invasiveness

Hydrilla forms dense surface mats and subsurface canopies that can reduce [[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]] that inhibit growth of competing algal and plant species, a trait documented in both laboratory and field studies.

Vegetative reproduction is extraordinarily prolific. A single stem fragment with one node can produce a new colony. Subterranean turions are produced at densities of up to 5,000 per square meter of sediment, creating a persistent propagule bank that frustrates eradication efforts.

Both biotypes produce turions, but the monoecious form also produces abundant axillary turions (leaf-bud turions) that break free and drift to new locations. This dual turion strategy makes monoecious hydrilla particularly difficult to manage in temperate climates.

## Management and Control

Herbicide treatment is the most widely used management approach, with fluridone, endothall, diquat, penoxsulam, and bispyribac-sodium all registered for hydrilla control. Fluridone applied at low concentrations (10–20 ppb) over 8–12 weeks is the standard systemic treatment.

Grass carp (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) have been used for [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. Bottom barriers (benthic covers) are effective in small areas such as boat ramps and swimming areas but are impractical for large water bodies.

The hydrilla leaf-mining fly (*Hydrellia pakistanae*) and hydrilla stem-boring weevil (*Bagous affinis*) have been investigated as biological control agents with limited success. None have achieved the population levels needed for effective landscape-scale suppression in U.S. infestations.

## Physiological Characteristics

*H. verticillata* exhibits C₃ photosynthesis with high rates of [[cervantes-breeding-program-design-goal-setting]].

## Cultural and Research Significance

Hydrilla has been the subject of extensive research on aquatic plant invasion biology, allelopathy, and biological control. Studies on hydrilla have contributed to understanding of turion dormancy mechanisms, clonal dispersal dynamics, and the evolution of invasive phenotypes.

The species is regulated as a noxious weed in numerous countries and U.S. states. Transport, sale, and cultivation of hydrilla are prohibited in most jurisdictions. Despite regulatory restrictions, illegal introduction through the aquarium and water garden trade continues to occur.
