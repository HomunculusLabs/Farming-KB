# Wood Wide Web

## Description

The "wood wide web" is a metaphorical term for the underground network of mycorrhizal fungal mycelia that connects individual plants and trees within an ecosystem. This network enables the interplant transfer of nutrients, carbon, water, and chemical signals between individuals — including between plants of different species. The concept was popularized by the landmark work of [[suzanne-simard]] and colleagues (1997), who demonstrated net carbon transfer between ectomycorrhizal tree species in the field using isotopic tracing. David Read (1998) further articulated the idea that [[mycorrhizal-networks]] serve as an underground infrastructure linking the entire plant community.

## Classification

- **Concept type**: Ecosystem-level fungal function
- **Primary fungal groups involved**: [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] (connecting trees), [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] (connecting herbaceous plants)
- **Discovery**: Simard et al. (1997a,b,c); conceptual framework by Read (1998)
- **Metaphor origin**: Popularized in the late 1990s, drawing analogy to the World Wide Web

## Key Facts

- Ectomycorrhizal connections link tree roots below ground, enabling interplant movement of nutrients and carbon (Amaranthus and Perry, 1994; Read, 1998; Rayner, 1998).
- Arbuscular [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] similarly connect herbaceous plants (Heap and Newman, 1980; Newman and Eason, 1989; Eason et al., 1991).
- Simard et al. (1997b,c) demonstrated reciprocal transfer of carbon isotopes between ectomycorrhizal Betula papyrifera and Pseudotsuga menziesii in the field.
- The discovery that plants of different species can be connected by underground mycelial networks fundamentally altered concepts about plant interspecific interactions.
- Previously, plant communities were thought to arise primarily from competition; the wood wide web concept introduced a paradigm of balance between competition and synergism.
- Mycorrhizal networks facilitate recruitment of new individuals into plant communities, especially after disturbance (Amaranthus and Perry, 1989; Jumpponen et al., 1999, 2002).
- Fragmentation of mycorrhizal hyphal networks (e.g., through soil disturbance) facilitates invasion by exotic species into existing ecosystems (Hart et al., 2001).
- Individual fungal mycelia can cover hectares of forest floor, functioning as true ecosystem engineers (Smith et al., 1992; Lawton and Jones, 1995).
- Rayner (1993, 1998) likened fungal networks in forests to urban infrastructure: communication, power supply, plumbing, and sewage systems.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- The wood wide web concept is fundamental to understanding why maintaining healthy mycorrhizal communities is critical for cultivation systems.
- In agroforestry, orchards, and forest gardens, the below-ground mycorrhizal network can distribute resources from established plants to seedlings, improving establishment rates.
- Disturbance of mycorrhizal networks through tilling, soil compaction, or chemical inputs can disrupt the wood wide web, reducing crop resilience and nutrient access.
- Inoculation practices should consider not just individual plant-fungal partnerships but the potential for creating functional networks that connect multiple plants.
- The concept supports polyculture approaches where different plant species share resources through a common mycelial network, improving overall system productivity.
- Understanding mycorrhizal network dynamics is essential for restoration ecology, reforestation, and the establishment of sustainable cultivation systems.
