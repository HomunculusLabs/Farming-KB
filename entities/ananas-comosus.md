## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Liliopsida → Poales → Bromeliaceae → *Ananas comosus* (L.) Merr. The species was originally described by Carl Linnaeus as *Bromelia comosus* in 1753 and transferred to *Ananas* by Elmer Merrill in 1917.

The genus *Ananas* includes two additional wild species: *A. bracteatus* and *A. ananassoides*, both of which hybridize freely with cultivated pineapple. The cultivated form is self-incompatible and highly heterozygous.

Pineapple is a terrestrial herb reaching 0.8–1.5 m in height with a rosette of 30–80 sword-shaped leaves. Leaves are waxy, fibrous, 50–180 cm long, with sharp serrated margins and a prominent midrib.

The leaves are arranged in a spiral phyllotaxis (often 5/13 or 8/21 Fibonacci spirals), forming a funnel that channels water to the base. Trichomes on the leaf surface absorb water and nutrients, making pineapple a facultative CAM (crassulacean acid metabolism) plant.

The inflorescence is a terminal spike bearing 50–200 hermaphroditic flowers, each subtended by a bract. The edible fruit is a sorosis — a multiple fruit formed by the fusion of individual berries with the central rachis and bracts.

The fruit is cylindrical to conical, 15–30 cm long, with a tough, segmented rind bearing a rosette of crown leaves. Flesh color ranges from pale yellow to deep gold depending on cultivar and maturity.

## Cultivation

Pineapple is propagated vegetatively using crowns, slips, suckers, or hapas (stem sections). Crowns from fruit tops are the most common propagule for commercial planting. Planting density ranges from 30,000 to 70,000 plants per hectare.

The crop performs best in well-drained sandy or loamy soils at pH 4.5–6.5, in areas receiving 1,000–1,500 mm annual rainfall. Optimal growth temperature is 25–32 °C, with minimum temperatures above 18 °C.

Natural flowering is induced by cool temperatures and short daylengths, but commercial growers apply ethephon or calcium carbide to synchronize flowering. This practice, called "forcing," allows year-round production scheduling.

Major pests include the pineapple mealybug (*Dysmicoccus brevipes*), which transmits pineapple wilt disease (mealybug wilt-associated virus). Nematodes (*Meloidogyne* spp.) and heart rot caused by *Phytophthora* spp. are also significant constraints.

## Nutritional Value

Pineapple provides approximately 50 kcal per 100 g serving, making it one of the lower-calorie tropical fruits. It is an excellent source of vitamin C, providing roughly 80% of the daily value per 100 g.

The fruit is consumed worldwide in diverse forms. Fresh pineapple is eaten as a dessert or used in savory dishes across Southeast Asian, Latin American, and Caribbean cuisines. Canned pineapple rings and crushed pineapple are staples in baking, beverage production, and pizza toppings.

The fruit contains manganese (providing 40–60% of the daily value per serving), thiamin, vitamin B6, and dietary fiber (1.4 g per 100 g). Its sweetness comes primarily from sucrose, fructose, and glucose.

Pineapple is the only commercially significant dietary source of bromelain, a mixture of cysteine proteases including stem bromelain and fruit bromelain. Bromelain accounts for approximately 0.5–1% of fresh fruit weight.
