## Taxonomy and Morphology

Dodecatheon meadia was first described by the English botanist Thomas Walter in 1788 in his Flora Caroliniana. The specific epithet honors the English physician and botanist Richard Mead.

The genus Dodecatheon historically contained approximately 15 species, all native to North America. Molecular systematics has shown the genus to be derived from within Primula section Dodecatheon.

The plant grows from a thickened, fleshy crown producing a rosette of basal leaves. Leaves are oblong to elliptic, 10 to 25 centimeters long, and 2 to 5 centimeters wide.

Leaf margins are entire or irregularly wavy, and the surface is smooth to sparsely hairy. The leaves are green above and lighter green beneath with prominent parallel venation.

The flowering scape arises from the center of the rosette and is 15 to 45 centimeters tall. The scape is hairless or sparsely glandular-pubescent.

The inflorescence is an umbel of 6 to 20 flowers nodding at the tips of slender pedicels. The pedicels are 1 to 3 centimeters long and curve downward.

Each flower has five reflexed petals 12 to 20 millimeters long, swept sharply backward. Petals are typically white to pale pink with a yellow or purplish ring around the center.

The fused stamens form a prominent beak or cone projecting forward from the flower center. This structure gives the flower its characteristic shooting-star silhouette.

The fruit is a cylindrical capsule 12 to 20 millimeters long containing numerous small seeds. Seeds are released as the capsule splits open at maturity.

## Distribution and Habitat

Dodecatheon meadia is native to [[dighton-fungi-acidifying-pollutants-forest-ecosystems]].

Several insect herbivores feed on Dodecatheon meadia. The larvae of a specialist plume moth, Pterophorus, have been recorded feeding on the flowers and developing seed capsules.

White-tailed deer occasionally browse the foliage and flower scapes. Heavy deer pressure can significantly reduce flowering and seed set in populations near suburban areas.

## Cultivation
