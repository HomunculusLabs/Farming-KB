## Overview

Crocosmia aurea is a summer-flowering cormous geophyte native to eastern southern Africa. It produces arching spikes of bright orange to red-orange flowers that earn it the common name "falling stars."

The species is one of the parents of the widely grown hybrid Crocosmia × crocosmiiflora (montbretia). Its genetic contributions include heat tolerance and vibrant flower color.

Unlike many Crocosmia species from the Cape winter-rainfall zone, Crocosmia aurea grows in summer-rainfall areas. This makes it better adapted to warm, humid climates than its relatives.

The flowers are pollinated by sunbirds in their native range and by hummingbirds where naturalized. The tubular shape and bright color are classic adaptations to bird pollination.

The dried flowers emit a saffron-like fragrance when placed in water, which gives the genus its name. This characteristic is shared with Crocosmia × crocosmiiflora and other species in the genus.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Monocots
- **Order:** Asparagales
- **Family:** Iridaceae
- **Genus:** Crocosmia
- **Species:** C. aurea (Hook.f.) Planch.

The species was first described as Tritonia aurea by Hooker in 1855. Planchon transferred it to Crocosmia in 1856.

The genus name Crocosmia comes from the Greek "krokos" (saffron) and "osme" (smell), referring to the saffron-like scent of dried flowers. The species epithet "aurea" means golden, describing the flower color.

Crocosmia comprises approximately 8 species, all native to southern and eastern Africa. The genus is closely related to Tritonia and some species have been shuffled between the two genera.

Two subspecies are recognized: subsp. aurea and subsp. pauciflora. Subsp. pauciflora has fewer, larger flowers and a more restricted distribution.

## Morphology

Crocosmia aurea grows from a flattened corm 15–25 mm in diameter. The corm tunic is fibrous and netted.

Leaves are ensiform (sword-shaped), 4–6 in number, and 30–60 cm long. They are bright green, moderately rigid, and form a basal fan.

The flowering stem is erect at the base but arches toward the tip. It reaches 60–100 cm in height and is branched in robust specimens.

The inflorescence is a panicle of 3–8 spreading branches, each bearing 2–5 flowers. Bracts are green, 10–15 mm long, and membranous at the margins.

Flowers are bright orange to red-orange, tubular, and 30–45 mm long. The perianth tube is curved and approximately 15–20 mm long.

The six tepals flare outward at the tips, creating a wide-mouthed trumpet. The upper three tepals are slightly larger than the lower three.

Stamens are three, exserted from the flower tube. Anthers are versatile, 4–6 mm long, and produce yellow pollen.

The style is exserted, arching over the stamens, with three short stigmatic branches. Capsules are ovoid, 6–10 mm long, containing many small seeds.

Flowers open sequentially over a period of 3–4 weeks. Each individual flower lasts 2–3 days before wilting.

## Distribution and Habitat

Crocosmia aurea is native to eastern South Africa, Swaziland, and southern Mozambique. It occurs along the eastern escarpment from the Eastern Cape to Mpumalanga and Limpopo.

The species grows in forest margins, along stream banks, and in moist grasslands. It prefers partially shaded to sunny sites with reliable summer moisture.

Elevations range from near sea level to 1800 m in the Drakensberg foothills. Populations are typically associated with areas receiving 700–1200 mm of annual rainfall.

Soils are typically humus-rich and well-drained, derived from shale or dolerite. The species tolerates a range of soil pH from slightly acidic to neutral.

The species has become naturalized in parts of eastern Australia and New Zealand. In these regions it colonizes damp, disturbed sites along roadsides and stream margins.

## Cultivation

Crocosmia aurea is less commonly cultivated than its hybrid descendants but is valued by collectors. It requires a warm, moist growing season with winter dormancy.

Plant corms in spring at a depth of 5–8 cm in rich, well-drained soil. A position in dappled shade to full sun is suitable.

Regular watering during the growing season promotes vigorous growth and flowering. The species tolerates wetter conditions than most Cape Crocosmia species.

In cold climates, mulch heavily in autumn to protect corms from freezing. The species is hardy to about −5 °C with protection.

Propagation is by seed, corm division, or cormels. Seed should be sown fresh in spring and kept warm and moist.

The species is resistant to most pests and diseases. Spider mites can be problematic on indoor or greenhouse-grown plants.

Crocosmia aurea is particularly useful in subtropical and warm-temperate gardens. It fills the midsummer flowering gap between spring and autumn bulbs.

## Ecological Role

The bright orange flowers are adapted for sunbird pollination in the native range. The southern double-collared sunbird (Cinnyris chalybeus) is a frequent visitor.

Where naturalized outside Africa, hummingbirds serve as effective substitute pollinators. This bird-pollination shift contributes to the species' invasive potential in the Americas.

Crocosmia aurea provides late-summer nectar when few other native plants are flowering. This resource supports pollinator populations during a critical period.

The species hybridizes readily with other Crocosmia species in cultivation. These crosses have produced many of the garden Crocosmia cultivars available today.

In its native range, the species is part of the Afromontane forest margin community. These ecotone habitats are biodiversity-rich but increasingly threatened by forestry plantations.
