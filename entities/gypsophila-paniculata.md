## Overview

Gypsophila paniculata, commonly known as baby's breath or common gypsophila, is a long-lived herbaceous perennial in the family Caryophyllaceae. It is native to central and eastern Europe and central Asia and is among the most commercially important cut-flower species worldwide.

The plant produces clouds of tiny white flowers on tall, airy panicles from midsummer to early fall. The effect is ethereal and misty, making it a staple filler in floral arrangements and bridal bouquets.

The genus name Gypsophila means "chalk-lover," reflecting the species' preference for calcareous soils. The species epithet paniculata refers to the branched, paniculate inflorescence.

Baby's breath has been in commercial floriculture since the Victorian era and remains one of the top ten cut-flower crops globally. Major production centers include the Netherlands, Israel, Kenya, and Ecuador.

The species is a significant economic crop, with global production estimated at several hundred million stems annually. Israel alone produces over 100 million stems per year for export markets.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Eudicots
- **Order:** Caryophyllales
- **Family:** Caryophyllaceae
- **Genus:** Gypsophila
- **Species:** Gypsophila paniculata L.

The genus Gypsophila contains approximately 150 species, with centers of diversity in the Mediterranean region and central Asia. Gypsophila paniculata is the most widely cultivated species in the genus.

It is closely related to Gypsophila elegans (annual baby's breath) and Gypsophila repens (creeping baby's breath), both also grown ornamentally. The three species differ in habit: perennial vs. annual and upright vs. prostrate.

Chromosome number is 2n = 34. The species is tetraploid relative to some of its congeners, which contributes to its vigorous growth and large flower panicles.

Polyploidy in Gypsophila is common, with species ranging from diploid (2n = 17) to hexaploid (2n = 51). The tetraploid condition of Gypsophila paniculata is associated with larger flower size and greater environmental tolerance.

## Morphology

Plants grow 60–120 cm tall with a spread of 60–90 cm, forming an upright, many-branched mound. The root system is a deep, thick taproot that can extend 2–3 m into the soil.

Stems are slender, branching, and glabrous to slightly pubescent. They are gray-green and somewhat wiry, creating the airy, open habit that distinguishes the species.

Leaves are opposite, lanceolate to linear, 3–8 cm long, and gray-green. They are sessile and relatively inconspicuous among the profuse flowering branches.

Flowers are tiny, 3–6 mm in diameter, with five white petals and a faint sweet fragrance. They are borne in large, open, much-branched panicles that create the characteristic misty effect.

Each flower has ten stamens and a superior ovary that develops into a small, globular capsule. The seeds are minute, kidney-shaped, and black, approximately 1 mm long.

The flowering period extends over 4–6 weeks from July through August. Double-flowered cultivars have a longer display period than single forms.

Plants produce a compound called saponin in their roots and tissues, which gives them a bitter taste and some resistance to herbivory. The saponins also contribute to the plant's use as a soil conditioner in some traditional agricultural systems.

## Distribution and Habitat

Gypsophila paniculata is native to a broad region spanning central Europe, Ukraine, Russia, the Caucasus, and central Asia. It occurs naturally on dry, grassy steppes, limestone hillsides, and sandy riverbanks.

The species is strongly calcicole, thriving in soils with pH 7.0–8.5 and high calcium content. It is absent from acid soils and peatlands throughout its native range.

It has become naturalized in [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], where it is considered invasive in parts of the Great Lakes region and the [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]]. Dense stands can displace native vegetation on sandy soils and dunes.

The species is listed as a noxious weed in California and several other western states. Control efforts focus on preventing [[clay-pellet-seed-dispersal]] from garden plantings and commercial flower operations.

In the Great Lakes dune systems, Gypsophila paniculata can produce over 13,000 seeds per plant per season. This prolific [[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]] drives its invasive potential in sandy, open habitats.

## Cultivation

In gardens, Gypsophila paniculata requires full sun and very well-drained, alkaline to neutral soil. It tolerates drought, poor soils, and exposed sites but resents wet conditions, especially in winter.

The deep taproot makes transplanting difficult, so plants should be placed in their permanent position when young. Established plants should not be disturbed once the root system has developed.

Key cultivars include 'Bristol Fairy' (double white, the commercial standard), 'Perfecta' (large double flowers), and 'Flamingo' (double pink). 'Bristol Fairy' accounts for the majority of commercial cut-flower production.

For cut-flower use, stems are harvested when approximately one-third of the buds have opened. Vase life is 7–10 days for fresh stems; dried stems retain their form indefinitely.

The dried flower industry relies heavily on Gypsophila paniculata, with stems air-dried or silica-dried for permanent arrangements. Properly dried flowers maintain their white color for several years.

Plants are hardy to USDA zones 3–9 and require good winter drainage to prevent crown rot. Heavy mulching is not recommended, as it can promote rot at the crown.
