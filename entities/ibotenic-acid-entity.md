# Ibotenic Acid

## Overview

Ibotenic acid (IBO) is a psychoactive amino acid and the primary toxic
constituent of Amanita muscaria (fly agaric) and related Amanita species.
It acts as a potent glutamate receptor agonist, mimicking the neurotransmitter
glutamate at both ionotropic (NMDA, AMPA, kainate) and metabotropic receptors.
Ibotenic acid is the biosynthetic precursor of muscimol, the primary
psychoactive compound formed by decarboxylation during drying or heating.

The compound was first isolated from Amanita muscaria by Japanese researchers
in 1964 and characterized by Eugster and colleagues. Its pharmacological
profile is distinct from classical psychedelics — effects arise from
excitotoxic glutamatergic stimulation rather than serotonergic 5-HT2A agonism.

## Chemical Identity

- **IUPAC name:** (2S)-2-Amino-2-(3-hydroxyisoxazol-5-yl)acetic acid
- **CAS Number:** 2552-55-8
- **Molecular formula:** C₅H₆N₂O₄
- **Molecular weight:** 158.11 g/mol
- **Structure:** α-Amino acid with an isoxazole ring substituted at C-5.
  Contains a chiral center at the α-carbon (S-configuration in natural
  product).
- **Appearance:** Colorless crystalline solid
- **Solubility:** Soluble in water, slightly soluble in ethanol, insoluble
  in nonpolar organic solvents
- **Melting point:** ~150°C (decomposes)
- **pKa:** α-COOH ~2.3, α-NH₃⁺ ~9.4

## Biosynthesis and Occurrence

Ibotenic acid is produced by fungi in Amanita section Amanita. Key source species:

- **Amanita muscaria** — Most abundant source; cap contains 0.03–0.15% IBO
  by dry weight
- **Amanita pantherina** — Comparable or higher concentrations
- **Amanita gemmata** — Moderate levels
- **Amanita regalis** — Significant ibotenic acid content

Biosynthesis begins with L-glutamic acid, which undergoes ring closure to form
the isoxazole moiety. Conversion to muscimol occurs via decarboxylation,
proceeding spontaneously during drying, cooking, or gastric acid exposure.

### Seasonal and Tissue Variation

Concentrations vary with developmental stage, tissue, and geography. Caps
contain 5–10× more ibotenic acid than stipes. Young fruiting bodies have higher
concentrations than mature specimens. Northern European populations tend to
accumulate more than North American populations. Environmental stress may
upregulate production.

## Pharmacology

### Receptor Activity

Ibotenic acid is a broad-spectrum glutamate receptor agonist:

- **NMDA receptors:** Agonist with EC₅₀ ≈ 2–5 µM. Activates NMDA-gated ion
  channels, causing Ca²⁺ influx and neuronal depolarization.
- **AMPA receptors:** Agonist with EC₅₀ ≈ 5–10 µM. Direct depolarization
  of AMPA receptor-expressing neurons.
- **Kainate receptors:** Weak partial agonist.
- **Metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluR):** Agonist at Group I (mGluR1/5)
  and Group II (mGluR2/3) subtypes.

This broad agonist profile distinguishes ibotenic acid from more selective
glutamate analogs and is responsible for its complex, often dysphoric
psychoactive effects.

### Effects Profile

- **Nausea and gastrointestinal distress** — the most consistently reported
  effect, often preceding psychoactive symptoms
- **Confusion, disorientation, and ataxia** — cerebellar dysfunction from
  excitotoxic stimulation
- **Visual distortions** — not classical hallucinations but altered size
  perception (Alice in Wonderland effect), blurred vision, and color shifts
- **Dissociation and depersonalization** — in higher doses
- **Muscle twitching and fasciculations** — peripheral excitotoxic effects
- **Clonic-tonic convulsions** — at near-toxic doses
- **Duration:** 4–8 hours; aftereffects may persist 12–24 hours

⚠️ **Ibotenic acid has a narrow therapeutic index.** Doses producing
psychoactive effects are close to those causing neurotoxicity. Unpleasant
physical effects (nausea, sweating, agitation) frequently dominate the
experience at psychoactive doses.

### Toxicology

- **Oral LD₅₀ (mice):** ~38 mg/kg
- **Oral LD₅₀ (rats):** ~129 mg/kg
- **Neurotoxicity:** Chronic or high-dose exposure causes excitotoxic neuronal
  damage, particularly in the hippocampus and cerebellum. Lesions similar to
  those produced by kainic acid.
- **Treatment:** Activated charcoal if ingestion is recent. Benzodiazepines
  for seizures. No specific antidote exists.

## Conversion to Muscimol

The decarboxylation of ibotenic acid to muscimol is the key transformation
that determines the psychoactive character of Amanita preparations:

- **Thermal decarboxylation:** Occurs above 70°C. Boiling 15–30 minutes
  converts 60–80% of ibotenic acid to muscimol.
- **Drying:** Air-drying 2–4 weeks achieves partial conversion (30–50%).
  Oven-drying at 70–80°C is more efficient.
- **In vivo:** Gastric acid facilitates decarboxylation, meaning some
  conversion occurs after ingestion regardless of preparation.

This conversion is central to traditional Amanita use — Siberian shamans
prepared Amanita by drying, and dried material produces a qualitatively
different (more pleasant) experience than fresh specimens.

## Legal Status

Ibotenic acid is unscheduled in most jurisdictions. In the United States, it
is not listed under the Controlled Substances Act. In the UK, the Psychoactive
Substances Act 2016 potentially covers its sale for human consumption.
Amanita muscaria itself is legal to possess, sell, and cultivate in most
countries, though several nations restrict sale of extracted ibotenic acid.
