# Pluteus nigroviridis

**Pluteus nigroviridis** is a species of Pluteus mushroom in the family Pluteaceae known to contain [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]]. The species name combines *nigro* (black) and *viridis* (green), referring to the dark cap coloration and the distinctive greenish-blue bruising reaction that develops on the stipe and cap when the fruiting body is damaged. It is considered one of the more reliably psychoactive species within the genus, alongside [[pluteus-salicinus]] and [[pluteus-glaucotinctus]].

## Taxonomy

*P. nigroviridis* belongs to the genus Pluteus, which is characterized by free gills and pinkish spore prints — traits that distinguish it from most other small mushrooms growing on wood. Within Pluteus, the species is placed in section *Celluloderma*, which is defined by a cap cuticle composed of inflated, spheropedunculate cells (a cellular cutis) rather than the filamentous trichoderm found in section *Pluteus* (which includes the common [[pluteus-cervinus]]). The family Pluteaceae is within the order Agaricales. Molecular phylogenetic studies have confirmed the monophyly of section *Celluloderma* and its distinction from the larger section *Pluteus*.

## Morphology

- **Cap**: 1.5 to 4 cm in diameter, conic to bell-shaped when young, becoming convex to broadly convex with a slight umbo at maturity. Surface is smooth to finely wrinkled or slightly viscid when moist. Color is distinctly dark — ranging from dark olive-brown to nearly black, especially at the center, with a somewhat lighter margin. The cap is hygrophanous and may fade to a grayish-olive when dry. Bruising or handling often produces a conspicuous greenish-blue to bluish-green discoloration, one of the most distinctive field characters.
- **Gills**: Free from the stipe, close to moderately spaced. Initially white, becoming pink with maturity, eventually producing a salmon-pink spore print. Gill edges are white and finely fringed or entire.
- **Stipe**: 3 to 6 cm long, 2 to 4 mm thick, cylindrical and equal. Surface is smooth, silky, and white to pale gray. Bruising produces a vivid greenish-blue to teal discoloration, often more intense than in other psilocybin-containing Pluteus species. The bluing typically begins at the base and progresses upward. The stipe is hollow or stuffed.
- **Flesh**: Thin and white in the cap, pale in the stipe. Odor is mild, sometimes slightly farinaceous or radish-like. Taste is not distinctive or mildly mealy.
- **Spore print**: Salmon-pink to flesh-pink. Spores are smooth, broadly ellipsoid to subglobose, measuring approximately 6 to 8 by 5 to 7 µm.
- **Microscopic features**: The pileipellis (cap cuticle) is a cutis of inflated, ellipsoid to spheropedunculate cells — the hallmark of section *Celluloderma*. Cheilocystidia are abundant, clavate to utriform with narrow necks, sometimes with apical excrescences. Pleurocystidia are present but less numerous, similar in morphology to the cheilocystidia. Pileocystidia are cylindrical to clavate with brown intracellular pigment.

## Distribution and Habitat

*P. nigroviridis* has been primarily documented in Europe, with additional reports from North America. It is a saprotrophic species, growing exclusively on dead and decaying hardwood. Preferred substrates include fallen logs, stumps, and branches of oak (*Quercus*), beech (*Fagus*), birch (*Betula*), [[maple-syrup-production]] (*Acer*), and other deciduous trees. It is typically found in mature deciduous forests and woodlands where large woody debris has accumulated. Fruiting occurs from early summer through late autumn, peaking during warm, humid periods. The species tends to fruit on wood that has been decomposing for several months to a few years — neither freshly fallen nor heavily decayed.

## Psychoactive Constituents

*P. nigroviridis* contains both [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]], confirmed through analytical methods including TLC, HPLC, and GC-MS. Among the psilocybin-containing Pluteus species, *P. nigroviridis* is reported to have moderate to relatively high concentrations, potentially comparable to mid-range Psilocybe species. The vivid greenish-blue bluing reaction is a strong visual indicator of psilocybin presence, resulting from the enzymatic oxidation of psilocin to blue-colored quinoid compounds.

Alkaloid concentrations are influenced by substrate composition, moisture levels, temperature, and the degree of wood decomposition. Collections from nutrient-rich hardwood substrates with moderate moisture tend to show higher alkaloid levels. The species may also contain [[baeocystin]], [[norbaeocystin]], and possibly trace amounts of other indole derivatives.

## Comparative Potency

Among the psilocybin-containing Pluteus species, potency varies considerably. *P. nigroviridis* is generally considered one of the more potent members of the genus, though it still falls below the most potent Psilocybe species such as [[psilocybe-azurescens]] or [[psilocybe-cyanescens]]. Comparative analyses have suggested the following approximate potency ranking within Pluteus: *P. nigroviridis* ≥ *P. salicinus* > *P. glaucotinctus* > *P. americanus*. However, these rankings are based on limited sample sizes and considerable individual variation exists. The relatively small size of Pluteus fruiting bodies means that even the more potent species require collection of multiple specimens to achieve a psychoactive dose, making them less practical for this purpose than larger, more abundant psilocybin mushrooms.

## Identification and Safety

The combination of dark cap coloration, free pink gills, growth on hardwood, and — most importantly — vivid greenish-blue bruising provides a reasonable field profile for *P. nigroviridis*. However, the genus Pluteus contains many species that are similar in general appearance, and microscopic examination is essential for confident identification. The bluing reaction, while a useful indicator, is not unique to psilocybin-containing species and can occasionally appear in non-psychoactive mushrooms due to other oxidative reactions.

No Pluteus species are known to be lethally toxic, and the genus does not contain the dangerous amatoxins found in Amanita and Galerina species. However, misidentification with non-psychoactive species would result in consuming material of unknown composition. As with all wild mushrooms, proper identification by an experienced mycologist is essential before any consideration of consumption.

## Seasonal and Ecological Patterns

*P. nigroviridis* typically fruits from early summer through late autumn (June to November in the Northern Hemisphere), with peak fruiting during warm, humid periods in late summer. The species shows a preference for hardwood logs and stumps that have been decomposing for approximately six months to three years, occupying a middle successional position among wood-inhabiting fungi. It is most commonly found on fallen trunks and large branches that retain sufficient moisture content but have begun to soften from white-rot decomposition. The species appears to be more abundant in mature, structurally complex forests with significant accumulations of coarse woody debris, as these habitats provide the greatest diversity of substrate conditions suitable for colonization. Forest management practices that remove dead wood can significantly reduce available habitat for *P. nigroviridis* and other wood-decay Pluteus species.

## Molecular Phylogeny

Molecular phylogenetic studies using ITS and LSU rDNA sequences have confirmed the placement of *P. nigroviridis* within section *Celluloderma* of the genus Pluteus. This section is monophyletic and well-supported by both molecular data and the shared morphological character of the inflated-cell cap cuticle. Within section *Celluloderma*, *P. nigroviridis* clusters with other dark-capped, bluing species including [[pluteus-glaucotinctus]] and [[pluteus-salicinus]], forming a subgroup of psilocybin-containing species. The phylogenetic distribution of psilocybin within Pluteus is scattered across multiple clades, suggesting that the biosynthetic capacity for psilocybin production may have evolved independently multiple times within the genus, or alternatively, may have been present in a common ancestor and subsequently lost in most lineages.

## Cultivation

There are no established cultivation protocols specifically developed for *P. nigroviridis*, and the species is not cultivated commercially or by hobbyists. Like other wood-inhabiting Pluteus species, it would likely require hardwood-based substrates and conditions similar to those used for other wood-decay fungi. The limited interest in cultivating this species reflects both its relative rarity in the wild and the availability of more easily cultivated psilocybin-containing species. Any attempt at cultivation would require obtaining a fresh specimen for tissue culture, as spore germination in Pluteus species can be unreliable without specific environmental triggers.

## Similar Species

[[pluteus-salicinus]] is the most commonly encountered psilocybin-containing Pluteus and shares the bluing reaction, but has a more silvery-gray cap and is generally found on willow and alder. [[pluteus-glaucotinctus]] is similar but typically has a lighter, more brownish cap with less vivid bluing. [[pluteus-americanus]] is a North American counterpart. Non-psychoactive species such as [[pluteus-cervinus]] are much larger and more robust, with a distinctly darker brown cap but no bluing reaction. *Pluteus atromarginatus* has dark-edged gills but is not known to contain psilocybin.

## Legal Status

As a confirmed psilocybin-containing species, *P. nigroviridis* is subject to the same legal restrictions as other psychoactive mushrooms in most jurisdictions. In the United States, it is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance. European countries generally prohibit the possession and sale of psilocybin-containing fungi under their respective drug control legislation. The species' rarity and the difficulty of distinguishing it from non-psychoactive Pluteus species provide some practical anonymity, though this does not constitute a legal defense.
