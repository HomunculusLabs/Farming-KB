## Overview

Ocimum gratissimum, commonly known as African basil, clove basil, or fever plant, is a
perennial aromatic herb in the family Lamiaceae. It is native to Africa, Madagascar,
southern Asia, and the Bismarck Archipelago, but has naturalized widely across tropical
and subtropical regions.

The species is distinguished by its intensely clove-scented leaves, attributable to
high concentrations of eugenol in its [[blesching-cannabis-gastrointestinal-disorders]].

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Plantae — **Family:** Lamiaceae — **Genus:** Ocimum

Ocimum gratissimum L. was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. Several infraspecific
taxa have been recognized, with some authors treating the species as part of the
Ocimum gratissimum complex alongside O. viride and O. suave.

The plant grows as an erect, woody-based perennial reaching 1–3 meters in height.

Its stems are square in cross-section (typical of Lamiaceae), branching freely from
the base. Leaves are ovate to lanceolate, 5–13 cm long and 3–7 cm wide, with
serrate margins and a pubescent underside.

Inflorescences are terminal or axillary racemes bearing small white to pale green
flowers. The calyx is campanulate with five equal teeth. Corollas are bilabiate,
approximately 6 mm long, and white or slightly tinged with purple.

## Distribution and Habitat

O. gratissimum is pantropical in distribution, occurring throughout sub-Saharan
Africa, South and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]]. It thrives
in a range of ecological zones from coastal savanna to forest margins.

The species prefers well-drained soils with moderate fertility and responds well
to annual rainfall of 800–1,500 mm. It grows readily on disturbed ground, road
verges, and cultivated fields, often behaving as a ruderal weed.

Elevation range extends from sea level to approximately 1,500 meters. It tolerates
partial shade but produces maximum essential oil yield under full sun exposure.

## Essential Oil Composition

The essential oil of O. gratissimum is one of its most commercially significant
features. Chemotypic variation is considerable across geographic populations.

The predominant chemotype, found in West Africa, is eugenol-rich (50–80% eugenol).
Other reported chemotypes include thymol-dominant (Nigeria), geraniol-dominant
(Brazil), and citral-dominant (India) varieties.

Additional constituents include β-caryophyllene, α-humulene, 1,8-cineole,
linalool, and methyl eugenol. The thymol chemotype also contains significant
γ-terpinene and p-cymene.
