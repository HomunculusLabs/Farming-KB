Sargassum muticum, commonly known as wireweed or Japanese wireweed, is a large brown alga in the family Sargassaceae. Native to the Northwest Pacific, it has become one of the most successful invasive marine macrophytes globally, establishing populations across the Northern Hemisphere since the mid-20th century.

The species forms dense canopy-forming stands in the intertidal and shallow subtidal zones. Its bushy, highly branched thallus can reach 3–16 m in length, with numerous lateral branches bearing small leaf-like blades and spherical pneumatocysts.

S. muticum was first recorded outside its native range in the 1940s and has since spread to Europe, [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]]. Its introduction is attributed to commercial shipping and [[lichens-primary-production-soil-formation-dighton]] and biodiversity.

Despite its negative ecological impact, the species provides structural habitat for fish and invertebrates. Juvenile fish and crustaceans utilize Sargassum beds as nursery areas, benefiting from the physical complexity of the thallus.

Decomposition of dislodged Sargassum biomass generates large quantities of detritus. This organic input can alter sediment chemistry and fuel microbial decomposition that depletes near-bottom [[laminaria-digitata]]
