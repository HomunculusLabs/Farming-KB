## Overview

Pilocarpine is a parasympathomimetic alkaloid derived from the leaves of Pilocarpus plants (jaborandi), historically investigated as a potential antidote for [[amanita]] [[amanita-toxins-and-mushroom-poisoning]]. Its use as a counter-agent to amanitine toxicity was explored by M. Sicard in his 1883 experiments on dogs poisoned with the [[amanita-alkaloid-medicinal-uses-pilocarpine-treatment]].

## Chemical Properties

- **Classification**: Imidazole alkaloid
- **Source**: Pilocarpus jaborandi and related Rutaceae species
- **Pharmacological action**: Parasympathomimetic — stimulates muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, promoting sweating (diaphoresis), salivation, and lacrimation
- **Historical form used**: Nitrate of pilocarpine (for subcutaneous injection)

## Historical Use in Mushroom Poisoning

### Sicard"s Experiments (c. 1883)

M. Sicard tested pilocarpine as an antidote after poisoning dogs with the Amanita alkaloid:

1. **First injection**: 2 milligrammes of nitrate of pilocarpine, subcutaneously in the middle of the back
2. **Second injection** (30 minutes later): Same dose, a little to the left of the heart
3. **Supportive measures**: Straw friction to encourage the sudorific response; 5 grammes of nitrate of potash dissolved in 100 grammes of marshmallow solution administered orally
4. **Result**: A feeble return of heat at the skin surface; pupils became sensitive and contracted, showing a change in symptoms. The dog never returned to normal condition, but the progress of the poison ceased, and he at least lived.

### Mechanism of Action

Pilocarpine's theoretical basis as an antidote rested on its powerful sudorific (sweat-inducing) properties. The reasoning was that if the amanitine alkaloid had been absorbed into the bloodstream, promoting massive perspiration might help eliminate the toxin through the skin.

## Significance to Mycology

Pilocarpine represents one of several alkaloids investigated in the 19th century as potential antidotes to mushroom poisoning, alongside atropine, daturine, hyoscyamine, and nicotine. While pilocarpine showed partial efficacy in Sicard's animal experiments, it did not achieve full recovery and was ultimately superseded by atropine as the treatment of choice.

## Modern Context

Pilocarpine remains in clinical use today for treatment of glaucoma, management of xerostomia following radiation therapy, and diagnostic sweat testing for cystic fibrosis. Its parasympathomimetic properties are well-characterized in modern pharmacology, though it is no longer used in the treatment of mushroom poisoning.

## Comparison with Other Investigated Antidotes

Pilocarpine was one of several alkaloids investigated in the 19th century as potential antidotes to mushroom poisoning. The [[fukuoka-relative-merits-natural-scientific-agriculture]] of each were debated in the medical literature:

- **Atropine** (from [[atropa-belladonna]]): Ultimately proved most effective; competitive muscarinic receptor antagonist
- **Pilocarpine** (from Pilocarpus): Partial efficacy in animal models; parasympathomimetic mechanism
- **Daturine** (from [[datura-stramonium]]): Clinical success reported by Dr. T. F. Wood
- **Hyoscyamine** (from [[hyoscyamus-niger]]): Also successful in clinical use
- **Stramonium** (Datura preparations): Advocated by Professor Schiff of Italy
- **Tannin**: Attempted by Letellier to precipitate the poison; of limited efficacy
- **Nicotine**: Suggested by Palmer for non-tobacco users

The diversity of approaches reflects the desperate need for effective treatments and the limited understanding of amatoxin pharmacology at the time.

## Why Pilocarpine Was Superseded

Pilocarpine's partial efficacy can be understood through modern pharmacology. While pilocarpine promotes sweating and may accelerate toxin elimination through the skin, it does not directly counteract the cellular mechanism of [[bloomfield-amatoxin-poisoning-mechanism]]. Amatoxins inhibit RNA polymerase II, causing progressive cell death in the liver and kidneys — a mechanism unaffected by cholinergic stimulation.

Atropine, by contrast, effectively counteracts the muscarinic symptoms (the cholinergic crisis) that contribute to the acute phase of poisoning, buying time for supportive care and potential recovery.

## Legacy

Pilocarpine's investigation by Sicard represents an important chapter in the history of mycological toxicology, demonstrating the systematic exploration of potential antidotes that characterized late 19th-century pharmacology.

## Botanical Source and Ethnopharmacology

Pilocarpine is extracted from the leaves of Pilocarpus species (jaborandi), shrubs and small trees native to [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], particularly Brazil. Indigenous peoples of the Amazon region traditionally used jaborandi leaves to induce sweating, and the plant was introduced to European pharmacopoeia in the 1870s.

The introduction of pilocarpine into European medicine coincided with the period when mycologists were actively seeking antidotes for mushroom poisoning, making it a logical candidate for investigation. Its powerful sudorific (sweat-inducing) properties suggested a mechanism for eliminating absorbed toxins through the skin — a theoretical approach that was reasonable given the limited understanding of amatoxin pharmacology at the time.

## Chemical Structure and Pharmacology

Pilocarpine is an imidazole alkaloid (molecular formula C11H16N2O2) that acts as a non-selective muscarinic acetylcholine receptor agonist. Unlike the solanaceous alkaloids (which are muscarinic antagonists), pilocarpine stimulates the same receptors that muscarine overactivates. This pharmacological paradox — using a parasympathomimetic to treat what appeared to be parasympathomimetic poisoning — reflects the incomplete 19th-century understanding of the different toxic mechanisms at play in [[amanita-poisoning-identification]].

- **Drug class**: Parasympathomimetic / Cholinergic agonist
- **Source plant**: Pilocarpus jaborandi
- **Historical relevance**: Investigated antidote for Amanita poisoning
