## Poi

## Description

Poi is a traditional fermented staple food of Hawaii and Polynesia made from taro ([[colocasia-esculenta]]) tubers. The tubers are cooked, pounded into a paste, and allowed to ferment through [[lactic-acid-bacteria]] activity, producing a slightly sour, purple-grey paste that serves as the primary carbohydrate staple in traditional Hawaiian diet. Mollison describes poi in the context of the rich polyculture systems developed around traditional Hawaiian taro terrace cultivation.

## Classification

- **Category:** Fermented staple food
- **Primary ingredient:** Taro (Colocasia esculenta) tubers
- **Origin:** Hawaii and Polynesia
- **Processing method:** Cooking, mashing, and lactic acid fermentation
- **Dietary role:** Primary carbohydrate staple

## Key Facts

- Made from specially selected taro varieties bred for poi production
- Hawaiian culture recognized approximately 1,100 taro varieties, with perhaps 100 still preserved
- Special taro varieties exist for different uses: boiling, baking, fermenting to poi, and cooking as green leaf spinach (luau taro)
- All taro must be cooked before consumption due to calcium oxalate crystals (raphides) that cause severe irritation
- Poi was the dietary staple of Hawaii alongside breadfruit and rice in other parts of Polynesia
- Fermentation process produces beneficial lactic acid bacteria, making poi a probiotic food

## Production Process

1. Harvest taro tubers (typically at 1 kg market size after 8–15 months growth)
2. Cook tubers thoroughly to neutralize oxalic acid crystals
3. Pound cooked tubers into a smooth paste using traditional stone pounders
4. Allow paste to ferment naturally through ambient lactic acid bacteria
5. Fermentation progresses from sweet (fresh) to increasingly sour over several days

## Role in Traditional Hawaiian Agriculture

Poi production was embedded within a complex polyculture system that included:
- Taro terraces with integrated fish, prawn, and crayfish culture
- Upper terraces where kukui (Aleurites) tree stems were stood to rot, producing edible ear fungus
- Canal and wet terrace systems connecting multiple production zones
- Accessory margin crops: ti (Cordyline), papaya, banana, coconut, sugar cane
- Traditional livestock: pigs and ducks, with pens positioned over feeder canals for nutrient cycling

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Poi represents an exemplary model of food preservation and staple crop processing in [[permaculture]] systems:

1. **Zero-waste processing:** Every part of the taro plant is utilized — tubers for poi, leaves for luau (spinach), stems for propagation
2. **Fermentation as preservation:** Lactic acid fermentation extends shelf life without refrigeration or energy input
3. **Probiotic nutrition:** Fermented poi delivers beneficial gut bacteria alongside caloric nutrition
4. **Cultural keystone:** Poi production anchored the entire Hawaiian agricultural system, providing the caloric base that made complex polyculture economically viable
5. **Crop diversification:** The existence of 1,100 taro varieties selected for different end-uses demonstrates the depth of traditional plant breeding

Mollison presents the taro-poi system as evidence that tropical polycultures can exceed the productivity of monocultures while maintaining long-term sustainability — a core permaculture argument.
