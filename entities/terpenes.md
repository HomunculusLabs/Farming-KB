## Description

Terpenes (also called terpenoids when modified with functional groups) are a large and diverse class of organic compounds produced by plants, particularly conifers and aromatic species. In the context of soil science and organic gardening, terpenes are significant because they are **volatile chemicals toxic to many plants** — they act as natural herbicides and antimicrobial agents. Fresh pine needles and cedar chips contain high levels of terpenes that can inhibit seed germination, damage plant tissues, and suppress microbial activity. Understanding terpene content is essential for proper mulch selection and application.

## Classification

- **Type**: Organic chemical compounds (hydrocarbons)
- **Biosynthetic class**: Isoprenoids (built from isoprene units, C₅H₈)
- **Subcategories**: Monoterpenes (C₁₀), sesquiterpenes (C₁₅), diterpenes (C₂₀), triterpenes (C₃₀)
- **Occurrence**: Abundant in conifer resins, citrus, cannabis, mint, cedar, and many aromatic plants

## Key Facts

### Phytotoxicity in Mulch
- **Fresh pine needles** contain terpenes that are toxic to many plants — they must be **aged** before use as mulch to allow terpene volatilization and degradation.
- **Cedar chips** contain particularly high levels of terpenes and should be **avoided as mulch** for most garden applications.
- The volatile nature of terpenes means they dissipate over time through evaporation and microbial decomposition.
- Aged pine needles (brown, not green) have significantly reduced terpene content and are safe for mulch use.

### Mechanism of Phytotoxicity
- Terpenes disrupt cell membrane integrity in plant tissues.
- Volatile terpenes can inhibit seed germination in nearby plants (allelopathy).
- Some terpenes have antimicrobial properties that can suppress beneficial soil microorganisms.
- These properties evolved as plant defense mechanisms against herbivores, pathogens, and competing vegetation.

### Natural Occurrence and Ecological Function
- Plants produce terpenes as defense compounds against insect herbivores and fungal pathogens.
- Conifers secrete terpene-rich resins to seal wounds and prevent infection.
- Many terpenes serve as attractants for pollinators when produced in flowers (e.g., linalool, limonene).
- Some terpenes act as signaling compounds between plants and beneficial insects.

### Common Terpenes and Sources
| Terpene | Primary Source | Properties |
|---------|---------------|------------|
| α-Pinene | Pine, cedar, rosemary | Pine aroma, antimicrobial, bronchodilator |
| β-Pinene | Pine, parsley, dill | Woody aroma, anti-inflammatory |
| Limonene | Citrus, juniper, mint | Citrus aroma, antifungal, insecticidal |
| Myrcene | Hops, mango, cannabis | Earthy aroma, sedative, antimicrobial |
| Linalool | Lavender, birch, cannabis | Floral aroma, anxiolytic, antifungal |
| Caryophyllene | Black pepper, cloves, cannabis | Peppery aroma, anti-inflammatory |

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

1. **Mulch safety**: Fresh pine and cedar mulches release terpenes that can damage or kill sensitive crop plants — always age these materials before garden use.
2. **Allelopathic effects**: Terpene-producing plants (e.g., black walnut, certain eucalyptus) can inhibit growth of nearby crops through terpene exudation into soil.
3. **Antimicrobial properties**: Terpene-rich environments can suppress both beneficial and pathogenic soil microorganisms, affecting soil food web dynamics.
4. **Mushroom cultivation**: Some mushroom species (e.g., *Pleurotus* / oyster mushrooms) produce terpenes as secondary metabolites, and terpenes are key flavor/aroma compounds in many edible mushrooms.
5. **Cannabis cultivation**: Terpenes are major quality determinants in cannabis, contributing to aroma, flavor, and therapeutic effects. Growing conditions affect terpene profiles significantly.
6. **Substrate preparation**: Conifer-derived substrates (pine shavings, cedar chips) may release terpenes inhibitory to mushroom mycelium — aging or heat treatment may be necessary.

## Practical Management

- **Age pine needles** for 6+ months before using as mulch to allow terpenes to dissipate.
- **Avoid cedar chips** entirely as mulch in food-growing areas.
- **Compost high-terpene materials** separately to allow microbial degradation before garden application.
- **Monitor** for signs of terpene phytotoxicity: stunted growth, chlorosis, poor germination near fresh conifer mulch.
