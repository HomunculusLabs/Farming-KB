## Overview

Gentiana clausa, commonly called the closed gentian or bottle gentian, is a perennial herb native to [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]]. It is notable for its deep blue, bottle-shaped flowers that never fully open, relying exclusively on bumblebees for pollination.

The species grows 30–70 cm tall in moist meadows, floodplains, and wetland margins across the eastern United States and southeastern Canada. It is one of the most widespread closed gentians in North America.

Gentiana clausa is closely related to [[gentiana-andrewsii]], and the two species are frequently confused. The primary distinction is the relative length of the calyx lobes compared to the corolla pleats.

The closed flower mechanism is an adaptation to ensure only strong bumblebees can access the nectar. This selective pollination strategy reduces nectar robbery by less effective pollinators.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Clade: Angiosperms
- Clade: Eudicots
- Clade: Asterids
- Order: Gentianales
- Family: Gentianaceae
- Genus: Gentiana
- Species: G. clausa Raf.

The species was described by Constantine Samuel Rafinesque in 1837. It belongs to Gentiana section Pneumonanthe, subsection Ochroleucae.

Gentiana clausa is distinguished from G. andrewsii by calyx lobes that are equal to or shorter than the corolla pleats. In G. andrewsii, the calyx lobes exceed the corolla pleats.

Plants produce erect, unbranched stems with opposite, sessile leaves. Leaves are lanceolate to ovate, 5–12 cm long, with prominent parallel veins and smooth margins.

Flowers are borne in dense terminal clusters, sometimes with additional clusters at upper leaf axils. The corolla is deep blue to violet-blue, 2.5–4 cm long, with the lobes remaining appressed and never spreading.

The corolla pleats between the lobes are prominent and darker blue than the surrounding tissue. Inside the corolla tube, nectar glands produce concentrated sugar solution accessible only through forceful entry.

The fruit is an ellipsoid capsule containing numerous small, winged seeds. Seeds are light brown, approximately 1 mm long, and dispersed by wind and water.

Chromosome number is 2n = 26, consistent with other members of section Pneumonanthe. Hybridization with G. andrewsii produces intermediates with partially opening corollas.

## Distribution and Habitat

Gentiana clausa ranges from Nova Scotia and Quebec westward to Minnesota, and southward to Georgia and Alabama. It is most common in the Great Lakes region and northeastern states.

The species occupies a variety of wet to mesic habitats. These include wet meadows, prairie swales, floodplain forests, stream margins, and roadside ditches with consistent moisture.

Unlike many gentians, G. clausa tolerates a range of soil pH from acidic to mildly alkaline. It is found on both organic peat soils and mineral loams.

Populations are generally stable across the core of the range. However, peripheral populations in the southeastern states are often small and vulnerable to habitat loss.

The species benefits from moderate disturbance that maintains open conditions. Mowing in late fall or winter can prevent woody encroachment in meadow habitats.

In the Great Lakes region, it is often associated with fen and sedge meadow communities. These calcium-influenced wetlands provide ideal moisture and nutrient conditions.

## Ecology

Flowering occurs from August to October, peaking in September. The closed flowers produce a strong floral scent that attracts bumblebees from considerable distance.

Only large bumblebees, primarily queens of Bombus species, are strong enough to force open the corolla lobes. Smaller insects may chew holes in the corolla base to access nectar without pollinating.

Gentiana clausa is a long-lived perennial with individual plants surviving 15–30+ years. It produces a deep taproot that stores energy for early spring growth.

Vegetative reproduction occurs occasionally through short rhizomes, forming small clonal clusters. However, [[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]] is the primary means of population expansion.

The species co-occurs with other late-blooming wetland plants including goldenrods, asters, and turtlehead. This co-flowering community provides sequential resources for migrating bumblebees.

[[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] with arbuscular fungi enhance nutrient absorption from wetland soils. The fungal partners are especially important for phosphorus uptake in organic peat substrates.

## Cultivation

Gentiana clausa is one of the easier [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] gentians to cultivate. It prefers moist to wet soils in full sun to partial shade.

Seeds require 60–90 days of cold stratification at 4°C for germination. Surface sow in fall for natural stratification, or refrigerate moist seed in sealed bags before spring sowing.

Germination is often irregular, with some seeds germinating in the second year. Transplant seedlings when they develop 3–4 true leaves.

Established plants are relatively low-maintenance. Consistent moisture is the most critical requirement, with drought causing rapid decline.
