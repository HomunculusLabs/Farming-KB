# Chaetomium globosum

**Chaetomium globosum** is a saprotrophic ascomycete fungus in the order
Sordariales, family Chaetomiaceae. It is a common soil fungus and
cellulose decomposer that plays important roles in organic matter
decomposition, soil food webs, and interactions with soil fauna,
particularly earthworms. The genus Chaetomium contains numerous
cellulolytic species that are important decomposers of plant litter and
cellulosic materials in soil environments.

## Taxonomy and Classification

Chaetomium globosum produces characteristic perithecia (flask-shaped
fruiting bodies) covered with specialized hairs, containing asci with
eight ascospores each. The species is widely distributed in soils
worldwide and is frequently isolated from decaying plant material,
straw, compost, and cellulose-rich substrates.

## Earthworm Feeding Preferences

Moody et al. (1995) investigated the feeding preferences of three
earthworm species (Lumbricus terrestris, Aporrectodea longa, and
Allolobophora chlorotica) when offered straw baits colonized by
different saprotrophic fungal species. Chaetomium globosum showed
moderate palatability: L. terrestris took 9.2 baits (4th of 6
species), A. longa took 4.4 (5th), and A. chlorotica took only 0.6
(last). The broadly similar preferences among earthworm species
combined with differential palatability of fungal species indicate that
earthworms are selective fungivores that can differentially affect
saprotrophic fungal community composition through grazing.

## Spore Survival Through Earthworm Gut

Moody et al. (1996) demonstrated species-specific patterns of fungal
spore viability after passage through earthworm guts. Chaetomium
globosum showed 80.5% survival through Lumbricus terrestris (33 of 41
spores viable) and a remarkable 155% increase through Aporrectodea
longa (76 of 49 spores viable), suggesting that earthworm gut fluids
stimulate spore germination. In contrast, Fusarium lateritium failed
to survive passage through either species, and Agrocybe temulenta also
failed through Lumbricus. The decline in spore germination was caused
by intestinal fluid action, not soil abrasion; abrasion actually
stimulated germination. This survival and enhanced germination suggests
adaptation to faunal dispersal, with implications for how earthworm
activity structures fungal communities.

## Nematode Food Resource

Ruess and Dighton (1996) showed that Chaetomium globosum supported
populations of 1,427 nematodes per plate, with 99% being Aphelenchoides
saprophilus. This made it the most productive saprotrophic fungus
tested, far exceeding Agrocybe gibberosa (349 nematodes) and Mucor
heimalis (24). While its capacity was lower than the best
ectomycorrhizal fungi (Lactarius rufus at 25,890), C. globosum was
the premier saprotrophic nematode food source. The near-complete
dominance of A. saprophilus indicates particularly suitable conditions
for this nematode species.

## Fungal Dispersal by Earthworms

Earthworms transport propagules of saprotrophic, mycorrhizal, and
pathogenic fungi. Gange (1993) showed that earthworm feeding
concentrates on dead and dying root material, resulting in ingestion of
large amounts of arbuscular mycorrhizal spores. Viable spores
deposited in casts provide local inoculum for establishing plant
species. Doube et al. (1994a,b; 1995) further showed that Aporrectodea
species assist plant roots in colonization by biocontrol bacteria
antagonistic to root pathogenic fungi.

## Soil Fertility Enhancement

Earthworm casts are localized sites for elevated numbers of arbuscular
mycorrhizal spores and soil nutrients. Brussard et al. (1993) showed
that in alley-cropping agroecosystems, earthworm casts had
significantly higher contents of major plant nutrients than inter-row
soil. Casts contained 401 mg/g nitrogen, 8 mg/g phosphorus, 42 mg/g
potassium, 191 mg/g calcium, and 23 mg/g magnesium, compared to
inter-row soil with only 72 mg/g N, 1.4 mg/g P, 7.5 mg/g K, 27 mg/g
Ca, and 3 mg/g Mg. The combined influence of tree-derived leaf litter,
earthworm consumption, and fungal decomposition including Chaetomium
globosum significantly improves soil fertility in both agricultural and
natural ecosystems.

## Ecological Significance

Chaetomium globosum occupies an important position in soil food webs,
serving as a link between primary decomposition and secondary
production by soil fauna. As a cellulolytic decomposer, it converts
complex organic matter into fungal biomass that feeds fungivorous
nematodes, earthworms, and other soil invertebrates, channeling carbon
and nutrients from the decomposer pathway into the soil faunal food
web. Its ability to survive and benefit from earthworm gut passage
gives it a dispersal advantage, and its role in the earthworm-cast
nutrient enhancement cycle contributes to overall soil fertility.
