# Coniine

**Coniine** (systematically 2-propylpiperidine) is a highly toxic piperidine alkaloid found primarily in poison hemlock (*Conium maculatum*). It is one of the most infamous plant-derived neurotoxins in Western history, having been used to execute the Greek philosopher Socrates in 399 BCE. Coniine acts as a non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocker by antagonizing nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, ultimately causing death by respiratory paralysis while the victim remains conscious.

## Chemical Identity

- **IUPAC name:** 2-Propylpiperidine
- **CAS number:** 322-51-4
- **Molecular formula:** C₈H₁₇N
- **Molecular weight:** 127.23 g/mol
- **SMILES:** CCCC1CCCCN1
- **Structure:** A piperidine ring with a propyl substituent at the 2-position. The naturally occurring (S)-enantiomer is the pharmacologically active form.
- **Appearance:** Colorless to pale yellow oily liquid at room temperature.
- **Odor:** Characteristic mousy or mouse-like odor (particularly when freshly distilled from plant material).
- **Solubility:** Slightly soluble in water; freely soluble in ethanol, ether, chloroform, and fixed oils.
- **Boiling point:** 166–167°C at atmospheric pressure.

## Biosynthesis

Coniine is biosynthesized in *Conium maculatum* via the polyamine pathway:

1. **L-lysine** is decarboxylated by lysine decarboxylase to form **cadaverine** (1,5-diaminopentane).
2. Cadaverine undergoes oxidative deamination to form **5-aminopentanal**.
3. Intramolecular imine formation yields **Δ¹-piperideine**.
4. A second molecule of cadaverine (or a derivative) provides a C₃ unit that is incorporated via a [3+3] condensation mechanism.
5. Subsequent reduction yields (S)-coniine.

The biosynthetic pathway shares intermediates with nicotine biosynthesis in tobacco, reflecting an evolutionary relationship between the two piperidine alkaloids.

## Occurrence

### Primary Source: Conium maculatum

- **Poison hemlock** (*Conium maculatum*, Apiaceae) is the principal natural source of coniine, containing 0.5–1.0% total alkaloids in dried foliage, with concentrations highest in green fruit (up to 3.5%).
- **Tissue distribution (highest to lowest):** Seeds/fruit > young leaves > stems > roots. Alkaloid concentration peaks during flowering and early seed set.
- **Seasonal variation:** Alkaloid content is highest in spring growth and lowest in senescent tissue.
- **Geographic distribution:** Native to Europe and North Africa; naturalized in [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], South America, Australia, and New Zealand. Common in meadows, roadside ditches, stream banks, and disturbed ground.

### Secondary Sources

- **Aethusa cynapium** (fool's parsley) — contains small quantities of coniine-related alkaloids.
- **Cicuta virosa** (water hemlock) — contains cicutoxin (a different toxin), but is often confused with poison hemlock. *Cicuta* does **not** contain coniine.

## Mechanism of Toxicity

Coniine is a **competitive antagonist at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs)** at the neuromuscular junction:

1. **Binding:** Coniine binds to the α-subunit of the nicotinic receptor, competing with acetylcholine (ACh).
2. **Blockade:** Unlike depolarizing agents (e.g., succinylcholine), coniine produces a **non-depolarizing block** — the receptor is occupied but not activated, preventing ion channel opening.
3. **Failure of transmission:** Motor end-plate potentials fail to reach threshold, and action potentials cannot propagate to muscle fibers.
4. **Ascending paralysis:** Small, rapidly firing muscles are affected first (extraocular → neck → limbs → diaphragm). The diaphragm is typically the last muscle to fail.
5. **Respiratory failure:** Paralysis of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles causes asphyxia. The victim remains fully conscious until hypoxia causes loss of consciousness.

**LD₅₀ values:**
- **Oral (human, estimated):** ~150 mg (approximately 5–8 g of fresh hemlock leaf or 10–15 seeds)
- **Oral (rat):** 100–200 mg/kg
- **Intravenous (mouse):** 6–8 mg/kg

## Clinical Presentation

### Symptoms (Onset 30–60 minutes after ingestion)

- **Early (30–90 min):** Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, salivation, burning sensation in mouth and throat, blurred vision, mydriasis (dilated pupils), tachycardia.
- **Progressive (1–3 hours):** Progressive ascending muscular weakness beginning in the lower extremities. Ptosis (drooping eyelids), dysarthria (slurred speech), dysphagia (difficulty swallowing). Loss of deep tendon reflexes. Sensorium remains intact — the patient is awake and aware.
- **Late (3–6 hours):** Respiratory muscle paralysis → cyanosis → hypoxic cardiac arrest. Seizures may occur secondary to hypoxia. Death, if it occurs, is typically from respiratory failure.

### Fatal Dose and Survival

- **Adult fatal dose:** Approximately 150–300 mg of coniine (equivalent to 6–8 fresh leaves or 15–20 seeds). However, significant individual variation exists, and survival has been reported after ingestion of substantially larger quantities.
- **Pediatric fatal dose:** Estimated 15–30 mg (a few leaves or 3–4 seeds). Children are at disproportionate risk due to lower body weight.
- **Survival factor:** If respiratory support is maintained (mechanical ventilation) until the toxin is metabolized and eliminated (typically 24–72 hours), full recovery is expected without permanent sequelae. Coniine does not cause organ damage directly — death is solely from respiratory arrest.

### Treatment

- **Decontamination:** Gastric lavage or activated charcoal if within 1–2 hours of ingestion.
- **Respiratory support:** Mechanical ventilation is the cornerstone of treatment. Maintain SpO₂ > 94%.
- **Antidote:** No specific pharmacological antidote exists. Atropine is **ineffective** (unlike organophosphate poisoning) and may worsen tachycardia.
- **Monitoring:** Continuous cardiac monitoring, serial neurological assessments, ABG monitoring.
- **Elimination:** Coniine is metabolized hepatically and renally excreted. No enhanced elimination techniques are established.

## Historical Significance

### Death of Socrates (399 BCE)

The most famous historical reference to coniine is the execution of Socrates by hemlock poisoning, as described in Plato's *Phaedo*. Socrates was condemned to death by an Athenian jury for "corrupting the youth" and "impiety." Plato's account describes:

- A gradual ascending paralysis beginning in the legs.
- Preservation of consciousness throughout.
- Socrates' final words addressing Crito regarding a debt owed to Asclepius.

Modern toxicological analysis suggests the symptoms described by Plato are consistent with coniine poisoning, though the dose may have been sublethal initially, with repeated doses administered.

### Other Historical Uses

- **Ancient Greece and Rome:** Hemlock was used as a method of capital punishment. The Greek word *kôneion* (κώνειον) referred both to the plant and the execution method.
- **Medieval Europe:** Occasionally used as a poison for political assassinations, though its characteristic taste and delayed onset made it less practical than arsenic.
- **19th-century medicine:** Coniine was briefly investigated as a possible muscle relaxant and antispasmodic agent but was abandoned due to its narrow [[cannabis-therapeutic-index-overview]].

## Modern Relevance

- **Agricultural hazard:** Poison hemlock is one of the most commonly ingested toxic plants in livestock. Cattle, sheep, goats, horses, and poultry are all susceptible. Animals typically avoid the plant when other forage is available but may consume it when incorporated into hay.
- **Foraging risk:** Poison hemlock closely resembles wild carrot (*[[daucus-carota]]*) and other edible Apiaceae. The distinguishing feature is the purple-red blotching on the stem (*"the root of evil"*) and the lack of hairs on the stem.
- **[[psilocybin-accidental-ingestion-australia]]:** Most contemporary poisonings occur in foragers misidentifying the plant, children attracted to the flowers, or livestock in contaminated hay.

## Forensic and Analytical Detection

- **Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS):** Standard method for detection in biological fluids and plant material. Coniine produces a characteristic mass spectrum with base peak at m/z 84 (piperidine fragment).
- **Liquid chromatography-tandem MS (LC-MS/MS):** More sensitive, suitable for detecting low-level exposure.
- **Detection window:** 24–72 hours in urine; 12–24 hours in blood.
