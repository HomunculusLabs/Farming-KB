## Overview

Dryopteris marginalis, commonly known as the marginal wood fern or marginal
shield fern, is an evergreen fern native to [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]]. It is one of
the most common woodland ferns in its range, valued for its durable, leathery
fronds that persist through winter.

The species epithet "marginalis" refers to the position of the sori (spore-
bearing structures) on the margins of the pinnule undersides, a distinctive
field character that separates it from most other Dryopteris species in eastern
forests.

Marginal wood fern is widely cultivated as a shade-garden ornamental, prized
for its reliability, [[cannabis-disease-pest-resistance-breeding-clarke]], and evergreen foliage. It is one of the
most commercially available native ferns in the [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] horticultural
trade.

The species tolerates a broad range of soil conditions and light levels, making
it adaptable to both wild and garden settings. Its robust nature has earned it
a reputation as one of the easiest native ferns to establish.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Dryopteris marginalis (L.) A. Gray was described by Asa Gray in 1848. The
basionym is Polypodium marginale L. (1753). It belongs to the family
Dryopteridaceae within the order Polypodiales.

Recent phylogenetic studies place D. marginalis in the "marginalis clade"
within Dryopteris subgenus Dryopteris. This clade includes several eastern
[[north-american-dmt-containing-plants-phalaris-desmanthus-peganum]] species that share evergreen frond habits and marginal sori.

Fronds arise from a compact, ascending rhizome covered with persistent
lanceolate scales. The rhizome produces a dense crown of fronds that forms a
tidy, shuttlecock-shaped rosette.

Fronds are 30–90 cm long and 15–30 cm wide, with a stout, scaly stipe that
accounts for approximately one-third of total frond length. The stipe is
densely clothed in brown, lanceolate scales at the base.

The lamina is bipinnate-pinnatifid with 12–20 pairs of pinnae. Pinnae are
lanceolate, deeply lobed, with margins that are dentate to slightly
spinulose. The rachis is prominently grooved on the adaxial surface and bears
scattered scales.

Sori are circular, medium-sized, and positioned near the margins of the
pinnule undersides, typically between the margin and midrib. Indusia are
reniform (kidney-shaped). Spores are brown, monolete, and produced from July
to October.

## Distribution and Habitat

Dryopteris marginalis is widespread in eastern North America, ranging from
Nova Scotia and southern Ontario west to Minnesota and eastern Iowa, and south
to northern Georgia and northeastern Oklahoma. It is absent from the coastal
plain of the southeastern [[dmt-maoi-plants-united-states]].

The species occurs most abundantly in rich, rocky woodlands, especially on
north-facing slopes and in ravines with moist, well-drained soils. It is a
characteristic component of the herbaceous layer in mixed mesophytic forests
and oak-hickory associations.

It frequently colonizes rock crevices, cliff bases, talus slopes, and the
margins of limestone outcrops. Its preference for calcareous substrates
distinguishes it from several co-occurring Dryopteris species that favor
acidic soils.

Elevation ranges from near sea level to approximately 1,200 m in the southern
Appalachians. It is most abundant between 200 and 800 m elevation in regions
with moderate to high annual precipitation.
