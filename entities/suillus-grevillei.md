# Suillus grevillei (Larch Bolete)

## Overview

Suillus grevillei, commonly known as the **larch bolete** or **Greville's bolete**, is an ectomycorrhizal mushroom in the family Suillaceae (traditionally placed in Boletaceae). It is one of the most recognizable and abundant boletes associated with larch (Larix spp.) forests across the Northern Hemisphere. The species forms an obligate mutualistic relationship with larch roots, making it a reliable indicator species for old-growth and managed larch ecosystems. Characterized by its slimy, viscid orange cap, bright yellow pore surface, and a distinctive ring on the stipe, S. grevillei is widely collected as an edible mushroom in Europe, Russia, and parts of Asia, where it is particularly valued in Russian, Polish, and Chinese cuisine. In permaculture and forest garden design, it serves as a model ectomycorrhizal partner for larch-based agroforestry systems.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Division**: Basidiomycota
- **Class**: Agaricomycetes
- **Order**: Boletales
- **Family**: Suillaceae
- **Genus**: Suillus
- **Species**: S. grevillei (Klotzsch) Singer (1945)
- **Basionym**: Boletus grevillei Klotzsch (1832)
- **Synonyms**: Ixocomus grevillei (Klotzsch) Quél.; Boletus elegans Fr. (non Schaeff.); Suillus elegans (Fr.) Snell
- **Common names**: Larch bolete, Greville's bolete, slippery Jack (confused with S. luteus), larch Suillus
- **Etymology**: Named in honor of Scottish botanist Robert Kaye Greville (1794–1866)

## Morphology

**Cap**: 5–15 cm diameter, convex becoming broadly convex to plano-convex with age; surface is conspicuously viscid to glutinous when wet, smooth, and typically bright orange to rusty orange or apricot-colored. Cap cuticle peels readily, a characteristic suillid trait. Cap margin is inrolled when young, extending with age. Flesh is pale yellow to ochre, soft, and does not change color when cut.

**Pore surface**: Tubes are adnate to slightly decurrent, 5–12 mm long. Pores are angular, small (2–3 per mm when young), and bright yellow to lemon-yellow, darkening to olive-brown with age. Does not bruise blue.

**Stipe**: 6–12 cm long, 1–2.5 cm thick, cylindrical to slightly clavate, solid. Surface is white to pale yellow above the ring and typically decorated with brownish glandular dots below. The ring is membranous, persistent (though often collapsed), pale yellow, and positioned in the upper third of the stipe. Ring is a key diagnostic feature distinguishing S. grevillei from the ringless S. viscidus.

**Spore print**: Cinnamon-brown to ochre-brown.
**Spores**: 7–10 × 3–4.5 μm, ellipsoid to oblong, smooth, inamyloid.
**Chemical reactions**: Cap surface with KOH turns dark reddish-brown. Flesh with FeSO₄ turns greyish-green.

## Ecology and Distribution

**Host specificity**: Suillus grevillei is an obligate ectomycorrhizal symbiont of larch species (Larix spp.), including:
- European larch (Larix decidua)
- Japanese larch (Larix kaempferi)
- Dahurian larch (Larix gmelinii)
- Tamarack (Larix laricina)
- Himalayan larch (Larix griffithii)

This tight host specificity makes S. grevillei an excellent bioindicator for the presence of larch in mixed forest stands. The mycorrhizal association enhances larch nutrition (particularly phosphorus uptake) and provides the fungus with a reliable carbohydrate source.

**Geographic range**: Widely distributed across the Northern Hemisphere in temperate and boreal zones. Common throughout Europe (especially Scandinavia, the Alps, Carpathians, and British Isles), Russia, Siberia, northern China, Japan, and North America (where it occurs with native and introduced Larix species). Most abundant in montane and subalpine larch forests at elevations of 500–2,500 m.

**Seasonality**: Fruits from mid-summer through autumn (July–October in the Northern Hemisphere), typically peaking in August–September. Often appears in abundance 2–3 weeks after sustained rainfall in larch plantations.

**Habitat**: Found on soil in larch forests, plantations, and ornamental larch plantings. Prefers acidic to neutral soils (pH 4.5–6.5) with good drainage. Common along forest edges and in young larch stands (10–30 years). Tolerates disturbed habitats and is one of the first ectomycorrhizal boletes to colonize recently planted larch seedlings.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

Suillus grevillei is considered an **excellent edible** mushroom when properly prepared, though some general guidelines apply to all Suillus species:

- **Preparation essential**: The viscid cap cuticle should be peeled before cooking, as it can cause gastrointestinal discomfort in some individuals and has an unpleasantly slimy texture
- **Cooking required**: Must be thoroughly cooked before consumption; raw specimens may cause digestive upset
- **Nutritional profile** (per 100 g fresh weight, approximate): Calories 25–30 kcal, protein 1.8 g, carbohydrates 3.5 g, fat 0.5 g, fiber 1.5 g. Rich in potassium, phosphorus, and B vitamins
- **Culinary applications**: Widely used in Eastern European cuisine — pickled, sautéed with onions and sour cream, dried for winter soups, or made into preserves. In Russian and Polish traditions, larch boletes are considered among the finest wild mushrooms for pickling due to their firm texture and mild, slightly nutty flavor
- **Preservation**: Dries well with good flavor retention; also freezes well after blanching

**Caution**: As with all wild mushrooms, proper identification is critical. S. grevillei can be confused with Suillus viscidus (also on larch, but lacks a ring), Suillus luteus (associated with two-needle pines, has a more brownish cap), and Tylopilus felleus (bitter bolete, though this has pink pores). No deadly lookalikes are associated with larch, but consumption of misidentified boletes from other hosts should be avoided.

## Bioactive Compounds

Research on Suillus grevillei has identified several notable bioactive constituents:
- **Polysaccharides**: Water-soluble β-glucans with immunomodulatory activity; demonstrated macrophage-activating and antitumor properties in vitro
- **Grevillin pigments**: Characteristic orange carotenoid-like pigments unique to Suillus species with antioxidant activity
- **Phenolic compounds**: Including p-hydroxybenzoic acid, gallic acid, and protocatechuic acid with radical-scavenging properties
- **Linoleic acid**: Predominant fatty acid (~50% of total lipids) with anti-inflammatory properties
- **Ergosterol**: Precursor to vitamin D₂; S. grevillei contains 2.4–4.1 mg/g ergosterol (dry weight), comparable to other boletes

## Permaculture and Agroforestry Relevance

Suillus grevillei is a valuable species in permaculture and forest garden contexts:
- **Ectomycorrhizal inoculant**: Can be introduced to larch plantings to accelerate mycorrhizal establishment and improve seedling survival, particularly in reforestation and mine reclamation sites
- **Larch companion planting**: The mutualism enhances phosphorus and micronutrient uptake in larch, supporting faster growth in nutrient-poor or disturbed soils
- **Food forest integration**: In temperate food forests incorporating larch for timber or nitrogen cycling, S. grevillei provides a secondary yield of edible mushrooms
- **Soil ecology**: Contributes to soil aggregation, organic matter cycling, and formation of a distinctive larch forest soil microbiome that benefits understory plant communities
- **Succession indicator**: Presence of S. grevillei indicates healthy, functioning ectomycorrhizal networks in larch stands, a sign of ecosystem maturity

## Relevance

Suillus grevillei exemplifies the intimate ecological partnerships between fungi and trees that underpin forest health. Its obligate association with larch makes it both a valuable bioindicator and a practical tool in ecological forestry and permaculture. As a widely consumed edible mushroom with documented bioactive compounds, it bridges the gap between mycological science, culinary tradition, and sustainable food systems. Its resilience in managed forest landscapes and ability to improve tree nutrition position it as a key species for temperate agroforestry and reforestation efforts.
