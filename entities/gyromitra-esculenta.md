# Gyromitra esculenta

## Overview
Gyromitra esculenta is a species of ascomycete fungus in the family Discinaceae.
It is notorious for its toxicity and resemblance to true morels (Morchella spp.).
Commonly called the false morel or brain mushroom, it is frequently misidentified by novice foragers with potentially fatal consequences.
Despite its scientific name (esculenta meaning "edible"), the species contains gyromitrin, which hydrolyzes to monomethylhydrazine (MMH) — a potent toxin also used as rocket fuel.
In some European traditions, G. esculenta is consumed after extensive parboiling that reduces but does not eliminate toxicity.
Fatal poisonings have been documented even among experienced foragers who failed to properly prepare the mushroom.

## Taxonomy and Classification
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Ascomycota
- **Subdivision:** Pezizomycotina
- **Class:** Pezizomycetes
- **Order:** Pezizales
- **Family:** Discinaceae
- **Genus:** Gyromitra
- **Species:** G. esculenta (Pers.) Fr. 1849
The species was originally described by Christian Hendrik Persoon in 1800 as Helvella esculenta.
It was later transferred to Gyromitra by Elias Magnus Fries in 1849.
The genus name derives from the Greek gyros (round) and mitra (headdress), referring to the cap shape.
Phylogenetic analysis has confirmed Gyromitra as a distinct lineage within Pezizales.
Related species include G. gigas, G. infula, G. caroliniana, and G. brunnea.

## Morphology
G. esculenta produces a distinctive [[bloomfield-mushroom-formation-fruiting-body-development-cultivation]] that superficially resembles true morels:
- **Cap:** 4–12 cm tall, 3–8 cm wide, irregularly brain-like or convoluted, lacking the defined pits and ridges of true morels.
Color ranges from reddish-brown to dark brown or purplish-brown.
The cap attaches to the stem at multiple points — a key distinguishing feature.
- **Stem:** 3–8 cm tall, 2–4 cm thick, stout, solid or slightly chambered, cream-colored to pale tan.
The base may be slightly enlarged or rooting.
- **Flesh:** Thin, waxy, brittle in the cap, white to cream-colored.
Odor is mild to slightly fruity when young, becoming unpleasant with age.
- **Spores:** Elliptical, smooth, hyaline, with two oil droplets, 18–24 × 8–12 µm.
[[boil-a-bag-spore-print-technique-jarrold]] is cream to yellowish.
- **Asci:** Operculate, cylindrical, 200–350 µm long, typically containing 8 spores.
Paraphyses are septate with slightly enlarged tips containing brownish pigment.

## Key Differentiation from True Morels
Distinguishing G. esculenta from true morels (Morchella) is critical for forager safety:
- True morels have a cap with distinct, regularly arranged pits and ridges forming a honeycomb pattern.
G. esculenta has an irregular, brain-like surface without organized pits and ridges.
- True morels are hollow when cut lengthwise. G. esculenta is chambered with tissue partitions.
- True morel caps attach to the stem only at the apex. G. esculenta attaches at multiple points along the stem.
- True morels typically fruit later in spring, while G. esculenta often appears 1–2 weeks earlier.
The "fork test" (splitting longitudinally with a knife) is the most reliable [[palmer-mushroom-hunting-field-identification]] method.

## Distribution and Habitat
G. esculenta has a wide distribution across the Northern Hemisphere.
It is common throughout central, northern, and eastern Europe, especially Scandinavia, Germany, and the Baltic states.
In [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], it occurs across much of the continent, particularly in coniferous forests of the Pacific Northwest and Rocky Mountains.
The species fruits in spring to early summer (March to June), typically appearing before or alongside true morels.
It grows primarily on sandy or loamy soils in coniferous and mixed forests under spruce, pine, fir, and aspen.
Fruiting is stimulated by warming soil temperatures and spring rainfall.
The species also appears in disturbed sites including roadsides, gravel pits, and recently burned areas.
Specimens at higher altitudes or in boreal zones may contain higher gyromitrin concentrations.

## Toxicology
G. esculenta contains gyromitrin (N-methyl-N-formylhydrazone), which hydrolyzes in the body to monomethylhydrazine (MMH).
MMH inhibits pyridoxine (vitamin B6)-dependent enzymes, particularly glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD).
