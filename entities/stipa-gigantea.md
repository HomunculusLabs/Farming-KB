## Overview

**Stipa gigantea** is a large evergreen [[savory-perennial-grass-and-desertification]] in the family Poaceae, commonly known as golden oats or giant feather grass.

It is native to the western Mediterranean region, including the Iberian Peninsula, Morocco, and southern France.

The species is celebrated in horticulture for its dramatic flower spikes, which rise well above the foliage on slender stems and catch light throughout [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

Golden oats has received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit and is widely planted in temperate gardens worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Stipa gigantea is placed within the tribe Stipeae of the subfamily Pooideae.

The species was originally described by Heinrich Friedrich Link in 1800.

Some taxonomic treatments have transferred it to the genus *Celtica*, though *Stipa gigantea* Link remains the most widely used name in horticultural and ecological literature.

It is a diploid species with 2n = 44 chromosomes.

The plant forms a dense, evergreen basal tussock of narrow, arching leaf blades 60 to 100 centimeters long and approximately 2 millimeters wide.

Leaves are grey-green, tough, and rolled in the bud, forming a neat mound of foliage.

Flowering stems are produced from late spring through midsummer, rising 1.5 to 2.5 meters above the foliage.

The inflorescence is an open, airy panicle 20 to 40 centimeters long with widely spaced spikelets.

Each spikelet bears a single floret with an exceptionally long awn 15 to 25 centimeters in length.

The awn is bent and twisted, golden-brown in color, and gives the panicle its characteristic glistening appearance.

As the awns mature and dry, they persist on the plant through autumn and into winter, providing extended ornamental interest.

## Distribution and Habitat

Stipa gigantea is native to the western Mediterranean Basin.

Its native range extends through central and southern Spain, Portugal, and southern France into northern Morocco.

The species occurs primarily on dry, stony hillsides, rocky outcrops, and open scrubland at elevations from sea level to approximately 1,500 meters.

It is characteristic of thermophilous grassland communities and is frequently associated with species of *Quercus* coccifera, *Thymus*, and *Lavandula* scrub.

The plant thrives in well-drained, calcareous or slightly acidic soils and tolerates both nutrient-poor and moderately rich substrates.

Mediterranean climate conditions with wet, mild winters and hot, dry summers are ideal for vigorous growth.

The species has naturalized in parts of California, New Zealand, and southeastern Australia where conditions approximate its native habitat.

Naturalized populations are generally small and localized, and the species is not considered a significant invasive threat outside cultivation.

## Cultivation and Uses

Stipa gigantea is one of the most popular [[ornamental-grasses-guide]] in temperate horticulture.

It is prized for its architectural form, combining a neat basal mound with tall, translucent flower spikes that create dramatic vertical accents in borders and meadow plantings.

The species is widely used in naturalistic garden designs, prairie-style plantings, and Mediterranean-themed landscapes.

It performs best in full sun and well-drained soil, showing excellent [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] once established.

Excessive soil moisture or poor drainage leads to crown rot and decline, particularly in winter.

Plants are typically propagated by seed, which germinates readily when sown in containers in spring.

Division of mature clumps is possible in spring but should be undertaken infrequently, as the deep root system resents disturbance.

In mild climates, the foliage is semi-evergreen and retains a neat appearance through winter.

In colder regions, foliage may be cut back in late winter before new growth emerges.
