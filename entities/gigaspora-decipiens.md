# Gigaspora decipiens

## Description

Gigaspora decipiens is a species of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus (AMF) in the genus Gigaspora, suborder Gigasporineae, order Glomales. The species is notable because it was claimed to possess a sexual reproductive phase (Tommerup and Sivasithamparam 1990), an assertion that is remarkable because the vast majority of evidence, including genetic analysis, indicates that AMF reproduce exclusively through clonal (mitospore) reproduction. This claim makes G. decipiens a species of particular interest in studies of AMF reproductive biology and population genetics.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Eukarya
- **Phylum:** Zygomycota (Glomeromycota in modern taxonomy)
- **Order:** Glomales
- **Suborder:** Gigasporineae
- **Family:** Gigasporaceae
- **Genus:** Gigaspora
- **Species:** Gigaspora decipiens

## Key Facts

- **Alleged sexual reproduction:** Tommerup and Sivasithamparam (1990) reported evidence of a sexual reproductive phase in G. decipiens, which would be unique among known AMF.
- **Genetic evidence to the contrary:** Genetic analysis (Rosendahl and Taylor 1997) supports the prevailing view that AMF, including G. decipiens, reproduce clonally via mitospores.
- Belongs to Gigaspora, a genus with only five described species — the lowest species count attributable to developmental constraints on spore wall variation during germination.
- Members of Gigaspora produce auxiliary cells (not vesicles) and belong to the suborder Gigasporineae.
- Auxiliary cells and detached hyphae of Gigasporineae are generally non-infective, unlike the highly infective hyphae and spores of the suborder Glomineae.

## Reproductive Biology Controversy

The question of whether AMF possess a sexual cycle is one of the most significant unresolved questions in mycorrhizal biology:

- **Tommerup and Sivasithamparam (1990):** Reported structures in G. decipiens consistent with sexual reproduction (zygospore-like bodies).
- **Rosendahl and Taylor (1997):** Genetic analysis of AMF populations found no evidence of recombination consistent with sexual reproduction, supporting clonal propagation.
- **Implications:** If sexual reproduction does occur in any AMF species, it would fundamentally change our understanding of their population genetics, evolutionary potential, and species concepts.
- **Current consensus:** The weight of evidence favors clonal reproduction as the dominant or exclusive mode, but the question remains open for investigation.

## Ecological Distribution

- Found in diverse habitats globally, though less frequently encountered than Glomus species due to the lower species richness of Gigaspora.
- Spores are large, contributing disproportionately to spore biovolume measurements in community surveys.
- Auxiliary cells produced on external hyphae in soil are transitory and serve as short-term lipid storage structures.
- Colonization patterns and sporulation are regulated by host phenology and environmental conditions.

## Spore Morphology

- Large spores (typically >200 µm diameter), characteristic of the genus Gigaspora.
- Spore wall differentiation culminates in germination — Gigaspora is the only known AMF genus where germination represents the terminal event in spore wall differentiation.
- This developmental constraint limits the range of spore wall variation possible in Gigaspora, explaining the low species count (five species).
- Spores are borne singly on bulbous suspensor-like structures.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Reproductive biology:** G. decipiens remains a focal species for investigating whether sexual reproduction occurs in AMF, with implications for understanding their evolutionary potential and genetic diversity.
- **Population genetics:** The species is relevant to studies of clonal population structure, genetic drift, and local adaptation in AMF.
- **Species concepts:** The difficulty of distinguishing sexual from asexual structures in G. decipiens reflects broader challenges in defining species boundaries in predominantly clonal organisms.
- **Inoculum production:** Auxiliary cells and detached hyphae are non-infective, so inoculum must include intact spores or mycorrhizal root fragments — a practical consideration for cultivation and restoration applications.

## Identification

- Large, globose spores (typically 200–400 µm) borne on bulbous suspensor cells.
- Spore walls with characteristic laminate structure; germination occurs through the wall layers.
- Auxiliary cells formed on external hyphae, knobby or lobed in appearance.
- Best identified from healthy spores in trap cultures using DIC microscopy.
