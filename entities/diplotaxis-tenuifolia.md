## Overview

**Diplotaxis tenuifolia** is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Brassicaceae, commonly known as wild rocket, perennial wall-rocket, or simply rocket. It is native to the Mediterranean region and western Asia.

The species is commercially important as a salad green, prized for its intense peppery, bitter flavor that is stronger than that of cultivated rocket (*Eruca vesicaria* subsp. *sativa*). Wild rocket has become a staple ingredient in Italian cuisine and is widely used in restaurants worldwide.

Commercial cultivation has expanded significantly since the 1990s, with Italy, the United Kingdom, and France as major producers. The species is increasingly grown hydroponically for year-round supply.

It grows in dry, rocky, and sandy habitats including roadside verges, field margins, walls, rocky outcrops, and disturbed ground. The species tolerates poor, calcareous soils and is drought-resistant once established.

Optimal growth occurs at 15–25°C in well-drained soils. The plant is adapted to Mediterranean climates with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters. It tolerates light frost and can persist as a short-lived perennial.

The species exhibits some degree of halotolerance and is occasionally found in coastal habitats. It is a common constituent of Mediterranean annual and perennial grassland communities.

## Culinary Use

Wild rocket is among the most commercially significant salad herbs in European cuisine. Its leaves have a distinctive pungent, peppery, and slightly bitter flavor that intensifies as the plant matures.

The species is used raw in salads, as a pizza topping, blended into pesto, and as a garnish. In Italian cuisine, it is a key ingredient in the classic rocket and parmesan combination and is often served with bresaola or carpaccio.

Young, tender leaves are preferred for raw consumption. Mature leaves can be wilted into pasta dishes, risottos, or soups, where the bitterness mellows with cooking. The flowers are edible and add a peppery note to salads.

Wild rocket has largely replaced cultivated rocket (*Eruca sativa*) in premium markets due to its more intense and complex flavor profile. The two species are sometimes sold mixed, though purists prefer pure wild rocket.

The leaves are highly perishable and have a [[hamilton-salad-vegetables-organic]].

Vitamin K content is among the highest of any leafy green, with approximately 250 µg per 100 g fresh weight. The leaves are also rich in vitamin C (110 mg/100 g), vitamin A (as carotenoids), and folate.

The species is notable for its glucosinolate content, primarily glucoerucin, glucoraphanin, and dimeric 4-mercaptobutyl glucosinolate. These sulfur-containing compounds are responsible for the characteristic pungent flavor and are precursors to isothiocyanates.

Isothiocyanates derived from glucosinolate hydrolysis have demonstrated anticancer, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activities in laboratory studies. The glucosinolate profile of D. tenuifolia differs significantly from that of cultivated rocket.

Flavonoids including [[kaempferol]], and isorhamnetin glycosides contribute to the antioxidant capacity. Total phenolic content ranges from 30–50 mg GAE per gram dry weight.
