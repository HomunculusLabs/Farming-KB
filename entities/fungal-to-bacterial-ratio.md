# Fungal to Bacterial Ratio (F:B Ratio)

## Description

The fungal to bacterial ratio (F:B ratio) is the relative biomass of fungi compared to bacteria in a given soil. It is a critical indicator of soil ecosystem condition and determines which forms of nitrogen predominate in the soil, which plants will thrive, and how nutrients cycle. Research pioneered by Dr. Elaine Ingham at Oregon State University in the 1980s established that different plant communities prefer different F:B ratios, and that soil disturbance dramatically shifts this balance.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Soil microbiology / Soil ecology
- **Category:** Soil biological indicators
- **Subcategory:** Microbial biomass ratios

## Key Facts

- Agricultural soils typically have an F:B ratio of 1:1 or less (bacteria dominate or are equal to fungi).
- Forest soils have F:B ratios of 10:1 or greater (fungi strongly dominate).
- The least disturbed soils (old-growth timber stands) have far more fungal than bacterial biomass.
- Disturbed soils (rototilled, compacted, or chemically treated) shift strongly toward bacterial dominance.
- **Plant preferences correlate with F:B ratio:**
  - Perennials, trees, and shrubs prefer fungally dominated soils.
  - Annuals, grasses, and vegetables prefer bacterially dominated soils.
- **Nitrogen form depends on F:B ratio:**
  - In bacterially dominated soils (alkaline pH from bacterial bioslime), nitrogen-converting bacteria thrive and convert ammonium (NH4+) to nitrate (NO3-).
  - In fungally dominated soils (acidic pH from fungal acids), nitrifying bacteria are suppressed, and nitrogen remains in ammonium form.
- Chemical fertilizers typically provide nitrogen as nitrates — which is the wrong form for plants that prefer fungally dominated soils.
- Gardeners can deliberately shift the F:B ratio through management practices: compost, mulch, and no-till favor fungi; tilling and nitrogen fertilizers favor bacteria.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The F:B ratio is one of the most actionable concepts in organic cultivation. Matching the soil's microbial dominance to the crop's preference ensures optimal nutrient delivery. For mycology, this ratio explains why mushroom-producing fungi thrive in forest soils and compost-heavy beds: fungal dominance means less competition from bacteria, more complex carbon sources from woody material, and acidic conditions that favor fungal growth. Compost tea brewing, mulching with woody material, and avoiding tillage are all strategies to shift soils toward fungal dominance.
