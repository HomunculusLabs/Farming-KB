## Description

The log disk method is a spawn expansion and fruiting technique that uses 6- to 12-inch diameter logs cut into smooth rounds (disks) approximately 6 to 8 inches thick, with colonized cardboard spawn sandwiched between the cut faces. The method was documented by [[tradd-cotter]] as a way to rapidly expand mycelium from cardboard cultures into wood substrates, creating a scalable system that can produce inoculated rounds indefinitely. It is primarily designed for growers with access to a chainsaw.

## Classification

- **Category**: Spawn expansion and fruiting technique
- **Complexity**: Moderate
- **Equipment**: Chainsaw (required), colonized cardboard sheet spawn, water
- **Recommended species**: [[oyster-mushrooms]], shiitake
- **Timeline**: Colonization in 2–4 weeks; fruiting begins once rounds are moved to fruiting area

## Key Facts

- Requires a chainsaw for cutting 6- to 12-inch diameter logs into 6- to 8-inch thick rounds
- Colonized sheet of cardboard is placed between two stacked disks
- Both cardboard and disks are watered generously
- Stack is covered loosely with plastic or leaves to preserve humidity
- After 2 weeks, inspect the cut faces for an even white layer of mycelium
- If gaps exist, replace the rounds and wait another 1–2 weeks
- Once an even white layer covers the face, flip the disks so the uncolonized sides face the cardboard
- Expand by adding fresh wet cardboard at each end, sandwiched by new disks
- Mycelium quickly runs through the wet cardboard and colonizes the new disks
- In under a month, you can have 4 disks and 4 sheets of cardboard fully inoculated
- Process can be repeated to expand rounds indefinitely

## Fruiting Strategy

- Move colonized rounds from the expansion group to a separate fruiting area
- Best fruited in stacked totems so rounds can fuse and combine water and energy resources
- Locating the lowest round flush or partially submerged provides access to extra ground moisture
- Original "mother" disks should be kept for expansion rather than fruited
- Fruiting the original disks weakens the culture through excessive cell replication
- Using downstream disks for fruiting keeps cultures more vigorous

## Culture Maintenance

- Keep the culture strong by not fruiting the original expansion disks
- Shuffle rounds every few weeks to maintain vigor
- Allow mycelium to grow into different wood types or media to challenge its enzymes
- Varying the food source extends culture strength by preventing enzymatic complacency
- The method can sustain rounds into perpetuity with proper management

## Comparison with Wafering Method

The log disk method is faster to colonize and fruit compared to the wafering method because the cut faces provide large surface area for direct mycelial contact. However, it requires cutting logs into sections (needing a chainsaw) and is more labor-intensive during setup. The wafering method works with intact logs and standing timber but takes years to produce.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The log disk method demonstrates how a small amount of cardboard spawn can be leveraged into a large-scale inoculation operation through systematic expansion. It provides a middle ground between low-tech cardboard cultivation and laboratory-based [[spawn-production]], requiring only basic tools (a chainsaw) and colonized cardboard. The method's emphasis on maintaining "mother" cultures for expansion while fruiting downstream rounds mirrors agricultural practices of keeping seed stock separate from harvest stock.
