## Overview

*Amaranthus hypochondriacus* is a species of annual flowering plant in the family Amaranthaceae, cultivated primarily for its edible seeds which serve as a **pseudo-cereal** grain. It is one of the three principal grain amaranths alongside *A. caudatus* and *A. cruentus*, collectively known as **huauhtli** in Nahuatl and **amaranth** in English.

The species produces large, drooping inflorescences bearing thousands of tiny seeds approximately 1 mm in diameter. These seeds are nutritionally exceptional, containing 14–16% protein with a balanced amino acid profile notably rich in lysine, an amino acid deficient in most cereal grains.

*A. hypochondriacus* was a major staple crop of the Aztec civilization and held central religious significance in pre-Columbian Mesoamerica. Spanish colonial authorities banned its cultivation in the 16th century due to its association with indigenous religious practices, nearly driving the crop to extinction.

Modern interest in amaranth has surged since the 1970s as a drought-tolerant, nutrient-dense alternative to conventional cereals. The crop is now cultivated across Mexico, [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]].

Leaves are large (8–20 cm long), ovate to lanceolate, with entire margins and long petioles. Foliage color ranges from green to deep red or purple, with ornamental varieties selected for vivid pigmentation. Leaves are also edible and consumed as a nutritious potherb.

The inflorescence is a large, terminal, paniculate spike 30–60 cm long, composed of compact cymose clusters of small flowers. The characteristic drooping or nodding habit of the inflorescence distinguishes *A. hypochondriacus* from the more erect *A. cruentus*. Bracts and bracteoles are shorter than the tepals.

Seeds are lenticular (lens-shaped), 1–1.5 mm in diameter, with a glossy surface. Color varies from white to cream, gold, pink, or black depending on cultivar. Thousand-seed weight ranges from 0.5–1.0 g. Each plant produces 50,000–200,000 seeds under optimal conditions.

## Distribution and History

The center of origin for *A. hypochondriacus* is the central Mexican highlands, where it was domesticated from the wild relative *Amaranthus hybridus* approximately 6,000–8,000 years ago. Archaeological evidence confirms its cultivation at Tehuacán, Puebla, by 4000 BCE.

The Aztec civilization considered amaranth a sacred crop second only to maize. Seeds were ground into flour, mixed with honey or blood, and formed into ritual figures of Huitzilopochtli (the war god) during the Panquetzaliztli festival. After the ceremony, the figures were broken and distributed for consumption — a practice combining religious devotion with communal nutrition.

Following the Spanish conquest (1521), colonial authorities banned amaranth cultivation as part of efforts to suppress indigenous religious practices. The crop survived only in remote highland communities where it was grown in small quantities for subsistence. This suppression eliminated *A. hypochondriacus* from commercial agriculture for nearly 400 years.

Scientific reassessment beginning in the 1970s, particularly by the Rodale Research Center in Pennsylvania, documented the crop's exceptional nutritional profile and agronomic potential. This triggered a global revival, with breeding programs in Mexico, India, China, and the United States developing improved varieties for grain and forage production.

Current cultivation extends from Mexico and Central America through the Andean highlands to India, Nepal, China, and East Africa. The species performs well in tropical highlands (1,500–2,500 m elevation) and warm temperate regions with growing seasons exceeding 90 days.

Global production of grain amaranth is estimated at 10,000–15,000 tonnes annually, with Mexico, India, and China as the largest producers. Despite its nutritional advantages, amaranth remains a niche crop due to limited processing infrastructure, consumer unfamiliarity in mainstream markets, and the dominance of established cereal commodity chains.

Breeding programs at CIMMYT (Mexico) and various national agricultural research institutes have developed improved varieties with non-shattering seed heads, uniform maturity, and higher yield potential. Ornamental varieties of *A. hypochondriacus* are widely sold in the horticultural trade for their dramatic, deep-red inflorescences.

## Nutritional Value

Amaranth grain is nutritionally superior to most conventional cereals. Protein content ranges from 14–16% on a dry-weight basis, compared to 7–12% for wheat and 8–10% for rice. The protein quality is exceptional due to a favorable amino acid composition.

Lysine content (5–7 g/100 g protein) is 2–3 times higher than in wheat, corn, or rice, addressing the primary protein deficiency in cereal-based diets. Methionine and cysteine levels are adequate, and the overall protein digestibility-corrected amino acid score (PDCAAS) approaches 0.75–0.80.

The grain contains approximately 6–8% fat, with a favorable fatty acid profile dominated by linoleic acid (C18:2, omega-6) at 40–50% and oleic acid (C18:1, omega-9) at 20–30%. Squalene, a triterpene with antioxidant and potential anti-cancer properties, is present at 2–5% of total lipids.

Carbohydrate content is 60–65%, predominantly starch with small amounts of dietary fiber (6–8%). The starch has a low glycemic index and contains amylose at 20–25%, contributing to slower glucose release compared to high-amylopectin cereals.

Micronutrient density is remarkable: iron (15 mg/100 g), calcium (160–250 mg/100 g), magnesium (300 mg/100 g), zinc (3–4 mg/100 g), and phosphorus (500 mg/100 g). Folate content (80–100 μg/100 g) is among the highest of any grain, supporting its value for pregnant and lactating women.

## Cultivation

*Amaranthus hypochondriacus* is a C4 plant with high [[photosynthetic-efficiency]], water use efficiency, and heat tolerance. It thrives in temperatures of 25–30°C and is capable of producing grain under conditions where maize and wheat fail due to drought or heat stress.

Seed is typically direct-sown at rates of 0.5–2 kg/ha, with row spacing of 40–75 cm depending on mechanical cultivation requirements. Optimal plant density is 20–40 plants per square meter. The crop requires minimal nitrogen input (40–60 kg N/ha) due to efficient nitrogen metabolism, though phosphorus and potassium applications improve yields.

The growing season is 90–150 days from sowing to grain maturity, depending on cultivar and temperature. [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] make it economically attractive for marginal environments.

Major pests include the amaranth weevil (*Hypolixus truncatulus*), stem borer (*Conogethes punctiferalis*), and various leaf-chewing caterpillars. Fungal diseases include damping-off (*Pythium* spp.) in seedlings and root rot (*Fusarium* spp.) in established plants. However, the crop's rapid growth rate often outpaces [[hamilton-pest-and-disease-control-organic]] damage without chemical intervention.

Weed competition is most significant during the first 30 days after emergence. Once established, the tall, dense canopy of amaranth effectively suppresses most weed species. No herbicides are registered for amaranth in most countries, making the crop inherently compatible with organic production systems.
