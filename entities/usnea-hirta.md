## Overview

**Usnea hirta** (L.) F.H. Wigg. is a fruticose beard lichen in the family
Usneaceae, commonly known as the **common old man's beard**. It is one of
the most widespread *Usnea* species, occurring on tree bark and wood
throughout temperate and boreal regions of the Northern Hemisphere.

The species is characterized by its abundant, irregular branching and
distinctive central chord (anatomical axis) visible in cross-section.
*U. hirta* has a long history of use in traditional medicine across
Europe, Asia, and [[blesching-cannabis-wound-care]].

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Usnea hirta* was originally described as *Lichen hirtus* by Linnaeus in
1753 and later transferred to *Usnea* by Wiggers in 1780. It is the type
species of the genus and has a complex taxonomic history with numerous
infraspecific taxa that have been synonymized.

The thallus forms bushy, pendulous tufts 5-20 cm long, arising from a
single holdfast on bark. Branching is irregular to subdichotomous, with
numerous short lateral branchlets giving the thallus a ragged appearance.

Branches are circular in cross-section, 0.3-0.8 mm diameter, with a tough
central chord occupying roughly one-third of the branch diameter. The cortex
is smooth to somewhat roughened, greyish-green to yellowish-green, lacking
soredia.

Apothecia are rare but when present they are terminal, disc-shaped, 2-5 mm
diameter, with a pale brown to reddish-brown disc. Spores are bacillar,
7-12 × 2-3 µm, colourless, and multiseptate at maturity.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Usnea hirta* is widely distributed across temperate and boreal North
America, Europe, and Asia. In North America it ranges from Alaska to
northern Mexico, and in Europe from the Mediterranean to the Arctic Circle.

The species is epiphytic, growing primarily on the bark of deciduous and
coniferous trees in forests with good [[air-pollution-fungal-community-responses]],
particularly sulphur dioxide, and has declined significantly in
industrialized regions of Europe since the 19th century.

Elevation ranges from lowlands to approximately 2500 m in mountainous
areas. It also colonizes fence posts and weathered wood in rural settings.
