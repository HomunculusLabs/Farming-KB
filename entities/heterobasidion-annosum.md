# Heterobasidion annosum

Heterobasidion annosum is one of the most economically important forest pathogens in the temperate and boreal zones of the Northern Hemisphere. It causes annosum root rot and butt rot in coniferous trees, resulting in significant timber losses, increased tree mortality, and reduced [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]], and Asia.

## Taxonomy and Classification

H. annosum is a basidiomycete polypore in the Bondarzewiaceae within the Russulales. The species complex has been the subject of extensive taxonomic study, with several intersterility groups recognized that correspond to different host preferences among conifer species. These intersterility groups include:

- The "S" type (European), primarily attacking [[picea-abies]] (Norway spruce)
- The "P" type (European), primarily attacking [[pinus-sylvestris]] (Scots pine)
- The "F" type ([[comparison-brown-rot-vs-white-rot-bioremediation]] of roots and butts, progressively weakening the structural integrity of the tree
- Reduced growth rates due to root system damage
- Increased susceptibility to windthrow and secondary attack by insects and other pathogens
- Mortality of severely infected trees
- Internal decay of the lower stem, reducing timber quality

### Economic Significance

H. annosum results in estimated losses of billions of dollars annually in European and North American forestry. In Europe, it is considered the most economically important forest pathogen, affecting millions of cubic meters of timber annually. In North America, the F-type intersterility group causes comparable losses in fir and hemlock forests.

## Host Range

The H. annosum species complex infects a wide range of coniferous hosts including:

- Species of Pinus (pine) — particularly P. sylvestris, P. contorta, and P. ponderosa
- Species of Picea (spruce) — particularly P. abies and P. sitchensis
- Species of Abies (fir) — particularly A. alba and A. grandis
- Pseudotsuga menziesii (Douglas fir)
- Tsuga species (hemlock)
- Larix species (larch)

Different intersterility groups show host specialization, with some forms preferentially attacking pines and others attacking spruces or firs. This host specialization has important implications for [[plant-disease-management-solomon]] in mixed-species forests.

## Biological Control and Antagonists

Antagonists of the mycelium of H. annosum have been studied intensively as potential biocontrol agents, representing one of the most successful examples of applied biological control in forestry:

### Phlebiopsis gigantea

phlebiopsis-gigantea (syn. Peniophora gigantea) is the most successful competitor of H. annosum (Rishbeth 1963). Application of P. gigantea spore suspensions to freshly cut stumps immediately after felling is a widely used commercial biocontrol strategy in European forestry. The mechanism involves:

- Rapid colonization of stump surfaces by P. gigantea, preempting H. annosum infection
- Competitive exclusion of H. annosum basidiospore germination and establishment
- Possible production of antifungal metabolites
- A durable, long-term protective effect that can last for decades

### Scytalidium species

Among the antibiotically most active antagonists, Scytalidium species produce polyphenol oxidases that induce brown discoloration and contribute to competitive activity (Klingstrom and Johansson 1973). The enzymatic activity may degrade fungal cell wall components or produce toxic oxidation products.

### Other Antagonists

Additional antagonists that have been studied include various Trichoderma species and bacterial antagonists, though none has achieved the practical success of P. gigantea in forestry applications.
