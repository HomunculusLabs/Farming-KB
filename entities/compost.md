## Description

Compost is decomposed organic matter produced through the controlled biological breakdown
of plant and animal residues. It is a cornerstone of [[crumb-structure]], buffering pH, enhancing [[soil-life]], and
increasing water-holding capacity. Mollison distinguishes between hot (aerobic) and cold
(anaerobic) composting processes, each with different benefits, limitations, and applications.

## Classification

- **Category:** Organic soil amendment
- **Types:** Hot compost (aerobic, 50–60 C), cold compost (anaerobic/silage)
- **Related practices:** Mulching, green manuring, biogas digestion, vermicomposting
- **Temperature ranges:** Hot 55–60 C, Biogas 30–35 C, Cold ambient

## Key Benefits

- Preserves soil crumb structure and builds new aggregates.
- Buffers [[soil-ph]] against rapid changes.
- Improves sugar content and flavour of vegetables (confirmed in taste tests).
- Produces gums and gels that create crumb structure, aerate soil, and darken it for
  faster spring warming.
- Humic acids assist root development dramatically — even at 60 ppm carbon.
- Transforms compacted or mined-out soil into productive medium in 3–5 years.

## Hot Compost (Aerobic)

- Heats to 55–60 C (130–140 F), killing weed seeds and pathogens.
- Risk of severe nitrogen (ammonia) loss through volatilisation.
- Adding 1% superphosphate prevents ammonia escape.
- Chinese method: build heap with bamboo pole air tunnels, cover with mud, heat to
  55–60 C for a few days, then seal all holes for anaerobic completion — combines
  the seed-killing benefits of hot composting with the nitrogen conservation of cold.

## Cold Compost (Anaerobic/Silage)

- Does not lose nitrogen to volatilisation.
- Does not kill weed seeds — a significant limitation.
- Pitted or silage-style methods.
- Better nutrient conservation in the finished product.
- Sealed boxes allow either hot or cold processes.

## Production Methods (Ranked by Efficiency)

1. **Direct mulch on surface (above 12 cm):** Least effort, best energy use. Controls weeds.
2. **Composted then spread on gardens:** Most effort, some ammonia loss, effective.
3. **Applied to intensive plots and seed beds:** Good results with moderate effort.
4. **Drilled below surface or sprayed as liquid:** For specific nutrient delivery.
5. **Biogas production (30–35 C):** Energy yield as biogas plus nutrient-rich sludge.

## Rapid Composting (UK Method)

- Liquid manures sprinkled on straw in silos/tanks with forced air draught.
- Produces compost in about one week.
- Open piles encased in straw need 10 days.
- Suited to manurial sludge from biogas digesters.
- In cold climates, large piles (10–50 m3) provide slow heat for greenhouses over winter.
- Compost applied to soil in spring.

## Care in Selection of Materials

Contamination risks identified by Kevin Handreck (Organic Growing, Autumn 1987):

- **Containers:** Galvanized, copper, or brass release excess zinc and copper into wet
  manures. Use clay, stainless steel, glass, or iron instead.
- **Manures:** Pig, poultry, cattle, or sewage sludges add excessive copper, zinc, nickel,
  boron, lead, or cadmium — especially dangerous in acid sandy soils.
- **Worm casts:** From contaminated wastes can build up excess zinc.
- **Animal feeds:** Copper, arsenic, and other minerals commonly added to stock feeds.
- **Limit:** Maximum 10% worm casts in potting mixes.
- **Safe practice:** Grow green manures from leguminous hedgerows, windbreaks, intercrops.

## Compost Material Categories

- **(a) Bulking:** Nut husks, shells, coffee/tea/cocoa residues, shredded paper/branches,
  bark, woodchips, sawdust, old natural-fibre carpets and canvas.
- **(b) Weedy materials:** Hay with seed heads, weeds in flower, bulbous weeds — must be
  hot-composted to kill seeds.
- **(c) Nitrogen-rich:** Sewage, sullage, liquid manure, urine, meat/animal trimmings,
  household wastes. Add lime and 1% superphosphate for hot compost.
- **(d) Digestate:** Sludge from biogas digesters, weed-free manures.
- **(e) Concentrated:** Chicken/bird manures, animal litter, blood, bone, feathers, seaweeds.
- **(f) Liquids:** Dissolved minerals, urine, seaweed and manure teas.

## Water and Mulch Benefits

- 11% better water infiltration on low-tillage mulched plots (Conacher, 1979, Nigeria).
- Mulch buffers excessive soil temperatures — cooler by day, warmer by night/winter.
- Seed germination enhanced under mulch.
- Major nutrients (N, P, K) remain satisfactory long-term.
- Only under mulch do earthworm populations significantly increase.

## Relevance to Cultivation

- Single most valuable soil amendment in organic systems.
- Grow green manures as primary compost source for self-sufficiency.
- Return food wastes (including urine and well-composted faeces) to close nutrient loops.
- Avoid over-reliance on imported manures that may carry contaminants.

## Relevance to Mycology

- Compost piles are active fungal ecosystems — fungi are primary lignocellulose decomposers.
- Fungi comprise up to 50% of soil biomass and outperform bacteria on woody material.
- Thermophilic composting creates selective substrate favouring mushroom mycelium.
- Compost is the primary substrate for Agaricus bisporus (button mushroom) cultivation.
- Mycorrhizal networks thrive in compost-fed soils with high organic matter.
