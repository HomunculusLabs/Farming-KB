## Description

Serpula lacrimans (formerly Merulius lacrimans) is a brown-rot basidiomycete fungus commonly known as the true dry rot fungus. It is one of the most economically significant wood-decay fungi in the world, causing extensive damage to structural timber in buildings. It is notable for its ability to transport water and nutrients over long distances through specialized rhizomorphs (cords), allowing it to colonize wood that is not directly in contact with moisture.

## Key Facts

### Biology and Ecology
- Produces specialized rhizomorphs (mycelial cords) capable of long-distance transport of water and nutrients across nutritionally inert surfaces (Jennings, 1982).
- These cords allow S. lacrimans to cross non-nutritive barriers (brick, concrete, metal) to reach new wood substrates — a rare ability among fungi.
- Causes brown rot, selectively degrading cellulose and hemicellulose while modifying (but not fully removing) lignin, leaving wood darkened and cross-checked into cubical fragments.
- Capable of regulating its internal water balance, producing teardrop-shaped droplets (lacrimans = "weeping") on its mycelium.

### Ecological and Economic Significance
- One of the most destructive building pathogens worldwide, causing billions of dollars in damage annually.
- Studied extensively as a model organism for understanding fungal translocation and cord formation.
- Its ability to transport resources over meters through cord systems makes it relevant to understanding nutrient movement in forest ecosystems by analogous cord-forming fungi.

### Role in Fungal Ecology Research
- Referenced by Jennings (1982) as a key example of fungal movement from substrate to substrate over nutritionally inert surfaces.
- Demonstrates the principle that fungal mycelia are not confined to a single resource but can bridge gaps through translocation networks.
- Represents the "combatitive" (C-strategist) end of the life history spectrum — slow-growing but heavily invested in biomass and functional capabilities.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

While S. lacrimans itself is a destructive pest rather than a cultivated species, its biology offers key insights for mushroom cultivation. The cord-forming ability demonstrates how mycelial networks translocate nutrients over distance — the same mechanism cultivated species use to mobilize resources for fruiting body production. Understanding dry rot is essential for cultivators working in wooden grow rooms or using wood-based substrates, as contamination by S. lacrimans or related wood-decay fungi can compromise infrastructure. The brown-rot mechanism (selective cellulose removal) contrasts with the white-rot mechanism used by many cultivated species like shiitake and oyster mushrooms, which degrade both cellulose and lignin.
