## Mycogone perniciosa

## Taxonomy

Mycogone perniciosa Magnus is a [[spore-dispersal]]. Watering infected areas further spreads
this contaminant to healthy regions of the crop.

## Role in Mushroom Cultivation

Mycogone perniciosa is one of the most damaging [[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]].

This is a vigorous and resilient contaminant. Its spores are killed at 120 degrees F
when exposed to moist heat (pasteurization) for 24 hours. If Mycogone appears during the
first flush, its spores were probably introduced via the casing, either at the time of
application or during the spawn run through it, a period of about two weeks. Later
infestations are more probably spread by flies, workers, air currents, or other means.

## Control Measures

Effective control of Mycogone perniciosa requires a multi-faceted approach:

- **Casing Hygiene:** Use of clean [[mushroom-cultivator-casing-materials-buffering-agents]] is essential
- **Temperature Management:** Moderation of temperature, keeping conditions below the
  organism's preferred range
- **Isolation:** Without touching the casing, infected mushrooms should be removed from
  the bed
- **Alkaline Treatment:** The localized area should be sprinkled with salt, baking soda,
  or a similar alkaline substance after infected mushrooms are removed
- **Watering Restriction:** Do not water until the infected area has been treated
- **Chlorinated Water:** Kneebone recommends the use of chlorinated water (150 ppm)
  during normal crop watering to impede the germination of spores
- **Ventilation:** Increasing ventilation limits the spread of Mycogone
- **Bed Cleaning:** Proper bed cleaning techniques between cropping cycles are critical
- **Worker Hygiene:** Strict regimen of hygiene, especially between cropping cycles

## Interactions

Mycogone perniciosa is closely related to and often compared with other mushroom
pathogens. Its Verticillium-like conidiophores reflect a morphological similarity to
Verticillium species, another major mushroom pathogen. The organism shares habitat and
transmission pathways with Dactylium, Trichoderma, and Verticillium, and outbreaks of
Mycogone often occur alongside or precede infections by these other contaminants.
