# Gliricidia sepium

## Overview

Gliricidia sepium, commonly known as madre de cacao, Mexican lilac, or quick
stick, is a medium-sized nitrogen-fixing leguminous tree native to the dry
tropical forests of Mexico and Central America. It is one of the most widely
utilized multipurpose agroforestry trees in the tropics, valued for rapid
growth, tolerance of poor soils, and diverse applications as living fences,
shade for crops, green manure, animal fodder, and erosion control. The species
forms a symbiotic relationship with Rhizobium bacteria, enabling atmospheric
nitrogen fixation that significantly improves soil fertility.

The name "madre de cacao" (mother of cacao) reflects its traditional use as a
shade tree in cacao plantations throughout Central and South America.

## Taxonomy

**Kingdom:** Plantae → **Order:** Fabales → **Family:** Fabaceae →
**Subfamily:** Faboideae → **Tribe:** Robinieae → **Genus:** *Gliricidia* →
**Species:** *G. sepium* (Jacq.) Kunth ex Walp.

Synonyms include *Robinia sepium* Jacq., *Gliricidia maculata* (Kunth) Steud.,
and *Gliricidia rugosa* (Benth.) Walp. The genus contains only two species:
*G. sepium* and the rarer *G. sepium* var. *pallida*. The specific epithet
"sepium" derives from Latin "sepes" (hedge), referring to its widespread use
as a living fence.

## Morphology

Gliricidia sepium is a fast-growing deciduous tree reaching 10–15 meters with
a trunk diameter of 20–40 cm. In managed systems it is typically maintained as
a shrub of 2–5 meters through regular coppicing.

Bark is smooth to slightly rough, grayish-brown, with a whitish, bitter-tasting
inner bark. Leaves are alternate, odd-pinnately compound, 15–30 cm long, with
7–15 opposite leaflets per leaf; leaflets are ovate to lanceolate, 3–8 cm
long, dark green above, paler beneath. Flowers are showy, pink to light purple
or white, arranged in axillary racemes 5–15 cm long; each flower is
papilionaceous, 1.5–2.5 cm long, blooming during the dry season. Fruit is a
linear flat pod, 10–20 cm long, containing 4–10 brown seeds 8–12 mm long with
a hard seed coat benefiting from scarification. The root system includes a
deep taproot (up to 2 m) with extensive laterals and prolific nodulation.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to the Pacific coast of Mexico and Central America (Sinaloa to Costa
Rica), Gliricidia has been widely introduced throughout the tropics and is now
naturalized in Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia, the Pacific Islands, and
northern South America. It thrives in tropical and subtropical climates below
1,500 m elevation with 700–2,500 mm annual rainfall and a pronounced dry
season. It tolerates well-drained to moderately heavy soils including acidic
and slightly alkaline substrates but is intolerant of waterlogging. Full sun
is preferred; frost damages below 2°C. USDA Hardiness Zones 10–12.

## Agroforestry Applications

**Living fences:** Stem cuttings (1–2 m long, 5–10 cm diameter) root readily
in moist soil, establishing fences within 6–12 months that provide boundary
demarcation, windbreaks, and forage. A single hectare of living fence can
yield 5–10 metric tons of fresh foliage per year through regular pruning.

**Shade and intercropping:** In cacao, coffee, and tea plantations, Gliricidia
provides optimal shade levels (30–50% canopy cover) when pruned to 3–5 m.
Leaf litter adds nitrogen and organic matter. Alley cropping pairs Gliricidia
with maize, beans, or cassava, with pruned foliage incorporated as green
manure.

**Soil improvement:** Through biological nitrogen fixation (50–100 kg N/ha/
year), leaf litter decomposition, and root turnover, Gliricidia increases
soil nitrogen, organic carbon, and cation exchange capacity. It recovers
phosphorus from deep soil layers and cycles it to the surface through litter.

**Erosion control:** Deep root systems and rapid above-ground growth stabilize
slopes, riparian zones, and degraded lands effectively.

**Fodder:** Leaves contain 18–28% crude protein and are palatable to cattle,
goats, and sheep. Seeds and fresh bark contain toxic coumarin-derived
compounds; wilting leaves for 24–48 hours before feeding reduces anti-
nutritional factors.

## Propagation and Management

Propagation is primarily from large stem cuttings (1–3 m × 5–15 cm diameter).
Seed propagation requires scarification (hot water or acid treatment) and
produces more genetically diverse stands. Establishment spacing is 1–2 m for
living fences, 4–6 m for alley cropping, 3–5 m for shade trees. The tree
tolerates frequent coppicing (every 2–4 months for fodder, every 6–12 months
for mulch) and resprouts vigorously. Growth rate is 2–4 m/year in the first
3 years, with a productive lifespan of 20–30 years in managed systems.

## Pests, Diseases, and Environmental Notes

Gliricidia is relatively pest-resistant but can be affected by defoliating
caterpillars (*Eurema* spp.), stem borers, and fungal leaf spots
(*Pseudocercospora* spp.). The psyllid *Heteropsylla cubana* has caused
significant damage in some regions, though the tree typically recovers through
regrowth. In some tropical regions, Gliricidia has become invasive outside its
native range in disturbed habitats. Local risk assessments should be conducted
before introduction; sterile cutting propagation is preferred in sensitive
areas.
