# Pelton Wheel

## Description

The Pelton wheel is a type of water turbine designed by Lester Allan Pelton in
the 1870s. It is an impulse turbine that converts the kinetic energy of a
high-head water stream into rotational mechanical energy. In permaculture and
rural energy systems, the Pelton wheel (along with the related Turgo impulse
wheel) is cited as one of the most efficient devices for converting falling
water into shaft horsepower, achieving efficiencies of 80–95%.

## Classification

- **Type:** Hydraulic turbine (impulse type)
- **Category:** Micro-hydro energy generation
- **Inventor:** Lester Allan Pelton (1870s)
- **Domain:** Renewable energy, [[permaculture-energy-systems-and-conversion-efficiency]]
- **Working Principle:** Impulse energy conversion via split bucket design

## Key Facts

- Impulse-type water turbine suited for high-head, low-flow water sources
- Achieves 80–95% [[energy-conversion-efficiency-comparison]] from falling water to shaft horsepower
- When coupled to an air compressor, can supply compressed air for pneumatic tools
- Well-designed Pelton wheels and Turgo impulse wheels are among the most
  efficient water-to-power conversion devices available
- Suitable for small-scale and village-level energy systems
- Requires a head of water (vertical drop) — typically 10 metres or more
- Relatively simple to manufacture and maintain compared to reaction turbines
- Can be coupled directly to generators for electrical power production
- Operates most efficiently when the bucket speed is approximately half the jet speed
- The split-bucket design deflects water to both sides, balancing forces on the runner

## How It Works

The Pelton wheel exploits the impulse of a high-velocity water jet:

1. Water is directed through a nozzle, converting pressure head into a high-speed jet
2. The jet strikes the split buckets attached to the runner (wheel)
3. Each bucket splits the jet, deflecting it nearly 180 degrees
4. The momentum transfer pushes the bucket and rotates the wheel
5. Water exits the bucket at low velocity, having transferred most of its kinetic energy

The key innovation is the split bucket with a central ridge, which ensures the
jet is deflected smoothly to both sides without interfering with the incoming jet.

## Comparison with Other Water Wheels

| Device | Efficiency Range | Best Application |
|--------|-----------------|------------------|
| Undershot straight-paddle wheels | 18–20% | Low-head, high-flow streams |
| Undershot Poncelot (curved vane) | 65–70% | Moderate head streams |
| Breast water wheels (curved vanes) | 75–80% | Medium head |
| Overshot water wheels (well-designed) | 90%+ | High head |
| **Pelton / Turgo impulse wheels** | **80–95%** | **High head, low-to-moderate flow** |

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

1. **Off-Grid Cultivation Facilities:** [[duggar-mushroom-growing-history-and-methods]] operations and greenhouse
   cultivation require reliable electricity for [[mollison-pdc-trellis-systems-climate-control]], lighting, and
   pumping. A Pelton wheel system can provide this from a stream on the property.

2. **[[water-management]] Integration:** Permaculture designs incorporating swales,
   dams, and ponds at different elevations naturally create the head pressure
   needed for Pelton wheel operation.

3. **Pneumatic Systems:** When coupled to an air compressor, a Pelton wheel can
   power pneumatic systems for automated substrate handling in mushroom
   cultivation, or for spray systems in greenhouses.

4. **Sustainable Energy for Rural Agriculture:** For remote permaculture sites
   far from grid connections, micro-hydro via Pelton wheels offers the most
   consistent renewable energy source — it runs 24/7 unlike solar or wind.

5. **Low Environmental Impact:** Pelton wheels require no dams in many cases —
   they can operate from piped water taken from a stream, making them
   ecologically benign compared to large hydroelectric installations.

## Design Considerations for Permaculture Sites

- Survey the site for available head (vertical drop) and flow rate
- A minimum of 2 metres head with reasonable flow can produce useful power
- Penstock (pipe) design is critical — minimise friction losses
- Can be integrated with aquaculture ponds and [[irrigation-systems]]
- Consider seasonal variation in stream flow
- Pair with battery storage or pumped storage for consistent supply
