## Overview

**Lysimachia quadrifolia** is an erect herbaceous perennial in the family Primulaceae, native to [[lysimachia-terrestris]]** (swamp candles) and **Lysimachia hybrida**, with which it shares similar floral morphology and chromosome numbers.

Plants grow 30–90 cm tall from slender creeping rhizomes. Stems are erect, simple or sparingly branched, and typically unmarked or slightly angular.

Leaves are lanceolate to ovate-lanceolate, 4–10 cm long and 1–3 cm wide, with entire margins and acute apices. The characteristic whorls usually bear four leaves but may have five or six on vigorous stems.

The upper leaf surface is medium green and glabrous, while the lower surface is paler with scattered fine hairs along the midrib. Leaf margins are sometimes minutely ciliate.

Flowers are borne singly on short pedicels in the axils of the upper leaf whorls. The calyx is 4–6 mm long with five lanceolate green sepals.

The corolla is rotate, 10–15 mm in diameter, with five pale yellow petals. Each petal has a distinct reddish or brownish spot near the base, which aids in pollinator orientation.

Stamens are five, unequal in length, with filaments that are notably broader and flattened at the base, forming a small corona around the ovary. The ovary is superior and globose.

The fruit is a small globular capsule approximately 4 mm in diameter, containing numerous tiny seeds. Seeds are oblong, brown, and approximately 0.8 mm long.

## Distribution and Habitat

**Lysimachia quadrifolia** is widespread in eastern North America, from Quebec and Ontario south to Georgia and Alabama, and west to Minnesota and Arkansas. It is absent from the Atlantic coastal plain south of New Jersey.

The species occurs in deciduous and mixed woodlands, woodland edges, thickets, and partially shaded meadows. It prefers moist but well-drained soils rich in organic matter.

Elevation ranges from near sea level to approximately 1,200 meters in the southern Appalachian Mountains. Plants in higher-elevation populations tend to be shorter and more compact.

Associated species include *Maianthemum canadense*, *Smilacina racemosa*, *Aster divaricatus*, *[[lysimachia-punctata]]** or **[[lysimachia-nummularia]]**.

The main cultivation challenge is ensuring adequate moisture during dry spells. Plants may go dormant prematurely under drought stress but typically recover with the return of rainfall.

Unlike the aggressively spreading **Lysimachia nummularia** (creeping Jenny), **L. quadrifolia** is well-behaved in garden settings. Its modest rhizomatous spread makes it suitable for mixed borders without risk of becoming weedy.

## Uses and Ethnobotany

Traditional Native American ethnobotanical records indicate limited medicinal use of **L. quadrifolia**. The Cherokee used an infusion of the whole plant as an eye wash and for treating skin conditions.

No significant modern medicinal applications have been documented for this species. Phytochemical studies of related Lysimachia species have identified triterpenoid saponins and flavonoid glycosides.

The primary modern use of **L. quadrifolia** is in native plant gardens, woodland restoration projects, and ecological landscaping. It provides a delicate vertical element in shaded plantings.

Its whorled leaf arrangement and pale star-shaped flowers add textural interest to naturalistic gardens. The plant pairs well with ferns, wild geraniums, and other woodland perennials.
