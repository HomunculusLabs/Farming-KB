# Urtica dioica

Urtica dioica, commonly known as stinging nettle, common nettle, or simply nettle, is a
herbaceous perennial plant in the family Urticaceae. Widely distributed across temperate
regions of Europe, Asia, North Africa, and North America, it is one of the most
nutritionally dense wild plants and occupies a central role in permaculture as a dynamic
accumulator, compost activator, companion plant, medicinal herb, and multi-purpose forage
crop.

## Taxonomy and Etymology

The genus name Urtica is derived from the Latin urere, meaning to burn, referring to
the painful sting caused by the trichomes. The species epithet dioica means two houses in
Greek, referring to the plant's dioecious habit (separate male and female plants). The
family Urticaceae also includes ramie (Boehmeria nivea), an important fiber crop, and
various tropical ornamental species. Several subspecies of Urtica dioica are recognized,
with U. dioica subsp. dioica (European nettle) and U. dioica subsp. gracilis (American
nettle) being the most widely distributed.

## Botany and Identification

Urtica dioica is a perennial herbaceous plant growing 1 to 2.5 meters tall in favorable
conditions, though it typically reaches 0.5 to 1.5 meters. It produces extensive creeping
rhizomes (yellow underground stems) that spread horizontally 30 to 60 cm below the soil
surface, enabling rapid colony formation and making established nettle patches persistent
and extremely difficult to eradicate. Stems are erect, square in cross-section (a
characteristic shared with the mint family, Lamiaceae), unbranched or sparingly branched,
and covered with stinging and non-stinging hairs (trichomes). Stem color ranges from green
to reddish-green.

Leaves are opposite, ovate to cordate, 3 to 15 cm long and 2 to 8 cm wide, with serrate
margins (coarsely toothed), a pointed tip (acuminate apex), and a base that is typically
cordate (heart-shaped) on lower leaves and truncate or subcordate on upper leaves. The
upper leaf surface is dark green and sparsely pubescent, while the lower surface is
lighter green with denser hair coverage, particularly along the prominent veins. Leaf
petioles are 1 to 5 cm long and bear stipules at the base.

The stinging hairs (trichomes) are hollow, silica-reinforced structures 1 to 2 mm long,
tipped with a brittle, bulbous cap containing a mixture of histamine, acetylcholine,
[[serotonin]], formic acid, and leukotrienes. When touched, the tip breaks like a tiny
hypodermic needle and injects these compounds into the skin, producing the characteristic
burning, itching, and wheal formation (urticaria) that can persist for minutes to hours
depending on sensitivity. Non-stinging hairs are finer and flexible, providing a general
pubescence without stinging capability.

The species is dioecious, with separate male and female plants. Male flowers are
arranged in drooping, branched panicles 5 to 15 cm long, greenish-yellow, with four tepals
and four protruding stamens that release pollen readily when brushed. Female flowers are
in shorter, more compact, erect or spreading racemes, green, with two pairs of tepals that
enclose the ovary. Female flowers develop into small, dry, one-seeded achenes (1 to 2 mm),
enclosed by persistent tepals. Flowering occurs from June to September. Seeds are
dispersed by wind and water, and germinate readily on disturbed, nitrogen-rich soils. A
single large female plant can produce thousands of seeds.

## Nutritional Profile

Stinging nettle is one of the most mineral-rich wild edible plants available. Dried
nettle leaf contains approximately 25 to 30 percent protein (by dry weight), exceeding the
protein content of most leafy vegetables and rivaling some legumes. The amino acid profile
is well-balanced, containing all essential amino acids. The mineral content is
exceptional: dried nettle provides significant quantities of iron (up to 40 mg per 100 g
dry weight, approximately 200 percent of daily requirements), calcium (approximately 2,900
mg per 100 g, roughly 3 times the calcium content of dairy milk per equivalent dry
weight), magnesium (approximately 800 mg per 100 g), potassium (approximately 2,100 mg per
100 g), phosphorus, zinc, manganese, boron, and silica.

Vitamin content includes substantial amounts of vitamin A (as beta-carotene,
approximately 10,000 IU per 100 g dry), vitamin C (approximately 333 mg per 100 g fresh
weight, higher than oranges), vitamin K, and several B vitamins including B1 (thiamine),
B2 (riboflavin), B3 (niacin), and folate. Once cooked, dried, or properly processed, the
stinging compounds are completely deactivated, rendering the leaves safe and palatable for
consumption. Young spring shoots and leaves are harvested before flowering for the best
flavor and nutritional quality, as the leaves become tougher and may develop cystoliths
(calcium carbonate deposits) after flowering that can irritate the urinary tract if
consumed in quantity.

Nettle is used in soups (nettle soup is a traditional spring dish across much of
Europe), pestos, herbal teas, smoothies, and as a spinach substitute in cooked dishes. The
flavor is mild, earthy, and similar to spinach with a slightly herbaceous quality. Dried
nettle leaf is a popular ingredient in herbal tea blends and can be stored for long
periods without significant nutrient loss.

## Dynamic Accumulator Properties

In permaculture, Urtica dioica is classified as a premier dynamic accumulator, meaning
it draws minerals from deep soil layers through its extensive root system and concentrates
them in above-ground biomass. Nettle is particularly noted for accumulating iron, calcium,
magnesium, potassium, nitrogen, sulfur, copper, and silica. This makes it an outstanding
biomass crop for compost production and liquid fertilizer.

Nettle tea (a fermented liquid extract made by steeping 1 kg of fresh nettle leaves in
10 liters of water for 2 to 4 weeks) is one of the most effective organic liquid
fertilizers available, providing a balanced NPK nutrient boost along with trace minerals
and growth-promoting compounds. The fermentation process produces a dark, odoriferous
liquid that should be diluted 1:10 to 1:20 before use. It is used as a foliar spray and
soil drench, promoting vigorous growth in vegetable crops, fruit trees, and ornamentals.
Nettle is also a powerful compost activator, its nitrogen-rich leaves (C:N ratio of
approximately 15:1) accelerating the [[decomposition]] of carbonaceous materials in compost
piles. A layer of nettle greens added to each 10 to 15 cm of carbon material in a compost
heap significantly increases decomposition temperature and speed.

## Companion Planting and Ecological Functions

Nettle serves several important ecological functions in garden and farm systems. It is
a host plant for the larvae of numerous butterfly species, including the small
tortoiseshell (Aglais urticae), peacock (Aglais io), red admiral (Vanessa atalanta),
painted lady (Vanessa cardui), and comma (Polygonia c-album), making it a keystone species
for [[pollinator-conservation]] in temperate regions. These butterflies rely exclusively or
primarily on nettle as a larval food source.

The presence of nettle patches in or near vegetable gardens has been empirically
observed to improve the health and pest resistance of neighboring crops, though the
mechanisms are not fully understood and may involve improved soil biology, volatile
compound signaling, or enhanced predatory insect populations. Nettle is believed to
release volatile compounds that deter certain pest insects while attracting beneficial
predatory insects such as ladybirds (Coccinellidae), lacewings (Chrysopidae), and
parasitic wasps (Ichneumonidae, Braconidae). It is often interplanted with tomatoes to
improve their flavor and disease resistance, a practice supported by traditional gardening
experience and some horticultural research.

as an alternative food source (a biological reservoir) for predatory insects, reducing

rarely cause significant damage to the nettle itself.

## Medicinal Properties

Nettle has an extensive history of use in European herbal medicine spanning over 2,000
years, documented by Dioscorides, Pliny the Elder, and virtually every subsequent herbal
tradition. Key medicinal applications include treatment of benign prostatic hyperplasia
(BPH), where multiple clinical trials have demonstrated modest but statistically
significant improvements in urinary flow rate, post-void residual volume, and
International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS). The mechanisms may involve 5-alpha-reductase
inhibition, anti-inflammatory effects on prostate tissue, and modulation of sex hormone
binding globulin (SHBG) levels.
