## Overview

**Urtica dioica**, commonly known as stinging nettle, is a perennial herbaceous flowering plant in the family Urticaceae. It is native to Europe, Asia, northern Africa, and western North America, and is widely naturalized across temperate regions worldwide.

Despite its notorious stinging hairs (trichomes), which deliver histamine, acetylcholine, and serotonin-containing venom upon contact, U. dioica has been used for millennia as food, medicine, and fiber. It is among the most nutritionally dense wild greens available and has a long history in European herbal medicine dating to antiquity.

Nettle has experienced a resurgence in permaculture and ecological agriculture due to its role as a dynamic accumulator, its value as a companion plant, and its utility in producing nutrient-rich liquid fertilizers from biomass.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:**

- Kingdom: Plantae
- Division: Tracheophyta
- Class: Magnoliopsida
- Order: Rosales
- Family: Urticaceae
- Genus: *Urtica*
- Species: *U. dioica* L.

The species was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The specific epithet "dioica" refers to the dioecious nature of the plant, with separate male and female individuals. Several subspecies are recognized, including subsp. *dioica* (European) and subsp. *gracilis* (North American).

**Morphology:**

U. dioica grows 1–2 m tall in the growing season, dying back to the ground in winter. Stems are square (characteristic of Lamiaceae allies), erect, and covered with stinging and non-stinging trichomes. Leaves are opposite, cordate at the base, serrate on margins, 3–15 cm long, with a prominent central vein.

The stinging trichomes (cystoliths) are hollow silica-tipped hairs that function like hypodermic needles. When touched, the tip breaks and injects a cocktail of bioactive compounds including histamine, acetylcholine, serotonin, and formic acid, producing the characteristic burning, erythematous reaction.

Inflorescences are axillary panicles or spikes, with male flowers in branched racemes and female flowers in catkin-like clusters. Flowers are wind-pollinated and inconspicuous, greenish-white to yellowish.

## Distribution and Habitat

U. dioica is native to temperate regions of Europe, Asia (especially the Caucasus and Siberia), and northern Africa. In North America, it is both native (subsp. *gracilis*) and naturalized from European introduction. It has been introduced to South America, Australia, and New Zealand.

The species thrives in nitrogen-rich soils and is an indicator of fertile ground. Common habitats include:

- Disturbed sites, floodplains, and riparian zones
- Forest edges, clearings, and abandoned farmland
- Waste grounds, roadsides, and urban ruderal areas
- Areas with high soil nitrogen from animal waste or decomposition

Nettle spreads aggressively through rhizomatous root systems that can extend 2+ meters horizontally. This rhizomatous growth makes it both a valuable ground cover in permaculture systems and an aggressive weed in conventional agriculture. It tolerates a wide pH range (5.0–8.0) but prefers slightly acidic to neutral soils.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

U. dioica contains a diverse array of bioactive phytochemicals:

- **Flavonoids**: Quercetin, kaempferol, and rutin glycosides with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity.
- **Phenolic acids**: Caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, and ferulic acid contribute to antioxidant capacity.
- **Lignans**: Secoisolariciresinol and neoolivil, particularly concentrated in roots, with documented 5α-reductase inhibitory activity relevant to benign prostatic hyperplasia.
- **Lectins (UDA)**: Urtica dioica agglutinin, a small lectin with immunomodulatory and antiviral properties.
- **Carotenoids**: β-carotene, lutein, and lutein epoxide, contributing to provitamin A activity.
- **Minerals**: Exceptionally rich in iron (41.8 mg/100g dry), calcium (481 mg/100g dry), magnesium, potassium, and zinc.

Clinical evidence supports the use of nettle root extract for symptomatic relief of benign prostatic hyperplasia, with multiple randomized controlled trials demonstrating improvements in International Prostate Symptom Scores and urinary flow parameters. Leaf preparations have shown efficacy in allergic rhinitis and osteoarthritis in clinical trials.

## Culinary and Nutritional Use

Young nettle shoots (collected before flowering in spring) are among the most nutritious wild greens available:

- Protein: 25–30% of dry weight (exceptionally high for a leafy green)
- Iron content exceeds spinach by 2–3 fold
- Vitamin A (as carotenoids): 5,000–7,700 µg/100g dry
- Vitamin C: 333 mg/100g fresh (higher than citrus when fresh)
- Calcium: 481 mg/100g dry

Culinary applications include nettle soup (traditional across Northern and Eastern Europe), nettle pesto, nettle tea, and cooked greens as a spinach substitute. Cooking or drying neutralizes the stinging trichomes, rendering the plant safe to handle and consume.

Nettle has historically been used as a rennet substitute in cheese-making, as the plant contains enzymes that coagulate milk. Nettle beer and nettle cordial are traditional beverages in Britain and Scandinavia.

## Permaculture and Agroforestry Applications

U. dioica is one of the most valuable plants in permaculture and ecological design:

- **Dynamic accumulator**: Deep roots mine subsoil minerals (iron, calcium, magnesium, potassium, nitrogen) and concentrate them in above-ground biomass. Nettle biomass contains 3–7% nitrogen on a dry weight basis, rivaling legumes.
- **Liquid fertilizer (nettle tea)**: Fermented nettle biomass produces a nitrogen-rich liquid fertilizer widely used in organic agriculture. Typical dilution: 1:10 with water for foliar or soil application.
- **Compost activator**: Nettle shoots added to compost piles accelerate decomposition through their nitrogen content and diverse microbial associations.
- **Biodiversity support**: Serves as a larval food plant for numerous butterfly species including the small tortoiseshell (*Aglais urticae*), peacock (*Aglais io*), and red admiral (*Vanessa atalanta*). Ladybird populations are enhanced in nettle stands.
- **Companion planting**: Dense nettle patches improve disease resistance in neighboring plants and increase essential oil content in aromatic herbs.
- **Fiber crop**: Historically, nettle stems were processed for textile fiber comparable to linen. Nettle fiber production is experiencing a commercial revival as a sustainable alternative to cotton.

## Traditional Medicine and Ethnobotany

Nettle has an extensive ethnobotanical record spanning at least 3,000 years. Hippocrates documented 61 nettle-based remedies in the 4th century BCE. Dioscorides recommended nettle for respiratory ailments, joint pain, and wound healing in *De Materia Medica*.

In European folk medicine, nettle was used as a diuretic, anti-rheumatic, and galactagogue. Urtication — the deliberate application of fresh stinging nettles to the skin — was a traditional treatment for rheumatoid arthritis and chronic joint pain, supported by modern evidence that the histamine and serotonin in trichomes produce a counter-irritant effect that modulates pain signaling.

In Ayurvedic medicine, nettle is classified as a rasayana (rejuvenating herb) and used for respiratory conditions, kidney stones, and anemia. Traditional Chinese medicine uses nettle (called "xun ma") for dispersing wind-heat and treating skin eruptions.

Modern herbalists most commonly use nettle for seasonal allergies, benign prostatic hyperplasia, arthritis, and as an iron supplement for anemia. The dried leaf is preferred for allergies and nutritional supplementation, while root extracts are used specifically for prostate conditions. Nettle seed has gained attention in recent years as an adaptogenic substance, with preliminary evidence suggesting nephroprotective and regenerative effects on kidney tissue.

## Cultivation and Propagation

U. dioica can be propagated from seed, root cuttings, or rhizome division. Root division is the preferred method for establishing dense stands, as seeds have low germination rates without cold stratification.

Nettle grows vigorously in most temperate soils and requires minimal care once established. It thrives in full sun to partial shade and benefits from regular moisture. In permaculture systems, nettle is often planted in guild configurations with fruit trees and berry bushes, where its dynamic accumulator properties cycle nutrients from deep soil layers into the root zone of companion plants.

Harvesting for culinary or medicinal use is typically done in spring when shoots are young and tender (before 30 cm height). Cutting back encourages regrowth and multiple harvests per season. For fiber production, stems are harvested in late summer when fully mature, then retted in water to separate the bast fibers from the woody core.
