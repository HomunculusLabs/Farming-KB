## Overview

**Canna edulis** Ker Gawl., commonly known as **achira**, **canna**, or **Queensland arrowroot**, is a perennial rhizomatous plant in the family Cannaceae, native to the Andean region of South America. It is cultivated primarily for its edible starchy rhizomes, which have been a food source for indigenous Andean peoples for thousands of years.

The species is both an important traditional food crop and a widely grown ornamental plant. It produces large, banana-like leaves and showy flowers in shades of red, orange, and yellow, making it a dual-purpose species for food production and landscape design.

Despite its potential as a high-yielding carbohydrate source, *C. edulis* remains underutilized outside its native range. Interest in climate-resilient root crops has renewed attention to achira as a candidate for tropical and subtropical food systems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- Kingdom: Plantae
- Division: Angiosperms
- Class: Commelinids
- Order: Zingiberales
- Family: Cannaceae
- Genus: *Canna*
- Species: *C. edulis*

**Authority:** Ker Gawl. (1817), published in *Botanical Register*.

**Synonyms:**

- *Canna discolor* Lindl.
- *Canna orientalis* Roscoe (in part)
- *Canna esculenta* Lodd.

The taxonomy of cultivated *Canna* is complicated by centuries of hybridization. Many ornamental cannas sold commercially are complex hybrids of uncertain parentage involving *C. edulis* and other species.

**Growth habit:** Herbaceous perennial, 1.5–3 m tall. Erect, unbranched stems arising from a basal clump of thick rhizomes. Forms dense stands through vegetative spread.

**Leaves:** Large, alternate, ovate to oblong-lanceolate, 30–60 cm long and 15–30 cm wide. Bright green, sometimes with bronze or purple tints. Petioles sheathing at the base, wrapping around the stem.

**Flowers:** Terminal racemes, 20–40 cm long, bearing showy asymmetric flowers 5–8 cm across.tepals in red, orange, yellow, or bicolor combinations. Labellum prominently marked. Blooming period varies by latitude; typically summer to early autumn.

**Rhizomes:** Thick, cylindrical, segmented tuberous roots 5–15 cm long and 3–8 cm in diameter. Skin brown to purplish. Flesh white to pale yellow, firm and starchy. A single mature plant can produce 3–10 kg of fresh rhizomes.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Canna edalis* is native to the **Andean highlands** of South America, with its center of origin in Peru, Bolivia, and Ecuador. It has been cultivated in this region for at least 4,000 years, as evidenced by archaeological rhizome remains.

The species is now grown pantropically, from sea level to approximately 2,500 m elevation. It thrives in **moist, warm environments** with full sun to partial shade. Optimal growth occurs at 20–30°C with annual rainfall exceeding 1,200 mm.

Soil preferences include fertile, well-drained loams with high organic matter content. The species tolerates periodic waterlogging and a wide pH range (5.0–7.5), making it adaptable to marginal agricultural sites.

Naturalized populations exist in tropical Africa, Southeast Asia, Australia (where it is known as Queensland arrowroot), and Pacific islands, largely as a legacy of historical cultivation.

## Nutritional Value and Culinary Use

The rhizomes are composed primarily of **starch (approximately 70–80% dry weight)**, making them one of the most starch-dense root crops available. The starch granules are notably large (40–100 µm), which gives achira starch unique functional properties.

**Macronutrients (per 100 g fresh weight):**

- Energy: ~80–100 kcal
- Carbohydrates: 18–23 g (predominantly starch)
- Protein: 1–2 g
- Fat: <0.5 g
- Dietary fiber: 2–3 g

**Key minerals:** Potassium, phosphorus, magnesium, and calcium in moderate amounts. Vitamin C content is low compared to other root vegetables.

**Culinary applications in the Andes:**

- Rhizomes are peeled, sliced, and boiled or roasted
- Boiled slices are eaten as a starchy vegetable side dish
- Flour ground from dried rhizomes is used for breads and porridges
- The starch is extracted and used as a thickening agent (arrowroot substitute)
- In Colombia, achira is traditionally consumed during Holy Week

**Processing:** The large starch granules gelatinize at a relatively high temperature (~65–72°C), producing a clear, glossy gel with excellent freeze-thaw stability. This makes achira starch commercially interesting for food industry applications.

## Cultivation

Achira is propagated vegetatively through **rhizome division**, as most cultivated clones are sterile triploids that produce little or no viable seed.

**Planting:** Rhizome pieces with 2–3 buds are planted 5–10 cm deep, spaced 60–100 cm apart in rows 100–150 cm apart. Planting is typically done at the onset of the rainy season.

**Growth cycle:** 8–12 months from planting to harvest. Plants reach maximum leaf area by 3–4 months. Flowering begins at 4–6 months. Rhizome bulking accelerates during months 6–10.

**Yields:** Well-managed plantings produce 20–50 tonnes/ha of fresh rhizomes, with exceptional yields exceeding 70 t/ha under optimal conditions. These yields compare favorably with cassava (10–25 t/ha) and sweet potato (15–25 t/ha).

**Pest and disease pressure is relatively low.** The most significant pest is the **canna leaf roller** (*Calpodes ethlius*), a skipper butterfly larva that binds leaves together. Fungal diseases include leaf spot (*Cercospora* spp.) and rust (*Puccinia thaliae*).

## Permaculture and Agroforestry Applications

*Canna edulis* offers several valuable functions in designed ecosystems:

- **Erosion control:** Dense root mats and rhizome networks stabilize soil on slopes and riparian margins
- **Water filtration:** Root systems absorb excess nutrients from greywater and runoff
- **Ornamental food production:** Showy flowers provide aesthetic value alongside food yield in home gardens
- **Mulch generation:** Large leaves provide abundant biomass for sheet mulching
- **Edge planting:** Thrives at the interface between aquatic and terrestrial zones

The species integrates well in **food forest** understories where filtered light and moist conditions prevail. Its height and leaf area make it a useful windbreak for smaller crops.
