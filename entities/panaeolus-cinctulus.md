# Panaeolus cinctulus

**Panaeolus cinctulus**, commonly known as the **banded mottlegill**, is a widely distributed
psilocybin-containing mushroom in the family Bolbitiaceae. Formerly classified as
[[panaeolus-subbalteatus]] and sometimes referred to by that name in older literature,
P. cinctulus is one of the most cosmopolitan psychoactive mushrooms, found across temperate
and subtropical regions worldwide. It was also briefly placed in the genus Copelandia
by some authors before molecular phylogenetics clarified its placement in Panaeolus.

## Taxonomy and Nomenclature

The taxonomic history of Panaeolus cinctulus has been particularly turbulent, leading to
considerable confusion in both the scientific and popular literature:

- Originally described as **Agaricus subbalteatus** by Berkeley and Broome in 1871
- Later transferred to Panaeolus as **P. subbalteatus**
- Some authorities placed it in Copelandia due to its bluing reaction
- Molecular studies by Gerhardt and others confirmed its placement in Panaeolus
- The currently accepted name **Panaeolus cinctulus** was established following
  nomenclatural review, with "cinctulus" (Latin for "belted" or "girdled") referring
  to the characteristic zonate banding on the cap

The synonymy with P. subbalteatus is the source of the most persistent confusion. Many
field guides and online resources still use P. subbalteatus, and the species is widely
known by that name among mushroom enthusiasts. The transfer to the name cinctulus
followed priority rules in botanical nomenclature, as an earlier epithet was discovered
that had priority over subbalteatus. This nomenclatural change has been slow to be adopted
in popular mycology references.

## Description

**Cap:** Convex to broadly convex, sometimes nearly plane with age. Surface is smooth and
hygrophanous, typically dark brown to reddish-brown when moist, with a distinct paler zone
or band near the margin creating a two-toned appearance that gives the species its common
name "banded mottlegill." As the cap dries, it fades to a grayish-tan color. Cap diameter
ranges from 15 to 40 mm, occasionally larger. The cap margin is often slightly
translucent-striate when moist.

**Gills:** Adnate to slightly adnexed, moderately close. The gills are characteristically
mottled, a defining feature of the genus Panaeolus. They appear grayish at first,
developing dark spots as spores mature on different parts of the gill surface at different
rates, creating a chequered black-and-white pattern at maturity. This uneven maturation
is caused by basidia that release spores asynchronously.

**Stem:** Cylindrical, equal, hollow. Surface is smooth, silvery-white to pale gray, often
with a slightly powdery coating. The base may be slightly thickened. Length ranges from
40 to 100 mm, with a thickness of 2 to 4 mm. A partial veil may be present in young
specimens but is evanescent and rarely leaves a ring zone on the stem.

**Spore print:** Black.

**Spores:** Ellipsoid to lemon-shaped in face view, with a distinct germ pore. Spore
dimensions are approximately 11 to 15 μm by 7 to 9 μm. The spore wall is thick and
smooth. Under phase-contrast microscopy, the spores appear dark brown to black due to
their pigmentation.

**Taste and odor:** Mild to slightly farinaceous, not distinctive.

**Bruising:** The stem base and occasionally the cap context bruise bluish or bluish-green
when damaged, though the bluing reaction is often less pronounced than in Psilocybe
species. This bluing indicates the presence of psilocybin and psilocin. The reaction may
take several minutes to develop and is best observed on freshly cut tissue.

**Microscopic features:** Basidia are two- and four-spored. Cheilocystidia are abundant,
ventricose to lageniform. The pileipellis is a cutis of parallel, narrow hyphae. Clamp
connections are present.

## Distribution and Habitat

Panaeolus cinctulus has a genuinely cosmopolitan distribution, documented from virtually
every continent:

- **North America:** Throughout the [[hallucinogenic-plants-native-to-united-states]] and southern Canada, particularly
  common in urban and suburban settings
- **Europe:** Widespread across the continent from the british isles to eastern europe
- **South America:** Reported from Brazil, Argentina, Colombia, and other countries
- **Asia:** Found in Japan, India, and [[fungi-of-southeast-asia]]
- **Oceania:** Documented in Australia and [[allen-new-zealand-psychoactive-fungi]]
- **Africa:** Scattered reports from southern and eastern regions

The species is remarkably adaptable in its habitat preferences. While many Panaeolus
species are strictly coprophilous (dung-inhabiting), P. cinctulus is a generalist:

- Horse and cow pastures (growing directly on dung or enriched soil)
- Compost piles and municipal mulch
- Garden beds and landscaped areas with rich organic matter
- Grass clippings and accumulated thatch
- Occasionally on straw or other plant debris
- Wood chip mulch in gardens and parks

Fruiting occurs from late spring through autumn in temperate regions, with peak fruiting
in early summer and again in autumn. In warmer climates, the species may fruit year-round
following rainfall.

## Chemistry

Chemical analysis of Panaeolus cinctulus has confirmed the presence of [[psilocybin-compound]] and
[[psilocin]] as the primary psychoactive alkaloids. Reported psilocybin concentrations
vary considerably between collections, ranging from approximately 0.1% to 0.8% of dry
weight. The variability likely reflects differences in genetics, substrate, environmental
conditions, and analytical methodology.

Other tryptamine compounds reported from P. cinctulus include:

- [[baeocystin]]: Present in minor quantities as a typical constituent
- [[norbaeocystin]]: Detected in trace amounts
- [[serotonin]]: Occasionally reported, possibly a biosynthetic intermediate
- Urea and related nitrogenous compounds: Present due to nitrogen-rich substrates

The lower psilocybin concentrations compared to many Psilocybe species mean that
P. cinctulus is generally considered a weak to moderate psychoactive mushroom, often
requiring larger collections for noticeable effects.

## Similar Species

Several non-psychoactive species closely resemble P. cinctulus:

- [[panaeolus-foenisecii-taxonomy-debate]]: Very common in lawns, does not bruise blue, non-psychoactive
- [[panaeolus-campanulatus]]: Lacks bluing and psychoactive properties
- [[panaeolus-acuminatus]]: More pointed cap, non-psychoactive
- [[panaeolus-fimicola]]: More conic cap, variable and generally negligible psilocybin
- deconica coprophila: Brown spores, lacks mottled gill pattern

The key distinguishing features are the banded two-tone cap, mottled gills, black spore
print, and bluing reaction on the stem base.

## Cultivation

Panaeolus cinctulus has been cultivated successfully on composted horse manure, enriched
straw substrates, and pasteurized grass-based substrates. It is generally considered more
demanding to cultivate than Psilocybe species due to its preference for specific
nitrogen-rich substrates and susceptibility to contamination.
