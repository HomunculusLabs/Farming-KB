# Verticillium dahliae

**Type:** Species (Ascomycete)
**Category:** Soil-Borne [[cervantes-fusarium-vascular-wilt-diseases]] Pathogen
**Also known as:** None

## Overview
Verticillium dahliae is a soil-borne ascomycete plant pathogen
that causes vascular wilt diseases in a remarkably wide range of
economically important crops. It persists in soil through the
formation of durable microsclerotia, making it one of the most
challenging plant pathogens to manage through conventional
[[mycorrhizal-agricultural-practices]]. Biocontrol against V. dahliae has been
attempted repeatedly with varying success.

The species has an exceptionally broad [[powdery-mildews-erysiphaceae-diversity-host-range]], infecting
hundreds of plant species across dozens of families. Major crop
hosts include potato, eggplant, tomato, cotton, olive,
strawberry, mint, and alfalfa. The economic impact of
Verticillium wilt is substantial, particularly in potato
production where the disease causes significant yield losses and
quality reductions across major growing regions worldwide.

V. dahliae belongs to the Ascomycota as an anamorphic
hyphomycete, producing conidia on whorled phialides arranged in
verticils (hence the genus name). It is one of two major
Verticillium species causing vascular wilt diseases, the other
being V. albo-atrum. The genus Verticillium is ecologically
diverse, containing both destructive pathogens and beneficial
mycoparasitic species.

## Taxonomy and Classification
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Sordariomycetes
- **Order:** Hypocreales
- **Family:** Plectosphaerellaceae
- **Genus:** Verticillium
- **Species:** V. dahliae

The teleomorph of V. dahliae has not been definitively
identified. Under modern classification, some Verticillium
species have been transferred to the genus Lecanicillium based on
molecular phylogenetic data, but V. dahliae retains its original
name. Related species include V. albo-atrum (another wilt
pathogen), V. biguttatum (a mycoparasite), and Lecanicillium
lecanii (an entomopathogen).

## Key Details

### Microsclerotia: Structure and Survival
Microsclerotia are the key to V. dahliae persistence and the
primary reason this pathogen is so difficult to control. These
melanized, compact masses of densely packed hyphae can survive in
soil for 10 to 15 years in the absence of a susceptible host.
Microsclerotia are formed in infected plant tissue and released
into soil upon decomposition. They germinate in response to root
exudates from susceptible plants.

Microsclerotia are dispersed through soil movement (tillage,
erosion), contaminated equipment, irrigation water, and infected
planting material. Their density in soil can be estimated by
selective plating on semiselective media, providing a measure of
disease risk before planting. The 10-15 year survival period far
exceeds typical rotation cycles, making [[comparison-crop-rotation-vs-cover-cropping]] largely
ineffective.

### Infection Process
Upon germination near a susceptible root, V. dahliae hyphae
penetrate the root surface and enter the vascular system (xylem
vessels). The fungus produces conidia within the vessels that are
carried upward in the transpiration stream, spreading the
infection throughout the plant. The resulting vascular occlusion,
caused by [[brassinosteroid-fungal-growth-promotion]], tylose formation, and gel production,
leads to wilting, chlorosis, stunting, and often plant death.
