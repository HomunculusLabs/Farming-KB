The plant produces distinctive horned or spined fruits that float on the water surface and contain a single large starchy seed.

Trapa bispinosa is economically important as a traditional food crop in South and East Asia, and ecologically significant as an invasive species in parts [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]].

It has been widely introduced outside its native range for cultivation and has naturalized in parts of Europe, North America, and Australia.

The species grows in shallow freshwater habitats including ponds, lakes, slow rivers, and irrigation ditches.

It prefers warm, still or slow-moving water with muddy bottoms and full sun exposure.

Trapa bispinosa tolerates a wide range of water depths, from 30 centimeters to 2 meters.

In its native range, it is a common component of rice paddy margins and village ponds.

## Ecology and Cultivation

Trapa bispinosa is an annual that reproduces entirely from seed. Seeds germinate in spring when water temperatures exceed 18 degrees Celsius.

The submerged stem grows rapidly during summer, reaching the surface within four to six weeks.

Floating leaf rosettes form a dense canopy that can shade out submerged vegetation and reduce water oxygen levels.

In introduced ranges, this shading effect makes Trapa bispinosa a problematic invasive species that alters aquatic [[hamilton-beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]] in traditional pond systems.

## Nutritional and Traditional Uses

The starchy seed of Trapa bispinosa is eaten raw, boiled, roasted, or dried and ground into flour.

In India, the dried seeds are known as singhara and are consumed during fasting periods, particularly in Hindu religious observances.

In China, the fresh or boiled nuts are eaten as a snack or used in stir-fries and soups.

Nutritionally, the seeds contain approximately 20 percent starch, 5 percent protein, and small amounts of fat, vitamins B and C, and minerals including potassium and phosphorus.
