## Overview

Trapa bispinosa, commonly known as the water caltrop or singhara nut, is an annual aquatic plant in the family Trapaceae (sometimes placed within Lythraceae).

It is native to warm temperate and [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]] of Eurasia and has been cultivated for its edible fruits for thousands of years.

The plant produces distinctive horned or spined fruits that float on the water surface and contain a single large starchy seed.

Trapa bispinosa is economically important as a traditional food crop in South and East Asia, and ecologically significant as an invasive species in parts [[berry-growing]] and Australia.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Trapa bispinosa was described by Roxburgh in 1832. The genus Trapa was traditionally placed in its own family Trapaceae, but modern classifications include it within Lythraceae based on molecular phylogenetic evidence.

The species is closely related to Trapa natans and has sometimes been treated as a variety or synonym of that species.

Trapa bispinosa is an annual plant with a submerged stem that can extend 1 to 5 meters from the rooted base.

The upper stem reaches the water surface and produces a rosette of floating leaves.

Floating leaves are rhombic to triangular, 4 to 8 centimeters long, with dentate margins and a swollen, spongy petiole that acts as a float.

Submerged leaves are finely dissected and feathery, resembling roots, and serve primarily in nutrient absorption.

Flowers are small, white, and borne singly in the leaf axils, with four petals approximately 8 millimeters long.

The fruit is a hard, bony nut 3 to 5 centimeters wide, typically armed with two sharp horns or spines.

Inside the fruit lies a single large seed rich in starch and protein, which is the primary edible portion.

## Distribution and Habitat

Trapa bispinosa is native to southern and eastern Asia, including India, Bangladesh, China, Japan, Korea, and [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]].

It has been widely introduced outside its native range for cultivation and has naturalized in parts of Europe, North America, and Australia.

The species grows in shallow freshwater habitats including ponds, lakes, slow rivers, and irrigation ditches.

It prefers warm, still or slow-moving water with muddy bottoms and full sun exposure.

Trapa bispinosa tolerates a wide range of water depths, from 30 centimeters to 2 meters.

In its native range, it is a common component of rice paddy margins and village ponds.

## Ecology and Cultivation

Trapa bispinosa is an annual that reproduces entirely from seed. Seeds germinate in spring when water temperatures exceed 18 degrees Celsius.

The submerged stem grows rapidly during summer, reaching the surface within four to six weeks.

Floating leaf rosettes form a dense canopy that can shade out submerged vegetation and reduce water oxygen levels.

In introduced ranges, this shading effect makes Trapa bispinosa a problematic invasive species that alters aquatic [[edaphic-determinants-cannabis-microbiome-community-structure]].

In parts of the northeastern United States, Trapa natans and related species have been subject to aggressive eradication programs using mechanical harvesters and herbicides.

Cultivation of Trapa bispinosa as a food crop involves broadcasting seeds into shallow ponds in spring and harvesting floating fruits in autumn.

Yields of 2 to 5 tonnes per hectare are typical in traditional cultivation systems.

The plant is relatively low-maintenance and does not require fertilization or [[hamilton-beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control]] in traditional pond systems.

## Nutritional and Traditional Uses

The starchy seed of Trapa bispinosa is eaten raw, boiled, roasted, or dried and ground into flour.

In India, the dried seeds are known as singhara and are consumed during fasting periods, particularly in Hindu religious observances.

In China, the fresh or boiled nuts are eaten as a snack or used in stir-fries and soups.

Nutritionally, the seeds contain approximately 20 percent starch, 5 percent protein, and small amounts of fat, vitamins B and C, and minerals including potassium and phosphorus.
