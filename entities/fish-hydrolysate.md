# Fish Hydrolysate

## Description

Fish hydrolysate is a liquid or paste produced by enzymatically breaking down whole fish or fish waste using enzymes (rather than heat and chemicals). It retains the full protein, oil, and mineral content of the fish, making it a potent fungal food and source of nitrogen, amino acids, and trace minerals for [[compost-tea-brewing]] and soil application. It is distinguished from fish emulsion, which uses heat processing that destroys some beneficial compounds.

## Classification

- **Category**: Organic fertilizer / compost tea ingredient
- **Production**: Cold enzymatic digestion of fish waste
- **Nutrient profile**: High in nitrogen, amino acids, oils, minerals
- **Microbial role**: Primarily a fungal food resource
- **Related products**: Fish emulsion (heat-processed), fish meal (dried, ground)

## Key Facts

1. **Fungal Food**: Fish hydrolysate is one of the primary fungal foods used in compost tea brewing. It favors fungal dominance in the tea, which is desirable for most perennial crops, trees, and vines that prefer fungal-dominated soils.

2. **Enzymatic vs Heat Processing**: Fish hydrolysate is made through cold enzymatic digestion, preserving proteins, oils, and amino acids in their natural form. Fish emulsion uses heat and chemical extraction, degrading many beneficial compounds. Ingham specifically recommends hydrolysate over emulsion.

3. **Nitrogen Source for Compost**: When compost temperature stays low due to antibiotic or chemical residues, adding fish hydrolysate as a nitrogen source can help restart microbial activity and get the composting process back on track.

4. **De-wormer Recovery**: Ingham specifically recommends fish hydrolysate when de-wormer residues in manure are suspected of suppressing composting activity. The additional nitrogen from fish hydrolysate feeds the bacteria that decompose these pharmaceutical compounds.

5. **Sticker-Spreader Role**: Fish hydrolysate can serve as a sticker-spreader in foliar compost tea applications, helping organisms adhere to leaf surfaces. However, it must be used carefully — too much can cause osmotic shock to the organisms.

6. **Amino Acid Content**: The amino acids in fish hydrolysate serve as direct chelating agents for minerals, improving micronutrient availability in both soil and foliar applications.

7. **C:N Ratio Contribution**: Fish hydrolysate adds nitrogen to the compost tea mix, helping balance the carbon-heavy ingredients like kelp, rock dust, and humic acids. The C:N ratio of the final tea determines whether bacterial or fungal organisms dominate.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Compost Tea Formulation
For fungal-dominated compost tea (appropriate for trees, vines, and perennials):
- Fungal compost as base
- Fish hydrolysate as primary nitrogen/fungal food
- [[humic-acid]], kelp, rock dust as additional fungal foods
- Molasses in moderation (favors fungi over bacteria at proper ratios)

### Soil Drench Applications
Fish hydrolysate in soil drenches provides slow-release nitrogen through microbial processing. Unlike synthetic nitrogen, the nitrogen in fish hydrolysate is immobilized in microbial biomass and released gradually through protozoan and nematode predation.

### Mushroom Substrate Supplementation
While not directly discussed by Ingham in this context, fish hydrolysate principles apply to [[mushroom-cultivation]]: protein-rich supplements feed the thermophilic and mesophilic organisms that precondition substrates. The amino acid and mineral content supports robust mycelial growth.

### Organic Certification
Fish hydrolysate is generally approved for organic production. Check with specific certification bodies (OMRI, IFOAM, state programs) for product-specific approval.

### Application Rates
- **Compost tea**: 1-2 tablespoons per 5 gallons of tea as fungal food
- **Soil drench**: Dilute according to product specifications
- **Compost additive**: Use when temperature stalls to restart microbial activity
- **Foliar spray**: Use carefully — dilute well to avoid osmotic shock to leaf organisms
