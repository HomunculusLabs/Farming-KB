Curcuma longa, commonly known as turmeric, is a perennial herbaceous
plant in the family Zingiberaceae. Native to the Indian subcontinent
and Southeast Asia, it has been cultivated for over 4,000 years for its
rhizomes, which are the source of the bright yellow-orange spice
turmeric. The species is the most commercially important member of the
Curcuma genus and is valued both as a culinary spice and as a medicinal
plant with a substantial body of modern pharmacological research
supporting its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer
properties. The primary bioactive compounds are curcuminoids, with
curcumin being the most studied.

The species was first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The genus
name derives from the Arabic "kurkum" meaning saffron or yellow, a
reference to the intense color of the rhizome. The specific epithet
"longa" means long, referring to the elongated rhizome shape. Common
names include turmeric (English), haldi (Hindi), haridra (Sanskrit),
jiang huang (Chinese), and ukon (Japanese). The plant is sometimes
referred to as "Indian saffron" due to its use as a saffron substitute
in some culinary traditions.

The taxonomy of Curcuma has been revised multiple times, and several
synonyms exist including Curcuma domestica Valeton. The species is
distinguished from related Curcuma species by its large rhizome size,
the orange-yellow internal color, and the presence of a prominent
central "mother" rhizome with lateral "fingers."

## Botanical Description

### Growth Habit
Curcuma longa is a rhizomatous herbaceous perennial growing to 0.6-1.0 m
tall. It produces annual leaf shoots from the underground rhizome
system each growing season. The plant dies back to the rhizome during
dry or cool periods. Under cultivation, the crop cycle is typically
7-10 months from planting to harvest.

### Leaves
Leaves are large, elliptic to lanceolate, 30-60 cm long and 8-20 cm
wide, arranged alternately along a pseudostem formed by overlapping leaf
sheaths. The leaf surface is dark green above and lighter green below,
with a distinct midrib. Leaf margins are entire. The leaves have a
subtle aromatic fragrance when crushed.

### Inflorescence
The inflorescence is a cylindrical spike or cone, 10-15 cm long,
emerging from the rhizome separately from the leaf shoot or
occasionally appearing alongside it. The spike bears numerous bracts;
the upper bracts are white or pale green with pink tips, while the
lower bracts are green. True flowers are yellowish-white, three-petaled,
and relatively inconspicuous compared to the showy bracts.

### Rhizome
The rhizome is the primary economic organ. It is thick, fleshy, and
knobby, consisting of a central "mother" rhizome (bulbous primary
rhizome) and numerous lateral "fingers" (secondary rhizomes) extending
outward. Externally, the rhizome is brownish-yellow with a rough,
ringed surface. Internally, the flesh is deep orange-yellow to bright
orange, with a characteristic earthy aroma and bitter, slightly warm,
pungent taste. Fresh rhizomes contain 70-80% moisture.

## Chemical Composition

### Curcuminoids (Primary Bioactives)
| Compound | Proportion of Curcuminoids | Key Properties |
|----------|--------------------------|----------------|
| Curcumin (diferuloylmethane) | 60-70% | Major bioactive, anti-inflammatory |
| Demethoxycurcumin | 15-25% | Antioxidant, anti-inflammatory |
| Bisdemethoxycurcumin | 10-15% | Antimicrobial, antioxidant |
| Cyclocurcumin | Trace | Formed during processing |

Total curcuminoid content in dried turmeric rhizome typically ranges
from 2-9%, with commercial turmeric powder averaging 3-5%. The
curcuminoid content varies significantly with cultivar, growing
conditions, harvest timing, and processing methods.

### Essential Oil
The essential oil (2-7% of dry weight) contains:

- **Turmerone** (ar-turmerone, alpha-turmerone, beta-turmerone):
  30-50% of the oil, contributes to aroma and has bioactive properties
- **Zingiberene**: 10-20%, warm spicy aroma
- **Curcumene**: 5-15%
- **Borneol**: 2-5%
- **Cineole**: 1-3%
- **Various sesquiterpenes and monoterpenes**: remainder

### Other Constituents
- Polysaccharides (ukonan A-D): immunomodulatory activity
- Proteins (6-8% dry weight)
- Minerals: calcium, iron, potassium, manganese, zinc
- Vitamins: vitamin C, niacin (B3), vitamin E
- Fiber and starch: bulk constituents

## Pharmacological Activities

Modern research has documented extensive pharmacological activities for
curcumin and turmeric extracts:

### Anti-inflammatory
Curcumin inhibits NF-kB, COX-2, LOX, and TNF-alpha signaling pathways.
It reduces prostaglandin E2 production and downregulates inflammatory
cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, IL-8). Clinical trials demonstrate efficacy in
osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis at doses of 500-1500 mg/day,
comparable to ibuprofen in some studies but with fewer gastrointestinal
side effects.

### Antioxidant
Curcumin scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen
species (RNS) directly. It upregulates endogenous antioxidant enzymes
including SOD, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase via Nrf2
activation. The phenolic hydroxyl groups and the beta-diketone moiety
are primarily responsible for radical-scavenging activity.

## Cultivation

### Growing Conditions
- **Climate**: Tropical to subtropical; optimal temperature 20-35°C
- **Rainfall**: 1000-2000 mm annually, well-distributed or with
  irrigation
- **Soil**: Well-drained loamy or sandy loam soils, pH 5.5-7.0
- **Propagation**: Primarily vegetative, using rhizome "seed" pieces
  (setts) weighing 25-40 g each with 1-2 viable buds
- **Planting**: At onset of monsoon or when soil temperature is
  consistently above 20°C; spacing 25-30 cm between plants in rows
  25-30 cm apart

### Crop Management
- Requires consistent moisture during the active growth period
- Shade tolerance is moderate; partial shade (30-40%) improves yield
  in hot climates
- Organic amendments (farmyard manure, neem cake) are preferred in
  traditional cultivation
- Fertilizer requirements: 60 kg N, 50 kg P2O5, 50 kg K2O per hectare
  (split applications)
- Weed management is critical during the first 2-3 months

### Harvest and Processing
- Harvest at 7-10 months when leaves begin to yellow and dry
- Rhizome yields: 15-25 tonnes/ha fresh weight under good conditions
- Processing: rhizomes are boiled (45-60 minutes), dried in sun or
  forced-air dryers (10-15% moisture), and polished to remove rough
  outer skin
- Polished turmeric has better market appearance but slightly reduced
  curcumin content compared to unpolished

## Ethnobotanical and Cultural Significance

Turmeric holds profound cultural significance across South and Southeast
Asia:
- Central to Hindu religious ceremonies, weddings, and festivals
- Used in Ayurvedic medicine for over 3,000 years (listed in
  Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita)
- Traditional Ayurvedic applications include wound healing, digestive
  disorders, respiratory conditions, skin diseases, and joint pain
- Used as a food preservative, natural dye, and cosmetic ingredient
- Applied topically as a paste for skin conditions and beauty treatments
- Integral component of curry powder and countless Asian cuisines
- In traditional Chinese medicine, used to invigorate blood and move
  Qi; prescribed for menstrual pain, chest pain, and traumatic injuries
