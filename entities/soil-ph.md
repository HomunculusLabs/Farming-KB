## Description

Soil pH (potential hydrogen) is a measure of the acidity or alkalinity of soil, expressed on a logarithmic scale from 0 (strongly acid) to 14 (strongly alkaline). It is one of the most fundamental parameters in soil and water science because it directly governs the solubility and availability of essential plant nutrients, trace elements, and the viability of soil organisms. In [[permaculture-design]] because it determines whether the nutrient mineral cycle can function. Mollison emphasises that gardeners should not obsess over a single pH number. The natural mosaic of pH across soil microsites means that roots can find suitable conditions if the overall range is kept between 4.5 and 10.0, and especially between 6.0 and 7.5. The most reliable strategy for pH management is building humus through mulching and composting, which provides natural buffering capacity far more resilient than repeated lime or sulphur applications. This aligns with the permaculture principle of working with natural systems rather than fighting them with annual chemical corrections.

## Measurement Methods

Soil pH is measured using pH meters with soil-water slurries (1:1 or 1:2.5 ratio).
Portable field meters provide quick readings, while laboratory analysis offers
higher precision. Indicator dyes offer a low-cost alternative with accuracy
to 0.5 pH units.

## Crop pH Preferences

Blueberries require pH 4.0-5.0, while most vegetables prefer 6.0-7.0.
Alfalfa thrives at pH 6.5-7.5. Understanding crop-specific pH needs guides
amendment decisions and crop rotation planning in permaculture systems.
