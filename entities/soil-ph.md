## Description

Soil pH (potential hydrogen) is a measure of the acidity or alkalinity of soil, expressed on a logarithmic scale from 0 (strongly acid) to 14 (strongly alkaline). It is one of the most fundamental parameters in soil and water science because it directly governs the solubility and availability of essential plant nutrients, trace elements, and the viability of soil organisms. In [[permaculture]] and broadacre agriculture, managing pH is central to maintaining productive, living soils without reliance on synthetic chemical inputs.

## Classification

- **Category:** Soil chemistry parameter
- **Domain:** Edaphology / soil science
- **Scale:** 0-14 (logarithmic, each unit = 10x change in H+ concentration)
- **Neutral point:** pH 7.0

## Key Facts

### Natural Range
- In nature, pH rarely falls below 1.9 (lime juice) or rises above 11.0 (alkali flats).
- Most productive agricultural soils operate between pH 4.5 and 10.0.
- The optimal range for most garden vegetables is pH 6.0-7.5.
- Below pH 4.0 or above pH 10.0, only specialised bacteria or extremophile organisms can survive.

### Acid Sources in Soil
- **Carbonic acid:** Formed when atmospheric CO2 dissolves in rainwater; weak but omnipresent.
- **Sulphuric acid:** Produced from industrial sulphur emissions or from pyrites in rocks.
- **Phosphoric and nitric acids:** Formed when phosphorus and nitrogen compounds react with water.
- **Silicic acid:** From dissolved silica.
- **Humic acids:** Derived from decomposing organic matter.

### Alkaline Sources in Soil
- Metallic oxides (bases) that form alkaline solutions with excess hydroxide ions (OH-).
- Common alkali-producing elements: lithium, sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium.
- Soluble solid bases: magnesium, calcium, potassium, barium, sodium, selenium.

### pH Is Not Constant
- pH exhibits diurnal and seasonal fluctuations driven by rain, plant growth, and temperature changes.
- It is a mosaic across soil crumb structure, varying at the surface of colloids and at microsites.
- Vertical gradients exist: surface mulches tend to be more acid, while deeper layers become more alkaline where evaporation, worm casts, and capillary action draw bases upward.
- Larger-scale mosaics are imposed by slope, rock type, and vegetation type.

### pH and Nutrient Availability
- pH directly controls the solubility of macro- and micronutrients.
- At extreme pH values, key nutrients become locked in insoluble compounds and unavailable to plants.
- The widest availability of most nutrients occurs in the pH 6.0-7.5 range.
- Humus acts as a powerful pH buffer; limed and mulched gardens rarely show deficiency symptoms even when pH fluctuates.
- Calcium (lime) is also an effective buffer.

### Practical Management
- Mulching with organic material buffers pH naturally.
- Adding lime (calcium carbonate) raises pH in acid soils.
- Sulphur amendments lower pH in alkaline soils.
- Root hairs can both create and seek out ideal pH microenvironments if gross imbalances are absent.
- Grinding mixed soil samples to damp pastes gives averaged pH, but this destroys the natural mosaic information.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Understanding soil pH is foundational to [[permaculture-design]] because it determines whether the nutrient mineral cycle can function. Mollison emphasises that gardeners should not obsess over a single pH number. The natural mosaic of pH across soil microsites means that roots can find suitable conditions if the overall range is kept between 4.5 and 10.0, and especially between 6.0 and 7.5. The most reliable strategy for pH management is building humus through mulching and composting, which provides natural buffering capacity far more resilient than repeated lime or sulphur applications. This aligns with the permaculture principle of working with natural systems rather than fighting them with annual chemical corrections.

## Measurement Methods

Soil pH is measured using pH meters with soil-water slurries (1:1 or 1:2.5 ratio).
Portable field meters provide quick readings, while laboratory analysis offers
higher precision. Indicator dyes offer a low-cost alternative with accuracy
to 0.5 pH units.

## Crop pH Preferences

Blueberries require pH 4.0-5.0, while most vegetables prefer 6.0-7.0.
Alfalfa thrives at pH 6.5-7.5. Understanding crop-specific pH needs guides
amendment decisions and crop rotation planning in permaculture systems.
