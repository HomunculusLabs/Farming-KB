## Panicum maximum (Guinea Grass)

## Description

Panicum maximum, commonly known as Guinea grass, is a large, tufted perennial tropical grass native to Africa and widely naturalized across tropical regions worldwide. It is one of the most important cultivated tropical forage grasses. In Bill Mollison"s *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual*, it is listed among the grass species as "guinea, Panicum maximum."

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Poaceae (Grass family)
- **Genus**: Panicum
- **Species**: P. maximum
- **Note**: Recently reclassified by some authorities as Megathyrsus maximus, though Panicum maximum remains in widespread horticultural and agricultural use.
- **Common names**: Guinea grass, green panic, buffalo grass (Caribbean), colonião (Brazil)

## Key Facts

- **Growth habit**: Large, tufted perennial grass growing 1–3 meters tall, sometimes reaching 4 meters under ideal conditions. Forms dense clumps with broad, arching leaves. Produces an extensive, deep root system.
- **Native range**: Tropical Africa, from Senegal eastward to Ethiopia and southward to South Africa. Introduced and naturalized throughout the tropics including the Americas, Southeast Asia, Pacific Islands, and northern Australia.
- **Climate adaptation**: Highly adapted to warm, humid tropical and subtropical environments with annual rainfall of 800–2500 mm. Shows moderate shade tolerance, making it suitable for understory use in agroforestry and silvopastoral systems. Can survive dry seasons of 4–6 months.
- **Soil requirements**: Adaptable to a wide range of well-drained soils. Prefers fertile, moist loams but tolerates sandy and clay soils. pH range approximately 5.0–8.0. Does not tolerate prolonged waterlogging.
- **Seed production**: Produces large quantities of small seeds that are dispersed by wind and animals. This natural seeding ability contributes to its aggressive colonization of disturbed sites and its widespread naturalization.
- **Shade tolerance**: Notably more shade-tolerant than most other tropical grasses, maintaining reasonable productivity under 30–50% shade. This characteristic makes it particularly valuable in agroforestry designs.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

### Silvopastoral Systems

The shade tolerance of Guinea grass makes it the premier grass species for silvopastoral systems — the integration of trees, forage, and livestock. It grows productively beneath scattered canopy trees, beneath plantation timber, and in the alleys between hedgerows. In permaculture designs incorporating food forests or timber belts, Guinea grass provides a productive understory pasture layer that many other grasses cannot match under partial shade.

### High-Yield Forage Production

Guinea grass is one of the most productive tropical grasses, yielding 15–40 tonnes of dry matter per hectare per year under good management. It provides nutritious forage for cattle, goats, and other ruminants, with crude protein levels of 7–14% depending on growth stage and fertility. In cut-and-carry systems common in smallholder permaculture, it provides reliable daily fodder harvests.

### Biomass for Mulch and Compost

The high biomass production of Guinea grass makes it an excellent source of mulch material in permaculture systems. Regular slashing produces large volumes of carbon-rich organic matter that can be applied directly to crop beds, used in compost piles, or fed to livestock. In food forest management, Guinea grass mulch helps suppress weeds, retain soil moisture, and moderate soil temperature.

### Weed Suppression and Pioneer Function

The vigorous, competitive growth of Guinea grass enables it to suppress many weed species through shading and root competition. In permaculture site establishment, it can be used as a pioneer cover crop to occupy ground, build soil organic matter, and suppress undesirable vegetation. However, this same vigor means it can also suppress desirable crops, so it must be managed carefully in mixed plantings.

### Erosion Control

The dense, fibrous root system of Guinea grass provides excellent soil binding and erosion control on slopes and disturbed sites. It is frequently used for roadside stabilization, gully control, and stream bank protection in tropical regions. In permaculture earthworks design, it can be planted on swale berms, dam walls, and contour banks.

### Integration with Legumes

Guinea grass combines exceptionally well with climbing tropical legumes such as siratro (Macroptilium atropurpureum), centro (Centrosema pubescens), and puero (Pueraria phaseoloides). The grass provides a climbing frame for the vines while the legumes contribute nitrogen and improve the protein content of the forage. These grass-legume associations are self-sustaining and require minimal external nitrogen input.

## Management Considerations

Guinea grass requires regular defoliation to maintain forage quality — uncut plants become tall, stemmy, and less palatable. Under heavy continuous grazing, it can be grazed out over time; rotational grazing with adequate rest periods maintains persistence. The species can become weedy and invasive in some environments, particularly in disturbed native ecosystems. In permaculture designs, containment strategies such as mowed borders or physical barriers may be needed to prevent unwanted spread into garden beds.

## Varieties and Cultivars

Key cultivars include 'Hamil' (robust, high-yielding), 'Makueni' (drought-tolerant), 'Tobiata' (leafy, late-flowering), 'Tanzania' (upright, productive), and 'Petrie' (Green Panic — a finer-leafed, more shade-tolerant type). Select cultivars based on local conditions, shade levels, and intended use.

### Role in Tropical Soil Organic Matter Building

Guinea grass contributes significantly to soil organic matter pools in tropical systems. The substantial root biomass, combined with regular leaf litter and slash returns, builds humus layers that improve soil structure, water-holding capacity, and cation exchange capacity. On degraded tropical soils where organic matter has been depleted, Guinea grass can restore soil health within 3–5 years under proper management.

### Carbon Sequestration Potential

The high biomass production and deep root systems of Guinea grass contribute to carbon sequestration in tropical soils. Root carbon inputs at depth are particularly valuable, as deep soil organic carbon is more stable and less prone to decomposition than surface carbon. In permaculture systems designed for carbon farming, Guinea grass can be a productive component of carbon-positive land management.

### Integration with Permaculture Zones

Guinea grass fits primarily in Zone 3 (broad-scale farming) and Zone 4 (forest grazing/wild food gathering) in permaculture zone planning. In Zone 3, it provides the forage base for livestock integration. In Zone 4, it supports extensive grazing under scattered trees. Its shade tolerance means it can extend into the edges of Zone 2 (orchard/food forest) where it serves as a productive ground cover and mulch source.

### Cultural and Historical Significance

Guinea grass was one of the first African grasses to be widely distributed throughout the tropical world during the colonial period, valued for its productivity and adaptability. It has played a central role in the development of tropical ranching systems in the Americas, Pacific Islands, and Southeast Asia. In traditional African pastoral systems, it has supported livestock production for centuries.
