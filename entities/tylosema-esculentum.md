## Overview

Tylosema esculentum, commonly known as the marama bean or morama bean, is a perennial tuberous legume native to the [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-arid-regions]] of southern Africa.

It produces both large edible seeds comparable to peanuts and substantial underground tubers that serve as a carbohydrate and water reserve.

The species has been a vital wild food source for indigenous San and Khoi peoples for millennia.

It is considered one of the most promising underutilized crops for food security in [[fungal-diversity-arid-environments]].

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Tylosema esculentum was first described by George Bentham in 1883.

It belongs to the family Fabaceae, subfamily Cercidoideae, making it a relative of Bauhinia.

The plant is a long-lived perennial vine that develops from a large underground tuber.

Tubers can weigh 10–250 kg, though 50–100 kg is typical for mature plants.

The tuber flesh is white, starchy, and has a mild, pleasant flavor when roasted or boiled.

Above ground, the vine trails along the ground, reaching 2–3 m in length.

Leaves are bilobed, resembling those of Bauhinia species, 5–10 cm across.

Flowers are bright yellow, approximately 3 cm in diameter, and hermaphroditic.

The seed pods are large, woody, and contain 1–3 seeds each.

Seeds are roughly the size of large peanuts, brown, and extremely nutrient-dense.

## Distribution and Habitat

Tylosema esculentum is endemic to the Kalahari Desert region of southern Africa.

Its range extends across Botswana, Namibia, and northern South Africa.

The species grows in deep sandy soils characteristic of semi-arid savanna and desert margins.

Annual rainfall in its native range is 150–500 mm, predominantly in summer.

It is found in open sandy plains and dune systems with good drainage.

The plant is remarkably drought-tolerant, surviving multi-year drought periods through its tuber.

## Cultivation

Cultivation of Tylosema esculentum remains experimental, with no established commercial production.

Propagation is primarily from seed, though germination is slow and irregular.

[[mollison-designers-seed-dormancy-and-germination-treatments]] can be broken through mechanical scarification or acid treatment.

Plants require full sun and well-drained sandy soils, replicating their native habitat.

Tuber development is slow, with harvestable tubers typically requiring 3–4 years from seed.

Yield potential is high once established, with mature tubers producing abundant seeds annually.

Research at the University of Namibia has focused on domestication and agronomic optimization.

## Nutritional Profile

Marama seeds are exceptionally rich in protein, containing 30–38% protein by dry weight.

The oil content is 35–45%, with a fatty acid profile dominated by oleic and linoleic acids.

This composition is comparable to or exceeds that of peanuts and soybeans.
