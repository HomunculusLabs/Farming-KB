# Desert Wadi Agriculture

## Description

[[wadi]] agriculture is the practice of establishing food production systems in and around desert wadis — the dry riverbeds and canyon systems that carry intermittent but sometimes violent floodwaters through arid landscapes. Mollison identifies wadi systems as the primary site for human settlement and food production in arid regions, offering natural water concentration, flood-deposited fertility, and defensible locations. The wadi system represents one of the oldest continuous agricultural strategies on Earth.

## Classification

- **Type**: Agricultural system — arid land food production
- **Geographic distribution**: Worldwide arid regions — North Africa, Middle East, Central Australia, American Southwest
- **Water source**: Intermittent flash floods, groundwater, and runoff collection
- **Key crops**: Dates, citrus, grapes, apricots, pistachios, almonds, melons
- **Settlement pattern**: Box canyon and wadi floor dwelling

## Key Facts

- Wadis concentrate water from vast catchment areas into narrow channels, making irrigation possible even in regions with less than 10 inches of annual rainfall.
- Flash floods in wadis carry immense sediment loads — water may surge 3-6 feet high through constricted channels, depositing silt and organic matter that creates fertile planting zones.
- Flood-deposited soils remain damp for up to two years after a rain event, providing an extended growing window without irrigation.
- The most satisfactory village location is in box canyons within the wadi system, where a single fence across the entrance excludes feral animals.
- Gardens on the wadi floor must be walled against flood damage, while trees tolerate flooding without protection.
- Settlement is positioned up off the wadi floor but below the escarpment — the escarpment is hard iron rock unsuitable for agriculture.
- Drying rooms cut into the opposite (sun-facing) scarp provide natural food preservation using solar heat — dates, apricots, and nuts are dried rapidly.
- The North African desert ecology of "date, melon, goat, and coffee" represents a complete self-sustaining food system that has operated for thousands of years.

## Water Management Strategies

1. **Silt traps and check dams**: Formal structures in wadi floors to capture flood sediment and create permanent lagoons.
2. **Brushwood fences**: Low diversion banks reinforced with brush across wadi floors to create absorption pans.
3. **Siphon systems**: Interconnected absorption pans where overflow siphons from one filled pan to the next, ensuring complete soil charging across the system.
4. **Scarp-top collection**: Drains cut along cliff tops redirect runoff that would otherwise fall as waterfalls, channeling it to storage.
5. **High mesa catchment**: Drain systems on elevated mesas capture water at head, above the wadi floor, for gravity-fed irrigation.
6. **Groundwater wells**: Drilling near the pediment accesses permanent groundwater with relatively low salinity.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Wadi agriculture embodies core [[permaculture-principles]] in an extreme environment:

1. **Working with natural patterns**: Rather than fighting the desert's hydrology, wadi agriculture uses the natural concentration of water and fertility that flash floods provide.

2. **Zonation by water demand**: Hardy plants at the system's periphery, water-demanding plants closest to the source — a direct parallel to Mollison's Zone system applied to water availability rather than human access.

3. **Integrated settlement design**: Housing, food production, water storage, and animal management are designed as an integrated system within the canyon geometry.

4. **Three-year water storage**: Systems must be designed to carry through droughts of three or more years, requiring substantial water reserves and conservative usage.

5. **Flood-friendly design**: Trees that tolerate flooding line the wadi floor, while garden beds are elevated and walled. The system works with flood dynamics rather than attempting to prevent them.

## Crops and Productivity

- **Date palms**: Primary crop in soaked desert sands; reach maximum vegetation density in post-flood conditions
- **Citrus and grapes**: Fruiting grapes from cuttings within 15 months given water
- **Apricots and almonds**: Typical desert-adapted tree crops
- **Melons**: All desert-adapted cucurbits thrive with minimal water
- **Pinion pines**: In American Southwest, provide staple food — a family can gather 60 bushels of pine nuts per day in good years
- **Desert tubers**: Enormous leguminous tubers (yala) provide emergency food storage in dunes
