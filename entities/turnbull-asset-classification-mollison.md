# Turnbull Asset Classification

## Description

The Turnbull Asset Classification is a framework for categorising community and regional assets by their ecological and economic function, attributed to Turnbull (1975) and discussed extensively by [[bill-mollison]] in *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual*, Chapter 14. The framework provides a lens through which communities can evaluate whether their investments build or erode long-term wealth. Mollison extended Turnbull's original three categories with two additional types (informational and conservative), creating a five-category system for assessing the sustainability and regenerative capacity of any economic activity or community investment decision. The classification system underpins Mollison's broader argument that responsible financial management must mirror energy management, requiring social, environmental, and ethical accounting.

## The Five Asset Categories

### 1. Degenerative Assets
Assets that decay, rust, or wear out without regenerating themselves. These include buildings, roads, cars, furnishings, and appliances of society. Mollison warns that too many degenerative assets in any region will impoverish the region in the long term, as constant energy and material inputs are required for maintenance and replacement. These represent the least sustainable category of investment.

**Cultivation examples:** Plastic trays that crack, metal shelving that rusts, single-use substrate bags, disposable filters, temporary poly sheeting that degrades under UV.

### 2. Generative Assets
The tools of society — things that manufacture or process raw materials into useful products. These include huskers, grinders, blenders, lathes, furnaces, and other processing equipment. While generative assets do wear out, they can be used to repair each other in properly equipped workshops. All communities need some tools of processing and repair. A wise farmer hires out or shares such tools.

**Cultivation examples:** Autoclaves, laminar flow hoods, grain mills, compost turners, dehydrators, extraction equipment, workshop tools for maintenance.

### 3. Procreative Assets
Living systems that reproduce and multiply: trees, wildlife, fish, invertebrates, mammals, and domestic livestock. People who maximise a procreative asset base can support the use of some tools and modest degenerative assets. People who maximise degenerative assets eventually fail in their attempts to organise upkeep and repair — hence so many ruined castles and stately homes, as Mollison vividly observes.

**Cultivation examples:** Mushroom cultures and spawn, worm populations for vermicomposting, beneficial insect colonies, heritage seed stocks, [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-inoculum-production-best-practices]], compost microbiome communities.

### 4. Informational Assets (Mollison addition)
Information, education, and data that enable the best use of all other assets. Applied intelligence decides balances in the asset base, assesses future trends, and foresees needs and changes. Seeds have a high information content, as do books or databases. This category recognises knowledge itself as a fundamental form of wealth that compounds over time and never depletes with use.

**Cultivation examples:** [[mycology-research-methods]] databases, cultivation logs, culture collection records, species identification knowledge, thermal tolerance data for [[substrate-preparation]].

### 5. Conservative Assets (Mollison addition)
Assets that guard resources for future use and are essential to sustainable systems. These include insulation, dams, money recycling systems, good storage areas, and strategic forests to guard against erosion or desertification. All these guard resources for future use and are essential to a sustainable system.

**Cultivation examples:** Climate-controlled culture libraries, insulated fruiting chambers, water storage and greywater systems, soil carbon reserves, [[spore-print]] archives, cold storage for cultures.

## The Core Principle

Mollison states clearly: expenditure on categories 3-5 (procreative, informational, and conservative) conserves and creates wealth in any society. If a great many wealth-producing assets are available, then some degenerative assets can be supported, but any society which spends only on categories 1 and 2 will first pollute and then eventually extinguish its resource base.

## Application Beyond Finance

The Turnbull-Mollison framework extends beyond purely financial considerations into the design of sustainable systems. Mollison argues that we should develop or create wealth just as we develop landscapes: by concentrating on conservation of energy and natural resources (reducing the need to earn), by developing procreative assets (proliferating forests, prairies, and life systems), by reducing the creation of degenerative assets (roads, monuments, cities), and by constantly divesting ourselves of any surplus wealth to these ends.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- [[mushroom-spawn]] and living cultures are the ultimate procreative asset, multiplying exponentially from a single culture
- Invest in quality sterilizers, flow hoods, and processing equipment that can maintain and repair each other
- Choose durable stainless steel over plastic; build permanent infrastructure over temporary structures
- Maintain detailed cultivation logs, strain performance records, and species databases as informational wealth
- Insulate growing rooms, build water storage, and maintain genetic libraries in cold storage as conservative assets

## Practical Auditing Method

Communities and cultivation operations can use this framework as a practical auditing tool. List all assets and categorise each into one of the five types. Calculate the ratio of investment (time, money, space) allocated to each category. Mollison's prescription is clear: if the majority of investment flows into categories 1 and 2, the system is on a path toward resource depletion. If investment flows predominantly into categories 3-5, the system builds long-term regenerative capacity. This simple audit can be performed annually and used to guide investment decisions for the coming year.

## Philosophical Significance

The Turnbull-Mollison classification carries a deeper philosophical message about the nature of wealth. In Mollison's view, real wealth is not measured in money but in the capacity of a system to sustain and reproduce itself. A forest is wealth. A seed bank is wealth. A well-insulated home is wealth. A fleet of cars is not wealth — it is a drain on wealth that requires constant inputs. This reframe of wealth from accumulation to regeneration is central to the permaculture ethic of earth care, people care, and fair share.

## Historical Context

The Turnbull classification emerged during the 1970s, a period of significant economic upheaval and questioning of conventional growth-based economics. Mollison encountered and adopted this framework because it aligned with the core permaculture insight that sustainable systems must be based on renewal and regeneration rather than extraction and consumption. The addition of informational and conservative categories reflects Mollison's recognition that knowledge and careful stewardship are as important as biological reproduction in building lasting community wealth. The framework remains relevant as communities worldwide grapple with the limits of degenerative asset-based economies.
