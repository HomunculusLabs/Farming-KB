## Ipomoea tricolor

**Ipomoea tricolor* Cav. (syn. *Ipomoea violacea* auct. non L.), commonly known as the [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]] and is one of the most significant entheogenic plants in Mesoamerican ethnobotany. The seeds of *Ipomoea tricolor* contain ergoline alkaloids, principally [[lysergic-acid-amide]] (LSA, also known as ergine), and have been used since pre-Columbian times in ritual, divinatory, and healing contexts by indigenous peoples of central and southern Mexico.

## Taxonomy and Description

*Ipomoea tricolor* was first described by the Spanish botanist Antonio José Cavanilles in 1795, based on plants cultivated at the Royal Botanic Garden of Madrid. The species epithet "tricolor" refers to the characteristic three-colored appearance of the flowers (blue petals, white throat, yellow center). The species has a complex and confusing taxonomic history that has persisted for over two centuries.

Much of the ethnobotanical, psychoactive, and horticultural literature published before the 1980s refers to this species as *Ipomoea violacea* L. However, *Ipomoea violacea* sensu stricto is a distinct Old World species (native to tropical Asia and the Pacific islands) with violet-purple flowers and a different chromosome number. The widespread misapplication of the name *I. violacea* to the Mexican morning glory caused significant confusion in the literature that was not fully resolved until the taxonomic revisions of the 1970s and 1980s. The correct name for the Mexican entheogenic morning glory is *Ipomoea tricolor* Cav., and this name is used here.

The species belongs to the genus *Ipomoea* within the tribe Ipomoeeae of the family Convolvulaceae. The genus *Ipomoea* is one of the largest genera of flowering plants, with over 600 described species distributed throughout the tropical and subtropical regions of the world. It includes economically important species such as sweet potato (*I. batatas*) as well as numerous ornamental and weedy species.

*Ipomoea tricolor* is a vigorous annual or short-lived perennial vine that can climb to heights of 3 to 5 meters using twining stems that coil around any available support. The stems are slender, green to purplish, and bear characteristic adventitious lateral roots at the nodes when in contact with moist soil or water. This rooting ability makes the plant a vigorous colonizer and contributes to its success as both a cultivated ornamental and an introduced weed.

The leaves are alternate, simple, and heart-shaped (cordate), 5 to 12 cm long and 4 to 10 cm wide, with a smooth (glabrous) or slightly hairy surface and entire margins. The leaf base is deeply cordate, and the apex is acute to acuminate. The petiole is 3 to 8 cm long.

The flowers are large (6 to 10 cm in diameter), funnel-shaped (infundibuliform), and open in the morning, typically closing by early to mid-afternoon, which is the characteristic "morning glory" behavior that gives the common name. The most famous and widely cultivated variety is "Heavenly Blue,' which has brilliant sky-blue flowers with a white throat and a bright yellow center. Other popular horticultural varieties include 'Pearly Gates' (pure white flowers), 'Flying Saucers' (blue flowers with white stripes and radiating patterns), 'Wedding Bells' (rose-pink flowers), 'Scarlet O'Hara' (deep rose-red flowers), and 'Blue Star' (light blue with darker blue veins). The flowers are pollinated by bees, butterflies, hummingbirds, and sphinx moths. Each flower lasts only a single day, but the plant produces flowers prolifically throughout the growing season.

The fruit is a globose to ovoid capsule approximately 8 to 12 mm in diameter, containing 2 to 4 dark brown to black seeds that are roughly triangular or wedge-shaped, with a rough or finely pitted surface. The seeds are 4 to 6 mm long and are extremely hard and durable.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Ipomoea tricolor* is native to the tropical and subtropical regions of Mexico and Central America, extending from central Mexico (states of Puebla, Oaxaca, Guerrero, Morelos, Michoacán, and Veracruz) through Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, and possibly into Nicaragua and Costa Rica. The species occurs in a variety of habitats including disturbed areas, roadsides, forest edges, cultivated fields, tropical lowland forests, and montane cloud forests, typically at elevations below 2,000 meters.

The plant has been widely introduced and naturalized throughout the tropics and subtropics worldwide, including the Caribbean islands, South America (particularly Brazil and Argentina), sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia (India, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Indonesia, Philippines), and northern and eastern Australia. It is also extensively cultivated as an ornamental annual vine in temperate regions of [[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]]* (ergot) and of the semi-synthetic compo lysergic acid diethylamide mide (LSD). The discovery of ergoline alkaloids in a higher plant by [[plants-of-the-gods-turbina-corymbosa-ololiuqui]]* (ololiuqui), they constituted the principal hallucinogenic complex of the Aztec (Mexica) and other central Mexican peoples, and their use is among the best-documented of all pre-Columbian entheogenic practices.

Spanish colonial chronicles provide detailed accounts of the use of these seeds. Francisco Hernández, the royal physician to Philip II of Spain, who spent seven years (1570 to 1577) studying the natural history and medicinal practices of Mexico, documented the ritual use of tlitliltzin and ololiuqui among the Aztecs. His work, "Rerum Medicarum Novae Hispaniae Thesaurus" (published posthumously in 1651), described how the seeds were consumed by priests and soothsayers (teopixqui) to induce visionary states for divination, diagnosis of illness, communication with the gods and spirits of the dead, and revelation of hidden knowledge including the causes of disease, the location of lost objects, and the outcome of future events.

The Jacinto de la Serna manuscript (circa 1620) and other colonial-era documents describe the ritual context in which the seeds were used. The ceremony typically involved fasting, purification (bathing, confession), and the burning of copal incense before ingestion. The seeds were ground, mixed with water, and sometimes combined with other plants. The resulting visions were interpreted by the practitioner within a rich symbolic framework.

After the Spanish conquest, the use of tlitliltzin and ololiuqui was vigorously suppressed by colonial and ecclesiastical authorities, who classified the practice as idolatry, witchcraft, and devil worship. The Spanish Inquisition actively persecuted practitioners, and the Inquisition records contain numerous trial proceedings related to the use of these seeds. Despite this persecution, the tradition survived in some indigenous communities of Oaxaca, Guerrero, Veracruz, and Puebla, where the seeds continue to be used by curanderos and shamans in healing and divinatory rituals.

Albert Hofmann's identification of lysergic acid amide and other ergoline alkaloids in the seeds of *Ipomoea tricolor* and *Turbina corymbosa* in 1960 was a landmark discovery in ethnobotany and phytochemistry. It represented the first documented occurrence of lysergic acid derivatives in a higher plant (previously known only from the ergot fungus *Claviceps purpurea*). This discovery raised the important question of whether the ergoline alkaloids in morning glories are produced by the plant itself or by an endophytic fungal symbiont (possibly a *Claviceps* or *Periglandula* species) that lives within the plant tissues. Modern research has confirmed that ergoline-producing fungi of the genus *Periglandula* are associated with *Ipomoea* and *Turbina* species, supporting the hypothesis that the alkaloids are of fungal rather than plant origin.

## Modern Use

In the mid-20th century, *Ipomoea tricolor* seeds gained popularity as a recreational psychedelic, particularly in the counterculture of the 1960s and 1970s. Commercial seed packets of 'Heavenly Blue' and other varieties were widely available at garden centers and through mail-order catalogs, and the seeds were ingested for their psychoactive effects. Typical recreational doses ranged from 50 to 300 seeds, depending on the variety and the sensitivity of the individual.

The experience is generally described as milder, more sedating, and more dreamlike than LSD, with visual hallucinations (color enhancement, pattern recognition, geometric imagery), altered perception of time and space, mild euphoria, and significant physical side effects including nausea, drowsiness, lethargy, and a heavy or drugged body sensation. The experience typically lasts 6 to 10 hours. Many users report that the physical side effects are unpleasant and that the psychoactive effects are considerably less impressive than those of LSD or psilocybin-containing mushrooms.

Many commercial seed suppliers began treating morning glory seeds with fungicides (such as captan, thiram, or methyl mercury compounds) and other chemicals to discourage psychoactive use, and the seeds sold today for gardening purposes are frequently coated with toxic compounds that make ingestion dangerous. Seeds sold for planting should never be ingested without thorough washing and verification that they are untreated.

## Cultivation

*Ipomoea tricolor* is one of the most widely cultivated ornamental vines in the world. It is grown as an annual in temperate climates, where it provides rapid, colorful coverage of trellises, fences, walls, arbors, and mailboxes. The plant prefers full sun, well-drained moderately fertile soil, and moderate to regular moisture. It is tolerant of poor soils and drought conditions once established but performs best with consistent moisture.

Seeds are sown directly in the ground after the last frost, or started indoors 4 to 6 weeks before the expected planting date. The hard seed coat should be nicked or filed (scarified) and the seeds soaked in warm water for 12 to 24 hours before planting to improve germination. Germination typically occurs within 1 to 2 weeks at soil temperatures of 20 to 25°C. Seedlings should be thinned to 20 to 30 cm spacing. The plant is not cold-hardy and is killed by frost; it dies at the end of the growing season in temperate climates.

## Tlitliltzin and Ololiuqui: The Twin Seeds of Aztec Divination

The seeds of *Ipomoea tricolor* (tlitliltzin) and *Turbina corymbosa* (ololiuqui) constituted the most important hallucinogenic complex in the Aztec pharmacopoeia and were among the few entheogens that survived the Spanish conquest with their [[ethnomycology-cultural-significance-sacred-fungi]] at least partially intact, unlike the teona  ushrooms) and peyotl (peyote) which were more thoroughly suppressed.

While both seeds were used for similar purposes, there were some distinctions in their use and cultural associations. Ololiuqui (the seeds of *Turbina corymbosa*) was considered the more sacred and prestigious of the two, and its use was more closely associated with the highest levels of Aztec religious practice. Tlitliltzin (*Ipomoea tricolor* seeds) was used in a somewhat broader range of contexts, including divination, healing, and less formal ceremonial settings.

Both types of seed were typically prepared in the same manner: ground to a fine powder on a metate (grinding stone), mixed with water, and sometimes combined with other plants. The resulting beverage was consumed by the practitioner in a ritual context involving fasting, purification, and prayer. The visions that followed were interpreted within a rich symbolic framework that drew on Aztec cosmology and mythology.

The persistence of tlitliltzin and ololiuqui use into the colonial and modern periods is remarkable given the intensity of the Spanish suppression of indigenous religious practices. In part, this may be due to the relatively inconspicuous nature of the seeds (they resemble ordinary seeds and are easy to transport and conceal) and the fact that the morning glory vines themselves are common ornamental and weedy plants that attracted little attention from colonial authorities.
