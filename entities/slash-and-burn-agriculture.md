## Slash-and-Burn Agriculture (Swidden)

## Description

Slash-and-burn agriculture, also known as swidden agriculture, is a traditional farming system in which small areas of forest are cleared, burnt, and planted with a diverse mixture of perennial crops that form food forests. David Holmgren describes the swidden systems of New Guinea as "the most evolved and sustainable example" of pulsing agricultural systems, and "the most energy-efficient form of agriculture documented by scientists." The system relies on long fallow periods of forest regrowth to rebuild soil fertility between cropping phases, maintaining a small ratio of productive garden to regenerating forest at any given time.

## Classification

- **Category:** Agricultural System / Technique
- **Domain:** Tropical agriculture, indigenous land management, pulsing ecosystem model
- **Geographic Association:** New Guinea, Southeast Asia, tropical regions worldwide
- **Also Known As:** Swidden agriculture, shifting cultivation

## How It Works

### 1. Clearing
Small areas of rainforest are felled and the vegetation is allowed to dry. The clearing is deliberately small relative to the total forest area, ensuring that most of the landscape remains in regenerative forest cover at any time.

### 2. Burning
The dried vegetation is burnt, releasing nutrients stored in the biomass as ash. This provides an immediate pulse of fertility — particularly phosphorus and potassium — that supports vigorous crop growth. The burning also reduces weed competition and may help control pests and diseases.

### 3. Planting
A great mixture of perennial crops is planted, forming food forests with multiple layers of tree, shrub, vine, and root crops that mimic the structure of the rainforest itself. This multi-layered architecture minimises leaching of nutrients from the soil by maintaining continuous root systems at multiple depths.

### 4. Harvesting
Crops are harvested over several years as the food forest matures. Initially, short-lived pioneer crops dominate, but over time the garden becomes harder to harvest as woody non-food plants increase in abundance. Yields gradually decline as the initial pulse of fertility is exhausted.

### 5. Abandonment
The garden is abandoned and allowed to return to forest. Naturally regenerating forest trees may be retained during the cropping phase and are known as "mother of the gardens" in recognition of the essential role they play in rainforest regeneration and soil fertility rebuilding.

### 6. Fallow Regeneration
Forest regrowth rebuilds soil fertility over approximately 20 years before another cycle of gardening begins. During this phase, deep-rooted trees bring minerals up from subsoil layers, nitrogen is fixed by leguminous species, and organic matter accumulates in the soil profile.

## Key Facts

- The rotation time under regrowth forest is critical, with approximately 20 years generally necessary to fully rebuild fertility.
- The ratio of garden to forest is always small — at any given time, most of the land is in the regrowth/forest phase.
- In the New Guinea highlands, this system sustainably supported dense populations before European contact in the 1930s.
- The structure of the food garden mimics the rainforest, with multiple canopy layers that minimise leaching of nutrients.
- Retained forest trees ("mother of the gardens") are recognised as essential for rainforest regeneration.
- Currently regarded as unsustainable in many tropical regions because population pressure has shortened the fallow period below the critical 20-year threshold.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Pulsing ecosystem model:** Slash-and-burn is the most evolved example of the pulsing agricultural ecosystem model, with a productive consumption phase alternating with a long biological accumulation phase.

- **Food forest design:** The multi-layered crop structure directly inspired permaculture food forest design, which mimics natural forest architecture for productive purposes.

- **Fertility management:** The system demonstrates that biological processes can rebuild soil fertility without external inputs, provided sufficient time is allowed for the regeneration phase.

- **Cautionary lesson:** The current unsustainability of many slash-and-burn systems illustrates the generalised principle that pulsing the system too frequently leads to a downward spiral of land degradation.

- **Scale and ratio:** The small ratio of garden to total land area provides a fundamental design principle for permaculture systems: productive zones must be balanced with regenerative zones.

- **Energy descent model:** The system's remarkable energy efficiency makes it relevant as a model for post-fossil-fuel agriculture and permaculture design.

## Connections

- Directly inspired permaculture food forest concepts described by Mollison and Holmgren
- Represents the pulsing ecosystem model at its most refined and sustainable
- Related to cell grazing and pasture ley cropping as other examples of pulsing agricultural systems
- The failure mode (shortened fallow) illustrates the generalised model of land degradation through over-pulsing
- Connects to the concept of ecological succession in permaculture garden design
