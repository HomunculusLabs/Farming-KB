## Dimargaritaceae

## Description

The Dimargaritaceae are a family of specialized biotrophic mycoparasitic fungi in the Zoopagales (Zygomycota). They are obligate parasites of other fungi, primarily Mucorales, and are characterized by sporangiophores bearing distinctive sporiferous branchlets and complex haustoria that penetrate host hyphae. The family includes the genera [[dimargaris]], Dispira, and Tieghemiomyces. Species of Dimargaris are among the most morphologically distinctive mycoparasites due to their crystalline cell inclusions and elaborate haustorial systems.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Eukaryota
- **[[kingdom-fungi]]
- **Division:** Zygomycota
- **Class:** Zygomycetes
- **Order:** Zoopagales
- **Family:** Dimargaritaceae

## Key Genera and Species

1. **Dimargaris** — Type genus of the family
   - **D. cristalligena** — The best-known species, named for its characteristic crystalline cell inclusions. Produces sporangiophores with sporiferous branchlets and complex haustoria inside host hyphae (Benjamin 1959).

2. **Dispira** — Includes species with specific host requirements
   - **D. simplex** — Grows on Chaetomium species
   - **D. parvispora** — Grows on Chaetomium and Monascus species
   - **D. cornuta** — An exceptional strain for which starch was the best carbon source (Singh 1975)

3. **Tieghemiomyces** — Less frequently encountered genus in the family

## Morphological Characteristics

- **Sporangiophores:** Erect, bearing heads with sporiferous branchlets at the apex. Dimargaris cristalligena has a characteristic sporangiophore head with complex branching patterns
- **Sporiferous branchlets:** Specialized [[spore]]-bearing structures at the tips of sporangiophore branches
- **Haustoria:** Complex, branching haustorial systems that penetrate host hyphal walls and expand inside host cells. Illustrated for D. cristalligena by Benjamin (1959)
- **Crystalline inclusions:** D. cristalligena produces distinctive crystalline bodies within its cells, giving the species its name
- **[[mycelium]]:** Delicate hyphae that grow over host colony surfaces

## Ecology and Host Range

- **Primary hosts:** Mucorales (Mucor, Chaetomium, Monascus), but NOT Mortierella
- **Habitat:** Soil, dung, and decaying organic matter where host fungi are present
- **Specificity:** More host-specific than many other mycoparasites; Dispira species show preferences for particular host genera

## Nutritional Requirements

The Dimargaritaceae have unusual and specific nutritional requirements:

1. **Carbon source:** Cannot utilize glucose (unusual among fungi). Axenic growth requires glycerol or, exceptionally, starch (Singh 1975)
2. **Nitrogen:** Organic nitrogen sources are required
3. **Vitamins:** Various vitamin supplements are needed for axenic growth
4. **Cultural studies:** Brunk and Barnett (1966), Kurtzman (1968), Barnett (1970), and Singh (1975) characterized nutritional requirements
5. **Growth factors:** Media must be rich in proteins with added growth factors

## Mycoparasitic Mechanism

1. **Host contact:** Parasitic hyphae grow over and contact host hyphae
2. **Haustorial penetration:** Complex haustoria penetrate the host cell wall and expand inside the host cell
3. **Biotrophic extraction:** Nutrients are extracted from living host cells
4. **Crystalline formation:** The characteristic crystalline inclusions may relate to metabolic processing of host-derived nutrients

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Nutritional biology:** The inability to utilize glucose makes the family uniquely interesting for fungal nutrition studies
- **Biocontrol potential:** Parasitism of Mucorales could be relevant to controlling spoilage fungi, though the biotrophic requirement limits practical application
- **Evolutionary significance:** Represents an ancient lineage of obligate mycoparasitism with highly specialized haustoria
- **Model system:** D. cristalligena has been widely illustrated as a model biotrophic mycoparasite

## Key References

## Notes

The Dimargaritaceae share the biotrophic mycoparasitic habit with the [[piptocephalidaceae]] but differ in their haustorial morphology, crystalline inclusions, and inability to utilize glucose. The family is taxonomically placed in the Zoopagales, an order of predominantly parasitic fungi. Their obligate dependence on living fungal hosts makes them difficult to study but scientifically fascinating as examples of extreme nutritional specialization.
