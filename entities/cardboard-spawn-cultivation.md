## Description

Cardboard spawn cultivation is a primitive, low-tech method for propagating mushroom mycelium using corrugated cardboard as both the growth medium and the spawn carrier. The technique allows cultivators to create viable spawn without laboratory equipment, sterilization infrastructure, or specialized media — making it ideal for off-grid operations, budget-constrained growers, and emergency situations. Developed and documented extensively by [[tradd-cotter]], the method relies on the natural affinity of many wood-loving [[saprophytic-fungi]] for the cellulose-rich corrugated layers in cardboard.

## Classification

- **Category**: Cultivation technique / Spawn production
- **Complexity**: Low (beginner-friendly)
- **Equipment required**: Cardboard, water, plastic bags, mushroom stem bases or tissue
- **Best suited for**: Oysters, blewits (*Clitocybe nuda*), king stropharia (*Stropharia rugosoannulata*)

## Key Facts

- Easiest mushrooms to expand with this method are oysters, blewits, and king stropharia
- Not suitable for compost/secondary decomposers (almond portabella, paddy straw, shaggy mane)
- Definitely not for mycorrhizal species (truffles, chanterelles, porcini) which require living hosts
- Wild-harvested mushrooms are preferred because they carry beneficial soil microbes some species need for fruiting
- Cultivated mushrooms can be used but may lack these beneficial microbial partners
- Process: chop stem bases → scatter on soaked cardboard → roll into "burrito" → seal in plastic bag → incubate
- Incubation at temperatures above 60°F (16°C) requires airholes for gas exchange
- Refrigerated or cool incubation minimizes molds and bacterial rot
- Colonized cardboard can be expanded by layering with fresh cardboard in tubs, bins, or buckets
- Each expansion takes a few days to a week
- The resulting "sheet spawn" can replace plug, sawdust, or grain spawn for inoculation of logs, stumps, wood chips, and pasteurized media
- Cardboard spawn is not sterile (pure) but is clean and maintains vigor across transfers

## Step-by-Step Process

1. Remove the base of a fresh mushroom (wild is best) and chop into small pieces; save cap for eating
2. Soak corrugated cardboard in water until thoroughly saturated
3. Scatter stem base pieces across wet cardboard
4. Roll up like a burrito and seal in a plastic bag
5. Incubate in the coolest place possible for several weeks
6. Check for mycelial growth; if contamination appears (green, yellow, black mold), transplant clean mycelium to fresh cardboard
7. Once fully colonized, expand by layering colonized sheets with fresh wet cardboard in a container
8. Repeat expansion to build volume of sheet spawn
9. Use final spawn to inoculate target substrates

## Critical Skills Required

- Must be able to distinguish mushroom mycelium from mold mycelium (especially *Trichoderma* spp.)
- Any powdery growth on cultures should trigger immediate disposal and sanitation
- Hands, tools, and work surfaces must be sanitized after contact with contaminated cultures

## Off-Grid Applications

- Requires no electricity or lab equipment
- Can be performed while traveling by carrying cardboard and plastic bags
- Stem bases can be regenerated on cardboard even if the species doesn't use cardboard as a food source — buying time for later transfer to sterile agar
- Dried stem bases stored in cardboard remain viable for 9–12 months for later cloning

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Cardboard spawn cultivation democratizes mushroom cultivation by removing the dependency on sterile laboratory techniques and expensive equipment. It enables mycelium propagation in resource-limited environments, disaster zones, and developing regions. The technique also preserves beneficial microbial communities from wild specimens that might be lost in sterile lab isolation, potentially improving fruiting success for species that depend on microbial partners.
