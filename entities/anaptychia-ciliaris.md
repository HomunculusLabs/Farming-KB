## Overview

**Anaptychia ciliaris** is a large, foliose lichen in the family Physciaceae, commonly known as the **ciliate fringe lichen**. It is named for the conspicuous dark cilia (hair-like projections) along its lobe margins.

The species is widespread in temperate regions of Europe, western North America, and parts of [[psilocybe-semilanceata-worldwide-temperate-distribution-guzman-1998]]. In Europe, it occurs from Scandinavia and the British Isles south to the Mediterranean basin. In North America, it is found along the Pacific Coast from Alaska to California.

The species is corticolous (bark-dwelling), preferring the trunks and branches of deciduous trees, especially **oak** (*Quercus* spp.), **elm** (*Ulmus* spp.), and **ash** (*Fraxinus* spp.). It occasionally colonizes fence posts and weathered wood.

It favors open woodland edges, hedgerows, and parkland with good light availability. The species is moderately shade-tolerant but reaches maximum size in well-lit situations.

In urban and suburban environments, *A. ciliaris* can persist on mature street trees and garden specimens. However, it is less common in heavily managed landscapes where regular tree maintenance removes suitable bark substrates.

Elevation ranges from sea level to approximately 1500 m. It is more common in oceanic climates with moderate year-round humidity than in continental interiors.

## Chemistry and Bioactivity

The species is not known to contain any toxic compounds and has no documented history of use in traditional medicine. Its chemistry is relatively simple compared to many other foliose lichens.

## Ecology and Symbiosis

The mycobiont (fungal partner) of *A. ciliaris* associates with a green algal photobiont, typically *Trebouxia* species. This symbiosis allows the lichen to photosynthesize and produce carbohydrates in nutrient-poor bark habitats.

The species plays a role in bark microhabitat complexity. Its thallus provides shelter for microarthropods, nematodes, and tardigrades. Lichen-feeding moth larvae of the genus *Clemensia* may utilize this species.

Birds occasionally use fragments of *A. ciliaris* as nesting material. The cilia may help anchor the lichen fragments within the nest structure.

Anaptychia ciliaris is moderately sensitive to air pollution, particularly [[dighton-nitrogen-deposition-mycorrhizal-community-shifts]]. It has declined in parts of western Europe where agricultural ammonia emissions have increased bark nutrient levels, favoring nitrophytic species.

The ciliate margin may serve a defensive function, deterring invertebrate herbivores from grazing on the lobe edges where new growth occurs.

### Growth and Longevity

Growth rates for *A. ciliaris* have not been extensively studied, but related Physciaceae species typically increase thallus diameter by 1–3 mm per year under optimal conditions. Large specimens likely represent decades of growth.

The species is capable of surviving extended periods of desiccation. During dry conditions, the thallus becomes brittle and curled, resuming normal morphology within hours of rehydration.

Vegetative reproduction through thallus fragmentation is probably the primary dispersal mechanism. Fragmented pieces can establish on new substrates if they land on suitable bark surfaces.
