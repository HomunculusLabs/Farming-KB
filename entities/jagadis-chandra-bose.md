# Jagadis Chandra Bose

Sir jagadis chandra bose Bose (1858–1937) was a Bengali polymath and pioneering
scientist who made foundational contributions to both physics and [[stephen-hales-plant-physiology]]. He demonstrated wireless electromagnetic signaling before Marconi,
essentially founded the field of microwave optics, and invented the crescograph
— an ultra-sensitive instrument that revealed plant growth and response
patterns invisible to the naked eye. As one of the first Indian scientists to
achieve international recognition during the British colonial period, Bose is
a central figure of the Bengal Renaissance and an enduring symbol of scientific
excellence emerging from colonial India.

## Early Life and Education

Bose was born on 30 November 1858 in Mymensingh, Bengal Presidency, British
India (now Bangladesh). His father, Bhagawan Chandra Bose, was a deputy
magistrate. He attended St. Xavier’s College, Kolkata, where the Jesuit priest
Father Lafont inspired his interest in science, earning a B.A. in 1879. He
briefly studied medicine at the University of London (1880) before transferring
to Christ’s College, Cambridge, where he earned a B.A. in the Natural Sciences
Tripos in 1884 under Lord Rayleigh. He received a B.Sc. from the University of
London the same year and was awarded an honorary D.Sc. in 1896.

## Radio and Microwave Research

Between 1894 and 1895, while at Presidency College, Kolkata, Bose conducted
pioneering experiments with very short-wavelength electromagnetic waves
(millimeter waves, ~5mm). He demonstrated wireless transmission across a room
— ringing a bell remotely and detonating gunpowder — predating Guglielmo
Marconi’s public demonstrations. He was one of the first to systematically
investigate the optical properties of microwaves, studying polarization,
reflection, refraction, diffraction, and birefringence at millimeter
wavelengths, essentially founding the field of microwave optics.

His improved semiconductor-based **coherer** (using a spiral iron spring with
mercury) was more sensitive than Édouard Branly’s original design and earned
US Patent 755,840 (filed 1901, granted 1904). In 1900, he demonstrated his
millimeter wave apparatus at the Royal Institution in London before an audience
including Lord Rayleigh. In 2012, the IEEE honored Bose with a Milestone plaque
at Presidency College recognizing his pioneering radio and microwave science.

## Plant Physiology and the Crescograph

After 1900, Bose shifted his focus to plant physiology, conducting experiments
that demonstrated plants respond to environmental stimuli (light, heat, cold,
chemicals, mechanical stress) with measurable electrical responses analogous to
animal nervous impulses. He showed that plants experience fatigue, can be
anesthetized with chloroform or ether, and recover — just as animals do.

His most famous invention, the **crescograph** (c. 1901–1902), used levers
and mirrors to magnify plant growth movements up to 10,000 times on a smoked
glass plate, detecting growth as small as one millionth of an inch. It revealed
that plant growth occurs in rhythmic pulses rather than smoothly, challenging
prevailing assumptions. His related **resonant recorder** captured plant
electrical responses to stimuli with unprecedented sensitivity.

## Academic Career and the Bose Institute

Bose served as Professor of Physics at Presidency College, Kolkata, from 1885
to 1915. He was initially offered only half the salary of European professors
due to racial discrimination and refused his salary for three years in protest
until it was equalized. Despite institutional obstacles, he conducted world-class
research using equipment he largely built himself.

In 1917, he founded the **Bose Institute** in Kolkata, inaugurated on his 59th
birthday and funded by Indian donations as an act of scientific self-reliance.
He served as its Director for life. The institute continues as a major
multidisciplinary research center and is one of India’s premier scientific
institutions.

## Connection to Plant Consciousness Research

Bose’s work is the primary historical foundation cited in *The Secret Life of
Plants* (1973) by Peter Tompkins and Christopher Bird. He argued that the nervous
impulse in plants is of the same nature as in animals — a radical thesis that
remains influential. Modern plant science has validated his core observations:
plants generate and transmit [[tompkins-plant-electrical-signals]] (action potentials, variation
potentials) in response to stimuli. His work anticipates plant neurobiology
(coined 2006) and [[plant-electrophysiology]], and his philosophical position —
that there is no hard boundary between life and non-life in stimulus response
— informs contemporary discussions of plant sentience and biosemiotics.

## Controversies

Bose’s priority over Marconi in wireless signaling remains debated. Bose used
millimeter waves (short range within a room) while Marconi developed practical
long-range wireless telegraphy. Bose chose not to pursue commercial applications
or vigorously press his priority claims, though Lord Kelvin publicly
acknowledged his contributions after the 1897 Royal Institution demonstration.
His claims about plant sentience — that plants can be “drugged” and show
“fatigue” — were controversial among Western scientists who accused him of
anthropomorphizing plant responses, a debate partly colored by colonial attitudes.

## Cultural Significance

A close friend of Rabindranath Tagore, Bose shared the poet’s vision of unity
between science, art, and philosophy. He wrote *Niruddesher Kahini* (1896),
considered one of the first Bengali science fiction stories. As a figure of the
Bengal Renaissance, Bose demonstrated that world-class science was possible under
colonial constraints. His career embodied scientific self-reliance: he built his
own equipment, founded India-funded research institutions, and insisted on equal
treatment in the colonial academy, inspiring generations of Indian scientists.
