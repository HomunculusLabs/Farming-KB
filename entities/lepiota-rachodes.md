# Lepiota rachodes

Lepiota rachodes, commonly known as the Shaggy Parasol, is a large, edible mushroom closely related to [[lepiota-procera]] (the Parasol Mushroom). It is widely collected as an edible in Europe and North America, though it causes gastrointestinal upset in some individuals, making it a species that requires careful consumer awareness. The Shaggy Parasol is distinguished from the true Parasol by its stockier build, more shaggy appearance, and different flesh reaction when cut. The spores of this species can be collected by placing the cap on two panes of glass, as illustrated in cultivation guides. See [[lepiota-procera]] for the true Parasol Mushroom and [[agaricus-campestris]] for another common grassland species.

## Taxonomy and Description

- **Order:** [[agar]]icales
- **Family:** Agaricaceae
- **Synonyms:** Macrolepiota rachodes (Vittadini) Singer, Lepiota rhacodes (alternate spelling), Chlorophyllum rachodes (Vittadini) Vellinga
- **Common names:** Shaggy Parasol, Shaggy Parasol Mushroom

### Morphology

- **Cap:** Large, 8-20 cm diameter, egg-shaped when young, expanding to broadly convex with a distinct umbo. Surface covered with prominent, shaggy, cinnamon-brown to reddish-brown scales on a white to cream background. More densely scaly than [[lepiota-procera]]. The scales are coarse and stand up from the cap surface, giving a distinctly shaggy appearance that distinguishes it from the smoother-scaled true Parasol.
- **Gills:** Free from the stem, white, crowded. Gills are broad and close together, a characteristic of the genus.
- **Stem:** 8-20 cm long, 1-3 cm thick, cylindrical with a bulbous base. Surface is smooth above the ring and covered with small, brownish scales below. Solid when young, becoming hollow with age. The bulbous base is more pronounced than in L. procera.
- **Ring:** Large, thick, single (not double like L. procera), membranous, often with a brownish margin. Fixed to the stem (not movable like L. procera's ring). This is one of the most important distinguishing features between the two species.
- **Flesh:** White to cream, thick, firm. When cut, the flesh often turns **reddish-orange to brownish**, especially in the stem base. This color change is a key distinguishing feature from L. procera, whose flesh typically does not change color dramatically.
- **Spore print:** White to cream.
- **Spores:** Broadly ellipsoid, smooth, with a germ pore, approximately 12-18 x 8-11 µm.
- **Odor:** Pleasant, mild, sometimes slightly nutty. Not unpleasant in any way.

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Global range:** Widespread across Europe and North America. Also reported from other temperate regions including parts of Asia and Australia. Common in the United Kingdom, France, and across central and southern Europe.
- **Habitat:** Open grasslands, pastures, woodland edges, compost heaps, gardens, and disturbed ground rich in organic matter. Often found in similar habitats to [[lepiota-procera]], and the two species can sometimes be found growing near each other.
- **Seasonality:** Late summer through autumn, typically August through November in the Northern Hemisphere. Most abundant during warm, humid periods following rain.
- **Growth pattern:** Usually found singly or in small groups, occasionally in fairy rings. Can appear in the same locations year after year, indicating persistent soil mycelium.

## Identification Notes

Key features distinguishing L. rachodes from L. procera:

- **Single ring** (not double) — fixed to the stem, not movable. L. procera has a distinctive double ring that slides freely.
- **Flesh turns reddish-orange when cut** — especially at the stem base. This is the most reliable field identification feature. L. procera flesh typically does not show this reaction, or only very faintly.
- **Denser, more shaggy scales** on the cap — the scales of L. rachodes are coarser and more prominent than the relatively neat scales of L. procera.
- **Stockier build** — proportionally shorter and thicker than the elegant, tall L. procera. L. rachodes has a more robust, chunky appearance overall.
- **Bulbous base** — more pronounced than in L. procera. The base of L. rachodes is distinctly swollen.
- **Spore size** — L. rachodes spores are larger (12-18 µm) compared to L. procera (14-16 µm), though this requires microscopy to verify.

## Warnings and Toxicity

L. rachodes is edible for most people but is controversial due to several important factors:

- **Gastrointestinal upset:** Some individuals experience nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea after eating this mushroom, even when properly cooked. Sensitivity varies greatly between individuals. Some people can eat it with no ill effects while others become quite ill.
- **Raw consumption:** Should never be eaten raw. Cooking is essential, though even cooked specimens cause reactions in sensitive individuals.
- **Alcohol interaction:** Some reports suggest adverse reactions when consumed with alcohol, similar to the Coprinus effect, though this is not well-documented or universally accepted.
- **Confusion with deadly Lepiota species:** Small Lepiota species (L. brunneoincarnata, L. subincarnata, and L. helveola) contain lethal amatoxins, the same toxins found in [[amanita-phalloides]] (Death Cap). L. rachodes is much larger, but collectors should never harvest small, immature specimens that cannot be clearly identified. The deadly species are typically under 10 cm in cap diameter.
- **Individual tolerance:** First-time consumers should eat a small portion to test for personal sensitivity. Wait several hours before consuming more.
- **Not recommended for commercial sale** due to the variability in individual tolerance.

## Culinary Value

For those who tolerate it well, L. rachodes is considered a good edible:

- **Flavor:** Mild, nutty, and pleasant — similar to but generally considered less refined than L. procera. The flavor intensifies with cooking.
- **Texture:** Firm and meaty when young, becoming softer and less appealing with age. Young, firm specimens are the best for eating.
- **Preparation:** Caps can be prepared similarly to L. procera — breaded and pan-fried like a schnitzel or cutlet. The cap is peeled before cooking to remove the tough skin. Stems are typically discarded or used for stock due to their fibrous texture.
- **Cooking:** Must be thoroughly cooked. Never eat raw. Sautéing, grilling, or deep-frying are common methods.
- **Storage:** Best used fresh. Does not dry well compared to some other species.

## Ecological Role

L. rachodes is a saprotrophic species that decomposes organic matter in soils. It is commonly found in nutrient-rich environments including compost heaps and well-manured pastures. The species benefits from the breakdown of organic material by primary decomposers and typically fruits from well-decomposed substrates. It contributes to nutrient cycling in grassland and woodland-edge ecosystems and its persistent mycelium suggests it plays a long-term role in soil health.

## Seasonal Patterns and Geographic Distribution

L. rachodes follows predictable seasonal patterns across its range:

- **Spring emergence:** Can appear as early as June in warm years, though July is more typical
- **Peak season:** August through October, with September often being the month of greatest abundance
- **Late season:** May fruit into November during mild, wet autumns
- **Temperature preference:** Fruits when soil temperatures are between 55-70°F (13-21°C)
- **Rainfall dependence:** Requires adequate moisture. Dry summers result in poor or absent fruitings. Heavy rain followed by warm, humid conditions triggers the best flushes.
- **Geographic distribution:** Most abundant in Europe, particularly France, the UK, and central Europe. Common throughout North America. Less common in Mediterranean climates with hot, dry summers.

## Microscopic Features

For definitive identification, microscopic examination provides reliable distinguishing characters:

- **Spores:** Broadly ellipsoid, smooth, with a germ pore at the apex. Dimensions approximately 12-18 x 8-11 µm. Spores are significantly larger than those of L. procera.
- **Basidia:** Clavate (club-shaped), typically four-spored, 25-35 x 10-15 µm
- **Cheilocystidia:** Numerous, varied in shape — clavate, utriform, or lageniform. Size approximately 20-50 x 10-20 µm
- **Pileipellis:** A cutis composed of interwoven hyphae, with the scales on the cap surface formed by bundles of erect hyphae
- **Clamp connections:** Present on hyphae, which helps distinguish Lepiota/Chlorophyllum from some similar genera

## Nutritional Value

L. rachodes is a nutritious food source for those who tolerate it:

- **Protein:** Approximately 20-25% dry weight
- **Vitamins:** Good source of B vitamins, particularly niacin (B3) and riboflavin (B2)
- **Minerals:** Contains potassium, phosphorus, selenium, and copper
- **Fiber:** Moderate fiber content from chitinous cell walls
- **Calories:** Low calorie, approximately 25 calories per 100 grams fresh weight
