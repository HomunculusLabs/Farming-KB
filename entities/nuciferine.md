# Nuciferine

## Overview

Nuciferine is an aporphine alkaloid and the primary psychoactive constituent
of *Nelumbo nucifera* (sacred lotus). It occurs in the leaves, seeds, and
embryos of the plant at concentrations of 0.02–0.06% dry weight.

The compound has attracted pharmacological interest for its complex
receptor profile, acting as a partial agonist at dopamine D2 and D3
receptors and a serotonin 5-HT2A receptor antagonist. Despite structural
similarity to classical aporphine alkaloids like apomorphine, nuciferine
is not a direct dopamine agonist.

Historically, lotus preparations containing nuciferine have been used in
traditional Egyptian, Chinese, and Indian medicine for their sedative,
anti-diarrheal, and mild psychoactive properties. The blue lotus
(*Nymphaea caerulea*) is sometimes confused with sacred lotus as a
source of nuciferine but contains only trace amounts.

## Chemical Properties

Nuciferine (IUPAC: 1,2-dimethoxyaporphine) has the molecular formula
C19H21NO2 and a molecular weight of 295.38 g/mol. It exists as two
enantiomers, with (−)-nuciferine being the naturally occurring and
pharmacologically active form.

The structure consists of a tetracyclic aporphine skeleton with two
methoxy substituents at positions 1 and 2. The compound is a weak base
(pKa ~7.5) and is moderately lipophilic (logP ~3.1), facilitating blood-
brain barrier penetration.

Nuciferine is sparingly soluble in water but soluble in ethanol, acetone,
and chloroform. It is stable at room temperature and degrades slowly upon
prolonged exposure to light and air.

## Pharmacology

Nuciferine's pharmacological profile is notably complex, involving
activity at multiple monoamine receptors. It acts as a partial agonist
at dopamine D2 and D3 receptors, with moderate affinity (Ki values in
the low micromolar range).

At serotonin receptors, nuciferine shows antagonist activity at 5-HT2A
and 5-HT2C subtypes. This antagonist profile at 5-HT2A distinguishes it
from classical psychedelics, which act as agonists or partial agonists
at this receptor.

Additional receptor activities include antagonism at α1-adrenergic
receptors and histamine H1 receptors. These activities likely contribute
to its reported sedative and antihistaminergic effects in traditional use.

Bhattacharya et al. (1978) demonstrated that (−)-nuciferine produced
dose-dependent sedation and catalepsy in rodents, effects that were
reversed by dopaminergic antagonists, confirming a dopaminergic
mechanism of action.

Recent research has identified anti-inflammatory and potential
antitumor properties. Nuciferine inhibits NF-κB signaling and induces
apoptosis in several cancer cell lines, though these effects require
significantly higher concentrations than those achievable through
traditional lotus preparations.

## Effects and Dosage

Traditional lotus preparations (infusions of leaves or petals) contain
relatively low concentrations of nuciferine. Reported effects from these
preparations include mild relaxation, anxiolysis, and subtle mood
elevation rather than pronounced hallucinogenic experiences.

In experimental settings, isolated nuciferine at doses of 50–200 mg
orally produces mild sedation, reduced anxiety, and mild perceptual
changes. The effects are significantly less intense than classical
psychedelics like psilocybin or mescaline.

Duration of effects is approximately 2–4 hours when taken orally.
Onset occurs within 30–60 minutes. No serious [[blesching-cannabis-safety-adverse-effects-evidence]] have been
documented at traditional doses.

The compound does not appear to produce tolerance, dependence, or
withdrawal. Its relatively benign [[cannabis-safety-profile-and-overdose-risk]] and lack of pronounced
psychedelic effects place it at the mild end of the psychoactive
alkaloid spectrum.

## Natural Sources and History

*Nelumbo nucifera* (sacred lotus, family Nelumbonaceae) is the primary
natural source. All plant parts contain nuciferine, with the highest
concentrations in the leaves and seed embryos.

The lotus has been cultivated for at least 3,000 years across South and
[[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]] and was known to the ancient Egyptians, who used lotus
preparations in religious and medicinal contexts. Nuciferine was first
isolated from lotus leaves in the late 19th century.

Confusion between sacred lotus and blue lotus (*Nymphaea caerulea*) is
common in both the ethnobotanical literature and commercial products.
While *N. caerulea* contains aporphine alkaloids, nuciferine is
characteristic of *Nelumbo*, not *Nymphaea*.

Minor amounts of nuciferine have also been detected in *Nymphaea alba*
(white water lily) and some *Papaver* species, though these are not
significant sources.

## Legal Status

Nuciferine is not specifically scheduled in most jurisdictions. In the
[[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], it is not listed on the DEA Controlled [[controlled-substances-act-and-psychedelic-research]]
and exists in a legal gray area as a naturally occurring plant alkaloid.

In the European Union, nuciferine is not regulated as a controlled
substance. Lotus preparations and extracts are generally legal to
possess and sell. Some countries may restrict sale under novel food
regulations or unapproved supplement rules.
