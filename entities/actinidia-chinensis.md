## Overview

**Actinidia chinensis** Planch., commonly known as the golden kiwifruit or simply kiwi, is a deciduous climbing vine in the family Actinidiaceae. It is native to central and eastern China and is the species from which the commercial kiwifruit industry was originally developed.

The species produces edible berries with a characteristic brown, fuzzy skin (in the common green-fruited form) or smooth, hairless golden skin (in the yellow-fruited cultivar group). The flesh is emerald green or golden-yellow, with a sweet-tart flavor and distinctive aroma.

Global kiwifruit production exceeds 4 million metric tons annually, with New Zealand, Italy, China, Chile, and Greece as the leading producers. The fruit has become a commercially significant crop in temperate and subtropical regions worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Actinidia chinensis was described by Jules Émile Planchon in 1847. The genus Actinidia comprises approximately 60 species native to [[neurotropic-fungi-japan-east-asia-species-cultural-context]], with A. chinensis and A. deliciosa being the two most commercially important.

The authority is **Actinidia chinensis** Planch. The closely related A. deliciosa (A. Chev.) C.F. Liang & A.R. Ferguson was long treated as a variety of A. chinensis but was elevated to species rank in 1984 based on morphological, cytological, and chemical differences.

A. chinensis is a vigorous, deciduous, dioecious climbing vine that can reach 9–15 meters in height when supported. The stems are woody, brown, and covered with reddish-brown lenticels. Young shoots are densely pubescent with reddish-brown hairs.

Leaves are alternate, broadly ovate to cordate, 8–15 cm long and 6–12 cm wide. Leaf margins are finely serrate with reddish-brown lenticels along the margin. The upper surface is dark green and glabrous, while the lower surface is lighter with prominent venation.

Flowers are fragrant, creamy white, 3–5 cm in diameter, and borne in axillary cymes of 1–3 flowers. Male flowers have numerous well-developed stamens; female flowers have a superior ovary with a well-developed style. Flowering occurs in May–June.

Fruit is a berry 4–8 cm in diameter, ovoid to oblong. The skin of A. chinensis var. chinensis (golden kiwifruit) is smooth and hairless, turning brownish-yellow at maturity. The skin of A. chinensis var. hispida (syn. A. deliciosa, green kiwifruit) is brown and densely covered with stiff hairs.

## Distribution and Habitat

Actinidia chinensis is native to eastern and central China, with a natural range extending from Zhejiang and Fujian provinces northward to Shaanxi and Gansu, and westward to Sichuan and Yunnan. The species also occurs naturally in Taiwan and Korea.

The species inhabits forest margins, thickets, and stream valleys in mountainous regions at elevations of 200–2,300 meters. It is a component of broadleaf and mixed forest communities, often climbing on canopy trees in forest-edge situations.

## Cultivation

Commercial kiwifruit orchards are typically established on T-bar or pergola trellis systems at spacings of 4–6 meters between rows and 2–3 meters between vines. Male-to-female planting ratios of 1:5 to 1:8 are standard for adequate pollination.

The species is dioecious, requiring separate male and female plants for fruit production. Pollination is primarily by insects, particularly honeybees and bumblebees. Inadequate pollination results in misshapen fruit with reduced seed count and poor size.

Pruning is performed twice annually: winter pruning to establish the framework and remove unproductive wood, and summer pruning to control vigor and maintain light penetration. The vine is extremely vigorous and can produce 8–12 meters of new shoot growth per season.

Major commercial cultivars include 'Hort16A' (marketed as Zespri Gold, a golden-fleshed A. chinensis selection) and 'Sungold' (a later golden cultivar). Green-fleshed cultivars including 'Hayward' (A. deliciosa) remain the most widely planted worldwide.

Soluble solids content at harvest should reach at least 6.2 °Brix for golden kiwifruit and 6.5 °Brix for green kiwifruit to ensure acceptable eating quality after ripening. Dry matter content is increasingly used as a harvest maturity indicator.

## Nutritional Value and Chemistry

Kiwifruit is among the most nutrient-dense of all commonly consumed fruits. A single serving (approximately 100 g) provides more than 100% of the recommended daily intake of vitamin C (80–120 mg/100 g), exceeding that of citrus fruits.

The fruit is rich in vitamin K (40–50 μg/100 g), vitamin E (1.5–2.5 mg/100 g), folate (25–30 μg/100 g), and potassium (300–350 mg/100 g). Dietary fiber content ranges from 3–4 g/100 g, with both soluble and insoluble fractions.

Actinidin, a cysteine protease unique to Actinidia species, is the major protein in kiwifruit flesh. It constitutes 1–2% of fresh weight and is responsible for both the fruit's tenderizing properties in culinary applications and its allergenic potential in sensitive individuals.

Polyphenolic compounds include flavonoids ([[quercetin]], kaempferol, myricetin), phenolic acids, and carotenoids. Golden kiwifruit cultivars contain significantly higher levels of carotenoids (β-carotene, lutein) than green-fleshed varieties.

## Commercial Industry

New Zealand pioneered the commercial kiwifruit industry, with the first export shipment of A. deliciosa 'Hayward' in 1952. The country remains a global leader in kiwifruit breeding, production technology, and marketing under the Zespri brand.

China is now the world's largest kiwifruit producer, with production concentrated in Shaanxi, Sichuan, and Guizhou provinces. Total Chinese production exceeds 2 million metric tons annually, representing approximately half of global supply.

Italy is the second-largest producer and the largest in the Northern Hemisphere. Italian kiwifruit production is concentrated in the Latina province of Lazio and in Piedmont. Chile and Greece round out the top five producing nations.

The kiwifruit industry faces several phytosanitary challenges, most notably Pseudomonas syringae pv. actinidiae (Psa), a bacterial canker disease that caused devastating losses in Italy and New Zealand from 2010 onward. Breeding for Psa resistance is now a major priority.
