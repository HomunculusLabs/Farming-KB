# Agrobacterium tumefaciens

## Description

**Agrobacterium tumefaciens** is a gram-negative, soil-dwelling bacterium best known as the causal agent of **crown gall disease** in dicotyledonous plants. This pathogenic bacterium is remarkable for its ability to transfer a segment of its own DNA (T-DNA) into the genome of host plant cells, genetically transforming them to produce opines — compounds that the bacterium uses as a carbon and nitrogen source. This natural genetic engineering mechanism has made *A. tumefaciens* one of the most important tools in plant biotechnology.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Bacteria
- **Phylum:** Proteobacteria
- **Class:** Alphaproteobacteria
- **Order:** Rhizobiales
- **Family:** Rhizobiaceae
- **Genus:** *Agrobacterium* (reclassified as *Rhizobium* in some systems)
- **Species:** *A. tumefaciens*

## Key Facts

- Causes crown gall disease — tumor-like growths on stems and roots of susceptible plants.
- Uses a tumor-inducing (Ti) plasmid to transfer T-DNA into plant cell genomes.
- The only known organism that naturally transfers DNA between kingdoms (bacteria → plants).
- Transformed plant cells produce opines — unique amino acid derivatives that serve as bacterial nutrients.
- Primarily infects dicots (broadleaf plants); most monocots (grasses) are resistant.
- Enters plants through wounds — pruning cuts, insect damage, or mechanical injury.
- Crown galls disrupt water and nutrient transport, weakening or killing the host plant.
- Found worldwide in soils, particularly in agricultural settings.
- Closely related to beneficial nitrogen-fixing *Rhizobium* species.

## Role in the Soil Food Web

In Lowenfels' framework, *A. tumefaciens* represents the pathogenic bacterial element:

- **Plant pathogen:** Attacks over 600 plant species, causing economic losses in orchards, vineyards, and nurseries.
- **Opine niche creation:** Crown galls create a unique ecological niche by producing opines that specifically feed the pathogen, demonstrating how soil food web dynamics can be hijacked by pathogens.
- **Soil reservoir:** Can persist in soil for years without a host, waiting for wound entry points.
- **Biological control:** Beneficial soil bacteria (e.g., *Agrobacterium radiobacter* K84) can competitively exclude the pathogen, illustrating the importance of maintaining diverse bacterial populations.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Orchard management:** Crown gall is a serious problem in fruit trees, grapes, roses, and ornamental nursery stock. Sanitation and avoiding plant wounds are key preventive measures.
- **Biotechnology:** *A. tumefaciens* is the workhorse of plant genetic engineering, used to introduce genes for pest resistance, herbicide tolerance, and nutritional enhancement.
- **Soil health:** Healthy, microbially diverse soils suppress *A. tumefaciens* through competition, antibiosis, and induced plant resistance. Compost and compost tea applications can help.
- **Nursery practices:** Dip treatments with biological control strains (K84) protect transplants and cuttings.
- **Mycology connection:** Agrobacterium-mediated transformation has been adapted for fungal genetic transformation, enabling molecular studies of mushroom-producing fungi.
- **Grape vine susceptibility:** Grape vines are particularly susceptible to crown gall, especially after freeze injury creates wound entry points.

## Management and Control

- **Prevention:** Use certified disease-free nursery stock; avoid wounding during planting and cultivation.
- **Biological control:** *Agrobacterium radiobacter* strain K84 (commercially available as Galltrol, Nogall).
- **Cultural practices:** Good drainage, proper pruning, and avoiding excessive nitrogen fertilization.
- **Soil biology:** Maintain diverse microbial communities through compost and organic matter additions.
