# Hardiness Zones

## Description

Hardiness zones are a **standardized climate classification system** used primarily in North American horticulture to categorize geographic regions based on their average annual minimum winter temperature. The system divides regions into numbered zones, each defined by a 10°F (approximately 5.5°C) range of average annual minimum temperatures. As cited by Mollison from **Hortus Third**, the system provides 12 zones ranging from arctic tundra (below -50°F / -45°C) to equatorial rainforest (above 50°F / 10°C).

## Classification

- **Domain:** Horticulture and Agriculture
- **Category:** Climate classification system
- **Primary metric:** Average annual minimum temperature
- **Source authority:** USDA (United States Department of Agriculture), documented in Hortus Third

## Key Facts

- **12 zones** defined by average annual minimum temperature:
  - Zone 1: Below -50°F (-45°C) — Arctic tundra
  - Zone 2: -50 to -40°F — Cold prairie and conifers
  - Zone 3: -40 to -30°F — Conifers and mixed forests
  - Zone 4: -30 to -20°F — Cold interiors of continents
  - Zone 5: -20 to -10°F — Mixed forests, cool prairies
  - Zone 6: -10 to 0°F — Broadleaf and deciduous forests
  - Zone 7: 0 to 10°F — Broadleaf forests
  - Zone 8: 10 to 20°F — Arid grasslands, savannah
  - Zone 9: 20 to 30°F — Semi-arid coasts and basins
  - Zone 10: 30 to 40°F — Sub-tropical, palms, coasts
  - Zone 11: 40 to 50°F — Tropical forests, deserts
  - Zone 12: Over 50°F — Equatorial rainforests, monsoon
- Zones are commonly appended to plant listings in **nurseries and seed catalogs** throughout the USA.
- The system has **important limitations**: it considers only minimum temperature and ignores precipitation, humidity, soil conditions, and summer heat.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Mollison presents hardiness zones as a useful but limited tool for [[permaculture-design]]:

- **Initial screening**: Hardiness zones provide a quick first-pass filter for plant viability at a site based on winter survival.
- **Limitation awareness**: Mollison emphasizes that averages (including hardiness zone averages) are poor guides — extremes matter more. A plant may survive the average winter but die in an exceptional cold event.
- **Complementary systems**: Hardiness zones work best alongside the **Köppen classification** (for broad climate understanding) and the **Holdridge Life Zone Matrix** (for more refined plant-climate matching).
- **Fail-safe design**: [[permaculture]] designers should select species rated for at least one zone colder than their site to account for extreme years.

The hardiness zone concept reinforces Mollison's broader point that **climatic averages are insufficient** for reliable design — designers must account for variability, extremes, and local modifying factors such as slope, aspect, and proximity to water bodies.
