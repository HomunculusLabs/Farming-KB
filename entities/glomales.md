## Description

Glomales is a clade of fungi forming arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) associations with plant roots. This group is estimated to associate with approximately 80% of all plant species, making it one of the most ecologically important fungal lineages on Earth. Phylogenetically, Glomales is a sister clade to the Ascomycota/Basidiomycota lineage, and together these three groups constitute the monophyletic "crown fungi"—the most derived grouping within [[kingdom-fungi]].

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Historically Zygomycota; now recognized as separate clade within crown fungi
- **Order**: Glomales
- **Traditional families**: Glomaceae, Gigasporaceae, Acaulosporaceae
- **Newly recognized families**: Archaeosporaceae, Paraglomaceae
- **Ecological role**: Obligate arbuscular [[mycorrhiza]]l symbionts

## Key Facts

### Ecological Significance

- Associated with an estimated 80% of all plant species worldwide
- Origin and diversification probably coincided with origin of land plants (Simon et al. 1993; Blackwell 2000; Redecker et al. 2000a; Berbee and Taylor 2001)
- Essential for plant nutrient acquisition from soil
- Indispensable for terrestrial ecosystem function
- Crown-fungus clade represents the most derived monophyletic grouping within [[kingdom-fungi]]
- AM associations formed by Glomales are among the most widespread types of [[endomycorrhizal-fungi]]
- Their global distribution and ancient co-evolution with plants makes them a key focus of [[biodiversity-fungal-species-abundance-diversity]] research in terrestrial ecosystems

### Phylogenetic Position

- Sister clade to [[ascomycota]]/[[basidiomycota]] (Simon et al. 1993; Nagahama et al. 1995; Gehrig et al. 1996; Redecker et al. 2000b; Platt and Spatafora 2000; Gernandt et al. 2001)
- Together with [[ascomycota]] and [[basidiomycota]] forms the "crown fungi" monophyletic clade
- Previously classified within Zygomycota but phylogenetically distinct
- The ancestor linking Glomales to other crown fungi is yet to be identified
- Research by [[meredith-blackwell]] and [[joseph-spatafora]] helped clarify these relationships within the broader [[biodiversity-of-fungi-fungal-phylogeny-kingdom-classification]] framework

### Major Lineages

- **Glomaceae**: Traditional family with broad host range; includes [[glomus-fasciculatum]], [[glomus-intraradices]], and [[glomus-mosseae]]
- **Gigasporaceae**: Sister group to Glomaceae; includes [[gigaspora-marginata]]
- **Acaulosporaceae**: Traditional family within Glomales; includes [[acaulospora-laevis]]
- **Archaeosporaceae**: Previously unrecognized family; evolved from outside the Glomalean lineage despite superficial morphological similarities
- **Paraglomaceae**: Another previously unrecognized family; also evolved independently of core Glomales

### Surprising Discoveries

- Archaeosporaceae and Paraglomaceae share many superficial morphological similarities with Glomales but evolved from outside the lineage
- Differences in mycorrhizal morphology, fatty acid profiles, and immunological reactions against monoclonal antibodies all agree with molecular evidence
- *Geosiphon*, a symbiont of cyanobacteria, may be derived from within the Glomales rather than being ancestral to AM fungi (Redecker et al. 2000b; Morton and Redecker 2001)
- Earlier morphological studies did not provide an accurate view of the group's genetic diversity
- Molecular surveys now reveal much higher [[biodiversity-glomeromycota-diversity]] than morphology-based estimates

### Diversity and Distribution

- Cosmopolitan distribution in terrestrial habitats
- Cannot be cultured without a plant host (obligate symbionts)
- Previously thought to have low species diversity; molecular studies reveal much higher diversity
- Form characteristic structures: arbuscules (tree-like intracellular hyphal networks), vesicles (storage structures), and auxiliary cells
- Molecular detection methods have transformed our understanding of [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]] within this group

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

While Glomales species are not directly cultivated for mushroom production (they do not produce macroscopic fruiting bodies), they are critically important in cultivation contexts. Many cultivated plants benefit from or require AM fungal associations for optimal growth, nutrient uptake, and disease resistance. Understanding that Glomales forms a separate phylogenetic lineage within the crown fungi helps explain their unique biology—they share some features with Zygomycota and [[basidiomycota]] but are neither, representing an ancient and distinct lineage that co-evolved with land plants. In sustainable agriculture and permaculture, AM fungal inoculation is increasingly used to enhance crop productivity.

The [[vam-vesicular-arbuscular-mycorrhizae]] formed by these fungi are particularly important for phosphorus uptake in crops, and inoculum production is a growing commercial sector. Understanding Glomales phylogeny informs [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-inoculum-production-best-practices]] by helping identify which lineages are most effective for particular host plants and soil conditions.

Glomalean fungi are among the oldest known mycorrhizal associations, with fossil evidence from the Ordovician period (approximately 460 million years ago) confirming their ancient coevolution with land plants. This deep evolutionary history explains why approximately 80% of vascular plant families form arbuscular mycorrhizal partnerships with glomalean fungi.

In agricultural systems, glomalean inoculants are increasingly used to reduce dependence on synthetic fertilizers. Field trials demonstrate that [[mycorrhiza|mycorrhizal]] inoculation can improve phosphorus uptake by 40-60% in marginal soils, making these fungi critical tools for sustainable agriculture and [[permaculture]] design.
