## Description

Wallabies are small to mid-sized macropod marsupials native to Australia and New Guinea. Mollison references his own studies of wild wallaby behavior to illustrate ecological scheduling — the concept that animals do not control fixed territories but rather occupy time-slots in shared spaces. Wallabies in Tasmania exhibit two prime activity peaks over 24 hours, both at night: crepuscular (just after sundown) and auroral (just before dawn), permitting rest periods between feeding.

## Classification

## Key Facts

- Mollison's field observations in Tasmania revealed:
  - Wallabies have two prime activity peaks: crepuscular (post-sunset) and auroral (pre-dawn)
  - This scheduling allows digestive and recuperative rest periods
  - Weaker animals denied preferred time slots are displaced to suboptimal periods
  - Possums can displace wallabies at feeding places through aggression
  - Individuals hold feeding places only briefly before moving on
- Wallabies are herbivorous, grazing on grasses and browsing shrubs
- Body size ranges from 1.6 kg (dusky pademelon) to 24 kg (whiptail wallaby)
- Like all macropods, wallabies use hopping locomotion, which is highly energy-efficient at speed
- Many species are nocturnal or crepuscular, avoiding daytime heat and predation
- Mollison notes that wallabies, urban people, and possums all demonstrate time-slot sharing rather than fixed territoriality

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Understanding grazing schedules informs [[mycoremediation]] of pasture land — timing fungal inoculation around animal movement
- Wallaby dung supports coprophilous (dung-loving) fungi including *Panaeolus*, *Coprinus*, and *Panaeolina* species
- The scheduling principle applies to substrate management: different decomposer fungi occupy the same substrate at different stages
- Marsupial digestive systems are less efficient than ruminants, producing dung that is more intact and favorable for fungal colonization
- The time-slot sharing concept parallels how multiple fungal species can sequentially colonize the same substrate
