# Inocybe coelestium
## Overview
*Inocybe coelestium* is a rare psychoactive mushroom in the family Inocybaceae, placed within section Rimosae. It is one of the few [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-inocybe-species]] species known to contain psilocybin and psilocin. First documented in Europe, it has been recorded from France, the Netherlands, Germany, and Scandinavia.
The species epithet *coelestium* (Latin for "sky-colored" or "celestial") refers to the characteristic blue-green tinge the basidiocarp often displays when fresh. This feature is subtle and transient, making field identification particularly challenging even for experienced mycologists.
Potency is reportedly low to moderate compared to well-known [[psilocybin-mushrooms]]. The species is not cultivated commercially and must be identified with extreme caution due to the high prevalence of toxic muscarine-containing lookalikes within the same genus. Misidentification carries genuine risk of serious poisoning.
## Taxonomy and Naming
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Inocybaceae
- **Genus:** Inocybe (Fr.) Fr.
- **Section:** Rimosae (Quélet) Singer
- **Species:** *I. coelestium*
The species belongs to a small group of neurotropic Inocybe species that also includes *I. haemacta* and *I. tricolor*. These species share the unusual trait of producing psilocybin within a genus otherwise dominated by toxic, muscarine-containing mushrooms. The discovery of psilocybin in Inocybe was pharmacologically significant, as it demonstrated that tryptamine alkaloid production had evolved independently across multiple genera within Agaricales.
The taxonomic history of *I. coelestium* is complex, with some historical collections having been conflated with other bluing Inocybe species. Modern molecular phylogenetic studies using ITS sequencing have helped clarify its placement within section Rimosae, though species-level delimitation within this section remains challenging due to morphological conservatism and potential cryptic diversity.
Additional phylogenetic work using multilocus sequencing (ITS, LSU, RPB2) has confirmed that the neurotropic Inocybe species form a monophyletic clade within section Rimosae, distinct from the muscarine-containing lineages. This supports the hypothesis that psilocybin biosynthesis in Inocybe arose from a single evolutionary event within this clade, rather than through multiple independent acquisitions.
No named varieties or subspecies of *I. coelestium* are currently recognized. Some authors have treated certain collections as distinct but closely allied taxa based on subtle differences in spore morphology and cheilocystidial shape, but these distinctions lack molecular support at present.
## Description
### Cap
Small to medium, 15–45 mm diameter. Conical to bell-shaped (campanulate) when young, expanding to broadly umbonate or nearly plane with age. Surface hygrophanous, fibrillose-silky with fine radial fibrils. Color pale grey-brown when dry, developing a distinctive slight blue-green tinge when fresh and moist — the feature for which the species is named.
The blue-green coloration is thought to result from the oxidation of psilocybin and psilocin compounds within the cap tissue, analogous to the [[field-guide-psilocybin-bluing-reaction-identification]] observed in many *Psilocybe* species. However, the bluing in *I. coelestium* is notably less intense and more restricted in distribution compared to typical *Psilocybe* bruising reactions.
This coloration is most evident on the disc and is best observed in fresh, undisturbed specimens under good lighting conditions. The blue-green hue fades rapidly as specimens desiccate. Margin often slightly appendiculate in young specimens, irregularly cracked or rimose with age. Flesh thin, pale greyish, not notably changing on exposure or bruising.
### Gills
Adnate to sinuate attachment, moderately crowded. Initially pale grey, developing a brownish tone as spores reach maturity. Gill edges paler than the faces (slightly pruinose from the projecting cheilocystidia). Lamellulae (short gills) of varying lengths present between full gills, adding to the crowded appearance. Gills are not notably decurrent and do not stain blue.
In contrast to some *Psilocybe* species, the gills of *I. coelestium* do not exhibit bluing even when the cap shows the characteristic blue-green tinge. This discrepancy between cap and gill coloration is a notable feature that can help distinguish it from bluing *Psilocybe* species with which it might otherwise be superficially confused.
### Stem
Slender, equal or slightly tapering toward the base, 40–80 mm long, 2–5 mm thick. Surface pruinose (frosted appearance) near the apex from fine crystalline deposits, becoming smooth to faintly fibrillose below. Whitish to pale brownish overall.
Some specimens may show a slight bluish discoloration at the base when handled, though this is variable and not reliable for identification. Stem flesh stuffed when young, becoming hollow with age. No distinct ring or volva present. The stem base may be slightly enlarged or bulbous in some collections.
### Spores
[[spore-print]] brown (mid-brown, similar to other Inocybe species). Spores smooth, bean-shaped to amygdaliform, thin-walled, with a small but distinct apical germ pore visible under oil immersion. Spore wall does not stain in Melzer's reagent (inamyloid).
The Q (Quotient) value for spores — the ratio of length to width — typically ranges from 1.5 to 1.8, which is consistent with the broader range reported for section Rimosae. This metric can assist in distinguishing *I. coelestium* from species with markedly different spore proportions.

## Distribution and Habitat
*Inocybe coelestium* is known from western and central Europe, with confirmed records from France, the Netherlands, Germany, and Scandinavia. It is considered rare throughout its range and is likely underreported due to identification difficulty and the general tendency of foragers and mycologists alike to avoid the genus Inocybe entirely.
The species fruits in deciduous and mixed forests, particularly in association with rich, composted soils. It is also found in parks, gardens, and disturbed ground with significant organic matter accumulation. Fruiting season is late summer to autumn (August–November in the Northern Hemisphere), with peak fruiting typically in September and October.
It may be ectomycorrhizal or facultatively saprotrophic, consistent with many Inocybe species that occupy an ecological niche between mycorrhizal associat  position. Collections have been made most commonly from nutrient-enriched sites, suggesting a preference for soils with elevated nitr  lability. This may explain its occasional appearance in horticultural settings and managed green spaces.
Associated tree hosts in deciduous forests include *Quercus* spp. (oak fagus sylvatica tica* (European beech), *Betula* spp. (birch), and *Carpinus betulus* (hornbeam). In mixed forests, it may also associate with *[[picea-abies]]* (Norway s pinus sylvestris s sylvestris* (Scots pine), though reports under conifers are less common. The species is typically found as solitary specimens or in small scattered groups of 2–5 basidiocarps, rarely in large troops.
Environmental conditions favoring fruiting include sustained periods of warm, humid weather following rainfall. Soil temperatures between 12–18°C and moderate to high humidity appear to be optimal. The species has been recorded at elevations ranging from near sea level to approximately 800 meters.
## Chemistry and Active Compounds
*Inocybe coelestium* contains the classical tryptamine alkaloids:
- **Psilocybin** — the prodrug phosphorylated form; primary psychoactive compound, acts as a 5-HT2A receptor agonist
- **Psilocin** — the active dephosphorylated metabolite; responsible for the primary subjective effects
No quantitative analytical data (e.g., HPLC-MS values expressed as mg/g dry weight) have been widely published for this species. Based on limited bioassays reported in the mycological literature, potency is estimated as low to moderate relative to well-known psilocybin mus [[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]] ilocybe cubensis* or *P. semilanceata*.
Importantly, the species does **not** contain detectable levels of muscarine, unlike the majority of Inocybe species. The presence of psilocybin in *I. coelestium* and its close relatives is of particular evolutionary interest, as it represents an independent acquisition of tryptamine biosynthesis within a genus not otherwise known for this pathway. The selective pressures driving this convergence remain unclear and are an active area of research in fungal chemistry.
Minor alkaloids that may be present include baeocystin and norbaeocystin, which are common co-occurring compounds in psilocybin-containing mushrooms, though these have not been specifically confirmed in *I. coelestium* through analytical methods. Aeruginascin, a indole alkaloid with potential neuromodulatory properties found in some Inocybe species (e.g., *I. aeruginascens*), has not been reported from *I. coelestium*.
The pharmacological profile of ingested *I. coelestium* is expected to be similar to other psilocybin-containing species, with typical effects including visual and auditory alterations, altered perception of time, synesthesia at moderate doses, and introspective or mystical-type experiences. Onset is typically 20–60 minutes post-ingestion, with effects lasting 4–6 hours depending on dose and individual metabolism.
Due to the low to moderate potency and the significant risk of misidentifying this species, intentional consumption is extremely ill-advised. The consequences of confusing *I. coelestium* with a muscarine-containing Inocybe species far outweigh any expected psychoactive effects.

## Field Identification Tips
1. **Blue-green tinge on fresh cap** is the most distinctive macroscopic feature; however, it is subtle, transient, and easily missed or confused with incidental coloration from algae or debris.
2. **Habitat in parks and gardens** on rich, composted soil is suggestive but not diagnostic, as many Inocybe species grow in identical settings.
3. **Absence of a strong mealy or spermatic odor** (common in many Inocybe species) may help narrow identification but is not definitive.
4. **[[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] is essential** — cheilocystidia with crystalline incrustations and the presence of clamp connections at all septa are key diagnostic characters.
5. **Spore color and morphology** (brown print, amygdaliform spores) align with the genus but do not distinguish species within it.
6. **Never rely on macroscopic features alone.** Many Inocybe species are morphologically similar, and misidentification can have serious or life-threatening health consequences.
7. **Photograph specimens in situ** immediately upon discovery, capturing cap surface coloration under natural light before any handling or desiccation occurs. This documentation is invaluable for post-collection identification.
8. **Collect complete specimens** including any attached substrate or root material, and record precise habitat data, associated tree species, GPS coordinates, and date. These ecological details can provide important corroborating evidence for identification.
## Toxic Lookalikes Warning
**Extreme caution is required.** The genus Inocybe contains numerous species that produce muscarine, a dangerous cholinergic alkaloid. Ingestion of muscarine-containing species causes:
- Excessive salivation, sweating, and lacrimation (SLUDGE syndrome)
- Nausea, vomiting, and abdominal cramping within 30 minutes to 2 hours
- Bradycardia, hypotension, and bronchoconstriction
- Respiratory distress in severe cases; hospitalization may be required
*I. calamistrata*, *I. patouillardii*, and *I. geophylla* are among the common toxic Inocybe species found in similar habitats across Europe. These species lack the blue-green tinge but can appear similar when partially dried, degraded, or poorly lit.
There is no reliable field test (including bruising reactions) to distinguish psychoactive from toxic Inocybe species. Atropine is the specific pharmacological antidote for muscarine poisoning, but symptoms can progress rapidly and respiratory support may be necessary. Positive identification requires microscopic analysis by an experienced mycologist. Foraging for Inocybe species for recreational purposes is strongly discouraged.
It should also be noted that some Inocybe species contain both muscarine and other toxic compounds, potentially compounding the danger. *I. geophylla*, for example, contains not only muscarine but also relatively high levels of the dangerous amatoxin-like compounds in some populations. This underscores the importance of treating all unidentified Inocybe specimens as potentially hazardous.
## Conservation Status
*Inocybe coelestium* has not been formally assessed by the IUCN Red List. Its rarity, limited known distribution, and association with disturbed or managed habitats suggest it may be locally uncommon throughout its range. The species is not protected under any specific legislation in its known range countries.
Population trends are unknown due to insufficient data and underreporting. Habitat loss through urbanization and changes in park management practices could pose a threat, though the species' apparent tolerance of anthropogenic environments may afford some resilience. Collection should be minimal and restricted to scientific documentation. Voucher specimens should be deposited in recognized herbaria with accompanying microscopic documentation.
## Legal Status
The legal status of *Inocybe coelestium* varies by jurisdiction. In most European countries, the species is not specifically named in controlled substances legislation. However, its psilocybin content means it may fall under laws prohibiting the possession of psilocybin-containing fungi in jurisdictions such as the United Kingdom (under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 as a Class A drug), the Netherlands (where fresh psilocybin mushrooms were banned in 2008), and other countries with psilocybin-specific scheduling.
In Germany and France, psilocybin and psilocin are controlled substances under national drug laws, and possession of psilocybin-containing mushrooms — including *I. coelestium* — may be prosecuted accordingly. Researchers studying this species should ensure compliance with all applicable local and national regulations regarding collection, transport, and possession of psychoactive fungi.
Under the 1971 United Nations Convention on Psychotropic Substances, psilocybin and psilocin are listed in Schedule I, which may affect international transport of specimens for research purposes regardless of domestic laws in the country of origin.
