# Inocybe coelestium
## Overview
*Inocybe coelestium* is a rare psychoactive mushroom in the family Inocybaceae, placed within section Rimosae. It is one of the few [[psilocybin-mushrooms]]. The species is not cultivated commercially and must be identified with extreme caution due to the high prevalence of toxic muscarine-containing lookalikes within the same genus. Misidentification carries genuine risk of serious poisoning.
## Taxonomy and Naming
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Inocybaceae
- **Genus:** Inocybe (Fr.) Fr.
- **Section:** Rimosae (Quélet) Singer
- **Species:** *I. coelestium*
The species belongs to a small group of neurotropic Inocybe species that also includes *I. haemacta* and *I. tricolor*. These species share the unusual trait of producing psilocybin within a genus otherwise dominated by toxic, muscarine-containing mushrooms. The discovery of psilocybin in Inocybe was pharmacologically significant, as it demonstrated that tryptamine alkaloid production had evolved independently across multiple genera within Agaricales.
The taxonomic history of *I. coelestium* is complex, with some historical collections having been conflated with other bluing Inocybe species. Modern molecular phylogenetic studies using ITS sequencing have helped clarify its placement within section Rimosae, though species-level delimitation within this section remains challenging due to morphological conservatism and potential cryptic diversity.
Additional phylogenetic work using multilocus sequencing (ITS, LSU, RPB2) has confirmed that the neurotropic Inocybe species form a monophyletic clade within section Rimosae, distinct from the muscarine-containing lineages. This supports the hypothesis that psilocybin biosynthesis in Inocybe arose from a single evolutionary event within this clade, rather than through multiple independent acquisitions.
No named varieties or subspecies of *I. coelestium* are currently recognized. Some authors have treated certain collections as distinct but closely allied taxa based on subtle differences in spore morphology and cheilocystidial shape, but these distinctions lack molecular support at present.
## Description
### Cap
Small to medium, 15–45 mm diameter. Conical to bell-shaped (campanulate) when young, expanding to broadly umbonate or nearly plane with age. Surface hygrophanous, fibrillose-silky with fine radial fibrils. Color pale grey-brown when dry, developing a distinctive slight blue-green tinge when fresh and moist — the feature for which the species is named.
The blue-green coloration is thought to result from the oxidation of psilocybin and psilocin compounds within the cap tissue, analogous to the [[spore-print]] brown (mid-brown, similar to other Inocybe species). Spores smooth, bean-shaped to amygdaliform, thin-walled, with a small but distinct apical germ pore visible under oil immersion. Spore wall does not stain in Melzer's reagent (inamyloid).
The Q (Quotient) value for spores — the ratio of length to width — typically ranges from 1.5 to 1.8, which is consistent with the broader range reported for section Rimosae. This metric can assist in distinguishing *I. coelestium* from species with markedly different spore proportions.

## Distribution and Habitat
*Inocybe coelestium* is known from western and central Europe, with confirmed records from France, the Netherlands, Germany, and Scandinavia. It is considered rare throughout its range and is likely underreported due to identification difficulty and the general tendency of foragers and mycologists alike to avoid the genus Inocybe entirely.
The species fruits in deciduous and mixed forests, particularly in association with rich, composted soils. It is also found in parks, gardens, and disturbed ground with significant organic matter accumulation. Fruiting season is late summer to autumn (August–November in the Northern Hemisphere), with peak fruiting typically in September and October.
It may be ectomycorrhizal or facultatively saprotrophic, consistent with many Inocybe species that occupy an ecological niche between mycorrhizal associat  position. Collections have been made most commonly from nutrient-enriched sites, suggesting a preference for soils with elevated nitr  lability. This may explain its occasional appearance in horticultural settings and managed green spaces.
Associated tree hosts in deciduous forests include *Quercus* spp. (oak fagus sylvatica tica* (European beech), *Betula* spp. (birch), and *Carpinus betulus* (hornbeam). In mixed forests, it may also associate with *[[brassinosteroid-morphological-effects-psilocybe-cubensis-fruiting]] ilocybe cubensis* or *P. semilanceata*.
Importantly, the species does **not** contain detectable levels of muscarine, unlike the majority of Inocybe species. The presence of psilocybin in *I. coelestium* and its close relatives is of particular evolutionary interest, as it represents an independent acquisition of tryptamine biosynthesis within a genus not otherwise known for this pathway. The selective pressures driving this convergence remain unclear and are an active area of research in fungal chemistry.
Minor alkaloids that may be present include baeocystin and norbaeocystin, which are common co-occurring compounds in psilocybin-containing mushrooms, though these have not been specifically confirmed in *I. coelestium* through analytical methods. Aeruginascin, a indole alkaloid with potential neuromodulatory properties found in some Inocybe species (e.g., *I. aeruginascens*), has not been reported from *I. coelestium*.
The pharmacological profile of ingested *I. coelestium* is expected to be similar to other psilocybin-containing species, with typical effects including visual and auditory alterations, altered perception of time, synesthesia at moderate doses, and introspective or mystical-type experiences. Onset is typically 20–60 minutes post-ingestion, with effects lasting 4–6 hours depending on dose and individual metabolism.
Due to the low to moderate potency and the significant risk of misidentifying this species, intentional consumption is extremely ill-advised. The consequences of confusing *I. coelestium* with a muscarine-containing Inocybe species far outweigh any expected psychoactive effects.

## Field Identification Tips
1. **Blue-green tinge on fresh cap** is the most distinctive macroscopic feature; however, it is subtle, transient, and easily missed or confused with incidental coloration from algae or debris.
2. **Habitat in parks and gardens** on rich, composted soil is suggestive but not diagnostic, as many Inocybe species grow in identical settings.
3. **Absence of a strong mealy or spermatic odor** (common in many Inocybe species) may help narrow identification but is not definitive.
4. **[[fungal-microscopic-examination-mounting-media-techniques]] is essential** — cheilocystidia with crystalline incrustations and the presence of clamp connections at all septa are key diagnostic characters.
5. **Spore color and morphology** (brown print, amygdaliform spores) align with the genus but do not distinguish species within it.
6. **Never rely on macroscopic features alone.** Many Inocybe species are morphologically similar, and misidentification can have serious or life-threatening health consequences.
