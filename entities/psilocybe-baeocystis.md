# Psilocybe baeocystis

**Psilocybe baeocystis** Singer & Smith is a moderately to highly potent psilocybin
mushroom native to the Pacific Northwest of North America. Common names include baeos
and knobby tops. The species epithet derives from the Greek words "baion" (small) and
"kystis" (bladder), referring to the shape of the spores, which are characteristically
mango-shaped. P. baeocystis is taxonomically significant as the species from which the
alkaloid baeocystin was first identified, lending its name to this lesser-studied
psychoactive compound.

## Taxonomy and Classification

P. baeocystis was described by Rolf Singer and Alexander H. Smith. It belongs to the
genus Psilocybe within the family Hymenogastraceae. Taxonomically, its closest relative
is Psilocybe aztecorum, its subtropical Mexican cousin. Both species share the tendency
to bleach nearly white when dried or exposed to sunlight. The species is placed in the
section Cyanescens or a closely related group based on its morphological and chemical
affinities.

## Morphology

The cap measures 1.5 to 5.5 cm broad, conic to obtusely conic to convex, expanding to
plane only in extreme age. The margin is incurved when young and distinctly undulated
when convex, translucent-striate and often tinted greenish. Color ranges from dark olive
brown to buff brown, occasionally steel blue, becoming copper brown at the center when
drying, hygrophanous, and fading to pallid white. The cap bruises bluish easily. The
surface is viscid when moist from a gelatinous pellicle that is usually separable.

Gills are adnate to sinuate, close, grayish to dark cinnamon brown with pallid edges.
The stem is 50 to 70 mm long by 2 to 3 mm thick, equal to subequal, pallid to
brownish and sometimes covered with fine whitish fibrils, often more yellowish towards
the apex. The stem is brittle, stuffed with loose fibers, and has distinct rhizomorphs
at the base. The partial veil is thinly cortinate and rapidly becomes inconspicuous.

## Microscopic Features

Spores are purplish brown in deposit and characteristically mango-shaped to ellipsoid,
measuring 10 to 13 by 6 to 7.5 micrometers, with thick walls and a distinct germ pore
at the apical end. Basidia are 4-spored, clavate, measuring 18 to 28 by 5 to 8
micrometers. Pleurocystidia are absent. Cheilocystidia are lageniform to fusoid with a
narrow neck, measuring 20 to 40 by 4.5 to 9 micrometers. The pileipellis is a cutis
composed of repent, interwoven hyphae, with a gelatinous overlay that gives the cap its
viscid texture when moist. Clamp connections are present.

## Distribution and Habitat

P. baeocystis grows gregariously to subcespitose on decaying conifer mulch, in wood
chips, or in lawns with high lignin content. It occasionally fruits from fallen seed
cones of Douglas fir. The species is found in the fall to early winter and rarely in the
spring, with exceptional collections documented as early as June 20. Its known range
extends from British Columbia, Canada, south through Washington and Oregon to northern
California. It is common throughout the Pacific Northwest of the United States and is
particularly associated with conifer duff and woody debris in landscaped and natural
settings. It is frequently found among ivy encroaching over cultivated landscapes near
newly constructed buildings where conifer bark mulch has been applied. In one frequently
studied rhododendron garden, P. baeocystis is found growing side by side with
Psilocybe cyanofibrillosa. The species has not been reported outside the Pacific
Northwest region.

## Chemistry and Potency

P. baeocystis is moderately active but loses significant potency during drying or from
physical damage. Reported alkaloid concentrations range from 0.15% to 0.85% psilocybin,
up to 0.59% psilocin, and up to 0.10% baeocystin (Repke et al. 1977; Beug and Bigwood
1981, 1982b). McCawley et al. (1962) reported 0.63% psilocybin and less than 0.1%
psilocin from specimens analyzed following an intoxication incident. Total psilocybin
content is typically moderate compared to other section Cyanescens species, generally
ranging from 0.2% to 0.6% per dry weight.

The species is notable for its relatively high baeocystin content compared to other
Psilocybe species, with some analyses reporting up to 0.36% baeocystin — the highest of
any species for which this compound has been measured. Baeocystin
(4-phosphoryloxy-N-methyltryptamine) is a phosphorylated tryptamine alkaloid
structurally similar to psilocybin but with one fewer methyl group. It serves as a
biosynthetic intermediate between norbaeocystin and psilocybin in the tryptamine
biosynthetic pathway. Despite early concerns following a fatal poisoning incident
attributed to P. baeocystis consumption in 1960-1961, baeocystin has not been
demonstrated to be toxic at dosages commonly consumed in psychoactive mushroom use. The
pharmacological activity of baeocystin is not fully characterized, but it appears to be
less potent than psilocybin and may contribute to the overall psychoactive profile when
present in significant concentrations.

## The 1961 Poisoning Incident

In 1960 and 1961, eight individuals in the Pacific Northwest experienced unintended
intoxication from mushrooms identified as P. baeocystis. One child died after three days
of hospitalization with a 106 degree F fever and cerebral edema, symptoms consistent
with amatoxin poisoning from Galerina or Amanita species. Subsequent review by Paul
Stamets revealed that photographs of the mushrooms in question showed specimens
resembling Psilocybe cyanescens rather than P. baeocystis. The identifying mycologist,
Alexander Smith, upon confrontation with the photographic discrepancy, retracted his
identification and noted that other mushroom species were present in the yard but were
not preserved. The child's death spurred the search for and discovery of baeocystin as
a novel alkaloid, but P. baeocystis is now considered erroneously attributed to this
poisoning event.

## Similar Species

P. baeocystis can be confused with P. cyanescens (which shares the wood-chip habitat
and blue bruising) and P. cyanofibrillosa (which often grows alongside it). It is
distinguished by its incurved, sometimes inrolled cap margin and its irregular form. The
dark-colored cap is well camouflaged against wood chip backgrounds. Other species in the
Cyanescens complex including P. caerulipes and P. caerulescens share overlapping
morphological features.

## Cultivation

Psilocybe baeocystis has not been widely cultivated and is generally considered more
difficult to grow than P. cubensis or P. cyanescens. The species does not readily fruit
from standard indoor cultivation substrates, and reliable cultivation protocols have not
been published. Wild specimens are strongly preferred by those seeking this species.
Some amateur mycologists have reported limited success colonizing sterilized hardwood
sawdust or wood chip substrates supplemented with bran, similar to methods used for P.
cyanescens, but fruiting initiation appears to require specific environmental conditions
that are difficult to replicate indoors. The species may benefit from outdoor bed
cultivation using conifer duff or bark mulch in shaded, humid environments, mirroring
its natural habitat. Sclerotia production has not been documented for this species.
