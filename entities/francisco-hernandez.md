## Overview

Dr. Francisco Hernández (1514–1587) was a Spanish physician and naturalist who served as the personal physician to King Philip II of Spain. In 1651 (published posthumously from earlier fieldwork), he reported on three types of psychoactive mushrooms worshipped by Mexican natives, providing one of the earliest European documentations of [[teonanacatl]]. His detailed descriptions of the mushrooms" effects constitute invaluable evidence that the Aztec mushroom cult was a real and significant practice, not merely a Spanish misinterpretation of other substances.

## Key Contributions

### Documentation of Teonanacatl (1651)

Hernández's report, based on his fieldwork studying Central American Indian [[herbal-medicine]], described three distinct types of mushrooms venerated by indigenous Mexicans. His descriptions are remarkable for their specificity and accuracy:

**Type 1**: Mushrooms causing "not death but a madness that on occasion is lasting, of which the symptom is a kind of uncontrolled laughter... these are deep yellow, acrid, and of a not displeasing freshness."

**Type 2**: Mushrooms that "without inducing laughter, bring before the eye all sorts of things, such as wars and the likeness of demons."

**Type 3**: Mushrooms "not less desired by princes for their festivals and banquets, and these fetch a high price. With night-long vigils are they sought, awesome and terrifying. This kind is tawny and somewhat acrid."

### Significance of the Report

Hernández"s documentation is significant for several reasons:

- **Three distinct types**: His description of three different mushroom types with different effects suggests that indigenous people were discriminating between multiple [[psilocybin]] species, each with different potency and effect profiles
- **Social context**: The reference to princes and banquets indicates that mushroom use was not merely a peasant practice but was integrated into elite ceremonial culture
- **Economic value**: The mention of high prices and night-long searches indicates significant demand and trade in psychoactive mushrooms
- **Ritual context**: "Night-long vigils" and descriptions of the mushrooms as "awesome and terrifying" confirm the religious/ritual context of use
- **Effect descriptions**: The accounts of uncontrolled laughter, visual hallucinations of wars and demons, and the non-lethal but mind-altering nature of the mushrooms are consistent with psilocybin effects as understood today

## Historical Context

Hernández was sent to New Spain (Mexico) by Philip II in 1570 to study the natural history and medicinal plants of the Americas. He spent seven years (1570–1577) traveling and documenting indigenous knowledge, making him one of the most important early European chroniclers of Mesoamerican ethnobotany.

His work on teonanacatl was part of a broader effort to catalog the medicinal and psychoactive plants used by indigenous peoples. While his primary mission was to identify plants of potential medical value, his careful documentation of the mushroom cult preserved crucial evidence that would later help resolve the scholarly debate over whether the "sacred mushrooms" described by earlier Spanish chroniclers were real.

## Relationship to the Teonanacatl Debate

Hernández"s 1651 report was one of the key historical documents cited during the rediscovery of the Mexican mushroom cult in the 20th century. Along with Sahagún's *Florentine Codex* and Reko's correspondence, Hernández's descriptions helped establish that:

1. The mushrooms described by the Spanish were real organisms, not misidentified peyotl buttons (as William Safford claimed in 1915)
2. Multiple species were used with different effects
3. The mushroom cult was widespread and deeply embedded in Mesoamerican culture
4. The effects described were consistent with psilocybin intoxication

## Legacy

Hernández's documentation remains one of the most important primary sources for the pre-Columbian use of psychoactive mushrooms in Mesoamerica. His detailed, objective descriptions of the mushrooms and their effects provided a foundation for the ethnomycological investigations of the 20th century, from Reko's early reports to the Wassons' watershed discoveries in the 1950s.
