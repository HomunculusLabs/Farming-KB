## Seventh Generation Principle

## Description

The Seventh Generation Principle is an ethical framework originating from the Iroquois (Haudenosaunee) Confederacy, which holds that decisions should be considered in terms of their impact on descendants seven generations into the future — approximately 100 years. Bill Mollison cites this principle in *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* (Chapter 14) as foundational to the permaculture ethic of long-term stewardship.

## Classification

- **Category**: Ethical principle / Governance concept
- **Origin**: Iroquois (Haudenosaunee) Confederacy — Great Law of Peace
- **Adoption**: Referenced in permaculture ethics, environmental philosophy, and sustainability discourse
- **Time horizon**: ~100 years (seven generations at ~15 years per generation)

## Key Facts

- Embedded in the Great Law of Peace of the Iroquois Confederacy as a requirement for all decision-making by sachems (chiefs).
- Requires that every action be evaluated not for immediate benefit but for its consequences on the seventh generation of descendants.
- Mollison uses the principle to explain why tribally managed lands were historically rich in natural resources, while colonial and industrial management has produced widespread ecological degradation.
- Contrasts sharply with the short-term thinking of political systems, which Mollison argues sacrifice long-term environmental health for the short-term policies of elites maintaining power.
- The principle extends beyond environmental management to encompass all aspects of governance, economy, and community life.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Directly informs the permaculture design principle of designing for long-term sustainability — [[building-soil-fertility-long-term]], establishing perennial food systems, and maintaining [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-networks]] that may take decades to fully develop.
- In mycology, the principle supports practices like sustainable mushroom foraging (leaving enough mycelium intact for future fruiting), preserving old-growth forests as fungal habitat, and avoiding soil disturbances that destroy decades-old mycorrhizal networks.
- Encourages the design of polyculture systems that improve over time (food forests, agroforestry) rather than annual monocultures that deplete soil.
- Supports the case for building durable composting systems, maintaining long-term soil health records, and preserving heirloom seed and spawn lines.

## Context in Permaculture Design

Mollison positions the seventh generation principle as a core element of the ethical basis for [[strategies-for-an-alternative-nation]] — one of three key sustainability axioms alongside [[pdc-right-livelihood-and-permaculture-economics]] the development of conserver societies. He argues that sustainable societies must emphasise duties and responsibilities to nature equal to those owed to other people.
