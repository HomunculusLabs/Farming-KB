---# Pueraria montana (Kudzu)

## Overview

**Pueraria montana**, commonly known as kudzu, is a fast-growing, perennial climbing vine in the family Fabaceae (legume family). Native to East Asia (Japan, China, Korea), kudzu was widely introduced to the United States and other regions as an ornamental plant, forage crop, and [[natural-ground-cover-and-straw-mulching]] biomass producer.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Eudicots
- **Clade:** Rosids
- **Order:** Fabales
- **Family:** Fabaceae
- **Subfamily:** Faboideae
- **Genus:** Pueraria
- **Species:** P. montana

The taxonomy of kudzu has been the subject of considerable confusion. P. montana is sometimes treated as a variety of P. lobata (P. lobata var. montana) or considered synonymous with P. lobata. Current molecular phylogenetic work supports P. montana as a distinct species with several varieties:
- **P. montana var. montana:** The invasive variety in the southeastern United States
- **P. montana var. lobata:** Widespread in East Asia, the primary medicinal variety
- **P. montana var. thomsonii:** Cultivated as a vegetable in [[extending-growing-season]] under optimal conditions
- **Vine length:** 10–30 meters (30–100 feet), forming dense mats over vegetation, structures, and terrain
- **Root system:** Massive tuberous root system with a central taproot that can reach 2–3 meters deep and weigh up to 180 kg (400 lbs). The roots store large carbohydrate reserves that enable rapid regrowth after damage or winter dormancy.
- **Lifespan:** Perennial, capable of persisting for 50+ years from established root crowns

### Morphology

- **Stems:** Herbaceous to semi-woody, covered with bristly hairs (hispid). Stems are twining, climbing by clockwise rotation. Become woody at the base with age, developing a rope-like appearance up to 2.5 cm diameter.
- **Leaves:** Compound, trifoliate (three leaflets), 10–20 cm long. Leaflets are broadly ovate, 8–15 cm long and 5–12 cm wide, with entire margins. The lateral leaflets are asymmetrically lobed, giving a distinctive appearance. [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]]:** Symbiotic rhizobia (*Bradyrhizobium* spp.) in root nodules fix atmospheric nitrogen, giving kudzu a competitive advantage on nitrogen-poor soils and altering soil chemistry to favor further invasion
- **Root reserves:** Massive carbohydrate reserves in tuberous roots allow survival through repeated defoliation, cutting, burning, and even herbicide application
- **Vegetative reproduction:** Spreads primarily through stem rooting at nodes — any stem segment contacting soil can form new roots and establish a new plant
- **Low seed requirement:** While seed germination is low, the species spreads effectively through vegetative means

### Environmental Consequences

- **Biodiversity loss:** Dense kudzu mats eliminate native plant communities by shading, creating near-monocultures
- **Forest mortality:** Trees are killed by heavy vine loads that break branches and trunks, and by [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]] increases soil nitrogen, favoring weedy, nitrophilic species over native plants adapted to low-nitrogen soils
- **Altered fire regimes:** Dead kudzu mats create abnormal fuel loads that carry fire into forest canopies, a pattern not typical of native southeastern forests
- **Erosation paradox:** While planted [[mollison-designers-earthworks-for-erosion-control]], kudzu can increase long-term erosion risk by killing deep-rooted trees and creating shallow-rooted vine monocultures
