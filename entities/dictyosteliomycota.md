## Description

Dictyosteliomycota (cellular [[slime-molds]]) are a group of social [[amoebae]] that exist as independent, single-celled organisms for most of their life cycle but aggregate into multicellular structures when food becomes scarce. They inhabit damp, rotting wood, leaves, manure, lawn thatch, rotting mushrooms, and other moist organic material in soils worldwide.

Unlike plasmodial slime molds ([[myxomycota]]), cellular slime molds retain their individual cell membranes and walls throughout the aggregation process. The aggregated mass is a collection of distinct cells working cooperatively rather than a true syncytium.

Key genera include *Dictyostelium*, *Polysphondylium*, and *Acytostelium*. The model organism *Dictyostelium discoideum* is the most intensively studied species, with thousands of published research papers.

Approximately 100–150 species have been described, though many more likely exist in undersampled habitats.

## Life Cycle

### Vegetative Phase

1. **Spore germination**: Dormant spores germinate in moist conditions, releasing individual amoebae (myxamoebae)
2. **Myxamoebae**: Single-celled organisms that crawl through soil pores using pseudopodia
3. **Feeding**: Myxamoebae ingest bacteria, fungal spores, and small protozoa by phagocytosis (engulfing food particles)
4. **Growth and division**: Well-fed myxamoebae divide by binary fission, increasing the population
5. **[[dighton-fungal-nutrient-immobilization-turnover]]**: Ingested nutrients are locked up inside myxamoebae cells, preventing leaching from soil

### Starvation and Aggregation

6. **Starvation signal**: When bacterial food becomes depleted, myxamoebae begin secreting cyclic AMP (cAMP)
7. **Chemotaxis**: Neighboring myxamoebae detect the cAMP gradient and move toward its source
8. **Relay signaling**: Each responding cell amplifies and relays the signal, creating outward-propagating cAMP waves
9. **Stream formation**: Cells align into converging streams, flowing toward aggregation centers
10. **Mound formation**: Up to 125,000+ cells aggregate into a hemispherical mound

### Multicellular Phase

11. **Tip formation**: The mound elongates into a finger-like projection that falls over to form a migrating "slug" (pseudoplasmodium or grex)
12. **Slug migration**: The slug moves toward light and warmth, seeking an appropriate location for fruiting
13. **Differentiation**: Cells within the slug differentiate into prestalk (front, ~20% of cells) and prespore (rear, ~80%) types
14. **Culmination**: The slug stops and reorganizes vertically, with prestalk cells forming a stalk
15. **Fruiting body**: A mature sorocarp (fruiting structure) consists of a stalk supporting a spore-containing sorus at its tip

### Dispersal

16. **Spore release**: Spores are dispersed by wind, water, or passing animals
17. **Germination**: Spores germinate in new locations with adequate moisture and food, restarting the cycle

### Key Distinction: Altruism

The stalk cells die during [[fungal-fruiting-body-formation-environmental-triggers]]—they sacrifice themselves to elevate spore-producing cells for better dispersal. This makes *Dictyostelium* a classic model for studying the evolution of altruism and cooperation.

## Cellular vs. Plasmodial Slime Molds

| Feature | Dictyosteliomycota (Cellular) | Myxomycota (Plasmodial) |
|---------|-------------------------------|--------------------------|
| Cell walls during aggregation | **Retained** (cells remain distinct) | **Lost** (cells fuse into syncytium) |
| Feeding stage | Individual myxamoebae | Multinucleated plasmodium |
| Size of feeding stage | Individual cells (10–20 μm) | Visible macroscopic mass (mm–cm) |
| Aggregation trigger | Starvation (cAMP signaling) | Often environmental cues |
| Nuclear number per cell | Always one (uninucleate) | Thousands (multinucleate) |
| Movement | Amoeboid crawling on substrate | Cytoplasmic streaming within plasmodium |
| Fruiting body | Stalked sorocarp from many cells | Sporangium from single plasmodium |
| Ecological role | Bacterial population control, nutrient cycling | Organic matter processing, nutrient cycling |

## Ecological Role

### Bacterial Population Regulation

Dictyostelid slime molds are significant predators of soil bacteria. Through their voracious phagocytosis during the vegetative phase, they help regulate bacterial populations and prevent any single bacterial species from dominating the soil community.

### Nutrient Cycling

By ingesting bacteria and immobilizing nutrients within their cells, cellular slime molds:

- Prevent nutrient leaching from soil
- Slow-release nutrients as cells die and decompose
- Concentrate dispersed nutrients into aggregated masses
- Make nutrients available to plants and other organisms upon cell lysis

### Soil Structure

The slime produced by individual myxamoebae helps bind soil particles together, contributing to soil aggregation and improved soil structure.

### Food Web Position

Cellular slime molds occupy a middle trophic position:

- **Prey for**: Insect larvae, specialized beetles with mandibles adapted for scooping soft organisms, and worms
- **Predators of**: Bacteria (primary), fungal spores, and small protozoa
- **Competitors with**: Other bacterivorous organisms including nematodes, protozoa, and other slime molds

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Dictyosteliomycota were historically classified among fungi due to their spore-producing fruiting bodies, but molecular phylogenetics places them within Amoebozoa. Despite this phylogenetic distance from fungi, cellular slime molds share ecological niches with fungi — similar habitats (moist organic matter, leaf litter), overlapping food sources (bacteria), and parallel roles in decomposition [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]].

*D. discoideum* is one of the most important non-metazoan model organisms, used to study chemotaxis, cell differentiation, pattern formation, and the evolution of altruism. The stalk cells die during fruiting body formation, sacrificing themselves to elevate spore-producing cells for better dispersal.

While cellular slime molds are not directly managed in cultivation, understanding their role helps explain why maintaining soil moisture [[mollison-humus-and-organic-matter-in-soils]] supports a diverse [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]], and how bacterial populations are naturally regulated in healthy soil.

## Interesting Facts

- *D. discoideum* was discovered in 1935 by Kenneth Raper in forest soil from North Carolina
- Some *Dictyostelium* species "farm" bacteria — carrying bacteria during migration and seeding them at new locations
- Over 150 species have been described worldwide
- Aggregation is coordinated by pulses of cyclic AMP (cAMP) in remarkably precise waves
