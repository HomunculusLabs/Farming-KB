## Overview

**Cibotium schiedei** Schltdl. & Cham., known commonly as the Mexican tree fern, is a large arborescent fern endemic to the cloud forests of central and southern Mexico. It is the northernmost species of Cibotium in the Americas.

The species produces a stout, erect trunk reaching 3–6 meters in height, topped by a crown of large, arching fronds up to 3 meters long. It is a characteristic component of Mexican cloud forest understories.

Cibotium schiedei is one of three Cibotium species native to Mexico, alongside Cibotium chamissoi and the rare Cibotium regale. It is distinguished by its golden-brown trunk hairs and large, hairy croziers.

The species faces habitat loss from cloud forest deforestation and climate-driven upslope migration. It is considered vulnerable throughout much of its range.

Mexican tree ferns represent a relictual element of the Neotropical flora, with Cibotium schiedei serving as a living reminder of the ancient connections between American and Asian fern lineages.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Polypodiophyta
- **Class:** Polypodiopsida
- **Order:** Cyatheales
- **Family:** Dicksoniaceae
- **Genus:** Cibotium
- **Species:** Cibotium schiedei Schltdl. & Cham.

The species was described by Diederich von Schlechtendal and Adelbert von Chamisso in 1830. The specific epithet honors Christian Julius Wilhelm Schiede, a German physician and collector who worked in Mexico.

Cibotium schiedei belongs to the Neotropical clade of Cibotium, which diverged from the Asian-Pacific clade approximately 50 million years ago. The genus shows an ancient Gondwanan distribution pattern.

No synonyms are widely accepted, though some herbarium specimens have been misidentified as Cibotium regale due to overlapping morphology. Spore and scale characters reliably separate the two species.

Chromosome studies indicate a base chromosome number of x = 68 for Cibotium, consistent across the genus. Polyploidy has not been documented in Cibotium schiedei, unlike some Asian congeners.

## Morphology

The trunk is erect, 8–15 cm in diameter, typically 3–6 meters tall. The surface is covered with a thick layer of golden-brown woolly hairs and persistent stipe bases.

Fronds are bipinnate to tripinnate, 2–3 meters long, forming a broad arching crown. The stipe is stout, dark brown, densely covered with long golden-brown hairs and narrow scales.

Pinnae are oblong-lanceolate, 30–50 cm long, with pinnules that are deeply lobed and have serrate margins. The rachis bears scattered golden hairs throughout its length.

Sori are positioned near the pinnule margins, each enclosed in a distinctive bivalvate indusium. The outer valve is formed from a modified leaf segment, while the inner valve is a true indusial structure.

Crozier unfurling produces new fronds throughout the growing season. The emerging crosiers are densely golden-hairy, creating a striking appearance against the dark mature fronds.

The golden hairs on the trunk and croziers can reach 3–5 cm in length, forming a dense woolly covering. These hairs shed water effectively and protect delicate tissues from UV radiation at high elevations.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cibotium schiedei is endemic to Mexico, occurring in the states of Veracruz, Puebla, Oaxaca, Hidalgo, Chiapas, and Guerrero. It is restricted to cloud forest and montane rainforest habitats.

The species grows at elevations of 1,200–2,400 meters on steep, shaded slopes with persistent cloud cover. Annual rainfall in its habitat typically exceeds 2,000 mm, with frequent fog and mist.

It occurs sympatrically with Cibotium chamissoi in parts of Veracruz and Chiapas, though Cibotium schiedei generally occupies higher elevations and wetter sites than its congener.

Key populations occur in the Sierra Madre Oriental (Hidalgo, Veracruz), the Sierra Madre de Oaxaca, and the Sierra Norte de Chiapas. Many sites are within protected areas such as the Reserva de la Biosfera El Cielo.

The species grows on volcanic soils, preferring humus-rich, well-drained substrates. It is most abundant in ravines and on north-facing slopes where moisture accumulates.

In the Sierra de Juárez of Oaxaca, Cibotium schiedei forms dense groves along streams, creating a cathedral-like understory beneath the canopy of oaks and sweetgum trees. These populations are among the most pristine remaining.

## Ecology and Conservation

Cibotium schiedei is classified as Vulnerable by IUCN due to ongoing habitat loss and fragmentation. Cloud forest is among the most threatened ecosystems in Mexico, with deforestation rates exceeding 1% annually.

The species serves as a keystone understory plant in Mexican cloud forests. Its trunk provides substrate for epiphytic orchids, bromeliads, and a diverse community of bryophytes and filmy ferns.

Fallen fronds create a thick mulch layer that retains moisture and supports a rich soil invertebrate fauna. Salamanders of the genus Pseudoeurycea are frequently found sheltering under frond debris near the trunk base.

Climate change poses a significant long-term threat. Warming temperatures push the cloud condensation level higher, reducing the area of suitable montane habitat available for Cibotium schiedei.

Livestock grazing in montane forests damages tree fern regeneration by trampling gametophytes and young sporophytes. Cattle and goats browse on emerging croziers, preventing frond development.

Conservation measures include protection within biosphere reserves and national parks. Ex situ conservation at botanical gardens in Mexico City and Xalapa maintains genetic material from threatened populations.

Community-based conservation initiatives in Oaxaca and Veracruz involve local landowners in protecting cloud forest fragments. Payment for ecosystem services programs provide economic incentives to maintain tree fern habitat.

## Cultivation and Uses

Cibotium schiedei is cultivated as an ornamental in Mexican botanical gardens and occasionally in international fern collections. Its golden croziers and arching fronds make it an attractive specimen.

The species requires cool, humid conditions with protection from direct sun and wind. It grows best in well-drained, organic-rich soil with consistent moisture throughout the year.

Cold tolerance extends to approximately -2°C for brief periods, making it suitable for frost-free subtropical gardens. Prolonged cold causes frond loss but the trunk typically survives and resprouts.

Propagation from spores is possible but slow, with germination taking 6–10 weeks. Sporophytes require 2–3 years of nursery care before reaching a size suitable for outplanting.

In traditional Mexican medicine, trunk fibers have been used as a wound dressing, similar to the pulu of Hawaiian Cibotium species. The hairs contain tannins with mild antimicrobial properties.

The trunk core was historically eaten as a starch source during food scarcity in some indigenous communities of Veracruz and Oaxaca. This use has largely disappeared with modern food systems.

Ethnobotanical surveys in the Sierra Norte de Puebla document continued use of Cibotium schiedei hairs as a styptic for minor cuts and scrapes. The practice is particularly common in remote Nahua communities.
