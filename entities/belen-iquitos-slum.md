## Description

Belen is an urban slum district of Iquitos, Peru, located on the banks of the Amazon River. It served as the primary fieldwork site for anthropologist Marlene Dobkin de Rios during her year-long study (1968-1969) of ayahuasca healing practices. Belen is one of the most significant anthropological field sites for the study of Amazonian folk healing and psychedelic plant use in urban contexts. The community of some 12,000 people dwells in houses built on rafts, and served as the primary source for data on drug healers and patients during Dobkin de Rios"s research.

## Classification

- **Type:** Urban community / ethnographic field site
- **Location:** Iquitos, Loreto Region, Peruvian Amazon
- **Population:** Approximately 12,000 at time of study (1968-1969)
- **Geographic features:** Built on rafts and stilts due to seasonal Amazon River flooding (at least 4 months per year)
- **Economic status:** Extreme poverty, high unemployment, chronic malnutrition
- **Primary researcher:** Marlene Dobkin de Rios (1968-1969 fieldwork)

## Key Facts

- Belen functions as a receiving area for vast numbers of forest migrants who come to urban centers such as Iquitos in search of work and better schooling for their children
- The community suffers from exceedingly high unemployment, excessive malnutrition, family breakdown, prostitution, vandalism, chronic illness, and a host of other social pathologies
- Houses are built on balsa log supports or on balsa rafts, which allows them to rise with the changing water level during flood season
- Householders must use canoes to get to market or the city proper during the flooded months
- The local economy centers on small-scale buying and reselling of vegetable or forest produce by slum residents who possess small amounts of capital
- Wholesalers called rematistas fill the largest single occupational category in Belen
- Some entrepreneurs, called regatones, own motor-powered boats loaded with staples such as rice, sugar, coffee, and gasoline, which they sell at considerable markup to one-crop river hamlets
- Forest peasants devote much of their time to hunting precious animal pelts, neglecting agricultural activity, and thus provide the regaton with a steady market for food staples
- Fishing in the nearby Itaya River was historically important but depleted by growing populations and indiscriminate fishing; fishing trips today take men away from Belen for periods of four to six weeks
- Women are left behind, staying alive as best they can by reselling produce in the market; their children often work at odd jobs to help out
- Formal medical consultation in the city of Iquitos is far too costly for most slum residents; an initial visit costs the equivalent of two days' food budget for many
- The city hospital has the reputation as a place one goes in order to die, especially because of its casual way of dealing with poor people

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Belen is a key site for understanding how traditional Amazonian plant medicine practices, including ayahuasca healing, adapt to and persist within urban, impoverished contexts. The community hosts large numbers of both ayahuasqueros (folk healers) and brujos (witches) who use plant hallucinogens in curing and sorcery. The slum's conditions of extreme poverty, social pathology, and limited access to modern medicine create a social environment where traditional plant-based healing continues to thrive alongside pharmaceutical remedies. For cultivators and researchers, Belen exemplifies the real-world context in which ayahuasca and other plant medicines are consumed and administered, a context very different from the ceremonial settings familiar to Western audiences.

## Cultural Notes

- Folk illnesses prevalent in Belen include susto, dano, pulsario, and mal de ojo
- Healers and fortune tellers (curiosas) charge small fees for culturally valued services
- Love magic practices are elaborate and linked to harsh economic conditions
- The naipes (fortunetelling cards) are a common method of divination and data gathering used by both healers and the anthropologist
- Dietary proscriptions surrounding ayahuasca use include abstention from salt, lard, or sweets for at least 24 hours preceding and following the use of the purge
- Sexual abstinence may also be demanded by the healer prior to the session
- Poorly trained medical aides called sanitarios are frequent slum visitors who dispense penicillin or antibiotic injections for a small fee

## Ethnographic Fieldwork

Dobkin de Rios conducted her fieldwork from June 1968 to May 1969 in Belen. She established a small consultation center in a local Belefino house by telling fortunes with naipes (fortunetelling cards), obtaining a reputation as a curiosa -- a fortuneteller who charged a mere pittance for her services. She charged a small amount of money to make the service culturally valued, and used the funds to purchase gifts of medicine and food for slum residents. Throughout the year's fieldwork, rapport was established with many slum families through gifts of black and white photographs, which facilitated invitations to festivals and funerals.

During the course of the year, contact was established with ten healers and several sessions were observed. Most data on belief systems were gathered from four healers willing to spend time discussing their activities. To cross-check data, she paid particular attention to card-reading materials, where various clients interpreted misfortune cards in light of illnesses they were experiencing. Toward the end of her stay, she administered the Thematic Apperception Test to slum residents.

## Disease Etiology in Belen

Among Belefinos who live in an area of immense overcrowding, suffer from chronic underemployment, and are subject to high levels of malnutrition, disease is a constant companion. Informants describe various types of illness, some of which they consider to be simple and God-given. Colds, upper-respiratory infections, and some skin disorders can be easily treated. When a simple fever, pain, or ache does not respond well to treatment, illness is frequently attributed to the malice of another person or to the punishment of a natural spirit. Perhaps a taboo has been broken by a person who has offended a spirit of nature.

Most slum residents suffer from magical illnesses -- those that afflict patients whose anxieties, fears, projections of hostility and hatred toward others would propel them toward psychiatric help in Western medicine. Drug-healing in the Peruvian Amazon in many ways represents a very old and time-honored tradition of dealing with psychological problems that predates Freudian analysis by many centuries.

## Ayahuasca Healing in Belen

In Belen, the healer (ayahuasquero) conducts drug ceremonies for groups of patients in clearings at the edge of the city. The ayahuasca potion, made from cutting and boiling the woody vine several hours (and often containing additives besides various Banisteriopsis species), is taken by the healer and most of his patients. Healing sessions always occur at night. The healer takes out a communal cup, reciting orations and whistling to protect each person who drinks. He varies the amount of potion in accordance with his assessment of the patient's body weight or physical strength.

Throughout the session, each person receives counseling and is ritually exorcised by the healer. The healer moves around the circle with his schacapa rattle, blowing cigarette smoke over the body of sick persons and sometimes sucking dolorous areas to bring forth spines or thistles believed to have been magically introduced. At 2:00 or 3:00 a.m., after some four to five hours of strong drug intoxication, patients return to their homes unless they elect to spend the night in a nearby tambo (a wallless shelter).

## Research Methodology

Dobkin de Rios employed innovative fieldwork methods in Belen. After some months in the field, she took 100 micrograms of Sandoz LSD, and at a later date also took ayahuasca in ritual context. She believed the researcher must have some subjective experience in order to understand the nature of informants' reports. Her own ayahuasca experience provided an excellent entry into the world of informants' personal experience, since they were more apt to discuss their own visions with another person who had participated in their ritual. The potion she received contained B. caapi and B. rusbyana mixed together.

She also used the Thematic Apperception Test with slum residents, comparing results with over 200 card readings she had made using the naipes. When she returned to the United States and read Karsten's 1923 account of ayahuasca use among the Jivaro, located in the eastern Ecuadorian rain forest almost 400 air miles away, she found his description of Jivaro belief systems linked to the spirit of the ayahuasca plant presented in very much the same language as one healer, don Jose, had used in Iquitos -- a remarkable replication of data despite changes in time and space.
