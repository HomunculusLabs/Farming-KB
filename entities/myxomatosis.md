## Myxomatosis

## Description

Myxomatosis is a viral disease caused by the myxoma virus (*Leporipoxvirus*), which
primarily affects rabbits (genus *Oryctolagus*, particularly the European rabbit
*Oryctolagus cuniculus*). The disease causes severe swelling of the eyes, nose,
genitals, and other mucous membranes, leading to blindness, secondary infections,
and typically death in susceptible populations. It is transmitted by biting insects
such as mosquitoes and fleas.

In Australia, myxomatosis holds a pivotal place in the history of wildlife
management [[odonata-dragonflies-damselflies-ecology-and-biological-control]]. Introduced deliberately in 1950 as a biological
control agent to combat the devastating rabbit plague, it initially killed over
99% of infected rabbits and dramatically reduced the rabbit population from an
estimated 600 million to approximately 100 million within two years. This represented
one of the most dramatic examples of biological control in history.

Holmgren references myxomatosis in the context of sustainable harvesting, noting
that rabbit trappers were the primary control on rabbit populations in Australia
before biological control through myxomatosis in the 1950s, and that these trappers
practised a form of sustainable "farming" of wild rabbits.

## Classification

- **Category**: Viral Disease / Biological Control Agent
- **Pathogen**: Myxoma virus (genus *Leporipoxvirus*, family *Poxviridae*)
- **Primary Host**: European rabbit (*Oryctolagus cuniculus*)
- **Transmission**: Arthropod vectors (mosquitoes, fleas, biting flies)
- **Origin**: South America (native to cottontail rabbits)
- **Introduction to Australia**: 1950

## Key Facts

- The myxoma virus is native to the Americas, where it causes only mild disease
  in native cottontail rabbits (*Sylvilagus* species) but is lethal to European
  rabbits.
- Deliberately released in Australia in 1950 after years of research by CSIRO
  scientists, initially near the Murray River.
- Initial mortality rates exceeded 99%, reducing Australia"s rabbit population
  by an estimated 500 million within two years.
- Rabbit trappers were the main control on rabbits in Australia before
  myxomatosis. These trappers "farmed" rabbits by not setting traps on breeding
  mounds, practising a form of [[sustainable-wild-harvesting]].
- The success was short-lived as natural selection favoured both less virulent
  virus strains and genetically resistant rabbits, leading to co-evolutionary
  adaptation.
- By the 1970s, mortality rates had dropped to approximately 50%, with continued
  resistance development in rabbit populations.
- Myxomatosis was also introduced to France (1952, deliberately by a doctor
  aiming to control rabbits on his estate), the United Kingdom (1953, accidental),
  and other European countries with varying impacts.
- The case remains one of the most studied examples of host-pathogen co-evolution
  in ecology.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Biological Control Paradigm**: Myxomatosis represents the classic case study
  in biological control, providing lessons applicable to biological management
  of [[mushroom-pests-and-diseases]] in cultivation systems, including fungal pathogens.
- **Ecological Balance**: The co-evolutionary response (both virus attenuation and
  rabbit resistance) illustrates the futility of one-time interventions versus
  ongoing adaptive management — relevant to managing fungal diseases in crops.
- **Sustainable Harvesting**: The rabbit trappers' practice of avoiding breeding
  mounds represents a model of sustainable wild harvesting applicable to
  [[emcdda-wild-mushroom-foraging-misidentification-hazards]] and other fungal [[savory-holistic-resource-management-animal-impact]].
- **Vector-Borne Disease Management**: Understanding the mosquito and flea vectors
  of myxomatosis has parallels in managing insect vectors of fungal diseases
  in agricultural and horticultural systems.
- **Soil Impact**: The dramatic reduction in rabbit populations had significant
  effects on vegetation, soil compaction, and erosion across vast areas of
  Australia, indirectly affecting soil microbial and fungal communities.
- **[[integrated-pest-management]]**: The myxomatosis experience informed development
  of [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] (IPM) strategies that combine biological,
  cultural, and chemical controls — approaches applicable to managing
  competitor moulds and pathogens in [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]].
