# Hemerocallis fulva

**Hemerocallis fulva** (tawny daylily, orange daylily, ditch lily) is a clump-forming perennial herb in the family Asphodelaceae. It is native to East Asia and has become widely naturalized across [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]], it forms extensive roadside monocultures.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Hemerocallis fulva was described by Carl Linnaeus in Species Plantarum (1753). The genus Hemerocallis was traditionally placed in Liliaceae but has since been moved to Asphodelaceae based on molecular phylogenetics.

The triploid form H. fulva var. fulva (2n=33) is the most commonly encountered. It is sterile and reproduces exclusively by vegetative means, which accounts for its clonal spreading habit.

Plants grow 60–120 cm tall from a fibrous root system with tuberous-thickened roots. The arching, lanceolate leaves are 40–80 cm long and 1.5–3 cm wide, arranged in a basal fan.

Scapes bear 5–15 flowers in a terminal cyme. Each flower is 10–15 cm across with six reflexed tepals, a prominent throat, and a sweet fragrance.

The flowers are typically tawny-orange to copper-red with a paler throat. They open in the morning and wither by evening, with new flowers opening on successive days.

Several named forms exist in cultivation. The most common is the double-flowered 'Kwanso', which has ruffled, petaloid stamens creating a fuller appearance. 'Flore Pleno' is another sterile double form.

The species has contributed extensively to modern daylily breeding. Thousands of registered Hemerocallis cultivars trace some portion of their lineage to H. fulva, particularly for cold hardiness and vigor.

## Distribution and Habitat

H. fulva is native to China, Japan, and Korea, where it grows in forest margins, grassy slopes, and stream banks. It has been cultivated in East Asia for over 2,000 years as both an ornamental and a food crop.

The species was introduced to Europe by the 16th century and to North America by the 18th century. It has escaped cultivation throughout much of the eastern United States and southeastern Canada.

Naturalized populations are most abundant along roadsides, ditch banks, and abandoned homesteads. The sterile triploid form spreads aggressively through rhizomatous growth, forming dense stands.

In several U.S. states, H. fulva is listed as an invasive or noxious weed. It can outcompete native meadow and roadside vegetation, particularly in disturbed habitats.

The species tolerates a wide range of soil conditions from acidic to alkaline. It prefers moist, well-drained loams but survives in heavy clay and thin rocky soils.

Established clumps are remarkably drought-tolerant due to their tuberous root system. This adaptation allows H. fulva to persist in roadside ditches and railway embankments with minimal rainfall.

The species is largely free of serious [[biodiversity-fungal-genetic-diversity]].

Tissue [[cancer-medicinal-mushrooms-schizophyllan-spg-clinical-trials]] in humans are lacking and the therapeutic relevance remains uncertain.

The plant also contains small quantities of colchicine-related alkaloids. While concentrations are far below toxic thresholds, this chemistry connects H. fulva to the broader Liliales alkaloid profile.
