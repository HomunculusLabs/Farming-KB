## Overview

Cibotium chamissoi is a species of tree fern in the family Dicksoniaceae, endemic to the Hawaiian Islands. It is one of three Cibotium species native to Hawai'i and is distinguished by its large size and golden-brown trunk hairs.

The species is commonly known as hāpuʻu in Hawaiian, though this name is also applied to other native Cibotium species. It is a culturally significant plant in Hawaiian tradition, used for various practical and ceremonial purposes.

Mature plants produce an erect trunk 3–6 meters tall, crowned with a spreading rosette of large, tripinnate fronds. The trunk is thickly covered with golden, silky hairs that are among the most distinctive features of the species.

Cibotium chamissoi is found in wet and mesic forests on the islands of Kaua'i, O'ahu, Moloka'i, Lāna'i, and Maui. It is an important component of the native Hawaiian montane wet forest ecosystem.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Cibotium chamissoi was described by Georg Friedrich Kaulfuss in 1824 from specimens collected on O'ahu. The species epithet honors Ludolf Karl Adelbert von Chamisso, a German botanist and poet.

The genus Cibotium comprises approximately 12 species distributed across Hawai'i, Southeast Asia, and Central America. Hawaiian Cibotium species form a distinct clade within the genus based on molecular data.

The trunk is erect, 3–6 m tall and 15–25 cm in diameter. It is densely covered with golden-brown, silky hairs (trichomes) up to 3 cm long, giving it a distinctive fuzzy appearance.

Fronds are tripinnate, 2–4 m long, with a stout stipe densely covered with the same golden hairs. The rachis is dark brown and bears scattered hairs along its length.

Pinnae are 40–80 cm long, with pinnules that are deeply lobed. The lamina is bright green and soft in texture, in contrast to the leathery fronds of many other tree ferns.

Sori are marginal, borne on the lobes of the fertile pinnules. Each sorus is protected by a two-valved indusium, the outer valve formed from the modified leaf margin.

Spores are trilete, approximately 35–45 μm in diameter, with a smooth to slightly granulate perispore. Spore production peaks during the wetter winter months.

## Distribution and Habitat

Cibotium chamissoi is endemic to the Hawaiian Islands, occurring on Kaua'i, O'ahu, Moloka'i, Lāna'i, and Maui. It is absent from the island of Hawai'i, where Cibotium glaucum is the dominant tree fern.

The species inhabits wet and mesic forests at elevations of 300–1,800 meters. It is most abundant in montane wet forests receiving 2,500–5,000 mm of annual rainfall.

Preferred habitats include sheltered valleys, stream banks, and north-facing slopes with deep, organic soils. The species thrives in the cloud forest zone where persistent fog supplements rainfall.

On Maui, populations are concentrated along the windward slopes of Haleakalā. Kaua'i populations occur in the Alaka'i Wilderness and along the Nä Pali coast valleys.

The species co-occurs with Cibotium menziesii at mid-elevations, though C. chamissoi generally occupies wetter sites. The two species can be distinguished by trunk hair color and frond architecture.

Hybridization between Cibotium chamissoi and C. menziesii has been reported where their ranges overlap. Hybrid individuals show intermediate morphology but reduced fertility.

The species is notable for its slow growth rate, averaging 2–5 cm of trunk height per year. Large specimens may be 100–200 years old based on growth rate estimates.

## Ecology

Cibotium chamissoi is a keystone species in Hawaiian montane wet forests. Its trunk provides critical substrate for epiphytic plants including native ferns, mosses, and the endemic Hawaiian lobeliads.

The root crown collects rainwater and organic debris, creating a suspended garden that supports invertebrates and microorganisms. Native Hawaiian tree snails (Achatinellidae) are frequently found on Cibotium trunks.

Feral pigs represent a major threat to the species by uprooting young plants and disturbing the forest floor. Pig damage accelerates soil erosion and creates conditions favorable for invasive plant establishment.

Frond production is continuous but peaks during the wet season. Each frond persists for 18–24 months, and the crown typically holds 15–25 live fronds.

The species is moderately shade-tolerant as an adult but requires gap-phase light for spore germination and gametophyte development. Dense canopy cover limits recruitment.

## Cultivation

Cibotium chamissoi is cultivated as an ornamental in Hawaiian gardens and tropical botanic collections worldwide. It requires consistently moist, humus-rich soil and partial shade.

The species is sensitive to frost and requires frost-free conditions year-round. It is suitable for USDA zones 11–12, though some protected specimens survive in zone 10b.

Propagation from spores is possible but slow, requiring 8–18 months to produce transplantable seedlings. The golden trunk hairs make young plants attractive even before trunk development.

The species does not tolerate prolonged drought or strong, drying winds. Wind protection and regular irrigation are essential for healthy growth in cultivation.

Established plants benefit from a thick organic mulch layer around the base. Avoid disturbing the root crown, which is sensitive to physical damage.

Container cultivation is possible for young plants but becomes impractical as the trunk develops. Specimens in the ground generally outperform potted individuals.

In cultivation, the golden trunk hairs are a distinctive ornamental feature. These hairs should not be harvested from living plants, as they protect the growing point from desiccation.

The species has been successfully grown in tropical botanic gardens outside Hawai'i, including Singapore, Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden in Florida, and Kew Gardens in London.

Established plants can recover from defoliation caused by storms or brief cold events. Regrowth from the crown typically occurs within 4–8 weeks under favorable conditions.

## Conservation and Uses

Cibotium chamissoi is not currently listed on the IUCN Red List but is considered vulnerable due to habitat loss and degradation. Feral pig damage, invasive plants, and climate change threaten natural populations.

The species is protected within Hawai'i's natural area reserves and national parks. Fencing projects to exclude feral pigs have shown positive results for Cibotium regeneration.

Hawaiian cultural uses of hāpuʻu include harvesting the golden trunk hairs (pulu) for wound dressing, embalming, and stuffing pillows and mattresses. The starchy trunk pith was eaten as a famine food.

The trunk core was used to grow vegetatively propagated plants, particularly kalo (taro). This traditional practice demonstrates the ecological role of Cibotium as a nurse plant.

Modern conservation efforts include ex situ cultivation in botanic gardens and spore banking. The National Tropical Botanical Garden maintains living collections of all three Hawaiian Cibotium species.

The pulu trade of the 19th century harvested large quantities of trunk hairs from wild populations. Historical accounts describe extensive damage to native forests from commercial pulu collection.

Restoration planting using nursery-grown sporelings has shown promise in fenced exclosures. Survivorship exceeds 80% when seedlings are planted in protected, shaded microsites.
