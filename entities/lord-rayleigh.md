# Lord Rayleigh

## Overview

Lord Rayleigh was John William Strutt, 3rd Baron Rayleigh, a British physicist.
He lived from 12 November 1842 to 30 June 1919.
He is best known for rigorous work in sound, optics, gases, and fluid mechanics.
He received the 1904 Nobel Prize in Physics for investigations of gas densities.
That work led directly to the discovery of argon in the atmosphere.
In the wiki he is connected to [[jagadis-chandra-bose]] studied there.
Bose's Cambridge training placed him inside elite British experimental physics.
The concept page notes Rayleigh among Bose's teachers and later supporters.
Bose learned physics in an environment shaped by Rayleigh's standards.
Those standards valued careful instrumentation and quantitative response curves.
Bose later applied similar precision to plants, metals, and electromagnetic waves.
Rayleigh also consoled Bose during opposition to his plant-response work.
In that context Rayleigh appears as a senior scientist recognizing difficult novelty.
His presence helps connect Bose's plant physiology to mainstream physics.
The link is important because Bose's work crossed disciplinary boundaries.

## Positions and Institutions

- Cavendish Professor of Physics at the University of Cambridge from 1879.
- Professor of Natural Philosophy at the Royal Institution from 1887 to 1905.
- President of the Royal Society from 1905 to 1908.
- Chancellor of the University of Cambridge from 1908 until 1919.
- Worked extensively at Terling Place after leaving the Cavendish chair.

## Named Concepts

- Rayleigh scattering: elastic scattering by particles smaller than wavelength.
- Rayleigh waves: surface waves important in seismology and materials science.
- Rayleigh number: dimensionless number for buoyancy-driven convection.
- Rayleigh criterion: angular resolution criterion in optics.
- Rayleigh-Jeans law: classical radiation law with ultraviolet catastrophe limits.
- Rayleigh-Taylor instability: density-driven instability in fluids.

## Character and Method

Rayleigh was known for simplicity, precision, and theoretical restraint.
He preferred explanations that reduced complexity without erasing evidence.
He was attentive to measurement error but did not dismiss stubborn anomalies.
His career shows the power of combining mathematics with bench experiment.
He influenced fields that later became separate engineering disciplines.
His authority also mattered socially within British science.
For colonial scientists such as Bose, recognition from figures like Rayleigh carried weight.
Rayleigh's method is relevant to plant bioelectricity because it legitimized precise response measurement.
The same general posture appears in Bose's crescograph work.
Small signals, carefully amplified, could reveal hidden continuity in nature.
