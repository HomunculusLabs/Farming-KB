## Overview

**Alpinia purpurata**, commonly known as red ginger, ostrich plume, or pink cone ginger, is a striking tropical perennial in the family Zingiberaceae.

It is native to Melanesia and widely cultivated throughout the tropics as an ornamental plant. The species is prized for its large, vividly colored bract inflorescences that resemble ostrich plumes.

The showy red or pink structures are not petals but modified leaves (bracts) that enclose small, inconspicuous white flowers. These bract spikes can reach 15–30 cm in length and persist for weeks, making them popular in the cut-flower trade.

The plant can grow 2–4 meters tall in ideal conditions, forming dense clumps from rhizomatous rootstock.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Zingiberales
- **Family:** Zingiberaceae
- **Subfamily:** Alpinioideae
- **Tribe:** Alpinieae
- **Genus:** *Alpinia*
- **Species:** *A. purpurata* (K. Schum.) K. Schum.

The species was described by Karl Moritz Schumann in 1904. Several cultivar selections exist, including red-bracted and pink-bracted forms.

The most common cultivar is 'Jungle King' (red bracts), with 'Jungle Queen' (pink bracts) being another popular selection. A compact dwarf form is also grown commercially.

**Morphological features:**

The pseudostems arise from thick rhizomes and reach 2–4 m in height. Leaves are lanceolate, 30–60 cm long and 5–10 cm wide, with a prominent midrib and smooth margins.

The inflorescence is a terminal or subterminal cone-like spike composed of overlapping, tightly packed bracts. Bracts are bright red to deep pink in cultivated forms, becoming green toward the tip.

True flowers are small, white, and tubular, emerging individually from bract axils. Each flower lasts only a single day, but the bract display persists for 3–6 weeks.

Fruits are fleshy capsules containing numerous small seeds with an arillate coating.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Alpinia purpurata* is native to the Bismarck Archipelago, Solomon Islands, Vanuatu, and New Caledonia in the southwestern Pacific.

It has been widely introduced and naturalized across tropical and subtropical regions, including Hawaii, Fiji, Tahiti, Costa Rica, Puerto Rico, and parts of [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]].

Optimal growth occurs at temperatures of 20–30°C with annual rainfall exceeding 1500 mm. The plant is frost-sensitive and will not survive prolonged temperatures below 10°C.

## Cultivation

Propagation is primarily vegetative, by division of rhizome clumps. Each division should include 2–3 actively growing shoots and a healthy rhizome section.

Seeds are viable but rarely used in horticulture due to slow germination and high variability. Tissue [[query-how-do-cover-crops-suppress-weeds]].

The species is relatively pest-resistant but can be affected by ginger rust (*Puccinia psidii*) and mealybugs. Rhizome rot, caused by *Pythium* species, occurs in poorly drained soils.

Flowering occurs year-round in [[holzer-permaculture-landscape-design]].

Commercial cultivation for the cut-flower industry is significant in Hawaii, Costa Rica, and parts of Southeast Asia. The long vase life of the bract spikes (2–4 weeks) makes them economically valuable for export.

The global trade in red ginger cut flowers generates millions of stems annually. Hawaii alone produced an estimated 500,000 stems per year before industry consolidation in the 2010s.
