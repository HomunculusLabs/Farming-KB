## Overview

**Lagenaria siceraria**, commonly known as **bottle gourd**, **calabash**, or **opo squash**, is an annual vine in the family Cucurbitaceae. It is one of the oldest cultivated plants, with a domestication history spanning over 10,000 years.

The species is unique among cultivated cucurbits for its dual use as both a food crop and a source of durable containers. When harvested mature and dried, the hard-shelled gourds have been used as vessels, musical instruments, and floats across cultures worldwide.

The young, tender fruits are consumed as a vegetable in South Asia, [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]], and Africa. The mature dried gourds are used for storage vessels, water containers, pipe bowls, and resonators in stringed instruments.

*Lagenaria siceraria* is diploid with 2n = 26 chromosomes. It is the only widely cultivated species in the genus *Lagenaria*.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Cucurbitales
- **Family:** Cucurbitaceae
- **Genus:** *Lagenaria* Ser.
- **Species:** *L. siceraria* (Molina) Standl.

**Basionym:** *Cucurbita lagenaria* L., 1753.

**Synonyms:** *Cucurbita lagenaria* L.; *Lagenaria vulgaris* Ser.; *Lagenaria leucantha* Rusby.

The genus *Lagenaria* contains 6–7 species, all native to the [[datura-metel-old-world-ethnobotany]] tropics. *L. siceraria* is the only one with significant economic importance.

Molecular phylogenetics places *Lagenaria* in a basal position within the cucurbit phylogeny, distinct from the *Cucumis*, *Citrullus*, and *Cucurbita* lineages. It diverged from its closest relatives approximately 50 million years ago.

### Morphology

*Lagenaria siceraria* is a vigorous annual vine with climbing or trailing stems reaching 3–9 m in length. Tendrils are bifid and coil around supports for climbing.

Leaves are large, broadly ovate to cordate, 10–30 cm across, with 5–7 shallow lobes and a softly hairy surface. Leaf color is pale to medium green.

Flowers are large (4–8 cm diameter), white, and open at night (moth-pollinated). They are monoecious, with separate male and female flowers on the same plant. Male flowers are borne in long pedunculate clusters; female flowers are solitary.

The fruit is a pepo (berry) of enormously variable shape and size depending on cultivar. Shapes include bottle, club, dumbbell, round, crookneck, and serpentine forms. Mature fruits range from 10 cm to over 1 m in length.

Young fruits have soft, green skin and white, spongy flesh. At maturity (90–120 days), the skin hardens into a tough, woody shell and the flesh dries to a thin, fibrous lining. Seeds are numerous, flat, and white, approximately 1.5 × 0.8 cm.

## Distribution and History

*Lagenaria siceraria* is native to tropical Africa, with wild populations distributed from West Africa to East Africa and Madagascar. The wild progenitor is believed to be *L. siceraria* subsp. *asiatica* or a closely related African wild form.

Archaeological evidence from Thailand dates bottle gourd use to approximately 10,000 years ago, making it one of the earliest domesticated plants. The gourd reached the Americas by approximately 10,000 years ago, likely via ocean drift from Africa.

In Africa, it spread early as both a food and a container crop. Asian cultivation developed independently, with distinct fruit-shape varieties selected for different uses.

Today, bottle gourd is cultivated throughout the tropics and subtropics, with major production in India, China, Nigeria, and [[guzman-thailand-southeast-asia-psychoactive-fungi-distribution]]. It is also grown as an ornamental gourd in [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]].

## Nutritional Value

Young bottle gourd fruits are low in calories (14–20 kcal per 100 g fresh weight) and high in water content (92–96%). They are valued as a light, easily digestible vegetable.

The flesh contains modest amounts of vitamin C (10–15 mg/100 g), B vitamins (thiamine, riboflavin, niacin), and minerals (calcium, phosphorus, iron, potassium).

Medicinal use in Ayurvedic and traditional Chinese medicine includes treatment of digestive disorders, diabetes, and cardiovascular conditions. These traditional uses have partial support from modern pharmacological studies.

The seeds are edible and contain 30–40% protein and 35–45% oil, rich in linoleic acid. Seed extracts have shown anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective properties in animal studies.

## Cultivation

Bottle gourd requires warm temperatures (25–35°C) and a long [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] (120–180 days for mature gourds). It is intolerant of frost and grows poorly below 18°C.

The crop prefers fertile, well-drained soils with adequate moisture. It is often grown along fences, trellises, or on the ground as a sprawling vine in traditional systems.

Seeds are sown directly after the last frost, 2–3 cm deep, with spacing of 1–2 m between hills. Germination occurs within 5–10 days. Transplanting is possible but not common due to sensitive root systems.

Young fruits are harvested 60–80 days after sowing for vegetable use. Mature gourds for containers require 120–180 days and should be left on the vine until the stem dries and turns brown.

Yields of young fruits range from 15–30 tonnes/ha. Mature gourd yield is 5,000–15,000 fruits/ha depending on cultivar and management.
