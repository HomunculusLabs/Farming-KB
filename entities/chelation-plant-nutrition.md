## Chelation (Plant Nutrition)

## Description

Chelation is the biological process of neutralizing the positive charge on metal ions (cations) by binding them to organic molecules such as proteins, amino acids, or synthetic ligands. This neutralization allows minerals to pass through cell membranes in plant roots and leaf surfaces. In soil ecology, chelation is primarily performed by bacteria and fungi, making it a fundamentally biological process essential for plant mineral nutrition.

## Classification

- **Category**: Biochemical process / mineral transport mechanism
- **Biological agents**: Bacteria, fungi, plant [[root-exudates]]
- **Synthetic agents**: EDTA, DTPA, EDDHA, lignin-based chelators
- **Natural agents**: Amino acids, proteins, humic acids, fulvic acids
- **Target elements**: Iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), copper (Cu), manganese (Mn), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg)

## Key Facts

1. **Charge Neutralization**: All biological cells have a net negative charge. Positively charged cations (Fe²⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, etc.) cannot easily cross cell membranes. Chelation wraps the cation in an organic molecule, neutralizing the charge and allowing membrane transport.

2. **Microbial Chelation is Primary**: Bacteria and fungi perform most chelation in natural systems. They produce proteins and amino acids that bind mineral elements. These microbe-chelated minerals are the form plants evolved to uptake. Synthetic chelators like EDTA are not found in natural soil.

3. **Chelated Minerals in Practice**: Chelated mineral fertilizers are less likely to leach and less harmful to plants than non-chelated forms. However, Ingham recommends checking organic certification lists (OMRI, IFOAM) before using synthetic chelated products, as residues may remain in plant tissue.

4. **Uptake Sequence**: The mineral-chelate complex crosses the cell membrane intact. Once inside the cell, plant enzymes cleave the mineral from the chelating agent. The mineral goes to where it is needed in cellular machinery, while the chelating agent is either recycled or expelled.

5. **Lignin Handling**: When lignin is used as a chelating agent, the plant typically expels it after mineral extraction. Lignin is difficult to break down and plants lack efficient enzymatic pathways for its metabolism. Synthetic chelators are similarly expelled.

6. **Amino Acid Chelators**: When amino acids or proteins serve as chelating agents, the plant can use both the mineral and the organic components. After enzymatic cleavage, amino acids feed into cellular synthesis pathways.

7. **Foliar Application**: Chelation is particularly important for foliar nutrient applications. Chelated minerals pass more rapidly through stomata and leaf cell walls. Microbial activity on leaf surfaces also contributes to chelation of foliar-applied minerals.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Compost Tea Mineral Delivery
Compost tea naturally contains chelated minerals because the bacteria and fungi in the tea produce chelating proteins. This is one reason compost tea is more effective than raw mineral applications — the minerals are already in biologically available, chelated form.

### Mycorrhizal Mineral Transport
[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] are expert chelators. Their hyphae extend far beyond root zones, chelating iron, phosphorus, zinc, and other minerals from soil particles and transporting them back to the host plant. This is a primary mechanism by which mycorrhizae improve plant nutrition.

### Calcium Management
Adding calcium to soil without improving fungal and bacterial populations is largely wasted. The organisms needed to chelate calcium and make it plant-available must be present. Ingham notes that without adequate protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, and mycorrhizal fungi, calcium stays locked in fungal biomass rather than cycling to plants.

### Practical Implications
- **Test plant tissue** before applying chelated minerals to confirm deficiency
- **Prefer biological chelation** (compost tea, [[mycorrhizal-inoculation-guide]]) over synthetic chelators
- **Feed the biology** — molasses, humic acids, and [[fish-hydrolysate]] feed the organisms that perform chelation
- **Check organic approval** before using synthetic chelated products
- **Understand that microbes chelate BEFORE membrane transport**, not after
