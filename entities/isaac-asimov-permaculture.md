## Description

[[isaac-asimov]] (1920–1992) was an American biochemist and one of the most prolific science writers of the 20th century. Mollison quotes Asimov's 1970 restatement of the [[laws-of-thermodynamics]] in *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual*: "The total energy of the universe is constant and the total entropy is increasing."

Mollison uses this quote to frame the permaculture designer's fundamental challenge: given that energy is constantly degrading from useful to less useful forms (entropy is always increasing), how can we design systems that capture and store as much energy as possible before it dissipates? This thermodynamic framing is central to permaculture's design methodology.

- **Era**: Mid 20th century

## Key Facts

- Authored or edited more than 500 books across nearly every category of the Dewey Decimal System
- Best known for the *Foundation* series and *I, Robot* in science fiction
- His popular science writing made complex topics in physics, chemistry, biology, and mathematics accessible to general audiences
- Formulated the Three Laws of Robotics, which remain a touchstone in discussions of artificial intelligence ethics
- His restatement of thermodynamic laws in accessible language made these concepts available to non-specialist designers like Mollison
- Professor of biochemistry at Boston University

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Asimov's thermodynamic framework, as applied by Mollison, is directly relevant to cultivation:

1. **Energy capture and storage**: The second law of thermodynamics means that in any cultivation system, energy is constantly being lost as heat and waste. Effective design maximizes the capture and storage of useful energy at every stage.
2. **Entropy in biological systems**: Decomposition is an entropy-increasing process, but the decomposer organisms (primarily fungi and bacteria) capture some of that energy to build their own complex structures. This is the thermodynamic basis for composting and fungal cultivation.
3. **System efficiency**: Asimov's clear statement of energy conservation provides the theoretical basis for evaluating cultivation system efficiency — how much of the energy entering the system is captured as useful product versus lost as waste heat.
4. **Nutrient cycling**: In thermodynamic terms, nutrients are energy carriers. Each time nutrients cycle through a system, some energy is lost to entropy, which is why external energy inputs (sunlight, decomposition of organic matter) are needed to maintain the system.
