## Quercus agrifolia

## Overview

**Quercus agrifolia** Née, commonly known as coast live oak, is a large evergreen tree native to California and northern Baja California. It is one of the most iconic and ecologically important tree species of the California Floristic Province, dominating coastal woodlands, chaparral margins, and riparian corridors from Mendocino County to the San Pedro Mártir Mountains.

Coast live oak is the most widely distributed of California's live oaks and plays a foundational role in the state's Mediterranean-climate ecosystems. The species provides critical habitat, food, and structural complexity for hundreds of native species.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Order:** Fagales
- **Family:** Fagaceae
- **Genus:** Quercus, section Quercus (white oaks, subsection Protobalanus — intermediate between white and red oaks)
- **Species:** Q. agrifolia Née
- **Authority:** Luis Née, 1801

The lower surface is paler, dull green to yellowish-green, with prominent tufts of hairs in vein axils.

**Flowers** are monoecious. Male catkins appear in March–May, pendulous, 4–8 cm long. Female flowers are small, solitary or paired, in leaf axils.

**Acorns** are 1.5–3.5 cm long, ovoid to oblong, borne singly or in pairs on a short peduncle (2–10 mm). The cupule is shallow to moderate, enclosing roughly one-quarter to one-third of the nut, with thin, reddish-brown, pubescent scales. Acorns mature in one season (August–November of the same year).

## Distribution and Habitat

Quercus agrifolia is distributed along the Pacific coast of [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]] in the nutrient-poor soils characteristic of California's chaparral and woodland ecosystems.

The species plays an important role in **watershed protection** in the California Coast Ranges and foothills. Oak woodland soils have high infiltration rates and low erosion potential, contributing to groundwater recharge and streamflow regulation.

## Conservation Status

Quercus agrifolia is not listed as threatened or endangered at the state or federal level, but its habitat has been significantly reduced. Approximately 1–2 million hectares of coast live oak woodland remain in California, representing a substantial decline from pre-settlement extent due to agricultural conversion, urbanization, and oak woodland clearance.

The most significant current threat is **sudden oak death** (SOD), caused by the introduced pathogen *Phytophthora ramorum*. First detected in the mid-1990s in Marin County, SOD has killed millions of coast live oak and tanoak trees along the California coast. Infected trees develop bleeding cankers on the trunk and may die within 2–5 years of initial infection. The pathogen spreads primarily through rain splash and wind-driven moisture, with highest disease incidence in cool, moist coastal fog belt forests.

Management strategies for SOD include phosphonate injections (which boost tree defenses), removal of infected bay laurel trees (the primary sporulation host for *P. ramorum*), and public education to reduce pathogen spread through human activities such as trail use and vegetation transport.
