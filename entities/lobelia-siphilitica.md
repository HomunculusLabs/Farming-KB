# Lobelia siphilitica (Great Blue Lobelia)

## Overview

**Lobelia siphilitica** is a clump-forming perennial herb in the family Campanulaceae, native to eastern and central North America. It is commonly known as great blue lobelia or blue cardinal flower.

The species produces striking spikes of vibrant blue to violet flowers from July through October. Individual plants reach 60–120 cm in height and can produce dozens of flower spikes from a single root crown.

Great blue lobelia is among the most recognizable native wildflowers of [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America and is widely cultivated for its showy late-season blooms. The specific epithet *siphilitica* refers to its historical use by Native Americans in treating syphilis, a use that was adopted but ultimately disproven by European settlers.

Despite the discredited syphilis application, the species remains an important plant in [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] ethnobotanical history and continues to be valued in horticulture and ecological restoration.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Asterales
- **Family:** Campanulaceae
- **Genus:** Lobelia
- **Species:** L. siphilitica
- **Authority:** L. (1753)

The species was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The genus *Lobelia* contains approximately 415 species distributed worldwide, with the center of diversity in tropical Africa and the Americas.

Within North America, *Lobelia* includes approximately 30 native species. *L. siphilitica* is most closely related to *L. cardinalis* (cardinal flower) and *L. puberula* (downy lobelia), with which it occasionally hybridizes in areas of range overlap.

### Morphology

**Stems:** Erect, unbranched or sparingly branched, 60–120 cm tall, often glaucous and hairless or with sparse pubescence. The stem contains a milky latex sap characteristic of the family.

**Leaves:** Alternate, lanceolate to ovate, 5–15 cm long and 1.5–5 cm wide, with serrate margins. Leaves are sessile or short-petiolate, becoming progressively smaller toward the inflorescence.

**Inflorescence:** A dense, terminal raceme, 10–30 cm long, with flowers densely packed along the upper stem. The raceme elongates as lower flowers open and upper buds develop.

Each plant typically produces 1–3 primary racemes, with large, well-established individuals occasionally producing 5 or more from lateral branches. The inflorescence architecture creates a striking vertical accent in the landscape.

**Flowers:** Each flower is 2–3 cm long with a two-lipped corolla: the upper lip has two small lobes, and the lower lip has three larger, spreading lobes. Color ranges from intense blue-violet to pale blue, rarely white.

**Fruit and seed:** A many-seeded capsule, 8–12 mm long, dehiscing at the apex. Seeds are numerous, tiny (0.3–0.5 mm), oblong, and light brown.

[[cervantes-seed-production-storage]] per plant ranges from 5,000 to 50,000 seeds, with higher output from larger, multi-stemmed individuals. Seeds lack endosperm and germinate on the soil surface where light exposure triggers germination.

## Distribution and Habitat

Lobelia siphilitica ranges from southern Maine and Ontario south to Georgia and Texas, and west to Minnesota and North Dakota. It is broadly distributed across eastern North America with no significant range disjunctions.

The species is most commonly found in moist to wet habitats including stream banks, swamp margins, wet meadows, spring seeps, and roadside ditches. It tolerates full sun to partial shade.

Optimal habitat conditions include consistently moist, organically rich soils with pH 5.0–7.0. The species frequently colonizes disturbed wet sites and persists in degraded wetlands, making it useful for restoration plantings.

The species has a fibrous root system and produces short stolons, enabling gradual clonal expansion. Individual clumps can reach 60–90 cm in diameter after several growing seasons.

## Ecology

Great blue lobelia is a primary nectar source for ruby-throated hummingbirds during their late-summer and fall migration. The tubular floral morphology is specifically adapted for hummingbird pollination, though bumble bees also serve as effective pollinators.

The species provides one of the latest nectar sources available in eastern woodlands, blooming through September and occasionally into October. This late-season resource is critical for migrating hummingbirds building fat reserves before trans-Gulf migration.

Bumble bees (*Bombus* spp.) are the most common insect visitors, accessing nectar through the primary floral opening. Smaller bees occasionally rob nectar by chewing holes near the base of the corolla tube.

The milky latex sap contains piperidine alkaloids (primarily lobeline) that deter most mammalian herbivores. White-tailed deer typically avoid browsing the foliage, contributing to the species' persistence in heavily deer-populated landscapes.

The species is an occasional host for the lobelia stem borer (*Papaipema nebris*) and may be affected by fungal rust (*Puccinia lobeliae*) in wet seasons, though neither causes significant mortality.

## Cultivation

Lobelia siphilitica is readily available from native plant nurseries and is one of the most popular native perennials for moist garden sites. It is hardy in USDA zones 4–9 and thrives in partial shade with consistent moisture.

The species is classified as a facultative wetland plant (FACW) by the US Army Corps of Engineers. This wetland indicator status reflects its [[brassinosteroid-natural-occurrence-plant-kingdoms]] in both wetland and upland habitats, making it versatile for rain gardens, bioswales, and naturalized water features.

Propagation from seed is straightforward — seeds germinate without stratification when surface-sown and kept consistently moist. Division of mature clumps in early spring is the preferred method for maintaining cultivar selections.

In garden settings, plants perform best with 2.5–5 cm of water per week during the growing season. They are relatively short-lived (3–5 years) but readily self-seed in favorable conditions. A white-flowered form ('Alba') is commercially available.

The species combines effectively with *Lobelia cardinalis* in wetland gardens, with the red cardinal flowers and blue great lobelia creating a complementary color display. Both species attract hummingbirds and share similar cultural requirements.

## Medicinal Uses and Phytochemistry

The specific epithet *siphilitica* reflects the species' most famous historical use: treatment of syphilis by Native American tribes, particularly the Cherokee and Iroquois. This use was adopted by European colonists and disseminated in 18th-century pharmacopeias. However, clinical efficacy for syphilis treatment was never demonstrated, and the practice was abandoned by the mid-19th century.

Early American physician Benjamin Smith Barton documented the Cherokee use of L. siphilitica in his 1798 "Collections for an Essay Towards a Materia Medica of the United States," lending the practice additional credibility that persisted for decades.

The Cherokee also used the plant as a cough remedy, anti-rheumatic, and treatment for respiratory infections. The Iroquois employed a root poultice for swelling and an infusion for "love medicine" ceremonies.

The Meskwaki used an infusion of the plant to treat colds and croup. Across tribal traditions, the root was most commonly used for internal medicine, while leaf preparations were applied externally as poultices and washes.

The principal bioactive compound is lobeline, a piperidine alkaloid found in all plant parts but concentrated in the leaves and seeds. Lobeline acts as a nicotinic receptor partial agonist and was historically investigated as a smoking cessation aid.

Additional alkaloids present in lesser concentrations include lobelanine, lobelanidine, and isolobelanine. Total alkaloid content in dried leaf material typically ranges from 0.2–0.5% by dry weight.

⚠️ Lobeline is toxic at therapeutic doses. Symptoms of lobeline poisoning include nausea, vomiting, dizziness, tremors, and potentially fatal cardiac arrhythmias. The plant should not be ingested without professional supervision.
