## Description

Diazinon (O,O-diethyl O-[4-methyl-6-(propan-2-yl)pyrimidin-2-yl]
phosphorothioate) is an organophosphate insecticide that was widely used in
agriculture and residential pest control. In [[elaine-ingham]]'s [[compost]] tea field
guide, diazinon is mentioned alongside [[dieldrin]], furans, and DDT as one of
the problematic chemical residues that can be present in compost starting
materials, particularly grass clippings, and that must be decomposed by
beneficial organisms during the [[composting]] process.

## Classification

- **Chemical class**: Organophosphate insecticide
- **CAS Number**: 333-41-5
- **Molecular formula**: C12H21N2O3PS
- **Mode of action**: Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitor
- **EPA classification**: Previously widely registered; residential uses
  cancelled in 2004, agricultural uses still permitted on certain crops

## Key Facts

- Diazinon works by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, causing toxic
  accumulation of acetylcholine at nerve synapses in insects and other animals
- Was one of the most widely used organophosphate insecticides in the United
  States before EPA restrictions took effect
- EPA cancelled all residential indoor and outdoor uses in December 2004 due
  to unacceptable health risks, particularly to children
- Agricultural uses remain limited but still permitted on certain crops under
  specific application restrictions
- Highly toxic to birds, fish, amphibians, and beneficial insects including
  pollinators and natural enemies of pests
- Relatively short half-life in soil (2-4 weeks) compared to persistent
  organochlorines like DDT, meaning it breaks down more rapidly
- Moderately soluble in water and can contaminate surface water and
  groundwater through runoff and leaching
- Common contaminant in urban grass clippings collected for composting

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Ingham includes diazinon among the pesticide residues that composters must be
aware of when sourcing materials. Grass clippings from lawns treated with
diazinon or other lawn chemicals may carry residues that inhibit microbial
activity in compost piles.

The primary indicator of such chemical contamination is when compost pile
temperatures stay low too long after starting the pile. The chemical residues
suppress the thermophilic organisms that normally drive composting temperatures
upward through their metabolic activity. Ingham lists the problem hierarchy as:
strongylid de-wormers being the worst, then herbicide residues, insecticides
including diazinon, fungicides, then copper and sulfur compounds.

The recommended remediation approach involves adding extra energy sources for
the decomposer organisms. Molasses is suggested as an additional food resource
to give organisms the energy needed to break down diazinon and similar
contaminants. If temperatures remain suppressed, commercial inoculants
containing bacteria specialized in decomposing recalcitrant chemicals may be
needed. Ingham specifically mentions EM inoculum, Biostart, Organica, and
Agri-Energy compost starters as products containing the bacteria that can
decompose these harmful materials.

For mushroom growers who produce their own compost from yard waste or
agricultural residues, monitoring initial pile temperatures is essential.
Delayed or reduced heating suggests chemical contamination should be
investigated. The biological remediation approach aligns with the broader soil
food web philosophy of using biology rather than chemistry to solve problems.

The ability of soil organisms to decompose diazinon and other pesticides
demonstrates the remarkable metabolic diversity of the soil microbiome.
However, this capacity has limits. High concentrations or repeated applications
of diazinon can overwhelm the decomposer community, leading to accumulation of
toxic residues in finished compost. This is why Ingham emphasizes monitoring
compost pile temperatures as an early warning system for chemical contamination
and recommends building compost from diverse, clean feedstocks whenever possible.
