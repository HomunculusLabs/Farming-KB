# Mitragynine
Mitragynine is the primary indole alkaloid found in [[mitragyna-speciosa]]
(kratom), a tropical tree native to Southeast Asia. First isolated in
1921 by E. J. Field, mitragynine constitutes approximately 66% of the
total alkaloid content in kratom leaf. It acts as a partial agonist at
mu-opioid receptors and an agonist at delta- and kappa-opioid receptors,
producing analgesic, stimulant, and anxiolytic effects that are
dose-dependent. Unlike classical opioids, mitragynine does not cause
fatal respiratory depression at therapeutic doses, making it a compound
of significant interest for pain management and opioid replacement
therapy. Its complex pharmacology continues to be the subject of active
research and regulatory debate.

## Pharmacology
### Receptor Binding Profile
| Receptor | Action | Ki (approximate) | Functional Effect |
|----------|--------|-----------------|-------------------|
| μ-opioid (MOR) | Partial agonist | ~150–800 nM | Analgesia, euphoria |
| δ-opioid (DOR) | Agonist | ~100–300 nM | Antidepressant-like |
| κ-opioid (KOR) | Weak agonist/antagonist | >1 µM | Dysphoria at high dose |
| α₂-adrenergic | Agonist | ~1–5 µM | Sedation, antinociception |
| [[serotonin]] 5-HT₂A | Antagonist | ~1 µM | Modulates mood |
| Dopamine D₂ | Weak antagonist | >10 µM | Limited dopaminergic |
| Adenosine A₂A | Unknown | — | May contribute to stimulation |
### Key Pharmacological Features
- **Partial Mu Agonism:** Mitragynine's partial agonism at MOR
  produces analgesia without the full efficacy of [[morphine]] or
  fentanyl, which correlates with its lower abuse potential and
  absence of fatal respiratory depression
- **Biased Signaling:** Preferentially activates G-protein over
  β-arrestin pathways at MOR — this "biased agonism" is associated
  with analgesia without respiratory suppression (similar to
  oliceridine/TRV130)
- **Metabolite 7-Hydroxymitragynine:** A minor metabolite that is
  30–46 times more potent at MOR than mitragynine itself; may
  contribute significantly to kratom's overall effects; its role
  is debated — some studies suggest it is present in very low
  concentrations in vivo
- **Dose-Dependent Biphasic Effects:** Low doses (1–5 g leaf)
  produce stimulant effects (dopaminergic/adrenergic); higher
  doses (5–15 g leaf) produce opioid-like sedation and analgesia
## Pharmacokinetics
- **Absorption:** Oral bioavailability ~3–21% (low due to first-pass
  metabolism); onset 5–15 minutes; peak plasma 60–90 minutes
- **Distribution:** Volume of distribution ~2.6 L/kg; crosses the
  blood-brain barrier; moderately protein-bound (~70%)
- **Metabolism:** Primarily hepatic via CYP3A4 and CYP2D6; major
  pathway: O-demethylation to 7-hydroxymitragynine; also:
  9-O-demethylation, 16-carboxylation, [[glucuronidation]]
- **Half-Life:** Terminal half-life ~3.5–9 hours (mitragynine);
  7-hydroxymitragynine ~2.5 hours
- **Excretion:** Primarily renal (urine); minor fecal excretion
- **Drug Interactions:** CYP3A4 inhibitors (ketoconazole,
  grapefruit) increase plasma levels; CYP2D6 inhibitors may
  reduce conversion to active metabolites
## Natural Occurrence
- **Primary Source:** Mitragyna speciosa (kratom) — leaf
- **Concentration in Kratom Leaf:** 0.5–1.5% dry weight (varies
  significantly by geographic origin, harvesting time, and leaf
  maturity); Thai and Malaysian varieties tend toward higher
  concentrations
- **Secondary Source:** Mitragyna hirsuta, Mitragyna javanica
  (related species, lower concentrations)
- **Other Alkaloids in Kratom Leaf:** Speciogynine (6–8%),
  paynantheine (9%), speciociliatine (1%), 7-hydroxymitragynine
  (0.01–0.02%), mitraphylline, rhynchophylline
## Therapeutic Potential and Research
### Pain Management
- Preclinical studies demonstrate analgesic efficacy comparable to
  morphine in rodent models at appropriate doses
- No significant respiratory depression observed even at doses 10×
  the analgesic ED₅₀ in animal studies
- Anecdotal reports from kratom users consistently cite effectiveness
  for chronic pain, fibromyalgia, and neuropathic pain
### Opioid Withdrawal and Replacement
- Widely self-reported as effective for managing opioid withdrawal
  symptoms and reducing opioid dependence
- Survey data (2021, ~2,700 respondents): 90% reported kratom
  effective for opioid withdrawal; 87% reported reduced opioid use
- FDA has not approved mitragynine or kratom for any medical
  indication; concerns about abuse potential and safety

## Toxicity and Safety Concerns
- **Acute Toxicity:** High doses (>15 g leaf equivalent) can cause
  nausea, vomiting, agitation, tachycardia, and seizures in rare cases
- **Hepatotoxicity:** Case reports of liver injury (primarily
  cholestatic pattern); typically resolves with discontinuation;
  product contamination may be a confounding factor
- **Dependence:** Regular use produces physical dependence with
  withdrawal symptoms (anxiety, irritability, insomnia, muscle aches,
  diarrhea) similar to mild opioid withdrawal
- **Fatalities:** Kratom-involved deaths are overwhelmingly associated
  with polydrug use (especially fentanyl, benzodiazepines); pure
  kratom deaths are extremely rare and disputed
- **Contamination:** Commercial kratom products have been found
  contaminated with salmonella, heavy metals, and synthetic opioids
  (notably the "Krypton" product adulterated with O-desmethyltramadol)
## Legal Status
- **United States:** Not federally scheduled (as of 2026); DEA
  proposed scheduling in 2016 but withdrew after public comment;
  FDA classifies as unapproved drug; kratom is banned in 6 states
  (Alabama, Arkansas, Indiana, Rhode Island, Vermont, Wisconsin)
  and several municipalities
- **Thailand:** Decriminalized in 2021; legal for medical use under
  regulation; previously banned since 1943 (Kratom Act)
- **Malaysia:** Classified as a dangerous drug under the Poisons Act;
  illegal to possess, sell, or use
- **EU/UK:** Generally legal but under increasing scrutiny; banned
  in some EU member states (Denmark, Finland, Latvia, Lithuania,
  Romania, Sweden)
- **Australia:** Schedule 9 (prohibited substance) since 2003
- **International:** Not scheduled under UN drug conventions
