# Maireana (Bluebush)

## Description

*Maireana* is a genus of salt-tolerant shrubs and subshrubs in the family Chenopodiaceae (now often placed in Amaranthaceae), commonly known as bluebush. Mollison lists bluebush (*Maireana* spp.) among salt-tolerant plants capable of thriving in soils with more than 0.4% sodium chloride, useful for supplanting salt-affected crops in dryland regions undergoing salinization.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Family:** Chenopodiaceae / Amaranthaceae
- **Genus:** *Maireana*
- **Common name:** Bluebush
- **Growth habit:** Low shrubs and subshrubs, typically 0.2–1.5 m tall
- **Species diversity:** Approximately 60–80 species, predominantly Australian

## Salt Tolerance

*Maireana* species are among the characteristic salt-tolerant plants of Australian arid and semi-arid zones:

- Thrive in soils exceeding **0.4% sodium chloride**
- Many species tolerate highly saline soils that support few other plants
- Adapted to the cyclic drought and salinity stress of Australian drylands
- Form dominant vegetation communities on saline flats and lake margins

## Role in Dryland Salinity Management

Mollison includes bluebush (*Maireana* spp.) alongside other salt-tolerant genera:

- **Saltbushes (*Atriplex*)**
- **Acacias**
- **Prosopis**
- **Puccinellia grass**
- **Samphire (*Crithmum*, *Salicornia*)**
- **Tall wheat grass (*Agropyron*)**
- **Wimmera rye grass (*[[interceptor-banks]] are remediating upslope causes
- **Wildlife habitat:** Provides cover and food for native fauna in restoration plantings
- **Wind erosion control:** Low, dense growth reduces wind speed at ground level
- **Soil building:** Accumulates organic matter that eventually supports less salt-tolerant species

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

*Maireana* provides permaculture designers with an Australian-native alternative to *Atriplex* (saltbush) for salt-tolerant shrub layers on degraded dryland sites. Its local adaptation to Australian conditions and co-evolution with native fauna make it particularly valuable for ecological restoration designs. As with all salt-tolerant species, Mollison cautions that bluebush should complement rather than replace structural solutions to salinity — interceptor banks and reforestation must address the underlying causes.
