## Overview

**Hordeum jubatum** is a perennial bunchgrass in the family Poaceae, commonly known as foxtail barley.

The species is native to much [[berry-growing]] and has become naturalized in parts of [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], Europe, Asia, and Australia.

It is readily recognized by its dense, silky-appearing inflorescences that resemble fox tails, which range from greenish to purplish-pink and fade to pale tan at maturity.

Foxtail barley occupies a dual role as an ornamental landscape grass and a troublesome agricultural weed, particularly in saline or disturbed soils.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Hordeum jubatum belongs to the tribe Triticeae within the subfamily Pooideae.

The species was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 in *Species Plantarum*.

No significant taxonomic synonyms are in current use, though some older texts reference the basionym *Hordeum jubatum* L. without variation.

It is diploid with a chromosome number of 2n = 28.

The plant forms dense tufts of narrow, flat leaf blades 15 to 60 centimeters long and 2 to 5 millimeters wide.

Leaf sheaths are glabrous to slightly scabrous, and the ligule is a short membranous collar 1 to 2 millimeters long.

The inflorescence is a dense, nodding spike 5 to 20 centimeters long, composed of triplets of spikelets at each node.

Each triplet consists of one sessile fertile spikelet and two pedicellate sterile spikelets that are reduced to long, awn-like bristles 3 to 10 centimeters in length.

These conspicuous bristles give the inflorescence its characteristic foxtail appearance.

Glumes and lemmas bear long awns that contribute to the silky texture of the flower head.

Anthesis occurs from June through August, depending on latitude and elevation.

## Distribution and Habitat

Foxtail barley is native to most of North America, ranging from Alaska and the Yukon across Canada to Newfoundland, and south through the United States into northern Mexico.

It has been widely introduced beyond its native range and is now naturalized in South America, Europe, East Asia, Australia, and New Zealand.

The species is highly adaptable and occupies a broad ecological amplitude, from sea level to over 3,000 meters in elevation.

It is particularly common in saline and alkaline environments including salt flats, alkali meadows, and edges of saline ponds.

Disturbed habitats such as roadsides, railroad embankments, abandoned fields, and drainage ditches are frequently colonized.

Foxtail barley also occurs in less saline sites including prairies, open woodlands, and riparian zones.

It tolerates a wide range of soil textures but shows a strong preference for moist, fine-textured soils with high salt content.

The species is moderately drought-tolerant once established, though it prefers sites with reliable moisture.

## Cultivation and Uses

Foxtail barley is occasionally cultivated as an ornamental grass for its showy, arching inflorescences.

It is valued in naturalistic plantings, prairie restorations, and low-maintenance landscapes where its graceful seed heads provide visual interest through summer and fall.

The species performs best in full sun and tolerates poor, saline soils that challenge many other [[ornamental-grasses-guide]].

In cultivation, plants are typically propagated by seed sown in fall or [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]], with germination occurring readily under moist conditions.

Division of established clumps is also possible but less commonly practiced due to the plant's relatively short lifespan of three to five years.

As a forage species, foxtail barley is of limited value.

Young plants are grazed by livestock but palatability declines rapidly as the inflorescences mature and the awns become stiff and irritating.

The barbed awns can cause injury to the mouths and digestive tracts of grazing animals, particularly when mature forage is consumed in quantity.

Indigenous peoples of the Great Plains reportedly harvested the seeds of foxtail barley as a minor food source, grinding them into flour.
