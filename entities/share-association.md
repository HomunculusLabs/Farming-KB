## S.H.A.R.E. (Self Help Association for a Regional Economy)

## Description

S.H.A.R.E. (Self Help Association for a Regional Economy) is a local nonprofit corporation
designed to encourage and finance small businesses producing necessary goods and services
for their community. Documented by [[bill-mollison]] in *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual*,
it operates as a revolving loan fund that channels community savings into local enterprise
creation. Originally established in the Berkshire area of Massachusetts, USA, S.H.A.R.E.
exemplifies the [[permaculture-principle-apply-self-regulation]] of keeping wealth circulating within a bioregion.

## Classification

- **Category:** Community finance organization / Revolving loan fund
- **Domain:** Permaculture -- Community economics, bioregional finance
- **Location:** Berkshire area, Massachusetts, USA
- **Legal Structure:** Local nonprofit corporation
- **Function:** Small business financing for essential community goods and services

## How It Works

1. **Membership:** Community members become S.H.A.R.E. members by opening a joint account
   with a participating local bank
2. **Interest Rate:** Members receive a modest 6% interest on their deposits
3. **Lending Rate:** The fund lends to new small businesses at 10% interest
4. **Spread:** The 4% spread between deposit and lending rates covers administrative costs
5. **Borrower Qualification:** Borrowers must collect references from community members who
   know them as responsible and conscientious
6. **Business Plan:** Borrowers must demonstrate that their proposed business will attract
   customers from within or outside the community
7. **Community Vetting:** The preliminary reference-gathering process ensures the community
   knows the borrower and has a stake in their success

## Key Principles

- **Self-Help:** The community funds its own development rather than relying on external capital
- **Regional Economy:** Keeps money circulating locally rather than leaking to distant institutions
- **Essential Services:** Prioritizes businesses producing necessary goods (food, shelter, tools)
  over luxury or speculative ventures
- **Community Accountability:** Borrowers are known to the community and accountable to their
  neighbors, not anonymous bankers
- **Modest Returns:** Lower interest rates on both deposits and loans keep the system accessible

## Key Facts

- Operates in conjunction with a local bank in the community
- The 6% deposit / 10% lending rate structure creates sustainable spread
- Borrowers must demonstrate market demand before receiving loans
- Reference collection builds community networks and trust alongside financing
- The community has a direct interest in seeing funded businesses succeed
- Functions as a revolving fund -- repaid loans are re-lent to new businesses

## Success Factors

- Community members must be willing to accept lower returns (6%) for social benefit
- Local bank partnership provides institutional credibility and banking infrastructure
- Rigorous but accessible borrower vetting prevents defaults while remaining inclusive
- Focus on essential services ensures genuine community demand for funded businesses
- The revolving structure means capital is continuously recycled within the region

## Comparison with Conventional Financing

| Feature | S.H.A.R.E. | Conventional Bank Loan |
|---------|-----------|----------------------|
| Interest to lender | 6% | 0-2% (savings) |
| Interest from borrower | 10% | 8-25% |
| Vetting | Community references | Credit scores |
| Default risk | Low (social pressure) | Variable |
| Capital retention | Local | Extracted |
| Focus | Essential services | Profit maximization |

## Broader Significance

Mollison presents S.H.A.R.E. alongside other revolving loan funds (C.E.L.T. in New Zealand,
Southern Cross Capital Exchange in Australia) as part of a global pattern of communities
taking financial self-determination into their own hands. These systems share common features:
local capital, community accountability, modest returns, and focus on essential production.

## Implementation Notes

- Start with a small group of committed depositors ($500-5,000 each)
- Partner with an existing local bank for transaction infrastructure
- Define clear criteria for what constitutes "essential" community goods and services
- Establish transparent accounting and regular reporting to all members
- Begin with low-risk loans (proven business models, experienced borrowers)
- Gradually expand as confidence and capital base grow
