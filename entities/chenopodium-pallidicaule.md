## Overview

**Chenopodium pallidicaule**, known as **cañihua** or **kañiwa**, is an annual seed crop in the family Amaranthaceae. It is closely related to quinoa (*[[chenopodium-quinoa]]*) and is native to the high Andes of Peru and Bolivia.

Cañihua is cultivated primarily for its small, nutrient-dense seeds, which are used as a grain similar to quinoa. It grows at higher altitudes (3,800–4,200 m) than quinoa and tolerates greater frost exposure, making it a critical food security crop for highland communities.

The seeds are approximately 1 mm in diameter, smaller than quinoa, and do not contain the bitter saponin coating that requires washing. This eliminates the processing step needed for quinoa preparation.

Cañihua has received increasing scientific attention as a potential superfood and climate-resilient crop for high-altitude agriculture worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Caryophyllales
- **Family:** Amaranthaceae
- **Genus:** *Chenopodium* L.
- **Species:** *C. pallidicaule* Aellen

The species was first formally described by the Swiss botanist Albert Thellung (published posthumously by Hermann Aellen in 1929). It is placed in *Chenopodium* subgenus *Chenopodium* section *Chenopodium*.

Molecular phylogenetics places *C. pallidicaule* as a close relative of *C. quinoa* within the American diploid lineage of *Chenopodium*. It is a diploid with 2n = 18 chromosomes.

### Morphology

Cañihua is an erect annual herb reaching 20–60 cm in height. The stem is branched, angular, and often reddish-pigmented at maturity.

Leaves are alternate, broadly triangular-ovate, 1–3 cm long, with irregularly dentate margins. Leaf surfaces are covered with mealy farinose (powdery) coating, characteristic of the genus.

The inflorescence is a dense, glomerulate panicle borne in leaf axils and at stem apices. Flowers are small, greenish, and perfect (bisexual), with 5 perianth segments.

Seeds are lenticular (lens-shaped), 0.8–1.2 mm in diameter, with a glossy black or dark brown seed coat. Thousand-seed weight averages 0.4–0.6 g, among the smallest of any cultivated grain.

Unlike quinoa, cañihua seeds lack significant saponin content in the pericarp, making them palatable without the extensive washing required for quinoa.

## Distribution and Origin

*C. pallidicaule* originated in the Andean altiplano, the high plateau stretching across southern Peru and western Bolivia.

Archaeological evidence suggests domestication occurred approximately 5,000 years ago, contemporaneous with quinoa. Both crops were staples of pre-Columbian Andean civilizations including the Tiwanaku and Inca cultures.

Today, cañihua cultivation is concentrated in the departments of Puno and Cusco in Peru, and the departments of La Paz and Oruro in Bolivia. Total cultivated area is estimated at 5,000–8,000 hectares, far less than quinoa.

The crop remains largely confined to smallholder farming systems in its native range. Outside the Andes, experimental cultivation has been trialed in the Himalayas, the Ethiopian highlands, and northern Europe.

## Nutritional Value

Cañihua seeds have an exceptional nutritional profile comparable to or exceeding that of quinoa and most conventional cereals.

Protein content ranges from 14–19% dry weight, higher than wheat, rice, or maize. The protein is well-balanced, with a favorable [[cho-fish-amino-acid-preparation]] profile rich in lysine (5.5–6.5% of protein), the limiting [[fish-amino-acid]] in most cereals.

The seeds contain 6–9% fat, predominantly unsaturated [[stable-carbon-isotope-signature-fatty-acids-mycorrhizal-carbon-tracking]] including linoleic acid (omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3). The omega-6:omega-3 ratio is favorable for human nutrition.

Mineral content is notably high, particularly iron (50–80 mg/kg), calcium (800–1200 mg/kg), and zinc (40–60 mg/kg). These concentrations are 2–4 times higher than in wheat or rice.

Dietary fiber content ranges from 8–14%, with both soluble and insoluble fractions. The seeds are gluten-free, making them suitable for celiac consumers.

Total phenolic content and antioxidant capacity exceed those of quinoa and most common cereals. The dark seed coat contributes significantly to this antioxidant activity.

## Cultivation

Cañihua is adapted to the extreme conditions of the high Andean altiplano: short [[jeavons-climate-adaptation-growing-seasons]] (120–150 days), intense UV radiation, large diurnal temperature fluctuations (-5 to 20°C), and poor, sandy soils.

The crop requires minimal inputs and is typically grown without irrigation or fertilization in traditional systems. It performs best on well-drained sandy-loam soils at pH 5.5–7.5.

Seeding rate is 8–12 kg/ha, broadcast or row-sown. Traditional intercropping with potatoes or other tubers is common. Plant density reaches 200–400 plants per square meter.

Frost tolerance is the key advantage over quinoa. Cañihua can survive repeated light frosts (-3 to -5°C) during [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]], and even moderate frost during grain filling. This allows cultivation at altitudes where few other grains survive.

Yields average 600–1,200 kg/ha under traditional management, reaching 1,500–2,500 kg/ha with improved varieties and agronomic practices. Yield is constrained by the small seed size and tendency toward seed shattering.
