## Overview

**Phaseolus lunatus** L., commonly known as the lima bean or butter bean, is a herbaceous legume in the family Fabaceae. It is one of the five domesticated Phaseolus species and is cultivated globally for its large, flat, edible seeds.

The species has two distinct centers of domestication: the Andes, which produced large-seeded varieties, and Mesoamerica, which produced smaller-seeded types. Both gene pools contribute to the diversity of modern cultivars.

Lima beans are a significant source of plant protein, dietary fiber, and minerals in tropical and subtropical diets. Like other legumes, they fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiosis with rhizobia, improving soil fertility.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Magnoliophyta → Magnoliopsida → Fabales → Fabaceae → Faboideae → Phaseolus → P. lunatus.

**Authority:** Carl Linnaeus, described in Species Plantarum (1753). The specific epithet *lunatus* refers to the crescent or half-moon shape of the seed.

The species is a climbing or bushy annual vine, 0.5–4 meters tall depending on variety. Bush types (determinate) reach 0.3–0.6 m; pole types (indeterminate) climb 2–4 m on supports.

Leaves are trifoliate with ovate to rhombic leaflets, 5–15 cm long. Stems are green or purple-tinged, angular in cross-section, and moderately pubescent.

Flowers are borne in axillary racemes, with white, pale green, or pale violet petals. The standard petal is approximately 1 cm long. Flowers are self-pollinating but can cross-pollinate by insects.

Pods are oblong, curved, 5–15 cm long and 1.5–3 cm wide, containing 2–6 large, flat, kidney-shaped seeds. Seed color ranges from white and cream to green, red, purple, and speckled patterns. Mature seeds measure 1–2 cm in length.

## History and Domestication

Archaeological evidence indicates lima bean cultivation began approximately 8,000–10,000 years ago in the Andes of Peru. The earliest known lima bean specimens come from the Guitarrero Cave site in Peru, dated to approximately 6,800 BCE.

A second, independent domestication occurred in Mesoamerica around 2,000–3,000 years ago. The Mesoamerican gene pool is characterized by smaller seeds and greater heat tolerance compared to Andean types.

The species was spread to the Caribbean and coastal South America by pre-Columbian trade networks. European colonists subsequently introduced lima beans to Africa and Asia, where they became important [[grain-legumes-and-pulse-crops]].

The common name "lima bean" derives from Lima, Peru, where the large-seeded Andean varieties were first encountered by European naturalists. The alternative name "butter bean" refers to the creamy texture of cooked seeds.

## Traditional and Culinary Use

In Latin America, lima beans are used in soups, stews, and side dishes. In Brazil, *feijão-de-lima* is a traditional ingredient in northeastern cuisine, often cooked with cassava and salted meat.
