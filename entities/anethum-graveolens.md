## Overview

**Anethum graveolens** L., commonly known as **dill**, is an annual herb in the family Apiaceae. Native to the Mediterranean region and southern Russia, it is cultivated worldwide as a culinary herb and spice.

Both the feathery leaves (dill weed) and the dried seeds are used extensively in European, Scandinavian, Middle Eastern, and Central Asian cuisines. The plant is also valued in traditional medicine as a carminative, digestive aid, and mild sedative.

Dill is an efficient companion plant in vegetable gardens, attracting beneficial insects including pollinators and predatory wasps that control aphid populations.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Classified within Apiaceae (Umbelliferae) → **Apioideae → *Anethum → *A. graveolens***.

A. graveolens is the sole accepted species in the genus *Anethum*. Two subspecies are recognized: subsp. *graveolens* (cultivated dill) and subsp. *anethum* (wild dill), the latter found in the Mediterranean and Central Asia.

The plant grows 40–120 cm tall, with a single hollow, branching stem. Leaves are finely divided, feathery, and thread-like — 2–3 times pinnately compound, resembling fennel foliage but more delicate.

Flowers are borne in compound umbels 5–15 cm across, with yellow petals. Each umbel contains 15–25 rays. Flowering occurs 8–12 weeks after sowing.

Fruits (commonly called "seeds") are oval, flattened, 3–5 mm long, with two mericarps. They are brown when mature, with three longitudinal ridges and two winged lateral ridges.

## Distribution and Cultivation

Originally native to the Mediterranean basin, the Caucasus, and Central Asia, dill has been naturalized across temperate regions worldwide. Major commercial producers include India, Egypt, Poland, Hungary, and the United States.

Dill is a cool-season annual that prefers full sun and well-drained, fertile loam with pH 5.5–6.5. Optimal germination temperature is 15–21°C; seeds germinate in 7–14 days.

Direct sowing is recommended, as dill forms a taproot sensitive to transplant disturbance. Succession sowing every 2–3 weeks ensures continuous harvest through the growing season.

Leaf harvest begins 6–8 weeks after sowing. Seed harvest occurs when flower heads turn brown, typically 90–110 days from sowing. Yields range from 1–2 t/ha of fresh herb or 0.5–1 t/ha of dried seed.

The plant is relatively pest-resistant but susceptible to aphids, carrot rust fly, and powdery mildew in humid conditions.

### Harvest and Preservation

Fresh dill weed is highly perishable and should be used within 1–2 days of harvest. It can be frozen (chopped, in ice cube trays with water) for up to 6 months with minimal flavor loss.

Dill seed keeps well in airtight containers for 1–2 years. For maximum essential oil retention, seeds should be harvested just as they begin to turn brown, before they shatter.

Dill pickles — cucumbers preserved with dill weed, seed, garlic, and vinegar — represent the most widespread culinary preservation method and the basis of a multi-billion dollar processed food industry globally.

## Culinary Uses

Dill is one of the most versatile culinary herbs, used in cuisines spanning Scandinavia to South Asia.

**Dill weed** (fresh leaves) has a fresh, grassy, slightly anise-like flavor. It is a key ingredient in Scandinavian gravlax, tzatziki, borscht, and potato salads. Leaves should be added at the end of cooking to preserve volatile aromatics.

**Dill seed** has a more concentrated, caraway-like flavor with camphoraceous undertones. It is essential in dill pickles, rye bread, sauerkraut, and many Indian dishes (particularly in the tempering of dal and curries).

**Dill flowers** are edible and used as a garnish, adding visual appeal and mild dill flavor to salads and cold dishes.

The essential oil, distilled from seeds, is used commercially in food flavoring, particularly for pickling spice blends, condiments, and liqueurs.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The essential oil of dill seed (1.5–6%) is dominated by **carvone** (30–60%), **limonene** (20–40%), and **dillapiole** (0–15%). Leaf oil differs significantly, with higher α-phellandrene and lower carvone content.

Carvone is responsible for the characteristic caraway-dill flavor and has documented antispasmodic activity on smooth muscle, supporting the herb's traditional use as a digestive aid.

Additional constituents include **apiol**, **myristicin**, **anethofuran**, and flavonoids (kaempferol, quercetin derivatives).

Pharmacological studies demonstrate **carminative** (gas-reducing), **antispasmodic**, and **antimicrobial** activities. In vitro studies show activity against *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Escherichia coli*, and *Candida albicans*.

Dill extracts exhibit **diuretic** and **hypolipidemic** effects in animal models, though clinical data in humans remains limited.

## Traditional Medicine

Dill has a long history of medicinal use across multiple healing traditions spanning over 2,000 years.

In **Ayurveda**, dill seed (*Shatapushpa*) is classified as a digestive tonic and is prescribed for bloating, colic, and indigestion. It is considered balancing for Vata and Kapha doshas.

**Traditional European medicine** employed dill water (an aqueous infusion of dill seed) as a remedy for infant colic, a practice documented since the Middle Ages and still recommended by some herbalists today.

In **Persian traditional medicine** (*Teb-e Sonati*), dill is used as an emmenagogue, diuretic, and antispasmodic. Dill seed tea is prescribed for menstrual cramps and digestive discomfort.

Modern herbalism continues to recommend dill primarily as a carminative and antispasmodic, often in combination with fennel and peppermint in digestive formulations.

## Companion Planting and Permaculture

Dill is an excellent companion plant in vegetable gardens and permaculture systems.

It attracts **beneficial insects** — particularly parasitic wasps (*Aphidius* spp., *Trichogramma* spp.), ladybugs, lacewings, and hoverflies (Syrphidae) — that prey on aphids, caterpillars, and other pests.

The umbelliferous flowers are highly attractive to **pollinators** including honeybees, bumblebees, and native solitary bees, making dill a useful addition to pollinator borders and insectary plantings.

Dill is compatible with most garden vegetables but is traditionally said to inhibit the growth of carrots when planted in close proximity (both are Apiaceae and may compete for the same nutrients and attract similar pests).

In permaculture design, dill serves as a fast-growing, self-seeding annual that fills niche spaces in guild plantings, providing both culinary yield and ecological services within a single season.

Volunteer dill seedlings often appear the following season from self-seeding, creating a low-maintenance, self-perpetuating herb patch that requires minimal ongoing input.
