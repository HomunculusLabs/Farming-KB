## Overview

**Anethum graveolens** L., commonly known as **dill**, is an annual herb in the family Apiaceae. Native to the Mediterranean region and southern Russia, it is cultivated worldwide as a culinary herb and spice.

Both the feathery leaves (dill weed) and the dried seeds are used extensively in European, Scandinavian, Middle Eastern, and Central Asian cuisines. The plant is also valued in traditional medicine as a carminative, digestive aid, and mild sedative.

Dill is an efficient companion plant [[maintaining-organic-matter-in-vegetable-gardens]], attracting [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] including pollinators and predatory wasps that control aphid populations.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Classified within Apiaceae (Umbelliferae) → **Apioideae → *Anethum → *A. graveolens***.

A. graveolens is the sole accepted species in the genus *Anethum*. Two subspecies are recognized: subsp. *graveolens* (cultivated dill) and subsp. *anethum* (wild dill), the latter found in the Mediterranean and Central Asia.

The plant grows 40–120 cm tall, with a single hollow, branching stem. Leaves are finely divided, feathery, and thread-like — 2–3 times pinnately compound, resembling fennel foliage but more delicate.

Flowers are borne in compound umbels 5–15 cm across, with yellow petals. Each umbel contains 15–25 rays. Flowering occurs 8–12 weeks after sowing.

Fruits (commonly called "seeds") are oval, flattened, 3–5 mm long, with two mericarps. They are brown when mature, with three longitudinal ridges and two winged lateral ridges.

## Distribution and Cultivation

Originally native to the Mediterranean basin, the Caucasus, and Central Asia, dill has been naturalized across [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] worldwide. Major commercial producers include India, Egypt, Poland, Hungary, and the United States.

Dill is a cool-season annual that prefers full sun and well-drained, fertile loam with pH 5.5–6.5. Optimal germination temperature is 15–21°C; seeds germinate in 7–14 days.

Direct sowing is recommended, as dill forms a taproot sensitive to transplant disturbance. Succession sowing every 2–3 weeks ensures continuous harvest through [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

Leaf harvest begins 6–8 weeks after sowing. Seed harvest occurs when flower heads turn brown, typically 90–110 days from sowing. Yields range from 1–2 t/ha of fresh herb or 0.5–1 t/ha of dried seed.

The plant is relatively pest-resistant but susceptible to aphids, carrot rust fly, and [[cervantes-powdery-mildew-prevention-treatment]] in humid conditions.

### Harvest and Preservation

Fresh dill weed is highly perishable and should be used within 1–2 days of harvest. It can be frozen (chopped, in ice cube trays with water) for up to 6 months with minimal flavor loss.

Dill seed keeps well in airtight containers for 1–2 years. For maximum [[permaculture-designers-manual-essential-oil-plants]] retention, seeds should be harvested just as they begin to turn brown, before they shatter.

Dill pickles — cucumbers preserved with dill weed, seed, garlic, and vinegar — represent the most widespread culinary preservation method and the basis of a multi-billion dollar processed food industry globally.

## Culinary Uses

Dill is one of the most versatile [[holzer-medicinal-and-culinary-herbs-compendium]], used in cuisines spanning Scandinavia to South Asia.

**Dill weed** (fresh leaves) has a fresh, grassy, slightly anise-like flavor. It is a key ingredient in Scandinavian gravlax, tzatziki, borscht, and potato salads. Leaves should be added at the end of cooking to preserve volatile aromatics.

**Dill seed** has a more concentrated, caraway-like flavor with camphoraceous undertones. It is essential in dill pickles, rye bread, sauerkraut, and many Indian dishes (particularly in the tempering of dal and curries).

**Dill flowers** are edible and used as a garnish, adding visual appeal and mild dill flavor to salads and cold dishes.

The essential oil, distilled from seeds, is used commercially in food flavoring, particularly for pickling spice blends, condiments, and liqueurs.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The essential oil of dill seed (1.5–6%) is dominated by **carvone** (30–60%), **limonene** (20–40%), and **dillapiole** (0–15%). Leaf oil differs significantly, with higher α-phellandrene and lower carvone content.

Carvone is responsible for the characteristic caraway-dill flavor and has documented antispasmodic activity on smooth muscle, supporting the herb's traditional use as a digestive aid.

Additional constituents include **apiol**, **myristicin**, **anethofuran**, and flavonoids (kaempferol, quercetin derivatives).

Pharmacological studies demonstrate **carminative** (gas-reducing), **antispasmodic**, and **antimicrobial** activities. In vitro studies show activity against *[[staphylococcus-aureus]]*, *Escherichia coli*, and *[[bloomfield-candida-albicans-yeast-hyphal-dimorphism]]*.
