# Amadou

## Description

Amadou is a spongy, flammable material prepared from the fruitbodies of [[fomes-fomentarius]] (iceman [[polypore]]) and related bracket fungi. It is one of the oldest known manufactured materials, with evidence of its use dating back to at least 3300 BCE, when it was found among the possessions of the frozen mummy known as [[otzi-the-iceman]]. Amadou has served historically as tinder for fire-starting, as a leather-like textile material, and as a medium for medicinal preparations. Its enduring utility across millennia speaks to the remarkable properties of polypore fungi as raw materials for human craft and industry.

## Classification

- **Category:** Traditional material / fungal product
- **Source organism:** Primarily Fomes fomentarius (iceman polypore)
- **Alternative names:** Tinder fungus material, German tinder, false tinder
- **Historical period of use:** At least 5,000+ years (Copper Age to present)

## Key Facts

- Prepared from the tough, fibrous interior of Fomes fomentarius conks
- One of the best natural tinder materials known; readily catches and holds a spark
- Has been used for over 5,000 years, with archaeological evidence from Otzi the Iceman (3300 BCE)
- Can be processed into a leather-like material suitable for pouches, hats, and garment padding
- Traditional preparation involves boiling, pounding, and treating with potassium nitrate
- Also used historically as a primitive surgical material for wound dressing and cauterization
- Found in the possession of Otzi, proving its importance to Copper Age travelers
- Remains valued in modern bushcraft and primitive skills communities

## Production Process

### Traditional Method
1. Harvest mature Fomes fomentarius conks from birch and other hardwood trees
2. Remove the hard outer rind (cuticle) of the conk
3. Slice the fibrous interior (tramal tissue) into strips or sheets
4. Boil the material for extended periods to soften fibers and remove bitter compounds
5. Pound the strips flat with a mallet to separate and flatten the fibers
6. Optionally treat with potassium nitrate (saltpeter) to enhance combustion properties
7. Dry the processed sheets thoroughly for storage and use

### Modern Variations
Some contemporary makers skip the potassium nitrate treatment for a more natural product, while others incorporate additional processing steps such as soaking in lye solutions or repeated boiling cycles to achieve desired softness and flammability characteristics.

## Historical and Cultural Significance

### Copper Age and Antiquity
The discovery of amadou among Otzi the Iceman's possessions (circa 3300 BCE) demonstrates that fungal processing was a sophisticated technology in prehistoric Europe. The material was clearly valued enough to be carried on long journeys through harsh alpine environments.

### Traditional European Craft
In traditional European cultures, particularly in Eastern Europe, amadou crafting was a specialized trade. Artisans produced hats, pouches, and decorative items from the processed material. The craft was especially developed in regions where Fomes fomentarius was abundant on birch trees. The material was prized for its softness, water resistance, and durability.

### Ethnomycological Importance
Amadou represents one of the earliest examples of fungal biotechnology, bridging the gap between foraging and manufacturing. It demonstrates a processed natural product with enhanced properties over the raw material, requiring knowledge of harvesting, preparation, and application.

## Properties and Applications

### Fire Starting
Amadou is one of the finest natural tinder materials available. Its fibrous structure catches sparks readily from flint and steel or friction-based fire-starting methods. Once ignited, it smolders slowly and reliably, providing a sustained heat source for igniting finer kindling. The potassium nitrate treatment enhances this property by lowering the ignition temperature.

### Textile and Leather Use
When properly processed, amadou resembles soft leather or suede. It can be cut, sewn, and shaped into various items including hats, pouches, sheaths, and garment patches. The material is naturally water-resistant due to its fungal origin and provides good insulation.

### Medicinal Applications
Historically, amadou was used as a wound dressing and cauterization material. The antimicrobial properties of Fomes fomentarius may have provided some protection against infection. In traditional medicine, the material was also used as a poultice base.

### Paper and Writing
The fibrous nature of processed amadou makes it suitable as a paper-making material. Historical accounts describe its use for creating writing surfaces, though this application was less common than its use as tinder and textile.

## Relevance to Modern Mycology

The historical importance of amadou underscores the deep relationship between humans and polypore fungi. Modern interest has been partially revived by the bushcraft and primitive skills movements, where it is valued as a reliable natural fire-starting material. More broadly, amadou inspires contemporary research into [[mycelium]]-based products for packaging, textiles, and construction materials. Companies like Ecovative and others developing mycelium composites are modern descendants of the ancient amadou craft tradition.

## Source Organism: Fomes fomentarius

The primary source of amadou is Fomes fomentarius, a perennial bracket fungus that grows on hardwood trees, especially birch, at higher elevations. Key characteristics:

- Grayish, banded, hoof-shaped conks that persist year-round
- New growth layers added annually; wild specimens can persist for over 50 years
- Mycelium is white, cottony, and delicate, oxidizing to dark chocolate brown with age
- Contains compounds similar to turkey tail, including polysaccharide-K (PSK)
- Demonstrates antibiotic properties suitable for mycofiltration applications
