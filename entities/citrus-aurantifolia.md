## Overview

**Citrus aurantifolia** (Christm.) Swingle, commonly known as the Key lime, Mexican lime, or West Indian lime, is a small evergreen tree in the family Rutaceae.

It is one of the most commercially important citrus species, valued globally for its highly aromatic, acidic [[bionutrients-lab-fermented-fruit-juice-ffj]].

The Key lime is the principal lime cultivar in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide. It is distinguished from the Persian lime (*Citrus latifolia*) by its smaller size, thinner skin, and more intense flavor.

The species has been cultivated for over 2,500 years, with origins tracing to [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]] or the Indo-Malayan region.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

The species was initially described by Johann Christoph Wild as *Citrus aurantifolia* in 1792, later reclassified by Walter Tennyson Swingle.

It belongs to the *Citrus* genus within the Aurantioideae subfamily. Genetic evidence suggests *C. aurantifolia* is likely a hybrid between *Citrus micrantha* (a papeda) and *Citrus medica* (citron).

**Key morphological features:**

- **Tree:** Small, spreading, reaching 3–6 m in height with dense, thorny branches.
- **Leaves:** Ovate to lanceolate, 4–8 cm long, dark green, with narrowly winged petioles.
- **Flowers:** Small, white, 1.5–2 cm diameter, borne in axillary clusters, intensely fragrant.
- **Fruit:** Small, globose to ovoid, 3–6 cm diameter, green turning yellow at full maturity. Thin, smooth rind. Greenish-yellow pulp, highly acidic juice.
- **Seeds:** Typically 2–6 per fruit, small, polyembryonic.

Fruit ripens approximately 90–120 days after flowering. The tree flowers intermittently throughout the year in [[fukuoka-natural-farming-tropical-climates]], enabling multiple harvests.

## Distribution and Habitat

Key lime is believed to have originated in the Indo-Malayan region. It was spread by Arab traders to the eastern Mediterranean, North Africa, and eventually the Americas via Spanish and Portuguese colonists.

Today it is cultivated throughout the tropics and subtropics, with major production in Mexico, Brazil, India, Egypt, and the West Indies.

In the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], it is famously associated with the Florida Keys, where it became the defining ingredient of Key lime pie.

The species thrives in warm, humid tropical [[query-how-to-grow-rare-tropical-fruits-in-marginal-and-subtropical-climates]]. It is more cold-sensitive than most citrus, suffering damage below 2°C.

Optimal growth occurs at 20–33°C with annual rainfall of 1,000–1,500 mm. It tolerates a wide range of well-drained soils, from sandy to calcareous.

## Cultivation

Key lime trees are typically propagated by seed (polyembryonic seeds produce true-to-type offspring) or by grafting onto rootstocks.

**[[mushroom-species-cultivation-parameters]]:**

- **Soil:** Well-drained, pH 5.5–7.5. Tolerates calcareous and sandy soils better than most citrus.
- **Light:** Full sun required for optimal [[query-how-do-i-prune-brambles-for-maximum-fruit-production]].
- **Water:** Regular irrigation during establishment; moderately drought-tolerant once mature.
- **Spacing:** 4–6 m between trees in commercial orchards.
- **Fertilization:** Requires regular nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium application.

Key lime is highly susceptible to citrus canker (*Xanthomonas axonopodis*) and citrus greening (*Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus*). These diseases have severely impacted production in Florida.

The tree begins bearing fruit 2–4 years after planting. Average yield ranges from 20–40 kg per tree annually under commercial management.

Trees are long-lived, remaining productive for 50+ years under favorable conditions.

## Chemistry and Nutrition

Key lime juice is rich in ascorbic acid (vitamin C), citric acid, and a diverse array of phytochemicals.

**Major [[guzman-magic-mushrooms-chemical-constituents]]:**

- **Ascorbic acid:** 25–35 mg per 100 mL juice, significant antioxidant contribution.
- **Citric acid:** 4–8% of juice by weight, responsible for characteristic tartness.
- **Flavonoids:** Hesperidin, naringin, and rutin identified in peel and juice.
- **[[permaculture-designers-manual-essential-oil-plants]]:** Limonene (30–45%), β-pinene (10–15%), γ-terpinene (5–10%) in peel oil.
- **Volatile aroma compounds:** Citral, linalool, and terpineol contribute to distinctive fragrance.

**Pharmacological findings:**

Extracts demonstrate antimicrobial activity against several foodborne pathogens and oral bacteria.

Animal studies report anti-inflammatory, antiulcerogenic, and hepatoprotective effects from aqueous leaf and peel extracts.
