## Overview

**Arracacia xanthorrhiza**, commonly known as arracacha, is a perennial root crop in the family Apiaceae. It is cultivated primarily in the Andean highlands of South America for its starchy, carrot-flavored taproots.

The species ranks among the most important root crops in Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Venezuela. Its roots contain 10–25% starch and are valued for a smooth texture resembling a blend of potato and celery.

Arracacha is increasingly recognized in permaculture and agroforestry systems for its adaptability to high-altitude tropical environments. It occupies a niche between cool-climate tubers and warm-climate roots.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Arracacia xanthorrhiza** Bancroft belongs to the family Apiaceae, tribe Apieae. The genus *Arracacia* contains approximately 30 species distributed from Mexico to northern Argentina.

The plant produces a rosette of compound leaves reaching 30–50 cm in height. Leaf petioles are stout and purplish at the base, with pinnately divided leaflets 5–15 cm long.

The edible storage roots are thickened taproots forming a compact crown, typically 5–15 cm in diameter. Skin color varies by cultivar: white, yellow, or purple, with the yellow types being most common in commerce.

Internal flesh color is uniform and correlates with skin pigmentation. Yellow-fleshed types contain higher concentrations of carotenoids, while purple types accumulate anthocyanins in the periderm layer.

Flowering stems arise from the crown and reach 1–1.5 m tall. Umbels bear small white flowers; seed production is variable, and most cultivation relies on vegetative propagation through root crowns.

## Distribution and Habitat

Arracacha is native to the Andean region, spanning from Venezuela and Colombia southward through Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia. It grows at elevations between 1,800 and 3,200 meters above sea level.

The species thrives in cool tropical climates with mean annual temperatures of 12–18°C and annual rainfall of 800–1,500 mm. It requires well-drained volcanic or loamy soils with moderate fertility.

Outside its native range, arracacha has been introduced to southern Brazil, Costa Rica, and parts of [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] return from leaf litter. Its canopy protects soil from erosion on steep Andean slopes, making it valuable in conservation agriculture.

In [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] requires planning but rewards with reliable yields in cool climates where few other root crops succeed.

The deep root system helps break compacted subsoil layers, making arracacha a useful pioneer species in soil rehabilitation projects. Its biomass contribution through leaf litter adds organic matter to the upper soil profile.

Arracacha flour is produced commercially in Colombia and Brazil as a gluten-free alternative to wheat flour. The processed product has a longer shelf life and is used in breads, pastries, and infant foods.

## Pests and Diseases

The most significant pathogen affecting arracacha is *Puccinia araujiae*, a rust fungus that causes orange pustules on leaf undersides. Severe infections can reduce yields by 20–40% when left untreated.

Root-knot nematodes (*Meloidogyne* spp.) pose a serious threat in areas where arracacha is grown continuously without rotation. Affected roots develop galls and become unmarketable.

Fungal crown rot caused by *[[sclerotinia-sclerotiorum]]* and *Fusarium* species can devastate stored roots, particularly under humid conditions. Improved post-harvest handling and prompt processing are the primary management strategies.

Insect pests include the arracacha weevil and various aphid species that serve as vectors for [[cervantes-viral-diseases-cannabis]]. Cultural controls such as crop rotation and removal of volunteer plants are standard practices.
