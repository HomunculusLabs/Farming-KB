## Description

albert bernard frank (1839–1900) was a German botanist and mycologist credited with first documenting the symbiotic relationship between plant roots and fungi, which he named "mycorrhiza" (from the Greek for "fungus-root") in 1885. In *[[teaming-with-microbes-actinomycetes]]*, Lowenfels and Lewis credit Frank with the foundational experiment that established mycorrhizal science: comparing pines grown in sterilized soil to those grown in sterilized soil inoculated with forest fungi, and finding that the [[truffle-inoculated-seedlings]] grew faster and much larger.

Frank"s work laid the groundwork for understanding what is now recognized as one of the most important [[fungi-in-the-environment-symbiotic-relationships]] in terrestrial ecology — at least 90-95% of all plant species form mycorrhizal associations.

## Classification

- **Profession:** Botanist, mycologist, plant physiologist
- **Nationality:** German
- **Institution:** University of Berlin (Royal Agricultural College, Berlin)
- **Era:** 19th century (1839–1900)
- **Key contribution:** Discovery and naming of [[mycorrhizal-symbiosis]]

## Key Facts

- Born January 17, 1839, in Dresden, Germany
- Died November 27, 1900, in Berlin, Germany
- Coined the term "mycorrhiza" in 1885, combining the Greek words *mykes* (fungus) and *rhiza* (root)
- Conducted the landmark experiment comparing pines in sterilized vs. inoculated soil
- Published extensively on plant pathology, lichens, and plant-fungal relationships
- His research demonstrated that forest fungi were not merely parasites but essential partners in plant nutrition

## The 1885 Experiment

Frank"s pivotal experiment involved growing pine seedlings under two conditions:

1. **Sterilized soil** — soil treated to kill all microorganisms, including fungi
2. **Inoculated soil** — sterilized soil to which forest fungi had been reintroduced

The results were clear and dramatic: seedlings in the inoculated soil grew significantly faster and larger than those in the sterilized soil. This demonstrated that soil fungi were not harmful to plants but rather essential for optimal growth. The experiment established that the fungal-root association was a beneficial symbiosis, not a pathological condition as many had assumed.

## Historical Significance

Frank's discovery was revolutionary for several reasons:

- **Paradigm shift** — before Frank, root-associated fungi were generally viewed as pathogens or parasites
- **Foundation of mycorrhizal science** — his work created an entirely new field of study
- **Term establishment** — the word "mycorrhiza" that he coined remains the standard term worldwide
- **Ecological implications** — his findings hinted at the interconnectedness of soil organisms and plant health

Despite the importance of his findings, it took over a century for mycorrhizal science to enter mainstream agricultural and horticultural practice. As Lowenfels notes, the terms mycorrhiza and mycorrhizal only started creeping into the agricultural industry"s lexicon in the 1990s.

## Legacy in Modern Science

Frank's work has been validated and expanded by modern research:

- **Mycorrhizal types** — scientists now recognize multiple types: arbuscular (endomycorrhizal), ectomycorrhizal, ericoid, [[ericoid-and-orchid-mycorrhizae]]
- **Global significance** — mycorrhizal associations are now known to be crucial for terrestrial ecosystem function, affecting carbon cycling, nutrient dynamics, and plant community structure
- **Agricultural applications** — mycorrhizal inoculants are commercially available and used in agriculture, horticulture, and restoration ecology
- **Molecular biology** — genomic studies have revealed the deep evolutionary history of mycorrhizal symbiosis, dating back ~450 million years

## Relevance to Cultivation

Frank's discoveries have direct practical implications for modern cultivation:

- **Inoculation practices** — gardeners and farmers can inoculate soil with mycorrhizal fungi to improve plant growth, directly applying Frank's observations
- **[[comparison-no-till-vs-reduced-tillage]]** — understanding that mycorrhizal fungi are fragile and easily destroyed by tillage supports no-till farming practices
- **Chemical awareness** — knowing that fungicides, pesticides, and [[ingham-inorganic-fertilizers-soil-biology-damage]] can harm mycorrhizal fungi encourages organic approaches
- **[[soil-food-web]] gardening** — Frank's work is the scientific foundation for the entire approach advocated in *Teaming with Microbes*

## Relationships and Influence

- Frank's work influenced later researchers including those who classified different types of mycorrhizal associations
- The genus *Frankia* (nitrogen-fixing actinomycete bacteria that form root nodules) is named in his honor
- His research on lichens complemented his mycorrhizal work, contributing to understanding of fungal symbioses broadly

## Interesting Facts

- The bacterial genus *Frankia* is named after Albert Bernhard Frank in recognition of his contributions to understanding plant-microbe symbioses
- Despite his groundbreaking 1885 paper, it took over 100 years for mycorrhizal awareness to reach mainstream gardening
- Frank was also a pioneer in plant pathology, writing influential texts on plant diseases
- The mycorrhizal associations Frank documented are now estimated to connect 90-95% of all plant species
- Plants likely began growing on Earth's surface only after fungi entered into relationships with aquatic plants, making mycorrhizae older than most terrestrial plant lineages
