# Leonotis leonurus

## Overview

Leonotis leonurus, commonly known as wild dagga, lion's ear, or wild cannabis, is a perennial evergreen shrub in the family Lamiaceae. Native to southern and eastern Africa, it has a long history of traditional use as a medicine, psychoactive smoking herb, and ornamental plant.

The species produces striking orange, tubular flowers arranged in whorled clusters along tall, square stems, giving the inflorescence the appearance of a lion's mane. The dried flowers and leaves are smoked traditionally for their mild sedative and euphoric effects, attributed to the diterpenoid leonurine and related compounds.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Angiospermae
- **Class:** Eudicots
- **Order:** Lamiales
- **Family:** Lamiaceae
- **Subfamily:** Lamioideae
- **Genus:** Leonotis
- **Species:** L. leonurus
- **Authority:** (L.) R.Br. (1810)
- **Synonyms:** Phlomis leonurus L.; Leonurus leonurus (L.) Houtt.

The genus name *Leonotis* combines Greek *leon* (lion) and *otis* (ear), referring to the lion's-ear shape of the flower clusters. The species epithet *leonurus* means "lion-colored," referring to the orange flowers.

## Morphology

### Growth Habit

An upright, branching perennial shrub reaching 1.5–3 m tall and 1–2 m wide. The plant forms a woody base with herbaceous upper branches that die back in colder climates.

Growth is rapid under favorable conditions, with plants reaching full size within 2–3 growing seasons. The lifespan in cultivation is typically 5–10 years before the woody base becomes too leggy.

### Stems and Leaves

Square stems (characteristic of Lamiaceae), covered in fine white hairs. Leaves are opposite, lanceolate to ovate, 5–12 cm long, with serrate margins and a rough, sandpapery texture.

The foliage is aromatic when crushed, emitting a pungent, herbaceous scent. Leaf color is medium to dark green with a lighter undersurface.

### Flowers

Tubular, two-lipped flowers, 3–5 cm long, arranged in dense whorled clusters at intervals along the upper stems. The most common color is bright orange, though yellow and white cultivars exist.

Each flower whorl is subtended by a cup-like involucre of spiny-tipped bracts, a distinctive feature of the genus. Flowers are rich in nectar and are highly attractive to sunbirds and butterflies.

### Fruit

Small nutlets (schizocarps), approximately 2 mm long, produced in groups of four per flower. Seeds are light brown and dispersed by wind and water.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to southern and eastern Africa, from South Africa northward through Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Botswana, Namibia, and eastern tropical Africa. Naturalized in many subtropical and warm temperate regions worldwide, including California, Hawaii, Australia, and the Mediterranean.

Inhabits grassland, savanna, forest margins, and disturbed areas at elevations up to 2,000 m. Tolerant of poor soils, drought, and moderate frost once established.

## Phytochemistry

### Leonurine

The primary bioactive compound, a diterpenoid alkaloid with cardiovascular, uterotonic, and mild psychoactive properties. Leonurine is also found in *Leonurus cardiaca* (motherwort), a related species used in European herbalism.

### Labdane Diterpenes

Includes leonotin, leonotinin, and marrubiin-type compounds. These contribute to the anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects observed in pharmacological studies.

### Essential Oil

The aerial parts yield 0.1–0.5% essential oil composed primarily of α-pinene, β-pinene, limonene, myrcene, and camphor. The oil has demonstrated antimicrobial activity in vitro.

### Flavonoids

Apigenin, luteolin, and their glycosides are present in the leaves and flowers, contributing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity.

## Traditional and Ethnobotanical Uses

In South African traditional medicine, *L. leonurus* is used to treat a wide range of conditions including colds, coughs, fever, headache, asthma, hemorrhoids, and dysentery. The Zulu people use infusions of the leaves and stems as a remedy for snakebite.

The dried flowers and leaves are smoked recreationally and medicinally, producing mild sedative and euphoric effects described as somewhat similar to cannabis but significantly milder. This practice has given the plant the Afrikaans name "wilde dagga" (wild cannabis).

Traditional healers (inyangas and sangomas) use the plant in purification rituals and as a ceremonial smoking herb. The Hottentot people of South Africa reportedly used it as an inebriant before the introduction of cannabis.

**Caution:** Excessive consumption can cause nausea, headache, and visual disturbances. The plant should not be used during pregnancy due to uterotonic properties of leonurine.

## Permaculture and Agroforestry Applications

Wild dagga is an excellent addition to subtropical and warm temperate permaculture systems as a fast-growing, drought-tolerant ornamental that supports pollinators. The flowers are among the best nectar sources for butterflies and sunbirds.

The plant acts as a dynamic accumulator, drawing minerals from deep soil layers through its extensive root system. Pruned material can be used as mulch or compost feedstock.

It serves as an effective windbreak and visual screen when planted in hedgerows. The drought tolerance and rapid growth make it useful for erosion control on slopes and degraded land.

## Cultivation

Easily propagated from seed or semi-hardwood cuttings. Seeds germinate readily in 2–4 weeks at 20–25°C. Cuttings root in 3–4 weeks under mist or high humidity.

The plant thrives in full sun to partial shade with well-drained soil. It is remarkably drought-tolerant once established, requiring minimal irrigation in Mediterranean and subtropical climates.

Hard pruning after flowering encourages bushy, compact growth and repeat blooming. In frost-prone areas, plants may die to the ground but typically resprout from the woody base in spring.
