## Overview

Dypsis decaryi, commonly known as the triangle palm, is a species of flowering plant in the family Arecaceae. It is endemic to Madagascar, where it grows in the southeastern Andohahela region near Tôlanaro (Fort Dauphin).

The species is named after Raymond Decary, a French botanist and ethnographer who collected extensively in Madagascar during the early [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]].

International trade in wild-collected seeds has raised concerns about sustainability. Conservation organizations recommend sourcing cultivated stock to reduce pressure on wild populations.

Dypsis decaryi is frequently used as a focal point specimen in residential and commercial landscape designs. Its symmetrical growth habit and distinctive trunk make it a highly photogenic garden subject.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Dypsis decaryi was described by Henri Lucien Jumelle in 1933, based on collections made by Decary in southern Madagascar. It belongs to the subfamily Arecoideae and the tribe Areceae within Arecaceae.

The genus Dypsis is endemic to Madagascar and the Comoro Islands, comprising approximately 140 described species. Dypsis decaryi is one of the most widely cultivated members of the genus outside its native range.

The palm grows to approximately 15 meters in height, with a solitary trunk reaching 30–40 centimeters in diameter. The trunk develops its characteristic three-sided shape as the leaf bases persist and wrap around the crownshaft, creating prominent ridges.

Dypsis decaryi is endemic to southeastern Madagascar, where it occurs in a narrow coastal band in the Taolanaro (Fort Dauphin) district of the Anosy region. It grows at low elevations, typically from sea level to approximately 200 meters.

## Ecology and Uses

In its native habitat, Dypsis decaryi provides food and habitat for wildlife, including lemurs and birds that consume the fruit. The species is an important component of gallery [[dighton-fungi-acidifying-pollutants-forest-ecosystems]] in southeastern Madagascar.

The palm's inflorescences are visited by a variety of insect pollinators, including bees and beetles. Fruit dispersal is primarily through animal vectors, with birds and mammals carrying seeds away from parent plants.

The palm is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List due to habitat loss from agricultural expansion and mining activities. Its native range has been significantly reduced, with remaining populations fragmented across isolated forest patches.

Conservation efforts include protection within the Andohahela National Park, which encompasses part of the species' native range. Ex situ conservation through botanical garden collections provides additional genetic safeguards.

Cultivated triangle palms serve primarily ornamental purposes in landscaping. The species is widely planted in Mediterranean-climate regions, including southern California, southern Florida, parts of Australia, and South Africa.
