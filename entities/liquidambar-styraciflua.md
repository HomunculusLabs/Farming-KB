## Overview

**Liquidambar styraciflua**, commonly known as American sweetgum, is a large
deciduous tree native to the southeastern United States, [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]], and
Mexico. It is the sole North American representative of the genus *Liquidambar*
and is renowned for its brilliant multicolored autumn foliage and distinctive
corky, winged bark on young branches.

The species is among the most important commercial hardwoods in the
southeastern United States. It is also widely planted as a street and shade
tree in temperate regions worldwide due to its symmetrical, conical crown and
reliable fall color.

Sweetgum typically reaches 25–35 meters in height with a straight trunk
diameter of 60–100 cm. In exceptional bottomland sites, heights exceeding
45 meters have been recorded. The tree can live 200–300 years under favorable
conditions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Liquidambar styraciflua** L. was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The
genus name derives from Latin *liquidus* (liquid) and *ambar* (amber),
referring to the aromatic resin produced by the tree. The species was
historically placed in Hamamelidaceae but is now classified in the family
Altingiaceae.

Leaves are 10–18 cm long and wide, palmately 5-lobed (occasionally 7-lobed)
with finely serrate margins and star-shaped venation. They are glossy dark
green above, paler below, and produce one of the most diverse autumn color
palettes of any native tree — simultaneous shades of yellow, orange, red,
purple, and burgundy.

The bark on mature trunks is deeply furrowed with narrow, interlacing ridges
resembling alligator hide. Young twigs bear distinctive corky, winged
projections (lenticels) that are diagnostic for the species. These bark wings
are most prominent on the second and third year growth.

Flowers are monoecious, appearing in April–May as inconspicuous, globular
heads. Male flowers are greenish-yellow in terminal clusters; female flowers
are in pendant, long-stalked heads. The fruit is a distinctive spherical,
woody cluster 2.5–4 cm in diameter, composed of 40–60 persistent,
beak-like capsules that persist on the tree through winter.

## Distribution and Habitat

American sweetgum ranges from southwestern Connecticut south through the
Atlantic coastal plain to central Florida, west to eastern Texas, and north
through the Mississippi Valley to southern Illinois and extreme southeastern
Missouri. Disjunct populations occur in the mountains of Mexico and Central
America.

The species is most abundant on moist, alluvial soils of floodplains and
bottomland hardwood forests. It is a common associate of [[quercus-alba]], and [[quercus-rubra]]
- [[fagus-grandifolia]]
- [[acer-rubrum]]
- [[carya-ovata]]
- [[populus-tremuloides]]
- [[betula-papyrifera]]
