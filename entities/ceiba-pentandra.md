The trunk is straight and cylindrical, typically 1 to 3 meters in diameter at breast height, covered in smooth, gray to pale green bark armed with thick, conical prickles when young. Older bark becomes relatively smooth and loses most of its prickles.

The crown is broad and spreading, forming a distinctive umbrella or pagoda shape. Branches are arranged in distinct horizontal tiers, a growth pattern known as sympodial branching that is characteristic of many tropical canopy trees.

Leaves are palmately compound with 5 to 9 leaflets, each leaflet 8 to 20 centimeters long and 2 to 6 centimeters wide. The leaflets are elliptic to lanceolate, with entire margins and a glabrous upper surface.

Flowers are produced in dense clusters on leafless branches during the dry season. The flowers are large, 5 to 15 centimeters in diameter, with creamy white to pale pink petals and a prominent central staminal column.

The fruit is a large, woody, pendulous capsule, 10 to 30 centimeters long, containing numerous seeds embedded in a mass of silky white floss. When the capsule splits open at maturity, the floss is dispersed by wind, carrying the seeds considerable distances.

Each capsule contains 100 to 200 seeds, each approximately 5 millimeters in diameter and surrounded by the characteristic silky fibers. The fiber-to-seed ratio is approximately 2:1 by weight, with individual fibers reaching 15 to 35 millimeters in length.

## Uses

The most economically significant product of Ceiba pentandra is the kapok fiber, a silky floss that surrounds the seeds within the capsules. This fiber is lightweight, buoyant, water-resistant, and has been used for centuries as a stuffing material for pillows, mattresses, and life preservers.

Kapok fiber is composed primarily of cellulose and lignin, with a hollow core that gives it exceptional buoyancy. Before the widespread adoption of synthetic fibers, kapok was a major global commodity, with [[lichens-primary-production-soil-formation-dighton]] centers. The fiber was particularly valued for stuffing life jackets during both World Wars due to its water-resistant and buoyant properties.

The lightweight wood is used for light construction, canoes, carving, and plywood. It is not considered a durable timber but is valued where low weight is important, such as in model making and lightweight crates.

The seeds contain an edible oil, kapok seed oil, which has been used in cooking, soap manufacture, and as a lubricant. The oil content ranges from 20 to 25 percent of seed weight, and the meal remaining after oil extraction is a protein-rich animal feed.

In traditional medicine, various parts of the tree have been used to treat fever, asthma, diabetes, and wound infections. The bark decoction is used as an emetic and diuretic in several West African traditional medical systems.

## Ecology

Ceiba pentandra is a keystone species in many tropical forest ecosystems, providing habitat, food, and nesting sites for a wide range of organisms. The large, spreading crown supports epiphytic communities including orchids, bromeliads, ferns, and lianas.

The flowers are pollinated primarily by bats, particularly fruit bats of the family Pteropodidae. The nocturnal flowers produce abundant nectar and have a musky odor that attracts bat pollinators, a pollination syndrome typical of many neotropical Bombacoideae.

The flowers are also visited by various nocturnal insects, including moths and beetles, which serve as secondary pollinators. The copious nectar production supports a diverse community of nectar-feeding organisms.

The seed capsules provide food for several species of birds and mammals, including parrots, monkeys, and squirrels. The kapok fiber, while serving as a seed dispersal mechanism, is also collected by birds for nest lining.
