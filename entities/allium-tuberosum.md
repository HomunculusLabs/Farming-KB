## Overview

**Allium tuberosum**, commonly known as Chinese chives, garlic chives, or jiu cai (韭菜), is
a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Amaryllidaceae native to the Shanxi region of
China. It is widely cultivated across East and [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]] for its flat, garlicky-flavored
leaves, [[comparison-scented-geraniums-vs-edible-flowers]], and ornamental value. The species is distinguished from common chives
(*A. schoenoprasum*) by its flat leaves, white star-shaped flowers, and distinctly garlicky
aroma.

Chinese chives are a staple vegetable in Chinese, Korean, and [[oner-southeast-asian-sativa-heritage-thai-vietnamese-cambodian]] cuisines. They
are also valued in permaculture systems as a hardy, low-maintenance ground cover, companion
plant, and pest deterrent. The species is extremely cold-hardy and adaptable to a wide range
of growing conditions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- Kingdom: Plantae — Division: Magnoliophyta — Class: Liliopsida
- Order: Asparagales — Family: Amaryllidaceae — Subfamily: Allioideae
- Genus: *Allium* — Species: *A. tuberosum* Rottler ex Spreng. (1801)

**Synonyms:** *Allium odorum* L. (1753), *[[allium-tricoccum]]* auct. non L.

**Morphology:** Chinese chives form dense clumps 20–40 cm tall from a fibrous root system
with small, elongated bulbs. The bulbs are cylindrical, 1–2 cm in diameter, and rarely
divide into offsets — propagation is primarily by seed or division of clumps.

Leaves are linear, flat (not tubular like *A. schoenoprasum*), 20–40 cm long and 4–8 mm
wide, with a smooth dark green surface and a pronounced garlic-onion flavor when crushed.

Flowering stems (scapes) rise 30–60 cm above the foliage, bearing umbels of white,
star-shaped flowers 5–7 mm across. Flowers bloom from mid-summer to early autumn and are
both edible and highly attractive to pollinators.

## Distribution and Habitat

*A. tuberosum* is native to the Chinese provinces of Shanxi, Gansu, and Inner Mongolia. It
has been cultivated for over 2,000 years and is now naturalized in many temperate and
subtropical regions worldwide, including Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia, Europe, [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]],
and parts of [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]].

The species is exceptionally hardy, tolerating temperatures as low as −30°C (USDA zones
3–9). It grows in most well-drained soils, from sandy loams to heavy clays, and tolerates
both acidic and alkaline conditions (pH 5.0–8.0). It prefers full sun but produces
acceptable yields in partial shade.

Chinese chives are moderately drought-tolerant once established, requiring roughly 500–800 mm
annual rainfall. They perform best in fertile, moisture-retentive soils with regular
watering during the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].

## Cultivation

Propagation is primarily from seed, sown directly in spring or autumn at a rate of 2–3 g
per square meter. Germination occurs in 7–14 days at 15–20°C. Clump division every 3–4
years maintains vigor and productivity.

First harvest can begin 60–80 days after sowing, cutting leaves 2–3 cm above ground.
Plants regrow rapidly and can be harvested 4–6 times per [[extending-growing-season]]. A "blanched"
growth form (yellow chives or *jiu cai huang*) is produced by excluding light during growth,
yielding tender, mild-flavored leaves prized in Chinese cuisine.

The species is virtually pest-free due to its sulfur-containing compounds, which deter
most insects and mammals. Thrips are the most common pest but rarely cause significant
damage. No major diseases affect established plantings [[query-how-to-grow-and-use-oca-in-temperate-climates]].

## Edibility and Culinary Use

Chinese chives are used extensively in East Asian cooking. The leaves are stir-fried with
meat or tofu, folded into dumplings and buns, added to egg dishes, and used as a garnish.
The unopened flower buds and open flowers are both edible, with a mild garlic flavor.
