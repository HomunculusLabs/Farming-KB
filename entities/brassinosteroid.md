# Brassinosteroid

Brassinosteroids are a class of steroid phytohormones recognized since the 1970s as a distinct group of plant-growth regulators. Over 30 naturally occurring brassinosteroids have been identified across a wide range of higher plants, including both Angiospermae and Gymnospermae, as well as in at least one green alga.

## Discovery and History

Brassinosteroids were first isolated in 1979 from rapeseed (*Brassica napus*) pollen by Grove et al., who identified brassinolide as the most biologically active member of the class. The name derives from *Brassica*, the genus of the source plant. Their status as bona fide phytohormones was confirmed through the identification of specific brassinosteroid receptor kinases (BRI1) and biosynthetic mutants displaying dwarf phenotypes that could be rescued by brassinosteroid application.

## Chemical Classification

Brassinosteroids are polyhydroxylated steroidal compounds characterized by a common structural motif: oxygen functions at C-2, C-3, and C-22/23, along with a side-chain lactone or ketone. ## Biosynthesis in Plants

Brassinosteroids are biosynthesized from the sterol precursor campesterol through two parallel pathways: the early C-6 oxidation pathway and the late C-6 oxidation pathway, both converging on castasterone and ultimately brassinolide. Key enzymatic steps include hydroxylation by cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (CYP85A and CYP90 families) and oxidation/dehydrogenation by DET2 (a steroid 5α-reductase homolog). The pathway is localized in the endoplasmic reticulum. Biosynthetic mutants such as *det2*, *cpd*, and *bri1* in arabidopsis thaliana display characteristic dwarf phenotypes with dark green, rounded leaves, shortened hypocotyls, and reduced fertility — phenotypes that are rescued by brassinosteroid application. These mutants were instrumental in establishing brassinosteroids as essential hormones rather than merely growth-promoting compounds.

## Metabolism and Homeostasis

Active brassinosteroids are maintained at very low concentrations in plant tissues (nanomolar range) through a combination of biosynthetic regulation and catabolic inactivation. Inactivation occurs via hydroxylation (by CYP734A/BAS1) and conjugation to glucose or fatty acids. The balance between biosynthesis and catabolism creates a dynamic hormonal gradient that varies by tissue type and developmental stage. Roots, young leaves, and reproductive organs typically show the highest brassinosteroid activity. The BRI1 signaling pathway includes negative regulatory mechanisms such as the GSK3-like kinase BIN2, which provides feedback control and prevents excessive brassinosteroid signaling.
