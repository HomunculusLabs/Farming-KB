## EMERGY Analysis

## Description

EMERGY (spelled with an "M") analysis is an environmental accounting methodology
developed by systems ecologist Howard T. Odum (1924-2002) at the University of
Florida. The term stands for "Energy Memory" or "Embodied Energy" and refers to the
total amount of solar energy that has been directly and indirectly required to
produce a given resource, product, or service. EMERGY provides a common unit — the
solar emjoule (sej) — for comparing all forms of energy, materials, information,
and human services on a common basis.

Unlike conventional energy analysis, which only accounts for the energy content of
fuels, EMERGY analysis traces all energy transformations back to the original solar
input, providing a more comprehensive picture of the true environmental cost of
products and processes. This approach reveals that many apparently "efficient" or
"sustainable" technologies actually represent net energy losses when their full
production chain is accounted for.

Holmgren draws extensively on EMERGY analysis throughout his text, using it as a
key framework for evaluating renewable energy systems, [[mycorrhizal-agricultural-practices]], and
industrial processes.

## Key Facts

- Developed by H.T. Odum as part of his broader systems ecology framework for
  understanding energy flows in natural and human systems.
- EMERGY measures the total solar energy required to produce a resource or
  service, accounting for all energy transformations in the production chain.
- The EMERGY yield ratio (EYR) compares the EMERGY output of a process to the
  EMERGY of inputs purchased from the economy. Ratios below 1.0 indicate a
  net energy loss.
- Studies of installed solar systems in Austin, Texas and Nashville, Tennessee
  in the 1990s showed EMERGY yield ratios of 0.41 and 0.36 respectively —
  meaning they consumed more embodied energy in manufacture than they produced
  over their lifetime.
- For comparison: lignite coal-produced electricity in Texas yields 2.1, and
  hydroelectric power in New Zealand yields 10.
- Metabolic energy for humans is approximately 2,500 kilocalories per day, while
  per capita fuel share in the USA is approximately 229,000 kilocalories per day.
- Per capita national share of EMERGY in the USA is approximately 557,500 coal
  equivalent embodied kilocalories per day.
- The methodology provides a framework for comparing the true energy costs of
  renewable versus non-renewable energy systems, agricultural systems, and
  industrial processes.
- EMERGY analysis suggests that human ingenuity, design skill, and culture are
  themselves products of past embodied energy from fossil sources.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Agricultural Energy Accounting**: EMERGY analysis can evaluate the true energy
  costs of different cultivation systems, including [[stamets-stump-cultivation-mushroom-production-forestry]], by
  accounting for all energy inputs (substrate production, sterilisation,
  climate control, labour).
- **Comparing Biological vs. Industrial Systems**: The framework supports
  comparing biological soil fertility methods ([[mycorrhizal-inoculation-guide]],
  composting) against industrial inputs (synthetic fertilisers, pesticides)
  on a common energy basis.
- **Mycorrhizal Services Valuation**: EMERGY can quantify the energy value of
  mycorrhizal fungal services (phosphorus mining, water uptake, disease
  suppression) compared to the energy costs of providing these services
  through industrial means.
- **Compost Energy Analysis**: The methodology can assess the EMERGY balance of
  [[composting-systems]], where fungal and bacterial decomposition converts waste
  materials into valuable soil amendments with minimal external energy input.
- **Sustainable System Design**: EMERGY analysis provides [[bill-mollison]]
  with a rigorous tool for evaluating whether cultivation systems are truly
  sustainable or merely consuming stored energy from other systems.
- **Forest Product Valuation**: The framework can value the total energy content
  of forest products (including mushrooms harvested from managed forests) in
  relation to the solar energy captured by the forest ecosystem.
- **Substrate Production**: [[fao-spent-mushroom-substrate-composting-recycling]] production (straw, sawdust,
  grain) can be evaluated using EMERGY to determine the most energy-efficient
  substrate sources for different mushroom species.
