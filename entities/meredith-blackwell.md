## Description

Meredith Blackwell is a prominent American mycologist and professor specializing in fungal systematics, evolution, and biodiversity. She is best known for her work on fungal phylogeny, the evolutionary relationships of fungi and fungus-like organisms, and her contributions to understanding the [[kingdom-fungi|Kingdom Fungi]] as a monophyletic group distinct from plants and more closely related to animals.

## Classification

- **Profession**: Mycologist, systematic biologist
- **Affiliation**: Department of Biological Sciences, Louisiana State University, Baton Rouge, Louisiana, USA
- **Specialty**: Fungal systematics, phylogeny, evolution, insect-associated fungi
- **Era**: Late 20th century–present

## Key Facts

### Academic Position

- Based at the Department of Biological Sciences at Louisiana State University (380 Life Sciences Building, Baton Rouge, Louisiana 70803-1715)
- Active contributor to major mycological reference works including *Biodiversity of Fungi*

### Research Contributions

- Co-authored the foundational chapter "Fungi and Their Allies" in *Biodiversity of Fungi* (with [[joseph-spatafora|Joseph W. Spatafora]]), providing a comprehensive overview of fungal phylogeny
- Advanced understanding that "fungi" are polyphyletic — their morphologies are convergent, derived independently from several eukaryotic lineages
- Helped establish that [[kingdom-fungi|Kingdom Fungi]] is a monophyletic group exclusive of slime molds and oomycetes, supported by molecular evidence (Barr 1992; Bruns et al. 1993; Baldauf et al. 2000; Keeling et al. 2000)
- Contributed to the understanding that fungi are more closely related to animals than plants, possibly through a choanoflagellate-like ancestor

### Taxonomic Framework

- Documented that fungi and fungus-like organisms span three kingdoms comprising 11 phyla:
  - **Kingdom Fungi**: [[ascomycota|Chytridiomycota]], Zygomycota, [[ascomycota|Ascomycota]], [[basidiomycota|Basidiomycota]]
  - **Kingdom Straminipila**: Oomycota, Hyphochytriomycota, Labyrinthulomycota
  - **Kingdom Protista**: Plasmodiophoromycota, Dictyosteliomycota, Acrasiomycota, Myxomycota
- Advocated use of clade names rather than formal taxon designations for higher taxa, following Hibbett and Donoghue (1998)
- Worked with ribosomal RNA genes (rDNA) and other molecular characters for phylogenetic reconstruction

### Major Publications

- Co-author of chapters in *Biodiversity of Fungi* ([[gregory-mueller|Mueller]], [[gerald-bills|Bills]], and Foster, editors)
- Contributed to multi-volume *The Mycota* series (Springer-Verlag)

## Insect-Associated Fungi Research

A significant portion of Blackwell's research has focused on fungi associated with insects:

1. **Laboulbeniales:** Blackwell studied these obligate insect-associated ascomycetes, which grow externally on arthropod hosts. Their high host specificity makes them model organisms for studying coevolution.
2. **Beetle-fungus associations:** She investigated mutualistic relationships between bark beetles and their fungal symbionts, where fungi provide nutrients and beetles provide dispersal.
3. **Evolutionary implications:** These insect-fungus associations represent some of the most ancient and ecologically significant symbioses on Earth, providing insights into the coevolutionary processes that shape both groups.

## Phylogenetic Methodology

Blackwell's systematic work employed multiple approaches to reconstructing fungal evolutionary relationships:

1. **Ribosomal DNA sequencing:** The use of 18S and 28S rRNA gene sequences for resolving deep phylogenetic relationships among fungal phyla.
2. **Multi-gene analyses:** Combining data from multiple loci to produce more robust phylogenetic trees.
3. **Morphological integration:** Correlating molecular phylogenies with morphological characters to understand trait evolution.
4. **Clade-based classification:** Advocating for classification systems that reflect evolutionary history rather than convergent morphological features.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Blackwell's phylogenetic work provides the taxonomic foundation essential for all mycological studies, including cultivation. Her clarification of fungal relationships helps cultivators understand which organisms are true fungi (and thus may share cultivation-relevant biology) versus fungus-like organisms in other kingdoms (which require fundamentally different approaches). Her documentation of convergent evolution among fungi explains why morphological similarity does not always indicate close relationship — a critical insight for taxonomy-driven cultivation research.

### Specific Applications

1. **Species identification:** Accurate phylogenetic classification is essential for verifying that cultivation stock is correctly identified, preventing costly mistakes with mislabeled cultures.
2. **Strain relatedness:** Understanding evolutionary relationships helps predict which strains might share cultivation characteristics or produce similar bioactive compounds.
3. **Substrate specificity:** Phylogenetic patterns in substrate utilization help predict what substrates might work for newly cultivated species based on their relatives.
4. **Breeding potential:** Knowledge of evolutionary relationships guides efforts to cross strains or develop improved cultivars.
