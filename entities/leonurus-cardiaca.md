## Overview

**Leonurus cardiaca** L., commonly known as motherwort, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Lamiaceae.

Native to central Europe and Asia, it has been widely naturalized across temperate regions worldwide.

The species has a long history of use in European traditional medicine and Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) as a remedy for cardiovascular and gynecological conditions.

Its Latin name *cardiaca* reflects its traditional association with heart health, while *Leonurus* ("lion's tail") describes the distinctive leaf shape.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Angiosperms → Eudicots → Asterids → Order Lamiales → Family Lamiaceae → Subfamily Lamioideae → Genus *Leonurus* → Species *L. cardiaca*.

**Authority:** Carl Linnaeus, *Species Plantarum* (1753).

The plant grows 60–120 cm tall with an erect, square-stemmed habit typical of the mint family.

Stems are ribbed, branched in the upper portion, and covered with fine pubescence.

Leaves are opposite, palmately lobed with 3–7 pointed lobes, 3–10 cm long and 2–8 cm wide.

Leaf margins are coarsely toothed, and the upper surface is dark green with a lighter, sometimes purplish underside.

Flowers are arranged in whorled clusters (verticillasters) in the leaf axils of the upper stem.

Each flower is 8–12 mm long, two-lipped, pinkish to lilac, with a hairy calyx.

The fruit is a cluster of four small nutlets (mericarps), each approximately 2–3 mm long, brownish-black when mature.

Root system consists of a woody taproot with fibrous lateral roots.

## Distribution and Habitat

*L. cardiaca* is native to central and southern Europe, temperate Asia (Siberia, Mongolia, China), and has been introduced to North America.

In North America, it is naturalized throughout the northeastern and midwestern United States and parts of Canada.

The species thrives in disturbed habitats including roadsides, waste ground, abandoned fields, and riverbanks.

Preferred soils are moist, nitrogen-rich loams, though it tolerates a range of soil types including clay and sandy substrates.

It grows in full sun to partial shade and is hardy to USDA zones 3–8.

Flowering occurs from June through September, with seed set continuing into early autumn.

## Bioactive Compounds

The aerial parts of *L. cardiaca* contain a complex mixture of bioactive constituents.

Iridoid glycosides, particularly leonurine and leonuridine, are considered the primary pharmacologically active compounds.

Leonurine (4-guanidino-n-butyl syringate) has demonstrated vasodilatory, uterotonic, and antioxidant activities in preclinical studies.

Flavonoids include rutin, quercetin, hyperoside, and kaempferol derivatives, contributing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects.

Diterpenoids of the labdane and clerodane type, including leocardin and preleoheterin, have been isolated from the leaves.

Phenylpropanoids such as caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, and rosmarinic acid are present in moderate concentrations.

Essential oil (0.05–0.15% yield) contains β-caryophyllene, germacrene D, and humulene as major components.

Alkaloids, including stachydrine and leonuridine, occur at low concentrations and may contribute to cardiovascular effects.

## Traditional and Modern Pharmacology

In TCM, *L. cardiaca* (Yì Mǔ Cǎo, "beneficial mother herb") is indicated for postpartum bleeding, dysmenorrhea, and menstrual irregularity.

European herbal tradition uses motherwort as a cardiotonic for palpitations, mild heart failure, and anxiety-related tachycardia.

The German Commission E has approved *L. cardiaca* herb for nervous cardiac disorders and as a support for hyperthyroid-associated palpitations.

Animal studies demonstrate that leonurine reduces blood pressure through calcium channel blockade and nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation.

Uterotonic effects have been confirmed in isolated rat uterine preparations, supporting traditional use for menstrual and postpartum conditions.

Antioxidant activity of the flavonoid fraction is comparable to that of ascorbic acid in standard DPPH radical scavenging assays.

Antiplatelet aggregation activity has been observed in vitro, though clinical relevance remains undetermined.

Human clinical data are limited to small open-label studies; large randomized controlled trials are lacking.

## Cultivation

*L. cardiaca* is easily propagated from seed (stratification at 4°C for 2–4 weeks improves germination) or by division of established root clumps.

Seed sowing is done in spring; germination typically occurs within 14–21 days at 20°C.

Plants should be spaced 30–45 cm apart in rows 60 cm wide.

Aerial parts are harvested during full flowering (June–August) when bioactive compound concentrations peak.

Post-harvest drying should be rapid, in a well-ventilated area at temperatures below 40°C to preserve iridoid glycosides.

Yield of dried herb ranges from 2–4 tonnes per hectare under commercial cultivation conditions.

The plant can become invasive in favorable conditions; containment strategies may be necessary in agricultural settings.
