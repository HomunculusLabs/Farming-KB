## Amanitine

## Description

Amanitine is the poisonous alkaloid found in mushrooms of the *Amanita* genus, first named by Dr. Letellier in 19th-century France. It is one of the most dangerous natural toxins known to mycology, responsible for the majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide. The alkaloid is tasteless, odorless, and gives no indication of its presence in the fungus, making it particularly treacherous to unsuspecting foragers.

## Classification

- **Type:** Alkaloid (fungal toxin)
- **Source organisms:** *Amanita* genus, especially *A. phalloides*, *A. verna*, and *A. virosa*
- **Naming:** Dr. Letellier coined the term "Amanitine"; Schmiedeberg and Koppe used "Muscarine" for a related compound from *A. muscaria*; Dr. Boudier proposed "Bulbosine" for the alkaloid from *A. bulbosa*
- **Modern understanding:** Amanitine refers primarily to the amatoxin class of cyclic peptides ([[alpha-amanitin]], beta-amanitin, etc.)

## Key Facts

- **Onset of symptoms:** 8–15 hours after ingestion in fatal cases; sooner with smaller doses
- **Symptoms:** Dizziness, nausea, purging, fainting, delirium, and death — closely resembling Asiatic cholera
- **Lethality:** A single mushroom the size of a silver dollar mixed into a wholesome dish can make five to six persons dangerously sick
- **Absorption mechanism:** The alkaloid enters the system through the intestinal villi by absorption; even after the fungus is expelled, the poison may remain in the body
- **Transdermal absorption:** Can be absorbed through the skin — holding an *Amanita* in the closed hand can produce symptoms of poisoning, even convulsions
- **Inhalation risk:** Vapor or scent alone can cause nausea, vomiting, and facial discoloration (livid whites of eyes, leaden face)
- **No known antidote (historical):** Physicians in the 19th century confessed utter inability to find a reliable antidote
- **Atropine as counteragent:** Subcutaneous injections of atropine (active principle of *[[atropa-belladonna]]*) were proposed as the most promising treatment by Schmiedeberg and Koppe

## Historical Poisoning Cases

1. **Sautry, near Corbeil, France:** Two persons ate *[[amanita-bulbosa]]*; despite a physician using coffee, tea, antimony, ether, egg whites, laudanum, milk, leeches, ice, and emollients, both died within three days
2. **October 2, 1876 (USA):** Four persons poisoned; three died between Thursday and Friday morning. One survivor had put vinegar on her toadstool before eating, which may have neutralized the alkaloid
3. **Unspecified case:** Two children died in 36 hours; the father suffered severe delirium, jumping from a second-story window

## Remedial Measures (Historical)

- **Vinegar:** Applied before ingestion may neutralize the alkaloid; administered after ingestion worsens symptoms by aiding absorption
- **Emetics and cathartics:** Considered worse than useless by most mycologists, as they may hasten absorption
- **Sweet oil (olive oil):** Helpful — one poisoned dog vomited the fungus mixed with whitish mucus after sweet oil and recovered on a milk diet
- **Warm water enemata:** Relieve tenesmus and assist natural expulsion
- **Atropine/belladonna:** Subcutaneous injection is the most promising historical counteragent; the throbbing heart of a frog silenced by muscarine is restored by a drop of atropine

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Amanitine represents the primary safety concern in [[emcdda-wild-mushroom-foraging-misidentification-hazards]]. Understanding its properties is essential for:
- **Forager education:** Recognizing *Amanita* species and avoiding the entire genus
- **Medical preparedness:** Rapid identification of [[amanitine-poisoning-and-absorption-mechanisms]] based on delayed onset (8+ hours)
- **Taxonomic knowledge:** The *Amanita* genus has universal distinguishing marks (volva, white gills, warts on pileus) that even children can learn to recognize
- **Toxicological research:** The atropine–muscarine antagonism discovered through amanitine research became foundational in pharmacology
