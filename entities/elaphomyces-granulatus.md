# Elaphomyces granulatus

**Elaphomyces granulatus** is a hypogeous (underground) ectomycorrhizal
ascomycete fungus in the family Elaphomycetaceae, commonly known as the
deer truffle. It forms subterranean [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]] and relies entirely
on animal vectors for [[adaptive-anemotropism-spore-dispersal-optimization-mushroom-evolution]], representing a unique ecological
strategy among [[query-what-are-ectomycorrhizal-fungi-and-which-plants-do-they-associate-with]].

## Taxonomy and Distribution

Elaphomyces granulatus produces underground fruiting bodies (ascocarps)
resembling small, rough-skinned potatoes. It is widely distributed
across temperate forests of the Northern Hemisphere, forming
[[ectomycorrhizal-associations]] with various tree species, particularly
conifers and oaks. The rough, warty exterior darkens with maturity from
light brown to nearly black, while the interior spore mass becomes
powdery and dark brown. Unlike epigeous ectomycorrhizal species, E.
granulatus produces fruiting bodies below ground while maintaining an
ectomycorrhizal relationship with host tree roots.

## Ectomycorrhizal Association

The mycelium of E. granulatus forms a sheath around fine root tips,
extending the effective absorbing surface area of the root system and
accessing mineral nutrients and water from soil volumes beyond the reach
of uncolonized roots. The subterranean habit requires different
investment in spore dispersal compared to epigeous species that release
spores into air currents.

## Nutritional Composition

Gronwall and Pehrson (1984) analyzed the protein and mineral content of
E. granulatus sporocarps as part of a study of fungal food consumed by
red squirrels (Sciurus vulgaris). The species had relatively low
nutritional value compared to other fungal foods:

- **Protein**: 17% (vs. [[lactarius-deliciosus]] and [[boletus-edulis-entity]] at 30%)
- **Phosphorus**: 0.21% (the lowest of all species tested; L. deliciosus
  0.60%)
- **Potassium**: 0.6% (extremely low vs. [[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]] at 7.9%)
- **Sodium**: 0.10% (the highest of all species tested)

Fogel and Trappe (1978) showed that fungi generally consist of 70-94%
water and contain fewer calories per unit weight than nuts, eggs, and
meat, making them relatively low-energy food items for mycophagous
mammals.

## Nitrogen Immobilization

More than 80% of the nitrogen in E. granulatus sporocarps is in
complex, indigestible forms, effectively immobilizing nitrogen within
the fungal biomass and the [[dighton-ericoid-mycorrhizal-nutrient-acquisition]] cycle rather than
losing it to the broader soil food web. This nitrogen conservation
strategy limits the nutritional return to mycophagists despite their
consumption of the fruiting bodies.

## Animal Dispersal

Cork and Kenagy (1989b) demonstrated that spores of E. granulatus pass
through the gut of ground squirrels and deer mice, retain viability in
feces, and colonize new seedling plants in nutritionally favorable
environments. Trappe and Maser (1976) showed similar spore dispersal by
the Oregon vole and chickaree, and Kotter and Farentinos (1984a,b)
demonstrated viable spore dispersal by the tassel-eared squirrel.

Hypogeous fungi have evolved complete dependency on small mammals for
spore dispersal. Animals locate underground fruiting bodies by scent,
excavate and consume them, and deposit viable spores in feces. This
assists colonization during initial phases of plant succession (Trappe,
1988). Despite relatively low [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]], E. granulatus is an
important food resource for mycophagous mammals including squirrels,
chipmunks, deer mice, voles, and occasionally wild pigs, providing a
reliable food source when above-ground fungi are scarce.
