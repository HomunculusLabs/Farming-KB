## Overview

*Trametes cinnabarina* (Jacq.) Fr. is a striking bracket fungus in the family Polyporaceae, widely recognized by its vivid cinnabar-red to orange pore surface. Unlike most *Trametes* species which are white-pored, its colorful underside makes it one of the most easily identified polypores in the field.

It is a white-rot saprotroph found on dead hardwoods across temperate and subtropical regions worldwide. The species has attracted interest for its production of cinnabarinic acid, a red benzoxazinone pigment with documented antimicrobial and antioxidant properties.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Basidiomycota → Agaricomycetes → Polyporales → Polyporaceae → *Trametes* → *T. cinnabarina*.

**Authority:** Described as *Boletus cinnabarinus* by Jacquin in 1774, transferred to *Trametes* by Fries in 1821. The species has also been placed in *Pycnoporus* by some authors, though molecular phylogenetics supports its placement in *Trametes* sensu stricto.

**Basidiocarp:** Semi-circular to fan-shaped brackets, 2–8 cm across and 1–3 cm thick. The upper surface is zonate with concentric furrows, ranging from cream-tan to reddish-brown, often with an algae-green tinge when aged. The margin is acute to rounded.

**Pore surface:** Bright cinnabar-red to orange-red, a diagnostic feature that distinguishes it from all other temperate *Trametes* species. Pores are circular, 3–5 per mm, with slightly thick dissepiments.

**Context:** Tough and corky, dual in structure. Upper layer is pale cream; the red pigment is concentrated in the tube layer. Drying intensifies the coloration.

**Spores:** Cylindrical to allantoid, hyaline, smooth, 5–7 × 1.5–2.5 µm. Spore print white.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Trametes cinnabarina* has a broad global distribution across temperate hardwood forests in Europe, Asia, North America, and parts of Africa and South America. It fruits year-round in mild climates, predominantly from late summer through autumn in temperate zones.

The species is exclusively found on dead hardwood, especially angiosperm branches and small-diameter fallen wood. Preferred substrates include oak (*Quercus*), beech (*Fagus*), birch (*Betula*), hazel (*Corylus*), and various fruit trees. It rarely occurs on conifers.

It typically colonizes shaded, moist microhabitats on recently fallen branches still attached to standing trees or on the undersides of logs close to the ground. Multiple fruiting bodies often form overlapping clusters on the same substrate.

## Edibility and Uses

*Trametes cinnabarina* is not considered edible due to its tough, corky texture and bitter taste. No toxic compounds have been identified, but the species has no culinary value.

The primary practical application is as a **source of natural red dye**. The cinnabarinic acid pigment can be extracted in hot water or ethanol solutions to produce vibrant orange-to-red dyes for textiles and paper. Traditional dyers in Europe and Asia have used this species, though synthetic dyes have largely replaced natural fungal pigments.
