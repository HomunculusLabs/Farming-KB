## Overview

**Lespedeza capitata** is a perennial herbaceous legume in the family Fabaceae, commonly known as roundhead lespedeza, bush clover, or roundhead bushclover.

It is native to eastern and central North America, ranging from Quebec and Ontario south to Georgia and Texas.

The species is valued in native plantings, [[comparison-prairie-restoration-vs-silvopasture]], and [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]] improvement for its hardiness, nitrogen-fixing capability, and high [[cannabis-controlled-pollination-seed-production]].

Roundhead lespedeza provides excellent forage for deer, rabbits, and upland game birds, and serves as a host plant for several butterfly species.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Lespedeza capitata** Michx. belongs to the family Fabaceae, subfamily Faboideae, tribe Desmodieae.

The genus name honors Vicente Manuel de Céspedes, a Spanish governor of East Florida. The specific epithet *capitata* refers to the dense, head-like inflorescences.

It is a perennial forb growing 60–150 cm tall from a woody base. Stems are erect to ascending, usually unbranched above the lower portions, and covered with spreading hairs.

Leaves are trifoliolate with leaflets 2–5 cm long. Leaflets are elliptic to obovate, thinly pubescent on both surfaces, and tipped with a short mucro.

Flowers are produced in dense, rounded terminal clusters 1–2 cm in diameter. Individual flowers are small, papilionaceous, creamy white to pale pink with purple-tipped keels.

The calyx is deeply lobed and becomes hardened at maturity. Flowering occurs from July through September.

Fruit is a small, one-seeded legume (loment) 3–4 mm long, flattened, and reticulated. The segments separate at maturity, aiding in [[clay-pellet-seed-dispersal]].

## Distribution and Habitat

Roundhead lespedeza occurs across much of eastern and central North America.

Its native range extends from southern Ontario and Quebec west to Minnesota and eastern Nebraska, and south to Georgia, Louisiana, and eastern Texas.

It is most abundant in the tallgrass prairie region and the Ozark Plateau, where it is a common component of native grassland communities.

The species inhabits prairies, open woodlands, glades, roadsides, old fields, and railroad rights-of-way.

It tolerates a wide range of soil conditions, from sandy loams to clay soils, and is moderately drought-tolerant once established.

Optimal growth occurs in full sun on well-drained soils with a pH of 5.5 to 7.0. It is less common on very acidic or waterlogged sites.

## Ecology and Wildlife Value

**Lespedeza capitata** is a nitrogen-fixing legume that forms root nodules with Bradyrhizobium bacteria, contributing significant nitrogen to prairie soils.

Its seeds are a high-protein food source for quail, wild turkey, prairie chickens, and songbirds during fall and winter.

White-tailed deer and rabbits browse the foliage, particularly in early growth stages before stems become woody.

The plant serves as a larval host for several butterfly species, including the eastern tailed-blue (Cupido comyntas) and the gray hairstreak (Strymon melinus).

In prairie ecosystems, roundhead lespedeza contributes to plant diversity and provides structural habitat within mixed grass-forb communities.

Its late-season flowering makes it an important nectar source for [[query-how-to-attract-native-bees-and-beneficial-insects]] other pollinators when many other prairie species have finished blooming.

## Cultivation and Uses

Roundhead lespedeza is widely used in native seed mixes for prairie restoration and habitat plantings throughout the Midwest and Great Plains.

Seed should be planted at a depth of 0.6–1.3 cm in early spring or as a dormant seeding in late fall. A seeding rate of 3–5 kg/ha pure live seed is recommended for pure stands.

Stratification at 2–5°C for 30 days improves germination, which typically ranges from 40–70% for untreated seed.

The species establishes slowly but is long-lived, persisting for decades once mature. It requires minimal management in established plantings.

In pasture systems, it provides moderate forage quality with crude protein content of 12–16% in mid-summer, though palatability declines as stems mature.

Native Americans traditionally used the plant for medicinal purposes. The Cherokee used an infusion of leaves for diarrhea, and the Delaware applied poultices of bruised leaves to wounds.

Some contemporary herbal traditions use the aerial parts for their purported anti-inflammatory and diuretic properties, though clinical evidence is limited.
