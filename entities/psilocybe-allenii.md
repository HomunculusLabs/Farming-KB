# Psilocybe allenii

**Psilocybe allenii** is a psilocybin-containing mushroom in the family Hymenogastraceae, found along the Pacific coast of North America. It was first described scientifically in 2012 by mycologists Jan Borovička, Alan Rockefeller, and Peter G. Werner, named in honor of mycologist John W. Allen, who collected the first specimens recognized as a distinct species.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Hymenogastraceae
- **Genus:** Psilocybe
- **Species:** P. allenii

The species was long confused with [[psilocybe-cyanescens]] due to similar appearance and habitat. Molecular phylogenetic analysis of the ITS rDNA region confirmed it as a distinct species. It belongs to Psilocybe section Cyanescens, a clade of primarily wood-inhabiting, bluing species that includes P. cyanescens, [[psilocybe-azurescens]], and [[psilocybe-subaeruginosa]].

## Description

### Cap (Pileus)
- 1.5 to 5 cm in diameter
- Convex to broadly convex, becoming plane with age
- Hygrophanous, caramel-brown to ochraceous when moist, drying to pale buff or nearly white
- Surface smooth, often with a slight umbo
- Margin often decorated with remnants of the partial veil in young specimens
- Margin not typically wavy or undulating (distinguishing it from [[psilocybe-cyanescens]])

### Gills
- Adnate to adnexed attachment
- Grayish at first, becoming dark purplish-brown to nearly black with spore maturity
- Subdistant to close, with 2 to 3 tiers of lamellulae (short gills)
- Edges even, sometimes slightly fimbriate

### Stipe (Stem)
- 4 to 10 cm long, 2 to 5 mm thick
- Equal to slightly enlarged at the base
- Whitish to pale brown, bruising bluish where damaged
- Surface covered with fine white to silvery fibrils, especially in the upper portion
- Hollow, with cottony white pith filling in younger specimens
- Partial veil evanescent, sometimes leaving a fibrillose annular zone

### Flesh
- Thin, whitish to pale brown
- Bruising and staining blue to bluish-black when cut or handled
- Odor and taste farinaceous (mealy or flour-like)

### Spores
- Basidiospores 11 to 14 by 7 to 9 µm
- Rhomboid to subrhomboid in face view, ellipsoid in side view
- Thick-walled with a distinct germ pore
- Spore print dark purplish-brown to nearly black

### Microscopic Features
- Cheilocystidia ventricose to lageniform, sometimes with a slight constriction at the neck
- Pleurocystidia absent or very rare
- Pileipellis a cutis of filamentous hyphae, with occasional erect terminal cells
- Clamp connections present at septa of hyphae

## Distribution and Habitat

psilocybe allenii is found along the Pacific coast of North America, from British Columbia, Canada, south through Washington and Oregon to northern California. The core of its known distribution is the Puget Sound region of Washington and the Willamette Valley of Oregon.

It fruits in late autumn through winter, typically from October to January, with peak fruiting in November and December. Cool temperatures combined with abundant moisture from Pacific storms trigger fruiting.

The species is primarily associated with wood chips and mulched garden beds in urban and suburban areas, particularly those containing alder (Alnus) wood chips. It grows gregariously to cespitose, often in large, dense clusters. The species is strongly associated with human-landscaped environments and is rarely found in undisturbed natural habitats. This ecology mirrors that of [[psilocybe-cyanescens]], with which it frequently co-occurs.

## Chemistry

psilocybe allenii contains the psychoactive compounds [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]], along with baeocystin and norbaeocystin as minor alkaloids. Analytical studies using HPLC and GC-MS show total tryptamine content typically ranging from 0.6% to 1.2% of dried weight, placing it in the moderate range among psilocybin mushrooms.

The ratio of [[psilocybin]] to [[psilocin]] varies between collections. In freshly dried specimens, [[psilocybin]] typically predominates, while prolonged storage or inadequate drying can result in progressive dephosphorylation of [[psilocybin]] to [[psilocin]]. Environmental factors including substrate composition, temperature during fruiting, and humidity influence alkaloid profiles, consistent with patterns observed across the genus.

## Similar Species

psilocybe allenii is most commonly confused with:

- **[[psilocybe-cyanescens]]** — Similar habitat and appearance; P. cyanescens typically has a more pronounced wavy (undulate) cap margin and slightly larger spores (12 to 15 µm). P. cyanescens also tends to have a stronger blueing reaction.
- **[[psilocybe-azurescens]]** — Larger and more robust with a more conic cap when young; restricted to coastal dune grass habitats, not urban wood chips
- **[[psilocybe-subaeruginosa]]** — Found in Australia and New Zealand; distinct geographic range but morphologically similar
- **[[gymnopilus-spectabilis]]** — Much larger with orange-brown colors and bitter taste; contains different active compounds

## Toxicity and Safety

psilocybe allenii is not physically toxic, and fatal overdoses from psilocybin mushrooms alone have not been documented. However, psychological risks include anxiety, panic reactions, and transient psychotic episodes, particularly at high doses or in susceptible individuals. The species should be distinguished from potentially lethal look-alikes such as species of Galerina, which can resemble small brown mushrooms and contain deadly amatoxins.

## Cultivation

psilocybe allenii can be cultivated on sterilized wood chip substrates supplemented with grain spawn. It requires cooler fruiting temperatures (10 to 16°C), similar to [[psilocybe-cyanescens]]. Outdoor cultivation on alder chip beds has been successfully demonstrated, with colonization taking several months before fruiting is triggered by autumn temperature drops and increased moisture.

Indoor cultivation is more challenging due to the cool temperature requirements and the species' preference for wood-based substrates over grain or compost. Colonized wood chip spawn can be used to establish outdoor beds that may fruit for several years.

## Historical Context

The recognition of psilocybe allenii as a distinct species represents an example of cryptic speciation within the genus Psilocybe that was only resolved through molecular techniques. Before 2012, the diversity of wood-inhabiting psilocybin mushrooms in the Pacific Northwest was thought to be lower, with most collections being attributed to [[psilocybe-cyanescens]]. The description of P. allenii, along with other recently described species in the section, has refined our understanding of psilocybin mushroom biodiversity in western North America.

## Life Cycle and Ecology

The life cycle of psilocybe allenii follows the typical pattern for Psilocybe species. Basidiospores germinate on suitable substrate, forming a monokaryotic mycelium that must fuse with a compatible mate to form a dikaryotic mycelium capable of fruiting. The dikaryotic mycelium colonizes wood chips extensively, forming a dense white network of hyphae.

Fruiting is triggered by cool temperatures (below 15°C) combined with high humidity and shortening day length in autumn. Primordia form at the substrate surface and develop into mature basidiocarps (mushrooms) over a period of 5 to 10 days. Each mushroom produces millions of basidiospores, which are dispersed by wind and, to a lesser extent, by animals moving through the substrate.

The species plays a role in decomposing hardwood in urban and suburban environments, contributing to carbon and nutrient cycling in landscaped areas. Its mycelium competes with other wood-decomposing fungi for access to the substrate, and its success in urban environments may partly reflect reduced competition compared to natural forest ecosystems.

## Ethnobotanical Notes

Before its formal description in 2012, collections of psilocybe allenii were typically recorded under the name [[psilocybe-cyanescens]]. The species has been consumed for its psychoactive properties by recreational foragers in the Pacific Northwest for decades prior to scientific recognition. John W. Allen, the species' namesake, was instrumental in recognizing its distinct characteristics through extensive field work and documentation in the Seattle area during the 1970s and 1980s.

The psychoactive effects of psilocybe allenii are typical of psilocybin-containing mushrooms, including visual and auditory hallucinations, altered perception of time, enhanced introspection, and euphoria. The intensity of effects is dose-dependent and varies between collections due to natural variation in alkaloid content.

## Legal Status

As a psilocybin-containing species, psilocybe allenii is illegal to possess, cultivate, or distribute in most jurisdictions. In the United States, it is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under the Controlled Substances Act. Following the 2020 decriminalization of psilocybin mushrooms in Oregon and subsequent measures in Washington State and other jurisdictions, enforcement priorities have shifted in some areas, though the species remains federally controlled.

In Canada, psilocybin and its derivatives are controlled under the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act. Internationally, [[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]] are listed as Schedule I substances under the UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances.
