## Overview

Ranunculus arvensis L., the corn buttercup or field buttercup, is an annual herb in the family Ranunculaceae. It is native to Europe and western Asia but has spread widely as an agricultural weed across [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] worldwide.

The species is instantly recognizable by its spiny achenes, which form a distinctive prickly fruiting head unlike any other European buttercup. It has been associated with cultivated land since the Neolithic period.

All plant tissues contain ranunculin, a glycoside that converts to the vesicant protoanemonin when cells are damaged. This chemical defense renders the plant toxic to livestock and causes contact dermatitis in humans.

Despite its toxicity, R. arvensis has declined dramatically across western Europe due to modern herbicide use. It is now considered a rare or threatened arable weed in several countries.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Plantae → Class Magnoliopsida → Order Ranunculales → Family Ranunculaceae → Genus *Ranunculus* → Species *R. arvensis* L.

Linnaeus described the species in 1753 in *Species Plantarum*. The epithet *arvensis* means "of the fields," reflecting its agricultural habitat.

The plant reaches 15–50 cm in height with an erect, sparingly branched stem covered in appressed hairs. The root system consists of a slender taproot with few lateral branches.

Basal leaves are palmately divided into three to five primary segments, each further subdivided into narrow, linear lobes. They are borne on long petioles and form a rosette early in [[query-how-to-protect-plants-from-frost-and-extend-the-growing-season]].

Cauline leaves are sessile and progressively reduced upward, with deeply divided narrow segments. The uppermost leaves are filiform, barely 1 mm wide.

Flowers are 8–15 mm in diameter with five glossy, pale to golden-yellow petals. The five sepals are reflexed against the pedicel at anthesis.

Each petal bears a basal nectary covered by a small scale. Petals are obovate, 5–8 mm long, and slightly overlapping at the margins.

The fruiting receptacle forms a globose head 6–10 mm across. Each achene is 3–4 mm, flattened, with a broad marginal wing bearing sharp, hooked spines.

The spiny achenes are diagnostic for the species and distinguish it from all other European Ranunculus. They resemble tiny burs and are readily dispersed by attachment to fur and clothing.

Chromosome number is 2n = 32, placing it in the [[ranunculus-auricomus]] aggregate's broader cytological context. The species shows little morphological [[psilocybin-psilocin-variation-across-flushes]] its wide European range.

## Distribution and Habitat

Ranunculus arvensis is native across Europe from Portugal and the British Isles eastward to the Urals and the Caucasus. Its range extends into western Siberia, northern Iran, and the mountains of Central Asia.

It has been introduced to [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]], where it occurs sporadically in the northeastern United States and southeastern Canada. Small populations are also recorded in southeastern Australia and New Zealand.

The species is a strict calcicole, favoring neutral to alkaline clay and loam soils. It occurs almost exclusively in arable fields, particularly winter cereals and root crops.

Unlike meadow buttercups (R. acris, R. bulbosus), R. arvensis avoids permanent grassland. It requires periodic soil disturbance for germination and establishment.

Archaeobotanical records show its achenes in Neolithic grain deposits from across Europe. It is considered an obligate segetal weed — a species that depends entirely on cultivated habitats.

Populations have declined by more than 80% in the United Kingdom since the 1950s. Similar declines are reported from Germany, France, and Scandinavia due to herbicide intensification and seed cleaning.

In some regions, the species persists only in field margins and conservation set-aside plots. These marginal populations serve as reservoirs for the soil seed bank.

## Toxicology

All green tissues contain ranunculin at concentrations of 0.2–1.5% of fresh weight. The glycoside is stored intact in vacuoles and is harmless until cell rupture occurs.

Enzymatic hydrolysis by β-glucosidase converts ranunculin to protoanemonin within seconds of tissue damage. Protoanemonin is a volatile, unsaturated lactone with strong irritant and vesicant properties.

Livestock poisoning presents as oral blistering, profuse salivation, colic, and bloody diarrhea. Cattle and horses are most susceptible, though animals usually avoid the fresh plant due to its acrid taste.

Dried plant material in hay is substantially less toxic because protoanemonin polymerizes to anemonin upon desiccation. However, contamination of green forage remains a risk during spring.

Human contact dermatitis has been documented among agricultural workers handling the plant. The rash typically presents as vesicular erythema lasting 24–48 hours.

Traditional folk medicine employed the fresh plant as a vesicant counterirritant for rheumatic joints. This dangerous practice has largely been abandoned due to the risk of severe blistering.

## Ecology and Conservation

Ranunculus arvensis germinates in autumn or early spring as a winter or spring annual. Flowering occurs from May through July, with seed set completed by August.

Individual plants produce 50–200 achenes per season. The spiny fruits are adapted for epizoochorous dispersal, attaching to mammals and birds for transport between fields.

Seed longevity in the soil seed bank is estimated at 10–30 years, enabling persistence despite intermittent control. This long-lived seed bank has allowed populations to survive periods of intensive herbicide use in some locations.

The flowers attract small generalist pollinators, particularly hoverflies (Syrphidae) and [[comparison-honey-bees-vs-native-solitary-bees]] (Halictidae). However, the species is not a major nectar resource compared to other field-margin flora.
