## Overview

Brachiaria brizantha is a tropical perennial bunchgrass in the family Poaceae, native to sub-Saharan Africa and widely naturalized across [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-tropical-regions]] of South America, Southeast Asia, and northern Australia.

Known as **signal grass** or **palisade grass**, it is one of the most important forage grasses in the tropics, cultivated on an estimated 100+ million hectares worldwide, primarily in Brazilian cattle ranching systems.

The species is valued for its high dry matter productivity, good [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]], [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]], and compatibility with legume intercropping in silvopastoral and integrated crop-livestock systems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Taxonomy:** Kingdom Plantae → Poaceae → subfamily Panicoideae → tribe Paniceae → genus *Brachiaria* (syn. *Urochloa*).

*Brachiaria brizantha* (A.Rich.) Stapf was described by Otto Stapf in 1919, based on an earlier description by Achille Richard. The genus name honors the Italian botanist Vincenzo Brachiaria.

Many authorities now place the species in *Urochloa* as *Urochloa brizantha* (A.Rich.) R.D.Webster, following molecular phylogenetic evidence. Both names are in common use in the agronomic literature.

The plant forms robust tussocks 60–150 cm tall, with erect to semi-erect culms that are unbranched. Stems are smooth, with swollen nodes that are a characteristic diagnostic feature.

Leaf blades are lanceolate to linear-lanceolate, 15–40 cm long and 1–2 cm wide, with a prominent midrib. The margin is scabrous and the base is clasping (amplexicaul) around the stem.

The inflorescence is a panicle composed of 2–6 racemes arranged on a central axis, 10–25 cm long. Spikelets are arranged in two rows along each raceme, elliptic, 4–6 mm long, and hairy.

The root system is deep and extensive, reaching 1–2 m in well-drained soils. A dense mat of surface roots provides excellent soil stabilization, making the species effective [[mollison-designers-earthworks-for-erosion-control]].

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is native to East and Central Africa, with a natural range spanning Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Ethiopia, and the Democratic Republic of Congo. It was introduced to tropical South America in the mid-20th century.

In Brazil, *B. brizantha* now covers an estimated 50–70 million hectares, making it the single most widely planted forage species in the country. It is also extensively cultivated in Colombia, Venezuela, and [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-caribbean-central-america]].

The species is adapted to humid and subhumid [[fukuoka-natural-farming-tropical-climates]] with annual rainfall of 800–2,500 mm. It tolerates seasonally dry conditions (3–5 month dry season) but performs poorly in arid zones.

It grows on a wide range of soils, from acidic oxisols and ultisols to sandy loams. The species has moderate aluminum tolerance, an important trait for cultivation on the highly weathered, acidic soils common in tropical savannas.

Optimal growth occurs at temperatures of 25–35°C. Growth ceases below 15°C, and the species is killed by frost, limiting its use to tropical and subtropical zones.

The species shows moderate shade tolerance compared to other tropical forage grasses, which makes it suitable for silvopastoral systems where trees and pasture coexist. Shade reduces dry matter yield but improves nutritional quality through increased leaf-to-stem ratio.

Waterlogging tolerance is low — the species performs poorly on waterlogged or poorly drained soils. Root development is inhibited by prolonged saturation, making soil drainage a critical site selection criterion.

## Cultivation and Uses

Establishment is primarily from seed, planted at rates of 5–10 kg/ha in prepared seedbeds. Seed dormancy is common and is broken by acid scarification or dry heat treatment before planting.

The species establishes relatively slowly, requiring 3–4 months to achieve full ground cover. A nurse crop or companion legume is often used during establishment to suppress weeds and provide early forage.

As a forage grass, signal grass produces 8–20 tonnes of dry matter per hectare per year under adequate rainfall and fertility. Crude protein content ranges from 7–12%, with higher values in young regrowth.

It is primarily used for grazing by beef and dairy cattle. [[comparison-rotational-grazing-vs-continuous-grazing]] systems are recommended, with a rest period of 30–45 days between grazing events to allow adequate regrowth and maintain stand longevity.

The species integrates well with forage legumes such as *Stylosanthes* spp., *Arachis pintoi*, and *Desmodium* spp. in mixed pastures. Legume intercropping improves nitrogen nutrition, forage quality, and animal performance.

Signal grass is also used for cut-and-carry systems, hay production, and as a cover crop in integrated crop-livestock-forestry (ICLF) systems. Its deep root system and drought tolerance make it valuable for conserving soil moisture in agroforestry arrangements.

Phosphorus fertilization is critical for optimal production on the low-phosphorus soils typical of tropical savannas. The species responds strongly to phosphorus application, with yield increases of 50–100% common.

The species has also been identified as having [[biological-nitrification-inhibition]] (BNI) capacity, meaning it releases compounds from its roots that suppress soil nitrification. This reduces nitrogen losses through leaching and denitrification, improving nitrogen use efficiency in mixed pastures.

In integrated crop-livestock systems, signal grass is used as a cover crop between grain crop cycles. The pasture phase restores soil structure [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]], while the subsequent crop benefits from improved soil health and residual fertilizer nutrients.

## Ecological Role

In its native African range, *B. brizantha* is a component of tropical savanna and woodland grass communities. It provides forage for native herbivores and contributes to the fire regime through its combustible dry matter production.

In South America, the species has become naturalized and in some areas invasive. It can colonize disturbed sites, forest edges, and riparian areas, potentially displacing native vegetation in sensitive ecosystems.

The dense root mat and rapid ground cover make signal grass effective [[mollison-designers-vetiver-grass-for-erosion-control]] on slopes, road cuts, and degraded lands. It is commonly included in revegetation seed mixes for tropical land restoration projects.

Brachiaria pastures can sequester significant [[soil-organic-carbon-fractions-turnover-dynamics]], particularly under no-till management. Research in Brazil has demonstrated carbon accumulation rates of 0.5–1.0 tonnes C/ha/year in well-managed Brachiaria-dominated pastures.

The BNI trait also has environmental benefits beyond nitrogen efficiency. By reducing [[nitrous-oxide]] (N₂O) emissions from soil — a potent greenhouse gas — Brachiaria pastures have a lower greenhouse gas footprint compared to other tropical forage systems.
