## Overview

Brachiaria brizantha is a tropical perennial bunchgrass in the family Poaceae, native to sub-Saharan Africa and widely naturalized across [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]], [[mollison-designers-earthworks-for-erosion-control]].

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is native to East and Central Africa, with a natural range spanning Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Ethiopia, and the Democratic Republic of Congo. It was introduced to tropical South America in the mid-20th century.

In Brazil, *B. brizantha* now covers an estimated 50–70 million hectares, making it the single most widely planted forage species in the country. It is also extensively cultivated in Colombia, Venezuela, and [[fukuoka-natural-farming-tropical-climates]] with annual rainfall of 800–2,500 mm. It tolerates seasonally dry conditions (3–5 month dry season) but performs poorly in arid zones.

It grows on a wide range of soils, from acidic oxisols and ultisols to sandy loams. The species has moderate aluminum tolerance, an important trait for cultivation on the highly weathered, acidic soils common in tropical savannas.

Optimal growth occurs at temperatures of 25–35°C. Growth ceases below 15°C, and the species is killed by frost, limiting its use to tropical and subtropical zones.

The species shows moderate shade tolerance compared to other tropical forage grasses, which makes it suitable for silvopastoral systems where trees and pasture coexist. Shade reduces dry matter yield but improves nutritional quality through increased leaf-to-stem ratio.

Waterlogging tolerance is low — the species performs poorly on waterlogged or poorly drained soils. Root development is inhibited by prolonged saturation, making soil drainage a critical site selection criterion.

## Cultivation and Uses

Establishment is primarily from seed, planted at rates of 5–10 kg/ha in prepared seedbeds. Seed dormancy is common and is broken by acid scarification or dry heat treatment before planting.

The species establishes relatively slowly, requiring 3–4 months to achieve full ground cover. A nurse crop or companion legume is often used during establishment to suppress weeds and provide early forage.

As a forage grass, signal grass produces 8–20 tonnes of dry matter per hectare per year under adequate rainfall and fertility. Crude protein content ranges from 7–12%, with higher values in young regrowth.

In integrated crop-livestock systems, signal grass is used as a cover crop between grain crop cycles. The pasture phase restores soil structure [[mollison-designers-vetiver-grass-for-erosion-control]] on slopes, road cuts, and degraded lands. It is commonly included in revegetation seed mixes for tropical land restoration projects.
