# Largemouth Bass (Micropterus salmoides)

## Description

The largemouth bass is a freshwater predatory fish native to North America and one of the most important species in recreational and permaculture pond management. As a top-level predator in pond ecosystems, it plays a critical role in controlling prey fish populations, preventing overpopulation and stunting, and maintaining healthy size distributions in polyculture systems.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Animalia
- **Phylum**: Chordata
- **Class**: Actinopterygii
- **Order**: Perciformes
- **Family**: Centrarchidae (sunfishes)
- **Genus**: Micropterus
- **Species**: M. salmoides

## Key Facts

- Native to eastern North America, now introduced worldwide for sport fishing
- Apex predator in freshwater pond systems -- consumes smaller fish, frogs, crayfish, and insects
- Stocked at low density (200-350 fish/ha) as a population control agent rather than a primary food fish
- Fingerlings are cannibalistic, reducing their own numbers naturally
- Can reach 2-4 kg in well-managed pond systems
- Often fetches higher market prices than prey species, making its fiscal contribution disproportionate to biomass

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Mollison identifies the predator role as essential for well-maintained fish growth to market size. Largemouth bass exemplify the permaculture principle of "culling of excess small stock":

### Population Control
- Bass stocked with bluegill increase total yield by 30-50% simply by preventing bluegill overpopulation
- Example: Bluegill alone (316.6 kg/ha) vs. Bluegill plus bass (484.4 kg/ha) -- a 53% increase
- Bass, bluegill, and fathead minnows together achieve 282.6 kg/ha in a balanced polyculture

### Selection and Efficiency
When bass fingerlings are selected for fast growth under high-density conditions (5,000-10,000/ha), remarkable improvements in food conversion efficiency emerge:
- Selected fast-growing strains: 3.4:1 food conversion
- Unselected average: 7.3:1
- Worst individuals: 24.2:1

This range of metabolic efficiency within a single species exceeds the typical differences between species, highlighting the importance of selective breeding in aquaculture.

### Predator Integration Strategies
- Can be separated from breeding prey fish by mesh screens that allow young fish to pass through
- Stocked at low rates (200-350/ha) to thin populations without devastating them
- Alternative predators serving the same function include pike, snakeheads, and soft-shelled turtles

## Cultivation Notes

- Requires live forage or trained to accept artificial feeds
- Best results in ponds 1-3 m deep with adequate dissolved oxygen
- Breeding requires suitable substrate (gravel/sand nests) and appropriate water temperature (16-24 degrees C)
- Cannibalism among fingerlings requires careful size grading

## Role in Landscape Mosaic

Mollison's vision of pond complexes includes bass as one component of a broader landscape mosaic incorporating forest, marsh, prairie, and open water. The forest upstream provides clean nutrient-rich water and indirect food inputs (insects and frass), while the marsh zone supports the crustacean and amphibian populations that sustain bass between harvests of prey fish.

In this integrated design, bass occupy the predator niche across multiple pond sizes -- from 100-500 square metre specialty ponds up to 5+ hectare impoundments. Their adaptability to different scales makes them a versatile component in permaculture [[water-management]] systems.

## Economic Considerations

While bass biomass yield increases are modest (30-50% over prey-only systems), their market value per kilogram typically exceeds that of prey species. The fiscal economics of yield are therefore greater than weight figures alone suggest. In some markets, bass command 2-3 times the price per kilogram of bluegill or catfish.
