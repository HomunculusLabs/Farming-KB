## Description

'Sylvetta' is a wild arugula cultivar, botanically *[[diplotaxis-tenuifolia]]* (distinct from cultivated arugula, *Eruca vesicaria*). It is also known as wild rocket or perennial wall rocket. Valued in winter salad production for its intense, nutty-peppery flavor and exceptional cold hardiness, it is one of the hardiest components in the winter salad mix.

## Classification

- **Type**: Cultivar (Wild arugula)
- **Species**: *Diplotaxis tenuifolia*
- **Category**: Salad green, baby-leaf crop
- **Growth habit**: Perennial, deeply lobed narrow leaves

## Key Facts

- Significantly more cold-hardy than standard cultivated arugula (*Eruca vesicaria*)
- Perfectly hardy through winter in unheated cold houses in Zone 5 Maine — does not require cool-house protection
- Has a more intense, complex flavor than standard arugula — nutty with a pronounced peppery bite
- Sold commercially as 'Sylvetta' to distinguish it from standard arugula
- Requires further research on soil-fertility needs and planting timing to reach its full potential
- Included in the winter salad mix as a hardy flavor component

## Cultivation Notes

- Unlike standard arugula, which loses quality under inner row covers during the coldest months and requires cool-house protection, 'Sylvetta' maintains quality in unheated structures
- [[eliot-coleman]] notes that the farm needs to learn more about its specific soil-fertility requirements and optimal planting schedule
- The narrow, deeply lobed leaves are visually distinct from broad-leaf arugula, adding texture diversity to salad mixes
- Regrows after cutting for multiple harvests

## Relevance to Cultivation

'Sylvetta' arugula illustrates the value of wild-type and perennial crop relatives in winter production systems. Where standard arugula falters in deep cold, the wild type thrives without additional energy inputs. Its inclusion in the salad mix demonstrates how selecting the hardiest available cultivar of each crop species can expand the range of unheated winter production. The ongoing learning about its fertility and timing requirements also shows that even established crops benefit from continued cultivar-specific research.
