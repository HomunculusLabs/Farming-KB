## Antonio Apache

## Description

Antonio Apache was an aged Apache informant who provided critical historical information about peyote use and transmission among the Apache tribes of the American Southwest and northern Mexico. He served as a primary source for understanding how peyote ceremonialism spread between indigenous groups in the region.

## Classification

- **Type:** Historical figure / ethnographic informant
- **Tribal affiliation:** Apache (Mescalero/Lipan context)
- **Role:** Elder informant and cultural knowledge keeper
- **Historical period:** Late 19th century informant (information reported by Opler, 1938)

## Contributions to Ethnographic Knowledge

Antonio Apache's testimony provided key information about:

- **Peyote transmission routes:** According to Antonio Apache, the [[lipan-apache]] obtained peyote from the [[carrizo-indians]] of northern Mexico (as reported by Opler, 1938)
- **Intertribal exchange:** His account helped document the network of cultural transmission that carried peyote ceremonialism from its Mesoamerican origin northward
- **Chronology:** His testimony supported the timeline that Mescaleros learned peyote rites from the Lipans not long before 1870

## Historical Significance

As an aged informant, Antonio Apache represented the living connection to the historical period of active peyote use among the Apache tribes before its proscription. His knowledge of the Carrizo-Lipan-Mescalero transmission chain was crucial for understanding:

- The route by which peyote entered Apache culture
- The role of the Lipans as intermediaries between Mexican peyote traditions and Apache practices
- The distinction between Apache peyote ceremonialism and the [[native-american-church]] (Peyote Religion)

## Key Facts

- His testimony was recorded by Morris Opler, a prominent anthropologist of Apache culture
- He identified the Carrizo Indians as the original source of peyote for the Lipan Apache
- His account is cited in both Opler (1938) and La Barre (1938) regarding peyote diffusion
- He represents the oral historical tradition through which peyote knowledge was transmitted and documented

## Role in Peyote Diffusion Studies

Antonio Apache's testimony contributed to the broader understanding of how peyote ceremonialism spread across North America. The transmission chain he documented runs:

1. **Carrizo Indians** (northern Mexico) — original source in this chain
2. **Lipan Apache** — received peyote from the Carrizo
3. **[[mescalero-apache]]** — learned peyote rites from the Lipans not long before 1870

Alternative transmission routes suggested by other sources include the Tonkawa, Yaqui, and other non-Apachean groups of northern Mexico (Opler, 1936).

## Relevance to Ethnobotany

Antonio Apache's testimony exemplifies the critical role of indigenous informants in documenting ethnobotanical knowledge transmission. Without elder informants like him, the complex networks through which psychoactive plant knowledge spread across cultural boundaries would be lost to history.

## References in Source

Antonio Apache is mentioned in the chapter on shamanism and peyote use among the Apaches by Boyer, Boyer, and Basehart, who cite Opler's 1938 work recording Antonio Apache's testimony about peyote origins.
