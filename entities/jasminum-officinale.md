# Jasminum officinale (Common Jasmine)

## Overview

Jasminum officinale is a deciduous climbing shrub in the family Oleaceae, native to the Caucasus, western Asia, and the Himalayas.

It is the most widely cultivated jasmine species, prized for its intensely fragrant white flowers.

The species is the primary botanical source of jasmine absolute, a key ingredient in perfumery.

J. officinale is the national flower of Pakistan, where it is known as chambeli.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Lamiales
- **Family:** Oleaceae
- **Genus:** Jasminum
- **Species:** J. officinale
- **Authority:** L. (1753)
- **Synonyms:** Jasminum affine, Jasminum grandiflorum subsp. officinale

Stems are slender, climbing, and can reach 6–10 m in length when supported.

Bark is smooth and green on young stems, becoming gray-brown and slightly fissured with age.

Leaves are opposite, pinnately compound, with 5–7 leaflets per leaf.

Each leaflet is ovate to lanceolate, 2–5 cm long, with an entire or shallowly crenate margin.

Flowers are produced in open cymes of 3–12 blooms from summer through early autumn.

Individual flowers are 2–3 cm in diameter with five or six white petals.

The corolla tube is slender, 10–20 mm long, and the flowers open in the evening.

Fragrance is exceptionally strong and sweet, detectable from several meters away.

Fruit is a small, glossy black berry, 6–8 mm in diameter, rarely produced in cultivation.

## Distribution and Habitat

J. officinale is native to a region spanning the Caucasus, northern Iran, Afghanistan, and the western Himalayas.

It grows in open woodlands, scrubland, and along stream banks at elevations of 100–2,500 m.

The species prefers warm, sheltered positions with well-drained soil and full sun to partial shade.

It has been cultivated for centuries across the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and South Asia.

In cultivation, it has naturalized in parts of southern Europe, North Africa, and the Americas.

It is hardy to approximately USDA zone 7 but may die back in severe winters.

## Cultivation

Common jasmine is propagated from semi-hardwood cuttings taken in summer or by layering.

It grows rapidly in warm conditions and can flower within two years of planting from cuttings.

Regular pruning after flowering maintains shape and encourages new flowering wood.

Plants grown against a warm south-facing wall perform best in temperate climates.

It is widely used in container cultivation and is a standard greenhouse specimen.

The cultivar 'Grandiflorum' is particularly valued for its larger, more strongly scented flowers.

'Affine' and 'Aureum' (variegated foliage) are other named selections in the horticultural trade.

## Chemistry and Uses

The essential oil of J. officinale contains benzyl acetate, linalool, indole, and farnesene.

Jasmine absolute is extracted through solvent extraction, yielding approximately 0.2% from fresh flowers.

It is one of the most expensive floral absolutes in perfumery, used in Chanel No. 5 and Joy.

The flowers are also used to flavor jasmine tea, particularly in China.

Green tea leaves are scented by layering them with fresh jasmine blossoms overnight.

In traditional medicine, jasmine flowers have been used as an anti-inflammatory and antidepressant.

Some studies suggest anxiolytic effects from inhalation of jasmine fragrance.

## Ecological Role

The night-opening flowers attract sphinx moths and other nocturnal pollinators.

Daytime visitors include bees, butterflies, and hoverflies seeking nectar.

The dense foliage provides cover for small birds and insects in garden settings.

In naturalized populations, J. officinale can form thickets that suppress ground-level vegetation.

Seed dispersal is primarily by birds that consume the black berries.

## Cultural Significance

Jasmine has been cultivated for over 2,000 years, with origins in the Persian and Indian cultural spheres.

In the Hindu festival of Navaratri, jasmine garlands are offered to deities and worn by devotees.

The plant features extensively in Persian, Arabic, and South Asian poetry as a symbol of love and beauty.

The Mughal emperor Jahangir planted extensive jasmine gardens in the early 17th century.

In modern aromatherapy, jasmine absolute is classified as an aphrodisiac and mood enhancer.
