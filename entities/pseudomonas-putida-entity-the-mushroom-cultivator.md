# Pseudomonas putida

## Taxonomy

Pseudomonas putida is a Gram-negative rod-shaped bacterium belonging to the class
Schizomycetes, order Pseudomonadales, family Pseudomonaceae. The genus name Pseudomonas
derives from the Greek "pseudes" meaning false or deceptive and "monas" meaning a single
unit, referring to the variable forms of this single-celled bacterium. More than 140
species have been identified in the genus Pseudomonas, though only a few have been
identified as affecting mushrooms.

Two proven stimulative strains of Pseudomonas putida are deposited with the American Type
Culture Collection: ATCC number 12633 and ATCC number 17419.

## Morphology

Pseudomonas putida is a cylindrical (bacilli) bacterium measuring approximately 0.4 to
0.5 by 1.0 to 1.7 microns. Cells in this genus typically have one or more flagella
("motile hairs") at one or both poles, distinguishing them from Bacillus species which
have flagella along their entire outer periphery. These flagella are used for locomotion
through aqueous environments.

As a Gram-negative organism, Pseudomonas putida does not retain the crystal violet stain
in the Gram staining procedure, a characteristic that distinguishes it from Gram-positive
bacteria like Bacillus species. The organism can be cultured on malt [[agaricus-brunnescens]],
nor do all strains of mushrooms respond similarly. Some strains have no effect
whatsoever, while others are most stimulative when grown on acetone-based media.

The mechanism by which Pseudomonas putida stimulates primordia formation remains
unanswered. Some believe its effect is indirect, removing chelating compounds that
inhibit mushroom initiation. Others (Fritsche, 1981; Visscher, 1981) suspect its
influence is more direct and biologically oriented. Eger (1972) first suggested a
practical application for commercial cultivators: incubating freshly prepared moist
casing soil with acetone for several days apart from mushroom cultures. If acetone has
a stimulative effect on the microflora that induces fructification, soil treated with
acetone should allow earlier pinhead formation than control samples.

The role of Pseudomonas putida exemplifies the complex microbial ecology of the casing
layer. The casing layer functions not only to protect the colonized substrate from
drying and to provide a humid microclimate for primordia formation, but also to support
the growth of fructification-enhancing microorganisms. The interplay between mushroom
mycelium and its associated bacterial flora represents an area of ongoing research with
significant commercial implications.

From the perspective of the cultivator, maintaining conditions favorable to beneficial
Pseudomonas putida populations while suppressing pathogenic species like Pseudomonas
tolaasii represents a key challenge in crop management. Proper casing preparation,
moisture management, and hygiene practices all influence the balance of microbial
populations in the growing environment.
