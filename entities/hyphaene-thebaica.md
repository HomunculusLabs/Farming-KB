## Overview

Hyphaene thebaica, commonly known as the [[doum-palm]], is a branching palm species native to northern and eastern Africa. It is one of the few palm species that naturally produces a branched trunk, giving mature trees a distinctive, candelabra-like silhouette.

The species has been utilized by human communities for thousands of years across the Sahel, Nile Valley, and Horn of Africa. Its edible fruit, fiber, and timber have made it a cornerstone of traditional livelihoods in arid and semi-arid regions.

Doum palm is exceptionally drought-tolerant and thrives in environments where few other large plants can survive. It is commonly found along seasonal watercourses, around oases, and on sandy plains across its extensive range.

The fruit is perhaps the most economically significant product of the doum palm. It has been found in ancient Egyptian tombs, indicating its importance in pharaonic culture and trade networks spanning thousands of years.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Hyphaene thebaica was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 and is the type species of the genus Hyphaene. The genus belongs to the subfamily Coryphoideae and the tribe Borasseae, which also includes Bismarckia, Borassus, and Lodoicea.

The genus name derives from the Greek "hyphaino" (to weave), referring to the strong fibers obtained from the leaves. The specific epithet "thebaica" refers to Thebes in ancient Egypt, reflecting its historical association with Nile Valley civilizations.

The doum palm is unique among Coryphoideae for its dichotomously branched trunk, a trait rarely seen in palms. Branching typically begins after the palm reaches 3 to 5 meters in height.

Trunks are stout, grey, and covered with prominent leaf scar rings. Individual trunks may reach 15 meters in height, though most specimens are shorter. The crown of each trunk bears 15 to 30 fan-shaped leaves.

Leaves are large, palmate, and costapalmate, reaching 1.5 to 3 meters in length. Leaf segments are stiff and divided nearly to the base, with bluish-green upper surfaces and waxy, grey-green undersides.

Petioles are robust, armed with sharp, recurved teeth along the margins, and measure 1 to 1.5 meters in length. The leaf sheaths are fibrous and persistent, forming a protective covering around the trunk apex.

Inflorescences are interfoliar and spicate, with male and female flowers borne on separate plants. The fruit is an ovoid to spherical drupe, 5 to 8 cm in diameter, with a smooth, glossy, orange-brown epicarp when ripe.

The mesocarp is thick, fibrous, and sweet-tasting, while the hard endocarp encloses a single, oil-rich seed. The seed kernel contains approximately 35 to 40 percent oil and is nutritionally significant.

## Distribution and Habitat

Hyphaene thebaica has one of the largest natural distributions of any African palm, spanning from Mauritania and Senegal eastward to Egypt, Sudan, Eritrea, and Somalia. It extends southward through East Africa to northern Tanzania and Kenya.

The species also occurs in the Arabian Peninsula, with populations in Yemen, Saudi Arabia, and Oman. These Arabian populations may represent natural dispersal or ancient human introduction along Red Sea trade routes.

Isolated populations have been reported from the Canary Islands, where the palm may have been introduced by early settlers. These populations are small and not considered part of the natural range.

Doum palm is strongly associated with riparian habitats in arid and semi-arid regions. It dominates the vegetation along the Nile River and its tributaries, forming extensive palm groves that define the riverine landscape.

Beyond riverbanks, the species colonizes sandy plains, wadi beds, and seasonally flooded depressions. It is a common component of Acacia-Commiphora savanna and Sahelian woodland communities.

Elevation ranges from near sea level along the Red Sea coast to approximately 1,500 meters in the Ethiopian highlands. It tolerates [[panax-ginseng]].

The kernel within the hard endocarp is rich in oil and protein. It is extracted by cracking the shell and is consumed raw, roasted, or pressed for cooking oil and soap production.

In modern times, doum palm oil has attracted research interest for its potential as a biofuel feedstock. The oil's fatty acid profile includes significant proportions of lauric and myristic acids.

Leaf fibers are used extensively for weaving mats, baskets, ropes, and thatch. The strong, durable leaves are also employed in construction of temporary shelters and fencing.

Trunk wood is hard, dense, and resistant to decay, making it valuable for construction timbers, tool handles, and water pipes. Hollowed trunk sections have traditionally been used as irrigation conduits.

## Cultivation

Doum palm cultivation has been practiced for millennia along the Nile Valley and across the Sahel. Traditional cultivation relies primarily on [[fukuoka-natural-farming-in-arid-regions]]. They begin producing fruit at approximately 7 to 10 years of age.

Commercial plantations are relatively rare, with most production coming from semi-wild managed groves. Interest in domestication has increased due to the palm's [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] and nutritional products.

In traditional medicine, various parts of the doum palm are used to treat respiratory ailments, digestive disorders, and fever. The root is used in some communities for treating snakebites and scorpion stings.
