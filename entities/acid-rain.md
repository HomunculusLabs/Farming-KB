## Acid Rain

## Description

Acid rain refers to any form of precipitation with elevated levels of hydrogen ions (low pH), primarily caused by emissions of sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) from fossil fuel combustion. These compounds react with atmospheric water to produce sulfuric acid and nitric acid, which are deposited onto ecosystems as wet deposition (rain, snow, fog) or dry deposition (particles and gases). Acid rain has been identified as a major driver of ectomycorrhizal fungal decline and forest ecosystem degradation across Europe and North America.

## Classification

- **Category**: Environmental Pollutant / Atmospheric Deposition
- **Primary Agents**: Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), Nitric acid (HNO₃)
- **Precursor Emissions**: Sulfur dioxide (SO₂), Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ)
- **Deposition Forms**: Wet (rain, snow, fog), Dry (gases, particulates)
- **pH Range**: Typically pH 4.0–4.5 in affected regions (vs. natural ~5.6)

## Key Facts

- Documented as a primary cause of ectomycorrhizal fungal decline across European forests (Arnolds, 1991).
- Soil acidification increases solubility of toxic metals (aluminum, manganese, heavy metals) that damage fungal mycelia.
- Jansen, Dighton, and Bresser (1988) published a dedicated CEC report on ectomycorrhiza and acid rain.
- Hütterman (1982, 1985) pioneered early diagnosis of emission damage in forest root zones and documented [[emcdda-psilocybin-cardiovascular-risks-acute-physiological-effects]] on forest ecosystems.
- Reduces ectomycorrhizal colonization potential in Scots pine forests in Sweden (Arnebrant and Söderström, 1992).
- Affects [[fungal-decomposition-processes]], altering [[nutrient-cycling-in-soil]] forest soils.
- Netherlands experienced severe acid deposition impacts on macrofungal diversity, documented extensively by Arnolds (1985, 1988, 1989a–d).

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Mycorrhizal Disruption**: Acid deposition damages the fine roots and mycorrhizal structures essential for tree nutrient uptake, weakening host plants and reducing fungal colonization rates.
- **Species Decline**: Multiple studies link acid rain to documented declines in fruiting body production and species richness of [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]], including commercially important species like [[cantharellus-cibarius-entity]].
- **Soil Chemistry Alteration**: Acidification mobilizes aluminum and heavy metals in soil, creating toxic conditions for fungal mycelia and disrupting enzymatic processes such as [[mycorrhizal-phosphatase-activity-elf97-detection]].
- **Decomposition Effects**: Dry-deposited SO₂ alters fungal saprotroph communities on leaf litter, changing decomposition rates and [[nutrient-mineralization]] (Newsham et al., 1992a, 1992b).
- **Cultivation Impact**: Outdoor cultivation of [[mycorrhizal-mushrooms]] in acid-impacted regions faces reduced yields and increased difficulty establishing symbiotic partnerships.
- **Recovery Monitoring**: Liming experiments in acidified forests (Veerkamp et al., 1997) demonstrate shifts in lignicolous macromycete species composition, providing data on ecosystem recovery trajectories.

## Impact on Fungal Communities

Acid rain affects fungi through both direct and indirect pathways. Direct effects include damage to hyphal membranes and enzymatic inhibition at low pH. Indirect effects operate through soil chemistry changes—acidification leaches base cations (calcium, magnesium, potassium) from soils and mobilizes toxic aluminum ions. The combined effect reduces mycorrhizal colonization, shifts species composition toward acid-tolerant taxa, and diminishes overall fungal biomass and diversity in affected ecosystems.

## Historical Context

The acid rain issue peaked in Europe during the 1970s–1990s, with Scandinavian and Central European forests suffering the most severe impacts. The research documented in Dighton"s "Fungi in Ecosystem Processes" reflects this period of intensive investigation into air pollution effects on fungal ecology, with extensive studies from The Netherlands, Sweden, Germany (Solling), and the United Kingdom.
