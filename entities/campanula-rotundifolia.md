## Overview

Campanula rotundifolia, commonly known as harebell or bluebell of Scotland, is a slender perennial herb in the family Campanulaceae. It is widely distributed across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere, including Europe, Asia, and North America.

The species is valued as a native wildflower and ornamental garden plant, producing delicate nodding blue-violet bell-shaped flowers from June through September. Its common name "harebell" derives from the folk belief that hares frequented places where it grew.

It is the county flower of several Scottish counties and serves as the official flower of Dyfed in Wales. The plant has deep cultural significance in Celtic and Norse traditions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Campanula rotundifolia was first formally described by Carl Linnaeus in his 1753 Species Plantarum. The species epithet "rotundifolia" refers to the rounded shape of the basal leaves, which are distinctive but often wither by flowering time.

The plant typically grows 15–60 cm tall with slender, wiry stems that are often curved or nodding at the tips. Basal leaves are long-stalked, rounded to heart-shaped, with toothed margins reaching 2–5 cm in diameter.

Stem leaves are narrow, linear, and sessile, quite different in appearance from the basal foliage. The flowers are solitary or in loose racemes, each 12–25 mm long, with five fused petals forming the characteristic bell shape.

The fruit is a small ribbed capsule that opens by basal pores to release numerous tiny seeds. Chromosome number varies with ploidy level; diploid (2n=34), tetraploid (2n=68), and hexaploid (2n=102) cytotypes are known.

The C. rotundifolia complex has historically been taxonomically challenging. Polyploid races in North America were formerly treated as separate species such as Campanula intercedens before being merged.

Roots are fibrous and slender, with a spreading rhizomatous system that allows vegetative colonization. The rootstock persists through winter, producing new basal rosettes each spring.

Flower color ranges from pale lavender to deep violet-blue, rarely white. The calyx lobes are narrowly triangular and appressed to the corolla tube during flowering.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species has a circumboreal distribution, occurring across Europe from Iceland and northern Scandinavia south to the Mediterranean mountains. In North America, it ranges from Alaska and Greenland south through Canada to the northern United States, with disjunct populations in the Rocky Mountains.

It also occurs across northern and central Asia, including Siberia, the Caucasus, and the Himalayan foothills. This broad range makes it one of the most widely distributed Campanula species globally.

Harebell thrives in dry, well-drained habitats including grasslands, heathlands, rocky outcrops, cliff faces, and sandy soils. It prefers neutral to slightly alkaline conditions but tolerates mildly acidic substrates.

The species is often found on nutrient-poor soils where competition from taller vegetation is limited. It is characteristic of species-rich calcareous grasslands and montane meadows.

In mountainous regions, it ascends to elevations above 2,000 meters in the Alps and Pyrenees. Populations at higher altitudes tend to be more compact with proportionally larger flowers.

Altitudinal variation has led to the description of numerous infraspecific taxa. Subspecies alpigena is recognized from alpine European populations, while subspecies groenlandica occurs in Greenland and Arctic North America.

## Cultivation

Campanula rotundifolia is cultivated as a rock garden and border perennial, hardy in USDA zones 3–8. It prefers full sun to light shade and well-drained, moderately fertile soil.

Propagation is by seed or division of established clumps in spring or autumn. Seeds require light for germination and should be surface-sown in late winter or early spring. Germination typically occurs within 14–30 days at 18–21°C.

The plant is relatively low-maintenance once established but can be short-lived in rich, damp soils. Shearing after the first flush of flowers encourages a second bloom period. It naturalizes readily in suitable conditions, self-seeding prolifically.

No serious pest or disease problems are common, though slugs may damage young growth. The Royal Horticultural Society has awarded it the Award of Garden Merit (AGM).

Several cultivars are available, including 'Olympica' with larger flowers and 'Alba' with white blooms. The cultivar 'White Gem' is a compact form suitable for trough gardens.

## Ecological Role

The nodding bell-shaped flowers are pollinated primarily by bees, particularly small solitary bees and bumblebees. The flower structure provides a landing platform and nectar guide for insect visitors.

Harebell serves as a larval food plant for several moth species, including the netted pug (Eupithecia venosata) and the lime-speck pug (Eupithecia centaureata). The foliage is occasionally grazed by rabbits and deer.

The species is an indicator of relatively undisturbed grassland communities. Its persistence in a site often signals long-term ecological continuity.

It forms mycorrhizal associations with arbuscular fungi, enhancing nutrient uptake in the poor soils it typically inhabits.

Seed production is prolific, with individual plants capable of producing several hundred seeds per season. Seeds are dispersed by wind through the poricidal capsule openings.

Harebell populations can persist in grazed grasslands due to their basal rosette growth form, which tolerates defoliation. However, heavy grazing during flowering reduces seed set significantly.

## Uses

In traditional herbal medicine, the plant was used as a mild astringent and anti-inflammatory agent. Infusions of the aerial parts were applied to wounds and sore throats in European folk practice.

The leaves and young shoots are edible raw or cooked, though rarely gathered due to their small size. The flowers are attractive as edible garnishes in salads.

Campanula rotundifolia is primarily valued today as an ornamental and ecological plant. It features prominently in native wildflower seed mixes for meadow restoration and pollinator habitat creation in both Europe and North America.

The species has been featured in poetry and literature for centuries, appearing in works by William Shakespeare and Emily Brontë. In Scottish folklore, the harebell was believed to be a fairy flower, and picking it was said to bring misfortune.

Research has identified flavonoids and phenolic acids in the aerial parts, including luteolin and apigenin derivatives. These compounds contribute to the plant's traditional medicinal properties.
