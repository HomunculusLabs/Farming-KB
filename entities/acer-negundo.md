## Overview

**Acer negundo**, commonly known as box elder, boxelder maple, or Manitoba maple, is a fast-growing deciduous tree native to [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] maple, ranging from southern Canada to Guatemala.

Box elder is distinctive among maples for its compound leaves, which resemble those of ash or poison ivy. It is highly adaptable, colonizing disturbed sites, riparian corridors, and urban environments with equal vigor.

The species is considered invasive in parts of Europe, Asia, and Australia. In its native range, it serves important [[north-american-dmt-containing-plants-phalaris-desmanthus-peganum]] form. *A. negundo* var. *violaceum* (Kirchn.) Jaeger has glaucous, violet-tinted shoots. *A. negundo* var. *californicum* (Torr. & Gray) Sarg. is restricted to California.

**Morphology:**

Box elder is a small to medium tree, typically 10–25 m tall with a DBH of 30–60 cm. Exceptional specimens reach 30 m. The crown is broad, irregular, and often multi-stemmed.

Bark is light gray-brown, becoming furrowed and ridged with age. Young twigs are green to purplish-green with a distinct glaucous bloom and conspicuous lenticels. Branches are brittle and prone to breakage in storms.

Leaves are pinnately compound, 15–25 cm long, with 3–7 (usually 5) leaflets. Each leaflet is 5–10 cm long, lanceolate to ovate, coarsely serrate or lobed, and lighter green on the lower surface.

Box elder is dioecious, with separate male and female trees. Male flowers are borne in dense, hanging racemes; female flowers in pendulous racemes that develop into paired samaras (winged fruits) in clusters.

## Distribution and Habitat

Box elder is native to most of the contiguous United States and southern Canada, extending south through Mexico to Guatemala. It has been widely planted and naturalized in Europe, Asia, [[cervantes-seed-production-storage]] make it a successful colonizer of open sites.

Optimal growth occurs on moist, fertile alluvial soils in full sun. The species is hardy to USDA Zone 2, making it one of the most cold-tolerant maples.

## Cultivation and Uses

Box elder grows rapidly, reaching 3–5 m in the first 5 years. It is short-lived for a tree, with typical lifespans of 60–100 years. Senescence and crown dieback begin by age 40–50.

The wood is light, soft, and close-grained with a specific gravity of 0.44. It is of limited commercial value due to its small size and tendency to develop heartwood rot. Uses include crates, pulpwood, and inexpensive furniture.

Box elder wood is sometimes used for woodturning and carving. Red-stained heartwood, caused by the fungus *Fusarium reticulatum*, produces a vivid pink-to-red figure prized by woodworkers. This "flame box elder" commands premium prices.

The species has limited ornamental value. Several cultivars exist, including 'Variegatum' with white-edged leaves and 'Flamingo' with pink new growth. The cultivar 'Violaceum' has purple-tinted branches.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

Box elder contains typical maple [[stable-carbon-isotope-signature-fatty-acids-mycorrhizal-carbon-tracking]] including linoleic, oleic, and palmitic acids. They have been investigated as a potential oilseed crop, though yields are low compared to dedicated oilseed species.

No significant pharmacological research has been conducted on box elder specifically. Its medicinal use in traditional practices is attributed to general tannin astringency rather than unique bioactive compounds.

## Invasive Potential

Box elder is classified as invasive in several European countries, including Austria, Hungary, and parts of the Balkans. It spreads rapidly along waterways, displacing native riparian vegetation.

In Australia, box elder is a declared environmental weed in Victoria and New South Wales. It forms dense monocultures along streams, reducing biodiversity and altering hydrological processes.

The species' prolific seeding, with female trees producing 100,000+ seeds annually, enables rapid colonization. Seeds are wind-dispersed and remain viable for 1–2 years in the soil seed bank.
