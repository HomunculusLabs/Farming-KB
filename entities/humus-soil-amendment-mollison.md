## Description

Humus is the fully decomposed organic fraction of soil, representing the stable end-product of biological breakdown of plant and animal residues. It serves as the primary reservoir of slowly-available plant nutrients, a buffer for pH and heavy metals, and the foundation of soil colloidal activity.

## Classification

- **Category**: Soil organic matter
- **Composition**: Complex mixture of humic acids, fulvic acids, and humins
- **Natural range**: 2% to near 100% of soil volume (peats at the upper extreme)

## Key Facts

### Humus Content in Soils

- Soils in nature range from 2% humus to nearly 100% (peats)
- Garden soils under compost or mulch regimes typically contain 10-30% humus
- At 40% or more humus, soils effectively block heavy metal uptake by plants through colloid binding
- Humus provides both physical benefits (water retention, erosion prevention) and chemical benefits (colloid formation, nutrient cycling)

### Humus as Buffer

- Humus acts as a pH buffer alongside calcium (lime)
- Limed and mulched gardens rarely show plant deficiency symptoms even when pH fluctuates
- Humus can persist for hundreds to thousands of years in undisturbed soils
- At Rothamsted (UK), soil humus has a mean age of 1,400 years, likely deriving from forests that preceded wheat cropping

### Humus Loss Mechanisms

- **Ploughing and aeration**: Oxidises humus to carbon dioxide escaping to atmosphere
- **Burning vegetation**: Rapidly destroys soil humus with additional pollutants (terpenes, creosotes, nitrogen, dust)
- **Continuous cultivation**: Wheat cropping in Canada reduced humus to 1% of original levels over much of wheat country

### Humus Sources at Scale

- Root mass and above-ground biomass of grasses, trees, and plants
- Prairie grasses and broadleaf trees are particularly effective humus builders
- Returning food wastes to gardens (including composted humanure) minimises humus loss
- No-till and perennial crop systems preserve existing humus reserves
