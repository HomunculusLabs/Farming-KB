# Hallucinogenic Plants of the Old World

Based on [[richard-evans-schultes]]'s Golden Guide. While the New World has 90+ hallucinogenic species in active cultural use, the Old World has far fewer (15-20), many abandoned in antiquity. This is not explained by botanical distribution but likely by cultural factors.

---

## Amanita — The Siberian Fly Agaric

### Amanita muscaria (L. ex Fr.) Hooker
- **Family**: Amanitaceae
- **Distribution**: Boreal and temperate forests across Northern Hemisphere — Siberia, northern Europe, North America
- **Use**: Central to shamanic practices of Siberian tribes: Koryak, Kamchadal, Chukchi. Used ceremonially and recreationally. Also used independently in Oaxaca, Mexico by Mazatec peoples — a remarkable case of convergent cultural discovery. Celtic and Viking use has been hypothesized (e.g., berserkers, fly agaric as "Soma") but is speculative
- **Preparation**: Dried mushroom eaten directly, or boiled in water to make an intoxicating beverage. Drying converts ibotenic acid to muscimol (the active compound). Urine of intoxicated persons is sometimes consumed to recycle the active compounds (Koryak practice)
- **Chemistry**: Ibotenic acid (alpha-amino-3-hydroxy-5-isoxazolacetic acid), muscimol (muscazone). Not a tryptamine — unique psychoactive chemistry among plant hallucinogens. Muscimol is a GABA-A receptor agonist
- **Effects**: Narcotic, inebriating, soporific. Distinct from tryptamine or phenylethylamine hallucinogens. Described as producing visions of "little people" (Koryak)

### Amanita pantherina, A. strobiliformis, A. gemmata
- Related species with similar chemistry; used in limited contexts

## Cannabis

### Cannabis sativa L.
- **Family**: Cannabaceae (formerly Moraceae)
- **Distribution**: Originally Central Asian; now cultivated worldwide
- **Historical use**: One of the oldest psychoactive plants known. Documented in:
  - **China**: As a medicine (pharmacopoeia of Emperor Shen Nung, c. 2737 BCE); fiber plant (ma)
  - **India**: Extensive religious and recreational use; associated with Shiva; bhang, ganja, charas preparations; Sushruta Samhita mentions intoxicating properties
  - **Middle East**: Assyrian use (qunubu), Scythian use (Herodotus, c. 450 BCE — hemp smoke in tents)
  - **Africa**: Widespread across sub-Saharan Africa; kif (Morocco), dagga (South Africa); used in the "riamba" cult of the Congo-Brazzaville region; introduced by Arab traders
  - **Europe**: Medieval medicinal use; 19th century medical use (tincture of cannabis); largely abandoned as hallucinogen in modern Europe
- **Chemistry**: Delta-1-THC (principal psychoactive), CBD, CBN, CBC, THCV, and dozens of other cannabinoids. THC is a terpenophenolic dibenzopyran — unique among hallucinogens. See [[hallucinogenic-plant-chemistry]]
- **Effects**: Mildly hallucinogenic at high doses; primarily euphoric, sedative, and perceptually altering

## Solanaceous Plants (Tropane Alkaloids)

### Belladonna — Atropa belladonna L.
- **Family**: Solanaceae
- **Distribution**: Europe, western Asia, naturalized in North America
- **Use**: Historically used in European witchcraft and folk medicine. The "flying ointments" of medieval witchcraft may have contained belladonna extracts. Also used medicinally as mydriatic (pupil dilator — hence "belladonna" = "beautiful lady")
- **Chemistry**: Hyoscyamine (principal), scopolamine, atropine

### Henbane — Hyoscyamus niger L.
- **Distribution**: Europe, western Asia, North Africa
- **Use**: Oracle of Delphi may have been induced by henbane fumes (some scholars). Used in medieval European folk magic and medicine
- **Chemistry**: Hyoscyamine (principal), scopolamine

### Mandrake — Mandragora officinarum L.
- **Distribution**: Mediterranean region
- **Use**: Extremely important in Western magical tradition. The root was believed to scream when pulled from the ground, killing whoever heard it. Associated with fertility, love magic, and witchcraft. Used in the Circe myths (Odyssey). Jacobson's organ (vomeronasal organ) named after the Danish physician who proved mandrake's effects
- **Chemistry**: Hyoscyamine, scopolamine, mandragorine (a unique alkaloid)

### Thornapple — Datura stramonium L.
- **Distribution**: Native to Americas but widely naturalized in Old World (Europe, Asia, Africa)
- **Use**: Used medicinally for asthma in 19th century Europe; historically in European folk magic. See New World page for Native American use

### Latua — Latua pubiflora (Griseb.) Phil.
- **Distribution**: Endemic to Chile (Valdivian region)
- **Use**: Extremely potent hallucinogen used by Mapuche Indians of southern Chile. Considered the most toxic of all hallucinogenic solanaceous plants. Reputedly used to drive victims insane or as a method of execution
- **Chemistry**: Scopolamine is the principal alkaloid

## African Hallucinogens

### Iboga — Tabernanthe iboga Baillon
- **Family**: Apocynaceae
- **Distribution**: Gabon, Congo, Cameroon (equatorial West Africa)
- **Use**: Central to the Bwiti religion of the Fang, Mitsogo, and other peoples of Gabon. Root bark consumed in massive doses (up to 50g) during initiation ceremonies. Also used in lower doses for stimulant and aphrodisiac effects during hunting. Bwiti is a syncretic religion incorporating ancestor worship and Christian elements
- **Chemistry**: Ibogaine (principal among ~12 indole alkaloids), ibogamine, ibogaline, tabernanthine. See [[iboga-tabernanthe]]
- **Effects**: Long-acting (24-48 hours). Described as producing contact with ancestors, visions of the forest, and ego-dissolution. In high doses, causes ataxia, nausea, then profound visionary state

### Thang (African Morning Glory) — Ipomoea tricolor Cav.
- **Distribution**: West Africa; similar to New World morning glories
- **Use**: Used in West African ethnomedicine; less documented than New World counterparts

## Asian Hallucinogens

### Betel Nut — Areca catechu L.
- **Family**: Palmae (Arecaceae)
- **Distribution**: Southeast Asia, Pacific Islands, East Africa
- **Use**: The most widely used stimulant in the world after tobacco, alcohol, and caffeine. Chewed with betel leaf (Piper betle) and lime. Estimated 200-400 million users. Mildly euphoric, stimulating. Used socially and ceremonially throughout South and Southeast Asia
- **Chemistry**: Arecoline (alkaloid), arecaidine. Not strongly hallucinogenic but included in Schultes's guide for its psychoactive properties
- **Effects**: Mild stimulation, euphoria, increased salivation, warming sensation. Long-term use stains teeth red/black and is associated with oral cancer

### Kava — Piper methysticum Forster
- **Family**: Piperaceae
- **Distribution**: Polynesia, Melanesia, Micronesia (Oceania)
- **Use**: Central to social, ceremonial, and political life throughout the Pacific. Prepared by chewing or pounding the root, then mixing with water or coconut milk. Used in formal ceremonies, conflict resolution, and as a social beverage. The kava ceremony is the most important ritual in many Pacific cultures
- **Chemistry**: Kavalactones (kavain, dihydrokavain, methysticin, dihydromethysticin, yangonin, desmethoxyyangonin) — not typical alkaloids. Piperidine derivatives
- **Effects**: Mildly euphoric, relaxing, anxiolytic. Produces a state of calm without impairment of mental clarity. Unlike alcohol, does not impair cognitive function at typical doses. Described as "peaceful and contented" feeling

### Nutmeg — Myristica fragrans Houtt.
- **Family**: Myristicaceae
- **Distribution**: Native to Maluku Islands (Indonesia); cultivated in tropics
- **Use**: Used as a spice and occasionally as an intoxicant. Ingestion of large amounts (5-30g) produces hallucinogenic effects, but with extremely unpleasant side effects (nausea, dehydration, tachycardia, paranoia). The psychoactive use is not traditional — it is a modern discovery
- **Chemistry**: Myristicin (3-methoxy-4,5-methylenedioxyallylbenzene), elemicin, safrole. Myristicin is structurally related to MMDA (a phenylethylamine hallucinogen)
- **Effects**: Delayed onset (3-6 hours); cannabis-like intoxication with visual disturbances, dry mouth, tachycardia. Unpleasant and potentially dangerous — not recommended

### Syrian Rue — Peganum harmala L.
- **Family**: Zygophyllaceae
- **Distribution**: Mediterranean, Middle East, Central Asia; naturalized in American Southwest
- **Use**: Seeds used in Middle Eastern folk medicine (abortifacient, emmenagogue). Used by Iranian and Turkish peoples. Also used in ayahuasca analogs (as MAOI source). Known in Arabic as "harmel" or "esfand"; burned as incense to ward off evil eye
- **Chemistry**: Harmine, harmaline (beta-carbolines) — the same MAO inhibitors found in Banisteriopsis caapi. Up to 4% beta-carbolines in seeds. The Old World equivalent of the ayahuasca vine
- **Effects**: Not hallucinogenic alone; inhibits MAO, potentiating other compounds. Large doses produce nausea, tremors, and visual effects

### Datura metel L.
- **Distribution**: Indian subcontinent; widely cultivated
- **Use**: Important in Ayurvedic medicine (as "dhattura"); used in Hindu rituals; associated with Shiva. One of the daturas of classical Indian medicine
- **Chemistry**: Scopolamine, hyoscyamine

## Sinicuichi — Heimia salicifolia Link et Otto
- **Family**: Lythraceae
- **Distribution**: Mexico, Central America, South America; also found in Texas (as ornamental)
- **Use**: Known to the Aztecs as "sinicuichi" or "sun opener." Leaves are crushed and soaked in water, then allowed to ferment slightly. Produces auditory hallucinations — a rare property. User hears voices of departed ancestors and recalls past events
- **Chemistry**: Cryogenine (vertine) — the most active of 6 alkaloids found. Also lythrine, nesodine, heimidine, lyfoline, demethylcryogenine
- **Note**: The auditory hallucination effect is unusual among hallucinogens and has not been reliably duplicated with isolated compounds

## Abandoned Old World Hallucinogens

Several Old World hallucinogens known from antiquity but no longer in active use:
- **Water Lily** (Nymphaea nouchali, N. stellata) — used in ancient Egypt; may have been a component of the "lotus" of the Odyssey. Contained aporphine alkaloids (nuciferine)
- **Blue Water Lily** (Nymphaea caerulea) — Egyptian sacred flower; possible mild psychoactive
- **Poleo Mint** (Hedeoma pulegioides) — North American; pulegone is mildly psychoactive
- **Peruvian Bark** — used as a tonic; quinine is not hallucinogenic
