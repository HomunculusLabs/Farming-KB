# Brugmansia aurea

**Brugmansia aurea** is a species of flowering plant in the family **Solanaceae** (nightshade family), commonly known as **Angel's Trumpet**. It is the most widespread species of Brugmansia in the Andes and has been used as a hallucinogen by indigenous peoples for millennia.

Closely related to Datura, all Brugmansia species are arborescent (tree-like) in habit and are believed to be ancient cultigens unknown in the truly wild state. They are biologically very complex and appear to have been used as hallucinogens since antiquity.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Family:** Solanaceae (Nightshade Family)

- **Genus:** Brugmansia

- **Species:** B. aurea

- **Common names:** Angel's Trumpet, Campanula, Floripondio, Toe

Brugmansia was historically classified within the genus Datura but has since been separated based on its arborescent growth habit and other morphological differences. The taxonomic history of the genus has been complicated, with B. aurea frequently misidentified in horticultural literature as B. (or Datura) arborea, which is in reality a much less common plant.

The separation of Brugmansia from Datura is now widely accepted. Brugmansia species are characterized by their woody, tree-like growth and pendant flowers, while Datura species are herbaceous with erect flowers.

## Morphology

Brugmansia aurea is a shrub or small tree growing up to 30 feet (9 meters) tall. The leaves are oblong-elliptic, often minutely hairy, with the blade measuring 4 to 16 inches (10 to 40 cm) long and 2 to 6.5 inches (5 to 16 cm) wide, borne on a petiole up to 5 inches (13 cm) long.

The flowers are nodding, not wholly pendulous, usually 7 to 9 inches (18 to 23 cm) long and very fragrant, especially in the evening. The flowers are typically white, though yellow forms also occur, with the white form being more common.

The large, trumpet-shaped flowers give the genus its common name of Angel's Trumpet. The fruit is a spindle-shaped capsule containing numerous seeds.

## Distribution

Brugmansia aurea is native to the Andean highlands of [[ethnobotanical-use-of-sacred-mushrooms-in-indigenous-cultures]] throughout the Andes and western Amazonia.

### Jivaro Use

The Jivaro people of the upper Amazon used Brugmansia species extensively in shamanic practices. Recalcitrant children were given a drink of B. sanguinea with parched maize; when intoxicated, the children were lectured so that the spirits of the ancestors might admonish them.

This practice served as a form of spiritual discipline within Jivaro culture, reflecting the Andean belief that [[brugmansia-sanguinea]] by the name **Huaca** or **Huacachaca**, meaning "plant of the tomb." This name reflects the plant's deep association with death, spirits, and the ancestral world in Andean cosmology.

The term "huaca" in Quechua refers broadly to sacred objects, places, or beings of spiritual significance.

### San Pedro Additive

B. aurea is frequently used as a vegetal additive to **San Pedro** ([[trichocereus-pachanoi]]) cactus preparations. Along with B. sanguinea, it is considered a potent hallucinogen in its own right that enhances the visionary qualities of the cactus brew known as **cimora**.

The addition of Brugmansia to cimora is one of the most significant examples of psychoactive plant combination in Andean ethnopharmacology. Other plants, including Iresine (which has the reputation of curing "insanity"), may also be added to the cimora preparation.

### Other Brugmansia Species

The genus contains several species used similarly across South America:

- **B. suaveolens** and **B. insignis** occur in warmer parts of South America, especially western Amazonia, where they are employed alone or mixed with other plants, usually under the name Toe

- **B. versicolor** and **B. vulcanicola** also occur in Andean highlands

- **B. sanguinea** prefers the cool, wet highlands and ranges from Colombia to Chile
