# Uncinula necator

## Overview

uncinula necator is an obligate biotrophic fungal pathogen that causes powdery mildew of grapevines (Vitis spp.). It is one of the most economically important fungal pathogens in viticulture worldwide, affecting both wine grapes and table grapes across all major grape-growing regions. The species belongs to the phylum [[ascomycota-phylogeny-major-classes-lineages]] and is the type species of the genus Uncinula.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Eurotiomycetes
- **Order:** Erysiphales
- **Family:** Erysiphaceae
- **Genus:** Uncinula
- **Species:** U. necator

The species has undergone several taxonomic revisions and is also known by the synonym Erysiphe necator, following the transfer of many powdery mildew species to the genus Erysiphe based on molecular phylogenetic analyses. The older name Oidium tuckeri is sometimes used to refer to the anamorphic (asexual) stage.

## Morphology and Identification

uncinula necator is an obligate biotroph, meaning it can only grow on living host tissue. Its morphological features include:

- **Mycelium:** Superficial, white to grayish-white, growing on the surface of leaves, stems, shoots, and berries. The mycelium is ectophytic, covering the host surface with a characteristic powdery coating.
- **Haustoria:** Specialized feeding structures that penetrate host epidermal cells to absorb nutrients without killing the cell.
- **Conidiophores:** Erect, arising from the superficial mycelium, bearing chains of conidia (powdery spores).
- **Conidia:** Hyaline, ellipsoidal to barrel-shaped, produced in chains (catenulate). They measure approximately 25 to 30 micrometers in length. Conidia are responsible for the powdery appearance of infected tissue and serve as the primary means of secondary spread during the growing season.
- **Cleistothecia:** The sexual fruiting bodies (chasmothecia) are globose, dark brown to black, and approximately 80 to 120 micrometers in diameter. They contain asci with typically 4 to 8 ascospores.
- **Appendages:** The most diagnostic feature of the genus Uncinula is the appendages on the cleistothecia, which are uncinate (hooked) at their tips, a characteristic that distinguishes it from other powdery mildew genera.

## Ecology and Habitat

uncinula necator is an obligate pathogen of Vitis species and their cultivars. It is found worldwide in all temperate grape-growing regions, including:

- Europe (Mediterranean, Atlantic, and continental regions)
- North America (California, Pacific Northwest, eastern states)
- South America (Chile, Argentina)
- Australia and New Zealand
- South Africa
- Asia (China, India, Japan)

The fungus overwinters as cleistothecia on bark or as mycelium within dormant buds. In spring, ascospores or conidia from infected buds initiate primary infections on young leaves and shoots. The disease develops most rapidly under warm, dry conditions with cool nights, which favor spore production and infection.

## Role in Ecosystem Processes

### Plant Disease Dynamics

uncinula necator is a specialist pathogen that has co-evolved with its grapevine host. In natural or semi-natural ecosystems, the pathogen contributes to the regulation of wild Vitis populations. In agricultural ecosystems (vineyards), it is a major production constraint.

The disease cycle of grape powdery mildew involves:

- **Primary infection:** Initiated by ascospores from overwintering cleistothecia or by conidia from infected dormant buds
- **Secondary spread:** Conidia produced on infected tissue are dispersed by wind to initiate new infections
- **Multiple infection cycles:** The pathogen can complete multiple generations within a single growing season, leading to epidemic development under favorable conditions

### Effects on Host Physiology

Infection by uncinula necator affects host plant physiology in several ways:

- Reduces photosynthetic capacity of infected leaves by covering the leaf surface
- Alters carbohydrate partitioning from host tissue to fungal biomass
- Causes berry splitting and secondary infections by other microorganisms
- Reduces fruit quality, including sugar content and flavor compounds
- Can cause premature defoliation, reducing vine vigor and cold hardiness

## Interactions with Other Organisms

### Mite-Mediated Biocontrol

A particularly interesting ecological interaction involving uncinula necator was documented by Norton et al. (2000), as cited in Dighton (2003). This research demonstrated that tydeid mites (Orthotydeus lambi), which inhabit acarodomatia (specialized mite shelters on leaf surfaces), can significantly reduce the incidence of grape powdery mildew.

Key findings include:

- The density of domatia per leaf was positively correlated with the level of mite mycophagy ([[fungal-spores]]g)
- At high domatia densities, the feeding activity of tydeid mites significantly reduced the incidence of mildew on grape leaves
- This represents a tritrophic interaction involving the grapevine (providing domatia), the mites (consuming fungal spores and mycelium), and the powdery mildew pathogen (being consumed)

This interaction illustrates the concept of biological cobacillus-subtilisrough plant-provided structures and highlights the complexity of species interactions in agricultural ecosystems.

### Antagonistic Microorganisms

Various microorganisms, including [[ampelomyces-quisqualis]] (a hyperparasitic fungus), Bacillus subtilis, and other biocontrol agents, have been investigated for their ability to suppress uncinula necator. These organisms can parasitize the mildew mycelium, compete for leaf surface space, or produce antifungal compounds.

### Grapevine Host Interactions

Grapevine cultivars vary in their susceptibility to uncinula necator. Resistance mechanisms include physical barriers (leaf hair density, cuticle thickness), biochemical defenses (production of phytoalexins and pathogenesis-related proteins), and ontogenic resistance (reduced susceptibility of older tissues).

## Economic and Practical Significance

uncinula necator is arguably the most important fungal pathogen in viticulture globally:

- **Yield losses:** Uncontrolled powdery mildew can cause yield losses of 20 to 40 percent or more in susceptible cultivars.
- **Quality impacts:** Even subclinical infections can reduce fruit quality, affecting sugar accumulation, flavor compounds, and wine sensory characteristics.
- **Management costs:** Powdery mildew management, primarily through fungicide applications, represents one of the largest variable costs in grape production. Vineyards may receive 10 to 15 or more fungicide applications per season specifically for powdery mildew control.
- **Breeding programs:** The development of powdery mildew-resistant grape cultivars is a major objective of grape breeding programs worldwide.

## Management

Management strategies for grape powdery mildew include:

- Chemical control using sulfur, sterol demethylation inhibitors (DMIs), quinone outside inhibitors (QoIs), and other fungicide classes
- Cultural practices including canopy management to improve air circulation
- Use of resistant cultivars and rootstocks
- Biological control using microbial antagonists and mite-mediated predation
- Monitoring and forecasting systems to optimize fungicide timing

### Biochemical Properties

uncinula necator produces several classes of secondary metabolites that influence its pathogenicity and interactions with the host plant:

- **Ergosterol:** A membrane sterol essential for fungal growth; ergosterol biosynthesis inhibitors (triadimefon, myclobutanil, and other DMIs) target this pathway and are the foundation of powdery mildew chemical control
- **Oidial glycoproteins:** Surface proteins on conidia that mediate adhesion to host leaf surfaces and may trigger host defense responses
- **Suppressors of host defense:** The fungus produces compounds that suppress or evade host plant immune responses, facilitating successful colonization
- **Pectinolytic enzymes:** Enzymes that degrade pectin in the host cell wall, facilitating haustorial penetration

## Cultural Significance

The history of grape powdery mildew is closely tied to the history of viticulture itself. The disease is believed to have originated in North America and was inadvertently introduced to Europe in the mid-19th century, where it caused devastating epidemics in European vineyards. This event, along with the contemporaneous introduction of grape phylloxera, transformed European viticulture and led to the development of modern plant pathology and disease management practices.

The introduction of sulfur as a fungicide for powdery mildew control in the 1850s is considered one of the first examples of systematic chemical disease control in agriculture. This innovation laid the groundwork for the development of the modern crop protection industry.

## Research Notes

Dighton (2003) references uncinula necator in the context of tritrophic interactions among plants, arthropods, and fungi. The Norton et al. (2000) study demonstrating mite-mediated reduction of powdery mildew on grape leaves serves as a compelling example of how plant-provided structures (domatia) can facilitate biological control of fungal pathogens, with implications for designing more sustainable agricultural systems.
