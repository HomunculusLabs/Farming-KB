The species is most frequently encountered growing from fallen pine cones in
coniferous forests across the Northern Hemisphere. Despite its unassuming size,
it is one of the most reliably identifiable cone-inhabiting fungi, making it
a favorite teaching specimen in mycology courses.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

Basidiomycota → Agaricomycetes → Russulales → **Auriscalpiaceae → *Auriscalpium → A. vulgare***

Originally described by Samuel Frederick Gray in 1821 as *Hydnum vulgare*, the
species was transferred to *Auriscalpium* by Petter Adolf Karsten in 1879.
No significant synonymy exists; the species is morphologically stable and
genetically monophyletic.

### Morphology

- **Cap (pileus):** 5–30 mm, semicircular to kidney-shaped, laterally attached,
  brown to dark reddish-brown, dry, finely tomentose. Surface often radially
  wrinkled or zoned. Margin incurved when young.
- **Hymenium:** Ventral surface of the cap bears crowded, fine teeth (1–3 mm long)
  that are concolorous with the cap surface or slightly paler.
- **Stipe (stipitus):** 15–50 mm long, 1–3 mm thick, eccentric or lateral,
  dark brown to nearly black, tough and wiry, arising from the cone.
