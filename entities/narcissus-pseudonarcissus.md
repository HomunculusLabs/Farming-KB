## Overview

**Narcissus pseudonarcissus** L., the wild daffodil or Lent lily, is a bulbous perennial of the family Amaryllidaceae native to western Europe. It is the parent species of the majority of modern cultivated daffodil cultivars.

The genus *Narcissus* comprises approximately 50–70 species distributed across Europe, North Africa, and western Asia. *N. pseudonarcissus* is the most culturally and economically significant member of the genus.

The genus name has been connected to the Greek word *narke* (numbness), referring to the narcotic properties of the bulbs. This etymology contrasts with the more commonly cited mythological origin involving the youth Narcissus.

The species is instantly recognizable by its golden-yellow trumpet (corona) surrounded by a ring of six pale-yellow tepals. It has been a symbol of spring in [[hamilton-vegetative-propagation]] via bulb offsets is the primary mode of reproduction in the wild.

## Distribution and Habitat

*N. pseudonarcissus* is native to western and central Europe, with a natural range extending from the Iberian Peninsula through France, the British Isles, the Low Countries, Germany, and into northern Italy and the western Balkans.

Its native habitat includes open woodlands, woodland edges, meadows, pastures, rocky outcrops, and stream banks. It prefers well-drained, moderately acidic to neutral soils in partial shade to full sun, typically at elevations below 1,500 m.

In its native range, *N. pseudonarcissus* is a characteristic component of ancient woodland and species-rich meadow communities. It flowers from February to April, with peak bloom typically coinciding with the Christian observance of Lent, hence the common name "Lent lily."

The species has been widely naturalized beyond its native range through centuries of cultivation and bulb trade. Bulbs planted in autumn typically root within 4–6 weeks and produce visible shoots by midwinter. Flower quality and bulb vigor decline if foliage is removed prematurely after flowering; leaves should be allowed to senesce naturally for at least six weeks.

The species is hardy to USDA Zone 4 and tolerates a wide range of soil conditions. It performs best in full sun to light shade with moderately fertile, well-drained soil. Bulbs should be planted at a depth of approximately three times their height in early autumn.

Modern daffodil cultivars are classified into 13 horticultural divisions based on flower form, with Division 1 (trumpet daffodils) and Division 2 (large-cupped) deriving primarily from *N. pseudonarcissus* parentage. Popular Division 1 cultivars include 'King Alfred', 'Dutch Master', and 'Golden Harvest'.

The commercial daffodil bulb industry is centered in the Netherlands and the United Kingdom, with the Netherlands producing over 9 billion narcissus bulbs annually for the global market. The Netherlands also hosts the world's primary daffodil gene bank at the Centre for Genetic Resources.

Major pests include the narcissus bulb fly (*Merodon equestris*), narcissus eelworm (*Ditylenchus dipsaci*), and basal rot caused by *Fusarium* species. Virus diseases, particularly narcissus yellow stripe virus, can cause significant losses in commercial production.

## Related Species and Hybrids

*N. pseudonarcissus* readily hybridizes with other *Narcissus* species, producing fertile offspring that have been selectively bred into the modern cultivar range. The most important hybrid partners include *N. poeticus* (poet's daffodil), *N. jonquilla* (jonquil), and *N. cyclamineus*.

Interspecific hybridization within the genus has been remarkably productive, yielding over 27,000 registered cultivars. Most modern daffodils sold commercially are complex polyploid hybrids involving three or more species in their ancestry.

The species *N. pseudonarcissus* subsp. *major* is a larger, more robust form with broader leaves and bigger flowers, widely used in breeding programs. Subsp. *pseudoobscurus* from the Iberian Peninsula has been less frequently used in horticulture but offers potential for novel color combinations.
