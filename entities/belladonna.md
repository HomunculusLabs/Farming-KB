# Belladonna (Atropa belladonna)

## Description

*[[atropa-belladonna]]*, commonly known as **Deadly Nightshade** or simply **Belladonna**, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the nightshade family (Solanaceae). Its active principle, **atropine**, is cited by [[julius-auboineau-palmer]] as the most promising antidote for *Amanita* (amanitine/muscarine) poisoning when administered via subcutaneous injection.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Family:** Solanaceae (nightshade family)
- **Genus:** *Atropa*
- **Species:** *A. belladonna*
- **Active compound:** Atropine (also called daturine)
- **Common names:** Deadly Nightshade, Belladonna, Dwale

## Key Facts

- The active principle atropine acts as a physiological antagonist to muscarine/amanitine
- A consulting physician independently suggested belladonna treatment after observing the effects of fungal poison on patients
- A few drops of tincture of belladonna injected hypodermically appeared to improve a severely poisoned patient
- Palmer notes the consulting physician had never heard of the use of atropine as advocated by Schmiedeberg and Koppe, yet arrived at the same recommendation independently through clinical observation
- The nightshade family (Solanaceae) provides the most trustworthy antidotes for *Amanita* poisoning, including daturine, nicotine, and atropine
- Caution required: if the system has fully absorbed amanitine, administering enough atropine to counteract it could itself prove fatal through atropine poisoning

## Pharmacological Significance

Belladonna's significance in mycological toxicology rests on the atropine-muscarine antagonism:

- **Muscarine** stimulates the parasympathetic nervous system (via muscarinic acetylcholine receptors)
- **Atropine** blocks those same receptors, counteracting muscarine's effects
- This antagonism was demonstrated in Schmiedeberg's frog heart experiments
- The principle that atropine counteracts muscarinic poisoning remains valid in modern emergency medicine

## Historical Treatment Protocol

Palmer's account suggests:
1. Subcutaneous injection of atropine (or tincture of belladonna)
2. Continue treatment rather than stopping after initial improvement
3. Avoid emetics and cathartics, which are worse than useless
4. Use enemata of warm water with oil to relieve tenesmus
5. Monitor for the risk of atropine poisoning if amanitine has been fully absorbed

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Understanding belladonna's role as a source of atropine is essential historical context for the treatment of mushroom poisoning. Palmer's work helped popularize the use of atropine for *Amanita* poisoning in American medical practice. The Solanaceae family's role as a source of antidotes to fungal toxins represents a fascinating intersection of botanical and mycological toxicology.
