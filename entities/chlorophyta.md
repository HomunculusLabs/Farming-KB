# Chlorophyta

## Description

**Chlorophyta** (green algae) is a division of photosynthetic eukaryotic organisms that includes approximately 7,000 known species. Green algae are found in freshwater, marine, and terrestrial environments, including on soil surfaces, tree bark, and rocks. In soil ecosystems, Chlorophyta contribute to primary production, nitrogen cycling (some species fix nitrogen), and soil formation. They are evolutionary ancestors of land plants and share many characteristics including chlorophyll a and b pigments, cellulose cell walls, and starch storage.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Eukarya
- **Kingdom:** Plantae (or Protista, depending on classification)
- **Division:** Chlorophyta
- **Classes:** Chlorophyceae, Ulvophyceae, Trebouxiophyceae, Chlorodendrophyceae, others

## Key Facts

- Contain chlorophyll a and b, giving them their characteristic green color.
- Store energy as starch inside chloroplasts — same strategy as land plants.
- Cell walls composed of cellulose, linking them to the soil carbon cycle.
- Range from microscopic single-celled organisms to large multicellular seaweeds.
- Found in soil surface crusts where they contribute to soil stabilization and organic matter.
- Some species form symbiotic relationships with fungi to create lichens.
- Reproduce through both sexual and asexual means, including fragmentation, zoospores, and conjugation.
- Common soil genera include *Chlorella*, *Chlamydomonas*, and *Trebouxia*.
- Important oxygen producers in aquatic and soil surface environments.

## Role in the Soil Food Web

In Lowenfels' soil food web framework, Chlorophyta (green algae) contribute:

- **Primary production:** As photosynthesizers, green algae on soil surfaces convert sunlight and CO₂ into organic matter, contributing to the soil food web's energy base.
- **Soil crust formation:** Soil-surface algae bind soil particles together, reducing erosion and improving water infiltration.
- **Nitrogen contribution:** Some green algae associate with nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria in biological soil crusts.
- **Food source:** Green algae are consumed by protozoa, nematodes, and microarthropods in the soil food web.
- **Decomposition substrate:** When algae die, their cellulose-containing cell walls become food for bacteria and fungi.
- **Moisture retention:** Algal crusts help retain soil surface moisture, benefiting other soil organisms.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Soil health:** The presence of green algae on soil surfaces indicates adequate moisture and light. Their absence may indicate chemical contamination or excessive disturbance.
- **Composting:** Algae in compost contribute green (nitrogen-rich) material and microbial diversity.
- **Compost tea:** Algae and their associated microorganisms contribute to the biological complexity of compost tea.
- **Mushroom cultivation:** Algae are generally not direct participants in mushroom substrate preparation, but their presence in outdoor growing environments indicates healthy biological activity.
- **Aquaponics:** Green algae are common in aquaponic systems; *Chlorella* species are cultivated as nutritional supplements and fish feed.
- **Bioremediation:** Some Chlorophyta species are used for wastewater treatment and heavy metal bioremediation.
- **Biofuel research:** Green algae (especially *Chlorella*, *Scenedesmus*) are extensively researched for biodiesel and biofuel production due to high lipid content.

## Environmental Conditions

- **Light:** Requires light for photosynthesis; found on exposed soil surfaces and in shallow water.
- **Moisture:** Requires adequate moisture; most active in wet conditions.
- **Temperature:** Wide range tolerance; most active in moderate temperatures (15–30°C).
- **pH:** Most species prefer neutral to slightly acidic conditions.
- **Nutrients:** Require inorganic nutrients (N, P, K) for growth.

## Ecological Significance

Chlorophyta play vital roles in aquatic ecosystems as primary producers, contributing significantly to global oxygen production. Many species form symbiotic relationships with fungi to create lichens, while others serve as model organisms in scientific research.
