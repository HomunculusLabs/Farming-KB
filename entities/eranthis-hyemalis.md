## Overview

Eranthis hyemalis, commonly known as winter aconite, is a low-growing perennial tuberous plant in the family Ranunculaceae. It is one of the earliest flowering plants in [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]].

All parts contain cardiac glycosides similar to those found in hellebores, making the plant toxic if ingested. Despite this, it is extensively planted in gardens for its valuable late-winter color.

The genus name Eranthis derives from the Greek "er" (spring) and "anthos" (flower), literally meaning "spring flower." In reality the plant often blooms well before the official start of spring.

Eranthis hyemalis is a member of the tribe Caltheae within Ranunculaceae. It is most closely related to the genus Caltha (marsh marigolds) based on molecular evidence.

The species is sometimes called "winter wolf"s bane" in older herbals, though this name is now largely disused. It has no relationship to the true wolfsbane ([[eranthis-cilicica]] from Turkey, which has larger flowers and more deeply divided bracts. The two species hybridize readily in cultivation.

## Morphology

Winter aconite is a small plant, reaching only 5–8 cm in height at flowering time. The foliage expands significantly after flowering, eventually reaching 15–20 cm.

Each tuber produces one to three flowering stems bearing a single terminal flower. Flowers are bright yellow, 2–3 cm in diameter, with 5–8 petaloid sepals surrounding a ring of tubular nectaries.

True petals are absent; the showy yellow "petals" are actually modified sepals. Below each flower is a whorl of three deeply divided green bracts forming a collar-like ruff.

Leaves are basal, long-stalked, and palmately divided into five to nine lobes. They emerge after flowering and persist through spring before dying back by early summer.

The plant grows from a small, irregularly shaped tuber 1–2 cm in diameter. Tubers resemble small lumps of dark earth when dried, making them easy to overlook during planting.

Fruit is a small follicle containing 3–5 olive-brown seeds. Seeds have a small elaiosome and are dispersed by ants.

The seeds require warm stratification followed by cold treatment to germinate. This complex dormancy mechanism ensures seedlings emerge at the optimal time in early spring.

Germination rates are typically low, but seedlings that establish are long-lived. Colonies expand primarily through self-seeding rather than tuber multiplication.

## Distribution and Habitat

Eranthis hyemalis is native to southern and central Europe, from eastern France through Switzerland, Italy, and the Balkans to Bulgaria. Natural populations occur in deciduous woodland on calcareous soils.

The species is widely naturalized across northern Europe, particularly in the United Kingdom, Netherlands, and Germany. Naturalized populations persist around old estates, churchyards, and parkland.

It has also naturalized in parts of [[galanthus-nivalis]]), crocuses, and early iris species. This combination creates the classic "late winter bulb garden" effect.

Winter aconite is one of the first nectar sources available to early-emerging bees and flies. Honeybees actively forage on the flowers during warm winter days when few other plants are in bloom.

Notable cultivars include 'Flore Pleno' with double flowers and 'Guinea Gold' with bronze-tinted bracts. The species is hardy to USDA zones 3–7 and RHS hardiness rating H7.
