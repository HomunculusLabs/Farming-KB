# Ipomoea violacea L.

## Common Names

Morning Glory, Tlililtzin, Piule, Badoh Negro, Heavenly Blue Morning
Glory, Pearly Gates.

## Taxonomy

**Family:** Convolvulaceae (Morning Glory Family)
**Order:** Solanales
**Genus:** Ipomoea
**Species:** I. violacea L.

**Synonym:** Ipomoea rubrocaerulea Hook.

The taxonomy of psychoactive morning glories has been a source of
considerable confusion. Ipomoea violacea is frequently listed alongside
[[turbina-corymbosa]] and Argyreia nervosa as bindweeds with
psychoactive seeds. The species has often been confused or synonymized
with Turbina corymbosa (Rivea corymbosa) in ethnobotanical literature,
owing to the similarity of their seeds and their parallel use in
Mesoamerican ritual. However, the two species are morphologically
distinguishable and represent distinct lineages within the
Convolvulaceae.

The genus Ipomoea is the largest in the Convolvulaceae, comprising
over 500 species distributed throughout tropical and subtropical
regions worldwide. Most are twining vines, and several species are
cultivated as ornamentals, food crops (sweet potato, I. batatas), or
for their psychoactive seeds.

## Description

Ipomoea violacea is an annual twining vine that can grow rapidly,
producing long, trailing or climbing stems. The vines are herbaceous,
smooth or slightly hairy, and may extend several meters in length
during a single growing season when supported by adjacent vegetation
or structures.

Leaves are entire, ovate, and deeply cordate at the base, measuring
2.5 to 4 in (6 to 10 cm) in length and 0.75 to 3 in (2 to 8 cm)
in width. The leaf blade is smooth on both surfaces, with prominent
palmate venation. The petioles are slender and 1.5 to 4 in (4 to 10
cm) long, allowing the leaves to orient toward available light.

The inflorescence is typically three- or four-flowered, borne on
short peduncles arising from the leaf axils. The calyx consists of
five green, lanceolate to ovate sepals.

The corolla is trumpet-shaped (funnelform), one of the most striking
features of the plant. Flowers vary in color from white to red,
purple, blue, or violet-blue, measuring 2 to 2.75 in (5 to 7 cm)
wide at the mouth of the corolla. The throat is often paler or white.
Flowers open in the early morning and close by midday, giving the
plant its common name. The popular ornamental cultivars "Heavenly Blue"
and "Pearly Gates" are selections of Ipomoea violacea.

The fruit is a globose to ovoid capsule, approximately 0.5 in (1.2
cm) in diameter, containing four to six seeds. Seeds are ovoid to
somewhat angular, dark brown to black, and 0.15 to 0.2 in (4 to 5
mm) long. The seed coat is hard and durable.

## Distribution and Habitat

Ipomoea violacea is native to the region extending from Mexico to
South America, with its center of diversity in Oaxaca, southern Mexico.
It is commonly found in disturbed areas, along fences and roadsides,
in cultivated fields, and at the edges of tropical and subtropical
forests. The plant is widely cultivated as an ornamental annual vine
throughout the world and has become naturalized in many tropical and
subtropical regions outside its native range.

## Chemistry

The seeds of Ipomoea violacea contain the same ergoline alkaloids as
Turbina corymbosa, though the relative proportions may differ between
species. The principal psychoactive constituents include:

- **Lysergic acid amide (LSA, ergine)** — the major active alkaloid,
  producing mild to moderate hallucinogenic effects including visual
  distortions, altered thought patterns, and dreamlike states.
- **Lysergic acid hydroxyethylamide (LSH)** — a related ergoline
  alkaloid contributing to the psychoactive profile.
- **Other ergoline derivatives** — including isolysergic acid amide,
  chanoclavine, and elymoclavine in trace amounts.

The total alkaloid content of the seeds is relatively low compared
to synthetic lysergamides, and the psychoactive effects require the
ingestion of a substantial number of seeds (typically 50 to several
hundred).

## Ethnobotanical Uses

Ipomoea violacea is known to the Aztecs as Tlililtzin (from the
Nahuatl meaning "black"), reflecting the dark color of the seeds. The
seeds were employed in essentially the same manner as those of
[[turbina-corymbosa]] (Ololiuqui), being ground, soaked in water,
and consumed as an infusion to induce visionary states during
religious and divinatory ceremonies.

The plant is known by various indigenous names across southern Mexico:

- **Piule** — used by the Chinantec and Mazatec peoples of Oaxaca,
  who continue to employ the seeds in traditional healing and
  divinatory practices.
- **Badoh Negro** — the Zapotec name, distinguishing the black seeds
  of Ipomoea violacea from the lighter seeds of Turbina corymbosa
  (Badoh Blanco).

The Chinantec use piule seeds in ceremonial contexts similar to those
of other indigenous groups, often under the guidance of a curandero or
shaman. The Mazatec incorporate the seeds into healing rituals that
may also involve the use of psychoactive mushrooms and salvia.

## History

The ritual use of Ipomoea violacea seeds in Mesoamerica extends back
to pre-Columbian times, as documented in early colonial chronicles.
Despite centuries of suppression by colonial and later Mexican
authorities, the tradition of using morning glory seeds in indigenous
healing and ceremonial practice has persisted in Oaxaca and surrounding
regions. In the 20th century, the psychoactive properties of the seeds
were confirmed through chemical analysis by [[albert-hofmann]] and others,
who identified the ergoline alkaloids responsible for their effects.
