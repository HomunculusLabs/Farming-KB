## Description

Sectoring is a strain isolation technique in [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] where different mycelial strains growing on the same agar plate are separated based on their morphological characteristics and growth rates. When multiple strains from a single spore germination develop as distinct "islands" of mycelium on an agar plate, cultivators can select and transfer the most vigorous sectors to fresh plates to purify individual strains. The technique is fundamental to developing productive commercial mushroom cultures from wild spore collections.

## Classification

- **Category**: Cultivation technique / Strain isolation
- **Purpose**: Selecting superior strains from mixed spore germinations
- **Equipment**: Agar plates, [[laminar-flow-hood]], scalpel or dental pick, alcohol lamp
- **Skill level**: Intermediate; requires ability to distinguish mycelial morphologies

## Key Facts

- Multiple strains from a single spore streak plate can have completely different morphology, climate and soil preferences, metabolic requirements, and growth characteristics
- Mycelium will not fuse with genetically non-identical mycelium; where different strains meet, they build walls and develop thickening lines of division called "barrage zones"
- Colonies that are more aggressive in growth rate may make better candidates for expansion and further experimentation
- To sector, excise a small bit of the outermost mycelium (the hyphal tips) from the most promising colony and transfer to a fresh agar plate
- After transfer, a purified sector shows much more even and symmetrical growth, a textbook sign of successful strain purification from its original spore group

## Process

1. Germinate spores on an agar plate via [[spore-streaking]] technique
2. Allow multiple strains to develop as distinct colonies or "islands"
3. Observe the colonies for differences in growth rate, density, and morphology
4. Identify areas where colonies collide and form walls (thickening lines of division)
5. Select the most aggressive or healthy-looking colony based on growth rate and morphology
6. Using a sterile scalpel or dental pick, excise a small section of the outermost hyphal tips
7. Transfer the excised tissue to the center of a fresh agar plate
8. Allow the culture to colonize the new plate
9. Evaluate the growth pattern: uniform, symmetrical colonization indicates a successfully purified strain

## Selection Criteria

- **Growth rate**: Faster-colonizing sectors are generally preferred for cultivation; watch for colonies that "surge ahead" of others
- **Density**: Thick, cottony, rhizomorphic growth is typically more vigorous than thin or appressed growth
- **Uniformity**: Even, symmetrical colonization of the new plate suggests a pure isolate rather than a mixed culture
- **Morphology**: Different strains may show fluffy, appressed (flat), cottony, or rhizomorphic (strand-like) growth patterns
- **Aggressiveness**: Colonies that outpace their neighbors on the original plate often carry desirable vigor traits

## Relationship to Other Techniques

Sectoring is closely related to and often follows spore streaking (germinating spores on agar). The resulting isolated sectors can then be expanded through the culture pipeline: agar to master [[grain-spawn]] (generation 1), grain to grain (generation 2), grain to substrate spawn (generation 3), and finally substrate to fruiting. Proper sectoring at the earliest stage ensures that the most vigorous genetics propagate through the entire production system.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Sectoring is a fundamental skill for cultivators working with spore-based propagation. Since each spore germination produces genetically unique individuals with different characteristics, sectoring allows the cultivator to screen multiple strains and select the most promising ones for further development. This process is essential for developing productive commercial strains, maintaining genetic diversity in cultivation programs, and discovering superior cultivars from wild collections.
