# Oil Spill Mycoremediation

## Description

Oil spill mycoremediation is the application of fungi—particularly [[oyster-mushrooms]] ([[pleurotus-species]])—to break down and decompose hydrocarbon-based pollutants from oil spills. Fungi accomplish this using their extracellular enzymes, which cleave hydrocarbon molecules into smaller units that are more easily biodegraded by other fungi and bacteria in a sequential degradation process. This technique leverages the natural similarity between hydrocarbons and lignin (the woody component of wood), which fungi have evolved to decompose efficiently.

## Classification

- **Category:** Bioremediation technique
- **Field:** Environmental mycology / Mycoremediation
- **Primary organisms:** Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus spp.)
- **Target contaminants:** Hydrocarbons, petroleum products, oils

## Key Facts

- Fungi use extracellular enzymes to break apart and cleave hydrocarbon molecules into smaller units that are more easily biodegraded by other organisms in sequence.
- Hydrocarbons are chemically similar in architecture to lignin, the woody component of wood that fungi naturally decompose, so adaptation to oil degradation occurs quickly—typically within a few weeks.
- Oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus species) are the primary organisms used, feeding on oil after exhausting their natural food source (substrate such as spent [[oyster-mushroom]] media).
- The classroom-scale protocol involves simulating an oil spill using vegetable oil in an aquarium, combining the oil with shredded dried grass as an absorbent carrier, then layering the oiled plant matter between sheets of oyster-mycelium-colonized cardboard in sealed bins.
- Within weeks, large yellow droplets or metabolites form on the myceliated surface, representing enzymes exuded by the mycelium to break down the oil.
- After approximately one month, red wiggler worms can be added to bins to test whether the treated material has become safe enough to support macro-invertebrate life.
- The experiment can extend up to three months, with bins tested at weeks 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 by adding worms at different stages.
- Real-world applications range from addressing major oceanic oil spills to small-scale homeowner oil contamination from vehicle maintenance.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Oil spill mycoremediation demonstrates the remarkable versatility of fungal enzymes and their potential for environmental cleanup. For mushroom cultivators, this application represents a value-added use for spent substrate that would otherwise be composted or discarded. The technique highlights the biochemical sophistication of fungi, particularly their ability to adapt their enzymatic pathways to novel substrates. Understanding the mechanism—where fungi transition from decomposing lignin to breaking down structurally similar hydrocarbons—provides insight into fungal metabolism and substrate adaptation that is directly relevant to cultivation practices.

## Classroom Protocol Summary

1. Prepare oyster-mycelium-colonized cardboard sheets two weeks in advance.
2. Fill aquarium half full with water and add measured vegetable oil.
3. Add shredded dried grass to absorb the floating oil.
4. Layer oiled grass between colonized cardboard sheets in small bins.
5. Cover bins with lids drilled with gas exchange holes.
6. Inspect weekly, observing mycelium and yellow metabolite formation.
7. After approximately 4 weeks, begin adding red wiggler worms to test safety.
8. Monitor for up to 12 weeks, recording observations at each interval.
