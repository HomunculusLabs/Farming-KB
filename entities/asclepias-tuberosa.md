## Overview

**Asclepias tuberosa**, commonly known as butterfly weed, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Apocynaceae (formerly Asclepiadaceae). It is native to eastern and central North America, ranging from southern Canada through Florida and west to Arizona.

The species is among the most widely recognized milkweeds due to its brilliant orange to yellow inflorescences and its importance as a nectar source for adult butterflies. It is listed as endangered or threatened in several northeastern states due to habitat loss.

Unlike most milkweeds, it produces little to no milky latex sap. This characteristic, combined with its showy flowers and [[fukuoka-ideal-rice-plant-morphology-compact-growth-high-yield]] habit, has made it one of the most commercially popular native perennials in North American horticulture.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Asclepias tuberosa** L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The specific epithet *tuberosa* refers to the tuberous root system. Several infraspecific taxa have been proposed but are not widely accepted.

Plants grow 30–90 cm tall from a deep tuberous taproot. Stems are upright, hairy, and sparingly branched. Leaves are alternate, lanceolate to oblong, 5–12 cm long, with entire margins.

Flowers are borne in terminal umbels, each with 20–80 individual florets. Corolla lobes are bright orange to yellow-orange, reflexed, 7–10 mm long. The corona hoods are erect and orange, exceeding the horns.

Follicles (seed pods) are 8–12 cm long, spindle-shaped, and covered in soft spines. Seeds are flat with tufts of silky white coma (filamentous hairs) for wind dispersal.

The root system consists of a large, fleshy tuberous taproot that can extend 30+ cm deep. This root structure stores carbohydrate reserves that enable survival through drought and fire.

Unlike most *Asclepias* species, *A. tuberosa* produces little to no milky latex when stems or leaves are cut. The absence of copious latex is a diagnostic field character that distinguishes it from sympatric milkweeds.

Several cultivars have been selected for ornamental use, including 'Hello Yellow' (yellow-flowered), 'Gay Butterflies' (mixed orange-red tones), and 'Orange Marmalade' (deep orange). These selections are widely available in the native plant nursery trade.

## Distribution and Habitat

Butterfly weed is native to most of the eastern and central [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], extending into southern Ontario and Quebec. Its range spans from New England south to Florida and west through the Great Plains to New Mexico and Arizona.

The species occupies open, dry habitats including prairies, glades, savannas, roadsides, and open woodlands. It prefers well-drained sandy or gravelly soils and full sun exposure.

It is tolerant of drought and poor soils once established, making it a common component of tallgrass prairie remnants and xeric limestone glades. It is hardy to USDA zones 3–9.

The species has been documented at elevations up to 1,500 m in the Appalachian Mountains. In the western portion of its range, populations become increasingly scattered, limited to mesic canyon bottoms and riparian edges.

Fire suppression has reduced the open habitat that *A. tuberosa* depends on in many regions. Prescribed fire and mechanical disturbance can benefit existing populations by reducing woody encroachment and competing vegetation.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

Like other *Asclepias* species, *A. tuberosa* contains cardenolide glycosides, though at lower concentrations than *A. syriaca* or *A. curassavica*. Major cardenolides include uzarigenin and its glycosides.

Root tissue contains glycosyloxyphenanthro[1,10-b]lactones, flavonoid glycosides, and triterpenoids. The cardenolide content varies significantly among populations and growing conditions.

The species has demonstrated anti-inflammatory activity in vitro. Root extracts showed inhibitory effects on cyclooxygenase pathways in rodent models, supporting some traditional anti-inflammatory applications.

Additional in vitro studies have identified moderate antimicrobial activity of root extracts against Gram-positive bacteria. Cardenolide compounds from the species have also shown cytotoxic activity against certain cancer cell lines, though clinical relevance has not been established.

The cardenolide profile of *A. tuberosa* differs notably from other milkweeds. It contains lower total cardenolide concentrations than *A. syriaca* or *A. incarnata*, which may explain the reduced toxicity reported for butterfly weed compared to other species in the genus.

## Ethnobotanical and Medicinal Use

Indigenous peoples of [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America used the root of *A. tuberosa* extensively in traditional medicine. The Cherokee used root preparations for pleurisy, pulmonary ailments, and as a diaphoretic. The Menominee employed it for digestive disorders and as a general tonic.

The common name "pleurisy root" derives from its historical use in treating pleurisy and other pulmonary conditions by Euro-American herbalists in the 19th century. It was an official drug in the United States Pharmacopoeia from 1820 to 1905.

Physicians of the Eclectic medical school frequently prescribed *A. tuberosa* root for respiratory infections, bronchitis, and pneumonia. The dried root was a common component of patent medicines sold in the late 19th and early 20th centuries across the United States.

The root was typically prepared as a decoction or tincture. Recommended doses in historical texts ranged from 0.5–2 g of dried root. The plant is NOT considered edible — all parts may be toxic if ingested in quantity due to cardenolide content.

## Ecological Role

*A. tuberosa* is a larval host plant for the monarch butterfly (*Danaus plexippus*), though monarchs lay eggs on it less frequently than on other milkweeds. The low latex production may make it a less suitable host compared to *A. syriaca*.

The flowers are exceptionally attractive to pollinators, including numerous native bee species, butterflies, and hummingbirds. It is considered one of the premier nectar plants for butterfly gardens in eastern North America.

The deep taproot makes the species highly drought-resistant and provides soil stabilization on slopes and in disturbed sites. It contributes to prairie ecosystem diversity and serves as an indicator species for high-quality native grasslands.

Beyond monarchs, the flowers support a diverse assemblage of [[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]] including soldier beetles, syrphid flies, and native bees such as bumblebees (*Bombus* spp.) and sweat bees (*Lasioglossum* spp.). It ranks among the top native perennials for pollinator species richness in eastern tallgrass prairie studies.

The silky seed coma has historically been used as a substitute for kapok in pillow stuffing and life preservers. During World War II, milkweed seed floss was collected commercially as a buoyancy material, with *A. tuberosa* among the species harvested.

## Cultivation

Butterfly weed is widely cultivated as an ornamental perennial and is a staple of native plant gardens and pollinator habitat restoration projects. It is propagated from seed or by division of the tuberous root.

Seeds require cold stratification at 4°C for 30–60 days for reliable germination. Direct sowing in fall or [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] is effective. Transplanting is difficult due to the deep taproot — container-grown seedlings are preferred over bare-root divisions.

Seed viability is relatively short, typically declining after 1–2 years of dry storage at room temperature. Storing seeds at cool, dry conditions extends viability. Germination rates of 70–90% are achievable with proper cold stratification and light exposure during germination.

The species performs best in full sun with well-drained soil. It is intolerant of heavy clay and waterlogged conditions. Established plants are highly drought-tolerant and require minimal supplemental irrigation. Flowering occurs from June through August.

Deadheading spent flowers can extend the bloom period by several weeks. However, leaving some follicles to mature allows for seed dispersal and naturalization. Plants typically take 2–3 years from seed to reach flowering size in cultivation.
