## Overview

**Euphorbia milii** Des Moul. (crown of thorns) is a woody, spiny succulent shrub native to Madagascar. It is one of the most widely cultivated ornamental Euphorbia species, grown for its persistent clusters of colorful bracts in shades of red, pink, yellow, orange, and white.

The common name **crown of thorns** references the dense, sharp spines along the stems, which are said to recall the biblical crown of thorns. The species has been cultivated for over 200 years and was introduced to Europe by Baron Milius, the French governor of Réunion, around 1821.

Euphorbia milii is remarkable for its **nearly continuous flowering** habit. Under warm conditions with adequate light, plants produce blooms year-round, making them among the longest-flowering houseplants available.

The plant contains a **toxic, caustic latex** typical of Euphorbia species. The sap can cause skin irritation, eye damage, and gastrointestinal distress if ingested, requiring careful handling during pruning.

Numerous cultivars and hybrids have been developed, particularly the **Thai Crown of Thorns** series, which features enormous bracts and compact growth habits bred over decades by Thai horticulturists.

Euphorbia milii holds the **Guinness World Record** for the longest-flowering potted plant, with documented specimens producing blooms continuously for over 20 years in botanical garden collections.

In its native Madagascar, the plant is known as **songosongo** or **fatidahy**, names that reference its thorny, impenetrable thickets that serve as living fences in rural villages.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Euphorbia milii belongs to the family **Euphorbiaceae**, subgenus *Lacanthis*, section *Goniostema*. The species was described by Charles des Moulins in 1826 and named in honor of Baron Pierre Bernard Milius.

The species is highly **polymorphic** in its native Madagascar, with numerous described varieties and forms. Major varieties include var. *milii* (large bracts), var. *imperatae* (small bracts), and var. *tananarivae* (dwarf form).

The plant forms a **woody, scrambling shrub** 30–180 cm tall with succulent, greyish-brown stems armed with sharp, 1–3 cm spines. Stems are angular in cross-section when young, becoming more cylindrical with age.

Leaves are **bright green, obovate to spatulate**, 2–6 cm long, and are produced primarily on new growth. Under drought stress, the plant sheds leaves and enters a semi-dormant state, relying on stem photosynthesis.

The true flowers are **tiny cyathia** surrounded by pairs of showy, petal-like bracts that persist for weeks. Bract color ranges from the classic scarlet-red to deep pink, salmon, yellow, cream, and bicolors.

## Distribution and Habitat

Euphorbia milii is endemic to **Madagascar**, where it occurs in the dry deciduous forests and spiny thickets of the western and southern regions. It grows on limestone outcrops, in coastal scrub, and along forest margins.

The natural habitat features a **tropical dry climate** with a pronounced dry season lasting 6–8 months. Annual rainfall is 400–800 mm, falling almost entirely during the warm, humid summer months (November–March).

Plants grow as **scrambling shrubs** in thickets, often climbing through other vegetation using their spines for support. They are adapted to harsh, sun-exposed conditions with nutrient-poor, well-drained soils.

The species has become **naturalized in many tropical regions** worldwide, including Southeast Asia, East Africa, the Caribbean, and parts of Central and South America. In some areas it is considered a minor weed.

In Hawaii and Florida, escaped populations have established in **disturbed coastal habitats**, though the species is not generally considered aggressively invasive.

The species has been declared the **official flower of Guam** since 1965, reflecting its widespread cultivation across the Pacific islands. In Guam it is known locally as "flor de mayo" (May flower).

In East Africa, Euphorbia milii is planted as a **living security fence** around homesteads, taking advantage of its dense, impenetrable, thorny branches to deter intruders and livestock.

## Cultivation

Euphorbia milii is among the **easiest succulents to grow** indoors, tolerating a wide range of conditions including low humidity, irregular watering, and standard room temperatures. It is hardy to approximately USDA Zone 10.

Plants require **bright light to full sun** for best flowering. A minimum of 4–6 hours of direct sunlight daily ensures continuous bloom production. Insufficient light results in sparse flowering and leggy growth.

Watering should be **moderate during active growth**, allowing the soil to dry between waterings. During cooler months or when the plant shows signs of dormancy (leaf drop), watering should be reduced significantly.

The species thrives in **standard potting mix** amended with perlite or pumice for improved drainage. Unlike many succulents, Euphorbia milii tolerates regular potting soil provided it is not kept constantly wet.

Propagation is by **stem cuttings**, which root readily in warm, moist conditions. Cuttings should be allowed to callus for 2–3 days before planting to prevent rot. The latex flow should be stopped with cold water or rooting hormone before insertion.

Regular **fertilization** during the growing season (spring through autumn) promotes continuous flowering. A balanced, water-soluble fertilizer applied at half strength every two weeks is standard practice among commercial growers.

Repotting should be done **every 2–3 years** in spring, moving to a pot only slightly larger than the current one. Overpotting leads to excessive soil moisture and root rot.

## Toxicity and Precautions

The **milky latex** of Euphorbia milii contains diterpene esters (primarily phorbol esters) that are potent irritants and tumor promoters. Skin contact can cause redness, blistering, and dermatitis within minutes.

Eye exposure is a **medical emergency** — the latex can cause corneal burns and potentially permanent vision damage. Protective eyewear is strongly recommended when pruning or repotting.

Ingestion causes **severe gastrointestinal distress** including vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal pain. The plant should be kept away from children and pets, particularly cats, which are attracted to chew on the leaves.

Despite the toxicity, Euphorbia milii has been used in **traditional medicine** in Madagascar and Southeast Asia for treating parasitic infections, warts, and as a fish poison. These uses are not recommended due to the danger of the latex.

The latex has been investigated for **antimicrobial and anticancer properties** in laboratory studies. Phorbol esters show potent biological activity but are too toxic for direct therapeutic application.

Pets, particularly dogs and cats, are at **significant risk** from chewing on the stems or leaves. The ASPCA lists Euphorbia milii as toxic to cats, dogs, and horses.

## Horticultural Significance

Euphorbia milii is a **major commercial ornamental crop**, ranking among the top 10 potted flowering plants sold worldwide. The Thai horticultural industry has developed hundreds of named cultivars with exceptionally large, colorful bracts.

The **Thai Crown of Thorns** breeding program, begun in the 1970s, has produced cultivars with bracts up to 5 cm across in colors including deep crimson, pure white, golden yellow, and multicolor blends.

Modern breeding also targets **compact, self-branching habits** suitable for small containers and hanging baskets. Dwarf varieties such as 'Mini Bell' series remain under 30 cm tall with full-sized bracts.

The plant's long flowering period and drought tolerance make it **ideal for xeriscaping** in frost-free climates, where it can be planted as a permanent landscape specimen.

The plant's symbolism varies by culture — in Madagascar it represents **protection and endurance**, while in Thailand it is associated with good fortune and is commonly given as a housewarming gift.

In Brazil, Euphorbia milii is called **coroa-de-cristo** (crown of Christ) and is widely planted in cemetery gardens and church courtyards as an ornamental hedge.

Global annual production is estimated at **over 50 million plants**, with major production centers in Thailand, the Netherlands, and China supplying the worldwide potted plant market.
