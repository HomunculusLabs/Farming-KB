## Overview

*Phalaris arundinacea*, commonly known as reed canary grass, is a perennial rhizomatous grass in the family Poaceae.

It is native to [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of Europe, Asia, and [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]], but it has become a problematic invasive in wetland habitats.

It forms dense, monotypic stands that displace native vegetation and reduce biodiversity in riparian and wetland ecosystems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Phalaris arundinacea* L. is a member of the tribe Poeae within the subfamily Pooideae of Poaceae.

Plants are robust and rhizomatous, forming extensive clonal colonies that can cover large areas.

Stems are hollow, erect, and 60 to 200 cm tall, with swollen nodes and prominent ligules.

Leaves are flat, 10 to 30 cm long and 6 to 18 mm wide, with a prominent midrib and rough margins.

The inflorescence is a dense, cylindrical panicle 8 to 20 cm long, appearing from May to July.

Spikelets are 5 to 7 mm long, each containing a single floret with a short awn.

The rhizome system is extensive and aggressive, enabling rapid vegetative spread through wetland soils.

Roots penetrate deeply, contributing to the plant's ability to stabilize stream banks and tolerate flooding.

## Distribution and Habitat

Reed canary grass has a circumboreal distribution, occurring across Europe, temperate Asia, and North America.

Its native range likely includes Europe and western Asia; [[comparison-constructed-wetland-vs-backyard-pond]] treatment systems.

The species demonstrates significant capacity for [[phytoremediation]], accumulating heavy metals and excess nutrients from contaminated water.

However, its aggressive growth habit makes it one of the most problematic invasive grasses in North American wetlands.

Dense monocultures reduce plant species diversity and alter habitat structure for wildlife, particularly wetland birds.

The grass creates dense litter layers that inhibit seed germination of native species, further reinforcing monoculture.

Native insect herbivores provide limited control of invasive populations in North America.

## Cultivation and Management

Several cultivars have been developed for forage use, including 'Palaton', 'Rise', and 'Venture'.

Forage management requires cutting or grazing before heading to maintain acceptable [[coleman-winter-greens-nutritional-quality-cold-sweetening]].

Control of invasive populations relies on integrated approaches combining herbicide application, mowing, and flooding.

Glyphosate-based treatments followed by native species reseeding have shown moderate success in wetland restoration.

Repeated late-season mowing over multiple years can gradually reduce rhizome carbohydrate reserves.
