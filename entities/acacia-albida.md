# Acacia albida (Faidherbia albida)

## Description

*[[acacia]] albida* (now reclassified as *[[faidherbia-albida]]*), commonly known as the apple-ring acacia or winter thorn, is a nitrogen-fixing leguminous tree native to Africa and the Middle East. It is referenced by [[bill-mollison]] as a biological "chisel plough" used by African farmers on land too steep and stony for mechanical implements. This species is one of the most important agroforestry trees in sub-Saharan Africa due to its unique phenology — it is deciduous during the wet season and in leaf during the dry season.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Family:** Fabaceae (Leguminosae)
- **Subfamily:** Mimosoideae
- **Genus:** Faidherbia (formerly *Acacia*)
- **Species:** *F. albida*
- **Common names:** Apple-ring acacia, winter thorn, ana tree, gabindó, haraz (Sudan), kad (Sahel)

## Key Facts

- **Reverse phenology:** Unlike most tropical trees, *F. albida* sheds its leaves during the rainy season and retains them through the dry season. This allows crops beneath the tree to receive full sunlight when they need it most, while the canopy provides shade and leaf litter during the dry season.
- **Nitrogen fixation:** As a legume, it fixes atmospheric nitrogen through root-associated rhizobia, enriching the soil for surrounding crops. Studies have documented significant yield increases for crops grown under or near *F. albida* canopies.
- **Deep root system:** Roots penetrate deeply into the soil profile, bringing up nutrients and moisture from subsoil layers. This deep rooting makes it effective as a biological soil aerator — the "chisel plough" role Mollison describes.
- **Pod production:** Produces nutritious pods that serve as dry-season fodder for livestock, creating a valuable protein supplement when other forage is scarce.
- **Soil improvement:** Leaf fall during the dry season adds organic matter and nutrients to the soil surface precisely when crops are being established.
- **Drought tolerance:** Highly adapted to semi-arid and arid climates with rainfall as low as 250–400 mm annually.

## Traditional Use

African farmers have long recognised the value of *F. albida* in farming systems. Trees are deliberately retained in crop fields, where their reverse phenology eliminates competition for light during the growing season while providing shade, fodder, and soil enrichment during the dry season. This indigenous knowledge system predates and validates modern agroforestry science. In the Sahel, mature *F. albida* trees are often the only survivors of drought, providing critical ecosystem services when all other vegetation has died.

## Ecological Role

The species plays multiple ecological roles simultaneously:

- **Canopy shade:** Reduces soil temperature and evaporation during the dry season.
- **Leaf litter:** Adds organic matter and nutrients to soil surface.
- **Root channels:** Creates pathways for water infiltration and aeration at depth.
- **Pod production:** Supports livestock and wildlife through the driest months.
- **Habitat:** Provides nesting sites and shelter for birds and insects.
- **Windbreak:** Reduces wind erosion and desiccation of nearby crops.

## Permaculture Applications

- Mollison identifies *Acacia albida* as one of several species used globally as biological alternatives to [[mechanical-soil-conditioning]], alongside *Gliricidia*, *Casuarina*, and *Tamarix*.
- In [[permaculture-design]], it exemplifies the principle of using biological agents to perform work that would otherwise require fossil fuel energy and machinery.
- For agroforestry systems in tropical and subtropical regions, it provides a model for integrating trees with crops without competition for light.
- Its role in soil structure improvement through deep root penetration is directly relevant to rehabilitation of compacted agricultural lands.
- The nitrogen fixation capability reduces or eliminates the need for synthetic nitrogen fertilisers in associated cropping systems.

## Design Considerations

When incorporating *F. albida* into permaculture or agroforestry designs, consider:

- Spacing based on mature canopy size (typically 15–25 m diameter).
- The need for pod harvest if livestock integration is planned.
- The timing of leaf fall relative to crop establishment schedules.
- Trees should be established several years before intercropping begins to allow root systems to develop their soil-conditioning function.

## Relevance to Cultivation

The reverse phenology means that crops and trees are not competing for the same resources at the same time — a principle that can guide species selection in any polyculture design. For cultivators working in semi-arid tropical regions, *F. albida* represents one of the most valuable support species available, combining soil conditioning, nitrogen fixation, fodder production, and microclimate modification in a single species.

## Distribution

Widespread across sub-Saharan Africa from Senegal to Ethiopia, south to South Africa. Also found in the Middle East and the Indian subcontinent. Typically occurs in savanna, woodland, and along watercourses in semi-arid zones. The species has been introduced to other semi-arid regions worldwide for agroforestry purposes.
