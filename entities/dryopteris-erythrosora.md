## Overview

Dryopteris erythrosora, commonly known as the autumn fern or Japanese shield fern, is an evergreen to semi-evergreen fern in the family Dryopteridaceae. It is native to East Asia, including Japan, Korea, China, and Taiwan.

The species is widely cultivated in temperate gardens for its distinctive coppery-pink new fronds that mature to glossy dark green. This seasonal color change gives the plant its common name, as the spring flush of new growth resembles autumn foliage.

Autumn fern typically reaches 45-60 cm in height and 60-90 cm in spread, forming dense, arching clumps of broadly triangular fronds. It is valued for its shade tolerance, pest resistance, and low maintenance requirements.

The species was awarded the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit (AGM) in the United Kingdom, confirming its reliability and garden worthiness under British growing conditions.

The species is one of the most cold-hardy Dryopteris species in cultivation, surviving temperatures to -15°C (5°F) when established. This hardiness, combined with its ornamental qualities, has made it a staple of shade gardens worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Dryopteris erythrosora was first described by D.C. Eaton in 1858 from specimens collected in Japan. The species epithet erythrosora means "red spore," referring to the reddish color of immature sori.

The species belongs to the genus Dryopteris, family Dryopteridaceae, order Polypodiales. Modern phylogenetic studies place it within the Dryopteris carthusiana group, closely allied to D. cycadina and D. dilatata.

Fronds are bipinnate, 40-70 cm long and 15-30 cm wide, with a stipe that is one-third to one-half the total frond length. The stipe is densely covered with pale brown, lanceolate scales, especially near the base.

Pinnae are 10-15 pairs, lanceolate to ovate-lanceolate, with serrate margins and an auricle at the base of the upper pinnae surface. New fronds emerge with a striking coppery-pink to orange-red coloration.

Sori are medial, positioned roughly halfway between the midrib and pinna margin. The indusium is kidney-shaped (reniform) and persistent. Immature sori are reddish, darkening to brown as spores mature.

Spores are ellipsoidal, brown, and 35-50 μm long. The species is a sexual diploid with 2n = 82 chromosomes.

The chromosome count of 2n = 82 places D. erythrosora in the diploid range for the genus. Some populations in southern Japan show slight morphological variation, suggesting possible incipient differentiation.

## Distribution and Habitat

Dryopteris erythrosora is native to eastern Asia, occurring in Japan (Honshu, Shikoku, Kyushu), the Korean Peninsula, eastern and central China, and Taiwan. It is found at elevations from 100 to 2,000 meters.

The species grows in moist, shaded forests, forest margins, ravines, and along stream banks. It prefers acidic to neutral soils rich in organic matter, typical of forest understories in temperate and subtropical regions.

Soil temperatures in its native forest habitats remain relatively stable year-round, buffered by the forest canopy. This stability allows the evergreen fronds to persist through mild winters without significant cold damage.

It occurs as both a lithophyte on moist rocky outcrops and as a terrestrial plant in humus-rich forest floor soils. The species tolerates deeper shade than many cultivated ferns.

In its native range, autumn fern is a common component of the understory in deciduous broadleaf forests, mixed conifer-broadleaf forests, and bamboo thickets. It often forms extensive colonies through rhizomatous spread.

The species has been introduced to cultivation in North America and Europe and occasionally naturalizes in sheltered locations. Naturalized populations have been reported in the southeastern United States and parts of the British Isles.

## Cultivation

Dryopteris erythrosora is one of the most popular ferns for temperate shade gardens, widely available in nurseries throughout Europe, North America, and temperate Asia. It is considered one of the easiest ferns to cultivate successfully.

The species performs best in partial to full shade with consistent moisture. It tolerates morning sun in cooler climates but requires protection from hot afternoon sun, which scorches the fronds.

A soil pH range of 5.0-7.0 is acceptable, with slightly acidic conditions preferred. The addition of compost, leaf mold, or other organic matter at planting improves soil structure and moisture retention.

Established plants are moderately drought-tolerant but perform best with regular watering during dry periods. A 5-8 cm layer of organic mulch helps conserve soil moisture and suppress weeds.

Several named cultivars are widely available. 'Prolifica' produces new fronds over an extended period. 'Brilliance' has more intensely colored new growth. 'Crispa' features wavy, crested pinna margins.

Propagation is straightforward by division of mature clumps in early spring. Spore propagation is also possible but slower, requiring 1-2 years to produce plants of transplantable size.

The species is resistant to deer browsing, making it particularly valuable in gardens where deer pressure is a concern. Rabbits generally avoid it as well.

Established plants are long-lived and can persist for decades in the same location without requiring division. A single clump may eventually spread to 1 meter in diameter.

## Ecology and Uses

In its native habitats, Dryopteris erythrosora provides cover and foraging habitat for small forest fauna. The dense frond canopy shelters ground-dwelling invertebrates and amphibians.

The species has no significant recorded uses in traditional medicine or cuisine. Its primary value is ornamental, and it is not known to be toxic to humans or domestic animals.

Some Dryopteris species contain filicic acid and related phloroglucinol derivatives that have anthelmintic properties. However, D. erythrosora has not been specifically investigated for bioactive compounds.

In garden settings, autumn fern serves as an effective groundcover for shaded areas. Its evergreen to semi-evergreen habit provides year-round interest in mild climates, while the coppery new fronds provide seasonal color contrast.

The species is not known to be invasive in any region where it has been introduced for cultivation. Its moderate growth rate and clumping habit limit its potential for unwanted spread.

Autumn fern is generally free from serious pest and disease problems. Slugs and snails may feed on young fronds in humid conditions, but damage is rarely severe enough to warrant treatment.

The genus Dryopteris contains approximately 250-300 species distributed worldwide, with centers of diversity in eastern Asia and the Himalayas. The name derives from the Greek drys (oak) and pteris (fern), reflecting the common habitat of many species in oak woodlands.

## Related Species

Dryopteris erythrosora is part of a complex of closely related East Asian species that can be difficult to distinguish in cultivation:

- **Dryopteris cycadina** (Shaggy shield fern) has darker, more heavily scaly stipes and less colorful new frond emergence. It is native to Japan and China.

- **Dryopteris dilatata** (Broad buckler fern) is a European species with more deeply divided pinnae and a more open growth habit. It is less heat-tolerant than D. erythrosora.

- **Dryopteris filix-mas** (Male fern) is a widespread European and North American species with taller fronds and less ornamental new growth. It is one of the most common temperate garden ferns.

- **Dryopteris affinis** (Golden shield fern) has golden-brown scales on the stipe and rachis, giving the fronds a distinctive two-toned appearance. It is native to western Europe.

- **Polystichum acrostichoides** (Christmas fern) is a North American species with a similar evergreen habit and landscape use, though it belongs to a different genus.
