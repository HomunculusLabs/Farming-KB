## Overview

Dryopteris erythrosora, commonly known as the autumn fern or Japanese shield fern, is an evergreen to semi-evergreen fern in the family Dryopteridaceae. It is native to East Asia, including Japan, Korea, China, and Taiwan.

The species is widely cultivated in temperate gardens for its distinctive coppery-pink new fronds that mature to glossy dark green. This seasonal color change gives the plant its common name, as the spring flush of new growth resembles autumn foliage.

Autumn fern typically reaches 45-60 cm in height and 60-90 cm in spread, forming dense, arching clumps of broadly triangular fronds. It is valued for its shade tolerance, [[dryopteris-carthusiana]] group, closely allied to D. cycadina and D. dilatata.

Fronds are bipinnate, 40-70 cm long and 15-30 cm wide, with a stipe that is one-third to one-half the total frond length. The stipe is densely covered with pale brown, lanceolate scales, especially near the base.

Pinnae are 10-15 pairs, lanceolate to ovate-lanceolate, with serrate margins and an auricle at the base of the upper pinnae surface. New fronds emerge with a striking coppery-pink to orange-red coloration.

Sori are medial, positioned roughly halfway between the midrib and pinna margin. The indusium is kidney-shaped (reniform) and persistent. Immature sori are reddish, darkening to brown as spores mature.

Spores are ellipsoidal, brown, and 35-50 μm long. The species is a sexual diploid with 2n = 82 chromosomes.

The chromosome count of 2n = 82 places D. erythrosora in the diploid range for the genus. Some populations in southern Japan show slight morphological variation, suggesting possible incipient differentiation.

## Distribution and Habitat

Dryopteris erythrosora is native to eastern Asia, occurring in Japan (Honshu, Shikoku, Kyushu), the Korean Peninsula, eastern and central China, and Taiwan. It is found at elevations from 100 to 2,000 meters.

The species grows in moist, shaded forests, forest margins, ravines, and along stream banks. It prefers acidic to neutral soils rich in [[fungal-ecology-forest-floor]] soils. The species tolerates deeper shade than many cultivated ferns.

In its native range, autumn fern is a common component of the understory in deciduous broadleaf forests, mixed conifer-broadleaf forests, and bamboo thickets. It often forms extensive colonies through rhizomatous spread.

The species has been introduced to cultivation in [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] and parts of the British Isles.

## Cultivation

Dryopteris erythrosora is one of the most popular ferns for temperate shade gardens, widely available in nurseries throughout Europe, [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] at planting improves soil structure and moisture retention.

Established plants are moderately drought-tolerant but perform best with regular watering during dry periods. A 5-8 cm layer of organic mulch helps conserve soil moisture and [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]]. Spore propagation is also possible but slower, requiring 1-2 years to produce plants of transplantable size.

The species is resistant to deer browsing, making it particularly valuable in gardens where deer pressure is a concern. Rabbits generally avoid it as well.

Established plants are long-lived and can persist for decades in the same location without requiring division. A single clump may eventually spread to 1 meter in diameter.

## Ecology and Uses

In its native habitats, Dryopteris erythrosora provides cover and foraging habitat for small forest fauna. The dense frond canopy shelters ground-dwelling invertebrates and amphibians.

The species has no significant recorded uses in traditional medicine or cuisine. Its primary value is ornamental, and it is not known to be toxic to humans or [[cordyceps-cancer-bioactive-compounds]].

In garden settings, autumn fern serves as an effective groundcover for shaded areas. Its evergreen to semi-evergreen habit provides year-round interest in mild climates, while the coppery new fronds provide seasonal color contrast.

The species is not known to be invasive in any region where it has been introduced for cultivation. Its moderate growth rate and clumping habit limit its potential for unwanted spread.
