## Overview

Gladiolus cardinalis is a striking winter-growing geophyte endemic to the Western Cape of South Africa. It is known for its brilliant scarlet to crimson-red flowers that bloom in late spring.

This species is highly valued as an ornamental garden plant and has contributed genetics to many modern Gladiolus hybrids. Its vivid coloring and elegant spike form make it one of the most recognizable wild Gladiolus species.

The species was introduced to European horticulture in the late 18th century. It remains a parent of several registered cultivars bred for cut-flower production.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Tracheophytes
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Monocots
- **Order:** Asparagales
- **Family:** Iridaceae
- **Genus:** Gladiolus
- **Species:** G. cardinalis (Burm.f.) Aiton

The species was first described by Nicolaas Burman and later reclassified by William Aiton in 1789. It belongs to the section Blandus of Gladiolus, which includes several other red-flowered species.

Synonyms include Gladiolus blandus Aiton and Gladiolus speciosus Andrews. The species epithet "cardinalis" refers to the vivid scarlet color resembling a cardinal's robes.

Molecular phylogenetic studies place G. cardinalis firmly within the Cape clade of Gladiolus. This clade represents a rapid radiation linked to the diversification of fynbos pollinators.

## Morphology

Gladiolus cardinalis grows from a globose corm approximately 15–25 mm in diameter. The corm tunic is fibrous and often densely layered.

Plants reach 40–80 cm in height during the flowering season. The stem is erect, slender, and usually unbranched.

Leaves are narrow, linear, and typically 3–5 in number. They are sword-shaped (ensiform), gray-green, and reach 20–40 cm long.

The inflorescence is a unilateral spike bearing 4–8 flowers. Each flower is 40–55 mm long, bright scarlet to crimson, with a tubular perianth.

The lower three tepals are marked with a white to cream median streak edged in dark red. This contrasting throat pattern serves as a nectar guide for pollinators.

Stamens are unilateral and arch forward. The anthers are 8–12 mm long and produce cream-colored pollen.

The style arches over the stamens, terminating in three short branches. The ovary is inferior and three-locular.

Flowers open sequentially from the base of the spike upward. Each flower lasts 2–3 days, giving a total display period of about two weeks per stem.

The fruit is a loculicidal capsule that splits along three valves at maturity. Seeds are light brown, angular, and approximately 2–3 mm in diameter.

Capsules are oblong, 15–20 mm long, and contain many angular brown seeds. Seeds are winged, aiding wind dispersal in their native fynbos habitat.

## Distribution and Habitat

Gladiolus cardinalis is endemic to the Western Cape Province of South Africa. Its range extends from the Cape Peninsula eastward to the Hottentots Holland Mountains.

It grows in damp, marshy sites along streams and seeps at low to mid-elevations. Populations are typically found in areas with winter rainfall and Mediterranean-type climate.

The species is associated with fynbos vegetation and often occurs in nutrient-poor, acidic soils. It prefers sites with full sun to partial shade and reliable winter moisture.

Population sizes fluctuate with rainfall patterns. Years of abundant winter precipitation produce spectacular massed flowering displays.

Flowering occurs from October to December in the southern hemisphere. The winter-growing habit means plants are dormant during the hot, dry summer months.

## Cultivation

Gladiolus cardinalis requires a Mediterranean climate regime with wet winters and dry summers. Corms should be planted in autumn at a depth of 5–8 cm in well-drained soil.

The species prefers full sun exposure and benefits from consistent moisture during the growing season. A low-phosphorus fertilizer suits the fynbos-adapted roots.

Container cultivation is common where rainfall patterns do not match the species' requirements. Pots should be allowed to dry completely during the summer dormancy period.

In suitable climates, mass plantings create dramatic drifts of red flowers. The species combines well with other winter-growing Cape bulbs such as Watsonia and Babiana.

Corms can be propagated by offsets or seed. Seed-grown plants may take 2–3 years to reach flowering size.

The species is not frost-hardy and requires protection below −2 °C. In cold climates, corms must be lifted and stored dry during winter.

Pest problems include thrips and spider mites, particularly under glass. A dry summer dormancy helps reduce fungal disease pressure on corms.

## Ecological Role

The red tubular flowers of Gladiolus cardinalis are primarily pollinated by the orange-breasted sunbird (Anthobaphes violacea). This bird-pollination syndrome is common among red-flowered Cape Iridaceae.

The species is a component of the Cape Floristic Region, one of the world's biodiversity hotspots. It contributes to the seasonal floral display that supports diverse pollinator communities.

Corms serve as a food source for small mammals such as Cape porcupines and striped field mice. This herbivory pressure has likely driven the evolution of the fibrous corm tunic.

The species shows adaptations to periodic fire in the fynbos. Corms survive underground while above-ground vegetation is consumed, resprouting vigorously post-fire.

Gladiolus cardinalis may hybridize naturally with other section Blandus species where ranges overlap. These natural hybrids have been important in the development of garden Gladiolus cultivars.

Fire plays a role in the species' life cycle. Many Cape Gladiolus species flower more prolificately in the first year after fynbos fires, stimulated by smoke and increased light.

The species is classified as Near Threatened due to habitat loss from urban expansion and invasive alien plants. Several populations near Cape Town have been extirpated by development.
