## Taxonomy and Morphology

Persea palustris (Raf.) Sarg. belongs to the family Lauraceae, subfamily Lauroideae, tribe Perseae. The species was first described by Constantine Samuel Rafinesque and later validated by Charles Sprague Sargent.

The tree is typically 10 to 20 meters tall with a straight trunk 20 to 40 centimeters in diameter. In favorable wetland conditions, specimens occasionally exceed 25 meters in height with a dense, broadly rounded crown.

Bark is reddish-brown to gray, divided into irregular plates and ridges. Inner bark has a characteristic spicy, camphor-like aroma when cut, similar to other members of the genus.

Leaves are simple, alternate, elliptic to lanceolate, 5 to 15 centimeters long and 2 to 5 centimeters wide. The upper surface is dark green and glossy, while the lower surface is paler with fine pubescence along the midrib.

## Distribution and Habitat

Persea palustris ranges from southeastern Virginia southward through the coastal plain of the Carolinas, Georgia, Florida, Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana, extending into eastern Texas. The species is most abundant in Florida and along the Gulf Coastal Plain.

The tree occupies a variety of wetland habitats, including cypress swamps, bayhead swamps, wet flatwoods, pond margins, and streamside forests. It tolerates both permanently saturated soils and seasonally flooded conditions.

Elevation range is typically from sea level to approximately 100 meters. The species is largely restricted to the Atlantic and Gulf Coastal Plains, rarely extending inland beyond the fall line.

Swamp bay commonly occurs in association with other wetland tree species including [[nyssa-sylvatica]], [[ph-and-nutrient-availability-garden-soils]].

Fire ecology is an important aspect of swamp bay habitat. The species resprouts vigorously from the root crown after fire, but severe fire during drought conditions can cause significant mortality, especially in smaller diameter individuals.

Periodic fire in flatwoods ecosystems maintains the open canopy structure that allows swamp bay to persist alongside fire-adapted pines. Fire suppression has allowed some wetland hammocks to succeed toward closed-canopy hardwood forest, reducing swamp bay abundance.

## Ecology and Interactions

Persea palustris provides critical food and habitat for numerous wildlife species. The drupes are consumed by a wide variety of birds including northern bobwhite, wild turkey, woodpeckers, thrushes, and mockingbirds.

Mammalian frugivores including raccoons, opossums, foxes, and black bears also consume the fruit and disperse seeds. Seed passage through animal digestive tracts may enhance germination rates through scarification of the seed coat.

The species is the primary larval host plant for the palamedes swallowtail butterfly, Papilio palamedes. Caterpillars feed exclusively on Lauraceae foliage, and the decline of Persea populations due to laurel wilt has negatively impacted this butterfly species.

Laurel wilt disease, caused by the fungus Raffaelea lauricola and vectored by the Asian redbay ambrosia beetle, Xyleborus glabratus, has caused catastrophic mortality in Persea palustris populations since its detection in Georgia in 2003.

The disease has spread rapidly throughout the southeastern United States, killing an estimated 300 million redbay and swamp bay trees across 11 states. Mortality rates approach 90 percent or higher in infected stands, with no effective treatment available for wild trees.

The ecological consequences extend beyond Persea palustris itself. Loss of this canopy species alters [[mollison-wet-tropical-forest-structure-and-polyculture]], increases light penetration to the understory, and reduces food resources for the many animal species that depend on its fruit and foliage.

Understory plant communities in laurel-wilt-affected stands show measurable shifts in species composition. Shade-intolerant species increase in abundance while shade-adapted species decline, fundamentally changing the successional trajectory of affected forests.

The ambrosia beetle vector, Xyleborus glabratus, is attracted to Persea wood volatiles and can locate host trees from considerable distances. This highly efficient host-finding mechanism contributes to the rapid spread of the disease across the landscape.
