Its broad, umbrella-shaped crown and vibrant floral display have made it an iconic street and landscape tree across the tropics.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Delonix regia* was originally described as *Poinciana regia* by William Jackson Hooker in 1827 and transferred to the genus *Delonix* by Constantine Samuel Rafinesque in 1837.

The genus name derives from the Greek *delos* (evident) and *onyx* (claw), referring to the prominent clawed petals.

The tree is fast-growing, typically reaching 8–12 meters in height with a crown spread of 12–15 meters at maturity.

Exceptional specimens may exceed 15 meters in height, with trunk diameters of 50–80 cm.

The bark is smooth and grey on young trees, becoming rough and fissured with age.

Leaves are bipinnately compound, 30–60 cm long, with 10–25 pairs of pinnae, each bearing 15–30 pairs of small, oblong leaflets.

Each leaflet is approximately 8–13 mm long and 3–5 mm wide, giving the foliage a fine, feathery appearance.

The tree is deciduous, shedding its leaves during the dry season before or during flowering.

Flowers are borne in large, terminal racemes and are strikingly colored with four scarlet-red or orange-red petals and one upright petal marked with white and yellow.

The uppermost "standard" petal is the largest, approximately 5–7 cm long, with a distinctive white-yellow blotch near the base.

Fruits are large, flat, woody pods 30–60 cm long and 4–5 cm wide, turning dark brown at maturity.

Each pod contains 20–40 hard, oblong seeds approximately 1.5 cm long.

## Distribution and Habitat

*D. regia* is endemic to the dry deciduous forests of western Madagascar, where it is now endangered in its native range.

The species was introduced to tropical regions worldwide beginning in the early 19th century and has become naturalized in many areas.

It is extensively planted throughout the Caribbean, Central and [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]], East Africa, and northern Australia.

In its native Madagascar, it occurs in seasonally dry forest on well-drained soils at low elevations (0–500 meters).
