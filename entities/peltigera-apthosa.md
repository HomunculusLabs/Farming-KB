# Peltigera aphthosa

## Overview

**Peltigera aphthosa**, commonly known as the **dog lichen** or **common freckle pelt**, is a large foliose lichen in the family Peltigeraceae. It is one of the most recognizable members of the genus *Peltigera*, distinguished by conspicuous cephalodia on its upper surface.

The species has a broad circumpolar distribution across boreal and montane regions of the Northern Hemisphere. It is a tripartite cyanolichen, housing both green algae and cyanobacteria as photobionts, which gives it ecological versatility.

Its common name "dog lichen" derives from the resemblance of its cephalodia to flecks on a dog's coat. The species has a long history of use in traditional medicine, particularly in Nordic and Siberian folk practices.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Peltigera aphthosa** (L.) Willd. was originally described by Linnaeus as *Lichen aphthosus* in 1753 and later transferred to *Peltigera* by Carl Ludwig Willdenow in 1787.

The thallus is large, reaching 10–30 cm in diameter, with broadly lobed margins that are often ascending at the tips. The upper surface is grayish-green to brown when dry, becoming darker green when moist.

The lower surface is pale with prominent, raised veins that form a reticulate network. Rhizines are concentrated along the margins and along the veins, appearing as tufted white to brown hairs.

The most distinctive feature is the presence of **cephalodia** — dark green to brown gall-like structures on the upper surface. These contain the cyanobacterial photobiont *Nostoc*, which fixes atmospheric nitrogen.

Apothecia are produced along the lobe margins and are reddish-brown with disc-shaped fruiting bodies. They are relatively uncommon compared to vegetative propagation through thallus fragmentation.

The photobiont partnership is tripartite: the primary photobiont is the green alga *Coccomyxa*, while *Nostoc* is housed within the cephalodia. This dual photobiont strategy is shared with several other *Peltigera* species.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Peltigera aphthosa* has a circumpolar distribution across Europe, Asia, and North America. It occurs throughout Scandinavia, the British Isles, the Alps, and across Siberia to the Russian Far East.

In North America, it ranges from Alaska across Canada to the northeastern United States and extends southward in mountainous regions. It is particularly abundant in the Pacific Northwest and the Canadian boreal forest.

The species is found in moist, shaded habitats including coniferous and mixed forests, muskegs, and peatlands. It frequently colonizes mossy substrates, rotting logs, and soil overlying rocks in humid microsites.

Elevation ranges from sea level to approximately 2,000 meters, though it is most common below 1,500 meters. It prefers areas with high humidity and relatively stable temperatures, avoiding exposed, dry sites.

It is often found in association with other cyanolichens such as *Peltigera membranacea* and *Peltigera leucophlebia*, forming mixed lichen communities on forest floors.

## Ecology and Symbiosis

As a tripartite lichen, *P. aphthosa* benefits from both photosynthetic partnerships. The green alga *Coccomyxa* provides carbon through photosynthesis under moderate light conditions.

The cyanobacterium *Nostoc* housed in cephalodia performs both photosynthesis and nitrogen fixation. This gives the lichen a competitive advantage on nitrogen-poor substrates, contributing significantly to forest floor nitrogen budgets.

Studies have estimated that *Peltigera* species with cephalodia can fix between 0.5 and 5.0 kg of nitrogen per hectare per year. This makes them ecologically important in boreal forest ecosystems where nitrogen is often limiting.

The lichen serves as a food source for various invertebrates, including oribatid mites and springtails. Caribou and reindeer occasionally browse *Peltigera* species during winter when other forage is scarce.

Fungal parasites of *P. aphthosa* include *Illosporiopsis christiansenii* and *Stigmidium peltigerae*, which are specialist lichenicolous fungi found exclusively on *Peltigera* hosts.

The thallus is sensitive to air pollution, particularly sulfur dioxide and excess nitrogen deposition. Its presence is considered an indicator of relatively clean air quality in forest environments.

## Traditional and Modern Uses

In traditional Nordic and Siberian medicine, *P. aphthosa* was used as a poultice for wounds, burns, and skin infections. The lichen was believed to have antimicrobial properties, likely due to secondary metabolites produced by the mycobiont.

Sami people of northern Scandinavia reportedly used *Peltigera* species as an emergency food source. The lichens were boiled to remove bitter compounds and then eaten, though they were considered a famine food rather than a dietary staple.

In Russian folk medicine, decoctions of *P. aphthosa* were used to treat coughs and lung ailments, a practice paralleling the use of *Lobaria pulmonaria* in European herbalism. The doctrine of signatures may have influenced this application.

Modern chemical analysis has identified several bioactive compounds including tenuiorin, methyl gyrophorate, and peltigerine. Some of these show antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activity in laboratory assays.

Research interest in *Peltigera* secondary metabolites has increased with the search for novel antimicrobial agents. Extracts of *P. aphthosa* have shown activity against Gram-positive bacteria in vitro.

## Chemistry and Secondary Metabolites

*Peltigera aphthosa* produces a characteristic suite of lichen secondary metabolites, known as lichen acids. These compounds serve defensive functions, deterring herbivory and microbial colonization.

The primary secondary metabolites include tenuiorin, methyl gyrophorate, and gyrophoric acid. These triterpenoids and depsides are deposited in the cortical layers of the thallus.

Additional compounds identified from *P. aphthosa* include peltigerine, zeorin, and various unknown fatty acids. The chemical profile is useful for distinguishing *P. aphthosa* from morphologically similar *Peltigera* species.

TLC (thin-layer chromatography) is the standard method for identifying *Peltigera* secondary metabolites. *P. aphthosa* can be reliably separated from *P. leucophlebia* and *P. membranacea* by its distinct spot pattern.

Some of these compounds have demonstrated antibacterial properties in bioassays. Tenuiorin shows moderate activity against *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Bacillus subtilis* in vitro.

## Similar Species

*Peltigera aphthosa* is most easily confused with *Peltigera leucophlebia*, which also bears cephalodia. However, *P. leucophlebia* has conspicuous white veins on the upper surface that are absent in *P. aphthosa*.

*Peltigera membranacea* lacks cephalodia entirely and has a smoother, more continuous upper surface. Its lower surface is also less prominently veined than that of *P. aphthosa*.

*Peltigera canina* is another similar species but typically has smaller thalli and narrower lobes. Its cephalodia, when present, are usually smaller and less conspicuous than those of *P. aphthosa*.

The color of the upper surface can help distinguish these species when fresh. *P. aphthosa* tends to be a brighter green when moist compared to the more grayish tones of *P. canina*.

## Conservation Status

*Peltigera aphthosa* is not currently listed as threatened on a global scale. However, it has experienced declines in parts of its range due to habitat loss and air pollution.

In central and western Europe, populations have decreased significantly since the industrial era. Acid rain and atmospheric deposition of nitrogen compounds have degraded many formerly suitable habitats.

The species is listed on several national red lists, including those of Switzerland and parts of Germany. In boreal regions of Fennoscandia and Canada, populations remain robust.

Conservation recommendations include maintaining old-growth forest stands with high humidity and protecting peatland habitats where the lichen is most productive.
