# Yucca filamentosa

**Common names:** Adam's Needle, Beargrass, Needle Palm, Spoonleaf Yucca
## Overview
- **Growth form:** Evergreen perennial shrub, acaulescent (stemless or nearly so)
- **Lifespan:** Long-lived; individual rosettes persist 20+ years; clonal colonies indefinite
- **Native range:** SE United States, Virginia to Florida, west to Louisiana
- **Key trait:** Classic obligate pollination mutualism with yucca moths (Tegeticula spp.)
- **Landscape role:** Accent specimen, xeriscape focal point, mass planting for erosion control
- **Multi-use:** Edible flowers/stalks, fiber crop, soap-producing roots
- **Ornamental value:** Dramatic architectural form; showy white panicles in early summer
- **Hardiness:** One of the most cold-hardy yuccas; zones 5-10

## Taxonomy
- **Binomial:** Yucca filamentosa L.
- **Family:** Asparagaceae (subfamily Agavoideae)
- **Order:** Asparagales
- **Authority:** Carl Linnaeus, 1753 (Species Plantarum)
- **Genus:** Yucca L. (~40-50 species, Americas and Caribbean)
- **Synonyms:**
  - Yucca filamentosa var. concava (Haw.) Engelm.
  - Yucca filamentosa var. smalliana (Fernald) H.E. Ahles
  - Yucca concava Haw.
  - Yucca smalliana Fernald (sometimes separate species)
  - Yucca flaccida Haw. (often merged with Y. filamentosa)
- **Etymology:** "filamentosa" refers to conspicuous curly white filaments along leaf margins

## Morphology
- **Habit:** Stemless evergreen shrub; dense basal rosette 60-120 cm diameter
- **Leaves:** 30-75 cm long, 2.5-5 cm wide, rigid, lanceolate, sword-shaped, dark green
- **Leaf margins:** Entire with numerous thin, curly white filaments (peeling leaf fibers)
- **Leaf apex:** Sharp terminal spine; handle with care
- **Trunk:** Usually absent; very old specimens may develop short procumbent stem to 30 cm
- **Inflorescence:** Tall paniculate flower spike 1.5-3 m from center of rosette
- **Flowers:** Pendulous, bell-shaped, creamy-white, 5-7 cm long, 6 tepals, waxy texture
- **Flowering period:** June to July; blooms once per rosette, then rosette dies
- **Fruit:** Erect, woody, loculicidal capsule 5-8 cm long, 2.5-4 cm broad
- **Seeds:** Numerous, black, flat, winged, ~6-8 mm; 20-30% consumed by moth larvae
- **Root system:** Deep taproot with fibrous laterals; extremely drought-resistant
- **Monocarpic:** Flowering rosette dies after seed set; offsets persist

## Distribution & Habitat
- **Native range:** SE US coastal plain from VA through Carolinas, GA, northern FL
  - Inland to Piedmont, extending west to AL, MS, LA
  - Disjunct populations north to NJ along Atlantic coast
- **Naturalized:** Widely beyond native range in eastern US and parts of Midwest
- **Habitat:** Dry sandy coastal plains and sandhills; rocky barrens and glades
  - Oak-pine woodlands, forest edges, roadsides, disturbed areas, dune systems
- **Elevation:** Sea level to ~300 m
- **Soil:** Sandy, gravelly, or rocky; tolerates poor low-fertility substrates
- **Moisture:** Very drought-tolerant; intolerant of prolonged wet or waterlogged soils
- **Light:** Full sun to partial shade; flowers best in full sun
- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 5-10 (to -29°C / -20°F with good drainage)

## Ecological Relationships
- **Obligate pollination mutualism:**
  - Exclusively pollinated by yucca moth *Tegeticula yuccasella* (Prodoxidae)
  - Female collects pollen into a ball, actively transfers to yucca stigma
  - Moth oviposits eggs into ovary; larvae develop within seed capsule
  - Larvae consume ~20-30% of seeds; remainder remain viable
  - Neither species reproduces without the other — textbook coevolution
- **Cheater species:** *Parategeticula* spp. lay eggs without pollinating
- **Herbivory resistance:** Saponins + tough fibers deter browsing; highly deer-resistant
- **Leaf herbivores:** Yucca plant bug (*Halticotoma valida*), scale insects
- **Nesting:** Dense rosettes shelter small mammals and invertebrates
- **Successional role:** Pioneer on sandy, disturbed, or nutrient-poor sites

## Ethnobotanical Use
- **Fiber production:**
  - Leaves yield strong, durable fibers via retting or scraping
  - Used for rope, cordage, fishing line, baskets, and mats
  - Southeastern Indigenous peoples (Cherokee, Choctaw, Seminole) used fiber extensively
- **Soap and shampoo:**
  - Roots rich in saponins; crushed roots produce rich lather with water
  - Used for washing clothes, hair, and body; mild antiseptic properties
  - Traditionally used for dandruff and skin conditions
- **Edible parts:** Flowers raw or cooked (mild, sweet); young stalks roasted like asparagus; buds pickled; immature pods occasionally roasted
- **Medicinal (traditional):** Root poultices for joint pain and arthritis (saponins/yuccaols); topical for wounds and skin eruptions
  - Caution: saponins toxic in large doses
- **Other uses:** Dried stalks as walking sticks; root saponins as fish stupefiant; ornamental in Europe since 1600s

## Active Compounds
- **Saponins (steroidal glycosides):**
  - Primary bioactive class; 3-10% dry weight in roots, also in leaves
  - Major aglycones: sarsasapogenin, smilagenin, markogenin
  - Glycosides: yucca saponins YS-1 through YS-8
  - Responsible for foaming, hemolytic activity, anti-inflammatory effects
- **Yuccaols A-E:**
  - Unique phenolic compounds (resveratrol oligomers) from Yucca species
  - Potent anti-inflammatory; inhibit NF-κB and COX-2 pathways
  - Strong free radical scavenging; yuccaol E most potent
- **Resveratrol derivatives:**
  - Trans-resveratrol in roots and leaves
  - Unique oligostilbenes from oxidative coupling of resveratrol units
  - Antioxidant and potential chemopreventive activity
- **Other:** Flavonoids (quercetin, kaempferol glycosides), phenolic acids, trace coumarins and tannins
- **Pharmaceutical interest:** Arthritis, cholesterol reduction, livestock feed additives

## Permaculture Applications
- **Xeriscaping:** Zero supplemental irrigation once established; strong architectural form
- **Erosion control:** Deep roots stabilize sandy banks, slopes, road cuts, dune restoration
- **Deer resistance:** Sharp tips + saponins eliminate browsing; valuable in high-deer areas
- **Fire resistance:** Succulent leaves provide fire retardancy; recovers from root crown after fire
- **Fiber crop:** Perennial zero-input fiber source on marginal land; homestead-scale cordage/baskets
- **Food production:** Flowers and stalks provide seasonal edible yield for food forests
- **Pollinator support:** Essential host for yucca moths; nocturnal nectar extends pollinator hours
- **Companion planting:** Pairs with Opuntia, Echinacea, Baptisia, Schizachyrium on dry sites
- **Marginal land:** Thrives on degraded, compacted, nutrient-poor soils; reclamation species

## Cultivation Notes
- **Light:** Full sun (6+ hours); tolerates light afternoon shade in hot climates
- **Soil:** Well-drained sandy, gravelly, or loamy; pH 5.5-7.5 (tolerates alkaline)
- **Moisture:** Extremely drought-tolerant once established; water sparingly first season
- **Spacing:** 90-120 cm for mass planting; 150+ cm as specimen
- **Propagation — offsets:** Most reliable; separate in spring or early fall; callus 1-2 days
- **Propagation — seed:** Stratify 30 days at 4°C; sow at 18-24°C; germinates 2-4 weeks
- **Fertilization:** Not required; avoid excess nitrogen (promotes soft, disease-prone growth)
- **Pruning:** Remove dead leaves and spent stalks; wear gloves
- **Pests:** Generally pest-free; watch for yucca plant bug, scale, agave snout weevil (rare)
- **Disease:** Root/crown rot in poorly drained soils — most common cause of death
- **Winter:** No protection needed in zones 5-10 if well-drained; cold-wet soils more dangerous than cold
