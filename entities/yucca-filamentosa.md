# Yucca filamentosa

## Overview

- **Common names:** Adam's Needle, Beargrass, Needle Palm,
  Spoonleaf Yucca
- **Growth form:** Evergreen perennial shrub, acaulescent
  (stemless or nearly so)
- **Lifespan:** Long-lived; individual rosettes persist 20+
  years; clonal colonies are indefinite
- **Native range:** Southeastern [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], Virginia to
  Florida, west to Louisiana
- **Key trait:** Classic obligate pollination mutualism with
  yucca moths (Tegeticula spp.)
- **Multi-use:** Edible flowers and stalks, fiber crop,
  soap-producing roots
- **Hardiness:** One of the most cold-hardy yuccas; USDA
  Zones 5-10

## Taxonomy

- **Binomial:** Yucca filamentosa L.
- **Family:** Asparagaceae (subfamily Agavoideae)
- **Order:** Asparagales
- **Authority:** Carl Linnaeus, 1753 (Species Plantarum)
- **Genus:** Yucca L. (approximately 40-50 species in the
  Americas and Caribbean)
- **Synonyms:**
  - Yucca filamentosa var. concava (Haw.) Engelm.
  - Yucca filamentosa var. smalliana (Fernald) H.E. Ahles
  - Yucca concava Haw.
  - Yucca smalliana Fernald (sometimes treated as separate
    species)
  - Yucca flaccida Haw. (often merged with Y. filamentosa)
- **Etymology:** "filamentosa" refers to the conspicuous curly
  white filaments along the leaf margins

## Description and Morphology

- **Habit:** Stemless evergreen shrub; dense basal rosette
  60-120 cm in diameter
- **Leaves:** 30-75 cm long, 2.5-5 cm wide, rigid,
  lanceolate, sword-shaped, dark green
- **Leaf margins:** Entire with numerous thin, curly white
  filaments (peeling leaf fibers) -- the namesake feature
- **Leaf apex:** Sharp terminal spine; handle with care
- **Trunk:** Usually absent; very old specimens may develop
  a short procumbent stem to 30 cm
- **Inflorescence:** Tall paniculate flower spike 1.5-3 m
  rising from the center of the rosette
- **Flowers:** Pendulous, bell-shaped, creamy-white, 5-7 cm
  long, 6 tepals, waxy texture; highly fragrant at night
- **Flowering period:** June to July; blooms once per rosette,
  then the flowering rosette dies (monocarpic)
- **Fruit:** Erect, woody, loculicidal capsule 5-8 cm long,
  2.5-4 cm broad
- **Seeds:** Numerous, black, flat, winged, approximately
  6-8 mm; 20-30% consumed by moth larvae
- **Root system:** Deep taproot with fibrous laterals;
  extremely drought-resistant
- **Monocarpic flowering:** The flowering rosette dies after
  seed set, but surrounding offsets (pups) persist and
  continue the colony

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Native range:** Southeastern US coastal plain from
  Virginia through the Carolinas, Georgia, northern Florida
- **Inland range:** Extending west to Alabama, Mississippi,
  Louisiana, and into the Piedmont region
- **Disjunct populations:** North to New Jersey along the
  Atlantic coast
- **Naturalized:** Widely beyond native range in eastern US
  and parts of the Midwest; also naturalized in Europe
- **Habitat:** Dry sandy coastal plains and sandhills; rocky
  barrens and glades; oak-pine woodlands, forest edges,
  roadsides, disturbed areas, and dune systems
- **Elevation:** Sea level to approximately 300 meters
- **Soil:** Sandy, gravelly, or rocky; tolerates poor
  low-fertility substrates; pH 5.5-7.5
- **Moisture:** Very drought-tolerant; intolerant of prolonged
  wet or waterlogged soils
- **Light:** Full sun to partial shade; flowers best in full
  sun
- **Hardiness:** USDA Zones 5-10 (survives to -29 degrees C
  with good drainage)

## Ecological Relationships

- **Obligate pollination mutualism:**
  - Exclusively pollinated by yucca moth Tegeticula
    yuccasella (family Prodoxidae)
  - Female moth collects pollen into a ball, actively
    transfers it to the yucca stigma
  - Moth oviposits eggs into the ovary; larvae develop
    within the seed capsule
  - Larvae consume approximately 20-30% of seeds; the
    remainder remain viable
  - Neither species reproduces without the other -- a
    textbook example of coevolution
- **Cheater species:** Parategeticula spp. lay eggs without
  pollinating, parasitizing the mutualism
- **Herbivory resistance:** Saponins plus tough leaf fibers
  deter browsing; the species is highly deer-resistant
- **Leaf herbivores:** Yucca plant bug (Halticotoma valida),
  scale insects
- **Nesting habitat:** Dense rosettes shelter small mammals
  and ground-dwelling invertebrates
- **Successional role:** Pioneer species on sandy, disturbed,
  or nutrient-poor sites; prepares ground for later
  successional species

## Ethnobotanical Uses

- **Fiber production:**
  - Leaves yield strong, durable fibers via retting or
    scraping
  - Used for rope, cordage, fishing line, baskets, and mats
  - Southeastern Indigenous peoples including Cherokee,
    Choctaw, and Seminole used the fiber extensively
- **Soap and shampoo:**
  - Roots rich in saponins; crushed roots produce rich lather
    with water
  - Used for washing clothes, hair, and body; mild
    antiseptic properties
  - Traditionally used for dandruff and [[cannabis-for-skin-conditions-detailed]]
- **Edible parts:**
  - Flowers can be eaten raw or cooked (mild, sweet flavor)
  - Young stalks roasted like asparagus
  - Buds can be pickled; immature pods occasionally roasted
- **Medicinal (traditional):**
  - Root poultices for joint pain and arthritis (saponins and
    yuccaols have anti-inflammatory activity)
  - Topical application for wounds and skin eruptions
  - Caution: saponins are toxic in large doses
