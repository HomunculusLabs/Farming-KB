# Ramaria botrytis (Coral Mushroom)

## Overview

**Ramaria botrytis**, commonly known as the clustered coral mushroom, pink-tipped coral, or rosy coral, is an edible basidiomycete mushroom in the family Gomphaceae. It is one of the most visually striking forest mushrooms, producing large, cauliflower-like fruiting bodies with branching coral-like structures that are white to cream-colored at the base, transitioning to beautiful pinkish-rose at the branch tips. Despite its showy appearance, it is often overlooked because it grows on the forest floor partially buried in [[dighton-fungal-decomposition-leaf-litter-dynamics]]. When young and fresh, it is considered a choice edible, though some individuals experience mild gastrointestinal upset.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Phylum:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Gomphales
- **Family:** Gomphaceae
- **Genus:** Ramaria
- **Species:** R. botrytis

The genus Ramaria is large and taxonomically challenging, containing over 200 described species worldwide. The name Ramaria derives from the Latin "ramus" (branch), referring to the characteristic branching coral-like fruiting bodies. The species epithet "botrytis" comes from the Greek "botrys" (cluster of grapes), describing the clustered branching pattern. Molecular phylogenetic studies have refined the classification of Ramaria species, and R. botrytis is placed in the subgenus Ramaria, section Laeticolora. Several varieties have been described based on color and geographic distribution, though their taxonomic status remains debated.

## Morphology and Identification

Ramaria botrytis produces some of the largest fruiting bodies among coral mushrooms:

- **Overall size:** 8–20 cm tall and 6–15 cm broad, exceptionally up to 25 cm. Individual specimens can weigh over 1 kg.
- **Branching pattern:** Repeatedly branched from a thick, fleshy base. The main stalk is short and stout, 2–4 cm thick, dividing into several primary branches that subdivide into numerous secondary and tertiary branches. The branching pattern is erect and symmetrical, somewhat resembling a cauliflower or coral colony.
- **Coloration:** The base and primary branches are white to pale cream or buff. The branch tips and younger growth are distinctly pink to rose-colored, sometimes with salmon or apricot tints. With age, the pink fades and the entire [[comparison-fruiting-body-vs-mycelium-extracts-medicinal-mushrooms]] may become brownish or ochraceous. The flesh is white.
- **Flesh:** Firm and white when young, becoming soft and crumbly with age. The flesh has a mild, pleasant odor and a mildly peppery or slightly bitter taste.
- **[[ramaria-formosa]]:** Similar in size and general appearance but has more orange to salmon-pink coloration throughout and a distinctly bitter or acrid taste. R. formosa is considered poisonous and causes gastrointestinal distress. The two species can be very difficult to distinguish without tasting (a small amount touched to the tongue and spat out).
- **[[beta-glucans]] and other polysaccharides contribute to immune system stimulation
- **Antimicrobial:** Some Ramaria species show activity against Gram-positive bacteria and select fungi

The pink pigmentation of the branch tips is due to carotenoid compounds, which may have additional antioxidant properties. The specific carotenoid profile of R. botrytis has not been fully characterized.

## Ecological Significance

As an ectomycorrhizal fungus, Ramaria botrytis contributes to [[fukuoka-pine-forest-ecosystem-collapse-cascade-matsutake]] health:

- Forms mutualistic associations with tree roots, enhancing nutrient and water uptake
- Contributes to soil [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] through decomposition of fruiting bodies
- Provides food habitat for invertebrates, particularly beetle larvae that develop within the fleshy branches
- Serves as a food source for small mammals, including squirrels and rodents
- The presence of diverse Ramaria species is an indicator of mature, undisturbed forest ecosystems with rich mycorrhizal communities

## Conservation Status

Ramaria botrytis is not currently listed as threatened, but like many ectomycorrhizal species, it is sensitive to:

- Forest clearing and habitat fragmentation
- Soil compaction from logging equipment
- Air pollution and acid deposition
- Changes in forest composition due to [[dighton-fungi-climate-change-feedback]]

In some European countries, the species has declined in areas with intensive [[mollison-pdc-forest-management-products]]. Collection for food should be done sustainably, leaving some specimens to complete their [[cantharellus-cibarius-entity]] — Golden Chanterelle, another prized mycorrhizal edible
