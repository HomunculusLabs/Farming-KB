# Jen-Hu Chang

## Description

Jen-Hu Chang was a geographer and agricultural climatologist whose seminal work *Climate and Agriculture: An Ecological Survey* (Aldine Publishing, 1968) became one of the most frequently cited references in Mollison's *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual*. Chang's quantitative approach to the relationship between climate factors and agricultural productivity provides much of the scientific data underpinning Mollison's recommendations for windbreak design, [[photosynthetic-efficiency]] analysis, and latitude effects on crop production.

## Key Facts

- Chang authored *Climate and Agriculture* (1968), a comprehensive ecological survey examining how climatic factors (temperature, radiation, wind, moisture) control agricultural productivity across global latitudinal and altitudinal gradients.
- **Cited extensively by Mollison** throughout Chapter 5 for multiple quantitative relationships:
  - Wind damage to crops: torn or frayed leaf laminae and broken midribs cause photosynthetic inefficiency of 20-85% in crops like corn and bananas.
  - Shelterbelt effects on crops: wind protection extends to 10 times the height of windbreak within which significant yield improvements are measurable.
  - Average radiation data across latitudes: 440 Langleys at 13 degrees N (Madras), 680 at 36 degrees N (Fresno), 450 at 64 degrees N (Fairbanks).
  - Photosynthetic efficiency: temperate radiation levels are 1-5 times that of the tropics in terms of useful agricultural light during the growing season.
- Chang's data on the relationship between wind speed and transpiration rates informed Mollison's recommendations on windbreak height-to-spacing ratios and the critical importance of permeability in windbreak design.
- His work provided the scientific basis for understanding why high-latitude summer agriculture can be highly productive despite short growing seasons: the combination of long photoperiods, moderate temperatures, and high radiation levels approaches the photosynthetic optimum.
- Chang's analysis of wind effects on different crop categories (wind-tolerant, exposure-tolerant, wind-sensitive) directly informed Mollison's windbreak species recommendations and configuration designs.

## Influence on Permaculture

Chang's quantitative climate data appears throughout Mollison's Chapter 5 (Permaculture Design for Climate):

- **Windbreak spacing**: The 10x-height rule for effective shelter and the 20x-height limit for soil conservation are derived from Chang's measurements of wind speed reduction behind various barrier types.
- **Crop yield data**: Citrus damage rates (50% unsheltered vs. 18.5% sheltered), cotton yield increases (17.4% at 5x height, falling to 7.9% at 10x height), and crop-specific wind sensitivity classifications.
- **Latitude and radiation**: The counter-intuitive finding that temperate summer radiation exceeds tropical radiation, supporting permaculture strategies for high-latitude food production and challenging assumptions about tropical agricultural superiority.
- **Wind-sensitive crop identification**: Chang's classification of crops by wind sensitivity (tolerant, exposure-tolerant, wind-sensitive) directly informs species selection and windbreak priority in permaculture design.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Mushroom growing structures**: Chang's data on wind speed effects on evapotranspiration applies to designing [[mushroom-fruiting-chambers]] where humidity control is critical. Windbreaks around outdoor mushroom yards can reduce misting requirements by 30-50% by reducing the drying effect of wind on substrates and fruiting bodies.
- **Photosynthetic efficiency and CO2**: Chang's work on CO2 limitation at high light intensity supports the practice of coupling mushroom cultivation (which produces CO2 as a metabolic byproduct) with greenhouse crop production (which consumes CO2) in integrated systems that benefit both production streams.
- **Site selection**: Understanding wind exposure effects on temperature and moisture helps identify optimal locations for mushroom log yards and outdoor cultivation beds, particularly the interaction between wind, slope, and aspect in creating favourable microclimates.
- **Climate data interpretation**: Chang's methodology for analysing climate factors provides a model for cultivators assessing new sites, evaluating radiation, wind, temperature, and moisture as interacting rather than independent variables that must be considered holistically.
