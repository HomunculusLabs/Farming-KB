## Description

Regolith is the layer of loose, unconsolidated rock and mineral material that overlies solid bedrock. In tropical regions, regolith is a defining landscape feature: it consists of deeply weathered and rotten rock, sometimes extending tens of metres below the surface, formed by millions of years of chemical weathering under hot, wet conditions. In permaculture and tropical land management, understanding regolith is essential for [[cation-exchange-capacity]] (CEC) means that any nutrients added to the soil are easily leached unless held by organic matter or plant roots.
- In oxidic kaolinitic soils (common in weathered volcanics), only kaolin clays and oxides of iron-aluminium remain, giving soil particles a net negative charge that is pH-dependent.

### Water Movement

- Regolith acts as a deep aquifer in many tropical landscapes, storing and slowly releasing water to streams and springs.
- The deep, permeable profile allows rapid infiltration of rainfall but also rapid lateral flow to streams, contributing to flash flooding in deforested catchments.
- Earthworks design in tropical regolith must account for the deep drainage capacity: swales and infiltration pits need to be sized appropriately to capture water before it moves through the regolith to depth.
- Groundwater in regolith zones is a critical water source for perennial streams and springs in tropical catchments.

### Root Zone

- The deep, loose nature of tropical regolith allows tree roots to penetrate far deeper than in temperate soils, accessing water and minerals from the weathered rock zone.
- This deep root access is a key advantage of perennial tree-based systems over annual crops in the tropics: trees can mine nutrients from the regolith that shallow-rooted annuals cannot reach.
- The mycelial web of pioneer legume roots is particularly important for maintaining the benefit of applied nutrients in deep regolith profiles.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

- **Earthworks design**: Understanding regolith depth and permeability is essential for designing swales, dams, and water storage in tropical landscapes. Regolith's high permeability can make dam sealing challenging without clay cores or gley techniques.
- **Tree crop advantage**: The deep regolith profile favours perennial tree-based polycultures over annual cropping systems. Trees access the full depth of the weathered profile for water and minerals.
- **Mineral supplementation**: Because tropical regolith is depleted in many essential minerals, permaculture systems often require mineral amendments such as crushed basalt, dolomite, or rock phosphate to sustain production.
- **Erosion vulnerability**: Disturbance of the vegetation cover exposes the nutrient-poor regolith to rapid erosion, converting productive land to bare ferricrete or silcrete hardpan. This is one of the most destructive outcomes of tropical deforestation.
- **Dam and pond construction**: The deeply weathered, permeable nature of tropical regolith often necessitates specialised sealing techniques (gley, clay cores, plastic liners) for water storage structures.

## Comparison with Temperate Regolith

| Feature | Tropical Regolith | Temperate Regolith |
|---------|------------------|-------------------|
| Depth | 5-50+ metres | 1-5 metres |
| Composition | Iron/aluminium oxides, kaolinite | Mixed minerals, some calcium |
| Nutrient status | Severely depleted | Moderately depleted |
| Formation time | Millions of years (no glaciation) | Thousands of years (post-glacial) |
| Permeability | High (deep drainage) | Variable |
| pH | Typically acidic (4.0-5.5) | Variable |

## Soil Rehabilitation in Deep Regolith

On deep granitic sands (a form of tropical regolith), Mollison recommends:

- Broadscale planting of palms, Albizzia, Inga, Acacia, and adapted leguminous trees to establish a light canopy.
- Small amounts of nutrient added at regular intervals to support pioneer establishment.
- Mulch-filled pits for palm establishment, with legume detritus used to establish fruit trees.
- Trench gardening with sealed bases to capture water and nutrients in free-draining sandy regolith.
