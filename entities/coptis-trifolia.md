## Overview

Coptis trifolia, the threeleaf goldthread or goldthread, is a small evergreen perennial herb in the family Ranunculaceae. It is named for its bright golden-yellow rhizomes, which have been used medicinally by Indigenous peoples [[huckleberries-and-wild-berries-of-north-america]] for centuries.

The plant forms low mats of glossy, three-parted leaves on moist forest floors and in boreal bogs. Its diminutive white flowers appear in early spring on short stems barely rising above the foliage.

The species has a circumboreal distribution, occurring across northern North America, northeastern Asia, and parts of northern Europe. It is one of approximately 15 species in the genus *Coptis*, most of which are Asian.

The common name "goldthread" refers to the thin, bright yellow rhizomes that creep through the moss and organic substrate. These rhizomes contain berberine and other isoquinoline alkaloids with documented [[mycorrhizal-networks-and-forest-communication]] healthy forest floor ecology.

## Chemistry and Medicinal Use

The golden rhizomes of *Coptis trifolia* contain a suite of isoquinoline alkaloids, primarily berberine (1–3% dry weight). Berberine is a well-characterized antimicrobial compound active against bacteria, fungi, and protozoa.

Additional alkaloids include coptisine, palmatine, and jatrorrhizine, though in lower concentrations than berberine. These compounds contribute synergistically to the plant's antimicrobial activity.

Indigenous peoples of northeastern North America used goldthread rhizomes as a bitter tonic, mouthwash for canker sores, and treatment for digestive ailments. The Micmac, Malecite, and Penobscot peoples all documented traditional uses.

The rhizomes were chewed directly or prepared as decoctions for treating sore throats and oral inflammation. Early European settlers adopted these practices, and goldthread appeared in the U.S. Pharmacopeia from 1820 to 1920.

Modern research confirms berberine's antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antidiabetic properties. However, clinical evidence for *C. trifolia* specifically remains limited compared to the extensively studied Asian species *[[plants-without-mycorrhizal-associations]] that enhance [[aact-microbial-foliar-nutrient-uptake-co2-stomata-ingham]] in the nutrient-poor, acidic soils it typically inhabits. These fungal partnerships are critical for growth in peatland environments.
