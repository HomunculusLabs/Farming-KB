## Overview

**Neonothopanus nambi** (Speg.) R.H. Petersen is a bioluminescent basidiomycete
fungus in the family Omphalotaceae (Agaricales). It is one of the brightest
known bioluminescent fungi, producing a steady greenish glow (λmax ≈ 530 nm)
visible to the naked eye in complete darkness. The species is native to
tropical and subtropical forests of [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], where it grows as a
saprotroph on [[fungal-dead-wood-diversity]] and fallen tree trunks.

N. nambi gained significant scientific attention in 2018 when its complete
bioluminescence pathway was elucidated, enabling the first fungal luciferase
system to be expressed in heterologous organisms. This breakthrough opened
new avenues for bioluminescent imaging, [[singh-fungal-biosensors-environmental-monitoring]], and
synthetic biology applications.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Kingdom:** Fungi — **Phylum:** Basidiomycota — **Class:** Agaricomycetes — **Order:** Agaricales — **Family:** Omphalotaceae — **Genus:** Neonothopanus — **Species:** N. nambi (Speg.) R.H. Petersen

The species was originally described as *Agaricus nambii* by Spegazzini in
1889 from material collected in southern Brazil. It has undergone several
taxonomic revisions, placed variously in *Pleurotus* and *Omphalotus* before
being assigned to *Neonothopanus* by Petersen in 1996. Molecular phylogenetic
studies confirm its placement within the bioluminescent clade of
Omphalotaceae, closely related to *[[omphalotus-olearius]]*.

The basidiocarp is pleurotoid to flabelliform (shelf-like to fan-shaped),
growing singly or in overlapping clusters. The cap is 2–8 cm wide,
convex to plane, with a smooth surface that is pale ochre to tan when fresh,
darkening to brownish with age. The margin is initially inrolled, becoming
wavy and sometimes lobed at maturity.

Gills are decurrent (running down the stipe), narrow to moderately broad,
crowded, and pale cream, sometimes developing a pinkish tinge with age.
The stipe is lateral or eccentric, 1–3 cm long, short and stout, with the
same coloration as the cap surface.

Basidiospores are 5–7 × 4–5 μm, ellipsoid to subglobose, smooth,
hyaline, and inamyloid. The entire basidiocarp — cap, gills, stipe, and
mycelium — is bioluminescent, though the gills typically produce the
brightest glow.

## Distribution and Habitat

Neonothopanus nambi is distributed across tropical and subtropical South
America, with confirmed collections from southern Brazil (São Paulo, Paraná,
Rio Grande do Sul), Argentina (Misiones province), Paraguay, and Uruguay.
Recent reports also extend its range into the Caribbean and tropical Africa,
though these records require molecular confirmation.

The species is saprotrophic, fruiting on decaying wood of broadleaf trees
in humid forests. It is most commonly found on fallen trunks and branches
in various stages of decomposition, from recently fallen material to
well-rotted logs. Fruiting occurs during warm, wet periods, typically
spring through autumn in its native range.

## Bioluminescence Biochemistry

Fungal bioluminescence operates through a luciferin-luciferase system
fundamentally different from those of fireflies, bacteria, or marine
organisms. The pathway was fully elucidated in 2018 by Kotlobay et al.,
who identified the complete four-enzyme cascade:

1. **Hispidin synthase (HispS)** converts malonyl-CoA to hispidin, a
   p-coumaroyl triketone precursor.
2. **Hispidin hydroxylase (HispH)** hydroxylates hispidin to form
   3-hydroxyhispidin, the fungal luciferin.
3. **Luz luciferase** catalyzes the oxidation of 3-hydroxyhispidin in
   the presence of molecular oxygen and ATP, emitting light at 530 nm.
