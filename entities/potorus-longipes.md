## Description

**Potorus longipes** (brush-tailed potoroo) is a small marsupial native to southeastern Australia. It is one of the most mycophagous (fungus-eating) mammals known, with fungi comprising more than 25% of its diet by volume at all times of year. The brush-tailed potoroo is a critical dispersal agent for hypogeous (subterranean-fruiting) fungi, particularly ectomycorrhizal truffles, making it a keystone species in forest ecosystem function.

## Key Facts

### Mycophagy

Claridge and May (1994) identified the brush-tailed potoroo as the most mycophagous of Australian mammals:

- Fungi comprise **>25% of diet by volume** at all times of year
- Fungal spores found in feces **90%+ of the time** during most months, never falling below 80%
- Consumes the most varied fungal diet of any animal: **36 fungal taxa** identified
- Diet dominated by hypogeous (underground-fruiting) fungi, especially truffles
- Year-round dependence on fungal food sources, unlike other species that are seasonal mycophagists

### Ecological Role as Spore Disperser

The potoroo's intense mycophagy makes it essential for:

1. **Truffle [[spore-dispersal]]:** Hypogeous fungi cannot disperse spores without animal vectors
2. **[[mycorrhizal-inoculation-guide]]:** Dispersed spores colonize new tree roots, maintaining ectomycorrhizal networks
3. **Nutrient cycling:** By consuming and processing fungal tissue, potoroos accelerate [[nutrient-mineralization]]
4. **Forest regeneration:** The mycorrhizal partnerships enabled by potoroo dispersal are critical for seedling establishment

### Body Size and Fungal Diet Diversity

Small mammals (<150 g body weight) tend to feed mainly on arbuscular mycorrhizal spores of the Endogonaceae. Larger animals like potoroos (~1.5 kg) consume a wider range including ectomycorrhizal truffles. The feral pig, being much larger, eats the widest variety of fungal species.

### Conservation Status

- **Endangered** (IUCN Red List)
- Threatened by habitat loss, predation by introduced foxes and cats
- Conservation is linked to forest health because of their role as mycorrhizal spore dispersers
- Loss of potoroos could cascade to reduced mycorrhizal diversity and forest degradation

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Mycorrhizal Inoculum Dispersal

The potoroo-fungal relationship provides insights for cultivation:

1. **Spore viability:** Potoroo gut passage does not kill truffle spores; understanding digestive chemistry could improve artificial [[inoculation-methods]]
2. **Inoculation patterns:** Potoroos create spatially clustered spore deposits that mirror natural mycorrhizal colonization patterns
3. **Fungal diversity maintenance:** The consumption of 36+ fungal taxa demonstrates the importance of diverse disperser communities

### Truffle Cultivation

The potoroo's dietary preference for hypogeous fungi has implications for truffle cultivation:

- Confirms that native mammals can serve as effective spore vectors in truffle orchards
- Suggests that maintaining habitat for mycophagous mammals near cultivation sites could enhance natural inoculation
- Understanding which truffle species potoroos prefer could inform selection of species for cultivation

### Ecosystem Management

The interdependence of potoroos, hypogeous fungi, and forest trees creates a three-way mutualism:

- **Potoroos** depend on fungi for food
- **Fungi** depend on potoroos for spore dispersal
- **Trees** depend on fungi for nutrient acquisition
- Managing forests requires considering all three components
