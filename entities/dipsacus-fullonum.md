# Dipsacus fullonum

## Overview

Dipsacus fullonum, commonly called Fuller's teasel or wild teasel, is a biennial plant in the family Caprifoliaceae (formerly Dipsacaceae). Native to Eurasia and North Africa, it has become widely naturalized in the Americas, southern Africa, and Australasia. The species is best known for its distinctive egg-shaped flower heads armed with stiff, recurved bracts.

Historically, the dried flower heads were a critical tool in the [[staycare-fungal-treatment-of-textile-industry-wastewater]] for raising the nap on woven woolen cloth. This "teaseling" process gave the plant its common name and made it economically significant for centuries.

The plant is a robust biennial, forming a low rosette of prickly leaves in its first year and sending up a tall flowering stem 1 to 2.5 meters in its second year. After setting seed, the plant dies, completing its biennial lifecycle.

- Synonyms: *Dipsacus sylvestris* Huds.

The basal leaves form a rosette in the first year, oblong-lanceolate, 15 to 40 cm long, with prickly margins. The leaves are dark green and slightly rough to the touch.

Stem leaves are opposite, sessile, and joined at the base, forming a cup-like structure that collects rainwater. These "basins" were historically observed to trap insects, leading to speculation about carnivorous tendencies.

The flower heads are cylindrical to ovoid, 5 to 10 cm long, covered with stiff, recurved bracts. Individual flowers are small, lavender to purple, blooming from July through September.

Each flower head produces hundreds of small seeds (achenes) approximately 5 to 7 mm long. The dried flower heads persist through winter as distinctive, dark brown, spiny cylinders on stiff stems.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Dipsacus fullonum* is native to Europe, western Asia, and North Africa. Its original range extended from the British Isles and Iberian Peninsula eastward to the Caucasus and Iran.

The species was introduced to [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]] in the 18th century, likely both intentionally for textile use and accidentally as a seed contaminant. It is now naturalized across most of the United States and southern Canada.

It thrives in disturbed habitats including roadsides, field margins, waste ground, and riparian areas. The plant prefers moist, fertile soils but tolerates a wide range of conditions.

*Teasel* is considered invasive in several US states, particularly in the Midwest and [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]]. It can form dense monocultures that displace native vegetation in prairie remnants and natural areas.

First-year rosettes are shade-tolerant and can persist in semi-shaded conditions for extended periods before bolting. This shade tolerance aids establishment in dense vegetation.

In its native European range, teasel is not considered problematic and coexists with other meadow species. The invasive behavior is primarily observed in [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] and Australasian ecosystems where natural controls are absent.

Birds, particularly goldfinches, feed heavily on teasel seeds in autumn and winter. The tall, stiff stems also provide perching sites for small birds in open habitats.

## Cultivation

*Dipsacus fullonum* is rarely cultivated intentionally outside of historical textile demonstration gardens. It readily self-seeds and persists in suitable habitats without any human assistance.

Germination requires a period of cold stratification or natural winter chilling. Seeds sown in autumn germinate the following spring and produce rosettes in the first [[extending-growing-season]].

The plant is extremely low-maintenance and tolerant of poor soils, drought, and neglect. These hardy characteristics contribute to its success as a naturalized species.

For those wishing to grow it, direct sowing in autumn in a sunny location with moist soil is effective. Space plants 30 to 45 cm apart to accommodate the mature flower stalks.

Control of invasive populations requires preventing seed set by mowing or cutting flower heads before seed maturation. Repeated annual control over 3 to 5 years depletes the soil seed bank effectively.

## Uses and Ethnobotany

The primary historical use of *Dipsacus fullonum* was in textile finishing. The dried flower heads were mounted on frames and drawn across woolen cloth to raise and align the fibers, creating a soft, even nap.

This "fulling" process was essential to the production of broadcloth and other finished woolen fabrics from Roman times through the 19th century. The term "teasel" derives from the Old English *tæsan* (to tease or pull apart).

In traditional European herbalism, the root was used as a diuretic, stomachic, and wound healer. Nicholas Culpeper's *Complete Herbal* (1653) recommends teasel root for cleansing wounds and treating [[cannabis-for-skin-conditions-detailed]].

The water collected in the leaf bases was traditionally used as an eyewash and skin lotion. Modern analysis has found this water contains trace amounts of plant-derived compounds with mild antiseptic properties.

Recent research has investigated *Dipsacus* species for osteoprotective compounds, particularly in Traditional Chinese Medicine where related species are used. Akebiaoside and other iridoid glycosides show promise in bone metabolism studies.
