## Dipsacus fullonum

## Overview

Dipsacus fullonum, commonly called Fuller's teasel or wild teasel, is a biennial plant in the family Caprifoliaceae (formerly Dipsacaceae). Native to Eurasia and North Africa, it has become widely naturalized in the Americas, southern Africa, and Australasia. The species is best known for its distinctive egg-shaped flower heads armed with stiff, recurved bracts.

Historically, the dried flower heads were a critical tool in the [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]] in the 18th century, likely both intentionally for textile use and accidentally as a seed contaminant. It is now naturalized across most of the United States and southern Canada.

It thrives in disturbed habitats including roadsides, field margins, waste ground, and riparian areas. The plant prefers moist, fertile soils but tolerates a wide range of conditions.

*Teasel* is considered invasive in several US states, particularly in the Midwest and [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] and Australasian ecosystems where natural controls are absent.

Birds, particularly goldfinches, feed heavily on teasel seeds in autumn and winter. The tall, stiff stems also provide perching sites for small birds in open habitats.

## Cultivation

*Dipsacus fullonum* is rarely cultivated intentionally outside of historical textile demonstration gardens. It readily self-seeds and persists in suitable habitats without any human assistance.

Germination requires a period of cold stratification or natural winter chilling. Seeds sown in autumn germinate the following spring and produce rosettes in the first [[cannabis-for-skin-conditions-detailed]].

The water collected in the leaf bases was traditionally used as an eyewash and skin lotion. Modern analysis has found this water contains trace amounts of plant-derived compounds with mild antiseptic properties.

Recent research has investigated *Dipsacus* species for osteoprotective compounds, particularly in Traditional Chinese Medicine where related species are used. Akebiaoside and other iridoid glycosides show promise in bone metabolism studies.
