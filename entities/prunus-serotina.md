## Distribution and Habitat

The native range extends from Nova Scotia west to Minnesota and southeastern North Dakota, south to eastern Texas and central Florida. Disjunct populations occur in the mountains of southern Arizona, New Mexico, and Guatemala.

Black cherry is an early-successional to mid-successional species that colonizes disturbed sites, forest openings, and abandoned agricultural land. It is a common component of mixed hardwood forests throughout the [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] peoples used the fruit extensively, consuming it fresh, dried, or pounded into cakes with animal fat for winter storage. The bark was brewed into medicinal teas for treating colds, coughs, and digestive ailments.

The fruit is relished by numerous bird species, which are the primary agents of seed dispersal. American robins, cedar waxwings, and thrushes are particularly important consumers.

## Wood and Timber

Black cherry produces a fine, straight-grained wood with a rich reddish-brown heartwood that darkens with age and exposure to light. The sapwood is narrow and pale yellowish, sharply demarcated from the heartwood.

The wood has a specific gravity of approximately 0.50, with good strength properties, moderate shrinkage, and excellent machining characteristics. It takes stain and finish exceptionally well, developing a deep luster.

Primary uses include fine furniture, cabinetry, paneling, gunstocks, and musical instruments. Cherry wood is the standard material for professional-quality acoustic guitar bodies and is widely used in piano interiors.

The timber is air-dried or kiln-dried with care to prevent warping and honeycombing. Green lumber is highly perishable and must be processed quickly to prevent fungal staining. Rotation ages for [[fukuoka-seed-selection-methods]] to encourage regeneration.

Veneer-grade cherry commands the highest prices, with logs meeting strict specifications for color, grain pattern, and absence of defects. The best veneer logs originate from trees 50–80 cm in diameter at breast height.

## Bioactive Compounds and Toxicity

All vegetative parts of black cherry contain cyanogenic glycosides, primarily amygdalin (D-mandelonitrile-β-D-gentiobioside). Wilted leaves are particularly dangerous, as wilting increases cyanide release potential.

Livestock poisoning occurs most frequently when cattle or horses consume wilted leaves after storm damage or branch cutting. Clinical signs include rapid breathing, muscle tremors, convulsions, and death from respiratory failure.
