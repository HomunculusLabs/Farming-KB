## Overview

**Black cherry** (*Prunus serotina*) is a medium to large deciduous tree native to eastern North America, valued for its fine woodworking timber and distinctive dark, bitter fruit. It is the largest of the native North American *Prunus* species, reaching 25–30 meters in height in forest conditions.

The species is an important timber tree, producing prized cherry wood used in fine furniture, cabinetry, and musical instruments. Black cherry lumber commands premium prices among North American hardwoods, second only to black walnut in per-board-foot value.

All parts of the tree except the ripe fruit pulp contain cyanogenic glycosides, primarily amygdalin, which release hydrogen cyanide upon tissue damage. This toxicity is a significant consideration for livestock management and limits the species' use in pastures.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Prunus serotina* Ehrh. belongs to family Rosaceae, subfamily Amygdaloideae, genus *Prunus* (subgenus *Padus*). Several infraspecific taxa have been described, though their taxonomic validity remains debated.

Bark on mature trees is distinctive: dark, nearly black, with conspicuous horizontally-oriented lenticels that give a characteristic "burnt potato chip" appearance. Bark plates break into irregular scales that curl away at the edges.

Leaves are simple, alternate, oblong-lanceolate, 5–15 cm long and 2–7 cm wide. Leaf margins are finely serrate, and the upper surface is dark glossy green while the lower surface is lighter with scattered hairs along the midrib.

Autumn color is variable, ranging from yellow to orange to reddish. The most reliable fall color develops on trees growing in full sun with moderate moisture stress.

Flowers are arranged in slender racemes 10–15 cm long, each flower approximately 8 mm across with 5 white petals. Bloom occurs in late April to May, after most other *Prunus* species.

Fruit is a drupe 1–1.5 cm in diameter, ripening from green to red to nearly black in August to October. The flesh is thin and tart, while the stone (pit) is large relative to fruit size.

The root system is moderately deep with a well-developed taproot on upland sites and a more spreading root system on moist bottomlands. Black cherry forms both ectomycorrhizal and arbuscular mycorrhizal associations, enhancing nutrient uptake.

Winter buds are long, pointed, and 6–12 mm in length, with several visible bud scales giving a characteristic elongated appearance. Terminal buds are absent — the species is determinate in shoot elongation.

## Distribution and Habitat

The native range extends from Nova Scotia west to Minnesota and southeastern North Dakota, south to eastern Texas and central Florida. Disjunct populations occur in the mountains of southern Arizona, New Mexico, and Guatemala.

Black cherry is an early-successional to mid-successional species that colonizes disturbed sites, forest openings, and abandoned agricultural land. It is a common component of mixed hardwood forests throughout the Appalachian region and Ozark Plateau.

The species grows on a wide range of site conditions, from dry upland ridges to moist coves and bottomlands. Best growth occurs on deep, fertile, well-drained loam soils with pH 5.0–6.5.

It is shade-intolerant in youth but develops increasing shade tolerance with age. Mature individuals persist as canopy or sub-canopy trees in late-successional forests, though they require canopy gaps for successful regeneration.

Seed dormancy is broken by cold stratification, and the soil seed bank can persist for 3–5 years. Germination is stimulated by disturbance and exposure to mineral soil, which explains the species' association with forest openings and abandoned land.

In parts of Europe, *P. serotina* has become invasive after introduction as an ornamental and timber tree. It spreads rapidly in disturbed habitats, particularly in pine plantations and open woodlands, altering understory composition and competing with native vegetation.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

The ripe fruit of black cherry is edible when fully mature, though the flesh is thin and tart compared to cultivated cherries. It is most commonly used for jellies, jams, wines, and flavoring extracts rather than fresh consumption.

Black cherry flavor is widely used in commercial food products, beverages, and pharmaceuticals. The characteristic almond-like aroma of the bark has historically been used to flavor cordials and cough medicines.

Native American peoples used the fruit extensively, consuming it fresh, dried, or pounded into cakes with animal fat for winter storage. The bark was brewed into medicinal teas for treating colds, coughs, and digestive ailments.

The fruit is relished by numerous bird species, which are the primary agents of seed dispersal. American robins, cedar waxwings, and thrushes are particularly important consumers.

## Wood and Timber

Black cherry produces a fine, straight-grained wood with a rich reddish-brown heartwood that darkens with age and exposure to light. The sapwood is narrow and pale yellowish, sharply demarcated from the heartwood.

The wood has a specific gravity of approximately 0.50, with good strength properties, moderate shrinkage, and excellent machining characteristics. It takes stain and finish exceptionally well, developing a deep luster.

Primary uses include fine furniture, cabinetry, paneling, gunstocks, and musical instruments. Cherry wood is the standard material for professional-quality acoustic guitar bodies and is widely used in piano interiors.

The timber is air-dried or kiln-dried with care to prevent warping and honeycombing. Green lumber is highly perishable and must be processed quickly to prevent fungal staining. Rotation ages for timber production range from 40–60 years in managed stands.

Black cherry is an important component of the Appalachian hardwood forest type, where it occurs in mixture with oaks, hickories, maples, and yellow-poplar. Silvicultural systems typically use shelterwood or group selection methods to encourage regeneration.

Veneer-grade cherry commands the highest prices, with logs meeting strict specifications for color, grain pattern, and absence of defects. The best veneer logs originate from trees 50–80 cm in diameter at breast height.

## Bioactive Compounds and Toxicity

All vegetative parts of black cherry contain cyanogenic glycosides, primarily amygdalin (D-mandelonitrile-β-D-gentiobioside). Wilted leaves are particularly dangerous, as wilting increases cyanide release potential.

Livestock poisoning occurs most frequently when cattle or horses consume wilted leaves after storm damage or branch cutting. Clinical signs include rapid breathing, muscle tremors, convulsions, and death from respiratory failure.

The bark contains coumarin derivatives and benzaldehyde, which contribute to its characteristic almond-like aroma. These compounds have been investigated for anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties in laboratory studies.

Ripe fruit pulp contains significantly lower concentrations of cyanogenic glycosides than leaves and bark, making it safe for human consumption. However, the pits retain high amygdalin concentrations and should not be crushed or consumed in quantity.

Traditional herbal medicine uses include preparations of the inner bark as a mild sedative and cough suppressant. Wild cherry bark remains an ingredient in some commercial cough syrups, though its efficacy has not been rigorously demonstrated in controlled clinical trials.

The fruit contains anthocyanins, particularly cyanidin-3-glucoside and cyanidin-3-rutinoside, which contribute to its dark pigmentation and have demonstrated antioxidant activity in vitro. These compounds are of interest for their potential anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties.
