## Overview

**Tylecodon pearsonii** (Schönland) Tölken is a dwarf caudiciform succulent in the family Crassulaceae. It is endemic to the arid Northern Cape of South Africa, where it grows on quartzite outcrops in the Succulent Karoo biome.

The species produces a compact, sculptural caudex and winter-growing deciduous leaves. Its small stature and ornamental appeal make it prized among collectors of miniature caudiciform plants.

It was first described by Selmar Schönland as a species of *Cotyledon*. Tölken transferred it to the genus *Tylecodon* in his 1978 revision based on its deciduous leaf habit.

The specific epithet honors Henry Harold Welch Pearson. Pearson was the founding director of Kirstenbosch National Botanical Garden in Cape Town and a pioneer of South African botany.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

The genus *Tylecodon* comprises approximately 50 species of deciduous succulents endemic to southern Africa. Tölken segregated the genus from *Cotyledon* in 1978 based on spiral leaf arrangement and the deciduous growth cycle.

Tylecodon pearsonii develops a small, globose to irregular caudex up to 4 cm in diameter. The caudex is partially exposed above the soil surface, with smooth to slightly fissured grey-brown bark.

Prominent leaf scars mark the caudex surface from previous growth seasons. These scars create a textured, sculptural appearance that intensifies with age and is highly valued by collectors.

Leaves are arranged in a loose spiral rosette and are succulent, oblanceolate to spatulate. Each leaf reaches 2–4 cm long and 0.8–1.2 cm wide, with a glabrous, bright green to grey-green surface.

A faint glaucous bloom on the leaves helps reflect intense sunlight in the exposed habitat. Leaves appear in autumn with the onset of winter rains and are fully deciduous by late spring.

This seasonal leaf cycle is a defining character of the genus. It distinguishes Tylecodon species from the evergreen members of the closely related genus *Cotyledon*.

Inflorescences are erect, slender peduncles 8–15 cm tall. Each bears a terminal cyme of 3–8 nodding flowers at the apex.

Corollas are tubular, 10–14 mm long, and pale pink to salmon-colored. The petal tips are recurved, with exserted stamens bearing dark reddish anthers that contrast with the pale corolla.

## Distribution and Habitat

This species is restricted to the Richtersveld and southern Namibia region within the Succulent Karoo biome. It occurs at elevations of 200–800 m on quartzite gravel plains and rocky slopes.

Plants grow in crevices and shallow pockets among quartzite rocks. They are often found in association with lichen crusts and other miniature succulents such as *Conophytum* and *Crassula* species.

The habitat receives winter rainfall of 50–150 mm annually. Coastal fog rolling inland from the Atlantic provides important supplementary moisture during dry periods.

Soils are extremely shallow, mineral-poor, and derived from weathered quartzite bedrock. Plants frequently colonize near-vertical rock faces where competition from larger vegetation is minimal.

The Succulent Karoo is recognized as a global biodiversity hotspot. It supports over 6,300 plant species and is considered the world's richest arid-zone flora.

Tylecodon pearsonii is one of many narrowly endemic species in this biome. Its restricted range makes it particularly vulnerable to environmental disturbance and climate shifts.

## Cultivation

Cultivation follows standard Tylecodon protocols with attention to the species' winter-growing habit. A well-drained, mineral-based substrate of coarse sand, pumice grit, and a small proportion of loam is essential.

Watering should commence in autumn when new leaves emerge from the caudex. Gradually reduce water as leaves begin to yellow in spring, then withhold all water during summer dormancy.

Summer dryness is critical to prevent caudex rot, the most common cause of death in cultivation. Plants tolerate drought far better than excess moisture at any time of year.

Bright, filtered light is ideal for active growth. Direct afternoon sun can scorch the thin succulent leaves, especially in warm climates.

A cool greenhouse or bright windowsill with good air circulation produces the best results. Temperatures of 10–20 °C during the growing season mirror the plant's natural winter rainfall environment.

Propagation is primarily by seed. Seeds are dust-fine and should be surface-sown on moist, sterile medium in autumn without any covering.

Germination occurs within 7–14 days at temperatures of 15–20 °C. Seedlings grow slowly and may take 3–5 years to develop a noticeable caudex.

Grafted specimens on *Cotyledon* rootstock grow more vigorously. However, they lose the characteristic caudex shape that collectors value, making seed-grown plants preferable.

## Ecology

Tylecodon pearsonii survives extreme aridity through its deciduous habit and water-storing caudex. The CAM photosynthetic pathway allows efficient carbon fixation while minimizing daytime water loss.

The species produces cardiac bufadienolide glycosides in all plant parts. These compounds are highly toxic to vertebrate herbivores and serve as an effective chemical defense.

Pollination is likely performed by sunbirds and small flying insects. The tubular, nectar-rich flowers are well-suited to bird pollination, a common strategy in southern African Crassulaceae.

The nodding flower orientation protects pollen from wind and rain damage. This is particularly important during the winter flowering season when occasional storms pass through the Richtersveld.

Seeds are tiny, wind-dispersed, and produced in copious quantities. This reproductive strategy maximizes the chance of at least some seeds landing in suitable rock crevices.

Seedling mortality is extremely high in the wild. Most seedlings succumb to desiccation during their first summer drought, with survival rates estimated below 1%.

## Horticultural Significance

The miniature caudex and compact growth make Tylecodon pearsonii a sought-after species among specialist collectors. It performs well in shallow bonsai-style containers and appears frequently in succulent show exhibitions.

The species is rarely available in mainstream horticultural commerce. Its slow growth and limited seed production restrict supply to specialist nurseries in South Africa and seed-exchange programs.

Illegal collection from wild populations has increased due to demand from collectors in Asia and Europe. The species is listed on CITES Appendix II to regulate and monitor international trade.

Climate modeling predicts significant habitat loss for Succulent Karoo endemics under warming scenarios. Many populations may face range contraction of 50–80% by 2050 as aridification intensifies.

The species is classified as Near Threatened on the South African Red List. Its restricted range and small population sizes contribute to this conservation status.

Ex situ conservation in botanical garden living collections provides an important safeguard. Several South African and European gardens maintain propagated stocks as insurance against wild population loss.

The species has received increased attention from conservation organizations since 2020. Monitoring programs now track wild population sizes at known localities in the Richtersveld National Park.
