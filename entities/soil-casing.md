## Description

**Soil casing** (also called casing soil or casing layer) is a layer of moisture-retaining material applied over the surface of colonized mushroom substrate to stimulate fruiting body formation. In [[maitake-cultivation]], soil casing has been demonstrated to significantly increase yields compared to uncased substrates. The casing layer modifies the microenvironment at the substrate surface, improving humidity, providing beneficial microorganisms, and supplying trace minerals. Soil casing is also a critical technique in agaricus cultivation (button mushroom farming) where it is essential for fruiting.

## Classification

- **Category:** Cultivation technique / Substrate amendment
- **Type:** Surface overlay for fruiting stimulation
- **Application:** Applied after full spawn run colonization
- **Primary use:** Maitake, button mushroom (Agaricus), and other species

## Key Facts

- Soil casing dramatically increases maitake yield: 300-500 g/bag with soil casing vs. 150-300 g/bag without (Wu et al., 1997).
- Treated soil from humus-rich hardwood forest, garden soil, or rice paddy soil is used for maitake casing.
- Optimal soil particle size is 0.5-0.8 cm in diameter for best results.
- Sandy soil or clay has NOT been found to be as beneficial as humus-rich soil types.
- In maitake cultivation, soil casing reduces fruiting time: 10-15 days surface growth above soil, 15-18 days to mature fruiting bodies.
- Without soil casing, the fruiting cycle is 14-21 days.
- The casing layer helps maintain a stable microclimate of high humidity at the substrate surface while allowing gas exchange.
- Soil casing may contribute beneficial bacteria (particularly from humus-rich forest soil) that stimulate pinning and fruiting body initiation.

## Casing Materials

### Forest Humus Soil
The preferred casing material for maitake cultivation is humus-rich soil collected from hardwood forests. This soil type contains:
- High organic matter content (5-15%)
- Diverse microbial communities including beneficial bacteria and actinomycetes
- Balanced mineral content including calcium, magnesium, and trace elements
- Good water-holding capacity with adequate drainage

### Peat-Based Casing
In agaricus cultivation (button mushroom production), peat moss mixed with limestone (calcium carbonate) is the standard casing material. While not commonly used for maitake, the principles are similar — providing a moist, microbially active surface layer that triggers fruiting.

### Alternative Casing Materials
- **Coconut coir:** A sustainable alternative to peat, offering similar moisture retention properties. See vermiculite and perlite for other moisture management amendments.
- **Spent mushroom compost:** Can be pasteurized and used as casing, though careful quality control is needed.
- **Vermicastings:** Earthworm-processed compost that adds beneficial microbes. See [[composting-and-soil-biology]] for composting background.

## Application Technique

The casing process for maitake follows a specific sequence:
1. **Timing:** Apply casing only after the mycelium has fully colonized the [[hardwood-sawdust-substrate]] and formed a complete mycelial coat on the surface.
2. **Preparation:** Screen forest soil to 0.5-0.8 cm particle size. Moisten to field capacity. Pasteurize or sterilize to eliminate harmful organisms while preserving beneficial microbes.
3. **Application depth:** Apply a uniform layer approximately 1.5-2.0 cm thick over the entire substrate surface.
4. **Environmental shift:** After casing, reduce temperature to 18-22°C and increase humidity to 90-95% to stimulate primordia initiation.
5. **Watering:** Mist the casing surface lightly to maintain moisture without waterlogging. Avoid direct streams of water that can damage developing primordia.

## Mechanism of Action

The casing layer promotes fruiting through several interconnected mechanisms:
- **Microbial stimulation:** Bacteria in the casing layer, particularly *Pseudomonas* species, produce volatile compounds that trigger mushroom pin formation. This is well-documented in agaricus cultivation research.
- **Humidity microclimate:** The casing creates a thin layer of near-saturation humidity at the substrate surface, which is essential for primordia development.
- **CO₂ gradient:** The porous casing layer creates a gradient of CO₂ concentration, with higher levels at the substrate surface and lower levels at the casing surface. This gradient helps direct fruiting body development upward.
- **Physical support:** The casing provides physical support for emerging fruiting bodies, reducing the chance of aborts and malformed mushrooms.
- **Nutrient supply:** Trace minerals and organic compounds in the casing material supplement the substrate, supporting the final stages of fruiting body development.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Yield improvement:** The near-doubling of yield with soil casing makes it one of the most impactful practices in commercial maitake cultivation, directly improving economic returns.
- **Microbial stimulation:** Forest humus soil contains diverse microbial communities that may produce volatile organic compounds or other signals promoting mushroom primordia formation. See the soil food web for soil biology context.
- **Water management:** The casing layer acts as a water reservoir, releasing moisture gradually to support fruiting body development without waterlogging the substrate.
- **pH buffering:** Calcium-rich casing materials can help stabilize substrate pH in the optimal range (5.5-6.5) for maitake fruiting.
- **Practical consideration:** Soil casing adds an extra step and material requirement to the cultivation process but pays for itself in increased yield.

For commercial mushroom production, [[mushroom-cultivation]] operations use casing layers primarily for [[agaricus]]-species and maitake, where the casing triggers fruiting body formation.
