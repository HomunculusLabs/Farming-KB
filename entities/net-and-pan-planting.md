## Net and Pan Planting Pattern

## Description

The net and pan planting pattern is an erosion control and revegetation technique used on overgrazed, eroded, mined, or bulldozed sites. It combines a network of diversion drains ("nets") with catchment basins ("pans") to capture water, trap silt, and establish vegetation on degraded landscapes. The pattern is particularly effective on steep or badly damaged sites where conventional planting would fail.

## Classification

- **Category:** Erosion control technique / Revegetation method
- **Type:** Combined earthwork and planting system
- **Scale:** Small to medium landscape applications
- **Complexity:** Moderate — requires design but simple materials

## Key Components

1. **Pans:** Catchment basins that collect water and silt. Can be made from:
   - Recycled tyres filled with mulch
   - Small earth basins dug into the slope
   - The diversion drains are led in above the tread level of tyre pans

2. **Nets (Diversion Drains):** Cross-slope channels that direct water into the pans
   - Arranged on a slight downhill grade so water zig-zags across the erosion face
   - Water is made to absorb into the ground rather than run off

3. **Silt Traps:** Accumulations of silt, leaves, and organic matter behind barriers:
   - Small logs and branches pegged across erosion channels
   - Wire netting fences with stone-weighted hay uphill
   - These build up a "layer-cake" of silt and leaves

## Companion Plantings

Recommended species for net and pan systems:
- **Willow (Salix spp.):** Fibrous-rooted, hardy, planted beside silt traps
- **Acacia spp.:** Nitrogen-fixing pioneers for bank stabilization
- **Lemongrass (Cymbopogon spp.):** Cross-swale plantings for silt trapping
- **Vetiver grass (Chrysopogon zizanioides):** Dense root system for erosion control
- **Pampas grass:** Steep slope stabilization
- **Bamboo:** Long-term bank holding
- **Chestnut, carob, olive:** Large upslope species that cascade seed downslope over time

## Applications

- **Post-mining rehabilitation:** Stabilizing devastated landscapes
- **Post-fire recovery:** Preventing mud flows on baked, hydrophobic soils
- **Overgrazed land restoration:** Re-establishing vegetation on bare slopes
- **Road and construction site rehabilitation:** Controlling erosion after earthworks
- **Gully erosion control:** Halting and reversing gully expansion

## Relevance to Cultivation

The net and pan pattern is a key technique for permaculture designers working on degraded land — which describes much of the world's agricultural landscape. It demonstrates the permaculture principle of turning problems into solutions by using water flow itself as the energy source for revegetation. The technique is low-cost, uses readily available materials, and becomes self-reinforcing as vegetation establishes and further slows water, captures more silt, and builds soil.
