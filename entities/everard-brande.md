## Description

Everard Brande was an English gentleman who, in 1799, published the **first documented account of [[psilocybin-mushroom]] intoxication in the scientific literature**. His report, addressed to the editors of *The Medical and Physical Journal* from No. 10 Arlington Street, London (dated November 16, 1799), described a remarkable case of mushroom poisoning involving a family in London"s St. James's Green Park.

## The 1799 Incident

On October 3, 1799, an impoverished family (identified only as "J.S." and his four children) picked mushrooms in St. James"s Green Park and prepared them for a meal in a tinned iron saucepan. The family had eaten mushrooms from the same area previously without incident. Shortly after consuming the mushrooms:

- **Edward** (eight years old), who ate a large proportion, was attacked with fits of immoderate laughter that could not be restrained by parental threats
- This was followed by **vertigo** and a great degree of **stupor**
- Pupils were dilated to nearly the circumference of the cornea, scarcely contracting in strong light
- Breathing was quick, pulse very variable — at times imperceptible, at others too frequent to count
- Feet were cold, livid, and contracted
- The father (aged 40) experienced vertigo and reported that everything appeared black, believing he was about to die
- Two older children (ages 12 and 18) who ate smaller amounts experienced the same symptoms

## Significance

Brande's account is of extraordinary historical importance:

1. **First scientific description** of a [[psilocybin]] syndrome, written over 150 years before psilocybin was identified
2. **Accurate symptomatology**: dilated pupils, spontaneous laughter, delirium, visual disturbances, wave-like cycling of symptoms
3. **Augmented by J. Sowerby** in *Coloured Figures of English Fungi or Mushrooms* (London, 1803), who identified the species as *[[agaricus]] glutinosus Curtis* — now known as *[[psilocybe-semilanceata]]*

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Brande's report demonstrates that the effects of *Psilocybe semilanceata* were experienced and documented long before the active compound was understood. The spontaneous recovery of all family members, with "no lasting side effects," also provides early evidence of the relative safety of [[psilocybin-mushrooms]] when compared to truly poisonous species.

## Key Facts

- Published the first scientific account of psilocybin mushroom intoxication in 1799
- Documented a family's reaction to *Psilocybe semilanceata* eaten in St. James's Green Park, London
- Described classic psilocybin symptoms: laughter, dilated pupils, vertigo, visual disturbances
- Symptoms resolved after several hours without lasting effects
- Case was subsequently identified by J. Sowerby in 1803
- Represents one of the earliest verifiable records of psychoactive mushroom use in England
