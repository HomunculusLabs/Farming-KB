## Taxonomy and Morphology

Prunus domestica was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The genus Prunus contains approximately 430 species of deciduous and evergreen [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]]. They appear before or simultaneously with the leaves.

The fruit is a drupe (stone fruit) with a fleshy mesocarp surrounding a hard, woody endocarp (stone). Fruit size ranges from 2 to 8 centimeters in diameter depending on cultivar and growing conditions.

Skin color varies widely among cultivars, from green and yellow to red, blue, purple, and nearly black. The flesh may be green, yellow, or red, with flavors ranging from tart to very sweet.

Cultivar groups within the species include prunes (dried plums), damsons (small, tart, blue-black fruits), greengages (sweet, green-skinned dessert plums), and yellow egg plums. These groups differ in fruit characteristics, uses, and genetic composition.

The hexaploid genome presents challenges for genetic mapping and marker-assisted breeding compared to diploid Prunus species. Despite this, several quantitative trait loci for fruit quality and disease resistance have been identified.

Rootstock-scion compatibility is generally excellent within the species, though inter-specific grafting with peach (*P. persica*) is not reliable. Myrobalan plum rootstock provides good compatibility and adapts the tree to a wide range of soil conditions.

The stone (endocarp) is lignified and relatively smooth in European plum compared to the deeply furrowed stones of many Asian plum species. Stone adherence to the flesh varies among cultivars, with clingstone and freestone types both occurring.

Bark characteristics include prominent lenticels and a tendency toward vertical cracking with age. The wood has a fine, even grain that takes polish well, historically used for inlay work and small decorative objects.

The species exhibits a high degree of self-fertility, with most cultivars setting satisfactory crops without cross-pollination. However, some cultivars benefit from inter-planting with compatible pollenizers for improved fruit set.

Leaf stomatal density and distribution influence water use efficiency and heat tolerance. Cultivars with higher stomatal density tend to perform better in warm, dry growing conditions.

Prunus domestica shows considerable phenotypic plasticity in response to environmental conditions, with fruit size, color, and sugar content varying significantly across growing regions and seasons.

## Distribution and Habitat

The European plum is widely believed to have originated in the Caucasus region and western Asia, with subsequent naturalization and cultivation across Europe. Wild populations of *P. domestica* are found from the Balkans eastward to the Caspian Sea.

Cultivated plums are grown across temperate regions worldwide, from approximately 40° to 55° latitude. Major production areas include China, Romania, Serbia, the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. The tree is moderately drought-tolerant once established.

European plum is more cold-hardy than Japanese plum (*P. salicina*) but less cold-hardy than many cherry species. Temperatures below -20°C can cause significant bud and wood damage during dormancy.

## Cultivation

Commercial plum orchards are typically established at densities of 200 to 600 trees per hectare depending on rootstock and training system. Trees begin bearing at four to six years and reach full production by eight to twelve years.

Propagation is by grafting or budding onto rootstock, with Myrobalan plum (*P. cerasifera*) and Pixy being widely used rootstocks. Rootstock selection influences tree vigor, precocity, and disease resistance.

Plums for fresh market are picked when fully colored but still firm, while those for processing may be allowed to soften on the tree.

## Nutritional Value

European plums are a good source of dietary fiber, vitamin C, and vitamin K. Dried plums (prunes) are particularly valued for their high fiber content, providing approximately 7 grams per 100 grams.
