## Overview

Prunus domestica, the European plum, is a deciduous tree in the family Rosaceae cultivated for its edible stone fruits. It is one of the most widely grown temperate fruit crops, with global production exceeding 12 million metric tons annually.

The species is believed to be an allopolyploid hybrid originating from the cross between blackthorn (*P. spinosa*) and cherry plum (*P. cerasifera*), with subsequent doubling of chromosomes. This hybrid origin explains its hexaploid chromosome number (2n = 48).

European plums are highly diverse in fruit characteristics, ranging from small tart damsons to large sweet dessert plums. They are consumed fresh, dried as prunes, processed into jams, and fermented into alcoholic beverages.

The species has been cultivated in Europe since antiquity, with evidence of plum cultivation dating to the Neolithic period. It remains a commercially important fruit crop across temperate regions worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Prunus domestica was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. The genus Prunus contains approximately 430 species of deciduous and evergreen trees and shrubs distributed across the temperate Northern Hemisphere.

The species is hexaploid (2n = 6x = 48), a rare ploidy level among Prunus species. Most other cultivated Prunus are diploid (2n = 16), making breeding and genetic studies of European plum more complex.

Trees typically reach 6 to 12 meters in height under cultivation, with a spreading crown and dark brown, fissured bark. The wood is hard, reddish-brown, and valued for fine woodworking and turning.

Leaves are alternate, simple, ovate to elliptic, measuring 5 to 10 centimeters long with finely serrate margins. The upper surface is dark green and glabrous, while the underside is lighter with pubescence along the midrib.

Flowers are white, approximately 2 centimeters in diameter, and borne in clusters of two to three on short pedicels in early spring. They appear before or simultaneously with the leaves.

The fruit is a drupe (stone fruit) with a fleshy mesocarp surrounding a hard, woody endocarp (stone). Fruit size ranges from 2 to 8 centimeters in diameter depending on cultivar and growing conditions.

Skin color varies widely among cultivars, from green and yellow to red, blue, purple, and nearly black. The flesh may be green, yellow, or red, with flavors ranging from tart to very sweet.

Cultivar groups within the species include prunes (dried plums), damsons (small, tart, blue-black fruits), greengages (sweet, green-skinned dessert plums), and yellow egg plums. These groups differ in fruit characteristics, uses, and genetic composition.

The hexaploid genome presents challenges for genetic mapping and marker-assisted breeding compared to diploid Prunus species. Despite this, several quantitative trait loci for fruit quality and disease resistance have been identified.

Rootstock-scion compatibility is generally excellent within the species, though inter-specific grafting with peach (*P. persica*) is not reliable. Myrobalan plum rootstock provides good compatibility and adapts the tree to a wide range of soil conditions.

The stone (endocarp) is lignified and relatively smooth in European plum compared to the deeply furrowed stones of many Asian plum species. Stone adherence to the flesh varies among cultivars, with clingstone and freestone types both occurring.

Bark characteristics include prominent lenticels and a tendency toward vertical cracking with age. The wood has a fine, even grain that takes polish well, historically used for inlay work and small decorative objects.

The species exhibits a high degree of self-fertility, with most cultivars setting satisfactory crops without cross-pollination. However, some cultivars benefit from inter-planting with compatible pollenizers for improved fruit set.

Leaf stomatal density and distribution influence water use efficiency and heat tolerance. Cultivars with higher stomatal density tend to perform better in warm, dry growing conditions.

Prunus domestica shows considerable phenotypic plasticity in response to environmental conditions, with fruit size, color, and sugar content varying significantly across growing regions and seasons.

## Distribution and Habitat

The European plum is widely believed to have originated in the Caucasus region and western Asia, with subsequent naturalization and cultivation across Europe. Wild populations of *P. domestica* are found from the Balkans eastward to the Caspian Sea.

Cultivated plums are grown across temperate regions worldwide, from approximately 40° to 55° latitude. Major production areas include China, Romania, Serbia, the [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], Turkey, and Iran.

The species requires a period of winter chilling (approximately 700 to 1,000 hours below 7°C) for proper bud break and fruit set. Insufficient chilling leads to delayed and irregular flowering.

Optimal growing conditions include well-drained loam soils with a pH of 5.5 to 6.5 and adequate moisture during the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]]. The tree is moderately drought-tolerant once established.

European plum is more cold-hardy than Japanese plum (*P. salicina*) but less cold-hardy than many cherry species. Temperatures below -20°C can cause significant bud and wood damage during dormancy.

## Cultivation

Commercial plum orchards are typically established at densities of 200 to 600 trees per hectare depending on rootstock and training system. Trees begin bearing at four to six years and reach full production by eight to twelve years.

Propagation is by grafting or budding onto rootstock, with Myrobalan plum (*P. cerasifera*) and Pixy being widely used rootstocks. Rootstock selection influences tree vigor, precocity, and disease resistance.

Training systems include open center (vase), central leader, and spindle configurations. Pruning is conducted during dormancy to maintain tree structure and remove dead, diseased, or crossing branches.

Thinning of fruit is sometimes necessary in heavy-cropping years to prevent biennial bearing and ensure adequate fruit size. Chemical thinners and hand thinning are both used commercially.

Major pests include plum curculio (*Conotrachelus nenuphar*), plum fruit moth (*Cydia funebrana*), and aphids. Brown rot (*Monilinia fructicola*) is the most significant [[bloomfield-amphibian-decline-fungal-disease-climate-interactions]] affecting fruit.

## Nutritional Value

European plums are a good source of dietary fiber, vitamin C, and vitamin K. Dried plums (prunes) are particularly valued for their high fiber content, providing approximately 7 grams per 100 grams.

Prunes contain significant amounts of sorbitol, a sugar alcohol that acts as a natural laxative. This property has made prune juice a traditional remedy for constipation.

The fruit is rich in phenolic compounds, particularly chlorogenic acid, neochlorogenic acid, and various anthocyanins. These compounds contribute antioxidant capacity that has been studied for potential health benefits.

Fresh plums provide approximately 46 kilocalories per 100 grams, while dried prunes provide approximately 240 kilocalories per 100 grams due to concentrated sugars. Potassium content is also notably high in dried fruit.

## History and Economic Significance

Archaeological evidence suggests plum cultivation in Europe dates to approximately 2,000 BCE. Remains of plum stones have been identified at Neolithic lake dwellings in Switzerland and early Bronze Age sites across central Europe.

The Romans cultivated numerous plum varieties and introduced improved types throughout their empire. Roman agricultural writers including Pliny the Elder described several distinct plum types with different fruit characteristics.

The name "damson" derives from Damascus, reflecting the belief that these small dark plums were introduced to Europe from Syria during the Crusades. Genetic evidence supports a Near Eastern origin for at least some European plum lineages.

Prune production became a significant industry in California during the late [[mckenna-club-des-hachischins-and-nineteenth-century-literary-hashish]], with the French prune cultivar dominating global dried plum markets. California remains the world's largest producer of dried plums.

European plum cultivation in the United States is concentrated in the Pacific Northwest, particularly Oregon and Idaho. These regions produce fresh market plums, prunes, and processing plums for jam and canning industries.

The global plum trade includes fresh fruit, dried prunes, plum juice, jam, and various processed products. China is the world's largest producer by total volume, while European countries lead in per-capita consumption.
