## Overview

**Eleocharis palustris** (common spikerush) is a widespread perennial emergent aquatic plant in the sedge family (Cyperaceae). It occurs in marshes, shallow wetlands, and along the margins of lakes and streams across much of the Northern Hemisphere.

The species is ecologically important as a primary colonizer of disturbed wetland substrates and a stabilizer of shorelines and streambanks. Its dense rhizomatous mats provide [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]] and habitat structure for wetland invertebrates and amphibians.

Common spikerush is one of the most cosmopolitan wetland sedges, occupying habitats from arctic tundra ponds to temperate marshes and subtropical wetlands. This broad ecological amplitude makes it a reliable indicator species for hydric soils and wetland hydrology.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Poales
- **Family:** Cyperaceae
- **Genus:** Eleocharis
- **Species:** E. palustris (L.) Roem. & Schult. (1817)
- **Basionym:** Scirpus palustris L. (1753)

The taxonomy of E. palustris sensu lato is complex. Several infraspecific taxa have been recognized, and the species has been divided by some authorities into multiple segregate species. [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] plants are sometimes treated as E. palustris ssp. palustris.

The genus Eleocharis contains approximately 250 species worldwide. E. palustris is one of the most widely distributed members of the genus.

Related species that frequently co-occur with E. palustris include E. acicularis (needle spikerush), E. obtusa (blunt spikerush), and E. erythropoda (red-rooted spikerush). Field identification often requires examination of achene and tubercle morphology under magnification.

## Morphology

Common spikerush is a rhizomatous perennial producing dense tussocks of cylindrical, unbranched stems. Stems are 15–80 cm tall, 1.5–4 mm in diameter, bright green to yellowish-green, and smooth to slightly roughened toward the apex.

The rhizome system is extensive and creeping, producing new shoots at regular intervals. A single genet can spread over several square meters, forming dense monospecific stands that exclude competing vegetation through both physical crowding and allelopathic [[mycorrhizal-root-exudates-pathogen-interactions]].

The leaves are reduced to basal sheaths surrounding the stem base. The sheaths are brownish to purplish at the summit, forming a characteristic collar around each stem. This leaf reduction is a common adaptation in emergent aquatic plants, minimizing transpiration while maintaining photosynthetic capacity through the green stem surface.

The inflorescence is a solitary terminal spikelet, 1–3 cm long and 4–6 mm wide, ellipsoid to ovoid. Glumes are 2.5–3.5 mm long, brownish to reddish-brown with a green midrib and broadly membranous margins.

Flowers are bisexual and arranged spirally on the rachilla. Each flower is subtended by a glume and has 2–3 stamens and a 2–3 branched style. The fruit is an achene (nutlet), 1.2–1.8 mm long, yellowish-brown to olive, with a persistent tubercle (style base) at the apex. The tubercle is conical to pyramidal, approximately 0.3 mm wide at the base — a key diagnostic character separating E. palustris from similar species.

Achene dispersal occurs primarily through water transport and animal movement. The small size and buoyant nature of the achenes facilitate long-distance dispersal along watercourses and between wetland basins.

## Distribution and Habitat

Eleocharis palustris has a circumboreal distribution, occurring across Europe, Asia, and North America. In North America, it ranges from Alaska and Canada south to California, New Mexico, and the northeastern United States.

In Europe, the species occurs from Scandinavia and the British Isles south to the Mediterranean basin and eastward through Russia to Siberia. It is widespread in central European lowland wetlands and mountain lake margins.

Disjunct populations exist in South America (Andean wetlands) and southern Africa, suggesting a more continuous historical distribution during cooler geological periods. These relict populations are often found in isolated high-elevation bogs with microclimatic conditions resembling the species' Pleistocene range.

The species is found in a wide variety of wetland habitats including marshes, fens, pond margins, lake shores, stream banks, ditches, and seasonally flooded depressions. It tolerates both standing water and moist soil, growing in water depths from 0 to 30 cm.

Elevation ranges from sea level to approximately 2,500 m. It is common in montane and subalpine wetland systems throughout the Rocky Mountains and Sierra Nevada, where it colonizes glacial kettle ponds and snowmelt-fed meadows.

The species exhibits a broad tolerance for water chemistry, occurring in both oligotrophic and eutrophic wetlands. It can tolerate moderate salinity, extending its distribution into brackish coastal marshes and inland saline wetlands of the Great Basin.

Soil substrate preferences include mineral-rich clays, organic mucks, and sandy loams. Optimal growth occurs in fine-textured, anaerobic substrates with high [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] content, though the species readily colonizes disturbed sites with exposed mineral soil.

## Ecological Role

Common spikerush is a primary producer in wetland ecosystems and contributes significantly to primary productivity in emergent marsh communities. Dense stands provide important cover and foraging habitat for waterfowl, shorebirds, and wading birds.

The dense rhizome network stabilizes wetland substrates and reduces erosion from wave action and water flow. This makes the species valuable in [[comparison-constructed-wetlands-vs-bioswales]] and natural shoreline restoration projects.

The plant provides habitat for aquatic invertebrates including dragonfly nymphs, damselfly larvae, and various aquatic beetles. Amphibian species including frogs and salamanders use dense spikerush stands for breeding sites and refuge from predators.

Migratory waterfowl feed on the achenes and tubers during autumn migration. The seeds and rhizomes are consumed by various waterfowl species, and the dense stems provide nesting material for rails, bitterns, and other marsh-nesting birds.

## Cultivation and Restoration

Propagation is achieved through seed sowing, rhizome division, or transplanting established tussocks. Seeds require cold stratification of 30–60 days for optimal germination. Under warm conditions with saturated soil, germination rates of 60–80% have been reported.

Establishment from seed is slow, with seedlings taking 2–3 growing seasons to develop into dense stands. Transplanting rhizome sections accelerates establishment and is the preferred method for restoration projects requiring rapid vegetation cover.

Rhizome transplants should be planted at a density of 15–25 plugs per square meter for effective erosion control within one growing season. In constructed wetland applications, plug spacing of 30–45 cm provides adequate coverage within two growing seasons while minimizing material costs.

The species is widely used in constructed wetlands for [[mollison-designers-wastewater-treatment-wetlands]]. Its root zone provides substrate for microbial communities involved in nitrogen and phosphorus removal from contaminated water.

Constructed wetland systems planted with E. palustris have demonstrated effective removal of total nitrogen (40–70%) and total phosphorus (30–50%) from municipal wastewater. The dense root mat creates an oxidized rhizosphere that enhances nitrification-denitrification cycling.

## Ethnobotanical Uses

Indigenous peoples of North America used common spikerush for weaving mats, baskets, and other items. The stems were harvested in late summer when fully mature, dried, and woven using traditional techniques.

Among the Paiute and Shoshone peoples of the Great Basin, spikerush stems were woven into tightly coiled baskets used for storage and water transport. The stems were also used as thatching material for temporary shelters and as stuffing for pillows and cushions.

The rhizomes were occasionally consumed as a starchy food source by some Indigenous groups. The raw rhizomes are bitter but can be cooked to improve palatability. In Europe, the young shoots were historically gathered as a minor vegetable in some regions.

In traditional Chinese medicine, Eleocharis tuberosa (water chestnut) is a closely related species widely cultivated for its edible corms. E. palustris rhizomes have been used similarly in some Indigenous North American traditions, though they are smaller and less palatable than the cultivated species.
