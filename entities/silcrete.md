## Description

Silcrete (also known as [[cangagua]] in Ecuador) is a grey to red shiny hard layer that develops below some tropical forest soils. It forms when soil particles are cemented by silica deposits, creating a glassy and impermeable surface when exposed after forest clearing. In Ecuador, an iron-silica pan following hill contours lies approximately 3 metres below the forest floor. Once exposed by deforestation and topsoil loss, this cangagua surface is daunting in its impermeability and resistance to plant establishment.

A related variant called durian forms as a silica-cemented non-wetting horizon that is earthy and brittle, found exclusively in volcanic areas. The red-brown hardpan form of silcrete occurs in many soil types (not exclusively volcanic) and can range from 10 cm to an extraordinary 30 metres thick. In semi-arid zones, silcrete is commonly encountered.

## Classification

- **Category:** Soil concretion / hardpan
- **Composition:** Silica-cemented soil particles (may include iron-silica combinations)
- **Colour:** Grey to red, shiny/glassy when exposed
- **Local names:** Cangagua (Ecuador)
- **Related variant:** Durian — silica-cemented non-wetting horizon, earthy and brittle, volcanic areas only
- **Thickness range:** 10 cm to 30 metres
- **Geographic occurrence:** Tropical forest subsoils, volcanic areas, semi-arid zones
- **Characteristics:** Glassy, impermeable surface when exposed

## Key Facts

- Gives a glassy surface when forests are cleared and topsoil is lost
- Follows hill contours at depth (observed at 3 m depth in Ecuador)
- Durian variant is found only in volcanic areas, is earthy and brittle rather than glassy
- Red-brown hardpan can be extraordinarily thick (up to 30 metres documented)
- If silcrete lies below forests, clearing the forest is considered unwise and effectively irreversible
- Semi-arid zones commonly affected
- Cangagua in Ecuador is an iron-silica combination, making it doubly impermeable
- The process is geologically slow but exposure and erosion accelerate surface hardening

## Relevance to Cultivation

Silcrete is arguably the most dangerous of the soil concretions for cultivation because it forms beneath apparently healthy tropical forests. Farmers who clear such forests may discover too late that the productive capacity resides entirely in the topsoil, with an impermeable silica pan underneath that cannot be rehabilitated. Once exposed, the glassy surface resists water infiltration, root penetration, and organic matter accumulation. Mollison's recommendation is unequivocal: where silcrete or cangagua exists, maintain perpetual forests for non-timber products (honey, fruits, medicines) as the only sustainable land use.

This is a powerful argument for forest conservation in tropical regions with known silcrete subsoils. The economic value of the forest as a perpetual polyculture far exceeds the short-term returns from clearing land that will become barren once the thin organic topsoil is lost.

## Management Approaches

- Do not clear forest over known silcrete subsoils — prevention is the only reliable strategy
- Where already exposed, deep ripping combined with massive organic matter addition may partially restore productivity
- Reforestation with pioneer species in mechanically ripped lines
- Mulch pits and terracing on sloping land above silcrete
- In volcanic areas, the durian variant may be more manageable due to its earthy, brittle nature
