## Overview

Echeveria pulidonis is a stemless, rosette-forming succulent in the family Crassulaceae. It is native to semi-arid regions of central Mexico, where it grows on rocky hillsides and limestone outcrops.

The species is prized in horticulture for its symmetric, tightly packed rosettes of pale blue-green leaves with vivid red margins. It produces bright yellow, bell-shaped flowers on arching inflorescences in spring and early summer.

Common names include Pulido's echeveria and "pulidonis." The species epithet honors Marcelino Pulido, a Mexican plant collector who contributed specimens to European botanical gardens in the early 20th century.

Like most Echeveria species, it is valued for its drought tolerance and low-maintenance requirements. It is widely grown in rock gardens, container arrangements, and xeriscaping projects worldwide.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Echeveria pulidonis was described by E. Walther in 1935 from material collected near Tehuacán, Puebla, Mexico. The genus Echeveria honors the 18th-century Mexican botanical artist Atanasio Echeverría y Godoy.

The species belongs to Echeveria series Urbinae, a group of small to medium rosette-forming species with glaucous leaves. Close relatives include Echeveria elegans and Echeveria runyonii.

The plant forms solitary or slowly offsetting rosettes 15–20 cm in diameter. Leaves are oblanceolate, 4–7 cm long, concave on the upper surface, and tipped with a short reddish mucro.

In cultivation, mature plants may reach 25 cm across with proper feeding and light. Offsets appear from the base after 2–3 years of growth.

Leaf coloration is a distinctive pale glaucous blue-green. Margins display a conspicuous red border that intensifies under bright light and drought stress.

The inflorescence is a single or branched cincinnus reaching 20–30 cm tall. Flowers are pentamerous, bright yellow, approximately 1.5 cm long, and appear from March through June.

Chromosome number is 2n = 54, consistent with many polyploid Echeveria species. The species hybridizes readily in cultivation with other members of series Urbinae.

The roots are fibrous and shallow, adapted to anchoring in thin soil pockets on rock faces. Root systems rarely extend deeper than 10 cm in mature specimens.

## Distribution and Habitat

The species is endemic to the Mexican states of Puebla and Oaxaca. It occurs at elevations of 1,500–2,000 m on calcareous rock faces and thin volcanic soils.

Populations grow in thorn-scrub and tropical deciduous forest margins. Plants occupy microsites with excellent drainage, full sun exposure, and minimal competition from surrounding vegetation.

Associated species include Agave spp., Hechtia spp., and Selaginella spp. in the rocky understory. The plant community is adapted to seasonal drought and intense solar radiation.

Rainfall in its native range is concentrated in a summer monsoon pattern. Annual precipitation is 400–700 mm, with a pronounced dry season from November through April.

Winters are dry, with plants entering a semi-dormant state requiring little moisture. During this period the outermost leaves may desiccate and form a protective papery sheath around the rosette.

## Cultivation

Echeveria pulidonis requires gritty, well-draining soil with a pH near neutral to slightly alkaline. A standard mix combines equal parts pumice, coarse sand, and a small proportion of organic matter.

Bright light is essential for maintaining compact rosettes and vivid red leaf margins. Plants grown in shade become etiolated, with elongated leaves and loss of coloration.

Water thoroughly only when the soil has dried completely. Overwatering is the primary cause of root rot and basal stem collapse in container specimens.

Propagation is by leaf cuttings, stem offsets, or seed. Leaf cuttings should be allowed to callus for 3–5 days before placing on damp substrate.

Seeds germinate in 7–14 days at 20–25 °C under surface-sown conditions. Seedlings grow slowly and benefit from protection from strong sun during their first year.

Division of established clumps is another reliable method. Each offset should retain some root tissue for fastest establishment.

The species is hardy to approximately −2 °C for brief periods. In frost-prone climates it is grown as a container plant moved indoors during winter.

Mealybugs and vine weevil larvae are the most common pests. Systemic insecticides applied as soil drenches in spring effectively prevent infestations.

The species responds well to balanced foliar feeding at quarter-strength during active growth (April–September). Excess nitrogen produces soft, elongated growth vulnerable to disease.

## Ecological Role and Conservation

In habitat, Echeveria pulidonis provides nectar for hummingbirds and native bees during its spring flowering period. Its compact rosette form reduces water loss in exposed rocky environments.

The species has a limited natural range and may be vulnerable to over-collection by plant poachers. Several populations occur near roadsides and agricultural margins where habitat degradation is ongoing.

It is listed as Near Threatened by some regional assessments due to its restricted extent of occurrence. Cultivation from seed and tissue-cultured nursery stock reduces pressure on wild populations.

The species is included in the succulent plant collections of several major botanical gardens. Kew Gardens, the Huntington Botanical Gardens, and the Arnold Arboretum maintain documented accessions.

CAM (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism) photosynthesis allows the plant to fix carbon dioxide at night, minimizing water loss. This adaptation is shared across nearly all Crassulaceae succulents.

The waxy cuticle on leaf surfaces further reduces transpiration and gives the foliage its characteristic glaucous appearance. This bloom can be damaged by handling or overhead watering.

## Similar Species

Echeveria pulidonis is frequently confused with Echeveria elegans in the nursery trade. E. pulidonis differs in its broader, more concave leaves and brighter yellow flowers.

Echeveria runyonii 'Topsy Turvy' has similarly glaucous foliage but displays strongly recurved, somewhat tubular leaves. E. pulidonis leaves remain flat to slightly concave.

Echeveria colorata is another blue-leaved species but has narrower, more lance-shaped leaves without the pronounced red margins. Geographic separation also distinguishes them, with E. colorata occurring in Jalisco.

The red leaf margins of E. pulidonis are more consistent and vivid than those of related species. In low-light conditions, this red border fades to pink or greenish.

Hybrid cultivars sold under the name E. × pulidonis crosses often display intermediate traits. These are popular in the succulent trade but are not true-to-type.

Echeveria pulidonis has received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit. This designation confirms its reliability and ornamental value in temperate gardens.
