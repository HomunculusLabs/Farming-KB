# Rexford F. Daubenmire

## Description

Rexford F. Daubenmire (1909-1995) was an American plant ecologist and botanist whose influential textbook *Plants and Environment* (first published 1947, multiple editions through 1974) became a foundational reference in permaculture design. Mollison cites Daubenmire's 1974 edition as an authority on the effects of slope orientation on local climate, specifically the relationship between aspect, solar radiation, and soil temperature. His quantitative approach to plant-environment relationships provided the scientific backbone for many permaculture design principles.

## Key Facts

- Daubenmire spent his career at Washington State University, where he conducted pioneering research on plant communities and environmental gradients in the Inland Northwest of the United States.
- His textbook *Plants and Environment* was among the first to systematically describe how individual environmental factors (light, temperature, water, soil, fire, wind, biotic factors) control plant distribution and growth at the species level.
- **Mollison's specific citation**: Daubenmire (1974) records that slopes of 5 degrees towards the poles "reduce soil temperatures as much as 168 km distance" towards the poles. This quantification of the slope-aspect effect on microclimate is one of the most frequently applied data points in permaculture site design.
- His research on forest ecology included landmark studies of old-growth forests in the Pacific Northwest, contributing to modern understanding of succession, climax communities, and forest structure.
- Developed the widely used **Daubenmire cover-class method** for estimating plant cover in ecological surveys, a standard technique still taught in ecology courses worldwide.
- His work on altitudinal and latitudinal vegetation zonation provided the scientific basis for Mollison's altitude-latitude equivalence principle (approximately 100 m altitude equals 1 degree latitude).
- Published extensively on the ecology of grasslands, shrub-steppe, and coniferous forest communities of the Pacific Northwest and Northern Rockies.

## Influence on Permaculture

Daubenmire's work underpins several core permaculture principles that appear throughout Mollison's manual:

- **Slope and aspect analysis**: The 5-degree slope equivalence to 168 km of latitude change directly informs [[permaculture-zone-and-sector-analysis]], guiding placement of frost-sensitive versus cold-hardy species on different slope orientations.
- **Altitudinal effects on climate**: His documentation of temperature lapse rates and their interaction with humidity, radiation, and wind provides the scientific framework for designing polyculture systems on hillsides and mountains, enabling cultivators to select appropriate species for each altitudinal band.
- **Environmental factor analysis**: The systematic approach to individual environmental factors (soil, light, temperature, wind, water) that Daubenmire pioneered became the model for Mollison's "designer's checklist" methodology found at the end of each chapter.
- **Vegetation-environment gradients**: Daubenmire's concept of environmental gradients controlling species distribution directly parallels the permaculture principle of "everything gardens" — that each element in a landscape creates specific conditions for other elements.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Site analysis**: Daubenmire's slope-aspect data guides placement of mushroom beds, composting operations, and fungal inoculation sites where specific temperature and moisture regimes are required for optimal growth.
- **Microclimate mapping**: His quantitative approach to environmental gradients helps cultivators identify favourable niches for outdoor mushroom cultivation, with north-facing slopes suiting cool-loving species and south-facing slopes for thermophilic species.
- **Vegetation-environment relationships**: Understanding how environmental factors control plant distribution is equally applicable to understanding fungal distribution patterns, particularly for ectomycorrhizal species that track their host trees along environmental gradients.
- **Gradient-based species selection**: The Daubenmire framework of matching species to specific environmental conditions rather than forcing species into unsuitable sites is fundamental to successful outdoor mushroom cultivation.
