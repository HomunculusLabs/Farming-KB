# Agaricus

## Description

Agaricus is a large genus of mushrooms containing some of the most widely cultivated and commercially important species in the world. The genus includes the common white button mushroom ([[agaricus-bisporus]]), portabella, cremini, and the almond portabella. Agaricus species are terrestrial saprobes that typically fruit on composted manure and rich organic substrates. They are among the few mushroom genera that strongly benefit from or require microbial interactions in [[casing-soil]] to trigger fruitbody formation.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Basidiomycota
- **Class**: Agaricomycetes
- **Order**: Agaricales
- **Family**: Agaricaceae
- **Genus**: Agaricus

## Key Facts

- Agaricus bisporus accounts for the majority of commercial mushroom production worldwide
- Unlike wood-decomposing species, Agaricus grows on composted substrates and requires casing soil for reliable fruiting
- Agaricus growers have discovered that sterilized casing soils — in which all microbes have been destroyed — result in no yields, while pasteurized casing soil almost always induces fruiting
- Grain spawn can be used directly for Agaricus on compost substrates, bypassing the substrate spawn generation
- The genus includes both cultivated edibles and toxic species (e.g., Agaricus xanthodermus)
- Agaricus species are secondary decomposers, thriving on substrates partially broken down by other organisms

## Relevance to Cultivation

Agaricus cultivation represents one of the most technologically advanced sectors of mushroom farming due to its commercial scale. The requirement for microbial interactions in casing soil makes Agaricus cultivation fundamentally different from cultivating wood-decomposing species. Growers must master composting, [[casing-soil-preparation]], and pasteurization techniques to preserve beneficial microbes while eliminating pathogens.

## Relevance to Mycology

The Agaricus-microbe interaction is a model system for studying fungal-microbial symbiosis and fruiting triggers. Research into specific microbial partners that stimulate Agaricus fruiting has implications for understanding fungal ecology, decomposition pathways, and the evolution of mutualistic relationships between fungi and bacteria.
