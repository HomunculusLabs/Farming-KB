## Overview

Desmanthus leptolobus is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Fabaceae, subfamily Mimosoideae, commonly known as slender-pod bundleflower or prairie mimosa. The species is native to the central and south-central United States and northern Mexico.

The plant has attracted ethnobotanical interest because it contains N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT) in its root bark, making it one of several North American legumes known to produce this psychoactive tryptamine alkaloid. The presence of DMT in Desmanthus leptolobus was first documented in the 1990s.

Desmanthus leptolobus is closely related to Desmanthus illinoensis, which is more widely known as a DMT-containing species. The two species are frequently confused in the ethnobotanical literature and in commercial seed offerings.

The plant is also valued in agriculture and land management for its nitrogen-fixing capabilities, drought tolerance, and value as a forage and cover crop. It is increasingly planted in prairie restoration projects throughout its native range. Several state and federal conservation agencies recommend Desmanthus species for native habitat reclamation.

## Taxonomy

**Kingdom:** Plantae

**Division:** Angiospermae

**Class:** Magnoliopsida

**Order:** Fabales

**Family:** Fabaceae

**Subfamily:** Caesalpinioideae (formerly Mimosoideae)

**Genus:** Desmanthus

**Species:** D. leptolobus

**Authority:** Torr. & A. Gray (1838)

The genus Desmanthus was established by Carl Ludwig Willdenow in 1791, with the name derived from the Greek "desme" (bundle) and "anthos" (flower), referring to the dense, bundled flower heads characteristic of the genus. The genus contains approximately 24 species distributed in the Americas, from the southern United States through Central America to Argentina.

The specific epithet "leptolobus" derives from the Greek "leptos" (slender) and "lobos" (pod or lobe), referring to the narrow, slender seed pods that distinguish this species from its close relatives.

The taxonomic distinction between Desmanthus leptolobus and Desmanthus illinoensis has been a source of confusion. The two species are morphologically similar and were sometimes treated as varieties of a single species. Modern treatments generally recognize them as distinct, with D. leptolobus having narrower leaflets and more slender pods.

## Morphology

Desmanthus leptolobus is an erect to spreading perennial herb growing 30 to 100 centimeters tall from a woody taproot. The root system is deep and extensive, with a prominent taproot that can reach 1 meter or more in depth. This deep root system contributes to the species' drought tolerance and also contains the highest concentrations of DMT.

The stems are slender, branching from the base, and green to purplish-green in color. Young stems may have a sparse covering of fine hairs, becoming glabrous (hairless) with age.

The leaves are bipinnately compound, 5 to 15 centimeters long, with 2 to 4 pairs of pinnae. Each pinna bears 10 to 25 pairs of small, linear-oblong leaflets, each 3 to 8 millimeters long and 1 to 2 millimeters wide. The leaflets are distinctive in their small size and numerous pairs, giving the foliage a fine, feathery appearance reminiscent of sensitive plant (Mimosa pudica).

The flowers are borne in dense, globose heads approximately 8 to 12 millimeters in diameter, on peduncles 1 to 3 centimeters long. Individual flowers are small and cream-white to pale yellow, with the typical mimosoid structure of 5 fused petals forming a tubular corolla and numerous conspicuous stamens. Flowering occurs from June through September.

The fruit is a linear, flat pod 4 to 8 centimeters long and 3 to 5 millimeters wide, typically containing 6 to 12 seeds. The pods are markedly narrower than those of Desmanthus illinoensis, which is the key morphological character distinguishing the two species. Seeds are brown, ovoid, and approximately 2 to 3 millimeters long.

## Distribution and Habitat

Desmanthus leptolobus is native to the central and south-central United States, occurring from Kansas and Missouri south through Oklahoma, Texas, Arkansas, and Louisiana, extending into northern and central Mexico. The species is most abundant in the Great Plains region, particularly in the Texas blackland prairies and the Cross Timbers ecoregion.

The plant grows in prairies, open woodlands, roadsides, pastures, and disturbed areas. It prefers well-drained clay or loam soils and is tolerant of both alkaline and slightly acidic conditions. It is commonly found in areas with 60 to 100 centimeters of annual rainfall.

Desmanthus leptolobus is well-adapted to the climate of the southern Great Plains, with its deep root system providing drought tolerance during hot, dry summers. It is one of the earlier species to green up in spring and can remain productive through the growing season with adequate moisture.

The species has been introduced outside its native range for agricultural and erosion control purposes, with established populations reported in parts of the southeastern United States and in Australia. In Australia, Desmanthus species are commercially cultivated as forage legumes for cattle production in the semi-arid tropics and subtropics.

## Chemistry and Bioactive Compounds

The root bark of Desmanthus leptolobus contains N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT) as its principal psychoactive alkaloid. DMT is a potent serotonergic psychedelic that acts as a partial agonist at the 5-HT2A receptor and other serotonin receptor subtypes.

DMT concentrations in the root bark are reported to range from approximately 0.05% to 0.3% of dry weight, depending on the population, growing conditions, and the specific plant part analyzed. The root bark generally contains higher concentrations than the roots or above-ground parts.

Trace amounts of other tryptamine derivatives have also been detected, including N-methyltryptamine (NMT) and 5-methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (5-MeO-DMT), though these are typically present at much lower concentrations than DMT.

The DMT in Desmanthus leptolobus is not orally active without co-administration of a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI), as the enzyme monoamine oxidase rapidly degrades DMT in the gastrointestinal tract and liver before it can reach the central nervous system. This pharmacological limitation applies to all botanical DMT sources.

Beyond its tryptamine alkaloids, Desmanthus leptolobus contains typical Fabaceae constituents including tannins, flavonoids, and various phenolic compounds. The forage quality of the aerial parts is considered good, with adequate protein content for livestock.

## Ethnobotanical and Agricultural Significance

Unlike many other DMT-containing plants such as Psychotria viridis or Diplopterys cabrerana, Desmanthus leptolobus does not have a well-documented history of indigenous ceremonial or medicinal use. The psychoactive properties of the plant were discovered through modern phytochemical investigation rather than through ethnobotanical fieldwork.

The species has, however, gained attention in modern ethnopharmacological circles as a locally accessible source of DMT for individuals in North America. The roots can be harvested and extracted using standard acid-base extraction techniques.

In agriculture, Desmanthus leptolobus is valued as a drought-tolerant, nitrogen-fixing forage legume. It provides good-quality forage for cattle and wildlife, and its deep root system helps prevent soil erosion. The species is increasingly used in prairie restoration and reclamation of degraded rangelands.

Desmanthus species, including D. leptolobus, are being investigated as potential perennial forage crops in semi-arid regions. Their drought tolerance, nitrogen fixation, and palatability make them attractive alternatives to annual forage crops in water-limited environments.

Cultivation from seed requires scarification of the hard seed coat, either through mechanical abrasion or hot water treatment, to achieve satisfactory germination rates. Established plants are long-lived and can persist for many years once the deep taproot is fully developed.
