## Overview

Desmanthus leptolobus is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Fabaceae, subfamily Mimosoideae, commonly known as slender-pod bundleflower or prairie mimosa. The species is native to the central and south-central United States and northern Mexico.

The plant has attracted ethnobotanical interest because it contains N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT) in its root bark, making it one of several [[reversed-phase-hplc-tryptamine-alkaloid-quantification-methodology]]. The presence of DMT in Desmanthus leptolobus was first documented in the 1990s.

Desmanthus leptolobus is closely related to [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]], and value as a forage and cover crop. It is increasingly planted in [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-caribbean-central-america]] to Argentina.

The specific epithet "leptolobus" derives from the Greek "leptos" (slender) and "lobos" (pod or lobe), referring to the narrow, slender seed pods that distinguish this species from its close relatives.

The taxonomic distinction between Desmanthus leptolobus and Desmanthus illinoensis has been a source of confusion. The two species are morphologically similar and were sometimes treated as varieties of a single species. Modern treatments generally recognize them as distinct, with D. leptolobus having narrower leaflets and more slender pods.

## Morphology

Desmanthus leptolobus is an erect to spreading perennial herb growing 30 to 100 centimeters tall from a woody taproot. The root system is deep and extensive, with a prominent taproot that can reach 1 meter or more in depth. This deep root system contributes to the species' drought tolerance and also contains the highest concentrations of DMT.

The stems are slender, branching from the base, and green to purplish-green in color. Young stems may have a sparse covering of fine hairs, becoming glabrous (hairless) with age.

The leaves are bipinnately compound, 5 to 15 centimeters long, with 2 to 4 pairs of pinnae. Each pinna bears 10 to 25 pairs of small, linear-oblong leaflets, each 3 to 8 millimeters long and 1 to 2 millimeters wide. The leaflets are distinctive in their small size and numerous pairs, giving the foliage a fine, feathery appearance reminiscent of sensitive plant (Mimosa pudica).

The flowers are borne in dense, globose heads approximately 8 to 12 millimeters in diameter, on peduncles 1 to 3 centimeters long. Individual flowers are small and cream-white to pale yellow, with the typical mimosoid structure of 5 fused petals forming a tubular corolla and numerous conspicuous stamens. Flowering occurs from June through September.

The fruit is a linear, flat pod 4 to 8 centimeters long and 3 to 5 millimeters wide, typically containing 6 to 12 seeds. The pods are markedly narrower than those of Desmanthus illinoensis, which is the key morphological character distinguishing the two species. Seeds are brown, ovoid, and approximately 2 to 3 millimeters long.

DMT concentrations in the root bark are reported to range from approximately 0.05% to 0.3% of dry weight, depending on the population, growing conditions, and the specific plant part analyzed. The root bark generally contains higher concentrations than the roots or above-ground parts.

Trace amounts of other tryptamine derivatives have also been detected, including N-methyltryptamine (NMT) and 5-methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (5-MeO-DMT), though these are typically present at much lower concentrations than DMT.

The DMT in Desmanthus leptolobus is not orally active without co-administration of a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI), as the enzyme monoamine oxidase rapidly degrades DMT in the gastrointestinal tract and liver before it can reach the central nervous system.
