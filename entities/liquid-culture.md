## Description

Liquid culture (LC) is a method of propagating mushroom mycelium in a nutrient-rich liquid
medium rather than on solid agar or grain. Mycelium is blended into a pourable slurry
using sterilized water and colonized agar or [[grain-spawn]], creating a homogenized
suspension of living fungal cells that can be rapidly distributed across large volumes of
grain or substrate.

Liquid culture offers significantly faster colonization rates than dry transfers such as
agar-to-grain or grain-to-grain methods. It also requires much less of the original
culture material per unit of spawn produced. A single agar plate can provide enough liquid
culture to inoculate dozens or hundreds of grain jars, dramatically reducing the labor and
time required for commercial-scale [[spawn-production]].

The technique is particularly valuable for commercial mushroom operations that need to
scale from a limited number of petri plate cultures to large volumes of production spawn.
However, the high water content of liquid culture also makes it more susceptible to
bacterial contamination, making quality control via streak testing essential before any
large-scale expansion.

## Key Facts

Preparation requires a blender jar that can be sterilized in an autoclave. Cotter uses a
regular Oster-type blender with the plastic lid and base removed before autoclaving,
keeping only the metal blade and rubber base which are heat-tolerant. The blender jar is
covered with aluminum foil on top and bottom, placed in an autoclavable bag, and
sterilized alongside a separate jar of water for 30 minutes.

After cooling under laminar flow, the plastic base and lid are disinfected with alcohol
and reattached. Colonized agar cubes or grain spawn chunks are added to a small amount of
sterilized water and blended until the mixture is thick but pourable. The resulting liquid
culture is stored in sterilized tubes or jars.

The standard inoculation rate is 10 ml of liquid culture per quart-size jar. Unlike agar
wedges, liquid culture requires no shaking after inoculation because the homogenized
suspension distributes evenly by gravity and capillary action throughout the grain.

The primary advantage of liquid culture over agar-based transfer is the much more rapid
colonization rate and the ability to use significantly less original culture. The primary
disadvantage is the increased risk of bacterial contamination due to the high water
content of the medium.

## Streak Test Screening

Before expanding liquid cultures, a streak test should be performed on antibiotic-free
[[malt-extract-agar]]. Potato-based agar should not be used because many bacteria species do
not propagate well on it. A loop is dipped into the liquid culture and dragged across a
fresh plate in a Z pattern.

The plate is sealed with Parafilm or tape and incubated for several days. A solid line of
mycelium without bacterial or mold colonies confirms the culture is clean. If contaminants
are found, the entire expansion lineage must be traced back through preceding generations
via serial streak tests to locate the contamination source.

If the streak test is positive for contaminants, repeat the process using every preceding
expansion of the contaminated culture, from the original isolate on up. This establishes
which expansions are contaminated and allows you to revert to an uncontaminated
generation.

## Storage Options

Liquid cultures can be refrigerated in sterile tubes or jars for short to medium term
storage. For long-term backup, they can be frozen with 5 percent glycerol as a
cryoprotectant. Before committing an entire collection to frozen storage, viability should
be tested by thawing one tube monthly and transferring to fresh agar.

Slurry preparation for freezing should include a 1 to 3 day recovery agitation period on a
magnetic stirrer to allow mycelial cells to repair the damage caused by the blender during
slurry preparation.

## Relevance to Cultivation

Liquid culture is the preferred method for commercial mushroom operations that need to
produce large volumes of spawn efficiently. It enables rapid scaling from a small number
of petri plates to thousands of pounds of inoculated grain or substrate. The streak test
is the essential quality control gate that should never be skipped before large-scale
expansion.

Traditional agar-to-grain and grain-to-grain transfers remain reliable but are slower and
more labor-intensive. Liquid culture trades some margin of safety for dramatically
improved efficiency and scalability, making it the workhorse technique of modern
commercial spawn production.
