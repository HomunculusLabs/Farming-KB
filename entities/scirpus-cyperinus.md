## Overview

Scirpus cyperinus, commonly known as woolgrass or woolly bulrush, is a perennial herbaceous plant in the family Cyperaceae. Despite its common name, the species is a sedge rather than a true rush or bulrush, reflecting the historical [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] sedges, recognised by its dense, woolly-looking inflorescences that give the plant a soft, fuzzy appearance during the late summer and autumn flowering period.

The distinctive woolly inflorescences serve an important [[mushroom-spore-visual-identification]]. The dense hairs surrounding each achene trap air bubbles, increasing the buoyancy of the diaspore and enhancing its ability to float on water surfaces for extended periods during hydrochorous dispersal.

Woolgrass is a clonal species that spreads through both rhizomes and seed dispersal, forming dense tussocks and extensive stands in suitable wetland conditions. It is considered an important component of freshwater marsh and wet meadow communities across much of its range.

## Taxonomy and Identification

The species was originally described as Schoenus cyperinus by Linnaeus in 1753 and was later transferred to the genus Scirpus by Kunth. Modern taxonomic treatments have variously placed it in Scirpus sensu stricto or in related genera, though the name Scirpus cyperinus remains widely used in floristic and ecological literature.

The Latin specific epithet cyperinus means resembling Cyperus, referring to the superficial similarity of its inflorescence to those of the flatsedge genus. This resemblance is most apparent in the arrangement of the spikelets and the overall umbellate structure of the flowering head.

Some older botanical references use the synonym Scirpus cyperinus var. creber, which was applied to more robust, densely tufted forms found in calcareous wetlands. Modern treatments generally do not recognise infraspecific taxa within Scirpus cyperinus, considering the observed variation to be environmentally induced rather than genetically fixed.

Scirpus cyperinus is a robust perennial, typically growing 60 to 150 centimetres tall. The culms are stout, erect, and triangular in cross-section, with leaf blades up to 10 millimetres wide concentrated on the lower third of the stem.

The inflorescence is a terminal cluster of numerous spikelets subtended by several long, leaf-like bracts that extend well beyond the flowers. Individual spikelets are 5 to 10 millimetres long and are densely covered in long, reddish-brown to copper-coloured hairs that give the inflorescence its characteristic woolly appearance.

The woolly hairs are actually elongated perianth bristles that persist and expand as the fruit matures, creating the conspicuous cottony tuft that makes the species so easily identifiable in the field even from a considerable distance.

These bristles are typically 5 to 15 millimetres long and vary in colour from pale copper to deep reddish-brown, depending on the maturity of the inflorescence and the local growing conditions. In some populations, the bristles are particularly long and silky, giving the inflorescence an almost feathery appearance.

## Distribution and Habitat

Scirpus cyperinus is widely distributed across eastern and central North America, ranging from Nova Scotia and southern Quebec westward to Minnesota and eastern Nebraska, and southward to eastern Texas and northern Florida. The species is most abundant in the mid-Atlantic and midwestern states.

The western range limit corresponds roughly to the eastern edge of the tallgrass prairie region, beyond which the species is replaced by other Scirpus and Schoenoplectus species adapted to prairie pothole and plains wetland conditions.

The species is absent from the southeastern coastal plain, the Gulf Coast, and most of the Great Plains, though isolated populations occur in suitable habitat within these regions. It reaches its greatest abundance in the glaciated landscapes of the northeastern and midwestern United States.

Populations in the southern Appalachians and the Ozark Highlands represent disjunct occurrences that may be relicts of a broader historical distribution. These montane populations are often found in seepage wetlands and spring-fed bogs at higher elevations.

Typical habitats include freshwater marshes, wet meadows, sedge meadows, fens, swamp margins, stream banks, pond edges, roadside ditches, and low-lying areas subject to seasonal flooding. It tolerates a wide range of hydrological conditions, from shallow standing water to seasonally saturated soils.

Woolgrass is often a dominant or codominant species in emergent marsh communities, particularly in areas with moderate [[carex-stricta]]–Scirpus cyperinus wet meadow association described in several midwestern state vegetation classifications.

## Ecology

Woolgrass provides significant ecological structure and function within wetland ecosystems. The dense tussocks created by clonal growth offer nesting habitat and cover for marsh birds, waterfowl, and small mammals, while the above-ground biomass supports a diverse invertebrate community.

Several bird species of conservation concern, including the Virginia rail, sora, and marsh wren, regularly nest within woolgrass stands. The dense vertical structure of the tussocks provides protection from predators and shelter from wind and weather.
