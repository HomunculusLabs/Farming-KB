## Overview

**Juniperus sabina**, commonly known as savin juniper, is a low-growing evergreen conifer native to mountainous regions of Europe and western Asia. It is widely cultivated as an ornamental ground cover but is notable for containing toxic compounds, particularly sabinene and sabinol.

The species typically forms a dense, prostrate shrub reaching 1–2 meters in height and 3–5 meters in spread. It is one of the most cold-hardy junipers, tolerating temperatures below -30°C. Its sprawling habit and bluish-green foliage make it popular in landscape plantings.

Savin juniper has a long history of use in traditional European [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]].

The bark is reddish-brown, thin, and exfoliating in long strips on older stems. Rooting at branch tips is common where branches contact moist soil, enabling vegetative spread.

The growth rate of savin juniper is slow to moderate, typically 10–20 cm of lateral spread per year under good conditions. Mature plants can exceed 100 years of age. The species is long-lived for a shrub, with some specimens in cultivation documented at over 200 years old.

### Ornamental Cultivars

Several cultivars are commercially available beyond 'Tamariscifolia'. 'Arcadia' is a compact, bright green selection useful for mass plantings. 'Blue Danube' offers silvery-blue foliage. 'Broadmoor' is a low, wide-spreading form with dense foliage that resists winter bronzing.

'Moor-Dense' is a German selection valued for its very dense, dark green canopy and superior winter hardiness. These cultivars share the toxic [[garden-myths-planting-and-pruning]] to avoid dermal exposure.

## Distribution and Habitat

Savin juniper is native to a broad range from southern and central Europe through the Caucasus and into the mountains of Central Asia. In Europe, it occurs from the Pyrenees and Alps eastward through the Carpathians and Balkans to Turkey and the Crimea.

The species typically grows at elevations of 1,000–3,300 meters in subalpine and alpine zones. It colonizes rocky slopes, scree fields, cliff ledges, and open subalpine meadows. It is particularly common on limestone substrates but also occurs on acidic igneous and metamorphic rocks.

Associated alpine flora includes dryas (*Dryas octopetala*), dwarf juniper (*Juniperus nana*), alpine avens (*Geum montanum*), and various saxifrages and sedums. In the Carpathians, savin juniper forms extensive mats intermixed with blueberry species and alpine roses.

Savin juniper is shade-intolerant and requires well-drained soils. It is highly drought-tolerant once established and tolerates both extreme cold and exposure to wind. In its native range, it often forms extensive mats on mountain slopes above the treeline.

The principal toxic constituents include sabinene, sabinol, sabinyl acetate, and thujone. These terpenoid compounds are concentrated in the foliage and berry-like cones.

The essential oil content of dried foliage ranges from 1.5% to 3.0%, depending on season and growing conditions. Sabinyl acetate is the dominant compound, typically comprising 30–50% of the total oil. The composition varies between populations and between seasonal growth flushes.

Ingestion of savin juniper foliage or berries causes severe toxic reactions in humans and livestock. Symptoms include burning of the mouth, nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, diarrhea, kidney irritation, and in severe cases, convulsions and death. The oils are abortifacient and have historically been used to induce abortion, frequently with fatal results for both mother and fetus.

Topical application of the essential oil can cause contact dermatitis and skin inflammation. The oils are readily absorbed through the skin, making even external use hazardous. Veterinary poisonings are reported in sheep, goats, and cattle that graze on savin juniper.

Despite its toxicity, the species has been used in traditional medicine as an emmenagogue, diuretic, and antirheumatic agent. These uses are strongly discouraged by modern toxicological authorities due to the narrow margin between therapeutic and toxic doses.

The cultivar 'Tamariscifolia' (tam juniper) is one of the most widely planted low-growing evergreens in temperate horticulture. It forms a dense, spreading mound 30–60 cm tall and 2–3 meters wide.

The species is valued for its tolerance of poor, dry, and alkaline soils where few other evergreens thrive. It requires full sun and good drainage.

It is highly resistant to urban pollution, de-icing salt, and browsing by deer, making it suitable for highway plantings and commercial landscapes. The species tolerates pH levels from 5.0 to 8.0, performing best in slightly alkaline conditions.
