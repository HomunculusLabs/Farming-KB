## Overview

Apium graveolens, commonly known as celery, is a biennial herbaceous plant in the family Apiaceae. It is cultivated worldwide for its crisp leaf stalks (petioles), aromatic seeds, and edible root (celeriac). The species has been used as both a food and medicinal plant for over 3,000 years, with origins traceable to the Mediterranean basin and marshlands of Eurasia.

Celery is valued in global cuisine for its distinctive flavor, which comes from a complex profile of volatile terpenes and phenylpropanoids. Medicinally, it has documented anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and hypotensive properties. The seeds are a major commercial source of celery seed extract used in phytotherapy.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Apium graveolens L. belongs to the order Apiales, family Apiaceae, subfamily Apioideae. The specific epithet *graveolens* means "strong-smelling" in Latin, referring to the pungent aroma of the foliage. Three main cultivar groups are recognized: dulce (stalk celery), rapaceum (celeriac), and secalinum (leaf celery or smallage).

The plant grows 30–100 cm tall with pinnately compound leaves divided into serrated leaflets. Stems are grooved and hollow, with the cultivated petiole varieties producing thick, fleshy, ribbed stalks in shades of pale green to gold. Flowers are small, white, and arranged in compound umbels typical of the Apiaceae.

The root system of wild-type celery is taprooted, while celeriac cultivars develop a large, swollen hypocotyl forming a bulbous root 8–14 cm in diameter. Seeds are small (1–1.5 mm), oval, light brown, and possess a characteristic furrow along one side.

## Distribution and Habitat

Wild celery (*A. graveolens* var. *graveolens*, known as smallage) is native to marshy areas and damp soils across Europe, North Africa, and temperate Asia. It grows along stream banks, ditches, and coastal salt marshes, preferring moist, nitrogen-rich soils in partial shade.

Wild populations exhibit greater genetic diversity than cultivated varieties, serving as a reservoir for disease resistance traits. Molecular studies using AFLP and SSR markers have identified distinct gene pools in Mediterranean, Central Asian, and East Asian wild celery populations.

Cultivated celery is grown commercially in temperate and subtropical regions worldwide. Major producers include the United States, China, India, Mexico, and Spain. California and Michigan account for the majority of US commercial production. The plant requires a long, cool growing season (150–180 days for seed-to-harvest) and is highly sensitive to both frost and heat stress.

## Cultivation

Celery is typically grown as an annual crop, though it is biennial in its natural lifecycle. Seeds are sown indoors 8–10 weeks before the last frost, as they require light for germination and consistent moisture. Transplanting occurs when seedlings reach 10–15 cm in height.

Optimal growth occurs at 15–21°C with consistent irrigation. Water stress causes pithiness, stringiness, and bolting. Commercial growers often blanch stalk celery by wrapping or earthing up to reduce bitterness and chlorophyll development. Spacing of 20–30 cm between plants is standard, with row spacing of 60–90 cm.

Common pests include celery leaf miner (*Euleia heraclei*), carrot rust fly (*Psila rosae*), and aphids. Fusarium yellows and late blight (*Septoria apiicola*) are the most significant diseases. Resistant cultivars and crop rotation with non-Apiaceae species are primary management strategies.

Celery has moderate nutrient requirements, with nitrogen being the most critical input for stalk development. Excessive nitrogen produces lush but brittle growth prone to lodging. Organic production relies on composted manures and cover cropping, with biological controls including *[[cervantes-bacillus-thuringiensis-bt-control]]* for lepidopteran pests. Hydroponic celery production is practiced commercially in controlled environments, reducing water usage by 80–90% compared to field cultivation.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

Celery contains a diverse array of [[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]]. The essential oil (0.1–0.5% of fresh weight) is dominated by **limonene** (60–70%), with significant contributions from **beta-selinene**, **sedanolide**, and **3-n-butylphthalide** (3nB). Phenylpropanoid compounds include **apiin**, **apigenin**, and **luteolin** glycosides.

3-n-Butylphthalide (3nB) is the most pharmacologically significant constituent. It has demonstrated vasodilatory activity, reducing blood pressure in multiple animal models and human [[mushroom-cancer-clinical-trials-overview]] at doses of 75–150 mg/day. Sedanolide and sedanonic anhydride exhibit chemopreventive activity against hepatic and colon carcinogenesis in rodent studies.

The flavonoid fraction, primarily **apigenin** and **luteolin**, contributes anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. Apigenin inhibits NF-κB signaling and TNF-α production in vitro.

Luteolin glycosides from celery demonstrate neuroprotective effects in animal models, reducing [[blesching-cannabis-oxidative-stress-and-antioxidant-defense]] markers in brain tissue. Both flavonoids show synergistic antioxidant activity with vitamin C and other polyphenols present in the plant matrix.

Celery seed extracts show COX-2 inhibitory activity comparable to non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs at standardized concentrations.

Traditional Chinese Medicine classifies celery as cooling and bitter, prescribing it for hypertension, gout, and urinary tract inflammation. Ayurvedic medicine uses celery seed for rheumatism, arthritis, and digestive complaints.

## Culinary and Nutritional Use

Celery stalks are consumed raw as a crudité, juiced, or cooked in soups, stews, and stuffings. Celeriac is a staple in European cuisine, used in remoulade, gratins, and purées. Celery seeds are ground into spice blends, pickled, or used whole in breads, salads, and tomato-based dishes.

Nutritionally, raw celery is approximately 95% water with only 16 kcal per 100 g. It provides meaningful amounts of vitamin K (29.3 µg/100 g), folate (36 µg/100 g), and potassium (260 mg/100 g). The dietary fiber content (1.6 g/100 g) supports digestive health. Celery juice has gained commercial popularity as a wellness beverage, though clinical evidence for specific health claims remains limited.

Celery salt, made from ground celery seed mixed with coarse salt, is a classic American condiment used on Bloody Mary cocktails, Chicago-style hot dogs, and potato salads. The seed oil is used commercially as a flavoring agent in processed foods, perfumes, and soaps. In East Asian cuisines, celery leaves are more commonly consumed than stalks, appearing in stir-fries, hot pots, and noodle soups as a fragrant garnish.

## Ecology and Applications

Celery flowers attract a wide range of pollinators, including syrphid flies, small beetles, and solitary bees. The plant serves as a larval host for the celery looper moth (*Anagrapha falcifera*) and black swallowtail butterfly (*Papilio polyxenes*). In agroecosystems, celery intercropped with allium species benefits from reduced pest pressure through natural insect repellency.

Celery residue and processing waste are being investigated for industrial applications. Seed cake remaining after oil extraction is rich in protein (20–25%) and has potential as animal feed. Essential oil fractions show antimicrobial activity against *Listeria monocytogenes* and *Escherichia coli*, suggesting applications as a natural food preservative.

## History and Ethnobotany

Celery was known to the ancient Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans primarily as a medicinal plant rather than a food. Homer's *Odyssey* references wild celery (selinon) growing near the cave of Calypso. Greek athletes were crowned with celery wreaths, and the plant was associated with funerary rites — leaves of celery were found in Tutankhamun's tomb.

The transition from medicinal wild smallage to the thick-stalked vegetable occurred gradually in Europe during the 16th–18th centuries. Italian and French gardeners developed the first blanched varieties. Celeriac was first described by the French botanist Tournefort in 1719 and became a staple of central European cuisine by the 19th century.

Celery was introduced to China via the Silk Road and is now one of the most widely used aromatic vegetables in Chinese cooking. In India, celery seed is a key ingredient in many spice mixtures and is used in Ayurvedic formulations for joint pain and digestive disorders. The commercial celery seed oil industry developed in the 20th century, with India and China as major producers.

## Notable Cultivars and Varieties

The three major cultivar groups of celery reflect distinct selection pressures. Stalk celery (var. *dulce*) includes green varieties like 'Pascal' and self-blanching gold types like 'Golden Self-Blanching'. Modern [[cervantes-hybrid-varieties-types]] such as 'Tango' and 'Conquistador' offer improved disease resistance, uniformity, and reduced stringiness.
