## Overview

**Suillus brevipes** is a common edible pored mushroom in the family
Suillaceae, widely distributed across coniferous forests of North America.
The species epithet *brevipes* ("short foot") refers to its characteristically
stumpy, nearly absent stipe. It forms ectomycorrhizal associations primarily
with two-needle pines (*Pinus* spp.) and is one of the most frequently
encountered Suillus species in the Pacific Northwest and Rocky Mountains.

Unlike many Suillus species, S. brevipes lacks a partial veil and therefore
produces no annulus or appendiculate cap margin, making field identification
relatively straightforward.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Boletales
- **Family:** Suillaceae
- **Genus:** *Suillus* Gray
- **Species:** *S. brevipes* (Peck) Kuntze (1898)

**Basionym:** *Boletus brevipes* Peck (1887). Described from specimens
collected in New York state under pine. Kuntze transferred it to *Suillus*
in his 1898 *Revisio Generum Plantarum*.

**Synonyms:** *Ixocomus brevipes* (Peck) Singer (1945); *Viscipellis
brevipes* (Peck) Murrill (1909).

## Morphology

**Cap:** 3–10 cm, convex becoming plano-convex to nearly flat with age.
Surface smooth, glabrous to slightly tacky when wet, dry and dull when
young. Color varies from pale yellow-brown to reddish brown, often with
a darker disc. Cuticle peels readily.

**Pore surface:** Adnexed to slightly decurrent. Tubes 4–10 mm deep,
pores angular, 1–2 per mm, initially pale yellow, aging to ochre-brown.
Does not stain blue.

**Stipe:** 1.5–4 cm long × 1–3 cm thick — conspicuously short relative
to cap diameter. Solid, equal or slightly tapered downward. Surface white
to pale yellow, sometimes with brownish streaks at the base. No ring.

**Flesh:** White to pale yellow, soft, unchanging when cut. Mild odor and
taste. Tubes sometimes slightly bitter in age.

**Spore print:** Olive-brown to cinnamon-brown.

**Spores:** 7–10 × 3–4.5 µm, smooth, elliptical to oblong, inamyloid.

**Chemical reactions:** KOH on cap surface negative to faint yellow.
Ammonia on cap flesh negative.

## Distribution and Habitat

S. brevipes is found across much of North America, from the Pacific
Northwest through the Rocky Mountains and into the northeastern United
States and eastern Canada. It also occurs in Mexico and has been reported
in Central America under introduced pine plantations.

The species is obligately ectomycorrhizal, forming associations primarily
with hard pines in subgenus *Pinus*: ponderosa pine (*P. ponderosa*),
lodgepole pine (*P. contorta*), jack pine (*P. banksiana*), and whitebark
pine (*P. albicaulis*). It fruits from late summer through autumn, often
in abundance following early autumn rains.

## Edibility

S. brevipes is edible but widely considered mediocre in quality. The cap
flesh is palatable when young and fresh, with a mild flavor, but the tubes
and stipe can become spongy and unappealing with age.

Preparation recommendations include peeling the slimy cap cuticle and
discarding the tubes. Best used in mixed mushroom dishes, sautéed or
added to soups. Not suitable for raw consumption due to potential
gastrointestinal irritation from undigested chitin.

As with all Suillus species, some individuals experience allergic
gastrointestinal reactions. First-time consumers should eat small
quantities. No cases of serious toxicity have been documented.

## Ecological Role

As an ectomycorrhizal fungus, S. brevipes facilitates nutrient and water
exchange with host pines, enhancing phosphorus uptake and drought
resistance in young trees. The species is a common early-successional
ectomycorrhizal associate in disturbed or regenerating pine forests.

Slugs, deer, and small mammals feed on the fruiting bodies. The mushroom
serves as a significant food source for the western red-backed vole and
other mycophagous mammals that subsequently disperse spores through their
feces.

## Cultivation

S. brevipes is not commercially cultivated due to its obligate
mycorrhizal dependency. Attempts at axenic culture have produced only
sparse mycelial growth without fruiting. Inoculation of pine seedling
root systems with spore slurries has been used experimentally in forestry
to improve seedling establishment on nutrient-poor sites.

## Similar Species

- *Suillus punctipes* — nearly identical but with conspicuous glandular
  dots on the stipe apex; western distribution.
- *Suillus cavipes* — has a hollow stipe and larger pores.
- *Suillus tomentosus* — has a tomentose (fuzzy) cap surface.
- *Suillus americanus* — associate of white pine (*P. strobus*), with
  more yellow tones.
