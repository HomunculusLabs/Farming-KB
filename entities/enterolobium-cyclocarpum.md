# Enterolobium cyclocarpum

**Enterolobium cyclocarpum** (Jacq.) Griseb., commonly known as the **ear pod tree**, **guanacaste**, or **parota**, is a large deciduous tree in the family Fabaceae. It is the national tree of Costa Rica, where it lends its name to the Guanacaste Province.

The species is among the largest and most conspicuous trees of the seasonally [[mollison-wet-dry-tropical-design-strategies]] forests of [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]]. It can reach heights of 30–40 m with a broad, spreading crown diameter exceeding 30 m.

Its distinctive spirally coiled pods resemble human ears, giving rise to both its scientific epithet (*cyclocarpum* = circular fruit) and common name.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Fabales
- **Family:** Fabaceae
- **Subfamily:** Caesalpinioideae
- **Tribe:** Mimoseae
- **Genus:** *Enterolobium*
- **Species:** *E. cyclocarpum*

The species was originally described by Nikolaus von Jacquin in 1763 as *Mimosa cyclocarpa*. It was later transferred to *Enterolobium* by August Heinrich Rudolf Grisebach in 1864.

The genus name *Enterolobium* derives from Greek (*enteron* = intestine, *lobos* = pod), referring to the coiled nature of the fruit.

**Morphological features:**

The trunk is straight and cylindrical, reaching 1.5–2 m in diameter at breast height. Bark is dark gray to brown, rough and fissured on mature trees.

Leaves are bipinnately compound, 15–40 cm long, with 4–6 pairs of pinnae. Each pinna bears 15–30 pairs of small leaflets (4–8 mm), creating a fine, feathery canopy.

Flowers are arranged in dense, spherical white to pale green heads, 2–3 cm in diameter. They are borne on short peduncles near the tips of branches.

The fruit is the most distinctive feature — a thick, woody, spirally coiled pod resembling an ear. Pods are 7–15 cm in diameter, dark brown at maturity, containing 8–20 seeds embedded in a sweet, fleshy pulp.

Seeds are brown, oval, approximately 1.5 cm long and 0.8 cm wide. They are hard and require scarification for germination, a common adaptation in leguminous trees.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Enterolobium cyclocarpum* ranges from southern Mexico through Central America to northern South America (Colombia, Venezuela). It has been widely introduced throughout the tropics.

In its native range, it occurs from sea level to about 900 m elevation. It is most abundant in seasonally dry tropical forests but also thrives in moist tropical and subtropical zones.

The species is highly tolerant of a range of soil types, including sandy, clay, and limestone-derived soils. It performs best on well-drained, fertile sites with annual rainfall of 750–2,500 mm.

It is a pioneer species, commonly found in disturbed areas, pastures, and secondary forests. Natural regeneration is abundant where seed-producing trees are present.

It is intolerant of frost and sustained temperatures below 5°C. Optimal growth occurs at mean annual temperatures of 24–30°C.

## Ecology and Applications

The tree forms **symbiotic relationships with rhizobia** (nitrogen-fixing bacteria) in root nodules, enriching soil fertility. This makes it valuable in agroforestry systems and silvopastoral arrangements.

The sweet, fleshy pod pulp is eagerly consumed by livestock (cattle, horses, goats) and wildlife including deer, peccaries, and numerous bird species. Seeds pass through the digestive tract unharmed, aiding dispersal.

In silvopastoral systems, *E. cyclocarpum* provides shade for cattle, reducing heat stress and improving forage quality beneath the canopy. The species is one of the most widely planted shade trees in Central American pastures.

As a nitrogen-fixing legume, it contributes significantly to soil nitrogen pools. Leaf litter decomposes rapidly, recycling nutrients and improving soil [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]].

The broad canopy creates a favorable microclimate for understory crops and forage grasses. In some systems, it is intercropped with coffee, cacao, or fruit trees.

## Cultivation and Uses

**Propagation:** Seeds require scarification (hot water treatment or mechanical nicking) to break physical dormancy. Germination occurs within 5–10 days after treatment. Seedlings grow rapidly, reaching 1–2 m in the first year.

**Growth rate:** Exceptionally fast, with annual height increments of 1.5–3 m during the first 5–10 years. Trees can reach 20 m within 15–20 years under favorable conditions.

**Wood properties:** Heartwood is light brown to reddish, moderately durable, and moderately heavy (specific gravity 0.45–0.55). It works easily and takes a good finish, used for general construction, furniture, and interior paneling.

The wood is also used for boat building, crates, and plywood. Sapwood is susceptible to insect attack and decay.

**Traditional uses:** The pod pulp is edible and sweet, consumed fresh by humans in some rural communities. Pods are also used as livestock feed, particularly during the dry season when other forage is scarce.

Bark extracts have been used in traditional medicine as an astringent and for treating [[cannabis-for-skin-conditions-detailed]]. Infusions of the bark have also been used as a febrifuge in folk medicine.

The tree has [[ethnomycology-cultural-significance-sacred-fungi]] in several Central American indigenous communities. It appears in pre-Columbian art and is associated with protection and shelter.

**Ornamental value:** Widely planted as a shade and ornamental tree in parks, avenues, and urban landscapes throughout the neotropics. Its broad, symmetrical crown makes it an excellent specimen tree.

In urban settings, the species is valued for rapid establishment and low maintenance requirements once established.

## Conservation Status

*Enterolobium cyclocarpum* is not currently considered threatened. It is listed as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List due to its wide distribution and abundance.

However, in some parts of its native range, mature trees have been heavily harvested for timber. Selective logging of the largest individuals has reduced average tree size in some populations.

Populations in agricultural landscapes are generally stable due to deliberate planting for shade. The species is widely cultivated throughout its range and beyond.

Genetic erosion of wild populations is a concern in some areas where continuous harvesting of the best-formed trees may reduce genetic diversity.
