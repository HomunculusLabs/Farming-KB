## Description

**Amanita** is a genus of basidiomycete fungi that contains some of the most deadly poisonous mushrooms known to humanity. The genus is recognized as the primary cause of nearly all fatal mushroom poisoning cases worldwide. In the text, [[julius-auboineau-palmer]] identifies ten varieties of Amanita found in the vicinity of Boston alone.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Amanitaceae
- **Genus:** *Amanita*

## Key Facts

- All or nearly all fatal cases of mushroom poisoning are attributed to the Amanita family
- The sub-genus is identified by three distinguishing marks that must exist together:
  1. A white wrapper (volva) around the base of the stem, close to the soil
  2. A membrane in the form of a veil or ragged ring (annulus) encircling the stem, about two-thirds up from the ground
  3. A scurf or irregular collection of warts on the top of the cap — quite defined in young plants, apt to be worn off in older specimens
- Gills and stem are always pure white
- The cap may be white, drab, golden, red, or greenish, and is soft and sticky when moist
- The most common variety has a yellow spot in the centre, shading gradually to pure white
- Contains a subtle alkaloid not detectable by smell or taste, yet " surer than arsenic in its fatal consequences"

## Poisoning and Treatment

- Physicians are described as "absolutely powerless to save life" in cases of Amanita poisoning
- The only treatment mentioned is the hypodermic injection of [[atropia]] (atropine) or some other alkaloid from the nightshade family
- This antidotal measure was described as "yet in its infancy" at the time of writing
- No antidote to fungus poison had been discovered according to Palmer's account

## Notable Species Mentioned

- **[[agaricus-bulbosus]]** — referenced by Dr. F. S. Cordier as a variety of the Amanita family used to test the silver spoon myth; it never blackened silver or gold
- Multiple varieties grow side by side with edible fungi, with their mycelial beds interlacing beneath the soil

## Ecological Observations

- Amanitas are found growing near edible Russula species in woodland settings
- They share similar appearance characteristics with Russulas: both have pure white gills and stems, and members may be found in corresponding shades from scarlet to pure white
- Despite visual similarity, Amanitas are nearly tasteless yet deadly, while non-acrid Russulas are safely eaten

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The [[amanita-genus]] represents the most critical identification challenge in foraging mycology. Palmer emphasizes that no general rule or test exists to distinguish Amanitas from edible species — only precise study of individual characteristics can ensure safety. The genus serves as a prime example of why species-level identification is essential in mycological practice, as visual similarities between deadly Amanitas and edible species like Russulas can be dangerously misleading.
