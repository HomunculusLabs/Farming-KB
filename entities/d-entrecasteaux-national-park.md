## D'Entrecasteaux National Park

## Description

D'Entrecasteaux National Park is a protected area on the wild south coast of Western Australia, notable for its spectacular landscape of contrasting vegetation types and its demonstration of dramatic ecological edge effects. The park features a mosaic of wildflower-filled heathlands with stunted heathy eucalypt woodland and reed-rush swamps, punctuated by islands of towering karri forest ([[eucalyptus-diversicolor]]) rising from the sandy plains on outcrops of red granitic loam.

## Classification

- **Type:** Place — National Park
- **Location:** South coast, Western Australia, Australia
- **Management:** Department of Biodiversity, Conservation and Attractions (WA)
- **Key feature:** Spectacular vegetation contrasts and edge effects

## Key Facts

- **Dramatic vegetation transitions:** The park contains some of the most striking ecological edges in Australia. Heathy woodland 6 metres tall transitions to dense karri forest 40 metres tall in a distance as little as the height of one mature karri tree — an extraordinarily abrupt change driven by soil type shifts.

- **Geological foundation:** The sandy landscapes were laid down over the last ten thousand years by relentless onshore south-westerly winds that blow the tops off massive coastal dune fields located 10 kilometres to the southwest.

- **Karri islands:** Rising out of the plain of sand, peat, and tea-coloured water are islands of red granitic loam that support towering forests of karri with a soft-leafed and ferny understorey. These islands represent dramatically different growing conditions within a uniform landscape matrix.

- **Chesapeake Road:** A 25-kilometre stretch of road running through the park passes straight through more than a dozen spectacular karri islands, creating what Holmgren describes as an awe-inspiring driving experience.

- **Rivers:** The park spans the area between the Gardiner and Shannon Rivers on Western Australia's south coast.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

D'Entrecasteaux National Park illustrates several key [[permaculture]] concepts:

1. **Edge at geological scale (Principle 11):** The park demonstrates that edges between soil types — in this case, sand/peat and granitic loam — can produce the most dramatic vegetation contrasts. The transition from 6-metre heath to 40-metre karri forest in the span of a single tree height is nature's expression of how productive edges can be.

2. **Soil as the primary edge:** The contrast between the karri islands and the surrounding heath is entirely driven by soil type, reinforcing the permaculture principle that the living soil is the most important edge of all for terrestrial life. Deep, well-drained, aerated granitic loam supports towering productive forest; shallow, sandy, peaty soil supports only stunted heath.

3. **Pattern for design:** The island pattern — concentrated zones of high productivity within a lower-productivity matrix — is a pattern that can be applied at property scale: identifying and enhancing pockets of superior soil, moisture, or microclimate within a broader landscape.

4. **Revegetation insight:** The dramatic vegetation contrast shows how sensitive plant growth is to soil conditions, and how even small areas of favourable soil can support disproportionately productive vegetation — encouraging designers to identify and maximise these productive islands within their sites.

5. **Protection of edge diversity:** The park's protection status ensures these dramatic edge effects are preserved, serving as a living reference for designers seeking to understand and replicate natural edge patterns.
