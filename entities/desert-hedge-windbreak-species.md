## Description

Desert hedges and windbreaks are multi-functional living barriers in dryland [[cupressus-sempervirens]]*): Tall, columnar, hardy evergreen windbreak.
- **Casuarina** spp.: Nitrogen-fixing, drought-hardy, tall windbreak tree. Soft foliage makes excellent compost mulch.
- **Acacia** spp.: Nitrogen-fixing, thorny species for livestock exclusion and windbreak.
- **Olives** (*[[jerusalem-artichoke]]** (*[[helianthus-tuberosus]]*): Perennial crop species windbreak.
- **Crotalaria** spp.: Mulch species for internal garden hedges, nitrogen-fixing.
- **Mikania** spp.: Vigorous vine for fedge (fence-hedge) systems; provides soft mulch and bee fodder.
- **Dolichos** beans (*Lablab purpureus*): Vine for fedge systems, productive legume.

### Fedge (Fence-Hedge) Design

- Strong rail and wire fence (posts termite-resistant or creosoted) carrying vigorous vines.
- Mikania provides soft mulch and bee fodder.
- Dolichos beans, lima beans, and passionfruit occupy parts of fedges.
- At 1.5-2 m high, fedges protect 9-10 m widths of garden.

### Perimeter Design Principles

- A vertical (to 1 m deep) plastic root barrier between garden and windbreak minimizes water competition.
- Choose highly drought-adapted windbreak species.
- Regularly check root invasion while trimming hedges for mulch.
- Investigation of local flora often yields many suitable species.
- Broad windbreaks of 300-400 m deep tree parks around settlements eliminate devastating desert winds.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Hedge clippings and leaf fall provide continuous organic matter for fungal-dominated compost systems.
- Nitrogen-fixing hedge species (*Acacia*, *Leucaena*, *Prosopis*, *Albizia*, *Crotalaria*) support mycorrhizal associations that benefit neighboring crops.
- Dense hedge margins create humid microclimates favorable for fungal fruiting in integrated food forests.
- Wood chip from hedge trimmings serves as primary substrate for mushroom cultivation.
- The layered windbreak system (outer tall trees, middle hedges, inner crop) creates diverse fungal niches at each level.
- Euphorbia and cactus barriers, while not fungal hosts themselves, protect fungal cultivation areas from livestock disturbance.

## North African Polyculture Reference

Williams (1974) describes a traditional layered system:
1. **Palm trees** — high canopy, first defense against sun, provide dates
2. **Olive trees** — grow under palms
3. **Fig trees** — grow under olives
4. **Pomegranate and vine** — grow under figs
5. **Grain and vegetables** — ground level

This multi-layered approach maximizes production per unit area while each layer provides shade and wind protection for layers below.
