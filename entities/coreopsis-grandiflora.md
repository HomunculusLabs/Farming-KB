## Overview

**Coreopsis grandiflora** (large-flowered tickseed, Atlantic coreopsis) is a clump-forming perennial herb in the family Asteraceae.

It is native to central and eastern North America and is among the most widely cultivated Coreopsis species in horticulture.

The species produces abundant bright golden-yellow flower heads with notched ray florets throughout the summer months.

Its long bloom period, drought tolerance, and ease of cultivation have made it a standard component of perennial borders and meadow plantings.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Coreopsis grandiflora** (Hogg ex Hook.) S.F. Blake was originally described by William Jackson Hooker and later revised by Sidney Fay Blake.

The genus name *Coreopsis* derives from Greek *koris* (bug) and *opsis* (appearance), referring to the tick-like appearance of the achenes.

Plants form compact clumps 30–60 cm tall and 30–45 cm wide with upright, branching stems.

Leaves are primarily basal, lanceolate to oblanceolate, 8–15 cm long, with entire to slightly toothed margins.

Cauline leaves are smaller, opposite, and sessile, reduced toward the inflorescence.

Flower heads are solitary or in loose corymbs, 4–6 cm in diameter.

Ray florets number 6–10, are bright yellow, deeply notched at the tips, and 15–25 mm long.

The disk is small, dome-shaped, 6–10 mm wide, with yellow to dark brown disk florets.

Achenes are flat, dark brown, winged, and approximately 3–4 mm long.

## Distribution and Habitat

The native range extends from Ontario and Minnesota south to Missouri, Arkansas, and Georgia.

It is most common in prairies, open woodlands, glades, and roadsides of the central United States.

Populations are concentrated in the Ozark region, the tallgrass prairie, and the Ohio River Valley.

The species prefers well-drained soils in full sun but tolerates partial shade and poor, rocky substrates.

It is hardy in USDA zones 4–9 and shows good heat tolerance in southern regions.

Naturalized occurrences have been recorded in parts of Europe and northeastern North America beyond its historical range.

## Cultivation

**Coreopsis grandiflora** is propagated readily from seed, division, and softwood cuttings.

Seed germinates in 14–30 days at 18–22°C and does not require cold stratification.

Plants bloom reliably in the second year from seed, though some cultivars flower in the first year.

Division every 2–3 years maintains plant vigor and prevents center die-out in mature clumps.

The species performs best in full sun with average, well-drained soil of moderate fertility.

Deadheading spent flowers extends the bloom period by several weeks and reduces unwanted self-seeding.

Excessive fertility or overly rich soils promote floppy growth and reduce flowering intensity.

Notable cultivars include 'Early Sunrise' (double flowers), 'Solar Flash', and 'Baby Sun' (compact habit).

The species is relatively pest-free, though aphids and leafhoppers may occasionally infest new growth.

Its compact form and reliable bloom make it suitable for borders, containers, mass plantings, and cut-flower production.

## Ecological Role

The flowers provide nectar and pollen for a wide range of pollinators, including native bees, honey bees, butterflies, and syrphid flies.

Long-tongued bees such as bumble bees (*Bombus* spp.) are especially effective pollinators of Coreopsis species.

The abundant and accessible pollen makes it a valuable resource for specialist and generalist pollinators alike.

Seeds are consumed by songbirds including goldfinches, sparrows, and towhees during fall and winter.

The foliage supports larvae of the dimorphic gray moth (*Rheumaptera hastata*) and several leaf-mining flies.

As a clump-forming species, it provides moderate ground cover that benefits soil stabilization in planting beds.

Its extended bloom period fills an important mid-season nectar niche in pollinator gardens and meadow mixtures.

## Cultural Significance

The species was among the first North American wildflowers exported to European horticulture, cultivated in England by the early 18th century.

It has received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit for reliable garden performance.

The genus *Coreopsis* was designated the state wildflower of Florida in 1991, though *C. leavenworthii* is the primary representative species there.

In ecological landscaping, *C. grandiflora* is frequently specified in native meadow seed mixes and roadside revegetation projects.

Its golden blooms are emblematic of the tallgrass prairie aesthetic in restoration plantings throughout the Midwest.

The species has contributed parentage to numerous hybrid Coreopsis cultivars developed for the ornamental trade.
