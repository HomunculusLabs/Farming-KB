## Diatomaceous Earth

## Description

Diatomaceous earth (DE), also known as diatomite or kieselgur, is a naturally occurring soft, lightweight sedimentary rock composed of the fossilised siliceous remains (frustules) of diatoms — microscopic single-celled algae (Bacillariophyceae) that lived in ancient freshwater and marine environments. When crushed to a fine powder, the microscopic silica shells retain their intricate geometric structures with extremely sharp edges at the microscopic level. These sharp edges abrade the waxy epicuticle of insects and other arthropods, causing lethal dehydration. In [[silicon]] dioxide (SiO2, 80-90%) with minor amounts of aluminium oxide, iron oxide, and other minerals
- **Origin**: Sedimentary deposits from ancient freshwater or marine lake beds, formed over millions of years
- **Colour**: White to off-white, sometimes pinkish or grey depending on mineral impurities
- **Primary uses**: Mechanical insecticide, ectoparasite control, grain storage protectant, soil amendment, industrial filter medium

## Mechanism of Action

Diatomaceous earth kills insects and arachnids through a purely physical (mechanical) process. The microscopic sharp silica fragments (typically 10-200 micrometres) abrade the thin waxy outer layer (epicuticle) of the insect exoskeleton and simultaneously absorb the lipid compounds that make up this waterproof layer. Without the waxy seal, insects rapidly lose body moisture through transpiration and die of desiccation within 24-48 hours. This mechanical mode of action means that insects and mites cannot develop physiological resistance to DE, unlike chemical insecticides that select for resistant populations. DE must remain dry to function effectively, as moisture causes the particles to clump and lose their abrasive properties.
