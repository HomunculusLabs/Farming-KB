## Overview

**Teloschistes chrysophthalmus** (L.) Th. Fr., commonly known as the **golden-eye lichen** or **gold-eye lichen**, is a striking fruticose lichen in the family Teloschistaceae.

It is recognized by its bright orange, pendulous thallus with distinctive orange apothecia featuring prominent cilia-fringed margins.

The species is widely distributed in temperate and subtropical regions, growing on both trees and rock surfaces.

It is particularly notable as a bioindicator of nitrogen enrichment and has experienced significant range expansions in some areas due to atmospheric [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-caribbean-central-america]], and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]].

European populations exist around the Mediterranean basin and along the Atlantic coasts of western Europe.

The species occurs in Africa, Asia, and Australia, with particularly strong populations in oceanic climates.

It grows on a variety of substrates including the bark of deciduous and coniferous trees, fence posts, and occasionally rock surfaces.

Preferred host trees include oaks, willows, alders, and various fruit trees in orchards.

The lichen is most common in open, well-lit situations with moderate to high humidity.

It is frequently found in coastal fog zones and riparian corridors.

## Ecology

*T. chrysophthalmus* is a facultative epiphyte that shows a strong preference for nutrient-enriched bark.

It is recognized as a reliable bioindicator of atmospheric nitrogen deposition, thriving where nitrogen levels are elevated.

In parts of western Europe and the Pacific Northwest, the species has expanded its range inland over recent decades.

This range expansion is attributed to increased agricultural ammonia emissions and vehicle nitrogen oxide pollution.

The lichen is moderately sensitive to sulfur dioxide and acid rain, limiting its distribution near industrial areas.

It serves as a microhabitat for various invertebrates, including lichen-specialist mites and insects.

Birds occasionally incorporate fragments of the thallus into nest construction material.
