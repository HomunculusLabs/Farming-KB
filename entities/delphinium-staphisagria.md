Some taxonomists segregate the annual and biennial Consolida species from Delphinium, but molecular evidence supports retaining them within a broadly defined Delphinium. Delphinium staphisagria falls within the core Delphinium clade.

The species epithet "staphisagria" derives from the Greek "staphis" (wild raisin) and "agria" (wild), referring to the grape-cluster appearance of the seed pods. The common name "stavesacre" may derive from the Italian "stafisagria."

Chromosome number is 2n = 16. The species is closely related to [[psilocybin-accidental-ingestion-australia]].

## Chemistry and Toxicity

The seeds of Delphinium staphisagria are the primary toxic organ, containing 1–2% diterpenoid alkaloids by dry weight. The principal alkaloid delphinine is a potent neurotoxin that blocks sodium channels in nerve tissue.

All parts of the plant should be considered toxic. Gardeners handling Delphinium staphisagria should wear gloves and avoid contact with sap, which can cause skin irritation in sensitive individuals.

## Distribution and Habitat

Delphinium staphisagria is native to the Mediterranean basin, occurring from Spain and Morocco eastward to Greece, Turkey, and the Levant. It grows in dry, stony hillsides, field margins, and disturbed ground at low to moderate elevations.

The species is particularly abundant in southern France, the Iberian Peninsula, and the Aegean islands. It has not been recorded east of the Taurus Mountains in Turkey, marking the eastern limit of its natural range.

The species prefers calcareous, well-drained soils in full sun to partial shade. It is adapted to the hot, dry Mediterranean summer and typically completes its life cycle as a biennial, germinating in autumn and flowering the following spring.

In its native garrigue and maquis habitats, Delphinium staphisagria grows among low shrubs and annual herbs on limestone slopes. It tolerates considerable summer drought once the taproot is established.

Populations show considerable morphological variation across the Mediterranean range. North African plants tend to be smaller with deeper flower color than their Iberian counterparts.

Plants are hardy to approximately USDA Zone 7–8. In colder climates, they may be grown as annuals from autumn-sown seed, though flowering may be reduced compared to Mediterranean conditions.

The species has naturalized sporadically in southern England and other parts of northwest Europe following historical cultivation for medicinal seed production. These naturalized populations are typically short-lived without ongoing disturbance.

## Historical Use

Stavesacre seeds have been documented in medical texts for over 2,500 years. Dioscorides described their use as a pediculicide and parasiticide in De [[herbal-materia-medica]] (c. 60 CE), recommending topical application in oil or vinegar.

The Greek physician Theophrastus mentioned the plant in his botanical writings, noting its cultivation for seed production. Roman agricultural writers including Columella and Pliny the Elder recorded its use for treating livestock parasites.

Stavesacre seeds were traded throughout the medieval Mediterranean as a valuable commodity. Monasteries maintained dedicated garden beds for cultivation, and seeds were exported as far as England and Scandinavia.

In medieval and Renaissance European medicine, stavesacre was a standard entry in the pharmacopoeia. It was listed in both the London and Edinburgh Pharmacopoeias as an external treatment for lice and scabies through the 18th century.
