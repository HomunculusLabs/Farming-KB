# Strychnine

## Overview

Strychnine is a highly toxic [[aminopropyl-silica-gel-chromatography-indole-alkaloid-purification]] found primarily in the seeds of *Strychnos nux-vomica* (strychnine tree) and related *Strychnos* species. It is one of the most potent convulsant poisons known, with a lethal dose in humans estimated at 50–100 mg. Strychnine acts as a competitive antagonist at glycine receptors (GlyR) in the [[blesching-cannabis-spinal-cord-injuries]] and brainstem, blocking inhibitory glycine-mediated neurotransmission and producing characteristic hyperreflexia, severe muscle spasms, and opisthotonus. Despite its extreme toxicity, strychnine has a long history of medical use as a stimulant, respiratory analeptic, and gastrointestinal tonic dating back to 16th-century European medicine. It remains an important compound in neuroscience research, forensic toxicology, and as a reference standard for glycine [[cannabinoid]].

## Chemical Profile

- **IUPAC name**: (2S,3aS,8aS)-2,3,3a,8,8a-Hexahydro-1H-2,8a-imino-1,3-diaza-fluorene
- **Molecular formula**: C₂₁H₂₂N₂O₂
- **Molecular weight**: 334.41 g/mol
- **CAS number**: 57-24-9
- **Melting point**: 268–290 °C (decomposes)
- **Boiling point**: Sublimes at approximately 270 °C
- **Density**: 1.36 g/cm³
- **Solubility**: Sparingly soluble in water (1:6400 at 25 °C); soluble in chloroform, slightly soluble in ethanol and ether; soluble in dilute acids forming salts
- **Optical rotation**: [α]D²⁰ = −139° (in chloroform)
- **pKa**: 8.26 (protonated tertiary amine)
- **Appearance**: Colorless to white crystalline powder; intensely bitter taste detectable at 1 ppm
- **UV absorption**: λmax 254 nm, 300 nm (in methanol)
- **Structure**: Complex pentacyclic indole alkaloid comprising seven rings, classified as a member of the Strychnos alkaloid family

## Natural Sources

Strychnine is isolated from several *Strychnos* species (family Loganiaceae), which are distributed across tropical and subtropical regions of Asia, Africa, and Australia. The biosynthetic pathway involves the condensation of tryptamine with secologanin to form strictosidine, followed by multiple enzymatic transformations.

- **Strychnos nux-vomica** (strychnine tree): Native to India, [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]], and northern Australia; seeds contain 1.0–1.5% strychnine and 0.5–1.0% [[brucine]]. This is the primary commercial source of strychnine alkaloids.
- **Strychnos ignatii** (Ignatius bean): Native to the Philippines and China; seeds contain 1.0–2.5% strychnine and smaller amounts of brucine. Historically used in traditional Chinese medicine as "Maqianzi."
- **Strychnos angustiflora**: Found in southern China and Southeast Asia; a traditional medicinal source of strychnine alkaloids.
- **Strychnos potatorum** (clearing-nut tree): Found in India and Sri Lanka; contains trace amounts of strychnine alongside other Strychnos alkaloids.
- **Strychnos icaja**: Central African species; bark and roots contain strychnine and related alkaloids used in traditional arrow poisons.

## Pharmacology

### Mechanism of Action

Strychnine is a potent, selective, and competitive antagonist of the strychnine-sensitive glycine receptor (GlyR), a ligand-gated chloride channel predominantly expressed in the spinal cord, brainstem, and retina. By binding to the glycine-binding site on the α subunit of GlyR, strychnine blocks the influx of chloride ions that normally produces hyperpolarization and neuronal inhibition. The resulting disinhibition leads to uncontrolled neuronal excitation, exaggerated reflex responses, and generalized convulsions. Strychnine has an exceptionally high affinity for GlyR (Ki ≈ 1–10 nM), making it one of the most specific GlyR antagonists available and an indispensable pharmacological tool for studying glycinergic neurotransmission.

### Pharmacokinetics

Strychnine is rapidly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract following ingestion, with peak plasma concentrations achieved within 10–30 minutes. It undergoes hepatic metabolism primarily via CYP3A4-mediated oxidative pathways, producing inactive metabolites that are excreted in urine. The elimination half-life ranges from 10–16 hours. Strychnine crosses the blood-brain barrier efficiently due to its lipophilic character, which contributes to its rapid central nervous system effects. Limited enterohepatic recirculation has been observed, and the alkaloid can be detected in urine for up to 48–72 hours post-exposure.

### Effects on Body Systems

- **Central nervous system**: Produces intense hyperreflexia, trismus, opisthotonus, and generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Consciousness is typically preserved until terminal hypoxic events. [[plant-sensory-perception]] remains intact.
- **Muscular**: Severe muscle rigidity and spasms triggered by minor sensory stimuli (light, sound, touch). Risus sardonicus (fixed grimacing) is a characteristic sign. Muscular contractions can be violent enough to cause bone fractures and rhabdomyolysis.
- **Cardiovascular**: Initial hypertension and tachycardia due to sympathetic activation, progressing to arrhythmias and cardiovascular collapse secondary to hypoxia from prolonged convulsions.
- **Respiratory**: Respiratory muscle spasms lead to respiratory acidosis and hypoxia, which are the primary causes of death in strychnine poisoning.

## Toxicology and Clinical Features

Strychnine poisoning produces one of the most dramatic clinical presentations in toxicology. Symptoms typically appear within 15–60 minutes of ingestion and progress through predictable stages:

1. **Prodromal phase** (minutes): Restlessness, agitation, hyperesthesia, muscle twitching, and nuchal rigidity
2. **Convulsive phase**: Generalized tonic-clonic seizures triggered by sensory stimuli; opisthotonus with the body arched in a characteristic "bridge" posture; risus sardonicus
3. **Exhaustion phase**: Seizures become more frequent and prolonged, culminating in respiratory failure and death from hypoxia

The lethal dose in adults is approximately 50–100 mg (0.7–1.4 mg/kg); in children, 15–30 mg can be fatal. Postmortem findings are typically nonspecific but may include pulmonary congestion, cerebral edema, and petechial hemorrhages. Antemortem diagnosis relies on clinical presentation, history of exposure, and detection via GC-MS, HPLC, or LC-MS/MS methods.

### Treatment

Management of strychnine poisoning is primarily supportive and focuses on controlling seizures and preventing secondary complications. Activated charcoal is administered if presentation is within 1 hour of ingestion. Intravenous diazepam (10–20 mg bolus) or barbiturates are the mainstays of seizure control; neuromuscular blockade with non-depolarizing agents (e.g., vecuronium) may be required for refractory convulsions. Intubation and mechanical ventilation are often necessary. Specific antidotes do not exist for strychnine poisoning. Recovery is complete if the patient survives the acute phase, as strychnine does not cause permanent neurological damage — it merely blocks glycine receptors without destroying neurons.
