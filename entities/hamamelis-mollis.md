## Overview

Hamamelis mollis (Chinese witch hazel) is a deciduous shrub or small tree in the family Hamamelidaceae, native to central and eastern China. It is the most fragrant of all witch hazel species, producing bright yellow to orange spider-like flowers in mid-winter.

The species was introduced to Western cultivation in 1879 by Charles Maries, who collected seed in Jiangxi province. It has since become one of the most valued winter-flowering shrubs in temperate gardens worldwide.

Maries initially sent seed to the Veitch Nurseries in England, where the first plants flowered in cultivation around 1884. The dramatic winter bloom quickly attracted attention from gardeners and botanists across Europe.

Mature specimens reach 3–5 m in height and spread with a broad, vase-shaped habit. The species epithet "mollis" refers to the soft, velvety pubescence covering the leaves and young shoots.

Chinese witch hazel is distinguished from its relatives by its superior fragrance, earlier flowering time, and the soft indumentum on its foliage. These traits make it the most horticulturally desirable species in the genus.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Hamamelis mollis belongs to family Hamamelidaceae, tribe Hamamelideae. The genus Hamamelis comprises 4–5 species distributed in [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] and East Asia.

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Family:** Hamamelidaceae
- **Genus:** Hamamelis
- **Species:** H. mollis Oliv.

Leaves are broadly obovate, 8–15 cm long, with an oblique base and wavy, serrate margins. They are dark green above and densely gray-tomentose beneath, giving the foliage a distinctive soft texture.

Flowers appear in January–March on bare branches, consisting of four narrow, crinkled petals 2–3 cm long. Petals range from pale yellow to deep golden-orange, depending on the individual and provenance.

The intense, sweet fragrance is the species' most distinctive characteristic, detectable from several meters away on warm winter days. This fragrance distinguishes it from the nearly scentless [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] Hamamelis species.

Fruit is a woody, two-valved capsule approximately 1.5 cm long. The capsules ripen over an extended period and explosively eject seeds up to 10 m when they finally split open in autumn.

Chromosome number is 2n = 24, consistent across the genus Hamamelis. This uniformity suggests a relatively recent diversification within the genus.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to central and eastern China, including Anhui, Fujian, Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi, Sichuan, and Zhejiang provinces. Populations occur at 200–1,500 m elevation on moist, wooded slopes and along stream banks.

The species grows as an understory shrub in broadleaved deciduous forests, often in association with Lindera, Sassafras, and various Acer species. It favors partially shaded positions with consistent soil moisture.

The species demonstrates moderate shade tolerance but flowers most prolifically when it receives at least 4–6 hours of direct sunlight daily. In deep shade, flowering is sparse and growth becomes leggy.

Climate in native range features hot, humid summers and relatively mild winters. Annual rainfall ranges from 1,000–1,600 mm, distributed throughout the year with a summer maximum.

Chinese populations show significant morphological variation, particularly in flower color and leaf pubescence density. This variation has been exploited by plant breeders to develop numerous cultivars.

The species' disjunct distribution pattern — with close relatives in eastern North America — represents a classic example of the Arcto-Tertiary geoflora concept. Molecular dating suggests the Asian-North American split occurred approximately 5–8 million years ago.

## Cultivation

Hardy in USDA zones 5–8. Performs best in acidic to neutral, organically rich, well-drained soils with consistent moisture.

Tolerates full sun to partial shade, but flower display and fragrance are strongest in sunnier positions. Autumn color is also more reliable in full sun exposures.

Notable cultivars include 'Pallida' (pale sulphur-yellow flowers, strong fragrance), 'Brevipetala' (short-petaled, deep gold), and 'Jermyns Gold' (large flowers, rich yellow). The cultivar 'Fred Chittenden' is noted for particularly intense fragrance.

The cultivar 'Wisley Supreme' produces some of the largest flowers in the genus, with petals reaching 3 cm. 'Westerstede' is valued for its [[fukuoka-ideal-rice-plant-morphology-compact-growth-high-yield]] habit and reliable heavy flowering.

Propagation is by seed (requiring 3 months warm + 3 months cold stratification), softwood cuttings in summer, or grafting. Many named cultivars are grafted onto [[hamamelis-virginiana]] rootstock.

Pruning should be minimal and limited to removing dead or crossing branches. The plant's natural vase shape is its most ornamental growth habit and should be preserved.

Growth rate is slow to moderate at 15–25 cm per year. The species is long-lived, with specimens in cultivation exceeding 80 years of age.

Autumn color ranges from yellow to rich orange-red, providing a secondary season of ornamental interest. Fall color is most vivid on plants grown in full sun with adequate moisture.

## Ecological Role and Uses

In native forests, Hamamelis mollis flowers during winter when few other nectar sources are available. This makes it ecologically valuable for early-emerging pollinators such as winter moths and hoverflies.

The species is the primary parent of most Hamamelis × intermedia cultivars, the most widely grown witch hazels in gardens. These hybrids combine the fragrance of H. mollis with the larger flowers and vigor of H. japonica.

Traditional Chinese medicine uses the bark and leaves for their astringent and anti-inflammatory properties. Extracts are applied topically [[cannabis-for-skin-conditions-detailed]], though less commonly than those of the North American Hamamelis virginiana.

The leaves contain significant quantities of tannins, including hamamelitannin, which has demonstrated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity in laboratory studies. These compounds contribute to the plant's astringent [[panax-ginseng]].
