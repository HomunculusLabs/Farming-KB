## Description

Immobilization and mineralization are the two complementary processes by which soil microorganisms regulate nutrient availability to plants. In immobilization, soil organisms absorb and retain nutrients (particularly nitrogen) within their bodies, making those nutrients temporarily unavailable to plants. In mineralization, nutrients locked in microbial biomass are released in plant-available forms when organisms are consumed, die, or excrete waste. Together, these processes form the core nutrient cycling mechanism of [[the-soil-food-web]].

## Classification

- **Domain**: Soil biology / Nutrient cycling
- **Category**: Biogeochemical processes
- **Primary nutrients involved**: Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Sulfur (S), and other essential elements
- **Key organisms**: Bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes

## Immobilization

### Definition
The process by which soil microorganisms assimilate inorganic nutrients into their body tissues, converting plant-available forms into organic forms that are temporarily unavailable to plants.

### Mechanism
- Bacteria and fungi consume organic matter and root exudates, incorporating nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients into their cellular biomass.
- These organisms act as "small bags of fertilizer" — nutrients are stored safely within their bodies rather than leaching away with groundwater.
- Immobilization is a protective function: without it, most important nutrients would drain from soil into the water table.

### Key Facts
- When carbon-rich organic matter is added to soil (e.g., wood chips, straw), microbes immobilize available nitrogen to fuel decomposition, potentially creating temporary nitrogen deficiency for plants.
- The C:N ratio of organic inputs determines whether net immobilization or net mineralization occurs: >30:1 favors immobilization, <20:1 favors mineralization.
- Fungal biomass has a higher C:N ratio than bacterial biomass, meaning fungally dominated soils hold more total immobilized carbon.

## Mineralization

### Definition
The process by which organic nutrients are converted back into inorganic, plant-available forms through microbial metabolism, predation, and decomposition.

### Mechanism
- Protozoa and nematodes eat bacteria and fungi primarily for carbon.
- The excess nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, etc.) are excreted as waste in inorganic, plant-available forms.
- This waste release occurs right in the rhizosphere where plant roots can immediately absorb the nutrients.
- When any soil organism dies, its body is decomposed by bacteria and fungi, releasing the immobilized nutrients.

### Nitrogen Mineralization Pathway
1. Organic nitrogen is immobilized in bacteria and fungi (protein, amino acids).
2. Protozoa and nematodes consume microbes for carbon.
3. Excess nitrogen is excreted as **ammonium (NH₄⁺)**.
4. In bacterially dominated, alkaline soils: ammonium is converted to **nitrate (NO₃⁻)** by nitrifying bacteria.
5. In fungally dominated, acidic soils: nitrogen remains as **ammonium (NH₄⁺)**.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- These processes explain why organic gardening works: the soil food web immobilizes nutrients safely and mineralizes them on-demand in the rhizosphere, providing a steady, balanced nutrient supply.
- Chemical fertilizers bypass immobilization entirely — most applied nitrogen drains past the root zone and into the water table, causing pollution and wasting money.
- Understanding the immobilization-mineralization balance is critical for compost management: properly finished compost has already undergone mineralization and provides immediately available nutrients.
- In mushroom cultivation, the substrate colonization phase is essentially massive immobilization — the mycelium absorbs and stores nutrients. When fruiting bodies form, mineralization of the substrate's nutrients supports mushroom development.
- Cover cropping and green manures work by growing plants that immobilize nutrients in their biomass, which is then mineralized when the cover crop is terminated and incorporated into the soil.
- The balance between immobilization and mineralization determines soil fertility: too much immobilization locks up nutrients; too much mineralization can lead to leaching losses.

## Key Distinction from Chemical Approach

| Feature | Soil Food Web (Biological) | Chemical Fertilizer |
|---|---|---|
| Nutrient storage | Immobilized in microbial biomass | Soluble in soil solution |
| Leaching risk | Very low (held in organisms) | Very high (drains past roots) |
| Nutrient form | Matched to plant preference | Usually nitrate only |
| Sustainability | Self-renewing | Requires repeated application |
| Soil structure impact | Builds aggregates and porosity | Salt damage degrades structure |

## Practical Applications
These soil principles directly inform management decisions in agriculture
and horticulture. Understanding soil structure dynamics helps growers
optimize tillage practices, amendment applications, and planting schedules.
Biological soil management focuses on feeding soil organisms rather than
directly supplying nutrients to plants, creating self-sustaining fertility.
Regular soil food web assessments guide adaptive management strategies.
