## Overview

**Cephalotus follicularis**, the Albany pitcher plant, is a monotypic species
of carnivorous plant in the family Cephalotaceae. It is endemic to a narrow
coastal strip in southwestern Western Australia, centered on Albany.

The species produces two distinct leaf types: flat non-carnivorous foliage
leaves and highly modified pitcher leaves. The pitchers are small, 3–5 cm tall,
with prominent translucent ribs and a fluted peristome. Their compact size and
striking appearance make the species popular among carnivorous plant collectors.

*Cephalotus* occupies an isolated taxonomic position. The family Cephalotaceae
contains only this single genus and species, representing one of the most
phylogenetically distinct carnivorous plant lineages.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Cephalotus follicularis** Labill. was described by Jacques Labillardière in
1806 from specimens collected near King George Sound, Western Australia. The
generic name derives from Greek *kephalotos* (headed), referring to the
pitcher shape, and the specific epithet means "bearing follicles."

Cephalotaceae is placed in the order Oxalidales based on molecular phylogenetic
data. Its closest relatives within the order include the families Brunelliaceae
and Cunoniaceae. This placement is unexpected and suggests carnivory evolved
independently in this lineage.

The plant forms a compact rosette 5–15 cm in diameter. Two leaf types are
produced: non-carnivorous leaves are flat, oval, and 2–4 cm long, while
pitcher leaves are 3–5 cm tall with a swollen body and narrow lid.

The pitcher interior is heavily glandular. Digestive glands occur in two zones:
a superior zone of nectar-secreting glands near the rim, and an inferior zone
of digestive enzyme-secreting glands in the lower pitcher. The rim (peristome)
is ribbed and produces copious nectar to attract prey.

Flowers are small, white, and inconspicuous, borne on scapes 10–20 cm tall.
