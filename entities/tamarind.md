# Tamarind

## Description


Tamarind (*Tamarindus indica*) is a large, long-lived tropical tree in the family Fabaceae, subfamily Detarioideae. It is the only species in its genus, making it monotypic. Native to tropical Africa, it has been cultivated for millennia across South and Southeast Asia, where it is now widely naturalized. The tree is prized for its edible fruit pulp, timber, shade, and ornamental value. It is a dominant component of dry tropical forests and savannahs.

## Classification


- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Fabaceae (Leguminosae)
- **Subfamily**: Detarioideae
- **Genus**: Tamarindus
- **Species**: *T. indica* (monotypic genus)

## Key Facts


- Large evergreen to semi-deciduous tree reaching 20–30 m in height with a spreading crown up to 15 m across.
- Produces curved, brown pods 5–15 cm long containing an acidic, sweet-sour pulp that is a staple flavoring in cuisines worldwide.
- Fruit pulp contains tartaric acid (8–18%), sugars, vitamin C, B vitamins, and minerals including potassium, magnesium, and calcium.
- Extremely drought-tolerant once established; survives in areas with as little as 500 mm annual rainfall.
- Trees can live and produce fruit for 150–200 years, with some specimens reported over 300 years old.
- The wood is hard, dense, and termite-resistant, valued for furniture, tool handles, and fuel.
- Leaves, flowers, and seeds are all utilized: leaves as fodder and in traditional medicine, flowers for bees, seeds as a source of polysaccharide gum.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture


Mollison identifies tamarind as a late-successional tree species in tropical ecological sequences:

- **Forest succession endpoint**: Listed among the larger trees (along with Vitex, Sapium, and palms) that represent the final successional stage from grassland to forest in the humid tropics. Tamarind establishes after the shrub stage (castor oil bean, Prosopis, thorny legumes) has modified the microclimate.
- **Dry-deciduous forests**: Mentioned in the context of dry-deciduous tropical broadleaf forests, where teak and bamboo were once extensive. Tamarind is a component of these endangered forest types, particularly in the Indian subcontinent.
- **Multi-purpose tropical tree**: Combines food production, timber, shade, and soil stabilization in a single long-lived perennial, embodying the permaculture principle of each element performing multiple functions.
- **Traditional agroforestry**: Has been integrated into homegardens and parkland systems across the tropics for centuries, providing reliable yields with minimal management input.

## Ecological Considerations


- Provides habitat and food for wildlife including birds, bats, and primates that disperse seeds.
- Dense canopy creates favorable microclimate for understory crops in tropical polycultures.
- Can dominate in mixed plantings due to long lifespan and large canopy; requires adequate spacing.

## Cultivation Notes


- Propagated by seed, though vegetative propagation (grafting, air layering) preserves desirable cultivar traits.
- Begins fruiting at 6–8 years from seed; grafted trees may fruit in 3–4 years.
- Prefers well-drained soils but tolerates a wide pH range (4.5–9.0).
- Mature trees produce 150–250 kg of fruit per year under good conditions.
- Minimal pest and disease pressure in most tropical environments.
