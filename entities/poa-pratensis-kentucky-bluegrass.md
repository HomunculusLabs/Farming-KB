# Poa pratensis (Kentucky Bluegrass / Whitegrass Tussock)

## Description

[[poa-pratensis]], commonly known as Kentucky bluegrass or smooth meadow-grass, is a perennial rhizomatous grass species in the Poaceae family. In the context of cold-climate [[permaculture]] as described by Mollison, it is listed as one of the characteristic species of cold-climate hayfields, growing alongside Alopecurus and Phleum. It is valued as a pasture and hay species in temperate and cold climates worldwide, and its rhizomatous growth habit makes it an effective soil stabilizer.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Poaceae
- **Genus**: Poa
- **Species**: P. pratensis
- **Common names**: Kentucky bluegrass, smooth meadow-grass, whitegrass tussock
- **Growth form**: Perennial rhizomatous grass
- **Native range**: Europe, Asia, North Africa; widely naturalized worldwide

## Key Facts

- Perennial grass with creeping rhizomes that form dense turf and tussocks
- Referred to as whitegrass tussock in Mollison's cold-climate hayfield context
- Characteristic species of cold-climate hayfields alongside Alopecurus (foxtail) and Phleum (timothy)
- In cold sandy deserts and tundra, it transitions to hayfield dominance when bogs are drained or areas are fertilized
- The shift to grass dominance over shrub and tussock species increases with fertilization, but also increases worm parasite problems
- Widely used as both pasture and hay grass in temperate and cold regions globally
- Rhizomatous root system provides excellent soil stabilization and erosion control
- Tolerant of cold temperatures and frost, making it suitable for harsh climates
- Produces high-quality forage with good palatability for livestock
- Can persist through drought via rhizome reserves

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Poa pratensis has several connections to mycology and soil ecology:

1. **Arbuscular mycorrhizal host**: Like most grasses, P. pratensis forms arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) associations with Glomeromycotan fungi, contributing to grassland fungal diversity
2. **Fungal disease susceptibility**: Kentucky bluegrass can be affected by various fungal pathogens including rusts (Puccinia), dollar spot (Sclerotinia homoeocarpa), and snow molds (Microdochium, Typhula) — the latter being particularly relevant in cold climates
3. **[[root-exudates]]**: Dense rhizomatous root systems produce exudates that feed soil microbial and fungal communities
4. **Soil structure**: The extensive root system creates soil aggregation that benefits fungal hyphal networks
5. **Hayfield ecology**: As a hayfield species, P. pratensis supports fungal communities involved in thatch decomposition and nutrient cycling
6. **Snow mold relationships**: In cold-climate contexts, the grass's relationship with snow mold fungi becomes ecologically and economically significant
7. **Pasture mycology**: Diverse pasture swards including P. pratensis support greater fungal diversity than monocultures

## Cold-Climate Context

In the cold sandy desert and tundra environments described by Mollison:
- Part of the hayfield community (with Alopecurus and Phleum)
- Increases in dominance as areas are drained or fertilized
- Grass dominance over shrubs and tussocks correlates with increased livestock worm parasite issues
- Represents a transition from natural hummock grassland to managed hayfield
