## Overview

**Rhizocarpon geographicum** (L.) DC., known as the **map lichen** or **geographic lichen**, is a widespread crustose lichen in the family Rhizocarpaceae.

It forms distinctive bright yellow-green to yellow-gray crusts with numerous black [[bloomfield-asterophora-and-mycoparasites-of-fruiting-bodies]] (apothecia).

The species has become one of the most scientifically important lichens due to its central role in lichenometric dating of glacial deposits.

It is among the longest-lived lichens known, with individual thalli documented to exceed 4,500 years of age.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

*Rhizocarpon geographicum* was originally described by Carl Linnaeus as **Lichen geographicus** in 1753.

Augustin Pyramus de Candolle later transferred it to the genus *Rhizocarpon*, maintaining its specific epithet referencing its map-like appearance.

It belongs to the order Rhizocarpaceae, a relatively small order of crustose lichens.

The thallus is crustose and areolate, forming a patchwork of angular to rounded areoles typically 0.5–2 mm across.

The areole surface is pale yellow to sulfur-yellow, sometimes grayish when heavily pruinose.

Black apothecia are abundant, measuring 0.3–1.0 mm in diameter, and are immersed to sessile within the areoles.

Each apothecium has a prominent black margin and a slightly convex, dark disc.

Spores are ellipsoid to broadly fusiform, 1-septate, colorless, and measure 15–25 × 6–10 μm.

The photobiont is a trebouxia-like green alga characteristic of many crustose lichens.

Chemically, the species contains rhizocarpic acid and numerous related depsidones as its primary [[guzman-allen-gartz-psilocybe-semilanceata-temperate-cosmopolitan-distribution]], occurring on every continent including Antarctica.

It is found across arctic, alpine, and [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]] populations occur in montane New Zealand, Patagonia, and the subantarctic islands.

The species colonizes exposed siliceous rock surfaces including granite, gneiss, basalt, and sandstone.

It is especially abundant on glacially polished rock surfaces and boulder fields in arctic and alpine zones.

Elevational range spans from sea level in arctic regions to over 5,000 meters in tropical mountains.

It is absent from shaded, humid environments and rarely found on tree bark or calcareous substrates.

The lichen is a primary colonist of freshly exposed rock surfaces following glacial retreat or geological disturbance.

## Ecology

*R. geographicum* is a pioneer species in [[dighton-fungi-volcanic-glacier-primary-succession]] on bare rock, playing a key role in substrate stabilization.

Its thallus contributes to [[dighton-fungal-rock-weathering-mineral-dissolution]] through both physical expansion of cracks and chemical dissolution of minerals.

The species is extremely slow-growing, with radial growth rates typically measured at 0.1–1.0 mm per year.

Annual growth rates decrease with increasing thallus diameter, following a predictable pattern that enables age estimation.

The lichen tolerates extreme conditions including prolonged desiccation, intense UV radiation, and freeze-thaw cycles.

It hosts diverse [[cannabis-rhizosphere-microbial-communities]] including bacteria, yeasts, and other fungi within its thallus structure.

Small arthropods and tardigrades inhabit the microhabitats created by the areolate thallus structure.

*R. geographicum* accumulates atmospheric pollutants and heavy metals, making it a useful bioindicator species.

## Chemistry and Applications

The primary [[antifungal-secondary-metabolites-coprophilous-fungi]] are rhizocarpic acid and stictic acid, both depsidones with reported biological activities.
