## Dr. Isaac Ott

## Description

Dr. Isaac Ott was a 19th-century American physician and physiologist who served as Demonstrator of Physiology at the University of Pennsylvania. He is notable in the history of mycological toxicology for his pioneering experimental work demonstrating that atropine acts as a specific physiological antidote to muscarine poisoning — the toxic principle found in Amanita muscaria and related species. His experiments, published as a pamphlet essay, provided the crucial laboratory evidence supporting the clinical use of atropine in treating deadly mushroom poisoning.

- **Type:** Historical figure — physician and physiologist

## Key Facts

### The Atropine-Muscarine Antagonism Experiments

Dr. Ott's most significant contribution was a series of elegant experiments demonstrating that atropine could reverse the cardiac arrest caused by muscarine. His experimental protocol, as quoted in Palmer's text:

**Experiment 1 — Frog:**

- **3:55 PM:** Heart beat recorded at 36 per minute (sternum removed for observation)
- **3:58 PM:** 0.0005 grain muscarine administered subcutaneously
- **4:00 PM:** Heart stopped in diastole; ventricle distended with blood, bulbous; only responded to direct physical prick with a contraction
- **4:03 PM:** 0.001 grain atropine administered subcutaneously
- **4:05 PM:** Heart spontaneously resumed beating at 28 per minute
- **4:09 PM:** Heart rate recovered to 36 per minute
- **Result:** Heart continued beating through the following morning; animal completely recovered from muscarine-induced paralysis

**Experiment 2 — Toad:**

- Produced nearly identical results, confirming the reproducibility of the atropine antidote effect

### Significance

These experiments were groundbreaking because they demonstrated:

1. Muscarine causes cardiac arrest by stopping the heart in diastole (relaxed phase)
2. Atropine specifically reverses this effect, restoring normal cardiac rhythm
3. The antidotal relationship is rapid — recovery began within 2 minutes of atropine injection
4. Complete recovery was possible when treatment was prompt

### Published Conclusions

Dr. Ott's pamphlet included the following conclusions about mushroom poisons, as summarized by Palmer:

1. The Agaricus genus comprises nearly one thousand varieties, the majority of which are poisonous (a view Palmer partially contested)
2. The edible variety may become poisonous under certain unknown conditions (Palmer disagreed)
3. In some Agaricus species, Letellier found a peculiar substance called amanitine
4. Agaricus muscarius contains an alkaloid called muscarine
5. A. muscarius also contains a non-poisonous base called amanitine (a naming confusion Palmer strongly disputed)

## Historical Context

Ott's work arrived at a time when the medical profession had no reliable treatment for Amanita poisoning. The standard approach — emetics and purgatives — was useless once the alkaloid had been absorbed into the bloodstream. Ott's experimental evidence that atropine could counteract the poison at a physiological level represented a paradigm shift in treatment.

Palmer noted the remarkable fact that multiple researchers independently arrived at the Solanaceae antidote conclusion simultaneously, comparing it to the concurrent discovery of ether anesthesia and the sewing machine. A New Jersey physician had suggested belladonna (the atropine source) based purely on clinical observation in 1877, while Ott demonstrated the mechanism experimentally around the same period.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Dr. Ott's work is directly relevant to mushroom safety and foraging:

- Established the scientific basis for treating Amanita poisoning with atropine
- His frog heart experiment remains a classic demonstration of competitive receptor antagonism
- The atropine-muscarine relationship he documented is still the foundation of modern treatment protocols
- His work underscores the critical importance of rapid medical intervention in cases of suspected Amanita ingestion
