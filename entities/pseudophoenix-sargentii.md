## Overview

Pseudophoenix sargentii, commonly known as the buccaneer palm or Florida cherry palm, is a medium-sized pinnate-leaved palm native to the Caribbean basin and extreme southern Florida. The species is named in honor of Charles Sprague Sargent, the first director of the Arnold Arboretum.

It is distinguished by its swollen, bottle-shaped trunk and stiff, arching, dark green fronds. The fruit is round, turning from green to bright red or orange at maturity, which accounts for the "cherry palm" common name.

Leaves are pinnate, 2–3 meters long, with 80–120 leaflets per side. Leaflets are stiff, linear, and arranged in a single plane, giving the crown a formal, symmetrical appearance. The petiole is 30–60 centimeters long and armed with small teeth along the margins.

Inflorescences are interfoliar, arising among the leaves, and reach 60–80 centimeters in length. Flowers are unisexual, with male and female flowers on the same inflorescence. The fruit is a globose drupe, 1.2–2 centimeters in diameter, ripening from green through yellow to bright red.

The mesocarp of the fruit is fleshy and sweet-tasting, which contributes to its attractiveness to frugivorous animals. Each fruit contains a single hard, globose seed approximately 1 centimeter in diameter. Mature palms can produce several hundred fruits per reproductive cycle.

Propagation is primarily from seed, which germinates within 2–6 months under warm, moist conditions. Seedlings grow slowly, producing only 2–4 leaves per year in the first several years. Transplant success is moderate when young plants are moved with minimal root disturbance.

Seeds retain viability for several months if stored cool and dry, but germination rates decline significantly after one year. Pre-soaking seeds in water for 24–48 hours before sowing can improve and accelerate germination.

Irrigation needs are low to moderate once established. The species is relatively drought-tolerant but benefits from regular watering during prolonged dry periods. Fertilization with a palm-specific micronutrient blend supports healthy growth, particularly in alkaline soils where manganese and iron availability is limited.

Pests include palm weevils (Rhynchophorus cruentatus) and scale insects, though serious infestations are uncommon in healthy specimens. Lethal bronzing disease, caused by the phytoplasma Candidatus Phytoplasma palmae, has been reported in related Florida palms and may pose a future threat.

## Ecological Role

The fruit of Pseudophoenix sargentii is consumed by a variety of Caribbean birds and mammals, including the white-crowned pigeon, northern mockingbird, and key deer. [[clay-pellet-seed-dispersal]] is primarily avian, with birds carrying fruit to new germination sites.

Key deer, an endangered subspecies of white-tailed deer endemic to the Florida Keys, are known to browse on fallen fruit and seedlings. This herbivory can reduce recruitment rates in already small populations.

The palm provides structural habitat in tropical hardwood hammock ecosystems. Its fronds offer shelter for invertebrates, lizards, and nesting birds. The fibrous leaf sheaths harbor epiphytic mosses, ferns, and bromeliads.
