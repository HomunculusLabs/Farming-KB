## Overview

**Mimosa hostilis** (syn. *Mimosa tenuiflora* (Willd.) Poir.), commonly known as jurema preta or tepezcohuite, is a perennial shrub or small tree in the family Fabaceae.

Native to northeastern Brazil and parts of Central America, it is one of the most significant entheogenic and medicinal plants in the Americas.

The root bark of M. hostilis contains exceptionally high concentrations of N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), making it a primary botanical source for ayahuasca analogs and other DMT-containing preparations.

The bark also has a long history of use in traditional medicine for wound healing, burns, and skin conditions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Angiospermae
- **Class:** Magnoliopsida
- **Order:** Fabales
- **Family:** Fabaceae
- **Subfamily:** Mimosoideae
- **Genus:** Mimosa
- **Species:** M. hostilis Benth. (1840), syn. M. tenuiflora (Willd.) Poir. (1810)

The taxonomic distinction between M. hostilis and M. tenuiflora has been debated extensively. Modern treatments tend to synonymize them under M. tenuiflora based on morphological continuity, though M. hostilis remains the name most commonly used in entheogenic and ethnobotanical literature.

**Growth habit:** Perennial shrub or small tree, typically 2–8 m tall, multi-stemmed with a spreading crown.

Bark is dark grey to brown, deeply fissured on older trunks. Inner bark is reddish and fibrous.

**Leaves:** Bipinnately compound, 5–10 cm long, with 2–4 pairs of pinnae. Each pinna bears 10–25 pairs of small oblong leaflets (3–6 mm).

Leaflets are sensitive to touch — the foliage folds when disturbed (thigmonasty), a characteristic feature of the Mimosa genus.

**Flowers:** Spikes 2–5 cm long, pale pink to white, appearing from November to March in the Southern Hemisphere.

**Fruit:** Flat pods 3–6 cm long and 6–10 mm wide, containing 3–5 seeds. Pods are brown at maturity and dehiscent.

**Roots:** Extensive and deep root system with nitrogen-fixing nodules. The root bark is the primary source of DMT and other bioactive alkaloids.

## Distribution and Habitat

M. hostilis is native to the Caatinga biome of northeastern Brazil, where it is one of the dominant woody species. Its range extends from northeastern Brazil through Central America to southern Mexico.

The species thrives in arid and semi-arid tropical environments. It is well-adapted to poor, rocky soils and prolonged drought conditions.

It occurs at elevations from sea level to approximately 1,000 m. In disturbed habitats, M. hostilis often forms dense thickets that shade out competing grasses and herbaceous species.

In its native range, M. hostilis is a pioneer species that colonizes disturbed areas. It regenerates rapidly after deforestation or fire, resprouting from the root crown. This resilience has made it important for land restoration in degraded tropical areas.

## Bioactive Compounds

The root bark of M. hostilis is one of the richest natural sources of DMT. Concentrations range from 0.5–1.5% dry weight.

**N,N-Dimethyltryptamine (DMT):** The primary psychoactive alkaloid at 0.5–1.5% in dried root bark. A potent serotonergic psychedelic acting as a 5-HT2A receptor agonist.

**N-Methyltryptamine (NMT):** Present in lower concentrations than DMT. Less psychoactive but may contribute to the overall pharmacological profile.

**β-Carbolines:** Present in trace amounts in the root bark, including harmine and harmaline. These monoamine oxidase inhibitors are significant because they could theoretically render orally consumed DMT active without a separate MAOI source.

**Non-alkaloidal constituents:** The inner bark contains tannins (10–15%), saponins, flavonoids, and mucilaginous polysaccharides. These compounds are responsible for the plant's traditional dermatological applications.

## Ethnobotanical and Traditional Use

M. hostilis has deep cultural significance in northeastern Brazil, where it is the primary ingredient in **jurema** — a psychoactive beverage prepared from the root bark. Jurema ceremonies have been documented among indigenous peoples since at least the 16th century.

Traditional preparation involves scraping the outer root bark, drying and powdering the inner bark, and performing a cold-water extraction. The resulting beverage is consumed in ritual contexts for healing, divination, and spiritual purposes.

In Mexican traditional medicine, the bark (known as tepezcohuite) is used topically for burns, wounds, and skin infections. Commercial tepezcohuite-based skin products are widely available in Mexico.

The wound-healing properties are attributed to tannin, flavonoid, and polysaccharide content, which promote tissue regeneration and have antimicrobial activity.

## Cultivation

M. hostilis is relatively easy to cultivate from seed. Seeds require scarification (mechanical nicking or brief acid treatment) and hot water soaking to break physical dormancy.

Germination typically occurs within 1–3 weeks after treatment. The species is fast-growing and drought-tolerant once established.

Optimal growth occurs at 25–35°C with annual rainfall of 400–1,000 mm in well-drained soils. It is frost-sensitive and does not tolerate prolonged freezing temperatures.

**Permaculture applications:** As a nitrogen-fixing legume, M. hostilis improves soil fertility and can serve as a nurse tree in agroforestry systems. Its rapid growth makes it suitable for erosion control and land rehabilitation in arid tropical regions.

Harvesting root bark for DMT extraction is destructive to the plant. Overharvesting of wild populations in Brazil and Mexico has led to increased regulation and conservation concerns. Sustainable cultivation practices and root harvesting from established plants are recommended over wild collection.

## Ecology and Applications

M. hostilis plays an important ecological role in the Caatinga as a pioneer species and nitrogen fixer. Its root nodules harbor Rhizobium bacteria that convert atmospheric nitrogen into plant-available forms, enriching impoverished soils.

The flowers provide nectar and pollen for bees and other pollinators. The foliage serves as browse for livestock and wildlife, though thigmonastic response and spines on younger stems offer some protection from excessive grazing.

In permaculture design, M. hostilis can be integrated as a nitrogen-fixing support tree in food forests and syntropic agriculture systems. Its deep root system helps break compacted subsoil layers and brings up minerals from deep soil horizons.
