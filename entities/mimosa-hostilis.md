# Jurema (Mimosa hostilis)

## Overview

[[Mimosa hostilis]], known as **Jurema Preta** in Brazil, is a perennial shrub or small tree native to the northeastern [[Brazil]]ian caatinga (dry scrubland) and extending into Central America and southern [[Mexico]]. It belongs to the legume family (Fabaceae, subfamily Mimosoideae) and is one of the most significant entheogenic plants in the Americas, serving as the primary botanical source of [[N,N-dimethyltryptamine|DMT]] for traditional and contemporary ayahuasca-analogue preparations.

The root bark of M. hostilis contains some of the highest naturally occurring concentrations of DMT among all known plant species, typically ranging from 0.5–1.5% by weight in dried material. This remarkable phytochemical profile has made it central to the indigenous spiritual practices of northeastern Brazil for centuries, and increasingly important in the global context of psychedelic research and plant medicine.

**Nomenclatural note:** The species was originally described as *Mimosa tenuiflora* by Poiret in 1806, and *M. hostilis* by Martius in 1837. The taxonomic community has debated the correct name for decades. *M. tenuiflora* has been more widely adopted in recent botanical literature, but *M. hostilis* remains the dominant name in ethnobotanical and phytochemical contexts. Both names refer to the same species.

## Botanical Description

M. hostilis is a fast-growing, thorny perennial shrub reaching 2–8 meters in height. Key morphological features include:

- **Bark**: Dark brown to gray, rough and fissured on older stems. The root bark — the most pharmacologically significant part — is a deep reddish-brown with a fibrous texture.
- **Leaves**: Bipinnately compound, 5–10 cm long, with 2–4 pairs of pinnae each bearing 10–25 pairs of small linear leaflets (3–5 mm long). The leaves exhibit nyctinastic movement (folding at night), characteristic of the genus.
- **Flowers**: Pale pink to white, arranged in cylindrical spikes 2–5 cm long, appearing from November to March in its native range.
- **Fruit**: Flat, linear pods 3–5 cm long containing 2–5 seeds.
- **Roots**: An extensive taproot system with a thick, DMT-rich bark layer.

The plant is exceptionally drought-tolerant and thrives in poor, sandy soils. It is a nitrogen fixer through its rhizobial symbiosis, making it ecologically valuable for soil restoration in degraded landscapes — a characteristic that connects it to [[permaculture]] applications.

## Phytochemistry

The root bark of M. hostilis is extraordinarily rich in bioactive tryptamines:

- **N,N-Dimethyltryptamine (DMT)**: The primary psychoactive alkaloid, typically 0.5–1.5% in dried root bark. Some analyses have reported concentrations up to 1.7%. DMT is present predominantly as the freebase in fresh material, with some conversion to salts during drying.
- **N-Methyltryptamine (NMT)**: Present at lower concentrations (0.01–0.05%), contributing to the overall tryptamine profile.
- **β-Carbolines**: Trace amounts of harmine and related β-carboline alkaloids have been detected in the bark, though at insufficient concentrations for monoamine oxidase inhibition (MAOI activity).
- **Other constituents**: Tannins, flavonoids, saponins, and various phenolic compounds contribute to the bark's antimicrobial and wound-healing properties.

The stem bark, leaves, and seeds contain negligible amounts of DMT — the alkaloid is concentrated almost exclusively in the root bark.

## Traditional & Cultural Use

### Vinho da Jurema

The most significant traditional preparation is **Vinho da Jurema** (Jurema Wine), a sacramental brew consumed by indigenous peoples of northeastern Brazil, including the Fulni-ô, Xukuru, and Pankararu nations. The preparation involves:

1. Harvesting and shredding fresh root bark
2. Boiling in water, sometimes with the addition of other admixture plants
3. Fermentation and concentration over several hours
4. Consumption in ceremonial contexts for divination, healing, and spiritual communion

The Vinho da Jurema tradition predates European contact and represents one of the oldest continuous entheogenic practices in the Americas. Unlike Amazonian ayahuasca, which uses a β-carboline-rich admixture plant (typically [[Banisteriopsis caapi]]) to render orally active DMT through MAO inhibition, the traditional Jurema preparation's oral activity has been attributed to other synergistic plant admixtures and/or unique preparation methods.

### Medicinal Uses

In Brazilian folk medicine, M. hostilis root bark has been used topically for:
- Burns and wound healing (one of its most widely documented applications)
- Skin infections and inflammation
- Dental pain

The bark's tannin content provides astringent and antimicrobial properties, while the wound-healing effects may involve both anti-inflammatory and cell-proliferative mechanisms.

## Contemporary Applications

### Psycho-spiritual Use

M. hostilis root bark has become one of the most widely used botanical sources of DMT in the global community. It is commonly employed as the DMT source in:
- Pharmaceutical ayahuasca analogues (combined with Peganum harmala or Banisteriopsis caapi as the MAOI source)
- DMT extraction for smoked or vaporized administration
- Changa blends (smoking mixtures combining extracted DMT with harmala alkaloids and other herbs)

### Dermatological Research

The wound-healing properties of M. hostilis bark have attracted pharmaceutical interest. In 2006, a Brazilian patent was filed for a proprietary extract marketed as "Mimosa tenuiflora" for burn treatment and skin regeneration. Clinical studies have demonstrated accelerated epithelialization and reduced scarring.

### Ecological Restoration

As a nitrogen-fixing, drought-resistant pioneer species, M. hostilis has applications in reforestation and soil rehabilitation projects in arid and semi-arid regions. Its ability to thrive in degraded soils while improving nitrogen availability makes it a candidate for [[permaculture]] and [[agroforestry]] systems in tropical drylands.

## Cultivation & Propagation

M. hostilis is relatively easy to cultivate from seed, which is an important consideration given pressures on wild populations. Key cultivation parameters include:

- **Seed germination**: Seeds require scarification (mechanical nicking or brief acid treatment) followed by soaking in warm water for 24 hours to break physical dormancy. Germination rates of 60–80% are achievable under optimal conditions.
- **Soil requirements**: Adaptable to a wide range of soils but prefers well-drained sandy or loamy substrates. Tolerates poor, nutrient-deficient soils due to its nitrogen-fixing capability.
- **Climate**: Thrives in tropical and subtropical conditions (USDA Zones 9–12). Tolerates drought once established but grows best with moderate rainfall.
- **Growth rate**: Fast-growing under warm conditions; can reach 2–3 meters within 2 years from seed.
- **Root harvest**: The DMT-rich root bark is typically harvested from plants 3–5 years old, though younger plants produce usable material. Sustainable harvesting practices involve taking bark from lateral roots while preserving the primary taproot.

## Legal Status

The legal status of M. hostilis is complex and varies by jurisdiction, reflecting the ambiguity of regulating a plant that is simultaneously a traditional medicine, a cosmetic ingredient, and a source of a controlled substance:

- **Brazil**: The plant itself is not scheduled, and its use in traditional indigenous ceremonies is legally protected. However, extraction of DMT for non-indigenous use exists in a legal gray area.
- **United States**: The plant is not specifically scheduled at the federal level, but DMT extracted from it is a Schedule I controlled substance. Possession of the plant material itself is generally tolerated, though prosecution has occurred in cases where extraction intent was clear.
- **European Union**: Legal to grow and possess as an ornamental or ethnobotanical specimen; extraction of controlled alkaloids is prohibited.
- **Australia**: M. hostilis is not listed as a controlled plant in most states, though some jurisdictions restrict its sale.

## Conservation Status

Increasing demand for M. hostilis root bark in global markets has raised concerns about overharvesting. Wild populations in northeastern Brazil have been significantly depleted in accessible areas, leading to deeper harvesting in more remote regions. The species is not currently listed on CITES, but conservationists have called for sustainable harvesting practices and cultivation programs to protect wild populations and ensure the long-term availability of this culturally and pharmacologically significant plant. Ethical sourcing programs and cultivated supplies are increasingly available from specialty botanical vendors.
