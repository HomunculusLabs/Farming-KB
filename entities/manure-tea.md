# Manure Tea

## Description

Manure tea is an anaerobic liquid preparation created by suspending a bag of manure in water for several weeks. It is one of the oldest liquid fertilizer traditions in agriculture, employed by farmers and gardeners for centuries. Despite its long history of use, manure tea carries significant pathogen risks and is not recommended as a safe biological inoculant.

## Classification

- **Category:** Traditional agricultural amendment / liquid fertilizer
- **Type:** Anaerobic preparation
- **Source material:** Animal manure (various livestock)
- **Related to:** Compost extract, compost leachate, AACT

## Key Facts

- Prepared by placing manure in a porous bag (such as burlap) and steeping it in water for several weeks.
- Entirely anaerobic — no aeration is provided during the steeping process.
- Virtually assures the presence of E. coli and other pathogenic bacteria due to the combination of manure as source material and anaerobic brewing conditions.
- Using raw manure as the starting material dramatically increases pathogen risk compared to finished compost.
- Historically valued for its nutrient content but carries unacceptable food safety risks by modern standards.
- Should not be confused with actively aerated compost tea, which uses finished compost and maintains aerobic conditions.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Manure tea represents a cautionary example in the history of liquid biological amendments. While the intuition — extracting nutrients and biology from organic matter into water — is sound, the anaerobic method and use of raw manure create conditions that favor human pathogens over beneficial soil organisms.

Modern cultivators should use actively aerated compost tea instead, which maintains the aerobic conditions that favor beneficial organisms while suppressing pathogens. The key lesson is that keeping biological brews aerobic is essential for safety and effectiveness — anaerobic conditions select for organisms that produce toxic metabolites and can include dangerous human pathogens.
