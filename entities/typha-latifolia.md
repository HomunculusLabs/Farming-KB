# Typha latifolia

## Overview

**Typha latifolia**, commonly known as broadleaf cattail, common cattail, or bulrush, is a
perennial aquatic herbaceous plant in the family Typhaceae. It is among the most widely
recognized wetland plants in the Northern Hemisphere, identified by its distinctive cylindrical
brown flower spikes and tall, flat, sword-like leaves.

The species is a vigorous clonal colonizer of shallow freshwater wetlands, capable of forming
dense monospecific stands that dominate the vegetative community. It plays important ecological
roles in water filtration, shoreline stabilization, and [[hemenway-backyard-wildlife-habitat]] provision.

Nearly all parts of the plant are edible at specific [[psilocybe-cubensis-developmental-stages]], making it one of
the most widely utilized wild food plants among indigenous peoples of [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] and Europe.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Poales
- **Family:** Typhaceae
- **Genus:** Typha
- **Species:** T. latifolia
- **Authority:** L.

**Synonyms:** Typha elongata (Dudley), Typha pontederiformis (Raf.)

The plant grows from a thick, creeping rhizome system that spreads horizontally through
saturated soil and shallow water. Rhizomes are 2–5 cm in diameter, starchy, and produce
extensive branching networks that enable rapid vegetative colonization. A single clone can
cover hundreds of square meters.

Leaves are basal, distichous, linear, and flat, measuring 1–3 m tall and 1–3 cm wide. They
are spongy internally with aerenchyma tissue that facilitates [[hemoglobin-oxygen-transport-biochemistry]] to submerged
rhizomes. Leaf bases sheath the stem and overlap tightly, forming a dense stand structure.

The inflorescence is a dense, cylindrical spike borne at the tip of a stout, triangular stem.
The plant is monoecious: the upper portion of the spike bears male (staminate) flowers while
the lower portion bears female (pistillate) flowers, separated by a short bare internode.
Male flowers are shed after pollen release, leaving the characteristic persistent brown
cattail spike composed of female florets. Each pistillate floret produces numerous minute
seeds, each bearing a tuft of long, silky hairs for wind dispersal.

A single spike can produce over 200,000 seeds, enabling rapid colonization of newly available
wetland habitat.

## Distribution and Habitat

Typha latifolia has a circumboreal distribution, occurring across [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]] from Alaska
to Mexico, throughout Europe and much of Asia, and in northern Africa. It has been introduced
to Australia, New Zealand, and southern South America, where it is sometimes considered
invasive.

The species inhabits shallow freshwater wetlands including marshes, swamps, pond margins,
ditches, and slow-moving stream banks. It tolerates water depths up to approximately 80 cm
and prefers nutrient-rich, fine-textured substrates. Optimal growth occurs in full sun with
standing water 10–30 cm deep.

T. latifolia is more tolerant of deeper water and lower nutrient conditions than the closely
related T. angustifolia (narrowleaf cattail). The two species hybridize to form T. × glauca,
which is often more aggressive than either parent and has expanded aggressively across North
American wetlands.

## Edibility and Uses

**Edible parts:** Rhizomes are harvested in autumn and winter, containing 30–40% starch by
dry weight. They can be roasted, boiled, dried and ground into flour, or processed to extract
starch through water settling. Young shoots (spring shoots emerging from rhizome tips) are
eaten raw or cooked as a vegetable, with a flavor resembling asparagus or bamboo shoots.
