## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Poales
- **Family:** Typhaceae
- **Genus:** Typha
- **Species:** T. latifolia
- **Authority:** L.

**Synonyms:** Typha elongata (Dudley), Typha pontederiformis (Raf.)

The plant grows from a thick, creeping rhizome system that spreads horizontally through
saturated soil and shallow water. Rhizomes are 2–5 cm in diameter, starchy, and produce
extensive branching networks that enable rapid vegetative colonization. A single clone can
cover hundreds of square meters.

Leaves are basal, distichous, linear, and flat, measuring 1–3 m tall and 1–3 cm wide. They
are spongy internally with aerenchyma tissue that facilitates [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]] from Alaska
to Mexico, throughout Europe and much of Asia, and in northern Africa. It has been introduced
to Australia, New Zealand, and southern South America, where it is sometimes considered
invasive.

The species inhabits shallow freshwater wetlands including marshes, swamps, pond margins,
ditches, and slow-moving stream banks. It tolerates water depths up to approximately 80 cm
and prefers nutrient-rich, fine-textured substrates. Optimal growth occurs in full sun with
standing water 10–30 cm deep.

T. latifolia is more tolerant of deeper water and lower nutrient conditions than the closely
related T. angustifolia (narrowleaf cattail). The two species hybridize to form T. × glauca,
which is often more aggressive than either parent and has expanded aggressively across North
American wetlands.

## Edibility and Uses

**Edible parts:** Rhizomes are harvested in autumn and winter, containing 30–40% starch by
dry weight. They can be roasted, boiled, dried and ground into flour, or processed to extract
starch through water settling. Young shoots (spring shoots emerging from rhizome tips) are
eaten raw or cooked as a vegetable, with a flavor resembling asparagus or bamboo shoots.
