## Description

Drip irrigation (also called trickle irrigation) is a water-efficient irrigation method that delivers water directly to plant root zones through a network of tubes, emitters, and perforated pipes. In [[water-management]] / Dryland agriculture
- **Scale**: Home garden to broadscale orchard
- **Complexity**: Low to high (ranging from simple unglazed pots to automated sensor-driven systems)

## Key Facts

### System Types

1. **Solid-line emitter systems**: Extruded plastic hose with drip emitters of various efficiency and flow rates. Can include automatic timers, 7- or 8-line sequences, and soil-sensing nutrient addition systems.

2. **Twin-pipe Bi-wall systems**: A high-pressure pipe joined and perforated to a low-pressure pipe, perforated at intervals to suit row crops. Uses nylon mesh insert filters (or old stockings in larger pipe) as filters.

3. **Low-technology alternatives**: Unglazed earthen jars central to small circle gardens, inverted bottles with leaky tops, short tubes of pebbles (hand-filled), and bucket systems. These serve to help trees survive severe drought conditions.

### Water Efficiency

- Drip irrigation uses only 10-50% of the water required by sprinkler systems, especially effective in orchard and tree establishment.
- Only low head pressure is needed, and water is placed directly at the plants.
- Trees adjusted to local climate need water only in the early establishment phase.

### Salt Management

- Should be used with caution in waters exceeding 800 ppm salt on bare ground, as local salts and carbonates accumulate at the plants.
- For trees, this effect is overcome by letting drips fall into stand pipes that pool water at root level rather than evaporating to air.

### Nutrient Film and Injection Techniques

- Direct infusion of water and nutrients into tree or vine stems (nutrient drip) is used in Israel.
- Plastic film technique used in India to grow Napier grass (*Pennisetum*) for forage.
- Part of the root mass can be in earth, part in nutrient solution in plastic gullies.
- Wick watering using hessian or nylon wicks to nearby plant roots from slotted pipes.

### Automatic Irrigation

- Electronic soil probes sense dryness at root level by measuring soil ionic exchange capacity.
- Mechanical alternatives exist: evaporation-based systems that self-regulate in rain, switch off after sufficient rainfall (e.g., 3 cm downpour), and automatically trigger irrigation.
- Design developed from data by Kevin Handreck, CSIRO, Australia.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Drip irrigation maintains consistent soil moisture levels critical for mycorrhizal development and beneficial fungal networks in soil.
- Subsurface drip placement creates moist root zones favorable for [[mushroom-cultivation]] substrates in integrated food forest systems.
- The precise moisture control enables cultivation of moisture-sensitive fungi in controlled environments.
- Greywater-fed drip systems integrate nutrient cycling with fungal decomposition of organic particulates.

## Integration with Permaculture Design

- Combines with swales for maximum water harvesting and distribution efficiency.
- Pairs with mulch systems (stone, organic, plastic sheet) to minimize evaporation losses.
- Can be powered by gravity flow from tanks, swales, or elevated water storage.
- Domestic wastewater can be routed through subsurface drip lines after basic filtration.
