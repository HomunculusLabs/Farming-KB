# Berlese Funnel

## Description

The **Berlese funnel** is a simple but effective trapping device used to extract, collect, and census [[microarthropods]] and other small soil organisms from soil and duff samples. Named after Italian entomologist **Giovanni Berlese** (1863-1927) who invented it, the Berlese funnel is a fundamental tool for assessing [[soil-food-web]] health by allowing gardeners and researchers to inventory the microarthropod populations present in their soils.

## Classification

- **Category:** Soil biology sampling tool / [[cannabis-reflux-extraction-apparatus]]
- **Inventor:** Giovanni Berlese (1863-1927)
- **Also known as:** Tullgren funnel (a modified version using a light bulb as heat source, named after Albert Tullgren)

## Key Facts

- **Purpose:** Extracts microarthropods (mites, springtails, beetle larvae, etc.) and other small organisms from soil samples
- **Principle of operation:** Uses heat and light (or chemical repellents like mothballs) to drive soil organisms downward through a screen into a collection container
- **Minimum extraction time:** 3 days; 1 week recommended for complete extraction
- **Materials needed:** Plastic bottle, window screening (1.5-3 mm openings), collection container, 40-60 watt light bulb, optional antifreeze or ethyl alcohol

## Construction and Setup

### Materials
1. **Funnel:** A liter-sized plastic bottle (soda or juice bottle) with the bottom cut off, inverted so the drinking end faces down
2. **Screen:** A 2-inch square of window screening with openings approximately 1.5-3 mm, placed inside the bottle neck
3. **Collection container:** A quart-sized container (e.g., recycled yogurt or cottage cheese container) that holds the funnel and collects organisms
4. **Heat source:** A 40-60 watt light bulb suspended about 6 inches (15 cm) above the funnel opening
5. **Optional preservative:** Antifreeze or ethyl alcohol in the collection container to kill organisms and prevent cannibalism

### Procedure
1. Cut the bottom off a plastic bottle and invert it to create a funnel
2. Place window screening inside the bottle neck
3. Set the funnel into the collection container
4. Optionally add a small amount of antifreeze or ethyl alcohol to the container
5. Fill the funnel with soil and duff from the top 8 inches (20 cm) of the target area
6. Position a 40-60 watt light bulb approximately 6 inches above the funnel
7. Leave undisturbed for a minimum of 3 days (1 week optimal)
8. Count and identify organisms in the collection container using a magnifying glass or MacroScope

### Alternative Method
Mothballs can be placed on top of the soil instead of using a light bulb, achieving similar results by driving organisms downward through chemical repellency.

## What You Can Find

A typical Berlese funnel extraction from healthy soil will reveal:
- **Mites** (Acari) — often the most numerous microarthropods
- **Springtails** ([[collembola]]) — hexapod decomposers
- **Beetle larvae** — immature stages of various beetle species
- **Other microarthropods** — pseudoscorpions, symphylans, pauropods
- **Occasional gastropods** — small slugs or snails
- **Worms** — small oligochaetes and nematodes

## Interpretation of Results

### Healthy Soil Food Web Indicators
- High diversity of microarthropod types
- Numerous individuals across multiple species
- Presence of both predatory and decomposer organisms
- Indicates adequate [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-bacterial-and-fungal-dominant-soil]] populations to support the microarthropod community

### Concerning Results
- Low diversity (few different organism types)
- Low numbers overall
- Empty traps suggest missing links in the food chain
- Indicates need for soil food web restoration efforts

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

1. **Soil health assessment:** The Berlese funnel provides a direct, visual method for gardeners to assess their soil food web without expensive laboratory equipment
2. **Fungal cultivation monitoring:** Microarthropod populations reflect the fungal and [[cannabis-endorhiza-bacterial-communities]] that support [[teaming-with-microbes-fungi-and-mycorrhizal-relationships]]
3. **Compost quality testing:** Berlese funnels can be used to assess the biological diversity of compost before application
4. **[[solomon-insects-and-diseases-integrated-approach]]:** Should be combined with visual surveys of larger organisms (worms, beetles) and professional laboratory testing for complete [[soil-food-web-assessment]]
5. **Educational tool:** The Berlese funnel is an accessible entry point for understanding soil biology, requiring minimal equipment and expertise

## Limitations

- Captures only mobile organisms present at the time of sampling (a snapshot, not a complete inventory)
- Cannot capture bacteria, most fungi, or organisms too small to pass through the screen
- Requires supplementary methods (laboratory analysis) for complete microbial assessment
- Identification of captured organisms requires additional expertise or reference materials
