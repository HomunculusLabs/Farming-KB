# IM

**PIHKAL Entry #91** -- Isomescaline; 2,3,4-Trimethoxyphenethylamine

## Chemical Name

2,3,4-Trimethoxyphenethylamine

## Dosage and Duration

- **Dosage:** greater than 400 mg

- **Duration:** unknown

## Qualitative Effects

IM is essentially inactive in humans:

- At 300 mg: no effects whatsoever

- At 400 mg: "Maybe a slight tingle at the hour-and-a-half point. Maybe not. Certainly nothing an hour later." Rated as "without action"

## Synthesis

Synthesized from commercially available 2,3,4-trimethoxybenzaldehyde via nitrostyrene formation with nitromethane and ammonium acetate in nitromethane, followed by LAH reduction. The nitrostyrene had a mp of 73-74 degrees C. The product was isolated as 2,3,4-trimethoxyphenethylamine hydrochloride.

## Extensions and Commentary

Fifty years before PIHKAL, this material was given the name "reciprocal mescaline" based on the belief that it exacerbated clinical symptoms in schizophrenic patients. The original report stated: "We have discovered an extremely remarkable dependency of the intoxicating action upon the position of the three methoxy groups." [[mescaline-entity]] (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine) produces stronger intoxication in normal subjects than in schizophrenic patients, whereas IM has the opposite effect -- little action in healthy individuals but reportedly potent in the schizophrenic.

The promised further metabolic studies never appeared. A later study using radioactive labelling found that when loaded with methionine, schizophrenics showed lesser amounts of demethylation. However, no independent confirmation of these properties has been reported, and the conventional thinking is that these findings probably cannot serve as a diagnostic test for schizophrenia.

## Chemical Family and Historical Context

IM represents one of the earliest positional isomers explored in the phenethylamine series, with a research history stretching back to the 1930s. The concept of "reciprocal mescaline" emerged from a fascinating period in psychiatric research when phenethylamines were investigated as potential diagnostic tools and therapeutic agents for schizophrenia.

The early work on IM was conducted in an era when the biological basis of schizophrenia was poorly understood and researchers were seeking chemical markers that could distinguish psychotic from non-psychotic individuals. The hypothesis that IM selectively affected schizophrenic patients reflected a broader research paradigm that ultimately proved unfruitful, but which generated valuable structural information about phenethylamines.

The compound's formal name -- 2,3,4-trimethoxyphenethylamine -- reveals its relationship to mescaline (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine) as a positional isomer. The three methoxy groups are shifted one position around the aromatic ring, from the 3,4,5-pattern to the 2,3,4-pattern. This seemingly minor change completely abolishes psychedelic activity.

## Pharmacological Discussion

The complete inactivity of IM at doses up to 400 mg provides a powerful demonstration of the positional sensitivity of psychedelic phenethylamines. The 5-HT2A receptor, the primary target of compounds like mescaline and LSD, has very precise geometric requirements for agonist binding. The spatial arrangement of the three methoxy groups in the 2,3,4-pattern apparently fails to satisfy these requirements.

Computational receptor docking studies and molecular modelling suggest that the 3,4,5-trimethoxy pattern allows optimal interaction with a hydrophobic pocket on the 5-HT2A receptor, while the 2,3,4-pattern positions one methoxy group in a region that may cause steric clash or fail to form productive interactions.

The "reciprocal mescaline" hypothesis -- that IM differentially affects schizophrenic patients -- is now considered historically interesting but not scientifically validated. Modern understanding of schizophrenia as a complex, multifactorial disorder makes it unlikely that any single compound could serve as a diagnostic tool. The original observations may have reflected placebo effects, the natural variability of psychotic symptoms, or metabolic differences between patient populations rather than a genuine pharmacological selectivity.

## Comparison to Related Mescaline Isomers

The systematic exploration of trimethoxyphenethylamine isomers provides a textbook example of structure-activity relationships in this compound class:

- 2,3,4-TMA (IM): Inactive at up to 400 mg

- 2,3,5-TMA: Not well characterized; not in PIHKAL

- 2,4,5-TMA: Active (mescaline analogue with shifted pattern)

- 2,4,6-TMA (TMA-2): Active at 20-40 mg (highly potent)

- 3,4,5-TMA (mescaline): Active at 200-500 mg (classic psychedelic)

- 3,4,6-TMA: Not well characterized

The pattern that emerges is that methoxy substitution at the 2-position, when combined with certain other arrangements, can either dramatically reduce or enhance activity depending on the full substitution pattern.

## Harm Reduction and Safety Considerations

While IM is inactive as a psychedelic, it is still a biologically active amine compound:

- The compound's effects on cardiovascular function, even at inactive psychedelic doses, have not been systematically studied

- High doses (400+ mg) of any phenethylamine can produce unpleasant physical side effects including tachycardia, hypertension, and gastrointestinal distress

- Any reported differential effects in psychiatric populations are unconfirmed and should not be relied upon

- The compound should not be used as a self-diagnostic tool for any medical condition

## Legal Status

IM is not specifically scheduled in most jurisdictions. As a trimethoxyphenethylamine, it could theoretically fall under analogue legislation related to mescaline (Schedule I in the US), but its lack of psychoactivity makes such enforcement unlikely. The compound is primarily of academic interest and has not appeared on recreational markets.

## Cultural and Historical Significance

The story of "reciprocal mescaline" is a cautionary tale in the history of psychopharmacology. It illustrates how early enthusiasm for simple chemical explanations of complex psychiatric disorders can lead to findings that cannot be replicated. The compound's history also demonstrates the importance of the Shulgins' systematic approach: by personally testing compounds at a range of doses, they were able to definitively establish inactivity where earlier researchers had reported ambiguous or contradictory results.

The episode also reflects the ethical complexities of psychiatric research in the mid-20th century, when experimental compounds were administered to institutionalized patients with what would now be considered inadequate informed consent and oversight.
