# Roland Griffiths

Roland Robert Griffiths (1944–December 16, 2023) was an American psychopharmacologist and professor at Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine who is widely regarded as the most important figure in reestablishing psychedelic science as a rigorous academic discipline in the 21st century. Over a 50-year career at Hopkins, he authored approximately 400 scientific publications and founded the Johns Hopkins Center for Psychedelic and Consciousness Research in 2019.

His 2006 psilocybin study is the most cited and influential paper in the modern psychedelic literature, and the center he founded has become the field’s flagship institution.

## Education and Early Career

Griffiths earned his B.A. in Psychology from Occidental College in 1966 and his Ph.D. in Pharmacology from the University of Minnesota in 1970. His doctoral research focused on behavioral pharmacology and drug self-administration. He joined Johns Hopkins as a postdoctoral fellow in 1970 and remained on the faculty for over five decades, holding joint appointments in the Departments of Psychiatry and Behavioral Sciences and Neuroscience. He held the Oliver Lee McCarty Distinguished Professor of Neuropsychopharmacology endowed chair. He also held adjunct positions at the University of Maryland and the Maryland Psychiatric Research Center earlier in his career.

Before his psychedelic research, Griffiths was a highly respected mainstream pharmacologist. He pioneered gold-standard methods for assessing the abuse liability of drugs and conducted influential research on caffeine, demonstrating that it meets pharmacological criteria for a drug of dependence including withdrawal symptoms and reinforcement. His caffeine research was widely cited and influenced FDA regulatory thinking. He also researched benzodiazepines, sedative-hypnotics, and other CNS-active compounds. This decades-long record of rigorous, conventional pharmacology gave him unique credibility when he later pivoted to studying psychedelics.

It was a critical factor in gaining institutional approval for controversial research that had been dormant since the 1970s.

## Relaunching Psychedelic Research (2000–2006)

In 2000, after nearly 30 years of near-total suppression of psychedelic research in the United States, Griffiths and his team at Johns Hopkins became the first to obtain FDA and IRB approval to study psilocybin in healthy human volunteers. Their landmark study, published in *Psychopharmacology* in 2006, demonstrated that under carefully controlled, supportive conditions, a single dose of psilocybin could produce profound mystical-type experiences in the majority of participants. At 14-month follow-up, approximately 67% rated the experience among the five most personally meaningful of their lives, and 64% ranked it among the five most spiritually significant. This study is widely credited with kickstarting the modern psychedelic renaissance in academic medicine.

It catalyzed billions in investment and dozens of clinical trials worldwide.

## Mystical Experience Quantification

Griffiths and colleagues developed and validated the Mystical Experience Questionnaire (MEQ30), a 30-item self-report instrument for quantifying mystical-type experiences that has become a standard tool across the psychedelic research field. His work demonstrated that the intensity of the mystical experience during a psilocybin session was a strong predictor of long-term positive outcomes—a finding replicated across multiple studies. This quantification work was critical for making psychedelic research scientifically rigorous and publishable in top-tier journals. By establishing the subjective mystical experience as a legitimate variable of scientific inquiry, Griffiths elevated it from “anecdotal noise” to a measurable mediator of therapeutic change—a paradigm-shifting contribution that has influenced every subsequent clinical trial in the field.

## Cancer Distress Studies (2010–2016)

Griffiths led a landmark double-blind, crossover study published in *JAMA Psychiatry* (2016) examining psilocybin for cancer-related anxiety and depression. Participants with life-threatening cancer diagnoses and clinically significant anxiety or depression received either a high dose (22 mg/70 kg) or very low active dose (1 mg/70 kg) of psilocybin. At 5 weeks, approximately 80% of the high-dose group showed clinically significant reductions in depression and anxiety, with benefits sustained or increased at 6-month follow-up. Approximately 80% reported moderately to greatly increased well-being and life satisfaction.

About 70% rated the experience as among the top five most meaningful experiences of their lives. This study, alongside a concurrent NYU study by Stephen Ross’s team which found similar results, provided the “one-two punch” that dramatically shifted mainstream medical perception of psychedelics. See [[psilocybin-and-death-anxiety]].

## Smoking Cessation (2014)

Griffiths published a pilot study showing remarkable success using psilocybin for smoking cessation. Fifteen long-term smokers (average 6 prior quit attempts) received psilocybin in a structured setting alongside cognitive behavioral therapy for nicotine dependence. The study achieved an 80% abstinence rate at 6-month follow-up—dramatically higher than conventional treatments such as varenicline or nicotine replacement (typically 35% at best). Long-term follow-up at 2.5 years showed 67% continued abstinence, confirmed by biochemical verification. This was one of the first studies to apply psilocybin to addiction treatment and opened a new frontier for psychedelic-assisted therapy beyond depression and end-of-life anxiety. Subsequent research at Hopkins and other institutions has explored psilocybin for alcohol dependence, cocaine addiction, and other substance use disorders, building on Griffiths’ foundational work.

## Major Depressive Disorder (2021)

Published in *JAMA Psychiatry* (2021), Griffiths’ team conducted a randomized clinical trial of psilocybin for major depressive disorder. Twenty-four adults with MDD received two doses of psilocybin (20 mg/70 kg then 30 mg/70 kg) with supportive psychotherapy, producing a large, rapid, and sustained antidepressant effect with an effect size of d = 2.5 (very large by psychiatric standards). Approximately 71% met criteria for clinical response and 54% achieved remission at 4-week follow-up. The rapid onset and large effect size distinguished psilocybin from conventional antidepressants, which typically require weeks to achieve modest effects. See [[psilocybin-depression-anxiety-treatment]].

## Long-Term Survey Studies

Griffiths also led large online survey studies examining the real-world use and effects of psychedelics outside laboratory settings. A 2016 survey published in *PNAS* of approximately 2,000 people who reported challenging psychedelic experiences found that 84% reported benefiting from the experience retrospectively. A 2018 survey in *Scientific Reports* found that psychedelic users reported greater connection to nature and environmental concern compared to non-users. These naturalistic studies complemented his controlled clinical trials by examining psychedelic experiences in diverse, real-world contexts. The challenging experience study was particularly influential because it addressed a common public concern about “bad trips,” finding that even difficult experiences often led to personal growth when properly integrated.

## Religious Leaders Study (2017)

Griffiths published a study in the *Journal of Religion and Health* involving clergy and spiritual leaders who received psilocybin in a controlled setting. The study found that the experience deepened participants’ spiritual practices and was generally well-received by religious professionals, suggesting that psychedelic experiences could complement rather than conflict with established religious traditions. This work addressed a common concern among religious communities that psychedelic experiences might undermine faith. Participants reported that the psilocybin experience helped them feel more connected to the transcendent dimensions of their existing religious practice, rather than replacing or diminishing it.

## Founding the Center (2019)

In 2019, Griffiths founded the Johns Hopkins Center for Psychedelic and Consciousness Research—the first psychedelic research center at a major U.S. academic institution in over 50 years. It was initially funded by a $17 million private donation from a group of private donors. The center has trained dozens of researchers, conducted clinical trials across multiple indications including depression, addiction, anorexia, and obsessive-compulsive disorder, and served as a model for similar centers at UC Berkeley, Imperial College London, Yale, and other institutions worldwide. Its establishment institutionalized the field and provided the infrastructure for training the next generation of psychedelic scientists. The center’s work has directly informed the clinical development programs of companies like [[compass-pathways]] seeking regulatory approval for psilocybin-based therapies.

## Methodological Contributions

Griffiths established the gold-standard clinical trial methodology for psychedelic research that has been adopted worldwide. This includes double-blind designs with active placebo controls, rigorous participant screening, trained therapist guides, structured dosing sessions with music in controlled environments, validated outcome measures (MEQ30, GRID-HAMD, MADRS), and long-term follow-up assessments. His insistence on methodological conservatism insulated the field from criticism and helped establish credibility with regulatory agencies and the broader medical community. See [[psilocybin-assisted-therapy]] and [[psilocybin-psychotherapy]].

## Stance on Commercialization

As psychedelics attracted billions in venture capital investment, Griffiths expressed concern that premature commercialization could compromise scientific rigor and patient safety. He advocated for careful, evidence-based approaches over hype and was vocal about the risks of moving too quickly from research to market. He also expressed concern about the potential for psychedelics to be “medicalized” in ways that stripped them of their broader spiritual and philosophical significance. He frequently testified before FDA panels and advocated for rescheduling psilocybin from Schedule I, arguing that its therapeutic potential and safety profile warranted easier research access. He warned that the rush to commercialize could lead to inadequate therapist training, insufficient attention to set and setting, and the prioritization of profit over patient welfare.

## Critiques and Limitations

A persistent critique of psychedelic research including Griffiths’ studies is that the psychoactive effects of psilocybin make true double-blinding difficult—participants and guides can usually distinguish the active dose from placebo. Griffiths acknowledged this limitation and used very low active doses (1 mg/70 kg) as a comparator rather than full placebo. Some psychedelic activists felt his medical-model approach was too conservative, arguing it risked “medicalizing” what they viewed as fundamentally spiritual practices. Despite these critiques, Griffiths’ cautious approach is widely credited with earning mainstream scientific legitimacy for the entire field.

## Influence on Drug Development

Griffiths’ research program directly enabled the emergence of the commercial psychedelic medicine industry. The clinical evidence base he built—demonstrating safety, efficacy, and durable outcomes—provided the scientific foundation that companies like [[compass-pathways]], Cybin, and MindMed used to attract billions in investment capital and advance their own drug development programs. His methodological frameworks and validated assessment instruments have been adopted across the industry as standard practice. Without Griffiths’ decades of groundwork, it is unlikely that any psychedelic compound would have reached Phase 3 clinical trials by the mid-2020s.

The FDA’s 2018 Breakthrough Therapy designation for psilocybin in treatment-resistant depression—granted to [[compass-pathways]]—was made possible in large part by the clinical evidence Griffiths’ program produced.

## Awards and Recognition

Named by *TIME* magazine in 2015 as one of the “25 People in the World Who Are Changing the World.” Received the Solomon H. Snyder Award for distinguished contributions to neuropsychopharmacology. Elected Fellow of the American College of Neuropsychopharmacology. Received the Joseph Cochin Award from the College on Problems of Drug Dependence. Served on numerous NIH review panels and editorial boards throughout his career. His research has been cited thousands of times and has directly influenced drug policy discussions at the FDA, DEA, and international regulatory bodies. His work was featured extensively in major media including *The New York Times*, *The New Yorker*, *Wired*, *Scientific American*, *60 Minutes*, and Michael Pollan’s bestselling book *How to Change Your Mind*.

## Legacy

Griffiths is widely regarded—alongside Rick Strassman, Robin Carhart-Harris, and David Nichols—as a founding architect of the modern psychedelic renaissance. His unique combination of mainstream pharmacological credibility, methodological precision, and willingness to tackle a taboo research area made him the ideal figure to break the decades-long research freeze.

Unlike earlier psychedelic researchers such as Timothy Leary, Griffiths avoided counterculture associations and maintained relationships with regulatory agencies throughout his career. His scientific framework, validated instruments (MEQ30), and trained researchers continue to drive psychedelic medicine forward. He died of colon cancer on December 16, 2023, at his home in Baltimore. In his final months, he gave extensive interviews reflecting on his life’s work with characteristic calm and philosophical depth. He is survived by his wife and children. Colleagues and students remember him for his warmth, humor, scientific rigor, and genuine sense of wonder about consciousness.
