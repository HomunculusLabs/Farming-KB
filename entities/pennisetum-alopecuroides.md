The species is widely cultivated as an ornamental landscape grass for its arching habit, fuzzy bottlebrush inflorescences, and reliable fall color.

It has become naturalized in parts of North America, Europe, and Australia, raising concerns about its invasive potential in certain climates.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Pennisetum alopecuroides (L.) Spreng. was originally described as Alopecurus alopecuroides by Linnaeus in 1753.

The genus name derives from the Latin penna (feather) and seta (bristle), referring to the long bristles surrounding the spikelets.

It forms dense tussocks of narrow, arching leaf blades reaching 60 to 100 centimeters in height.

The foliage is medium green during [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]], southern Europe, eastern Australia, and New Zealand.

It is listed as an invasive species in several jurisdictions, including California, Hawaii, and parts of Australia, where it can displace native vegetation in grasslands and riparian areas.

The species prefers well-drained soils and full sun but tolerates light shade, clay, and sandy substrates.

It is moderately drought-tolerant once established and can survive periodic flooding.

## Cultivation

Fountain grass is propagated primarily by seed and by division of established clumps in early spring.

Seeds require no cold stratification and germinate readily at temperatures above 20 degrees Celsius.

Division is the preferred method for named cultivars, as it preserves exact ornamental characteristics.

Plants perform best in full sun with well-drained soil, though they tolerate a wide range of soil types including clay and poor soils.

Once established, Pennisetum alopecuroides requires minimal irrigation and is considered a low-maintenance ornamental.

Recommended spacing in landscape plantings is 60 to 90 centimeters between plants.

Fertilizer requirements are low; excessive nitrogen can produce floppy, weak stems that reduce the plant's architectural quality.

Hardiness ranges from USDA zones 5 to 9, with winter survival depending on snow cover and drainage in the colder portions of its range.

Plants should be cut back to 5 to 10 centimeters above ground in late winter or early spring before new growth resumes.

Named cultivars include Hameln, a compact dwarf form reaching 60 centimeters, and Moudry, valued for its dark flower spikes.
