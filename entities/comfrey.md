# Comfrey (*Symphytum officinale*)

## Overview

Comfrey is a vigorous perennial herb in the family Boraginaceae, native to Europe and naturalized across temperate regions worldwide. Renowned in permaculture as one of the most valuable "dynamic accumulator" plants, comfrey produces deep taproots that mine minerals from subsoil layers and concentrate them in its leaves, making it an exceptional biomass producer, compost activator, and mulch crop. Historically valued in herbal medicine for wound healing and bone repair (hence folk names like "knitbone" and "boneset"), comfrey's internal use is now controversial due to hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs), while topical preparations remain widely used.

## Taxonomy and Identification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Division:** Angiosperms
- **Class:** Eudicots
- **Order:** Boraginales
- **Family:** Boraginaceae
- **Genus:** *Symphytum*
- **Species:** *S. officinale* (common comfrey)

### Common Names

Knitbone, boneset, bruisewort, gum plant, healing herb, slippery root, ass ear, wallwort, blackwort, consound

### Key Species and Cultivars

1. ***Symphytum officinale*** — Common comfrey, the traditional medicinal species. Contains PAs.
2. ***Symphytum × uplandicum*** (Russian comfrey) — Hybrid of *S. officinale* × *S. asperum*. The most widely cultivated variety in permaculture. 'Bocking 14' is the most famous cultivar — sterile, high-yielding, and deep-rooted.
3. ***Symphytum asperum*** — Prickly comfrey, one parent of Russian comfrey.
4. ***Symphytum tuberosum*** — Tuberous comfrey, a smaller species with edible roots in some traditions.

### Morphology

- **Height:** 60–150 cm (2–5 feet)
- **Spread:** 60–120 cm, forming dense clumps
- **Leaves:** Large, lanceolate to ovate, 15–60 cm long, covered in coarse hairs. Lower leaves are petiolate; upper leaves clasp the stem. Surface is rough and bristly.
- **Stems:** Winged, erect, branching in upper portions, covered in fine hairs
- **Flowers:** Bell-shaped, pendulous, 10–15 mm long. *S. officinale* flowers are typically purple-blue to violet (rarely white or pink). *S. × uplandicum* flowers range from blue-violet to pink or white. Flowers in cymose clusters from late spring through summer.
- **Roots:** Extensive, deep taproot system reaching 1.5–3 meters into the subsoil. Thick, black, mucilaginous, and branching. Roots are difficult to eradicate once established.
- **Seeds:** Four nutlets per flower (in fertile varieties); each is a glossy black teardrop shape

### Identification Notes

Comfrey can be confused with foxglove (*Digitalis purpurea*), which is highly toxic. Key differences:
- Comfrey leaves are arranged in a winged (decurrent) pattern along the stem; foxglove leaves form a basal rosette
- Comfrey flowers are bell-shaped and drooping; foxglove flowers are tubular and arranged on one side of a tall spike
- Comfrey stems are distinctly winged where leaves meet the stem

## Nutritional and Mineral Profile

Comfrey's exceptional value in permaculture comes from its ability to concentrate minerals from deep subsoil layers:

| Nutrient | Concentration (dry weight) |
|----------|---------------------------|
| Nitrogen (N) | 1.8–8.0% |
| Potassium (K) | 1.2–8.0% |
| Calcium (Ca) | 1.0–4.0% |
| Phosphorus (P) | 0.3–0.8% |
| Magnesium (Mg) | 0.2–0.5% |
| Iron (Fe) | Trace |
| Silica | Moderate |

The potassium concentration in comfrey leaves (up to 8% in dried tissue) is exceptionally high, making comfrey the organic equivalent of liquid kelp fertilizer for potash-hungry crops like tomatoes and potatoes.

## Permaculture Applications

### Dynamic Accumulator

Comfrey is the poster child of the dynamic accumulator concept in permaculture. Its deep taproot (up to 3 m) accesses minerals and nutrients that are unavailable to shallow-rooted plants, pulling them into its leaf biomass where they become available through:

- **Composting:** Comfrey leaves accelerate decomposition and enrich finished compost
- **Mulching:** Fresh or wilted leaves used as a nutrient-dense mulch around plants
- **Liquid feed:** Comfrey tea (fermented leaf extract) provides a concentrated liquid fertilizer
- **Chop-and-drop:** Cutting comfrey and leaving it in place as green manure

### Bocking 14 Cultivar

The 'Bocking 14' cultivar, developed by Lawrence D. Hills at the Henry Doubleday Research Association (HDRA) in the 1950s, is the standard for permaculture use:

- **Sterile triploid:** Produces no viable seeds, preventing unwanted spread
- **High yield:** 5–7 cuts per season possible in temperate climates
- **Deep roots:** Exceptional mineral extraction capacity
- **Disease resistant:** Minimal pest and disease issues
- **Propagation:** Root cuttings only (crown divisions or root pieces)

### Comfrey Tea (Liquid Fertilizer)

The most popular preparation for garden use:

1. Harvest comfrey leaves and pack loosely into a container (bucket or barrel)
2. Fill with water (or allow to decompose without water for a more concentrated extract)
3. Cover and let ferment for 3–6 weeks (this will produce a strong odor)
4. Dilute 1:10 to 1:15 with water before applying
5. Use as a potassium-rich foliar feed or soil drench for fruiting crops

Weight-for-weight, comfrey tea provides comparable potassium to commercial tomato fertilizers.

### Companion Planting Uses

- **Fruit tree understory:** Comfrey planted beneath fruit trees mines calcium and potassium from deep soil layers, making them available as surface mulch
- **Orchard floor:** Dense comfrey plantings suppress weeds and provide a continuous supply of mulch material
- **Poultry forage:** Chickens will eat wilted comfrey leaves, gaining additional protein and minerals
- **Comfrey barrier:** Dense root systems can help stabilize soil on slopes and prevent erosion

### Biomass Production

In ideal conditions, a well-established comfrey patch can produce:
- **Yield:** 3–5 kg of fresh leaf material per plant per year
- **Cuts:** 4–7 harvests per growing season
- **Density:** 4–6 plants per square meter recommended
- **Standing biomass:** Up to 20 tonnes per hectare per year in ideal conditions

## Medicinal Uses

### Traditional Applications

Comfrey has been used medicinally for over 2,500 years:

- **Wound healing:** Applied externally as a poultice for cuts, abrasions, burns, and ulcers
- **Bone and joint repair:** Folk use for fractures, sprains, and osteoarthritis (hence "knitbone")
- **Anti-inflammatory:** Reduces swelling and inflammation in bruises and muscle injuries
- **Skin conditions:** Eczema, psoriasis, and dermatitis
- **Digestive support:** Historically taken internally for gastric ulcers and respiratory conditions

### Active Compounds

- **Allantoin:** Cell proliferant that stimulates tissue repair and wound healing. This is the primary compound responsible for comfrey's reputation as a wound-healing herb.
- **Rosmarinic acid:** Anti-inflammatory antioxidant
- **Mucilage:** Soothing, demulcent properties for mucous membranes
- **Tannins:** Astringent, reduce bleeding and discharge
- **Symphytine and other pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs):** Hepatotoxic compounds concentrated in the root

### Safety Concerns — Pyrrolizidine Alkaloids

PAs in comfrey (primarily symphytine, echimidine, and lycopsamine) are **hepatotoxic, mutagenic, and carcinogenic**:

- **Hepatotoxicity:** PAs cause hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD), potentially fatal liver damage
- **Carcinogenicity:** Classified as possibly carcinogenic to humans (IARC Group 2B)
- **Root vs. leaf:** Roots contain 10–20× higher PA concentration than leaves
- **Internal use:** Not recommended. Germany banned internal use of comfrey preparations in 1992. Australia, the UK, and other countries restrict or prohibit internal sale.
- **External use:** Topical application on intact skin is generally considered safe for short-term use (up to 4–6 weeks) due to minimal systemic absorption. Avoid on broken skin or open wounds.
- **PA-free cultivars:** 'Bocking 14' has lower PA levels than wild comfrey but is not PA-free

### Regulatory Status

- **EU:** Internal use banned; external use restricted to products containing <0.007 μg PAs/day (effectively requiring PA-free preparations)
- **US:** FDA issued a warning against internal use in 2001; available as a dietary supplement with cautionary labeling
- **UK:** MHRA restricts internal products; external use permitted with warnings
- **Australia:** TGA classifies comfrey as a prescription-only substance for internal use
