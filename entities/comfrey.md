# Symphytum officinale

**Common Comfrey** · **Knitbone** · **Boneset** · **Bruisewort**

*Symphytum officinale* is a perennial herbaceous plant in the Boraginaceae (borage) family, native to Europe and naturalized across temperate regions worldwide. It is one of the most important plants in both permaculture systems and traditional Western herbalism. Valued for its extraordinary ability to accumulate nutrients from deep soil layers, its rapid biomass production, and its historical use in wound healing and bone repair, comfrey occupies a unique position at the intersection of sustainable agriculture and botanical medicine.

The genus *Symphytum* contains approximately 30 species distributed across Europe and western Asia. Key related species include:

- *S. × uplandicum* (Russian comfrey) — the most commonly cultivated variety in permaculture, a natural hybrid (*S. officinale* × *S. asperum*)
- *S. asperum* (prickly comfrey) — rough-haired parent of Russian comfrey
- *S. tuberosum* (tuberous comfrey) — smaller species with edible tuberous roots

**Etymology:** *Symphytum* from Greek *symphyein* (to unite/join together), referring to its traditional use in knitting broken bones. *Officinale* denoting its status as an official apothecary herb. Common name "knitbone" directly references its bone-mending reputation.

## Morphology

- **Growth habit:** Coarse, clump-forming perennial, 60–150 cm tall and 60–120 cm wide. Deep taproot system reaching 1.5–3 meters into the subsoil. Forms dense, persistent crowns that can live for decades.
- **Leaves:** Large, broadly lanceolate to ovate, 15–60 cm long, 5–20 cm wide. Lower leaves petiolate (stalked), upper leaves sessile or clasping. Surface is covered in fine, bristly hairs (rough texture). Dark green, sometimes slightly hairy on both surfaces. Leaves emerge from a basal rosette in early spring, followed by tall flowering stems.
- **Stems:** Erect, branched above, hollow, winged at base, covered in spreading hairs. 60–150 cm tall, 5–10 mm diameter at base.
- **Flowers:** Bell-shaped (campanulate), 12–18 mm long, pendant on one-sided curved racemes (cincinnus inflorescence). Color ranges from creamy-white to pale purple, pink, or violet (most commonly purple-violet). Corolla has 5 fused petals with a conspicuous fringed corona. Flowers from May to July (or August). Attractive to bees and other pollinators — comfrey is an excellent bee forage plant.
- **Fruit:** Cluster of 4 nutlets (schizocarp), each 4–5 mm, black, glossy, covered in hooked barbs that facilitate animal dispersal.
- **Root system:** Massive, deep-reaching. Primary taproot penetrates 1.5–3 meters with extensive lateral branching. Roots are black on the outside, white and fleshy internally, containing allantoin and mucilage.

## Cultivation

### Propagation
- **Root cuttings (preferred):** Cut 5–10 cm sections of root horizontally and plant 2–5 cm deep in spring. Each section produces multiple shoots. This is the standard commercial propagation method and produces true-to-type plants.
- **Crown divisions:** Divide established clumps in spring or autumn. Each division should have at least one growing bud and root section.
- **Seed:** Possible but rarely used — germination is irregular and slow (30–90 days with cold stratification). *S. officinale* seeds are less commonly available than the sterile hybrid *S. × uplandicum* cultivar 'Bocking 14'.

### Bocking Cultivars
Developed by Lawrence D. Hills at the Henry Doubleday Research Association (HDRA) in the 1950s–1960s. The Bocking series are selections of *S. × uplandicum* (Russian comfrey):

| Cultivar | Potency | Disease Resistance | Notes |
|----------|---------|-------------------|-------|
| **Bocking 4** | Moderate | Good | Higher potash content, good for mulch |
| **Bocking 14** | High | Excellent | Most widely grown. Sterile (no seed production), highest protein and nutrient content |
| **Bocking 1** | Lower | Moderate | Earlier cropper but less productive overall |

Bocking 14 is the gold standard for permaculture applications: it is sterile (preventing unwanted spread), disease-resistant, and produces the highest biomass of any comfrey cultivar.

### Growing Conditions
| Parameter | Ideal Range |
|-----------|-------------|
| Hardiness | USDA zones 3–9 |
| Soil pH | 6.0–7.5 (tolerates 5.0–8.0) |
| Light | Full sun to partial shade |
| Water | Moderate — drought-tolerant once established due to deep roots |
| Soil | Tolerates most soils including heavy clay; prefers moisture-retentive, fertile ground |
| Spacing | 60–90 cm between plants |

### Harvest
Comfrey can be cut 4–6 times per growing season. First cut at ~15 cm height, subsequent cuts when regrowth reaches 30–60 cm. Average annual yield: 2–5 kg fresh matter per plant, or 0.4–1.0 kg dried. Yields peak from year 2 onward.

## Permaculture Applications

### Dynamic Nutrient Accumulation
Comfrey is the quintessential "dynamic accumulator" in permaculture design. Its deep taproot mines nutrients from subsoil layers inaccessible to shallow-rooted plants and concentrates them in its leaves:

| Nutrient | Concentration in Dry Leaves | Comparison to Manure |
|----------|---------------------------|---------------------|
| Nitrogen (N) | 1.8–3.7% | Comparable to poultry manure |
| Potassium (K) | 2.0–3.5% | Higher than farmyard manure |
| Phosphorus (P) | 0.3–0.8% | Moderate |
| Calcium (Ca) | 1.5–3.0% | Very high |
| Trace minerals | Significant | Contains B, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, Mg |

This nutrient profile makes comfrey leaves equivalent to a complete organic fertilizer when properly utilized.

### Comfrey Tea (Liquid Fertilizer)
The primary method for utilizing comfrey's accumulated nutrients:

1. Stuff a container with comfrey leaves (no seeds or stems if possible)
2. Weigh down with a brick or stone
3. Fill with water to cover (ratio ~1:10 leaves:water by weight)
4. Cover loosely (the fermentation smell is extremely pungent — sulfurous)
5. Steep for 3–6 weeks
6. Dilute 1:10 with water before applying to plants

The resulting liquid is nitrogen-rich and excellent for heavy-feeding crops (tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, corn, fruit trees). Apply every 2 weeks during the growing season.

### Mulch
Fresh comfrey leaves can be laid directly around plants as a nutrient-rich mulch. They break down rapidly (2–4 weeks), releasing nutrients and adding organic matter. Particularly effective around:
- Fruit trees and berry bushes (potassium boost for fruit development)
- Tomatoes and peppers (prevents blossom end rot via calcium)
- Potatoes (promotes tuber development via potassium)

**Warning:** Comfrey leaves contain pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) that can be absorbed by root crops grown through comfrey mulch. Avoid using comfrey mulch around root vegetables intended for food (carrots, potatoes, radishes).

### Compost Accelerator
Comfrey leaves added to compost piles dramatically accelerate decomposition due to their high nitrogen content and moisture. Layer comfrey between carbon-rich materials (brown leaves, cardboard, straw) at a ratio of approximately 1:5 (comfrey:carbon). The leaves also introduce beneficial microorganisms and retain moisture in the compost pile.

### Companion Planting
- **Fruit trees:** Comfrey planted in the drip line of fruit trees mines subsoil nutrients and provides potassium-rich mulch
- **Comfrey understory in food forests:** Functions as a ground cover, nutrient cycler, and pollinator attractant in layered polyculture systems
- **Pest deterrence:** The coarse, hairy leaves may deter some slug species; comfrey traps slugs (they feed on leaves instead of target crops)
- **Nutrient buffer zones:** Planted between heavy feeders and light feeders to cycle nutrients from soil to surface

### Bee Forage
Comfrey flowers are exceptionally rich in nectar and produce high-quality honey. Flowering period extends over 6–8 weeks, providing critical forage during the early summer "June gap" when other nectar sources may be scarce. The flowers are accessible to both short-tongued and long-tongued bee species.

### Fodder
Historically used as livestock fodder (particularly for pigs and poultry). The high protein content (up to 30% of dry matter) makes it nutritionally comparable to lucerne/alfalfa. **Important:** PAs make comfrey fodder controversial; it is not recommended for regular livestock consumption. Occasional, small amounts for poultry are generally considered acceptable by permaculture practitioners.

## Medicinal Use

### Historical Applications
Comfrey has one of the longest documented histories of medicinal use in European herbalism, dating back to Dioscorides (1st century CE) and Pliny the Elder:

- **Bone and wound healing:** Applied as poultices to fractures, sprains, and wounds — the allantoin stimulates cell proliferation and tissue repair
- **Anti-inflammatory:** External applications for arthritis, bursitis, gout, and tendonitis
- **Skin conditions:** Eczema, psoriasis, burns, rashes, and ulcerations
- **Gastrointestinal:** Historically used internally for ulcers, gastritis, and diarrhea (now contraindicated due to hepatotoxicity concerns)

### Active Constituents
- **Allantoin (0.3–1.0%):** Cell proliferant and wound-healing agent. Stimulates fibroblast production, accelerates granulation tissue formation, and promotes epithelialization. This is the primary wound-healing compound.
- **Rosmarinic acid:** Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant. Inhibits 5-lipoxygenase and cyclooxygenase.
- **Mucilage (up to 30% of root dry weight):** Demulcent, soothes irritated mucous membranes and skin
- **Tannins:** Astringent, reduce bleeding and tighten tissues
- **Symphytine and other PAs (0.01–0.3%):** Hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids — the primary safety concern

### Safety Warning — Pyrrolizidine Alkaloids (PAs)
**This is the critical safety consideration for comfrey use.**

Comfrey contains hepatotoxic PAs (primarily symphytine, echimidine, and lycopsamine). These compounds are:
- **Hepatotoxic:** Cause hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD), a potentially fatal liver condition
- **Carcinogenic:** Classified as possible human carcinogens (IARC Group 2B)
- **Mutagenic:** DNA cross-linking and adduct formation demonstrated in vitro
- **Cumulative:** Damage is dose-dependent and accumulates over time with repeated exposure

**Current consensus:**
- **External use only:** Topical application to intact skin is generally considered safe, as PAs are poorly absorbed through unbroken skin. Avoid on open wounds, broken skin, or large surface areas.
- **Internal use NOT recommended:** Oral consumption of comfrey is contraindicated due to well-documented hepatotoxicity. The German Commission E, Health Canada, and most regulatory bodies have withdrawn approval for internal comfrey products.
- **PA-free preparations:** Some commercial comfrey preparations use PA-free extracts (cultivated under controlled conditions, or PA-removed). These are safer but not universally available.
- **Vulnerable populations:** Avoid all comfrey use during pregnancy, lactation, in children, and in individuals with liver disease.

## Ecological Notes

- **Invasiveness:** *S. officinale* can spread aggressively via root fragments. Any piece of root left in the soil can regenerate. The sterile Bocking 14 cultivar prevents seed spread but does not prevent vegetative spread.
- **Ecosystem role:** Provides early-season nectar, supports soil microfauna, cycles deep nutrients to surface, adds significant biomass to soil organic matter
- **Wildlife value:** Pollinator support, some moth caterpillars feed on leaves, ground beetles use the dense leaf canopy for shelter
- **Soil building:** Comfrey roots create deep channels in compacted soils, improving water infiltration and aeration. Decomposed root channels persist for years, improving soil structure
