# Comfrey (*Symphytum officinale*)

**Comfrey** is one of the most valuable and versatile plants in permaculture design, herbal medicine, and organic gardening. Renowned for its deep taproot, prolific biomass production, and dynamic nutrient accumulation, comfrey has earned the title "queen of the permaculture garden" among sustainable agriculture practitioners. The genus name *Symphytum* derives from Greek *symphyein* (to unite), referencing its traditional wound-healing applications. Its common name comes from Latin *con firma* (with strength), reflecting the plant's robust nature.

## Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Clade:** Angiosperms
- **Clade:** Eudicots
- **Clade:** Boraginales
- **Family:** Boraginaceae (borage family)
- **Genus:** *Symphytum*
- **Species:** *S. officinale* (common comfrey)

The genus *Symphytum* contains approximately 35 species native to Europe and Asia. The most commonly cultivated species include:

- ***Symphytum officinale*** — common comfrey (traditional medicinal species)
- ***Symphytum × uplandicum*** — Russian comfrey (S. officinale × S. asperum hybrid; most common permaculture variety, particularly the 'Bocking 14' cultivar)
- ***Symphytum asperum*** — prickly comfrey
- ***Symphytum tuberosum*** — tuberous comfrey (less invasive, lower biomass)

## Botanical Description

Comfrey is a robust, herbaceous perennial with the following characteristics:

- **Height:** 60–150 cm (2–5 ft), depending on variety and growing conditions
- **Spread:** Can form clumps 60–120 cm wide
- **Root system:** Extensive, deep-reaching taproot that can extend 1.5–3 m (5–10 ft) into the subsoil. The thick, black, branching roots are extremely persistent and can regenerate from the smallest fragment.
- **Stems:** Hairy, winged (especially in Russian comfrey), hollow, erect
- **Leaves:** Large (15–60 cm long), broadly lanceolate to ovate, rough-hairy (bristly), arranged in a rosette at the base and alternating up the stem. The upper leaves clasp the stem (decurrent).
- **Flowers:** Bell-shaped (campanulate), borne in coiled cymes (scorpioid inflorescences) that uncurl as flowers open. Color varies by species:
  - *S. officinale:* Cream-white to pale yellow (sometimes purplish)
  - *S. × uplandicum:* Blue-violet to purple
  - *S. asperum:* Blue-purple
- **Fruit:** Four nutlets (schizocarps), glossy black when mature
- **Blooming period:** May to August (Northern Hemisphere)

## Permaculture Applications

### Dynamic Nutrient Accumulation

Comfrey's most celebrated property in permaculture is its role as a **dynamic accumulator** — a plant that mines nutrients from deep soil layers and brings them to the surface in its biomass:

| Nutrient | Comfrey Leaf Content (dry weight) | Comparison |
|----------|----------------------------------|------------|
| **Nitrogen (N)** | 1.8–8.0% | Comparable to animal manure |
| **Potassium (K)** | 1.2–3.2% | Higher than farmyard manure |
| **Phosphorus (P)** | 0.3–1.0% | Moderate |
| **Calcium (Ca)** | 2.0–4.0% | Very high |
| **Magnesium (Mg)** | 0.3–0.5% | Moderate |
| **Iron (Fe)** | Trace | Significant micronutrient contribution |
| **Silica (Si)** | Trace | Plant structural support |

The deep taproot accesses minerals and nutrients that have leached beyond the reach of shallow-rooted crops, effectively "mining" the subsoil. When comfrey biomass is cut and used as mulch or compost, these nutrients are returned to the topsoil in an accessible form.

### Biomass Production

Russian comfrey (*S. × uplandicum*, particularly 'Bocking 14') is extraordinarily productive:

- **4–5 cuts per year** are possible in temperate climates
- **Yield:** 2–5 kg (wet weight) per plant per cut; 10–20+ kg total per plant per year
- **Dry weight ratio:** Approximately 10:1 (wet to dry)
- **Establishment:** Plants are productive from year 1, reaching maximum yield by year 3
- **Lifespan:** 20+ years once established

### Uses in the Permaculture Garden
1. **Compost activator:** Comfrey leaves added to compost piles provide nitrogen and moisture, dramatically accelerating decomposition. The high potassium content makes comfrey compost especially valuable for fruiting and flowering plants.
2. **Comfrey tea (liquid fertilizer):** Leaves are steeped in water for 2–6 weeks to produce a concentrated liquid feed. This "comfrey tea" is rich in potassium and is an excellent feed for tomatoes, potatoes, peppers, and fruiting crops. The resulting liquid has a distinctive and potent odor.
3. **Mulch:** Freshly cut comfrey leaves can be laid directly around plants as a nutrient-rich mulch. They break down rapidly, releasing nutrients and suppressing weeds. Particularly beneficial around hungry crops like tomatoes, currants, and fruit trees.
4. **Chop-and-drop green manure:** In food forest and agroforestry systems, comfrey is cut and dropped in place as a living mulch beneath fruit trees and berry bushes, cycling nutrients within the system.
5. **Pest barrier:** Some gardeners interplant comfrey as a sacrificial trap crop for slugs, which are attracted to the large leaves but may leave nearby crops alone.
6. **Bee forage:** Comfrey flowers are excellent nectar sources for bumblebees and long-tongued bees. The long tubular flowers are particularly favored by Bombus species. Blooming extends over several weeks, providing sustained forage.
7. **Erosion control:** The extensive root system stabilizes soil on banks and slopes.

### The 'Bocking 14' Cultivar

Developed by Lawrence D. Hills at the Henry Doubleday Research Association (HDRA) in the 1950s–60s, 'Bocking 14' is the gold standard comfrey for permaculture use:

- **Sterile hybrid:** Does not produce viable seed, eliminating the risk of uncontrolled spread (a significant concern with common comfrey)
- **High biomass:** Selected for maximum leaf production
- **High protein:** Selected for nutritional content
- **Disease resistant:** More resistant to comfrey rust (Melampsorella symphyti) than other varieties
- **Propagation:** Only by root cuttings (crown divisions or root pieces), which is both a limitation and a safety feature

## Cultivation

### Propagation

- **Root cuttings:** The primary method. Pieces of root 2–5 cm long planted horizontally 5–10 cm deep. Each piece will generate a new plant.
- **Crown divisions:** Established plants can be divided in spring or autumn.
- **Offsets:** Side shoots from established crowns.
- **Seed:** Only for fertile species (not 'Bocking 14'). Sow in spring, germination in 2–4 weeks.

### Growing Conditions

- **Soil:** Extremely adaptable — tolerates clay, loam, and sandy soils. Prefers moist, fertile soils but establishes in poor conditions due to its deep roots.
- **pH:** 6.0–7.5 (tolerates acidic to slightly alkaline)
- **Light:** Full sun to partial shade. Produces more biomass in full sun.
- **Water:** Moderate to high water needs for maximum biomass production. Drought-tolerant once established (deep taproot accesses groundwater).
- **Hardiness:** USDA zones 3–9. Extremely cold-hardy; dies back to the crown in winter and regrows vigorously in spring.
- **Spacing:** 60–90 cm (2–3 ft) between plants

### Harvesting

- Begin harvesting once plants are well-established (second year after planting, or late first year from root cuttings)
- Cut 5–10 cm above the crown to avoid damaging the growing point
- Leave at least one growth flush per year for the plant to recover (avoid cutting in late autumn to allow winter root reserves to build)
- Wear gloves — the hairy leaves can cause skin irritation in sensitive individuals

## Medicinal Uses and Safety Concerns

### Traditional Uses

Comfrey has a 2,000+ year history of medicinal use:

- **Wound healing:** Applied topically for cuts, burns, bruises, and sprains (the name "knitbone" reflects this use)
- **Bone healing:** Traditional treatment for fractures (not as a replacement for proper medical care)
- **Anti-inflammatory:** Joint pain, arthritis, gout
- **Skin conditions:** Eczema, psoriasis, dermatitis
- **Digestive:** Historically taken internally for ulcers and gastritis

### Active Compounds

- **Allantoin:** Cell proliferant that promotes tissue regeneration and wound healing
- **Rosmarinic acid:** Anti-inflammatory, antioxidant
- **Mucilage:** Soothing, protective (demulcent)
- **Tannins:** Astringent
- **Vitamin B12:** Trace amounts (one of few plant sources, though not nutritionally significant)

### Pyrrolizidine Alkaloid (PA) Warning

**CRITICAL SAFETY CONCERN:** Comfrey contains hepatotoxic **pyrrolizidine alkaloids** (PAs), primarily symphytine, symviridine, and intermedine. These compounds can cause:

- **Hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD):** Potentially fatal liver damage
- **Hepatocellular carcinoma:** Liver cancer risk with chronic exposure
- **Pulmonary hypertension:** Lung damage

PAs are absorbed through the skin to a limited degree, but the primary risk is from **oral ingestion**. As a result:

- **Internal use is NOT recommended** by most regulatory agencies (FDA, EMA, MHRA)
- **Topical use on intact skin** is considered low-risk for short-term application (the alkaloids are poorly absorbed through unbroken skin)
- **Avoid topical use on broken skin or open wounds** — PAs can be absorbed systemically
- **Do NOT use during pregnancy or breastfeeding**
- **PA-free comfrey cultivars** have been developed but are not widely available

Despite these concerns, comfrey remains widely used in herbal medicine, with topical preparations available in many countries (often labeled with appropriate warnings). The traditional use of comfrey root (which contains higher PA concentrations than leaves) internally has been largely abandoned in evidence-based herbalism.

## Companion Planting

Comfrey associates well with:

- **Fruit trees:** Planted in the drip line as a nutrient-cycling companion
- **Berry bushes:** Similar benefits; comfrey mulch feeds shallow-rooted berries
- **Nitrogen-fixing plants:** Combinations with comfrey, legumes, and fruit trees form classic permaculture guilds
- **Potatoes and tomatoes:** Comfrey mulch provides potassium these crops demand

Comfrey should NOT be planted near:

- **Shallow-rooted crops** that compete for surface moisture
- **Areas where you may want to eradicate it later** — the root system is nearly impossible to fully remove

## Invasive Potential

Common comfrey (*S. officinale*) can become aggressively invasive through seed dispersal and root fragmentation. Even small root pieces left in soil can regenerate. This is why the sterile 'Bocking 14' cultivar is strongly preferred in permaculture — it provides all the benefits without the invasive risk.
