# Comfrey

## Description

Comfrey (*Symphytum* spp.) is a genus of perennial flowering plants in the family Boraginaceae, native to Europe and Asia. The most commonly cultivated species is *Symphytum × uplandicum* (Russian comfrey), a naturally occurring hybrid valued in permaculture and organic gardening as a dynamic accumulator, medicinal herb, and biomass producer. Comfrey forms large clumps of broad, hairy leaves from a deep taproot system, producing clusters of bell-shaped flowers in shades of purple, blue, pink, or white.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Boraginaceae
- **Genus**: Symphytum
- **Notable species**: *S. × uplandicum* (Russian comfrey), *S. officinale* (common comfrey), *S. asperum* (rough comfrey)

## Key Facts

- Deep taproot system extends 2–3 meters into the subsoil, mining minerals (potassium, calcium, phosphorus, silica, nitrogen) and bringing them to the surface via leaf biomass.
- Can be cut 4–5 times per year in temperate climates, producing 15–30 kg of fresh leaf matter per plant annually.
- Leaves contain allantoin, a compound that promotes cell proliferation and wound healing, giving comfrey its traditional medicinal name "knitbone."
- Nitrogen content of leaves approaches 3–4% on a dry-weight basis; potassium content is among the highest of any temperate plant.
- Rarely sets viable seed (especially the sterile Bocking cultivars), making it non-invasive by seed dispersal.
- Attracts pollinators, particularly bumblebees, during its extended flowering period.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Mollison lists comfrey as a special mulch plant for tropical areas, grown specifically to provide nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium:

- **Dynamic accumulator**: Comfrey's deep roots access minerals unavailable to shallow-rooted crops, making it an ideal "nutrient pump" in permaculture designs. When leaves are cut and used as mulch, these minerals become available to surrounding plants.
- **Liquid fertilizer**: Leaves can be steeped in water for 2–4 weeks to produce a potent liquid plant feed rich in potassium and nitrogen, particularly beneficial for fruiting crops like tomatoes.
- **Compost activator**: High nitrogen content accelerates decomposition in compost piles, functioning as a "hot" compost ingredient.
- **Weed barrier**: Dense growth habit and large leaves shade out weeds, making comfrey effective as a living border or weed-suppressing ground cover around garden beds.
- **Animal fodder**: Historically used as high-protein feed for poultry, pigs, and cattle, though modern use has decreased due to pyrrolizidine alkaloid concerns.

## Ecological Considerations

- Contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) in roots and to a lesser extent in leaves; internal medicinal use is now discouraged due to potential liver toxicity.
- Very persistent once established; the deep taproot makes comfrey difficult to eradicate completely from a site.
- The Bocking-14 cultivar is the most widely planted sterile variety, ensuring it stays where placed.

## Cultivation Notes

- Propagated by root cuttings (crown divisions or root segments); Bocking cultivars must be vegetatively propagated as they are sterile.
- Plant in permanent positions at 60–90 cm spacing; comfrey will persist for decades.
- Cut leaves to 5 cm above ground when plants reach 60 cm tall; regrowth is rapid.
- Prefers moist, fertile, well-drained soils but tolerates a wide range of conditions including partial shade.
- Top-dress with compost or manure annually to maintain vigorous growth.
