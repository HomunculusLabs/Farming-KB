## Overview

Fritillaria meleagris, the snake's head fritillary, is a spring-flowering bulbous perennial in the family Liliaceae. It is celebrated for its distinctive checkerboard-patterned nodding flowers in shades of purple, pink, and white.

Native to damp meadows and river valleys across Europe, the species has declined dramatically in the wild due to agricultural intensification and drainage of wet grasslands. It is now a conservation priority in several European countries.

The common name refers to the flower's resemblance to a snake's head when in bud. The species is the county flower of Oxfordshire and the provincial flower of Uppland in Sweden.

Flowering typically occurs from late March through mid-May, making it one of the earliest spring bulbs in damp habitats. Peak bloom lasts approximately 2–3 weeks depending on spring temperatures.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Fritillaria meleagris was described by Linnaeus in 1753 in Species Plantarum. The genus name derives from the Latin fritillus (dice box), referencing the checkered pattern. The specific epithet meleagris refers to the guineafowl, also patterned.

The species is hexaploid with 2n = 36, unusual within a genus where most species are diploid. Phylogenetic analysis places it in the subgenus Fritillaria, section Fritillaria.

Bulbs are small, globose, 2–3 cm in diameter, composed of few fleshy white scales. Stems reach 20–40 cm tall, slender, and bear 3–5 narrowly lanceolate grey-green leaves in an alternate arrangement.

Flowers are solitary (occasionally paired), broadly campanulate and nodding, measuring 3–5 cm across. Tepals display the characteristic dark purple-pink checkered pattern on a lighter ground.

A pure white form, Fritillaria meleagris var. unicolor subvar. alba, occurs naturally at low frequency in wild populations. It lacks anthocyanin pigmentation entirely and is popular in cultivation.

Hardiness spans USDA zones 4–8. The species requires a cold winter dormancy and does not perform well in areas with mild, frost-free winters. Snow cover provides beneficial insulation.

Best flowering occurs in full sun to light dappled shade. Plants grown in deep shade produce fewer flowers and gradually weaken. Companion plantings with Primula, Caltha, and Cardamine create naturalistic damp meadow effects.

## Uses

Fritillaria meleagris is primarily an ornamental plant, valued for naturalizing in damp meadows, lawn edges, and alongside streams. The checkered flowers provide unique early-season interest in spring gardens.

The species has limited medicinal history compared to other Fritillaria species used in traditional Chinese medicine. European herbal traditions occasionally used bulb preparations as expectorants, though this use is not well documented.

The genus Fritillaria contains various steroidal alkaloids with pharmacological activity. Fritillaria meleagris contains lower concentrations than the Chinese species Fritillaria cirrhosa and Fritillaria thunbergii used in TCM.

In the cut flower trade, stems are sold as a specialty spring item. Their short vase life of 3–5 days limits commercial use but they are prized for high-end spring arrangements and wedding work.

The plant has appeared in English [[fly-agaric-in-victorian-literature-and-art]], notably in illustrations by Stella Ross-Craig and in the poetry of Gerard Manley Hopkins. The checkerboard pattern has inspired textile and ceramic designs.
