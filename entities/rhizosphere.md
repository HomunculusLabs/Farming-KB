## Rhizosphere

## Description

The rhizosphere is the narrow zone of soil immediately surrounding plant roots, extending outward approximately one-tenth of an inch (a couple of millimeters) from the root surface. It is one of the most biologically active microenvironments on Earth, serving as the primary interface between plants and the soil food web. Under an electron microscope, the rhizosphere can appear jelly-like or jam-like due to the dense accumulation of root exudates, microbial secretions, and cellular debris.

- **Domain:** Soil science / Plant-microbe interactions

## Key Facts

- The rhizosphere contains a constantly changing mix of soil organisms including bacteria, fungi, nematodes, protozoa, and larger organisms all competing for exudates, water, and minerals.
- Plants secrete carbohydrates (including sugars) and proteins as root exudates into the rhizosphere, which attract and sustain specific beneficial bacteria and fungi.
- As root tips grow, cells are sloughed off into the rhizosphere, providing additional organic matter for microbial consumption.
- Nutrient cycling reaches its peak efficiency in the rhizosphere: protozoa and nematodes consume bacteria and fungi for carbon, excreting excess nitrogen and other nutrients as waste in plant-available forms directly where roots can absorb them.
- A teaspoon of rhizosphere soil can contain over a billion bacteria, several yards of fungal hyphae, several thousand protozoa, and a few dozen nematodes.
- The rhizosphere functions analogously to perspiration in humans — exudates are the plant"s way of "sweating" carbon compounds to recruit a beneficial microbiome.
- Plants can control the composition of their rhizosphere community by varying the types of exudates they produce across different stages of the growing season.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The rhizosphere is central to understanding how plants obtain nutrients without chemical fertilizers. In cultivation and mycology, the rhizosphere concept explains why living soil systems outperform sterile or heavily fertilized media. Mycorrhizal fungi establish their symbiotic relationships within the rhizosphere, extending the plant's nutrient-foraging capacity far beyond the root zone. Fungal-dominated rhizospheres support perennials, trees, and shrubs, while bacterial-dominated rhizospheres favor annuals, grasses, and vegetables. Managing the rhizosphere biology through compost, mulch, and minimal disturbance is foundational to organic cultivation and soil food web gardening.
