## Description

Acaulospora laevis is a species of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus in the family Acaulosporaceae. It is one of the most studied species within the genus Acaulospora and is widely recognized for its role in forming symbiotic associations with the roots of a broad range of host plants, particularly herbaceous species and grasses. Unlike Glomus species, Acaulospora laevis produces characteristic acaulosporoid spores that develop laterally from a hyphal neck, and it is distinguished taxonomically by spore wall morphology.

## Key Facts

- Acaulospora laevis forms arbuscular mycorrhizal associations, penetrating host root cortical cells and developing arbuscules at the plant-fungus interface for nutrient exchange.
- Research by Clark and Zeto (2000), cited in Dighton, demonstrated that A. laevis has extraradical hyphal extension rates of approximately 20 mm per week, which is among the fastest in [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]].
- This rapid hyphal growth allows the fungus to explore a significantly larger soil volume than many Glomus species (which extend less than 10 mm per week), enhancing phosphorus uptake for the host plant.
- The depletion zone around roots colonized by A. laevis can extend up to 110 mm from the root surface, substantially greater than the 10-20 mm typical of non-mycorrhizal roots or those colonized by slower-growing AMF species.
- The genus Acaulospora is identified primarily by the anatomy of its spores, which are produced within or outside the host root. A. laevis spores are typically hyaline to pale yellow and globose to subglobose.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Acaulospora laevis is of considerable interest in agriculture and horticulture because of its efficiency in phosphorus acquisition. Its fast-growing extraradical hyphae make it a strong candidate for inoculum in low-phosphorus soils, where nutrient accessibility limits crop productivity. Understanding the differences in hyphal extension rates between Acaulospora and Glomus species helps inform selection of appropriate mycorrhizal inoculants for specific soil and crop conditions.

In ecological research, A. laevis serves as a model organism for studying the mechanics of phosphorus depletion zones and the cost-benefit dynamics of the [[mycorrhizal-symbiosis]]. Its relatively rapid soil exploration capacity illustrates how different AMF species contribute differently to [[plant-nutrient-uptake]], with implications for plant community structure and competition.

The species also contributes to soil aggregation through glomalin production and hyphal enmeshment of soil particles, linking its ecological role to both nutrient cycling and soil stability. In restoration ecology, A. laevis may be particularly valuable for re-establishing [[mycorrhizal-networks]] in degraded or disturbed soils where rapid hyphal colonization confers a competitive advantage.
