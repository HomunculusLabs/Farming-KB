# Community Barter Club

## Description

The Community Barter Club is a local exchange system described by [[bill-mollison]] in *[[permaculture]]: A Designers' Manual* as a practical mechanism for community-level [[informal-economy-and-community-currencies]]. Unlike simple one-to-one barter, the Community Barter Club uses a multi-lateral credit and debit system where members can exchange a wide range of goods and services without needing direct reciprocal exchanges or conventional currency. The system allows a member who provides a service to one person to receive a completely different service from a different person, with all exchanges tracked through a central accounting system managed by a club organiser. Mollison presents this as a key strategy for enabling communities to meet their needs independently of the formal financial system.

## Classification

- **Category:** Community organisation / Multi-lateral barter system
- **Domain:** [[permaculture-community-design]] economics, informal economy
- **Related concepts:** [[let-system]], time banking, mutual credit, informal economy
- **Scale:** Community or neighbourhood level (optimal: 30-600 members)

## Key Facts

- Membership is open to community residents and operates at neighbourhood or community scale
- One credit equals one hour of service, with value agreed upon by donor and recipient for each transaction
- Members offer goods, services, and skills across a broad spectrum from landscaping to massage, mowing to printing
- A Club secretary or organiser maintains records of all debits and credits, and is themselves paid in credits
- People are not limited to one-to-one swaps; the organiser's records enable complex multi-party exchanges across many members
- No cash is required for transactions as long as services are desired and members have credit available
- Donor and recipient agree among themselves what a job is worth in credit hours, providing flexible and context-sensitive pricing
- The system particularly serves people who are unemployed, underemployed, or who need services but cannot afford cash
- The organiser role keeps the system running smoothly and is compensated through the same credit mechanism

## Operational Model

The Community Barter Club operates through six key processes:

1. **Registration:** Residents join and list the goods, services, and skills they can offer to other members
2. **Cataloguing:** A directory or newsletter lists all available offerings for members to browse and request
3. **Matching:** Members contact each other directly or through the organiser to arrange specific exchanges
4. **Transaction:** The service is performed; donor and recipient mutually agree on the credit value
5. **Recording:** The organiser records the transaction, debiting the recipient and crediting the donor in the central ledger
6. **Continuity:** Credits earned can be spent with any other member across the full range of offerings available

## Informal Economy Context

Mollison places the Community Barter Club within the broader informal economy, which also includes purely volunteer labour, exchanges of gifts, and taking responsibility for specific community projects or areas. For example, convivial tree-planting on community common areas should be part of every household's responsibilities. The Barter Club sits between pure gift economy and formal business transactions, providing structure without the overhead of conventional commerce.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The Community Barter Club model has direct applications for cultivation and [[mycology]] communities:

- Experienced cultivators can trade consulting, culture isolation, or identification services for substrate materials or equipment
- Multiple growers can exchange harvest help, inoculation assistance, or facility maintenance across different growing seasons
- Equipment such as laminar flow hoods, sterilizers, or microscopes can be offered as services charged by the hour
- Mushroom cultivators can trade fresh or dried products for vegetables, [[compost]], woodworking, or other goods they need
- Members with dehydrators, extraction equipment, or packaging capabilities can offer processing services for credits
- Experienced mycologists can offer training credits redeemable for materials, labour, or future workshop participation

## Design Principles

1. **Use and value diversity** — supports the widest possible range of goods and services within the community
2. **Use edges and [[permaculture-principle-use-edges-and-value-the-marginal]]** — specifically serves those excluded from the formal economy
3. **[[permaculture-integrate-rather-than-segregate]] segregate** — connects diverse skills and needs into one unified exchange system
4. **[[holmgren-produce-no-waste-waste-hierarchy-refuse-reduce-reuse-repair-recycle]]** — transforms unused time and skills into valuable exchanges that benefit all parties

## Implementation Notes

- A dedicated organiser or secretary is essential for record-keeping and coordination of multi-lateral exchanges
- The system works best in communities where members know each other personally (optimal range: 30-600 people)
- Credit valuation by mutual agreement provides flexibility but requires a foundation of trust among members
- Regular newsletters or computerised directories help members discover available services and initiate exchanges
- The model can be layered with other systems such as LET systems and cooperatives for greater economic resilience

## Critical Assessment

The Community Barter Club model, while powerful, depends heavily on the quality and dedication of the organiser role. If the organiser leaves or becomes unreliable, the entire record-keeping system can collapse. Unlike the LET System which uses computerised central accounting, the Barter Club model as described by Mollison relies more heavily on a single human coordinator. Communities implementing this model should consider redundancy in the organiser role, regular backup of accounting records, and clear succession planning. The system also requires a minimum threshold of active members to provide enough diversity of offerings to be useful — too few members means too few services available, leading to a downward spiral of disengagement.
