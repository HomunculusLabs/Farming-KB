# Marupa Sorcery

## Description

Marupa sorcery (hechicería marupa) is a form of harmful magical practice in Amazonian vegetalismo in which the sorcerer keeps miniature animals — insects, snakes, scorpions, bats, rays, toads, and other creatures — inside their [[mariri]] (magical phlegm) and sends them to attack victims. The Cocama ethnic group is specifically associated with this form of sorcery. The sorcerer plans the attack in advance through visionary experience, then makes the victim feel compelled to go to a location where the magical animal will strike.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Amazonian vegetalismo / mestizo [[shamanism]]
- **Category**: Sorcery technique, animal-based attack
- **Practitioners**: Primarily Cocama sorcerers
- **Weapon**: Miniature magical animals stored in mariri

## Key Facts

- The Cocama sorcerer manipulates marupas — animal forms including insects, snakes, scorpions, bats, rays, and toads — stored in the mariri and dispatched to cause specific harm to targeted victims.
- The attack is premeditated through [[amaringo-ayahuasca-visions-shipibo-iconography]]: the sorcerer plans exactly how to proceed, then uses compulsion to make the victim go to a specific location where the magical animal waits.
- Diagnosis is through ayahuasca: the curandero observes larger waves in the visionary light surrounding the patient, distinguishing marupa sorcery from chonta damage (zigzagging lights) and [[huani-sorcery]] (small waves crossed by dark lines).
- Marupa sorcery is treatable by skilled vegetalistas, unlike huani sorcery which is considered incurable.
- The snakes used by sorcerers include yana-machaco, nacanaca (Micrurus sp.), urillo, tinga, paiche machacuy, tahuampa shushupi (Lachesis muta), jergón (Bothrops atrox), and iguana machacuy — a taxonomy of actual venomous species that suggests the magical practice [[maps]] onto real envenomation knowledge.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Marupa sorcery's use of actual venomous species as magical templates reflects detailed ethnozoological knowledge. The precise taxonomy of snake species listed (Micrurus, Lachesis muta, Bothrops atrox) demonstrates that Amazonian peoples maintain accurate classification systems for dangerous animals. This knowledge extends to understanding which snake venoms cause which symptoms, enabling the diagnostic distinction between sorcery types. For ethnopharmacological research, the plants used to treat marupa-type attacks (likely corresponding to actual envenomation) represent a valuable repository of anti-venom plant knowledge. The system also illustrates how indigenous peoples encode toxicological expertise in spiritual frameworks.
