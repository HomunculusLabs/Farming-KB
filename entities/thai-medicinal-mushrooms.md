# Thai Medicinal Mushrooms (Traditional Health Properties)

## Description

Thai traditional knowledge attributes specific health properties to various mushroom species, many of which are commercially cultivated in Thailand and Southeast Asia. The FAO training manual includes a table of traditional Thai health properties attributed to commonly cultivated and wild-harvested mushrooms, compiled from research at Chulalongkorn University. This knowledge base informs both dietary recommendations and market demand for specific mushroom species in Thai cuisine and traditional medicine.

## Classification

- **Category**: Traditional medicinal knowledge
- **Type**: Ethnomycological health properties
- **Source**: Dr. Sunan Pongsamart & staff, Biochemistry Department, Faculty of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Chulalongkorn University, Thailand
- **Region**: Thailand, Southeast Asia

## Key Facts

- Mushrooms provide high protein and essential amino acids
- Mushrooms are low in fat and high in fiber
- Regular mushroom consumption stimulates the immune system
- Eating 2-3 types of mushroom per day can provide sufficient essential amino acids for the body
- Normal adult consumption: 200-800 grams per day
- Elderly people: 200 grams sufficient
- Children: 500 grams sufficient
- The health properties listed are based on traditional Thai knowledge and ongoing scientific research

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The documented health properties of mushrooms serve multiple purposes in the FAO training program:

1. **Nutritional justification**: Demonstrates that mushroom farming addresses both income generation and nutritional security
2. **Market education**: Farmers can use health properties as marketing points when selling mushrooms
3. **Species selection**: Health properties may influence which species farmers choose to cultivate based on local demand
4. **Cultural integration**: Traditional Thai knowledge about [[medicinal-mushroom-health-benefits]] supports market acceptance and consumer demand

The nutritional data from Chulalongkorn University provides scientific backing for the health claims and supports the argument that mushroom cultivation contributes to food security in rural communities. For people with disabilities, the nutritional benefits of mushroom farming extend beyond income — they directly improve household nutrition and health.
