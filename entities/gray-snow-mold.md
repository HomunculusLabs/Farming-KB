# Gray Snow Mold

## Description

Gray snow mold is a cold-weather turfgrass disease caused by fungi in the genus *Typhula*. It develops under snow cover during winter months when conditions are cold, moist, and light-deprived. In the soil food web framework, gray snow mold can be suppressed by fungally dominated compost teas, which establish competitive fungal communities in the soil before winter sets in, preventing the pathogen from gaining dominance under snow cover.

## Classification

- **Causal organisms:** *Typhula* spp. (basidiomycete fungi)
- **Type:** Cold-weather turfgrass disease
- **Conditions:** Develops under prolonged snow cover in winter
- **Symptoms:** Circular, matted, grayish-white patches in turf after snow melt
- **Related disease:** Pink snow mold (*Microdochium* spp.)

## Key Facts

- Caused by *Typhula* species — basidiomycete fungi adapted to cold, wet conditions
- Develops under snow cover where light is absent and moisture is persistent
- Appears as circular patches of matted, grayish-white mycelium on turfgrass after snow melts
- Actively grows even at the snow-soil interface where temperatures warm enough for limited microbial activity
- Suppressed by fungally dominated compost teas that establish diverse fungal communities before winter
- The competitive fungi occupy ecological niches that *Typhula* would otherwise exploit
- Part of a group of snow mold diseases including pink snow mold (*Microdochium* spp.)
- Most damaging when snow falls on unfrozen ground and persists for extended periods
- Cultural controls include avoiding late-season nitrogen applications and mowing grass short before snowfall

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Gray snow mold illustrates several important mycological concepts:

- **Cold-adapted fungi:** *Typhula* species are psychrophilic (cold-loving) basidiomycetes that remain active at low temperatures when most other fungi are dormant
- **Competitive exclusion:** Pre-establishing diverse fungal communities through compost tea creates biological barriers that prevent *Typhula* colonization
- **Timing is critical:** Compost tea applications should be made in autumn before first snowfall to allow beneficial fungi to establish
- **Winter soil biology:** Even under snow cover, microbial activity continues at the soil-snow interface — the soil food web does not fully shut down in winter
- **Fungal diversity as defense:** A diverse fungal community provides more robust disease suppression than any single biocontrol agent
