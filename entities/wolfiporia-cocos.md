## Overview

**Wolfiporia cocos** (F.A. Wolf) Ryvarden & Gilb. is a wood-decay polypore fungus in the family Polyporaceae, notable for producing large underground sclerotia. It is one of the most important medicinal fungi in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), where the dried sclerotium is known as **Fuling** (茯苓) and has been used for over 2,000 years.

The fungus is a brown-rot decay organism that primarily attacks the roots and lower trunks of coniferous trees, especially species of **Pinus**. Unlike most polypores that produce visible fruiting bodies above ground, W. cocos forms massive subterranean sclerotia that can weigh over 20 kg.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Kingdom: Fungi, Phylum: Basidiomycota, Class: Agaricomycetes, Order: Polyporales, Family: Polyporaceae.

Genus authority: F.A. Wolf (1922). Current combination: Ryvarden & Gilb. (1987).

Previous names include **[[poria-cocos]]** (F.A. Wolf) Ryvarden & Gilb., which remains widely used in the pharmacological literature, and **Macrohyporia cocos** (Schwein.) I. Johans. & Ryvarden. The synonym Poria cocos is still the standard in TCM monographs and many pharmacology papers.

The sclerotium is irregularly shaped, roughly spherical to oblong, weighing 0.5–20 kg. The exterior surface is dark brown to black, rough, and deeply wrinkled. The interior flesh is white to pale pink, granular, and somewhat mealy when dried.

Fruiting bodies are rare in nature and infrequently observed. When formed, they are sessile to effused-reflexed, with a poroid hymenophore surface. Pores are circular, 3–5 per mm, with thin dissepiments. Basidiospores are cylindrical to slightly curved, hyaline, smooth, 6–9 × 2.5–3.5 µm.

The hyphal system is dimitic, with generative hyphae bearing clamp connections and skeletal hyphae that are thick-walled and unbranched. Amyloid reaction is negative in Melzer's reagent.

## Distribution and Habitat

W. cocos is found across subtropical and temperate regions worldwide. Its distribution spans much of East Asia (China, Japan, Korea), southeastern North America, and parts of [[lichens-primary-production-soil-formation-dighton]] areas are in Yunnan, Guizhou, Hubei, Anhui, and Zhejiang provinces.

The fungus is an obligate parasite and saprotroph on coniferous wood, showing strong preference for **Pinus massoniana** and **Pinus yunnanensis** in China, and various hard pines in the southeastern United States. It colonizes roots and extends into heartwood, causing a characteristic brown rot that leaves the remaining wood brittle and blocky.

Sclerotia develop at the interface between infected root tissue and surrounding soil. Formation typically requires 8–12 months under favorable conditions of adequate moisture, warm temperatures (20–28°C), and well-drained acidic soils (pH 4.5–6.5).

## Bioactive Compounds

The primary bioactive constituents of W. cocos sclerotia are **triterpenoids** of the lanostane type, with pachymic acid, dehydrotumulosic acid, and poricoic acids being the most studied. Over 40 triterpenoids have been isolated from various fractions.

**Polysaccharides** are the other major class of active compounds, particularly a water-soluble β-(1→3)-D-glucan with β-(1→6) branching side chains. This pachyman-type glucan is the primary immunomodulatory component.

Other constituents include ergosterol, ergosterol peroxide, choline, histidine, and various amino acids. The sclerotium also contains poricoic acids A through Z, tumulosic acid, and 3-epi-dehydropachymic acid.

## Pharmacology

In TCM, Fuling is classified as a diuretic, sedative, and spleen tonic. It is prescribed to promote urination, drain dampness, strengthen the spleen, and calm the spirit. It is a component of over 200 classical Chinese herbal formulas.

Modern pharmacological studies have demonstrated several activities. The triterpenoids show anti-inflammatory effects through inhibition of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways. Pachymic acid exhibits notable antitumor activity against various cancer cell lines, inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest.

The polysaccharide fraction demonstrates immunomodulatory activity, enhancing macrophage phagocytosis, stimulating NK cell activity, and promoting cytokine production. It has shown hepatoprotective effects in models of chemical-induced liver injury.

Anti-diabetic effects have been documented, with the polysaccharide fraction improving glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in animal models. Antioxidant activity is attributed to both triterpenoid and polysaccharide fractions.

## Cultivation

Commercial cultivation of W. cocos is a major agricultural industry in China, with annual production exceeding 10,000 tonnes. Traditional cultivation involves inoculating pine logs or root segments with spawn and burying them in prepared soil beds.

Modern methods use sawdust-based substrates supplemented with rice bran and mineral nutrients, contained in polypropylene bags or buried beds. Optimal colonization occurs at 22–26°C with 65–75% relative humidity and adequate ventilation.

Harvesting typically occurs 10–14 months after inoculation when sclerotia reach sufficient size and maturity. Yield ranges from 15–30% of substrate dry weight under controlled conditions. Post-harvest processing involves washing, slicing, and repeated steaming and drying cycles.

## Nutritional Profile

The dried sclerotium of W. cocos is composed primarily of carbohydrates (approximately 70–80% by dry weight), with the remainder being protein, lipids, and ash. The polysaccharide content is dominated by pachyman, a β-D-glucan that is largely insoluble in cold water but becomes water-soluble after alkaline treatment.

Protein content ranges from 2–4% dry weight, containing all essential amino acids with notable amounts of leucine, valine, and lysine. Lipid content is low at approximately 0.5–1.0%, composed mainly of free fatty acids including palmitic, oleic, and linoleic acids.

Mineral analysis reveals significant potassium, magnesium, and calcium content. Trace elements include zinc, iron, manganese, and copper. The ash content is typically 2–4% of dry weight.

## Ecological Role

W. cocos functions as a primary brown-rot decomposer in coniferous forest ecosystems. Its enzymatic system selectively degrades cellulose and hemicellulose while leaving lignin largely intact, producing the characteristic brown, cubical wood residue of brown rot.

The fungus plays a role in forest [[gadd-mycelial-network-dynamics]] connects multiple host trees and can influence soil [[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]] structure.

In its native range, W. cocos is parasitic on living pines, contributing to root disease complexes. However, its economic and ecological impact as a pathogen is considered minor compared to other root rot fungi such as Armillaria mellea and [[heterobasidion-annosum]].
