## [[rhizobia-legume-nodule-formation|legume]]-rhizobia partnership
- After leguminous plants are cut, the root zone continues releasing nitrogen for up to 6 years
- Essential for establishing nitrogen-fixing pioneer species in permaculture designs
- [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]] occurs in a series of zones extending from the roots into the surrounding soil
- Even after nitrogenous plants such as Acacia, Albizzia, and Eleagnus are cut, the root zone releases nitrogen

## Symbiotic Relationships

Rhizobia infect legume root hairs and trigger the formation of nodules where nitrogen fixation occurs. The plant provides carbon compounds (energy) to the bacteria, while the bacteria provide fixed nitrogen to the plant. Key partnerships include:

- **Rhizobium leguminosarum**: Peas, lentils, vetches, clovers
- **Bradyrhizobium japonicum**: Soybeans
- **Sinorhizobium meliloti**: Alfalfa, sweet clover
- **Mesorhizobium loti**: Lotus, crown vetch
- **Rhizobium spp.**: Acacia, Albizzia, and other tropical leguminous trees

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Soil Fertility
- Primary biological mechanism for introducing atmospheric nitrogen into soil food webs
- Leguminous [[forest-gardens]]
- Rhizobia-inoculated plants release nitrogen during their lifetimes and for years after
- Much higher levels of nitrogen than previously thought are fixed in root zones

### Permaculture Design Applications
- Legumes may not be needed in older forests and typically die out under canopy
- Only a few larger leguminous trees (Samanea, Acacia melanoxylon) persist in mixed forest
- Nitrogen-fixing pioneer species are essential for establishing food forests on depleted soils
- Inoculation with appropriate rhizobial strains is necessary when introducing legumes to new areas

### Inoculation Practices
- Commercial rhizobial inoculants are available for most major legume crops
- Seed coating with appropriate rhizobial strains ensures effective nodulation
- Soil that has previously grown the same legume species may already contain suitable rhizobia
- Inoculation is especially important when growing legumes in soils where they have not been grown before

### Mycological Connections
- Rhizobial nitrogen fixation supports fungal communities by enriching soil nitrogen
- Decomposing legume roots and nodules provide nitrogen-rich organic matter for fungal growth
- In [[outdoor-mushroom-cultivation|mushroom]] cultivation, spent legume substrates contribute nitrogen to compost formulations
- Rhizosphere interactions between rhizobia and mycorrhizal fungi create synergistic nutrient cycling

## Factors Affecting Nitrogen Fixation

- **pH**: Most rhizobia prefer neutral to slightly acidic soils (pH 6.0-7.0)
- **Temperature**: Optimal 25-30 degrees C; activity declines below 10 degrees C
- **Soil moisture**: Adequate moisture required; waterlogging inhibits most strains
- **Existing nitrogen**: High soil nitrogen levels suppress nodulation and fixation
- **Phosphorus**: Adequate P is essential for effective nodulation
- **Molybdenum**: Required as a cofactor for the nitrogenase enzyme
