# Boletus felleus

## Description

Boletus felleus (now reclassified as [[tylopilus-felleus]]), commonly known as the bitter bolete, is a large bolete mushroom that closely resembles the prized [[boletus-edulis-and-pore-fungi-polyporaceae]] (king bolete / porcini). The cap is smooth and brown, the pore surface is initially white becoming pinkish with age, and the stem is robust with a distinctive dark brown net-like pattern (reticulation). The key distinguishing feature is its extremely bitter taste, which makes it inedible though not poisonous. It is one of the most common causes of misidentification by hopeful porcini foragers and has ruined many otherwise fine mushroom dishes.

## Classification

- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Boletales
- **Family:** Boletaceae
- **Genus:** Tylopilus (formerly Boletus)
- **Species:** T. felleus (Bull.) Karsten
- **Common names:** Bitter bolete, bitter tylopilus

## Morphology

- **Cap:** 7-20 cm diameter, convex to flat; smooth, dry; brown to dark brown, sometimes with a grayish or yellowish tint
- **Pore surface:** White when young, becoming pink to pinkish-brown with age; does NOT bruise blue
- **Tubes:** White, becoming pinkish; long, depressed around the stem
- **Stem:** 6-12 cm tall, thick and robust; covered with a prominent dark brown reticulation (net pattern) that is coarser and darker than that of B. edulis
- **Flesh:** White, firm; does not change color when cut
- **Taste:** Extremely bitter, even a tiny piece on the tongue is unmistakable
- **[[boil-a-bag-spore-print-technique-jarrold]]:** Pinkish-brown to vinaceous-brown
- **Odor:** Not distinctive

## Key Facts

- Closely resembles Boletus edulis in overall appearance — brown cap, robust stature, reticulate stem — making it the most common bolete misidentification
- The pore surface is white when young, turning pink to pinkish-brown with age (unlike the white-to-yellowish pores of B. edulis)
- Extremely bitter taste that intensifies with cooking; even a single small specimen can ruin an entire dish with its bitterness
- Not poisonous but completely inedible due to overwhelming bitterness
- The reticulation (net pattern) on the stem is typically darker brown and more pronounced/coarse than the fine white reticulation of B. edulis
- Mycorrhizal with both hardwood and coniferous trees
- Found in similar habitats to B. edulis, increasing the risk of confusion
- One of the most frequently misidentified bolete species worldwide

## Historical Context

Julius A. Palmer specifically noted B. felleus as the one bolete he would consistently avoid. He listed it as the only species among those depicted on his "varieties to be avoided" plate that he still rejected after years of experience. All other bolete species on that plate he had come to use as food. This is significant because it demonstrates that even an adventurous and experienced forager like Palmer respected this species' inedibility.

Palmer's discussion of B. felleus serves as an early example of two important mycological principles: that appearance alone can be misleading in bolete identification, and that taste testing (a small piece on the tongue, not swallowed) is a valuable and sometimes essential diagnostic tool for distinguishing similar-looking species.

## Ecology and Distribution

- Ectomycorrhizal with both hardwoods (oaks, beech) and conifers (pine, spruce)
- Found in forests, woodland paths, and clearings
- Prefers acidic soils
- Widespread across Europe and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-north-america]]
- Season: Summer to late autumn
- Often fruits in the same habitats as B. edulis, leading to frequent confusion

## Distinguishing from Boletus edulis

| Feature | B. felleus (Bitter Bolete) | B. edulis (King Bolete) |
|---------|---------------------------|------------------------|
| Pore surface | White → pink | White → yellowish |
| Stem reticulation | Dark brown, coarse | White, fine |
| Taste | Extremely bitter | Mild, nutty |
| Flesh color change | None | None |
| Spore print | Pinkish-brown | Olive-brown |

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Misidentification risk:** The single most common bolete misidentification globally; often confused with B. edulis
- **Diagnostic taste:** The extreme bitterness is the definitive identification character — a tiny piece on the tip of the tongue reveals the identity instantly
- **Pore color:** The pinkish pore surface in mature specimens is a key distinguishing feature from B. edulis
- **Taxonomic note:** Now placed in Tylopilus, separated from Boletus by spore color (pinkish vs. olive) and pore surface coloration
- **Teaching specimen:** Commonly used in mycology courses and foraging workshops as the primary example of why careful identification matters
- **Culinary warning:** Even experienced foragers should taste-test questionable boletes, as the visual similarity to porcini is remarkably close
