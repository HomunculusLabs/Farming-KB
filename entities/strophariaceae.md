## Overview

The Strophariaceae are a family of fungi in the order Agaricales that includes many of the psilocybin-containing genera. This family is of particular interest in mycology and cultivation due to its inclusion of *Psilocybe* (formerly *Stropharia*) and related genera that produce the hallucinogenic compounds psilocybin and psilocin.

## Classification

- **Domain**: Eukarya
- **Kingdom**: Fungi
- **Phylum**: Basidiomycota
- **Class**: Agaricomycetes
- **Order**: Agaricales
- **Family**: Strophariaceae

## Key Genera

The Strophariaceae encompasses several genera of significance to cultivation and ethnomycology:

- **Psilocybe** — the primary psilocybin-producing genus, reclassified from *Stropharia* by mycologist [[rolf-singer]]. Contains dozens of psychoactive species.
- **Stropharia** — includes both psychoactive and non-psychoactive species. *[[stropharia-cubensis]]* was reclassified as *[[psilocybe-cubensis]]* but the older name persists in cultivation literature.
- **Conocybe** — a genus of small mushrooms, some species containing psilocybin
- **Panaeolus** — includes several psilocybin-containing species found in dung habitats
- **Copelandia** — tropical psilocybin-containing mushrooms, closely related to *Panaeolus*
- **Hypholoma** — includes species like *H. sublateritium* (brick cap)
- **Pholiota** — includes various edible and ornamental species

## Distinguishing Features

Members of the Strophariaceae share several morphological characteristics:

- **[[spore-print]] color**: Typically purple-brown to dark purplish-brown
- **Gill attachment**: Adnate to adnexed in most species
- **Veil presence**: Many species have a partial veil that leaves a ring (annulus) on the stem
- **Blue-staining reaction**: Species containing psilocybin exhibit a distinctive blue coloration when bruised or broken, caused by enzymatic oxidation of indole substrates

## Genetic Complexity

The Strophariaceae are genetically complex with respect to mating compatibility. They are heterothallic and tetrapolar, meaning:

- Sexual reproduction requires fusion of two compatible monokaryotic mycelia
- Compatibility is governed by two independent sets of genetic factors (A and B)
- Successful mating requires differences in both factor sets (e.g., AB × ab)
- This system produces four mating types from spores of a single basidiocarp
- Hundreds of distinct mating types can exist within a species

This tetrapolar heterothallism promotes outcrossing and genetic diversity, which is advantageous for species survival but also relevant to cultivation — multispore germination ensures compatible mating types will be present.

## Blue-Staining Reaction

A hallmark of psilocybin-containing Strophariaceae is the blue-staining reaction observed when the flesh is bruised or broken. This reaction is caused by enzymatic oxidation of indole substrates such as:

- Tryptophan
- 5-Hydroxytryptamine (serotonin)
- Psilocybin itself

This blue-staining serves as a fairly reliable field indicator of psilocybin presence. However, some non-[[psilocybin-mushrooms]] in other genera (e.g., *Russula* section Nigricantinae, some *Boletus* species) also exhibit blue-staining from different chemical mechanisms.

## Metol Test

A more specific chemical test for psilocybin in Strophariaceae involves metol (p-methylaminophenol), a common photographic darkroom reagent:

- Dissolve metol in 20 times its weight of water
- Apply a few drops to a section of crushed stem
- A deep purple color developing within 1–30 minutes indicates psilocybin
- The solution is unstable and must be used immediately after mixing

## Ecological Role

Most Strophariaceae are saprotrophic, decomposing dead organic matter including dung, wood, and plant debris. *Psilocybe cubensis* specifically grows on cow dung in pastures, giving it a circumtropical distribution linked to cattle ranching. This intimate association with domesticated cattle has likely existed for as long as humanity has practiced pastoralism.

## Relevance to Cultivation

The Strophariaceae, particularly *Psilocybe cubensis*, are among the most cultivated mushrooms worldwide. Their relatively simple growth requirements, world-wide spore availability, and reliable fruiting under artificial conditions make them ideal for cultivation. The family's tetrapolar mating system means that multispore inoculations reliably produce fertile dikaryotic mycelium capable of fruiting.
