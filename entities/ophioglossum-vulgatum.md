The genus Ophioglossum is remarkable for having the highest chromosome count of any known organism, with some species exceeding 2n = 1,400. Ophioglossum vulgatum itself has 2n = 240–480, varying by population.

Unlike most ferns, Ophioglossum belongs to an ancient group called eusporangiate ferns, which produce thick-walled sporangia that develop from groups of cells rather than single cells. This places it evolutionarily distant from the common leptosporangiate ferns.

The plant has no known medicinal or economic uses, but it holds significant interest for evolutionary biologists studying ancient fern lineages and extreme polyploidy in plants.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Ophioglossum vulgatum was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. It belongs to the family Ophioglossaceae, an ancient lineage of eusporangiate ferns that diverged early from the leptosporangiate ferns that comprise most living fern species.

The family includes the genera Ophioglossum, Botrychium, and Helminthostachys. Ophioglossum is distinguished by its simple or lobed sterile blade and its fertile spike that emerges from the base of the sterile blade.

The sterile blade is 3–10 cm long, ovate to elliptic, with a smooth margin and a conspicuous network of veins. The blade is fleshy and pale green, arising from a short, subterranean rhizome.

The fertile spike is 2–6 cm long, narrowly cylindrical, and bears two rows of embedded sporangia. The spike emerges in late spring to early summer and releases spores from dehiscent sporangia.

The rhizome is short, erect, and bears fleshy roots that lack root hairs. The plant relies on [[aact-microbial-foliar-nutrient-uptake-co2-stomata-ingham]], particularly phosphorus, in the nutrient-poor soils where it often grows.

Each rhizome typically produces only one frond per year, making the plant particularly vulnerable to damage from grazing, trampling, or premature mowing during its brief growing season.

Spores are yellowish, subglobose, and 35–50 µm in diameter. They are produced in large numbers, with each sporangium containing 2,000–6,000 spores.

The species exhibits remarkable morphological [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]] of the Northern Hemisphere. In Europe, it occurs from Iceland and the British Isles through Scandinavia to the Mediterranean, reaching east to the Caucasus and Siberia.

In North America, it is found across southern Canada and the northern United States, with disjunct populations in the Rocky Mountains. Asian records extend from Turkey through Central Asia to Japan and Korea.

The species grows in damp to wet meadows, pastures, marshy grasslands, and open deciduous woodlands. It prefers neutral to slightly acidic soils with good moisture retention and moderate organic content.

It is often found in traditionally managed hay meadows and pastures that have escaped agricultural intensification. The species benefits from low competition, tolerating light grazing and occasional mowing.

The fern can persist in small habitat fragments and isolated meadow patches, surviving underground as rhizomes for years before producing above-ground fronds when conditions are favorable.

Population sizes are typically small, with scattered individuals rather than dense stands. The plant is easily overlooked due to its small size and short above-ground growing season of only 6–8 weeks.

In the British Isles, the strongest populations occur in the hay meadows of northern England, Wales, and Scotland. Species-rich Nardus stricta grasslands and Cynosurus cristatus-Centaurea nigra meadows are particularly important habitats.

## Conservation

Ophioglossum vulgatum has declined significantly across western Europe due to agricultural intensification, drainage of damp meadows, and the application of herbicides and fertilizers. It is considered threatened in several European countries.

In the United Kingdom, the species has shown marked declines in lowland England but remains relatively frequent in parts of Scotland, Wales, and Ireland. It is listed as Vulnerable on the English Red List.

Conservation measures include protection of species-rich meadow habitats through agri-environment schemes, maintenance of traditional hay-cutting regimes, and avoidance of drainage and fertilization at known sites.
