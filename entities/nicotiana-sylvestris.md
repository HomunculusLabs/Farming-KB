## Overview

Nicotiana sylvestris, commonly known as woodland tobacco or flowering tobacco, is a tender perennial species of Nicotiana native to South America.

It is prized in ornamental horticulture for its tall, elegant habit and intensely fragrant, tubular white flowers that open in the evening.

The species is of significant scientific importance as the maternal parent of Nicotiana tabacum, the cultivated tobacco, following an ancient hybridization event.

Nicotiana sylvestris has also served as a model organism in plant genetics and molecular biology research.

All parts of the plant contain nicotine and other alkaloids and should be considered toxic if ingested.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Nicotiana sylvestris Speg. & Comes was described by Carlos Spegazzini and Ernesto Comes in 1897.

Molecular phylogenetic studies confirm that Nicotiana sylvestris is the maternal donor in the allopolyploid origin of Nicotiana tabacum, which also involved Nicotiana tomentosiformis as the paternal parent.

The species belongs to Nicotiana section Sylvestres, a small South American group within the genus.

Nicotiana sylvestris forms a basal rosette of large, broadly elliptic leaves 15 to 30 centimeters long during its vegetative phase.

The flowering stem rises to 100 to 180 centimeters, producing a tall, open, somewhat branching inflorescence.

Individual flowers are tubular, approximately 5 to 7 centimeters long, pure white, and strongly reflexed at the mouth.

Flowers open in late afternoon and evening, releasing a powerful jasmine-like fragrance throughout the night.

The floral tube is notably long relative to other Nicotiana species, an adaptation to pollination by long-tongued hawkmoths.

Each flower produces a capsule containing numerous tiny seeds approximately 0.5 millimeters in diameter.

## Distribution and Habitat

Nicotiana sylvestris is native to the Andean foothills and adjacent lowlands of northwestern Argentina, specifically in the provinces of Salta, Jujuy, and Tucuman.

It typically grows in semi-shaded habitats along streambanks, in ravines, forest margins, and disturbed sites at elevations between 1000 and 2500 meters.

The species prefers moist, fertile, well-drained soils in positions with partial shade, though it adapts to full sun in cultivation.

In its native habitat, it often colonizes disturbed sites and forest clearings where competition from taller vegetation is reduced.

The natural range is relatively restricted, making the species potentially vulnerable to habitat loss in its native Andean populations.

It has naturalized occasionally in temperate regions where it is cultivated, though it is not considered invasive due to its sensitivity to frost.

## Cultivation

Nicotiana sylvestris is easily grown from seed, which is typically sown indoors 6 to 8 weeks before the last frost date in temperate climates.

Seeds require light for germination and should be pressed into the soil surface without covering.

Germination occurs in 10 to 21 days at temperatures of 18 to 22 degrees Celsius.

The species is grown as an annual in most temperate gardens, though it is technically a tender perennial hardy only in USDA zones 10 to 11.

Plants prefer rich, moist, well-drained soil in partial shade to full sun, with afternoon shade beneficial in hot climates.

Regular watering and fertilization promote vigorous growth and abundant flowering throughout the summer and into early autumn.

Deadheading spent flowers encourages continued bloom but reduces seed production for those wishing to save seed.

The species is relatively pest-free in garden conditions, though aphids and tobacco hornworms may occasionally feed on the foliage.

No named cultivars are widely available, though white-flowered selections with enhanced fragrance exist in the specialty seed trade.

## Chemistry and Genetics

Like all Nicotiana species, Nicotiana sylvestris produces nicotine as its primary alkaloid, concentrated in the leaves at concentrations of approximately 0.5 to 2 percent of dry weight.

Additional alkaloids include nornicotine, anabasine, and anatabine, though in lower concentrations than in cultivated tobacco.

The species also produces volatile organic compounds responsible for its nocturnal fragrance, primarily benzyl acetate and related aromatic esters.

Nicotiana sylvestris holds a central position in tobacco genetics as the confirmed maternal parent of Nicotiana tabacum.

The hybridization event that produced cultivated tobacco occurred approximately 6 to 10 million years ago, making Nicotiana tabacum an allotetraploid with subgenomes derived from both parents.

The chloroplast genome of Nicotiana tabacum is nearly identical to that of Nicotiana sylvestris, confirming the maternal inheritance.

The species has been used extensively in genetic research as a model system for studying organelle genetics, cytoplasmic male sterility, and interspecific hybridization barriers.

Its large, easily manipulated flowers make it useful for pollination biology research.

## Cultural and Historical Significance

Nicotiana sylvestris was first collected by European botanists during late nineteenth-century explorations of the Andean foothills of northern Argentina.

Its introduction into European horticulture is credited to the late Victorian era, when it became valued for its evening fragrance and statuesque habit.

The species was famously used by Luther Burbank in his breeding programs, contributing genetic material to some of his ornamental Nicotiana hybrids.

In modern horticulture, Nicotiana sylvestris is widely regarded as the most fragrant and elegant of the ornamental tobacco species.

It has received the Royal Horticultural Society's Award of Garden Merit in the United Kingdom, confirming its garden value.

The species is a staple of cottage gardens, white gardens, and moon gardens designed for evening enjoyment.

Its historical significance in the origin of cultivated tobacco gives it a unique dual identity as both an ornamental flower and a key ancestor of a major world crop.
