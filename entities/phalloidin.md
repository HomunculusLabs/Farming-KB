## Description

Phalloidin is a bicyclic heptapeptide toxin found in deadly *Amanita* species, particularly *Amanita phalloides*. It is one of three principal toxins isolated from the Death Cap, alongside amanitin and phallin. Phalloidin acts quickly compared to amanitin but is destroyed by heat, which partly explains why it is not the primary cause of death in mushroom poisoning cases. It binds tightly to F-actin filaments in cells, preventing their depolymerisation and disrupting normal cellular function.

## Classification

- **Type:** Mycotoxin (phallotoxin class)
- **Chemical family:** Bicyclic heptapeptide
- **Source organisms:** *Amanita phalloides*, *Amanita verna*, *Amanita virosa*
- **Mechanism:** Binds and stabilises F-actin, preventing cytoskeletal dynamics
- **Stability:** Quickly active but destroyed by heat

## Key Facts

- One of three toxins isolated from *Amanita phalloides* alongside phallin (a glucoside) and amanitin (an amatoxin).
- Acts quickly upon ingestion but is destroyed by heat, making it less lethal than the heat-stable amanitin.
- Binds to actin filaments with extraordinary affinity, stabilising them and preventing normal cellular processes.
- In experimental animals, phalloidin produces liver damage but the majority of lesions found in human post-mortems are attributable to amanitin rather than phalloidin.
- The discovery that multiple toxins with different properties coexist in the same fungus was crucial to understanding the variable clinical presentation of *Amanita* poisoning.
- Modern cell biology uses fluorescently labelled phalloidin (e.g., phalloidin-FITC) as a standard tool for visualising actin filaments under the microscope.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Phalloidin is significant both historically and practically. Historically, the isolation of multiple toxins from *Amanita phalloides* demonstrated that a single species can produce a cocktail of poisonous compounds with distinct mechanisms. This understanding is essential for medical treatment — knowing that both a fast-acting and a slow-acting, heat-stable toxin are present explains the biphasic clinical course of [[bloomfield-amatoxin-poisoning-mechanism]]. Practically, phalloidin has become one of the most important tools in cell biology for actin staining, turning a deadly natural product into an indispensable research reagent.

## Related Toxins

- **Amanitin (α-amanitin):** The primary lethal toxin; heat-stable, resistant to digestion, inhibits RNA polymerase II.
- **Phallin:** A haemolytic glucoside; destroyed by heat and digestive juices, not the primary cause of death.
