# Danthonia spicata

## Overview

**Danthonia spicata** (L.) P. Beauv. ex Roem. & Schult., commonly known as poverty oat grass or poverty grass, is a perennial cool-season bunchgrass native to open woodlands, barrens, and dry prairies across eastern and central North America. It is named for its ability to persist on impoverished, nutrient-poor soils where more productive species cannot survive.

The species typically grows 30–70 cm tall and forms loose, open tussocks with fine-textured, often slightly curly leaves. It is distinguished by its compact, one-sided spike-like panicle and characteristic woolly leaf bases.

Poverty oat grass is an important component of oak savanna, pine barrens, and dry upland forest understories. Its shade tolerance and drought resistance make it one of the more versatile native grasses in [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] American ecosystems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Poales
- **Family:** Poaceae
- **Subfamily:** Danthonioideae
- **Tribe:** Danthonieae
- **Genus:** Danthonia DC.
- **Species:** D. spicata (L.) P. Beauv. ex Roem. & Schult.

**Bas synonym:** Avena spicata L. The species was originally described by Linnaeus in Avena and transferred to Danthonia by Palisot de Beauvois. The genus Danthonia contains approximately 20 species worldwide, with D. spicata being the most widespread in North America.

### Morphology

Danthonia spicata is a cool-season (C3) perennial bunchgrass forming loose, open tussocks 15–30 cm in diameter. Culms are slender, erect, and 30–70 cm tall, often geniculate at the lower nodes.

Leaf blades are flat to involute, 5–25 cm long and 1–3 mm wide, with a distinctive tendency to curl or twist when dry. The basal leaves often have a dense covering of woolly hairs at the sheath base, a key identification feature.

The ligule is a short membrane, 1–3 mm long, often irregularly toothed or erose at the tip. Auricles are absent. Sheaths are round to slightly compressed and may be hairy.

The inflorescence is a narrow, compact, one-sided panicle 3–8 cm long, appearing spike-like at maturity. Spikelets are 8–14 mm long, containing 3–6 florets, and are green to purplish in color.

A distinctive feature is the prominent awn arising from near the lemma midpoint, 5–12 mm long and geniculate (bent). The callus hairs at the base of the floret are 1–3 mm long, shorter than in many other Danthonia species.

## Distribution and Habitat

Danthonia spicata is distributed across eastern North America from Newfoundland and Quebec west to Minnesota and Iowa, and south to Georgia, northern Alabama, and eastern Oklahoma. Disjunct populations occur in the Black Hills of South Dakota.

The species occupies dry, acidic, infertile soils in open woodlands, oak-hickory forests, pine barrens, sand barrens, glades, rock outcrops, and dry prairie remnants. It is notably tolerant of shallow, rocky, and sandy substrates.

It is moderately shade-tolerant, often serving as a dominant groundcover species in open-canopy woodlands and forest edges. Full sun is preferred for flowering and seed production, but the species persists under 40–60% canopy closure.

Elevation ranges from near sea level to approximately 1,500 m in the southern Appalachian Mountains. It is most abundant in the Piedmont, Appalachian, and Ozark regions.

The species is classified as a facultative upland species (FACU) in wetland delineation. It is sensitive to prolonged soil saturation and is absent from wetlands and poorly drained sites.

## Cultivation

Danthonia spicata is available from native plant nurseries specializing in regional ecotypes. [[cannabis-seed-germination]] is moderate, typically 40–60% without special treatment, though cold stratification for 30 days may improve rates.

The species establishes slowly from seed, requiring 2–3 years to develop mature flowering tussocks. This slow establishment makes it less competitive with aggressive weeds during the critical early growth phase.

It thrives in dry, infertile, acidic soils in full sun to partial shade. Once established, it requires no irrigation, fertilization, or intensive management. Excessive fertility promotes weed competition and can reduce stand longevity.

In prairie and woodland restoration projects, Danthonia spicata is typically included at 5–10% of the seed mix by weight. It is particularly valuable for dry, shaded sites where warm-season prairie grasses perform poorly.

No significant pest or disease problems affect established plants. The species is generally too low in productivity for commercial forage use, though it provides acceptable seasonal grazing in native rangelands.

Controlled burning every 3–5 years can reduce accumulated thatch and stimulate new growth, though the species does not depend on fire to the extent that many warm-season prairie grasses do.

## Ecological Role

Danthonia spicata plays a significant role in maintaining native plant diversity in dry woodland and barrens ecosystems. Its moderate growth habit and shade tolerance create a persistent but not dominant ground layer that allows herbaceous forbs to coexist.

The dense fibrous root system provides effective [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]] on steep, dry slopes and thin soils. Root biomass contributes to [[knf-leaf-mold-humus]] accumulation [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-fertiliser-and-nutrient-cycling]] in nutrient-poor sites.

Several species of grass-feeding Lepidoptera use Danthonia as a larval host, including the cobweb skipper (Hesperia metea) in pine barren communities. The seeds provide food for sparrows, juncos, and other ground-feeding birds.

As a cool-season species, it provides green forage in early spring and late autumn, complementing warm-season grasses in mixed plantings. This phenological complementarity extends the period of active photosynthesis in restored ecosystems.
