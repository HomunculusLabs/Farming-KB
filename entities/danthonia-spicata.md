## Danthonia spicata

## Overview

**Danthonia spicata** (L.) P. Beauv. ex Roem. & Schult., commonly known as poverty oat grass or poverty grass, is a perennial cool-season bunchgrass native to open woodlands, barrens, and dry prairies across eastern and central North America. It is named for its ability to persist on impoverished, nutrient-poor soils where more productive species cannot survive.

The species typically grows 30–70 cm tall and forms loose, open tussocks with fine-textured, often slightly curly leaves. It is distinguished by its compact, one-sided spike-like panicle and characteristic woolly leaf bases.

Poverty oat grass is an important component of oak savanna, pine barrens, and dry upland forest understories. The species establishes slowly from seed, requiring 2–3 years to develop mature flowering tussocks. This slow establishment makes it less competitive with aggressive weeds during the critical early growth phase.

It thrives in dry, infertile, acidic soils in full sun to partial shade. Once established, it requires no irrigation, fertilization, or intensive management. Excessive fertility promotes weed competition and can reduce stand longevity.

In prairie and woodland restoration projects, Danthonia spicata is typically included at 5–10% of the seed mix by weight. It is particularly valuable for dry, shaded sites where warm-season prairie grasses perform poorly.

No significant pest or disease problems affect established plants. The species is generally too low in productivity for commercial forage use, though it provides acceptable seasonal grazing in native rangelands.

Controlled burning every 3–5 years can reduce accumulated thatch and stimulate new growth, though the species does not depend on fire to the extent that many warm-season prairie grasses do.

## Ecological Role

Danthonia spicata plays a significant role in maintaining native plant diversity in dry woodland and barrens ecosystems. Its moderate growth habit and shade tolerance create a persistent but not dominant ground layer that allows herbaceous forbs to coexist.

Several species of grass-feeding Lepidoptera use Danthonia as a larval host, including the cobweb skipper (Hesperia metea) in pine barren communities. The seeds provide food for sparrows, juncos, and other ground-feeding birds.

As a cool-season species, it provides green forage in early spring and late autumn, complementing warm-season grasses in mixed plantings. This phenological complementarity extends the period of active photosynthesis in restored ecosystems.
