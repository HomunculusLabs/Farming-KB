The plant is valued as both a culinary herb and a medicinal plant, with a sweet aniseed flavor that complements both sweet and savory dishes.

Sweet cicely has a long history of use in European folk medicine and traditional cuisine, particularly in Alpine regions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Myrrhis odorata is the sole accepted species in the genus *Myrrhis*, placed in the tribe Scandiceae of Apiaceae.

The genus name derives from the Greek *myrrhis* (myrrh), referring to the aromatic fragrance of the foliage.

The specific epithet *odorata* (Latin for "fragrant") highlights the plant's distinctive sweet scent.

### Vegetative Characteristics

Sweet cicely grows 60–120 cm tall from a thick, fleshy taproot with a characteristic aniseed aroma.

Leaves are bipinnate or tripinnate, 15–30 cm long, with ovate leaflets 2–5 cm long that are soft and hairy.

The foliage emerges early in spring and the entire plant dies back to ground level after the first frost.

All above-ground parts are aromatic when crushed, releasing a sweet scent reminiscent of anise and licorice.

### Flowers and Fruit

Flowers are produced in compound umbels 3–6 cm across, with 8–15 rays, each bearing small white flowers.

Individual flowers are approximately 3 mm across with slightly unequal petals, blooming from May to July.

The fruit is a distinctive elongated, cylindrical schizocarp, 15–25 mm long, dark brown to black when ripe.

Seeds are ribbed and marked with dark longitudinal stripes, with a pleasant sweet flavor when green.

## Distribution and Habitat

Myrrhis odorata is native to the mountains of central and southern Europe, from the Pyrenees eastward to the Balkans and Carpathians.

The primary volatile constituents include **anethole**, **myrtenol**, and various sesquiterpenes.

### Key Compounds

Trans-anethole is the dominant aromatic compound, contributing the characteristic anise-like flavor.

The plant also contains coumarins, flavonoids, and vitamin C, particularly in the young leaves and green seeds.

Furano-coumarins including bergapten have been identified, which can cause photosensitivity in sensitive individuals.
