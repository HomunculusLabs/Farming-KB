# Myrrhis odorata

## Overview

**Myrrhis odorata** (L.) Scop. is a herbaceous perennial plant in the family Apiaceae, commonly known as **sweet cicely** or **garden myrrh**.

It is native to mountainous regions of central and southern Europe and has been widely naturalized across temperate regions worldwide.

The plant is valued as both a culinary herb and a medicinal plant, with a sweet aniseed flavor that complements both sweet and savory dishes.

Sweet cicely has a long history of use in European folk medicine and traditional cuisine, particularly in Alpine regions.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Myrrhis odorata is the sole accepted species in the genus *Myrrhis*, placed in the tribe Scandiceae of Apiaceae.

The genus name derives from the Greek *myrrhis* (myrrh), referring to the aromatic fragrance of the foliage.

The specific epithet *odorata* (Latin for "fragrant") highlights the plant's distinctive sweet scent.

### Vegetative Characteristics

Sweet cicely grows 60–120 cm tall from a thick, fleshy taproot with a characteristic aniseed aroma.

Leaves are bipinnate or tripinnate, 15–30 cm long, with ovate leaflets 2–5 cm long that are soft and hairy.

The foliage emerges early in spring and the entire plant dies back to ground level after the first frost.

All above-ground parts are aromatic when crushed, releasing a sweet scent reminiscent of anise and licorice.

### Flowers and Fruit

Flowers are produced in compound umbels 3–6 cm across, with 8–15 rays, each bearing small white flowers.

Individual flowers are approximately 3 mm across with slightly unequal petals, blooming from May to July.

The fruit is a distinctive elongated, cylindrical schizocarp, 15–25 mm long, dark brown to black when ripe.

Seeds are ribbed and marked with dark longitudinal stripes, with a pleasant sweet flavor when green.

## Distribution and Habitat

Myrrhis odorata is native to the mountains of central and southern Europe, from the Pyrenees eastward to the Balkans and Carpathians.

It has been widely introduced and naturalized in northern Europe, the British Isles, North America, and parts of New Zealand.

The species prefers partial shade to full shade in moist, humus-rich soils along woodland edges and stream banks.

It grows at elevations from 200 to 2,000 m and tolerates cold winters with good snow cover.

Naturalized populations often establish in hedgerows, old gardens, and disturbed shady sites near human habitation.

## Chemistry and Pharmacology

The essential oil of Myrrhis odorata contains several bioactive compounds responsible for its flavor and medicinal properties.

The primary volatile constituents include **anethole**, **myrtenol**, and various sesquiterpenes.

### Key Compounds

Trans-anethole is the dominant aromatic compound, contributing the characteristic anise-like flavor.

The plant also contains coumarins, flavonoids, and vitamin C, particularly in the young leaves and green seeds.

Furano-coumarins including bergapten have been identified, which can cause photosensitivity in sensitive individuals.

### Traditional and Validated Uses

In European folk medicine, sweet cicely was used as a digestive aid and carminative, particularly for flatulence and colic.

The roots were traditionally boiled to produce a syrup given to children as a cough remedy and expectorant.

Modern pharmacological studies confirm mild spasmolytic activity consistent with traditional digestive applications.

The high vitamin C content of young spring leaves made the plant valuable for preventing scurvy before citrus fruits were widely available.

## Culinary and Edible Uses

Sweet cicely is one of the few culinary herbs with a genuinely sweet flavor, allowing it to reduce sugar in recipes.

Young leaves are used raw in salads or cooked as a pot herb, and they complement both fruit desserts and savory dishes.

The green seeds are used whole in fruit conserves, stewed fruits, and rhubarb preparations to reduce tartness.

The roots can be cooked as a root vegetable with a sweet parsnip-like flavor, though this use is less common today.

The plant was historically used to flavor liqueurs, cordials, and herbal teas throughout central Europe.

## Cultivation

Myrrhis odorata is easily cultivated from seed, which requires a period of cold stratification for reliable germination.

The plant prefers rich, moist soil in partial shade and is hardy to USDA zone 4 once established.

It self-seeds readily in favorable conditions and can become a persistent but not invasive garden resident.

Division of established clumps in early spring or autumn is the most reliable vegetative propagation method.

The plant requires minimal maintenance once established, making it suitable for low-input permaculture and woodland gardens.
