# Jane Jacobs

## Description

Jane Jacobs was an American-Canadian journalist, author, and urban theorist whose work on the value of marginal urban systems — particularly rundown neighbourhoods as incubators of economic and cultural innovation — is cited by [[david-holmgren]] as supporting [[permaculture]] Principle 11: Use Edges and Value the Marginal. Her insights into how urban renewal programs destroy the very conditions that generate economic and cultural vitality parallel permaculture's understanding of how simplification eliminates productive edges.

## Classification

- **Type:** Person — urban theorist, author, activist
- **Nationality:** American-born, later Canadian
- **Born:** 1916, Scranton, Pennsylvania, USA
- **Died:** 2006, Toronto, Ontario, Canada
- **Fields:** Urban planning, economics, community development

## Key Facts

- **Value of rundown neighbourhoods:** Jacobs identified that rundown urban neighbourhoods provided cheap rents in old warehouses, shops, and houses where small start-up businesses and companies could establish themselves. These marginal areas functioned as incubators for entrepreneurial and cultural innovation precisely because they were not fully controlled or optimised.

- **Urban renewal critique:** She provided evidence that the elimination of these marginal areas through urban renewal programs in the 1950s and 1960s was killing the economic life — as well as the artistic and cultural life — of American cities. By cleaning up and standardising these areas, planners destroyed the conditions that allowed innovation to emerge.

- **Self-organising urban systems:** Jacobs argued that healthy cities function as complex, self-organising systems with diverse uses, ages of buildings, and scales of enterprise — not as centrally planned monocultures of function.

- **The Death and Life of Great American Cities:** Her most influential work (1961) challenged the orthodox urban planning of the era, arguing that the dense, diverse, mixed-use neighbourhoods that planners sought to replace were actually the most vibrant and successful urban environments.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

Jane Jacobs' urban analysis [[maps]] directly onto [[permaculture-principles]]:

1. **Value of marginal systems (Principle 11):** Just as rundown neighbourhoods provide the cheap, unregulated space where innovation emerges, marginal areas in gardens and farms — the untidy edges, the wild corners, the unproductive zones — often harbour the greatest diversity and the most interesting ecological dynamics.

2. **Diversity as productivity (Principle 10):** Jacobs demonstrated that diverse, mixed-use urban areas are more economically productive than planned monocultures of residential, commercial, or industrial zones — exactly paralleling how diverse polycultures outperform monocultures in total system productivity.

3. **Tension between tidiness and emergence:** Holmgren explicitly draws the parallel: There is tension between the tidy up mentality that wants to recycle and make full use of everything, and the mindset that values leaving things be to see what emerges. Jacobs showed this dynamic at urban scale.

4. **Self-organisation over central planning:** Her critique of top-down urban planning mirrors permaculture's preference for working with natural patterns rather than imposing simplified designs. Both recognise that complex, self-organising systems are more resilient and productive than centrally planned ones.

5. **Scale-appropriate intervention:** Jacobs' emphasis on the neighbourhood as the fundamental unit of urban planning parallels permaculture's emphasis on designing at the scale where observation and management are practical.
