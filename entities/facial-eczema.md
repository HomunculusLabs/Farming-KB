## Description

Facial eczema is a mycotoxic disease of livestock, particularly cattle and sheep, caused by the fungal toxin sporidesmin. The toxin is produced by the saprophytic fungus *Pithomyces chartarum*, which grows on dead pasture litter during warm, humid conditions. The disease causes severe liver damage and photosensitization (skin inflammation triggered by sunlight exposure), particularly on exposed facial skin. Mollison cites facial eczema as one of the chronic illnesses that plague high-producing, stressed livestock herds.

## Classification

- **Category**: Mycotoxic livestock disease
- **Causative organism**: *Pithomyces chartarum* (fungus)
- **Toxin**: Sporidesmin
- **Affected species**: Primarily cattle and sheep; also goats and deer
- **Geographic distribution**: New Zealand, Australia, South Africa, parts of South America

## Key Facts

- Caused by ingestion of spores of *Pithomyces chartarum* growing on dead grass in pastures
- Sporidesmin causes oxidative damage to the bile ducts and liver (hepatobiliary toxicity)
- Liver damage leads to phylloerythrin accumulation, causing photosensitization — lesions on exposed skin, especially face and ears
- Particularly prevalent in New Zealand, where Mollison notes veterinary costs reach $120 per stock unit in high-producing herds
- Less stressed pastures and farms see veterinary costs drop to ~$20 per unit, with reduced disease incidence
- Risk peaks in late summer/autumn when warm nights (>12°C) and high humidity favor fungal growth
- Prevention strategies include [[pasture-management]] (removing litter), zinc supplementation, fungicide application, and breeding resistant stock

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- Facial eczema is directly caused by a fungal pathogen — understanding [[fungal-ecology]] is essential for managing this disease
- *Pithomyces chartarum* is a saprophytic fungus that thrives on dead pasture material — the same substrate cycling principles that govern [[mushroom-cultivation]] apply
- Biological control approaches include applying beneficial fungi (e.g., *Trichoderma*) that compete with *P. chartarum* on pasture litter
- The disease illustrates how pushing maximum yield creates conditions for fungal disease outbreaks
- Mycotoxin management is a critical skill in both mushroom cultivation (preventing contamination) and livestock management
- Permaculture approaches (diverse pastures, reduced stress, biological controls) offer alternatives to chemical management
