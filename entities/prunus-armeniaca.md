## Overview

**Prunus armeniaca** L., commonly known as apricot, is a deciduous tree species in the family Rosaceae, subfamily Amygdaloideae. It is cultivated worldwide for its edible stone fruits, which are consumed fresh, dried, and processed into preserves, juices, and liqueurs.

The species likely originated in Central Asia, with China being a primary center of diversity. Apricots have been cultivated for over 4,000 years and were spread along the Silk Road to the Mediterranean, where they became a significant crop in Turkey, Iran, and southern Europe.

Global production exceeds 4 million tonnes annually, with Turkey, Uzbekistan, Iran, and Italy as the leading producers. The fruit is valued for its sweet flavor, nutritional content, and versatility in food processing.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Prunus armeniaca** L. was described by Linnaeus in 1753. The specific epithet *armeniaca* reflects the historical belief that the species originated in Armenia, though modern botanical evidence points to a Central Asian origin.

## Cultivation

Apricot trees prefer well-drained, deep, loamy soils with a pH of 6.0–7.5. They are drought-tolerant once established but produce best with consistent irrigation during fruit development.

Trees are typically propagated by budding or grafting onto rootstocks such as *Prunus armeniaca* seedling, *P. cerasifera* (myrobalan plum), or *P. persica* (peach).

## Compounds and Hazards

Apricot kernels contain amygdalin, a cyanogenic glycoside that hydrolyzes to release hydrogen cyanide (HCN) upon digestion. Bitter kernels contain 3–5% amygdalin, while sweet kernels contain less than 0.1%.
