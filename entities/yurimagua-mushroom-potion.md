## Overview

The Yurimagua mushroom potion is a potently intoxicating beverage prepared from tree-dwelling mushrooms by the Yurimagua Indians of the western Amazon (Peru). Documented by Jesuit missionaries in the 17th and early 18th centuries, this preparation represents one of the earliest European accounts of psychoactive mushroom use in South America. The mushrooms involved are now believed to be a species of *Gymnopilus*, likely closely related to *[[gymnopilus-purpuratus]]*.

## Classification

- **Type**: Ethnobotanical preparation / psychoactive potion
- **Cultural origin**: Yurimagua Indians, western Amazon, Peru
- **Active ingredients**: Presumed [[psilocybin]] and related tryptamines
- **Source mushrooms**: Likely *Gymnopilus* species (possibly *Gymnopilus purpuratus* or close relative)
- **Historical documentation**: Jesuit missionaries, 17th–18th centuries

## Historical Documentation

Jesuits traveling to the western Amazon region of Peru during the 17th and early 18th centuries documented that the Yurimagua Indians habitually prepared a potently intoxicating potion derived from tree-dwelling mushrooms. Key details from their accounts:

- The mushrooms appeared on fallen trees as a "reddish growth" with a "spicy taste"
- The potion was prepared by processing these mushrooms into a beverage
- The potion was so potent that "nobody who swallowed three mouthfuls of the brew was able to resist its effects"
- The preparation was integrated into the indigenous cultural and possibly religious practices

## Botanical Identity

### Original Classification
The mushroom was initially considered to be *[[psilocybe-yungensis]]* Singer & Smith by some mycologists.

### Revised Understanding
The identification as *Psilocybe yungensis* is considered questionable for several reasons:

1. **Color**: The historical accounts describe a "reddish growth," which matches *Gymnopilus* species rather than *Psilocybe* species
2. **Substrate**: The mushrooms were described as growing on dense fallen tree trunks, a habitat typical of *Gymnopilus* species. *Psilocybe* species typically grow on wood sprigs and tree bark debris rather than solid trunks
3. **Growth habit**: Tree-dwelling on solid wood is characteristic of *Gymnopilus*, particularly *G. purpuratus* which is known to grow on dead tree trunks in South America
4. **Taste**: The "spicy taste" described is more consistent with *Gymnopilus* than *Psilocybe*

The mushroom was most likely closely related to *Gymnopilus purpuratus*, a species native to South America (Chile) and Australia that has been confirmed to contain psilocybin, [[psilocin]], and [[baeocystin]].

## Significance for Gymnopilus Research

The Yurimagua accounts gain new significance in light of the discovery of psilocybin in *Gymnopilus* species:

- They represent early evidence that *Gymnopilus* species have been used as psychoactive agents for centuries
- They support the hypothesis that psilocybin occurs in multiple *Gymnopilus* species across different geographic regions
- The discovery of psilocybin in *Gymnopilus* marked the first time this substance had been found in a member of the family Cortinariaceae, expanding the known distribution of psilocybin in the fungal kingdom

## Pharmacological Implications

The extreme potency described in the historical accounts ("nobody who swallowed three mouthfuls was able to resist its effects") suggests either:

- Very high concentrations of psychoactive alkaloids in the source mushroom
- A preparation method that concentrated the active compounds
- Synergistic effects between multiple compounds in the mushroom
- Cultural exaggeration in the historical accounts

Modern analyses of *Gymnopilus purpuratus* show psilocybin levels of 0.21–0.32% and psilocin levels of 0.02–0.29% of dry weight, which are moderate to high concentrations that could produce powerful effects in a concentrated preparation.

## Cultural Context

The Yurimagua were an indigenous people of the western Amazon who, like many Amazonian cultures, developed sophisticated systems of plant-based medicine and ritual use of psychoactive substances. The mushroom potion was part of a broader Amazonian pharmacopoeia that included ayahuasca, various DMT-containing snuffs, and other psychoactive preparations.

## Relationship to Modern Research

The Yurimagua accounts provide historical precedent for:

- South American indigenous use of *Gymnopilus* species as psychoactive agents
- The long-standing knowledge of psychoactive mushrooms beyond the well-documented Mexican species
- The potential for discovering psilocybin in additional *Gymnopilus* species, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions
