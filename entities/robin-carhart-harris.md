## Robin Carhart-Harris

Robin Carhart-Harris (born 1977) is a British neuroscientist and
professor at the University of California, San Francisco. He
pioneered the use of fMRI and other neuroimaging techniques to
study how psychedelics affect the human brain.

## Early Career

Carhart-Harris studied psychology at the University of Bristol and
completed his PhD at the University of Bristol in 2005. His early
research focused on the neurobiology of dreaming and consciousness.
He became interested in how altered states of consciousness relate
to brain function and neural dynamics.

He joined Imperial College London as a postdoctoral researcher
under the mentorship of [[lsd]] effects in healthy volunteers. The study
used fMRI and magnetoencephalography (MEG) to map brain activity
during psychedelic states. Results showed reduced connectivity
within the default mode network and increased global connectivity.

The study made international headlines and was published in
*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*. Images of the
brain under LSD showed patterns dramatically different from normal
waking consciousness. The visual results communicated psychedelic
brain effects to a broad audience for the first time.

### Default Mode Network Research

Carhart-Harris developed a neurobiological model linking
psychedelic effects to changes in the default mode network (DMN).
The DMN is a set of brain regions active during self-referential
thinking and mind-wandering. His research demonstrated that
psychedelics decrease DMN activity, correlating with experiences
of ego dissolution and expanded awareness.

He proposed the relaxed beliefs under psychedelics (REBUS) model
with Karl Friston. REBUS suggests psychedelics loosen the brain"s
top-down predictive processing, allowing new patterns of thought
to emerge. This framework connects psychedelic neuroscience to
the free energy principle in theoretical neuroscience.

### Psilocybin Brain Mapping

His team conducted detailed psilocybin brain imaging studies using
fMRI, showing decreased blood flow in the DMN and increased
connectivity between normally segregated brain networks. These
findings supported the entropic brain hypothesis, which proposes
that psychedelics increase the entropy or disorder of brain
signaling patterns.

The entropic brain hypothesis became a foundational model in
psychedelic neuroscience. It explains both the therapeutic
potential and the psychological risks of psychedelic states
through a single framework of increased neural flexibility.

## Clinical Trials

### Psilocybin for Depression

Carhart-Harris led the first modern clinical trial of psilocybin
for treatment-resistant depression, published in *The Lancet*
Psychiatry in 2016. Patients received two psilocybin sessions with
psychological support. Results showed significant reductions in
depression scores sustained for at least three months.

A larger follow-up trial at Imperial College compared psilocybin
therapy with the SSRI escitalopram. Results published in 2021
showed psilocybin was at least as effective as the standard
antidepressant. This was the first head-to-head comparison of a
psychedelic against a conventional antidepressant.

### Other Clinical Work

His team explored psilocybin for anorexia nervosa, end-of-life
anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive disorder. Each trial used
neuroimaging alongside clinical outcomes to understand the brain
mechanisms underlying therapeutic effects. This dual approach
characterized the Imperial research model.

## Move to UCSF

In 2021, Carhart-Harris moved to the University of California,
San Francisco to direct the new Neuroscape Psychedelics Division.
The move reflected the growing institutional acceptance of
psychedelic research in American academic medicine. At UCSF, he
continues neuroimaging studies and clinical trials.

## Legacy

Carhart-Harris transformed psychedelic research from a fringe
interest into a mainstream neuroscience discipline. His brain
imaging studies provided the first visual evidence of how
psychedelics reorganize brain activity. The entropic brain
hypothesis and REBUS model gave researchers testable frameworks
for understanding psychedelic mechanisms.

His clinical trials demonstrated that psilocybin could compete
with standard antidepressants in rigorous controlled settings.
This evidence base accelerated the global expansion of psychedelic
clinical research and influenced regulatory decisions in the
United States and Europe.
