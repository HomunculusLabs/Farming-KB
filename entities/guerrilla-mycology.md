# Guerrilla Mycology

**Guerrilla mycology** refers to the unsanctioned, grassroots practice of introducing fungal species into degraded, neglected, or polluted environments for ecological restoration and community resilience. Drawing on the principles of **mycoremediation** (using fungi to degrade or sequester environmental contaminants) and **guerrilla gardening** (cultivating plants on land without legal permission), guerrilla mycology represents an intersection of fungal ecology, environmental activism, and permaculture design.

The term is closely associated with the **Radical Mycology** movement, founded by Peter McCoy, which promotes accessible fungal education and community-scale ecological intervention.

## Core Principles

### 1. Fungi as Ecological Engineers

Fungi occupy a unique position in ecosystem function. As primary decomposers, mycorrhizal partners, and soil architects, they mediate nutrient cycling, build soil structure, and facilitate plant community dynamics. Guerrilla mycology leverages these inherent capacities:

- **Wood decomposition:** White-rot fungi (*Pleurotus*, *Trametes*, *Ganoderma*) secrete ligninolytic enzymes (laccase, manganese peroxidase, lignin peroxidase) that break down lignin and cellulose in dead wood
- **Soil aggregation:** Mycelial networks bind soil particles into stable aggregates, improving water retention and erosion resistance
- **Mycorrhizal networking:** Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) connect plant root systems, facilitating nutrient exchange and chemical signaling between individuals
- **Microbial community modulation:** Fungal hyphae create physical channels and chemical gradients that shape bacterial and archaeal community composition

### 2. Low-Tech, Open-Source Methods

Guerrilla mycology deliberately avoids expensive laboratory equipment and proprietary techniques. Core methods include:

- **Spore slurries:** Mature mushroom caps blended with water and a surfactant (e.g., mild soap) to create a suspension for direct application
- **Cardboard spawn:** Mycelium grown on wetted corrugated cardboard — an accessible, low-cost substrate that works for many saprotrophic species
- **Wood chip inoculation:** Spawning fungi into piles of arborist wood chips to create inoculum for distribution
- **Bokashi pre-composting:** Fermenting organic waste with effective microorganisms before fungal inoculation
- **Toilet paper tube spawn:** Using cardboard tubes as a contained spawn medium for transport and planting

### 3. Anti-Authoritarian Ethos

Guerrilla mycology challenges institutional gatekeeping around ecological knowledge. Key tenets include:

- Knowledge should be freely shared, not patented or restricted
- Ecological restoration is a community right, not a professional service
- Direct action is legitimate when regulatory systems fail to protect ecosystems
- Experimental methods and citizen science contribute valid ecological data

## Mycoremediation Applications

### Heavy Metal Accumulation

Certain fungal species can bioaccumulate heavy metals from contaminated soils:

| Metal | Effective Fungal Species | Mechanism |
|-------|------------------------|-----------|
| Lead (Pb) | *Pleurotus ostreatus*, *Ganoderma lucidum* | Biosorption onto cell walls |
| Cadmium (Cd) | *Trametes versicolor*, *Pleurotus* spp. | Intracellular sequestration |
| Arsenic (As) | *Suillus* spp. (mycorrhizal) | Chelation and compartmentalization |
| Mercury (Hg) | *Coprinus* spp. | Volatilization to less toxic forms |

**Important caveat:** Fungi that accumulate heavy metals should never be consumed. The metals concentrate in fruiting bodies. The goal is soil decontamination through sequestration, not harvesting for food.

### Petroleum Hydrocarbon Degradation

White-rot fungi are particularly effective at degrading petroleum products:

- **PAHs (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons):** *Pleurotus ostreatus* and *Trametes versicolor* can degrade 60–90% of PAHs in contaminated soil over 8–12 weeks
- **BTEX (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylene):** Fungal enzyme systems oxidize these volatile compounds
- **Motor oil and diesel:** Wood chip beds inoculated with *Pleurotus* species can filter and degrade oil-contaminated runoff

### Organic Pollutant Degradation

- **Dyes and textile effluents:** *Trametes versicolor* laccase enzymes decolorize synthetic dyes used in textile manufacturing
- **Pharmaceuticals:** Certain fungi can degrade antibiotics, hormones, and other pharmaceutical residues in wastewater
- **Pesticides:** Fungal consortia accelerate the breakdown of organophosphate and organochlorine pesticides

### Erosion Control

Mycelial mats act as living erosion barriers:

- **Slope stabilization:** Inoculating disturbed slopes with saprotrophic fungi accelerates the establishment of a microbial soil crust that resists water erosion
- **Stream bank restoration:** Mycelial "socks" — mesh tubes packed with inoculated substrate — placed along eroding stream banks reduce sediment loss
- **Post-fire recovery:** Fungal inoculation of burn scars accelerates organic matter decomposition and soil rebuilding

## Species Selection Guide

### For Beginners

| Species | Use Case | Difficulty | Notes |
|---------|----------|------------|-------|
| *Pleurotus ostreatus* (Oyster) | General decomposition, PAH cleanup | Easy | Most versatile; grows on straw, paper, wood |
| *Stropharia rugosoannulata* (Wine Cap) | Garden mulch, soil building | Easy | Excellent for wood chip beds |
| *Ganoderma lucidum* (Reishi) | Wood decomposition, heavy metals | Moderate | Slower but powerful decomposer |
| *Trametes versicolor* (Turkey Tail) | Stump decomposition, dye cleanup | Moderate | Very hardy; grows on diverse substrates |

### For Advanced Practitioners

| Species | Use Case | Difficulty | Notes |
|---------|----------|------------|-------|
| *Pisolithus tinctorius* (Dead Man's Foot) | Mine reclamation, mycorrhizal | Advanced | Ectomycorrhizal; requires host trees |
| *Fomes fomentarius* (Tinder Conk) | Long-term wood decomposition | Advanced | Very slow-growing but persistent |
| *Laetiporus sulphureus* (Chicken of the Woods) | Deadwood decomposition | Moderate | Aggressive; can compete with other fungi |

## Practical Implementation

### Site Assessment

Before any intervention:

1. **Identify contaminants:** Test soil for heavy metals, hydrocarbons, or other pollutants when possible
2. **Assess existing fungal life:** Healthy ecosystems already have fungal communities — evaluate whether augmentation is needed
3. **Match species to conditions:** Substrate availability, moisture, temperature, and light conditions determine which species will thrive
4. **Consider ecological impact:** Introducing non-native fungal species can disrupt existing mycological communities

### Ethical Considerations

Guerrilla mycology raises important questions:

- **Informed consent:** Neighboring landowners and communities may be affected by ecological interventions
- **Non-native species:** Introducing fungi outside their native range carries ecological risks
- **Liability:** Unauthorized introduction of organisms into public or private land may have legal consequences
- **Effectiveness claims:** Anecdotal success stories should not be overstated — controlled studies are essential for validating techniques

### Community Applications

- **Urban food forests:** Integrating edible and medicinal mushrooms into community gardens
- **Brownfield restoration:** Remediation of abandoned industrial sites for community use
- **Waste stream diversion:** Using coffee grounds, spent grain, and other waste substrates for mushroom cultivation
- **Education:** Teaching fungal biology and ecology through hands-on community workshops

## Limitations and Criticisms

- **Scale:** Guerrilla methods are effective at the community scale but cannot replace industrial remediation for heavily contaminated Superfund sites
- **Monitoring:** Without systematic monitoring, outcomes are anecdotal and hard to evaluate
- **Regulatory barriers:** In many jurisdictions, introducing organisms into the environment without permits is illegal, regardless of intent
- **Oversimplification:** Popular accounts sometimes overstate the capabilities of mycoremediation — fungi cannot solve all contamination problems
- **Reproducibility:** Field conditions introduce enormous variability; results from controlled lab studies may not translate directly
