## Overview

**Nyssa sylvatica**, commonly known as black tupelo, blackgum, or sour gum, is
a medium-sized deciduous tree native to [[mollison-designers-legume-shade-tree-pasture-systems]] and street tree
due to its straight trunk, absence of significant pest problems, and tolerance
of urban conditions.

Black tupelo typically reaches 15–25 meters in height with a trunk diameter
of 50–80 cm. In bottomland sites it may exceed 30 meters. The species is
extremely long-lived, with individuals exceeding 600 years documented in old-
growth forests.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Nyssa sylvatica** Marsh. was described by Humphrey Marshall in 1785. The
genus name honors the mythological Greek water nymph Nyssa, reflecting the
tree's affinity for wet habitats. The specific epithet *sylvatica* means "of
the forest." It is classified in the family Nyssaceae, though some treatments
place it in Cornaceae.

Leaves are 5–12 cm long and 3–6 cm wide, simple, elliptic to obovate, with
entire to finely serrate margins. They are dark glossy green above and paler
below, turning brilliant scarlet, orange, or purple in autumn. The color
development is triggered by cool nights and typically peaks in mid-October.

The bark on mature trees is distinctive: dark gray to nearly black, deeply
furrowed into irregular, blocky plates resembling alligator hide. Young bark
is reddish-brown and smooth. Branching pattern is characteristically horizontal
with distinctive swollen or "crotched" branch unions.

Flowers are small, greenish-white, and inconspicuous, appearing in May–June.
The species is polygamo-dioecious, with male and female flowers typically on
separate trees. The fruit is a dark blue to black drupe, 8–15 mm long,
fleshy and somewhat sour-tasting, ripening in September–October.

## Distribution and Habitat

Black tupelo ranges from southwestern Maine and extreme southern Ontario west
to southern Michigan, eastern Missouri, and eastern Texas, and south to
central Florida. It is most abundant in the Atlantic and Gulf coastal plains
and the lower Mississippi Valley.

The species occupies a remarkably broad ecological amplitude. It occurs on
dry upland ridges, acidic sandy soils of the coastal plain, well-drained
slopes, and poorly drained bottomland swamps. In the southeastern United
States, trees from swamp sites were historically recognized as a separate
variety (*N. sylvatica* var. *biflora*), now often treated as the distinct
species *Nyssa biflora* (swamp tupelo).

Common overstory associates include [[fagus-grandifolia]], and [[acer-rubrum]]
- [[ulmus-americana]]
- [[carya-ovata]]
