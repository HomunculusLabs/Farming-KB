# Archaeospora

## Description

Archaeospora is a small genus of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) belonging to the family Archaeosporaceae within the order Glomales. The genus was delimited relatively recently based on differences in selective rDNA primer sequences in polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analyses. Archaeospora contains only two described species but represents one of the most phylogenetically ancient lineages within the Glomales. The genus is notable because some species produce both Acaulospora-like and Glomus-like spores from the same thallus, a rare phenomenon among AMF.

## Classification

- **Domain:** Eukarya
- **Phylum:** Zygomycota (Glomeromycota in modern taxonomy)
- **Order:** Glomales
- **Family:** Archaeosporaceae
- **Genus:** Archaeospora

## Key Facts

- Contains only **two described species**, one of which is Archaeospora leptoticha.
- Species in this genus are genetically more closely related to Geosiphon pyriforme than to other AMF, as revealed by 18S ribosomal DNA (rDNA) sequence analysis.
- Some Archaeospora species produce **both Acaulospora-like and Glomus-like spores from the same thallus**, challenging traditional genus-level taxonomy based on spore morphology.
- Placed in the family Archaeosporaceae, one of two new families (along with Paraglomaceae) erected to accommodate phylogenetically ancient glomalean lineages.
- Additional species currently classified in other genera are expected to be transferred to Archaeospora as molecular analyses continue.

## Phylogenetic Significance

Archaeospora occupies a basal position in the phylogeny of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. 18S rDNA sequences place Archaeospora species closer to Geosiphon pyriforme — a non-mycorrhizal fungus that hosts photosynthetic Nostoc cyanobacteria — than to the core Glomales. This relationship suggests that the common ancestor of Archaeospora and Geosiphon may represent one of the earliest divergences within the glomalean lineage, potentially dating back over 400 million years.

## Ecological Distribution

- Globally distributed across diverse habitats, including tropical forests, grasslands, deserts, and temperate old fields.
- Archaeospora leptoticha is reported as widely distributed on most continents.
- Frequently recovered in second-cycle trap cultures from habitats in Kenya, Venezuela, Namibia, Arizona, and West Virginia.
- Typically represented by one species per site in community surveys.
- Found in both temperate and tropical climates, indicating broad environmental tolerance.

## Spore Morphology

- Spores are relatively small and may exhibit either Acaulospora-type or Glomus-type morphology, depending on the species and developmental context.
- The production of morphologically divergent spore types from a single fungal individual is exceptional among AMF and has important implications for species identification.
- Molecular methods (PCR with selective primers) are essential for reliable identification, as morphological characters alone are insufficient.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Evolutionary biology:** Archaeospora provides critical evidence for understanding the early evolution of the mycorrhizal symbiosis, one of the oldest mutualisms on land.
- **Taxonomic reform:** The genus exemplifies the ongoing revolution in AMF taxonomy driven by molecular phylogenetics, where DNA sequence data have revealed that morphological characters alone are inadequate for genus-level classification.
- **Biodiversity assessment:** Likely undercounted in surveys that rely solely on spore morphology; molecular probing methods are needed for accurate detection.
- **Co-evolution:** The relationship between Archaeospora and its plant hosts spans hundreds of millions of years, offering insights into the co-evolutionary dynamics of mycorrhizal symbioses.

## Isolation and Culture

- Successfully cultured on Sudangrass (Sorghum sudanense) in greenhouse pot cultures at INVAM.
- Detected through trap-pot cultures, where it often appears in first or second culture cycles.
- Spores extracted via standard wet-sieving and sucrose-density gradient centrifugation.
- Molecular identification using selective rDNA primer sequences (Morton and Redecker 2001).
