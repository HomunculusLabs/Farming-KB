## Overview

**Genipa americana**, commonly known as genipap, jagua, or genipa berry, is a medium-sized evergreen tree native to tropical regions of the Americas.

It ranges from the Caribbean and southern Mexico through Central America to northern South America, including the Amazon Basin.

The species is renowned for its edible fruit and its use as a natural body paint, producing a blue-black dye that has been used by Indigenous peoples for millennia.

The fruit also has a long history of use in traditional medicine across its range, with applications for respiratory ailments, digestive disorders, and wound healing.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Genipa americana** L. belongs to the family Rubiaceae, subfamily Ixoroideae, tribe Gardenieae.

It was described by Carl Linnaeus in the 1753 first edition of *Species Plantarum*.

The tree typically reaches 8–20 m in height, with a trunk diameter of 30–60 cm.

The bark is grayish-brown, rough, and fissured, often exfoliating in rectangular plates.

Leaves are opposite, simple, elliptic to obovate, 15–35 cm long and 7–15 cm wide, with a glossy dark green adaxial surface.

The inflorescence is a terminal or axillary cyme bearing numerous fragrant, tubular flowers.

Flowers are yellowish-white, 4–6 cm long, with a narrow corolla tube and five spreading lobes.

The fruit is a berry, ovoid to subglobose, 10–15 cm long and 8–12 cm in diameter, weighing 200–400 g.

The rind is thin, yellowish-brown at maturity, with a leathery texture.

The pulp is brownish, gelatinous, and aromatic, containing numerous flattened seeds embedded in a soft mesocarp.

## Distribution and Habitat

Genipap is distributed from southern Florida and the Caribbean (Cuba, Jamaica, Puerto Rico) through Central America to Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, and Brazil.

It is most abundant in lowland tropical moist and wet forests, typically below 500 m elevation.

The species is also cultivated in home gardens and orchards throughout its native range and has been introduced to parts of West Africa and Southeast Asia.

It grows well in a range of tropical soils but prefers deep, well-drained, fertile loams with pH 5.5–7.0.

Annual rainfall requirements are 1200–3000 mm, with a distinct dry season tolerated.

## Culinary and Traditional Uses

The ripe fruit pulp is consumed fresh, often sweetened with sugar or mixed with milk to make beverages, jams, and desserts.

In Brazil, genipap is used to prepare *refresco de genipapo*, a popular regional drink.

The fruit is also fermented into wine and used in the production of liqueurs.

The unripe fruit produces a blue-black dye upon contact with skin or cloth, a property resulting from the enzymatic oxidation of genipin.

Indigenous peoples throughout the Amazon use jagua dye for temporary body painting, ritual tattooing, and clothing decoration.

The dye darkens over 12–24 hours from pale blue-green to deep blue-black and persists on skin for 1–3 weeks.

## Bioactive Compounds

The most significant compound is genipin, an iridoid glycoside aglycone responsible for the dye properties.

Genipin is formed by enzymatic hydrolysis of geniposide (genipin-1-O-β-D-glucoside) present in the unripe fruit.

Genipin reacts with amino acids and proteins to form blue-black pigments, a reaction exploited in both traditional body art and modern food chemistry.

The fruit also contains geniposidic acid, gardenoside, and several other iridoid glycosides.

Pharmacological studies have demonstrated genipin has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, hepatoprotective, and neuroprotective activities in vitro and in animal models.

Antimicrobial activity against *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Escherichia coli*, and *Candida albicans* has been documented for genipap extracts.

## Cultivation

Genipap is propagated primarily from seeds, which require scarification and warm, moist conditions for germination.

Seed germination typically takes 30–60 days, with viability declining rapidly after 2–3 months of storage.

Trees begin bearing fruit at 3–5 years from seed under favorable conditions.

Fruiting occurs mainly during the rainy season, though in some regions trees produce two crops per year.

Yields per tree range from 50 to 200 fruits annually, depending on tree age and growing conditions.

The species shows promise for agroforestry systems due to its tolerance of partial shade and its multiple-use character (fruit, dye, timber).

The wood is moderately heavy, durable, and used locally for construction, tool handles, and furniture making.
