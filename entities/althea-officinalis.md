## Overview

*Althaea officinalis*, commonly known as marshmallow, is a herbaceous perennial in the family Malvaceae. Native to Europe, Western Asia, and North Africa, the species has been used medicinally for over 2,000 years, with its root and leaf preparations serving as primary demulcent remedies in Western herbalism.

The plant derives its common name from its natural habitat in salt marshes and damp meadows. Its therapeutic reputation centers on the abundant mucilage contained in its root, which provides soothing, anti-inflammatory, and wound-healing properties when applied topically or consumed internally.

Marshmallow root remains an important herb in contemporary phytotherapy, particularly in European and [[dmt-containing-plants-united-states-halpern]] and parts of eastern Canada.

In cultivation, marshmallow adapts to a range of soil types but performs best in deep, fertile, moisture-retentive loams. The plant is winter-hardy to USDA zone 3 and tolerates temporary waterlogging.

## Bioactive Compounds and Pharmacology

The defining constituent of *A. officinalis* root is its mucilage content, comprising 20-30% of dry root weight. This mucilage is a heteropolysaccharide composed primarily of galacturonic acid, rhamnose, galactose, and glucuronic acid residues.

Mucilage acts as a demulcent by forming a protective, soothing film over mucous membranes. This physical barrier action reduces irritation and inflammation in the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and urinary tracts, explaining the plant's efficacy in coughs, sore throats, and gastric irritation.

Additional constituents include flavonoids (hypolaetin-8-glucoside, isoquercitrin), phenolic acids (coumaric, caffeic, and ferulic acid derivatives), and trace amounts of coumarins (herniarin). Scopoletin has been identified in the root and may contribute mild spasmolytic activity.

The root contains approximately 5-10% pectin, which enhances the mucilaginous properties and contributes to the plant's wound-healing and gastroprotective effects. Polysaccharide fractions have demonstrated immunomodulatory activity in vitro, stimulating phagocytosis.

Anti-inflammatory activity has been confirmed in animal models, with aqueous root extracts showing significant reduction of edema and carrageenan-induced inflammation. These effects are attributed to the combined action of mucilage, flavonoids, and phenolic acids.

## Traditional and Modern Uses

The genus name *Althaea* derives from the Greek *altho* (to heal), reflecting the plant's longstanding medicinal reputation. Theophrastus and Dioscorides both described marshmallow as a remedy for wounds, coughs, and digestive complaints.

In traditional European herbalism, the root was prepared as a cold maceration (decoction destroys mucilage) to extract maximum mucilage content. This mucilaginous tea was used for bronchitis, whooping cough, gastritis, and cystitis.

The French confection pâte de guimauve, the original marshmallow, was historically made from marshmallow root mucilage whipped with sugar and egg whites. Modern commercial marshmallows no longer contain any *Althaea* constituents.

Contemporary applications include herbal cough syrups, throat lozenges, and digestive teas. The German Commission E and EMA have approved marshmallow root for irritation of oral and pharyngeal mucosa and for dry coughs.

## Nutritional Profile

The leaves of *A. officinalis* are edible and have been consumed as a vegetable in traditional diets across Europe and the Middle East. Young leaves can be eaten raw in salads or cooked as a pot herb, with a mild, slightly sweet flavor.

The root, while primarily valued for mucilage, contains modest amounts of vitamins and minerals including vitamin C, calcium, magnesium, and iron. However, the primary nutritional contribution of marshmallow preparations is dietary fiber in the form of soluble polysaccharides.
