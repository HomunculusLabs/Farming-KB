## Overview

**Morella inodora** (Bartram ex W. Bartram) Kartesz, commonly known as scentless bayberry or odorless wax myrtle, is an evergreen to semi-evergreen shrub in the family Myricaceae. It is native to the southeastern United States, where it occurs in wetland margins, sandy streambanks, and pocosin habitats.

The species epithet "inodora" refers to its lack of aromatic fragrance, distinguishing it from the closely related and strongly scented *Morella cerifera*. Despite the name, the plant does produce wax-coated fruits typical of the genus.

The genus name *Morella* commemorates the French botanist Pierre Morelet. The combination of non-aromatic foliage and wetland habitat preference distinguishes this species from all other North American *Morella* taxa.

It serves an important ecological role as a nitrogen-fixing woody shrub in wetland and riparian ecosystems. Its root-associated actinorhizal symbionts enrich poor sandy soils.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

### Taxonomy

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Fagales
- **Family:** Myricaceae
- **Genus:** *Morella* L.
- **Species:** *M. inodora* (Bartram ex W. Bartram) Kartesz

The species was historically treated within *Myrica* and has also been cited as *Myrica inodora* Bartram. John Kartesz transferred it to *Morella* in the late 20th century following taxonomic revisions of Myricaceae.

*Morella inodora* is most closely related to *M. cerifera* and *M. caroliniensis*, with which it frequently co-occurs and occasionally hybridizes. It was first described by William Bartram from material collected in the southeastern coastal plain.

### Morphology

*M. inodora* is a rounded shrub typically reaching 1.5 to 3 meters in height. It forms dense, multi-stemmed thickets through rhizomatous spread in favorable conditions.

Leaves are alternate, narrowly oblanceolate to obovate, 4 to 10 cm long and 1 to 2.5 cm wide. Leaf margins are entire or remotely serrulate toward the tip. Unlike *M. cerifera*, the foliage lacks the characteristic aromatic scent when crushed.

The upper leaf surface is dark green and sparsely pubescent with simple trichomes. The lower surface is lighter green with yellowish to brownish glandular dots. Leaves are persistent through mild winters, with partial abscission in colder portions of the range.

Flowers are unisexual, borne in compact catkins that emerge in early spring before or with new leaf growth. Male catkins are 1 to 2.5 cm long with bracteate scales. Female catkins are shorter, 5 to 10 mm, bearing reduced flowers in axillary clusters.

Fruit is a drupaceous nutlet, approximately 3 to 4 mm in diameter, borne in compact spikes. Each fruit is covered with a thin waxy coating, though less conspicuous than that of *M. cerifera*.

Fruits ripen from late summer through early autumn. The wax coating is composed primarily of triglycerides of palmitic and myristic acids, characteristic of the Myricaceae.

The bark is smooth and gray-brown on young stems, becoming rougher and shallowly fissured with age. Branches are slender and flexible, with prominent lenticels on younger growth.

## Distribution and Habitat

*M. inodora* is endemic to the southeastern United States. Its range extends from southeastern Virginia southward through the coastal plain to northern Florida and westward to eastern Texas.

The species also occurs disjunctly in the southern Appalachian foothills and Piedmont, primarily along major river drainages. It is most abundant in the Atlantic and Gulf coastal plains.

Preferred habitats include sandy or peaty margins of swamps, bays, and blackwater streams. It is a common component of pocosin vegetation in the Carolinas and Virginia, where it grows alongside *Vaccinium* spp., *Kalmia latifolia*, and *Comptonia peregrina*.

It tolerates seasonally saturated soils and periodic flooding, though it does not persist in permanently inundated sites. It is most competitive on acidic, nutrient-poor sands where its nitrogen-fixing capacity provides a significant advantage.

The species exhibits moderate salt tolerance and can be found on maritime forest edges and barrier island interiors. Soil pH preference ranges from 4.5 to 6.5, consistent with the acidic soils of Atlantic and Gulf coastal plain wetlands.

Elevation range is from sea level to approximately 150 meters. It is most frequently encountered on flat topography within the coastal plain, though Piedmont occurrences are typically along stream terraces at slightly higher elevations.

## Ecology and Interactions

As a member of Myricaceae, *M. inodora* forms root nodules containing the actinomycete *Frankia* spp. This symbiosis enables atmospheric [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]], making the species an important nutrient contributor in oligotrophic wetland ecosystems.

The fruits are consumed by a variety of birds, including northern bobwhite, wild turkey, and various songbirds. The dense foliage provides nesting cover and protective shelter for numerous bird species.

White-tailed deer browse the foliage lightly, particularly in winter when other forage is scarce. The shrub's thicket-forming habit creates valuable escape cover for small mammals.

In fire-prone pocosin landscapes, *M. inodora* resprouts vigorously from its rhizomatous root system after fire. It is considered fire-adapted, though less tolerant of frequent fire than *M. cerifera*.

Nitrogen fixation rates in *Morella* species are influenced by soil moisture and [[query-how-does-phosphorus-availability-change-across-soil-ph-levels]]. In saturated pocosin soils, nitrogen inputs from *M. inodora* can significantly enhance the fertility of otherwise nutrient-poor sites.

## Cultivation and Uses

*M. inodora* is occasionally cultivated as a native landscape shrub in the southeastern United States. It is valued for its dense evergreen foliage, tolerance of wet soils, and wildlife-attracting fruit.

It performs well in full sun to partial shade on acidic, sandy to loamy soils. Established plants exhibit good [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] once the root system develops. Propagation is achieved through semi-hardwood cuttings or seed, though seeds require cold stratification.

Cold stratification of 60 to 90 days at 2 to 5 degrees Celsius improves germination rates significantly. Seed viability declines rapidly in storage, making fresh seed collection important for propagation programs.

The species has limited ornamental use compared to *M. cerifera*, largely because it lacks the aromatic foliage that makes the latter popular for hedges and specimen plantings. It is primarily used in ecological restoration and wetland mitigation plantings.

Several native plant nurseries in the southeastern U.S. offer *M. inodora* for habitat restoration projects. Its tolerance of compacted and poorly drained soils makes it suitable for stormwater management installations and riparian buffer plantings.

The wax coating on fruits was historically rendered for candle-making, though yields are lower than from *M. cerifera*. [[halpern-sacramental-peyote-and-native-american-church]] groups used various *Morella* species medicinally, but specific ethnobotanical records for *M. inodora* are sparse.

Some ethnobotanical literature suggests that decoctions of the bark and leaves of related *Morella* species were used to treat fever and gastrointestinal ailments. The specific application to *M. inodora* has not been well documented in the ethnographic record.

## Conservation Status

*M. inodora* is not currently listed as threatened or endangered at the federal level. However, it is considered rare or uncommon throughout much of its range, particularly in the northern and western portions.

Coastal plain habitat loss from development and agriculture has reduced populations in some areas. The conversion of pocosin wetlands to forestry and agriculture has been the primary threat.

It is tracked as a species of conservation concern by several southeastern state heritage programs. The species' dependence on wetland habitats makes it vulnerable to drainage and hydrological alteration.

Climate change poses a potential long-term threat through altered precipitation patterns and sea-level rise in the coastal plain. Increased drought frequency may stress populations at the xeric end of the species' tolerance range, while saltwater intrusion could degrade inland pocosin habitats.
