## Avalanche

## Description

An avalanche phenomenon is a rapid flow of snow down a sloping surface, representing one of the most powerful and destructive natural phenomena in mountainous and cold-climate regions. Avalanches can incorporate not only snow but also mud, boulders, clay, and vegetation, generating forces that destroy forests, infrastructure, and buildings across their path. Mollison provides extensive technical coverage of avalanche mechanics, drawing heavily on G.M. Kuaeva's 1975 research on the Greater Caucasus, and integrates this knowledge into cold-climate [[permaculture-design-principles]].

## Classification

- **Category**: Natural hazard / Geomorphic process
- **Types**: Snow avalanche, rock avalanche, debris avalanche, mixed snow-mud avalanche, snow-dust avalanche
- **Frequency classifications**: Episodic (approximately every 35 years), Systematic (annual — unusual), Periodic (rare extreme events from heavy snowfall)

## Key Facts

### Triggering Conditions

Avalanches occur on slopes greater than 6 degrees wherever snow builds up to 15 cm or more. Specific triggers include:

- Crust breakage by strong winds exceeding 13-18 km/sec
- Sudden freeze followed by thaw cycles creating weak layers
- Warm winds causing snowmelt and lubrication between layers
- Saturation of deeper unstable snow layers by meltwater over buried hoarfrost or ice/earth layers
- Sudden falls of 50-69 cm of snow followed by long cloud-free periods
- Direct triggers: gunshots, collapsing snow cornices, strong wind gusts, skiers, antelope, rabbits

80% of all avalanches occur where old, compact, or dry snows are dislodged by condensation of moisture from air with greater than 70% humidity. Remaining avalanches typically result from gusty winds or meltwater lubrication during thaw periods, usually between midday and 3 PM in fine weather.

### Progressive Snow Movement Modes

Snow moves through six progressive modes before full avalanche, each with increasing velocity and destructive potential:

1. **Microcreep**: Freeze/thaw cycles and gravity advance downslope a few centimetres per day
2. **Slip (Snow Boards)**: Thick snow masses slip metres per day on weak underlying layers
3. **Snow mound advance**: Streamflow-like advance from rocky uplands
4. **Snow bodies**: En masse movement down short slopes
5. **Snow slides**: Velocities of 4-5 m/sec, can be arrested by terraces
6. **Full avalanche**: Accelerating on long slopes, reaching speeds up to 6.5 m/sec

### Destructive Forces

The destructive power of avalanches comes from two components:

- **Snow mass impact**: 4-54 tonnes per square metre depending on total mass and entrained material
- **Compressed air blast**: Travels at up to 330 m/sec (speed of sound), the primary destructive agent. Snow-dust avalanches produce waves of 10-100 m/sec and develop pressures of 35 atmospheres — sufficient to fell trees and demolish buildings

The air blast often travels beyond the avalanche mass itself, carrying upslope onto opposite valley walls and destroying structures not directly in the avalanche path.

### Natural Indicators

Trees serve as reliable long-term avalanche indicators:

- Downslope lean of trunks from repeated impact and pressure
- Absence of lower limbs and understory vegetation in avalanche tracks
- Bare "trains" with different age classes of pioneer species, especially aspen
- Blowdown forests radiating from avalanche snout paths
- These indicators persist for 20-30 years in coniferous forests

### Prevention and Mitigation

Effective avalanche defence operates at multiple scales:

- Preservation of steep-slope forests as the primary natural defence
- Placement of essential services in tunnels through avalanche zones
- V-shaped barriers designed to split avalanche fronts
- Prevention of settlement and infrastructure in mapped high-risk zones
- Snow barriers on plateaus above slopes to hold snow in place above vulnerable terrain
- Cross-slope terraces and walls (less effective but supplementary)
- Monitoring and warning systems for triggering conditions

## Relevance to Site Design

In [[permaculture-design]] for [[query-best-kiwifruit-varieties-for-cold-climates]], avalanche risk assessment is a fundamental [[coleman-greenhouse-orientation-site-selection-wind-protection-coleman]] criterion. Structures, gardens, and animal systems must be located outside mapped avalanche paths and runout zones. The integration of forest preservation on upper slopes with settlement in valley floors provides both avalanche protection and productive land use, reflecting the [[permaculture]] principle of working with [[natural-patterns-in-permaculture-design]] rather than attempting to engineer against them.

### Snow Crust and Stability

The [[albedo-and-solar-radiation]] crust that forms on snow surfaces plays a critical role in slope stability. This crust is a seasonal protection only, breaking up when iced or shattered by wind. Understanding crust formation and degradation helps predict avalanche timing:

- Crust forms during clear, calm periods when solar radiation melts and refreezes surface snow
- Crust degradation occurs during warm, humid, or windy conditions
- The transition from stable crust to unstable snow mass can happen within hours
- Monitoring crust condition is a key component of avalanche forecasting programmes

### Integration with Permaculture Zone Planning

Avalanche risk directly influences zone planning in cold-climate permaculture:

- Zone 0 (dwelling): Must be located in verified safe zones outside any avalanche path or runout zone
- Zone 1 ([[comparison-zone-1-intensive-garden-vs-zone-5-wilderness]]): Also requires safe location, as year-round access is essential
- Zone 2-3 (orchard, pasture): Can extend toward but not into avalanche-prone slopes
- Zone 4-5 (forest, wilderness): May overlap with avalanche terrain; [[forest-management-strategies]] here serves dual purposes of production and avalanche defence
- Access roads: Must provide year-round connectivity, requiring protection or routing away from avalanche chutes
