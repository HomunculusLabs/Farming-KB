# Stylosanthes guianensis (Stylo / Townsville Stylo)

## Description

Stylosanthes guianensis, commonly referred to as stylo or Townsville stylo, is a perennial tropical legume widely used as a pasture and forage species in the tropics and subtropics. In [[bill-mollison]]'s *Permaculture: A Designer's Manual*, it appears in the plant species listing as "Stylo, Townsville," recognizing its importance as a tropical forage legume in designed agricultural systems.

## Classification

- **Kingdom**: Plantae
- **Family**: Fabaceae (Legume family)
- **Subfamily**: Faboideae
- **Genus**: Stylosanthes
- **Species**: S. guianensis
- **Common names**: Stylo, Townsville stylo, Brazilian lucerne, Schofield stylo

## Key Facts

- **Growth habit**: Erect to semi-erect perennial herb or small shrub, typically growing 30–150 cm tall, depending on variety and growing conditions.
- **Native range**: Tropical and subtropical regions of Central and South America, from Mexico through Brazil. Now widely distributed across the tropical world, particularly in Australia, Southeast Asia, and Africa.
- **Climate adaptation**: Well-adapted to warm, humid tropical and subtropical climates with annual rainfall of 700–3000 mm. Exhibits good drought tolerance once established. Some varieties show moderate tolerance to flooding.
- **Soil requirements**: Exceptionally tolerant of infertile, acid soils with high aluminum saturation — a condition that limits many other legumes. Prefers well-drained soils but tolerates a range of textures from sands to heavy clays. pH tolerance ranges from approximately 4.0 to 7.5.
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Forms effective nodules with Bradyrhizobium bacteria, fixing atmospheric nitrogen and contributing to soil fertility improvement. This makes it a valuable green manure and companion species in mixed plantings.
- **Seed production**: Produces hard-coated seeds that can persist in the soil seed bank for years, enabling natural regeneration after disturbance or dry seasons.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Permaculture

### Tropical Pasture Improvement

Stylosanthes guianensis is one of the most extensively used tropical pasture legumes globally. It is sown into native or improved grass pastures to increase protein content and overall carrying capacity. When grown in association with grasses such as Panicum maximum (guinea grass) or Brachiaria spp., it can significantly improve animal weight gain compared to grass-only pastures.

### Soil Fertility Restoration

As a nitrogen-fixing legume adapted to poor soils, stylo plays a critical role in restoring fertility to degraded tropical lands. It has been widely used in land rehabilitation programs, particularly in northern Australia and sub-Saharan Africa. The species can build soil organic matter and nitrogen levels over several years, preparing sites for more demanding crops.

### Ground Cover and Weed Suppression

The dense, spreading growth habit of S. guianensis provides effective ground cover that suppresses weed growth and reduces soil temperature. In permaculture systems, it can be used as a living mulch beneath trees or between crop rows. Its persistence through dry seasons helps maintain soil protection year-round.

### Integration in Alley Cropping

In alley cropping and hedgerow intercropping systems, stylo can be sown as an understory legume between rows of woody perennials. It contributes nitrogen to the system, provides high-quality forage for cut-and-carry livestock feeding, and can be slashed to produce green manure mulch for adjacent crop beds.

### Seed Harvest and Persistence

The hard-seeded nature of stylo is both an advantage and a management consideration. Once established, the soil seed bank enables natural reseeding and long-term persistence even under heavy grazing or drought. However, initial establishment may require scarification or hot water treatment of seeds to break dormancy.

### Biodiversity and Habitat

Stylo provides nectar and pollen for a range of beneficial insects, including bees and predatory wasps. In diverse permaculture plantings, it contributes to the overall biodiversity and ecological function of the system, supporting pollination and natural pest control.

## Varieties and Cultivars

Several commercially important cultivars have been developed, including 'Schofield', 'Graham', 'Cook', and 'Endeavour'. These vary in their adaptation to specific rainfall zones, soil types, and disease resistance profiles. Anthracnose disease (caused by Colletotrichum gloeosporioides) has been a major limitation in some regions, driving the development of resistant lines.

## Management Considerations

Stylo should be managed with appropriate grazing pressure — continuous heavy grazing can deplete the seed bank over time. Rotational grazing or periodic spelling allows seed set and seed bank replenishment. In permaculture contexts, strategic cutting for mulch or forage at flowering stage maximizes both nitrogen contribution and biomass return. The species is relatively low-maintenance once established, requiring no nitrogen fertilizer and minimal phosphorus on most tropical soils.

### Integration with Perennial Crop Systems

In established permaculture orchards and food forests, stylo can be maintained as a productive understory ground cover. It tolerates partial shade and continues to fix nitrogen under tree canopies, providing a self-renewing fertility source for the overstory trees. When managed by periodic slashing, it provides in-situ mulch without the need to import organic matter from external sources.

### Role in Succession Planting

Stylo fits naturally into succession planting strategies. As an early-to-mid successional species, it can be used to bridge the gap between initial site clearing and the establishment of long-term perennial crops. It builds soil, suppresses weeds, and creates favorable microclimates for the establishment of woody perennials. As the overstory matures and shade increases, stylo naturally declines, making way for more shade-tolerant understory species.

## Ecological Interactions

The deep root system of Stylosanthes guianensis supports mycorrhizal associations that enhance phosphorus uptake from infertile tropical soils. These mycorrhizal networks can connect with neighboring plants, facilitating nutrient exchange within the plant community. This below-ground connectivity is an important consideration in polyculture design.
