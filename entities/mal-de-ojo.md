## Description

Mal de Ojo ("evil eye") is a folk illness found throughout Peru and Latin America, caused by the magical action of one person's glance upon another -- not necessarily with evil intentions, though it can be motivated by envy. The condition is especially common among children whose personal attraction may catch the evil eye. It is documented by Valdivia (1964) and recognized alongside [[susto]], dano, and pulsario as one of the major categories of magical illness in the Peruvian Amazon.

## Classification

- **Type:** Folk illness / culture-bound syndrome
- **Geographic distribution:** Throughout Latin America, Mediterranean regions, and globally across many cultures
- **Primary demographic:** Especially common in children
- **Causation model:** Magical -- caused by the power of a person's gaze
- **Alternative names:** Evil eye, evil eye syndrome
- **Related conditions:** Susto, Dano, Pulsario

## Key Facts

- Symptoms include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, fever, loss of weight, insomnia, and sadness
- In popular belief, the illness is caused by the magical action of one person's glance upon another
- The glance need not carry evil intentions -- admiration or attention can also trigger the condition
- Mal de ojo can be motivated by envy, particularly toward attractive or healthy children
- The illness is very frequent among children whose personal attraction may catch the evil eye
- Mothers place charms or amulets on the wrist or neck of their youngsters as preventive measures
- Daily exorcism with tobacco smoke is practiced by mothers to ward off the evil eye
- The condition represents a culturally patterned explanation for childhood illness that lacks clear biomedical diagnosis
- In Belen (Iquitos), mal de ojo is recognized alongside susto, dano, and pulsario as one of the major magical illnesses
- The condition is documented by Valdivia (1964:84) with characteristic symptom profiles

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

Mal de ojo is part of the illness taxonomy that structures ayahuasca healing in the Peruvian Amazon. While it is not always treated with ayahuasca directly (mothers' preventive rituals with tobacco smoke and amulets are the first line of defense), severe or persistent cases may be brought to an ayahuasquero for treatment. The condition highlights the role of tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) as a ritual plant with protective and exorcistic properties in Amazonian folk medicine. For cultivators and researchers, mal de ojo illustrates how plant-based healing traditions address not only acute conditions but also everyday anxieties about children's health, demonstrating the pervasiveness of magical thinking in Amazonian health culture. The preventive use of tobacco smoke connects to broader patterns of ritual plant use in the Amazon.

## Preventive and Treatment Practices

- Charms and amulets placed on children's wrists or necks
- Daily exorcism with tobacco smoke performed by mothers
- For severe cases, consultation with an ayahuasquero for ritual treatment
- Ayahuasca visions may help identify the person whose gaze caused the affliction
- Traditional healing techniques including whistling, singing, and blowing smoke over the patient
- The use of a narcotic-like tobacco (possibly Nicotiana tabacum) in exorcistic rituals

## Global Parallels

Mal de ojo is one of the most widespread folk illness beliefs in the world, found not only throughout Latin America but also in Mediterranean Europe, the Middle East, North Africa, and South Asia. The belief that a person's gaze can cause illness, especially in children, appears to be a near-universal cultural pattern. In the Peruvian Amazon, mal de ojo is integrated into the broader system of magical illness that also includes susto, dano, and pulsario, each distinguished by its specific causal mechanism.

## Tobacco as Protective Plant

The use of tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) in preventing and treating mal de ojo highlights the plant's role beyond its recreational use. In the Amazonian context, tobacco serves multiple ritual functions:
- Exorcistic tool: Mothers blow tobacco smoke over children daily to ward off the evil eye
- Healing instrument: Healers blow cigarette smoke over the body of sick patients
- Suggestive agent: Tobacco may have mild hallucinogenic properties that enhance the healer's ritual effectiveness (see Janiger and Dobkin de Rios, n.d.)
- Protective boundary: Tobacco smoke creates a ritual barrier between the vulnerable person and malevolent spiritual forces

## Preventive Practices in Detail

The daily rituals mothers perform to protect children from mal de ojo include:
- Placing charms or amulets on the wrist or neck of youngsters
- Exorcising children each day with tobacco smoke
- Teaching children to avoid drawing excessive attention or admiration
- Monitoring children for early symptoms (nausea, fever, sadness, weight loss)
- Consulting a healer if preventive measures fail and symptoms persist

## Cultural Significance

Mal de ojo serves important social functions beyond its medical dimension. The belief encourages moderation in expressing admiration and teaches children to be aware of social dynamics. The attribution of childhood illness to the evil eye provides an explanatory framework that reduces parental guilt and directs attention to the social environment rather than personal failure.

## Ayahuasca Treatment for Mal de Ojo

When preventive measures fail and a child is believed to have contracted mal de ojo, the family may consult an ayahuasquero for treatment. The healer uses ayahuasca visions to identify the person whose gaze caused the affliction. During the ceremony, the healer blows cigarette smoke over the child's body, recites orations, and performs exorcistic rituals. The healer's ability to name the source of the evil eye and to neutralize its effects through ritual intervention provides the family with reassurance and a sense of control over the child's illness.

The treatment of mal de ojo with ayahuasca demonstrates how the folk healing system addresses conditions across the full spectrum of severity, from everyday childhood ailments to life-threatening bewitchment. The ayahuasquero serves as a general practitioner of magical medicine, treating conditions that range from the common (mal de ojo) to the complex (dano with multiple magical agents).

## Cultural Meaning of the Gaze

In the Amazonian folk illness system, the gaze carries enormous power. A person's glance can cause illness regardless of intent, which creates a social environment where attention and admiration become potentially dangerous. This belief system has several social consequences:
- It encourages moderation in expressing admiration, especially toward children
- It provides an explanation for sudden childhood illness that does not blame the parents
- It creates a social awareness of the power of envy and jealousy
- It reinforces the role of healers as mediators between the vulnerable and the powerful
- It integrates the everyday experience of looking and being looked at into a cosmic framework of cause and effect
