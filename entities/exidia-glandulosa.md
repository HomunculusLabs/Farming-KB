## Overview

Exidia glandulosa is a jelly fungus in the family Auriculariaceae, commonly
known as black witch's butter or black jelly roll. It is one of the most
frequently encountered saprotrophic fungi on dead hardwood branches in
temperate forests of Europe and [[comparison-fruiting-body-vs-mycelium-extracts-medicinal-mushrooms]] is gelatinous when wet, shriveling to a dark crust in dry
conditions and reviving with rain. Despite its unremarkable appearance, E.
glandulosa plays an important role in early-stage [[fungal-dead-wood-diversity]], ranging from 1 to 5 cm across. Colonies can coalesce into larger
patches covering extensive bark areas.

The upper surface is dark brown to black, densely covered with small raised
papillae giving a granular appearance. The surface is shiny when wet, dull and
wrinkled when dry.

Unlike most jelly fungi, the spore-bearing hymenium is on the upper surface.
The flesh is translucent, dark, and very gelatinous with no distinctive odor.

Basidia are longitudinally septate, producing allantoid (sausage-shaped)
basidiospores measuring 12–18 × 4–6 μm, smooth and hyaline.

## Distribution and Habitat

E. glandulosa has a broad distribution across temperate Northern Hemisphere
regions including Europe, North America, and parts of Asia. It fruits almost
year-round but is most abundant during cool, wet autumn and winter periods.

The species is found almost exclusively on dead attached or recently fallen
hardwood branches, especially oak (Quercus), beech (Fagus), birch (Betula), and
hazel (Corylus). It typically colonizes thin branches (1–5 cm diameter) as a
primary colonizer, preferring shaded, humid microhabitats.

## Ecological Role

As a white-rot saprotroph, E. glandulosa decomposes cellulose and lignin in
dead hardwood branches. Its ability to repeatedly rehydrate suits it to the
fluctuating moisture conditions of exposed branch surfaces.

The gelatinous fruiting body retains moisture, enabling continued spore
production through intermittent dry periods. E. glandulosa is among the first
fungal colonizers of freshly dead twigs, preparing substrate for subsequent
decomposer communities.
