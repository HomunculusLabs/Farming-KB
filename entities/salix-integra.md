## Overview

Salix integra, commonly known as the dappled willow or flamingo willow, is a deciduous shrub species in the family Salicaceae. It is native to eastern Asia, primarily Japan, Korea, and northeastern China.

The species is one of approximately 400 species in the genus Salix, which has a nearly [[hamilton-vegetative-propagation]].

Winter buds are small, reddish-brown, and appressed to the stem, with a single visible bud scale. Bud break occurs early in spring, making the species susceptible to late frost damage in colder regions.

## Distribution and Habitat

In its native range, Salix integra grows along riverbanks, stream margins, and damp lowland areas in temperate East Asia. It occurs from sea level to approximately 1,500 meters elevation.

The species thrives in full sun to partial shade and prefers consistently moist to wet soils. It is adapted to alluvial soils and floodplain environments where periodic inundation occurs.

Outside its native range, it has naturalized in parts of Europe and North America, particularly in riparian corridors. It is considered less invasive than some other Salix species, though it can spread vegetatively from broken stem fragments.

The species tolerates a pH range from approximately 5.0 to 7.5 and can grow in soils with moderate salinity. This adaptability has contributed to its success as an ornamental and bioengineering plant across diverse climates.

In urban settings, Salix integra is planted along stormwater retention basins and drainage swales where its root system helps filter runoff pollutants. Its tolerance of compacted soils makes it suitable for roadside plantings.

## Ecology

Salix integra provides important ecological services in riparian and wetland ecosystems. Its dense root network stabilizes stream banks and reduces soil erosion along waterways.

The catkins are an early-season nectar and pollen source for bees and other pollinators. Foliage serves as browse for deer, rabbits, and other herbivores.

## Cultivation and Uses

Salix integra is primarily propagated through hardwood cuttings taken in late autumn or early spring. Softwood cuttings under mist also root readily.

The species is hardy to USDA zone 5 (−23 °C) and adapts to a wide range of soil types, from heavy clay to sandy loam. It performs best with consistent moisture and benefits from annual pruning to maintain compact form.

Pests include willow aphids (Chaitophorus spp.), which can cause minor foliar damage but rarely threaten plant health. Canker diseases (Cytospora spp.) may infect stressed or damaged stems.

Rust fungi of the genus Melampsora are the most significant foliar pathogens, causing orange pustules on leaf undersides in late summer. Resistant planting stock and removal of infected debris reduce disease pressure.

Establishment is rapid, with rooted cuttings capable of 60 to 90 cm of growth in the first growing season under favorable conditions. Mulching around the base helps retain soil moisture and suppress weed competition.

The cultivar 'Hakuro-nishiki' (syn. 'Flamingo') is the most widely grown form, prized for its tricolor foliage. It is used in mixed borders, container plantings, and as a specimen shrub.

Regular renewal pruning — removing one-third of the oldest stems at ground level each year — maintains the most vibrant foliage color. Without pruning, the shrub becomes leggy and the variegation fades on older wood.

The species responds well to coppicing, producing long, straight shoots suitable for living willow structures and garden features. In commercial production, plants are often grown as standards with a 60 to 90 cm clear stem.

Container cultivation is successful provided plants are watered frequently during [[fungal-competition-survival-contaminated-soils]]. Harvested biomass can be safely disposed of or processed for metal recovery.
