## Snow Mold

## Description

Snow mold is a group of winter turf diseases caused by fungi that develop under snow cover or during cold, wet conditions in late winter and early spring. Two primary types are recognized: **[[gray-snow-mold]]** (caused by *[[typhula]]* spp.) and **pink snow mold** (caused by *[[microdochium]] nivale*, formerly *Fusarium nivale*). Both types produce circular, matted patches of dead or damaged turf that become visible when snow melts. Snow molds are among the turf diseases that can be suppressed using fungally dominated compost teas.

## Classification

### Gray Snow Mold
- **Causal organism:** *Typhula* spp. (primarily *T. incarnata* and *T. ishikariensis*)
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Basidiomycota
- **Class:** Agaricomycetes
- **Order:** Agaricales

### Pink Snow Mold
- **Causal organism:** *Microdochium nivale*
- **Kingdom:** Fungi
- **Division:** Ascomycota
- **Class:** Sordariomycetes
- **Order:** Amphisphaeriales

## Key Facts

- Develops under snow cover or during prolonged cold, wet periods (typically 30+ days of snow cover for gray snow mold)
- Gray snow mold requires snow cover; pink snow mold can develop without snow cover during cool, wet weather
- Both types produce circular patches (6–12 inches for gray; up to 18 inches for pink) of matted, dead turf
- Pink snow mold gets its name from the pinkish-white mycelium visible at the patch margins
- Gray snow mold produces sclerotia (survival structures) visible as tiny dark dots on infected tissue
- Most damaging on closely mown turf (golf greens, bowling greens) and in areas with prolonged snow cover
- Snow mold fungi remain active at near-freezing temperatures, continuing to colonize turf even under snow

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

### Fungal Competition and Suppression

Both gray and pink snow molds are listed among diseases suppressible by **fungally dominated compost teas**:

- Beneficial fungi in compost tea establish competitive colonies in the thatch and soil before snow falls
- A diverse fungal community occupies the ecological niches that snow mold fungi would otherwise exploit
- Fungal mycelia from beneficial species create physical and chemical barriers to snow mold colonization
- Applications of fungally dominated compost tea in late autumn (before first snowfall) can preemptively establish protective fungal communities

### Soil Food Web Context

Snow mold severity is increased by:

- Excessive nitrogen applications in late fall (promotes lush, susceptible growth)
- Poor drainage and compacted soils
- Thatch accumulation providing habitat for the pathogen
- Lack of beneficial fungal competitors in [[the-soil-food-web]]

Managing snow mold through [[soil-food-web]] principles involves:

1. Discontinuing late-season nitrogen applications
2. Applying fungally dominated AACT in late autumn (after leaf fall, before first snow)
3. Top-dressing with fungally dominated compost
4. Maintaining good drainage and aeration
5. Avoiding excessive thatch buildup

### Over-Winter Soil Biology

Even under snow cover, microbial activity continues at the soil-snow interface where temperatures warm slightly. This means:

- Compost tea microbes applied in autumn remain active through winter
- Snow mold pathogens must compete with these established beneficial communities
- Spring applications of tea can help turf recover from any snow mold damage that did occur

## Practical Notes

- Apply fungally dominated compost tea in late October/November before the first lasting snow
- Avoid applying nitrogen fertilizer within 6 weeks of expected first snowfall
- Mow turf to recommended height going into winter (not too short — longer grass is more susceptible, but excessive height promotes matting)
- In affected areas, lightly rake matted patches in spring to promote air circulation and recovery
- Pink snow mold is generally more aggressive than gray snow mold and can occur without snow cover
