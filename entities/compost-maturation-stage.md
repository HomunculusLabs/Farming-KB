## Description

The compost maturation stage is the final phase of the composting process, beginning when the thermophilic phase subsides and temperatures drop back into the mesophilic range (below 104F / 40C). During maturation, the most resistant plant components -- particularly lignin -- are decomposed, and the compost develops its final character: dark, crumbly, coffee-colored, sweet-smelling humus teeming with diverse soil life. This stage is characterized by the return of mesophilic organisms from heat-protected endospores, intense activity by actinomycetes and basidiomycetes, and vigorous grazing by macrofauna (nematodes, springtails, worms, beetles). The maturation stage is critical because incomplete maturation leaves toxic intermediates and undecomposed compounds that can harm plants.

## Classification

- **Type:** Composting process phase (Phase 3 of 3)
- **Temperature range:** 68-104F (20-40C), gradually declining toward ambient
- **Duration:** Weeks to months, depending on pile management
- **Preceded by:** Thermophilic phase (104-150F / 40-65C)
- **Key characteristic:** Lignin decomposition, earthy smell development, soil aggregate formation

## Key Facts

- **Lignin decomposition:** The maturation stage is when the decay of the most resistant plant component, lignin, is completed. The bonds holding chains of alcohols in lignin together are extremely strong and structurally much more difficult to attack than almost anything else in the pile. This is why lignin breakdown occurs last.

- **Actinomycetes and basidiomycetes:** The chain-like actinomycetes (bacteria that resemble fungi) continue their attack on the most resistant plant remnants during maturation. These organisms produce the earthy smell associated with good compost and soil, which comes from their decay of cellulose, lignin, chitin, and protein. The basidiomycetes (club fungi, including "regular" mushrooms) remain at work as the major fungal participants in this final stage.

- **Mesophilic organism return:** The mesophilic organisms whose specially protected spores enabled them to survive the thermophilic phase now reassert themselves and replace the thermophilic organisms. Bacillus spp. and Heliospirillum spp. endospores germinate as temperatures fall.

- **Macrofauna grazing:** Nematodes, springtails, centipedes, and other organisms graze on fungi and bacteria. This grazing paradoxically causes the populations of fungi and bacteria to increase (through stimulated growth), which in turn increases their soil-binding activities. Many nematodes were killed by thermophilic heat, but survivors find abundant food.

- **Worm activity:** Earthworms work the organic matter, exposing it to bacteria and coating particles with mucus that binds them into aggregates. This aggregate formation is crucial for soil structure.

- **Arthropod contributions:** Ants, snails, slugs, mites, spiders, rove beetles, and sow bugs enter the pile to forage, shredding organic matter and making it easier for microbes to attack. This physical decomposition supports the microbial team.

## Temperature Management During Maturation

- **Optimal range:** 104-131F (40-55C) after the initial thermophilic peak
- **If temperature drops below 104F (40C) before maturity:** Add green, high-nitrogen material to stimulate bacterial heating
- **If temperature stays above 131F (55C):** Add more brown, carbon-containing material to moderate activity
- **Turning:** Ensures outside material gets into the center for complete treatment
- **Cooling methods:** Turning (aerating), adding water, adding brown materials
- **Sign of completion:** The pile no longer heats up when turned -- the microbes have consumed the available energy sources

## Indicators of Mature Compost

- **Appearance:** Dark, coffee-colored, crumbly texture. Original ingredients are no longer recognizable.
- **Smell:** Earthy and fresh (not bad, ammonia-like, vinegary, or putrid)
- **Temperature:** No longer heats up when turned
- **Plant test:** Supports healthy plant growth when used as a growing medium
- **Moisture:** Squeezing a handful should yield just a few drops of water, no more

**Warning signs of immature compost:**
- Bad smell (vomit, putrefying matter, vinegar) = anaerobic organisms present; do not use
- Ammonia smell = not finished; needs more time
- Recognizable original materials = incomplete decomposition
- Plant deficiencies when tested = insufficient predator activity to cycle nutrients

## Key Organisms in the Maturation Stage

**Bacteria:**
- Mesophilic bacteria returning from endospores
- Actinomycetes (Streptomyces and allies) -- attack resistant plant remnants, produce earthy smell

**Fungi:**
- Basidiomycetes -- continue lignin decomposition
- Various mesophilic fungi -- complete decomposition of remaining organic matter

**Grazers and Physical Decomposers:**
- Nematodes -- graze bacteria and fungi, release nutrients
- Springtails -- break apart organic matter
- Centipedes -- predatory role, also break apart material
- Earthworms -- pull material into burrows, create castings, bind particles with mucus
- Ants, snails, slugs, mites, spiders, rove beetles, sow bugs -- shred and forage

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Compost quality for mushroom cultivation:** Mature compost has undergone complete lignin decomposition and contains a diverse, balanced microbial community. This is essential for mushroom substrates where incomplete decomposition could produce compounds toxic to mycelium.

- **Pathogen-free guarantee:** Compost that has passed through both thermophilic and proper maturation phases is free of plant pathogens and weed seeds, making it safe for use as casing material or substrate amendment.

- **Soil aggregate formation:** The mucus-mediated binding of particles by worms during maturation creates soil aggregates that improve water retention, aeration, and nutrient-holding capacity. These aggregates are the physical foundation of healthy soil structure.

- **Beneficial biology inoculation:** Mature compost contains the complete complement of soil food web organisms -- bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes -- in balanced proportions. When applied to soil, this diversity inoculates depleted areas with a full food web.

- **Nutrient cycling verification:** The plant test during maturation verifies that nutrients are being properly cycled through the food web. If plants show deficiencies, it indicates that protozoan and nematode predators are insufficient to graze bacteria and fungi and release plant-available nutrients.

## Cool/Cold Composting Alternative

The text also describes an alternative maturation approach: cold composting, where organic matter is simply piled and left to decay slowly over a year or more. Cool compost has some advantages:
- Higher populations of worms, beetles, millipedes, and other macroarthropods
- Greater organism diversity
- Better ability to eliminate or control pathogens through competition
- Recommended to keep a cool compost pile running alongside hot compost
