## Overview

Solorina crocea, commonly known as the chocolate chip lichen or orange felt lichen, is a distinctive foliose lichen in the family Peltigeraceae. It is named for the bright orange apothecia that contrast sharply with its dark green to brown thallus surface, resembling chocolate chips embedded in the thallus.

The species is bipolar in distribution, occurring in arctic and alpine regions of both hemispheres. In the Northern Hemisphere, it ranges across the Arctic and high mountain ranges, while [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]] and gives the species considerable ecological significance in nitrogen-limited arctic and alpine ecosystems.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

Solorina crocea was first described by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 under the name Lichen croceus and later transferred to the genus Solorina by August Heinrich Rudolf Grisebach. The genus Solorina contains approximately eight species, all of which are relatively uncommon and restricted to cold or high-elevation habitats.

The genus name Solorina is derived from the Latin word for "soles" or "sandals," referring to the thallus shape of some species. Molecular phylogenetic studies place Solorina within the Peltigeraceae, closely related to Peltigera and Lobaria, with which it shares the bipartite photobiont system.

The thallus is foliose, 3 to 12 centimeters in diameter, and closely appressed to the substrate in younger portions but becoming loosely attached and somewhat undulate at the margins. The overall shape is roughly circular to irregularly lobate.

Individual lobes are 5 to 15 millimeters wide with rounded to subtruncate tips. The thallus thickness ranges from 0.3 to 0.6 millimeters, and the internal stratification includes a well-developed upper cortex, algal layer, medulla, and lower cortex with rhizines.

The upper surface is dark olive-green to brown or blackish-green, with a smooth to slightly wrinkled texture. The margins are often paler and may show a bluish-green tinge when moist. The lower surface is pale tan to brown with a sparse tomentum and scattered rhizines.

Rhizines are simple to sparingly branched, brown, and typically 1 to 3 millimeters long. They are most abundant near the thallus margins and provide attachment to the substrate in areas where the thallus is loosely appressed.

The most distinctive feature is the presence of large, bright orange to reddish-orange apothecia, 1 to 5 millimeters in diameter, that are immersed in the thallus surface. These fruiting bodies give the species its common name of chocolate chip lichen and are the primary field identification character.

Spores are brown, ellipsoid to fusiform, muriform (multi-septate), and measure 35 to 65 by 12 to 18 micrometers. The muriform [[spore]] type and bright orange apothecia distinguish Solorina crocea from all other species in the genus.

The muriform spore septation is one of the most distinctive cytological features of the genus Solorina, with individual spores containing 10 to 20 transverse and longitudinal septa. This complex septation pattern is relatively uncommon among lichenized ascomycetes and aids in spore identification under the microscope.

The photobiont layer contains green algae of the genus Coccomyxa in the upper cortex and Nostoc cyanobacteria in an internal layer, similar to the arrangement found in Lobaria and Peltigera species. This bipartite arrangement is relatively rare among foliose lichens.

## Distribution and Habitat

Solorina crocea has a bipolar distribution pattern, occurring in both the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. In the north, it ranges across the Arctic from Greenland and Svalbard to northern Scandinavia, Siberia, and northern Canada, extending southward in mountain ranges to the Alps, Rockies, and Himalayas.

Southern Hemisphere populations occur in Patagonia and Tierra del Fuego in South America, the South Island of New Zealand, and southeastern Australia. The bipolar distribution is unusual among lichens and has been attributed to long-distance dispersal events during Pleistocene glacial cycles.

Genetic studies comparing Northern and Southern Hemisphere populations have revealed relatively low genetic divergence, supporting the hypothesis of relatively recent long-distance dispersal rather than ancient vicariance. Bird-mediated dispersal of lichen fragments is the most likely mechanism for trans-equatorial colonization.

The species is terricolous, growing on soil, moss, or over humus in arctic tundra, alpine meadows, and subalpine habitats. It prefers moist, well-drained soils that are rich in organic matter and is often found in association with mosses such as Polytrichum and Racomitrium.

In arctic habitats, Solorina crocea frequently colonizes the edges of snow patches and late-melting snowbeds, where prolonged moisture availability supports thallus growth. In alpine environments, it is commonly found on north-facing slopes, in gullies, and near streams where humidity remains elevated.

Elevational range extends from near sea level in the Arctic to approximately 3,500 meters in tropical mountain ranges. The species is most common between 1,500 and 3,000 meters in temperate mountain systems and is a characteristic component of the arctic-alpine lichen community.

At high elevations, Solorina crocea often occurs in exposed, wind-scoured sites where taller vegetation is absent and competition from vascular plants is minimal. In the Arctic, it is a member of the cryptogamic ground cover community that dominates tundra landscapes.

## Chemistry and Uses

Solorina crocea contains several [[query-how-does-nitrogen-cycling-work-in-a-permaculture-system]]. Studies have measured nitrogen fixation rates in Solorina crocea that are comparable to those of other bipartite Peltigeraceae, with rates of 1 to 3 kilograms per hectare per year in favorable conditions.

Nitrogen fixation is temperature-dependent, with optimal rates occurring between 15 and 25 degrees Celsius and declining sharply at temperatures below 5 degrees. This thermal constraint limits nitrogen input to the brief arctic and alpine growing season when temperatures are sufficient for cyanobacterial activity.

## Ecological Role

Solorina crocea plays an important role in nitrogen input to arctic and alpine ecosystems through the activity of its cyanobacterial photobiont. In nitrogen-limited tundra soils, this input can be ecologically significant, particularly during the short growing season when [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]] demand is highest.

Solorina crocea contributes to soil stabilization in arctic and alpine habitats, where its thallus helps bind loose substrate and reduces erosion. Its presence is often associated with well-developed moss-lichen mats that form a critical component of the cryptogamic ground cover in cold ecosystems.
