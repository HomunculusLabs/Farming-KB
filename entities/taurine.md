Despite its common classification as an amino acid, taurine is technically an amino sulfonic acid — it contains a sulfonic acid group rather than a carboxylic acid group.

It is not incorporated into proteins but exists as a free molecule, with particularly high concentrations in the heart, retina, skeletal muscle, and brain.

Taurine is a conditional essential nutrient, meaning it must be obtained from the diet under conditions of stress, illness, or developmental immaturity.

## Chemical Properties

**Molecular formula:** C2H7NO3S — **Molecular weight:** 125.15 g/mol — **IUPAC name:** 2-aminoethanesulfonic acid

The compound is a white crystalline solid at room temperature, highly soluble in water, and stable under normal conditions.

Taurine has a pKa of 1.5 (sulfonic acid) and 8.82 (amino group), existing as a zwitterion at physiological pH.

It is biosynthesized in the liver from cysteine via the cysteine sulfinic acid pathway, requiring vitamin B6 as a cofactor.

The sulfonic acid group makes it chemically distinct from proteinogenic amino acids and resistant to peptidase degradation.

## Pharmacology

Taurine acts as a neuromodulator and neuroprotectant through multiple mechanisms: GABA_A receptor agonism, glycine receptor modulation, and regulation of intracellular calcium homeostasis.

It functions as an osmolyte, regulating cell volume by balancing intracellular [[reactive-oxygen-species-and-oxidative-stress]] production.

Taurine conjugates with bile acids to form taurocholic acid, which is essential for fat digestion and cholesterol solubilization in the small intestine.

## Effects and Dosage

Typical dietary intake ranges from 40–400 mg/day, with higher intake in populations consuming seafood and meat.

Supplementation doses commonly range from 500–3,000 mg/day, with studies using up to 6,000 mg/day without significant [[weil-the-natural-mind-altered-states-normalcy]] of consciousness.

## Dietary Sources and Deficiency

Taurine is found in high concentrations in animal-based foods: shellfish (especially scallops and mussels), dark-meat poultry, and fish.

Human breast milk contains significant taurine, and it is routinely added to infant formula because neonates have limited endogenous synthesis capacity.

Plant-based foods contain negligible taurine, making supplementation potentially important for strict vegetarians and vegans.

Deficiency states have been documented in patients receiving long-term parenteral nutrition lacking taurine supplementation.

Symptoms of deficiency include cardiomyopathy, retinal degeneration, impaired immune function, and developmental delays in children.

## Energy Drinks and Commercial Use

Taurine is one of the most common ingredients in energy drinks, typically present at 1,000–2,000 mg per serving.

Despite widespread concern, the European Food Safety Authority concluded that taurine at levels used in energy drinks poses no safety risk to healthy adults.

The combination of taurine with caffeine in energy drinks appears to have synergistic [[blesching-cannabis-hypertension-cardiovascular-effects]], though research remains inconclusive.

Global taurine production exceeds 200,000 metric tons annually, with the majority synthesized chemically from ethylene oxide and sodium bisulfite.
