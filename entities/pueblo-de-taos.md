## Pueblo de Taos

## Description

Pueblo de Taos (also known as Taos Pueblo) is an ancient Native American settlement
located in northern New Mexico, USA, near the town of Taos. It is reputed to be the
oldest continuously inhabited building in the world, with an estimated age exceeding
900 years. The pueblo is a multi-storied residential complex constructed entirely of
adobe (sun-dried mud brick) and timber, representing one of the most remarkable
examples of traditional earthen architecture in North America.

The community is home to the Taos people (Tiwa-speaking Pueblo Native Americans),
who have maintained continuous occupation and cultural practices at the site since
its construction. The pueblo consists of two main structures — the North House and
the South House — separated by the Red Willow Creek, which flows from the nearby
Sangre de Cristo Mountains.

Holmgren cites Pueblo de Taos as a powerful example of how even humble, non-durable
building materials like mud brick can achieve extraordinary longevity through
consistent, committed maintenance over generations.

- **Category**: Building / Archaeological Site / Living Community
- **Location**: Taos Pueblo, New Mexico, USA

- **Cultural Affiliation**: Taos Pueblo people (Tiwa-speaking)
- **UNESCO Status**: World Heritage Site (1992)

## Key Facts

- The pueblo is estimated to be over 900 years old, with some archaeological
  evidence suggesting construction began between 1000 and 1450 CE.
- Constructed entirely from adobe (sun-dried mud bricks) and local timber —
  materials that are inherently non-durable by modern construction standards.
- The community has maintained the structures through continuous maintenance
  and periodic replastering with adobe, demonstrating that commitment to
  maintenance can overcome material limitations.
- Consists of two main multi-storied adobe complexes: the North House (Hlauuma)
  and the South House (Hlaukwima), each reaching five stories in height.
- Red Willow Creek flows between the two main structures, providing the
  community's water supply.
- The walls are several feet thick at the base, tapering as they rise, providing
  excellent thermal mass for passive climate control in the high desert
  environment.
- Designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1992 and a National Historic
  Landmark in 1960.
- Approximately 150 people live within the pueblo full-time, with a larger
  community of about 2,800 Taos Pueblo members in the surrounding area.
- The pueblo demonstrates that mud brick buildings, far from being inherently
  temporary, can outlast most modern construction when maintained with care
  and cultural commitment.
- The adobe walls require regular replastering, typically performed as a
  community activity before the annual Taos Pueblo Pow-wow.

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Soil-Based Construction**: [[adobe-construction]] directly uses soil — the same
  medium in which [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] and soil microorganisms operate. The
  longevity of earthen structures depends on moisture management, a principle
  equally critical in fungal cultivation.
- **Maintenance Philosophy**: The Taos Pueblo demonstrates that consistent,
  generational maintenance can overcome inherent material weakness — a principle
  applicable to managing substrate conditions, humidity, and contamination
  in [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]] over time.
- **Thermal Mass**: The thick adobe walls provide passive temperature regulation,
  a principle applicable to designing [[mushroom-fruiting-chambers]] and incubation
  spaces that require stable temperature control.
- **Local Material Use**: The use of locally sourced adobe and timber parallels
  the permaculture principle of using local, available materials for cultivation
  infrastructure rather than importing specialised components.
- **Community-Scale Systems**: The communal maintenance model at Taos Pueblo
  provides a template for community-scale cultivation projects where shared
  infrastructure and collective labour achieve what individual efforts cannot.
- **Material Degradation and Fungi**: Adobe walls are susceptible to water damage
  and biological colonisation by fungi — understanding these processes informs
  both building maintenance and cultivation system design in humid environments.
- **Long-Term Thinking**: The 900-year continuity at Taos Pueblo embodies the
  permaculture principle of designing for future generations, relevant to
  establishing perennial cultivation systems including long-term mushroom
  production beds and [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]].
