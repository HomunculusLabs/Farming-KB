## Campa (Tribe)

## Description

The [[campa]] (also known as Asháninka or Ashaninka) are an indigenous Arawakan-speaking people of the eastern Peruvian Amazon rain forest. In the ethnographic literature on [[hallucinogens-and-shamanism]], they are notable for their communal ayahuasca ceremonies in which the shaman plays a **directorial role as leader**, distinguishing their practice from the more individualistic approaches of groups like the Jivaro.

## Classification

- **Language family:** Arawakan (Maipurean)
- **Geographic region:** Eastern Peru, Amazon Basin
- **Population:** One of the largest indigenous groups in the Peruvian Amazon

## Key Facts

- The Campa participate together in *Banisteriopsis* (ayahuasca) sessions, with the shaman serving as **director and leader** of the ceremonies.
- Their ayahuasca practice provides a model for understanding the **evolutionary transition from shamanism to priesthood**, as discussed by [[gerald-weiss]].
- The shaman's directorial role in the Campa ceremony may shed light on how **priests originally developed from shamans** — a key question in the anthropology of religion.
- Their practice suggests how hallucinogens may have been used in **early priesthoods**, where drugs permitted otherwise uninspired persons in appointed positions to achieve trance states.

## Evolutionary Significance

Weiss's analysis of the [[campa-ayahuasca-ceremony]] raises broader questions:
- Some hierarchically organized religions may have originally used hallucinogens as their "blessed sacraments" (as Wasson suggested for India).
- Direct revelations, even restricted to an elite, eventually conflict with orthodox dogma fundamental to state religions.
- Use of hallucinogenic substances tends to be **eventually forbidden** in hierarchical religious systems (as occurred in Europe).

Comparative example: Zuñi rain priests in the southwestern United States are known to employ hallucinogens in their practice (Stevenson, 1915).

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

The Campa represent an intermediate form between individualistic shamanism and institutionalized priesthood, illustrating how hallucinogenic plant use becomes formalized and hierarchical. Their practice provides ethnographic evidence for the theory that organized religion may have roots in psychedelic plant use — a hypothesis with ongoing relevance to the study of psychoactive plants in human cultural evolution.
