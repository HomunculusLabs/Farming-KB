# Borago officinalis

## Overview

Borago officinalis, commonly known as borage, starflower, or bee bread,
is an annual herbaceous plant in the family Boraginaceae. Native to the
Mediterranean region, borage has been cultivated for over 2,000 years
as both a culinary herb and a medicinal plant. It is widely recognized
for its edible, cucumber-flavored leaves and striking star-shaped blue
flowers, which are among the most important nectar sources for
pollinators in temperate gardens and agricultural systems. Borage seed
oil is one of the richest natural sources of gamma-linolenic acid
(GLA), an omega-6 fatty acid with anti-inflammatory properties, making
the species commercially significant in herbal medicine and cosmetics.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Kingdom:** Plantae — **Clade:** Tracheophytes, Angiosperms,
  Eudicots, Asterids — **Order:** Boraginales
- **Family:** Boraginaceae — **Genus:** Borago
- **Species:** B. officinalis

The genus Borago contains only a few species, with B. officinalis
being by far the most widely cultivated. The epithet "officinalis"
indicates its long history of use as an official medicinal herb in
European pharmacopeias.

## Morphology

Borage is a robust, coarse-textured annual growing 30–100 cm tall,
occasionally exceeding 1 m under favorable conditions:

- **Stem:** Erect, hollow, thick, covered in stiff prickly white
  hairs (hispid). Branches freely from the base. Entire plant has a
  bristly, rough texture.
- **Leaves:** Alternate, simple, ovate to oblong, 5–15 cm long.
  Basal leaves petiolate with winged stalks; upper leaves sessile and
  clasping. Crushed leaves emit a pronounced fresh cucumber aroma.
- **Flowers:** Perfect, star-shaped, 1.5–3 cm diameter, in loose
  terminal cymes. Corolla blue to violet-blue, rarely white or pink,
  with five petals fused at the base. Stamens form a prominent cone.
  Produced prolifically from early summer until frost.
- **Fruit:** Cluster of four rough-textured nutlets (schizocarp),
  4–6 mm, brown to black. Deep taproot with extensive lateral
  branching contributes to dynamic mineral accumulation.

## Distribution and Habitat

Native to the eastern Mediterranean — southern Europe, North Africa,
and western Asia. Widely naturalized across temperate Europe, North
and South America, and parts of Asia and Australia. Thrives in well-
drained, moderately fertile soils in full sun to partial shade.
Drought-tolerant once established. Hardy in USDA zones 3–10.

## Edibility and Culinary Use

All above-ground parts are edible, though stiff hairs make mature
leaves somewhat unpalatable raw:

- **Young leaves:** Raw in salads or blended into cold soups for
  refreshing cucumber flavor. Best harvested under 10 cm for tenderness.
- **Flowers:** The most popular edible component. Blue petals used as
  garnishes for cocktails (Pimm's Cup, gin drinks), salads, desserts.
  Hold color well when candied or frozen into ice cubes. Mild sweet
  flavor with honey-like notes.
- **Cooked leaves:** Sautéed, added to risottos, frittatas, and
  stuffings. Cooking softens hairs. Used similarly to spinach in
  Mediterranean cuisines.
- **Tea:** Dried leaves and flowers steeped as an herbal infusion,
  traditionally consumed for calming and diaphoretic properties.

## Medicinal Properties

Borage has a long medicinal history dating to Dioscorides and Pliny
the Elder. Modern research focuses on borage seed oil:

- **Borage seed oil:** Contains 17–25% gamma-linolenic acid (GLA),
  higher than evening primrose oil. GLA is a precursor to
  prostaglandin E1 with anti-inflammatory and vasodilatory effects.
  Used as a supplement for rheumatoid arthritis, eczema, diabetic
  neuropathy, and PMS, though clinical evidence remains mixed.
- **Traditional uses:** Tea used as diaphoretic for fevers,
  expectorant for respiratory conditions, galactagogue for breast
  milk production, and mood enhancer. The Welsh phrase "borage for
  courage" reflects its traditional reputation as a restorative.
- **Safety concerns:** Leaves and flowers contain small amounts of
  hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids (lycopsamine, amabiline).
  Occasional culinary use is safe; prolonged medicinal leaf use is
  discouraged. Commercial borage oil is typically PA-certified.
  Not recommended during pregnancy. Avoid with liver conditions
  or anticoagulant use.

## Permaculture and Ecological Value

Borage is one of the most valuable companion plants in permaculture
and organic gardening:

- **Pollinator support:** Exceptional nectar plant for honeybees,
  bumblebees, and native bee species. One of the top honey-producing
  herbs. Also attracts predatory wasps and hoverflies for pest control.
- **Companion planting:** Widely interplanted with tomatoes, squash,
  strawberries, and brassicas. May improve tomato flavor and deter
  hornworms by attracting parasitoid wasps. Deep taproot mines
  minerals from subsoil as a dynamic accumulator.
- **Living mulch:** Fast-growing canopy suppresses weeds. Cut foliage
  contributes potassium, calcium, and trace minerals as surface mulch.
- **Self-seeding:** Reliably self-sows, establishing persistent
  volunteer populations with minimal intervention.

## Cultivation

Easily grown from seed sown directly after last frost. Germinates in
5–10 days above 15°C. Thin to 30–45 cm spacing. Minimal care: moderate
water, no fertilization beyond compost, full sun for best flowering.
Succession sowing every 3–4 weeks extends harvest window.
