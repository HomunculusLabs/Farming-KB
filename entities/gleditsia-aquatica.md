Water locust typically reaches heights of 9–15 meters, with a spreading crown and often leaning or leaning habit when growing in frequently flooded sites. It is a moderately fast-growing tree with strong wood.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Plantae → Tracheophyta → Magnoliopsida → Fabales → Fabaceae → Caesalpinioideae → Gleditsia → G. aquatica Marshall

The species was first described by Humphry Marshall in 1785. The specific epithet *aquatica* refers to the tree's association with wet, riparian habitats.

Water locust is one of approximately 14 species in the genus *Gleditsia*, which has a disjunct distribution across [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]], and eastern Asia. The genus is characterized by bipinnate leaves and often formidable thorns.

**Leaves** are bipinnate, 15–30 cm long, with 8–15 pairs of pinnae. Each pinna bears 12–20 small leaflets, each 8–15 mm long. Unlike honey locust, water locust rarely produces tripinnate leaves.

**Thorns** are present on the trunk and branches but are generally shorter and less abundant than those of honey locust. They typically measure 3–8 cm long, occasionally reaching 15 cm on vigorous shoots.

**Flowers** are small, greenish, and appear in May to June in axillary racemes. The species is polygamo-dioecious, with some trees bearing only staminate flowers and others bearing perfect flowers.

**Fruit** is a flat, reddish-brown legume pod 15–25 cm long and 2–3 cm wide. The pods contain 6–12 seeds embedded in a sweet, pulpy matrix that was historically used as [[growing-gourmet-north-american-reishi-species]] hardwood species.

Water locust is adapted to periodic inundation and can survive root submersion for several weeks during flood events. It frequently grows with baldcypress (*[[nyssa-aquatica]]*), and overcup oak (*Quercus lyrata*).

It is hardy in USDA zones 6–9 and tolerates both acidic and alkaline soil conditions. Optimal growth occurs in full sun on deep, alluvial soils with high [[comparison-vetiver-grass-vs-bamboo-for-erosion-control]] but may require management in cultivated settings.

## Cultivation and Uses

Water locust is primarily used in [[tropical-rainforest-seedling-mycorrhiza-ecological-restoration]] and riparian buffer plantings. Its tolerance of seasonal flooding makes it valuable for stabilizing streambanks and restoring degraded wetland sites.

The wood is heavy, hard, and durable with a reddish-brown heartwood. It has been used for fence posts, tool handles, and construction lumber where its rot resistance is advantageous.

The sweet, pulpy seed pods were historically gathered as fodder for livestock, particularly hogs and cattle. This practice gave rise to the common name "honey locust" for the related species, which has sweeter pods.

Propagation is primarily from seed, which requires scarification or acid treatment to break physical dormancy. Seeds germinate readily after treatment and seedlings establish quickly in moist conditions.

The species fixes atmospheric nitrogen through root-associated rhizobia, contributing to soil fertility in riparian ecosystems. This nitrogen-fixing capacity enhances its value in restoration plantings.

Water locust has limited ornamental use due to its thorny branches and wetland requirements. It is occasionally planted in large-scale wetland gardens and naturalistic landscapes.

## Ecological Role

Gleditsia aquatica provides important habitat and food resources in southeastern bottomland ecosystems. The seed pods are consumed by waterfowl, wild turkeys, and various mammals including raccoons and squirrels.

The tree offers nesting sites for cavity-nesting birds in riparian forests. Its spreading canopy provides shade that moderates water temperatures in small streams and swamp margins.

As a nitrogen-fixing legume, water locust contributes to the nitrogen economy of floodplain ecosystems. This input supports the growth of neighboring non-fixing tree species and understory vegetation.

Water locust is a host plant for several species of Lepidoptera, including the silver-spotted skipper (*Epargyreus clarus*). The foliage supports moderate insect herbivore diversity.

The species plays a role in floodplain succession, establishing in frequently inundated sites where few other tree species can survive. It may serve as a nurse tree for less flood-tolerant species as alluvial deposits accumulate.

Mature water locust trees create microhabitats beneath their canopies that support diverse herbaceous plant communities. These shaded, moist conditions are particularly important during summer dry periods in bottomland forests.
