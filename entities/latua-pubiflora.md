# Latua pubiflora

**Latua pubiflora** (Griseb.) Baill., commonly known as **Latue** or **Arbol de [[latua-pubiflora-arbol-de-los-brujos-ethnobotany]]** ("Tree of the Witches"), is a rare and powerful hallucinogenic plant endemic to Chile. It is one of the most mysterious psychoactive plants of South America, formerly used exclusively by Mapuche Indian shamans.

## Taxonomy and Classification

- **Family:** Solanaceae (Nightshade Family)
- **Genus:** Latua (monotypic genus)
- **Species:** L. pubiflora
- **Authority:** (Griseb.) Baill.
- **Common names:** Latue, Arbol de los Brujos (Tree of the Witches), Latua

Latua is a monotypic genus, meaning L. pubiflora is the only species in its genus. This rarity contributes to its enigmatic status in ethnobotany. The species was first described by the German botanist Grisebach and later placed in its own genus by Baillon. Its exact phylogenetic position within the Solanaceae remains a subject of debate, though it is generally placed within the tribe Cestreae, which also includes Cestrum and Sessea.

## Morphology

Latua pubiflora is a branched evergreen shrub with woody stems that grows to approximately 6 to 9 feet (2.5 to 3 meters). Its wood has a distinctive yellow color and a characteristic scent of vanilla.

The green leaves are lanceolate, with a continuous margin tapered at the petiole, measuring 4 to 5 inches (12 to 15 cm) long. The leaves are arranged alternately along the stems and have a slightly rough surface texture. The flowers are white, occasionally with rose speckles, and bell-shaped (up to 7 mm long), hanging in clusters from the tips of the branches. The fruit is a black berry containing numerous tiny seeds.

The plant's distinctive vanilla-scented wood and attractive flowers make it a striking species in its native habitat, though its extreme rarity means few people have encountered it in the wild.

## Distribution

Latua pubiflora is endemic to Chile, where it grows in the Valdivian region. Its restricted range and rarity make it one of the most geographically limited psychoactive plants known. The plant grows in the temperate rainforests of southern Chile, in areas with high rainfall and mild temperatures. It is typically found in forest margins, clearings, and along watercourses.

Conservation status is poorly documented, but the plant's restricted range and the ongoing destruction of Valdivian temperate rainforest habitat suggest that L. pubiflora may be vulnerable to habitat loss.

## Ethnobotanical Uses

### Mapuche Shamanic Use

Latua pubiflora was formerly used by the Mapuche Indian shamans of Valdivia, Chile. The Mapuche witch doctors (who were predominantly female) employed Latue as a powerful hallucinogen in their shamanic practices. The plant was considered among the most potent and dangerous of all psychoactive plants used by the Mapuche.

### Preparation Methods

[[kava-traditional-preparation-ceremonial-context]] methods for Latue are not well documented in the ethnographic literature. It is believed that the leaves and possibly the fruits were the primary plant parts used. Preparations may have included teas, infusions, or possibly smoking of dried plant material. The secrecy surrounding Mapuche shamanic practices has meant that much knowledge about the specific preparation and use of Latue has been lost.

### Shamanic Context

The use of Latue was deeply embedded in Mapuche spiritual practice. The plant was considered a tool for contacting the spirit world and was treated with great reverence and caution. Its use was restricted to trained shamans, reflecting both its power and its danger. The name "Arbol de los Brujos" (Tree of the Witches) reflects the fear and respect with which both indigenous and colonial communities regarded the plant.

### Mapuche Shamanism

The Mapuche shamanic tradition, known as machitun, is one of the oldest continuously practiced spiritual systems in South America. The machi (shaman), who has historically been more often female than male, serves as a healer, diviner, and intermediary between the human and spirit worlds. Psychoactive plants like Latue, [[lobelia-tupa]] (Tabaco del Diablo) were important tools in the machi's pharmacopoeia.

## Chemistry

As a member of the Solanaceae family, L. pubiflora is presumed to contain tropane alkaloids similar to those found in other psychoactive nightshades:

- **Scopolamine** (likely) — a potent deliriant and anti-cholinergic
- **Hyoscyamine** (likely) — a tropane alkaloid with mydriatic and anti-spasmodic properties
- **Atropine** (possible) — the racemic form of hyoscyamine

The specific alkaloid profile of Latua has not been fully characterized, and chemical investigation of this rare plant remains a priority for ethnopharmacologists. Preliminary phytochemical screening has confirmed the presence of tropane alkaloids, though detailed quantitative analysis has been hampered by the difficulty of obtaining plant material.

## Pharmacology

If L. pubiflora does contain tropane alkaloids as suspected, its pharmacology would be similar to that of other tropane-containing Solanaceae. Tropane alkaloids are competitive antagonists at muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, producing a characteristic syndrome known as anticholinergic toxidrome:

- Mydriasis (dilated pupils)
- Tachycardia (rapid heartbeat)
- Dry mouth and skin
- Hyperthermia
- Visual and auditory hallucinations
- Confusion and delirium
- Amnesia of the experience

The powerful and potentially dangerous nature of tropane alkaloid intoxication is consistent with the extreme caution with which the Mapuche treated Latue and its use.

## Safety Considerations

Latua pubiflora is considered extremely dangerous. Tropane alkaloid poisoning can be life-threatening, with symptoms including:

- Severe delirium and loss of contact with reality
- Tachycardia and cardiac arrhythmias
- Hyperthermia (dangerously elevated body temperature)
- Respiratory depression
- Seizures and convulsions

There is no safe recreational dose for plants containing tropane alkaloids. The narrow margin between psychoactive and toxic doses makes accidental poisoning a significant risk. Modern pharmaceutical treatment of tropane alkaloid poisoning includes the administration of physostigmine as a specific antidote.

## Cultural Significance

Latua pubiflora occupies a unique position in the ethnobotany of South America. Its common name "Arbol de los Brujos" (Tree of the Witches) reflects the fear and awe with which it was regarded by both indigenous peoples and colonial observers. The fact that Mapuche shamanism was predominantly female adds an important gender dimension to the study of this plant's cultural significance.

The plant is so rare and restricted in distribution that it represents one of the most geographically limited hallucinogenic plants in the world. Its continued existence in the wild is potentially threatened by habitat destruction, making conservation of both the plant and the traditional knowledge surrounding its use a matter of urgency.

## Historical Documentation

The earliest European references to Latue appear in the writings of 19th-century naturalists and ethnographers working in southern Chile. The plant was brought to wider scientific attention by Richard [[richard-evans-schultes-updated]] and his colleagues, who documented its use among the Mapuche. Despite this attention, L. pubiflora remains one of the most poorly characterized psychoactive plants in terms of its [[psilocybin-psilocin-chemistry-and-pharmacology-emcdda]].

## Conservation Status

Latua pubiflora is potentially threatened by habitat destruction in the Valdivian temperate rainforest region of Chile. This ecosystem is one of the most endangered forest types in South America, with significant areas having been converted to agriculture or plantation forestry.

The plant's restricted range makes it particularly vulnerable to habitat loss. Conservation efforts should focus on protecting remaining habitat and documenting traditional knowledge about the plant's use and preparation. L. pubiflora has been successfully cultivated in a few botanical gardens, which provides an important safeguard against extinction.

## Comparison with Solanaceae Hallucinogens

L. pubiflora can be compared with other hallucinogenic members of the Solanaceae family:

- [[brugmansia-aurea]] — Golden Angel's Trumpet, widely used in [[chiric-caspi-brunfelsia-south-american-hallucinogen]] ceremonies
- [[datura-innoxia]] — another Datura species with tropane alkaloids

The key distinguishing feature of L. pubiflora is its extreme rarity and geographic restriction. While other Solanaceae hallucinogens are widely distributed and well-characterized chemically, Latua remains one of the most poorly understood members of this pharmacologically important plant family.
