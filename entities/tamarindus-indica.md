## Overview

Tamarindus indica L., the [[comparison-berry-growing-vs-fruit-tree-pruning]] in the family Fabaceae, subfamily Detarioideae. It is the sole species in its genus, native to tropical Africa and widely naturalized across South and [[guzman-psilocybin-mushrooms-south-america]].

The species is remarkably drought-tolerant once established, thriving in areas with 500–1500 mm annual rainfall and tolerating prolonged dry seasons of 6–8 months.

It grows on a wide range of soils including sandy, loamy, limestone, and even moderately saline soils. Optimal growth occurs on deep, well-drained alluvial soils with pH 5.5–7.5.

Frost tolerance is limited; young trees are damaged below 0°C, though mature trees can survive brief exposure to −3°C with leaf drop and regrowth.

## Bioactive Compounds

The pulp is rich in organic acids, primarily tartaric acid (8–18%), malic acid, and [[luteolin]], apigenin, taxifolin), and phenolic acids (caffeic, ferulic, syringic acid).

Seeds contain approximately 55–65% polysaccharides (xyloglucan/tamarind seed gum), 15–20% protein, and 6–10% oil. The seed gum has significant industrial applications.

Leaves contain flavonoids (luteolin-7-O-glycoside, apigenin), alkaloids, and saponins with documented antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activities.

## Traditional and Modern Pharmacology

In Ayurvedic medicine, tamarind pulp is used as a digestive aid (for constipation and bilious disorders), and leaf decoctions treat jaundice and fevers.

African traditional medicine employs bark preparations for wound healing, leaf infusions for diarrhea and helminth infections, and root decoctions for stomachache.

Modern studies have confirmed hypolipidemic activity of pulp extract in animal models, with reductions in total cholesterol and LDL of 15–25% at therapeutic doses.

Antioxidant capacity (DPPH assay) of the pulp is comparable to vitamin C on a molar basis, attributed primarily to the proanthocyanidin and flavonoid content.

Anti-inflammatory effects have been demonstrated through inhibition of COX-2, iNOS, and NF-κB pathways in vitro, with IC₅₀ values in the 50–100 µg/mL range.

## Culinary and Nutritional Use

The pulp is consumed fresh, dried, or as a concentrate. It is a key ingredient in Indian sambar and chutneys, Thai massaman curry, Mexican agua de tamarindo, and Worcestershire sauce.

Nutritional value per 100 g raw pulp: approximately 239 kcal, 62.5 g carbohydrates, 2.8 g protein, 0.6 g fat, 5.1 g dietary fiber, and significant amounts of thiamin, iron, and magnesium.

Tamarind seed kernel powder (TKP) is used as a thickening and stabilizing agent in food processing, and the seed oil has potential as a biodiesel feedstock.
