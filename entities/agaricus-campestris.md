# Agaricus campestris

Agaricus campestris, commonly known as the Meadow Mushroom or Field Mushroom, is one of the most widely recognized edible mushrooms in the world. Found in lawns, pastures, and grasslands across temperate regions, this species is the wild ancestor of the commercially cultivated button mushroom ([[agaricus-brunnescens]]). Despite its ubiquity, it is seldom cultivated commercially due to its difficulty in indoor cultivation compared to its domesticated relative. See [[agaricus-brunnescens]] for the cultivated button mushroom and [[agaricus-subrufescens]] for the almond-flavored relative.

## Taxonomy and Description

- **Order:** Agaricales
- **Family:** Agaricaceae
- **Common names:** Meadow Mushroom, Field Mushroom
- **Related species:** A. arvensis (Horse Mushroom), A. bitorquis (Pavement Mushroom), A. subrufescens (Almond Agaricus)

### Morphology

- **Cap:** White to cream, smooth, convex to broadly convex, expanding to nearly plane with age. Cap diameter typically 5-10 cm.
- **Gills:** Free from the stem, initially pink, progressing to dark brown with spore maturity.
- **Stem:** White, cylindrical, with a thin membranous ring (annulus) that may disappear with age.
- **Flesh:** White, firm, with a mild, pleasant mushroom odor (not phenolic).
- **Spore print:** Dark chocolate brown.
- **Bruising:** Flesh may stain slightly yellowish when cut, but should not stain bright yellow (which would indicate A. xanthodermus, a toxic species).

## Distribution and Habitat

- **Global range:** Widespread throughout temperate regions of North America, Europe, Asia, and Australia. One of the most common mushroom species worldwide.
- **Habitat:** Lawns, pastures, meadows, grasslands, and other open grassy areas. Often appears in rings or arcs (fairy rings), similar to [[marasmius-oreades]].
- **Seasonality:** Late summer through fall, often appearing after rain.
- **Ecological role:** A secondary decomposer that fruits from well-decomposed organic matter in grassy soils. Requires the action of primary and secondary decomposing microorganisms to prepare its substrate.

## Identification Notes

Key features distinguishing A. campestris from similar species:

- **Pink gills** that darken to chocolate brown (vs. white gills in deadly Amanita species)
- **Dark brown spore print** (vs. white spore print in Amanita species)
- **Mild odor** (not phenolic or inky, which would indicate toxic species)
- **Habitat in open grasslands** (not in forests)
- **Bruising reaction:** Flesh may turn slightly yellowish but never bright chrome yellow (A. xanthodermus stains bright yellow and has a phenolic odor)

## Nutritional and Culinary Value

- A. campestris is a classic edible mushroom with a mild, agreeable flavor
- Can be used in any recipe calling for button mushrooms
- Often considered superior in flavor to the commercial A. brunnescens
- Rich in protein, B vitamins, and minerals

## Cultivation Considerations

A. campestris is classified as a secondary decomposer. This means it requires:

1. **Composted substrates** prepared by primary and secondary microorganisms
2. **Complex composting process** involving microbial succession
3. **Casing layer** for fruiting initiation
4. **Controlled environmental conditions** similar to commercial button mushroom growing

Unlike the commercial A. brunnescens, A. campestris has not been extensively domesticated for commercial cultivation. Most consumption is from wild collection. See the-mushroom-cultivator-stamets for detailed composting methods applicable to Agaricus species.

## Ecological Role in Permaculture

In permaculture systems, A. campestris can serve as an indicator of healthy, organically rich soils. Its appearance in pastures and lawns suggests a well-functioning decomposer food web. The species plays a role in nutrient cycling in grassland ecosystems, breaking down organic matter and making nutrients available to grasses and other plants.

## Related Agaricus Species

- **Agaricus brunnescens** — The commercial Button Mushroom; see [[agaricus-brunnescens]]
- **Agaricus arvensis** — The Horse Mushroom, larger with an anise-like odor; see [[agaricus-arvensis]]
- **Agaricus bitorquis** — The Pavement Mushroom, tolerant of compacted soils; see [[agaricus-bitorquis]]
- **Agaricus subrufescens** — The Almond Agaricus, with a distinct almond aroma; see [[agaricus-subrufescens]]
- **Agaricus sylvicola** — The Sylvan Agaricus, found in wooded areas; see [[agaricus-sylvicola]]
- **Agaricus xanthodermus** — The Yellow Stainer, toxic, causes gastric upset

## Warnings

Always verify identification carefully. The genus Agaricus contains both edible and toxic species. The key toxic species to avoid is A. xanthodermus (Yellow Stainer), which stains bright chrome yellow when cut and has a distinctive phenolic (iodine-like) odor. Also ensure specimens are not confused with Amanita species in the button stage by always checking for a volva (cup at the base) and white gills.

## Growth Characteristics

As a secondary decomposer, A. campestris has specific substrate requirements that differ from primary decomposers like [[pleurotus-ostreatus]] and [[stropharia-rugoso-annulata]]. The species requires substrates that have been pre-digested by bacterial and fungal communities. In nature, this occurs as organic matter in grasslands is broken down by successive waves of microorganisms. The button mushroom industry has refined this process into a controlled composting operation.

The life cycle of A. campestris in nature follows a predictable pattern:

1. Organic matter (grass clippings, animal manure, plant debris) accumulates in meadows
2. Primary decomposers (bacteria, actinomycetes) begin breaking down the material
3. Secondary decomposers further modify the substrate chemistry
4. As temperatures and moisture conditions become favorable, A. campestris mycelium colonizes
5. After full colonization, environmental triggers (temperature drop, rain) stimulate fruiting
6. Mushrooms appear, often in rings reflecting the radial growth pattern of the underground mycelium

## Seasonal Patterns

A. campestris typically fruits in late summer through fall, with peak abundance in September and October in the Northern Hemisphere. Fruitings are triggered by:

- Cooling temperatures after summer heat
- Adequate rainfall (1-2 inches in the preceding week)
- Shortening day length
- Soil temperatures in the 55-65°F (13-18°C) range

The species may fruit repeatedly in the same location for many years if the soil conditions remain favorable. Fairy rings can expand at a rate of several inches to over a foot per year, with the oldest rings being centuries old.

## Nutritional Profile

A. campestris is a nutritious food source, comparable to the commercial button mushroom:

- **Protein content:** Approximately 20-25% dry weight
- **Vitamins:** Rich in B vitamins, particularly riboflavin (B2), niacin (B3), and pantothenic acid (B5)
- **Minerals:** Good source of potassium, phosphorus, selenium, and copper
- **Fiber:** Contains both soluble and insoluble dietary fiber
- **Calories:** Low calorie, approximately 22 calories per 100 grams fresh weight
- **Antioxidants:** Contains ergothioneine, a unique antioxidant also found in [[lentinula-edodes]] and other medicinal mushrooms

## Role in Myco-Permaculture

In permaculture systems, A. campestris serves multiple functions:

- **Soil health indicator:** Its presence signals active decomposition and healthy soil biology
- **Nutrient cycling:** Converts organic matter into forms available to grasses and other plants
- **Grazing benefit:** Meadows with active fungal decomposition produce more nutritious forage
- **Aesthetic value:** Fairy rings are visually striking features in pasture landscapes
- **Food production:** Provides a secondary yield from pasture and meadow systems

## History of Use

A. campestris has been collected as food for thousands of years across Europe and was one of the first mushroom species to be described in the scientific literature. It was the primary meadow mushroom collected before the commercial cultivation of [[agaricus-brunnescens]] became widespread in the 18th and 19th centuries. In many rural European communities, the seasonal appearance of meadow mushrooms was an important food event, and various traditional recipes developed around their use.
