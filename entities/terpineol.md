## Overview

Terpineol is a naturally occurring monoterpenoid alcohol found in over 150 plant species. It exists as three structural isomers: alpha (α), beta (β), and gamma (γ), with alpha-terpineol being the most common and commercially significant form.

The compound contributes a distinctive lilac, floral, and slightly pine-like aroma to essential oils. It is widely used in perfumery, flavoring, and aromatherapy due to its pleasant scent and reported [[raw-cannabis-thca-non-psychoactive-therapeutic-properties]].

Alpha-terpineol constitutes one of the most abundant aroma-active compounds in several herbs. Its molecular formula is C₁₀H₁₈O with a molecular weight of 154.25 g/mol.

The name derives from turpentine, from which the compound was first isolated in the 19th century. French chemist Marcellin Berthelot characterized its structure during early terpene chemistry investigations.

## Chemical Properties

- **IUPAC name:** 2-(4-methylcyclohex-3-en-1-yl)propan-2-ol (α-terpineol)
- **Molecular formula:** C₁₀H₁₈O
- **Molecular weight:** 154.25 g/mol
- **Boiling point:** 217–218 °C (α-terpineol at 760 mmHg)
- **Density:** 0.934 g/cm³ at 20 °C
- **Solubility:** Slightly soluble in water; miscible with ethanol, ether, and oils
- **Optical rotation:** [α]D = +100° (pure enantiomer)
- **Refractive index:** 1.483 at 20 °C

Alpha-terpineol is a colorless, viscous liquid at room temperature. It is optically active, existing as both (+) and (-) enantiomers, with the (+) form being more common in nature.

The compound can exist as a crystalline solid (melting point 35–37 °C for α-terpineol). It forms readily from the acid-catalyzed hydration of terpinene and related pinene derivatives.

Beta-terpineol (trans- and cis-) and gamma-terpineol are rarer isomers with slightly different organoleptic properties. The gamma isomer has a more pronounced earthy character compared to the floral alpha form.

Spectroscopic identification uses characteristic IR absorption at 3400 cm⁻¹ (O-H stretch) and 1650 cm⁻¹ (C=C stretch). GC-MS retention indices are 1690–1700 on DB-5 columns.

Terpineol is classified as a tertiary alcohol, meaning the hydroxyl group is bonded to a carbon with three other carbon substituents. This structural feature contributes to its relative stability and resistance to oxidation compared to primary alcohols.

## Natural Sources

Terpineol occurs naturally in a broad range of aromatic plants. Major sources include lilac flowers ([[syringa-vulgaris]]), pine trees (Pinus species), and tea tree ([[melaleuca-alternifolia]]) essential oil.

It is also found in significant concentrations in cajeput oil, petitgrain oil, and neroli oil. The compound frequently appears as a dehydration product of terpin hydrate during essential oil distillation.

In cannabis, terpineol is a minor but notable terpene found in some cultivars with high [[pinene]] content. It often co-occurs with [[myrcene]] and [[limonene]] in the same plant profiles.

Other notable [[dmt-botanical-sources-pharmacology]] include eucalyptus leaves, sage, and rosemary. Tea tree oil typically contains 1–8% terpineol depending on chemotype and harvest conditions.

Concentration varies widely across species: pine needle oil can contain up to 5% terpineol, while some tea tree chemotypes exceed 10%. Geographic origin and harvest timing significantly affect yield.

Biosynthesis proceeds via the mevalonate pathway in most plants, producing geranyl pyrophosphate as the direct precursor. Cyclization by terpene synthase enzymes generates the terpinyl cation intermediate that yields terpineol.

Seasonal variation affects terpineol content significantly in many species. Spring-harvested pine needles show higher terpineol concentrations than autumn samples due to increased [[arbuscule-isolation-metabolic-activity-assays]].

## Pharmacology

Terpineol exhibits a range of documented bioactivities. Studies show notable anti-inflammatory effects through suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α and IL-6.

The compound demonstrates antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and some fungi. Research indicates minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) in the range of 0.5–2.0 mg/mL against [[staphylococcus-aureus]] strains.

Antioxidant properties have been confirmed through DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assays. Terpineol also shows antinociceptive (pain-relieving) effects in rodent models at doses of 25–50 mg/kg.

A 2014 review in *Molecules* catalogued terpineol's antispasmodic, anticonvulsant, and sedative properties. The sedative effect appears linked to GABAergic modulation, though the precise mechanism remains under investigation.

The compound has demonstrated cytotoxic activity against several human cancer cell lines in vitro. IC₅₀ values range from 50–200 μM against MCF-7 (breast) and HeLa (cervical) cancer cells.

Inhalation studies in mice show reduced locomotor activity at 1–2% concentrations. This sedative profile supports traditional uses of terpineol-rich essential oils as sleep aids and relaxants.

Anti-anxiety effects have been observed in elevated plus-maze tests at sub-sedative doses. The anxiolytic activity does not impair motor coordination, suggesting a favorable therapeutic window.

## Applications

In perfumery, alpha-terpineol serves as a fixative and heart-note ingredient in lilac, muguet, and pine-type fragrances. It is one of the most cost-effective floral aroma chemicals available to the industry.

The food and beverage industry uses terpineol as a flavoring agent in citrus, berry, and spice flavor profiles. It is generally recognized as safe (GRAS) by the FDA for food use.

In aromatherapy, terpineol is valued for its reported calming and sedative effects. Diffused terpineol-containing oils are used to promote relaxation and improve sleep quality.

Pharmaceutical applications include use as a penetration enhancer in topical formulations. It also serves as an intermediate in the synthesis of other terpenoid compounds and pharmaceuticals.
