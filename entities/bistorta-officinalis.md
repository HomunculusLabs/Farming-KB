## Overview

Bistorta officinalis (syn. Polygonum bistorta), commonly known as bistort or common bistort, is a rhizomatous perennial herb in the family Polygonaceae. It is native to damp meadows and marshy ground across Europe and temperate Asia.

The plant produces dense spikes of pink flowers on erect stems reaching 30–75 cm tall. Its twisted, snake-like rhizome gives rise to the common name "bistort," derived from the Latin *bistorta* meaning "twice-twisted."

Bistorta officinalis has a long history of use in traditional European [[basidiomycete-mating-systems-sexual-reproduction]].

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:**
- Kingdom: Plantae
- Order: Caryophyllales
- Family: Polygonaceae
- Genus: Bistorta
- Species: B. officinalis (L.) Delarbre

Notable synonyms include Polygonum bistorta L. and Persicaria bistorta (L.) Samp. The genus Bistorta was separated from Polygonum based on molecular phylogenetic studies showing it to be a distinct lineage.

Basal leaves are large, oblong-lanceolate, and slightly cordate at the base, typically 10–30 cm long with undulate margins. The leaf upper surface is dark green and slightly glossy.

Stem leaves are smaller, narrowly lanceolate, and sessile with ochreate stipules. The ochrea is brown and papery, characteristic of the Polygonaceae.

The inflorescence is a dense, cylindrical spike 3–7 cm long composed of numerous small pink to rose-pink flowers. Flowering occurs from June to August in the Northern Hemisphere.

Each flower has five tepals, eight stamens, and three fused carpels. The fruit is a dark brown trigonous achene approximately 3–4 mm long.

The rhizome is thick, dark brown externally, and conspicuously twisted or coiled. Internally it is reddish-pink with a high tannin content that becomes visible when cut.

## Distribution and Habitat

Bistorta officinalis occurs across most of Europe from Scandinavia to the Mediterranean, extending eastward through temperate Asia to Siberia and the Himalayas.

It favors damp to wet habitats including marshy meadows, stream banks, alpine pastures, and wet grasslands. The species thrives in neutral to slightly acidic soils with good moisture retention.

Populations are most abundant in submontane to montane zones, typically at elevations of 100–2,500 m. It is increasingly planted in ornamental damp-garden settings outside its native range.

In Britain, bistort is a characteristic plant of species-rich hay meadows and traditionally managed pastures. Its presence is considered an indicator of agriculturally unimproved grassland of high conservation value.

The species has been introduced to North America as a garden ornamental and has naturalized locally in parts of the northeastern [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] or autumn, or by seed sown in [[query-how-do-i-choose-between-a-cold-frame-row-cover-and-low-tunnel]]. Seeds require a period of cold stratification for reliable germination.

In cultivation it forms spreading clumps that benefit from division every 3–4 years to maintain vigor. The dense flower spikes are attractive to bees and other pollinators, making it suitable for wildlife gardens.

No serious pests or diseases are commonly reported. Some garden cultivars, such as 'Superbum' with larger flower spikes, are available in the horticultural trade.

Bistorta officinalis is well-suited to rain gardens, pond margins, and bog gardens where its moisture requirements are naturally met. It combines effectively with other moisture-loving perennials such as ligularia, astilbe, and marsh marigold.

Deadheading spent flower spikes can encourage a secondary bloom in late summer. The foliage remains attractive through autumn, turning reddish-bronze before dying back for winter.
