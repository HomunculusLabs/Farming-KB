## Description

[[cation-exchange-capacity]], nutrient solubility, buffer systems

## Key Facts

- pH affects the solubility and availability of all key and trace nutrients in soil
- Metallic oxides (bases) form alkaline solutions with excess hydroxide ions; non-metallic oxides form acidic solutions with excess hydrogen ions
- pH is not constant but exhibits diurnal and seasonal changes, vertical soil gradients, and mosaic patterns within soil [[crumb-structure]] and colloid surfaces
- Surface mulches tend to be more acidic while deeper layers become more basic due to evaporation, wormcasts, and capillary action
- Root hairs can create and seek out ideal pH microenvironments if gross imbalances are absent
- Gardens with adequate humus and calcium (lime) function as buffers, growing plants satisfactorily even when pH fluctuates

### pH Ranges and Plant Growth

- **pH 4.5 to 10.0**: Wide range of plants can grow if calcium is present
- **pH 6.0 to 7.5**: Optimal range for most garden vegetables
- **Below pH 4.0 or above pH 10.0**: Only specialised bacteria or higher organisms can cope

### Natural Acid Sources

- Carbonic acid from CO2 dissolved in rainwater
- Sulphuric acids from industrial processing or pyrites
- Phosphoric and nitric acids from phosphorus and nitrogen
- Silicic acids from silica dissolution
- Hydrochloric acid from chlorine
- Weak nitric acid acts as a plant growth stimulant

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

In cultivation, maintaining appropriate pH is critical because it directly controls which nutrients are bioavailable. For [[mushroom-cultivation]], substrate pH influences mycelial colonization speed, contamination resistance, and fruiting triggers. Many basidiomycete fungi prefer slightly acidic conditions (pH 5.0 to 6.5). Agaricus species prefer compost near pH 7.0, while oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus) tolerate a broader range. Understanding pH mosaic effects in soil helps explain why mycorrhizal associations thrive in undisturbed soils where microsite pH variation supports both fungal and plant partners simultaneously.

## Design Considerations

Mollison recommended testing pH at multiple locations within a permaculture
site before designing plantings. Microclimate variations create pH zones that
can be exploited by matching plant species to their preferred pH range.

## Observations and Monitoring

Regular pH monitoring with simple probe meters allows early detection of
soil acidification trends. Mollison suggested seasonal testing at the same
locations to build a longitudinal understanding of soil chemistry dynamics.

## Nutrient Availability by pH Range

At pH 5.0-5.5, aluminum and manganese become highly soluble and toxic to many
plants. Phosphorus forms insoluble compounds with iron and aluminum in acid soils.
Between pH 6.5-7.0, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, sulfur, and calcium reach
their widest availability window. Above pH 7.5, iron, manganese, zinc, and boron
become increasingly unavailable, causing chlorosis in sensitive species.

## Mollison's Key Principles

Mollison argued that working with natural soil pH is more sustainable than
attempting to force radical pH changes. Choose plant varieties adapted to
existing conditions. Build organic matter to buffer pH naturally rather than
relying on repeated lime or sulfur applications.

## Historical Context

Mollison developed his pH management philosophy through decades of observation
in Tasmanian and Australian soils. He noted that traditional agricultural
approaches to pH modification often failed because they ignored the underlying
soil biology. His permaculture approach prioritizes biological solutions over
chemical amendments, recognizing that healthy soil food webs naturally regulate
pH within optimal ranges for most plant species.
