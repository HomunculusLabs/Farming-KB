## Description

Sow bugs (also known as woodlice, pill bugs, or roly-polies) are terrestrial crustaceans of the order Isopoda that are commonly found in compost piles during the maturation stage. They are among the macroarthropods that contribute to the physical decomposition of organic matter in compost by shredding and foraging through the material, making it easier for microbial communities to attack and decompose. Sow bugs are detritivores that feed primarily on decaying plant material, playing an important role in the soil food web as primary decomposers of leaf litter, wood fragments, and other organic debris. Their presence in compost is a sign of healthy biological activity and a maturing pile.

## Classification

- **Phylum:** Arthropoda
- **Subphylum:** Crustacea
- **Class:** Malacostraca
- **Order:** Isopoda
- **Suborder:** Oniscidea (terrestrial isopods)
- **Common names:** Sow bugs, woodlice, pill bugs, roly-polies

Unlike insects, sow bugs are crustaceans -- more closely related to crabs and shrimp than to beetles or ants. They are among the few crustaceans that have fully adapted to terrestrial life, though they remain dependent on moist environments because they breathe through gill-like structures that must remain damp.

## Key Facts

- **Compost macroarthropods:** Sow bugs are listed among the diverse macroarthropods that inhabit compost piles during the maturation stage, alongside ants, snails, slugs, mites, spiders, and rove beetles.

- **Physical decomposers:** Sow bugs open up organic matter as they forage, shredding it and making it easier for microbes to attack. This mechanical breakdown complements the chemical decomposition performed by bacteria, fungi, and actinomycetes.

- **Detritivores:** Their primary diet consists of decaying plant material, dead leaves, rotting wood, and other decomposing organic matter. They are among the first animals to process fallen leaves and dead plant material in natural ecosystems.

- **Moisture dependence:** Sow bugs require moist environments to survive because they respire through gill-like pleopodal lungs that must remain damp. Compost piles provide the ideal humid, moist microclimate they need.

- **Crustacean identity:** Despite their appearance and terrestrial habitat, sow bugs are not insects but crustaceans. This makes them unique among the common compost macrofauna and explains their dependence on moisture.

## Role in the Soil Food Web

In the compost food web described by Lowenfels and Lewis:

1. **Organic matter shredding:** Sow bugs chew and break apart decaying leaves, wood fragments, and other organic materials, dramatically increasing the surface area available for microbial colonization.

2. **Microbial inoculation:** As they feed, sow bugs ingest bacteria and fungi and transport them through the compost pile, effectively inoculating new areas with decomposer organisms.

3. **Nutrient cycling:** Their digestive process breaks down complex organic molecules and their frass (excrement) returns partially processed nutrients to the compost, making them more accessible to microorganisms.

4. **Soil aeration:** Their movement through the compost creates small channels that improve aeration and water infiltration.

5. **Prey base:** Sow bugs serve as food for predatory organisms in the compost food web, including spiders, centipedes, and certain beetles, supporting higher trophic levels.

## Common Compost Species

- **Armadillidium vulgare** (pill bug / roly-poly): Can roll into a complete ball when disturbed; very common in compost
- **Porcellio scaber** (rough woodlouse): Cannot roll into a ball; has a rough, textured exoskeleton
- **Oniscus asellus** (common shiny woodlouse): Smooth, shiny appearance; common in leaf litter
- **Philoscia muscorum** (common striped woodlouse): Faster-moving; often found in surface litter

## Life Cycle and Behavior

- **Breeding:** Females carry eggs in a brood pouch (marsupium) on their underside
- **Development:** Young emerge as miniature versions of adults and molt repeatedly as they grow
- **Lifespan:** Typically 1-3 years depending on species and conditions
- **Activity:** Primarily nocturnal; hide under debris during the day
- **Aggregation:** Tend to cluster together in moist, dark locations -- commonly seen in large groups in compost

## Habitat Requirements in Compost

- **Moisture:** Critical requirement; they will desiccate and die in dry conditions. Compost piles that maintain adequate moisture support healthy sow bug populations.
- **Temperature:** Prefer moderate temperatures (mesophilic range); they avoid hot zones in active compost
- **Shelter:** Need hiding places -- surface debris, leaf layers, and coarse organic matter provide shelter
- **Food source:** Decaying organic matter, especially leaves and wood fragments

## Relevance to Cultivation and Mycology

- **Compost maturation indicator:** The presence of sow bugs in compost indicates that temperatures have dropped to mesophilic levels and conditions are suitable for diverse macrofauna. Their abundance is a positive sign of compost maturity.

- **Leaf decomposition:** In outdoor mushroom cultivation sites, sow bugs contribute to the decomposition of leaf litter and wood chip substrates, helping prepare and maintain substrate for fungal colonization.

- **Cool compost contributors:** Sow bugs are more abundant in cool (slow) compost piles than in hot compost, because the lower temperatures allow them to survive throughout the process. Cool compost with abundant sow bugs produces well-processed organic matter.

- **Soil food web diversity:** Higher diversity of soil organisms (including sow bugs) means better ability to eliminate or control pathogens through direct attack or competition for nutrients and space. This is a key principle of the soil food web approach.

- **Minimal crop damage:** While sow bugs can occasionally nibble on tender seedlings or fruit touching the ground, their primary role is beneficial decomposition. In well-managed gardens and mushroom beds, their positive contributions far outweigh any minor damage.
