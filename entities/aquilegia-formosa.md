## Overview

*Aquilegia formosa* Fisch. ex DC., commonly known as western columbine or crimson columbine, is a perennial herb in the family Ranunculaceae. It is native to western North America from Alaska to Baja California and east to the Rocky Mountains.

The species is prized for its showy red and yellow nodding flowers with long, distinctive spurs. It is a popular ornamental in native plant gardens and hummingbird gardens throughout its range.

The red tubular flowers are a classic example of hummingbird pollination syndrome. The bright red sepals and yellow petals provide a visual beacon for hummingbirds, which are the primary pollinators.

Several indigenous peoples of western North America used the plant for medicinal and ceremonial purposes. The roots and leaves were prepared as infusions for various ailments.

The species is sometimes called "red columbine" or "sitka columbine" in regional floras. These common names reflect its [[aquilegia-canadensis]]* where ranges overlap. These natural hybrids show intermediate floral morphology and spur length.

Hybrid zones are particularly well-documented in the Cascade Range and Sierra Nevada. Research on these hybrid zones has contributed to understanding of speciation in the genus.

## Distribution and Habitat

*Aquilegia formosa* occurs from sea level to subalpine elevations across western North America. Its range extends from southeastern Alaska through British Columbia, Washington, Oregon, and California.

Eastward it reaches Montana, Idaho, Wyoming, and Colorado in the Rocky Mountains. Isolated populations occur in the mountains of southern California and Baja California.

The species inhabits moist, open to partially shaded sites including meadows, forest edges, streambanks, and rocky talus slopes. It prefers well-drained soils with moderate organic content.

Soils range from acidic to neutral pH, often derived from granitic or volcanic parent material. The plant is moderately drought-tolerant once established but prefers consistent moisture.

Populations in coastal areas experience Mediterranean climate with wet winters and dry summers. Inland and montane populations receive more even precipitation throughout the year.

The species is listed as Secure (G5) by NatureServe across most of its range. Local populations may be threatened by habitat loss from urban development and forest management practices.

## Ecological Role

Hummingbirds, particularly rufous hummingbirds (*Selasphorus rufus*), are the primary pollinators of *Aquilegia formosa*. The long, tubular spurs are precisely matched to hummingbird bill length.

Sphinx moths and bumblebees also visit the flowers but are less effective pollinators due to shorter proboscises. The flowers produce copious nectar to reward pollinators making the energetic cost of hovering worthwhile.

The foliage serves as a food plant for larvae of the columbine duskywing butterfly (*Erynnis lucilius*). Several species of leaf-mining flies also specialize on *Aquilegia* leaves.

The plant is relatively short-lived as a perennial, typically persisting 3–5 years. It compensates by producing abundant seeds that establish readily in disturbed microsites.

Deer and elk generally avoid the foliage due to the presence of cyanogenic glycosides. This chemical defense allows columbine populations to persist in areas with heavy browsing pressure.

The cyanogenic glycoside aquilegiin has been identified as the primary chemical defense compound. Hydrogen cyanide is released when leaf tissue is damaged, deterring herbivory by insects and mammals.

## Ethnobotany

Multiple indigenous peoples of western North America traditionally used *Aquilegia formosa* for medicinal purposes. The plant is documented in the ethnobotanical records of the Bella Coola, Cowlitz, Lummi, and Miwok peoples.

Root infusions were used to treat diarrhea, stomachaches, and urinary complaints. Some groups applied crushed leaves as a poultice for skin irritations and insect bites.

The Quileute and Cowlitz peoples used the plant as a love charm and in ceremonial contexts. The striking red flowers held symbolic significance in several [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]] cultures.

Seeds were occasionally ground and used as a perfume or incense material. The plant was not typically used as a food source due to the presence of toxic glycosides.

Modern herbalists occasionally use very small doses of the plant, though its toxicity limits [[turner-harmala-alkaloids-preparation-and-dosage]] control.

All parts of the plant are considered toxic if ingested in quantity. Symptoms of poisoning include nausea, vomiting, and cardiac irregularities due to the cyanogenic glycosides.
