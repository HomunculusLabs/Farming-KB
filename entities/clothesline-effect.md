# Clothesline Effect

## Description

The clothesline effect is a micrometeorological phenomenon described by Jen-hu
Chang in 1968, where advected (horizontally transported) dry, hot air from desert
regions blows into crop areas, causing dramatically accelerated water loss. Named
by analogy to how wet clothes dry faster on a windy clothesline than in still air,
this effect is one of the most significant challenges in desert agriculture and the
primary driver of windbreak design in arid [[prosopis]]), mulch to reduce soil heating.

## Key Facts

- Described by Jen-hu Chang, 1968
- Primary driver of advective water loss in desert gardens
- Decreases exponentially with distance from shelter
- Requires 50-100m deep tree belts at desert borders
- National-scale 1-5 km belts needed at savannah edges
- Without shelter, desert becomes self-propagating
- Field size irrelevant under oasis effect conditions

## Relevance to Cultivation

Understanding the clothesline effect is essential for any [[permaculture-design]] in
arid or semi-arid regions. It determines windbreak depth, settlement layout,
garden placement, and even national-scale forestry policy. Without this knowledge,
designers place ineffective narrow windbreaks and crops suffer excessive water
loss despite apparently adequate irrigation.
