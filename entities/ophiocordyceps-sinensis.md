## Overview

**Ophiocordyceps sinensis**, commonly known as **caterpillar fungus**, **Yartsa gunbu** (Tibetan), or **Dongchong Xiacao** (冬虫夏草, Chinese), is an entomopathogenic fungus that parasitizes the larvae of ghost moths (Hepialidae).

It is one of the most valuable medicinal fungi in the world, with wild-harvested specimens commanding prices exceeding USD $50,000 per kilogram on the wholesale market.

## Taxonomy and Morphology

**Classification:** Kingdom Fungi → Division [[ascomycota]] → Class Sordariomycetes → Order Hypocreales → Family Ophiocordycipitaceae → Genus *Ophiocordyceps* → *O. sinensis*.

**Authority:** (Berk.) G.H. Sung, J.M. Sung, Hywel-Jones & Spatafora (2007).

**Basionym:** *Sphaeria sinensis* Berk. (1843).

The species was reclassified from *[[cordyceps-sinensis]]* to *Ophiocordyceps sinensis* in 2007 based on molecular phylogenetic analysis.

**Stroma (fruiting body):** 4–10 cm tall, cylindrical, dark brown to black, emerging from the head of the mummified larva.

The stroma is the above-ground reproductive structure, club-shaped and slightly swollen at the apex where perithecia develop.

**Host larva:** 3–5 cm long, yellowish-brown mummified caterpillar of *Thitarodes* spp. (ghost moths), with 20–30 visible abdominal segments.

The fungus completely colonizes the larva's hemocoel, replacing internal tissue with a dense mycelial mat while preserving the external exoskeleton.

**Perithecia:** Embedded in the stroma surface, flask-shaped, producing filiform ascospores 120–230 × 4–5 µm.

## Distribution and Habitat

*O. sinensis* is endemic to the alpine meadow and shrubland zones of the Tibetan Plateau and surrounding Himalayan regions.

Its range spans elevations of 3,000–5,000 meters across the Tibet Autonomous Region, Qinghai, Sichuan, Yunnan, and Gansu provinces in China.

Additional populations exist in Nepal, Bhutan, and northern India, though the species is most abundant on the Tibetan Plateau.

The fungus requires very specific ecological conditions: cold temperatures (annual mean 0–5°C), high altitude, and the presence of *Thitarodes* larvae in the soil.

Fruiting occurs in spring (April–June), when the thawing permafrost layer allows stroma emergence through the soil surface.

## Traditional and Modern Medicinal Use

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), *O. sinensis* has been used for over 500 years as a tonic for respiratory, renal, and reproductive conditions.

It is classified as a "sweet, warm" herb that tonifies the kidneys, lungs, and blood, and is traditionally prescribed for fatigue, chronic cough, and sexual dysfunction.
