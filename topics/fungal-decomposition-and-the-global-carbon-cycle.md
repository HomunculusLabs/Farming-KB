# Fungal Decomposition and the Global Carbon Cycle

Fungi are among the most consequential organisms on Earth, yet their
contributions to ecosystem function are frequently underappreciated. As the
primary decomposers of lignocellulosic material, fungi orchestrate the
breakdown of the vast majority of terrestrial plant biomass, driving nutrient
cycling, soil formation, and the global carbon cycle. Without fungal
decomposition, the planet would be buried under layers of undecomposed plant
litter, nutrient cycles would grind to a halt, and primary productivity would
collapse. This article examines the mechanisms, ecology, and global
significance of fungal decomposition, from the enzymatic machinery that
dismantles woody polymers at the molecular scale to the planetary-scale
implications for carbon sequestration and climate change.

## The Fundamental Role of Fungi in Ecosystem Processes

The first law of thermodynamics dictates that matter can neither be created
nor destroyed, and within ecosystems, this law governs the transformations of
energy and nutrients between compartments. Energy and nutrients are contained
within the living biomass of plants and animals forming communities, and the
transformations that occur among them through trophic interactions within food
chains and webs are regarded as the processes of ecosystems. Fungi occupy a
central position in these transformations, linking the fixation of carbon by
plants through photosynthesis to the release of carbon dioxide through
decomposition and the mineralization of nutrients that sustain future plant
growth.

John Dighton, in his comprehensive treatment of fungi in ecosystem processes,
identifies three fundamental ecosystem services carried out by fungi. The first
is the making of nutrients available through the breakdown of dead plant and
animal remains by microbes and animals. During decomposition, mineral nutrients
are released in a soluble form as inorganic ions from the breakdown of the
organic complexes within plant and animal remains. This process, called
mineralization, provides fertility to the ecosystem and occurs in terrestrial,
freshwater, and marine environments. The second service is assisting primary
production through symbiotic associations with higher plants, the mycorrhizal
relationship that enhances nutrient uptake for approximately 90 percent of all
plant species. The third encompasses the roles of fungi in food webs, as
highly nutritious food sources for animals ranging from reindeer and moose to
microscopic collembola and nematodes.

The saprotrophic lifestyle is defined by the secretion of extracellular
enzymes into the environment to degrade complex organic substrates. This mode
of nutrition is inherently less efficient than the ingestion-based strategies
employed by animals, because a fraction of the degradation products are lost to
the surrounding environment rather than being fully absorbed by the fungus. This
inefficiency, however, is precisely what makes fungi so critical to ecosystem
function: the nutrients that leak from the fungal feeding zone become available
to other organisms in the soil, fueling the microbial loop and maintaining soil
fertility. Dighton describes this as one of the key functions of saprotrophic
soil fungi, the mineralization of nutrients into the soil nutrient pool.

Fungi interact with the mineral component of soil in ways that extend beyond
organic matter decomposition. The secretion of organic acids, particularly
oxalic acid, assists in the weathering of calcareous bedrock, while lichens
produce lichenic acid that facilitates rock solubilization and the formation of
protosoils. Once true soil has formed, fungi contribute to the stability of
soil aggregates through the binding properties of their hyphal networks and the
polysaccharides they exude. These aggregates serve as refugia for bacteria,
protozoa, nematodes, and other microscopic organisms, creating the
microhabitats in which nutrient mineralization occurs. Recent debates regarding
intensive agricultural practices have shown that minimal- or no-till methods
reduce the disruption of hyphal networks in soil, improving aggregate stability
and fertility.

## Wood Decay: White Rot, Brown Rot, and the Chemistry of Ligninolysis

Among the most ecologically significant activities of fungi is their ability
to decompose wood, a substrate composed primarily of cellulose, hemicellulose,
and lignin. Lignin is a complex, three-dimensional polymer of phenylpropanoid
units that is notoriously resistant to degradation. It is the second most
abundant organic polymer on Earth after cellulose, and its decomposition is
almost exclusively the domain of fungi, particularly basidiomycetes. While some
bacteria and a few actinomycetes can partially modify lignin, only fungi possess
the enzymatic machinery to accomplish its complete mineralization to carbon
dioxide and water.

Basidiomycetes are the major agents of decomposition and nutrient cycling in
forest ecosystems, occurring as both saprotrophs and mycorrhizal symbionts.
The saprotrophic basidiomycetes that decompose wood are broadly categorized
into white rot and brown rot fungi, based on the visual appearance of the
decayed wood and the underlying biochemical mechanisms involved. White rot
fungi, such as Trametes versicolor, possess the ability to degrade all cell
wall components, including lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose. They achieve
lignin degradation through the production of lignin peroxidases, manganese
peroxidases, and laccases, which are extracellular oxidative enzymes that
generate highly reactive free radicals capable of cleaving the aromatic rings
and ether bonds of the lignin polymer. The activity of these enzymes leaves the
wood with a bleached, whitish appearance because the dark lignin is
preferentially removed from the cell wall matrix.

Brown rot fungi, in contrast, primarily degrade the cellulose and
hemicellulose components of wood while leaving the lignin largely intact,
though chemically modified. Species such as Serpula lacrymans and those in the
genera Gloeophyllum and Postia employ a non-enzymatic mechanism involving the
Fenton reaction, in which hydrogen peroxide reacts with ferrous iron to
generate hydroxyl radicals that depolymerize cellulose. Brown rot fungi also
produce oxalic acid, which can overproduce in copper-tolerant species, aiding
in the solubilization of metal ions that participate in the Fenton reaction.
The brown rot process leaves behind a brown, crumbly residue of modified lignin
enriched in carbon and depleted in other elements.

The ecological implications of this division between white rot and brown rot
strategies are profound. White rot fungi tend to dominate in the decomposition
of hardwood species and in environments where complete mineralization of
organic matter is favored, while brown rot fungi are particularly important in
coniferous forests, where their ability to rapidly access cellulose from the
lignin-encrusted cell walls of gymnosperm wood provides a competitive
advantage. The differential decay strategies also affect the fate of carbon in
forest ecosystems. Studies comparing the two groups have found that brown rot
wood has a carbon content of approximately 43.5 percent compared to 41.6
percent in white-rotted wood, reflecting the concentration of lignin-derived
carbon in the brown rot residue. White rot promotes more complete carbon
release as carbon dioxide, while brown rot leaves behind a more recalcitrant
carbon pool that can contribute to long-term soil carbon storage.

## Mycelial Foraging Strategies in Heterogeneous Environments

The growth form of fungi as filamentous networks of hyphae imposes both
constraints and opportunities on their ability to locate and exploit organic
resources. In natural environments, nutrients are often patchily and sparsely
distributed, and may be locked in recalcitrant materials such as lignin. Many
other organisms are encountered, including other fungi, bacteria, and
invertebrates, and microclimate is constantly changing. The saprotrophic
basidiomycetes that ramify at the soil-litter interface, interconnecting
disparate litter components, have evolved a remarkable array of strategies for
coping with this environmental heterogeneity.

A key distinction among decomposer fungi is whether they are resource-unit
restricted or non-resource-unit restricted. Resource-unit restricted fungi can
only be disseminated as spores to new resources, heterogeneously dispersed in
space and time. Non-resource-unit restricted fungi can grow out of one
resource in search of others, and it is the latter group that has developed the
most sophisticated foraging behaviors. These fungi risk loss of a large amount
of biomass in the quest for new resources, but they have minimized this risk by
adopting different strategies for obtaining new resources and different
responses when resources have been encountered.

The cord-forming basidiomycetes such as Phanerochaete velutina, Resinicium
bicolor, Hypholoma fasciculare, and Stropharia caerulea provide the most
studied examples of these strategies. They operate both a sit-and-wait
strategy, whereby a large mycelial network waits for resources to land on it
and then actively colonizes those resources, and an active search strategy
involving directed growth through soil. The architecture of mycelial systems
differs between species in ways that reflect these strategies. Species that
produce mass fractal mycelia are characterized by open systems with
well-defined, rapidly extending cords that function as high-conductivity
channels, adapted for discovering large, sparsely distributed resources such
as fallen branches and tree stumps. In contrast, species that produce surface
fractal mycelia are characterized by diffuse, slowly extending search fronts
that explore areas intensively, better suited to discovering abundant,
relatively homogeneous resources.

When a new resource is encountered, the mycelial network undergoes dramatic
remodeling through a complex combination of growth, branching, hyphal fusion,
and regression of different regions. Carbon and nutrients are translocated from
older, resource-depleted parts of the mycelium to support colonization of the
new resource. This translocation capability allows fungi to maintain growth at
sites far from the original resource base and to buffer the effects of local
nutrient depletion. Not only does morphology change but so also does a complex
set of physiological processes associated with uptake, storage, and
redistribution of nutrients throughout the network, all highly coordinated to
ensure that locally perceived environmental changes are responded to both
locally and over larger spatial scales.

## Saprotrophic Fungi in Leaf Litter Decomposition

In addition to wood decomposition, saprotrophic fungi play a central role in
the decomposition of leaf litter, which represents a major pathway of carbon
and nutrient flow in most terrestrial ecosystems. The decomposition of leaf
litter follows a well-characterized sequence in which different groups of
fungi dominate at different stages. Early-stage decomposers, typically
fast-growing sugar fungi such as Mucor and Rhizopus species, rapidly colonize
freshly fallen leaves and exploit the readily available soluble sugars and
amino acids. As these labile compounds are depleted, the community shifts
toward fungi capable of degrading more recalcitrant compounds, including
cellulose and eventually lignin.

The rate and trajectory of leaf litter decomposition are influenced by a
complex interplay of factors, including the chemical composition of the
litter, the composition and activity of the fungal community, and
environmental conditions such as temperature, moisture, and nutrient
availability. Litter from different plant species decomposes at different
rates, with angiosperm litter generally decomposing more rapidly than
gymnosperm litter due to differences in lignin chemistry and leaf morphology.
The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio and lignin-to-nitrogen ratio of the litter are
particularly important predictors of decomposition rate, with lower ratios
generally associated with more rapid decomposition and nutrient release.

Fungal community structure on decomposing leaf litter is not random but
reflects the niche requirements and competitive abilities of the constituent
species. Different fungal species occupy distinct positions along resource
quality gradients and show characteristic patterns of temporal succession. The
functional consequences of these community-level patterns are significant:
changes in fungal community composition can alter the rate of decomposition,
the pattern of nutrient release, and the fate of carbon in the decomposing
litter. The differential response of fungal species to environmental
conditions means that changes in temperature, moisture, or pollution can shift
community composition in ways that have cascading effects on ecosystem
function.

The interaction between saprotrophic fungi and the soil fauna adds another
layer of complexity. Fungal-feeding nematodes, collembola, and mites consume
fungal hyphae and spores, with consequences that propagate through the food
web. Selective grazing by these organisms can alter the species composition of
fungal communities on decomposing resources, potentially changing the rates
and patterns of decomposition. Earthworm activity fragments and mixes leaf
litter, increases the surface area available for fungal attack, and generally
enhances decomposition rates. In arid and semiarid ecosystems, where bacterial
activity is limited by moisture, fungi dominate the decomposition process and
the soil faunal community is correspondingly dominated by fungivores. Some
fungivorous mites can remain inactive in a state of cryptobiosis during dry
periods, while nematodes survive through anhydrobiosis, both strategies
allowing them to persist until fungal activity resumes with moisture.

## Fungi in the Carbon Cycle: Mineralization and Sequestration

The global carbon cycle is fundamentally shaped by the balance between
photosynthesis, which fixes atmospheric carbon dioxide into organic matter, and
respiration, which returns organic carbon to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide.
Fungi are major contributors to both sides of this balance. As saprotrophs,
they drive the mineralization of organic carbon through respiration. As
mycorrhizal symbionts, they facilitate the fixation of carbon by enhancing
plant nutrient uptake and productivity. And through the production of
recalcitrant organic compounds and the physical stabilization of soil
aggregates, they contribute to the long-term sequestration of carbon in soil.

The distinction between saprotrophic and ectomycorrhizal fungi in terms of
carbon cycling is reflected in their isotopic signatures. Studies of natural
abundance of carbon-13 and nitrogen-15 have revealed a consistent difference
between these two trophic groups, a phenomenon known as the
saprotrophic-mycorrhizal divide. Ectomycorrhizal fungi are more depleted in
carbon-13 than saprotrophic fungi, reflecting the different carbon sources
they utilize and the different metabolic pathways involved. Radiocarbon dating
has provided additional insight: sporocarps of saprotrophic species were found
to contain carbon that had been photosynthetically fixed by plants more than
six years previously, whereas the carbon in ectomycorrhizal species was only
zero to two years old. This indicates that saprotrophic fungi draw on older,
more processed pools of soil organic matter, while ectomycorrhizal fungi
utilize recently fixed photosynthate transferred directly from host plants.

The contribution of fungi to soil carbon storage operates through multiple
mechanisms. Glomalin, a glycoprotein produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal
fungi, is remarkably resistant to decomposition and has been estimated to
account for 30 to 60 percent of the carbon in undisturbed soils. The hyphal
networks of both saprotrophic and mycorrhizal fungi physically enmesh soil
particles, contributing to the formation and stability of soil aggregates that
protect organic matter from microbial attack. The chitin-rich cell walls of
fungi are relatively recalcitrant and contribute to the stable organic matter
pool in soil. The combination of polysaccharide secretions from bacteria and
the binding property of fungal threads assists in maintaining the structure of
soil aggregates, which are refugia for microscopic organisms in soil.

Root systems and their mycorrhizal components are frequently overlooked as
potential sources and stores of carbon within ecosystems. In forested
ecosystems, below-ground biomass carbon accounts for between 16 percent in
Douglas fir to 64 percent in slash pine of total tree biomass carbon. Nearly
two-thirds of the soil respiration of a temperate mixed hardwood forest comes
from root activity, and the decomposition of root litter contributes 70 to 80
percent of total soil respiration across a range of forested ecosystems.
Coniferous trees allocate approximately twice as much carbon to roots as
deciduous species, reflecting a trade-off in nitrogen acquisition in which the
low leaf litter quality of conifers leads to low rates of nitrogen
mineralization and correspondingly greater below-ground carbon investment.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi and Carbon Allocation

Approximately 90 percent of all higher plant species are associated with
mycorrhizal fungi, a relationship that has profound implications for carbon
cycling. In the mycorrhizal symbiosis, the host plant supplies carbohydrates
to the fungal partner in exchange for enhanced nutrient uptake, particularly
of phosphorus and nitrogen. This bidirectional carbon-nutrient exchange means
that mycorrhizae are simultaneously sinks for plant-derived carbon and sources
of mineral nutrients for plant growth, creating a tight coupling between the
carbon and nutrient cycles that shapes the productivity of terrestrial
ecosystems.

The amount of carbon allocated below ground to support mycorrhizal
associates is substantial. Studies have shown five to six times greater
translocation of carbon to mycorrhizal root systems compared to nonmycorrhizal
roots. In oak seedlings, the presence of the ectomycorrhizal fungus Laccaria
laccata had more than an additive effect on growth in elevated carbon dioxide
conditions, suggesting synergistic interactions between the mycorrhizal
symbiosis and atmospheric carbon availability. The formation of ectomycorrhizal
root tips increases root branching, which increases the carbon demand of the
root system but also enhances the volume of soil exploited for nutrient
uptake. Species-dependent functional responses among mycorrhizal fungi mean
that the nature of the carbon sink below ground varies with community
composition.

A particularly significant aspect of mycorrhizal carbon dynamics is the
phenomenon of interplant carbon transfer through common mycorrhizal networks.
Mycorrhizal hyphae can interconnect the root systems of neighboring plants,
including individuals of different species, allowing the movement of carbon
and nutrients between them. Simard and colleagues demonstrated transfer of
carbon from paper birch to Douglas fir seedlings growing in both partial and
deep shade, considerably supplementing photosynthetically derived carbon in
the seedling plants. This mechanism enhances the survival of seedlings growing
under the canopy of parent trees. Amaranthus and Perry showed that Douglas fir
seedlings planted close to adult trees had survival approaching 90 percent,
whereas those planted at great distances had survival of only 50 percent after
two years, attributing the difference to the lack of a viable communal
ectomycorrhizal network.

The enzymatic capabilities of ectomycorrhizal fungi further influence carbon
cycling. Many ectomycorrhizal species produce enzymes that enable them to
access organic forms of nitrogen and phosphorus from soil organic matter,
effectively functioning as partial saprotrophs. In nitrogen-limited ecosystems,
this capability allows plants to access nutrients locked in recalcitrant
organic matter. The production of these enzymes represents a significant
carbon cost to the fungus, but the return is the acquisition of limiting
nutrients that support continued plant photosynthesis and carbon fixation.
Different ectomycorrhizal species show markedly different capacities for
organic nutrient acquisition, meaning that shifts in mycorrhizal community
composition can alter the overall carbon-nutrient balance of forest ecosystems.

## Fungi, Climate Change, and Feedback Loops

The relationship between fungi and the global carbon cycle takes on particular
urgency in the context of anthropogenic climate change. Rising atmospheric
carbon dioxide concentrations, increasing temperatures, and altered
precipitation patterns all have the potential to shift the balance between
carbon fixation and decomposition, and fungi are positioned at the center of
these feedback loops.

Elevated atmospheric carbon dioxide generally stimulates plant
photosynthesis, leading to increased allocation of carbon below ground to
roots and their mycorrhizal associates. In a Mediterranean old-field study, a doubling of carbon dioxide increased root biomass by 31 percent, root
length by 88 percent, soil microbial biomass by 42 percent, soil fungal
hyphal length by 20 percent, and total root colonization by arbuscular
mycorrhizae by 57 percent. These increases in fungal biomass are reflected
by increases in fungal-feeding nematodes, suggesting a higher rate of energy
transfer through the fungal-based food web at elevated carbon dioxide. A
five-fold increase in arbuscular mycorrhizal extraradical mycelium has been
observed under elevated carbon dioxide conditions, and in a long-term
exposure study along natural carbon dioxide springs, mycorrhizal root
colonization and soil hyphal length increased linearly with the carbon dioxide
gradient.

The effect of elevated carbon dioxide on plant litter chemistry adds further
complexity. Leaves produced under elevated carbon dioxide generally have
higher carbon-to-nitrogen ratios and lignin-to-nitrogen ratios, making them
less palatable to decomposer organisms. In Mediterranean oak forests, elevated
carbon dioxide increased the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of leaf litter to such
an extent that decomposition rates declined by 5 to 8 percent in field
experiments and 12.5 percent in microcosm experiments. Slower decomposition
means that carbon accumulates in the litter layer, but it also means that
nutrients are released more slowly, potentially limiting plant productivity
over the longer term. The significant increase in carbon-to-nitrogen and
lignin-to-nitrogen ratios of beech twigs grown in elevated carbon dioxide,
however, did not always translate into reduced decomposition rates,
illustrating that changes in leaf chemistry may not always be correlated with
the rate of decomposition processes.

Rising temperatures present a more direct challenge. Increased soil
temperatures raise the metabolic activity of saprotrophic microbial
communities, accelerating decomposition rates and the release of carbon
dioxide from soil organic matter. In a simulation of boreal forest
ecosystems, a five-degree Celsius rise in air temperature was predicted to
cause increased soil respiration losses of 439 grams of carbon per square
meter in birch forests and 675 grams per square meter in black spruce
forests. In white spruce ecosystems, decomposition losses were projected to
exceed carbon gains from enhanced photosynthesis, potentially converting these
forests from carbon sinks to carbon sources. The total gain in carbon from
increased photosynthesis effectively counteracted decomposition losses in birch
and black spruce ecosystems, but not in white spruce, highlighting the
ecosystem-specific nature of these responses.

The interaction between elevated carbon dioxide and increased temperature
creates competing pressures on fungal communities. Dighton and Jansen proposed
a model in which enhanced photosynthesis provides more carbohydrates to
support mycorrhizal development and diversity, potentially favoring species
capable of producing enzymes for acquiring major nutrients from organic
sources. Simultaneously, increased temperatures accelerate saprotrophic
decomposition, increasing the size of available nutrient pools and potentially
reducing plant dependence on mycorrhizae. The net effect on fungal community
structure and function is likely to be ecosystem-specific, depending on the
initial nitrogen status, plant species composition, and soil characteristics.

Simulation models also predict enhanced risks of fungal pathogen
outbreaks with increased temperatures and reduced rainfall. These changes
in pathogen pressure will interact with shifts in mycorrhizal function
and saprotrophic activity to reshape the fungal contribution to ecosystem
carbon dynamics.

## Fungal Biodiversity and Ecosystem Resilience

The functional diversity of fungi is vast and only partially characterized.
David Hawksworth estimated that at least 74,000 and possibly as many as
120,000 fungal species have been formally named, while the total number of
species on Earth has been conservatively estimated at 1.5 million. Evidence
accumulated since that estimate suggests the actual number may be even higher,
meaning that we have described at most five percent of the fungal species on
Earth. The implications for carbon cycling are significant. A diverse fungal
community is better able to maintain decomposition and nutrient cycling across
a range of environmental conditions and resource qualities. If climate change
or other perturbations alter environmental conditions in ways that favor some
fungal species over others, a diverse community is more likely to retain
species capable of performing essential functions. Conversely, the loss of
fungal diversity through habitat destruction, pollution, or other
anthropogenic pressures may erode the functional resilience of ecosystems,
potentially destabilizing carbon and nutrient cycles.

Fungi have demonstrated remarkable resilience to extreme conditions. Following the
Chernobyl nuclear disaster, fungal communities showed increased dominance of
melanin-containing species, which appeared protected against ionizing
radiation damage. Some fungi even evolved radiotropism, actively directing
their growth toward sources of radioactivity. Monitoring communities around
Chernobyl over 15 years resulted in the isolation of about 2,000 strains of
180 species from 92 genera, with community complexity decreasing but
melanized species proportion increasing with radioactive dose.

## Fungi in Ecosystem Restoration and Sustainable Systems

The understanding of fungal roles in decomposition has practical applications in
restoration and sustainable land management. In restoration projects, the
inoculation of plants with mycorrhizal fungi has been shown to significantly
increase plant survival, growth, and nutrient content on degraded sites. In a
study of surface lignite mine restoration, inoculation of three grass species
with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi increased survival by up to 30 percent and
above-ground biomass by up to 80 percent compared to uninoculated controls.

The concept of mycoforestry, advanced by Paul Stamets, involves matching
native fungi to native tree species and using inoculated wood chips to
accelerate soil development following forest disturbance. The mycelium binds
the substrate into a network that reduces erosion while decomposing wood
chips and building soil. Mushroom cultivation on agricultural wastes, from
spent brewery grain to coffee pulp, demonstrates how the fungal saprophytic
relationship can be applied in human systems, simultaneously producing food
and returning nutrients to the soil through spent mushroom substrate.

## The Rhizosphere as an Integrated Functional System

The zone of soil immediately surrounding plant roots, known as the rhizosphere,
is the site of the most intense interactions between fungi and other soil
organisms. Root exudates and dead root cells provide a rich source of readily
available carbohydrates that stimulate microbial activity, creating a hotspot
of decomposition and nutrient cycling. The fungal hyphae that ramify through
the rhizosphere serve simultaneously as decomposers of organic matter,
conduits for nutrient transport to plant roots, and substrates for
fungal-feeding soil fauna.

The importance of the fungal rhizosphere extends beyond individual plants. The
mycelial networks that connect multiple plants create a communication and
resource-sharing system that operates at the scale of whole plant communities.
Paul Stamets has described this as a living internet that possesses sentience,
intelligence, and inherent ecological healing properties. While this
characterization may be poetic, the functional reality is that mycelial
networks in soils support biodiversity and resilience, build soils, and give
habitats the ability to respond to catastrophes. The recognition of this
integrated fungal function is reshaping our understanding of how ecosystems
work and how they can be managed for sustainability.

## Conclusions

Fungal decomposition stands at the intersection of the carbon cycle, nutrient
cycling, and soil formation. The enzymatic capabilities of fungi, from the
lignin peroxidases of white rot basidiomycetes to the Fenton chemistry of
brown rot fungi, enable the breakdown of the most recalcitrant plant polymers,
releasing nutrients for primary production and carbon dioxide to the
atmosphere. The mycelial networks of saprotrophic and mycorrhizal fungi create
the physical and biological infrastructure of soil, stabilizing aggregates,
storing carbon, and facilitating the flow of energy and materials through the
soil food web. The immense biodiversity of fungi provides the functional
redundancy and resilience that ecosystems need to maintain these critical
processes under changing environmental conditions.

As the global climate continues to change, the response of fungal communities
will play a decisive role in determining whether terrestrial ecosystems remain
carbon sinks or become carbon sources. The interactions among elevated carbon
dioxide, rising temperatures, altered precipitation, nitrogen deposition, and
fungal community function are complex and ecosystem-specific, but the general
direction is clear: fungi will be both mediators of and responders to climate
change, and their conservation and management must be a central consideration
in any strategy for maintaining the functional integrity of the biosphere. The
recognition that the vast majority of fungal species remain undescribed and
their functions unknown is both a challenge and an opportunity, as within
that hidden diversity may lie the mechanisms that determine the capacity
of Earth's ecosystems to adapt to the changes of the Anthropocene.
