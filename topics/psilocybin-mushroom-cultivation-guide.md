# Psilocybin Mushroom Cultivation Guide

Growing psilocybin mushrooms is a fascinating intersection of mycology,
microbiology, and patience. Unlike growing plants, mushroom cultivation works
with an entirely different kingdom of life — fungi — requiring sterile
technique, microbial ecology, and specific environmental conditions to trigger
fruiting. The most commonly cultivated species is Psilocybe cubensis, the
easiest to obtain and cultivate, most disease-resistant, and among the most
potent. Adam Gottlieb's 1976 guide described it as the primary recommended
species for home cultivation, and Paul Stamets' *The Mushroom Cultivator*
(1983, co-authored with J.S. Chilton) and *Growing Gourmet and Medicinal
Mushrooms* remain the most comprehensive technical references.

This guide covers the complete cultivation process, from strain selection and
agar work through grain spawn, bulk substrate, fruiting, and harvest. The
methods represent current best practices synthesized from expert sources and
the accumulated wisdom of the online mycology community.

## Understanding the Mushroom Life Cycle

A mushroom is not the organism itself — it is the reproductive fruiting body
of a vast network of thread-like cells called mycelium. The lifecycle begins
with a spore, which germinates into monokaryotic mycelium (one set of
chromosomes). This must fuse with a compatible monokaryon to form dikaryotic
mycelium (two nuclei per cell), capable of forming fruiting bodies. With
multispore inoculation, millions of spores produce many compatible matings and
genetic diversity. When mycelium fully colonizes its food supply and encounters
fresh air exchange, light, and a temperature drop, it forms primordia (pins)
that develop into mature mushrooms.

This lifecycle clarifies why each step matters: agar isolates the strongest
genetics, grain spawn expands mycelial mass, bulk substrate provides water and
nutrients, and the fruiting chamber delivers environmental triggers for
reproduction.

## Strain Selection and Spore Sources

Approximately 40 Conocybe species, 20 Panaeolus species, 40 Psilocybe species,
and 9 Stropharia species are known to contain psilocybin. However, P. cubensis
is overwhelmingly the preferred species for home cultivation due to its
forgiving nature, fast colonization speed, and substantial yields. The species
has been known by several taxonomic names, including Stropharia cubensis and
Psilocybe cubensis var. cyanescens.

Within P. cubensis, dozens of named varieties circulate — Golden Teacher, B+,
Ecuador, Thai, Cambodian, Amazonian, Penis Envy, and many others. These are
isolated subpopulations selected for particular characteristics, not distinct
taxonomic varieties. Strain degradation occurs through continuous reinoculation
(senescence); mitigation involves alternating culture media and always
selecting the most vigorous mycelia.

Other species catalogued by Gottlieb include Copelandia cyanescens (optimum
80°F), P. caerulescens (very long 55–85 day incubation), P. baeocystis
(described as "most visually hallucinogenic"), and P. mexicana (the species
from which Hofmann first isolated psilocybin). Most are significantly more
challenging to cultivate than P. cubensis and are recommended only for
experienced growers.

An important safety consideration: spore exchange networks carry risks of
misidentification and even malicious substitution of toxic species. Gottlieb
strongly advises verifying species identity through proper mycological keys
(he recommends R. Shaffer's "Keys to Genera of Higher Fungi") before
consuming any cultivated mushrooms. Spore possession is legal in many
jurisdictions since spores do not contain controlled substances, but
cultivation is illegal in most countries.

## Agar Culture Work

### Why Work with Agar

Agar culture is the foundation of successful mushroom cultivation. While it
is possible to skip agar and inoculate grain directly from a spore syringe,
doing so means accepting whatever genetics you receive with no ability to
select for vigor, contamination resistance, or fruiting performance. Agar
provides a two-dimensional surface on which you can observe mycelial growth,
identify contamination, and isolate the strongest strains before committing to
the more expensive and time-consuming step of grain spawn production.

### Media Formulations

Malt Extract Agar (MEA) is the gold standard: 20 grams of light malt extract
powder and 20 grams of agar per liter of water. Light malt (not dark or
diastatic) provides balanced nutrition without excess sugars that promote
bacterial contamination. Stamets recommends adding two grams of yeast per
liter. A lower-nutrient "light MEA" (10 grams malt per liter) produces
cleaner, slower growth that makes contamination easier to spot.

Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) uses 200 to 300 grams of sliced potatoes boiled
in one liter of water for 30 minutes, strained, with 10 to 20 grams dextrose
and 15 to 20 grams agar per liter of potato water. PDY Agar adds two grams
of yeast for a more nutritious general-purpose medium.

Other useful formulations include Dog Food Agar (DFA) — 40 grams of dry dog
food blended in a liter of water, boiled 20 minutes, strained, with 20 grams
agar per liter — which produces exceptionally fast, rhizomorphic growth but has
a shelf life of only two to three weeks. Grain Water Agar, made from water
left after boiling or soaking grain, is a zero-waste option. All media should
use distilled or RO water, as chlorine inhibits mycelial growth.

### pH and Sterilization

Most mushroom species prefer a pH of 5.0 to 6.5; since sterilization shifts pH
upward slightly, target the lower end. Adjust with dilute citric acid (to
lower) or calcium carbonate or KOH (to raise). Sterilize at 15 psi (250°F /
121°C) for 20 to 45 minutes — it is critical not to exceed 250°F, as sugars
caramelize at higher temperatures and produce compounds that inhibit mycelial
growth. Allow pressure to release naturally.

### Pouring Plates

Cool sterilized agar to 45 to 50°C (warm to the touch — holdable for about
10 seconds). Pour in a laminar flow hood or still air box, filling plates about
one-third to one-half full (~20 ml per 90 mm plate, or 0.3 to 0.5 cm depth).
Stack pouring minimizes condensation on lids. Allow plates to solidify
undisturbed and store inverted at room temperature.

### Reading Mycelial Growth

Learning to read mycelial growth on agar is essential. Rhizomorphic growth
appears as organized, strand-like extensions — fast-growing and typically
correlated with higher primordia production and prolific fruiting. This is the
type cultivators select for. Tomentose (cottony) growth is fluffy, aerial, and
slow-growing, correlating with reduced fruiting potential. Sectoring — wedge-
shaped areas of differing growth patterns — indicates multiple genetic strains
or mutation in older cultures.

### Isolation Techniques

The basic isolation workflow begins with germinating spores on agar — scraped
from a print with a flame-sterilized scalpel and streaked in an S-pattern.
Germination typically occurs in three to five days but can take up to four
weeks. Once growth appears, transfer small wedges from the fastest-growing,
most rhizomorphic sectors to fresh plates, repeating three to five times until
a uniform, sector-free isolate is obtained.

Tissue culture offers a shortcut: split open a fruiting body and transfer a
small piece of inner tissue (never touching the exterior) to agar. Mycelium
grows in three to seven days and is genetically identical to the parent
mushroom. Inoculating 10 or more plates from a single tissue sample increases
odds of obtaining clean cultures.

### Culture Storage

Agar plates should be stored inverted at room temperature (70 to 75°F) and
sealed with parafilm, best used within two to four weeks. For longer storage,
agar slants — test tubes filled one-third full with agar, cooled at a 15 to
30 degree angle — can be stored refrigerated (35 to 40°F) for six to twelve
months, with annual viability checks. Mineral oil overlay extends storage to
five to twenty years.

## Grain Spawn Production

### Choosing the Right Grain

Grain spawn is the bridge between the laboratory (agar) and the fruiting
stage (bulk substrate). The grain provides a nutrient-dense medium with
thousands of individual colonization points, allowing mycelium to multiply
dramatically before being introduced to the bulk substrate.

Rye is the standard grain, favored by Stamets and most commercial cultivators,
with approximately 30 kernels per gram (~25,000 per liter) providing fast
colonization (~76 hours) and low contamination rates (~11 percent). Millet
offers even more colonization points (166 per gram) but is slightly more
contamination-prone. Wheat, sorghum, brown rice, and perennial ryegrass are
viable alternatives.

### Grain Preparation Methods

Two fundamental methods exist for preparing grain. The boiling or steeping
method — preferred by most experienced cultivators — involves submerging grain
in boiling water for approximately one hour, draining thoroughly, and scooping
into containers. Cotter recommends removing grain from heat at about 160°F,
covering and soaking for 30 to 60 minutes. Properly prepared grains, when cut
in half, should be "mostly moist throughout with a small dried center."

The alternative places dry grain in jars with measured hot water, lets it sit
for 12 to 24 hours (germinating bacterial endospores so they can be killed
during sterilization), then sterilizes in the jar. Simpler, but produces less
even moisture distribution.

Moisture content is critical. The optimum range is 45 to 55 percent, with 50
percent being ideal. Below 40 percent, growth is retarded and mycelium appears
as fine, weak threads. Above 55 percent, kernels explode during sterilization,
creating clumps that impede gas exchange and promote bacterial blooms. A
standard quart jar recipe uses 200 grams of rye, 200 ml of water, and one
gram of gypsum (which provides calcium and prevents grain kernels from
sticking together).

### Sterilization

Spawn jars must be sterilized to eliminate the estimated 50,000 to 100,000
bacteria, more than 200,000 actinomycetes, 12,000 fungi, and numerous yeasts
present per gram of commercial grain. Standard quart jars require 60 to 90
minutes at 15 psi (250°F); half-gallon jars need 90 minutes; gallon jars
require two hours; polypropylene spawn bags require four hours. Allow pressure
to return to zero naturally — rapid depressurization causes jars to boil over.

### Inoculation and Colonization

Agar-to-grain transfer is the cleanest inoculation method. One to two wedges
per quart jar is standard; a single two-thirds colonized petri dish can
inoculate six to ten quart jars. The double wedge technique (cutting a single
wedge in half) increases contact points and significantly speeds colonization.

Grain-to-grain transfer is faster in terms of total volume — each quart of
fully colonized grain can inoculate six to ten additional quart jars — but
carries higher contamination risk because the original jar's filter has been
exposed to ambient air during colonization. A 5 to 10 percent transfer ratio
by volume is standard.

After inoculation, jars are incubated at 70 to 78°F for P. cubensis. Shaking
at 20 to 30 percent colonization speeds the process, but wait three to five
days after agar transfer for wedges to visibly attach before shaking. Full
colonization typically takes seven to ten days with agar-to-grain transfer.

Generation tracking is important for maintaining quality. G0 is the original
pure isolate from agar; G1 is the first master grain spawn; G2 and G3 are
successive expansions. Higher generations accumulate genetic errors, and spawn
viability declines significantly with age — 60-day-old spawn may be half as
viable as 30-day-old. Most cultivators limit themselves to three generations
and maintain master slants of the G0 culture for long-term storage.

## Bulk Substrate Preparation

### The CVG Standard

Once grain spawn is fully colonized, it is mixed with a bulk substrate — a
larger volume of moist, nutritious material that provides the water and
nutrients needed to produce substantial flushes of mushrooms. For P. cubensis,
the universal standard is CVG: coconut coir, vermiculite, and gypsum.

The standard recipe uses one 650-gram compressed coco coir brick, two quarts
of medium or coarse vermiculite, and one-half cup of agricultural-grade
gypsum (5 to 10 percent by volume). The coir is hydrated by placing the brick
in a five-gallon bucket and adding four quarts of boiling water. This "bucket
tek" method works because coir is naturally clean — pasteurized during
manufacturing — and the core temperature reaches approximately 160 to 180°F
(71 to 82°C), sufficient to pasteurize the coir. After 30 to 60 minutes,
the vermiculite and gypsum are added and mixed thoroughly. The substrate must
cool to 70 to 78°F before spawning.

Coconut coir holds 70 to 75 percent moisture at field capacity. Vermiculite
absorbs 75 to 80 percent. Together, the CVG mix provides excellent water
retention, aeration, and structure. Gypsum adds calcium and sulfur, prevents
substrate compaction, and helps buffer pH.

### Field Capacity

Field capacity — the moisture content at which the substrate holds maximum
water while retaining adequate air spaces — is critical. The "squeeze test" is
the practical assessment: grab a handful and squeeze firmly. One to three drops
should emerge, but no stream. The substrate should form a loose ball that breaks
apart when disturbed. For precise measurement, a gravimetric test can be
performed: weigh a 100-gram sample, dry at 200 to 250°F for two to three hours,
and calculate moisture as (wet weight minus dry weight) divided by wet weight,
times 100.

### Supplementation and Alternative Substrates

CVG can be supplemented with additional nutrients to increase yields, but
supplemented substrates require true pasteurization (held at 140 to 170°F for
60 to 120 minutes) rather than the simple bucket tek. Coffee grounds at 5 to
10 percent by volume can be added fresh to bucket tek (already pasteurized by
brewing). Wheat bran at 5 to 10 percent adds protein and carbohydrates. Blood
meal, bone meal, and ground grain are other options, though they increase
contamination risk.

### Spawn Ratio and Substrate Depth

The spawn-to-substrate ratio determines both colonization speed and
contamination resistance. A 1:1 ratio is fastest and most resistant but
expensive; 1:2 is the standard recommendation; 1:3 works for experienced
growers with clean spawn. Ratios above 1:4 are risky for beginners. Substrate
depth should be three inches minimum, with four to six inches being optimal. A
standard 66-quart monotub typically uses four to five inches, requiring eight to
twelve quarts of CVG plus spawn.

## Fruiting Chambers and the Art of Pinning

### The Monotub Method

The monotub is the most widely used cultivation method in the home mycology
community, and for good reason. A single plastic storage bin serves as both
the colonization vessel and the fruiting chamber, eliminating the need to
transfer colonized substrate to a separate fruiting container. The standard
workhorse is the 66-quart Sterilite or equivalent tub, though 54-quart tubs
are common for beginners and 80 to 110-quart tubs are used by experienced
growers.

The tub should have opaque or translucent sides to prevent side pinning.
Holes are drilled for fresh air exchange (FAE). The standard 66-quart
configuration uses four large holes (two inches) two to four inches from the
bottom on the long sides, stuffed loosely with polyfill, and four to eight
small holes (one inch) near the top on all sides, stuffed tightly.

The spawning process begins with a thin layer of plain CVG on the tub bottom.
Colonized grain spawn is then thoroughly mixed with the hydrated CVG substrate,
breaking up grain and distributing evenly. A quarter to half inch of plain CVG
is applied on top as a casing layer to maintain surface moisture and provide
a microclimate conducive to pinning.

### Colonization and Fruiting Initiation

During colonization, the tub lid is secured and holes taped over. Keep at 70
to 78°F in darkness or ambient light for seven to fourteen days. Some
cultivators wait an additional three to five days after full colonization for
mycelial consolidation.

Fruiting is initiated by removing tape and stuffing holes with polyfill.
Provide a 12-hour on/off light schedule using a 6500K LED — P. cubensis does
poorly in darkness, and mushrooms are phototropic. Evaporation from the
substrate surface is a critical pinning trigger, making FAE through polyfill
holes essential. First pins appear three to seven days after initiation, and
mushrooms mature five to ten days from pin to harvest.

### Environmental Parameters

For P. cubensis, the optimal fruiting temperature is 70 to 80°F with humidity
at 85 to 95 percent and two to three air exchanges per hour. Carbon dioxide
levels serve as a visual indicator: elongated stems with small caps indicate
excessive CO₂, meaning more FAE is needed.

### Expected Yields

A properly managed 66-quart monotub with four quarts of grain spawn produces
two to six ounces dry (56 to 170 grams) in the first flush, with subsequent
flushes yielding less — four to ten ounces dry (113 to 283 grams) total over
two to three flushes. Between flushes, dunk the substrate block for four to
twelve hours to rehydrate.

### The Shotgun Fruiting Chamber

An alternative to the monotub, the Shotgun Fruiting Chamber (SGFC) uses a
terrarium with holes drilled on all six sides for passive air exchange through
natural convection, with a layer of perlite at the base for humidification. It
requires manual fanning and misting but works well for smaller grows using
individual cakes or trays rather than bulk substrate.

## Contamination Prevention

### The Five Vectors

Paul Stamets identified five primary vectors of contamination, in order of
significance: the cultivator themselves (skin, breath, clothing shed
microorganisms constantly); the air (~10,000 particulates per cubic foot in an
average room vs. fewer than 100 in a sterile lab); the media (arriving with
millions of microorganisms per gram); the transferred mycelium (which may
carry hidden contamination); and tools, equipment, and the facility.

The goal of sterile technique is not absolute sterility but giving mushroom
mycelium enough of a head start to colonize the substrate before competing
organisms establish themselves.

### Sterile Technique

Gottlieb's "Sanitary Code" provides a practical framework. Before work, scrub
arms, hands, and nails with disinfectant soap. Wear a freshly cleaned short-
sleeve shirt, surgical mask, and hair cap. Gargle with antiseptic mouthwash.
The workspace should be clean, uncluttered, and draft-free — close doors, stuff
gaps with towels, and exclude animals. Work within a still air box or laminar
flow hood if available.

Glassware should be boiled for 30 minutes before use. Metal tools are flame-
sterilized until glowing red and re-flamed after each transfer. Containers are
autoclaved at 15 to 20 psi for 30 minutes (longer for larger volumes) — never
exceeding 250°F to avoid sugar caramelization.

### Identifying and Responding to Contamination

Healthy mycelium appears as pure white fibrous mats, sometimes with a light
bluish tinge (bruising, not contamination). Common contaminants include
Trichoderma (green mold — rapidly growing white spots with blue-green centers),
bacterial contamination (surface scums, slime, sour smells), and mold (fuzzy
patches in gray, black, yellow, green, or blue). The rule is absolute: discard
any contaminated culture entirely. Never open contaminated jars indoors, as
released spores can compromise future grows.

A diagnostic rule: contamination near the inoculation wedge indicates
contaminated inoculum; contamination at the plate periphery indicates airborne
contamination during pouring. Hold autoclaved containers at room temperature
for three days before inoculation to catch sterilization failures.

## Harvesting, Drying, and Storage

### When to Harvest

Mushrooms should be harvested just before or as the veil beneath the cap tears
— this thin membrane connecting the cap to the stem ruptures as the mushroom
matures. Harvesting at this stage maximizes potency relative to weight, as the
mushroom continues to add water weight after the veil tears without
significantly increasing alkaloid content. Grasp the mushroom at the base and
twist gently while pulling upward; avoid cutting at the stem base, as the
remaining stump can become a contamination site.

### Drying

Drying should begin within a few hours of harvest to preserve psilocin, which
degrades quickly. A food dehydrator at 95 to 115°F works well; temperatures
above 150°F risk degrading active compounds. Mushrooms are done when "cracker
dry" — snapping cleanly with no flexibility, typically 12 to 24 hours.

### Storage

Dried mushrooms should be stored in an airtight container with a silica-gel
desiccant packet, in a cool, dark place. Potency can be maintained for a year
or more; refrigeration extends storage life, and freezing can preserve potency
indefinitely if the container is properly sealed to prevent condensation.

## Safety Considerations

Cultivation safety encompasses biological, chemical, and legal dimensions.
The primary biological risks are misidentification (cultivating a toxic species
due to contaminated spore samples) and exposure to mold spores from
contaminated cultures. All contaminated materials should be discarded in sealed
bags. Chemically, methanol used for alkaloid extraction is toxic and highly
flammable, and pressure cookers must be operated within rated limits with
natural pressure release. Legally, cultivation remains illegal in most
jurisdictions worldwide; spores are legal to possess in many places because
they do not contain controlled substances, but the moment they germinate, the
resulting mycelium and mushrooms are illegal.
