# Psychoactive Tryptamines in Nature

Psychoactive tryptamines represent one of the most intriguing categories of bioactive
compounds found in nature. These substances, which include psilocybin, psilocin,
N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), 5-methoxy-DMT, bufotenine, and their chemical
relatives, occur across an astonishingly wide range of organisms spanning fungi,
plants, and even animals. Despite decades of research into their chemistry and
pharmacology, fundamental questions about why these compounds exist in nature, what
ecological roles they serve, and how they came to be distributed across the globe
remain subjects of active scientific inquiry and debate. The story of psychoactive
tryptamines in nature touches upon fundamental questions in evolutionary biology,
chemical ecology, biogeography, and the deep history of the relationship between
humans and the natural world.

## The Chemical Foundation: From Shikimate to Serotonin

All tryptamine-based compounds share a common biosynthetic lineage that begins with
the shikimic acid pathway, a metabolic route present in many plants, fungi, and
bacteria but conspicuously absent in animals. This pathway, which starts from
phosphoenolpyruvate and eryrose 4-phosphate, produces chorismate, a critical
branch-point metabolite that feeds into the synthesis of folate, ubiquinone,
phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan. The shikimate pathway is extremely
important for understanding the production of many different aromatic compounds,
including many antibiotics as well as the tryptamine-based psychedelic drugs like
DMT, the ergot alkaloids, and psilocybin. It is present in many different plants,
fungi, and bacteria, but is not present in animals, which must obtain tryptophan
through their diet. The pathway begins with phosphoenolpyruvate, a product of
glycolysis, and eryrose 4-phosphate, a product of the pentose phosphate pathway.
Through a series of seven enzymatic steps, these starting materials are converted
into chorismate, which then branches into the pathway leading to tryptophan
synthesis.

Tryptophan serves as the immediate precursor to all tryptamine alkaloids. Through
a series of decarboxylation, methylation, and hydroxylation steps, organisms
convert this essential amino acid into an array of psychoactive compounds. In the
case of psilocybin biosynthesis in fungi, the pathway proceeds from tryptophan
through tryptamine to 4-hydroxytryptamine (which is serotonin in its free form),
which then undergoes further modifications including N-methylation and
phosphorylation to produce 4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine phosphate, better
known as psilocybin. The related compound psilocin is the dephosphorylated, active
metabolite of psilocybin, and is the form that actually interacts with serotonin
receptors in the mammalian brain. Understanding the metabolic pathway is essential
for any scientific investigation of the production of these compounds, as each
enzymatic step represents a potential point of regulation, variation, or
evolutionary divergence.

The structural similarity between these naturally occurring compounds and the
neurotransmitter serotonin is no coincidence. Serotonin, or 5-hydroxytryptamine,
plays a central role in the biochemistry of the central nervous system of warm-
blooded animals, regulating mood, perception, appetite, sleep, and numerous other
physiological processes. As Schultes and Hofmann noted in Plants of the Gods, the
indole nucleus of the hallucinogens frequently appears in the form of tryptamine
derivatives, and one reason for the significance of the indolic hallucinogens may
be their structural similarity to serotonin present in nervous tissue. A chemical
relationship parallel to that between indolic hallucinogens and serotonin exists
between mescaline, a phenylethylamine base found in peyote, and the neurohormone
norepinephrine. These chemical similarities between hallucinogenic compounds and
neurohormones with roles in neurophysiology may help to explain hallucinogenic
activity and certain processes of the central nervous system.

## Global Biogeography of Neurotropic Fungi

The distribution of psychoactive tryptamine-producing organisms across the globe
reveals striking biogeographic patterns that have only become clear through decades
of mycological fieldwork and taxonomic study. The most comprehensive survey of
neurotropic fungi worldwide, conducted by Gaston Guzman, John W. Allen, and Jochen
Gartz, documented 216 known species of neurotropic fungi distributed across four
major groups: species containing psilocybin and related indole compounds, species
containing ibotenic acid, ergot fungi, and species used as sacred fungi for which
no reliable chemical studies have been confirmed.

Within the psilocybin-containing group, the genus Psilocybe dominates with 116
species, followed by Gymnopilus with 14 species, Panaeolus with 13 species,
Copelandia with 12 species, Hypholoma with 6 species, Pluteus with 6 species, and
Inocybe with 6 species. Additional genera contributing neurotropic species include
Conocybe, Panaeolina, Gerronema, and isolated species within Agrocybe, Galerina,
and Mycena. The ibotenic acid group comprises [[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]], Amanita pantherina,
and Amanita regalis, while the ergot group includes [[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]] and its
allies across five species of Claviceps and two of Cordyceps.

Perhaps the most striking biogeographic finding is the strong concentration of
neurotropic fungal diversity in or near the [[guzman-allen-gartz-africa-southern-hemisphere-underexplored-neurotropic-fungi]], particularly in
subtropical humid forests. Of the 85 neurotropic species of Psilocybe analyzed in
Guzman's seminal 1983 monograph, 59 occur in South America and Mexico, compared
with 18 in the United States and Canada, and only 9 in Europe. This disparity is
particularly remarkable because North America and Europe have been far more
thoroughly explored mycologically than many regions of the Southern Hemisphere,
suggesting that the true diversity of [[ethnobotanical-use-of-neurotropic-fungi-in-mexico]] tropical and subtropical
regions may be even greater than currently documented.

Mexico stands as the single most significant center of neurotropic fungal
diversity on Earth, harboring 76 species of neurotropic fungi, of which 44 belong
to the genus Psilocybe, representing approximately 39 percent of the world's known
Psilocybe species. Within Mexico, of the 42 neurotropic Psilocybe species
documented, 34 are associated with mesophytic forests, 4 with tropical forests, and
4 with coniferous forests, despite the fact that coniferous forests have been more
intensively surveyed by mycologists. The indigenous peoples of Mexico developed an
extraordinarily rich vocabulary for these fungi, with Guzman documenting more than
two hundred common names, many in indigenous languages. These included names such
as "apipiltzin" (kid or little boy), "atkad" (mayor or leader), "teonanacatl"
(divine mushroom), "san isidros" (after Saint Isidore, patron of agriculture),
"pajaritos" (little birds), and "derrumbes" (landslides).

## The Southern Hemisphere Pattern and Trans-Pacific Connections

The biogeographic patterns of neurotropic fungi reveal a fascinating connection
between the mycofloras of Mesoamerica, South America, and the South Pacific.
Horak's analysis of agarics and boletes in the South Pacific hemisphere
demonstrated that species from South America and Mesoamerica are closely related to
those of Southeast Asia, New Guinea, Indonesia, and Australia. This pattern is
particularly evident among neurotropic Psilocybe species: Psilocybe samuiensis from
Thailand and Psilocybe makarorae from New Zealand are both closely related to
[[dark-incubation-sclerotia-production-psilocybe-mexicana-tampanensis]] from Mexico and Guatemala, all belonging to the section
Mexicanae. Similarly, Psilocybe aucklandii from New Zealand is closely related to
Psilocybe zapotecorum from Mexico and South America, both belonging to the section
Zapotecorum.
