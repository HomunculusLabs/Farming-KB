# Plant Volatile Organic Compounds and Chemical Ecology

## Overview

Plants are not passive organisms. They continuously produce and release a diverse array of **volatile organic compounds (VOCs)** — low-molecular-weight lipophilic molecules with high vapor pressures that mediate interactions between plants, herbivores, predators, parasitoids, and the broader [[edaphic-factors-microbial-community-structure]]. The study of these [[query-how-do-plants-use-chemical-signals-to-defend-against-insects]] constitutes the field of **chemical ecology**, and it has transformed our understanding of how ecosystems function.

Plants release an estimated **1% of their fixed carbon** as VOCs. Over 1,700 distinct [[query-how-do-fungal-volatile-compounds-affect-plant-growth-and-other-microorganisms]] have been identified across the plant kingdom, belonging to several major biosynthetic classes: terpenoids, phenylpropanoids/benzenoids, fatty acid derivatives, amino acid derivatives, and various other specialized metabolites.

## Biosynthetic Pathways of Plant VOCs

### Terpenoids (Isoprenoids)

Terpenoids are the largest and most structurally diverse class of plant VOCs, built from five-carbon isoprene (C₅H₈) or dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP) precursors.

- **Hemiterpenes (C₅):** Isoprene itself, the single most abundant VOC emitted by plants globally (~500 Tg/year). Emitted primarily by broadleaf trees (oaks, poplars, eucalypts) as a thermoprotectant.
- **Monoterpenes (C₁₀):** α-pinene, β-pinene, limonene, linalool, myrcene, ocimene. Stored in specialized resin ducts or glandular trichomes in conifers, mints, citrus, and many aromatic herbs. Constitutively released and inducible upon herbivory.
- **Sesquiterpenes (C₁₅):** β-caryophyllene, (E)-β-farnesene, germacrene D. Often released in response to herbivore damage; β-caryophyllene is a key below-ground signal in maize.
- **Homoterpenes (C₁₁, C₁₆):** Derived from monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes with additional carbon units; (E)-4,8-dimethyl-1,3,7-nonatriene (DMNT) and (E,E)-4,8,12-trimethyl-1,3,7,11-tridecatetraene (TMTT) are classic herbivore-induced plant volatiles (HIPVs).

Terpenoids are synthesized via two pathways:
1. **MEP pathway (methylerythritol phosphate):** Operates in plastids; produces monoterpenes, diterpenes, and tetraterpenes.
2. **MVA pathway (mevalonate):** Operates in the cytosol/ER; produces sesquiterpenes and triterpenes.

### Phenylpropanoids and Benzenoids

Derived from the amino acid phenylalanine via the shikimate pathway:

- **Phenylpropanoids:** Eugenol (clove), isoeugenol, chavicol, estragole. Common in basil, cinnamon, and related aromatic species.
- **Benzenoids:** Benzaldehyde (bitter almond), benzyl alcohol, phenylacetaldehyde, methyl salicylate (oil of wintergreen). Methyl salicylate serves as a critical systemic acquired resistance (SAR) signal, both within the plant and between neighboring plants.
- **Phenylpropanoid-benzenoid intermediates:** Coniferyl alcohol derivatives, caffeic acid esters.

The phenylpropanoid pathway also feeds into lignin, flavonoids, and coumarins, linking volatile production to structural defense and UV protection.

### Fatty Acid Derivatives (Green Leaf Volatiles)

Also known as **GLVs**, these C₆ compounds are produced when **lipoxygenase (LOX)** enzymes oxygenate membrane lipids (linolenic and linoleic acids) upon tissue disruption:

- **Key compounds:** (Z)-3-hexenal (cut grass smell), (E)-2-hexenal (leaf aldehyde), hexenal, (Z)-3-hexenol (leaf alcohol), (Z)-3-hexenyl acetate (leaf acetate).
- **Rapid release:** GLVs are emitted within seconds of mechanical damage or herbivore feeding, making them the fastest plant distress signal.
- **Dual function:** GLVs serve as direct defenses (toxic to some herbivores and pathogens), wound-healing signals, and indirect defenses by attracting natural enemies of herbivores.

### Amino Acid Derivatives

- **Indole:** A nitrogen-containing heterocycle derived from tryptophan. Indole is a major component of HIPV blends, particularly in nightshades (tomato, maize). It synergizes with terpenoids to attract parasitic wasps.
- **Nitrogen-containing volatiles:** Alloxan, cyanogenic compounds, and various amines released during severe herbivore damage or tissue necrosis.

### Other Specialized Volatiles

- **Sulfur-containing compounds:** Dimethyl sulfide, methanethiol, isothiocyanates (from glucosinolate hydrolysis in Brassicaceae). Isothiocyanates are potent antimicrobial and anti-herbivore compounds.
- **Aliphatic and miscellaneous compounds:** Various alkanes, alkenes, and oxygenated compounds with ecological roles still being characterized.

## Ecological Functions of Plant VOCs

### Direct Defense Against Herbivores and Pathogens

Many VOCs have direct toxic, repellent, or antifeedant properties:

- **Monoterpenes** in conifer resin can trap and poison bark beetles (e.g., α-pinene, limonene).
- **GLVs** like (E)-2-hexenal possess antimicrobial activity and can inhibit fungal [[growing-gourmet-spore-germination-strain-isolation]].
- **Sulfur compounds** (isothiocyanates, allicin from garlic) are potent broad-spectrum antimicrobials.
- **Nicotine** and other alkaloid-derived volatiles are neurotoxic to insects at sufficient concentrations.

Plants can upregulate the production of these compounds within hours of herbivore attack, dramatically increasing the concentration of defensive volatiles at the damage site and systemically throughout the plant.

### Indirect Defense: Tritrophic Interactions

One of the most remarkable discoveries in chemical ecology is the **tritrophic interaction**: plants under herbivore attack release specific volatile blends that attract **predators and parasitoids** of the attacking herbivore.

#### The Classic Model: Parasitic Wasps and Caterpillars

The foundational work by **Ted Turlings and Marcel Dicke** in the late 1980s and early 1990s demonstrated that:

1. A caterpillar feeds on a maize or cotton leaf.
2. The caterpillar's oral secretions (containing **fatty acid-amino acid conjugates** or **FACs** like volicitin) are introduced into the wound.
3. The plant recognizes these FACs as herbivore-specific elicitors (not just mechanical damage).
4. The plant upregulates VOC production, releasing a blend of terpenoids, indole, and GLVs.
5. **Parasitic wasps** (e.g., *Cotesia marginiventris*, *Microplitis croceipes*) use these volatiles as long-range cues to locate the herbivore.
6. The wasp oviposits in or on the caterpillar, ultimately killing it.

This system operates across dozens of plant-herbivore-parasitoid combinations and has been documented in maize, cotton, tobacco, bean, cabbage, and many other crop species.

#### Predator Attraction

VOCs also attract generalist predators:
- **Methyl salicylate** attracts [[comparison-predatory-mites-vs-ladybugs-biocontrol]] (*Phytoseiulus persimilis*) to spider mite-infested plants.
- **DMNT and TMTT** attract predatory bugs (*Orius* spp., *Geocoris* spp.) to aphid-infested or lepidopteran-damaged plants.
- **β-caryophyllene** in maize root exudates attracts entomopathogenic nematodes to root-feeding larvae of the western corn rootworm (*Diabrotica virgifera*).

### Plant-to-Plant Communication (Eavesdropping)

Plants can perceive VOCs released by neighboring plants and prime their own defenses in response — a phenomenon called **priming** or **eavesdropping**.

#### Evidence and Mechanisms

- **Sagebrush–tobacco system:** When sagebrush (*Artemisia tridentata*) is clipped to simulate herbivory, neighboring wild tobacco (*Nicotiana attenuata*) plants experience less herbivore damage. The airborne signal is primarily **methyl jasmonate** and a blend of terpenoids.
- **Lima bean–lima bean system:** Plants infested with spider mites release volatiles that cause neighboring uninfested plants to upregulate defensive genes and attract predatory mites pre-emptively.
- **Methyl salicylate** can travel through the air and induce systemic acquired resistance (SAR) in neighboring plants, enhancing their resistance to subsequent pathogen attack.

#### Priming vs. Full Induction

Importantly, VOC-exposed plants typically undergo **priming** — their defense genes are upregulated but defense metabolites are not fully synthesized until actual attack occurs. This is metabolically efficient: the plant invests in preparedness without the full cost of defense deployment.

#### Ecological Context and Specificity

Plant-to-plant signaling is context-dependent:
- **Kin recognition:** Some studies suggest plants respond more strongly to VOCs from genetically related individuals (kin selection).
- **Reliability:** VOC signals can be unreliable indicators of actual threat, and plants may have evolved to only respond under certain conditions (e.g., when volatile concentrations exceed a threshold, or when multiple volatile cues coincide).
- **Airflow and distance:** Effective communication distances range from centimeters to several meters depending on wind speed, turbulence, volatile stability, and plant architecture.

### Plant-Microbe Communication via Volatiles

VOCs also mediate interactions between plants and soil/airborne microorganisms:

- **Bacterial VOCs:** Certain rhizobacteria (e.g., *Bacillus subtilis*, *Pseudomonas fluorescens*) release VOCs (2,3-butanediol, acetoin) that promote plant growth and induce systemic resistance (ISR). These bacterial VOCs can travel through soil air spaces and trigger defense priming in roots.
- **Fungal VOCs:** Mycorrhizal fungi and endophytic fungi release volatile blends that influence root architecture, stimulate lateral root formation, and enhance disease resistance.
- **Antimicrobial volatiles:** Plant-emitted terpenoids and GLVs inhibit pathogenic fungi and bacteria in the rhizosphere and phyllosphere.

### Pollinator Attraction and Reproductive Ecology

VOCs play a central role in plant reproduction by attracting pollinators:

- **Floral scent bouquets** are species-specific blends of terpenoids, benzenoids, and fatty acid derivatives that guide pollinators to flowers.
- **Temporal regulation:** Many flowers emit scent rhythms synchronized with pollinator activity (night-blooming jasmine, evening primrose).
- **Deceptive pollination:** Orchids and some other species mimic the sex pheromones of female wasps or bees to attract male pollinators (sexual deception).
- **Nocturnal moths:** Plants pollinated by hawkmoths emit high concentrations of benzenoids (benzaldehyde, phenylacetaldehyde) and oxygenated terpenoids (linalool) detectable at great distances.
