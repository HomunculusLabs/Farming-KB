# Permaculture Plant Guilds

A permaculture plant guild is a deliberately assembled community of plants,
animals, fungi, and other organisms that work together synergistically,
mimicking the structure and function of natural plant associations. Unlike
conventional [[foliar-pest-deterrence-aact-mechanisms]], guild design aims to create
dense webs of mutual support where each member performs multiple ecological
roles and every function is supported by multiple elements. The concept
draws from observations of how plants naturally organize in forests,
meadows, and other ecosystems, interconnecting through shared nutrient
cycles, pest management networks, [[mollison-tropical-home-garden-design]] gardens. Anthropologists visiting tropical
villages often mistook the lush, productive gardens surrounding homes for
wild jungle, so perfectly had inhabitants mimicked the structure of the
surrounding forest. In temperate climates, guild design is a newer and
rapidly evolving field that draws on ecological science, traditional
knowledge, and decades of practitioner experimentation.

## Guilds, Polycultures, and Companion Planting

Guilds and polycultures are closely related but distinct concepts that are
frequently conflated. Polycultures are defined by their diversity and
temporal succession: multiple species growing together, often replacing each
other through the season as early crops are harvested and later crops fill
the gaps. The emphasis is on spatial and temporal niche filling. Guilds, by
contrast, are defined by their interconnected functions: each species
supports the others through specific ecological services such as nitrogen
fixation, pest control, mulch production, and pollination. The emphasis
is on the web of relationships between members rather than the mere
coexistence of multiple species.

In practice, well-designed polycultures function as guilds, and guilds
employ polycultural techniques. The apple-centered guild is simultaneously
a guild, because of its functional interconnections, and a polyculture,
because multiple species share the same space. The distinction is more
conceptual than practical, since both aim to create plant communities that
are more productive, resilient, and self-maintaining than monocultures.
Companion planting, the simplest of the three concepts, typically involves
pairing two species for a single benefit: marigolds with tomatoes to deter
nematodes, or basil with peppers to repel aphids. Guilds subsume companion
planting as one of many interaction types within a richer community.

## Functional Roles Within a Guild

Toby Hemenway, in his influential book *Gaia's Garden*, identified seven
key roles that should be filled in any well-designed guild. These roles
represent the ecological services that a healthy plant community provides
for itself, services that conventional agriculture replaces with external
inputs like synthetic fertilizer, pesticides, and mechanical cultivation.

The central element anchors the guild. Usually a productive tree or shrub
such as an apple, peach, plum, or chestnut, the central element defines
the microclimate, root zone, and light conditions that all other guild
members must tolerate. Its needs, including pollination, soil fertility,
pest protection, and mulch, are analyzed first, and supporting species
are then chosen to meet those needs while also producing their own yields.

Nitrogen fixers form symbiotic relationships with rhizobacteria in the
case of legumes, or actinomycetes in the case of non-legumes like alder
and seaberry. They convert atmospheric nitrogen into ammonium, which becomes
available to neighboring plants through root exudation, leaf litter
decomposition, and green manure when the plants are cut. Common nitrogen
fixers for guilds include clover, alfalfa, lupine, Siberian pea shrub,
Russian olive, seaberry, goumi, and black locust. Place nitrogen fixers
within the root zone of heavy-feeding fruit trees and cut them periodically
to release nitrogen at the soil surface.
