## Tea Cultivation and Camellia sinensis Processing

## Overview

Tea (*Camellia sinensis*) is an evergreen shrub native to the borderlands of southern China, northern Myanmar, and northeast India. It has been cultivated for over 5,000 years and is the world's most widely consumed manufactured beverage after water — over 6 million tonnes of tea are produced annually. All true teas — green, white, yellow, oolong, black (red), and dark (pu'er) — come from this single species. The remarkable diversity in flavor, aroma, and chemistry arises from cultivar selection, terroir, and post-harvest processing.

Two primary botanical varieties are recognized:

- ***C. sinensis* var. *sinensis*** — the Chinese type, a smaller-leafed shrub adapted to cooler highland climates (hardy to roughly −10°C / 14°F). Produces delicate, nuanced flavors preferred for green and white teas.
- ***C. sinensis* var. *assamica*** — the Assam type, a larger-leafed tree reaching 15 m in the wild, adapted to tropical lowland conditions. Produces robust, malty liquors central to black tea production, especially in India, Sri Lanka, and East Africa.

A third subspecies, *C. sinensis* var. *dehungensis* (Yunnan large-leaf type), is sometimes distinguished from var. *assamica* by Chinese taxonomists and represents the ancestral gene pool from which cultivated tea radiated.

## Terroir and Climate Requirements

### Temperature

Tea requires a mean annual temperature of 12–30°C (54–86°F). The *sinensis* variety tolerates light frost and even brief freezes, while *assamica* requires frost-free conditions year-round. Optimal growth occurs at 18–25°C (64–77°F). Extreme heat above 35°C (95°F) slows growth and reduces leaf quality.

### Rainfall

Annual rainfall of 1,250–2,500 mm (50–100 inches) is ideal, distributed throughout the year. A dry season of 2–3 months is tolerable if irrigation is available. Drought stress concentrates polyphenols, producing bitter, astringent leaves.

### Altitude

High-altitude gardens (1,200–2,200 m / 4,000–7,200 ft) produce the most prized teas. Cooler temperatures slow leaf expansion, extending the growing period and concentrating amino acids (especially theanine) and aromatic compounds. The famous Darjeeling first flush and high-mountain Taiwanese oolongs both benefit from this elevation effect.

### Soil

Tea thrives on well-drained, acidic soils (pH 4.5–5.5) with high organic matter content. Volcanic soils (as in Japan and parts of Kenya) are especially favorable. The plant is intolerant of waterlogging and calcareous conditions. A loamy to sandy-loam texture with good structure is preferred.

### Light

Partial shade improves leaf quality in many traditions. In Japan, kabuse-cha (shaded tea) is grown under woven reed screens or black netting for 7–20 days before harvest, boosting chlorophyll and theanine while reducing catechin bitterness. Natural shade from overstory trees is employed in Korean and some Chinese production.
