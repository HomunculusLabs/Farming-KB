# Rainwater Harvesting and Graywater Recycling

Rainwater harvesting and graywater recycling are complementary strategies for
reducing dependence on municipal water supplies and groundwater extraction.
Together, they form the backbone of water-wise design in permaculture,
homesteading, and sustainable building. Rainwater harvesting captures
precipitation from roofs and other surfaces for later use, while graywater
recycling redirects gently used water from sinks, showers, and laundry machines
back into the landscape. These practices have deep historical roots and are
experiencing a modern revival as climate variability, population growth, and
aging infrastructure place increasing strain on conventional water systems.

The logic is straightforward: water that falls on or passes through a site need
not leave it. A typical household uses roughly 80 to 100 gallons per person per
day, yet much of that water goes to tasks not requiring potable quality. By
capturing rain for irrigation and routing graywater to gardens, a household can
cut municipal water demand by 50% or more while keeping nutrients cycling within
the local ecosystem.

From a permaculture perspective, water is the master variable in landscape
design. Bill Mollison emphasized that water determines the productivity,
resilience, and biodiversity of any site. His foundational insight, that "the
cheapest place to store water is in the soil," reframes the entire discussion.
Tanks and cisterns are tangible infrastructure, but the ultimate storage medium
is the living soil itself. Every earthwork, mulch basin, and planted swale
serves to hold water on the land long enough to infiltrate and nourish
biological activity.

## The Water Paradigm: Why Capturing Water Matters

Conventional water management follows a linear model: extract from distant
sources, treat to drinking standards, deliver under pressure, use once, and
discharge into a sewer. This model is energy-intensive, expensive, and
ecologically disruptive. The permaculture alternative is a closed-loop model in
which water is captured on site, used as many times as practical, and returned
to the soil where it recharges groundwater and supports plant growth.

The scale of the opportunity is remarkable. Research demonstrates that a mere 1%
increase in soil [[fukuoka-clover-cover-cropping-soil-ecology]]
can effectively create a distributed reservoir that dwarfs any tank or cistern.
This is why Mollison and subsequent designers place such emphasis on earthworks
like swales, contour berms, and keyline patterns that slow, spread, and sink
water across the landscape.

Water harvesting also addresses stormwater problems. In urbanized areas,
impervious surfaces generate enormous runoff volumes during rain events,
carrying pollutants into waterways and causing erosion. A 1,000-square-foot

gallons per year. If captured and infiltrated, that water becomes a resource

Conveyance systems carry water from the roof to storage. Standard gutters and
downspouts work well but should be sized to handle peak flow rates. A 2-inch
rainfall per hour event on a 1,500-square-foot roof generates approximately
15.5 gallons per minute, exceeding the capacity of small residential gutters.
Oversizing gutters or installing larger downspouts prevents overflow and water
loss during intense storms.

First-flush diverters are critical components that separate the initial volume
of contaminated water from the clean rain that follows. Dust, pollen, bird
droppings, and atmospheric pollutants accumulate on roofs during dry periods
and wash off in the first minutes of rainfall. The standard sizing rule is 1
gallon of diversion per 100 square feet of roof area. A 1,500-square-foot roof
therefore requires a first-flush diverter capable of holding at least 15
gallons. This water is directed to a landscape feature or infiltration area.
Commercial devices use floating balls or tipping buckets to automatically reset
between rain events, though simple standpipe diverters with drain valves are
inexpensive and effective.

Screening is the final defense before water enters the tank. Leaf screens on
gutters, inlet screens or basket strainers at the tank entry, and overflow
screens that prevent mosquitoes from entering are all recommended. In regions
where mosquitoes are a concern, every opening should be fitted with fine mesh,
and tanks should be sealed or fitted with tight-fitting lids.
