## Mycoremediation: Fungal Bioremediation Techniques and Applications

Mycoremediation is the use of fungi to degrade, transform, or immobilize
environmental pollutants. The term, popularized by mycologist Paul Stamets,
encompasses the deployment of fungal mycelium and their extracellular
enzymes against contaminants ranging from petroleum hydrocarbons to heavy
metals, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, and explosive residues.

Fungi are uniquely suited to pollution remediation because their decomposer
ecology evolved to break down lignin. The same nonspecific enzyme systems
that dismantle woody plant material can attack anthropogenic chemicals that
resist bacterial degradation.

This page synthesizes mycoremediation science across enzymatic mechanisms,
key fungal species, contaminant classes, field deployment strategies,
scale-up challenges, and emerging research frontiers.

## The Ligninolytic Enzyme System

The primary biochemical toolkit for fungal bioremediation is the
ligninolytic enzyme system produced by white-rot basidiomycetes. Three
major enzyme families work synergistically to oxidize recalcitrant organic
compounds that resist conventional treatment.

### Lignin Peroxidase

Lignin peroxidase (LiP) is a heme-containing glycoprotein that catalyzes
one-electron oxidation of non-phenolic aromatic compounds. These compounds
constitute approximately 90 percent of lignin's structure.

LiP requires hydrogen peroxide as a co-substrate and veratryl alcohol as
a redox mediator. It generates aryl cation radicals that initiate
depolymerization of complex polymers.

Phanerochaete chrysosporium was the first fungus in which LiP was
characterized. It can oxidize PAHs, chlorinated phenols, and dioxin
compounds to benzoquinone derivatives.

### Manganese Peroxidase

Manganese peroxidase (MnP) oxidizes Mn(II) to Mn(III), which forms
diffusible chelates with organic acids such as oxalate and malonate.

These chelates penetrate soil micropores to reach adsorbed pollutants far
from the hyphal front. MnP is more widely distributed among white-rot
fungi than LiP.

Low manganese levels limit MnP expression. Elevated manganese stimulates
both MnP and laccase production, making manganese availability a key
factor in site assessment.

### Laccase

Laccase is a multicopper oxidase that reduces molecular oxygen to water
while oxidizing phenolic compounds and aromatic amines.

With redox mediators such as ABTS or 1-hydroxybenzotriazole, laccase
extends its oxidative reach to non-phenolic substrates. This
enzyme-mediator system dramatically expands the range of treatable
contaminants.

Laccase is especially valuable because of its broad substrate specificity,
use of molecular oxygen as cosubstrate, and relative stability under
variable environmental conditions.

## Biosorption and Bioaccumulation Mechanisms

Beyond enzymatic degradation, fungi employ biosorption and bioaccumulation
to remove metals and radionuclides from contaminated environments.

Biosorption is the passive uptake of contaminants by fungal biomass through
ion exchange, surface complexation, and electrostatic attraction. Fungal
cell walls contain chitin, glucans, and proteins with abundant amino,
carboxyl, phosphate, and hydroxyl groups.

Dead fungal biomass can be equally or more effective than live cells for
metal biosorption. Metabolic processes do not interfere with surface
binding, and dead biomass is easier to handle and immobilize.

Bioaccumulation involves active, energy-dependent intracellular uptake
requiring living fungal cells. Species like Armillaria can concentrate
elements 50 to 100 times more than surrounding soil.

### Fenton Chemistry

Brown-rot fungi generate reactive oxygen species through Fenton-like
reactions. They produce extracellular oxalic acid and reduce ferric iron
to ferrous iron, which reacts with hydrogen peroxide to generate hydroxyl
radicals.

These hydroxyl radicals are among the strongest biological oxidants known.
They can depolymerize cellulose, lignin, and synthetic organic pollutants
through non-enzymatic, radical-driven processes.

### Extracellular Polymeric Substances

Fungal extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) create a hydrated matrix
that sequesters heavy metals through chelation and ionic interactions.

This matrix concentrates contaminants near fungal cells where enzymatic
degradation can occur. It also retains water, buffers pH changes, and
provides a physical barrier against toxic compounds.

## Key Fungal Species for Bioremediation

### Phanerochaete chrysosporium

The model white-rot fungus and first organism shown to degrade lignin.
Produces LiP, MnP, and hydrogen peroxide-generating enzymes under
nitrogen-limited secondary metabolism.

Degrades PAHs, azo dyes, pentachlorophenol, TNT, and polychlorinated
biphenyls. Achieves complete decolorization of Orange II, Azure B, and
Tropaeolin O.
