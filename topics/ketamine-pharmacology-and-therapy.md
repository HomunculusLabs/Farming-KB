# Ketamine Pharmacology and Therapy

Ketamine is a dissociative anesthetic compound (C13H16ClNO, molecular weight
237.73) belonging to the arylcyclohexylamine class of drugs. Developed in
1962 by Calvin Stevens at Parke-Davis as a safer alternative to phencyclidine
(PCP), it was approved by the FDA in 1970 under the brand name Ketalar and
has since become one of the most widely used anesthetics in both human and
veterinary medicine worldwide. Its discovery of potent psychedelic properties
at sub-anesthetic doses, and more recently its remarkable efficacy as a rapid-
acting antidepressant, have made it one of the most pharmacologically
interesting and clinically significant psychoactive substances known.

What sets ketamine apart from virtually all other psychoactive drugs is its
mechanism of action. Rather than acting primarily on serotonin receptors like
classical psychedelics (LSD, psilocybin, DMT) or on monoamine transporters
like conventional antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs), ketamine is a non-
competitive antagonist at the NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) glutamate receptor.
This glutamatergic mechanism triggers a cascade of neural events culminating
in rapid synaptogenesis in the prefrontal cortex, explaining why ketamine
can relieve treatment-resistant depression within hours rather than the weeks
required by conventional antidepressants.

The subjective effects of ketamine at psychedelic doses are extraordinary by
any standard. D.M. Turner, in his Essential Psychedelic Guide, rated ketamine
"10 to Infinity" on his intensity scale — the highest rating he assigned to
any substance, exceeding even DMT and 5-MeO-DMT. Users report complete
dissolution of bodily awareness, immersion in four-dimensional hyperspace,
experiences of ego death and rebirth, encounters with seemingly autonomous
entities, and states of consciousness that resist description in ordinary
language. These effects, while profoundly meaningful to some, can be
terrifying to others, making [[metzner-psycholytic-psychedelic-therapy-models]] community.

The clinical antidepressant potential of ketamine was discovered almost by
accident. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, researchers including Carlos
Zarate at the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) began
investigating ketamine's effects on mood in patients with treatment-resistant
depression. The results were striking: a single sub-anesthetic dose of
ketamine (0.5 mg/kg intravenously over 40 minutes) produced robust
antidepressant effects within hours, with response rates of 60 to 70 percent
in patients who had failed to respond to multiple conventional
antidepressants. These findings, published in 2006, triggered a revolution
in depression research and clinical practice.

In 2019, the FDA approved esketamine (the S-enantiomer of ketamine) as a
nasal spray under the brand name Spravato for treatment-resistant depression
and for major depressive disorder with acute suicidal ideation. This marked
the first approval in decades of a fundamentally new mechanism for treating
depression and represented a paradigm shift in psychiatric pharmacology. The
approval also sparked intense debate about the implications of a psychedelic-
adjacent substance entering mainstream psychiatric practice.

## Chemistry and Stereoisomers

Ketamine is a chiral molecule, existing as two enantiomers: S-ketamine
(esketamine) and R-ketamine (arketamine). The racemic mixture, containing
equal parts of both enantiomers, is the form most commonly used in clinical
and recreational settings. The two enantiomers differ significantly in
their pharmacological properties, particularly their affinity for the NMDA
receptor and their clinical effects.

S-ketamine has approximately three to four times greater affinity for the
NMDA receptor than R-ketamine, making it the more potent of the two
enantiomers for producing anesthesia and dissociation. Esketamine (Spravato)
leverages this greater potency, allowing lower total doses to achieve
therapeutic effects via intranasal administration. However, potency at the
NMDA receptor does not necessarily correlate with antidepressant efficacy,
and some research suggests that R-ketamine may have antidepressant properties
comparable to or even exceeding those of the S-enantiomer, with potentially
fewer dissociative side effects.
