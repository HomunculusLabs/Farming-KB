**[[herbal-materia-medica]]* (c. 50–70 CE), *Hypericum* was
recommended for sciatica, burns, and snake bites. Paracelsus, the
sixteenth-century Swiss physician, wrote extensively about the plant's
uses for melancholy and wounds, and his writings influenced centuries of
European herbal practice. The famous red oil infusion, called *Oleum
Hyperici* in pharmacopoeias, appeared in official dispensatories from the
sixteenth century onward. Medieval herbalists valued the herb as a
diuretic, vulnerary (wound-healing agent), and treatment for "melancholia"
and "daemonical visitations," descriptions that map closely onto what
modern medicine recognizes as depression and anxiety.

### Hyperforin

Hyperforin is now recognized as the most important constituent for the
herb's antidepressant activity. This prenylated phloroglucinol
derivative acts as a reversible, non-competitive inhibitor of synaptic
reuptake for serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, GABA, and
L-glutamate, giving it a broader pharmacological profile than most
conventional antidepressants. Hyperforin also modulates TRPC6 ion
channels, which may contribute to its neuroprotective effects.
Unfortunately, hyperforin is highly unstable, degrading rapidly upon
exposure to light, heat, and oxygen. Proper processing and storage are
critical for maintaining potency.

### Flavonoids and Xanthones

The flavonoid fraction includes quercetin, rutin, hyperoside,
isoquercitrin, amentoflavone, and biapigenin. Amentoflavone and
biapigenin (biflavonoids) bind to benzodiazepine receptors and may
contribute to the herb's anxiolytic effects. The xanthone mangiferin
has demonstrated anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity in
preclinical studies. Together, these polyphenolic compounds account for
the herb's significant antioxidant capacity, which supports its
traditional use as a vulnerary and anti-inflammatory agent.

## Pharmacology and Clinical Evidence

St. John's Wort is unique among herbal medicines for the sheer volume
and quality of clinical evidence supporting its use. Over 50 randomized
controlled trials and multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses
have evaluated its efficacy for mild to moderate depression.
