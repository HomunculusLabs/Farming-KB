# Teaming with Microbes — The Organic Gardener's Guide to the Soil Food Web

*Teaming with Microbes: A Gardener's Guide to the Soil Food Web*, first published in 2006 by Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis with a foreword by Elaine Ingham, is one of the most influential popular books on soil biology ever written. It bridges the gap between academic soil science and practical garden management, translating complex microbial ecology into actionable guidance for home gardeners, landscapers, and small farmers. The book went through three editions (2006, 2010, and the definitive 2017 Timber Press Guide), each expanding its scientific scope and practical recommendations. A companion volume, *Teaming with Nutrients* (2013), extended the framework into plant nutrition.

The book's central argument is deceptively simple: plants are in control of the soil food web. Through root exudates — a complex cocktail of sugars, proteins, carbohydrates, and signaling compounds — plants recruit and feed specific communities of bacteria and fungi in their rhizosphere. These microorganisms in turn support an entire food web of protozoa, nematodes, arthropods, and earthworms that cycle nutrients, suppress pathogens, and build soil structure. The gardener's job is not to feed plants directly but to support the biological system that feeds them.

## The Soil Beneath Your Feet: A Hidden Ecosystem

The book opens with a striking portrait of soil's hidden abundance. A single teaspoon of good garden soil contains roughly one billion bacteria, several yards of fungal hyphae, several thousand protozoa, and a few dozen nematodes. A square foot of good soil may harbor up to fifty earthworms. Scaled to an acre, good garden soil contains approximately 2,000 pounds of bacteria, 2,400 pounds of fungi, 900 pounds each of earthworms and arthropods, and 133 pounds of protozoa.

All of this life shares one fundamental requirement: carbon. The soil food web is an eat-and-be-eaten world running on photosynthate — the energy that plants capture from sunlight and pump into the ground through their roots. Understanding this carbon economy is the key to understanding everything else about soil health, nutrient cycling, and plant nutrition.

The organisms in soil are not random; they form structured trophic levels with predictable feeding relationships. Energy enters through photosynthesis at the top and cascades downward through decomposers, grazers, and predators. At each transfer, roughly ninety percent of energy is lost as heat. The critical function of this food web is not energy transfer per se but nutrient retention and mineralization — keeping nutrients cycling within the root zone rather than leaching away.

## Bacteria: The Foundation of the Soil Food Web

Bacteria are the smallest and most numerous organisms in soil. They are the primary colonizers of disturbed ground and the first decomposers to break down simple organic compounds. In *Teaming with Microbes*, Lowenfels and Lewis present bacteria as the foundation upon which the rest of the soil food web is built.

Bacteria concentrate in the rhizosphere — the narrow zone of soil immediately surrounding plant roots. Root exudates provide an abundant food source, and bacteria proliferate there in numbers far exceeding those in bulk soil. They also feed on cells sloughed off during root-tip growth and on simpler organic compounds found in young compost and green mulches. Not all soil bacteria live in the rhizosphere, however; many are free-living decomposers distributed throughout the soil matrix.

One of the most important functions of soil bacteria is biofilm formation. Bacteria create slime layers — complex matrices of sugars, proteins, and DNA — that serve multiple critical roles. Biofilms raise soil pH locally, trap pathogens by physically blocking them from reaching plant surfaces, coat root surfaces to form protective barriers, and act as a biological glue that binds soil particles into aggregates. This last function is fundamental to soil structure: bacterial slime is one of the primary agents that hold soil crumbs together, creating pore spaces for air and water movement.

Bacteria also play a key role in nutrient immobilization. When they consume organic matter, they incorporate nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and other nutrients into their cellular structures. These nutrients are temporarily unavailable to plants because they are locked inside living bacterial biomass. However, this immobilization serves a critical function: it prevents nutrients from leaching out of the soil. Nutrients held in bacterial cells stay precisely in the root zone until they are released through mineralization.

## Fungi: The Underground Network

Soil fungi are the second major group of decomposers and the critical partners for perennial plants through mycorrhizal symbiosis. While bacteria dominate in disturbed, early-succession soils, fungi become increasingly important as ecosystems mature. Fungi are the primary decomposers of lignin and cellulose — the tough structural compounds in woody material that bacteria cannot efficiently break down. This is why leaf mold and woody compost are dominated by fungal activity.

The book distinguishes between saprophytic fungi (free-living decomposers) and mycorrhizal fungi (root symbionts). Mycorrhizal associations, from the Greek for "fungus-root," are formed by at least ninety to ninety-five percent of all plant species. These relationships began roughly 450 million years ago and were fundamental to the colonization of land by plants. In exchange for root exudates, mycorrhizal fungi extend the effective reach of root systems by 700 to 1,000 times, mining water, phosphorus, copper, calcium, magnesium, zinc, and iron from soil volumes that roots alone cannot access.

Two major types of mycorrhizae exist. Ectomycorrhizal fungi grow close to root surfaces and form dense sheaths, associating primarily with hardwoods and conifers. Endomycorrhizal (arbuscular) fungi actually penetrate root cells and are preferred by most vegetables, annuals, grasses, shrubs, and perennials. Both types dramatically increase the effective surface area of root systems and form networks that can connect different plants, enabling interplant nutrient transfer.

Fungal acids play a crucial role in soil chemistry. They lower soil pH, which suppresses nitrifying bacteria. In fungally dominated soils, nitrogen therefore tends to remain in ammonium form rather than being converted to nitrate. This matters because most perennials, trees, and shrubs prefer ammonium nitrogen, while annuals and vegetables prefer nitrate. The fungal-to-bacterial ratio thus directly determines what form of nitrogen is available and, consequently, which plants will thrive.

Some soil fungi are predatory. *Arthrobotrys dactyloides* creates three-cell rings that swell to trap nematodes in one-tenth of a second, releasing chemicals that attract the prey. *Pleurotus ostreatus* (the oyster mushroom) emits toxic droplets from hyphal tips that paralyze nematodes. These predatory fungi are important natural controls on plant-parasitic nematode populations.

## Protozoa: The Nutrient Mineralizers

Protozoa are the critical nutrient mineralizers of the soil food web. They are single-celled organisms that feed primarily on bacteria — and occasionally on fungi and other protozoa. A teaspoon of good soil contains several thousand protozoa, ranging from 5 to 500 micrometers in size (compared to bacteria at 1 to 4 micrometers). The book offers a vivid scale comparison: if a single bacterium were the size of a pea, a paramecium would be as large as a watermelon.

Three major groups of soil protozoa are distinguished by their locomotion. Flagellates, the smallest, move using whip-like flagella and can access bacteria in smaller soil pores than other protozoa. Amoebae move using temporary pseudopods (false feet) and can squeeze through very small soil pores to reach bacteria; they include both naked and testate (shelled) forms. Ciliates, the largest, move using hair-like cilia and prefer aerobic conditions — their presence in high numbers generally indicates good aeration, though very high ciliate numbers can signal compacted, anaerobic conditions.

The nutrient mineralization role of protozoa is their most important function. Protozoa consume bacteria that contain immobilized nitrogen and other nutrients. They digest what they need for their own metabolism — roughly thirty to forty percent of the nitrogen — and excrete the excess sixty to seventy percent as waste, primarily in the form of ammonium. This mineralization happens right in the rhizosphere, exactly where plant roots can absorb it. Protozoa are, in the book's memorable phrase, the "fertilizer spreaders" of the soil food web.

All protozoa require moisture for active life. When soils dry out, they encyst — forming protective cysts — and go dormant until moisture returns. This moisture dependence means that the soil wetting and drying cycle directly affects the rate of nutrient mineralization. The presence and diversity of protozoa serves as a bioindicator of a functioning soil food web.

## Nematodes: Microscopic Roundworms

Nematodes are non-segmented, blind roundworms — the second most dominant form of animal life after arthropods. Over 20,000 species have been identified, and scientists estimate there may be as many as one million species in total. A teaspoon of good soil averages about twenty bacteria-eating nematodes, twenty fungal feeders, and a few predatory and plant-eating nematodes, totaling roughly forty to fifty individuals.

Nematodes are classified by their feeding habits. Bacterial-feeding nematodes consume bacteria with hollow tube mouthparts, capable of ingesting multitudes of bacteria per hour. Fungal-feeding nematodes use stylets to puncture the chitin cell walls of fungal hyphae. Predatory nematodes eat other nematodes, protozoa, and small arthropods, helping to control plant-parasitic nematode populations. Plant-parasitic nematodes have needle-like stylets that puncture plant cell walls, creating lesions, cysts, and root knots.

Like protozoa, nematodes are essential mineralizers. They consume bacteria and fungi containing immobilized nitrogen and excrete excess nitrogen as plant-available ammonium. Because their carbon-to-nitrogen ratio is higher than that of protozoa, nematodes actually release proportionally more nitrogen as waste — making them arguably the most important mineralizers in many garden soils.

The nematode community structure is a powerful bioindicator of soil health. A diverse community with all four feeding groups present indicates a mature, healthy soil food web. Dominance by root-feeders signals problems. The ratio of bacterial-feeders to fungal-feeders reveals whether the soil is bacterial or fungal dominated. Absence of predatory nematodes suggests a disturbed ecosystem where the food web has been simplified.

## Earthworms: The Ecosystem Engineers

Earthworms are among the most visible and important animals in the soil food web. Their presence is itself a bioindicator — it signals that organic matter, bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes are all present in sufficient quantities to support them. Worms are classified as shredders, physically breaking apart leaves and organic matter to greatly accelerate decomposition. Without worm shredding, leaves require one to two years to decay; with worms, decomposition takes only three months.

Worms contribute to soil in multiple ways. Their burrowing creates channels that improve soil aeration and water infiltration. Their casts — the processed organic matter they excrete — are rich in plant-available nutrients and loaded with beneficial microorganisms. Worms can deposit ten to fifteen tons of castings per acre annually. These castings have high cation exchange capacity, meaning they provide abundant charged surfaces where nutrients can attach and remain available for plant uptake.

The book emphasizes that earthworms both directly shred material and indirectly stimulate decomposition by creating more surface area for microbial attack, mixing microbes into their fecal pellets (creating protected enclaves for microbial activity), and competing with fungi and bacteria for nutrients in ways that stimulate overall microbial turnover.

## The Fungal to Bacterial Ratio

Perhaps the most influential concept to emerge from the book is the fungal-to-bacterial (F:B) ratio, drawn from the research of Dr. Elaine Ingham at Oregon State University in the 1980s. Ingham demonstrated that the ratio of fungi to bacteria in soil correlates strongly with the type of plant community above ground, and that this ratio follows a predictable pattern of ecological succession.

Early-succession soils — disturbed, recently tilled, or newly exposed ground — are heavily bacterially dominated, with F:B ratios well below 1:1. These soils favor weeds and annual plants. Mid-succession soils, such as grasslands and established vegetable gardens, approach a more balanced ratio of roughly 1:1. Late-succession soils, including old-growth forests, become highly fungally dominated, with F:B ratios of 10:1 or higher. These soils support perennials, shrubs, and trees.

This succession pattern has profound practical implications. Annual vegetables and lawn grasses evolved in bacterially dominated soils and prefer nitrate nitrogen. Perennials, shrubs, and trees evolved in fungally dominated soils and prefer ammonium nitrogen. When gardeners plant trees in repeatedly tilled, bacterially dominated soil, the trees struggle because they are growing in the wrong biological environment. Matching soil biology to the plants being grown is one of the book's most actionable recommendations.

Bacterial numbers stay roughly constant across environments — typically 100 million to one billion per teaspoon. What changes dramatically is fungal biomass: from several yards of hyphae in garden soil, to tens to hundreds of yards in prairie soil, to four to forty miles of hyphae per teaspoon in conifer forest soil. This fungal increase drives the shift from bacterial to fungal dominance.

## Mineralization and Immobilization: The Nutrient Cycling Engine

Mineralization and immobilization are the two complementary processes by which nutrients move between organic and inorganic forms in soil. Together, they form the core nutrient cycling mechanism of the soil food web, and understanding them is essential for anyone seeking to reduce or eliminate dependence on synthetic fertilizers.

Immobilization occurs when bacteria and fungi absorb nutrients from organic matter and incorporate them into their cellular structures. These nutrients are temporarily unavailable to plants — they are locked inside living microbial biomass. However, this lock-up is actually a feature, not a bug. It prevents nutrients from leaching past the root zone. Chemical fertilizers, by contrast, bypass this biological retention mechanism. A small fraction reaches the rhizosphere where roots can absorb it, but most drains through soil until it reaches the water table.

Mineralization reverses the process. When protozoa and nematodes graze on bacteria and fungi, they digest what they need and excrete the excess as waste — primarily ammonium and other mineral nutrients. Plant roots absorb these minerals directly from the rhizosphere. As much as eighty percent of the nitrogen a plant needs comes from the wastes produced by bacteria- and fungi-eating protozoa and nematodes.

The grazing-mineralization cycle operates as a continuous loop. Plants produce exudates through photosynthesis, attracting bacteria and fungi to the rhizosphere. These microorganisms multiply, incorporating nutrients into their bodies. Protozoa and nematodes graze on them, releasing excess nutrients as plant-available mineral waste. Plants absorb these minerals and produce more exudates. The cycle repeats endlessly in healthy soil.

## Humus, Glomalin, and Soil Structure

Humus is the relatively stable, dark, organic fraction of soil that remains after active decomposition is largely complete. It is not a single chemical compound but a complex mixture of partially decomposed organic materials and microbial synthesis products. While simple organic matter may decompose within weeks to months, humus can persist in soil for decades to centuries, providing a long-term reservoir of carbon and nutrients. Soil organic matter, of which humus is the stable fraction, stores more than twice the carbon found in the atmosphere.

Glomalin is a sticky glycoprotein produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi in the order Glomales. Discovered in 1996 by Sara Wright at the USDA, glomalin has revolutionized understanding of how soil aggregates form and persist. It acts as a biological superglue, coating soil particles and binding them into stable aggregates. These aggregates are further stabilized by fungal hyphae that enmesh particles, bacterial slime that glues them together, and earthworm casts that coat particles with mucus. The result is a hierarchical structure of particles within microaggregates within macroaggregates.

Humus also contains humic and fulvic acids, two categories of organic compounds with important roles in soil chemistry. Humic acids are larger molecular weight compounds that improve soil structure, increase cation exchange capacity, chelate micronutrients, stimulate root growth, and serve as fungal food sources. Fulvic acids are smaller, more soluble compounds that readily penetrate plant tissues, enhance nutrient uptake, and improve microbial activity. Both are particularly recommended for trees, shrubs, and perennials.

## Compost, Mulch, and Compost Tea: The Gardener's Three Tools

The book distills its practical program into three tools: applying the right kind of compost, mulching correctly with appropriate materials, and brewing and applying actively aerated compost tea (AACT). These three practices, used together, can build and maintain a healthy soil food web without synthetic chemical inputs.

Compost quality matters enormously. Bacterial-dominated compost, made from green materials and finished quickly at higher temperatures, is best for annual vegetables and lawns. Fungal-dominated compost, made from brown materials including leaves and woody debris and finished slowly at lower temperatures, is preferred for perennials, shrubs, and trees. The distinction mirrors the F:B ratio concept: you are essentially delivering the right microbial community for the plants you want to grow.

Mulching serves multiple functions simultaneously. It moderates soil temperature, conserves moisture, suppresses weeds, and feeds the soil food web as it decomposes. Green mulches like grass clippings favor bacterial growth. Brown mulches like shredded leaves, wood chips, and straw favor fungal growth. The book emphasizes that mulch should be left on the soil surface, not tilled in, to preserve fungal hyphae and maintain the soil structure they help create.

Actively aerated compost tea is the third tool, designed to overcome the limitations of bulk compost and mulch by delivering concentrated populations of beneficial microbes directly to plant surfaces and root zones. AACT is not compost leachate (which has minimal microbial life), compost extract (which is anaerobic), or manure tea (which risks pathogens). It is an aerobic mixture brewed by adding compost and microbial foods to dechlorinated water and aerating vigorously for twenty-four to thirty-six hours.

A basic DIY compost tea brewer requires a five-gallon bucket, an aquarium air pump with air stone, and plastic tubing. Key ingredients include chlorine-free water, good finished compost or vermicastings, bacterial foods (unsulfured molasses, cane syrup, fruit juices), and fungal foods (kelp meal, humic acids, fish hydrolysate). The tea is ready when it develops a sweet, earthy smell and should be applied within four hours of brewing.

## Tilling: The Most Destructive Practice

Rototilling and excessive soil disturbance are identified in the book as perhaps the most destructive traditional gardening practice — Rule #18 states this explicitly. Tilling breaks up fungal hyphae that took weeks or months to establish, destroying the plant's nutrient-gathering extension system and its physical protection against pathogens. Soil structure built by years of biological activity — worm tunnels, pore spaces, aggregate stability — is undone in minutes.

The first watering or rain after tilling begins a compaction cycle that continues with each subsequent irrigation. What appeared fluffy immediately after tilling collapses into a denser, less porous condition than before. The microbial community is displaced: microorganisms that depended on specific microenvironments are killed or scattered, and larger organisms are physically damaged or exposed to predation.

Tilling also resets the soil food web toward bacterial dominance. With fungal hyphae destroyed, bacteria recolonize faster than fungi can regrow. This is beneficial in the short term for vegetables and row crops that prefer bacterial soils, but it is a long-term loss for soil health. For perennial plantings, the book strongly recommends no-till approaches, using mulch layers and compost applications on the soil surface to build biology without disturbance.
