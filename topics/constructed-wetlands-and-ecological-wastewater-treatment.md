# Constructed Wetlands and Ecological Wastewater Treatment

Constructed wetlands are engineered ecosystems that replicate the water
purification processes found in natural marshes, swamps, and bogs to treat
wastewater, stormwater, and industrial effluent. They are among the most
cost-effective and environmentally sound water treatment technologies
available, using the combined action of wetland plants, substrate media, and
diverse microbial communities to remove organic matter, nutrients, pathogens,
heavy metals, and emerging contaminants from water. Unlike conventional
mechanical treatment plants, constructed wetlands require minimal energy input,
produce no chemical sludge, create wildlife habitat, and transform what society
calls "waste" into clean water, productive biomass, and recycled nutrients.

The fundamental insight behind constructed wetlands is one that Bill Mollison
championed throughout his work: wastewater is not waste. In a well-designed
ecological system, every outflow becomes an input for another process. The
conventional approach of mixing human excrement with large volumes of clean
drinking water and then spending enormous energy to separate them again is, by
any rational measure, both wasteful and polluting. Constructed wetlands offer
a fundamentally different paradigm: one that works with biological processes
rather than against them, and that produces value — clean water, plant biomass,
fertilizer, habitat — rather than concentrating pollution for disposal.

The technology has matured enormously since its earliest applications in the
1970s. Today, thousands of constructed wetland systems operate worldwide,
ranging from small household greywater marshes of a few square metres to
municipal treatment systems serving communities of tens of thousands. They
treat sewage, industrial effluent from food processing and pulp mills,
agricultural runoff, acid mine drainage, landfill leachate, and stormwater
from urban surfaces. Research has revealed that the microbial communities
within these systems — particularly the fungal communities — are far more
diverse and capable than was initially appreciated, opening new possibilities
for treating recalcitrant pollutants that resist conventional biological
treatment.

This article provides a comprehensive overview of constructed wetland
technology, covering the ecological foundations in natural wetland science,
the engineering of different wetland types, the biological and chemical
treatment mechanisms (with particular attention to the underappreciated role
of fungi), design principles for systems from household to community scale,
and the permaculture philosophy that frames wastewater treatment as productive
design rather than waste management.

## Natural Wetlands as Foundation

To understand constructed wetlands, one must first understand the ecosystems
they emulate. Natural wetlands — marshes, swamps, bogs, and fens — are among
the most productive and ecologically valuable landscapes on Earth. They have
been called "nature's kidneys" for their remarkable ability to filter,
absorb, and transform pollutants from water that passes through them. A
single acre of wetland can store over a million gallons of floodwater and
remove significant loads of nitrogen, phosphorus, and sediment from
agricultural runoff. Peatlands alone contain approximately thirty percent
of global soil carbon on just three percent of land area.

Wetlands are defined by their hydrology: water saturates the soil for at
least part of the growing season, creating conditions that favor
hydrophytic (water-adapted) vegetation. The seasonal pattern of water level
fluctuation, known as the hydroperiod, is the primary factor determining
wetland type and function. Marshes are dominated by herbaceous plants such
as cattails, bulrushes, and sedges, and are among the most productive
ecosystems on Earth, often exceeding agricultural productivity per acre.
Swamps are dominated by woody vegetation, with forested swamps providing
critical flood storage and wildlife corridors. Bogs are peat-accumulating
wetlands fed primarily by rainwater, creating acidic, nutrient-poor
conditions that support carnivorous plants and sphagnum moss. Fens are
groundwater-fed peatlands that are less acidic and support greater plant
diversity, including many rare species.

Wetland plants have evolved remarkable adaptations to survive in saturated,
oxygen-poor soils. Aerenchyma tissue — spongy tissue with air channels —
transports oxygen from leaves to roots, allowing plants to respire in
flooded conditions. This same adaptation is central to constructed wetland
treatment: the oxygen that plants transport to their root zones supports
aerobic microbial communities that would otherwise be excluded from the
waterlogged substrate. Pneumatophores, the vertical "knee" roots of bald
cypress, are another visible adaptation that facilitates gas exchange in
flooded soils.

The ecosystem services provided by natural wetlands — water filtration,
flood storage, groundwater recharge, carbon sequestration, shoreline
protection, and wildlife habitat — are precisely the functions that
constructed wetlands are designed to provide, but in a controlled and
optimized manner. Understanding these natural processes is essential for
designing effective treatment systems.

## Types of Constructed Wetlands

Constructed wetlands are classified by their hydrology and flow regime into
several distinct types, each with different design characteristics, treatment
mechanisms, and suitable applications. The choice of wetland type depends on
the nature of the wastewater to be treated, available land area, climate, and
the required effluent quality.

Surface flow constructed wetlands, also called free water surface wetlands,
are the simplest type. They consist of shallow basins, typically 30 to 60
centimetres deep, planted with emergent aquatic vegetation growing in
saturated soil or sediment. Water flows horizontally across the surface
through the standing water column and dense plant stands at low velocity.
The water surface is exposed to the atmosphere, allowing atmospheric oxygen
transfer, algal photosynthesis, and volatilization of certain contaminants.
Treatment occurs through sedimentation of suspended solids, microbial
degradation in both the aerobic water column and the anaerobic sediments,
plant uptake of nutrients and metals, and adsorption to substrate surfaces.
Surface flow wetlands provide excellent habitat value for wildlife and are
visually appealing, making them popular for stormwater treatment and tertiary
polishing. However, they require more land area than subsurface flow systems
and may present mosquito breeding concerns in standing water.

Subsurface flow wetlands route water through a porous medium such as gravel,
sand, or crushed rock planted with wetland vegetation. Water flows below the
surface, with no exposed standing water, which eliminates mosquito habitat
and reduces human exposure to contaminated water. Horizontal subsurface flow
wetlands are the most common type: water enters at one end of a shallow bed
filled with gravel (typically five to twenty millimetre diameter), flows
horizontally through the saturated media, and is collected at the opposite
end. Typical retention times range from two to seven days. The gravel
provides enormous surface area for microbial biofilm development, and the
constant contact between water and biofilm produces consistent treatment.

Vertical flow wetlands distribute water over the surface of a bed filled
with sand or fine gravel, allowing it to percolate vertically downward.
Between doses, the bed drains completely, allowing air to refill pore spaces
and maintain aerobic conditions. This intermittent flood-and-drain cycle
promotes nitrification — the conversion of ammonia to nitrate by aerobic
bacteria — significantly improving nitrogen removal compared to horizontal
flow systems. Vertical flow beds achieve better ammonia removal but require
more precise dosing and drainage design.

Hybrid systems combine horizontal and vertical flow stages in series, and
represent the state of the art for comprehensive treatment. A typical hybrid
configuration uses a vertical flow bed first to achieve nitrification under
aerobic conditions, followed by a horizontal flow bed where denitrification
occurs in the anoxic environment. This combination effectively removes both
organic matter and nitrogen, which is difficult to achieve in a single-stage
system because nitrification requires oxygen while denitrification requires
its absence.

Floating treatment wetlands represent a newer approach. They use buoyant
platforms supporting plants whose roots hang freely in the water column below.
The plant roots provide extensive surface area for biofilm attachment and
direct contact with the water. Their great advantage is deployability: they
can be placed in existing ponds, lakes, or retention basins without any
land excavation or substrate construction, making them ideal for retrofitting
existing water bodies with treatment capacity.

## Treatment Mechanisms

Constructed wetlands remove contaminants through a complex, interacting
network of physical, chemical, and biological processes. Understanding these
mechanisms is essential for effective design and for diagnosing treatment
problems when they arise.

Physical removal begins with sedimentation: as water velocity decreases in
the wetland, suspended solids settle to the bottom. Filtration occurs as
water passes through substrate media or dense vegetation stands, trapping
particles. In surface flow wetlands, volatilization removes certain
compounds from the water surface. Subsurface flow systems excel at physical
filtration because the gravel matrix provides a dense filter bed.

Chemical removal mechanisms include adsorption of metals and phosphorus to
substrate media, precipitation of metal hydroxides and sulfides, and
oxidation-reduction reactions. The choice of substrate media is particularly
important for phosphorus removal. Gravel, sand, and manufactured media with
high surface area and specific mineralogy (such as iron-rich or calcium-rich
media) vary widely in their phosphorus adsorption capacity and longevity.
Eventually, all adsorption media become saturated and require replacement,
which is a significant long-term design consideration.

Biological processes are the primary drivers of contaminant transformation.
Aerobic degradation of organic matter occurs in oxygenated zones near plant
roots and at the water surface, measured as BOD (biochemical oxygen demand)
and COD (chemical oxygen demand) removal. Anaerobic processes including
denitrification, sulfate reduction, and methanogenesis occur in anoxic
sediments and deeper substrate layers. The balance between aerobic and
anaerobic zones within a constructed wetland is critical to its performance,
and this balance is largely created and maintained by the wetland plants
themselves.

Wetland plants play a role that goes far beyond nutrient uptake. Their root
systems transport atmospheric oxygen through aerenchyma tissue to the root
zone, a process called radial oxygen loss. This creates aerobic microsites
within otherwise anaerobic substrate, supporting aerobic microbial
communities in the rhizosphere. The result is that nitrification (an aerobic
process converting ammonia to nitrate) and denitrification (an anaerobic
process converting nitrate to nitrogen gas) can occur in close proximity,
enabling effective nitrogen removal in a single wetland bed. The rhizosphere
of wetland plants supports microbial populations that are orders of magnitude
higher than in bulk water or substrate, and root exudates provide carbon
sources that sustain diverse microbial communities capable of degrading a
wide range of organic contaminants.

## The Role of Fungi in Wetland Treatment

The fungal communities in constructed wetlands have been dramatically
underappreciated relative to bacterial processes, but research is revealing
their critical importance. Harbhajan Singh's comprehensive review of fungal
bioremediation documents that studies of constructed wetlands receiving pulp
mill effluent have identified up to forty fungal species belonging to
twenty-four genera, performing essential decomposition functions that
complement and extend bacterial treatment.

The enzymatic capabilities of wetland fungi are remarkable. Approximately
seventy percent of fungi isolated from contaminated wetlands show protease
activity (breaking down proteins), about fifty percent exhibit amylase
activity (breaking down starches), and forty percent show xylanase activity
(degrading hemicellulose). Among actinomycetes — a group of bacteria with
fungus-like characteristics — more than ninety percent have amylase activity,
sixty percent have chitinase activity, and twenty-five percent have xylanase
activity. By comparison, only forty percent of bacteria show protease
activity, and very few have chitinase or xylanase capabilities. These
enzymatic profiles mean that fungi are particularly important for degrading
complex organic compounds that resist bacterial breakdown.

The most significant fungal contribution may be from white-rot fungi and
other ligninolytic species that produce extracellular enzymes — laccase,
manganese peroxidase, and lignin peroxidase — capable of degrading
recalcitrant aromatic pollutants including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons
(PAHs), phenolic compounds, and chlorinated organics from industrial
effluents. Pilot-scale constructed wetlands have successfully treated
PAH-contaminated water, with fungal species degrading high percentages of
fluoranthene and anthracene. Wastewater decolorization in constructed
wetlands depends heavily on the decomposition capabilities of diverse
microbial communities, with fungi playing a leading role.

The implications for wetland design are significant. Fungal-dominated
treatment processes may be optimized through substrate composition that
supports fungal colonization, vegetation selection that encourages
rhizosphere fungal communities, and hydraulic design that maintains the
oxygen availability and pH conditions that fungal enzymes require. Singh
notes that multi-process systems combining volatilization, photooxidation,
microbial remediation, and phytoremediation proved more than twice as
effective as phytoremediation alone for removing sixteen priority PAHs from
contaminated soil, underscoring the importance of designing wetlands as
integrated ecosystems rather than single-mechanism filters.

## Greywater and Blackwater: A Permaculture Approach

Central to the permaculture approach to wastewater is the separation of
greywater and blackwater at source. Greywater — wastewater from baths,
showers, bathroom sinks, and laundry — constitutes fifty to eighty percent
of total household wastewater volume and is relatively easy to treat because
it contains low concentrations of pathogens and organic matter. Blackwater —
wastewater from toilets containing human faeces — requires more intensive
treatment due to high pathogen loads and nutrient concentration.

Mollison argued that mixing these two streams together, as conventional
systems do, is fundamentally irrational: it creates a massive pollution
problem from what was mostly clean water, and then requires enormous energy
expenditure to separate them again. The permaculture approach treats each
stream appropriately at the smallest practical scale.

For greywater, the simplest systems require no wetland at all. Mulch basins
are the most basic approach: water from a bathroom or laundry outlet is piped
to a basin filled with woodchip mulch and planted with water-loving species
such as banana, willow, or comfrey. The mulch filters particles, supports
microbial decomposition, and distributes water to plant roots. No standing
water is present, eliminating mosquito habitat. Branched drain systems split
greywater through a series of branching pipes to multiple mulch basins,
distributing the load across a larger area and preventing waterlogging.
Laundry-to-landscape systems direct washing machine discharge through a hose
to a mulch basin or orchard, using the washing machine's pump for
distribution pressure.

For blackwater, the permaculture approach begins with composting toilets,
which eliminate the wastewater stream entirely by converting human waste to
sanitary compost. When blackwater treatment is necessary, it requires either
septic tank pretreatment followed by constructed wetland treatment, or more
robust multi-stage treatment systems. Mollison emphasized that treated
effluent should be used to irrigate non-food crops or fruit trees (where
contact between effluent and the edible portion is minimal) rather than root
vegetables or leafy greens eaten raw.

## Reed Bed Design and Construction

The design of subsurface flow reed beds for household wastewater treatment
is well-established, with clear guidelines that have been refined over
decades of implementation. A typical household system treating the greywater
of a family of four requires approximately eight to twenty square metres of
wetland area, at a depth of half a metre of gravel media. Vertical flow
beds can be somewhat smaller, approximately one to two square metres per
person, because their aerobic conditions support more efficient treatment.

The gravel substrate is critical. Mollison recommended washed gravel or
coarse sand of five to twenty millimetre diameter, free of fines that could
clog pore spaces. Larger media provides better hydraulic conductivity but
less surface area for biofilm development, so a balance must be struck. A
gentle slope of one to three percent ensures water flows through the media
without ponding. The bed must be lined with an impermeable membrane
(usually high-density polyethylene) to prevent contamination of groundwater.

Common reed (Phragmites australis) is the standard species for reed beds
worldwide, valued for its vigorous root system, high nutrient uptake, winter
hardiness, and efficient oxygen transport to roots. However, Phragmites is
invasive in many regions, and local native species should be used where
possible. Cattail (Typha latifolia) is another vigorous performer with dense
root mats and excellent nutrient uptake, though it can become dominant and
reduce biodiversity if not managed. Soft rush (Juncus effusus), club rush
(Scirpus species), yellow flag iris (Iris pseudacorus), and water mint
(Mentha aquatica) are all valuable additions. Mollison recommended planting
a diverse mix of species rather than monocultures, as this provides more
robust treatment performance, longer growing seasons, and greater habitat
value.

Maintenance requirements are modest but essential. Annual harvesting of
above-ground vegetation in late autumn or winter removes nutrients from the
system and prevents the bed from becoming choked with dead plant material.
The harvested material can be composted, closing the nutrient loop. Inlet
and outlet structures should be inspected regularly for clogging. Over ten
to twenty years, the gravel media may become clogged with accumulated solids,
requiring replacement — this is the most significant maintenance cost over
the system's lifetime.

## Homestead-Scale Wetland Systems

For the homesteader or homeowner, a constructed wetland represents one of
the most elegant and productive elements of a self-sufficient property
design. A household greywater marsh can be built with hand tools and readily
available materials, transforming wastewater from a disposal problem into a
resource that irrigates gardens, supports wildlife, and produces useful
biomass.

The design process begins with calculating household water usage to determine
the required wetland size. A frugal household of five with a waterless
composting toilet uses approximately 940 gallons (3,558 litres) of water
per week, nearly all of which can be reclaimed. The site should be downhill
from the house to allow gravity-fed flow, in an area where filtered water
can irrigate gardens or percolate into groundwater. The wetland must be
kept at a safe distance from drinking water wells.

Construction involves excavating a basin angled so water flows across the
entire area. The excavated soil is piled around the sides as a retainment
wall, planted with clover or other nitrogen-fixing ground covers to prevent
erosion. Inside, the filtration media are layered from bottom to top: a base
layer of gravel for drainage, a layer of sand for fine filtration, a layer of
sticks cut into fifteen-centimetre lengths for biological filtration surfaces
and aeration, and a final layer of straw and mulch at least twenty
centimetres deep. Local wetland plants — cattails, rushes, reed grasses,
horsetails, and water-loving flowers — are grown in the mulch layer. These
plants serve multiple functions: cattails are edible, rushes and reeds can be
used for basket-making, and all contribute to biological water purification.

Several rules must be followed for safe and effective operation. Use only
biodegradable soaps and avoid detergent products. The water level must never
flood over the top of the mulch layer. If capacity is exceeded, install a
tank with a valve to control flow or increase the system size. Storage tanks
must cycle every twenty-four hours or the water becomes anaerobic and toxic.
The system should be flushed with clean water monthly to prevent clogs, and
a diverter valve on the washing machine allows contaminated loads to be sent
to conventional disposal instead of the wetland.

In colder climates, the marsh may freeze in winter. Designing the system
deeper and using insulated layers helps prevent complete freezing. In dry
climates, additional water may be needed during drought to keep wetland
plants alive. Overflow mechanisms should handle heavy rain, and the system
should be designed to accept additional water inputs during dry spells. The
biomass produced by the wetland can be harvested for craft materials, mulch,
or compost, and the filtered water can irrigate orchards, food forests, or
pasture.

## Applications Across Scales

The versatility of constructed wetlands is one of their greatest strengths.
At the smallest scale, they treat individual household greywater and, with
septic pretreatment, domestic sewage. A typical household system requires two
to five square metres per person for secondary treatment. At the community
scale, constructed wetlands serve small towns and rural areas where
conventional mechanical treatment is cost-prohibitive. They are particularly
effective in developing countries where capital is limited but labour and land
are available.

Municipal applications range from primary to tertiary treatment. Surface flow
wetlands are commonly used for tertiary polishing of conventional plant
effluent, removing residual nutrients before discharge to sensitive receiving
waters. Subsurface flow systems can provide full secondary treatment for
small communities. Industrial applications include food processing wastewater,
textile effluent, pulp and paper mill effluent, and mining wastewater. Acid
mine drainage treatment is a well-established application that leverages the
sulfate-reducing and metal-precipitating capabilities of wetland systems to
neutralize acidic water and remove dissolved metals.

Stormwater management represents a growing application. Urban runoff carries
nutrients, sediments, metals from vehicle emissions, hydrocarbons from roads,
and bacteria from pet waste. Constructed wetlands in stormwater treatment
trains remove these pollutants while reducing peak flow rates that cause
flooding and stream erosion. Their ability to handle highly variable flow
rates — from trickle to torrent — makes them particularly well-suited to
stormwater applications where conventional treatment systems struggle.

Agricultural applications include treatment of animal waste lagoon effluent,
feedlot runoff, and irrigation return water. The nutrients captured from
agricultural wastewater can be returned to the land as plant biomass,
reducing the need for purchased fertilizers. Emerging applications include
treatment of landfill leachate (which contains a complex mixture of organic
and inorganic contaminants), removal of pharmaceuticals and personal care
products from municipal wastewater, and polishing of reverse osmosis
concentrate from desalination plants.

## Limitations and Future Directions

Despite their many advantages, constructed wetlands have important
limitations that must be acknowledged. Land area requirements can be
prohibitive in urban or densely developed areas. Surface flow wetlands
typically require five to ten times more area than subsurface flow systems
for equivalent treatment, and even subsurface flow systems require
substantially more land than conventional mechanical treatment. Treatment
performance varies seasonally with temperature and plant growth cycles;
biological activity slows during winter in cold climates, potentially
reducing treatment efficiency. Clogging of subsurface flow media reduces
hydraulic conductivity over time, eventually requiring media replacement.
Long-term phosphorus removal capacity is limited by substrate saturation,
and no indefinitely sustainable phosphorus removal mechanism has been
developed for constructed wetlands.

The fungal dimension of wetland treatment remains largely unexplored in
practice. Despite clear evidence that fungi play critical roles in
degrading recalcitrant compounds, most wetland design and monitoring
focuses exclusively on bacterial processes. Singh identifies several
research priorities: characterizing fungal community structure in wetland
systems, optimizing wetland design for fungal-dominated treatment processes,
developing monitoring tools for fungal activity assessment, integrating
fungal bioreactors with wetland treatment trains, and evaluating the
long-term sustainability of fungal communities in constructed wetlands.
Addressing these gaps could significantly expand the range of pollutants
that constructed wetlands can effectively treat.
