# Biological Soil Crusts (Biocrusts)

## Overview

Biological soil crusts — commonly called **biocrusts** or **cryptogamic covers** — are complex communities of cyanobacteria, algae, lichens, mosses, and microfungi that colonize the surface of undisturbed or minimally disturbed soils. They are especially dominant in **arid, semi-arid, and polar** environments where vascular plant cover is sparse, but they also occur in temperate grasslands, dune systems, and post-disturbance landscapes worldwide.

Biocrusts are not a single organism or taxonomic group; they are a **successional mosaic** of organisms that collectively perform ecosystem functions far exceeding what any member could achieve alone. They stabilize soil surfaces, fix carbon and nitrogen, cycle nutrients, influence water infiltration, and modulate surface energy balance.

Globally, biocrusts are estimated to cover roughly **12% of Earth's land surface** (Elbert et al., 2012), making them one of the most widespread biological communities on the planet, yet they remain among the least recognized and most threatened by land use change.

## Successional Stages of Biocrust Development

Biocrust communities develop through a recognizable sequence of successional stages, each characterized by dominant organism groups:

### Stage 1: Early Cyanobacterial Crust

The pioneer stage is dominated by **cyanobacteria** (formerly called blue-green algae), particularly filamentous genera such as *Microcoleus*, *Nostoc*, *Scytonema*, *Schizothrix*, and *Lyngbya*. These organisms are the first to colonize bare soil after disturbance.

Key features:
- Filamentous cyanobacteria secrete **extracellular polymeric substances (EPS)** — sticky polysaccharide sheaths that bind soil particles into a cohesive layer 1–3 mm thick.
- The EPS matrix increases soil aggregation and resistance to wind and water erosion by 2–10× compared to bare soil.
- *Microcoleus steen strains* are often the earliest colonists due to their ability to **migrate vertically** through soil, moving deeper during dry conditions and surfacing during wet periods (motion facilitated by slime secretion).
- Cyanobacteria in this stage fix atmospheric carbon via photosynthesis (contributing an estimated 2.9–7.3 Pg C/year globally) and nitrogen via heterocystous species (*Nostoc*, *Anabaena*, *Scytonema*).
- Nitrogen fixation rates in early cyanobacterial crusts range from **0.1 to 12 kg N/ha/year**, depending on moisture and temperature.
- This stage develops within weeks to months on suitable substrates but may take years in extremely arid environments.

### Stage 2: Light Cyanobacterial Crust with Algae

As the community matures, **eukaryotic algae** (green algae, diatoms, yellow-green algae) join the community, along with a more diverse suite of cyanobacteria.

Key features:
- Diatom diversity increases substantially, with genera like *Navicula*, *Nitzschia*, *Hantzschia*, and *Cymbella* common in arid crusts.
- Green algae such as *Chlorella*, *Trebouxia*, and *Chlamydomonas* contribute additional photosynthesis.
- The crust thickens to 3–5 mm and develops a darker pigmentation from accumulated pigments (scytonemin, carotenoids).
- Heterotrophic bacteria and actinomycetes colonize the EPS matrix, beginning to decompose organic matter and cycle nutrients.
- Microfauna (protozoa, nematodes, tardigrades) appear in small numbers.
- Duration: months to years, depending on climate and disturbance history.

### Stage 3: Dark Cyanobacterial/Moss Crust

With continued development, **bryophytes** (mosses) begin to establish, anchored by the existing cyanobacterial-algal mat. This stage represents a major increase in biomass and functional complexity.

Key features:
- Moss genera common in biocrusts include *Syntrichia* (formerly *Tortula*), *Bryum*, *Didymodon*, *Crossidium*, *Pottia*, and *Grimmia*.
- *Syntrichia ruralis* is one of the most widespread biocrust mosses globally, known for its extraordinary desiccation tolerance — it can survive losing 95% of its water content and resume photosynthesis within minutes of rehydration (poikilohydric strategy).
- The crust darkens significantly (dark brown to black), increasing surface albedo absorption by 5–15% compared to bare soil.
- Crust thickness reaches 5–10 mm, with substantially improved soil stabilization and water retention.
- Fungal hyphae become more prominent, connecting moss rhizoids to soil particles.
- Lichen propagules may begin to establish at this stage.
- Nitrogen fixation rates can peak at **10–25 kg N/ha/year**.
- Duration: years to decades.

### Stage 4: Lichen-Dominated Crust

The **climax stage** of biocrust succession is dominated by **lichens** — symbiotic associations of fungi (mycobionts) with photobionts (green algae or cyanobacteria). Lichen-dominated crusts represent the most functionally complex and biologically diverse biocrust type.

Key lichen types in biocrusts:
- **Squamulose lichens**: Scale-like, loosely attached — *Psora*, *Placidium*, *Diploschistes*, *Heppia*
- **Crustose lichens**: Tightly encrusted — *Endocarpon*, *Toninia*, *Caloplaca*, *Acarospora*
- **Foliose lichens**: Leaf-like, more three-dimensional — *Xanthoparmelia*, *Peltula*
- **Verrucaria** species and other cyanolichens (cyanobacterial photobionts) contribute significantly to nitrogen fixation.

Key features:
- Lichen-dominated crusts can reach 10–15 mm in thickness.
- Cyanolichens fix nitrogen at rates of **5–35 kg N/ha/year**, with *Collema*, *Peltula*, and *Heppia* among the most productive.
- These crusts have the highest biodiversity, supporting diverse communities of bacteria, fungi, microarthropods (springtails, mites), and protozoa.
- Water retention capacity is maximized — lichen crusts can absorb 2–8× their dry weight in water.
- This stage may require **20–100+ years** to develop after disturbance, making it essentially non-renewable on human timescales.

## Biogeochemical Functions

### Carbon Fixation

Biocrusts are globally significant carbon sinks. Elbert et al. (2012) estimated that cryptogamic covers fix approximately **3.9 Pg C/year** — nearly half of the global terrestrial carbon fixation. This carbon enters soil organic matter pools, contributing to long-term carbon storage in dryland soils.

Carbon fixation follows moisture pulses:
- **Photosynthesis activates** within minutes of wetting (for cyanobacteria and algae) to hours (for lichens and mosses).
- **Respiration pulses** occur immediately upon wetting (the "Birch effect") as accumulated osmolytes are metabolized.
- Net carbon gain depends on the balance between photosynthesis and respiration, which varies by crust type, temperature, and light intensity.
- Dark cyanobacterial crusts have higher gross photosynthesis but also higher respiration than light crusts; lichen crusts tend to have the most positive net carbon balance per wetting event.

### Nitrogen Fixation

Biocrusts are the **primary source of new nitrogen** in many dryland ecosystems, where vascular plant N-fixers are scarce.

- Nitrogen fixation is performed by **heterocystous cyanobacteria** (*Nostoc*, *Scytonema*, *Anabaena*) and **cyanolichens** (*Collema*, *Peltula*, *Leptogium*).
- Fixation follows a unimodal temperature response, peaking at 20–30°C, and is strongly moisture-dependent.
- Annual fixation rates range from 0.1 kg N/ha/year in cold deserts to 35+ kg N/ha/year in warm deserts with mature cyanolichen crusts.
- Fixed nitrogen is transferred to the broader ecosystem through:
  - Leaching of ammonium into soil solution during wetting events
  - Decomposition of crust biomass by heterotrophic microorganisms
  - Consumption of crust organisms by microfauna and their subsequent predation
  - Direct transfer to vascular plant roots via common mycorrhizal networks
- Rodriguez-Caballero et al. (2018) estimated biocrusts contribute roughly **49 Tg N/year** globally — approximately half of all terrestrial biological nitrogen fixation.

### Phosphorus and Other Nutrients

- Biocrust organisms solubilize mineral phosphorus through acid exudation (organic acids from fungal hyphae) and enzyme production (phosphatases).
- They cycle potassium, calcium, magnesium, and micronutrients (iron, manganese, zinc, copper) through uptake, biomass incorporation, and decomposition.
- Dust capture by crust surfaces adds both mineral nutrients and organic particles from atmospheric deposition.
- Crust organisms produce **siderophores** (iron-chelating compounds) that increase iron availability in alkaline soils.

## Hydrological Functions

### Water Infiltration

The relationship between biocrusts and water infiltration is complex and depends on crust type and environmental conditions:

- **Light cyanobacterial crusts** generally **increase infiltration** by protecting surface soil structure from raindrop impact and maintaining macropores.
- **Dark (mature) crusts** can **reduce infiltration** by 10–50% under high-intensity rainfall, because the EPS swells when wet, clogging surface pores. This is a significant concern in some managed drylands.
- **Moss-dominated crusts** create a spongy surface layer that can increase water retention at the surface but slow downward percolation.
- The net effect on infiltration varies by soil texture, slope, rainfall intensity, and crust community composition.

### Soil Moisture Retention

- Biocrusts increase soil moisture retention by reducing evaporation (the crust creates a physical barrier to capillary rise) and increasing water-holding capacity.
- The EPS matrix acts as a hydrogel, absorbing and slowly releasing water.
- Moss and lichen crusts can hold 3–8× their dry weight in water, creating a moist microhabitat that persists for days after surface drying.
- This moisture retention is critical for seed germination and seedling establishment of vascular plants.

### Runoff and Erosion Control

- Even thin (1–2 mm) cyanobacterial crusts reduce wind erosion by 50–80% by binding loose particles.
- Water erosion is reduced by 30–60% through increased surface roughness, aggregate stability, and infiltration (where it is not reduced).
- Crust destruction (by trampling, off-road vehicles, or grazing) can increase erosion by 10–100×.

## Effects on Vascular Plants

### Positive Effects

1. **Nutrient enrichment**: Biocrust-derived nitrogen and other nutrients create "fertility islands" that benefit neighboring plants.
2. **Seed trapping**: Rough crust surfaces trap wind-dispersed seeds, increasing germination sites.
3. **Moisture retention**: Crust-associated moisture benefits seedling establishment, especially for shallow-rooted species.
4. **Mycorrhizal facilitation**: Crust fungi include arbuscular mycorrhizal (AMF) species that can colonize plant roots.
5. **Hormone production**: Some cyanobacteria and crust-associated bacteria produce auxins, cytokinins, and gibberellins that stimulate plant growth.

### Negative Effects

1. **Infiltration reduction**: Dark, hydrophobic crusts can reduce water availability to deeper-rooted plants.
2. **Resource competition**: Crust organisms compete for water and nutrients, especially during brief moisture pulses.
3. **Allelopathy**: Some crust cyanobacteria produce bioactive compounds that inhibit seed germination of certain plant species.
4. **Thermal effects**: Dark crusts absorb more solar radiation, increasing surface temperature by 2–10°C, which can stress seedlings.

The net effect of biocrusts on vascular plants is generally **positive in drylands** and **context-dependent in more productive systems**.

## Global Distribution and Environmental Controls

### Climate Controls

- **Precipitation**: Biocrust cover generally increases with aridity up to a threshold (roughly 100–300 mm annual precipitation), then decreases as vascular plant competition intensifies in wetter regions.
- **Temperature**: Different crust types dominate different thermal regimes. Cyanobacterial crusts dominate hot deserts; moss-lichen crusts dominate cold deserts and polar regions.
- **Seasonality**: Winter-rainfall deserts (Mojave, Negev) tend to have well-developed cyanobacterial and lichen crusts; summer-rainfall deserts (Chihuahuan) have more moss-dominated crusts.

### Soil Controls

- **Texture**: Sandy soils generally support better crust development than clay soils, due to better aeration and light penetration.
- **pH**: Most biocrust organisms prefer neutral to alkaline soils (pH 7–9); acidic soils tend to support moss- and lichen-dominated crusts with fewer cyanobacteria.
- **Salinity**: Some biocrust cyanobacteria (*Microcoleus*) tolerate moderate salinity; most lichens and mosses are salt-sensitive.
- **Stability**: Crusts develop best on stable, undisturbed surfaces. Frequent disturbance prevents succession beyond the cyanobacterial stage.

## Threats and Conservation

### Anthropogenic Disturbance

- **Physical disturbance** (vehicle traffic, livestock trampling, military training) is the most significant threat. Recovery from severe disturbance can take decades to centuries.
- **Grazing pressure**: Even moderate livestock grazing crushes and buries crust organisms. Hoof action creates bare patches that become erosion hotspots.
- **Off-road vehicles**: A single vehicle pass can destroy 50–100 years of crust development.
- **Agricultural conversion**: Plowing eliminates crusts entirely and prevents re-establishment.
- **Urbanization and infrastructure**: Roads, pipelines, and solar/wind installations directly destroy crust habitat.

### Climate Change Impacts

- **Increased temperature**: Warming favors bacterial decomposition of crust organic matter, potentially tipping the carbon balance from sink to source.
- **Altered precipitation patterns**: More intense storms can erode disturbed crusts; fewer, smaller precipitation events reduce photosynthetic activity.
- **Nitrogen deposition**: Atmospheric N deposition from fossil fuel combustion shifts crust community composition toward chlorophyll-rich cyanobacteria and away from nitrogen-fixing organisms, potentially reducing N fixation capacity.
- **Dust emissions**: Crust destruction increases dust emission, creating a positive feedback loop (more dust → less crust → more erosion → more dust).

### Invasive Species

- Invasive annual grasses (e.g., *Bromus tectorum*, cheatgrass) can outcompete crust organisms for space and resources, especially after fire or disturbance.
- Fire kills crust organisms directly and can take decades to recover.

## Restoration and Reintroduction

### Passive Restoration

- Excluding disturbance (fencing, grazing management) allows natural recovery, but this is extremely slow (decades to centuries for lichen-dominated stages).
- Most effective in areas where propagule sources (intact crust patches) remain nearby.

### Active Restoration (Culturing and Inoculation)

Biocrust restoration has become an active research field with practical applications:

1. **Cyanobacterial inoculation**: The most widely practiced method. Filamentous cyanobacteria (*Microcoleus* spp.) are cultured in laboratory bioreactors, then applied to degraded soil as a liquid slurry. Success rates of 40–80% establishment have been reported within 1–3 years.

2. **Inoculum production methods**:
   - **Liquid culture**: Cyanobacteria grown in aerated tanks with BG-11 or similar medium; applied by spraying.
   - **Solid substrate culture**: Grown on sand, soil, or fabric mats; transplanted to field sites.
   - **Soil-crust inoculum**: Small pieces of intact crust are collected from donor sites and spread across degraded areas ("crust salvaging").
   - **Dried inoculum**: Cyanobacterial biomass is dried, ground, and mixed with carrier materials (sand, clay, straw) for easy transport and application.

3. **Field application techniques**:
   - Spraying liquid inoculum with agricultural equipment
   - Spreading dried inoculum by hand or mechanical spreader
   - Laying fabric mats pre-inoculated with crust organisms
   - Broadcasting salvaged crust fragments

4. **Success factors**:
   - Timing applications before seasonal rains
   - Protecting inoculated areas from disturbance (fencing, signs)
   - Matching inoculum species to site conditions (climate, soil, elevation)
   - Applying polysaccharide stabilizers (guar gum, xanthan gum) to protect inoculum from erosion and desiccation
   - Repeated applications may be needed (2–3 treatments over 2–3 years)

5. **Moss and lichen inoculation**: More challenging but increasingly feasible. Moss fragmentation and transplantation can establish moss-dominated crusts within 2–5 years. Lichen inoculation remains experimental but promising using lichen diaspores (soredia, isidia) propagated in culture.

## Monitoring and Assessment

### Visual Assessment

- Crust cover is estimated by **point-intercept** or **line-intercept** methods along transects.
- Crust type is classified by dominant organism (cyanobacterial, moss, lichen) and developmental stage (light, dark, pinnacled).
- Chlorophyll *a* content (measured by acetone extraction and spectrophotometry) is a standard proxy for crust biomass.

### Remote Sensing

- **Spectral signatures**: Biocrusts have distinctive reflectance patterns, particularly in the visible (chlorophyll absorption) and shortwave infrared (water absorption) regions.
- **NDVI and crust-specific indices**: Modified vegetation indices can distinguish crust types and estimate cover from aerial or satellite imagery.
- **Hyperspectral imaging**: Enables species-level identification and health assessment.
- **Drone-based monitoring**: Increasingly used for high-resolution mapping of crust distribution and condition.

### Functional Indicators

- **Nitrogenase activity**: Measured by acetylene reduction assay (ARA) to estimate N fixation rates.
- **Carbon exchange**: Measured by portable gas exchange chambers.
- **Aggregate stability**: Wet-sieving tests to quantify soil stabilization.
- **Infiltration rate**: Measured by disk permeameter or double-ring infiltrometer.

## Biocrusts in Permaculture and Regenerative Agriculture

### Relevance to Land Stewardship

While biocrusts are most prominent in arid and semi-arid regions, their principles are relevant to any land management context:

- **Minimize soil disturbance**: No-till and reduced-tillage practices protect developing crusts in all climates.
- **Manage grazing strategically**: Rotational grazing with adequate rest periods allows crust recovery between grazing events.
- **Avoid compaction**: Heavy machinery and concentrated livestock traffic destroy crust communities.
- **Protect from chemicals**: Herbicides and pesticides can kill crust organisms directly; fertilizers can shift community composition.
- **Consider crusts in earthworks**: Keyline plowing, swale construction, and other earthworks should consider existing crust communities and minimize destruction.

### Practical Applications

1. **Erosion control on degraded land**: Cyanobacterial inoculation is a cost-effective approach to stabilizing disturbed soils, especially in dryland regions.
2. **Nitrogen input**: Biocrusts provide free, sustainable nitrogen inputs to agricultural and pastoral systems — particularly valuable in organic systems.
3. **Dust mitigation**: Restoring crusts reduces dust emissions from disturbed land.
4. **Carbon sequestration**: Biocrusts contribute to long-term soil carbon storage in dryland soils.
5. **Biodiversity**: Crust communities support diverse microfauna that form the base of dryland food webs.

## Key Research Frontiers

- **Climate-resilient inoculum**: Selecting and engineering crust strains adapted to future climate conditions (higher temperature, altered rainfall).
- **Microbiome engineering**: Designing multi-species inocula with synergistic interactions for faster establishment and greater function.
- **Large-scale restoration**: Developing methods for hectare-scale crust restoration using mechanized application.
- **Biocrust-volatile organic compounds**: Recent research shows biocrusts emit VOCs that may influence cloud formation and atmospheric chemistry.
- **Carbon-climate feedback**: Quantifying the tipping point at which warming causes biocrusts to become carbon sources rather than sinks.
- **Genomics and metagenomics**: Understanding the full diversity and functional potential of biocrust microbial communities.
- **Crust-atmosphere interactions**: Role of biocrusts in global dust emission, radiative forcing, and trace gas exchange.

---

## Summary

Biological soil crusts are foundational ecosystem engineers in drylands and disturbed landscapes worldwide. They represent a remarkable example of **biological self-organization** — simple pioneer organisms creating the conditions for increasingly complex communities to develop over decades to centuries. Their contributions to soil stabilization, carbon and nitrogen cycling, water dynamics, and biodiversity are disproportionate to their thin, inconspicuous appearance.

The greatest threat to biocrusts is **physical disturbance** combined with **climate change**, and their greatest challenge is their **extremely slow recovery** from destruction. Active restoration through cyanobacterial inoculation offers a practical tool for land stewards, but protecting existing crusts through careful land management remains the most effective and economical conservation strategy.
