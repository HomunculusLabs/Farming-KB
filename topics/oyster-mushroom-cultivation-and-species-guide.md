# Oyster Mushroom Cultivation and Species Guide

Oyster mushrooms of the genus *Pleurotus* are among the most widely cultivated mushrooms on Earth, grown commercially on every inhabited continent.

Their popularity stems from a remarkable combination of traits: aggressive colonization, prolific fruiting on agricultural waste, and relatively simple infrastructure compared to button mushrooms or shiitake.

The genus encompasses over forty described species, with a handful dominating commercial production. Each brings distinct flavors, textures, colors, and growing requirements.

Paul Stamets described them as "by far the easiest and least expensive to grow," noting their unparalleled adaptability among all cultivated fungi.

Biological efficiency routinely exceeds 100 percent, meaning harvests can weigh more than the dry substrate consumed. This makes them extraordinarily productive relative to input costs.

They grow on dozens of substrates, from wheat straw to coffee grounds, making them ideal for waste-recycling cultivation systems in both rural and urban settings.

## Pleurotus ostreatus — The Classic Oyster

*Pleurotus ostreatus* is the type species and the most widely cultivated oyster worldwide. It produces gray to white shelf-like caps in overlapping clusters on hardwoods and straw.

Caps range from 5 to 25 cm, convex at first then flattening with wavy margins in age. The flesh is white and firm. Spores are white to slightly lilac, measuring 7.5 to 9.5 by 3 to 4 micrometers.

Clamp connections are present and the context is monomitic. Aged mycelium often secretes yellowish to orangish droplets of a metabolite that is toxic to nematodes.

In nature, it fruits on dead or dying hardwoods including cottonwood, oak, alder, maple, aspen, beech, birch, elm, willow, and poplar. It is primarily a saprophyte but behaves as a facultative parasite.

The most abundant wild fruitings appear in low valley riparian habitats during spring and fall. Occasionally it appears on composting bales of straw and coffee production waste pulp.

The species fruits at 10 to 20 degrees Celsius. Stamets documented peak primordia formation at 2,000 lux, with total yield maximized at 300 to 430 lux for 12 hours daily.

Thorn and Barron (1984) discovered that the mycelium exudes a metabolite toxic to nematodes, stunning them before the fungus invades to consume their internal organs for nitrogen.

## Pleurotus pulmonarius — The Phoenix Oyster

*Pleurotus pulmonarius*, the phoenix or Indian oyster, prefers warmer temperatures of 15 to 28 degrees Celsius. It dominates tropical and subtropical cultivation across South and Southeast Asia.

Compared to *P. ostreatus*, it has paler caps, thinner flesh, a finer stem, and faster mycelial growth. Cap color varies from grayish-white through beige to lilac-grey.

The species hosts a large complex of varieties, offering cultivators rich material for strain development. It is exceedingly aggressive on alder, cottonwood, poplar, oak, maple, elm, and aspen.

In the eastern United States it decomposes hardwoods while western populations colonize conifers including *Abies* and *Picea* at middle elevations between 1,200 and 3,000 meters.

It is the species most commonly misidentified as "P. sajor-caju" in cultivation literature. The true *P. sajor-caju* has a distinct veil and trimitic hyphae, whereas *P. pulmonarius* is monomitic.

It is favoured in many developing-country programs because of its adaptability to a wide range of substrates and tolerance of less rigorous environmental control.

## Substrate Selection and Worldwide Materials

Oyster mushrooms thrive on an extraordinary range of substrates. Stamets documented growth on hardwood sawdust, all cereal straws, corn cobs, bagasse, coffee residues, banana fronds, cottonseed hulls, agave waste, soy pulp, and paper.

The MushWorld substrate survey compiled data from dozens of countries, revealing biological efficiencies from 15 percent on pure bagasse to over 100 percent on supplemented cottonseed hulls and legume straws.

Cottonseed hulls are the best substrate requiring no heat treatment. Cotton waste consistently produces biological efficiencies of 56 to 86 percent across more than fifty cited studies.

Cereal straws, particularly wheat and rice, are the most widely used substrates globally. They are inexpensive, widely available, and produce reliable yields of 50 to 75 percent biological efficiency.

Corncobs have been used since 1956 in Hungary. Pea straw offers 43 percent cellulose and a C/N ratio of 45. Groundnut shells, cardamom pulp, lemon grass, and pepper leaves all support Pleurotus growth.

Spent *Volvariella* compost has been reused for oyster cultivation at 80 percent biological efficiency, demonstrating sequential mushroom farming viability.

## Pasteurization and Sterilization Methods

Hot water pasteurization is the simplest method for straw-based cultivation. Straw is chopped to 2 to 5 cm lengths and soaked at 65 to 80 degrees Celsius for 60 to 90 minutes.

This kills most competitors while preserving beneficial thermophilic bacteria that help oyster mycelium colonize. Steam pasteurization uses steam injection for larger bulk operations.

Lime pasteurization offers a low-energy alternative. Straw is soaked at pH 12 to 13 for 12 to 16 hours. Oyster mycelium tolerates the treatment while contaminants succumb to the alkaline conditions.

Autoclave sterilization at 15 psi for 60 to 90 minutes is used for supplemented sawdust, particularly king oyster. This eliminates all organisms and allows higher nutrient supplementation for greater yields.

Non-sterilized wheat straw methods developed in South Asia eliminate autoclaves entirely. Lime-treated straw is packed onto shelves, spawned with grain spawn, and sealed with polythene sheeting.

Yellow pulse powder is spread as a nitrogen supplement. Full colonization takes approximately 50 days, after which the polythene is removed to initiate fruiting.

## Spawn Production and Inoculation

Spawn quality is among the most decisive factors for cultivation success. Contaminated or weak spawn undermines all subsequent effort regardless of how well substrate is prepared.

Grain spawn using rye, wheat, sorghum, millet, or milo is the standard for oyster production. Grain provides abundant nutrients that help mycelium establish quickly after inoculation.

Sawdust spawn is less expensive per unit but offers fewer colonization points per kilogram. It is preferred for outdoor log cultivation and some straw methods where cost matters most.

Liquid culture offers rapid colonization but requires sterile technique and is more vulnerable to contamination during handling and inoculation.

For king oyster on straw, combining equal quantities of sawdust and grain spawn produces the best results. Every ton of wheat straw can yield up to 300 kg of fresh king oyster mushrooms.

Inoculation rates typically range from 5 to 10 percent of substrate dry weight. Higher rates accelerate colonization and reduce contamination risk but increase spawn costs.

## Colonization and Fruiting Environment

During colonization, bags or beds are kept at 20 to 27 degrees Celsius in darkness with elevated CO2. These conditions favor rapid mycelial growth across the substrate surface.

Spawn running takes 10 to 21 days for most species on straw, and 14 to 25 days for king oyster on supplemented sawdust. Full colonization is indicated by a solid white mycelial mat.

Fruiting initiation requires a temperature shift to the species-specific range, typically 2 to 8 degrees cooler than colonization. Humidity must reach 90 to 95 percent.

Primordia are extremely sensitive to drying and will abort if humidity drops below 80 percent. Once mushrooms reach 2 to 3 cm they become more resilient to minor fluctuations.

CO2 must drop below 1,000 ppm for normal fruiting. In stagnant air with high CO2, mushrooms develop long stringy stems and small caps rather than the desirable broad shelf-like form.

Light is required for normal development. *P. ostreatus* forms the most primordia at 2,000 lux, but total yield is maximized at 300 to 430 lux for 12 hours daily.

Total light exposure below 2,400 lux-hours per day triggers abnormal coral formations. Light exceeding 10,000 lux caused primordia to fail entirely.

## Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling

Oyster mushrooms should be harvested just before or as the cap margin begins to curl upward, signaling spore release onset. This stage ensures the best texture and flavor.

Most species reach harvestable size at 5 to 15 cm cap diameter depending on variety. The entire cluster is removed by twisting or cutting at the base with a clean knife.

Removing the full cluster reduces the risk of leaving stipe material that can rot and attract contaminants. A clean knife cut at the base is the standard commercial method.

First flush produces 60 to 70 percent of total yield. Subsequent flushes at 2 to 3 week intervals are progressively smaller. Commercial growers cycle blocks after 2 to 3 flushes.

Rapid spoilage is a significant challenge. Oyster mushrooms remain marketable for only a few days, supporting the argument that local producers should supply local markets.

Heavy spore production in growing rooms poses respiratory health risks. Sporeless strains with short gills and thicker flesh are highly valued in commercial operations for worker safety.
