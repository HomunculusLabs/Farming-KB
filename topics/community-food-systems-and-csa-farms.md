Community food systems represent a fundamental reimagining of how food moves
from farm to table. Unlike the industrial supply chain — where produce may
travel thousands of miles through processing facilities, distribution centers,
and retail shelves — community food systems prioritize local production, direct
relationships between growers and eaters, and economic recirculation within a
defined geographic area. At the heart of this movement sits Community Supported
Agriculture (CSA), a model that has grown from a handful of experimental farms
in the 1980s to a cornerstone of local food infrastructure across [[psilocybin-north-america-pacific-northwest]],
Europe, and increasingly across the Global South.

The appeal of community food systems extends far beyond romantic notions of
farm-fresh produce. They address structural vulnerabilities in the global food
chain: supply disruptions from pandemics, climate-driven crop failures,
consolidation that squeezes small producers, and nutritional degradation during
long-distance transport. David Holmgren, co-originator of permaculture, has
argued that energy descent will fundamentally reconfigure food distribution,
making local self-reliance not merely preferable but essential. His analysis
points out that marketplace diversity in wealthy nations has been built on cheap
energy and subsidized transport, creating what he calls a fragile illusion of
abundance.

Bill Mollison offered a striking statistic: in Russia, peasant farmers working
just four percent of the land produced sixty percent of the food. In the United
States, fifty-three percent of households garden, producing eighteen percent of
the national food supply on an average of only six hundred square feet each.
These numbers suggest that the productivity gap between industrial monoculture
and small-scale intensive growing is far narrower than commonly assumed, and
that enormous untapped potential exists within urban and suburban landscapes.

## Origins and History of Community Supported Agriculture

The CSA model emerged simultaneously in Europe and Japan in the 1960s and 1970s,
driven by overlapping concerns about food safety, environmental degradation,
and the disconnect between urban consumers and rural producers. In Japan, a
movement called teikei (partnership) developed in response to mercury
contamination in food and growing awareness of pesticide risks. Consumers
organized directly with farmers to secure clean, trustworthy food, establishing
the core principle that members would share in both the bounty and the risk of
[[fukuoka-agricultural-production-net-energy-deduction]].

In Europe, particularly Germany and Switzerland, the [[query-what-is-biodynamic-agriculture-and-how-does-it-differ-from-organic]]
community developed similar models. Jan Vander Tuin, a Swiss farmer who had
participated in European CSA experiments, brought the concept to the United
States in 1985, collaborating with Robyn Van En to establish the first American
CSA at Indian Line Farm in Massachusetts. Van En became a tireless advocate,
and by the mid-1990s, CSAs had spread to every region of the United States.

The growth has been remarkable. From fewer than 100 CSA farms in 1990, the
movement expanded to over 12,000 farms serving an estimated 1 million
households by the mid-2010s. The COVID-19 pandemic triggered another surge, as
supply chain disruptions and grocery store shortages drove consumers to seek
local alternatives. Many CSA farms reported waitlists of hundreds of households
during the 2020 season, and many have maintained that momentum.

## How CSA Models Work

At its simplest, a CSA involves three steps. First, a farmer plans seasonal
production based on expected membership. Second, members sign up and pay before
the growing season begins, typically between November and March. Third, the
farmer grows, harvests, and packs boxes weekly throughout the season, which
usually runs eighteen to twenty-six weeks in temperate climates. Some farms
offer winter shares extending the season to forty or more weeks using storage
crops, greenhouse greens, and season-extension techniques.

The risk-sharing principle is fundamental. Members accept that weather, pests,
or disease may reduce their share in any given week, while enjoying the
surplus when conditions favor abundance. This arrangement provides farmers with
upfront capital when they need it most, eliminates the uncertainty of finding
buyers at harvest time, and creates a committed community of supporters who
understand the realities of agricultural production.

## Structural Variations on the CSA Model

Not all CSAs look the same. The traditional model offers fixed box contents
determined by what the farm harvests each week, with perhaps two or three size
options. This is the simplest to administer but can frustrate members who
dislike certain vegetables or find unfamiliar items confusing. The market-style
CSA addresses this by setting up a display like a miniature farmers market at
pickup, where members choose items from a selection using credits or points.
This increases satisfaction but requires significantly more labor at each
pickup location.

Custom CSAs take flexibility further, using online ordering platforms such as
Harvie, Small Farm Central, or Barn2Door to let members select preferences in
advance. The farmer then packs boxes based on these selections within the
constraints of actual harvest availability. This model demands the most
sophisticated logistics but can dramatically improve member retention.

Many farms layer on additional revenue streams through add-on shares: a dozen
eggs per week from pastured poultry, monthly meat shares of beef, pork, or
chicken, weekly bouquets of cut flowers, fresh herbs, mushroom shares, or
value-added products like jam, hot sauce, honey, and soap. These add-ons can
contribute an additional five to fifteen thousand dollars to a fifty-member
