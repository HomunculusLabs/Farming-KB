# Hickory and Butternut Cultivation

## Overview

Hickories (*Carya* spp.) and butternut (*Juglans cinerea*) are among the most valuable native nut-producing trees in North America. Long-lived, deeply rooted, and ecologically foundational, they produce nutrient-dense nuts prized for culinary use, provide exceptional timber, and serve as [[keystone-species-and-trophic-cascades]] in forest ecosystems. Despite their slow growth, they offer unmatched longevity and resilience — individual trees can live 200–500 years and continue producing for centuries.

## Botanical Background

### The Carya Genus

The genus *Carya* contains approximately 18 species worldwide, with 12–15 native to North America. They are divided into two groups:

**True Hickories (Apocarya section):**
- Shagbark hickory (*C. ovata*) — the premier nut-producing species
- Shellbark hickory (*C. laciniosa*) — larger nuts, bottomland species
- Pignut hickory (*C. glabra*) — smaller nuts but very adaptable
- Mockernut hickory (*C. tomentosa*) — thick shell, widespread
- Sand hickory (*C. pallida*) — [[drought-tolerant-planting-design]], sandy soils
- Red hickory (*C. ovalis*) — upland species

**Pecan Hickories (Sinocarya section):**
- Pecan (*C. illinoinensis*) — commercially dominant, covered separately
- Bitternut hickory (*C. cordiformis*) — bitter kernel, ornamental value
- Water hickory (*C. aquatica*) — wetland species, poor nut quality
- Nutmeg hickory (*C. myristiciformis*) — rare, small range

### Butternut

Butternut (*Juglans cinerea*) is a close relative of [[black-walnut-juglone-plant-toxicity]], native to the eastern US and southeastern Canada. It produces oblong, sweet, oily nuts with a distinctive buttery flavor. Once common, butternut is now threatened by butternut canker disease (*Ophiognomonia clavigignenti-juglandacearum*), making cultivation and conservation critical.

## Species Profiles

### Shagbark Hickory (*Carya ovata*)

The gold standard for hickory nut production. Named for its distinctive shaggy, peeling bark that exfoliates in long plates.

**Characteristics:**
- Height: 60–80 ft (18–24 m), occasionally to 100 ft
- Spread: 30–50 ft (9–15 m)
- Growth rate: Slow (12–24 inches/year)
- Lifespan: 200–300+ years
- Nut size: 1.5–2 inches, rounded
- Kernel: Sweet, rich, 15–20% kernel by weight
- Hardiness: USDA Zones 4–8
- Chill hours: 1000–1500

**Key cultivars:**
- 'Weschcke' — Selected in Minnesota, excellent cold hardiness, thin shell
- 'Grainger' — Large nut, good cracking quality, from Tennessee
- 'Yoder' — Productive, good kernel percentage
- 'Neilson' — Iowa selection, large nut, good flavor
- 'Kentucky' — High yielding, consistent bearer
- 'Porter' — Thin-shelled, easy to crack

### Shellbark Hickory (*Carya laciniosa*)

Larger in all dimensions than shagbark, with even more dramatic bark. Prefers moist bottomland soils.

**Characteristics:**
- Height: 60–100 ft (18–30 m)
- Growth rate: Slow to moderate
- Nut size: 1.75–2.5 inches — largest of the hickories
- Kernel: Sweet, 12–18% kernel by weight
- Hardiness: USDA Zones 5–8
- Prefers deep, moist, fertile soils

**Key cultivars:**
- 'Floyd' — Large nut, thin shell
- 'Pleas' — Excellent producer from Missouri
- 'Wright' — Vigorous, large nuts
- 'Roe' — Good kernel quality

### Butternut (*Juglans cinerea*)

**Characteristics:**
- Height: 40–60 ft (12–18 m)
- Spread: 30–50 ft (9–15 m)
- Growth rate: Moderate (faster than hickories)
- Lifespan: 75–100+ years (shortened by canker)
- Nut size: 1.5–2.5 inches, oblong, pointed
- Kernel: Oily, buttery, sweet, ~40% kernel by weight
- Hardiness: USDA Zones 3–7
- Chill hours: 800–1200

**Butternut vs. Black Walnut:**
- Butternut kernels are oilier and sweeter, with a distinctive buttery flavor
- Butternut shells are softer and more easily cracked by hand
- Butternut trees are smaller and more cold-hardy
- Butternut wood is lighter and less valuable than black walnut
- Butternut is far more susceptible to disease

## Site Selection

### Climate Requirements

**Hickories:**
- Prefer continental climates with warm summers and cold winters
- Summer temperatures: 75–95°F (24–35°C) optimal for nut fill
- Winter cold: Dormancy requirement 1000+ chill hours
- Shagbark tolerates -30°F (-34°C) when established
- Late spring frosts can damage flowers — avoid frost pockets
- Need 140–200 frost-free days for full nut maturity
- Long, hot summers produce the best-filled nuts

**Butternut:**
- More cold-hardy than most hickories
- Tolerates temperatures to -40°F (-40°C) in dormancy
- Shorter growing season requirement: 120–160 frost-free days
- More tolerant of cool, short-season climates than hickories
- Less heat-tolerant — struggles in zones 8+

### Soil Requirements

**Hickories:**
- Deep, well-drained loam soils are ideal
- pH range: 5.5–7.5, prefers slightly acidic to neutral (6.0–7.0)
- Shagbark tolerates rocky, thin soils better than shellbark
- Shellbark demands deep, moist, fertile bottomland soils
- Sandy loam to clay loam acceptable if well-drained
- Poor tolerance of waterlogged soils (except water hickory)
- Deep taproot makes them drought-tolerant once established
- Benefit from 3–6 ft (1–2 m) of soil depth

**Butternut:**
- More adaptable than hickories regarding soil
- Prefers deep, well-drained loams
- Tolerates a wider pH range (5.0–8.0)
- More tolerant of somewhat wetter soils
- Can thrive on rocky hillsides and slopes
- Less demanding than black walnut regarding soil fertility

### Light and Spacing

- Full sun required for optimal nut production
- Seedlings tolerate partial shade for first 5–10 years
- Minimum spacing: 30 × 30 ft (9 × 9 m) for hickories
- Recommended spacing: 40–50 ft (12–15 m) between trees
- Butternut can be spaced slightly closer: 25–35 ft (8–11 m)
- Consider mature canopy spread when planning food forests
- Hickories are taprooted — avoid planting over underground utilities

## Planting and Establishment

### Seed Germination

**Hickory seed propagation:**
1. Collect nuts in fall after they drop naturally
2. Remove husks promptly (they contain tannins that can inhibit germination)
3. Cold stratify at 33–41°F (1–5°C) for 90–120 days
4. Plant 2–3 inches (5–8 cm) deep in well-drained medium
5. Germination is hypogeal — the cotyledons remain below ground
6. Germination rate: 30–70% for fresh seed
7. Seedlings develop a deep taproot quickly — use deep containers (tree tubes, 18+ inches)
8. Transplant before taproot hits container bottom (first season ideally)

**Butternut seed propagation:**
1. Collect nuts when husks begin to split
2. Remove husks and cure for 2–3 weeks
3. Cold stratify for 60–90 days at 33–41°F (1–5°C)
4. Plant 2 inches (5 cm) deep
5. Germination rate: 50–80% for fresh seed
6. Can also be fall-planted directly in the ground with mulch protection
7. Butternut germinates more readily than hickory

### Transplanting Seedlings

- Transplant in early spring before bud break, or fall after leaf drop
- Bare-root planting is possible but stressful — container-grown preferred
- Dig hole 2× wider than root ball, same depth as root collar
- Do NOT bend or circling the taproot — it must go straight down
- Backfill with native soil; avoid heavy amendments that discourage deep rooting
- Water deeply at planting; mulch 3–4 inches deep to 3 ft radius
- Staking usually unnecessary — the deep taproot provides stability
- Protect young bark from rodents with tree guards
- Deer browsing is a major issue — fencing may be needed for first 5–10 years

### Grafted Trees

Grafted cultivars begin bearing significantly earlier (5–8 years vs. 15–20 for seedlings). Key considerations:

- Grafting is typically done in late winter using whip-and-tongue or bark graft
- Scion wood collected in late January–February, stored cold and moist
- Graft union must be at least 6 inches above the ground line
- Hickory grafting has lower success rates than other nut trees (40–70%)
- Butternut grafting is somewhat easier than hickory
- Buy grafted trees from specialist nurseries (see resources below)
- Grafted trees cost $35–80 each vs. $5–15 for seedlings

## Care and Maintenance

### Irrigation

- Young trees need consistent moisture for first 3–5 years
- Apply 10–15 gallons per week during dry periods
- Drip irrigation or deep watering preferred over shallow sprinkling
- Established hickories are among the most drought-tolerant nut trees
- Butternut needs more consistent moisture than shagbark
- Critical irrigation periods: during nut fill (July–September)
- Water stress during fill causes poorly filled, shriveled kernels

### Fertilization

**Soil test first.** Hickories growing in good soils rarely need heavy fertilization.

**Young trees (1–5 years):**
- Apply 1/4 lb of 10-10-10 or equivalent per tree per year of age
- Broadcast in a circle 2–3 ft from the trunk, not against it
- Avoid high nitrogen that encourages vegetative growth over root development

**Bearing trees (5+ years):**
- Annual application: 1–2 lbs N per tree
- 1–3 lbs P₂O₅ (based on soil test)
- 1–3 lbs K₂O (based on soil test)
- Split applications: half in early spring, half in late spring
- Zinc is critical for nut trees — foliar zinc chelate spray in spring
- Boron deficiency causes blank nuts — 0.1–0.2 lbs borax per tree if needed
- Composted manure or leaf mold can replace synthetic fertilizers

### Mulching

- Maintain 3–4 inch organic mulch ring to the drip line
- Keep mulch 3–6 inches away from trunk bark
- Wood chips, leaves, or shredded bark all work well
- Mulch retains moisture, suppresses weeds, feeds soil biology
- Replenish annually
- Avoid walnut-family mulch around sensitive plants (juglone sensitivity)

### Pruning

**Young trees (formative pruning):**
- Establish a strong central leader in first 5–10 years
- Remove competing leaders, double tops, and weak branch angles
- Space scaffold branches 12–18 inches apart vertically
- Remove branches below 5–6 ft for clearance
- Minimal pruning is generally best — hickories have strong natural form

**Mature trees:**
- Remove dead, damaged, or diseased wood
- Thin the canopy every 5–10 years for light penetration
- Remove water sprouts and suckers
- Avoid heavy pruning — hickories are slow to close wounds
- Paint large wounds with tree wound dressing (debated, but common practice for Juglandaceae)
- Best pruned in late dormancy (February–March)

## Pest and Disease Management

### Major Pests

**Hickory Shuckworm (*Cydia caryana*):**
- Larvae tunnel into developing nuts
- Causes premature nut drop and kernel damage
- Monitor with pheromone traps (May–June flight)
- Spray Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) or spinosad at petal fall and 2 weeks later
- Remove and destroy dropped infested nuts

**Pecan Weevil (*Curculio caryae*):**
- Adults emerge from soil in August, feed on nuts, lay eggs inside
- Larvae destroy kernel; infested nuts drop prematurely
- Soil drench with entomopathogenic nematodes (*Steinernema carpocapsae*)
- Ground cloth beneath trees to catch and destroy adults during emergence
- Keep orchard floor clean — debris harbors pupating weevils

**Hickory [[bark-beetle-fungi-ophiostomatales-collection-isolation]] (*Scolytus quadrispinosus*):**
- Attacks stressed or weakened trees
- Maintains tree vigor as primary defense
- Remove and destroy heavily infested wood promptly

**Walnut Husk Fly (*Rhagoletis completa*):**
- Maggots in the husk of butternuts; usually don't reach kernel
- Sticky traps for monitoring
- Spinosad or kaolin clay sprays when adults are active

### Major Diseases

**Butternut Canker (*Ophiognomonia clavigignenti-juglandacearum*):**
- The most serious threat to butternut survival
- Fungal disease causing cankers that girdle branches and trunk
- No reliable cure once established
- Management strategies:
  - Plant disease-free nursery stock
  - Select and propagate apparently resistant trees
  - Remove cankered branches promptly (sterilize tools between cuts)
  - Butternut canker resistance breeding programs exist (USFS, universities)
  - Hybrids with Japanese walnut (*J. ailantifolia*) show resistance ("buartnut")
- If planting butternut, consider buartnut hybrids as a more reliable alternative

**Anthracnose (*Gnomonia spp.*):**
- Causes leaf spot, defoliation, and twig dieback
- More severe in wet, cool springs
- Rake and destroy fallen leaves in autumn
- Copper-based fungicide in severe cases
- Generally not life-threatening

**Leaf Spot (*Mycosphaerella caryigena*):**
- Common on hickories, rarely serious
- Good air circulation and sanitation usually sufficient

**Root Rot (*Armillaria* spp.):**
- Affects stressed trees, especially on poorly drained sites
- Prevention: proper site selection, avoid overwatering, maintain vigor
- No effective treatment once established

### Animal Pests

- **Squirrels** — the primary nut competitor; trunk guards, decoy crops, and controlled hunting help
- **Blue jays** — significant nut consumers; netting individual trees is impractical
- **Deer** — browse young shoots; fencing essential for establishment
- **Mice and voles** — girdle young trunks in winter; tree guards and mowing help
- **Black bears** — can destroy trees to reach nuts in high-bear areas

## Harvesting and Processing

### When to Harvest

- Nuts ripen September–October depending on species and latitude
- Harvest when husks split and nuts begin to drop naturally
- For shagbark: late September to mid-October
- For shellbark: early to mid-October
- For butternut: mid-September to late October
- Quality is highest when gathered promptly — do not leave on ground for weeks
- Squirrels will locate and cache nuts within days of drop

### Processing

**Hulling:**
- Remove husks within 1–2 weeks of harvest
- Husks contain dark staining tannins — wear gloves
- For small quantities, crack husks with a hammer or use a corn sheller
- For larger quantities, use a mechanical nut huller

**Drying and Curing:**
- Spread hulled nuts in a single layer in a dry, well-ventilated area
- Cure for 2–4 weeks at 60–75°F (15–24°C)
- Ideal moisture content: 8–10% for storage
- Stir occasionally for even drying
- Butternuts are higher in oil and benefit from cooler, slower drying
- Do NOT dry in direct sunlight or above 100°F — damages oil quality

**Cracking:**
- Hickory shells are among the hardest of all nut shells
- Standard nutcrackers are usually inadequate
- Use a specialized hickory nutcracker (lever-action) or vice
- 'Weschcke' and other thin-shelled cultivars crack more easily
- Butternut shells are much softer and yield to standard crackers
- A heavy hammer on a solid surface works in a pinch
- Aim to extract whole halves — pieces lose value quickly

**Storage:**
- In-shell: cool (35–45°F / 2–7°C), dry (50–60% RH), dark
- Properly dried nuts store 6–12 months in-shell
- Shelled kernels: refrigerate in airtight containers for up to 6 months
- Freeze shelled kernels for up to 2 years
- High oil content means they go rancid faster than pecans or walnuts
- Vacuum-sealing extends freezer storage to 3+ years

## Culinary Uses

### Hickory Nuts
- **Raw** — sweet, rich flavor eaten out of hand
- **Baking** — substitute for pecans or walnuts in pies, cookies, cakes
- **Hickory nut pie** — a Southern Appalachian specialty, similar to pecan pie
- **Ice cream** — hickory nut ice cream is a prized regional delicacy
- **Maple-hickory brittle** — combine with maple syrup for a quintessential flavor pairing
- **Ground as flour** — adds richness and nutrition to baked goods
- **Hickory syrup** — made from shagbark bark (not nuts), but the flavor profile complements

### Butternuts
- **Raw** — buttery, oily, distinctive flavor
- **Butter** — traditional butternut butter, similar to peanut butter
- **Baking** — excellent in quick breads, muffins, and cookies
- **Candied** — butternut candies are a traditional New England treat
- **Oil** — cold-pressed butternut oil for cooking and finishing
- **Sprinkled on salads** — crushed kernels add richness

## Timber Value

Both hickory and butternut produce valuable timber:

**Hickory:**
- Among the hardest, strongest, toughest of all North American woods
- Janka hardness: 1,820 lbf (shagbark) — harder than white oak
- Primary uses: tool handles, baseball bats, flooring, furniture, firewood
- Hickory firewood: 28 million BTU per cord — among the highest of any North American wood
- Trees with straight trunks and minimal branching are most valuable for timber
- Timber value: $1,000–3,000+ per thousand board feet for veneer-quality logs

**Butternut:**
- Lighter, softer wood with beautiful grain
- Historically used for carving, furniture, paneling, and church altars
- Light brown color with satiny luster
- Less commercially available due to canker disease
- Easier to work than hickory — favored by woodcarvers

## Ecological Value

### Wildlife Habitat
- Nuts are a critical food source for squirrels, chipmunks, turkeys, deer, bear, raccoons, woodpeckers, and many bird species
- Cavities in mature hickories provide nesting sites for birds, bats, and mammals
- Hickory leaves host over 200 species of Lepidoptera (caterpillars) — critical for breeding birds
- Butternut is less wildlife-dependent but still valuable for mast production

### Soil and Ecosystem
- Deep taproots break compacted subsoil and bring up minerals
- [[leaf-litter-microfungal-diversity-tropical-temperate]] contributes significant calcium and other minerals to forest floor
- Hickory leaf litter decomposes relatively slowly, building humus
- Root exudates support diverse mycorrhizal communities
- Cast shade tolerant of understory plantings in food forests

### Juglone Considerations
- Like walnuts, hickories and butternuts produce juglone (a growth inhibitor)
- Juglone is concentrated in roots, nut hulls, and leaves
- Sensitive plants: tomatoes, potatoes, peppers, eggplant, blueberries, azaleas
- Tolerant plants: most grasses, corn, beans, squash, black raspberry, yarrow, elderberry
- Juglone toxicity is much lower in hickories than in black walnut
- Butternut produces moderate juglone levels
- Root-zone competition is typically a bigger issue than juglone toxicity

## Integration in Permaculture Systems

### Food Forest Design
- **Canopy layer:** Use as long-lived overstory trees in food forests
- **Spacing:** 40–50 ft apart in the canopy tier
- **Understory:** Plant juglone-tolerant species beneath (elderberry, pawpaw, currants, serviceberry, comfrey)
- **Nurse trees:** Black locust, alder, or autumn olive can provide nitrogen and wind protection during establishment
- ** succession:** Hickories are climax species — they are the endgame, not the pioneer

### Alley Cropping
- Not ideal for close alley cropping due to [[wide-spacing-dry-gardening-low-irrigation-solomon]] needs
- Can work in wide alleys (50+ ft) with hay, pasture, or grain crops between rows
- Nut harvest logistics are simpler with grass understory than with tall crops

### Silvopasture
- Mature hickories in pasture provide excellent shade and mast for livestock
- Pigs can harvest fallen nuts efficiently ("mast-fed pork")
- Cattle and sheep graze beneath without issue (they don't eat the nuts)
- Manage grazing to protect young trunks from rubbing and browsing

### Agroforestry Yield Timeline

| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| 1–3 | Establishment, minimal growth above ground (root development) |
| 3–10 | Rapid vertical growth begins, 1–3 ft/year |
| 10–15 | First sparse nut production (seedlings) or full production (grafted) |
| 15–25 | Increasing yields, 20–50 lbs nuts/tree |
| 25–50 | Peak production, 50–100+ lbs nuts/tree |
| 50–100+ | Sustained production, some decline possible |
| 100–300+ | Still productive, though individual tree genetics vary |

## Propagation Methods

### Seed Propagation
- Most common for genetic diversity and conservation plantings
- See "[[seed-germination-techniques-solomon]]" section above
- Seedling trees have variable nut quality — some excellent, some poor
- Plant 2–3 seedlings per desired final tree, thin to best performer

### Grafting
- Whip-and-tongue graft in late winter (February–March)
- Bark graft in spring when bark is slipping
- Patch budding in late summer (August)
- Success rate for hickory: 40–70% (improves with experience)
- Butternut: 50–80% graft success
- Use established rootstocks: shagbark, shellbark, or bitternut (vigorous but variable)

### Budding
- Chip budding or T-budding in late summer
- Lower success rate than grafting for hickories
- Useful when scion wood is limited

### Hybridization
- **Hican** — hickory × pecan hybrid; combines hickory hardiness with pecan nut quality
- **Buartnut** — butternut × Japanese walnut hybrid; canker resistant, good nut quality
- **Heartnut × butternut** — cold-hardy with large, easy-crack nuts
- Hybrids are often more adaptable and disease-resistant than pure species
