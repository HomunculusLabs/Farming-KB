# Bauhaus Movement
The Bauhaus was a German school of art, design, craft, and architecture founded in 1919 by [[permaculture-urban-design-apartments-balconies]].
The Bauhaus is often associated with functionalism, geometric clarity, minimal ornament, and modern materials.
It was not merely a style; it was an educational experiment and a social project.
Its central question was how design should serve everyday life in an industrial age.
## Historical Background
The Bauhaus emerged from the crisis and reconstruction that followed the First World War.
Germany's Weimar Republic was politically unstable, economically strained, and culturally experimental.
Artists, architects, and reformers were searching for forms appropriate to a new society.
Gropius merged the Weimar Academy of Fine Art with the Grand Ducal School of Arts and Crafts.
His founding manifesto imagined a new guild of makers united around building as a collective art.
The medieval guild was used as a metaphor for collaboration, not as a rejection of modernity.
From the beginning, the school held a tension between handcraft, spiritual expression, and machine production.
That tension produced much of its creative energy.
## Educational Philosophy
Bauhaus education began with a preliminary course that trained perception before specialization.
Students studied color, material, form, texture, rhythm, balance, and contrast through direct exercises.
Johannes Itten shaped the early course with an expressionist and sometimes mystical approach.
Laszlo Moholy-Nagy later shifted the emphasis toward technology, photography, light, and industrial materials.
Josef Albers developed rigorous exercises in paper, glass, color, and the behavior of materials.
The aim was to teach students how to see relationships rather than copy historical styles.
Workshop practice followed the foundation course and placed students in contact with real materials.
This model influenced foundation programs in art and design schools [[anni-albers]] and other textile designers showed that weaving could be structural, experimental, and modern.
The typography and printing workshop explored sans-serif lettering, grids, asymmetry, and clear visual hierarchy.
The stage workshop, associated with Oskar Schlemmer, investigated movement, costume, space, and the human body.
## Architecture
Architecture was the symbolic center of the Bauhaus even before it became a formal program.
Gropius treated building as the place where many arts and crafts could converge.
The Dessau Bauhaus building, designed by Gropius, embodied the school's ideals in glass, steel, concrete, and functional zoning.
Its curtain walls, workshop wings, bridge, and flat roofs became icons of modern architecture.
Hannes Meyer, the second director, placed stronger emphasis on housing, social need, and measurable function.
Mies van der Rohe, the third director, brought a more disciplined and architecture-centered approach.
Bauhaus architecture helped shape the International Style, but the school itself contained broader experiments than that label suggests.
Its architectural legacy includes open plans, standardized components, efficient circulation, and the rejection of applied ornament.
## Aesthetic Characteristics
Bauhaus design often uses simple forms such as squares, circles, rectangles, planes, cylinders, and grids.
It favors the honest expression of materials and the visible logic of construction.
Objects are typically stripped of historical ornament unless decoration serves structure or communication.
Asymmetry is common, especially in graphic design and page layout.
Typography tends toward clarity, sans-serif lettering, lowercase experiments, and strong alignment.
Color is treated as a system of relationships rather than surface embellishment alone.
Furniture tends to be light, modular, and suitable for serial production.
The style is frequently described as minimal, but its best work is materially and perceptually rich.
## Directors and Key Figures
Walter Gropius provided the founding vision of unity between art, craft, and architecture.
Hannes Meyer redirected the school toward collective needs, affordability, and social function.
Ludwig Mies van der Rohe emphasized architectural order, refinement, and professional discipline.
Paul Klee taught visual thinking, color, and the poetic analysis of form.
Wassily Kandinsky connected abstraction with perception, spirituality, and compositional theory.
Laszlo Moholy-Nagy promoted photography, film, light experiments, and the designer's engagement with technology.
Josef Albers became a central figure in color education and material experimentation.
Marcel Breuer, Anni Albers, Gunta Stolzl, Herbert Bayer, and Oskar Schlemmer extended Bauhaus ideas into major fields of practice.
## Political Conflict and Closure
The Bauhaus was repeatedly attacked by conservative and nationalist political forces.
Its cosmopolitan faculty, experimental culture, and perceived leftist tendencies made it vulnerable.
The school moved from Weimar to Dessau in 1925 after political pressure threatened its support.
It moved again to Berlin in 1932 when the Dessau authorities came under Nazi influence.
In 1933 the faculty closed the school rather than submit to political control.
The closure ended the institution but amplified its international influence.
