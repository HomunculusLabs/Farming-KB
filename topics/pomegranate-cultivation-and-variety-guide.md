# Pomegranate Cultivation and Variety Guide

## Botanical Overview

The pomegranate (*Punica granatum* L.) is a deciduous or semi-evergreen shrub or small tree in the family Lythraceae. It typically grows 3–6 meters (10–20 feet) tall when left unpruned, though commercial orchards maintain trees at 2.5–4 meters for easier harvest. The plant is long-lived, with productive lifespans exceeding 100 years under good management.

### Key Botanical Features

- **Leaves:** Opposite or sub-opposite, glossy, lanceolate, 3–7 cm long. Deciduous in cold climates, semi-evergreen in mild regions.
- **Flowers:** Brilliant scarlet-orange, tubular, 3–4 cm across, appearing on new growth from spring through mid-summer. Most commercial varieties produce self-pollinating hermaphroditic flowers, but some also bear staminate (male) flowers that drop without setting fruit.
- **Fruit:** A large berry (6–12 cm diameter) with a thick, leathery rind (exocarp) enclosing hundreds of arils — juicy, fleshy seed coats that are the edible portion. Each aril contains a single seed.
- **Roots:** Relatively shallow, spreading laterally. Most active feeder roots are in the top 60 cm of soil, making pomegranates somewhat drought-adapted but also sensitive to waterlogging.

### Growth Habit

Pomegranates naturally form a multi-stemmed shrub with a tendency to sucker from the base. Commercial cultivation typically trains them to a single trunk or 2–3 trunk system for mechanical access. Young trees are vigorous; growth rate slows significantly once bearing begins (usually 3–5 years after planting).

## History and Origin

The pomegranate is one of humanity's oldest cultivated fruits. Its center of origin is modern-day Iran and the Caucasus region, where wild relatives still grow. Archaeological evidence places its domestication as early as 3000–4000 BCE in the Near East.

From Persia, cultivation spread to:
- **India** by ~1000 BCE, where it became deeply embedded in Ayurvedic medicine and Hindu tradition
- **The Mediterranean** via Phoenician traders (the genus name *Punica* derives from the Phoenicians)
- **North Africa** and **Southern Europe** through Greek and Roman expansion
- **The Americas** with Spanish missionaries in the 16th century — the variety 'Wonderful' was discovered as a chance seedling in Florida around 1896

Cultural significance spans millennia: pomegranates appear in Egyptian tombs, Greek mythology (the myth of Persephone), Jewish tradition (as one of the Seven Species' associated fruits), Islamic art, and Armenian symbolism.

## Climate Requirements

### Temperature

Pomegranates are remarkably heat-tolerant and moderately cold-hardy:

| Parameter | Range |
|-----------|-------|
| Optimal growing temperature | 25–35°C (77–95°F) |
| Heat tolerance | Up to 45°C (113°F) with adequate irrigation |
| Cold hardiness (dormant) | Down to -12°C (10°F) for short periods |
| Severe damage threshold | Below -15°C (5°F) kills above-ground wood |
| Rootstock survival | Roots may survive to -18°C (0°F) and resprout |
| Chilling requirement | 100–200 chill hours (low, suitable for mild winters) |

### Rainfall

- **Ideal annual rainfall:** 500–800 mm (20–32 inches), concentrated in winter/spring
- **Drought tolerance:** Established trees survive prolonged dry periods by dropping leaves and going dormant; fruit quality suffers under severe drought
- **Excess humidity:** The primary limitation — sustained humidity above 70% during flowering promotes fungal problems (Alternaria fruit rot, Botrytis) and reduces fruit set

### Sunlight

Full sun is essential. Pomegranates need a minimum of 6–8 hours of direct sunlight for optimal flowering and fruit production. Shade significantly reduces yield and increases susceptibility to disease.

## Soil Requirements

Pomegranates are notably adaptable to poor soils, a trait that has made them valuable in marginal agricultural regions worldwide.

### Preferred Conditions

- **Soil texture:** Sandy loam to loam is ideal, but they tolerate clay, sandy, and rocky soils
- **pH range:** 5.5–7.2 (slightly acidic to neutral); tolerate moderately alkaline soils up to pH 8.0
- **Drainage:** Good drainage is critical. Trees in waterlogged soils develop root rot and decline rapidly
- **Salinity tolerance:** Moderate — pomegranates tolerate EC (electrical conductivity) up to 5–7 dS/m, making them suitable for mildly saline irrigation water

### Soil Preparation

Before planting, incorporate:
- Well-rotted compost or aged manure at 5–10 kg per planting hole
- Phosphorus (rock phosphate or bone meal) for root establishment
- Avoid excessive nitrogen at planting, which promotes vegetative growth over root development

For heavy clay soils, consider raised beds or mounds 30–60 cm high to ensure drainage.

## Planting and Site Selection

### Site Selection Criteria

1. **Frost avoidance:** Plant on elevated sites or upper slopes where cold air drains away; avoid low frost pockets
2. **Wind protection:** Young trees benefit from windbreaks; hot dry winds (Santa Ana conditions) can desiccate flowers and reduce fruit set
3. **Full sun exposure:** Southern or western exposure in cooler climates
4. **Air circulation:** In humid climates, good airflow reduces disease pressure
5. **Water access:** While drought-tolerant, consistent irrigation during fruit development is essential for commercial quality

### Planting Procedure

**Timing:** Plant bare-root trees in late winter (dormant season). Container-grown trees can be planted year-round in mild climates but establish best when planted in early spring.

**Spacing:**
- Standard orchard: 4–5 meters between trees (12–16 feet), 5–6 meters between rows (16–20 feet) = ~200–400 trees/ha
- Dense hedgerow: 2–3 meters in-row for machine harvesting (some Israeli systems)
- Home garden: 3–4 meters apart

**Steps:**
1. Dig a hole 60 cm wide × 60 cm deep (twice the root ball width)
2. Loosen the sides and bottom of the hole to prevent root circling
3. If the soil is very poor, mix in compost — but keep the backfill similar to native soil to encourage roots to explore outward
4. Place the tree at the same depth it was in the nursery (the graft union, if present, should be 5–10 cm above soil level)
5. Backfill, firm gently, and water thoroughly
6. Create a watering basin 60–90 cm in diameter
7. Apply 5–10 cm of organic mulch, keeping it 10 cm away from the trunk
8. Stake if the site is windy; remove stakes after 1–2 years

## Varieties

Pomegranate varieties differ primarily in fruit size, aril color, juice quality, seed hardness (soft vs. hard), skin thickness, and climatic adaptation. Hundreds of named varieties exist globally; the following are the most significant for cultivation.

### Major Commercial and Garden Varieties

#### Wonderful

The dominant commercial variety worldwide, discovered in Florida around 1896 and commercialized in California by the 1920s.

- **Fruit:** Large (400–700 g), globular to slightly elongated; deep crimson skin
- **Arils:** Deep crimson, excellent juice color, moderate to high acidity, good sugar-acid balance
- **Seeds:** Hard; not ideal for fresh-eating
- **Tree:** Vigorous, upright, productive, reliable
- **Ripening:** Late season (October–November in Northern Hemisphere)
- **Cold hardiness:** Moderate
- **Best for:** Juice, commercial production, arils for food service

#### Angel Red

A newer selection known for extremely early maturity and soft seeds.

- **Fruit:** Medium-large, bright red skin
- **Arils:** Bright red, soft seeds, sweet with low acidity
- **Ripening:** Very early (late August–September)
- **Best for:** Fresh eating, early market, home gardens where soft seeds are preferred

#### Eversweet

As the name suggests, very sweet with minimal acidity.

- **Fruit:** Medium, pink-red skin
- **Arils:** Pink, very sweet (Brix 17–19%), virtually no tartness, soft seeds
- **Tree:** Deciduous, somewhat less vigorous than Wonderful
- **Ripening:** Early to mid-season
- **Best for:** Fresh eating, people sensitive to acidity, children

#### Parfianka

A Central Asian variety widely considered one of the finest-flavored pomegranates.

- **Fruit:** Medium-large, pink-red skin
- **Arils:** Large, crimson, exceptionally sweet with complex flavor notes, soft seeds
- **Tree:** Moderate vigor, spreading habit
- **Ripening:** Mid-season (September–October)
- **Cold hardiness:** Good
- **Best for:** Premium fresh market, home gardens, connoisseur eating quality

#### Ambrosia

A chance seedling discovered in California, noted for exceptionally large fruit.

- **Fruit:** Very large (up to 1 kg), pink skin with red blush
- **Arils:** Pink, sweet, soft seeds, juicy
- **Tree:** Vigorous, spreading
- **Ripening:** Mid-season
- **Best for:** Home gardens, farmers markets (novelty of large fruit)

#### Sienevyi (Sin-e-pe-1)

A Russian/Central Asian variety valued for cold hardiness.

- **Fruit:** Medium, orange-red skin
- **Arils:** Red-pink, sweet, soft seeds
- **Tree:** Very cold-hardy (survives -15°C), moderately vigorous
- **Ripening:** Mid-season
- **Best for:** Cold climate cultivation, fresh eating

#### Salavatski

Another Russian selection, extremely cold-hardy.

- **Fruit:** Medium-large, red-orange skin
- **Arils:** Red, sweet-tart balance, medium-soft seeds
- **Tree:** Exceptionally cold-hardy, vigorous
- **Ripening:** Mid to late season
- **Best for:** Cold climates (USDA Zone 6b with protection), reliable production in marginal areas

#### Nana (Dwarf Pomegranate)

A compact ornamental variety that also produces edible fruit.

- **Fruit:** Small (5–7 cm), red skin
- **Arils:** Pink-red, tart, edible but small
- **Tree:** Very compact (1–1.5 m), suitable for containers and bonsai
- **Best for:** Container growing, ornamental, bonsai, very small gardens,patio culture

#### Kazake

A Central Asian variety with an excellent balance of traits.

- **Fruit:** Medium-large, red skin
- **Arils:** Dark red, sweet with good acidity, soft seeds
- **Tree:** Cold-hardy, moderately vigorous
- **Ripening:** Mid-season
- **Best for:** Fresh eating, cold-climate gardens

#### Sharp Velvet

An Israeli commercial variety valued for juice production.

- **Fruit:** Medium-large, deep red skin
- **Arils:** Dark red, high anthocyanin content, good sugar-acid balance for juice
- **Tree:** Vigorous, productive
- **Ripening:** Mid-season
- **Best for:** Juice, industrial processing

### Variety Selection by Climate

| Climate Zone | Recommended Varieties |
|-------------|----------------------|
| Mediterranean (USDA 9–10) | Wonderful, Parfianka, Ambrosia, Eversweet, Sharp Velvet |
| Hot Arid (USDA 8–10) | Wonderful, Angel Red, Eversweet, Salavatski |
| Subtropical humid (USDA 8–9) | Eversweet, Parfianka (disease-resistant selections) |
| Temperate (USDA 7–8) | Sienevyi, Salavatski, Kazake, Nana |

| Container/patio | Nana, Eversweet, Angel Red |

## Irrigation and Fertilization

### Irrigation

Pomegranates are drought-deciduous — they can survive extended drought by dropping leaves — but commercial fruit quality requires consistent moisture management.

**Critical irrigation periods:**
1. **Spring growth and flowering:** Moderate water to support canopy development and flower formation
2. **Fruit set (post-bloom):** Consistent moisture prevents fruit drop; water stress at this stage causes significant yield loss
3. **Fruit development (summer):** Regular deep watering every 7–14 days depending on soil and temperature
4. **Fruit maturation (late summer–fall):** Reduce irrigation slightly to concentrate sugars, but don't stress severely (causes fruit cracking)

**Irrigation methods:**
- **Drip irrigation:** Most efficient; ideal for orchards. Place two emitters per tree, 30 cm from trunk, moving outward as tree grows
- **Micro-sprinklers:** Good for young orchards; coverage is broader
- **Basin/furrow:** Traditional method; works but less water-efficient
- **Mature tree water needs:** Approximately 25–50 mm per week during peak demand

**Signs of overwatering:** Yellowing leaves, root rot, increased susceptibility to Phytophthora, fruit splitting
**Signs of underwatering:** Leaf curling and drop, sunburn on exposed fruit, reduced fruit size, premature fruit drop

### Fertilization

Pomegranates are moderate feeders. Excessive nitrogen promotes vegetative growth at the expense of fruit production and can delay fruit maturity.

**Annual nutrient guidelines (per mature tree):**
- **Nitrogen:** 200–400 g actual N (avoid exceeding this; excess N causes soft fruit and delayed coloring)
- **Phosphorus:** 50–100 g P₂O₅ (primarily needed at planting and early years)
- **Potassium:** 200–400 g K₂O (important for fruit quality, sugar content, and tree hardiness)
- **Micronutrients:** Zinc (foliar spray in spring if deficiency symptoms appear), iron (chelated iron for alkaline soils), boron (important for fruit set)

**Application timing:**
- Apply nitrogen in two split applications: 50% at bud break, 50% after fruit set
- Potassium can be applied in a single application in early spring or split with nitrogen
- Foliar micronutrient sprays at 10% and 50% bloom

**Organic approach:**
- Well-rotted compost (10–20 kg per mature tree) applied as a surface mulch in spring
- Composted poultry manure (2–4 kg per tree) in early spring
- Kelp meal or seaweed extract for potassium and micronutrients
- Avoid high-nitrogen composts (fresh manure, grass clippings) during fruit development

## Pruning and Training

### Training Young Trees

The first 3 years establish the permanent framework. Two common systems:

**Single trunk (standard):**
- Select one strong central leader
- Remove all suckers from the base
- Scaffold branches at 60–90 cm above ground; select 3–5 well-spaced branches
- Remove competing upright shoots

**Multi-trunk (bush):**
- Allow 3–5 trunks to develop from the base
- Remove additional suckers annually
- More natural form, better cold recovery (if one trunk is killed, others survive)

### Annual Pruning

Pomegranates flower and fruit primarily on current season's growth and short spurs on 2–3 year old wood.

**When to prune:** Late winter (dormant season) before bud break. In mild climates, prune in January–February.

**Objectives:**
1. Remove dead, damaged, and diseased wood
2. Remove suckers from the base (unless maintaining a multi-trunk form)
3. Remove crossing branches and inward-growing shoots
4. Thin the canopy to allow light penetration and air circulation
5. Remove some older wood (3+ years) to encourage renewal growth
6. Maintain the desired shape (vase or modified central leader)

**Key principles:**
- Don't over-prune — heavy pruning reduces yield and delays maturity
- Keep the center of the tree open for light and air
- Retain fruiting spurs on older wood; they're productive for 3–4 years
- Shorten excessively long shoots to encourage branching
- Remove water sprouts (vigorous vertical shoots from the base) unless needed for renewal

### Rejuvenation Pruning

Old, neglected trees can be rejuvenated by:
- Removing 1–2 of the oldest trunks per year over 3–4 years
- Cutting back remaining trunks to strong lateral branches
- This restores productivity without the severe yield loss of a single drastic cut

## Pest and Disease Management

### Insect Pests

#### Pomegranate Butterfly (Virachola isocrates)
A major pest in India and the Middle East. Larvae bore into the fruit and feed on arils.
- **Management:** Remove and destroy infested fruit, bag young fruits, apply *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) or spinosad-based sprays

#### Leaf-footed Plant Bug (Leptoglossus clypealis)
Adults and nymphs feed on developing fruit, causing dark spots and premature drop. Common in California and the southern US.
- **Management:** Remove overwintering sites (debris, wood piles), shake branches onto drop cloths and destroy bugs, insecticidal soap for nymphs, kaolin clay as a deterrent

#### Aphids (Various species)
Cluster on new growth, excrete honeydew that promotes sooty mold.
- **Management:** Usually controlled by natural predators (lady beetles, lacewings). Strong water spray, insecticidal soap, or neem oil if infestation is severe.

#### Whitefly
Sap-feeding insects that weaken plants and transmit viruses.
- **Management:** Yellow sticky traps, encourage natural predators (Encarsia wasps), neem oil

#### Thrips
Feed on flowers, reducing fruit set. More problematic in humid conditions.
- **Management:** Blue sticky traps, reflective mulch, predatory mites

#### Ants
Don't directly damage fruit but farm aphids and other sap-feeding insects.
- **Management:** Tanglefoot barrier on trunk, boric acid baits

### Diseases

#### Alternaria Fruit Rot (Alternaria alternata)
The most significant disease in humid growing regions. Causes dark brown to black lesions on the fruit surface, often entering at the calyx.
- **Management:** Good air circulation (pruning), avoid overhead irrigation, remove fallen debris, pre-harvest fungicide sprays (copper-based or biological agents like *Bacillus subtilis*), resistant varieties

#### Botrytis (Gray Mold)
Affects flowers and developing fruit, especially in cool, damp conditions.
- **Management:** Improve air circulation, remove infected material, avoid excess nitrogen

#### Cercospora Leaf Spot
Causes circular spots on leaves, premature defoliation.
- **Management:** Remove infected leaves, copper sprays, avoid overhead irrigation

#### Phytophthora Root Rot
Devastating in poorly drained soils. Causes sudden wilt and death.
- **Management:** Ensure excellent drainage, avoid overwatering, resistant rootstocks, improve soil biology with compost and mycorrhizal inoculants

#### Xanthomonas axonopodis (Bacterial Blight)
Causes lesions on leaves, flowers, and fruit. Spreads by rain splash.
- **Management:** Copper-based sprays, remove infected material, avoid working in wet conditions

### Integrated Pest Management Approach

The most sustainable approach combines:
1. **Prevention:** Proper site selection, good drainage, adequate spacing, open canopy pruning
2. **Biological control:** Encourage or introduce beneficial insects (lady beetles, lacewings, predatory mites, parasitic wasps)
3. **Cultural practices:** Sanitation (remove fallen fruit and debris), weed management, appropriate irrigation
4. **Monitoring:** Regular inspection of flowers, developing fruit, and leaves
5. **Least-toxic interventions:** Insecticidal soap, neem oil, kaolin clay, Bt, copper before synthetic chemicals
6. **Resistant varieties:** Select varieties with known disease tolerance for your climate

## Pollination

Most commercial pomegranate varieties are self-fertile and self-pollinating, producing viable fruit from a single tree. This makes them excellent choices for home gardens where space is limited.

### Pollination Biology

- Flowers are hermaphroditic (perfect) with both male and female organs
- Some varieties produce a proportion of functionally male (staminate) flowers that do not set fruit
- Primary pollinators: Bees (honey bees, bumble bees), hummingbirds, and other nectar-feeding insects
- Wind plays a minor role

### Cross-Pollination Benefits

While not required, cross-pollination between different varieties can:
- Increase fruit set percentage
- Produce larger fruit with more arils
- Improve aril weight and juice content
- Enhance seed viability (important if saving seed)

### Enhancing Pollination

- Plant 2–3 varieties with overlapping bloom periods
- Avoid insecticide application during flowering
- Provide habitat for native pollinators (flowering understory plants, undisturbed soil for ground-nesting bees)
- Place honey bee hives in or near the orchard (2–4 hives per hectare for commercial operations)

## Harvesting and Storage

### Determining Maturity

Pomegranates do not ripen significantly after harvest, so correct harvest timing is critical.

**Indicators of maturity:**
- **Color:** Skin develops full characteristic color (variety-dependent — not all turn fully red)
- **Sound:** Tap the fruit — a metallic, hollow sound indicates ripeness
- **Shape:** Fruit becomes more angular as arils swell and press against the rind
- **Aril color:** Cut open a sample fruit; arils should be fully colored (deep red, pink, or white depending on variety)
- **Brix:** Minimum soluble solids of 14–17% depending on variety and market
- **Acidity:** Titratable acidity should be below 1.5% for sweet varieties

### Harvesting

- **Timing:** Generally 150–180 days from full bloom
- **Method:** Clip fruit from the tree with sharp pruners or a knife, leaving a short stem. Do not pull — this damages the fruit and the branch
- **Handle carefully:** Pomegranates bruise easily, and damaged fruit rots quickly
- **Wear gloves:** The juice stains permanently

### Storage

Pomegranates have exceptional post-harvest longevity compared to most fruits.

- **Room temperature:** 1–2 weeks
- **Refrigerated (5–7°C, 90–95% RH):** 2–3 months
- **Cold storage with modified atmosphere:** Up to 5–7 months (commercial operations)
- **Frozen arils:** 6–12 months (spread on a tray to freeze individually, then bag)

For best flavor, consume or process within 1–2 months of harvest. Prolonged cold storage gradually reduces aril juiciness and flavor intensity.

## Propagation Methods

### Seed Propagation

Simple but rarely used for commercial production because seedlings are variable and may not inherit desirable parent traits. Useful for breeding programs and rootstock production.

- Extract seeds from ripe fruit, wash off aril material
- Stratify at 1–5°C for 60–90 days to break dormancy
- Sow 1 cm deep in well-draining mix
- Germination in 30–60 days at 20–25°C
- Seedlings bear fruit in 3–5 years

### Hardwood Cuttings

The most common propagation method for commercial orchards. Produces true-to-type clones.

- **Timing:** Late winter (dormant season)
- **Selection:** Healthy, vigorous 1-year-old wood, pencil-thickness (6–10 mm diameter), 25–40 cm long with 3–5 buds
- **Preparation:** Cut base at a 45° angle, top straight. Dip base in rooting hormone (IBA 1000–3000 ppm)
- **Planting:** Bury 2/3 of the cutting in moist, well-draining propagation mix (sand:perlite:peat, 1:1:1)
- **Environment:** Bottom heat at 20–25°C, keep humidity high (mist system or covered frame)
- **Rooting:** 4–8 weeks; transplant to liner pots once rooted
- **Field-ready:** 12–18 months from cutting

### Softwood Cuttings

Used during the growing season for faster results but lower success rates.

- Take 10–15 cm cuttings from actively growing tips in late spring
- Remove lower leaves, dip in rooting hormone
- Root under intermittent mist with bottom heat
- Transplant when rooted (4–6 weeks)

### Air Layering

Effective but slow; useful for preserving specific parent trees.

- Select a 1–2 year old branch
- Girdle a 2–3 cm ring of bark
- Apply rooting hormone to the girdled area
- Wrap with moist sphagnum moss and plastic
- Roots form in 6–12 weeks
- Sever and pot once well-rooted

### Tissue Culture

Used commercially in some regions for mass propagation of selected varieties. Produces disease-free, uniform planting material.

## Companion Planting

### Beneficial Companions

Pomegranates integrate well into food forests and polyculture systems:

- **Legumes:** Alfalfa, clover, or subterranean clover as understory ground cover — fix nitrogen and attract pollinators
- **Herbs:** Rosemary, lavender, thyme — attract pollinators, deter pests with aromatic oils, share Mediterranean climate preferences
- **Alliums:** Garlic, chives, leeks — deter aphids and some borers
- **Comfrey:** Deep-rooted dynamic accumulator; chop-and-drop mulch provides potassium
- **Yarrow:** Attracts predatory insects (syrphid flies, parasitic wasps)
- **Dill, fennel, coriander:** Umbelliferous flowers attract beneficial insects
- **Nasturtium:** Trap crop for aphids

### Plants to Avoid Near Pomegranates

- **Walnuts:** Juglone from walnut roots is allelopathic to many plants, including pomegranates
- **Heavy shade trees:** Pomegranates need full sun
- **Invasive runners:** Mint, bamboo — compete for water and nutrients

## Pomegranate in Permaculture and Food Forests

### Design Functions

In permaculture design, pomegranates serve multiple functions:

1. **Food production:** High-value fruit with excellent shelf life
2. **Ornamental value:** Showy flowers, attractive form, autumn fruit display
3. **Windbreak:** Dense, thorny growth makes an effective barrier hedge
4. **Wildlife habitat:** Birds and small mammals eat fallen fruit; flowers attract pollinators
5. **Erosion control:** Spreading root systems stabilize soil on slopes
6. **Fire resistance:** High moisture content in leaves makes pomegranates moderately fire-resistant

### Food Forest Placement

- **Layer:** Understory tree / canopy edge (Zone 2–3 in permaculture zone design)
- **Position:** Full sun at the edge of the canopy layer, with smaller shrubs, herbs, and ground covers beneath
- **Guild members:** Leguminous ground covers, aromatic herbs, dynamic accumulators, pollinator plants
- **Succession:** Long-lived, stable element; plant early in food forest establishment

### Multi-Use Considerations

Beyond fruit, pomegranates provide:
- **Dye:** The rind produces yellow to red-brown dye
- **Medicine:** Rind is rich in ellagitannins (punicalagin); traditional uses include treatment of diarrhea, sore throat, and parasites
- **Cosmetics:** Pomegranate seed oil is used in skincare for its antioxidant properties
- **Tannin:** Rind and bark are a source of tannin for leather working
- **Timber:** Very hard, dense wood (useful for tool handles, walking sticks)

The primary bioactive compounds are **punicalagins** (hydrolyzable tannins in the rind and membrane) and **anthocyanins** (pigments in the arils, primarily cyanidin, delphinidin, and pelargonidin glucosides). Pomegranate juice has one of the highest polyphenol contents of any fruit juice.

### Culinary Uses

- **Fresh arils:** Eaten raw, added to salads, yogurt, oatmeal, desserts
- **Juice:** Fresh-pressed or commercial; blends well with other juices
- **Pomegranate molasses (dibs rumman):** Reduced juice concentrate used throughout Middle Eastern cuisine (in muhammara, fattoush, marinades)
- **Grenadine syrup:** Traditionally made from pomegranate juice (modern versions often use artificial flavors)
- **Seeds:** Dried and used as a spice (anardana) in Indian and Pakistani cooking
- **Rind:** Dried and powdered for medicinal use, or boiled for traditional remedies
- **Desserts:** Used in Turkish asure, Persian fesenjan (walnut-pomegranate stew), and numerous Middle Eastern confections
