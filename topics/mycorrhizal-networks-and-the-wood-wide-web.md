## Mycorrhizal Associations: Types and Biology

Mycorrhizae are mutualistic symbiotic associations between soil fungi and plant roots. The word derives from the Greek *mykes* (fungus) and *rhiza* (root). Approximately 80–90% of all terrestrial plant species form mycorrhizal associations, making this one of the most widespread symbioses on Earth.

### Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF)

**Order:** Glomeromycota | **Associates:** ~70% of all plant species, including most agricultural crops, grasses, and many tropical trees.

AMF are the oldest and most widespread mycorrhizal type, with fossil evidence dating back 460 million years. They penetrate root cortical cells and form highly branched structures called **arbuscules**, which are the primary sites of nutrient exchange. Key features:

- **Hyphal networks** extend 10–25 cm from roots, dramatically increasing the absorptive surface area by 10- to 100-fold
- **Intra-radical hyphae** spread between root cortical cells as intercellular hyphae
- **Vesicles** serve as lipid storage organs in many species
- **No host specificity** — individual AMF species can colonize dozens to hundreds of plant species simultaneously, making them the primary architects of common mycorrhizal networks in grasslands and agricultural systems
- **Obligate biotrophs** — cannot be cultured without a living plant host

Major genera include *Rhizoglomus*, *Funneliformis*, *Gigaspora*, *Scutellospora*, and *Acaulospora*. AMF are particularly important for phosphorus uptake, as their thin hyphae (2–5 μm diameter) can access soil pores too small for root hairs.

### Ectomycorrhizal Fungi (ECM)

**Associates:** ~2% of plant species, predominantly temperate and boreal forest trees (Pinaceae, Fagaceae, Betulaceae, Salicaceae, Myrtaceae, Dipterocarpaceae).

ECM fungi are the dominant mycorrhizal type in temperate and boreal forests. Unlike AMF, they do not penetrate root cells. Instead, they form:

- **Hartig net:** A dense hyphal mesh between root cortical cells — the primary exchange interface
- **Mantle:** A sheath of fungal tissue completely encasing the root tip, which can be 20–100 μm thick
- **Extrametrical mycelium:** Extensive external hyphae and rhizomorphs (cord-like aggregates) that extend meters into the soil

ECM fungi are typically more host-specific than AMF, with many species associating with only one genus or family of trees. This specificity contributes to the formation of distinct fungal networks around particular tree species. Major genera include *Amanita*, *Boletus*, *Lactarius*, *Russula*, *Suillus*, *Cenococcum*, and *Pisolithus*.

ECM networks are particularly important for nitrogen cycling in forest ecosystems. Many ECM fungi produce extracellular enzymes (proteases, peroxidases) that break down organic matter, releasing nitrogen that trees can absorb.
