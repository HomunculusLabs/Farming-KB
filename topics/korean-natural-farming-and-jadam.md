# Korean Natural Farming and JADAM

Korean Natural Farming (KNF) and JADAM are two closely related but distinct 
systems of natural agriculture that originated in South Korea in the latter 
half of the twentieth century. Both systems share a fundamental philosophy of 
working with nature rather than against it, using locally available materials 
and indigenous microorganisms to maintain soil health, plant nutrition, and 
pest management without reliance on synthetic chemical inputs. Together, they 
represent one of the most developed and widely practiced systems of natural 
agriculture in East Asia, with growing influence and adoption across the 
Americas, Africa, Europe, and Southeast Asia.

Korean Natural Farming was developed by Master Cho Han-kyu (1935 to 2019) 
beginning in the 1960s and refined over five decades of practice and teaching. 
Cho was a farmer and agricultural researcher who observed that conventional 
chemical farming degraded soil health and created dependency on expensive 
external inputs. Drawing on traditional Korean farming wisdom, the work of 
Japanese natural farming advocate Masanobu Fukuoka, and his own experiments 
with indigenous microorganisms, Cho developed a comprehensive system of farming 
inputs and management practices that he called Korean Natural Farming. The 
system is built around the collection and cultivation of indigenous 
microorganisms (IMOs) and their use in creating a suite of fermented natural 
inputs that replace synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and soil amendments.

JADAM was founded by Cho Young-sang, the son of Master Cho Han-kyu, as a 
further evolution and simplification of Korean Natural Farming. The name JADAM 
is a Korean word meaning "people who resemble nature." Young-sang Cho 
recognized that while KNF was effective, it still required the purchase of some 
materials, such as brown sugar and specific mineral inputs, which could be a 
barrier for very low-income farmers. He developed the JADAM system to take 
natural farming to its logical conclusion: a farming method that requires zero 
external purchases, using only materials that can be found on or near the farm. 
JADAM is sometimes described as "ultra-low-cost agriculture" and has gained a 
devoted following among smallholder farmers in Korea and around the world.

## Core Philosophy

The philosophical foundation of both KNF and JADAM rests on several 
interconnected principles. The first is the primacy of indigenous 
microorganisms. Rather than importing microbial products or laboratory-cultured 
strains, both systems rely on microorganisms that are native to the local 
environment. These indigenous microbes are already adapted to local soil 
conditions, climate, and crop species, and they are believed to be more 
effective and resilient than introduced strains. The farmer's role is to create 
conditions that favor beneficial indigenous microorganisms and to actively 
collect, cultivate, and multiply them for use as agricultural inputs.

The second principle is zero external inputs. In KNF, this is approached 
through the production of all necessary inputs from locally available 
materials: rice, brown sugar, fruits, fish, eggshells, and plant materials. In 
JADAM, the commitment is even more absolute: every input must be producible on 
the farm without purchasing anything from outside. JADAM practitioners use 
materials like indigenous microorganisms collected from the farm, plant 
extracts made from weeds growing on the farm, and mineral solutions derived 
from wood ash collected on the farm. This approach dramatically reduces farming 
costs and eliminates dependency on agricultural supply chains.

The third principle is the use of fermentation rather than chemical synthesis. 
Both KNF and JADAM rely heavily on lactic acid bacteria, yeast, and other 
fermentative microorganisms to transform raw materials into biologically active 
inputs. Fermentation preserves nutrients, produces beneficial metabolites, and 
creates products that are readily available for plant uptake. The fourth 
principle is the minimization of soil disturbance. Both systems discourage or 
prohibit tillage, recognizing that tilling damages soil structure, disrupts 
fungal networks, and releases stored carbon. Instead, they favor mulching, 
cover cropping, and surface application of inputs to build soil organic matter 
and biological activity over time.

The influence of Masanobu Fukuoka's philosophy of "do-nothing farming" is 
evident in both systems. Fukuoka's four principles of natural farming, which 
include no cultivation, no chemical fertilizer, no weeding by tillage or 
herbicides, and no dependence on chemicals, provided a philosophical framework 
that Cho Han-kyu adapted and expanded with his specific techniques for 
microbial cultivation and input production. However, KNF and JADAM are more 
interventionist than Fukuoka's approach, with a much more developed toolkit of 
specific inputs and management practices.

## Indigenous Microorganisms (IMO 1 through 4)

The collection and cultivation of indigenous microorganisms is the cornerstone 
of Korean Natural Farming. The IMO process is a multi-stage protocol that 
begins with the collection of wild microbes from the farm environment and 
culminates in the production of a rich, biologically diverse inoculant that can 
be applied to soil, compost, or plant surfaces.

IMO 1 is the initial collection of indigenous microorganisms from a natural 
environment. The standard method involves placing a wooden box, approximately 
30 by 40 by 10 centimeters, filled with steamed rice, in a location with good 
biological activity on or near the farm. Forest edges, bamboo groves, grassy 
fields, and the base of old fruit trees are preferred collection sites. The box 
is loosely covered to protect it from rain and animals while allowing air 
exchange. After 5 to 7 days, depending on temperature and moisture, the rice 
will be colonized by a diverse community of microorganisms. A successful IMO 1 
collection shows visible growth of white, gray, yellow, or other colored fungal 
mycelium on the rice surface. Colors other than white or gray, particularly 
black, green, or dark red, may indicate contamination by undesirable organisms 
and the collection should be discarded.

IMO 2 is the first stage of cultivation. The colonized rice from IMO 1 is mixed 
with an equal weight of brown sugar and packed into a container. The sugar acts 
as a preservative, drawing moisture out of the rice through osmotic pressure 
and creating a liquid that is rich in microbial metabolites and cells. The 
container is covered loosely and allowed to ferment for 5 to 7 days. During 
this time, the sugar draws out the liquid, producing a thick syrup that is rich 
in beneficial bacteria, yeast, and fungal propagules. This IMO 2 liquid can be 
stored for extended periods and serves as the base material for the next stage.

IMO 3 builds upon IMO 2 by providing additional substrate for microbial growth. 
A mixture of steamed rice, approximately 70 percent of the total volume, is 
combined with the IMO 2 liquid and a carbon-rich material such as sawdust, rice 
bran, or wheat bran, approximately 30 percent of the total volume. The moisture 
content is adjusted to approximately 60 to 65 percent, similar to a wrung-out 
sponge. This mixture is placed in a container, covered with breathable material 
such as newspaper or burlap, and allowed to ferment for 5 to 7 days at ambient 
temperature. The resulting material is a living inoculant teeming with diverse 
microorganisms and can be used directly as a soil amendment or carried forward 
to the next stage.

IMO 4 is the final stage of production and is designed for field application. 
The IMO 3 material is mixed with a larger volume of carbon-rich material, 
typically a 1:10 ratio of IMO 3 to a mixture of garden soil and leaf mold or 
compost. This is spread in a layer approximately 3 centimeters thick in a 
shaded, well-ventilated area and covered with breathable material. It is turned 
daily and maintained at appropriate moisture levels for 7 to 14 days. The 
finished IMO 4 has a sweet, earthy smell and is rich in beneficial 
microorganisms. It can be applied to soil as a top dressing, mixed into 
compost, or incorporated into potting mixes at rates of approximately 1 to 2 
kilograms per 10 square meters.

## Key KNF Inputs

Beyond the IMO series, Korean Natural Farming employs a diverse toolkit of 
fermented natural inputs, each designed for a specific purpose in crop 
production. These inputs are made from simple, locally available ingredients 
using straightforward fermentation techniques.

Fermented Plant Juice (FPJ) is made from the rapidly growing tips of plants 
during their active growth phase. The plant material, typically young leaves, 
shoots, or buds, is collected in the early morning when sugar and nutrient 
concentrations are highest. The material is chopped and mixed with an equal 
weight of brown sugar, packed into a container, and allowed to ferment for 5 to 
7 days. The sugar extracts the plant's sap, producing a concentrated liquid 
rich in plant growth hormones, enzymes, organic acids, and minerals. Different 
plants produce FPJ with different properties: mugwort FPJ is used for its 
mineral content, bamboo shoot FPJ for its silica content, and sweet potato vine 
FPJ for its potassium content. FPJ is typically diluted at a rate of 1:500 to 
1:1000 and applied as a foliar spray or soil drench during periods of active 
plant growth.

Fermented Fruit Juice (FFJ) is similar to FPJ but uses ripe fruits as the 
starting material. Soft fruits such as bananas, mangoes, persimmons, and 
strawberries are preferred because their high sugar and nutrient content 
produces a rich fermentation product. The fruit is mashed, mixed with an equal 
weight of brown sugar, and fermented for 5 to 7 days. FFJ is particularly rich 
in sugars, organic acids, and plant growth compounds that enhance fruit 
quality, sweetness, and disease resistance. It is applied during the flowering 
and fruiting stages of crop development.

Fish Amino Acid (FAA) is a nitrogen-rich liquid fertilizer made from fish waste 
or whole small fish. Fish material is mixed with brown sugar in a roughly 1:1 
ratio by weight and allowed to ferment for several weeks to months. The sugar 
preserves the fish and prevents putrefaction while allowing proteolytic 
bacteria and enzymes to break down proteins into amino acids and peptides. FAA 
provides a rapidly available source of nitrogen and minerals, making it a 
powerful input for promoting vegetative growth. It is applied at dilutions of 
1:500 to 1:1000, typically during the vegetative growth stage. Due to its 
strong odor, FAA is usually applied as a soil drench rather than a foliar spray.

Lactic Acid Bacteria (LAB) is produced by collecting naturally occurring lactic 
acid bacteria from the environment, typically from rice wash water. Rice is 
washed and the milky rinse water is collected and left at room temperature for 
2 to 3 days until it develops a slightly sour smell, indicating lactic acid 
bacterial growth. This rice wash water is then mixed with milk at a ratio of 
approximately 10:1 rice wash to milk and left for 5 to 7 days. The lactic acid 
bacteria ferment the milk sugars, causing the milk proteins to curdle and 
separate. The yellowish liquid that forms above the curd is the LAB serum, 
which is strained and can be stored. LAB is used to suppress pathogenic 
microorganisms, enhance soil biology, and improve compost quality. It is 
applied at dilutions of 1:1000 to 1:5000.

Oriental Herbal Nutrient (OHN) is a broad-spectrum natural pesticide and plant 
tonic made from a combination of oriental herbs. The traditional recipe uses 
garlic, ginger, licorice, cinnamon, and angelica, though variations exist. Each 
herb is prepared individually by steeping it in alcohol (typically soju or 
vodka) for varying periods, then the individual extracts are combined and 
fermented together with brown sugar. OHN is used as a pest repellent, disease 
preventative, and plant immune booster. It is applied at dilutions of 1:1000 as 
a foliar spray.

Water Soluble Calcium (WS Ca) is made by roasting eggshells or seashells at 
high temperature until they become white and brittle, then soaking them in 
brown rice vinegar for several days. The acid dissolves the calcium carbonate, 
producing a calcium-rich solution that is used to strengthen plant cell walls, 
improve fruit quality, and enhance disease resistance. Calcium Phosphate 
(CaPhos, sometimes called WCA or Water Soluble Calcium Phosphate) is made by 
burning animal bones at high temperature, then dissolving the resulting bone 
char in brown rice vinegar. CaPhos provides both calcium and phosphorus and is 
used during the flowering and fruiting stages to support reproductive 
development.

## JADAM Specific Methods

JADAM simplifies and extends the KNF approach with several distinctive 
techniques. The JADAM Microorganism Solution (JMS) is the JADAM equivalent of 
IMO and is produced using a simpler protocol. Leaf mold or forest soil is 
collected from a biologically active area and mixed with boiled potato, which 
serves as a food source for microorganisms. This mixture is fermented for 5 to 
7 days, then diluted and used as a soil drench or foliar spray. JMS can be 
produced in large quantities at very low cost, making it accessible to farmers 
with minimal resources.

The JADAM liquid fertilizer system replaces the individual FPJ, FFJ, and FAA 
inputs of KNF with a simpler approach based on plant and animal materials 
available on the farm. Weeds, crop residues, and other green plant materials 
are chopped and fermented with minimal added sugar (or no sugar at all in some 
JADAM protocols) to produce nutrient-rich liquid fertilizers. JADAM also 
emphasizes the use of leaf mold and compost produced from on-farm materials as 
the primary soil amendment, replacing imported compost or vermicompost.

JADAM's pest management approach is based on the JADAM Wetting Agent (JWA), a 
surfactant made by saponifying canola oil with potassium hydroxide derived from 
wood ash. This wetting agent allows pest-control solutions to spread evenly and 
penetrate insect bodies. The JADAM pesticide (JHS) is made by extracting plant 
material, typically from plants with insecticidal or repellent properties, and 
combining it with the JADAM wetting agent. The specific plant materials used 
vary by region and pest pressure, but commonly include garlic, chili peppers, 
neem, and various weeds.

JADAM's Ultra-Low-Cost Pesticide (JLP) is made by collecting wild grasses and 
fermenting them in water with a small amount of JWA. The principle is that the 
diverse secondary metabolites produced by the grasses during fermentation have 
broad-spectrum pesticidal properties. This approach is notable for its 
simplicity and its use of materials that cost nothing. JADAM practitioners 
report that consistent application of JMS and JLP, combined with healthy soil 
biology, can manage most pest and disease problems without the need for 
commercial pesticides.

## Application Methods

In both KNF and JADAM, inputs are applied through several methods depending on 
the purpose and timing. Seed treatment involves soaking seeds in a dilute 
solution of LAB or IMO before planting. This inoculates the seed with 
beneficial microorganisms that promote germination, protect against soilborne 
pathogens, and give the seedling a head start in establishing beneficial 
microbial associations. Typical seed soaking times range from 30 minutes to 
several hours, depending on the seed type and the concentration of the solution.

Soil drench involves applying liquid inputs directly to the soil around the 
root zone. This is the primary method for applying IMO, LAB, FAA, and other 
soil-active inputs. Soil drenches deliver microorganisms and nutrients directly 
to the rhizosphere, where they can interact with plant roots and soil biology. 
Applications are typically made at intervals of 1 to 4 weeks throughout the 
growing season, with the specific timing and frequency depending on crop type, 
growth stage, and soil conditions.

Foliar spray involves applying diluted inputs to the leaves and stems of plants 
using a sprayer. Foliar application allows for rapid absorption of nutrients 
and biological agents through leaf surfaces and is particularly effective for 
FPJ, FFJ, OHN, and pest management solutions. Foliar sprays are typically 
applied in the early morning or late afternoon when stomata are open and 
temperatures are moderate, which maximizes absorption and minimizes the risk of 
leaf burn. The standard dilution for most KNF foliar inputs is 1:500, though 
specific dilutions vary by input and application purpose.

## Integration with Crops and Livestock

Korean Natural Farming and JADAM techniques are applied across a wide range of 
crop types, including rice, vegetables, fruit trees, field crops, and 
ornamental plants. In rice production, KNF methods emphasize the use of 
IMO-treated nursery soil, seed soaking with LAB, and periodic applications of 
FPJ, FAA, and WS Ca throughout the growing season. Paddy fields managed with 
KNF methods typically show improved soil structure, reduced weed pressure, and 
equivalent or superior yields compared to chemically managed fields, according 
to practitioner reports and limited research trials.

In vegetable production, KNF inputs are applied through seed treatment, soil 
preparation, and regular foliar and soil applications throughout the growing 
season. The inputs are matched to plant growth stages: FPJ and FAA during 
vegetative growth, FFJ and CaPhos during flowering and fruiting, and OHN and WS 
Ca for disease prevention and plant strengthening. Livestock integration is 
also an important component of KNF systems. Pigs and poultry raised with KNF 
methods are fed natural diets supplemented with KNF inputs, and their manure is 
managed using IMO inoculation to produce high-quality compost without the odor 
problems associated with conventional manure management. JADAM has also 
developed specific livestock management protocols, including the use of JMS in 
animal housing to control odor and pathogens.

## Comparison with Organic Farming and Permaculture

Korean Natural Farming and JADAM share many principles with organic farming and 
permaculture, but they differ in important ways. Organic farming, as codified 
in certification standards, prohibits synthetic chemical inputs but permits a 
wide range of approved organic inputs including mined minerals, imported 
compost, and commercially produced biological products. KNF and JADAM go 
further by requiring that all inputs be produced locally from farm materials, 
eliminating dependency on any external supply chain. This makes KNF and JADAM 
more self-sufficient than most certified organic systems.

Compared to permaculture, KNF and JADAM are more focused on specific biological 
inputs and fermentation techniques, while permaculture takes a broader 
design-based approach to creating integrated systems. Permaculture's zone and 
sector analysis, guild design, and pattern understanding are complementary to 
but distinct from the KNF/JADAM emphasis on microbial cultivation and input 
production. Many practitioners combine elements of all three approaches, using 
permaculture design principles to lay out the overall system and KNF or JADAM 
techniques for day-to-day crop and soil management.

## Cost Savings and Economic Benefits

One of the most compelling aspects of KNF and JADAM is the dramatic reduction 
in input costs. Conventional rice farming in South Korea typically requires 
input costs of $1,000 to $2,000 per hectare for fertilizers, pesticides, and 
other chemicals. KNF and JADAM practitioners report reducing these costs by 70 
to 90 percent, with some JADAM farmers achieving near-zero input costs. The 
economic benefits are particularly significant for smallholder farmers in 
developing countries, where the cost of chemical inputs can represent a major 
barrier to profitable farming.

The transition to KNF or JADAM does require an initial investment of time and 
learning, as practitioners must master the techniques for collecting, 
cultivating, and applying indigenous microorganisms. However, once the skills 
are acquired, the ongoing costs are minimal. Many practitioners report that 
their yields are maintained or improved after the transition period, 
particularly as soil health improves over the course of several seasons. The 
long-term economic benefits also include reduced health risks from chemical 
exposure and improved resilience to market volatility and supply chain 
disruptions.

## Scientific Evidence and Global Adoption

The scientific evidence base for Korean Natural Farming and JADAM is growing 
but still limited compared to that for conventional or certified organic 
agriculture. Published research has demonstrated that IMO inoculation can 
improve soil microbial biomass and enzyme activity, that KNF inputs can enhance 
crop growth and yield in controlled conditions, and that LAB applications can 
suppress certain plant pathogens. However, large-scale, long-term replicated 
trials comparing KNF and JADAM with conventional management are still 
relatively few, and the mechanisms by which many of the inputs work are not 
fully characterized at the molecular level.

Despite the limited formal research, KNF and JADAM have been adopted by tens of 
thousands of farmers across South Korea, Japan, Southeast Asia, the Pacific 
Islands, Africa, the Americas, and Europe. The Cho Global Natural Farming 
organization has trained practitioners in over 40 countries, and JADAM has 
published detailed training materials in Korean, English, and several other 
languages. The global spread of these systems has been driven primarily by 
farmer-to-farmer knowledge exchange, supported by online forums, social media, 
and in-person workshops. The low cost and accessibility of the methods make 
them particularly attractive in developing countries, where they are often 
adopted as part of broader sustainable agriculture and food sovereignty 
movements.
