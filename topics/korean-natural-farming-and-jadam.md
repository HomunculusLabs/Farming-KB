# Korean Natural Farming and JADAM

Korean Natural Farming (KNF) and JADAM are two closely related but distinct 
systems of natural agriculture that originated in South Korea in the latter 
half of the [[beneficial-indigenous-microorganisms-bim]] to maintain soil health, plant nutrition, and 
pest management without reliance on synthetic chemical inputs. Together, they 
represent one of the most developed and widely practiced systems of natural 
agriculture in East Asia, with growing influence and adoption across the 
Americas, Africa, Europe, and Southeast Asia.

Korean Natural Farming was developed by Master Cho Han-kyu (1935 to 2019) 
beginning in the 1960s and refined over five decades of practice and teaching. 
Cho was a farmer and agricultural researcher who observed that conventional

collect, cultivate, and multiply them for use as agricultural inputs.

The second principle is zero external inputs. In KNF, this is approached 
through the production of all necessary inputs from locally available 
materials: rice, brown sugar, fruits, fish, eggshells, and plant materials. In 
JADAM, the commitment is even more absolute: every input must be producible on 
the farm without purchasing anything from outside. JADAM practitioners use 
materials like indigenous microorganisms collected from the farm, plant 
extracts made from weeds growing on the farm, and mineral solutions derived 
from wood ash collected on the farm. This approach dramatically reduces farming 
costs and eliminates dependency on agricultural supply chains.

The third principle is the use of fermentation rather than chemical synthesis. 
Both KNF and JADAM rely heavily on lactic acid bacteria, yeast, and other 
fermentative microorganisms to transform raw materials into biologically active 
inputs. Fermentation preserves nutrients, produces beneficial metabolites, and 
creates products that are readily available for plant uptake. The fourth 
principle is the minimization of soil disturbance. Both systems discourage or 
prohibit tillage, recognizing that tilling damages soil structure, disrupts 
fungal networks, and releases stored carbon. Instead, they favor mulching, 
cover cropping, and surface application of inputs to build soil organic matter 
and biological activity over time.

## Mahayana and Hinayana Natural Farming

# Mahayana and Hinayana Natural Farming Masanobu Fukuoka adopted Buddhist terminology to describe two distinct approaches to natural farming, which he called "Mahayana" (Great Vehicle) and "Hinayana" (Lesser Vehicle) natural farming. This framework is not a religious statement but a practical philosophical distinction that reflects the degree of human intervention in the farming system. ## Hinayana Natural Farming Hinayana natural farming represents an intermediate approach that reduces but does not eliminate human intervention. Practitioners of this method may still apply some compost, practice limited pruning, or use selective weeding. ## Four Principles of Natural Farming

# Four Principles of Natural Farming Masanobu Fukuoka's natural farming system is built upon four foundational principles that, when practiced together, create a self-sustaining agricultural ecosystem. These principles emerged from decades of observation and experimentation on his family farm in Shikoku, Japan, and represent a radical departure from both conventional and organic agriculture. ## First Principle: No Cultivation The first principle rejects all plowing, tilling, and soil disturbance. Fukuoka observed that the earth cultivates itself naturally through the activity of plant roots, soil organisms, and weather. Tilling disrupts soil structure, destroys mycorrhizal networks, and exposes organic matter to rapid oxidation. ...

## Natural Order and Microbial Ecology

# Natural Order and Microbial Ecology Masanobu Fukuoka's concept of "natural order" is fundamentally rooted in microbial ecology. He saw the invisible world of soil microorganisms as the foundation upon which all terrestrial life depends, and believed that human agriculture goes wrong precisely when it disrupts this microbial foundation. Understanding natural order, for Fukuoka, meant understanding the role of microbes in maintaining the health and balance of ecosystems. ## The Food Chain as Unified System Fukuoka viewed the entire food chain — from soil microbes through insects, plants, animals, and humans — as a single integrated system. In this view, microbes are not merely decomposers but the primary drivers of nutrient cycling, soil structure formation, plant health, and disease suppression. ...
