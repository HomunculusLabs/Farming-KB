Soil mineral management is the practice of analyzing, balancing, and replenishing the inorganic nutrient content of agricultural
soils. Unlike organic matter management, which feeds soil biology, mineral management targets the elemental building blocks plants
require for growth and nutritional quality.
Without adequate mineral balance, even biologically rich soils produce nutrient-poor crops vulnerable to pest pressure. This guide
synthesizes knowledge from Solomon, Mollison, Holmgren, Lowenfels, Cho, and Albrecht into a comprehensive resource for gardeners
and farmers at every scale.

## The Mineral Depletion Problem

Modern agricultural soils have lost significant mineral content over the past century. USDA research shows vegetables grown today
contain measurably less protein, vitamins, and minerals than those grown 50 years ago.
Declines in calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, and selenium affect virtually all major crops. The primary cause is incomplete
fertilizers providing only NPK while neglecting calcium, magnesium, sulfur, and trace minerals.
Commercial growers push potassium high for bulk yield, but extra yield comes as starch and fiber rather than protein, vitamins,
and minerals. Organic growers using only compost face the same problem when local soils lack specific elements.
Steve Solomon documents that NPK-only fertilization produces vegetables with measurable nutritional declines compared to crops
receiving complete mineral supplementation. Soil mineral content directly determines food mineral content.
JADAM founder Youngsang Cho identifies mineral depletion as the reason farming becomes harder each year. Modern soil tests cannot
accurately distinguish between solubilized and insolubilized nutrients, making targeted correction difficult.
Fukuoka Masanobu observed that ancient soils maintained fertility through natural rock weathering, leaf fall, and biological
cycling over millennia. Modern agriculture disrupted these cycles faster than natural processes can rebuild them.

## Primary Macronutrients: Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium

Nitrogen drives leafy growth and protein synthesis. Deficiency causes pale yellowing leaves and stunted growth. Organic sources
include seed meal at 5-7% N, blood meal at 12-0-0, fish meal, well-rotted manure, and legume cover crops.
Excess nitrogen promotes soft vegetative tissue susceptible to pest and disease pressure. The key is matching nitrogen supply to
crop demand through the growing season, using side-dressings for heavy feeders like corn and brassicas.
Phosphorus supports root development, flowering, and seed production. Deficiency shows as poor root growth and dark green or
purplish leaves. Rock phosphate releases P over years. Bone meal provides faster availability at 3-15-0.
Potassium strengthens cell walls, improves disease resistance, and regulates water movement through stomata. Most soils have
adequate K. Sources include wood ash at 3-7% K2O, kelp meal, greensand, and sulfate of potash.
Excess potassium blocks calcium uptake and raises sodium levels. This is one of the most common mineral imbalances in both
conventional and organic growing systems, particularly from over-application of wood ash or manure.

## Secondary Macronutrients: Calcium, Magnesium, Sulfur

Calcium builds cell walls and membrane integrity. It is the most powerful factor in soil structure, creating open friable fabric
allowing air and water infiltration. Low calcium levels also produce predominantly male offspring in livestock.
In dryland soils, sodium displaces calcium, requiring gypsum at approximately 30 tonnes per hectare to restore balance. Calcium
phosphate is the chief mineral in bones, linking soil calcium to animal and human skeletal health.
Magnesium sits at the center of every chlorophyll molecule and activates essential enzymes. Deficiencies occur primarily on sandy
soils. Dolomite supplies both calcium and magnesium simultaneously, buffering soil pH.
The ideal Ca:Mg ratio keeps magnesium below calcium to maintain good soil aeration. Excess magnesium makes soils tight, poorly
aerated, and difficult to work. This is common in soils derived from magnesium-rich parent material.
Sulfur is a component of amino acids and proteins. Gypsum supplies calcium and sulfur without changing pH. Elemental sulfur lowers
pH in alkaline soils. Thiobacilli bacteria oxidize sulfur to sulfuric acid for biological pH adjustment.
Mollison specifies ideal soil ion proportions as Ca:Mg:K:Na equal to 50:35:6:5. These ratios provide a framework for mineral
balancing programs in permaculture design, though most soils deviate significantly from this ideal.

## Micronutrients and Trace Elements

Micronutrients function as enzyme cofactors. Iron is essential for chlorophyll synthesis. Zinc activates growth hormones. Boron
governs calcium mobility within plant tissues and controls pollen tube growth and fruit set.
Molybdenum enables nitrogen fixation in legumes at just grams per hectare. Cobalt is essential for rhizobial bacteria that fix
nitrogen. Nickel is required for the urease enzyme that converts urea to ammonia in plant metabolism.
Iron and manganese interact closely, with excessive manganese inhibiting iron uptake. Copper and zinc compete for root absorption
sites. These antagonisms mean single-element supplementation can trigger secondary deficiencies.
Boron deficiency is particularly widespread and hard to diagnose visually until damage is severe. Symptoms include hollow stems,
cracked fruit, poor seed set, and distorted growing points. Leaf tissue analysis provides reliable assessment.
Trace applications in arid zones have produced dramatic results. Seven kg per hectare of zinc and copper sulfates increased
pasture carrying capacity 40 times in South Australia. Fifty grams per hectare of molybdenum enabled clover on acid soils.
Silicon, while not classified as essential for all plants, strengthens cell walls in grasses and reduces susceptibility to fungal
pathogens. Soluble silicon is absorbed as silicic acid and deposited as phytoliths in epidermal cell walls.

## Cation Exchange Capacity and Nutrient Holding

Cation exchange capacity (CEC) measures a soil's ability to hold positively charged nutrient ions against leaching. Clay particles
and organic matter carry negative charges that attract calcium, magnesium, potassium, and ammonium cations.
Sandy soils with low clay and organic matter have low CEC and lose nutrients rapidly through leaching. Humus has the highest CEC
per unit weight of any soil component, which is why adding organic matter dramatically increases retention.
Clay provides substantial CEC but restricts drainage when humus is insufficient for aggregate formation. The ideal soil balances
clay for mineral holding and humus for biological cycling and water management.
Root tip exchange of hydrogen cations for nutrient cations increases local hydrogen concentration, affecting rhizosphere pH. Fungi
and bacteria carry surface charges that further influence microsite pH around plant roots.
Building CEC is a long-term process. Each percent of organic matter contributes approximately 2 meq/100g of CEC. A soil increasing
from 3% to 5% organic matter gains roughly 4 meq/100g of nutrient holding capacity over time.

## Soil pH and Nutrient Availability

Soil pH governs availability of all mineral nutrients. Most nutrients are most available between pH 6.0 and 7.0. Below pH 5.5,
aluminum becomes toxic and phosphorus locks in insoluble compounds.
Above pH 7.5, iron, manganese, zinc, and boron become progressively less available. Many alkaline soils show micronutrient
deficiencies despite having adequate total nutrient content, because high pH renders them insoluble.
Solomon argues gardeners should focus on complete mineral nutrition rather than chasing a target pH. When the full mineral
spectrum is supplied in proper proportions, pH stabilizes through biological buffering from organic matter.
Organic matter acts as a pH buffer resisting both acidification and alkalinisation. Soils with 5% or more organic matter are
remarkably pH-stable. Well-made compost has near-neutral pH regardless of inputs.
Lime materials vary in neutralizing value: agricultural limestone (NV ~100) raises pH while supplying calcium. Dolomite adds
magnesium. Hydrated lime (NV 120-134) is more reactive. Gypsum supplies calcium without changing pH.

## Rock Dust and Soil Remineralization

Rock dust is finely ground rock applied as slow-release mineral source. Basalt rock dust is the most versatile type, containing
silica (45-52%), iron oxide (6-14%), calcium oxide (7-12%), magnesium oxide (4-10%), plus trace elements.
Basalt minerals weather through known pathways. Plagioclase feldspar releases calcium and silica. Pyroxene releases magnesium and
iron relatively quickly. Olivine weathers fastest. Magnetite provides long-term iron over decades.
Trials in Australia, Europe, and Brazil show yield increases of 10-30% with basalt dust on depleted soils. Greatest responses
occur on highly weathered tropical soils where mineral reserves are naturally low.
Application rates: 0.5-1 kg per square meter for general improvement. 5-10% by volume in compost for activation. 2-5 cups per
cubic foot in potting mixes. Apply in fall or early spring to allow weathering.
Mycorrhizal fungi play a critical role in rock dust weathering. Hyphae excrete gluconic and oxalic acids at growing tips that
solubilize mineral cations. Rock dust with mycorrhizae consistently outperforms rock dust alone.
