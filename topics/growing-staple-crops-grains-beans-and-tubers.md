# Growing Staple Crops: Grains, Beans, and Tubers

Staple crops are calorie-dense foods that form the foundation of human diets
worldwide, typically providing 50 to 70 percent of daily caloric intake for a
given population. They include the cereal grains (wheat, rice, maize, and
lesser-known grains such as sorghum and millet), leguminous crops (dry beans,
peas, lentils, chickpeas, and soybeans), and starchy tuber and root vegetables
(potatoes, sweet potatoes, cassava, and yams). Together these three categories
account for the vast majority of human nutrition across every culture and
climate zone on Earth. The domestication of staple crops beginning roughly
10,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent, the Yangtze and Yellow River basins,
Mesoamerica, and the Andes represents one of the most transformative events in
human history, enabling the shift from nomadic foraging to settled agricultural
civilization.

The so-called "Big Three" staple crops are wheat, rice, and maize, which
collectively supply more than half of all plant-derived calories consumed by
humans globally. Wheat dominates temperate regions, rice is the primary staple
across tropical and subtropical Asia, and maize underpins food systems
throughout the Americas and much of sub-Saharan Africa. However, regional
staples are equally important within their contexts: sorghum and millet in the
Sahel, teff in Ethiopia, potatoes in the Andes and northern Europe, cassava in
tropical Africa, and sweet potatoes across the Pacific Islands. Each of these
crops evolved in dialogue with its environment and the people who cultivated
it, resulting in extraordinary genetic diversity that modern growers can draw
upon.

In recent decades, interest in home-scale and small-farm staple crop production
has surged, driven by concerns about food supply chain fragility, the
nutritional decline of industrially processed foods, and a growing desire for
greater self-reliance. Authors such as Carol Deppe and Jack Lazor have
demonstrated that producing meaningful quantities of staple calories on a
backyard or homestead scale is not only feasible but can be done using organic
and ecological methods. The revival of heritage grain varieties, dry bean
landraces, and heirloom tubers reflects a broader movement toward reclaiming
the skills and genetic resources that once sustained communities before
industrial agriculture consolidated them into commodity monocultures.

This article provides a comprehensive overview of growing staple food crops
across the three major categories: grains, legumes, and tuber or root crops.
It covers selection, soil preparation, planting, pest management, harvesting,
processing, seed saving, storage, and integration into ecological farming
systems.

## Grain Crops

Grain crops are grasses (family Poaceae) or pseudo-cereals cultivated for their
edible seeds, which are rich in carbohydrates and provide a dense source of
calories. The major true cereal grains include wheat, rice, maize, oats,
barley, rye, sorghum, and millet, while quinoa, amaranth, and buckwheat are
pseudo-cereals from unrelated plant families that fill a similar nutritional
role. All share the basic pattern of annual growth from seed to mature grain
head within a single season.

### Wheat

Wheat is the most widely grown grain on Earth and adapts to a broad range of
temperate environments. It is divided into winter wheat, planted in autumn and
requiring a vernalization period of cold temperatures to trigger heading, and
spring wheat, planted in early spring and maturing without vernalization. Winter
wheat generally yields better because its established root system captures
winter and early spring moisture. Wheat is further classified by grain texture
(hard or soft) and color (red or white). Hard red wheat is preferred for bread
because of its high protein and gluten content, while soft white wheat is used
for pastries and flatbreads. Home-scale growers typically plant wheat in
broadcast or drilled rows at roughly 60 to 100 pounds per acre, depending on
seed size and row spacing. Harvest occurs when the grain is hard and the heads
have turned golden, typically by cutting with a scythe or sickle, followed by
threshing and winnowing.

### Corn (Maize)

Maize is the most productive grain crop in terms of calories per unit area
under good conditions. It is divided into field corn (dent or flint types for
grain, flour, or animal feed) and sweet corn (for fresh eating). For staple
calorie production, field corn varieties such as 'Oaxacan Green', 'Bloody
Butcher', and 'Floriani Red Flint' produce excellent flour corn. Corn is a
warm-season crop requiring soil temperatures above 50 degrees Fahrenheit (10
degrees Celsius) for germination and a frost-free season of 90 to 120 days.
It is a heavy feeder, particularly on nitrogen. The traditional Three Sisters
planting method, originating with Indigenous peoples of eastern North America,
interplants corn, beans, and squash in a polyculture exploiting complementary
growth habits: corn provides a trellis, beans fix nitrogen, and squash
suppresses weeds.

### Minor Grains and Pseudo-Cereals

Oats and barley are cool-season grains well-suited to northern climates and
marginal soils. Barley matures quickly and tolerates drought better than wheat.
Rye is the most cold-tolerant cereal, germinating in near-freezing soil, and
is invaluable for late-autumn plantings and winter cover crops. Sorghum and
millet are warm-season, drought-tolerant grains essential to arid regions;
sorghum can grow where maize fails due to heat and water stress, and pearl
millet is among the most efficient grains for producing calories under desert
conditions. Quinoa and amaranth are pseudo-cereals from the Andes and
Mesoamerica respectively, both producing protein-rich seeds with complete amino
acid profiles. Buckwheat, despite its name, is related to rhubarb and produces
a triangular seed used for flour; it grows rapidly as a 30 to 45-day crop and
excels as a summer cover crop or emergency grain. See
[[grain-and-staple-crops]] and [[grain-cultivation-small-scale]] for more.

## Legume Crops

Legumes are plants in the family Fabaceae that form symbiotic relationships
with rhizobium bacteria in their root systems, enabling them to convert
atmospheric nitrogen into forms available to plants. This nitrogen fixation
capacity makes legumes ecologically unique among staple crops and central to
sustainable agriculture. Beyond their nitrogen contribution, legumes provide
protein-rich seeds that complement the amino acid profile of cereal grains,
forming the nutritional foundation of cuisines worldwide: rice and beans in
Latin America, dal and chapati in South Asia, hummus and pita in the
Mediterranean, and tofu and rice in East Asia.

### Dry Beans

The common bean (*Phaseolus vulgaris*) encompasses a remarkable diversity of dry
bean types including kidney, black, pinto, navy, cannellini, and cranberry
beans, each developed by generations of farmers in distinct cultural contexts.
Dry beans are warm-season crops requiring 80 to 100 days to maturity. They are
typically direct-seeded after all danger of frost has passed, at a rate of
roughly four to six seeds per foot in rows spaced 18 to 30 inches apart.
Inoculation with the appropriate strain of rhizobium bacteria at planting time
improves nodulation and nitrogen fixation, though many soils already harbor
adequate rhizobium populations if beans have been grown previously. Dry beans
are harvested when the pods are fully dry and brittle, and the seeds rattle
inside. See [[bean-growing-guide]] for variety-specific guidance.

### Peas, Chickpeas, Lentils, and Cowpeas

Field peas (*Pisum sativum*) are cool-season legumes planted in early spring.
Chickpeas (garbanzo beans) require a longer, drier season and are more
challenging in humid climates. Lentils are among the oldest cultivated legumes
and produce well in dry, cool conditions with minimal inputs. Cowpeas
(black-eyed peas and related types) are heat-tolerant legumes from West Africa
that thrive where common beans struggle; they are essential staples in tropical
and subtropical regions. Fava or broad beans (*Vicia faba*) are among the most
cold-hardy legumes, capable of overwintering in mild climates and producing
high yields of protein-rich seeds. Soybeans are a versatile legume grown for
edible green beans (edamame), dry mature beans, or oil and meal. Peanuts
(*Arachis hypogaea*) are a unique legume that flowers above ground but develops
its seed pod below the soil surface (geocarpy), requiring loose, well-drained
soil for peg penetration.

### The Nitrogen Myth

A persistent misconception in gardening literature holds that beans planted
alongside corn will "feed" nitrogen directly to the corn through root
exudation. In reality, legumes fix nitrogen primarily for their own use, and
the surplus nitrogen becomes available to subsequent crops only after the
legume residues decompose. The [[legume-nitrogen-myth-beans-feed-corn]] concept
page addresses this in detail. While intercropping beans with corn has genuine
benefits for pest management, spatial efficiency, and overall system yield, the
nitrogen contribution to the companion corn plant during the same growing
season is minimal. The nitrogen benefit of legumes is realized by the crop that
follows them in rotation. See [[legume-nitrogen-dynamics]] and
[[legume-nitrogen-yields-by-species]] for quantitative data on nitrogen fixation
rates across legume species.

## Tuber and Root Crops

Tuber and root crops provide dense caloric energy in the form of starch stored
below ground. They are often the most productive staple crops per unit area in
temperate climates, with potatoes and sweet potatoes capable of yielding
substantially more calories per acre than cereal grains under good growing
conditions. Their underground storage organ also provides natural protection
from birds and many above-ground pests, though they face their own suite of
soil-dwelling insects and pathogens.

### Potatoes

Potatoes (*Solanum tuberosum*) originated in the Andes and are now the
world's fourth-largest food crop. They are propagated vegetatively from "seed
potatoes," which are small whole potatoes or cut pieces each containing at
least two eyes. Potatoes require well-drained, loose soil and consistent
moisture. Hilling, the practice of mounding soil around the base of the plant
as it grows, is essential: it protects developing tubers from greening (solanine
exposure to sunlight), suppresses weeds, and can increase yield by encouraging
additional tuber formation along the buried stem. Steve Solomon's
[[potato-growing-guide-solomon]] emphasizes that potatoes grown in widely-spaced
plantings with minimal irrigation develop denser, more flavorful, and
longer-storing tubers than those grown under intensive irrigation. Potatoes are
harvested after the vines die back naturally, then cured at 55 to 65 degrees
Fahrenheit in high humidity for two weeks before storage in cool, dark, and
humid conditions around 38 to 40 degrees Fahrenheit. See
[[potato-growing-guide]] for comprehensive growing instructions.

### Sweet Potatoes and Jerusalem Artichokes

Sweet potatoes (*Ipomoea batatas*) are a tropical vine in the morning glory
family, producing large starchy tubers rich in beta-carotene. They require warm
soil (above 60 degrees Fahrenheit), a long growing season of 90 to 150 days,
and are propagated from slips (vine cuttings). Jerusalem artichokes
(*Helianthus tuberosus*), also called sunchokes, are a perennial sunflower
relative producing small knobby tubers that are extremely productive and
cold-hardy. Their primary drawback is invasiveness: once established they are
nearly impossible to eradicate, and their inulin content causes digestive
discomfort in some people. They are best planted in contained areas.

### Other Root Crops

Carrots, onions, garlic, turnips, parsnips, beets, and radishes round out the
root crop category. While not as calorie-dense as potatoes or sweet potatoes,
they contribute essential nutrients, flavor diversity, and winter storage
vegetables to a self-sufficient food system. Carrots and parsnips require deep,
loose, stone-free soil for proper root development. Onions and garlic are
alliums grown from sets, transplants, or seed. Turnips and beets are
fast-growing, dual-purpose crops whose roots and greens are both edible.
Detailed guidance on these crops is available in [[root-crops-growing-guide]],
[[carrot-growing-guide]], [[onion-growing-guide]], and [[garlic-growing-guide]].

## Seed Saving for Staple Crops

Saving seed from staple crops is fundamental to long-term self-sufficiency and
genetic sovereignty. Staple crops are predominantly self-pollinating or
wind-pollinated, and each mating system imposes different requirements for
maintaining varietal purity. Self-pollinating crops such as wheat, oats,
barley, peas, beans, and tomatoes can often be maintained as pure varieties
with minimal isolation distance because their flowers fertilize themselves
before opening. Wind-pollinated crops such as corn, rye, and amaranth require
much larger isolation distances, typically one quarter mile to one mile or more
depending on the species, to prevent cross-pollination from neighboring fields
or varieties.

Suzanne Ashworth's *Seed to Seed* provides detailed isolation distances and
population size recommendations for each major staple crop. As a general
principle, maintaining a population of at least 80 to 100 individual plants
preserves sufficient genetic diversity for most self-pollinating species, while
cross-pollinating species benefit from larger populations of 200 or more
plants. Selection criteria should favor traits that matter for the growing
environment: disease resistance, earliness, drought tolerance, yield, and
storage quality. Selecting seed from the best-performing plants year after year
gradually adapts a variety to local conditions, a process sometimes called
"vernacular breeding." Heirloom varieties, maintained by generations of seed
savers, represent a reservoir of genetic diversity that is particularly
valuable for adapting to changing climate conditions. See
[[seed-saving-and-plant-breeding]] for a thorough treatment of these methods.

## Small-Scale Grain Production

Producing grain at home scale is more labor-intensive than growing vegetables
but is entirely feasible with appropriate tools and techniques. The basic cycle
of grain production at small scale follows four steps: harvesting (cutting the
stalks), threshing (separating grain from the chaff), winnowing (removing the
chaff from the grain), and milling (grinding the grain into flour). Traditional
hand tools remain effective for these tasks: a scythe or sickle for harvesting,
a flail or treading by foot for threshing, and a fan or the wind for winnowing.

Jack Lazor's *The Organic Grain Grower* and Sara Pitzer's *Homegrown Whole
Grains* describe these processes in detail. Modern homesteaders can also use
small-scale mechanical aids such as pedal-powered threshers, hand-cranked
winnowing machines, and affordable grain mills ranging from simple stone
burrs to steel impact mills. The [[grain-milling-at-home]] and
[[grain-processing-and-milling-guide]] concept pages cover equipment options and
techniques.

A common question is how much land is needed to produce a meaningful grain
supply. Rough estimates suggest that 1,000 to 2,000 square feet of grain
production can yield 50 to 100 pounds of wheat or similar grain per year under
reasonable home-garden conditions, enough for a modest but meaningful
contribution to a household's annual bread needs. Yields vary enormously based
on soil fertility, rainfall, variety, and grower skill.

Masanobu Fukuoka's natural farming approach, described in *The One-Straw
Revolution*, demonstrated that rice and barley could be grown successfully
without plowing, flooding, or chemical inputs. His method involves broadcasting
clover and grain seed directly onto harvested rice straw, allowing the winter
barley crop to grow through a living mulch of clover, followed by a summer rice
crop in the same field. See [[fukuoka-straw-rice-without-plowing]],
[[fukuoka-barley-clover-cultivation]], and
[[fukuoka-clover-and-green-manure-cropping]] for detailed descriptions of his
methods and their broader applications.

## Soil Preparation and Fertility

Staple crops have demanding fertility requirements because they remove large
quantities of nutrients from the soil when harvested. Grains are particularly
nitrogen-hungry; corn is among the heaviest nitrogen feeders of any crop.
Legumes, while capable of fixing atmospheric nitrogen, still require adequate
phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients for optimal growth and nitrogen
fixation. Root and tuber crops need phosphorus and potassium for root
development and can be sensitive to excess nitrogen, which promotes leafy
growth at the expense of tuber formation.

Bed preparation for root crops requires deep, loose, well-drained soil free of
rocks and compacted layers that would deform developing roots. Double-digging
or broadforking is beneficial for carrots, parsnips, and potatoes. Grains and
legumes are more forgiving of soil structure but respond well to incorporation
of organic matter and balanced fertility. Cover crop rotations are essential
for sustainable staple crop production: a typical rotation might alternate a
nitrogen-fixing legume with a nitrogen-demanding grain, with a cover crop of
grass or cereal rye over winter to protect soil and scavenge residual
nutrients. See [[cover-crops-and-green-manures]] and
[[no-till-farming-and-conservation-agriculture]] for strategies that build soil
fertility while minimizing disturbance.

## Pest and Disease Management

Each category of staple crop faces characteristic pests and diseases. Grain
crops are susceptible to fungal diseases including rusts, smuts, and blights,
as well as insect pests such as the Hessian fly, corn earworm, and
grasshoppers. Legumes face aphids, bean beetles, Mexican bean beetles, and
anthracnose. Potatoes are notoriously vulnerable to late blight
(*Phytophthora infestans*), the pathogen responsible for the Irish Potato
Famine, as well as Colorado potato beetles and wireworms. Root crops contend
with root maggots,
flea beetles, nematodes, and various soil-borne fungi.

Organic pest management relies on a layered approach. Crop rotation is the
single most effective tool for breaking disease and pest cycles; many pathogens
and insects are host-specific and decline when their preferred crop is absent
for several years. Resistant varieties provide genetic defense against many
diseases. Physical barriers such as row covers exclude flying insects.
Biological controls including predatory insects, parasitic wasps, and microbial
sprays such as *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) target specific pests. Removing
diseased plant material prevents pathogen buildup. Companion planting, as
described in [[companion-planting-and-polycultures]] and Louise Riotte's
*Carrots Love Tomatoes*, can provide marginal pest deterrence though its effects
are often modest compared with rotation and resistant varieties.

## Harvesting and Storage

Proper harvesting and storage are critical for preserving the value of staple
crop production. Each crop category has specific indicators of maturity and
optimal harvest methods. Grains are ready to harvest when the seed heads are
dry, the grain is hard when bitten, and the moisture content is below 14
percent. Harvesting too early results in shriveled, high-moisture grain
susceptible to mold; harvesting too late increases losses from shattering and
weather damage. Dry beans should be harvested when pods are fully dry and
brittle. Potatoes are harvested after vine die-back, when the skins have set
and resist rubbing.

Drying is essential for grains and legumes destined for long-term storage.
Grains can be dried on tarps in the sun, in small batches on screens, or in
low-heat dehydrators. Target moisture content is 12 to 14 percent for grain
and 13 to 15 percent for dry beans. Root cellar storage requires cool
temperatures (38 to 40 degrees Fahrenheit for potatoes, 32 to 34 degrees for
carrots and other root crops), high humidity (85 to 95 percent for root crops,
lower for potatoes to prevent sprouting), darkness, and ventilation. Pests
including weevils, grain moths, and rodents are constant threats in stored
grain; freezing grain for several days before storage kills most insect eggs.
Diatomaceous earth can be mixed with stored grain as a physical insect
deterrent. See [[grain-harvesting-and-storage]],
[[root-crop-harvest-and-storage]], and
[[food-preservation-and-storage-methods]] for comprehensive guidance.

## Integration in Permaculture Systems

Permaculture design seeks to integrate staple crops into perennial polyculture
systems that mimic natural ecosystem function. While most staple crops are
annuals, they can be incorporated into food forests and polycultures in ways
that enhance overall system resilience. The Three Sisters polyculture of corn,
beans, and squash is a classic example exploiting vertical stratification,
nitrogen fixation, and weed suppression in a single planting.

Perennial staple crops offer particular advantages in permaculture systems.
Siberian pea shrub (*Caragana arborescens*) produces edible seeds, nitrogen
fixation, and windbreak function simultaneously. Chestnuts, hazelnuts, and
acorns provide calorie-dense perennial starch sources requiring far less annual
labor than grain crops. Animals integrated with grain production can convert
crop residues into eggs, milk, or meat while returning fertility through
manure. Poultry can forage in harvested grain fields, cleaning up spilled
grain, consuming insect pests, and depositing manure. See
[[companion-planting-and-polycultures]] for design patterns integrating staple
crops with other system elements.

## Climate Adaptation

Selecting appropriate staple crops for a given climate zone is the most
fundamental decision a grower makes. In cold northern climates (USDA zones 3 to
5), hardy grains such as rye, oats, barley, and spring wheat combined with
potatoes and root vegetables form a reliable foundation. In hot, arid climates,
sorghum, pearl millet, cowpeas, tepary beans, and sweet potatoes outperform
temperate staples. Amaranth and quinoa are remarkably adaptable to heat and
drought. Climate change is accelerating the need for heat-tolerant and
drought-resistant varieties. Drought strategies include selecting adapted
species, reducing plant populations, using mulches, and timing plantings to
align with rainfall. In tropical regions, cassava, taro, and yam are staples
of extraordinary resilience, producing usable calories even under severe
neglect.

## Nutritional Considerations

Staple crops provide the caloric foundation of diets but must be combined
thoughtfully to meet complete nutritional needs. Cereal grains are rich in
carbohydrates and moderate protein but deficient in the essential amino acid
lysine. Legumes are rich in lysine but relatively low in methionine and
cysteine. When grains and legumes are consumed together, their amino acid
profiles complement each other, producing protein quality comparable to animal
products. This complementarity works over the course of a day, not necessarily
within a single meal.

Mineral content varies considerably among staple crops. Potatoes provide
significant potassium and vitamin C; quinoa is notably rich in iron, magnesium,
and zinc; and dark-colored bean varieties contain higher antioxidant levels.
However, all staple crops contain anti-nutritional factors that reduce mineral
bioavailability. Phytates in grains and legumes bind to iron, zinc, and calcium.
Oxalates in amaranth, quinoa, and beet greens interfere with calcium
absorption. Traditional processing methods such as soaking, sprouting,
fermenting, and sourdough leavening activate phytase enzymes that break down
phytic acid, dramatically improving mineral bioavailability. Carol Deppe
emphasizes that these processing techniques are essential nutritional strategies,
not mere culinary traditions, that transform staple crops from adequate to
truly nourishing foods.
