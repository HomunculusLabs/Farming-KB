# Garlic Cultivation and Allium Growing Guide

The allium family encompasses some of the most indispensable vegetables
and herbs in human agriculture. Garlic, onions, leeks, shallots, and
chives form the aromatic backbone of cuisines worldwide, and their
cultivation stretches back thousands of years. Despite their ubiquity in
the kitchen, each allium crop has distinct growing requirements that
reward the attentive gardener with reliable harvests and excellent
storage quality. This guide covers the full spectrum of allium
cultivation, from variety selection and soil preparation through
planting, maintenance, harvesting, and storage, with dedicated sections
on each major crop.

## Botanical Background

Alliums belong to the family Amaryllidaceae, subfamily Allioideae, and
are native to temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere, with centers
of diversity in Central Asia and the Mediterranean. The genus includes
over 700 species, though only a handful are widely cultivated for food.
What we call an onion or garlic bulb is not a true root or stem but
rather a compressed underground structure of swollen leaf bases layered
around a central growing point. This botanical reality explains why
alliums are so responsive to day length and temperature cues that govern
when they shift from vegetative leaf growth to bulb formation.

The sulfur-containing compounds that give alliums their characteristic
pungency — allicin in garlic, syn-propanethial-S-oxide in onions — are
defense chemicals produced when plant cells are damaged. These same
compounds are responsible for the well-documented antimicrobial and
health-promoting properties of alliums. From a gardening perspective,
these sulfur compounds also make alliums useful companion plants that
can help deter certain insect pests.

## Soil and Site Preparation

All allium crops share fundamental soil preferences. They need an open,
sunny position with well-drained soil and a pH between 6.0 and 7.0. If
the soil is acidic, adding lime to raise the pH above 6.5 improves
nutrient availability. Heavy, waterlogged soils promote rot in all allium
crops, so raised beds or sandy loams are ideal. Incorporating 2 to 3
inches of well-rotted compost or aged manure before planting provides
both nutrients and improved soil structure.

A critical caveat for onions: avoid applying fresh manure directly before
planting. High nitrogen from fresh manure promotes thick necks and poor
storage quality. Composted manure or well-rotted compost applied the
previous season is preferable. For garlic, moderate fertility is
sufficient; excessive nitrogen encourages leafy growth at the expense of
bulb development.

Phosphorus is important for root and bulb development in all alliums.
Working bone meal or rock phosphate into the planting bed provides a slow
release of phosphorus. Alliums have relatively shallow root systems
concentrated in the top 15 to 30 centimeters of soil, so surface
fertility matters more than deep soil amendments.

## Crop Rotation

Alliums are susceptible to several soil-borne diseases and pests,
particularly white rot (Sclerotium cepivorum), onion fly, and nematodes.
A minimum three to four year rotation away from any allium crop is
essential. In a traditional three-year rotation, alliums follow
brassicas, which leave the soil in good condition after heavy feeding.
They should never follow other alliums or legumes in the same plot.

Steve Solomon recommends saving your own planting stock from the best
garlic bulbs each year, selecting for adaptation to your specific soil
and climate. Over successive generations, this selection produces garlic
that is increasingly well-suited to local conditions. The same principle
applies to shallots and perennial onion varieties.

## Garlic: Varieties and Selection

Garlic (Allium sativum) is divided into two main types with distinct
characteristics. Hardneck garlic (var. ophioscorodon) produces a stiff
central flowering stalk called a scape, has fewer but larger cloves with
richer flavor, and requires cold winter vernalization. Softneck garlic
(var. sativum) lacks a stiff central stalk, produces more but smaller
cloves, stores significantly longer, and tolerates milder winters.

Hardneck varieties include Rocambole (rich, complex flavor, easy
peeling, poor storage), Porcelain (large cloves, bold heat, good
storage), and Purple Stripe (beautiful wrappers, excellent for roasting).
Popular cultivars include Music and Georgian Fire among Porcelains, and
Spanish Roja and German Red among Rocamboles.

Softneck varieties include Artichoke (the most common supermarket type,
mild flavor, 12 to 20 cloves) and Silverskin (strongest softneck flavor,
excellent storage up to 12 months, ideal for braiding). California Early,
California Late, and Silver Rose are widely grown cultivars.

Elephant garlic (Allium ampeloprasum) deserves a special note. It is not
a true garlic but a leek relative that produces enormous heads with 4 to
6 massive cloves. The flavor is milder and sweeter. It requires more
garden space but is visually striking and stores well for 8 to 10
months.

## Garlic: Planting

Garlic is planted in autumn, 4 to 6 weeks before the ground freezes.
This timing allows roots to establish before winter dormancy while
preventing premature top growth. Exact timing varies by zone: late
September to early October in zones 3 to 4, mid-October to early November
in zones 5 to 6, and November to December in zones 7 to 8.

Break heads into individual cloves just before planting. Plant only the
largest, healthiest cloves, keeping the papery wrapper intact. Plant
hardneck cloves 2 to 3 inches deep and softneck cloves 1 to 2 inches
deep, pointed end up, spaced 6 inches apart in rows 12 inches apart. In
raised beds or deep beds, a staggered grid pattern at 6-inch spacing
maximizes yield per square foot.

Mulch heavily after planting with straw, leaves, or compost. The mulch
suppresses winter weeds, insulates the soil against freeze-thaw cycles,
and conserves moisture. In spring, pull back mulch to let soil warm,
then reapply as plants grow to continue weed suppression.

## Garlic: Growing and Maintenance

Garlic emerges in early spring and grows most rapidly during cool weather
from March through May. Keep rows weed-free throughout the season. Garlic
has shallow roots and competes poorly with weeds; a thick organic mulch
is the most effective weed management strategy.

Water garlic moderately, providing about 1 inch per week during the
growing season. Reduce watering in the final 2 to 3 weeks before harvest
to help bulbs cure and prevent rot. Excessive nitrogen fertilization
during spring growth promotes lush foliage at the expense of bulb size.

For hardneck garlic, remove scapes when they have made one to two
complete coils. This redirects energy into bulb development and
typically increases bulb size by 15 to 25 percent. The removed scapes
are edible and can be used in stir-fries, pesto, soups, and grilled
dishes.

## Garlic: Harvesting and Curing

Harvest garlic when the lower 3 to 4 leaves have died back but 5 to 6
green leaves remain. Each green leaf corresponds to a wrapper layer on
the bulb; more green leaves at harvest mean better storage. Waiting
until all foliage is brown risks cloves separating and the bulb
splitting open, which ruins storage quality.

Loosen soil with a garden fork (not a shovel, which can cut heads) and
lift bulbs gently from below. Do not pull by the stem. Shake off excess
soil but do not wash garlic, as moisture promotes rot during curing.
Handle bulbs gently to avoid bruising, which also compromises storage.

Cure garlic by tying in bundles of 8 to 12 stems and hanging in a warm,
dry, well-ventilated space out of direct sunlight. Ideal curing
conditions are 70 to 80 degrees Fahrenheit with 50 to 60 percent
humidity. Hardneck garlic needs 3 to 4 weeks of curing; softneck needs
2 to 3 weeks. Garlic is properly cured when the stem is completely dry
and brittle, the outer wrapper is papery, and the neck feels tight.

## Garlic: Storage

After curing, trim roots to half an inch and cut stems to 1 to 2 inches.
For softneck varieties, leave stems long if you plan to braid. Do not
remove all protective wrapper layers, as they shield the cloves during
storage. Brush off remaining soil gently with a soft brush.

Store garlic in a cool (55 to 65 degrees Fahrenheit), dry (40 to 50
percent humidity), dark, well-ventilated area. Mesh bags, paper bags,
and open baskets all work well. Do not refrigerate garlic, as cold
combined with moisture triggers sprouting. Softneck garlic stores 6 to
9 months (Silverskin up to 12 months), while hardneck garlic keeps 4 to
6 months. Inspect stored garlic monthly and remove any heads showing
mold or sprouting immediately to prevent spread.

## Onions: Day-Length Types

Onions (Allium cepa) are the most day-length-sensitive common vegetable.
The trigger for bulb formation is not temperature or age but the number
of daylight hours. Choosing the correct day-length type for your
latitude is the single most critical decision in onion growing.

Short-day onions begin forming bulbs at 10 to 12 hours of daylight and
are suited to the southern United States (zones 7 to 10). Long-day
onions need 14 to 16 hours and grow in the northern United States and
Canada (zones 3 to 6). Day-neutral (intermediate-day) onions form bulbs
at approximately 12 to 14 hours and adapt to a wide middle range (zones
5 to 9), making them the safest choice for gardeners unsure of their
exact requirements.

Planting the wrong day-length type produces lush green tops that never
bulb, or premature bolting. As a rule of thumb, gardeners above 35
degrees latitude should grow long-day or day-neutral varieties, while
those below 35 degrees should choose short-day types. Day-neutral
varieties like Candy and Super Star are the most forgiving across
different locations.

## Onions: Planting Methods

Onions can be planted from sets, transplants, or seed, each with
distinct advantages. Sets are small dormant bulbs grown from seed the
previous year. They are the easiest method for beginners and produce the
quickest results, but they offer limited variety selection, tend to
produce smaller bulbs, and have a higher bolt rate. Sets larger than
three-quarters of an inch are especially prone to bolting and should be
planted close together and harvested as green onions.

Transplants are young onion plants grown from seed in a nursery, 8 to 12
weeks old. They offer wider variety selection, produce larger bulbs, and
bolt less frequently than sets. Trim tops to 4 inches and roots to 2
inches before planting. Space 4 to 6 inches apart in rows 12 to 18
inches apart.

Direct seeding offers the largest variety selection and the strongest,
most disease-resistant plants, but it is the slowest method. Start seeds
indoors 10 to 12 weeks before transplanting, or direct sow in the garden
as early as soil can be worked. Seed-grown onions store significantly
better than set-grown onions, making this the preferred method for
serious growers.

## Onions: Growing and Care

Onions are heavy nitrogen feeders, and consistent moisture is critical.
They have very shallow root systems and cannot tolerate drought during
bulb formation. Provide 1 to 1.5 inches of water per week, keeping soil
evenly moist but not waterlogged. Mulch with straw to retain moisture and
suppress weeds, which compete aggressively with onions.

Fertilize with a nitrogen-rich source every 2 to 3 weeks during the
active growing period. Stop fertilizing when bulbs begin to swell and
the neck starts to soften, typically 4 to 6 weeks before expected
harvest. Excess nitrogen late in the season produces thick necks and
poor storage quality.

## Onions: Harvesting and Storage

Harvest onions when foliage yellows and falls over naturally. Do not bend
or break the necks manually, as this can introduce disease. Lift bulbs
on a dry day and leave them on the ground for several days to dry in the
sun. If rain threatens, move them to a covered, well-ventilated area.

Cure onions for 1 to 2 weeks in a warm, dry location with good airflow.
Proper curing is essential for long storage. Once cured, the neck should
be completely dry and the outer skin papery. Store in a cool, dry,
well-ventilated area. Hanging in mesh bags or braiding (for softneck
types) works well. Storage life varies by variety from 1 to 8 months.
Pungent varieties generally store longer than sweet varieties.

## Leeks: Planting and Cultivation

Leeks (Allium porrum) are among the most valuable winter vegetables,
providing fresh harvests when little else is available in the garden.
They are hardy in all but the coldest climates and can be left in the
ground through winter, lifted as needed.

Leeks are typically started from seed in mid-spring, either in a
dedicated seedbed or in trays. When seedlings reach pencil thickness in
early summer, they are transplanted to their permanent positions. The
classic dibber method involves making holes 6 to 8 inches deep and 6
inches apart, dropping a trimmed seedling into each hole, and watering to
settle soil around the roots without filling the hole. This technique
produces long blanched stems without the labor of hilling.

Eliot Coleman refined this approach at Four Season Farm using a
36-inch-long dibber with a depth-stop plate 9 inches above the tip. This
tool consistently produces 9-inch blanched shanks with minimal effort.
Leek transplants are grown on the greenhouse floor in seedbeds rather
than in soil blocks, and are planted out when at least 10 inches tall.

As leeks grow, gradually earth up soil around the base to increase the
length of the blanched white stem. Take care to prevent soil from
getting between the leaves, as grit trapped inside is difficult to wash
out at harvest. Leeks can remain in the ground through winter; if soil
freezes solid, dig a few and store them in moist peat or sand.

## Shallots and Chives

Shallots (Allium cepa var. aggregatum) are grown like small onions but
each set produces a cluster of 6 to 12 bulbs rather than a single large
bulb. Plant sets in late winter or early spring, pushing them into soil
so half the bulb is visible. Space 6 to 9 inches apart. Harvest when
foliage yellows in midsummer. Shallots have a finer, more complex flavor
than onions and are prized in French cuisine. They store well for 6 to 8
months when properly cured.

Chives (Allium schoenoprasum) are the most low-maintenance perennial
allium. They grow in clumps of narrow hollow leaves and produce purple
pom-pom flowers in late spring. Chives are hardy from zones 3 to 9,
tolerate partial shade, and can be harvested repeatedly throughout the
growing season by cutting the leaves near ground level. They spread
slowly by division and rarely require replacement once established.
Garlic chives (Allium tuberosum) offer a similar profile with flat
leaves and white flowers, carrying a mild garlic flavor.

## Pest and Disease Management

All allium crops share vulnerability to several key pests and diseases.
White rot (Sclerotium cepivorum) is the most devastating, persisting in
soil for 15 to 20 years. Strict crop rotation is the only effective
management strategy. Onion fly maggots attack roots, especially in
rotation-heavy soils; floating row covers and companion planting with
carrots can reduce damage. Thrips are tiny sucking insects that streak
leaves with silver; insecticidal soap provides control.

Onion downy mildew and purple blotch are fungal diseases promoted by
cool, wet conditions and poor air circulation. Adequate spacing, raised
beds, and avoiding overhead watering minimize risk. Remove and destroy
infected plant material rather than composting it.

The pungent sulfur compounds in alliums provide natural pest deterrence,
making them valuable companion plants. Interplanting garlic among
tomatoes, peppers, or brassicas can reduce aphid and cabbage moth
pressure. Onions and carrots are a classic beneficial pairing, as each
repels the other's primary pest.

## Perennial and Alternative Alliums

Beyond the standard annual crops, several perennial alliums merit
attention for the home gardener. Potato onions (Allium cepa var.
aggregatum) are a perennial multiplier onion that replants itself from
small bulb offsets. Walking onions (Allium proliferum) produce bulbils at
the top of their stalks that bend to the ground and root, literally
walking across the garden. Both types produce smaller bulbs than standard
onions but require virtually no annual planting effort once established.

Egyptian walking onions are extremely cold-hardy and pest-resistant,
making them an excellent low-maintenance crop. The bulbils can be
harvested for cooking or allowed to root for next year's crop. The
green tops can be used like scallions throughout the growing season.
