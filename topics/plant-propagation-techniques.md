# Plant Propagation Techniques

Plant propagation is the deliberate creation of new plants from existing
parent stock. It encompasses two fundamental strategies: sexual
propagation through seeds, which generates genetic diversity through
recombination, and asexual or vegetative propagation, which produces
clones genetically identical to the parent.

Sexual propagation introduces variability — the raw material of evolution
and plant breeding — but specific traits are not reliably passed on. A
seed from a Delicious apple will not produce a Delicious apple tree.
Asexual methods preserve exact combinations of desirable characteristics
such as fruit quality, disease resistance, or ornamental form.

Understanding when and why to use each approach is central to
successful plant production at any scale, from a windowsill herb garden
to a commercial nursery producing millions of disease-free plants by
tissue culture.

## History of Propagation

The history of propagation mirrors the history of agriculture itself.
Seed saving began roughly 11,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent as
early farmers selected and replanted seeds from the most productive
cereal plants. Ancient Egyptian, Chinese, and Greek texts all describe
vegetative propagation methods for figs, grapes, and olives.

Roman agricultural writers including Cato and Columella documented
grafting techniques in detail. Medieval monasteries preserved fruit
varieties through grafting, keeping cultivars alive that might otherwise
have been lost. The scientific understanding of why grafting works — the
role of the cambium layer — awaited the development of plant anatomy in
the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

The twentieth century brought tissue culture and micropropagation,
enabling mass production of disease-free stock in sterile laboratories.
Today, automated grafting robots in commercial nurseries can join
thousands of rootstock-scion pairs per day, while home gardeners still
use techniques unchanged for millennia.

## Seed Propagation Fundamentals

Growing plants from seed is the most common and economical form of
propagation. Seeds contain the complete genetic blueprint for a new
plant and are the only practical method for many species, including most
annual vegetables, grains, and many herbs.

### Advantages and Limitations

Seeds offer large numbers of plants from minimal material, genetic
diversity valuable for adaptation and breeding, low cost per plant, and
excellent storage longevity. However, seeds do not preserve the exact
genetics of hybrid parents — F1 hybrids do not breed true. Some species
have long juvenile periods before flowering or fruiting, and others are
simply difficult or slow from seed.

### Seed Dormancy and Treatment

Many seeds possess dormancy mechanisms that prevent germination until
conditions are favourable. Three primary treatments address these
mechanisms.

**Stratification** mimics winter by providing cold, moist treatment.
Place seeds in moist sand or a damp paper towel inside a sealed plastic
bag and refrigerate for 30 to 120 days. Apples, peaches, cherries,
pawpaw, and many native perennials require stratification.

**Scarification** breaks or weakens the seed coat to allow water
absorption. Nick the seed coat with a file or sandpaper, or soak in hot
but not boiling water for 12 to 24 hours. Morning glories, locust,
redbud, and many legumes benefit from scarification.

**Soaking** for 6 to 24 hours before planting speeds germination in
species with naturally permeable seed coats. Beans, peas, corn, beets,
and squash respond well to soaking.

## Seed Starting Methods

Seeds may be sown directly outdoors or started indoors for later
transplanting. The choice depends on the species, climate, and intended
harvest timing.

### Direct Sowing

Direct sowing is the cheapest method and is preferred for root
vegetables like carrots and parsnips that resent transplanting, as well
as fast-growing crops like beans, squash, and radishes. Prepare a firm,
fine-tilth seedbed, sow thinly, cover with soil, and label rows.
