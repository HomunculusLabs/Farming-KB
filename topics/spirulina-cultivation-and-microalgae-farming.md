---# Spirulina Cultivation and Microalgae Farming

Spirulina (*Arthrospira platensis* and *Arthrospira maxima*) is a filamentous cyanobacterium that has been consumed as food for centuries — from the Aztecs harvesting it from Lake Texcoco to the Kanembu people of Chad who have gathered *dihe* from Lake Chad for over a thousand years. Today, spirulina is one of the most commercially significant microalgae, prized for its exceptional nutritional density, rapid growth rate, and relatively straightforward cultivation requirements.

## Cultivation Methods

### 1. Open Raceway Ponds

The most common and economical method for spirulina production, used by both commercial operations and home cultivators.

**Design**: Shallow channels (15–30 cm deep) arranged in oval raceways, mixed by paddle wheels that circulate the culture at 15–30 cm/second. Ponds are typically 0.1–0.5 hectares for commercial scale; 1–10 m² for home systems.

**Advantages**:
- Low capital cost ($5–15/m² for lined ponds)
- Simple construction and maintenance
- Natural sunlight provides free energy
- Easy to scale

**Disadvantages**:
- Vulnerable to weather and contamination
- Lower productivity than closed systems (15–25 g/m²/day)
- Significant evaporative water loss
- Limited control over environmental conditions

**Construction**:
- Food-grade PVC liner or HDPE geomembrane (never use PVC with plasticizers for food production)
- White or light-colored liner reflects light into the culture
- Paddle wheel driven by electric motor, solar panel, or manual crank for small systems
- Inlet/outlet plumbing with fine mesh screens (100–200 μm) to prevent loss

### 2. Closed Photobioreactors (PBRs)

Tubular, flat-panel, or bag systems that enclose the culture, allowing precise [[query-best-light-schedule-for-cannabis]] penetration.
- **Vertical bag/column systems**: Suspended polyethylene bags, common for lab-scale and nursery cultures.

**Advantages**:
- Higher productivity (25–35 g/m²/day)
- Better contamination control
- Reduced CO₂ off-gassing
- Can be operated indoors with artificial light
- Consistent year-round production

**Disadvantages**:
- High capital cost ($100–500/m²)
- Technical complexity (O₂ management, biofouling, temperature control)
- Energy-intensive for pumping and mixing
- Limited scalability compared to ponds

### 3. Home Cultivation Systems

Spirulina is uniquely suited to small-scale home production. A functional setup can be built for $50–200.

**Basic home setup**:
- 20–100 L food-grade container (glass aquarium, food storage tote, or custom acrylic tank)
- Gentle air pump with air stone for mixing and CO₂ delivery
- pH meter or test strips (pH 8.5–10.5)
- Harvesting cloth (50 μm mesh silk screen or nylon)
- Starter culture from reputable supplier ($15–30)
- Nutrient medium ingredients

**Lighting**: Direct sunlight for 8–12 hours daily, or LED grow lights (6,500 K full-spectrum). Avoid prolonged intense direct sun above 35°C as it can photo-oxidize the culture. A south-facing window with supplemental LED works well in temperate climates.

## Culture Medium and Water Chemistry

### Zarrouk's Medium

The standard medium for spirulina cultivation, developed by C. Zarrouk in 1966:

| Component | Concentration (g/L) | Role |
|---|---|---|
| NaHCO₃ (sodium bicarbonate) | 16.8 | Carbon source, alkalinity buffer |
| Na₂CO₃ (sodium carbonate) | 4.0 | pH buffer |
| K₂HPO₄ | 0.5 | Phosphorus source |
| KNO₃ | 2.0 | Nitrogen source |
| K₂SO₄ | 1.0 | Potassium, sulfur |
| NaCl | 1.0 | Sodium, chloride |
| MgSO₄·7H₂O | 0.2 | Magnesium |
| CaCl₂·2H₂O | 0.04 | Calcium |
| FeSO₄·7H₂O | 0.01 | Iron |
| EDTA | 0.08 | Iron chelator |
| A5 micronutrient solution | 1 mL/L | Trace metals (Mn, Zn, Cu, Mo, B) |

### Simplified Home Medium

For home cultivators, a simplified medium using readily available ingredients:

- **Baking soda** (sodium bicarbonate): 16 g/L
- **Washing soda** (sodium carbonate): 4 g/L (or make by heating baking soda at 200°C for 1 hour)
