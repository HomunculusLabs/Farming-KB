# Spirulina Cultivation and Microalgae Farming

Spirulina (*Arthrospira platensis* and *Arthrospira maxima*) is a filamentous cyanobacterium that has been consumed as food for centuries — from the Aztecs harvesting it from Lake Texcoco to the Kanembu people of Chad who have gathered *dihe* from Lake Chad for over a thousand years. Today, spirulina is one of the most commercially significant microalgae, prized for its exceptional nutritional density, rapid growth rate, and relatively straightforward cultivation requirements.

This page covers the biology, cultivation methods, harvesting, processing, and integration of spirulina and other microalgae into permaculture and homesteading systems.

## What Is Spirulina?

Despite being commonly called a blue-green alga, spirulina is a cyanobacterium — a photosynthetic prokaryote. It forms multicellular filaments (trichomes) that coil into distinctive helical spirals, giving it its name. It thrives in highly alkaline environments (pH 8.5–11) where few competing organisms can survive, making open-pond cultivation relatively contamination-resistant.

### Nutritional Profile

Spirulina is one of the most nutrient-dense foods on Earth:

| Component | Per 100g Dry Weight | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Protein | 55–70 g | Complete protein with all essential amino acids |
| Carbohydrates | 15–25 g | Primarily glycogen and rhamnose |
| Lipids | 6–8 g | Rich in gamma-linolenic acid (GLA) |
| Phycocyanin | 10–15 g | Potent antioxidant, blue pigment |
| Beta-carotene | 1,700–3,400 mcg | Provitamin A |
| Vitamin B12 | 0.5–2.5 mcg | Mostly pseudovitamin B12; bioavailability debated |
| Iron | 28–150 mg | Non-heme iron |
| Calcium | 120–1,200 mg | Variable by strain and medium |
| Magnesium | 190–390 mg | |
| Phycocyanin | Unique | Anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective |

Spirulina contains 18 amino acids including all 9 essential ones, with particularly high levels of leucine, valine, and isoleucine. Its protein digestibility is rated at 83–90%, comparable to meat.

## History of Human Use

- **Pre-Columbian era**: Aztecs harvested *tecuitlatl* from Lake Texcoco in the Valley of Mexico, drying it into cakes for trade and consumption.
- **Chad, West Africa**: Kanembu people harvest *dihe* from Lake Chad and smaller alkaline lakes, sun-drying and mixing it into sauces. Annual harvest is estimated at 40 tonnes.
- **1940**: French phycologist Dangeard identified the dihe organism as a filamentous cyanobacterium.
- **1967**: The International Association of Applied Microbiology declared spirulina "the best food for tomorrow" at their meeting in Paris.
- **1974**: UN World Food Conference named spirulina the "ideal food for mankind."
- **1980s–present**: Commercial cultivation expanded globally, with major production in China, India, the United States, and Myanmar.

## Cultivation Methods

### 1. Open Raceway Ponds

The most common and economical method for spirulina production, used by both commercial operations and home cultivators.

**Design**: Shallow channels (15–30 cm deep) arranged in oval raceways, mixed by paddle wheels that circulate the culture at 15–30 cm/second. Ponds are typically 0.1–0.5 hectares for commercial scale; 1–10 m² for home systems.

**Advantages**:
- Low capital cost ($5–15/m² for lined ponds)
- Simple construction and maintenance
- Natural sunlight provides free energy
- Easy to scale

**Disadvantages**:
- Vulnerable to weather and contamination
- Lower productivity than closed systems (15–25 g/m²/day)
- Significant evaporative water loss
- Limited control over environmental conditions

**Construction**:
- Food-grade PVC liner or HDPE geomembrane (never use PVC with plasticizers for food production)
- White or light-colored liner reflects light into the culture
- Paddle wheel driven by electric motor, solar panel, or manual crank for small systems
- Inlet/outlet plumbing with fine mesh screens (100–200 μm) to prevent loss

### 2. Closed Photobioreactors (PBRs)

Tubular, flat-panel, or bag systems that enclose the culture, allowing precise environmental control.

**Types**:
- **Tubular PBRs**: Transparent tubes (horizontal, vertical, or helical) with pump circulation. Surface area 50–200 m² typical.
- **Flat-panel PBRs**: Thin rectangular chambers (2–5 cm thick) with high surface-to-volume ratio. Best light penetration.
- **Vertical bag/column systems**: Suspended polyethylene bags, common for lab-scale and nursery cultures.

**Advantages**:
- Higher productivity (25–35 g/m²/day)
- Better contamination control
- Reduced CO₂ off-gassing
- Can be operated indoors with artificial light
- Consistent year-round production

**Disadvantages**:
- High capital cost ($100–500/m²)
- Technical complexity (O₂ management, biofouling, temperature control)
- Energy-intensive for pumping and mixing
- Limited scalability compared to ponds

### 3. Home Cultivation Systems

Spirulina is uniquely suited to small-scale home production. A functional setup can be built for $50–200.

**Basic home setup**:
- 20–100 L food-grade container (glass aquarium, food storage tote, or custom acrylic tank)
- Gentle air pump with air stone for mixing and CO₂ delivery
- pH meter or test strips (pH 8.5–10.5)
- Harvesting cloth (50 μm mesh silk screen or nylon)
- Starter culture from reputable supplier ($15–30)
- Nutrient medium ingredients

**Lighting**: Direct sunlight for 8–12 hours daily, or LED grow lights (6,500 K full-spectrum). Avoid prolonged intense direct sun above 35°C as it can photo-oxidize the culture. A south-facing window with supplemental LED works well in temperate climates.

## Culture Medium and Water Chemistry

### Zarrouk's Medium

The standard medium for spirulina cultivation, developed by C. Zarrouk in 1966:

| Component | Concentration (g/L) | Role |
|---|---|---|
| NaHCO₃ (sodium bicarbonate) | 16.8 | Carbon source, alkalinity buffer |
| Na₂CO₃ (sodium carbonate) | 4.0 | pH buffer |
| K₂HPO₄ | 0.5 | Phosphorus source |
| KNO₃ | 2.0 | Nitrogen source |
| K₂SO₄ | 1.0 | Potassium, sulfur |
| NaCl | 1.0 | Sodium, chloride |
| MgSO₄·7H₂O | 0.2 | Magnesium |
| CaCl₂·2H₂O | 0.04 | Calcium |
| FeSO₄·7H₂O | 0.01 | Iron |
| EDTA | 0.08 | Iron chelator |
| A5 micronutrient solution | 1 mL/L | Trace metals (Mn, Zn, Cu, Mo, B) |

### Simplified Home Medium

For home cultivators, a simplified medium using readily available ingredients:

- **Baking soda** (sodium bicarbonate): 16 g/L
- **Washing soda** (sodium carbonate): 4 g/L (or make by heating baking soda at 200°C for 1 hour)
- **Sea salt**: 1 g/L (provides trace minerals)
- **Potassium nitrate** (saltpeter): 2 g/L
- **Potassium phosphate**: 0.5 g/L
- **Epsom salt** (magnesium sulfate): 0.3 g/L
- **Iron supplement**: A few drops of liquid iron or a rusty nail in the tank (seriously — this traditional method works)

### Water Quality Parameters

| Parameter | Optimal Range | Critical Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 30–38°C | 20°C min growth; >42°C lethal |
| pH | 9.0–10.5 | <8.5: contamination risk; >11: growth inhibition |
| Salinity | 20–70 g/L | Highly tolerant; used to exclude competitors |
| Dissolved oxygen | 4–8 mg/L | >10 mg/L causes photo-oxidative damage |
| Light intensity | 3,000–6,000 lux | >10,000 lux: photoinhibition |
| Light:dark cycle | 12:12 to 16:8 | Continuous light OK with adequate mixing |
| Culture depth | 15–25 cm | Deeper: light limitation; shallower: temperature fluctuation |

### pH Management

Maintaining pH is critical. Spirulina consumes CO₂ (as bicarbonate) during photosynthesis, which raises pH. When pH exceeds 10.5, growth slows and the culture may crash.

**To raise pH**: Add sodium bicarbonate or sodium carbonate.
**To lower pH**: Add CO₂ (bubbling or carbonated water), or allow more dark time for respiratory CO₂ release. Some cultivators add dilute vinegar or citric acid in emergencies.

A good rule of thumb: if the culture is growing vigorously (turning deep green-blue and thickening), pH will rise 0.2–0.5 units per day. Plan to supplement CO₂ or harvest regularly.

## Growth Cycle and Monitoring

### Typical Growth Timeline

1. **Inoculation (Day 0)**: Add starter culture at 10–20% of final volume. Color should be visibly green.
2. **Lag phase (Days 1–3)**: Little visible change. Cells adapt to new medium.
3. **Exponential phase (Days 4–14)**: Rapid growth. Culture darkens noticeably each day. Optical density (OD at 560 nm) doubles every 2–4 days.
4. **Stationary phase (Day 15+)**: Growth slows as nutrients are depleted and self-shading increases. Time to harvest.

### Monitoring Indicators

- **Color**: Healthy spirulina is deep blue-green. Yellow-green indicates nutrient deficiency. Brown or black indicates culture crash.
- **Odor**: Fresh, slightly grassy/seaweed smell. Foul or rotten egg (H₂S) smell indicates anaerobic conditions — mix immediately.
- **Foam**: Some foam is normal during mixing. Excessive persistent foam may indicate contamination.
- **Clumping**: Spirulina should be uniformly suspended. Large clumps or strings suggest contamination or nutrient imbalance.
- **pH drift**: Steady rise confirms active photosynthesis. Sudden drop may indicate bacterial contamination.

### Growth Rate

Under optimal conditions, spirulina can double its biomass every 2–4 days. Practical harvest rates for home systems:

| System Size | Daily Harvest (wet weight) | Monthly Dry Yield |
|---|---|---|
| 20 L tank | 5–10 g | 15–30 g |
| 50 L tank | 15–25 g | 45–75 g |
| 100 L tank | 30–50 g | 90–150 g |
| 1 m² pond (200 L) | 60–100 g | 180–300 g |

Dry weight is approximately 8–10% of wet weight.

## Harvesting

### Filtration (Home Scale)

The most practical method for small-scale cultivation:

1. **Screen harvest**: Pour culture through a 50 μm mesh cloth (silk screen, nylon organdy, or specialized spirulina harvest screen).
2. **Rinse**: Gently rinse the collected biomass with fresh water to remove residual medium (salty/alkaline taste).
3. **Press**: Fold cloth and press gently to remove excess water. The collected paste is ~15–20% solids.
4. **Consume fresh**: Fresh spirulina paste can be eaten immediately, stored in refrigerator for 3–5 days, or frozen for months.

**Important**: Never consume unwashed spirulina straight from culture — the alkaline medium is unpalatable and can cause stomach upset.

### Centrifugation (Commercial Scale)

Industrial operations use continuous-flow decanter centrifuges or disk-stack centrifuges that achieve 15–20% solids concentration. Cost: $10,000–100,000+ for commercial equipment.

### Drying

- **Sun drying**: Spread thin layer on food-grade surface in full sun. Fastest but causes significant phycocyanin and carotene degradation (30–50% loss).
- **Shade drying**: Better nutrient retention. 24–48 hours depending on humidity.
- **Dehydrator**: Best for home use. Set to 40–45°C (never above 50°C to preserve heat-sensitive compounds). 6–12 hours.
- **Spray drying**: Industry standard for powder production. Instantaneous drying preserves most nutrients but equipment is expensive ($50,000+).
- **Freeze drying**: Highest quality retention but prohibitively expensive for most operations.

## Other Microalgae of Interest

### Chlorella (*Chlorella vulgaris*, *C. pyrenoidosa*)

- Single-celled green alga, 2–10 μm diameter
- 50–60% protein, rich in chlorophyll
- Hard cell wall requires mechanical disruption for bioavailability
- Cultivated in similar conditions to spirulina but prefers lower pH (6.5–7.5)
- Widely available as supplement; popular in Japan (chlorella tablets)
- Easier to culture in closed systems due to smaller size

### Nannochloropsis (*Nannochloropsis gaditana*, *N. oculata*)

- Marine microalga rich in EPA omega-3 fatty acids (30–40% of lipids)
- Used primarily for aquaculture feed (rotifer and copepod enrichment)
- Requires seawater or artificial seawater medium
- Oil content 25–35% dry weight — promising for biodiesel
- Cultivated in photobioreactors; not suitable for open ponds due to contamination vulnerability

### Haematococcus (*Haematococcus pluvialis*)

- Freshwater green alga that produces astaxanthin (up to 4% dry weight)
- Astaxanthin is the most powerful natural antioxidant known (6,000× stronger than vitamin C)
- Two-stage cultivation: green growth phase → stress phase (high light, nutrient starvation) to trigger astaxanthin accumulation
- Cell wall very tough; requires mechanical disruption
- High-value product ($2,000–5,000/kg astaxanthin)
- Challenging to cultivate; slow growth rate

### Dunaliella salina

- Halotolerant green alga that accumulates beta-carotene (up to 14% dry weight)
- Grows in extremely saline water (up to 35% NaCl — nearly saturated brine)
- Commercially cultivated in large evaporation ponds in Australia and Israel
- Natural source of mixed carotenoids (beta-carotene, lutein, zeaxanthin)

## Integration into Permaculture Systems

### Nutrient Looping

Spirulina cultivation can close nutrient loops on a homestead:

- **Waste CO₂**: Connect to compost heating systems or mushroom fruiting chambers that produce CO₂
- **Waste heat**: Use thermal mass from greenhouses or solar water heaters to maintain optimal temperature
- **Nutrient source**: Digestate from [[biogas-digesters-and-anaerobic-digestion-systems]] can provide nitrogen and phosphorus after sterilization
- **Spent medium**: After harvesting, the alkaline medium can be diluted and used as a foliar spray (dilute 1:10–1:20) for pest control, or neutralized for irrigation
- **Integration with [[aquaponics-and-backyard-aquaculture]]**: Fish wastewater provides nitrogen; spirulina can supplement fish feed

### Water Recycling

Spirulina cultivation is water-intensive (approximately 2,000–3,000 L water per kg dry product in open ponds), but:

- Evaporative losses can be captured with greenhouse enclosures
- Spent medium can be recycled by replenishing consumed nutrients (nitrogen, bicarbonate)
- In humid climates, condensation from greenhouse covers can be collected and returned

### Multi-Trophic Systems

- **Spirulina → Daphnia → Fish**: Natural food chain in a single water system
- **Spirulina → Black soldier fly larvae**: Use spirulina paste as supplemental feed for [[black-soldier-fly-farming-and-insect-protein]]
- **Spirulina → Compost**: Spent culture or dead batches can be composted — high nitrogen content accelerates decomposition

## Safety and Contamination

### Common Contaminants

| Contaminant | Identification | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| *Chlorella* sp. | Green specks that pass through 50 μm mesh | Maintain pH >9.5; spirulina outcompetes at high alkalinity |
| Diatoms | Brown tint; gritty texture | Maintain pH >9.0; reduce silica in medium |
| Mosquito larvae | Visible worms in culture | Use fine mesh covers; 200 μm screen on inlets |
| Bacterial contamination | Foul odor; cloudy culture; pH drops | Maintain sterile startup; avoid cross-contamination from tools |
| Mold/fungi | Floating fuzzy patches | Remove immediately; improve air circulation; reduce organic matter in medium |

### Microcystin Risk

Microcystins are potent hepatotoxins produced by some cyanobacteria (primarily *Microcystis aeruginosa*, not *Arthrospira*). However:

- Spirulina cultivated at pH >9.5 is inherently resistant to microcystin-producing species, which prefer pH 7–9
- Contamination is primarily a concern in harvested wild spirulina from natural lakes
- Buy tested commercial spirulina; home-grown spirulina from properly maintained cultures is generally safe
- Test strips for microcystins are available ($10–15 per test) for peace of mind

### Heavy Metal Accumulation

Spirulina can bioaccumulate heavy metals from contaminated water or medium. Use only food-grade water and reagent-grade or food-grade salts for the culture medium. Avoid using tap water in areas with known lead or arsenic contamination without filtration.

## Economic Analysis

### Home Scale (20–100 L)

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Tank/container | $10–50 |
| Air pump + tubing | $15–30 |
| pH meter | $10–50 |
| Harvest screen | $5–15 |
| Starter culture | $15–30 |
| Nutrients (annual) | $20–40 |
| **Total startup** | **$75–215** |
| **Annual operating cost** | **$20–50** |
| **Annual dry yield** | **200–1,500 g** |
| **Equivalent commercial cost** | **$80–600** |

Home cultivation breaks even within the first year and provides fresh spirulina with superior nutrient retention compared to dried supplements.

### Commercial Scale (>1,000 m²)

Commercial spirulina farming requires significant investment but can be profitable:

- Land and pond construction: $50,000–200,000
- Harvesting and drying equipment: $30,000–150,000
- Operating costs: $15–30/kg dry product
- Wholesale price: $30–80/kg
- Retail price: $80–200/kg for organic spirulina powder
- Break-even: Typically 2–4 years at 50+ tonnes/year production

Organic certification commands a significant price premium and is achievable since spirulina requires no pesticides and minimal inputs beyond bicarbonate and nutrients.

## Regulatory Considerations

- **United States**: Spirulina is GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) by the FDA. Commercial producers need food facility registration and GMP compliance.
- **European Union**: Approved as a novel food ingredient with specific purity standards (Regulation 2017/2470).
- **Organic certification**: Available through USDA NOP, EU organic, and other certifying bodies. Requires organic-approved nutrients and no synthetic processing aids.
- **Home production**: No regulations restrict growing spirulina for personal use. However, selling spirulina products requires compliance with local food safety regulations.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Culture turns yellow | Nitrogen or iron deficiency | Add KNO₃ (0.5 g/L) or iron supplement |
| Culture won't grow | Temperature too low | Add heater; move to warmer location; insulate tank |
| Foul smell | Anaerobic conditions | Increase aeration/mixing; reduce culture density |
| White/gray patches | Calcium precipitation | Reduce Ca²⁺ in medium; lower pH slightly |
| Culture crashes overnight | Contamination or pH spike | Maintain pH <10.5; check for contamination; restart with fresh culture |
| Thin, pale culture | Insufficient light | Increase light exposure; reduce culture depth |
| Excessive foaming | Organic contamination or over-aeration | Reduce air flow; check for contamination |
| Filaments clumping | Nutrient imbalance or dead culture | Check pH and nutrients; restart if necessary |
