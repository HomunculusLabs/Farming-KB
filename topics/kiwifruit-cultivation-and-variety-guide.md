# Kiwifruit Cultivation and Variety Guide

## Overview

Kiwifruit (*Actinidia* spp.) are vigorous, deciduous climbing vines native to East Asia, primarily China. The genus encompasses over 60 species, ranging from the familiar fuzzy green kiwi (*A. deliciosa*) to smooth-skinned golden varieties (*A. chinensis*), cold-hardy species (*A. arguta*, *A. kolomikta*), and even ornamental types. Kiwifruit are exceptionally rich in vitamin C, vitamin K, folate, and dietary fiber, making them both a nutritional powerhouse and a rewarding garden crop. Their climbing habit makes them ideal for permaculture food forests, vertical garden structures, and trellis-based orchard systems.

## Botany and Species

### The Genus *Actinidia*

*Actinidia* belongs to the family Actinidiaceae and is native to temperate and subtropical regions of East Asia, with the center of diversity in southwestern China. The genus is dioecious—most species produce separate male and female plants—though a few partially self-fertile cultivars have been developed.

Key species in cultivation:

- **Actinidia deliciosa** — The classic "fuzzy kiwi." Large, brown-fuzzy fruit with green flesh. Native to the mountains of eastern China. Most commercially significant species. Hardy to roughly USDA Zone 7–8 (–5 to –10°C with dormancy).
- **Actinidia chinensis** — Smooth-skinned, often golden-fleshed kiwifruit. Sweeter and less acidic than *A. deliciosa*. Includes the popular Sungold (Gold3) cultivar. Slightly less cold-hardy; best in Zones 8–9.
- **Actinidia arguta** — Hardy kiwi or "kiwi berry." Small (grape-sized), smooth-skinned, eaten whole. Extremely cold-hardy (USDA Zones 3–7). Fast-growing and prolific.
- **Actinidia kolomikta** — Arctic beauty kiwi. The most cold-hardy species (USDA Zones 3–5). Smaller fruit than *A. arguta*, but intensely flavored and very early ripening. Notable for its striking pink-and-white variegated foliage.
- **Actinidia polygama** — Silver vine kiwi. Known for its cat-attractant properties (actinidine). Edible fruit, used in traditional Japanese medicine. Hardy to Zone 4.
- **Actinidia eriantha** — White-fleshed kiwi with exceptionally high vitamin C content. Used commercially as a parent for new cultivars. Hardy to Zone 7.

### Growth Habit

All *Actinidia* species are woody, twining vines that require structural support. Under ideal conditions, a mature vine can grow 3–6 meters per year. The root system is extensive and relatively shallow, with most feeder roots in the top 30–60 cm of soil. Kiwifruit vines can live and produce for 50+ years when well-managed.

## Site Selection and Climate Requirements

### Temperature

- **Fuzzy kiwi (*A. deliciosa*):** Requires 600–800 chilling hours (7°C or below). Winter lows to –10°C are tolerated with dormancy; late spring frosts can kill flowers. Summer heat above 35°C causes sunburn and reduces fruit quality.
- **Hardy kiwi (*A. arguta*):** Extremely cold-tolerant; survives winters to –30°C (Zone 4). Requires 300–500 chilling hours. Less heat-tolerant than fuzzy kiwi.
- **Arctic kiwi (*A. kolomikta*):** Most cold-hardy, surviving to –40°C (Zone 3). Shortest growing season requirement.
- **Golden kiwi (*A. chinensis*):** Least cold-hardy; best suited to mild winter regions (Zone 8–9). Sensitive to frost damage during bloom.

### Sunlight

Full sun (6–8+ hours direct) is required for optimal fruit production. In hot climates (Zones 8–9), afternoon shade can reduce heat stress. Hardy kiwi can tolerate partial shade but will produce less fruit.

### Soil Requirements

- **Texture:** Well-drained loam is ideal. Kiwifruit tolerate sandy loam to clay loam but suffer in heavy clay or pure sand.
- **pH:** 5.5–7.0 (slightly acidic to neutral).
- **Drainage:** Critical. Waterlogged soil causes root rot (Phytophthora). Avoid planting in low-lying areas or heavy clay without amendment.
- **Organic matter:** High organic matter (3–5%) supports the extensive feeder root network. Incorporate compost before planting.
- **Salinity:** Kiwifruit are moderately salt-sensitive. Avoid sites near coastal salt spray or saline irrigation water.

### Wind Protection

Kiwifruit vines are highly susceptible to wind damage, especially during spring growth and fruiting. Strong winds can break young shoots, damage flowers, and scar fruit. Plant windbreaks (evergreen hedgerows, bamboo, or structural barriers) on prevailing wind sides.

## Planting

### Timing

Plant bare-root vines in late winter to early spring (while dormant). Container-grown plants can be planted spring through early fall, avoiding peak summer heat.

### Spacing

- **Fuzzy kiwi:** 3–5 m between vines in-row, 4–6 m between rows.
- **Hardy kiwi:** 2–3 m between vines, 3–4 m between rows.
- **Arctic kiwi:** 1.5–2.5 m between vines, 2.5–3.5 m between rows.

### Pollination Planning

Most kiwifruit are dioecious and require at least one male pollinator for every 6–8 female vines. Plant the male within 8–10 m of the females for effective bee pollination. Some key considerations:

- **Timing is critical:** Male and female flowers must bloom simultaneously. Match cultivars by bloom time.
- **Self-fertile cultivars:** 'Jenny' (fuzzy), 'Issai' (hardy kiwi — partially self-fertile but sets better with a male), and 'Ken's Red' (hardy) are exceptions but still benefit from a pollinator.
- **Bee activity:** Ensure adequate pollinator habitat nearby. Kiwifruit flowers are not highly attractive to bees; competition from other spring-blooming flowers can reduce pollination rates.

### Planting Process

1. Dig a hole 60 cm wide and 60 cm deep.
2. Mix excavated soil with compost or well-rotted manure (1:1 ratio).
3. Plant at the same depth as the nursery container or root crown. Do not bury the graft union.
4. Water thoroughly after planting; apply 5–10 cm of organic mulch (keep mulch 10 cm away from the trunk).
5. Install a temporary stake for support until the vine reaches the permanent trellis.

## Trellising and Training Systems

### Trellis Design

Kiwifruit require strong, permanent support structures. A mature fuzzy kiwi vine can weigh 50–100 kg. Common trellis systems include:

**T-Bar (Clothesline) Trellis:**
- Two 2.4 m posts, 2 m apart, with a 1.8 m cross-arm at the top.
- Two or three wires stretched along the cross-arms.
- Most common commercial system; allows good light penetration.
- Suitable for both fuzzy and hardy kiwi.

**Pergola / Overhead Trellis:**
- Sturdy posts (2.5–3 m tall) supporting a wire grid at the top.
- Vines trained across the overhead grid.
- Creates shaded understory (useful in permaculture).
- Higher installation cost but excellent light distribution.

**Fence / Wall Trellis:**
- Wire strung along existing fences or walls.
- Good for home gardens with limited space.
- Ensure the fence can support the vine weight.

**Bilateral Cordon:**
- Single wire at 1.8–2 m height, with vines trained left and right.
- Simple, inexpensive, but less productive than T-bar or pergola.

### Training

Training typically follows a "permanent cordon + replacement cane" system:

1. **Year 1:** Select the strongest shoot as the leader. Tie it to the center post. Pinch side shoots to encourage upward growth.
2. **Year 2:** Once the leader reaches the top wire, pinch the tip to encourage two lateral shoots. Train these along the wires as the permanent cordons.
3. **Year 3+:** Fruiting canes develop from the cordons. Each cane produces fruit for 2–3 years, then is replaced by new growth from the cordon.

### Pruning

Kiwifruit require rigorous annual pruning—more than almost any other fruit crop:

**Winter Pruning (Dormant Season):**
- Remove 70% of the previous season's growth.
- Retain 1-year-old canes spaced 15–20 cm apart along the cordon.
- Cut each retained cane back to 8–12 buds.
- Remove dead, damaged, or crossing wood.

**Summer Pruning (Growing Season):**
- Remove non-fruiting lateral shoots beyond the 6th leaf beyond the last fruit.
- Thin dense growth to improve air circulation and light penetration.
- Remove water sprouts and suckers from the base.
- Summer pruning can reduce winter pruning workload by 50%.

## Irrigation

Kiwifruit have high water requirements due to their large leaf area and rapid growth. Inconsistent irrigation causes fruit drop, poor sizing, and cracking.

- **Young vines (1–3 years):** 10–20 L per vine per week during the growing season.
- **Mature vines (4+ years):** 40–80 L per vine per week, depending on soil type and weather.
- **Critical periods:** Immediately after bud break, during fruit set (spring), and during fruit swell (late summer).
- **Drip irrigation** is strongly recommended. Overhead irrigation promotes fungal diseases.
- **Mulch** heavily (10–15 cm) to conserve moisture and regulate soil temperature.

## Fertilization

Kiwifruit are heavy feeders, particularly for nitrogen and potassium. A soil test should guide application rates.

### Annual Application Schedule (per mature vine)

| Nutrient | Application Rate | Timing |
|----------|-----------------|--------|
| Nitrogen (N) | 100–200 g actual N | Split: 50% at bud break, 50% after fruit set |
| Phosphorus (P) | 30–50 g actual P | Single application in early spring |
| Potassium (K) | 100–150 g actual K | Split: 50% in spring, 50% in mid-summer |
| Calcium (Ca) | 200–500 g gypsum | Fall application if soil test indicates deficiency |
| Magnesium (Mg) | 50–100 g Epsom salt | Spring if deficiency symptoms appear |

### Organic Fertilizer Options

- Well-composted manure (aged poultry or horse manure): 5–10 kg per vine per year.
- Blood meal or feather meal for nitrogen.
- Kelp meal for micronutrients and potassium.
- Wood ash for potassium and calcium (use sparingly; raises pH).
- Alfalfa meal as a balanced organic fertilizer.

### Avoid

- Chloride-heavy fertilizers (kiwifruit are chloride-sensitive).
- Excessive nitrogen (promotes vegetative growth at the expense of fruit).
- Fertilizer application after mid-summer (delays hardening off, increases frost damage risk).

## Pest and Disease Management

### Major Pests

- **Spider mites (*Tetranychus urticae*):** Common in hot, dry conditions. Cause bronzing of leaves. Manage with predatory mites (*Phytoseiulus persimilis*), horticultural oil, or neem oil.
- **Leafroller caterpillars:** Roll and feed on leaves, occasionally damage fruit. Bt (*Bacillus thuringiensis*) sprays are effective.
- **Japanese beetles (*Popillia japonica*):** Skeletonize leaves. Hand-picking, neem, or kaolin clay sprays.
- **Root-knot nematodes (*Meloidogyne* spp.):** Cause galling on roots. Crop rotation, marigold interplanting, and biological controls.
- **Scale insects:** Infest bark and stems. Dormant oil spray in winter.
- **Thrips:** Feed on flowers, reducing fruit set. Yellow sticky traps and reflective mulch.

### Major Diseases

- **Botrytis gray mold (*Botrytis cinerea*):** Affects flowers and fruit in cool, wet conditions. Improve air circulation, remove affected tissue, apply sulfur or biological fungicides.
- **Phytophthora root rot:** Most serious disease in poorly drained soils. Ensure excellent drainage; avoid overwatering. Trunk painting with copper-based fungicides can help.
- **Bacterial canker (*Pseudomonas syringae* pv. *actinidiae* — PSA):** Devastating disease of kiwifruit, particularly golden varieties. Causes cane dieback, oozing cankers, and vine death. No cure; prevention through clean nursery stock, copper sprays, and strict sanitation. Quarantine new plants.
- **Sclerotinia stem rot:** Favors cool, wet conditions. Remove infected plant material, improve drainage and airflow.
- **Armillaria root rot:** Attacks stressed or wounded vines. Remove infected roots; avoid planting in sites with a history of Armillaria.

### Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

1. Monitor regularly for early pest/disease signs.
2. Encourage beneficial insects (ladybugs, lacewings, predatory mites) with companion planting and insectary strips.
3. Use biological controls (Bt, Trichoderma, Beauveria) as first line of defense.
4. Reserve synthetic chemicals for severe outbreaks.
5. Maintain clean orchard floor—remove fallen leaves and fruit.

## Harvesting

### Timing

- **Fuzzy kiwi:** Harvest in late autumn (October–November in the Northern Hemisphere). Fruit should reach at least 6.5° Brix (sugar content). Pick firm; they ripen off the vine over 1–3 weeks at room temperature.
- **Hardy kiwi:** Harvest in late August to October, depending on variety. Fruit ripen on the vine but are also picked firm and stored.
- **Arctic kiwi:** Very early—July to August. Fruit drop when fully ripe; pick from the ground or use nets.

### Harvest Indicators

- Seeds turn from white to black.
- Fruit reaches minimum Brix level for the variety (6.5–8° for fuzzy kiwi, 8–14° for hardy kiwi).
- Fruit snaps cleanly from the stem when twisted.

### Storage

- Refrigerate at 0–2°C, 90–95% humidity for up to 6 months (fuzzy kiwi).
- Hardy kiwi store for 2–4 months under refrigeration.
- Ethylene exposure accelerates ripening; store away from apples, bananas, and other ethylene producers.
- For long-term storage, fuzzy kiwi can be frozen (peel and slice first) or dehydrated.

## Kiwifruit in Permaculture and Food Forests

### Multifunctional Roles

- **Food production:** High-value fruit with excellent nutritional density.
- **Canopy/shade:** Vigorous vines create dense shade; useful for shading poultry areas, seating areas, or understory crops.
- **Erosion control:** Extensive root systems stabilize slopes.
- **Ornamental value:** Variegated cultivars (*A. kolomikta*) provide striking foliage.
- **Wildlife habitat:** Flowers attract bees; fruit feeds birds (if unpicked).

### Companion Planting

- **Good companions:** Clover (nitrogen fixation), comfrey (dynamic accumulator), borage (pollinator attractant), nasturtiums (pest repellent), garlic (fungal deterrent).
- **Avoid planting near:** Walnut trees (juglone sensitivity), other heavy nitrogen feeders competing for resources.

### Integration Strategies

1. **Food forest canopy layer:** Train kiwifruit over pergolas with shade-tolerant crops (mint, currants, hostas) below.
2. **Living fence:** Grow hardy kiwi along perimeter fences for privacy, windbreak, and fruit.
3. **Chicken forage orchard:** Hardy kiwi over chicken runs provides shade and dropped fruit for foraging.

## Propagation

### Seed

Not recommended for named cultivars (seedlings are variable and sex cannot be determined until flowering, 3–5 years). Useful for breeding programs or ornamental plantings.

### Softwood Cuttings

Take 10–15 cm cuttings from new growth in early summer. Rooting hormone (IBA) improves success. Bottom heat (20–25°C) and mist. Roots in 4–8 weeks. 60–80% success rate.

### Hardwood Cuttings

Take 30–45 cm cuttings from dormant wood in late winter. Treat with IBA and root in sand or perlite. Lower success rate than softwood but simpler logistics.

### Grafting

Standard method for commercial propagation. Whip-and-tongue or cleft graft onto seedling rootstock in late winter. Use *A. deliciosa* seedling rootstock for fuzzy kiwi, *A. arguta* seedling rootstock for hardy kiwi.

### Layering

Simple layering of low-growing canes in summer. Weight canes to the ground and cover with soil at nodes. Sever from parent plant after 12–18 months when rooted.

## Common Problems and Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| Vine grows vigorously but no fruit | No male pollinator, or wrong bloom timing | Plant appropriate male; ensure bloom overlap |
| Flower drop before fruit set | Late frost, poor pollination, water stress | Frost protection (row covers, sprinklers); improve irrigation; add pollinator habitat |
| Small fruit | Insufficient pruning, nutrient deficiency, water stress | Annual pruning; soil test; consistent irrigation |
| Fruit cracking | Inconsistent soil moisture, calcium deficiency | Mulch heavily; regular irrigation; gypsum application |
| Yellowing leaves with green veins | Iron deficiency (high pH) | Sulfur application; chelated iron foliar spray |
| Wilting despite adequate water | Phytophthora root rot | Improve drainage; reduce irrigation; apply Trichoderma |
| Brown spots on fruit | Botrytis, sunburn, or hail damage | Improve air circulation; provide shade cloth in extreme heat; harvest promptly |
| Bark cankers with oozing sap | Bacterial canker (PSA) | Remove affected canes; copper spray; consult extension service |

## Cultural History

Kiwifruit were originally known in China as *macaque peach* (猕猴桃, *mihoutao*) and were gathered from the wild for centuries. The first recorded description dates to the Tang Dynasty (618–907 CE). The species was introduced to New Zealand in 1904 by Mary Isabel Fraser, who brought seeds from China. The fruit was commercialized in New Zealand in the mid-20th century and renamed "kiwifruit" in 1959 (after the kiwi bird, New Zealand's national symbol) for export marketing. Today, China is the world's largest producer, followed by Italy, New Zealand, Chile, and Greece.
