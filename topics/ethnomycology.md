## Ethnomycology

Ethnomycology is the study of the role of mushrooms and other fungi in the
cultural, religious, and practical lives of human societies. Coined by the
amateur mycologist and banker R. Gordon Wasson in the mid-twentieth century,
the term designates a subfield of ethnobotany that focuses specifically on the
myriad ways human beings have interacted with the fungal kingdom across
millennia. While ethnobotany broadly encompasses human relationships with all
plants, ethnomycology narrows its gaze to the organisms that belong to Kingdom
Fungi: mushrooms, truffles, yeasts, molds, and the vast networks of mycelium
that thread through soil and wood worldwide. The discipline encompasses
everything from the culinary and medicinal uses of fungi to the deeply
reverential sacramental roles certain mushrooms have played in religious and
shamanic traditions on every inhabited continent.

The field sits at a unique interdisciplinary crossroads, drawing on mycology,
anthropology, chemistry, history, linguistics, comparative religion, art
history, and pharmacology. Its practitioners range from professional academics
to independent scholars, and its subject matter extends from the highly
technical, such as the [[solanaceous-hallucinogens-of-medieval-european-witchcraft]] art or the folklore of fairy rings and
toadstools. What unites these diverse inquiries is a recognition that fungi
have played a far more significant role in human cultural evolution than has
traditionally been acknowledged, and that the study of human-fungal
interactions can illuminate fundamental questions about consciousness,
knowledge, and the origins of religion.

## Origins of the Discipline: Wasson and the Mycophile-Mycophobia Divide

The formal discipline of ethnomycology was born from a deeply personal
observation. In 1927, the American banker R. Gordon Wasson and his
Russian-born wife Valentina Pavlovna Wasson were walking in the Catskill
Mountains of New York when Valentina spotted some wild mushrooms and began
enthusiastically gathering them into her apron. Wasson, raised in the
Anglo-American tradition where mushrooms were viewed with suspicion and even
revulsion, was horrified. He called out to her that the fungi were poisonous,
"putrid," and toadstools. Valentina only laughed. That evening she prepared
them for dinner, seasoning the soup with her gathered fungi, garnishing the
meat with others, and threading still more together to dry for winter use. As
Wasson later recounted, she proved right and he wrong.

ceremony took place in darkness, accompanied by the rhythmic chanting of
prayers and invocations, and the mushrooms were consumed in pairs, with
