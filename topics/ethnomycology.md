# Ethnomycology

Ethnomycology is the study of the role of mushrooms and other fungi in the
cultural, religious, and practical lives of human societies. Coined by the
amateur mycologist and banker R. Gordon Wasson in the mid-twentieth century,
the term designates a subfield of ethnobotany that focuses specifically on the
myriad ways human beings have interacted with the fungal kingdom across
millennia. While ethnobotany broadly encompasses human relationships with all
plants, ethnomycology narrows its gaze to the organisms that belong to Kingdom
Fungi: mushrooms, truffles, yeasts, molds, and the vast networks of mycelium
that thread through soil and wood worldwide. The discipline encompasses
everything from the culinary and medicinal uses of fungi to the deeply
reverential sacramental roles certain mushrooms have played in religious and
shamanic traditions on every inhabited continent.

The field sits at a unique interdisciplinary crossroads, drawing on mycology,
anthropology, chemistry, history, linguistics, comparative religion, art
history, and pharmacology. Its practitioners range from professional academics
to independent scholars, and its subject matter extends from the highly
technical, such as the chemical analysis of psychoactive alkaloids in ancient
ritual contexts, to the broadly cultural, such as the interpretation of
mushroom imagery in medieval European art or the folklore of fairy rings and
toadstools. What unites these diverse inquiries is a recognition that fungi
have played a far more significant role in human cultural evolution than has
traditionally been acknowledged, and that the study of human-fungal
interactions can illuminate fundamental questions about consciousness,
knowledge, and the origins of religion.

## Origins of the Discipline: Wasson and the Mycophile-Mycophobia Divide

The formal discipline of ethnomycology was born from a deeply personal
observation. In 1927, the American banker R. Gordon Wasson and his
Russian-born wife Valentina Pavlovna Wasson were walking in the Catskill
Mountains of New York when Valentina spotted some wild mushrooms and began
enthusiastically gathering them into her apron. Wasson, raised in the
Anglo-American tradition where mushrooms were viewed with suspicion and even
revulsion, was horrified. He called out to her that the fungi were poisonous,
"putrid," and toadstools. Valentina only laughed. That evening she prepared
them for dinner, seasoning the soup with her gathered fungi, garnishing the
meat with others, and threading still more together to dry for winter use. As
Wasson later recounted, she proved right and he wrong.

This seemingly trivial domestic episode launched one of the most remarkable
intellectual odysseys of the twentieth century. The Wassons realized that
their individual attitudes toward mushrooms were not personal quirks but
reflections of deep-seated cultural orientations that characterized entire
peoples. They coined the terms "mycophilia" and "mycophobia" to describe these
opposing cultural stances: the mycophilic cultures of Slavic, Baltic, and
much of Mediterranean Europe, where mushroom gathering was (and remains) a
beloved seasonal activity celebrated in literature, art, and folklore; and the
mycophobic cultures of the Anglo-Saxon, Germanic, and Celtic world, where
mushrooms were associated with poison, decay, and death. The Russian language
possessed an apparently inexhaustible vocabulary for mushrooms, while English
could manage only three words: toadstool, mushroom, and fungus.

The Wassons devoted decades to exploring this cultural divide, assembling
vocabularies, studying folk traditions, and tracing the history of fungal
symbolism through art, religion, and literature. Their work led them far
beyond Europe, ultimately to the highlands of central Mexico, where in 1955
Gordon Wasson became the first known outsider to participate in a Mazatec
mushroom ritual. The journey from the Catskills to the Sierra Mazateca took
nearly three decades of research, and it transformed Wasson from an amateur
mycologist into one of the most influential figures in the study of
psychoactive plants and fungi.

## The Discovery of the Mexican Mushroom Cult

On the night of June 29 to 30, 1955, in the village of Huautla de Jimenez in
the Sierra Mazateca of Oaxaca, Mexico, R. Gordon Wasson sat in a darkened
room with the curandera Maria Sabina and consumed a bowl of psilocybin
mushrooms prepared according to Mazatec tradition. Wasson had traveled to
Mexico following clues in the historical and ethnographic literature,
particularly the work of the sixteenth-century Franciscan friar Bernardino de
Sahagun, who had documented the ritual use of "teonanacatl" among the Aztecs,
and the Harvard ethnobotanist Richard Evans Schultes, who had collected
specimens of psychoactive mushrooms in Oaxaca in the 1930s and identified them
as belonging to the genus Psilocybe.

The Mazatec called the mushrooms "nti si tho," meaning "the little ones that
spring forth." In the nocturnal ceremony known as a velada, the mushrooms
were consumed in a ritual context that combined elements of pre-Columbian
indigenous religion with Catholic symbolism introduced by Spanish missionaries.
Maria Sabina, like other Mazatec curanderas, understood the mushrooms not as
drugs but as sentient beings, spirits that spoke and that could be consulted
for guidance on matters of health, divination, and community welfare. The
ceremony took place in darkness, accompanied by the rhythmic chanting of
prayers and invocations, and the mushrooms were consumed in pairs, with
specific numbers of pairs consumed at specific intervals throughout the night.

Wasson published his account in a landmark 1957 article in Life magazine,
"Seeking the Magic Mushroom," which introduced the existence of psychoactive
mushroom use in Mesoamerica to the Western world and effectively inaugurated
the modern era of ethnomycology. The article was illustrated with photographs
by Allan Richardson, one of Wasson's traveling companions, including the
now-famous image of Maria Sabina illuminated by candlelight, her face a mask
of intense concentration. The Life article reached millions of readers and
triggered a wave of interest in psychoactive mushrooms that has not subsided
to this day.

## Teonanacatl: The Divine Flesh of the Aztecs

The Mesoamerican mushroom tradition documented by Wasson was the survival of
a practice that had once been widespread throughout the Aztec empire and its
predecessors. The Aztecs called these mushrooms teonanacatl, a Nahuatl term
commonly translated as "flesh of the gods" or "divine flesh." The Spanish
conquistadors and missionaries who witnessed the mushroom rites were
horrified, seeing them as diabolical superstitions. Sahagun recorded detailed
descriptions of the mushroom ceremonies while simultaneously condemning them
as idolatrous. He described how those who ate the mushrooms "saw visions and
sometimes had frightful and terrible dreams," and he reported that the
mushrooms were consumed before the altar of the god Xipe Totec during the
festival of Panquetzaliztli.

The Spanish Inquisition persecuted the use of psychoactive mushrooms with
particular ferocity, driving the practice underground where it survived in a
few remote indigenous communities of the Sierra Mazateca, Zapotec highlands,
and Mixtec regions of Oaxaca. The theological significance of teonanacatl was
precisely what made it so threatening to the Spanish: the idea that the
mushroom was literally the flesh of God resonated powerfully with the
Catholic doctrine of transubstantiation, and the Church could not tolerate a
rival source of divine communion. The suppression of the mushroom cult was
thus not merely an exercise in cultural imperialism but an act of religious
competition, a deliberate effort to eliminate a competing theology of the
sacred.

Maria Sabina's own description of the mushroom experience, recorded by the
Mazatec poet and scholar Henry Munn and quoted in Schultes and Hofmann's
"Plants of the Gods," captures the epistemological ambition of the mushroom
vision: "The more you go inside the world of Teonanacatl, the more things
are seen. And you also see our past and our future, which are there together
as a single thing already achieved, already happened. Millions of things I
saw and knew. I knew and saw God: an immense clock that ticks, the spheres
that go slowly around, and inside the stars, the earth, the entire universe,
the day and the night, the cry and the smile, the happiness and the pain. He
who knows to the end the secret of Teonanacatl can even see that infinite
clockwork."

Wasson's publicity attracted a flood of countercultural seekers to the
Mazatec highlands, disrupting the traditional contexts in which the mushrooms
had been used. Maria Sabina later lamented that the arrival of outsiders had
destroyed the purity of the velada ceremony, saying that the mushroom had
lost its power because it had been taken out of its proper ritual context.
Her lament points to a central concern of ethnomycology: the relationship
between psychoactive substances and the cultural frameworks that give them
meaning. The same mushroom that produced profound religious experiences in the
context of Mazatec tradition could produce recreational thrills or
psychological distress when consumed in the very different context of Western
countercultural tourism.

## The Siberian Mushroom Tradition: Amanita Muscaria

Long before Wasson's Mexican expedition, the psychoactive properties of
mushrooms had been documented among the indigenous peoples of Siberia. The
fly-agaric mushroom, Amanita muscaria, with its iconic red cap dotted with
white warts, has a deep history of ritual use across the circumpolar regions
of the Northern Hemisphere. Among the Koryak, Chukchi, Kamchadal, and other
Siberian peoples, Amanita muscaria was consumed for both recreational and
religious purposes, playing a central role in shamanic ceremonies, winter
festivals, and communal gatherings.

The Siberian use of Amanita muscaria was remarkably sophisticated. The Koryak
distinguished between first and second uses of the mushroom, recognizing that
the psychoactive compounds were concentrated in the urine of those who had
consumed it. Drinking the urine of someone who had eaten them was considered
more effective, and the practice of serial consumption was believed to produce
the strongest effects. This reflects a practical understanding of
pharmacokinetics: muscimol and ibotenic acid are excreted in a form that
retains psychoactive properties while eliminating many toxic constituents.

Siberian shamans consumed Amanita muscaria to enter trance states in which
they journeyed to the spirit world, communicated with ancestors, diagnosed
illness, and foretold the future. The mushroom's effects include visual
distortions, size alteration (macropsia and micropsia), auditory changes, and
dissociation from the body. These experiences mapped onto the shamanic
worldview in which the practitioner's soul was believed to leave the body and
travel through upper, middle, and lower worlds. The vivid red-and-white
imagery of the fly-agaric permeated Siberian material culture, appearing on
clothing, tools, dwellings, and ritual objects.

The striking appearance of Amanita muscaria has led numerous scholars to
propose that it may have influenced enduring cultural symbols of Eurasia. The
resemblance between the mushroom and the red-and-white costume of Santa Claus
has been frequently noted, and some researchers have argued that Christmas
traditions may encode an ancient memory of reindeer-consuming shamans whose
ecstatic visions involved aerial flight. While such claims remain speculative,
they illustrate the breadth of inquiry that ethnomycology encompasses.

## The Soma Question: Mushrooms and the Vedic Tradition

One of the most contentious and fascinating questions in ethnomycology
concerns the identity of Soma, the divine sacrament celebrated in the Rigveda,
the oldest sacred text of Hinduism, composed sometime between 1500 and 1200
BCE. The Rigveda contains over a thousand hymns, and a large proportion of
the most exalted among them are dedicated to Soma, described as a god, a
plant, and a drink that conferred immortality, divine vision, and ecstatic
union with the sacred. The priests who prepared and consumed Soma sang of its
golden or tawny color, its juice that was pressed from the plant through
filters of wool, its capacity to make the drinker feel as vast as the sky,
and its power to bestow strength, wisdom, and immortality. Despite the
centrality of Soma to Vedic religion, the identity of the actual plant was
lost to history, and by the time of the later Brahmanas and Upanishads,
substitute plants were being used in the ritual.

In 1968, R. Gordon Wasson proposed in his book "Soma: Divine Mushroom of
Immortality" that Soma was none other than Amanita muscaria. He argued that
the Vedic descriptions of Soma's appearance, effects, and preparation matched
the known properties of the fly-agaric mushroom. The tawny color matched the
mushroom's cap; the pressing through woolen filters matched the practice of
extracting the juice; the sense of divine enlargement matched the mushroom's
psychoactive effects. Wasson further argued that the original Soma cult had
been brought to India by Indo-Aryan migrants from the north, where Amanita
muscaria grew in birch and pine forests, and that the mushroom ceased to be
available in the Indian subcontinent, leading to the substitution of other
plants and the gradual forgetting of the original identity.

Wasson's Soma hypothesis generated enormous debate that continues to the
present day. Critics pointed out that Amanita muscaria does not grow
naturally in the lowland regions of India where the Vedic civilization
flourished, and that the mushroom's effects, characterized by sedation,
confusion, and nausea, differ considerably from the exhilarating, visionary
states described in the Rigveda. Alternative candidates for Soma have been
proposed, including Ephedra species, various species of rue, Peganum harmala,
and even psilocybin-containing mushrooms of the genus Panaeolus. Terence
McKenna, in "Food of the Gods," proposed that Stropharia cubensis, a
psilocybin-containing mushroom found growing on bovine dung in subtropical
regions, might have been the original Soma, arguing that its effects more
closely matched the Vedic descriptions of ecstatic visionary experience.

The pharmacologist Albert Hofmann, who discovered LSD and was a co-author
with Wasson on "The Road to Eleusis," expressed reservations about the
Amanita theory while acknowledging its plausibility. He noted that the
chemistry of Amanita muscaria, with its unique ibotenic acid and muscimol
profile, did not obviously correspond to the visionary intensity described in
the Vedic hymns. Hofmann's reservations carried considerable weight given his
unparalleled expertise in the chemistry of psychoactive fungi, and his
position illustrates the productive tension between ethnographic evidence and
pharmacological analysis that characterizes the best work in ethnomycology.

The Soma debate may never be definitively resolved, and it may be that the
question itself reveals more about the modern desire to identify a single
botanical agent for every ancient mystery than it does about Vedic religion.
Nevertheless, the debate has contributed enormously to ethnomycology by
demonstrating the depth and complexity of human relationships with
psychoactive fungi and by showing how the study of mushrooms can illuminate
some of the most ancient and foundational religious traditions of human
civilization.

## The Eleusinian Mysteries and the Kykeon

The Eleusinian Mysteries were the most sacred religious rites of ancient
Greece, performed annually for nearly two thousand years at Eleusis. The
central rite, the epopteia, involved consuming a mysterious potion called the
kykeon, after which initiates experienced a vision described as
life-changing and utterly transformative. Despite the openness of the cult,
the nature of the vision was kept as an absolute secret under penalty of
death, and remarkably, no ancient source describes what was seen. Sophocles
called the Mysteries the most awesome experience possible; Cicero declared
they taught people to live with joy and die with hope.

In 1978, Wasson, Hofmann, and classical scholar Carl A. P. Ruck published
"The Road to Eleusis," proposing that the kykeon contained an ergot-derived
alkaloid similar to LSD. Their argument drew on the known use of ergot in
folk medicine, the presence of ergot alkaloids in Mediterranean grain, the
description of kykeon preparation in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter (which
included barley, a common host for ergot), and the transformative character
of the experience. Hofmann demonstrated that water-soluble psychoactive
ergot alkaloids could be extracted while leaving toxic compounds behind. Ruck
contributed analysis of fungal symbolism in the iconography of Demeter and
Persephone and in Greek vase painting.

The Eleusinian hypothesis remains controversial, with critics arguing that
the experience could have been produced by non-pharmacological means. However,
it has gained considerable traction and is now widely discussed as at least
plausible. As Wasson noted, ethnomycology had with this work taken within its
purview "our own cultural past, our legacy from ancient Greece," a decisive
expansion of the field's scope.

## Allegro and Philological Ethnomycology

Perhaps the most controversial work in ethnomycology is John Marco Allegro's
1970 book "The Sacred Mushroom and the Cross." Allegro, a philologist and one
of the original team of scholars entrusted with translating the Dead Sea
Scrolls, argued that Christianity originated as a fertility cult centered on
the ritual consumption of Amanita muscaria. He proposed that the names of
Jesus, God, and numerous biblical figures were encoded references to the
mushroom, and that much of the language of the New Testament, when read
through the lens of Sumerian and other ancient Near Eastern languages,
revealed a mushroom cult that had been deliberately obscured by later
ecclesiastical editors.

Allegro's thesis was based on comparative philology, specifically the idea
that the original Sumerian cuneiform signs, which were pictographic in
origin, encoded references to mushrooms in their earliest forms. He argued
that words like "Christ," "Jesus," "Peter," and many others could be traced
back to Sumerian roots that referred to the mushroom, its cap, its stalk,
its juice, and its psychoactive effects. He further proposed that the
Christian Eucharist, the ritual consumption of bread and wine understood as
the body and blood of Christ, was a survival of a much older mushroom
sacrament, and that the cross itself was originally a symbol of the mushroom.

The reception of Allegro's book was overwhelmingly negative. Fellow Dead Sea
Scrolls scholars publicly repudiated his methods and conclusions, and the
book was widely dismissed as sensationalist and methodologically unsound.
Nevertheless, Allegro's work, whatever its flaws, raised important questions
about the relationship between religious language, botanical knowledge, and
the possibility that psychoactive substances played a role in the formation
of religious traditions that are now understood in purely symbolic or
theological terms.

More measured than Allegro but exploring similar territory is the work of
Donald Teeter and the Ambrosia Society, whose "Amanita Muscaria: Herb of
Immortality" traces the mushroom through Indo-European mythology, connecting
it to figures as diverse as Soma, Dionysus, Odin, the Holy Grail, and the
Christian Eucharist. Teeter's approach is more cautious than Allegro's,
acknowledging that the evidence is often circumstantial while arguing that
the sheer density of mushroom-related symbolism across cultures demands
explanation. The recurrent motifs of the "living bread," the "fountain of
the water of life," the "golden embryo," and the "herb of immortality" that
appear in Vedic, Greek, Norse, Persian, and Christian sources may, Teeter
suggests, reflect a common origin in the ritual use of a psychoactive
mushroom.

## Shamanism, Fungi, and the Anthropological Perspective

While the Mesoamerican and Siberian mushroom traditions are the most famous,
the use of psychoactive fungi in shamanic contexts extends far beyond these
regions. The anthropologist Michael Harner, whose 1973 edited volume
"Hallucinogens and Shamanism" was a landmark in the field, documented the
role of psychoactive substances in shamanic traditions worldwide, arguing
that the visionary experience induced by these substances was not incidental
but central to shamanic practice.

Harner noted that anthropologists had long underestimated the importance of
hallucinogenic substances in shamanic traditions, partly because very few had
experienced the substances themselves and partly because of a persistent bias
in Western anthropology against taking indigenous pharmacological practices
seriously. He argued that the subjective experience induced by psychoactive
plants was not merely a colorful accompaniment to shamanic ritual but
constituted the very medium through which shamans acquired their knowledge,
diagnosed illness, negotiated with spirits, and performed their essential
social functions. This insight, that the pharmacological experience is the
technology that powers the shamanic worldview, has become a foundational
principle of ethnomycology and the broader study of entheogenic practices.

In the Amazon basin, mushrooms have been used as part of complex
pharmacopoeias that also include ayahuasca, tobacco, and various other
psychoactive plants. The Matsés people of the Peruvian-Brazilian border
region use psychoactive mushrooms as part of a hunting magic system in which
visions are interpreted as maps of future hunting grounds. The Jivaro people,
whom Harner studied extensively, use psychoactive plants including ayahuasca
and, in some traditions, mushrooms to enter visionary states for soul
retrieval, spirit combat, and other shamanic operations. The full extent of
Amazonian mushroom use remains poorly documented, as many traditions have
been lost to colonial disruption and much knowledge remains closely guarded
by indigenous practitioners.

Harner's collection also included Henry Munn's influential essay "The
Mushrooms of Language," which explored the linguistic dimensions of the
Mazatec mushroom experience. Munn argued that the mushrooms functioned as a
kind of linguistic technology, enabling practitioners to access levels of
verbal expression and metaphorical complexity not available in ordinary
consciousness. The Mazatec chants performed during velada ceremonies exhibited
a density of imagery and syntactic complexity that exceeded ordinary speech,
suggesting that the mushroom experience genuinely expanded the expressive
capacities of those who consumed it.

## Mycophilia and Mycophobia: A Cultural Geography

One of the most enduring contributions of ethnomycology is the recognition
that human attitudes toward mushrooms are not uniform but are deeply
culturally conditioned. The Wassons' original observation about the contrast
between Russian and Anglo-American mushroom cultures has been confirmed and
elaborated by subsequent researchers, revealing a complex geography of fungal
attitudes that correlates with, but is not fully explained by, linguistic,
historical, and ecological factors.

In Slavic and Baltic cultures, mushroom gathering is a seasonal ritual of
enormous cultural importance. In Russia, the "quiet hunt" for mushrooms is
celebrated in literature by Pushkin, Tolstoy, Turgenev, and countless other
writers. Polish, Czech, and Slovak cultures similarly venerate the mushroom,
with elaborate folk taxonomies, seasonal festivals, and deeply ingrained
culinary traditions. In Italy, mushroom gathering is a passion that
transcends regional and class boundaries, and Italian markets offer dozens of
wild mushroom species. In the Basque Country and Catalonia, mushroom
knowledge is a point of fierce regional pride.

In contrast, the Anglo-Saxon and Germanic cultural world has historically
viewed mushrooms with suspicion. English folklore associates toadstools with
witches, fairies, and poison. In Germany, the Walpurgisnacht tradition links
mushrooms to witches' sabbaths and demonic revelry. In the United States,
where mushroom gathering was never a significant folk tradition, wild
mushrooms were widely regarded with suspicion or indifference. This cultural
divide has practical consequences: in mycophilic cultures, knowledge of
edible and poisonous mushrooms is transmitted intergenerationally and
mushroom poisoning is relatively rare, while in mycophobic cultures the
absence of such knowledge leads to more frequent identification errors.

## The Cultural History of the Magic Mushroom in the West

The history of psychoactive mushrooms in Western culture from Wasson's 1957
Life article to the present is itself a rich field of ethnomycological study.
Andy Letcher's "Shroom: A Cultural History of the Magic Mushroom" provides
the most comprehensive account of this history, tracing the transformation of
the magic mushroom from an obscure ethnographic curiosity into a global
cultural phenomenon. In the 1960s and 1970s, the mushroom became associated
with the counterculture, psychedelic movement, and the search for alternative
modes of consciousness. Timothy Leary, Richard Alpert, and other Harvard
researchers helped popularize psilocybin as a tool for consciousness expansion.
The development of simple cultivation techniques such as the PF Tek and the
spread of spore prints through underground networks made the mushrooms widely
available outside their traditional cultural contexts.

The cultural meaning of the magic mushroom has shifted repeatedly over the
decades: from 1960s rebellion and consciousness expansion, through 1970s New
Age spirituality, into 1990s rave and dance music culture, and onward into
the internet age of online forums and virtual communities. In the Netherlands,
"paddos" could be sold openly from market stalls and smart shops, and
Amsterdam became a destination for mushroom tourism. By the 2020s, the
mushroom had undergone yet another transformation, becoming the focus of
serious clinical research at institutions such as Johns Hopkins University,
Imperial College London, and the University of Zurich, where trials
demonstrated that psilocybin can produce rapid and sustained reductions in
depression and anxiety, particularly in patients with life-threatening
illnesses. The therapeutic model employed in these trials owes a clear
intellectual debt to the traditional contexts in which psychoactive mushrooms
have been used, where set and setting are carefully managed by experienced
practitioners.

Letcher is critical of what he calls the "myth-making" tendencies within
mushroom culture, arguing that many claims about ancient mushroom use are
unsupported by evidence and reflect the desires of modern enthusiasts more
than the historical record. His critical approach serves as a valuable
corrective to more speculative strands of ethnomycological writing.

## Ethnomycology and the Contemporary Psychedelic Renaissance

The resurgence of scientific interest in psychedelic mushrooms has given
ethnomycology new relevance. Contemporary researchers increasingly recognize
that indigenous knowledge documented by ethnomycologists contains practical
insights for modern therapeutic practice. The traditional emphasis on set and
setting, the role of ritual structure, the importance of integration, and the
understanding that the psychedelic experience is a culturally situated
practice: all of these insights are now being incorporated into clinical
psychedelic research protocols. This convergence raises ethical questions
about cultural appropriation, intellectual property rights over traditional
mycological knowledge, and the displacement of indigenous cosmologies by
biomedical frameworks. The Mazatec experience serves as a cautionary tale
about the consequences of cultural extraction without reciprocity.

## The Future of Ethnomycology

Ethnomycology stands at the intersection of biology, anthropology, chemistry,
history, and religious studies, a genuinely interdisciplinary field that
illuminates fundamental aspects of human experience. The study of human-fungal
relationships reveals the depth and complexity of our species' engagement with
the natural world, challenges simplistic distinctions between "nature" and
"culture," and raises profound questions about consciousness, knowledge, and
the origins of religion.

As the ecological importance of fungi becomes increasingly apparent through
research into mycorrhizal networks, fungal decomposition, and the global
carbon cycle, and as the therapeutic potential of psychoactive mushrooms gains
scientific validation, ethnomycology is poised to contribute ever more richly
to our understanding of both the fungal kingdom and the human condition. The
discipline reminds us that mushrooms have been our companions for far longer
than we have recognized, and that the stories we tell about them, from the
Rigveda to the modern laboratory, are ultimately stories about ourselves.
