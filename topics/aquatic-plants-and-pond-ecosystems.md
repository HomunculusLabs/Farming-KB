## Aquatic Plants and Pond Ecosystems
Aquatic plants and pond ecosystems are freshwater systems in which water,
sediments, plants, microbes, invertebrates, fish, waterfowl, and surrounding
land vegetation form a single ecological unit. In gardens and farms, the same
pond may store irrigation water, moderate microclimate, grow food plants, raise
fish, support ducks, filter nutrients, and provide habitat for amphibians and
[[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]] sources add
practical guidance for small wildlife ponds and domestic water gardens.

Pond ecosystems are also decomposer systems. Leaves, stems, duck manure,
plankton, and dead aquatic plants are transformed by bacteria and freshwater
fungi into dissolved and particulate nutrients. Managing a pond therefore means
managing both growth and decay: enough fertility to support plants and animals,
but not so much organic loading that oxygen falls and anaerobic conditions
dominate.

## Definition and Scope
A pond is a relatively small standing body of water shallow enough for light
and plant growth to influence much of its margin or bed. Unlike a tank, a
mature pond develops layered habitats, sediment chemistry, microbial biofilms,
and plant communities that regulate water quality. Unlike a natural lake, a
garden or farm pond is usually shaped, stocked, drained, harvested, or
otherwise managed by people.
The term aquatic plants includes submerged macrophytes, floating-leaved plants,
free-floating plants, emergent plants, and wet-margin species. Many are edible,
medicinal, or useful as mulch and animal feed. Others are valued primarily
because they oxygenate water, shade fish, absorb excess nutrients, hold mud in
place, or create shelter for insects, frogs, fry, and water birds.
The term pond ecosystem refers to the whole living and physical complex.
Hydrology controls how water enters, leaves, and stratifies. Plants control
light penetration and [[sepp-holzer-water-gardens-and-aquatic-plants]]
plants, but require attention to catchment size and seasonal evaporation.
Sepp Holzer's water landscapes illustrate a retention-based philosophy: keep
water on the land as long as possible, use it at several elevations, and let
each use support the next. Ponds, wetlands, ditches, terraces, and springs form
a connected network. Water descending through such a system can provide
drinking water, power, fish habitat, irrigation, microclimate, and wildlife
refuge before leaving the site.
