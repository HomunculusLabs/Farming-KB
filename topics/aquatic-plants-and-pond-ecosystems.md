# Aquatic Plants and Pond Ecosystems
Aquatic plants and pond ecosystems are freshwater systems in which water,
sediments, plants, microbes, invertebrates, fish, waterfowl, and surrounding
land vegetation form a single ecological unit. In gardens and farms, the same
pond may store irrigation water, moderate microclimate, grow food plants, raise
fish, support ducks, filter nutrients, and provide habitat for amphibians and
[[comparison-beneficial-insects-guide-vs-integrated-pest-management]].

The ecological structure of a pond is organized by depth, light, temperature,
oxygen, nutrients, and edge. Submerged oxygenators occupy the water column,
floating plants shade the surface, emergent plants root in shallow margins, and
damp-ground species bind the banks. These plant zones are not merely
ornamental: they control algae, absorb dissolved minerals, provide spawning and
refuge habitat, and form the base of pond food webs.

Permaculture writers such as Bill Mollison, Sepp Holzer, and Nicole Faires
treat ponds as productive polycultures rather than isolated basins of water.
Their designs emphasize sinuous edges, deep and shallow refuges, planted
shelves, islands, waterfowl access, bottom-dwelling animals, and careful
movement of nutrient-rich overflow into gardens. [[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]] sources add
practical guidance for small wildlife ponds and domestic water gardens.

Pond ecosystems are also decomposer systems. Leaves, stems, duck manure,
plankton, and dead aquatic plants are transformed by bacteria and freshwater
fungi into dissolved and particulate nutrients. Managing a pond therefore means
managing both growth and decay: enough fertility to support plants and animals,
but not so much organic loading that oxygen falls and anaerobic conditions
dominate.

## Definition and Scope
A pond is a relatively small standing body of water shallow enough for light
and plant growth to influence much of its margin or bed. Unlike a tank, a
mature pond develops layered habitats, sediment chemistry, microbial biofilms,
and plant communities that regulate water quality. Unlike a natural lake, a
garden or farm pond is usually shaped, stocked, drained, harvested, or
otherwise managed by people.
The term aquatic plants includes submerged macrophytes, floating-leaved plants,
free-floating plants, emergent plants, and wet-margin species. Many are edible,
medicinal, or useful as mulch and animal feed. Others are valued primarily
because they oxygenate water, shade fish, absorb excess nutrients, hold mud in
place, or create shelter for insects, frogs, fry, and water birds.
The term pond ecosystem refers to the whole living and physical complex.
Hydrology controls how water enters, leaves, and stratifies. Plants control
light penetration and [[plant-nutrient-uptake-mechanisms]]. Animals graze, browse, filter, excrete,
and disturb sediments. Fungi and bacteria decompose organic matter. The
designer's task is to place these processes in useful relationship rather than
to suppress them.
In the LLM Wiki source concepts, pond ecosystems range from small backyard
ponds lined with EPDM rubber to liner-free Holzer ponds sealed by compacted
subsoil, from Faires-style homestead fish ponds to Mollison's polyculture ponds
and duck systems. The common theme is that water becomes more stable and
productive when designed as habitat.

## Hydrology, Siting, and Water Retention
Hydrology is the first design constraint. A pond must have a reliable source of
water, a soil or liner system that holds it, and an overflow route that handles
heavy rainfall without eroding the banks. Springs and groundwater are highly
valued because they provide steady inflow and often cooler temperatures.
Rain-fed ponds are also useful, especially for water [[sepp-holzer-water-gardens-and-aquatic-plants]]
plants, but require attention to catchment size and seasonal evaporation.
Sepp Holzer's water landscapes illustrate a retention-based philosophy: keep
water on the land as long as possible, use it at several elevations, and let
each use support the next. Ponds, wetlands, ditches, terraces, and springs form
a connected network. Water descending through such a system can provide
drinking water, power, fish habitat, irrigation, microclimate, and wildlife
refuge before leaving the site.
Small garden ponds are normally placed where they receive sun for part of the
day but some protection from extreme afternoon heat. Too much shade reduces
plant growth, while full hot exposure can raise water temperature and lower
[[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]]. Overhanging deciduous trees are usually avoided because
fallen leaves add large organic loads, sour the water during anaerobic decay,
and can physically foul liners and pumps.
Farm ponds are sited by topography and soil. Natural hollows, swales, and
valley lines reduce excavation and can collect runoff. Clay and loam seal more
readily than sand, gravel, or fractured rock. Designers must also provide a
safe spillway, standpipe, or overflow route so that floodwater exits gently
into swales, wetlands, orchards, or other water-harvesting structures.
Depth is part of hydrology. Shallow water warms quickly, grows emergent plants,
and supports amphibians and invertebrates. Deep water stores cool temperatures,
buffers drought, provides fish refuge during heat and ice, and limits the
spread of aggressive rooted plants. Many designs therefore combine broad
shallow shelves with one or more deep pockets or kettles.
