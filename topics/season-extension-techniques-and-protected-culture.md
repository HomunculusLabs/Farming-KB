## Principles of Season Extension

### Microclimate Creation

Every season extension technique works by modifying one or more environmental variables:

- **Temperature** — trapping solar radiation, reducing radiative cooling, adding thermal mass, or supplementing with active heat
- **Wind** — blocking or redirecting cold air movement that strips warmth from plant tissues and soil
- **Humidity and condensation** — moderating moisture to prevent desiccation while limiting fungal disease
- **Light** — increasing intensity (via reflection), extending photoperiod (via supplemental lighting), or optimizing transmission (via material selection)

The underlying physics are straightforward: incoming shortwave solar radiation passes through glazing or covering materials, is absorbed by soil and plant surfaces, and re-emitted as longwave infrared radiation, which is partially trapped by the covering — the same greenhouse effect that warms a car interior on a sunny winter day.

### Degree-Days and Frost Dates

Effective season extension planning requires understanding your local climate data:

- **Last spring frost date** and **first autumn frost date** — USDA Hardiness Zones are a starting point, but microclimate and elevation create significant local variation
- **Growing degree days (GDD)** — accumulated heat units a crop needs to reach maturity (base 10°C for warm-season crops, base 0°C for cool-season crops)
- **Chill hours** — relevant for perennial fruit crops that require a period of cold temperatures to break dormancy properly

A simple cold frame might gain you 2–4 weeks on both ends of the season. A high tunnel can add 6–8 weeks. A heated greenhouse can effectively eliminate seasonal constraints. The goal is to match the technique to the crop's requirements and your climate's limitations.

- **Medium-weight (1.0–1.25 oz/yd²)** — provides ~4°F frost protection, transmits 70–85% light, moderate insect barrier
- **Heavyweight (1.5–2.0 oz/yd²)** — provides ~6–8°F frost protection, transmits 50–70% light, best for overwintering

Row covers work by creating a microclimate of still, humid air directly against the plant canopy. They also serve as physical barriers against insect pests like flea beetles, cabbage moths, and aphids. Secure edges with sandbags, soil, or staples to prevent wind lifting.

**Limitations:** row covers left on during warm weather cause rapid overheating. They must be removed for pollination of crops like squash and cucumbers unless using a pollinator-compatible mesh. They can abrade tender seedlings in windy conditions if not properly secured.

### Low Tunnels

Low tunnels add structure to the row cover concept by using hoops to suspend fabric or plastic over the crop, providing more headroom and better air circulation.

**Hoop materials:**
- **9-gauge galvanized wire** — cheapest, lasts 1–2 seasons, easy to bend
- **PVC conduit (½-inch)** — inexpensive, lasts 3–5 seasons, degrades in UV
- **Electrical conduit (EMT, ½-inch)** — more durable, lasts 5–10+ seasons, requires a bender
- **Galvanized steel tubing** — most durable, permanent installation, highest cost

**Covering materials:**
- Spunbond row cover (as above) — for frost protection and insect exclusion
- Clear polyethylene (6 mil greenhouse film) — for maximum heat retention, but requires careful venting
- Perforated poly — compromise between heat retention and ventilation

Space hoops every 3–4 feet and bury the cover edges in soil for a tight seal. Low tunnels provide 4–10°F of frost protection depending on the covering material. In sunny weather, internal temperatures can exceed 100°F — vent by lifting one side or using clips to create gaps.

**Advantages over flat row covers:** better air circulation, taller crops can be covered, reduced abrasion, easier to vent, reusable framework.

## Cold Frames

A cold frame is a bottomless box with a transparent lid, traditionally glazed with old windows and built from wood. It is the most cost-effective rigid season extension structure.

### Design Principles

- **Orientation:** face the sloped lid toward true south (in the Northern Hemisphere) to maximize solar gain
- **Slope:** the back wall should be 10–18 inches taller than the front wall, angled at roughly 30–45° from horizontal
- **Dimensions:** 3–4 feet wide (so you can reach the back from either side), 4–8 feet long
- **Insulation:** line the north wall with foam board insulation; pile straw bales around the exterior for extreme cold
- **Thermal mass:** place gallon jugs of water or dark-painted bricks inside to moderate temperature swings

### Materials

- **Glazing:** old single-pane windows (free but fragile), double-wall polycarbonate panels (expensive but durable and insulating), 6 mil poly film on a frame (cheap but short-lived)
- **Frame:** untreated 2×6 or 2×8 lumber, concrete blocks, or straw bales for temporary frames

### Venting

Cold frames can overheat rapidly on sunny days even when outside temperatures are below freezing. Automatic vent openers (gas-filled pistons that expand with heat) are inexpensive and reliable. Manual venting requires daily attention — open the lid 6+ inches on sunny days, close by mid-afternoon before temperatures drop.

### Hotbeds

A hotbed is a cold frame with a heat source beneath the growing bed. The traditional method uses fresh manure (horse is best) layered with straw and soil, which generates heat as it decomposes — reaching 21–27°C (70–80°F) for 4–6 weeks. Modern hotbeds use electric heating cables buried in sand at 10–15 cm depth, controlled by a thermostat set to the desired soil temperature.
