# Miso, Tempeh, and Soybean Fermentation

Soybeans (*[[glycine-max]]*) are among the most important legumes in human food systems, and much of their culinary significance comes from fermentation. Across East and Southeast Asia, communities independently developed distinct fermentation traditions that transform raw soybeans — hard, beany, and nutritionally challenging — into savory, digestible, and probiotic-rich foods. The three pillars of soybean fermentation are **miso** (Japan), **tempeh** (Indonesia), and **natto** (Japan), each employing different microorganisms, processing methods, and cultural contexts.

## The Raw Material: Why Ferment Soybeans?

Soybeans are nutritionally dense but present several challenges when consumed raw or simply cooked:

- **Trypsin inhibitors**: Antinutritional proteins that interfere with protein digestion unless deactivated by heat.
- **Oligosaccharides** (raffinose, stachyose): Carbohydrates that cause flatulence because humans lack the enzymes to digest them.
- **Phytic acid**: Binds minerals (iron, zinc, calcium), reducing bioavailability.
- **Beany flavor**: Lipoxygenase enzymes produce grassy, unpleasant off-flavors when soybean cells are ruptured.

Fermentation addresses all of these issues. Microorganisms break down trypsin inhibitors, metabolize oligosaccharides, partially degrade phytic acid, and generate umami compounds (glutamic acid, nucleotides) that replace the raw beany flavor. The result is a food that is more digestible, more nutritious, and far more flavorful than the starting material.

## Miso: The Koji Fermentation

### History and Cultural Significance

Miso originated in China as *jiang* (酱), a fermented paste made from soybeans, grains, and salt, documented as early as the Zhou Dynasty (1046–256 BCE). The technique arrived in Japan around the 7th century CE via Buddhist monks returning from study in China. Over centuries, Japanese artisans refined the process, developing regional varieties that are now central to Japanese cuisine.

Miso is deeply embedded in Japanese food culture. The phrase *ishiki no miso* (意识の味噌) — "one's own miso" — refers to the personal touch a maker brings to their ferment. Traditional miso-making was a household activity; many rural families maintained their own miso crocks (*miso kome*), tended across generations.

### The Koji Mold: *Aspergillus oryzae*

The defining microorganism of miso is *[[aspergillus-oryzae]]*, known in Japanese as **koji-kin** (麹菌). This filamentous fungus is one of the most important microorganisms in food science, responsible not only for miso but also for sake, shochu, mirin, amazake, and soy sauce.

*A. oryzae* is remarkable for its enzyme production. During the koji stage, it secretes:

| Enzyme | Function |
|--------|----------|
| **Amylase** (α-amylase, glucoamylase) | Breaks starch into sugars |
| **Protease** (alkaline, neutral, acidic) | Breaks proteins into amino acids (including glutamate → umami) |
| **Lipase** | Breaks fats into fatty acids |
| **Phytase** | Degrades phytic acid, freeing minerals |
| **Cellulase** | Breaks down [[plant-cell-membrane-transport-proteins-channels-carriers-and-pumps]] walls |

The koji stage is essentially an enzyme-production step. These enzymes then drive the long, slow fermentation that follows.

### Miso Production Process

#### 1. Soybean Preparation

Whole soybeans are washed and soaked in water for 12–18 hours (longer in winter, shorter in summer) until fully hydrated. They are then boiled or steamed until soft enough to crush between the fingers — typically 3–5 hours of boiling or 1–2 hours under pressure.

#### 2. Koji Cultivation

The cooked soybeans are cooled to about 35°C and mixed with steamed rice or barley that has been inoculated with *A. oryzae* spores (*tane-koji*, 種麹). The grain serves as the primary substrate for koji growth, providing starch for the mold to colonize rapidly.

The inoculated mixture is spread in trays or on cloth-covered racks and incubated at 30–35°C with high humidity for 36–48 hours. During this time, the mold grows throughout the grain, turning it white and fuzzy. The koji must be stirred periodically (*muri* turning) to distribute heat and moisture evenly. If temperatures rise above 40°C, the mold produces unwanted byproducts; if too cool, growth stalls.

#### 3. Salting and Moromi

The finished koji-soybean mixture is transferred to a fermentation vessel (traditionally large wooden or ceramic crocks, now often stainless steel or plastic) and mixed with salt and water. The salt concentration typically ranges from 5–12% depending on the desired style. This mash is called **moromi** (諸味).

#### 4. Long Fermentation

The moromi is pressed into the vessel, the surface is smoothed, and a weight may be placed on top. The vessel is covered and left to ferment at ambient temperature for anywhere from several weeks to several years. During this time, a complex succession of microorganisms contributes:

- ** bacteria** (*Tetragenococcus halophilus*, formerly *Pediococcus halophilus*) acidify the paste early in fermentation.
- **Yeast** (*Zygosaccharomyces rouxii*) produces ethanol and aromatic esters.
- **Bacillus species** contribute proteolytic activity.

The interplay of these organisms, moderated by the high salt concentration, creates the layered sweet-salty-umami flavor profile of finished miso.

### Miso Classification

Miso varieties are classified by several factors:

#### By Grain Substrate

| Type | Grain | Characteristics |
|------|-------|-----------------|
| **Kome miso** (rice miso) | Rice koji | Most common in Japan; mild to strong |
| **Mugi miso** (barley miso) | Barley koji | Nutty, slightly sweet; popular in Kyushu |
| **Mame miso** (soybean miso) | Soybean koji | Dark, intense umami; specialty of central Japan |

#### By Color

- **Shiro miso** (白味噌): Light-colored, short-fermented (weeks to months), low salt, high sweetness. Made with a high proportion of rice koji. Common in Kyoto.
- **Shinshu miso** (信州味噌): Medium-colored, medium fermentation (6–12 months), balanced flavor. The most widely consumed type in Japan.
- **Aka miso** (赤味噌): Dark red-brown, long-fermented (1–5 years), high salt, strong umami. Common in the Nagoya region and northern Honshu.

#### By Taste

- **Amakuchi** (甘口): Sweet miso — high koji ratio, short fermentation, lower salt
- **Karakuchi** (辛口): Salty/savory miso — low koji ratio, longer fermentation, higher salt

### Nutritional Profile

Miso is rich in:

- **Protein** (11–15%): Fully hydrolyzed by koji proteases, making amino acids highly bioavailable
- **Essential amino acids**: Particularly glutamic acid (umami), leucine, and lysine
- **B vitamins**: Produced by microbial activity, especially B1, B2, B6, B12 (in long-fermented varieties)
- **Minerals**: Iron, zinc, calcium, potassium (phytic acid partially degraded)
- **Isoflavones**: Genistein, daidzein, and their glucosides — converted to bioactive aglycone forms during fermentation
- **Probiotics**: Lactic acid bacteria and yeast (in unpasteurized miso)

### Culinary Uses

Miso is extraordinarily versatile:

- **Miso soup** (*misoshiru*): The most common use — dissolved in dashi with tofu, wakame, or vegetables
- **Marinades**: Miso-glazed fish (*saikyo yaki*), eggplant, or pork
- **Pickling**: Vegetables pickled in miso (*misozuke*) develop complex flavors over days to weeks
- **Dressings and sauces**: Miso mayonnaise, miso vinaigrette, miso butter
- **Seasoning**: A spoonful added to stews, braises, or ramen broth for depth

**Important**: Miso should not be boiled, as high heat destroys its volatile aroma compounds and kills beneficial microorganisms. Add it off-heat or at the very end of cooking.

## Tempeh: The Rhizopus Fermentation

### History and Cultural Significance

Tempeh originated on the island of Java, Indonesia, likely several hundred years ago. The earliest written reference appears in the *Serat Centhini*, a Javanese manuscript from the early 19th century, but the practice is certainly much older, passed down through generations of Javanese home cooks.

Tempeh is a daily staple in Indonesia, particularly in Central Java. It is sold in traditional markets (*pasar*) as dense, white-mycelium-bound cakes wrapped in banana leaves or plastic. Indonesia produces over 250,000 tonnes of tempeh annually, and it plays a role analogous to tofu or cheese in Western diets — an affordable, protein-rich food consumed across all socioeconomic levels.

Unlike miso, which requires sophisticated koji cultivation, tempeh fermentation is relatively accessible. The traditional inoculant, *usar* or *ragi*, is a dried starter culture made from hibiscus leaves (*waru*, *Hibiscus tiliaceus*) that naturally harbor *Rhizopus oligosporus* spores. This makes tempeh one of the most democratized [[fermented-foods-and-beverages]] — any household can produce it with minimal equipment.
