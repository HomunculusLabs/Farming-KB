# Miso, Tempeh, and Soybean Fermentation

Soybeans (*Glycine max*) are among the most important legumes in human food systems, and much of their culinary significance comes from fermentation. Across East and Southeast Asia, communities independently developed distinct fermentation traditions that transform raw soybeans — hard, beany, and nutritionally challenging — into savory, digestible, and probiotic-rich foods. The three pillars of soybean fermentation are **miso** (Japan), **tempeh** (Indonesia), and **natto** (Japan), each employing different microorganisms, processing methods, and cultural contexts.

## The Raw Material: Why Ferment Soybeans?

Soybeans are nutritionally dense but present several challenges when consumed raw or simply cooked:

- **Trypsin inhibitors**: Antinutritional proteins that interfere with protein digestion unless deactivated by heat.
- **Oligosaccharides** (raffinose, stachyose): Carbohydrates that cause flatulence because humans lack the enzymes to digest them.
- **Phytic acid**: Binds minerals (iron, zinc, calcium), reducing bioavailability.
- **Beany flavor**: Lipoxygenase enzymes produce grassy, unpleasant off-flavors when soybean cells are ruptured.

Fermentation addresses all of these issues. Microorganisms break down trypsin inhibitors, metabolize oligosaccharides, partially degrade phytic acid, and generate umami compounds (glutamic acid, nucleotides) that replace the raw beany flavor. The result is a food that is more digestible, more nutritious, and far more flavorful than the starting material.

## Miso: The Koji Fermentation

### History and Cultural Significance

Miso originated in China as *jiang* (酱), a fermented paste made from soybeans, grains, and salt, documented as early as the Zhou Dynasty (1046–256 BCE). The technique arrived in Japan around the 7th century CE via Buddhist monks returning from study in China. Over centuries, Japanese artisans refined the process, developing regional varieties that are now central to Japanese cuisine.

Miso is deeply embedded in Japanese food culture. The phrase *ishiki no miso* (意识の味噌) — "one's own miso" — refers to the personal touch a maker brings to their ferment. Traditional miso-making was a household activity; many rural families maintained their own miso crocks (*miso kome*), tended across generations.

### The Koji Mold: *Aspergillus oryzae*

The defining microorganism of miso is *Aspergillus oryzae*, known in Japanese as **koji-kin** (麹菌). This filamentous fungus is one of the most important microorganisms in food science, responsible not only for miso but also for sake, shochu, mirin, amazake, and soy sauce.

*A. oryzae* is remarkable for its enzyme production. During the koji stage, it secretes:

| Enzyme | Function |
|--------|----------|
| **Amylase** (α-amylase, glucoamylase) | Breaks starch into sugars |
| **Protease** (alkaline, neutral, acidic) | Breaks proteins into amino acids (including glutamate → umami) |
| **Lipase** | Breaks fats into fatty acids |
| **Phytase** | Degrades phytic acid, freeing minerals |
| **Cellulase** | Breaks down plant cell walls |

The koji stage is essentially an enzyme-production step. These enzymes then drive the long, slow fermentation that follows.

### Miso Production Process

#### 1. Soybean Preparation

Whole soybeans are washed and soaked in water for 12–18 hours (longer in winter, shorter in summer) until fully hydrated. They are then boiled or steamed until soft enough to crush between the fingers — typically 3–5 hours of boiling or 1–2 hours under pressure.

#### 2. Koji Cultivation

The cooked soybeans are cooled to about 35°C and mixed with steamed rice or barley that has been inoculated with *A. oryzae* spores (*tane-koji*, 種麹). The grain serves as the primary substrate for koji growth, providing starch for the mold to colonize rapidly.

The inoculated mixture is spread in trays or on cloth-covered racks and incubated at 30–35°C with high humidity for 36–48 hours. During this time, the mold grows throughout the grain, turning it white and fuzzy. The koji must be stirred periodically (*muri* turning) to distribute heat and moisture evenly. If temperatures rise above 40°C, the mold produces unwanted byproducts; if too cool, growth stalls.

#### 3. Salting and Moromi

The finished koji-soybean mixture is transferred to a fermentation vessel (traditionally large wooden or ceramic crocks, now often stainless steel or plastic) and mixed with salt and water. The salt concentration typically ranges from 5–12% depending on the desired style. This mash is called **moromi** (諸味).

#### 4. Long Fermentation

The moromi is pressed into the vessel, the surface is smoothed, and a weight may be placed on top. The vessel is covered and left to ferment at ambient temperature for anywhere from several weeks to several years. During this time, a complex succession of microorganisms contributes:

- **Lactic acid bacteria** (*Tetragenococcus halophilus*, formerly *Pediococcus halophilus*) acidify the paste early in fermentation.
- **Yeast** (*Zygosaccharomyces rouxii*) produces ethanol and aromatic esters.
- **Bacillus species** contribute proteolytic activity.

The interplay of these organisms, moderated by the high salt concentration, creates the layered sweet-salty-umami flavor profile of finished miso.

### Miso Classification

Miso varieties are classified by several factors:

#### By Grain Substrate

| Type | Grain | Characteristics |
|------|-------|-----------------|
| **Kome miso** (rice miso) | Rice koji | Most common in Japan; mild to strong |
| **Mugi miso** (barley miso) | Barley koji | Nutty, slightly sweet; popular in Kyushu |
| **Mame miso** (soybean miso) | Soybean koji | Dark, intense umami; specialty of central Japan |

#### By Color

- **Shiro miso** (白味噌): Light-colored, short-fermented (weeks to months), low salt, high sweetness. Made with a high proportion of rice koji. Common in Kyoto.
- **Shinshu miso** (信州味噌): Medium-colored, medium fermentation (6–12 months), balanced flavor. The most widely consumed type in Japan.
- **Aka miso** (赤味噌): Dark red-brown, long-fermented (1–5 years), high salt, strong umami. Common in the Nagoya region and northern Honshu.

#### By Taste

- **Amakuchi** (甘口): Sweet miso — high koji ratio, short fermentation, lower salt
- **Karakuchi** (辛口): Salty/savory miso — low koji ratio, longer fermentation, higher salt

### Nutritional Profile

Miso is rich in:

- **Protein** (11–15%): Fully hydrolyzed by koji proteases, making amino acids highly bioavailable
- **Essential amino acids**: Particularly glutamic acid (umami), leucine, and lysine
- **B vitamins**: Produced by microbial activity, especially B1, B2, B6, B12 (in long-fermented varieties)
- **Minerals**: Iron, zinc, calcium, potassium (phytic acid partially degraded)
- **Isoflavones**: Genistein, daidzein, and their glucosides — converted to bioactive aglycone forms during fermentation
- **Probiotics**: Lactic acid bacteria and yeast (in unpasteurized miso)

### Culinary Uses

Miso is extraordinarily versatile:

- **Miso soup** (*misoshiru*): The most common use — dissolved in dashi with tofu, wakame, or vegetables
- **Marinades**: Miso-glazed fish (*saikyo yaki*), eggplant, or pork
- **Pickling**: Vegetables pickled in miso (*misozuke*) develop complex flavors over days to weeks
- **Dressings and sauces**: Miso mayonnaise, miso vinaigrette, miso butter
- **Seasoning**: A spoonful added to stews, braises, or ramen broth for depth

**Important**: Miso should not be boiled, as high heat destroys its volatile aroma compounds and kills beneficial microorganisms. Add it off-heat or at the very end of cooking.

## Tempeh: The Rhizopus Fermentation

### History and Cultural Significance

Tempeh originated on the island of Java, Indonesia, likely several hundred years ago. The earliest written reference appears in the *Serat Centhini*, a Javanese manuscript from the early 19th century, but the practice is certainly much older, passed down through generations of Javanese home cooks.

Tempeh is a daily staple in Indonesia, particularly in Central Java. It is sold in traditional markets (*pasar*) as dense, white-mycelium-bound cakes wrapped in banana leaves or plastic. Indonesia produces over 250,000 tonnes of tempeh annually, and it plays a role analogous to tofu or cheese in Western diets — an affordable, protein-rich food consumed across all socioeconomic levels.

Unlike miso, which requires sophisticated koji cultivation, tempeh fermentation is relatively accessible. The traditional inoculant, *usar* or *ragi*, is a dried starter culture made from hibiscus leaves (*waru*, *Hibiscus tiliaceus*) that naturally harbor *Rhizopus oligosporus* spores. This makes tempeh one of the most democratized fermented foods — any household can produce it with minimal equipment.

### The Mold: *Rhizopus oligosporus*

Tempeh is produced by the zygomycete mold *Rhizopus oligosporus* (sometimes classified as *Rhizopus arrhizus* or *Rhizopus oligosporus* NRRL 2710). Key characteristics:

- **Rapid growth**: Colonizes soaked, cooked soybeans within 24–36 hours at 30–32°C
- **Firm mycelium**: Produces a dense, cottony-white network that binds soybeans into a cohesive cake
- **Strong proteolytic and lipolytic activity**: Breaks down soy protein and fat
- **Antibacterial properties**: Produces bacteriocin-like compounds that inhibit *Bacillus subtilis* and *Salmonella*, making tempeh remarkably food-safe
- **Reduced oligosaccharides**: Metabolizes raffinose and stachyose, eliminating the flatulence problem of whole soybeans

Other *Rhizopus* species used for tempeh include *R. oryzae* and *R. stolonifer*, but *R. oligosporus* is preferred for its clean flavor and strong binding.

### Tempeh Production Process

#### 1. Soybean Preparation

Whole soybeans are sorted, washed, and soaked in water (often acidified with lactic acid or vinegar to pH 4.5–5.0) for 8–16 hours. The acidification step is critical — it favors *Rhizopus* growth while suppressing bacterial contaminants, particularly *Klebsiella pneumoniae* which can cause off-flavors.

After soaking, the hulls are removed. This is traditionally done by hand-rubbing the beans in water, allowing hulls to float and be skimmed off. Commercially, the beans are dehulled using abrasive mills or air classifiers. Dehulling is essential for proper mycelial penetration.

#### 2. Cooking

The dehulled beans are boiled or steamed until fully cooked but not mushy — typically 60–90 minutes. They are then drained and dried on clean cloths or screens until the surface is relatively dry but the beans remain hydrated internally. This surface drying prevents excess moisture that would encourage bacterial growth over fungal growth.

#### 3. Inoculation

The cooled beans (below 37°C) are sprinkled with *R. oligosporus* spores. Traditional Javanese methods use *usar* (dried hibiscus leaf starter), while modern commercial production uses purified spore powder. The typical inoculation rate is 1–5 grams of spore powder per kilogram of dry beans.

#### 4. Incubation

The inoculated beans are spread in a thin layer (2–4 cm thick) in perforated containers — traditionally banana leaves, now plastic bags with holes, or shallow trays. Incubation temperature is maintained at 28–32°C for 24–48 hours.

During incubation, the mycelium grows rapidly:

| Time | Observation |
|------|-------------|
| 6–12 hours | White mycelium first visible on bean surfaces |
| 12–24 hours | Mycelium begins binding beans together |
| 24–36 hours | Firm, white cake formed; heat generation peaks |
| 36–48 hours | Fully mature; slight grayening may appear at the surface |

The beans must be ventilated during fermentation. *Rhizopus* is aerobic and generates significant metabolic heat. Without adequate ventilation, temperatures can exceed 40°C, promoting unwanted bacteria. Traditional banana-leaf wrapping provides natural ventilation; modern perforated bags serve the same function.

#### 5. Harvesting and Storage

Fresh tempeh is ready when the mycelium has fully bound the beans into a firm cake. It should be used within 2–3 days at room temperature or 5–7 days refrigerated. For longer storage, tempeh can be:

- **Frozen**: Up to 3 months; texture changes slightly after thawing
- **Pasteurized**: Brief steaming kills the mold and extends refrigerated shelf life to 2–3 weeks
- **Dehydrated**: Sun-dried or oven-dried tempeh chips (*tempeh kripik*) are a popular Indonesian snack
- **Deep-fried**: *Tempeh goreng* — pan-fried tempeh is the most common preparation in Java

### Nutritional Profile

Tempeh fermentation dramatically improves soybean nutrition:

- **Protein bioavailability**: *Rhizopus* proteases partially hydrolyze soy protein, increasing digestibility from ~65% to ~85%
- **Vitamin B12**: Tempeh is one of the few plant sources of vitamin B12, though the amount is variable and may come from bacterial contaminants rather than the mold itself
- **Reduced oligosaccharides**: Raffinose and stachyose are reduced by 80–90%, eliminating flatulence
- **Enhanced mineral absorption**: Phytic acid reduced by 30–50%
- **Antioxidants**: Isoflavone aglycones (genistein, daidzein) increase 2–3× during fermentation
- **Free amino acids**: Glutamic acid increases significantly, contributing umami flavor

### Beyond Soy: Tempeh Variations

While soybeans are the traditional substrate, tempeh can be made from many legumes and grains:

| Substrate | Notes |
|-----------|-------|
| **Black soybeans** (*koro hitam*) | Richer, earthier flavor; Javanese specialty |
| **Chickpeas** | Creamy texture, nutty flavor; popular in the West |
| **Lentils** | Faster fermentation; delicate flavor |
| **Mung beans** | Indonesian *tempeh mung*; lighter texture |
| **Fava beans** | Egyptian *tempeh ful*; robust, meaty |
| **Coconut press-cake** (*ampas kelapa*) | *Tempeh bongkrek* — **DANGER**: historical association with *Burkholderia cocovenenans* toxin outbreaks, now largely banned |
| **Grain-legume blends** | Oats + soy, barley + chickpea, etc. |

## Natto: The Bacillus Fermentation

### Overview

Natto is a Japanese fermented soybean product with a distinctive sticky, stringy texture and strong ammonia-like aroma. It is produced by *Bacillus subtilis subsp. natto*, a spore-forming bacterium that thrives at high temperatures.

Natto has been consumed in Japan for over 1,000 years. Legend attributes its discovery to Minamoto no Yoshiie (1039–1106 CE), who left cooked soybeans in a straw saddlebag and found them fermented days later. Straw (*wara*) was the traditional incubation material, as it naturally harbors *B. subtilis natto* spores.

### Production

Natto production is relatively simple:

1. Small soybeans are soaked and steamed until soft
2. Inoculated with *B. subtilis natto* spores
3. Incubated at 38–42°C for 18–24 hours
4. Aged at 0–5°C for 1–5 days to develop flavor and viscosity

The characteristic **natto threads** (natto-hiki) are produced by poly-γ-glutamic acid (γ-PGA), a viscous polymer synthesized by *B. subtilis* during fermentation. This polymer gives natto its unique stringy, mucilaginous texture when stirred.

### Nutritional Significance

Natto is notable for several unique nutritional properties:

- **Nattokinase**: A serine protease produced by *B. subtilis natto* that demonstrates fibrinolytic (blood-clot-dissolving) activity in vitro. Nattokinase supplements are marketed for cardiovascular health, though clinical evidence remains mixed.
- **Vitamin K2 (menaquinone-7)**: Natto is the richest known dietary source of MK-7, essential for bone health and cardiovascular function. A single serving (50g) provides 400–500 μg, far exceeding any other food.
- **High-quality protein**: Fully digestible due to extensive bacterial proteolysis
- **Probiotic**: *B. subtilis natto* spores survive stomach acid and can colonize the gut temporarily

### Culinary Use

Natto is typically served over steamed rice, mixed with soy sauce, karashi mustard, and chopped green onions. It is a traditional breakfast food in eastern Japan (Kanto region) but less popular in western Japan (Kansai), where its strong aroma is considered off-putting.

## Comparative Overview: Miso, Tempeh, and Natto

| Feature | Miso | Tempeh | Natto |
|---------|------|--------|-------|
| **Primary organism** | *Aspergillus oryzae* (fungus) | *Rhizopus oligosporus* (fungus) | *Bacillus subtilis natto* (bacterium) |
| **Salt content** | 5–12% | None | None (salted at table) |
| **Fermentation temp.** | 20–30°C | 28–32°C | 38–42°C |
| **Duration** | Weeks to years | 24–48 hours | 18–24 hours + aging |
| **Texture** | Paste | Firm cake | Sticky, stringy beans |
| **Primary use** | Seasoning/soup | Main dish protein | Side dish over rice |
| **Origin** | China → Japan | Java, Indonesia | Japan |
| **Key enzyme** | Protease (umami) | Protease + lipase | Nattokinase, γ-PGA |
| **Shelf life** | Months to years (salted) | Days fresh; months frozen | 1–2 weeks refrigerated |

## Making These at Home

### Miso

Making miso at home requires patience more than skill. A basic recipe:

1. **500g soybeans**: Soak overnight, boil 3–4 hours until very soft
2. **500g rice**: Steam 40 minutes, cool to 35°C
3. **Inoculate**: Mix rice with 5g *tane-koji* (*A. oryzae* spores), incubate 36–48 hours at 30°C → koji rice
4. **Mash**: Combine cooked beans with koji rice and 200g sea salt
5. **Pack**: Press into a ceramic crock, smooth the surface, cover with salt
6. **Ferment**: Store at cool room temperature (15–25°C) for 6–12 months minimum
7. **Stir**: Optional — some makers stir the miso periodically (*miso okaeshi*) to improve texture

### Tempeh

Home tempeh production is achievable with a simple incubator:

1. **500g soybeans**: Soak in acidified water (vinegar, pH 4.5) overnight
2. **Dehull**: Rub beans between hands, float off hulls, drain
3. **Cook**: Boil/steam 60 minutes, drain and dry surface
4. **Inoculate**: Cool to below 35°C, mix with 1g *R. oligosporus* spore powder
5. **Pack**: Spread in perforated ziplock bags (poke holes every 1cm), 2cm thick
6. **Incubate**: 30°C for 24–36 hours in a warm, humid place (oven with light on, cooler with heater, or purpose-built incubator)
7. **Check**: Ready when fully white with firm mycelium binding

### Natto

The simplest of the three:

1. **300g small soybeans**: Soak 12 hours, steam until very soft (3+ hours)
2. **Inoculate**: Cool to 45°C, mix with 0.1g *B. subtilis natto* spores dissolved in a spoonful of water
3. **Incubate**: Spread thin in a container, cover with cheesecloth, keep at 40°C for 20–24 hours
4. **Age**: Refrigerate 1–3 days before eating

## Health Considerations

### Benefits

- **Probiotics**: All three contain live microorganisms (in unpasteurized forms) that may support gut health
- **Improved digestibility**: Fermentation breaks down antinutritional factors and partially pre-digests proteins
- **Isoflavone bioavailability**: Fermentation converts isoflavone glucosides to aglycones, which are more readily absorbed
- **Reduced allergenicity**: Some soy allergens are degraded during fermentation
- **Bioactive compounds**: Nattokinase (natto), B vitamins (miso), antioxidants (all three)

### Concerns

- **Salt content**: Miso is high in sodium — those managing blood pressure should use it mindfully
- **Soy allergies**: While fermentation reduces allergenicity, highly sensitive individuals should still exercise caution
- **Histamine**: Long-fermented miso contains histamine, which can trigger symptoms in histamine-intolerant individuals
- **Phytoestrogens**: Soy isoflavones have weak estrogenic activity; the clinical significance of this is debated
- **Mold sensitivity**: Some individuals may react to mold-fermented foods

### Affirmation

Fermented soyfoods represent one of humanity's most sophisticated food technologies — a convergence of microbiology, culinary art, and cultural tradition that transforms a humble legume into some of the most flavorful and nutritious foods on Earth. Whether it's the deep umami of a years-old red miso, the nutty satisfaction of pan-fried tempeh, or the polarizing intensity of natto, these foods demonstrate the transformative power of controlled fermentation.
