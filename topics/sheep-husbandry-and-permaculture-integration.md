---# Sheep Husbandry and Permaculture Integration

Sheep (Ovis aries) are among the most versatile and manageable livestock for
small-scale farms and permaculture systems. Domesticated over 10,000 years ago
in the Fertile Crescent, sheep provide meat, wool, milk, and landscape
management services with relatively low infrastructure requirements.

Their natural flocking instinct makes them easier to contain and handle than
goats or cattle, while their efficient conversion of pasture to protein
makes them economically viable on modest acreage. This article covers breed
selection, housing, feeding, health management, shearing, breeding, and
[[duck-keeping-and-integration-into-permaculture-systems]] design systems.

## Botanical and Biological Overview

Sheep are ruminant herbivores in the family Bovidae, subfamily Caprinae.

They are classified as grazers rather than browsers, meaning they prefer
short grasses and forbs over woody vegetation. This distinguishes them
from goats, which are natural browsers of shrubs and trees.

A sheep's four-chambered stomach allows digestion of cellulose through
microbial fermentation, converting pasture that humans cannot eat into
high-quality protein. Their digestive efficiency makes them ideal for
marginal land unsuitable for crop production.

Adult sheep weigh 100-350 lbs depending on breed. Ewes typically produce
1-3 lambs per lambing, with a gestation period of approximately 147 days
(about 5 months). Natural lifespan is 10-12 years, though productive
breeding life is typically 5-7 years.

## Breed Selection

Choosing the right breed is the most consequential decision for a sheep
operation. Breeds vary enormously in size, wool type, meat yield, mothering
ability, [[jeavons-climate-adaptation-growing-seasons]], and management requirements.

### Meat Breeds

- **Suffolk** — Large, fast-growing, muscular. The most common commercial
  meat breed in North America. Excellent for crossbreeding programs.
- **Hampshire** — Similar to Suffolk but hardier in harsh conditions.
  Good mothers with strong foraging ability.
- **Katahdin** — Hair sheep requiring no shearing. Parasite-resistant and
  excellent mothers. Ideal for homesteads focused solely on meat production.
- **Dorper** — Another hair sheep breed, fast-growing and docile. Crosses
  well with wool breeds for market lambs.
- **Texel** — Exceptionally muscular, lean carcass. Popular as a terminal
  sire in crossbreeding systems.

### Wool Breeds

- **Merino** — Produces the finest wool in the sheep world, prized for
  spinning and garment making. Requires more management to prevent fly
  strike in skin folds.
- **Romney** — Long, lustrous wool excellent for handspinning. Hardy and
  adaptable to wet climates.
- **Lincoln** — Very long staple length with strong wool. Less common but
  valuable for specific textile applications.
- **Shetland** — Small, extremely hardy, naturally colored in multiple
  shades. Ideal for small-scale fiber operations.

### Dual-Purpose and Dairy Breeds

- **Columbia, Corriedale, Targhee** — American-developed dual-purpose
  breeds offering acceptable wool and meat production.
- **Icelandic** — Primitive breed with dual-coated fleece, excellent meat,
  and extreme cold hardiness. Well-suited to extensive grazing systems.
- **East Friesian** — The highest milk-producing sheep breed, used
  commercially for artisan cheese production worldwide.
- **Lacaune** — French dairy breed and the foundation of Roquefort cheese
  production. Adapted to rocky, dry landscapes.

## Climate and Environmental Requirements

Sheep are remarkably adaptable across climate zones, but breed selection
should match local conditions.

They tolerate cold exceptionally well due to their wool insulation, which
provides effective protection down to -30°F with wind shelter. Heat is
a greater concern — sheep suffer in sustained temperatures above 85°F
and require shade during summer months.

Ideal sward (grass) height for grazing is 3-6 inches. Sheep graze closer
to the ground than cattle and should be rotated before pasture is grazed
below 2 inches to prevent overgrazing and root damage.

Rainfall of 20+ inches annually supports productive pasture without
irrigation in most [[oyster-mushroom-log-cultivation-temperate-regions-short-log-method]]. Arid regions require supplementary
feeding or larger acreage per animal.

Stocking rate on good improved pasture with [[comparison-rotational-grazing-vs-continuous-grazing]] runs
4-6 sheep per acre. [[aquaculture-pond-yields-and-stocking-rates]] must be adjusted downward for marginal
land, arid climates, or continuous grazing systems.

## Housing and Shelter

Sheep need less elaborate housing than most livestock, but basic shelter
from wind, rain, and extreme heat is essential.

A simple three-sided shelter facing away from prevailing winds is
sufficient in moderate climates. Sheep tolerate cold well when dry, but
prolonged wet conditions lead to footrot and fleece damage.

Indoor space requirements are minimal: 10-15 square feet per sheep.
Lambing pens should provide 16-20 square feet per ewe-lamb pair.

Fencing must be predator-proof. Woven wire fencing at 48 inches height
with a strand of barbed wire at the bottom deters most predators.
Electronet fencing works well for rotational grazing and can be moved
easily between paddocks.

Guard animals are strongly recommended. Livestock guardian dogs (Great
Pyrenees, Anatolian Shepherd, Akbash) live with the flock and deter
coyotes, stray dogs, and other predators. Donkeys and llamas also serve
as effective guardians in some situations.

## Feeding and Pasture Management

Sheep are efficient converters of pasture to protein when managed well.
Their nutritional needs change significantly across the production cycle.
