## Irrigation Design for Small Farms

Irrigation design for small farms balances crop water needs, available
supply, and the labor or energy required to deliver water. Unlike
large-scale agriculture with center pivots and canal networks, small-farm
irrigation prioritizes flexibility, water efficiency, low capital cost,
and compatibility with diverse cropping systems that change each season.

This article draws from Steve Solomon's scientific garden irrigation,
John Jeavons' water-efficient biointensive methods, Bill Mollison's
permaculture earthworks, Sepp Holzer's observation-based watering,
Masanobu Fukuoka's critique of irrigation-dependent agriculture, and
Eliot Coleman's winter greenhouse irrigation practices.

## Water Sources and Supply Assessment

Before choosing any system, understand the available water supply. Sources
include wells, springs, streams, ponds, rainwater catchment, and
municipal connections. Each has different flow rates, reliability, and
legal considerations.

Flow rate determines system capacity. A well producing 3 GPM cannot
supply the same system as a stream delivering 50 GPM. Solomon's well
supplied less than 3 GPM, forcing him to use drip lines rather than
sprinklers out of necessity, not preference.

Water quality affects both system design and crop health. High mineral
content clogs drip emitters. Salty water damages sensitive crops and
accumulates in soil. Fukuoka observed from an airplane that center-pivot
irrigation across the American West was drawing dissolved salts to the
surface, gradually poisoning the soil it was meant to nourish.

## Soil-Water Relationships

Soil is the reservoir that holds water between irrigation events. Sandy
soils hold only about 0.8 inches of available water per foot of depth.
Clay soils hold 2.0 to 2.4 inches per foot but release it more slowly.
Loam soils occupy the productive middle at about 1.6 inches per foot.

Three reference points define plant-available water. Field capacity is
the moisture remaining after gravity drainage ceases. Permanent wilting
point is below what plants can extract. Available water capacity is the
difference — the usable reservoir between irrigation events.

Roots require oxygen to function. Overwatering suffocates roots by
displacing air from pore spaces. Effective irrigation delivers enough
water without creating anaerobic conditions. Solomon recommended the
jar test to determine clay content: shake soil with water and dish soap,
then observe settlement layers.

## Sprinkler Irrigation Systems

Sprinkler irrigation distributes water through the air, simulating
rainfall. It remains the most common and accessible garden irrigation
method, particularly for vegetable plots where uniform germination
matters.

Oscillating sprinklers suit rectangular beds. Impact sprinklers cover
large circular areas for pastures and orchards. Rotary nozzles provide
good coverage at lower application rates. Solomon considered sprinklers
superior to drip for most vegetable gardens because they distribute water
over an area, essential for seed germination and seedling establishment.

The main disadvantages are evaporation losses in hot or windy conditions
and foliar wetting that promotes fungal diseases. Watering early morning
minimizes both problems: temperatures are lower and foliage dries
quickly as the day warms.

## Drip Irrigation and Microirrigation

Drip irrigation delivers water directly to the root zone through tubes
and emitters, reducing water use by 30 to 50 percent compared to
sprinklers. For permanent plantings like orchards, vineberries, and
berry patches, drip is often the best choice.

Components include backflow preventers, filters, pressure regulators,
main lines, lateral lines, emitters, and timers. Emitter types range
from button drippers at 0.5 to 4 GPH to drip tape with built-in emitters
at 6 to 18 inch spacing for row crops.

However, drip has significant limitations for vegetable gardens. Solomon
used drip from 1982 through the early 1990s and found them expensive,
short-lived, and troublesome. Emitters clog even with filters. Lines
shift with temperature changes, missing seedlings. Drip delivers water
to a point rather than an area, making it unsuitable for germination.

Microirrigation is a hybrid using low-pressure tubing with miniature
sprinkler heads rated in gallons per hour. They work well under orchard
trees and in vineyards. Buy from agricultural suppliers rather than
garden center bubble packs for substantial savings.

## Passive Irrigation Methods

Passive irrigation delivers water without pumps, timers, or electricity,
relying on gravity, capillary action, and soil moisture dynamics. These
systems reduce labor and maintain consistent root-zone moisture.
