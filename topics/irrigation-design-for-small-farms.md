# Irrigation Design for Small Farms

Irrigation design for small farms is the art and science of delivering the
right amount of water to the right place at the right time, using systems
scaled to the resources and labor constraints of homesteads, market gardens,
and smallholder operations. Unlike large-scale agricultural irrigation, which
depends on center pivots, canal networks, and pump stations, small-farm
irrigation prioritizes flexibility, water efficiency, low capital cost, and
compatibility with diverse cropping systems that change from season to season.

The fundamental challenge of irrigation design is balancing three competing
demands: crop water needs, available water supply, and the labor or energy
required to deliver it. Every farm has a different combination of soil type,
climate, crop mix, topography, and water source, which means there is no
single correct irrigation system. The best design emerges from understanding
the principles that underlie all irrigation methods and then adapting them to
the specific conditions of a given site.

Water is the primary limiting factor in most terrestrial ecosystems. Soil
erosion, desertification, and water scarcity threaten food security for
billions of people worldwide. At the farm scale, even small improvements in
water management can dramatically increase yields, reduce labor, and build
resilience against drought. The difference between a farm that thrives in a
dry year and one that fails often comes down to how well its irrigation system
was designed and managed.

## Understanding Soil Moisture

Effective irrigation design begins with understanding how soil holds and
releases water. Soil moisture exists on a continuum between two extremes:
saturation, where every pore space is filled with water, and the permanent
wilting point, where remaining water is held so tightly by soil particles that
plant roots cannot extract it. Between these extremes lies the range of
available water that plants can actually use.

When soil has absorbed all the water it can hold against the force of gravity,
it reaches field capacity. This is the upper limit of useful soil moisture.
Like a fully soaked sponge, the soil cannot hold any more water without it
draining away. After saturation, any additional irrigation simply leaches
nutrients below the root zone and is wasted.

The amount of available water varies enormously by soil type. Sandy soils hold
the least water, roughly 0.8 inches per foot of depth per 1,000 square feet,
but they make most of it available to plants. Clay soils hold the most, up to
2.4 inches per foot of depth, but much of that water is held so tightly that
plants struggle to access it. Loam soils offer the best balance, holding about
1.6 inches per foot with good availability.

Water loss from soil follows predictable patterns that depend on climate. In
cool maritime climates like western Scotland or the Pacific Northwest coast,
daily evapotranspiration may be as low as 0.07 inches. In hot, dry desert
conditions, it can reach 0.28 inches per day or more. Understanding the local
evapotranspiration rate allows the farmer to calculate exactly how often
irrigation is needed and how much to apply.

The practical test for soil moisture is simple: dig down five to six inches
and grab a handful of soil. If it forms a ball that sticks together, moisture
is above 70 percent of field capacity and irrigation can wait. If it crumbles
and won't form a ball, it is time to water. This squeeze test, recommended by
Steve Solomon, is more reliable than any electronic sensor for the small
farmer.

## Irrigation Scheduling Principles

The goal of irrigation scheduling is to maintain soil moisture above 60
percent of field capacity in the root zone, watering back up to field capacity
when the threshold is reached. This means replacing water at approximately the
rate it is lost to evapotranspiration, without overwatering. Overwatering
leaches nutrients below the root zone, wastes water, and can create anaerobic
conditions that harm roots.

The basic scheduling plan follows a simple rhythm. Monitor soil moisture using
the squeeze test or a moisture meter. When the top foot of soil has dried to
about 60 percent of field capacity, apply enough water to bring it back to
capacity. In practice, this means watering every two to five days, with more
frequent applications on sandy soils and less frequent applications on heavy
clay soils.

For seedlings and newly transplanted crops, the schedule shifts to lighter,
more frequent applications. Germinating seeds and young transplants have
shallow root systems that dry out quickly. Once plants are established and
their roots have grown deeper, the interval between waterings can be extended,
encouraging the roots to follow the moisture downward.

Timing of application also matters. Watering in the early morning reduces
evaporative losses because the air is cool and wind is typically calm. Evening
watering is acceptable but can increase [[bloomfield-amphibian-decline-fungal-disease-climate-interactions]] pressure because
foliage stays wet overnight. Midday watering wastes the most water to
evaporation and should be avoided whenever possible.
