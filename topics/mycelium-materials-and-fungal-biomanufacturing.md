# Mycelium Materials and Fungal Biomanufacturing

## Introduction

Mycelium materials represent one of the most promising frontiers in sustainable manufacturing. By harnessing the vegetative body of fungi — the dense, branching network of hyphae known as mycelium — researchers and companies have developed a new class of biomaterials that can replace conventional plastics, animal leather, synthetic foams, and even certain construction materials. These materials are grown, not manufactured, using fungal biology as the production mechanism. The result is a circular, biodegradable, and often carbon-negative product class that aligns with pressing environmental imperatives.

The field sits at the intersection of mycology, materials science, industrial design, and sustainable engineering. Since the pioneering work of Ecovative Design (founded 2007 by Eben Bayer and Gavin McIntyre), mycelium materials have moved from academic curiosity to commercial products found in packaging, furniture, fashion, and architecture.

## The Biology Behind Mycelium Materials

### What Is Mycelium?

Mycelium is the vegetative part of a fungus, consisting of a mass of branching, thread-like hyphae. In nature, mycelium serves as the primary nutrient-gathering organ, secreting enzymes that break down organic matter and absorbing the resulting simple compounds. A single mycelial network can extend for kilometers underground, forming the largest organisms on Earth.

Key biological properties that make mycelium suitable for materials engineering:

- **Rapid growth**: Many fungal species can colonize several centimeters of substrate per day under optimal conditions
- **Mechanical strength**: Hyphal cell walls contain chitin and glucan polymers that provide structural rigidity
- **Self-adhesion**: Hyphae naturally fuse together (anastomosis), creating a continuous mat without binders
- **Enzymatic versatility**: Fungi produce a wide range of extracellular enzymes capable of modifying substrate chemistry
- **Environmental tolerance**: Many species tolerate a range of pH, temperature, and moisture conditions

### Species Selection

Different fungal species produce mycelium with distinct material properties:

| Species | Common Name | Material Properties | Primary Applications |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| *Ganoderma lucidum* | Reishi | Dense, hard, woody texture | Structural panels, furniture |
| *Pleurotus ostreatus* | Oyster mushroom | Fast-growing, moderate density | Packaging, insulation |
| *Trametes versicolor* | Turkey tail | Fine-textured, strong mat | Leather alternatives, composites |
| *Fomes fomentarius* | Tinder conk | Extremely dense, tough | Tool handles, structural elements |
| *Schizophyllum commune* | Split gill | Flexible, rubbery texture | Flexible materials, foams |
| *Lentinula edodes* | Shiitake | Dense, aromatic | Food-grade packaging, functional materials |
| *Ganoderma tsugae* | Hemlock varnish shelf | Very hard, water-resistant | Outdoor applications, tiles |
| *Pycnoporus cinnabarinus* | Cinnabar polypore | Produces natural pigments | Dyed materials, colored products |

### Substrate Engineering

The substrate — the organic material the mycelium colonizes — profoundly affects the final material's properties. Common substrates include:

**Agricultural residues:**
- Hemp hurds (hurd fibers from hemp stalk processing)
- Corn stalks and cob grits
- Wheat straw and rice husks
- Sugarcane bagasse
- Oat hulls
- Soybean hulls

**Wood products:**
- Sawdust (various hardwood and softwood species)
- Wood chips and shavings
- Milled wood fiber
- Paper pulp and cardboard waste

**Other substrates:**
- Cotton gin trash
- Coffee grounds and husks
- Spent grain from brewing
- Coconut coir (coconut fiber)

Substrate properties that influence material outcome:
- **Particle size**: Finer substrates produce denser, more uniform materials
- **Moisture content**: Typically 60–75% for optimal colonization
- **Nutrient availability**: Higher nitrogen supports faster growth but may produce softer materials
- **Carbon-to-nitrogen ratio**: Affects growth rate, density, and mechanical properties
- **Lignin content**: Higher lignin substrates produce stiffer, harder materials

## Manufacturing Processes

### Bulk Mycelium Composites (Foam and Insulation)

The most commercially mature mycelium material process produces rigid foam-like composites used for packaging insulation and structural panels:

1. **Substrate preparation**: Agricultural waste is milled to a consistent particle size (typically 1–5 mm), pasteurized (60–80°C for 30–120 minutes) to eliminate competing organisms, and adjusted to approximately 65% moisture content
2. **Inoculation**: Mycelium spawn (grain spawn or liquid culture) is mixed with the cooled substrate at a rate of 5–15% by weight
3. **Molding**: The inoculated substrate is packed into molds of the desired shape. Molds can range from simple rectangular forms to complex custom geometries
4. **Incubation**: Molds are held in controlled environments (22–28°C, 85–95% relative humidity, ambient CO₂, minimal light) for 3–7 days while the mycelium colonizes the substrate completely
5. **Growth cessation**: The material is dried (baked at 80–100°C) or otherwise treated to halt fungal growth and stabilize the structure
6. **Post-processing**: Optional treatments include additional drying, surface sealing, cutting, sanding, or coating

### Mycelium Leather (Mylo™ and Competitors)

Mycelium leather production follows a fundamentally different approach, optimized for producing thin, flexible, sheet-like materials:

1. **Substrate preparation**: A nutrient-rich liquid or semi-solid substrate (often based on sawdust, cellulose, or agricultural fiber) is prepared and sterilized
2. **Inoculation and growth**: Mycelium is grown on the substrate surface in shallow trays or on a supporting mat. Growth periods range from several days to two weeks
3. **Harvesting**: The mycelial mat is peeled from the substrate, resulting in a thick, fibrous sheet
4. **Processing**: The mat undergoes compression, drying, and finishing treatments:
   - **Tanning**: Chemical or plant-based tanning agents (similar to leather tanning) improve durability and water resistance
   - **Embossing**: Textures and grain patterns are pressed into the surface
   - **Dyeing**: Natural or synthetic dyes color the material
   - **Coating**: Protective coatings enhance water resistance and durability
   - **Thickness control**: Compression under heat reduces thickness to 0.5–2 mm

### Mycelium Bricks and Construction Materials

Mycelium-bound composites for construction represent a more recent development:

1. **Substrate selection**: Coarse agricultural fibers (straw, hemp hurds) provide bulk and structural matrix
2. **Inoculation and molding**: Mycelium is grown within formwork or brick molds, colonizing the fiber matrix over 5–14 days
3. **Drying and curing**: Materials are dried at elevated temperatures to kill the fungus and set the structure
4. **Compression (optional)**: Some processes apply mechanical compression during or after growth to increase density

Properties achieved:
- Compressive strength: 0.3–2.5 MPa (comparable to some lightweight concrete)
- Thermal conductivity: 0.04–0.06 W/(m·K) (excellent insulation)
- Density: 80–400 kg/m³ (lighter than most conventional building materials)
- Fire resistance: Naturally fire-retardant due to chitin and mineral content; can achieve Class A fire rating with additives

### Advanced Manufacturing Techniques

**3D printing with mycelium**: Researchers have developed bio-printing approaches where mycelium-laden bio-ink is extruded through nozzles to create three-dimensional structures. The living material continues to grow and strengthen after printing, self-healing small cracks and gaps.

**Electrospinning**: Mycelium-derived chitin and chitosan can be electrospun into nanofibers for advanced filtration, medical, and composite applications.

**Composite layup**: Mycelium sheets can be layered with natural fibers (hemp, flax, bamboo) in resin-free composite layups, creating laminated materials with enhanced strength and stiffness.

**Mineralization**: Introducing mineral precursors (calcium carbonate, iron oxides) during growth can produce mycelium-mineral composites with significantly enhanced mechanical properties, approaching those of some conventional materials.

## Material Properties and Performance

### Mechanical Properties

|----------|--------------|-----------------|----------------|

| Compressive strength | 0.05–0.5 MPa | N/A | 0.3–2.5 MPa |
| Tensile strength | 0.02–0.15 MPa | 5–25 MPa | 0.05–0.3 MPa |
| Flexural strength | 0.1–0.8 MPa | N/A | 0.1–0.5 MPa |
| Elongation at break | 1–5% | 10–60% | 1–3% |
| Young's modulus | 5–50 MPa | 100–500 MPa | 10–100 MPa |

### Environmental Properties

- **Biodegradability**: Fully biodegradable in soil environments within 30–180 days depending on material density and composition
- **Carbon footprint**: Carbon-negative to carbon-neutral depending on substrate sourcing; mycelium materials sequester atmospheric carbon during growth
- **Energy requirements**: Manufacturing energy is 60–90% lower than equivalent petroleum-based products
- **Water footprint**: Significantly lower than both synthetic materials and animal leather
- **End of life**: Can be composted, mulched, or in some formulations, used as a soil amendment
- **Toxicity**: Non-toxic; no VOC off-gassing; safe for indoor use without additional treatment

### Water Resistance

Untreated mycelium materials absorb moisture readily. Water resistance can be improved through:
- Application of natural wax coatings (beeswax, carnauba wax)
- Bio-based polymer coatings (shellac, plant oil treatments)
- Chemical modification of chitin (acetylation, cross-linking)
- Post-growth densification through compression
- Integration of hydrophobic fillers

## Applications

### Packaging and Protective Materials

The most commercially successful application to date. Mycelium packaging replaces expanded polystyrene (EPS/Styrofoam) and molded pulp:

- **Electronics packaging**: Custom-molded protective inserts for consumer electronics (Dell, IKEA partnerships)
- **Wine bottle shippers**: Protective molded inserts for shipping wine bottles
- **Furniture packaging**: Corner protectors and sheet foam for furniture transit
- **Cosmetics packaging**: Molded inserts for product protection and presentation

Advantages over EPS:
- Fully compostable end-of-life
- Comparable cushioning performance (drop-test verified)
- Can be grown to exact specifications
- Renewable feedstock
- No fossil fuel inputs

### Fashion and Textiles

Mycelium leather has emerged as a leading alternative to both animal leather and synthetic alternatives:

- **Footwear**: Adidas, Stella McCartney, and Lululemon have released products using mycelium leather (Bolt Threads' Mylo™)
- **Handbags and accessories**: Luxury brands including Hermes and Kering portfolio companies have prototyped mycelium leather goods
- **Clothing**: Jackets, pants, and other garments
- **Watch straps**: Luxury watchmakers exploring mycelium as a strap material

### Architecture and Construction

- **Insulation panels**: Rigid mycelium insulation boards for wall cavities and continuous insulation
- **Acoustic panels**: Mycelium's porous structure provides excellent sound absorption (NRC 0.6–0.9)
- **Bricks and blocks**: Load-bearing and non-load-bearing mycelium-bound masonry
- **Pavilion structures**: Several architectural installations have demonstrated mycelium as a primary structural material
- **Interior finishes**: Wall panels, ceiling tiles, and decorative elements

Notable projects:
- **Hy-Fi Pavilion** (MoMA PS1, 2014): Designed by The Living (David Benjamin), this installation used 10,000 mycelium bricks grown in just five days
- **The Growing Pavilion** (Dutch Design Week, 2019): A pavilion structure with mycelium roof and wall panels
- **KIT's MycoTree** (2017): A branching structural demonstration using mycelium and bamboo

### Other Applications

- **Filtration**: Mycelium's porous, chitin-rich structure can filter heavy metals, endocrine disruptors, and pathogens from water
- **Medical materials**: Chitosan derived from mycelium is used in wound dressings, drug delivery, and tissue engineering scaffolds
- **Automotive interior panels**: Door panels, headliners, and dashboard components
- **Cosmetics and personal care**: Mycelium-derived ingredients for skincare
- **Art and design**: Sculptures, lighting fixtures, and decorative objects

## Environmental Impact and Lifecycle Assessment

### Carbon Sequestration

Mycelium materials sequester carbon through two mechanisms:
1. **Substrate carbon**: The organic substrate (agricultural waste) stores carbon that would otherwise decompose or be burned
2. **Atmospheric carbon fixation**: During growth, fungi convert atmospheric CO₂ into fungal biomass

Quantification varies by species, substrate, and process, but estimates suggest:
- Mycelium foam: 0.5–2.0 kg CO₂ equivalent sequestered per kg of material
- Mycelium leather: 0.3–1.5 kg CO₂ equivalent sequestered per kg of material

### Waste Stream Utilization

Mycelium manufacturing creates value from waste streams:
- **Agricultural waste**: Diverts millions of tons of crop residues from burning or landfilling
- **Industrial byproducts**: Utilizes sawdust, spent grain, cotton gin trash, and food processing waste
- **Closed-loop potential**: End-of-life mycelium products can be composted and the resulting soil used to grow the substrate feedstock crops

## Challenges and Limitations

### Technical Challenges

- **Water sensitivity**: Untreated mycelium absorbs moisture and loses mechanical integrity when wet
- **Consistency**: Biological variability in growth leads to batch-to-batch property variation
- **Scalability**: Current manufacturing is labor-intensive and space-demanding compared to petrochemical processes
- **Mechanical performance**: While adequate for many applications, mycelium materials don't match the performance of engineered plastics or metals for high-stress applications
- **Shelf stability**: Living mycelium products have limited shelf life; fully stabilized products may be brittle
- **Fire performance**: While naturally more fire-resistant than many polymers, additional treatment may be needed for building code compliance

### Economic Challenges

- **Production costs**: Currently higher than mass-produced plastics and paper-based alternatives (though costs are declining with scale)
- **Capital investment**: Requires controlled environment facilities (clean rooms, incubation chambers)
- **Supply chain**: Substrate sourcing logistics and quality control add complexity
- **Market acceptance**: Consumer and industrial markets require education and performance validation
- **Regulatory hurdles**: Novel materials face regulatory scrutiny for food-contact, medical, and construction applications

### Biological Challenges

- **Contamination risk**: Molds, bacteria, and insects can compete with or degrade the target fungus during growth
- **Species optimization**: Selecting or engineering the ideal strain for specific material properties remains an active research area
- **Growth control**: Achieving uniform colonization in large or complex geometries is difficult
- **Genetic stability**: Maintaining consistent material properties across production generations requires careful strain management

## Research Frontiers

### Genetic Engineering and Synthetic Biology

CRISPR-based tools are being applied to fungal genetics to:
- Optimize growth rate and colonization efficiency
- Enhance mechanical properties through chitin and glucan pathway modification
- Introduce functional properties (self-coloring, antimicrobial activity, bioluminescence)
- Improve stress tolerance for more robust manufacturing
- Create strains optimized for specific substrate utilization

### Hybrid Materials

Combining mycelium with other bio-based materials:
- **Mycelium-hemp composites**: Enhanced strength using hemp fiber reinforcement
- **Mycelium-bamboo laminates**: Combining mycelium's insulation with bamboo's tensile strength
- **Mycelium-bacteria co-cultures**: Bacterial cellulose + mycelium composites for enhanced properties
- **Mycelium-mineral composites**: Bio-mineralization for construction-grade strength

### Smart Materials

- **Self-healing materials**: Living mycelium composites that repair cracks through continued growth
- **Responsive materials**: Mycelium that changes properties in response to humidity, temperature, or mechanical stress
- **Bioluminescent materials**: Incorporating bioluminescent fungal genes for glow-in-the-dark applications
- **Sensing materials**: Mycelium that detects and responds to environmental contaminants

### Scale-Up Innovation

- **Continuous manufacturing**: Moving from batch to continuous production processes
- **In-situ growth**: Growing materials directly at the point of use (e.g., insulation grown within wall cavities)
- **Vertical farming integration**: Co-locating mycelium manufacturing with mushroom cultivation or vertical farms
- **Decentralized production**: Container-based manufacturing units for local, distributed production

## DIY and Small-Scale Production

### Home Mycelium Material Production

It is possible to produce mycelium materials at home or in a small workshop with minimal equipment:

**Basic equipment needed:**
- Pressure cooker or large pot for substrate pasteurization
- Clean containers or molds
- Spray bottle for moisture maintenance
- Clean workspace (wipeable surfaces, 70% isopropyl alcohol)
- Spawn or liquid culture of chosen species
- Scale and measuring tools

**Simple process for mycelium blocks:**
1. Collect substrate (sawdust, straw, or coffee grounds)
2. Pasteurize by soaking in hydrated lime water (pH 12) for 12–18 hours, or heat pasteurize
3. Drain and mix with spawn (10% by volume)
4. Pack tightly into molds
5. Cover with microporous tape or plastic with holes
6. Incubate at room temperature (20–25°C) in a dark, humid space
7. Wait 5–10 days until fully colonized (white mycelium visible throughout)
8. Remove from mold and dry in oven at 80–100°C for 4–8 hours
9. Cool and use, or apply finish (wax, oil, paint)

**Safety considerations:**
- Work cleanly to avoid contamination by mold species that produce mycotoxins
- Ensure adequate ventilation during pasteurization and drying
- If allergic to mold spores, wear a respirator during handling
- Discard any material that produces unusual colors or odors during growth (orange, green, or black patches indicate contamination)

- **ISO 527**: Tensile testing of plastics

- **Cradle to Cradle Certified™**: Available for mycelium materials meeting material health, circularity, clean air, and social fairness criteria
- **OK Compost / OK Compost HOME**: Biodegradability certification for home and industrial composting
- **USDA BioPreferred®**: Federal procurement preference for biobased products
- **Forest Stewardship Council (FSC)**: For substrate sourcing verification

## Future Outlook

The mycelium materials industry is projected to grow from approximately $80 million in 2024 to over $1 billion by 2035, driven by:

- **Regulatory pressure** on single-use plastics (EU Single-Use Plastics Directive, extended producer responsibility)
- **Corporate sustainability commitments** and ESG reporting requirements
- **Consumer demand** for sustainable alternatives to animal products
- **Performance improvements** through strain optimization and process innovation
- **Cost reduction** through manufacturing scale-up and supply chain optimization

Key milestones anticipated:
- **2025–2027**: Mycelium leather achieves price parity with premium animal leather for luxury goods
- **2027–2030**: Mycelium insulation panels achieve building code approval in major markets
- **2028–2032**: Large-scale continuous manufacturing facilities reach commercial operation
- **2030+**: Mycelium-based structural materials become viable for low-rise construction

The convergence of biotechnology, materials science, and sustainable design positions mycelium materials as a cornerstone of the emerging bio-economy — a manufacturing paradigm where products are grown rather than extracted, and where waste from one process becomes the feedstock for another.
