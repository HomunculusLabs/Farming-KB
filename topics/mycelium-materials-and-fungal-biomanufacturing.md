## Mycelium Materials and Fungal Biomanufacturing

## Introduction

Mycelium materials represent one of the most promising frontiers in sustainable manufacturing. By harnessing the vegetative body of fungi — the dense, branching network of hyphae known as mycelium — researchers and companies have developed a new class of biomaterials that can replace conventional plastics, animal leather, synthetic foams, and even certain construction materials. These materials are grown, not manufactured, using fungal biology as the production mechanism. The result is a circular, biodegradable, and often carbon-negative product class that aligns with pressing environmental imperatives.

The field sits at the intersection of mycology, materials science, industrial design, and sustainable engineering. Since the pioneering work of Ecovative Design (founded 2007 by Eben Bayer and Gavin McIntyre), mycelium materials have moved from academic curiosity to commercial products found in packaging, furniture, fashion, and architecture.

## The Biology Behind Mycelium Materials

### What Is Mycelium?

Mycelium is the vegetative part of a fungus, consisting of a mass of branching, thread-like hyphae. In nature, mycelium serves as the primary nutrient-gathering organ, secreting enzymes that break down organic matter and absorbing the resulting simple compounds. A single mycelial network can extend for kilometers underground, forming the largest organisms on Earth.

Key biological properties that make mycelium suitable for materials engineering:

- **Rapid growth**: Many fungal species can colonize several centimeters of substrate per day under optimal conditions
- **Mechanical strength**: Hyphal cell walls contain chitin and glucan polymers that provide structural rigidity
- **Self-adhesion**: Hyphae naturally fuse together (anastomosis), creating a continuous mat without binders
- **Enzymatic versatility**: Fungi produce a wide range of extracellular enzymes capable of modifying substrate chemistry
- **Environmental tolerance**: Many species tolerate a range of pH, temperature, and moisture conditions

Different fungal species produce mycelium with distinct material properties:

2. **Inoculation**: Mycelium spawn (grain spawn or liquid culture) is mixed with the cooled substrate at a rate of 5–15% by weight
3. **Molding**: The inoculated substrate is packed into molds of the desired shape. Molds can range from simple rectangular forms to complex custom geometries
4. **Incubation**: Molds are held in controlled environments (22–28°C, 85–95% relative humidity, ambient CO₂, minimal light) for 3–7 days while the mycelium colonizes the substrate completely
5. **Growth cessation**: The material is dried (baked at 80–100°C) or otherwise treated to halt fungal growth and stabilize the structure
6. **Post-processing**: Optional treatments include additional drying, surface sealing, cutting, sanding, or coating

### Mycelium Leather (Mylo™ and Competitors)

Mycelium leather production follows a fundamentally different approach, optimized for producing thin, flexible, sheet-like materials:

1. **Substrate preparation**: A nutrient-rich liquid or semi-solid substrate (often based on sawdust, cellulose, or agricultural fiber) is prepared and sterilized
2. **Inoculation and growth**: Mycelium is grown on the substrate surface in shallow trays or on a supporting mat. Growth periods range from several days to two weeks
3. **Harvesting**: The mycelial mat is peeled from the substrate, resulting in a thick, fibrous sheet
4. **Processing**: The mat undergoes compression, drying, and finishing treatments:
   - **Tanning**: Chemical or plant-based tanning agents (similar to leather tanning) improve durability and water resistance
   - **Embossing**: Textures and grain patterns are pressed into the surface
   - **Dyeing**: Natural or synthetic dyes color the material
   - **Coating**: Protective coatings enhance water resistance and durability
   - **Thickness control**: Compression under heat reduces thickness to 0.5–2 mm

### Mycelium Bricks and Construction Materials

Mycelium-bound composites for construction represent a more recent development:

1. **Substrate selection**: Coarse agricultural fibers (straw, hemp hurds) provide bulk and structural matrix
2. **Inoculation and molding**: Mycelium is grown within formwork or brick molds, colonizing the fiber matrix over 5–14 days
3. **Drying and curing**: Materials are dried at elevated temperatures to kill the fungus and set the structure
4. **Compression (optional)**: Some processes apply mechanical compression during or after growth to increase density

Properties achieved:
- Compressive strength: 0.3–2.5 MPa (comparable to some lightweight concrete)
- Thermal conductivity: 0.04–0.06 W/(m·K) (excellent insulation)
- Density: 80–400 kg/m³ (lighter than most conventional building materials)
- Fire resistance: Naturally fire-retardant due to chitin and mineral content; can achieve Class A fire rating with additives

### Advanced Manufacturing Techniques

**3D printing with mycelium**: Researchers have developed bio-printing approaches where mycelium-laden bio-ink is extruded through nozzles to create three-dimensional structures. The living material continues to grow and strengthen after printing, self-healing small cracks and gaps.

**Electrospinning**: Mycelium-derived [[chitin-and-chitosan-in-agriculture]] can be electrospun into nanofibers for advanced filtration, medical, and composite applications.

**Composite layup**: Mycelium sheets can be layered with natural fibers (hemp, flax, bamboo) in resin-free composite layups, creating laminated materials with enhanced strength and stiffness.

**Mineralization**: Introducing mineral precursors (calcium carbonate, iron oxides) during growth can produce mycelium-mineral composites with significantly enhanced mechanical properties, approaching those of some conventional materials.
