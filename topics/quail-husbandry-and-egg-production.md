## Quail Husbandry and Egg Production

Coturnix quail (*Coturnix japonica*) are the most productive
micro-livestock available to small-scale farmers, homesteaders, and
urban growers. They mature in just six weeks, begin laying eggs at
seven to eight weeks of age, and produce 250 to 320 eggs per hen per
year — all while requiring a fraction of the space, feed, and
infrastructure demanded by chickens.

Their small size, quiet demeanor, and rapid generational turnover make
them ideal for permaculture systems where space is limited and
diversity is valued. A standard cage measuring sixty centimeters on
each side can house a dozen productive layers, yielding dozens of
rich, delicately flavored eggs each week.

This topic page covers breed selection, housing systems, feeding and
nutrition, breeding and incubation, health management, egg production
optimization, processing for meat, and [[duck-keeping-and-integration-into-permaculture-systems]]
designs.

## Why Choose Quail

Quail offer the fastest turnaround of any domesticated meat animal. An
egg can become a laying hen in seven weeks, or a table-ready bird in
eight to ten weeks. This speed allows multiple production cycles per
season and rapid flock replacement.

Their space requirements are minimal. Cage systems allocate roughly one
[[comparison-biointensive-vs-square-foot-gardening]] per bird, making them feasible on suburban lots, rooftops,
and even indoor spaces with proper ventilation. In many jurisdictions
quail are classified as game birds rather than poultry, which means
they fall outside chicken-keeping ordinances.

Males produce a soft, low-volume crow that rarely travels beyond
thirty meters. This makes quail far more neighbor-friendly than
roosters, which are prohibited in many residential areas.

Feed efficiency is another major advantage. Quail convert feed at a
ratio of roughly 2.5 to 3.0 pounds of feed per pound of egg mass or
body weight gain. A single hen consumes only seven to ten kilograms of
feed per year, at an annual cost of roughly three to eight dollars.

## Coturnix Quail Biology

Coturnix quail are native to [[neurotropic-fungi-japan-east-asia-species-cultural-context]] and have been domesticated for
centuries. Mature hens weigh 150 to 200 grams, while males are
slightly larger at 170 to 250 grams. Their natural lifespan is two to
five years, though productive laying declines after the first year.

Quail are ground-dwelling birds that prefer to walk and run rather than
fly. They do not perch like chickens and spend most of their time on
the cage or pen floor. This behavioral trait simplifies housing
design, as roosts and elevated nesting boxes are unnecessary.

Their reproductive cycle is strongly photo-periodic. Hens require
fourteen to sixteen hours of light per day to maintain peak egg
production. Under natural conditions, production peaks during spring
and summer and declines as [[coleman-persephone-months-winter-day-length]] shortens in autumn.

## Breed Varieties

All domesticated Coturnix quail belong to the same species, but
selective breeding has produced several distinct varieties suited to
different production goals.

### Dual-Purpose and Egg Breeds

The Pharaoh, or wild-type variety, retains the natural brown
camouflage coloration. It is hardy, productive, and serves well as a
dual-purpose bird for both eggs and meat. This is the original
Japanese quail from which all other varieties descend.

The Golden variety displays light tan to gold plumage. Golden quail
are attractive, reliable layers, and frequently chosen by keepers who
want visual variety alongside productivity.

Jumbo Brown quail are larger than the standard Pharaoh, reaching ten
to fourteen ounces at maturity. They are the most common homestead
variety and produce well for both meat and eggs.

### Meat-Focused Breeds

The Texas A&M variety was developed at Texas A&M University
specifically for meat production. Birds are white-feathered, larger
than standard Coturnix, and calmer in temperament. Dress-out
percentage is excellent due to their broad breast conformation.

Jumbo Brown also performs well as a meat bird, with fast growth and
good feed conversion reaching market weight in eight to nine weeks.

### Ornamental Color Varieties

Tibetan (Rosetta) quail are dark brown to black with a light belly.
The Tuxedo variety has brown plumage with a white chest. Scarlet or
Red Range quail display rust-colored feathers. Silver and Cinnamon
varieties offer additional color diversity.

These ornamental varieties are productive layers in their own right,
but they are typically kept for aesthetic appeal rather than
commercial-scale production. All Coturnix varieties interbreed freely.

## Housing Systems

Quail housing ranges from intensive wire cages to extensive floor and
aviary systems. The choice depends on production goals, available
space, and management style.

### Wire Cage Systems

Wire cages are the most common housing method for commercial and
semi-commercial egg production. Standard construction uses 1.3
centimeter (half-inch) galvanized hardware cloth for floors and one
centimeter mesh for sides.

A typical cage unit measures sixty by sixty by thirty centimeters and
houses twelve to fifteen birds. The floor slopes at eight to ten
degrees toward the front, allowing eggs to roll gently into a
collection tray. This design keeps eggs clean and simplifies daily
collection.

Manure trays slide beneath the wire floor for easy cleaning. Lining
trays with newspaper, cardboard, or dried leaves speeds the [[composting]]
process and reduces ammonia buildup.

External tube feeders attached to the cage front minimize waste and
contamination. One feeder serves six to eight birds. Nipple waterers,
one per four to six birds, provide the cleanest water delivery and
reduce spillage.

expression but carries higher risk of feather picking, aggression, and
