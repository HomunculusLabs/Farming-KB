# Permaculture Zone and Sector Analysis

Zone and sector analysis are the foundational spatial planning tools of
permaculture design, developed by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren in the
1970s.
Together they provide a systematic framework for placing every element of
a design in its optimal position relative to both human energy centers
and the natural forces that flow across a site.

Zones organize elements by the frequency of human use and maintenance
needs, radiating outward from the center of activity.
Sectors map the external energies, such as sun, wind, fire, water, and
wildlife, that flow through a site from outside its boundaries.
When combined, zones and sectors allow designers to create landscapes that
minimize energy input while maximizing yield, resilience, and ecological
health.

These tools are taught in every Permaculture Design Certificate course
and remain the most widely understood and applied concepts in the
permaculture design toolkit.
Despite their apparent simplicity, they contain layers of nuance that
reward careful study and thoughtful application.

## The Origin of Zones and Sectors

Bill Mollison first articulated the zone concept in his 1978 book
Permaculture One, co-authored with David Holmgren, and expanded it
significantly in Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (1988).
The idea grew from the observation that traditional farming villages
naturally organized themselves with the most intensively managed gardens
closest to homes and less managed pastures and forests at the periphery.

Holmgren later extended the framework beyond site design into a
meta-analysis of permaculture itself, describing zones of influence
that range from personal action to global systems.
In this broader reading, zones of power and responsibility start with
the self and extend outward through family, community, bioregion, and
planet.

Mollison presented zone and sector analysis as the culminating design
method in Chapter 3 of the Designers' Manual, describing it as a
primary energy-conserving placement pattern for the whole site.
He identified two fundamental energy categories: energy available on
site (people, animals, wastes, fuels) and energy entering or flowing
through the site (wind, water, sunlight, fire).

## The Zone System

Zones are ranked from 0 to 5, with each number denoting the amount of
human impact and the frequency of visitation.
They are often depicted as concentric circles for teaching purposes, but
in practice they are almost never circular.
Zones can be any shape: oblong, irregular, or even discontinuous
patches connected by pathways.
The key principle is that elements requiring frequent attention belong
close to the center of activity, while self-maintaining elements belong
farther away.

Mollison emphasized that zones are a tool for rough estimation, not
literal boundaries with sharp lines of delineation.
The zone number reflects both how often you need to visit an element
and how often the element needs you to visit it.
The golden rule of zone planning is to develop the nearest area first,
get it under control, and then expand the perimeter outward.

## Zone 0: The Home and Built Structures

Zone 0 is the house or dwelling itself, the origin from which all
permaculture design radiates outward.
It encompasses indoor living spaces, the kitchen, attached greenhouses
or shade structures, and any integrated living components such as roof
gardens, trellised vines, and companion animals kept indoors.

The design of Zone 0 focuses on energy efficiency, thermal comfort,
water conservation, and the integration of growing systems into daily
living spaces.
Rainwater catchment tanks, passive solar features, indoor sprouting
shelves, vermicompost bins under the kitchen sink, and fermentation
stations all belong within or immediately adjacent to Zone 0.

Mollison described Zone 0 as the energy center of the system, where
human and animal energy is most concentrated.
Everything needed frequently should be within a few steps of the door.
The house should ideally be positioned halfway up a slope for optimal
drainage, solar exposure, and wind protection.

Holmgren notes that many people make the mistake of focusing their
design efforts on distant zones while neglecting Zone 0.
Starting at the back door, he argues, reminds designers not to
overreach before the immediate territory is organized and working well.

## Zone 1: The Intensive Garden

Zone 1 is the area immediately surrounding the home, visited one to
five times daily.
This is the most intensively managed zone, containing elements that
require continual observation, frequent harvesting, or regular
maintenance.

Typical Zone 1 elements include annual vegetable beds, salad gardens,
culinary herbs, seedling nurseries, small greenhouses or cold frames,
compost bins close to the kitchen, worm farms, and small animal
systems like chickens or rabbits.
Dwarf fruit trees, espaliered against walls, and intensively managed
berry bushes can also fit within Zone 1.

Mollison described the Zone 1 garden as fully mulched with no bare
soil, well bordered against weeds, and protected from browsing animals.
Within about 6 meters of the home, householders can produce most of
the food necessary for existence.
The key principle: if you can see it from your window and walk to it
in slippers, you will actually harvest it.

Plant placement within Zone 1 follows use frequency.
Daily-pick plants like parsley, chives, lettuce, and soft herbs go
nearest the door.
Occasional-harvest crops like tomatoes and peppers go at mid-range.
Single-harvest crops like potatoes, winter squash, and root vegetables
go at the outer edge of Zone 1.

Companion planting systems, such as the Three Sisters guild of corn,
beans, and squash, are commonly employed in Zone 1 to maximize
production per square meter through vertical stacking and mutual
benefit.

## Zone 2: The Orchard and Food Forest

Zone 2 is visited every few days and contains elements that are
largely self-maintaining but still actively managed.
This is typically the home orchard and food forest zone, with main
fruit and nut trees, berry bushes, and understory plantings.

Elements commonly found in Zone 2 include standard and semi-dwarf
fruit trees, bramble fruits, larger poultry systems like chicken
tractors, beehives, ponds, and the main composting area.
Perennial vegetables, grazing animals, and larger propagation areas
also belong here.

The orchard zone demonstrates the principle of stacking, where trees,
shrubs, groundcovers, and root crops occupy the same vertical and
horizontal space.
A well-designed Zone 2 food forest can produce enormous yields with
minimal ongoing maintenance once established.

Mollison treats Zone 2 as less intensively managed than Zone 1, with
spot-mulching rather than complete coverage and pruning on a seasonal
rather than weekly basis.
The key design consideration is ensuring that the tree canopy captures
maximum light while the understory receives sufficient filtered
sunlight for productive growth.

## Zone 3: Broad-Scale Cropping and Pasture

Zone 3 is visited weekly to monthly and represents the commercial or
production-scale area of the property.
This zone contains broadacre crops, pasture for grazing animals,
large-scale tree crops like olives or chestnuts, and main earthworks
for water distribution.

Elements in Zone 3 include large grain or vegetable fields, rotational
grazing paddocks, orchards managed for wholesale production, windbreaks
and shelterbelts, and seed-saving areas.
This is where surplus is generated for trade, storage, and community
distribution.

Management in Zone 3 is less intensive, relying on designed systems
that largely maintain themselves.
Keyline plowing, swale systems, and rotational grazing patterns
reduce the need for frequent human intervention while maintaining
soil health and productivity.

The boundary between Zone 2 and Zone 3 is often defined by the extent
of permanent irrigation or the reach of daily management.
Beyond this point, systems must be resilient enough to function with
periodic rather than constant attention.

## Zone 4: Forage and Timber

Zone 4 is visited seasonally and represents the forage, timber, and
wild harvest zone.
This area contains managed woodlots, nut tree plantations, foraging
grounds for wild mushrooms and herbs, and wildlife habitat corridors.

Elements in Zone 4 include timber trees managed for sustainable
harvest, coppiced woodland for firewood, bee forage areas, hunting
and foraging grounds, and large-scale water catchment systems.
This zone is primarily self-maintaining, with human intervention
limited to seasonal harvesting and occasional management activities
like selective logging or prescribed burns.

Zone 4 serves as a buffer between the managed productive zones and
the true wilderness of Zone 5.
It provides resources like firewood, building materials, and wild
foods without the intensive management required by inner zones.

## Zone 5: The Wild

Zone 5 is the unmanaged wilderness area, visited primarily for
observation, education, and inspiration.
This zone is left to natural processes and serves as a reservoir of
biodiversity, genetic material, and ecological balance.

Mollison and Holmgren both emphasize that Zone 5 is essential even
on small properties, though it may be as modest as a fencerow or a
small untended corner.
In urban environments, a true Zone 5 may not exist at all, and
designers should acknowledge this limitation rather than forcing the
five-zone model onto every site.

Zone 5 provides pest control services through predator habitat,
pollinator populations, seed sources for wild plant propagation, and
a reference ecosystem against which the managed zones can be compared.
It is the zone of least human intervention and greatest ecological
integrity.

## Sector Analysis: Mapping External Energies

While zones organize elements by frequency of human use, sectors map
the external forces that flow across a site from outside its
boundaries.
Mollison defines sectors as the directional energies of sun, wind,
fire, water, noise, pollution, views, and wildlife that influence
site design.

Sectors are mapped as wedge-shaped zones on the site plan, radiating
outward from the center like spokes of a wheel.
Each wedge represents a different incoming energy.
The designer's task is to either channel desirable energies into the
system or deflect undesirable energies away from vulnerable elements.

Multiple energies may arrive from the same direction, creating compound
sector effects.
For example, cold winter winds and wildfire risk may both come from
the northwest, requiring a unified design response that addresses both
threats simultaneously.

There is no exhaustive list of sectors.
The designer determines which forces meaningfully affect the design
based on site-specific conditions, climate, and context.

## Sector Analysis: Sun, Wind, and Fire

Solar analysis maps the path of the sun across the site through the
year, tracking summer and winter sun angles, sunrise and sunset
positions, shadow patterns, and solar heat gain on buildings and
growing areas.
In the Northern Hemisphere, the sun tracks low in the south during
winter and high overhead in summer, informing placement of the home
and deciduous shade trees.

Wind analysis identifies prevailing wind directions, seasonal
variations, and storm patterns.
Windbreaks, shelterbelts, and building placement are designed to
block cold winds while channeling cooling summer breezes through
the site.

Fire sector analysis identifies the direction from which wildfire
would most likely approach and designs firebreaks, water access
points, and fuel-reduced zones accordingly.
This is critical in fire-prone regions and often determines the
placement of structures and access roads.

## Sector Analysis: Water, Wildlife, and Access

Water sectors map the flow of surface water, groundwater movement,
and flood risk across the site.
Swales, ponds, and drainage channels are positioned to capture,
store, and direct water from high-rainfall sectors toward productive
elements while diverting excess away from vulnerable areas.

Wildlife sectors track the movement patterns of animals, both
desirable and undesirable.
Designers create habitat corridors for beneficial species while
placing barriers or deterrents against pest animals.
Pollinator habitat, bird nesting sites, and predator perches are
positioned to support biological pest control.

Access sectors map roads, pathways, and points of entry.
The placement of gates, driveways, and pedestrian routes is designed
to minimize conflict between human movement and productive
landscape elements while ensuring efficient circulation.

Additional sectors may include noise from roads or industrial areas,
undesirable views that need visual screening, and desirable views
that should be framed or highlighted in the design.

## Integrating Zones and Sectors

The power of zone and sector analysis emerges when the two frameworks
are overlaid on the same site plan.
Zones tell you what goes where based on human use patterns.
Sectors tell you how to shape the response to natural forces at each
location.

For example, the house (Zone 0) should be positioned to capture
winter sun (a desirable solar sector) while being sheltered from
cold winds (an undesirable wind sector).
Zone 1 gardens should be placed on the sunny side of the house with
easy access from the kitchen, while windbreaks protect them from
drying winds.
Water-harvesting earthworks in Zone 3 intercept runoff from uphill
sectors and distribute it to lower productive zones.

Holmgren emphasizes that zones and sectors are not rigid prescriptions
but tools for thinking about energy flows.
A skilled designer uses them as starting points and then adapts the
placement of elements based on local conditions, observation, and
iterative refinement.

## Zone and Sector Planning for Urban Sites

Urban permaculture presents special challenges for zone and sector
analysis.
Many urban sites contain only Zone 0 and Zone 1, with Zone 5
essentially nonexistent.
Designers must work within these constraints rather than forcing the
full five-zone model onto every site.

In dense urban environments, zones may be vertical rather than
horizontal.
A balcony garden, rooftop greenhouse, and windowsill herb collection
can all constitute Zone 1, even though they are not contiguous.
Community gardens, shared orchards, and urban farms may serve as
de facto Zone 2 or Zone 3 for apartment dwellers.

Urban sector analysis often focuses on noise, pollution, shade from
adjacent buildings, and limited solar access.
Creative solutions include using walls as thermal mass, placing
shade-tolerant crops in shadow sectors, and designing wildlife
corridors through fragmented urban habitat.

## Zone and Sector Planning for Large Properties

On large rural properties, zones can extend across hundreds of acres.
Zone 3 may encompass entire crop fields and grazing paddocks, while
Zone 4 covers managed woodlots spanning many hectares.
The challenge on large sites is ensuring that the intensive inner
zones are not neglected while attention is spread too thin across
the broader landscape.

Mollison's golden rule applies with even greater force on large
properties: develop the nearest area first and expand outward only
when the inner zones are functioning well.
Many novice designers make the mistake of planting orchards and
pastures at the property boundary before establishing a productive
Zone 1 garden.

On large sites, zones may not be concentric at all.
Access roads, water features, and topography can create elongated or
irregular zone shapes.
The designer must map actual patterns of human movement and energy
flow rather than imposing idealized concentric circles.

## Common Mistakes in Zone Planning

One of the most common mistakes is overreach: attempting to manage
too large an area before the inner zones are productive and
well-organized.
Another frequent error is placing elements in the wrong zone based
on aspiration rather than actual use patterns.
A herb garden placed 50 meters from the kitchen will not be visited
daily, no matter how well designed.

Designers sometimes confuse zones with fixed boundaries, treating
them as immutable zones on a map rather than fluid patterns of
human interaction.
In reality, zones shift as land use changes and as the designer's
relationship with the site evolves over time.

Neglecting sector analysis is another common oversight.
Failing to account for cold winds, wildfire risk, or poor drainage
can undermine an otherwise well-planned zone layout.
The most productive zone placement is meaningless if crops are
destroyed by unaddressed sector forces.
