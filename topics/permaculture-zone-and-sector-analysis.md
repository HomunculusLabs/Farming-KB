# Permaculture Zone and Sector Analysis

Zone and sector analysis are the foundational spatial planning tools of
permaculture design, developed by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren in the
1970s.
Together they provide a systematic framework for placing every element of
a design in its optimal position relative to both human energy centers
and the natural forces that flow across a site.

Zones organize elements by the frequency of human use and maintenance
needs, radiating outward from the center of activity.
Sectors map the external energies, such as sun, wind, fire, water, and
wildlife, that flow through a site from outside its boundaries.
When combined, zones and sectors allow designers to create landscapes that
minimize energy input while maximizing yield, resilience, and ecological
health.

These tools are taught in every Permaculture Design Certificate course
and remain the most widely understood and applied concepts in the
permaculture design toolkit.
Despite their apparent simplicity, they contain layers of nuance that
reward careful study and thoughtful application.

## The Origin of Zones and Sectors

Bill Mollison first articulated the zone concept in his 1978 book
Permaculture One, co-authored with David Holmgren, and expanded it
significantly in Permaculture: A Designers' Manual (1988).
The idea grew from the observation that traditional farming villages
naturally organized themselves with the most intensively managed gardens
closest to homes and less managed pastures and forests at the periphery.

Holmgren later extended the framework beyond site design into a
meta-analysis of permaculture itself, describing zones of influence
that range from personal action to global systems.
In this broader reading, zones of power and responsibility start with
the self and extend outward through family, community, bioregion, and
planet.

Mollison presented zone and sector analysis as the culminating design
method in Chapter 3 of the Designers' Manual, describing it as a
primary energy-conserving placement pattern for the whole site.
He identified two fundamental energy categories: energy available on
site (people, animals, wastes, fuels) and energy entering or flowing
through the site (wind, water, sunlight, fire).

## The Zone System

Zones are ranked from 0 to 5, with each number denoting the amount of
human impact and the frequency of visitation.
They are often depicted as concentric circles for teaching purposes, but
in practice they are almost never circular.
Zones can be any shape: oblong, irregular, or even discontinuous
patches connected by pathways.
The key principle is that elements requiring frequent attention belong
close to the center of activity, while self-maintaining elements belong
farther away.

Mollison emphasized that zones are a tool for rough estimation, not
literal boundaries with sharp lines of delineation.
The zone number reflects both how often you need to visit an element
and how often the element needs you to visit it.
The golden rule of zone planning is to develop the nearest area first,
get it under control, and then expand the perimeter outward.

## Zone 0: The Home and Built Structures

Zone 0 is the house or dwelling itself, the origin from which all
permaculture design radiates outward.
It encompasses indoor living spaces, the kitchen, attached greenhouses
or shade structures, and any integrated living components such as roof
gardens, trellised vines, and companion animals kept indoors.

The design of Zone 0 focuses on [[fukuoka-textdoc-energy-efficiency-agricultural-labor]], thermal comfort,
water conservation, and the integration of growing systems into daily
living spaces.
Rainwater catchment tanks, [[comparison-passive-solar-vs-earth-sheltered]] features, indoor sprouting
shelves, vermicompost bins under the kitchen sink, and fermentation
stations all belong within or immediately adjacent to Zone 0.

Mollison described Zone 0 as the energy center of the system, where
human and animal energy is most concentrated.
Everything needed frequently should be within a few steps of the door.
The house should ideally be positioned halfway up a slope for optimal
drainage, solar exposure, and [[cervantes-wind-protection-outdoor-cannabis]].

Holmgren notes that many people make the mistake of focusing their
design efforts on distant zones while neglecting Zone 0.
Starting at the back door, he argues, reminds designers not to
overreach before the immediate territory is organized and working well.

## Zone 1: The Intensive Garden

Zone 1 is the area immediately surrounding the home, visited one to
five times daily.
This is the most intensively managed zone, containing elements that
require continual observation, frequent harvesting, or regular
maintenance.
