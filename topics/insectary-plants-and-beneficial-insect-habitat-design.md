---# Insectary Plants and Beneficial Insect Habitat Design

## Overview

Insectary plants are flowering species intentionally included in garden and farm designs to attract, feed, and shelter [[query-how-do-fungal-volatile-compounds-affect-plant-growth-and-other-microorganisms]] that aphids respond to, meaning that the presence of some aphids in the landscape can actually help recruit parasitoids to the area. This counterintuitive relationship is one reason why tolerating low pest levels is important for biological control.

Braconid and Ichneumonid wasps are larger parasitoids that attack caterpillars, beetle larvae, and other insect pests. They are often seen as dramatic biological control events, such as when braconid wasp cocoons appear on the backs of tomato hornworms. Adult females feed on nectar from umbelliferous flowers and composites.

Chalcid wasps parasitize whiteflies, scale insects, leaf miners, and other small pests. Many species are extremely small, measuring only one to three millimeters, and require tiny flowers for nectar feeding. Plants like sweet alyssum, thyme, and other small-flowered species are particularly important for sustaining chalcid wasp populations.

## Key Insectary Plant Families

Certain plant families are disproportionately valuable as insectary plants because their flower structures provide accessible nectar and pollen to a wide range of beneficial insect species.

The Apiaceae, or carrot family, is arguably the most important insectary plant family. Their flat, umbrella-shaped flower umbels provide ideal landing platforms for insects of all sizes, and the numerous tiny flowers produce abundant accessible nectar. Key species include dill, fennel, coriander, caraway, Queen Anne's lace, parsley, angelica, lovage, and yarrow. Successive sowings of annual umbellifers extend the bloom period through the entire growing season. Biennial species like parsley and parsnip, when allowed to flower in their second year, provide major insectary value.

The Asteraceae, or composite family, includes daisy-like flowers that attract a wide range of pollinators, ladybugs, and predatory bugs. Their composite flower heads provide many small florets in a single visit, making them highly efficient foraging stations. Top species include cosmos, zinnias, sunflowers, calendula, purple coneflower, black-eyed Susan, goldenrod, asters, coreopsis, Joe-Pye weed, and feverfew. Goldenrod and asters are particularly important as late-season nectar sources when few other plants are blooming.

The Lamiaceae, or mint family, provides long-blooming aromatic flowers that attract bees and other pollinators. Lavender, rosemary, thyme, oregano, bee balm, lemon balm, catnip, sage, and pennyroyal all serve as excellent insectary plants. Many species bloom over extended periods, and selecting several mint-family species ensures continuous nectar availability.

The Fabaceae, or legume family, serves a dual purpose by providing nectar for pollinators while also fixing atmospheric nitrogen. Clover, vetch, lucerne, lupine, cowpeas, and sweet peas attract predatory wasps and support pollinator populations. White clover as a living mulch between crop rows supports continuous populations of ground beetles and other soil-dwelling predators.

Buckwheat deserves special mention as a general-purpose insectary plant. It blooms over a long period, produces abundant nectar, attracts a wide range of beneficial insects, and can be used as a cover crop. Buckwheat is often recommended as the single best insectary plant for farms and gardens that can accommodate only one additional species.

## Designing for Continuous Bloom

Beneficial insects need food throughout the entire growing season. The most common mistake in insectary design is having a spectacular burst of flowers in midsummer followed by floral deserts in early spring and late fall. Effective insectary design plans for sequential bloom from the first warm days of spring through the first frosts.

Early spring bloom is critical for emerging overwintered predators and early-season pollinators. Crocus, snowdrops, winter-blooming heathers, hellebore, and early fruit tree blossoms provide the first nectar sources. These are followed by clover, phacelia, alyssum, candytuft, and early-blooming herbs like chives and thyme.

Summer brings the peak of insect activity and the greatest diversity of flowering insectary plants. Dill, fennel, yarrow, cosmos, zinnias, buckwheat, sunflowers, lavender, bee balm, basil, and coneflowers provide abundant nectar and pollen. This is when parasitic wasp populations peak and their need for nectar is greatest.

Late summer and fall bloom is often neglected but is essential for supporting migrating butterflies, building up overwintering populations of beneficial insects, and ensuring that late-season parasitoids have enough energy to lay eggs before winter. Asters, goldenrod, sedum, Joe-Pye weed, Japanese anemone, witch hazel, and late-blooming herbs like chrysanthemums fill this gap. Goldenrod in particular is one of the most important late-season nectar sources in temperate landscapes.

## Habitat Structure and Shelter Features

Insectary planting is most effective when combined with structural habitat features that provide shelter, overwintering sites, and microclimates for beneficial insects.

Beetle banks are raised earth mounds, typically about forty centimeters high and one and a half to two meters wide, planted with perennial grasses and positioned through crop fields. They provide overwintering habitat for ground beetles, spiders, and other beneficial invertebrates that disperse into crops in spring. Research from the United Kingdom demonstrates that beetle banks can increase ground beetle populations by fifty to one hundred percent within two years of establishment.

Insect hotels and log piles, constructed from bundles of hollow stems, drilled wooden blocks, and decaying logs, provide nesting sites for solitary bees, ladybirds, and predatory beetles. These structures are especially valuable in urban and suburban gardens where natural habitat is limited. Bamboo, elderberry stems, and bramble canes make excellent nesting materials.
