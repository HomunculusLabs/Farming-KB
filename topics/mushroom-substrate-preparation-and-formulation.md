---# Mushroom Substrate Preparation and Formulation

Mushroom substrate is the organic material on which mycelium grows and from
which fruiting bodies draw their nutrition. Selecting, preparing, and treating
the right substrate is arguably the single most important factor in successful
mushroom cultivation. Different species have evolved to decompose different
materials in nature, and matching substrate to species is a foundational skill.

This article covers the full substrate workflow: material selection, formulation
recipes, hydration and moisture management, heat treatment methods, supplementation
strategies, and [[oyster-mushroom-spent-substrate-recycling]]. It synthesizes knowledge from commercial
cultivation manuals, small-scale home growing guides, and sustainable farming
research from around the world.

## Substrate Categories and Material Types

Mushroom substrates fall into three broad categories based on the organic matter
they contain. Lignocellulosic substrates include [[hardwood-sawdust-substrate]], wood chips,
straw, and agricultural residues. These support species that decompose cellulose
and lignin — the primary and secondary decomposers.

Composted substrates consist of manure and straw that have undergone a thermophilic
composting process. Species like [[agaricus-bisporus]] (button mushroom) require this
partially broken-down material because they are secondary decomposers that cannot
efficiently break down raw lignin.

Supplemented substrates are base materials enriched with nitrogen-rich additives
such as bran, gypsum, or seed meal. Supplementation dramatically boosts yields
but requires stricter contamination controls because the added nutrients attract
competitor organisms equally.

## Primary Substrate Materials

Hardwood sawdust is the backbone of commercial shiitake, oyster, lion's mane, and
reishi cultivation. Oak, maple, beech, and alder are excellent choices. Softwoods
like pine and cedar contain resins [[terpenes-and-phenolic-compounds-soil]] inhibitory to most
cultivated species. Sawdust should come from recently cut trees and be stored dry.

Straw is the preferred base for oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus species). Wheat straw
is most common, but oat, rye, and rice straw all work. Straw is inexpensive and
widely available. It must be chopped to 2-5 cm (1-4 inch) lengths for optimal
hydration and packing.

Wood chips serve outdoor log-style cultivation and as a structural component in
mixed substrates. They provide air space and break down slowly, making them
suitable for long-term beds. Chips from hardwood species are preferred.

Alternative substrates include coffee grounds, paper waste, cottonseed hulls,
corn stalks, sugarcane bagasse, banana leaves, and spent brewery grain. These
materials expand substrate options in regions where traditional materials are
scarce or expensive.

## Juncao Technology and Grass-Based Substrates

Juncao (菌草) is a Chinese innovation meaning "fungi grass." It uses herbaceous
plants as substrate instead of wood, reducing pressure on forest resources.
Developed from 1983 to 2003, 37 Juncao grass species were selected for mushroom
cultivation, including napier grass, Chinese silver grass, and giant reed.

The technique resolved a fundamental conflict between increasing fungi production
and protecting forest ecosystems. Juncao substrates are particularly important
for tropical and subtropical regions where wood is scarce but grasses are abundant.

Selected grass species provide comparable carbon-to-nitrogen ratios to hardwood
sawdust. Their cellulose and hemicellulose content supports vigorous mycelial
colonization. This technology has been adopted in over 80 countries, particularly
in Africa and Southeast Asia, as a sustainable cultivation pathway.

## The Stamets Fruiting Formula

Paul Stamets developed the standard [[oyster-mushroom-supplemented-sawdust-blocks]] formula that remains
the benchmark for indoor gourmet and medicinal mushroom production. The base
formula combines 100 parts hardwood sawdust with 10 parts wheat bran by dry
weight, plus gypsum at 1-2% of total dry weight.

The sawdust provides carbon and structural matrix. Bran adds nitrogen, vitamins,
and minerals. Gypsum prevents grain kernels from clumping after sterilization,
provides calcium and sulfur for mushroom metabolism, and helps buffer pH.

This formula typically achieves biological efficiency (ratio of fresh mushroom
weight to dry substrate weight) of 75-150% depending on species and conditions.
For shiitake, the formula produces consistent, high-quality fruiting bodies with
good shelf life and flavor.

## CVG: The Standard Home Cultivation Substrate

For home cultivators growing Psilocybe cubensis and similar species, the CVG
substrate — coconut coir, vermiculite, and gypsum — has become the standard.
It is valued for simplicity, contamination resistance, and consistent results.

Coconut coir is processed from coconut husk fibers, typically sold in compressed
650g bricks. It is naturally resistant to contamination due to tannins and other
[[antimicrobial-compounds-mushroom-mycelium]]. Coir holds 8-9 times its weight in water and has a
near-neutral pH ideal for mushroom growth.

Vermiculite is a heat-expanded mineral that absorbs and retains water while
maintaining aeration. It is inert and sterile, adding structure without nutrition.
Medium or coarse grade is preferred over fine.

The standard recipe for a 66-quart monotub uses one 650g coir brick, 2 quarts
vermiculite, and half a cup of gypsum. This yields roughly 8-9 quarts of hydrated
substrate for a 1:2 spawn-to-substrate ratio.

## Grain Spawn Substrates

Grain spawn serves as the inoculum that introduces vigorous mycelium into bulk
substrates. Rye is the most common grain, though wheat, millet, sorghum, and
oats are also used. Moisture content is critical, with an optimal range of
45-55% (around 50% ideal).

A standard quart jar recipe uses 200 grams rye grain, 200 ml water, and 1 gram
gypsum. Larger gallon jars scale to 800 grams rye with 600 ml water and 4 grams
gypsum. The gypsum prevents clumping and supplies calcium and sulfur.

Grain that is too dry produces slow, wispy mycelial growth. Grain that is too
wet promotes bacterial contamination [[aact-ph-and-anaerobic-conditions]]. After sterilization
at 15 psi for 60-90 minutes, the grain should be loose and individual kernels
easily visible and separable.
