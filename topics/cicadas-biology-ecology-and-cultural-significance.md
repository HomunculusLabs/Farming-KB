# Cicadas — Biology, Ecology, and Cultural Significance

## Overview

Cicadas are large, hemipteran insects in the superfamily **Cicadoidea**, comprising roughly 3,400 described species worldwide. They are among the loudest insects on Earth, with some species producing calls exceeding 100 decibels at close range. Distributed across every continent except Antarctica, cicadas inhabit temperate and tropical ecosystems alike, where they play critical roles as herbivores, prey, and nutrient cyclers. Their life cycles range from annual to extraordinarily long periods of up to 17 years underground — a strategy unmatched among insects.

## Taxonomy and Classification

Cicadas belong to the order **Hemiptera** (true bugs), suborder **Auchenorrhyncha**, and are divided into two major families:

### Family Cicadidae (True Cicadas)
- The larger and more familiar group, containing the vast majority of species.
- Includes both annual and [[jadam-ambient-temperature-principle]].
- Many species have multiple call types: a primary "calling song" to attract mates, a "courtship song" at close range, and a "disturbance call" when captured by predators.
- Some species synchronize their calls, creating deafening choruses that can exceed 100 dB — comparable to a lawnmower or power tool at close range.
- The Australian double drummer (*Thopha saccata*) is considered the loudest cicada, with calls reaching ~120 dB.

## Life Cycle

Cicadas undergo **incomplete metamorphosis** (hemimetabolous development) with three life stages: egg, nymph, and adult.

### Eggs and Hatching

Mated females use their ovipositors to slice slits in the bark or twigs of host plants, typically depositing 400–600 eggs across multiple sites. Egg nests can cause significant damage — flagging, dieback, and breakage of small branches (a phenomenon called "flagging" that is especially visible during periodical cicada emergences). Eggs incubate for 6–10 weeks before hatching.

### Nymphal Stage

Newly hatched nymphs drop to the ground and immediately burrow into the soil, where they spend the vast majority of their lives — from 2 to 17 years, depending on the species. Nymphs are subterranean feeders, using modified forelegs to excavate tunnels and attach themselves to plant roots, from which they feed on xylem sap.

Key aspects of nymphal biology:
- Nymphs pass through five instars (molting stages), growing progressively larger.
- They construct feeding cysts attached to root xylem vessels, remaining largely sedentary for years.
- Nymphal tunnels aerate soil and contribute to soil structure.
- In the final instar, nymphs construct an exit tunnel to the surface, typically emerging at night.
- Just before emergence, final-instar nymphs may wait in sealed burrows for soil temperature to reach approximately 18°C (64°F).

### Eclosion and Adult Stage

Nymphs climb a vertical surface — usually the trunk of a tree — and anchor themselves with their tarsi. The exoskeleton splits along the dorsal midline, and the adult cicada slowly emerges in a process called **eclosion**. The newly emerged adult (called a **teneral** adult) is pale, soft-bodied, and flightless. Over several hours, the wings expand and harden, and the exoskeleton darkens and sclerotizes. The shed nymphal exoskeleton (exuvia) remains attached to the tree.

Adult cicadas live for only 2–6 weeks. Their sole purpose is reproduction:
- Males call to attract females.
- After mating, females begin oviposition.
- Adults do not feed significantly on solid food, though they may drink xylem sap.
- They are poor fliers and are easily captured by predators.

## Annual vs. Periodical Cicadas

### Annual Cicadas (Dog-Day Cicadas)

The so-called "annual" cicadas — including familiar [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] America, famous for their extraordinary synchronized emergences. They are divided into three groups based on life cycle length and sibling species:

**17-Year Broods** (three species):
- *Magicicada septendecim* (Pharaoh cicada) — largest species, orange stripes on abdomen
- *Magicicada cassini* — smaller, all-black abdomen
- *Magicicada septendecula* — intermediate size

**13-Year Broods** (four species):
- *Magicicada tredecim* — counterpart to *M. septendecim*
- *Magicicada neotredecim* — a recently described hybrid species
- *Magicicada tredecassini* — counterpart to *M. cassini*
- *Magicicada tredecula* — counterpart to *M. septendecula*

Each brood is designated by a Roman numeral (I–XVII for 17-year broods, XVIII–XXX for 13-year broods), though not all designations are currently active. The largest 17-year broods can involve billions — possibly trillions — of individuals emerging simultaneously across multiple states.

## Ecological Roles and Significance

### Nutrient Cycling

Cicada emergences are among the most dramatic pulsed nutrient events in temperate ecosystems. When millions of adult cicadas die after reproduction, their decomposing bodies release a massive pulse of nitrogen, phosphorus, and carbon into the soil. Studies have documented:
- Significant increases in soil nitrogen availability following emergences.
- Enhanced growth of understory plants in subsequent growing seasons.
- Elevated microbial activity and [[singh-entomopathogenic-fungi-biocontrol-agents]] (particularly *Massospora cicadina*, a bizarre fungus that infects periodical cicadas, replacing their abdomen with a mass of spores and altering their behavior to continue mating — effectively a "zombie" pathogen).
- **Adults**: Consumed by virtually every insectivorous predator in their range: birds (cicadas can constitute up to 50% of bird diets during emergence years), mammals (raccoons, opossums, squirrels, foxes, skunks), reptiles (snakes, turtles), amphibians, spiders, and predatory insects. The satiation of predators during mass emergences is called **predator satiation** — predators eat their fill but cannot make a significant dent in cicada populations, ensuring reproductive success.

### Plant Interactions

- **Xylem feeding**: Nymphal feeding on root xylem is generally non-lethal to mature trees but can reduce growth rates in saplings.
- **Oviposition damage**: The slits made by females laying eggs can kill the tips of small branches (flagging), which can be significant in orchards and nurseries. Young trees (<4 years) may suffer serious damage or death during large emergences.
- **Pollination**: While not significant pollinators, adult cicadas do visit flowers to feed on nectar and may transfer pollen incidentally.
