# Cicadas — Biology, Ecology, and Cultural Significance

## Overview

Cicadas are large, hemipteran insects in the superfamily **Cicadoidea**, comprising roughly 3,400 described species worldwide. They are among the loudest insects on Earth, with some species producing calls exceeding 100 decibels at close range. Distributed across every continent except Antarctica, cicadas inhabit temperate and tropical ecosystems alike, where they play critical roles as herbivores, prey, and nutrient cyclers. Their life cycles range from annual to extraordinarily long periods of up to 17 years underground — a strategy unmatched among insects.

## Taxonomy and Classification

Cicadas belong to the order **Hemiptera** (true bugs), suborder **Auchenorrhyncha**, and are divided into two major families:

### Family Cicadidae (True Cicadas)
- The larger and more familiar group, containing the vast majority of species.
- Includes both annual and periodical cicadas.
- Distributed globally; most diverse in tropical and subtropical regions.
- Subfamilies include Cicadinae, Cicadettinae, and Tettigadinae.

### Family Tettigarctidae (Hairy Cicadas)
- A relict family with only two extant species, both native to Australia (*Tettigarcta crinita* and *Tettigarcta tomentosa*).
- Considered the most primitive living cicadas.
- Lack the prominent tymbal organs of other cicadas; produce sound by stridulating wing veins against the body.
- Nocturnal and cryptic — rarely observed in the wild.

## Morphology

### External Anatomy

Cicadas are robust insects, typically measuring 2–5 cm in body length, though some tropical species (e.g., *Pomponia imperatoria*) reach 7 cm with a wingspan exceeding 18 cm.

- **Head**: Broad, with prominent compound eyes set wide apart. Three ocelli (simple eyes) form a triangle between the compound eyes. The proboscis is long, slender, and adapted for piercing plant xylem tissue to feed on sap.
- **Thorax**: Robust, bearing two pairs of membranous wings. Wings are typically transparent with prominent venation, though some species have opaque or tinted wings. Legs are stout and adapted for climbing tree trunks.
- **Abdomen**: Large and cylindrical in males, housing the tymbal organs. Females have a modified ovipositor at the tip of the abdomen for inserting eggs into plant tissue.

### Tymbal Organs and Sound Production

The male cicada's song is produced by specialized structures called **tymbals**, located on the first abdominal segment. Each tymbal consists of a ribbed membrane stretched over a resonant cavity. Rapid contraction and relaxation of tymbal muscles (firing at rates up to 400–500 Hz in some species) buckle and unbuckle the membrane, producing a series of clicks that merge into a continuous buzz or whine. The large, largely hollow abdomen acts as a resonating chamber, amplifying the sound.

Key features of cicada acoustics:
- Species-specific songs facilitate mate recognition and reproductive isolation.
- Songs are temperature-dependent; calling frequency increases with ambient temperature.
- Many species have multiple call types: a primary "calling song" to attract mates, a "courtship song" at close range, and a "disturbance call" when captured by predators.
- Some species synchronize their calls, creating deafening choruses that can exceed 100 dB — comparable to a lawnmower or power tool at close range.
- The Australian double drummer (*Thopha saccata*) is considered the loudest cicada, with calls reaching ~120 dB.

## Life Cycle

Cicadas undergo **incomplete metamorphosis** (hemimetabolous development) with three life stages: egg, nymph, and adult.

### Eggs and Hatching

Mated females use their ovipositors to slice slits in the bark or twigs of host plants, typically depositing 400–600 eggs across multiple sites. Egg nests can cause significant damage — flagging, dieback, and breakage of small branches (a phenomenon called "flagging" that is especially visible during periodical cicada emergences). Eggs incubate for 6–10 weeks before hatching.

### Nymphal Stage

Newly hatched nymphs drop to the ground and immediately burrow into the soil, where they spend the vast majority of their lives — from 2 to 17 years, depending on the species. Nymphs are subterranean feeders, using modified forelegs to excavate tunnels and attach themselves to plant roots, from which they feed on xylem sap.

Key aspects of nymphal biology:
- Nymphs pass through five instars (molting stages), growing progressively larger.
- They construct feeding cysts attached to root xylem vessels, remaining largely sedentary for years.
- Nymphal tunnels aerate soil and contribute to soil structure.
- In the final instar, nymphs construct an exit tunnel to the surface, typically emerging at night.
- Just before emergence, final-instar nymphs may wait in sealed burrows for soil temperature to reach approximately 18°C (64°F).

### Eclosion and Adult Stage

Nymphs climb a vertical surface — usually the trunk of a tree — and anchor themselves with their tarsi. The exoskeleton splits along the dorsal midline, and the adult cicada slowly emerges in a process called **eclosion**. The newly emerged adult (called a **teneral** adult) is pale, soft-bodied, and flightless. Over several hours, the wings expand and harden, and the exoskeleton darkens and sclerotizes. The shed nymphal exoskeleton (exuvia) remains attached to the tree.

Adult cicadas live for only 2–6 weeks. Their sole purpose is reproduction:
- Males call to attract females.
- After mating, females begin oviposition.
- Adults do not feed significantly on solid food, though they may drink xylem sap.
- They are poor fliers and are easily captured by predators.

## Annual vs. Periodical Cicadas

### Annual Cicadas (Dog-Day Cicadas)

The so-called "annual" cicadas — including familiar North American genera like *Neotibicen* (formerly *Tibicen*) — have life cycles of 2–5 years. They are called "annual" not because individuals live one year, but because overlapping generations ensure that some adults emerge every summer. Their emergence is typically staggered throughout July and August, hence the common name "dog-day cicadas." Individual species may be synchronized within a year but are not synchronized across years.

### Periodical Cicadas (Magicicada)

The genus *Magicicada* comprises seven species of periodical cicadas endemic to eastern North America, famous for their extraordinary synchronized emergences. They are divided into three groups based on life cycle length and sibling species:

**17-Year Broods** (three species):
- *Magicicada septendecim* (Pharaoh cicada) — largest species, orange stripes on abdomen
- *Magicicada cassini* — smaller, all-black abdomen
- *Magicicada septendecula* — intermediate size

**13-Year Broods** (four species):
- *Magicicada tredecim* — counterpart to *M. septendecim*
- *Magicicada neotredecim* — a recently described hybrid species
- *Magicicada tredecassini* — counterpart to *M. cassini*
- *Magicicada tredecula* — counterpart to *M. septendecula*

Each brood is designated by a Roman numeral (I–XVII for 17-year broods, XVIII–XXX for 13-year broods), though not all designations are currently active. The largest 17-year broods can involve billions — possibly trillions — of individuals emerging simultaneously across multiple states.

## Ecological Roles and Significance

### Nutrient Cycling

Cicada emergences are among the most dramatic pulsed nutrient events in temperate ecosystems. When millions of adult cicadas die after reproduction, their decomposing bodies release a massive pulse of nitrogen, phosphorus, and carbon into the soil. Studies have documented:
- Significant increases in soil nitrogen availability following emergences.
- Enhanced growth of understory plants in subsequent growing seasons.
- Elevated microbial activity and decomposition rates.
- Transfer of deep-soil nutrients (accumulated by long-lived nymphs feeding on deep roots) to the surface ecosystem.

This nutrient pulse is ecologically analogous to marine salmon runs in terrestrial streams.

### Food Web Dynamics

Cicadas are a critical food resource at every life stage:
- **Eggs**: Parasitized by specialist wasps and flies, including dryinid wasps and sarcophagid flies.
- **Nymphs**: Preyed upon by soil-dwelling predators including moles, shrews, ground beetles, and entomopathogenic fungi (particularly *Massospora cicadina*, a bizarre fungus that infects periodical cicadas, replacing their abdomen with a mass of spores and altering their behavior to continue mating — effectively a "zombie" pathogen).
- **Adults**: Consumed by virtually every insectivorous predator in their range: birds (cicadas can constitute up to 50% of bird diets during emergence years), mammals (raccoons, opossums, squirrels, foxes, skunks), reptiles (snakes, turtles), amphibians, spiders, and predatory insects. The satiation of predators during mass emergences is called **predator satiation** — predators eat their fill but cannot make a significant dent in cicada populations, ensuring reproductive success.

### Plant Interactions

- **Xylem feeding**: Nymphal feeding on root xylem is generally non-lethal to mature trees but can reduce growth rates in saplings.
- **Oviposition damage**: The slits made by females laying eggs can kill the tips of small branches (flagging), which can be significant in orchards and nurseries. Young trees (<4 years) may suffer serious damage or death during large emergences.
- **Pollination**: While not significant pollinators, adult cicadas do visit flowers to feed on nectar and may transfer pollen incidentally.

### Symbiotic Relationships

Cicadas host two obligate bacterial symbionts:
- **Candidatus Sulcia muelleri**: Provides essential amino acids not available in xylem sap.
- **Candidatus Hodgkinia cicadicola**: Provides additional nutrients; notable for its extreme genome reduction — some *Hodgkinia* lineages have fragmented into multiple distinct genomes within a single host cell, a unique phenomenon in symbiosis research.

## Biogeography and Distribution

Cicadas are found on every continent except Antarctica. Key patterns:

- **North America**: Approximately 170 species. The eastern deciduous forests host the periodical *Magicicada* broods. The arid Southwest has diverse desert cicadas (*Diceroprocta*, *Cacama*) adapted to extreme heat. *Neotibicen* species are ubiquitous summer insects across the continent.
- **Australia**: Over 200 species, the most diverse cicada fauna per unit area. Includes the giant greengrocer (*Cyclochila australasiae*), the double drummer (*Thopha saccata*), and the relict Tettigarctidae. Many Australian cicadas are associated with specific Eucalyptus species.
- **Southeast Asia**: Extremely diverse; includes the world's largest species (*Pomponia imperatoria*) and many tropical genera with remarkable coloration.
- **Africa**: Over 500 species, many endemic. Includes *Platypleura* species that serve as indicators of seasonal rainfall patterns in savanna ecosystems.
- **Europe and Asia (temperate)**: Lower diversity but include widespread genera like *Cicada orni* (the Mediterranean cicada) and *Lyristes plebejus*.

## Cultural Significance

### Art, Literature, and Music

Cicadas have been potent cultural symbols for millennia:

- **Ancient Greece**: The cicada was a symbol of Athens and represented immortality, rebirth, and artistic achievement. Plato's *Phaedrus* features Socrates comparing the cicadas to muses — observers of the Muses who sing unceasingly and are rewarded by the gods. The Greeks wore golden cicada brooches as ornaments. The word "tettix" (τέττιξ) is deeply embedded in Greek literature from Homer onward.
- **Ancient China**: The cicada (*chán*, 蝉) symbolized rebirth, immortality, and transcendence. Jade cicada amulets were placed in the mouths of the dead during the Han Dynasty to ensure resurrection. The shedding of the exoskeleton was seen as a metaphor for spiritual transformation. Chinese poetry from the Tang Dynasty frequently features cicadas as symbols of purity (living high in trees, drinking only dew) and melancholy (their late-summer song heralding autumn's approach).
- **Japan**: Cicadas (*semi*, 蝉) are iconic symbols of summer. Their song is deeply associated with nostalgia, the impermanence of life (*mono no aware*), and the passage of time. The Japanese language has onomatopoeic words for the songs of different cicada species: *min-min*, *tsuku-tsuku*, *kanakana*.
- **Native American cultures**: Several tribes recognized periodical cicada emergences. The Iroquois and other groups had specific names and mythological associations for cicadas. Some traditions considered them a food source during emergence years.
- **Modern culture**: Cicadas appear in literature (e.g., the Italian novelist Dino Buzzati's works), film (Japanese cinema), music (inspired minimalist composers), and as motifs in visual art and jewelry worldwide.

### As Food

Cicadas are edible and have been consumed by humans for centuries:
- High in protein (~40% dry weight) and low in fat.
- Nymphs and teneral adults (newly emerged, before exoskeleton hardens) are considered most palatable — described as nutty, earthy, or resembling asparagus or shellfish.
- They can be boiled, roasted, deep-fried, or made into flour.
- They are a traditional food in parts of China, Southeast Asia, and West Africa.
- There is growing interest in cicadas as a sustainable protein source in Western countries, particularly during periodical emergence events when billions are available.

### In Science

Cicadas have been model organisms for research in:
- **Bioacoustics**: Understanding sound production, hearing, and communication.
- **Chronobiology**: The molecular mechanisms underlying long developmental periods and synchronized emergence.
- **Symbiosis**: The co-evolution of cicadas with *Sulcia* and *Hodgkinia* endosymbionts.
- **Population ecology**: Predator satiation, pulsed resource dynamics, and life history evolution.
- **Phylogeography**: The biogeographic history of eastern North American forests, reconstructed using *Magicicada* diversification patterns.

## Climate Change and Conservation

Cicadas are sensitive to environmental change:
- **Phenology**: Warmer temperatures advance emergence dates and may disrupt the precise synchronization of periodical broods.
- **Range shifts**: As climate zones shift poleward, cicada distributions are changing. Some periodical brood ranges are contracting from their southern edges.
- **Brood instability**: There is evidence that some 17-year broods are switching to 13-year cycles, possibly in response to warming. Brood XIII and Brood XIX overlap geographically and temporally in some areas, producing hybrid zones.
- **Habitat loss**: Deforestation and urbanization reduce available host trees and fragment brood populations. Periodical cicadas are particularly vulnerable because their long underground development makes recolonization of lost habitat extremely slow.
- **Pesticides**: Broad-spectrum insecticides, including neonicotinoids applied as soil drenches, can kill nymphs feeding on treated roots.

## Key Species of Note

### *Magicicada septendecim* (Pharaoh Cicada)
The largest and most widespread of the 17-year periodical cicadas. Recognizable by its orange abdominal stripes and black body with reddish eyes. Its call is a distinctive, haunting "Pharaoh" call — a long, wavering note often compared to someone calling from a distance.

### *Magicicada cassini*
A smaller 17-year species with an all-black abdomen. Its song is a continuous, rattling buzz, quite different from the melodious call of *M. septendecim*. Forms dense, synchronizing choruses that can be overwhelmingly loud.

### *Neotibicen canicularis* (Dog-Day Cicada / Linne's Cicada)
One of the most familiar North American cicadas. Its rhythmic, buzzing "wee-ooo, wee-ooo, wee-ooo" call is the quintessential sound of late summer in eastern North America. Adults emerge annually (from staggered multi-year broods) from July through September.

### *Cyclochila australasiae* (Greengrocer)
Australia's most famous cicada, named for its bright green coloration. Its loud, continuous ringing call is one of the characteristic sounds of the Australian bush during summer. Color morphs include the yellow "Yellow Monday" and the rare blue "Blue Moon."

### *Thopha saccata* (Double Drummer)
The loudest cicada in the world, endemic to eastern Australia. Males have prominent paired tymbal covers that resemble drums, hence the name. The call is an extraordinarily powerful, rhythmic drumming that can be heard from hundreds of meters away.

### *Pomponia imperatoria*
The world's largest cicada, found in Southeast Asia. With a wingspan exceeding 18 cm and a body length of 5–7 cm, it is an impressive insect. Found in forested areas from India to Borneo and the Philippines.
