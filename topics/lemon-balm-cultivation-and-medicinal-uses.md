## Botanical Profile

Lemon balm is a herbaceous perennial hardy in USDA zones 4 through 9. It
typically reaches one to two feet in height and spreads one to three feet
through a combination of rhizomatous growth and self-seeding.

The plant produces heart-shaped, deeply veined leaves that release a strong
lemon fragrance when crushed. Square stems — characteristic of the Lamiaceae
family — support bushy clumps of foliage. Pale white to pale yellow flowers
appear from June through August in small whorled clusters at the leaf axils.

The entire above-ground portion of the plant is used medicinally and
culinarily. Leaves are at their most fragrant just before the plant flowers,
when essential oil concentrations peak.

## Phytochemistry and Active Compounds

The therapeutic properties of lemon balm derive from a complex mixture of
secondary metabolites. The most significant are rosmarinic acid, flavonoids
(-quercetin, luteolin, apigenin), and a volatile essential oil dominated by
citral (a mixture of geranial and neral), citronellal, and geranyl acetate.

Rosmarinic acid is the primary antioxidant and anti-inflammatory compound,
found at concentrations up to six percent in the dried leaf. This phenolic
acid also contributes to lemon balm's antiviral activity, particularly
against herpes simplex virus.

The essential oil, while present in relatively small quantities (0.02 to
0.3 percent of fresh weight), is responsible for the characteristic lemon
aroma and many of the carminative and antimicrobial properties. Tannins,
triterpenes (ursolic and oleanolic acid), and caffeic acid derivatives
round out the phytochemical profile.

## Medicinal Uses — Nervine and Anxiolytic Effects

Lemon balm is classified as a nervine relaxant, one of the gentlest herbs
for calming the nervous system. Unlike stronger sedatives, it promotes
relaxation without causing drowsiness or cognitive impairment at normal
doses.

Clinical studies support its use for reducing anxiety and improving mood.
A double-blind, placebo-controlled study found that a standardized lemon
balm extract significantly reduced self-reported anxiety and improved calmness
in healthy volunteers. The mechanism involves modulation of GABA
transaminase, which increases available GABA in the brain.

Lemon balm is frequently combined with [[valeriana-officinalis]] (*Valeriana officinalis*)
for enhanced anxiolytic and sedative effects. This combination is one of
the most well-studied herbal pairs in European phytotherapy and is approved
by the German Commission E for nervous insomnia.

The herb is considered safe for long-term use and is appropriate for
children, the elderly, and individuals sensitive to stronger nervines. A
simple tea of dried lemon balm leaves, taken two to three times daily, is
the most common preparation for ongoing anxiety support.

## Cultivation Guide — Site Selection and Soil

Lemon balm is highly adaptable and grows well across a wide range of
conditions. It performs best in partial shade to full sun, with afternoon
shade preferred in hot climates to prevent leaf scorching. In cooler
regions, full sun produces the most fragrant, oil-rich foliage.

The plant tolerates a wide range of soil types but thrives in fertile,
well-drained loam with a pH of 6.0 to 7.5. While reasonably drought-
tolerant once established, consistent moisture produces the lushest growth
and highest essential oil content.

Avoid planting in waterlogged sites, as the crown can rot in persistently
wet conditions. A layer of compost mulch helps regulate soil moisture and
suppress weeds while slowly feeding the shallow root system.

## Safety, Contraindications, and Dosage

Lemon balm is one of the safest medicinal herbs, well tolerated by most
people including children and the elderly. Side effects are rare and
typically limited to mild gastrointestinal upset at very high doses.

The primary contraindication is for individuals taking thyroid hormone
replacement medication (such as levothyroxine). Lemon balm may interfere
with thyroid hormone absorption, and supplementation should be separated
from medication by at least two hours.

Lemon balm may potentiate the effects of barbiturates and other sedative
medications. Individuals taking prescription sedatives should consult a
healthcare practitioner before using concentrated lemon balm preparations.

Standard dosages include: dried herb tea two to four grams per day,
tincture two to four mL three times daily, and standardized extract
300 to 600 mg two to three times daily.
