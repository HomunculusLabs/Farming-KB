# Composting and Soil Biology

Composting is the controlled biological decomposition of organic materials into 
a stable, humus-rich product that serves as a valuable soil amendment. At its 
heart, composting is a microbial process driven by the activity of billions of 
bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and other organisms that break down complex organic 
molecules into simpler compounds. The result is compost, a dark, crumbly, 
earthy-smelling material that improves soil structure, water-holding capacity, 
nutrient availability, and biological activity. Composting is one of the oldest 
and most fundamental practices in agriculture and horticulture, and its 
importance has only grown as modern understanding of soil biology has revealed 
the extraordinary complexity and vitality of living soil ecosystems.

The science of composting is inseparable from the science of soil biology. Soil 
is not simply a mineral substrate that holds plants upright. It is a living 
ecosystem teeming with organisms that cycle nutrients, decompose organic 
matter, fix nitrogen, suppress pathogens, and maintain soil structure. The soil 
food web, a concept developed and popularized by Dr. Elaine Ingham, describes 
the complex network of feeding relationships among soil organisms, from 
bacteria and fungi at the base through protozoa, nematodes, microarthropods, 
and larger organisms at higher trophic levels. Understanding the soil food web 
is essential for producing high-quality compost and for using that compost 
effectively to build healthy, productive soils.

Composting offers a practical way to recycle organic waste streams, reduce 
landfill burden, and create a valuable product from materials that would 
otherwise be discarded. From kitchen scraps and yard waste on a household scale 
to municipal green waste and agricultural residues on an industrial scale, 
composting transforms "waste" into a resource. The quality of the finished 
compost depends on the materials used, the management of the composting 
process, and the resulting biological community. High-quality compost is not 
merely a source of nutrients; it is an inoculum of beneficial organisms that 
can jumpstart soil biological activity and support plant health in ways that 
synthetic fertilizers cannot replicate.

## Aerobic vs Anaerobic Composting

Composting processes can be broadly divided into aerobic and anaerobic 
categories based on the type of microbial metabolism that predominates. Aerobic 
composting occurs in the presence of oxygen and is carried out primarily by 
aerobic bacteria and fungi. It is the faster of the two processes, typically 
producing finished compost in weeks to months, and it generates significant 
heat as a byproduct of microbial metabolism. Aerobic composting is the most 
common approach for home gardeners, commercial composting operations, and 
municipal green waste processing. Its main advantage is speed and the 
production of a well-stabilized, pathogen-free product. The primary management 
challenge is maintaining adequate oxygen levels, which requires regular turning 
or aeration of the compost pile.

Anaerobic composting, by contrast, occurs in the absence of oxygen and is 
carried out by anaerobic bacteria and archaea. This process is much slower than 
aerobic composting and produces intermediate compounds including organic acids, 
alcohols, methane, and hydrogen sulfide, which give anaerobic compost its 
characteristic foul odor. While anaerobic composting is generally less 
desirable for producing soil amendments, it has important applications 
including biogas production and bokashi fermentation. The end products of 
anaerobic digestion are different from those of aerobic composting and may 
require additional processing, such as aerobic curing, before being applied to 
soil.

## Hot vs Cold Composting

Hot composting, also called thermophilic composting, is an aerobic process that 
relies on the heat generated by microbial metabolism to raise the temperature 
of the compost pile to between 50 and 65 degrees Celsius. These elevated 
temperatures serve several important functions. They accelerate the rate of 
decomposition, destroy weed seeds and plant pathogens, and select for 
thermophilic organisms that can break down tough materials like cellulose and 
lignin. Hot composting requires careful management of the carbon-to-nitrogen 
ratio, moisture content, and oxygen supply. The classic hot composting method, 
popularized by the University of California Cooperative Extension as the 
Berkeley method, can produce finished compost in as little as 18 to 30 days 
with regular turning.

Cold composting, also called passive or cool composting, involves simply piling 
organic materials and allowing them to decompose slowly at ambient temperatures 
without active management. This approach is less labor-intensive but much 
slower, often taking six months to two years to produce finished compost. Cold 
compost piles do not reach temperatures high enough to kill weed seeds or 
pathogens, so care must be taken with the materials added. The biological 
community in cold compost is different from that in hot compost, with 
mesophilic organisms predominating and a greater proportion of fungi relative 
to bacteria. Cold compost tends to have higher fungal diversity and can be 
particularly beneficial for building fungal-dominated soils in perennial 
plantings, orchards, and forest gardens.

## Carbon-to-Nitrogen Ratios

The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, commonly abbreviated as the C:N ratio, is one of 
the most important factors in composting. Microorganisms use carbon as an 
energy source and nitrogen to build proteins and other cellular structures. The 
ideal C:N ratio for aerobic composting is generally considered to be between 
25:1 and 30:1, meaning 25 to 30 parts carbon for every 1 part nitrogen by 
weight. When the ratio is too high, with excess carbon, decomposition proceeds 
slowly because nitrogen becomes the limiting factor for microbial growth. When 
the ratio is too low, with excess nitrogen, the pile may become anaerobic and 
produce ammonia odors as surplus nitrogen is volatilized.

Understanding the C:N ratios of common composting materials allows the 
composter to blend ingredients to achieve the target range. High-carbon "brown" 
materials include dried leaves, straw, wood chips, sawdust, newspaper, and 
cardboard, with C:N ratios ranging from about 50:1 to over 400:1. High-nitrogen 
"green" materials include fresh grass clippings, kitchen scraps, manure, coffee 
grounds, and leguminous plants, with C:N ratios ranging from about 10:1 to 
30:1. A practical approach for home composters is to aim for roughly equal 
volumes of brown and green materials by volume, which typically produces a C:N 
ratio in the acceptable range. Monitoring the pile's temperature, moisture, and 
odor provides additional feedback for adjusting the mix.

## Microbial Succession in Composting

The composting process is characterized by a predictable succession of 
microbial communities as conditions within the pile change over time. 
Understanding this succession helps composters manage their piles effectively 
and diagnose problems. In the initial phase of composting, mesophilic bacteria 
dominate, rapidly metabolizing readily available sugars and simple 
carbohydrates. As these organisms generate heat, the pile temperature rises 
into the thermophilic range, triggering a shift to thermophilic bacteria, 
including species of Bacillus and Thermus, which can tolerate and even thrive 
at temperatures above 50 degrees Celsius.

During the thermophilic phase, which may last from several days to several 
weeks depending on pile management, decomposition rates are at their highest. 
Tough materials including cellulose and hemicellulose are broken down, and weed 
seeds, pathogenic organisms, and potential human pathogens are destroyed at 
sustained temperatures above 55 degrees Celsius. As the readily degradable 
materials are consumed and the pile begins to cool, the thermophilic organisms 
give way to a secondary wave of mesophilic organisms. At this stage, fungi 
become increasingly important, particularly actinomycetes, which are 
filamentous bacteria that resemble fungi and are responsible for much of the 
breakdown of more recalcitrant materials including lignin.

The final stages of composting, often called curing or maturation, are 
dominated by a diverse community of fungi, protozoa, nematodes, and 
microarthropods. These organisms complete the decomposition process, consuming 
the remaining organic matter and each other in a complex food web. The protozoa 
and nematodes play a particularly important role in nutrient cycling, consuming 
bacteria and fungi and releasing plant-available nitrogen in the process. This 
process, called the microbial loop or the predation pathway, is a key mechanism 
by which nutrients locked up in microbial biomass are made available to plants. 
A well-cured compost has a diverse and balanced community of organisms at 
multiple trophic levels.

## The Soil Food Web

The soil food web is the interconnected community of organisms that live in 
soil and drive its biological functions. At the base of the food web are the 
primary decomposers: bacteria and fungi that break down organic matter and 
convert it into microbial biomass. These organisms are consumed by protozoa, 
nematodes, and microarthropods, which in turn are consumed by larger predators 
including mites, springtails, beetles, centipedes, and spiders. At the top of 
the food web are predatory nematodes, ground beetles, spiders, and vertebrate 
animals. Each trophic level plays a specific role in nutrient cycling, disease 
suppression, and soil structure maintenance.

The relative abundance of bacteria and fungi in the soil food web has important 
implications for plant communities. Bacterial-dominated soils are 
characteristic of disturbed environments, annual grasslands, and row crop 
agriculture. They support rapid nutrient cycling and are well-suited to annual 
plants that grow quickly and have shallow root systems. Fungal-dominated soils 
are characteristic of mature ecosystems, forests, and perennial plantings. They 
support slower nutrient cycling, greater soil aggregation, and deeper nutrient 
distribution, which benefits perennial plants including trees and shrubs. The 
composter can influence the bacterial-to-fungal ratio of the finished compost 
by adjusting the materials and management practices used, creating a product 
tailored to specific plant communities.

## Mineralization vs Immobilization

Two key processes govern the availability of nutrients in compost and soil: 
mineralization and immobilization. Mineralization is the process by which 
organic nitrogen and other nutrients are converted from organic forms into 
inorganic, plant-available forms such as ammonium and nitrate. This conversion 
occurs primarily through microbial metabolism and the predation pathway, in 
which protozoa and nematodes consume bacteria and fungi and excrete excess 
nitrogen as ammonium. In a well-functioning soil food web, mineralization is a 
continuous process that provides a steady supply of nutrients to plants.

Immobilization is the reverse process, in which microorganisms incorporate 
inorganic nutrients into their biomass, temporarily making those nutrients 
unavailable to plants. Immobilization occurs when microorganisms have access to 
abundant carbon but limited nitrogen, causing them to scavenge nitrogen from 
the soil solution to support their growth. This is why incorporating 
high-carbon materials like sawdust or wood chips directly into soil can 
temporarily cause nitrogen deficiency in plants. In composting, the balance 
between mineralization and immobilization shifts over time, with immobilization 
predominating in the early stages as microbial populations grow rapidly, and 
mineralization increasing as the compost matures and microbial growth slows.

## Compost Tea: AACT vs Compost Extract

Compost tea is a liquid extract made from compost that is used as a soil drench 
or foliar spray to inoculate plants and soil with beneficial microorganisms. 
There are two main types of compost tea: actively aerated compost tea (AACT) 
and compost extract, sometimes called non-aerated compost tea. AACT is brewed 
by steeping high-quality compost in water with added food sources, such as 
molasses or fish hydrolysate, while vigorously aerating the mixture with an air 
pump or bubbler for 12 to 36 hours. The aeration and food sources promote rapid 
microbial growth, producing a tea with very high populations of bacteria, 
fungi, protozoa, and nematodes. AACT was developed and refined by Dr. Elaine 
Ingham and is widely used in organic agriculture, horticulture, and turf 
management.

Compost extract, by contrast, is made by simply soaking compost in water, often 
with mechanical agitation, without aeration or added food sources. The extract 
contains the organisms that were present in the compost but does not 
significantly increase their populations during the brewing process. Compost 
extract is easier and faster to make than AACT but has lower microbial 
diversity and population density. Both types of compost tea can be effective, 
but they serve somewhat different purposes. AACT is primarily used as a 
biological inoculant to introduce large populations of diverse microorganisms 
to soil or plant surfaces. Compost extract is more commonly used as a source of 
soluble nutrients and humic substances.

The quality of compost tea depends critically on the quality of the starting 
compost. Compost used for making AACT should be mature, biologically diverse, 
and free of pathogens and anaerobic conditions. Testing compost for biological 
activity, using methods such as direct microscopy or plate counts, is 
recommended to ensure that the compost will produce a high-quality tea. The 
water used for brewing should be free of chlorine and other antimicrobial 
chemicals that could harm the microbial community. Proper brewing equipment, 
including adequate aeration capacity and food-grade containers, is also 
important for consistent results.

## Vermicomposting

Vermicomposting is the process of using earthworms, primarily species of the 
genus Eisenia (commonly called red wigglers or composting worms), to decompose 
organic waste. The worms consume organic material and pass it through their 
digestive system, producing castings (vermicast) that are rich in 
plant-available nutrients, beneficial microorganisms, growth hormones, and 
humic acids. Vermicompost has been shown in numerous studies to improve plant 
growth, suppress soilborne diseases, and enhance soil biological activity, 
often with results comparable to or exceeding those of conventional 
thermophilic compost.

Vermicomposting can be done on a wide range of scales, from small indoor bins 
suitable for apartment dwellers to large commercial operations processing tons 
of organic waste per day. The process is mesophilic, meaning it occurs at 
ambient temperatures rather than the elevated temperatures of hot composting. 
This makes vermicomposting suitable for processing materials that would be 
damaged by high temperatures, such as manure from livestock treated with 
antibiotics or food waste containing active seeds. Vermicomposting systems 
include stacked tray bins, flow-through reactors, windrows, and in-ground 
systems. The worms require a moist, well-aerated environment with temperatures 
between 15 and 25 degrees Celsius and a balanced diet of nitrogen-rich and 
carbon-rich materials.

## Bokashi Fermentation

Bokashi is a composting method based on anaerobic fermentation using effective 
microorganisms (EM), a mixed culture of lactic acid bacteria, yeast, and 
photosynthetic bacteria developed by Japanese agricultural scientist Teruo 
Higa. In the bokashi method, organic waste, including materials that are 
difficult to compost aerobically such as meat, dairy, and cooked food, is 
layered with bokashi bran (a carrier material inoculated with EM cultures) in a 
sealed container. Over a period of two to four weeks, the microorganisms 
ferment the organic matter, producing a pickled product that is acidic, 
sweet-smelling, and partially decomposed.

The fermented bokashi product is not finished compost and must be further 
processed before use. It is typically buried in soil, where aerobic soil 
organisms complete the decomposition process over a period of two to four 
weeks. Alternatively, it can be added to a conventional compost pile or a 
vermicomposting bin. Bokashi is particularly well-suited to urban settings 
where outdoor composting space is limited, as the sealed fermentation system is 
odor-free and compact. The resulting product is rich in beneficial 
microorganisms and organic acids that can improve soil biological activity and 
suppress pathogens.

## Biochar Integration

Biochar is a carbon-rich, porous material produced by the pyrolysis of biomass, 
meaning the heating of organic material in the absence of oxygen. When 
incorporated into soil, biochar provides a stable carbon sink, improves soil 
structure, increases water-holding capacity, and provides habitat for 
beneficial microorganisms. The porous structure of biochar creates a refugium 
for soil organisms, protecting them from predation and desiccation. Biochar 
also has a high cation exchange capacity, meaning it can hold nutrients in the 
root zone and reduce leaching losses.

Biochar can be integrated into composting by adding it directly to the compost 
pile or by using it as a component of the composting matrix. Research has shown 
that "co-composting" biochar with organic materials can improve the quality of 
both the biochar and the finished compost. The biochar absorbs nutrients and 
organic compounds from the compost, reducing nutrient losses through 
volatilization and leaching, while the composting process charges the biochar 
with nutrients and beneficial microorganisms. The resulting biochar-compost 
blend combines the long-term benefits of biochar with the immediate biological 
activity of compost, creating a powerful soil amendment.

## Practical Composting Systems

Numerous composting systems have been developed to suit different scales, 
materials, and management levels. The Berkeley method, also called the fast 
composting or hot composting method, involves building a pile with carefully 
balanced materials, turning it every one to two days, and producing finished 
compost in 18 to 30 days. This method requires significant labor and material 
management but produces high-quality compost quickly. It is well-suited to 
situations where large volumes of material need to be processed rapidly.

Windrow composting is a common method for larger-scale operations. Organic 
materials are arranged in long, narrow piles called windrows that are 
periodically turned with a tractor-mounted turner or front-end loader. Windrows 
allow for passive aeration through convection and can be managed to produce 
finished compost in three to six months. This method is widely used by 
municipal composting facilities, large farms, and commercial compost producers.

Pallet bins are a simple and inexpensive composting system suitable for home 
gardens. They are constructed by wiring or strapping wooden shipping pallets 
together to form a three-sided enclosure. The open front allows easy access for 
adding materials and turning the pile. Multiple bins can be arranged in 
sequence to manage different stages of the composting process. Tumbling 
composters, also called compost tumblers, are enclosed drums mounted on a frame 
that can be rotated to mix and aerate the contents. They are convenient for 
small-scale composting, particularly in urban settings, but have limited 
capacity and may not achieve the high temperatures needed for thorough pathogen 
destruction.

## Mycorrhizal Interactions in Compost and Soil

Mycorrhizal fungi play a crucial role in soil biology and interact with compost 
in several important ways. While mycorrhizal fungi are not typically active in 
the hot composting process, which is too hot for their survival, they colonize 
the root zone of plants growing in compost-amended soils. High-quality compost 
provides a favorable environment for mycorrhizal colonization by improving soil 
structure, increasing organic matter, and supporting a diverse microbial 
community. Compost can also serve as a carrier for mycorrhizal inoculants, 
protecting the fungal propagules and providing a hospitable medium for their 
establishment.

Fungal-dominated composts, produced using higher proportions of woody materials 
and managed at lower temperatures, are particularly beneficial for supporting 
mycorrhizal fungi. These composts have higher fungal biomass and diversity, 
which can help establish a fungal-dominated soil food web that supports the 
growth and function of mycorrhizal networks. In agricultural and horticultural 
systems, applying fungal compost to the root zone at planting time can enhance 
mycorrhizal colonization and improve plant access to soil nutrients and water.
