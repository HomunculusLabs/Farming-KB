## Small-Farm Livestock Management

Small-farm livestock management encompasses the breeding, feeding, housing,
health care, and ecological integration of animals on properties typically
ranging from less than one acre to roughly fifty acres. Unlike industrial
confinement operations, small-scale systems are characterized by diversified
species, reliance on pasture and forage, direct stewardship by the farmer,
and an emphasis on closing nutrient cycles between animals, soil, and crops.
The animals most commonly kept include chickens and other poultry, rabbits,
goats, sheep, and pigs, each offering distinct products and performing
different ecological functions within the farm system.

The philosophical underpinning of small-farm livestock management draws
heavily from permaculture design, as articulated by Bill Mollison and David
Holmgren. Rather than treating animals as isolated production units,
permaculture integrates them into the broader farm ecosystem so that each
species contributes multiple functions: fertility cycling through manure,
pest control through foraging, vegetation management through grazing or
rooting, and soil cultivation through scratching and trampling. This

## Choosing Livestock Species

Selecting the right combination of livestock species is one of the most
consequential decisions a small farmer makes, depending on land area,
climate, infrastructure, experience level, zoning restrictions, and desired
products. Most small farms benefit from starting with one or two species and
expanding as knowledge and infrastructure develop.

Poultry, particularly chickens, are the most accessible entry point. They
require minimal space (three to four square feet per bird indoors, eight to
ten in an outdoor run), have low startup costs, and begin producing eggs at
eighteen to twenty-two weeks. Dual-purpose breeds such as Orpingtons and
Wyandottes provide both eggs and meat, while specialized layers like Leghorns
and Rhode Island Reds maximize egg output. Foraging breeds such as Sussex and
