# Duckweed and Azolla as Livestock Feed and Biofertilizer

## Overview

Duckweed (*Lemna*, *Spirodela*, *Wolffia* spp.) and Azolla (*Azolla caroliniana*, *A. filiculoides*, *A. pinnata*) are among the fastest-growing plants on Earth. Both are tiny floating aquatic plants capable of doubling their biomass in 2–3 days under ideal conditions. Their extraordinary growth rates, combined with impressive protein content and — in Azolla's case — symbiotic nitrogen fixation, make them invaluable resources for smallholder farmers, permaculture designers, and anyone seeking sustainable alternatives to purchased animal feed and synthetic fertilizers.

These organisms represent a bridge between aquaculture, livestock management, and soil fertility. They can be grown in small ponds, rice paddies, unused water bodies, or even constructed containers, and they require minimal inputs beyond water, sunlight, and modest nutrient sources. In integrated farming systems, they close nutrient loops by converting waste nutrients into high-value biomass.

## Duckweed (*Lemnaceae*)

### Biology and Identification

Duckweeds are free-floating aquatic plants in the family Lemnaceae. Despite their simplicity — each plant is essentially a flattened frond (thallus) with one or more roots dangling beneath — they are true flowering plants, not algae. The family comprises five genera:

- **Lemna** — The most common genus, with fronds 1–15 mm long. Species include *L. minor* (common duckweed), *L. gibba* (fat duckweed), and *L. turionifera*.
- **Spirodela** — Giant duckweed, fronds up to 15 mm. Includes *S. polyrhiza* (greater duckweed).
- **Wolffia** — Rootless duckweed, the smallest flowering plants on Earth at 0.5–1.5 mm. Includes *W. globosa*.
- **Wolffiella** and **Landoltia** — Less common genera with limited distribution.

Duckweed reproduces primarily through vegetative budding — new fronds emerge from pouches on the parent frond's margin. Under stress (cold, nutrient depletion, crowding), many species produce dense, starch-rich structures called turions that sink to the bottom and overwinter, resurfacing when conditions improve.

### Nutritional Profile

Duckweed's nutritional composition varies significantly with species, growing conditions, and nutrient availability:

| Component | Range (Dry Weight Basis) |
|-----------|--------------------------|
| Crude Protein | 25–45% |
| Crude Fiber | 5–15% |
| Crude Fat | 3–7% |
| Ash (Minerals) | 10–25% |
| Carbohydrates | 20–40% |
| Starch (when nutrient-limited) | 30–55% of dry weight |

Key amino acids include lysine, methionine, and tryptophan, though methionine can be limiting in some strains. Duckweed also accumulates minerals — particularly calcium, phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium — making it a good mineral supplement for livestock.

When grown in nutrient-rich wastewater, duckweed can accumulate heavy metals and should be tested before feeding to animals intended for human consumption.

### Cultivation Methods

**Site Selection and Pond Construction:**
- Ponds should be shallow (20–60 cm) to allow light penetration and easy harvesting.
- Surface area depends on feed requirements; roughly 1 m² can produce 50–100 g of dry duckweed per day in warm climates.
- Avoid locations with heavy runoff from agricultural chemicals or industrial sources.
- Ponds can be earthen, lined with plastic, or use existing farm ponds.

**Water Quality:**
- pH should be maintained between 6.0 and 7.5.
- Duckweed thrives in nutrient-rich water (wastewater, manure tea, compost leachate).
- Total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) should stay below 10–15 mg/L; higher levels become toxic.
- Dissolved oxygen is less critical for duckweed itself, but important for the pond ecosystem.

**Inoculation and Establishment:**
- Start with a small inoculum (10–20% pond surface coverage).
- Spread evenly across the surface; duckweed will colonize open water within 1–2 weeks.
- Source starter cultures from other growers, university extensions, or wild populations (with species identification).

**Nutrient Management:**
- Fresh manure (chicken, cow, pig) diluted 1:10 with water provides nitrogen and phosphorus.
- Compost tea or vermicompost leachate are excellent organic nutrient sources.
- Biochar added to the pond floor improves nutrient retention and provides substrate for beneficial bacteria.
- Avoid over-fertilization, which promotes algal blooms that outcompete duckweed.

**Temperature:**
- Optimal growth: 20–30°C (68–86°F).
- Growth slows below 15°C and essentially stops below 10°C.
- In temperate climates, outdoor production is limited to the frost-free season; cold-tolerant species like *L. turionifera* survive winter as turions.

**Harvesting:**
- Harvest when the pond surface is 80–100% covered to prevent shading and stagnation.
- Remove 50–70% of the surface biomass, leaving enough for rapid regrowth.
- Use fine nets, scoops, or floating booms to corral and harvest.
- Fresh duckweed is 93–95% water; dry to 8–10% moisture for storage (sun-drying, oven at 60°C, or dehydrator).
- Dried duckweed stores for 6–12 months in airtight containers.

### Duckweed as Livestock Feed

Duckweed has been successfully used as feed for a wide range of animals:

**Poultry:**
- Ducks and chickens readily eat fresh duckweed.
- Can replace 10–40% of commercial feed in broiler diets without performance loss.
- In laying hens, duckweed at 10–15% of diet improves yolk color (from accumulated carotenoids) and egg quality.
- Fresh duckweed can be scattered on water for ducks or mixed into feed rations for chickens.

**Fish:**
- Tilapia, carp, catfish, and koi all consume duckweed eagerly.
- Tilapia can grow on duckweed as a primary protein source when supplemented with grains.
- In integrated duckweed-fish systems, fish waste fertilizes the duckweed, which feeds the fish.

**Swine:**
- Duckweed meal can replace 10–20% of soybean meal in pig rations.
- Higher inclusion rates may cause palatability issues due to ash content.
- Best introduced gradually, mixed with familiar feeds.

**Ruminants (Cattle, Goats, Sheep):**
- Fresh duckweed can be fed directly as a supplement at 1–3 kg per animal per day.
- Dried duckweed meal replaces 10–15% of concentrate in dairy cow diets.
- Improves milk yield and protein content when replacing low-quality roughage.

**Rabbits:**
- Rabbits accept duckweed well, both fresh and dried.
- Can constitute up to 30% of the total diet.
- Particularly valuable during periods when other greens are scarce.

### Duckweed as Biofertilizer

- Fresh duckweed incorporated into soil decomposes rapidly, releasing nitrogen, phosphorus, and trace minerals.
- Duckweed compost (mixed with carbon-rich materials) creates a balanced, nutrient-dense soil amendment.
- Duckweed tea (fresh duckweed steeped in water for 24–48 hours) serves as a liquid foliar feed or soil drench.
- In rice paddies, duckweed suppresses weeds by blocking light, retains soil moisture, and adds nitrogen when incorporated before transplanting.
- Nutrient analysis of duckweed compost typically shows: N 2–4%, P 0.5–1.5%, K 2–4% (dry weight basis).

### Challenges and Limitations

- **Heavy metal accumulation:** Duckweed grown in contaminated water can bioaccumulate lead, cadmium, arsenic, and other toxins. Always test before feeding to food animals.
- **Oxalate content:** Some duckweed species contain moderate oxalate levels, which can interfere with calcium absorption at very high inclusion rates.
- **Drying costs:** Fresh duckweed spoils within 24–48 hours. Drying requires energy input; solar drying is the most sustainable option.
- **Species identification:** Wild duckweed populations may include toxic blue-green algae (*Microcystis*, *Anabaena*) growing among the fronds. Cultivating in controlled ponds reduces this risk.
- **Winter production:** In cold climates, continuous year-round production requires greenhouse or indoor setups with supplemental lighting.

## Azolla (*Azolla spp.*)

### Biology and Identification

Azolla is a genus of aquatic fern in the family Salviniaceae. Unlike duckweed, Azolla is a true fern with roots, stems, and overlapping scale-like leaves. Individual plants are typically 1–5 cm across and form dense green (or reddish under stress) mats on still water surfaces.

The most remarkable feature of Azolla is its **symbiotic relationship with the cyanobacterium *Nostoc azollae*** (formerly *Anabaena azollae*). This nitrogen-fixing bacterium lives within cavities in the Azolla leaf and converts atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) into ammonium that the fern can use. This relationship allows Azolla to thrive in nitrogen-poor waters and makes it one of the most productive nitrogen-fixing organisms known — capable of fixing 1–5 kg of nitrogen per hectare per day.

There are approximately seven species of Azolla:

- **Azolla caroliniana** — Native to North America; widely distributed globally.
- **Azolla filiculoides** — Native to South America; naturalized in Europe, Asia, and Africa. Considered invasive in some regions.
- **Azolla pinnata** — Native to tropical and subtropical Asia and Africa.
- **Azolla mexicana** — Native to Central and North America.
- **Azolla microphylla** — Native to tropical Americas.
- **Azolla nilotica** — Native to Africa.
- **Azolla rubra** — Native to Australia and New Zealand.

### Historical Significance

Azolla has an extraordinary paleobotanical history. During the Eocene epoch (~49 million years ago), a massive bloom of Azolla in the Arctic Ocean — known as the **Azolla Event** — is believed to have sequestered enormous quantities of atmospheric CO₂, contributing to the global cooling that transitioned Earth from a greenhouse to an icehouse climate. This event lasted approximately 800,000 years and deposited meters of Azolla-rich sediment on the Arctic seafloor.

In agriculture, Azolla has been used as a biofertilizer in rice paddies in China and Vietnam for over a thousand years. Its use declined with the advent of synthetic nitrogen fertilizers but has seen renewed interest as farmers seek sustainable alternatives.

### Nutritional Profile

| Component | Range (Dry Weight Basis) |
|-----------|--------------------------|
| Crude Protein | 20–35% |
| Crude Fiber | 10–18% |
| Crude Fat | 3–6% |
| Ash (Minerals) | 12–20% |
| Carbohydrates | 30–45% |
| Nitrogen (from fixation) | 4–6% of dry weight |

Azolla's protein content is slightly lower than duckweed's but still impressive. Its amino acid profile includes good levels of lysine but can be limiting in methionine and cysteine. The plant is rich in iron, zinc, carotenoids, and essential fatty acids including omega-3 and omega-6.

### Cultivation Methods

**Site Selection:**
- Azolla grows best in still or slow-moving water less than 30 cm deep.
- Ponds, rice paddies, irrigation canals, and even shallow containers work well.
- Prefers partial shade in tropical regions; full sun in temperate climates.

**Water Quality:**
- pH range: 5.5–7.5 (optimal 6.0–7.0).
- Unlike duckweed, Azolla does not require nutrient-rich water — it fixes its own nitrogen.
- However, phosphorus is essential for Azolla growth and nitrogen fixation. If phosphorus is limiting, add rock phosphate, bone meal, or wood ash.
- Tolerates moderate salinity (up to 1.5–2.0 dS/m).

**Temperature:**
- Optimal growth: 20–30°C.
- Many tropical species suffer below 15°C; *A. filiculoides* and *A. caroliniana* are more cold-tolerant and survive winters in temperate zones (going dormant or forming resistant spores).
- Above 35°C, growth slows and the fern turns reddish.

**Establishment:**
- Inoculate at 1–2 kg fresh weight per 10 m² of water surface.
- Ensure calm water — wind can push Azolla to pond edges where it dries out.
- Avoid introducing Azolla to natural waterways, especially *A. filiculoides*, which is invasive in many regions.

**Nutrient Management:**
- **Phosphorus** is the primary limiting nutrient. Apply rock phosphate at 20–40 kg/ha or superphosphate at 10–20 kg/ha every 4–6 weeks.
- **Potassium** can be supplemented with wood ash or muriate of potash.
- Trace elements (molybdenum, iron, cobalt) support nitrogen fixation; these are usually present in pond water or added via compost.
- In rice paddies, Azolla benefits from residual fertilizer applied to rice.

**Pest and Disease Management:**
- Azolla is susceptible to a few specific pests:
  - **Azolla weevil (*Stenopelmus rufinasus*)** — a small weevil that can devastate Azolla populations. Biological control with the same weevil is used in some regions.
  - **Caterpillars** — several moth larvae feed on Azolla fronds.
  - **Fungal diseases** — *Rhizoctonia* and *Pythium* can cause rotting, especially in dense mats with poor air circulation.
- Maintaining diverse pond ecology (dragonflies, frogs, fish) helps control pest populations.

**Harvesting:**
- Harvest when the pond is fully covered (every 3–7 days in peak season).
- Remove 60–70% of the biomass, leaving sufficient material for regrowth.
- Fresh Azolla can be fed directly or dried for storage.
- Annual dry matter yield: 8–20 tonnes per hectare in tropical climates.

### Azolla as Livestock Feed

**Poultry:**
- Chickens and ducks readily consume fresh Azolla.
- In broiler diets, Azolla can replace 5–10% of commercial feed with no adverse effects on growth.
- Laying hens fed 5% Azolla show improved egg production and yolk color.
- Higher inclusion rates (>15%) can reduce feed intake due to fiber content.

**Fish:**
- Azolla is widely used in aquaculture as a supplemental feed for carp, tilapia, and grass carp.
- Grass carp can consume Azolla as a primary feed source.
- In integrated rice-fish-Azolla systems, all three components benefit: rice gets nitrogen from Azolla, fish eat Azolla and pests, and Azolla uses fish waste.

**Swine:**
- Can replace 5–15% of conventional protein sources in pig diets.
- Higher rates require balancing with energy-dense feeds.

**Ruminants:**
- Fresh Azolla is an excellent supplement for cattle, goats, and sheep, especially during dry seasons.
- Can be mixed with straw or other roughage to improve overall diet quality.
- Improves milk yield in dairy cattle when fed at 1–2 kg fresh weight per day.

**Rabbits:**
- Azolla is highly palatable to rabbits.
- Can constitute 20–30% of the diet.
- Good source of protein during periods when other greens are limited.

### Azolla as Biofertilizer

Azolla's primary agricultural value historically has been as a **rice biofertilizer**:

- **Rice paddies:** Azolla is grown in flooded rice fields before transplanting or between rice crops. When incorporated into the soil, it decomposes and releases 20–40 kg of nitrogen per hectare per cropping cycle. This can replace 30–50% of synthetic nitrogen fertilizer requirements.
- **Green manure:** Azolla can be grown in dedicated ponds, harvested, and incorporated into garden beds or fields as a nitrogen-rich green manure. One tonne of fresh Azolla provides approximately 2–3 kg of nitrogen.
- **Compost activator:** Adding Azolla to compost piles provides nitrogen to fuel microbial decomposition and produces a phosphorus-rich finished compost.
- **Azolla tea:** Fermented Azolla tea (1 kg fresh Azolla in 10 L water, fermented 7–10 days) is a potent liquid fertilizer for foliar application or soil drench. NPK approximately 0.5-0.3-0.5.
- **Mulch:** Dried Azolla can be used as a surface mulch around garden plants, suppressing weeds and slowly releasing nutrients as it breaks down.

**Nitrogen Fixation Rates:**

| Condition | N Fixed (kg/ha/day) |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Optimal (25°C, P-sufficient) | 3–5 |
| Good (20–28°C) | 1.5–3 |
| Moderate (suboptimal nutrients) | 0.5–1.5 |
| Stress (cold, nutrient-poor) | <0.5 |

### Challenges and Limitations

- **Invasive potential:** *A. filiculoides* is classified as invasive in the UK, parts of Europe, South Africa, and New Zealand. Never release Azolla into natural waterways.
- **Phosphorus dependence:** Despite fixing its own nitrogen, Azolla requires adequate phosphorus. In phosphorus-poor conditions, growth stalls and nitrogen fixation ceases.
- **Cold sensitivity:** Most species are tropical or subtropical; only a few tolerate freezing. Year-round production in temperate zones requires overwintering strategies (greenhouses, indoor tanks, or collecting and storing spores).
- **Rapid decomposition:** Fresh Azolla decomposes quickly (within days), which is excellent for biofertilizer but problematic for storage. Drying or ensiling extends shelf life.
- **Disease susceptibility:** Dense Azolla mats are prone to fungal infections that can cause rapid die-offs. Good pond management (not over-crowding, maintaining clean water) minimizes this risk.

## Integrated Duckweed-Azolla Systems

Growing duckweed and Azolla together in the same pond or in complementary systems creates synergistic benefits:

- **Nutrient partitioning:** Azolla fixes nitrogen while duckweed absorbs excess nutrients. Together, they can clean wastewater more effectively than either alone.
- **Feed diversity:** Feeding both to livestock provides complementary amino acid profiles and a broader mineral spectrum.
- **Seasonal rotation:** In temperate climates, Azolla can be grown in cooler months while duckweed dominates in summer, extending the growing season.
- **Pest resistance:** Mixed-species mats are more resistant to pest outbreaks than monocultures.

A practical integrated system might include:
1. A primary pond growing Azolla on the water surface (fixing nitrogen, providing shade).
2. A secondary duckweed pond receiving nutrient-rich overflow from the Azolla pond.
3. Fish (tilapia or carp) in one or both ponds, feeding on both plants and providing manure.
4. Livestock (poultry, rabbits) fed daily with fresh harvests.
5. Planting beds fertilized with dried or composted surplus biomass.

## Economic Considerations

**Startup Costs:**
- Pond construction (earthen): $50–500 depending on size and whether using existing water bodies.
- Pond liner (plastic or EPDM): $1–5 per m².
- Inoculum: $10–30 for starter cultures (often available free from other growers).
- Nets and harvesting equipment: $20–100.

**Operating Costs:**
- Minimal — primarily labor for harvesting and occasional nutrient supplementation.
- If using manure as fertilizer, operating costs approach zero.

**Value Generated:**
- Duckweed or Azolla replaces commercial feed at $300–600/tonne (protein concentrate).
- Each m² of productive pond surface generates approximately $20–60 worth of feed protein per year in tropical climates.
- Biofertilizer value: 1 tonne of fresh Azolla provides the nitrogen equivalent of 5–10 kg of urea ($3–8).

**Return on Investment:**
- For small-scale livestock operations (poultry, rabbits, fish), duckweed/Azolla systems typically pay for themselves within 2–6 months.
- For larger operations, the primary value is in reducing purchased feed costs by 20–40%.

## Safety and Quality Considerations

1. **Test before feeding:** Send samples to a lab for heavy metal analysis if the water source is questionable.
2. **Introduce gradually:** Start livestock on 5% inclusion and increase over 2–3 weeks to allow gut adaptation.
3. **Balance the diet:** Neither duckweed nor Azolla is a complete feed. They should supplement — not replace — a balanced ration.
4. **Monitor for toxins:** Blue-green algae (*cyanobacteria*) can grow alongside Azolla in warm, nutrient-rich water. Their presence causes earthy odors and potential microcystin contamination. Maintain pond health through harvesting, nutrient management, and occasional water exchanges.
5. **Local regulations:** Some regions restrict the use of wastewater-grown plants as animal feed. Check local food safety regulations.
