# Breadfruit (Artocarpus altilis)

Breadfruit is a fast-growing, long-lived tropical tree in the mulberry family (Moraceae) that produces large, starchy fruits used as a staple carbohydrate across the Pacific Islands, Caribbean, and parts of Africa and Southeast Asia. A single mature tree can yield 200–600 fruits per year, making it one of the highest-producing food trees on Earth.

## Taxonomy and Botany

- **Kingdom:** Plantae
- **Order:** Rosales
- **Family:** Moraceae (mulberry family)
- **Genus:** Artocarpus
- **Species:** A. altilis (Parkinson) Fosberg
- **Synonyms:** A. communis, A. incisus

Breadfruit is closely related to jackfruit (*A. heterophyllus*) and breadnut (*A. camansi*). Wild breadfruit (*A. camansi*) from New Guinea is considered the ancestor of cultivated seedless breadfruit. The genus Artocarpus contains approximately 60 species distributed across Southeast Asia and the Pacific.

### Morphology

Breadfruit is a large, handsome, evergreen tree reaching 15–25 meters (50–80 feet) in height, though in cultivation it is often managed at 10–15 meters for easier harvesting. Key morphological features include:

- **Trunk:** Straight, cylindrical, 0.6–1.2 meters in diameter, with grey to brown bark that may develop shallow fissures with age. The tree occasionally produces buttress roots in wet conditions.
- **Leaves:** Large, glossy, deeply lobed, 30–90 cm long and 20–50 cm wide. Leaves are alternate, bright green above, and lighter green with prominent yellowish veins beneath. Young leaves are often yellow-green and may be reddish-tinged.
- **Flowers:** Monoecious (separate male and female flowers on the same tree). Male inflorescences are club-shaped, 10–30 cm long, yellowish-green, and appear first. Female inflorescences are rounded and develop into the fruit.
- **Fruit:** A large syncarp (compound fruit), 10–30 cm in diameter, weighing 0.5–6 kg. The skin is green, yellowish-green, or yellow at maturity, with a pattern of hexagonal faces marked by a small, brown, leathery plug at each vertex. The flesh is creamy white to pale yellow, fibrous, and starchy with a bread-like texture when cooked. Most cultivated varieties are seedless; seeded types exist and are called breadnut.
- **Roots:** Extensive lateral root system. Breadfruit is typically propagated vegetatively from root shoots or suckers.

## History and Origin

### Wild Ancestry

Breadfruit was first domesticated in the western Pacific, likely in the Bismarck Archipelago and/or the Solomon Islands, approximately 3,500 years ago. The wild progenitor, *Artocarpus camansi* (breadnut), is native to New Guinea and the Philippines and produces seeded fruits. Through centuries of selection, Pacific Islanders developed the seedless, high-yielding varieties that characterize modern breadfruit.

### Oceanic Dispersal

The spread of breadfruit across the Pacific is one of the great stories of plant domestication. Austronesian voyagers carried breadfruit saplings aboard their outrigger canoes as they settled islands across Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia. By the time European explorers arrived, breadfruit was a cornerstone crop from Fiji to Tahiti to Hawaii.

### The Bounty and European Contact

Breadfruit entered European consciousness through Captain James Cook's first voyage (1768–1771), when Sir Joseph Banks documented its importance in Tahiti. Impressed by its productivity, the British Royal Navy dispatched the HMS *Bounty* in 1787 under Lieutenant William Bligh to transport breadfruit seedlings from Tahiti to the Caribbean as a cheap food source for enslaved Africans on sugar plantations. The infamous mutiny intervened, but Bligh subsequently completed the voyage aboard HMS *Providence* in 1791–1793, successfully delivering 678 breadfruit plants to St. Vincent and Jamaica.

### Modern Spread

Today, breadfruit is grown throughout the tropics:
- **Pacific Islands:** Staple food in Fiji, Samoa, Tonga, Tahiti, Hawaii, and elsewhere
- **Caribbean:** Widely established in Jamaica, Trinidad, St. Vincent, Puerto Rico, and Haiti
- **Southeast Asia:** Indonesia, Philippines, Malaysia, Sri Lanka
- **Africa:** Ghana, Nigeria, Madagascar, Mauritius, Seychelles
- **Central and South America:** Limited cultivation in Costa Rica, Panama, and Brazil

## Propagation Methods

Breadfruit is almost always propagated vegetatively to preserve the seedless character of cultivated varieties. Several methods are used:

### Root Shoots and Suckers

The most traditional and widely practiced method. Breadfruit naturally produces root suckers that can be separated from the parent tree and transplanted:

1. Identify healthy suckers 30–60 cm tall arising from roots near the base of the tree
2. Sever the connecting root with a sharp spade, retaining as much of the sucker's root system as possible
3. Trim the top to reduce transpiration
4. Plant in a nursery pot or directly in the field
5. Maintain high humidity and partial shade until established (4–8 weeks)

This method produces true-to-type plants and is the preferred approach for small-scale growers. However, each parent tree produces only a limited number of suckers per year.

### Root Cuttings

Sections of roots 1.5–3 cm in diameter and 15–30 cm long can be induced to sprout:

1. Collect roots from healthy parent trees during the dormant or early growth period
2. Plant horizontally 5–10 cm deep in a well-drained propagation medium (sand, coir, or a mix)
3. Keep moist and warm (25–30°C)
4. Sprouts typically emerge in 4–8 weeks
5. Transplant when shoots are 15–30 cm tall

### Marcotting (Air Layering)

Branches can be air-layered to produce rooted plants while still attached to the parent:

1. Select a healthy branch 1–3 cm in diameter
2. Make an upward cut about one-third through the branch
3. Apply rooting hormone to the wound
4. Wrap with moist sphagnum moss and secure with plastic
5. Roots form in 2–4 months
6. Sever below the rooted section and pot up

### Tissue Culture (Micropropagation)

Modern laboratories can mass-produce breadfruit plantlets through tissue culture:

- Meristem or nodal explants are surface-sterilized and cultured on nutrient media
- Multiplication rates of 5–10× per subculture cycle
- Allows rapid production of disease-free planting material
- The Breadfruit Institute (NTBG) has pioneered this technique, producing thousands of plantlets of select varieties for distribution globally
- Essential for large-scale reforestation and food security projects

### Seeds

Seeded varieties (breadnut, *A. camansi*) can be grown from seed, but seeds lose viability quickly (within 2–4 weeks at room temperature). Seed-grown trees show genetic variation and may produce seeded fruit even when pollinated by seedless varieties.

## Growing Conditions

### Climate

Breadfruit is strictly tropical and subtropical:

- **Temperature:** Optimal range 21–32°C (70–90°F). Growth stops below 15°C (59°F), and temperatures below 5°C (40°F) cause severe damage or death.
- **Rainfall:** Optimal annual rainfall 1,500–2,500 mm (60–100 inches), well-distributed throughout the year. Breadfruit tolerates a 3-month dry season but may shed leaves and stop fruiting during extended drought.
- **USDA Hardiness Zones:** 10b–12. Frost is fatal.
- **Altitude:** Typically grown from sea level to 600 meters, with some varieties growing up to 1,000 meters in equatorial regions.

### Soil

Breadfruit tolerates a wide range of soil types but performs best in:

- Deep, well-drained, fertile loams or sandy loams
- Soil pH 6.0–7.5 (slightly acidic to neutral)
- Good drainage is essential — waterlogged conditions cause root rot
- Tolerates coral-derived, volcanic, limestone, and alluvial soils
- Salinity tolerance is moderate; can grow near coasts with some salt spray

### Light

Full sun is required for optimal growth and fruit production. Young trees benefit from partial shade during the first year but should be gradually exposed to full sun.

### Wind

Breadfruit has relatively brittle wood and large leaves that catch wind. Young trees require protection from strong winds. Mature trees in hurricane-prone areas may suffer significant branch breakage and uprooting. Windbreaks are recommended for orchard plantings.

## Planting and Care

### Site Selection

Choose a site with:
- Full sun exposure
- Well-drained soil
- Protection from strong winds (especially for young trees)
- Adequate space (minimum 10–12 m spacing between trees)

### Planting

1. Dig a hole 2–3× the width and depth of the root ball
2. Mix compost or well-rotted manure into the backfill soil
3. Plant at the same depth as the nursery container — do not bury the graft union or root collar
4. Firm the soil gently and water thoroughly
5. Apply a 10–15 cm layer of organic mulch (keep mulch away from the trunk)
6. Stake young trees to prevent wind damage

### Spacing

- **Orchard planting:** 10–12 m × 10–12 m (80–120 trees/ha)
- **Food forest / agroforestry:** 12–15 m spacing, interplanted with smaller understory crops
- **Home garden:** Single tree with at least 8 m clearance on all sides

### Watering

Young trees need regular watering during establishment (first 2–3 years). Once established, breadfruit is relatively drought-tolerant but produces best with consistent moisture. Apply 25–50 mm of water per week during dry periods. Mulching heavily helps retain soil moisture.

### Fertilization

Breadfruit responds well to organic fertilization:

- **At planting:** Mix 2–5 kg of compost or well-rotted manure into the planting hole
- **Year 1–2:** Apply 0.5 kg of balanced organic fertilizer (e.g., 6-6-6) every 3–4 months
- **Year 3–5:** Increase to 1–2 kg per application, 2–3 times per year
- **Mature trees:** Apply 2–4 kg of balanced fertilizer 2–3 times per year, or use a layer of compost and mulch
- Micronutrients: Breadfruit benefits from magnesium, iron, and zinc applications in deficient soils

### Pruning

Prune breadfruit to:

- Maintain a manageable height for harvesting (8–12 m)
- Remove dead, diseased, or crossing branches
- Open the canopy for light penetration and air circulation
- Shape young trees to a strong central leader or open vase form

Prune during the dry season when the tree is less active. Remove large branches with clean cuts and treat wounds with a tree sealant to prevent fungal infection. Always wear gloves when pruning — the sap is a mild skin irritant.

## Varieties

Hundreds of breadfruit varieties exist across the Pacific and Caribbean, varying in fruit size, shape, skin texture, flesh color, taste, maturity season, and tree habit. The Breadfruit Institute (NTBG) has identified and conserved over 350 varieties. Major variety groups include:

### Pacific Varieties

- **Ma'afala:** Popular Samoan variety; medium-sized fruit with smooth skin; excellent flavor; good for baking and frying
- **Mei Tauk:** Tongan variety; large fruit, rough skin; prolific bearer
- **Puou:** Tahitian variety; small to medium fruit; early-bearing
- **Ulu:** Hawaiian name for breadfruit; several local Hawaiian cultivars exist

### Caribbean Varieties

- **Yellow Heart:** Jamaican favorite; golden flesh, sweet flavor; excellent for roasting
- **White Heart:** Creamy white flesh; versatile for all cooking methods
- **Ma'afala (introduced):** Widely planted in the Caribbean for food security projects

### Seeded Varieties (Breadnut)

- *Artocarpus camansi* produces large, seeded fruits; the seeds are boiled or roasted and are high in protein and fat (similar to chestnuts)
- Grown primarily in Southeast Asia and parts of the Pacific

## Pests and Diseases

Breadfruit is relatively pest-free compared to many fruit trees, but several issues can affect it:

### Insect Pests

- **Breadfruit fruit fly (*Bactrocera umbrosa*):** The most significant pest; larvae tunnel into the fruit, causing rotting and premature drop. Management: bagging young fruits, sanitation (removing fallen fruit), pheromone traps, and biological control with parasitic wasps.
- **Soft scale insects:** Sap-sucking insects that produce honeydew and promote sooty mold growth. Control with neem oil or horticultural oil sprays.
- **Mealybugs:** Attack young shoots and fruit. Manage with insecticidal soap or biological controls.
- **Rhino beetle and coconut beetle:** May bore into trunk and branches of stressed trees.

### Diseases

- **Phytophthora root rot:** Caused by *Phytophthora* spp. in waterlogged soils. Prevention through good drainage and avoiding overwatering.
- **Pink disease (*Corticium salmonicolor*):** Fungal canker that attacks branches. Prune affected branches and apply copper-based fungicides.
- **Anthracnose (*Colletotrichum gloeosporioides*):** Causes dark spots on fruit. Manage with copper sprays and good sanitation.
- **Powdery mildew:** Affects young leaves in humid conditions. Improve air circulation; apply sulfur-based fungicides if needed.

## Harvesting and Yield

### Time to First Harvest

Grafted or vegetatively propagated trees typically begin bearing fruit 3–5 years after planting. Seed-grown trees may take 6–10 years. Tissue-cultured plants can bear in as little as 2–3 years under optimal conditions.

### Fruiting Season

In equatorial regions, breadfruit can produce year-round with peak seasons. In areas with a dry season, the main crop typically appears 2–4 months after the onset of rains. Some varieties bear two crops per year.

### Harvesting

- Fruits are harvested when mature but still firm — skin color changes from bright green to yellowish-green, and small drops of latex appear on the surface
- Cut fruits from the tree with a sharp knife or pruning pole, leaving a short stem
- Wear gloves — the latex sap stains clothing and skin
- Handle carefully to avoid bruising; breadfruit does not ripen significantly after harvest

### Yield

- A single mature tree produces 150–600 fruits per year
- Average fruit weight: 1–3 kg (some varieties up to 6 kg)
- Annual yield per tree: 150–700 kg
- Per hectare yield: 10–30 tonnes/year depending on spacing, variety, and management
- Trees can remain productive for 50–100+ years

## Culinary Uses

Breadfruit is one of the most versatile staple foods in the tropics. It is always cooked before eating — raw breadfruit is indigestible.

### Cooking Methods

- **Roasting:** The traditional Pacific method. Whole fruits are roasted over hot coals or in an earth oven (*umu*). The skin chars and peels away, revealing soft, fragrant flesh with a bread-like aroma and texture.
- **Baking:** Sliced or halved breadfruit baked in an oven until tender. Brush with coconut oil for enhanced flavor.
- **Boiling:** Peeled and cubed breadfruit boiled in salted water for 20–30 minutes. Used as a potato substitute in stews and curries.
- **Frying:** Thinly sliced breadfruit fried in oil until golden and crispy. A popular snack and side dish throughout the Caribbean and Pacific.
- **Steaming:** Retains more nutrients than boiling. Steamed breadfruit can be mashed like potatoes.
- **Grilling:** Thick slices grilled with spices and oil for a smoky flavor.

### Processed Products

- **Breadfruit flour:** Dried, ground breadfruit makes a gluten-free flour suitable for breads, pancakes, pasta, and pastries. Shelf life of 1–2 years when stored dry.
- **Chips:** Thinly sliced, fried, and seasoned — a popular snack comparable to potato chips.
- **Tostones / patacones:** Fried, flattened, and re-fried slices — a Caribbean preparation.
- **Breadfruit porridge/pudding:** Mashed breadfruit with coconut milk, sweetened with sugar or honey.
- **Fermented products:** In some Pacific cultures, breadfruit is fermented into a paste (*masi* in Fiji) or a sour porridge that can be stored for months.
- **Pickles:** Young, immature breadfruit can be pickled in vinegar and spices.

### Nut Pairings

Breadfruit pairs well with coconut milk, garlic, onions, tomatoes, chili peppers, curry spices, fish, and pork. Its mild, starchy flavor makes it an excellent canvas for diverse cuisines.

## Storage and Preservation

Fresh breadfruit is highly perishable. It ripens quickly at room temperature and becomes overripe (soft, brown, fermented-smelling) within 3–5 days of harvest.

### Short-Term Storage

- Store at room temperature for 2–3 days if used promptly
- Refrigerate at 12–15°C for 7–14 days (do not store below 10°C — chilling injury occurs)
- Once cut, wrap tightly and refrigerate; use within 2–3 days

### Long-Term Preservation

- **Drying:** Slice thinly and sun-dry or use a dehydrator. Dried breadfruit can be stored for months and reconstituted by soaking.
- **Freezing:** Peel, cut, and blanch for 3–5 minutes before freezing. Frozen breadfruit keeps for 6–12 months.
- **Flour production:** Dry slices completely, grind to flour, and store in airtight containers. This is the most practical long-term storage method for food security.
- **Fermentation:** Traditional fermented breadfruit products can be stored for weeks to months.

## Permaculture Integration

Breadfruit is an exceptional [[permaculture-zone-and-sector-analysis|permaculture]] tree that fits naturally into [[food-forest-gardening-and-food-forests|food forest]] designs:

### Zone Placement

- **Zone 2–3:** In permaculture zoning, breadfruit belongs in zones 2 or 3 — it requires regular attention during establishment but becomes largely self-managing once mature
- Position in the food forest canopy layer as a dominant overstory tree

### Food Forest Design

Breadfruit serves as the upper canopy in a multi-layered food forest system:

- **Canopy (15–25 m):** Breadfruit, coconut, mango, breadnut
- **Sub-canopy (5–15 m):** Bananas, papaya, cacao, citrus, jackfruit
- **Shrub layer (1–5 m):** Coffee, cacao (understory), heliconia, turmeric, ginger
- **Herbaceous layer:** Sweet potato, taro, cassava, [[tropical-root-crops-cassava-taro-sweet-potato-and-yam|tropical root crops]], lemongrass
- **Ground cover:** Sweet potato vines, peanut grass, centipede grass
- **Root layer:** Cassava, taro, yam
- **Climbing layer:** Yam, passionfruit, vanilla, pepper vines

### Agroforestry Systems

In [[agroforestry-and-alley-cropping|agroforestry]] systems, breadfruit provides:

- **Shade:** Filtered shade for understory crops like coffee, cacao, and kava
- **Mulch:** Copious leaf fall provides organic matter and nutrient cycling
- **Wind protection:** Tall canopy buffers younger plantings
- **Nitrogen:** Though not a nitrogen fixer, breadfruit's deep roots cycle nutrients from subsoil layers

### Companion Plants

Good companions for breadfruit in a food forest include:

- **Leguminous trees:** Pigeon pea, gliricidia, leucaena (nitrogen fixation)
- **Ground covers:** Sweet potato, peanut, tropical kudzu (weed suppression, soil protection)
- **Herbs:** Lemongrass, comfrey, turmeric, ginger (pest deterrence)
- **Root crops:** Taro, cassava, yam (utilize different soil horizons)
- **Fruit trees:** Banana, papaya, citrus, cacao (complementary canopy layers)

## Cultural Significance

Breadfruit holds deep cultural importance across Oceania and the Caribbean:

- **Polynesian navigation:** Breadfruit saplings were among the most important "canoe plants" carried by Polynesian voyagers across thousands of kilometers of open ocean
- **Hawaiian culture:** *'Ulu* is considered a kinolau (body form) of the war god Kū and is associated with prosperity and fertility
- **Fijian culture:** Breadfruit is central to communal feasts and ceremonies
- **Caribbean identity:** Breadfruit has become a symbol of Caribbean food culture and resilience, featured in national dishes across the region
- **Food security:** Organizations like the Breadfruit Institute and Trees That Feed Foundation promote breadfruit as a solution to hunger and malnutrition in tropical developing nations

## Climate Resilience Potential

Breadfruit is increasingly recognized as a climate-resilient food crop:

- **Drought tolerance:** Once established, trees survive extended dry periods with minimal irrigation
- **Storm resistance:** While branches can break in severe cyclones, trees typically recover and regrow rapidly
- **Low input requirements:** Mature trees require minimal fertilizer, pesticide, or irrigation
- **Long productive life:** Trees bear for decades, providing stable food supply across generations
- **Carbon sequestration:** Large, long-lived trees sequester significant carbon
- **Soil stabilization:** Extensive root systems prevent erosion on slopes and coastal areas
- **Nutritional security:** High carbohydrate, fiber, and micronutrient content per unit of land

## Challenges and Limitations

- **Frost sensitivity:** Limits cultivation to tropical and subtropical climates
- **Short shelf life:** Fresh fruit is highly perishable; processing infrastructure is needed for broader distribution
- **Large tree size:** Requires significant space, limiting urban and small-lot cultivation
- **Slow propagation:** Traditional methods (root suckers) are slow; tissue culture infrastructure is not universally available
- **Limited genetic diversity in some regions:** Caribbean breadfruit descended from a small number of introductions, creating vulnerability to pests and diseases
- **Latex allergens:** Breadfruit sap contains latex compounds that can cause allergic reactions in sensitive individuals
