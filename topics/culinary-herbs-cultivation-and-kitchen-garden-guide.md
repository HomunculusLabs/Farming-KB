# Culinary Herbs Cultivation and Kitchen Garden Guide

Culinary herbs are the aromatic leaves, stems, flowers, and sometimes roots of
plants used to flavor, garnish, and preserve food. They form the backbone of
every major world cuisine, transforming simple ingredients into distinctive
dishes through their [[mycovolatiles-fungal-volatile-compounds]]. Growing herbs at
home is one of the most accessible and rewarding entry points into gardening,
requiring minimal space, yielding rapid results, and providing a tangible
connection between garden and table that few other plant categories can match.

The distinction between culinary herbs and spices is botanical rather than
culinary: herbs derive from the leafy green parts of plants, while spices come
from seeds, bark, roots, flowers, or fruits. Many plants blur this boundary.
Cilantro leaves are an herb while its seeds are the spice coriander. Dill weed
and dill seed come from the same plant at different stages. Fenugreek leaves
are eaten fresh while its seeds are ground into curry powder. This guide
focuses on the leafy herb component, though many entries note their dual use.

Herbs have been cultivated for thousands of years. Ancient Egyptian papyri
document the use of coriander, dill, and fennel. The Romans spread herb
gardening across Europe, establishing the formal herb garden as a garden
typology that persisted through medieval monastery gardens and Renaissance
knot gardens into modern times. Every agricultural tradition on Earth has
developed a local herb palette adapted to climate, cuisine, and culture.

Modern interest in culinary herbs has surged alongside the local food movement,
with home gardeners recognizing that fresh herbs offer intensity of flavor that
dried supermarket equivalents cannot match. A sprig of just-picked basil or
thyme contains volatile aromatic compounds that begin degrading within minutes
of harvest, which is why restaurant kitchens maintain herb gardens even in
urban settings. For home growers, a modest herb garden of ten to fifteen
varieties can supply the majority of a household's annual fresh herb needs
from a surprisingly small footprint.

## Classification by Growth Habit

Understanding how herbs grow is more useful for garden planning than botanical
taxonomy. The three primary growth habit categories are annual, perennial, and
biennial, each demanding different cultivation strategies and expectations
about longevity and harvest windows.

Annual herbs complete their entire life cycle within one growing season. They
germinate, flower, set seed, and die, typically within sixty to ninety days.
This group includes basil, cilantro, dill, summer savory, borage, and
chervil. Annuals tend to produce abundant foliage quickly but require repeated
sowing for continuous harvest. Their short lifespan means they invest heavily
in reproduction, and gardeners must manage the flowering impulse through
successive planting and vigilant pinching of flower buds.

Perennial herbs persist for three or more years, often dying back to the ground
in winter and resprouting from their root systems in spring. Rosemary,
thyme, sage, oregano, mint, chives, tarragon, and lavender fall into this
category. Perennials establish more slowly than annuals, often taking a full
season to develop robust root systems, but they reward patience with years of
productive harvest. Their woody growth habit requires periodic pruning to
prevent legginess and maintain vigorous foliage production.

Biennial herbs produce foliage in their first year, overwinter as a rosette,
then flower and set seed in their second year before dying. Parsley is the
most familiar example, along with caraway and angelica. Biennials can be
tricked into behaving as short-lived perennials by removing flower stalks
promptly, though this strategy works for only one additional season at most.
Most gardeners treat biennials as annuals and resow each year for reliable
production.

A practical fourth category is the tender perennial, which lives indefinitely
in frost-free climates but cannot survive freezing temperatures. Rosemary,
lemongrass, pineapple sage, Mexican oregano, and bay laurel belong here. In
cold climates these plants must be overwintered indoors, grown in containers
that can be moved, or treated as annuals and replaced each spring.
