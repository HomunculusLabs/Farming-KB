## Natural Dye Plants and Textile Fiber Crops

The intersection of plant cultivation and textile production represents
one of humanity"s oldest and most intimate relationships with the
natural world. Before synthetic dyes dominated the market in the late
19th century, virtually all color on fabric, leather, and paper came
from plants, minerals, and insects grown or gathered within local
ecosystems. The knowledge of which plants yield which colors, and how
to extract and fix those colors onto fiber, constituted a vast body of
practical wisdom passed down through generations of dyers, weavers,
and farmers.

Today, [[psilocybe-southeast-asia-pacific]], making it one of the most
widespread dye technologies in human history. The Phoenicians
established Tyrian purple from Murex sea snails as a luxury dye that
became synonymous with royalty — the word "purple" itself derives from
the Greek porphyra, meaning the purple dye.

In medieval Europe, the dyer's craft was a closely regulated guild
profession. Woad (Isatis tinctoria) supplied blue, madder (Rubia
tinctorum) supplied red, and weld (Reseda luteola) supplied yellow —
the three primary colors from which a complete palette could be mixed.
The [[mollison-designers-wool-processing-natural-dyeing]] and dyeing workflows that
remained largely unchanged from medieval times through the 18th
century.

The discovery of synthetic mauveine by William Perkin in 1856
transformed the industry. Within decades, synthetic aniline dyes had
largely replaced natural dyes in commercial textile production.
Indigo cultivation in India and the American South collapsed as
cheaper synthetic indigo flooded the market. By the early 20th
century, the traditional knowledge of natural dyeing had been largely
confined to rural communities, indigenous peoples, and a handful of
artisans who maintained the craft.

## Major Natural Dye Plants

### Blue: Indigo and Woad

Indigo (Indigofera tinctoria) is the world's most important natural
blue dye. The pigment indigo is not actually present in the living
plant — it exists as the colorless glycoside indican, which must be
extracted through fermentation and then reduced to its soluble
leuco form before it will dye fiber. The chemistry of indigo dyeing
is unique among natural dyes: it requires an alkaline reduction bath,
and the color develops as the fiber is exposed to air, oxidizing the
leuco-indigo back to insoluble blue indigo. This vat dyeing process
produces extraordinarily wash-fast and light-fast results.

Woad (Isatis tinctoria) was the primary source of blue dye in
medieval Europe. Like indigo, woad contains the precursors indican
and isatan B, which convert to indigo through fermentation. Woad
produces a somewhat lighter, more turquoise blue than tropical
indigo, and the extraction process requires more plant material per
unit of dye. Woad is a hardy biennial that grows well in temperate
climates, making it accessible to growers in Europe, [[comparison-agave-vs-prickly-pear-cultivation]] cactus (Opuntia spp.) in Mexico, Peru,
and the Canary Islands. Other plant-based reds include brazilwood
(Caesalpinia echinata, which produces pinks and reds), safflower
(Carthamus tinctorius, yielding yellow-reds), and henna (Lawsonia
inermis, producing orange-reds on protein fibers).
