# Composting Toilets and Humanure Systems
Composting toilets and humanure systems are forms of ecological sanitation
that collect human excreta without conventional water carriage and process it
through controlled biological decomposition. Their central premise is that
human excreta is not merely a disposal problem but also a concentrated stream
of water, organic matter, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, trace minerals, and
microbial life. When the material is kept out of waterways and managed with
adequate carbon, oxygen, moisture control, heat, and time, it can be converted
into a stable humus-like amendment.

The subject spans household plumbing, public health, compost microbiology,
permaculture design, off-grid homesteading, and regulatory practice. A
commercial composting toilet may look like a manufactured bathroom fixture
with a fan, urine separator, drum, or remote vault. A humanure toilet may be a
simple seat over a removable bucket, followed by outdoor thermophilic
composting in a managed bin. Both belong to a wider family of dry toilets,
urine-diverting toilets, moldering toilets, vermicomposting toilets, vault
systems, and settlement-scale nutrient recovery systems.

Advocates, including Bill Mollison and Joseph Jenkins, have emphasized that
flush toilets often solve a household convenience problem by creating a
watershed pollution problem. A conventional toilet mixes relatively small
quantities of feces and urine with large quantities of clean water, then
requires sewers, septic fields, treatment plants, or leach systems to separate
or dilute what was combined. Composting toilets reverse that design choice by
keeping excreta concentrated, biologically active, and close to a place where
its nutrients can be used safely.

The practice is also contentious because it deals directly with pathogens,
smell, social taboos, and legal authority over sanitation. Poorly managed
humanure can spread disease, attract flies, contaminate wells, or create
nuisance odors. Properly managed systems, however, rely on the same principles
as other safe composting operations: cover fresh deposits, exclude vectors,
maintain aerobic decomposition, prevent leaching, achieve adequate retention
time, and apply the finished material in ways that minimize human exposure.
## Terminology and scope
The phrase composting toilet is often used for any toilet fixture that treats
human excreta by aerobic decomposition rather than by flushing it into a sewer
or septic tank. In a strict sense, the composting toilet is the collection and
primary processing device: the seat, bowl, chute, chamber, fan, agitator,
urine separator, drain, or vault. Some units attempt to complete composting
inside the device, while others only collect material for later composting.

Humanure is a more explicit term for human manure. It is used in the
homesteading and ecological sanitation literature to describe feces and urine
as potential compost feedstocks. The term does not imply that raw excreta is
safe to handle or apply. Rather, it frames the material as a resource that
requires a complete treatment chain before it becomes finished compost.

A humanure system therefore includes more than a toilet. It includes cover
material storage, collection vessels, transfer routines, outdoor compost bins,
pathogen management, curing time, application rules, handwashing, record
keeping, and emergency procedures for illness or system failure. The toilet is
only the visible component of a longer sanitation ecology.

Dry toilet is a broader term. It includes pit latrines, vault privies,
urine-diverting dry toilets, earth closets, bucket toilets, and composting
units. Not all dry toilets compost safely, and not every composting toilet is
waterless. Some systems use foam flushes, micro-flushes, or handbasin water,
while still avoiding the large water volumes of conventional flush toilets.

Ecological sanitation, often abbreviated as ecosan, places composting toilets
within a wider goal of closing nutrient loops. It separates waste streams at
the source, treats them according to their biological risk, and returns water
and nutrients to soil, trees, wetlands, or crops. Under this view, sanitation
is not an isolated disposal service but an element of watershed management and
soil fertility.
## Historical background and design philosophy
The basic idea of returning human excreta to soil is ancient. Agricultural
societies in many regions used night soil, cesspit residues, animal bedding,
and urine as fertilizers. These practices could be productive but were often
unsafe where raw material was handled, where intestinal parasites were common,
or where fecal matter contacted crops eaten raw. Modern composting toilet
design attempts to retain the nutrient logic while adding source separation,
aerobic treatment, temperature management, and long curing periods.

In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, urban sewerage became a
symbol of public health progress. It removed feces from crowded settlements,
reduced odors, and helped control some waterborne diseases when paired with
water treatment. Yet sewers also converted concentrated fertility into dilute
sewage and moved the burden downstream. In cities with combined storm and
sanitary sewers, rainfall events can still discharge untreated sewage into
rivers and coastal waters.

Permaculture authors criticized this pattern as an example of linear design.
Bill Mollison described the flush toilet as a technology that uses clean water
to transport valuable nutrients away from productive land. In his design
manual and related teaching, dry toilets, composting toilets, greywater reuse,
biogas digesters, reed beds, and biological treatment systems appear as
alternatives that match sanitation methods to climate, water availability,
landscape position, and nutrient goals.

Joseph Jenkins popularized a particularly simple household approach through
The Humanure Handbook. The Jenkins method separates the toilet from the
composting site. A sawdust toilet collects deposits in a bucket or container,
and each deposit is immediately covered with carbonaceous material. Full
buckets are emptied into an outdoor compost bin, covered again, and left to
thermophilically decompose and cure. The method is intentionally low-tech: no
water, no electricity, no manufactured chamber, and no dependence on a sewer.
## Nutrient cycling and water conservation
Human excreta contains the same nutrients that agriculture imports as
fertilizer. Urine carries much of the nitrogen and potassium, while feces
contribute phosphorus, organic matter, and microbial biomass. Estimates vary
by diet and body size, but a household's annual excreta can represent a
substantial fertility stream for orchards, woodlots, ornamental plantings, or
soil-building systems.

The water savings are equally important. Toilets commonly account for a large
fraction of indoor household water use. Even efficient flush toilets move
litres of potable or near-potable water with every use. Older fixtures or
poorly maintained systems can waste far more. A waterless toilet eliminates
this demand entirely, while micro-flush and handbasin-reuse systems reduce it
substantially.

By avoiding flush water, composting toilets also simplify the rest of the
homestead water system. Greywater from sinks, showers, and laundry can be
managed separately from blackwater. It may be directed to mulch basins,
branched drains, constructed wetlands, tree crops, or other landscape systems,
subject to local rules and safe soap selection. Separating toilet wastes from
greywater makes each stream easier to treat appropriately.

Nutrient recovery is not simply a matter of saving fertilizer. It reduces the
risk that nitrogen and phosphorus will enter rivers, lakes, and estuaries,
where they can drive algal blooms and eutrophication. Composting toilets are
therefore both an on-site fertility strategy and a pollution prevention
strategy. Their effectiveness depends on whether the system actually prevents
leachate, runoff, and premature application.
## Major system types
Self-contained composting toilets place the collection chamber inside the
bathroom fixture. They are common in cabins, boats, recreational vehicles,
small homes, and retrofit situations where there is no basement or remote
vault. Electric models may include fans, heaters, evaporators, or agitators;
non-electric models rely on passive ventilation, careful cover material use,
and manual emptying. Their main advantages are compactness and installation
simplicity. Their main limitation is small processing volume.

Central or remote composting systems separate the toilet fixture from a larger
chamber below the floor, in a basement, in a crawlspace, or outside the
building. A chute or drain carries deposits into a vault with more residence
time and better capacity. Central systems may use sloped chambers, rotating
drums, rakes, liquid drains, and roof vents. They are more costly and harder
to install than self-contained units, but they are better suited to full-time
households and public facilities.

Batch systems use two or more chambers, drums, bins, or containers. One unit
is active while another rests. When the active chamber fills, it is sealed or
removed from service, and a second chamber is used. The resting chamber then
undergoes decomposition and aging without receiving fresh material. Batch
design is valued because it avoids mixing new deposits with nearly finished
compost and makes retention time easier to verify.

Continuous systems use one large chamber in which fresh material enters one
end or level and older material is removed from another. In theory, the
material travels slowly through a decomposition gradient. In practice, the
designer must prevent short-circuiting, waterlogging, anaerobic pockets, and
removal of immature material. Continuous systems benefit from large volume,
clear drainage, reliable ventilation, and disciplined maintenance.

Urine-diverting dry toilets separate urine from feces at the point of use.
The bowl or seat has a front urine channel and a rear solids opening. Urine is
sent to a container, soakaway, greywater-compatible treatment train, or
dilution system, while feces are covered and composted separately. Diversion
reduces moisture in the fecal chamber, limits odor, decreases volume, and
turns urine into a separately managed nitrogen fertilizer.

Moldering toilets operate at lower temperatures and longer times. Instead of
relying on a large thermophilic phase, they allow fecal material, toilet
paper, and cover materials to decompose slowly in an aerated vault. They are
used in parks, trails, and remote sites where simplicity and low energy demand
are more important than rapid throughput. Conservative retention and
restricted compost use are especially important when thermophilic conditions
are not assured.

Simple humanure bucket systems are the most accessible and controversial
form. A toilet seat is mounted over a five-gallon bucket or similar vessel.
After each use, sawdust or another cover material is added until no fresh
material is visible. The full bucket is carried to an outdoor compost bin,
emptied into the center, rinsed if necessary, and returned with fresh cover
material. The method depends on responsible daily management rather than on a
manufactured appliance.
## The humanure collection process
The collection stage must prevent odor, flies, splashing, and casual contact.
A successful toilet keeps cover material within arm's reach and makes correct
use obvious to residents and guests. The user deposits feces, urine, or both,
then covers the surface completely with dry carbon material. The cover layer
acts as a biofilter and physical barrier. If odor remains after covering,
more cover is needed or the system is too wet.

Sawdust is the classic cover material because fine particles absorb moisture,
exclude flies, and supply carbon. The best sawdust comes from untreated wood,
ideally from sawmills cutting logs rather than from pressure-treated lumber,
painted wood, plywood, or composite boards. Other materials include shredded
leaves, chopped straw, rice hulls, peanut shells, shredded paper, peat moss,
coconut coir, or mixtures chosen for local availability.

The cover material should be stored dry. Wet cover loses absorbency and can
compact into anaerobic mats. Many household systems keep a lidded bin or pail
beside the toilet, with a scoop sized to encourage generous use. In public or
shared systems, signs and caretakers may be needed, because a single uncovered
deposit can cause odor and fly problems that discredit the whole system.
## Composting biology and pathogen reduction
Humanure composting relies on a succession of organisms. Mesophilic bacteria
begin decomposition at moderate temperatures. As microbial metabolism
accelerates, thermophilic bacteria and fungi can raise the core of a large,
well-balanced pile into the range of about 50 to 70 degrees Celsius. Later,
actinomycetes, fungi, earthworms, mites, beetles, and other decomposers help
stabilize the material during cooling and curing.

Pathogen reduction occurs through several mechanisms. Heat kills many
bacteria, viruses, protozoa, and helminth eggs when temperatures and exposure
times are sufficient. Microbial competition suppresses organisms adapted to
the human gut. Predation, desiccation, pH shifts, ammonia, antibiotics
produced by microbes, and simple time all contribute. No single mechanism
should be treated as the only barrier.

Thermophilic composting is the most reassuring pathway because it provides a
measurable sanitation event. Many guidelines cite temperatures around 55
degrees Celsius for several days as a meaningful pathogen reduction target,
with longer times or higher temperatures adding safety. Helminth eggs, such as
Ascaris, are often treated as resistant indicator organisms. The pile must be
large and well built enough for the entire mass to receive adequate heat or
long curing.

Small household piles do not always stay hot, especially in cold climates or
with low volumes. For this reason, conservative humanure systems rely on time
as well as heat. A bin may be filled for a year, then rested for another year,
or a three-bin sequence may provide two years of curing before use. Extended
curing gives mesophilic decomposition and ecological competition time to
finish what the hot phase did not accomplish.

Moisture should resemble a wrung-out sponge: damp enough for microbial life,
not wet enough to exclude oxygen. Excess urine, rain intrusion, poor drainage,
or too little cover material can push a pile anaerobic. Anaerobic conditions
create foul odors and slow pathogen reduction. Excess dryness also slows the
process. Covering the bin from rain while allowing air exchange is therefore a
basic design requirement.
## Compost bin and vault design
A household humanure compost bin must hold material securely while admitting
air and excluding excess water, pets, rodents, and flies. Common designs use
wooden pallets, framed lumber, concrete blocks, wire mesh, or masonry. A
removable front makes harvesting easier. A roof, hinged lid, or tarp prevents
rain saturation. A thick carbon sponge at the bottom absorbs liquids and
creates habitat for soil organisms.

Open-bottom bins connect the pile to soil organisms and allow limited
biological exchange. They should be located where leachate cannot reach wells,
surface water, drainage ditches, or seasonal flooding. Some designers prefer a
concrete slab or contained base to prevent any leaching, especially in dense
settlements, shallow soils, or high-water-table sites. The choice reflects
local hydrology, regulation, climate, and risk tolerance.

A common household arrangement is a two-bin system. The first bin receives
material for a season or a year while the second cures. At the changeover, the
finished or nearly finished bin remains undisturbed, and all fresh additions
move to the other bin. A three-bin system improves safety by adding more
curing time and operational flexibility. Community systems may require larger
bays, carts, mechanical handling, and formal records.

Vault systems built under toilets face additional design constraints. They
must support building loads, prevent insects from entering the living space,
vent odors above the roofline, drain or evaporate excess liquid, and provide
safe access for removing finished material. A poorly designed vault can become
a wet septic pit rather than a composting chamber. Adequate volume and
ventilation are more important than decorative fixtures.

Ventilation is both a comfort feature and a biological feature. Passive vents
use warm air rising through a dark pipe or chimney. Active systems use small
fans to draw air from the toilet room through the chamber and out above the
roof. A negative pressure path keeps odors from entering the bathroom. Screens
on vents and drains prevent flies, while access panels must seal well enough
to block insects.
## Urine diversion and use
Urine is usually far less pathogen-laden than feces when it leaves a healthy
person, and it contains much of the nitrogen in human excreta. Source
separation therefore has strong practical advantages. A household can manage a
smaller, drier fecal compost stream while using urine as a liquid fertilizer,
compost activator, or feedstock for a separate treatment process.

The simplest practice is dilution with water before soil application. Common
homestead guidance uses about one part urine to five or ten parts water,
applied to soil rather than leaves. Stronger applications can burn plants or
contribute to salt buildup. Urine is usually best directed to heavy-feeding
plants, compost piles, orchards, biomass crops, or non-leafy crops rather
than to salad greens near harvest.

Urine diversion requires user cooperation. Many urine-diverting seats work
best when all users sit, or when a separate urinal is provided. The urine line
must slope properly and be flushed or cleaned often enough to prevent mineral
buildup. Struvite and other precipitates can clog small pipes. Maintenance is
therefore part of the design, not an afterthought.
**Odor, insects, and nuisance control.** A well-managed composting toilet
should not smell like sewage. Odor is a signal that fresh material is exposed,
the system is too wet, airflow is wrong, or anaerobic decomposition has begun.
The first response is usually to add more dry cover material. If odor persists,
the operator checks drainage, ventilation, container fullness, urine line
blockages, and whether the compost bin has been left uncovered.

Flies are controlled by physical exclusion and prompt covering. Fresh deposits
must not be visible. Toilet lids, bucket lids, chamber seals, vent screens,
and compost bin covers all reduce access. If flies appear, the affected
container should be removed, emptied, cleaned, and the source of access found.
Insects are not merely unpleasant; they can mechanically transfer pathogens.

Chemical deodorants, disinfectants, and bleach are usually counterproductive.
They can inhibit the microbial community that the system depends on. A
composting toilet is a biological reactor, not a trash can. The remedy for
odor is better biology: carbon, oxygen, moisture balance, and time.
## Use of finished compost
Finished humanure compost should look and smell like mature compost: dark,
crumbly, earthy, and unrecognizable as toilet material. It should not contain
visible feces, toilet paper clumps, or raw-smelling pockets. If there is doubt
about maturity, the safest action is to let it cure longer. Time is cheap;
illness and contamination are not.

Many practitioners reserve finished compost for fruit trees, nut trees,
ornamental beds, windbreaks, timber plantings, and perennial shrubs. This
keeps the amendment away from edible leaves and roots. Application is often
made under mulch at the dripline of trees, where soil organisms incorporate
it gradually and human contact is limited.

Use on annual vegetable beds is more controversial. Some guidance permits it
after verified thermophilic composting and long curing, while other rules or
customs prohibit it. Cautious households avoid raw-eaten leafy greens and root
crops unless the composting process has been exceptionally well managed. The
social acceptability of the crop may matter even where biological risk is low.
## Integration with greywater and other sanitation systems
Composting toilets are often paired with greywater systems because removing
toilet wastes from the plumbing leaves a less hazardous water stream from
baths, showers, sinks, and laundry. Greywater can irrigate mulch basins,
orchards, shelterbelts, or constructed wetlands when soaps, slopes, soils, and
local codes allow. The separation reduces septic loading and can make a small
homestead less dependent on sewer infrastructure.

Greywater should not be confused with humanure leachate. Greywater has its
own risks, especially from kitchen sinks, diapers, harsh cleaners, and stored
anaerobic water, but it is normally managed under different rules than toilet
waste. Composting toilet liquid drains, if any, should be treated as a
blackwater component unless a jurisdiction specifically recognizes another
approved treatment path.

Biogas digesters are an alternative rather than merely a companion. An
anaerobic digester can convert manure, sewage, or food waste into methane for
cooking and a liquid effluent for fertilization. Mollison noted that dry
toilets may reduce the potential energy yield of sewage where digestion is
feasible. The design question is therefore not ideology but fit: water supply,
climate, maintenance capacity, fertilizer need, and legal permission.
## Regulation and public health
Regulation of composting toilets and humanure varies widely by country,
state, county, municipality, and building use. Some jurisdictions allow
approved composting toilets as primary sanitation. Others require a septic
system even if a composting toilet is installed. Some accept only certified
manufactured units, such as systems tested under standards like NSF/ANSI 41,
while DIY humanure systems may have no clear legal pathway.

The regulatory concern is understandable. Sanitation failures can affect not
only the household but neighbors, groundwater, surface water, renters,
visitors, and future occupants. Regulators therefore tend to prefer devices
that can be inspected, certified, and assigned responsibility. A bucket,
compost bin, and informal promise of good management may not satisfy public
health law even when the operator is competent.

Education is part of public health. Users must know what can be deposited,
how much cover to add, how to report odor or clogs, and why handwashing
matters. A composting toilet in a guest cabin, school, park, or community
building requires clearer instructions than a private household system used by
trained residents.
## Advantages, limits, and common failures
The strongest advantages are water conservation, nutrient recovery, reduced
sewer or septic dependence, and suitability for remote or off-grid sites.
Composting toilets can make cabins, tiny houses, boats, field stations,
construction sites, parks, and rural homesteads viable where water supply or
soil absorption is limited. They also make the nutrient cycle visible in a way
that encourages broader ecological thinking.

Cost can be either low or high. A simple sawdust toilet and outdoor bin are
inexpensive. A manufactured central composting system with fans, vaults,
plumbing, and professional installation can cost thousands of dollars. The
apparent cost comparison must include septic installation, water supply,
maintenance, replacement parts, electricity, carbon material, labor, and the
value or risk of the finished compost.

The main limitation is management. Flush toilets hide management in distant
infrastructure. Composting toilets bring it back to the household or site
operator. Someone must buy or gather cover material, empty containers, inspect
fans, clean urine lines, monitor bins, maintain records, and decide when
compost is finished. If nobody owns these tasks, the system will fail.

Climate affects performance. Cold slows decomposition and can freeze urine
lines or chambers. Hot dry climates may desiccate material before it composts.
Humid climates complicate evaporation and insect control. Designs that work
well in a summer cabin may fail in a year-round family home. Local adaptation
is therefore a core principle.
**Design selection and siting.** Choosing a system begins with context. A
remote cabin used on weekends may need a small self-contained unit or bucket
system. A full-time family home may need a central vault, multiple batch
chambers, or a disciplined outdoor humanure system. A public trailhead may need
a moldering vault with long retention and infrequent service. An ecovillage
may justify source separation and professional compost management.

Water availability is a decisive factor. In arid regions, every avoided flush
saves water for drinking, washing, trees, or gardens. In wet regions, the
priority may be preventing leachate and managing excess moisture. Where soils
are shallow, rocky, or saturated, conventional septic may be difficult and dry
sanitation attractive. Where land is scarce, compost curing space may be the
limiting factor.

Carbon supply should be assessed before installation. A sawdust toilet
without sawdust is not a system. The designer should identify year-round
sources of untreated sawdust, leaves, straw, coir, shredded paper, rice hulls,
or other cover. Storage volume matters because the cover must remain dry and
available in winter, storms, or supply interruptions.

The toilet room should be easy to clean and pleasant to use. The composting
area should be accessible by a safe path, protected from flooding, separated
from wells and watercourses, and screened from unnecessary public contact.
Local setbacks may specify distances from wells, property lines, dwellings,
and surface water. In the absence of specific rules, conservative siting is
prudent.
