# Agroforestry and Alley Cropping

Agroforestry is the intentional integration of trees and shrubs with crop and
livestock production systems to create environmental, economic, and social
benefits. Unlike conventional monoculture agriculture, agroforestry mimics
[[fukuoka-natural-ecosystem-food-chain-interconnection]] structures, generating multiple yields from the same unit
of land while simultaneously performing ecological services such as soil
conservation, water regulation, [[mycorrhizal-fungi-soil-carbon-sequestration-by-type]], and habitat creation.
The practice encompasses a broad family of land-use systems ranging from
simple two-component arrangements to complex multi-strata polycultures
resembling natural forest architecture.

The discipline draws on indigenous land management traditions from tropical,
temperate, and arid regions worldwide, and has been formalised through the
work of researchers and designers including J. Russell Smith, Bill Mollison,
David Holmgren, and the World Agroforestry Centre (ICRAF). In permaculture
design, agroforestry is considered one of the primary strategies for
creating permanent, self-maintaining agricultural systems that reduce or
eliminate external inputs over time.

## Major Agroforestry Systems

Agroforestry systems are classified by their structural and functional
characteristics. The principal categories recognised in both scientific
literature and permaculture practice include alley cropping, silvopasture,
forest gardening (food forests), riparian buffer strips, windbreaks and
shelterbelts, and multi-strata canopy systems. Each configuration addresses
specific site conditions and production goals, and many practical
implementations combine elements of several categories within a single
landscape.

Alley cropping, the most intensively studied and widely adopted agroforestry
practice in the tropics, involves planting rows of trees or shrubs at regular
intervals with agricultural crops cultivated in the alleys between them.
Silvopasture integrates trees with managed pasture and livestock, providing
shade, fodder, and timber while improving animal welfare and pasture
productivity. Forest gardening creates perennial polycultures modelled on
woodland ecosystems, producing fruits, nuts, herbs, and medicinal plants
across multiple canopy layers. Windbreaks are linear tree plantings designed
to reduce wind speed and protect crops, livestock, and soils. Multi-strata
canopy systems combine all of these approaches into vertically stratified
food-producing ecosystems.

## Alley Cropping

### Principles and Design

Alley cropping, also known as hedgerow intercropping or alley farming, is an
agroforestry system in which agricultural crops are grown in the alleys
formed between rows of closely spaced trees or shrubs. The hedgerow rows
serve multiple functions simultaneously: they fix atmospheric nitrogen,
produce leaf biomass for surface mulch, generate fodder for livestock,
provide wind protection, and intercept and slow surface water runoff along
contour lines.

The fundamental design principle involves arranging hedgerow rows on the
contour of the land so that they function as living erosion barriers. Water
running downslope is intercepted by the hedgerow rows, which slow its
velocity, promote infiltration, and reduce sheet erosion. This contour
alignment transforms the hedgerows from simple production elements into
integral components of the site's water management system, complementing
swales, contour bunds, and other earthworks.

### Hedgerow Dimensions and Spacing

The physical dimensions of an alley cropping system are determined by the
intended crop, the tree species selected, the climate, and the mechanisation
requirements of the farm. Hedgerow width is typically maintained at 1 to 2
metres, with individual trees spaced 25 to 50 centimetres apart within the
row. This dense within-row spacing ensures a continuous, effective barrier
for [[permplant-willows-salix-erosion-control-fodder]] and maximises [[dighton-fungal-biomass-production-aquatic-ecosystems]] per metre of hedgerow.

The spacing between hedgerow rows (the alley width) is the most critical
design variable, as it determines the balance between tree benefits and crop
competition for light, water, and nutrients. Three broad spacing categories
are recognised:

Close spacing of 3 to 5 metres is used for shade-tolerant crops such as
coffee, cacao, taro, and understorey vegetables. In these systems, the tree
canopy provides significant shade that moderates microclimate extremes and
reduces water stress on the companion crops.

Medium spacing of 6 to 10 metres suits light-demanding field crops such as
maize, beans, cassava, and sweet potatoes. At these distances, the hedgerows
provide substantial mulch and nitrogen contributions while allowing adequate
light penetration to the alley crops during the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]].
