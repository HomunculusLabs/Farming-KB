# Natural Plasters and Earthen Wall Finishes

Natural plasters are wall coatings made from earth, lime, gypsum, or other minimally processed materials, applied to protect and beautify buildings constructed with natural or conventional methods. They represent one of the oldest building traditions in human history, with examples dating back over 10,000 years. Unlike cement-based stucco or synthetic paints, natural plasters are breathable, non-toxic, and often sourced locally from subsoil, sand, and fiber.

## Overview and Historical Context

Plaster has served as the primary protective and decorative finish for earthen, stone, and masonry buildings across every inhabited continent. From the lime plasters of ancient Roman villas to the clay finishes of West African adobe architecture, these materials were refined over millennia to suit local climates and available materials. The industrial revolution and the rise of Portland cement displaced many of these traditions in the 20th century, but a resurgence of interest in [[natural-building-materials-guide]] since the 1990s has revived and modernized plastering techniques.

Natural plasters are valued in contemporary sustainable construction for their low embodied energy, breathability (vapor permeability), aesthetic warmth, and the fact that they can be made from on-site subsoil. They are essential companions to natural wall systems such as cob, [[straw-bale-construction]], light straw clay, [[rammed-earth]], and adobe, all of which require a breathable finish to manage moisture.

## Categories of Natural Plaster

### Clay-Based (Earthen) Plasters

Clay plasters are the simplest and most accessible natural finish. They are composed of clay soil, sand aggregate, and chopped fiber (typically straw), mixed with water to a workable consistency. The clay acts as the binder, the sand provides structure and reduces shrinkage cracking, and the fiber adds tensile strength.

**Composition:**
- **Clay**: 10–30% by volume; sourced from subsoil with adequate clay content
- **Sand**: 50–75% by volume; sharp angular sand is preferred for interlocking strength
- **Fiber**: 5–15% by volume; chopped straw (1–3 cm lengths), hemp hurds, or animal hair
- **Water**: Added to achieve a workable consistency (like thick brownie batter for base coats)

**Advantages:**
- Extremely low cost when clay is sourced on-site
- Fully breathable (high vapor permeability)
- Easy to repair and patch
- Can be tinted with natural pigments
- Zero toxicity during application and throughout lifespan
- Absorbs and releases humidity, moderating indoor climate
- Can be applied by unskilled workers with minimal training

**Limitations:**
- Not water-resistant; unsuitable for exterior exposure in wet climates without additional protection
- Softer than lime or cement plasters; more susceptible to abrasion damage
- Requires protection from direct rain splash
- Shorter lifespan on exterior surfaces in harsh climates
- Can be dusty if not sealed with a clay wash or natural sealant

### Lime Plasters

Lime plasters use hydrated lime (calcium hydroxide) as the binder, combined with sand aggregate. As the plaster cures, the lime undergoes carbonation — reacting with atmospheric carbon dioxide to form calcium carbonate (limestone), creating a hard, durable surface.

**Types of Lime:**
- **Type S hydrated lime**: Most common for plaster; available at building supply stores
- **Type N hydrated lime**: Less commonly used for plaster; slower to set
- **Hydraulic lime (NHL 2, 3.5, 5)**: Sets through hydration rather than carbonation; available in different strengths; NHL 3.5 is a good general-purpose choice
- **Lime putty**: Slaked lime aged for months or years; produces the finest, most workable plaster

**Composition:**
- **Lime**: 1 part by volume
- **Sand**: 2–3 parts by volume (leaner mixes are more flexible, richer mixes are harder)
- **Fiber**: Optional for base coats; horse hair, goat hair, or polypropylene fibers
- **Water**: Added to achieve workable consistency

**Curing Process:**
Lime plaster gains strength slowly through carbonation. A typical lime plaster takes 2–4 weeks to reach initial hardness and continues to harden for months or years. Proper curing requires keeping the plaster damp (mist-spraying) and protected from freezing, direct sun, and strong wind for at least 7–10 days after application.

**Advantages:**
- Highly durable; lime-plastered walls can last centuries
- Moderately water-resistant when fully carbonated
- Naturally antimicrobial (high pH inhibits mold and bacterial growth)
- Self-healing to some degree; hairline cracks fill with free lime that carbonates
- Beautiful, luminous finish quality
- Suitable for both interior and exterior applications
- Compatible with historic buildings and masonry

**Limitations:**
- More expensive than clay plaster
- Requires careful mix ratios and curing conditions
- Caustic during application (protective gear required for skin and eyes)
- Slower curing time than cement
- Less breathable than clay plaster (though still far more breathable than cement)
- Requires protection from freezing during curing (minimum 5°C / 41°F)

### Gypsum Plasters

Gypsum (calcium sulfate dihydrate) plasters set through crystallization when mixed with water, making them fast-setting and smooth. While not technically a "natural" plaster in the ecological sense (gypsum is mined and industrially processed), it is a traditional material with lower embodied energy than cement.

**Applications:**
- Interior finish coats over clay or lime base coats
- Historic restoration of plaster walls
- Smooth final surfaces for painting or wallpapering
- Commonly available as "gauging plaster" or "Keenes cement"

**Note for natural builders:** Gypsum is generally avoided on exterior surfaces and on straw bale walls because it is susceptible to moisture damage and can trap water. It is best reserved for interior use in dry conditions.

### Tadelakt

Tadelakt is a traditional Moroccan lime plaster technique that produces a waterproof, polished surface. It uses a specific lime (lime putty from the Marrakech region, though similar results can be achieved with other fat limes), fine marble sand, and black olive oil soap, which is burnished with a stone to create a seamless, water-resistant finish.

**Process:**
1. Apply 2–3 coats of lime plaster with increasing fineness
2. Allow to cure partially
3. Compress and polish with a hard stone (river stone or agate)
4. Apply olive oil soap with a flat trowel
5. Burnish again while the soap absorbs
6. Repeat the soap-and-burnish cycle 2–3 times

**Applications:**
- Bathrooms and shower surrounds
- Sinks and washbasins
- Exterior walls in arid climates
- Water features and fountains
- Kitchen countertops and backsplashes

Tadelakt is one of the few truly natural waterproof finishes, making it invaluable for wet areas in natural buildings.

### Aliz (Clay Paint/Wash)

Aliz is a thin clay-based paint applied as a final finish over earthen or lime plasters. Made from fine clay, water, and natural pigments (iron oxides, ochres), it provides color and a soft, matte finish while maintaining full breathability.

**Recipe:**
- 1 part fine clay (sifted through window screen)
- 1 part fine sand (optional, for texture control)
- Water to achieve the consistency of thin cream
- Natural pigments as desired
- Small amount of wheat paste or casein as binder (optional, improves durability)

Aliz can be applied with brushes, sponges, or sprayed, and is incredibly easy to refresh when worn. It is the most breathable and least toxic wall finish available.

## Application Techniques

### Wall Preparation

Proper wall preparation is critical for plaster adhesion and longevity:

**For cob and adobe walls:**
1. Allow the wall to dry thoroughly (weeks to months depending on thickness and climate)
2. Roughen the surface with a wire brush, stiff broom, or by carving shallow grooves
3. Dampen the wall thoroughly before applying plaster
4. Apply a "key" coat (thin, wet clay slip brushed onto the wall) immediately before plastering

**For straw bale walls:**
1. Shape and trim bales flush with framing
2. Apply a clay slip (also called "cobbing up") to fill gaps and create a keyed surface
3. Round external corners and shape niches before plastering
4. Keep the wall damp during plaster application

**For conventional substrates:**
1. Remove loose material, paint, or wallpaper
2. Repair cracks and damage
3. Apply a bonding agent (wheat paste, PVA for lime, or a slurry coat) if needed
4. Metal lath may be required on very smooth or non-porous surfaces

### Three-Coat System

Most natural plaster systems use three coats, each serving a distinct purpose:

**1. Scratch coat (base coat):**
- Thickest coat (12–25 mm / ½–1 inch)
- Richest in fiber for strength and crack resistance
- Surface scored or scratched with horizontal lines to provide keys for the next coat
- Applied in two passes ("brown" and "scratch") on thick walls
- Primary purpose: create a flat, even substrate and fill irregularities

**2. Brown coat (intermediate coat):**
- Medium thickness (6–12 mm / ¼–½ inch)
- Less fiber than scratch coat; finer sand
- Further straightens and levels the wall
- Keyed for the finish coat

**3. Finish coat:**
- Thinnest coat (3–6 mm / ⅛–¼ inch)
- No fiber; finest sand or screened clay
- May include fine marble dust for lime finishes
- Troweled smooth, textured, or burnished depending on desired effect
- Receives pigment, aliz, or sealant

### Application Tools

- **Hawk and trowel**: The fundamental plastering tools; stainless steel trowels are standard for lime, wooden trowels preferred by some for clay
- **Japanese trowel (j-trowel)**: Flexible, excellent for finish work and tight spaces
- **Magnesium float**: For floating and leveling base coats
- **Sponge float**: Produces a uniform, slightly textured finish
- **Spray bottle**: Essential for controlling drying rate and re-wetting between coats
- **Screed**: A straight board for leveling and establishing flat planes
- **Darby**: A long flat tool for leveling large areas

### Curing and Drying

**Clay plasters:**
- Dry through evaporation; typically ready for the next coat in 1–7 days depending on thickness, humidity, and temperature
- Should be protected from direct sun and wind during drying
- Mist-spray lightly if drying too fast
- Can be accelerated with fans in humid conditions

**Lime plasters:**
- Cure through carbonation; mist-spray regularly for 7–10 days
- Keep damp but not saturated
- Protect from freezing for at least 30 days
- Full strength develops over 3–6 months
- Do not apply in temperatures below 5°C (41°F)

Fiber is essential in clay plasters and useful in lime base coats:

| Barley straw | 2–4 cm | threshed barley | similar to wheat straw |

## Natural Pigments and Color

Natural plasters can be colored with mineral pigments mixed into the final coat:

- **Yellow ochre**: Iron oxide; warm yellows and golds
- **Red ochre**: Iron oxide; earth reds and terracottas
- **Burnt sienna**: Heat-treated iron oxide; deep warm browns
- **Raw umber**: Iron and manganese oxides; cool browns
- **Green earth (terre verte)**: Iron silicate clay; muted sage greens
- **Ultramarine blue**: Lapis lazuli derivative; deep blues (expensive)
- **Chromium oxide green**: Chromium oxide; strong greens
- **Manganese black**: Manganese dioxide; deep blacks
- **Lime white**: Titanium dioxide or chalk; whites and lightening

Pigments are typically added at 2–10% of the dry binder weight. Test batches are essential, as colors shift as the plaster dries and cures (lime plasters lighten significantly as they carbonate).

## Common Problems and Solutions

### Cracking

**Hairline cracks** in finish coats are normal in clay plaster and can be filled with a thin clay slurry or left as part of the natural aesthetic. In lime plaster, hairline cracks often self-heal through carbonation.

**Structural cracks** (wider than 2 mm) indicate underlying problems:
- Differential settlement of the wall
- Insufficient fiber in the mix
- Drying too fast (protect from sun and wind)
- Too much clay in the mix (add sand)
- Coats applied too thick (maximum 20 mm per pass)
- Poor adhesion to substrate (improve wall prep and dampening)

### Efflorescence

White crystalline deposits on lime plaster surfaces indicate moisture moving through the wall and depositing soluble salts. Solutions:
- Improve drainage around the building's foundation
- Add a damp-proof course if missing
- Ensure proper roof overhangs (minimum 600 mm / 24 inches)
- Brush off efflorescence with a dry brush; do not wash (redissolves salts)

### Plaster Delamination

Plaster pulling away from the wall is usually caused by:
- Wall not dampened sufficiently before application
- Coats applied over dusty or contaminated surfaces
- Excessive thickness in a single coat
- Freeze-thaw damage during curing
- Incompatible substrates (e.g., gypsum over earthen walls)

### Mold and Mildew

Natural plasters resist mold better than most finishes, but can develop growth in persistently damp conditions:
- Improve ventilation in the building
- Address moisture sources (leaks, condensation, rising damp)
- Lime plaster's high pH naturally inhibits mold
- Clay plaster can be treated with borax solution (1% by weight of dry mix) as a preventative

## Plaster for Specific Wall Systems

### Straw Bale Walls
Straw bale walls require a plaster coating for structural protection, fire resistance, and weatherproofing. Earthen plaster is the most common choice because it bonds exceptionally well to the straw. Minimum plaster thickness: 50–75 mm (2–3 inches) total for exterior walls, providing R-value contribution and fire resistance (2-hour fire rating with proper plaster thickness).

### Cob Walls
Cob walls are typically plastered with the same clay-sand-straw mix as the cob itself, but with finer sand and more clay for a smoother finish. Cob walls are extremely compatible with earthen plaster since the materials are essentially identical.

### Light Straw Clay (Slip-Straw)
Light straw clay walls require a plaster with good adhesion to the straw-clay matrix. Clay plaster bonds directly; lime plaster may require a clay-slip bonding coat.

### Rammed Earth and Adobe
These dense earth walls benefit from breathable plaster that allows moisture to escape. Lime plaster is often preferred for exterior applications in wetter climates due to its weather resistance, while clay plaster works well for interiors and arid exteriors.

### Stone and Masonry
Lime plaster is the traditional choice for stone walls, as it is flexible enough to accommodate slight thermal movement and has excellent adhesion to masonry. Portland cement should never be applied to historic stone or earth buildings, as it traps moisture and causes spalling.

### Conventional Drywall and Lath
Natural plasters can be applied over conventional substrates with proper preparation:
- Drywall: prime with a PVA or wheat-paste primer; may need expanded metal lath
- Wood lath: traditional substrate for lime plaster; still viable
- Blue board: gypsum board designed for veneer plaster; accepts thin lime or clay coats

## Sustainability Considerations

Natural plasters offer significant environmental advantages over conventional finishes:

- **Local sourcing**: Clay subsoil is available on most building sites, eliminating transportation
- **Low embodied energy**: Earthen plaster requires no firing or industrial processing
- **Zero VOC**: No volatile organic compounds during manufacturing or application
- **Biodegradable**: At end of life, clay and lime plasters return to the earth without toxicity
- **Carbon sequestration**: Lime plaster absorbs CO₂ during carbonation (approximately 0.75 kg CO₂ per kg of lime)
- **Durability**: Well-applied lime plaster can last 100+ years, reducing maintenance cycles
- **Indoor air quality**: Breathable walls regulate humidity and reduce mold risk

## Safety Considerations

- **Lime is caustic**: Wear gloves, eye protection, and long sleeves when mixing and applying lime plaster. Lime can cause chemical burns on skin and serious eye damage.
- **Silica dust**: Sand contains crystalline silica; wear a respirator (N95 minimum) when mixing dry materials.
- **Working at height**: Plastering often involves scaffolding and overhead work; follow standard fall protection protocols.
- **Heavy lifting**: Bags of sand and lime are heavy (25–50 kg each); use proper lifting technique.
- **Skin drying**: Clay and lime both dry skin; use barrier cream or gloves and moisturize after work.
