## Ergot Fungi, Lysergic Acid, and the History of Ergotism

*From medieval plagues and sacred mysteries to the birth of modern psychopharmacology*

Few organisms have shaped human civilization as profoundly as *Claviceps purpurea*,
a tiny parasitic fungus that infects cereal grasses. The dark sclerotia it produces
have caused devastating epidemics, fueled religious movements, and given rise to
modern psychedelic science through the discovery of LSD.

This article traces the arc from ancient grain infections through medieval
ergotism plagues, the sacred Eleusinian Mysteries of ancient Greece, the Salem
witch trials, and finally to [[albert-hofmann]]'s laboratory at Sandoz, where
[[lysergic-acid-diethylamide]] emerged from ergot chemistry in 1938.

## The Organism — Claviceps Biology and Life Cycle

Ergot fungi belong to the genus *Claviceps* in the family Clavicipitaceae,
a group of ascomycete pathogens that parasitize the inflorescences of grasses
and cereals. The most significant species is *Claviceps purpurea*, which
infects rye, wheat, barley, and numerous wild grasses across temperate regions.

The life cycle begins when airborne ascospores land on the stigmas of open
grass flowers during a brief window of receptivity. The fungus germinates and
invades the developing ovary, replacing the grain kernel with a dark, hardened
mass called a sclerotium — the ergot body. This sclerotium falls to the ground
at harvest and overwinters in the soil.

In spring, the sclerotium germinates to produce stalked stromata that release
new ascospores, initiating infections on the next season's crop. The entire
cycle is tightly synchronized with host plant development, making ergot most
prevalent during years when cool, wet weather coincides with the flowering
period of the host cereal.

The sclerotia contain a complex mixture of potent alkaloids that affect the
nervous system, circulatory system, and uterus. These compounds — ergotamine,
ergocristine, ergometrine, and [[lysergic-acid-amide]] among them — form the
chemical basis for both ergot's terrifying toxicity and its extraordinary
medicinal and psychoactive properties.

## Claviceps Species and Global Distribution

While *C. purpurea* dominates European historical records, several other
*Claviceps* species produce ergot alkaloids across the world's grasslands.

*C. paspali* parasitizes *Paspalum* grasses throughout tropical and
subtropical regions, including the entire Mediterranean basin. Critically,
*C. paspali* contains only hallucinogenic alkaloids — no ergotamine or
ergotoxine group compounds. This means the entire sclerotium could be used
directly in powder form without the risk of toxic vasoconstrictive alkaloids.

Hofmann himself noted this distinction, writing that "an easier method still
would have been to have recourse to some kind of ergot like that growing on
the grass *Paspalum distichum*, which contains only alkaloids that are
hallucinogenic." This observation became central to debates about ancient
psychoactive preparations.

*C. fusiformis* parasitizes pearl millet across Africa and India, producing
clavine alkaloids and causing significant agricultural losses. *C. gigas*
infects rice and other grasses in East Asia. Each species has evolved a
distinct alkaloid profile adapted to its [[ecological-niche]], but all share the
fundamental ergoline ring system that defines the ergot alkaloid family.

## Ergot Alkaloid Chemistry — The Ergoline Ring

The ergot alkaloids are derived from the tetracyclic ergoline ring system,
a four-ring structure containing both indole and amide functional groups.
Their structural similarity to serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine) explains why
ergot compounds have such profound effects on the central nervous system.

Ergot alkaloids fall into two pharmacologically distinct categories. The
water-insoluble peptide alkaloids include ergotamine and the ergotoxine group
(ergocristine, ergocryptine, ergocornine). These are primarily vasoconstrictive
and uterotonic, causing the gangrene characteristic of ergotism but also
providing the medicinal effects long exploited in obstetrics and migraine
treatment.

The water-soluble alkaloids include lysergic acid amide (LSA, also called
ergine), [[lysergic-acid-hydroxyethylamide]], and ergonovine (ergometrine). These
are psychoactive at relatively low doses — LSA is active at 1–2 mg orally,
roughly 10–50 times less potent than LSD — and produce visionary effects
through serotonergic receptor activation.

Clavine alkaloids such as agroclavine, elymoclavine, and lysergol represent
simpler ergoline derivatives. They serve as biosynthetic precursors to the more
complex alkaloids and are produced by several *Claviceps* species independently.

## Gangrenous Ergotism — St. Anthony's Fire

When ergot-infected grain was milled into flour and consumed as bread, it
caused epidemics of ergotism that devastated European populations for centuries.
Gangrenous ergotism, known as St. Anthony's Fire, was the most visually
horrific manifestation.

The condition began with tingling in the fingers, followed by vomiting and
diarrhea. Within days, gangrene developed in the toes and fingers as the
vasoconstrictive alkaloids shut off blood flow to the extremities. Entire
limbs were affected by dry gangrene, followed by spontaneous separation from
the body without bleeding.

An eighteenth-century English report described "a singular calamity, which
suddenly happened to a poor family in this parish, of which six persons lost
their feet by a mortification not to be accounted for." The condition was named
for the Order of St. Anthony, which operated hospitals in France specializing
in its treatment during the medieval period.

Ergotamine is preserved during baking, meaning contaminated rye flour retained
its toxic properties after bread was prepared. Since rye bread served as the
dietary staple across northern Europe, ergotism was a recurring public health
catastrophe whenever weather conditions favored fungal infection of the crop.

## Convulsive Ergotism and Mass Hysteria

The second clinical form of ergotism produced neurological rather than
circulatory symptoms. Convulsive ergotism began similarly with gastrointestinal
distress but progressed to painful spasms of limb muscles, culminating in
epileptic-like convulsions and delirium.
