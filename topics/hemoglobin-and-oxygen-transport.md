# Hemoglobin and Oxygen Transport
Hemoglobin is the principal oxygen-transport protein of vertebrate blood.
It is a red, iron-containing metalloprotein concentrated inside erythrocytes.
Its main function is to bind oxygen at respiratory surfaces and release it in tissues.
The molecule also helps transport carbon dioxide, buffer blood pH, and interact with nitric oxide signaling.
Because it couples gas chemistry to circulation, hemoglobin is central to physiology and medicine.
Its behavior is one of the classic examples of protein allostery.
The same protein must hold oxygen tightly in the lung and let it go in [[isolation-metabolically-active-arbuscules-intraradical-hyphae]] tissue.
That balance is achieved by heme chemistry, tetrameric structure, and small-molecule regulation.
## Molecular Structure
Adult human hemoglobin A is a tetramer made of two alpha chains and two beta chains.
Each globin chain surrounds one heme prosthetic group.
Each heme contains a protoporphyrin IX ring with a central ferrous iron ion.
The ferrous iron binds oxygen reversibly at its sixth coordination position.
A complete hemoglobin tetramer can therefore carry four oxygen molecules.
The globin fold positions a proximal histidine directly below the heme iron.
A distal histidine above the heme stabilizes oxygen and reduces the advantage of carbon monoxide.
The iron must remain in the Fe2+ state for oxygen transport.
Oxidation to Fe3+ forms methemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen normally.
Red blood cells use reductive enzymes to return methemoglobin to the functional ferrous state.
## Cooperative Oxygen Binding
Hemoglobin does not behave as four independent oxygen-binding sites.
Binding at one heme changes the shape and affinity of the remaining subunits.
This cooperative binding produces a sigmoidal oxygen dissociation curve.
At low oxygen pressure, hemoglobin has relatively low affinity.
At intermediate oxygen pressure, affinity rises steeply as binding promotes more binding.
At high oxygen pressure, the sites approach saturation.
The curve allows efficient loading in the lung while preserving unloading capacity in tissues.
A small fall in tissue oxygen pressure can release a large amount of oxygen.
Myoglobin, by contrast, is monomeric and has a hyperbolic binding curve.
That difference makes myoglobin better suited for storage and hemoglobin better suited for transport.
## T and R States
The cooperative behavior of hemoglobin is often described using T and R conformations.
The T state, or tense state, is the lower-affinity deoxygenated form.
The R state, or relaxed state, is the higher-affinity oxygenated form.
In the T state, salt bridges and subunit contacts stabilize a compact low-affinity structure.
When oxygen binds, the iron moves into the plane of the porphyrin ring.
That motion pulls the proximal histidine and shifts a nearby alpha helix.
The structural change travels to the alpha-beta subunit interfaces.
As the tetramer rotates toward the R state, other hemes become more receptive to oxygen.
Real hemoglobin exists as an ensemble of states rather than a rigid two-position switch.
Even so, the T-R model remains the most useful map of structure-function coupling.
## Loading in the Lung
In the lung, alveolar oxygen pressure favors oxygen binding and the R state.
Oxygen diffuses across the alveolar membrane into pulmonary capillary blood.
Hemoglobin binds the entering oxygen and maintains the diffusion gradient from airspace to blood.
Only a small fraction of blood oxygen is physically dissolved in plasma.
Most oxygen is carried reversibly on hemoglobin.
This greatly increases the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood.
Near-saturated arterial hemoglobin gives oxygenated blood its bright red color.
Efficient loading depends on ventilation, perfusion, membrane diffusion, and functional hemoglobin concentration.
Disease in the lung, blood, or circulation can therefore reduce oxygen delivery.
Hemoglobin is only one component of the larger respiratory transport system.
## Unloading in Tissues
Peripheral tissues consume oxygen for mitochondrial respiration.
This consumption lowers local oxygen pressure relative to arterial blood.
The gradient draws oxygen away from hemoglobin and into cells.
Active tissues also produce carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions, heat, and metabolites.
These local conditions lower hemoglobin affinity and favor oxygen release.
The response concentrates delivery where metabolism is highest.
Resting tissues extract only part of the oxygen carried in arterial blood.
During exercise, extraction increases as muscle oxygen pressure falls.
Hemoglobin therefore acts as a responsive delivery system, not merely a passive container.
Its oxygen release integrates blood flow, tissue demand, and local chemistry.
## The Bohr Effect
The Bohr effect is the fall in hemoglobin oxygen affinity caused by lower pH and higher carbon dioxide.
In respiring tissues, carbon dioxide is converted to bicarbonate and protons by carbonic anhydrase.
Protons bind hemoglobin residues and stabilize the T state.
Carbon dioxide also forms carbamino compounds on globin amino groups.
These changes shift the oxygen dissociation curve to the right.
A right shift means hemoglobin releases more oxygen at a given oxygen pressure.
In the lung, carbon dioxide is exhaled and pH rises again.
The curve shifts leftward, supporting oxygen loading.
The Bohr effect is a chemical link between metabolism and oxygen delivery.
It is one reason active tissue receives proportionally more oxygen.
## 2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate
2,3-bisphosphoglycerate, or 2,3-BPG, is a major allosteric regulator of hemoglobin.
It is produced inside erythrocytes from an intermediate of glycolysis.
The molecule binds in the central cavity of deoxygenated hemoglobin.
Its negative charges interact with positively charged residues, especially on beta chains.
This stabilizes the T state and lowers oxygen affinity.
The result is improved oxygen unloading in peripheral tissues.
At high altitude, red blood cells increase 2,3-BPG over time.
That adaptation helps compensate for lower atmospheric oxygen pressure.
Fetal hemoglobin binds 2,3-BPG less strongly than adult hemoglobin.
Its higher oxygen affinity helps draw oxygen from maternal blood across the placenta.
## Carbon Dioxide Transport
Hemoglobin contributes to carbon dioxide transport as well as oxygen transport.
Most carbon dioxide is carried as bicarbonate after conversion within erythrocytes.
Hemoglobin buffers the protons generated by that reaction.
Deoxygenated hemoglobin accepts protons more readily than oxygenated hemoglobin.
This property supports the Haldane effect, in which deoxygenated blood carries more carbon dioxide.
Some carbon dioxide also binds directly to terminal amino groups on globin chains.
These carbamino compounds are favored in deoxygenated hemoglobin.
In the lung, oxygenation reduces carbon dioxide carriage and promotes exhalation.
Oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide removal are therefore chemically coupled.
Hemoglobin functions as both respiratory pigment and blood buffer.
## Nitric Oxide and Vascular Signaling
Hemoglobin interacts strongly with nitric oxide.
Free nitric oxide can bind heme iron and be scavenged by hemoglobin.
Hemoglobin may also carry nitric oxide bioactivity through S-nitrosylation and related chemistry.
These reactions have been proposed to help match blood flow to oxygen demand.
When tissue oxygen is low, local vasodilation increases perfusion.
The exact contribution of hemoglobin-bound nitric oxide chemistry remains an active research area.
What is clear is that hemoglobin participates in vascular regulation as well as gas carriage.
This adds another layer to the relationship between blood chemistry and tissue metabolism.
## Developmental Forms
Human globin genes are expressed in a developmental sequence.
Embryonic hemoglobins appear early in development.
Fetal hemoglobin, composed of alpha and gamma chains, dominates before birth.
Adult hemoglobin A, composed of alpha and beta chains, becomes predominant after birth.
A small amount of adult hemoglobin A2 contains delta chains.
Fetal hemoglobin has higher oxygen affinity than adult hemoglobin.
This supports transfer of oxygen from maternal to fetal blood in the placenta.
Regulators such as BCL11A help silence fetal globin expression after birth.
Therapies for [[cannabis-and-sickle-cell-disease]] disease and beta thalassemia often try to reactivate fetal hemoglobin.
The developmental switch is both a biological process and a therapeutic target.
## Disorders and Evolution
Hemoglobin function can be impaired by mutation, reduced production, oxidation, or toxic ligands.
Sickle cell disease results from a beta-globin substitution that promotes polymerization of deoxygenated hemoglobin.
Thalassemias reduce alpha or beta globin synthesis and create imbalanced chain production.
Methemoglobinemia increases ferric heme that cannot bind oxygen normally.
Carbon monoxide poisoning forms carboxyhemoglobin and impairs oxygen carriage and unloading.
Anemia reduces total oxygen capacity even when each hemoglobin molecule is normal.
Globin proteins are ancient and occur in many organisms with diverse functions.
Vertebrate hemoglobin evolved through gene duplication, divergence, and cooperative assembly.
The persistence of sickle cell trait in malaria regions illustrates balancing selection.
Few proteins connect atomic structure, physiology, medicine, and evolution so clearly.
## Summary
Hemoglobin is a molecular machine optimized for reversible gas transport.
Its heme groups bind oxygen, but its tetrameric structure determines when oxygen is held or released.
Cooperativity, the Bohr effect, 2,3-BPG, and developmental isoforms tune it to changing physiological needs.
The same chemistry that makes hemoglobin powerful also makes it vulnerable to mutation, oxidation, and toxic competition.
Understanding hemoglobin illuminates protein structure, respiration, blood buffering, and human disease.
