## The Soil Food Web

The soil food web is the complex community of organisms living in soil that
interact with each other and with plants, organic matter, and the mineral
environment to drive the processes that sustain terrestrial life. Far from
being mere dirt, healthy soil is a living ecosystem teeming with billions of
organisms per teaspoon, including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes,
microarthropods, earthworms, and countless other creatures. These organisms
form an intricate web of feeding relationships that cycle nutrients, build
soil structure, suppress disease, decompose organic matter, and regulate the
availability of water and minerals to plants.

The concept of the soil food web was formalized and popularized by Dr. Elaine
Ingham, a soil microbiologist whose research and teaching have profoundly
influenced the fields of sustainable agriculture, composting, and ecological
restoration. Ingham's model describes the soil as a functioning ecosystem in
which organisms at different trophic levels interact through predation,
competition, and symbiosis to create the conditions that plants need to thrive.
Rather than viewing soil as an inert substrate that holds plants upright and
requires chemical inputs to be productive, the soil food web framework
understands soil as a living system that, when healthy, provides all the
nutrients and conditions that plants need.

Understanding the soil food web has transformative implications for agriculture
and land management. It suggests that many of the problems addressed by
synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and tillage are actually caused or
exacerbated by the very practices that claim to solve them. Chemical
fertilizers feed plants directly but bypass the soil biology that creates and
maintains soil structure, leading to compaction, erosion, and dependency on
continued inputs. Pesticides kill target pests but also devastate the
predatory organisms that would naturally keep those pests in check. Tillage
disrupts fungal networks and soil structure, releasing carbon and reducing
the soil's capacity to hold water and nutrients. The soil food web framework
offers an alternative: manage the soil biology, and the biology will manage
the soil.

## Trophic Levels in the Soil Food Web

The soil food web is organized into trophic levels, each consisting of
organisms that share a similar position in the flow of energy and nutrients
through the ecosystem. The primary trophic levels, moving from the base to the
top of the food web, are bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes,
microarthropods, macroarthropods, and earthworms. Each level plays a distinct
and essential role in nutrient cycling, [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]], nitrification,
and denitrification. Bacteria-dominated soils are characteristic of
disturbed or early-succession environments and are generally preferred by
annual crops and weeds, which have evolved to take advantage of the rapid
nutrient cycling that bacterial soils provide. Bacteria also contribute to
soil structure through the production of extracellular polymeric substances
that bind soil particles into microaggregates.

Fungi are the second major group of soil microorganisms and include both
saprotrophic decomposers and mycorrhizal symbionts. Soil fungi decompose more
recalcitrant organic materials than bacteria, including cellulose, lignin, and
complex organic compounds. Mycorrhizal fungi form mutualistic associations
with the roots of the vast majority of land plants, extending the plant's
effective root system through a hyphal network that can access water and
nutrients far beyond the reach of the plant's own roots. Fungal-dominated
soils are characteristic of mature, undisturbed ecosystems such as forests
and prairies, and are preferred by perennial plants, trees, and many
perennial crops. Fungi contribute to soil structure by binding soil particles
with hyphae and by producing glomalin, a sticky protein that stabilizes soil
aggregates and contributes significantly to soil carbon storage.

Protozoa are single-celled organisms that feed primarily on bacteria and are
the first level of predators in the soil food web. They include flagellates,
amoebae, and ciliates, each with different feeding strategies and habitat
preferences. Protozoa play a crucial role in nutrient cycling by consuming
bacteria and releasing the nutrients contained in bacterial biomass in forms
that are available to plants. This process, called the microbial loop or the
predator-prey nutrient cascade, is one of the most important mechanisms by
which nutrients are mineralized in soil. Without protozoa, nutrients locked
up in bacterial biomass would remain unavailable to plants for extended
periods.

Over 80% of plant species invest substantial amounts of photosynthetically-fixed carbon into these fungal symbionts.
