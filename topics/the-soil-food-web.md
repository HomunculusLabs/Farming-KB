# The Soil Food Web

The soil food web is the complex community of organisms living in soil that
interact with each other and with plants, organic matter, and the mineral
environment to drive the processes that sustain terrestrial life. Far from
being mere dirt, healthy soil is a living ecosystem teeming with billions of
organisms per teaspoon, including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes,
microarthropods, earthworms, and countless other creatures. These organisms
form an intricate web of feeding relationships that cycle nutrients, build
soil structure, suppress disease, decompose organic matter, and regulate the
availability of water and minerals to plants.

The concept of the soil food web was formalized and popularized by Dr. Elaine
Ingham, a soil microbiologist whose research and teaching have profoundly
influenced the fields of sustainable agriculture, composting, and ecological
restoration. Ingham's model describes the soil as a functioning ecosystem in
which organisms at different trophic levels interact through predation,
competition, and symbiosis to create the conditions that plants need to thrive.
Rather than viewing soil as an inert substrate that holds plants upright and
requires chemical inputs to be productive, the soil food web framework
understands soil as a living system that, when healthy, provides all the
nutrients and conditions that plants need.

Understanding the soil food web has transformative implications for agriculture
and land management. It suggests that many of the problems addressed by
synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and tillage are actually caused or
exacerbated by the very practices that claim to solve them. Chemical
fertilizers feed plants directly but bypass the soil biology that creates and
maintains soil structure, leading to compaction, erosion, and dependency on
continued inputs. Pesticides kill target pests but also devastate the
predatory organisms that would naturally keep those pests in check. Tillage
disrupts fungal networks and soil structure, releasing carbon and reducing
the soil's capacity to hold water and nutrients. The soil food web framework
offers an alternative: manage the soil biology, and the biology will manage
the soil.

## Trophic Levels in the Soil Food Web

The soil food web is organized into trophic levels, each consisting of
organisms that share a similar position in the flow of energy and nutrients
through the ecosystem. The primary trophic levels, moving from the base to the
top of the food web, are bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes,
microarthropods, macroarthropods, and earthworms. Each level plays a distinct
and essential role in nutrient cycling, disease suppression, and soil
structure development.

Bacteria are the smallest and most numerous organisms in the soil, with
populations in healthy soil ranging from hundreds of millions to billions per
gram. They are the primary decomposers of simple organic compounds such as
sugars, proteins, and amino acids, and they play a central role in the
nitrogen cycle through processes including nitrogen fixation, nitrification,
and denitrification. Bacteria-dominated soils are characteristic of
disturbed or early-succession environments and are generally preferred by
annual crops and weeds, which have evolved to take advantage of the rapid
nutrient cycling that bacterial soils provide. Bacteria also contribute to
soil structure through the production of extracellular polymeric substances
that bind soil particles into microaggregates.

Fungi are the second major group of soil microorganisms and include both
saprotrophic decomposers and mycorrhizal symbionts. Soil fungi decompose more
recalcitrant organic materials than bacteria, including cellulose, lignin, and
complex organic compounds. Mycorrhizal fungi form mutualistic associations
with the roots of the vast majority of land plants, extending the plant's
effective root system through a hyphal network that can access water and
nutrients far beyond the reach of the plant's own roots. Fungal-dominated
soils are characteristic of mature, undisturbed ecosystems such as forests
and prairies, and are preferred by perennial plants, trees, and many
perennial crops. Fungi contribute to soil structure by binding soil particles
with hyphae and by producing glomalin, a sticky protein that stabilizes soil
aggregates and contributes significantly to soil carbon storage.

Protozoa are single-celled organisms that feed primarily on bacteria and are
the first level of predators in the soil food web. They include flagellates,
amoebae, and ciliates, each with different feeding strategies and habitat
preferences. Protozoa play a crucial role in nutrient cycling by consuming
bacteria and releasing the nutrients contained in bacterial biomass in forms
that are available to plants. This process, called the microbial loop or the
predator-prey nutrient cascade, is one of the most important mechanisms by
which nutrients are mineralized in soil. Without protozoa, nutrients locked
up in bacterial biomass would remain unavailable to plants for extended
periods.

Nematodes are microscopic roundworms that occupy several trophic levels in the
soil food web. Bacterial-feeding nematodes consume bacteria and release
nutrients in a manner similar to protozoa. Fungal-feeding nematodes graze on
fungal hyphae, regulating fungal populations and cycling nutrients from
fungal biomass. Predatory nematodes feed on other nematodes and small
microarthropods, contributing to pest suppression. Plant-parasitic nematodes
feed on plant roots and can cause significant crop damage, but their
populations are normally kept in check by the predatory nematodes and other
organisms that prey on them. A healthy, diverse nematode community is a key
indicator of soil health.

Microarthropods, including springtails (Collembola) and soil mites
(Acarina), are larger than nematodes and feed on fungi, bacteria, and each
other. They are important regulators of fungal populations and contribute to
nutrient cycling by fragmenting organic matter, increasing its surface area
for microbial colonization. As they move through the soil, microarthropods
create pores and channels that improve soil aeration and water infiltration.

Macroarthropods and earthworms are the largest organisms in the soil food web
and have a profound impact on soil structure and organic matter distribution.
Earthworms are often called "ecosystem engineers" for their ability to
physically restructure soil through burrowing, casting, and the mixing of
organic and mineral material. Their burrows create channels for water
infiltration and root penetration, and their casts are rich in nutrients and
microbial activity. The presence of earthworms is one of the most reliable
indicators of healthy soil.

## Bacterial-Dominated Versus Fungal-Dominated Soils

The ratio of bacteria to fungi in soil is one of the most important
characteristics of the soil food web and has direct implications for plant
health and crop selection. Soil can be broadly categorized as bacterial-dominated,
fungal-dominated, or balanced, and each state favors different types of plants
and ecosystems.

Bacterial-dominated soils have a low fungal-to-bacterial biomass ratio,
typically less than 1:1, and are characteristic of recently disturbed
environments such as tilled agricultural fields, recently cleared land, and
early-successional ecosystems. Bacteria reproduce rapidly and thrive in
environments where organic matter is frequently disturbed and incorporated
into the soil, which is exactly what tillage does. The rapid nutrient cycling
in bacterial soils makes nitrogen and other nutrients quickly available in
soluble forms, which favors fast-growing annual plants that require readily
available nutrients. Most vegetable crops, grains, and weeds are adapted to
bacterial-dominated soils.

Fungal-dominated soils have a high fungal-to-bacterial ratio, typically
greater than 10:1, and are characteristic of mature, undisturbed ecosystems
such as forests, prairies, and old-growth pastures. Fungi grow more slowly
than bacteria but are more efficient at decomposing complex organic matter
and retaining nutrients in their biomass for extended periods. The slow,
steady nutrient release in fungal soils favors perennial plants, including
trees, shrubs, and many perennial crops, that have evolved to access
nutrients through mycorrhizal associations. The fungal network also provides
a more stable and resilient nutrient supply that is less prone to leaching.

Understanding this distinction is important for crop planning and soil
management. If the goal is to grow annual vegetables, maintaining a
bacterial-dominated soil through appropriate compost applications and
moderate disturbance may be appropriate. If the goal is to establish a food
forest, orchard, or perennial polyculture, building fungal dominance through
mulching, reduced disturbance, and fungal inoculation will produce better
long-term results. Conventional agricultural practices, particularly heavy
tillage and the application of high-nitrogen fertilizers, consistently shift
soils toward bacterial dominance, which is one reason why conventional
agriculture struggles to support perennial crops without heavy inputs.

## The Microbial Loop and Nutrient Cycling

The microbial loop describes the pathway by which nutrients contained in
microbial biomass are converted into plant-available forms through the process
of predation. This concept, borrowed from aquatic ecology, is central to
understanding how the soil food web feeds plants without the need for soluble
fertilizer inputs. The loop works as follows: bacteria and fungi absorb
nutrients from the soil solution and incorporate them into their biomass.
These nutrients are temporarily immobilized, meaning they are unavailable to
plants. When protozoa, nematodes, or microarthropods consume the bacteria or
fungi, they digest the microbial biomass and excrete the excess nutrients in
plant-available forms, primarily as ammonium.

The predator-prey nutrient cascade is remarkably efficient at matching
nutrient availability to plant demand. When plant roots exude sugars and amino
acids into the rhizosphere (the soil zone immediately surrounding the roots),
they feed bacteria and fungi, which proliferate in response. The increased
microbial population attracts protozoa and nematodes, which graze on the
microbes and release nutrients in plant-available forms precisely where the
plant needs them. This creates a self-regulating system in which nutrient
availability is driven by plant demand rather than by the timing of fertilizer
applications.

Mineralization is the process by which organic nutrients are converted into
inorganic, plant-available forms. In the soil food web, mineralization occurs
primarily through the microbial loop described above, as well as through the
direct enzymatic decomposition of organic matter by bacteria and fungi. The
rate of mineralization depends on environmental conditions including
temperature, moisture, pH, and the quality of the organic matter being
decomposed. Warm, moist conditions favor rapid mineralization, while cold or
dry conditions slow it considerably.

Immobilization is the opposite of mineralization: the uptake of inorganic
nutrients by soil microorganisms, which temporarily removes those nutrients
from the plant-available pool. Immobilization occurs when soil organisms
decompose organic matter with a high carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, such as wood
chips or straw, and need to absorb nitrogen from the soil to build their
biomass. This can create a temporary nitrogen deficiency for plants, though
the nitrogen is not lost; it is merely stored in microbial biomass and will
be released when the organisms die or are consumed by predators. Understanding
the balance between mineralization and immobilization is essential for managing
soil fertility with organic amendments.

## Mycorrhizal Networks

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic associations with the roots of approximately
90 percent of plant species, creating a vast underground network that
fundamentally alters the way plants interact with their environment. In
exchange for carbon compounds produced by the plant through photosynthesis,
mycorrhizal fungi provide the plant with water, phosphorus, nitrogen, and
other nutrients that they access through their extensive hyphal networks. The
mycorrhizal network can extend the effective absorptive surface area of a
plant's root system by orders of magnitude, dramatically improving the
plant's ability to acquire resources from soil.

There are two main types of mycorrhizal associations. Arbuscular mycorrhizal
fungi (AMF) are the most common, associating with the vast majority of
agricultural crops, vegetables, and grasses. AMF penetrate the root cells to
form specialized structures called arbuscules, where the exchange of nutrients
and carbon takes place. Ectomycorrhizal fungi (ECM) associate primarily with
trees, particularly in temperate and boreal forests, forming a sheath around
the root tips and a network between root cells without penetrating them.
Both types play critical roles in nutrient cycling, plant health, and soil
structure.

Common mycorrhizal networks (CMNs), sometimes called "wood wide webs," are
the hyphal connections that form between the roots of different plants through
shared mycorrhizal fungal partners. These networks can facilitate the transfer
of carbon, nutrients, water, and even chemical signals between plants. Research
has shown that carbon can move from older, sun-exposed trees to shaded
seedlings through common mycorrhizal networks, and that plants can send
chemical warning signals through the network that prime neighboring plants
for defense against insect attack or disease. The extent and ecological
significance of these inter-plant transfers remain active areas of research,
but the existence of the networks themselves is well-established.

Mycorrhizal associations are disrupted by many common agricultural practices.
Tillage severs hyphal connections and destroys the network structure that
takes months or years to develop. High rates of phosphorus fertilizer reduce
the plant's dependence on mycorrhizal fungi for phosphorus acquisition,
causing the plant to reduce carbon allocation to the fungal partner and
effectively starving the association. Fungicides, particularly systemic
products, can kill mycorrhizal fungi directly. Fallow periods without host
plants cause mycorrhizal populations to decline. Maintaining mycorrhizal
health requires minimizing soil disturbance, avoiding excessive fertilizer
applications, keeping the soil covered with living plants, and using cover
crops to maintain host root networks between cash crops.

## Biofilm Formation and Soil Aggregation

Biofilms are structured communities of microorganisms embedded in a
self-produced matrix of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). In soil,
biofilms form on the surfaces of soil particles, root surfaces, and organic
matter, creating microenvironments where microbial activity is concentrated
and protected. Biofilms are essential for soil aggregation, the process by
which individual soil particles are bound together into larger, more stable
structural units.

Soil aggregates are the fundamental structural units of soil and are
critical for water infiltration, gas exchange, root penetration, and erosion
resistance. Aggregates form through a combination of biological, chemical,
and physical processes, but the biological processes driven by the soil food
web are the most important for long-term aggregate stability. Bacteria and
their EPS bind fine soil particles into microaggregates, which are the basic
building blocks of soil structure. Fungal hyphae physically entangle soil
particles and bind microaggregates together into larger macroaggregates.

Glomalin is a glycoprotein produced by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi that
plays a major role in soil aggregation and carbon sequestration. Glomalin
acts as a biological glue that coats hyphae and binds soil particles
together, contributing significantly to the stability of soil aggregates. It
is highly resistant to decomposition and can persist in soil for decades,
making it one of the most important pools of stable soil carbon. Soils under
long-term mycorrhizal vegetation typically contain significantly more
glomalin and have more stable aggregates than soils under non-mycorrhizal
vegetation or bare fallow.

The destruction of soil aggregates through tillage or compaction has cascading
negative effects on the entire food web. When aggregates are broken, organisms
are exposed to predation and desiccation, protected organic matter decomposes
rapidly, and the soil loses structure, leading to compaction and reduced water
infiltration. Restoring aggregation requires building the food web from the
bottom up through organic matter addition and reduced disturbance.

## How Tillage Destroys the Food Web

Tillage is one of the most destructive practices for the soil food web. Every
pass of a plow, rototiller, or cultivator physically disrupts the habitat of
soil organisms, severs fungal networks, exposes buried organisms to the
surface, and mixes soil layers that have developed distinct biological
communities. The effects of tillage on the soil food web are profound and
long-lasting, and understanding these effects is key to understanding why
no-till and reduced-till farming systems consistently outperform conventional
tillage in terms of soil health.

The most immediate effect of tillage is the physical destruction of fungal
hyphae. Mycorrhizal networks that may have taken months or years to develop
are severed and buried in a single pass. Because fungi grow slowly compared
to bacteria, the recovery of fungal biomass after tillage takes considerably
longer than the recovery of bacterial biomass. This is why tilled soils are
invariably bacterial-dominated: the fast-growing bacteria recover quickly
and occupy the disturbed space before fungi can re-establish. The shift from
fungal to bacterial dominance has implications for nutrient cycling, soil
structure, and the types of plants that the soil can best support.

Tillage also stimulates the rapid decomposition of soil organic matter by
exposing previously buried organic material to oxygen and by physically
breaking up soil aggregates that protect organic matter from microbial
access. This burst of decomposition releases a flush of nutrients,
particularly nitrogen, which can benefit the next crop in the short term but
depletes soil organic matter reserves over time. The loss of soil organic
matter reduces the soil's water-holding capacity, cation exchange capacity,
and biological activity, creating a positive feedback loop that makes the
soil increasingly dependent on external inputs.

The physical disruption also affects larger organisms. Earthworms are killed or
driven deeper, their burrows filled in, reducing porosity and infiltration.
The mixing of soil layers disrupts vertical stratification of organisms. The
overall effect is a simplification and reduction of the food web's complexity.

## Compost Tea as Inoculant

Compost tea, specifically actively aerated compost tea (AACT), is a liquid
extract of high-quality compost that is brewed under controlled conditions to
multiply the beneficial microorganisms present in the compost. When applied
to soil or plant surfaces, compost tea serves as a biological inoculant that
introduces or augments the populations of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and
nematodes in the soil food web. It is one of the most accessible and
practical tools available to growers who want to actively manage their soil
biology.

The brewing process involves placing mature, high-quality compost in a mesh
bag suspended in dechlorinated water, adding a food source (such as molasses,
kelp meal, or fish hydrolysate) to feed the microorganisms, and aerating
the mixture vigorously for 24 to 48 hours. The aeration is critical because
it maintains oxygen levels that favor aerobic microorganisms over anaerobic
ones, and the food source determines which groups of organisms will
proliferate during the brewing period. Composts with higher fungal content
produce more fungal-dominated teas, while bacterial foods like molasses
produce more bacterial-dominated teas.

Compost tea can be applied as a soil drench to inoculate the soil food web,
as a foliar spray to protect plant surfaces from pathogenic organisms, or as
a seed treatment to promote early colonization of the rhizosphere. When
applied to soil, the tea introduces a diverse community of microorganisms
that can compete with pathogens, cycle nutrients, and contribute to soil
aggregation. When applied as a foliar spray, the microorganisms in the tea
can occupy leaf and stem surfaces, creating a biological barrier that
prevents pathogenic organisms from establishing. The effectiveness of
compost tea depends heavily on the quality of the starting compost, the
brewing conditions, and the timing and method of application.

## Cover Crops and Root Exudates

Cover crops are plants grown primarily for the benefit of the soil and the
soil food web rather than for harvest. They are one of the most powerful
tools available for building soil health, and their benefits are largely
mediated through their effects on the soil food web. Cover crops provide
living roots in the soil during periods when cash crop fields would otherwise
be bare, maintaining the root exudate supply that feeds soil microorganisms
and sustains mycorrhizal networks.

Root exudates are the sugars, amino acids, organic acids, and other
compounds that plant roots release into the rhizosphere. These exudates serve
multiple functions: they attract beneficial microorganisms, inhibit pathogens,
solubilize mineral nutrients, and modify the chemical environment around the
root. The quantity and composition of root exudates vary among plant species,
which means that different cover crops support different microbial communities.
Diverse cover crop mixtures, including grasses, legumes, brassicas, and other
families, promote the most diverse and resilient soil food web.

Leguminous cover crops such as clover, vetch, and peas form symbiotic
associations with rhizobium bacteria that fix atmospheric nitrogen, converting
it into forms available to plants and soil organisms. The nitrogen fixed by
legume cover crops becomes available to subsequent cash crops as the legume
residue decomposes, reducing or eliminating the need for synthetic nitrogen
fertilizer. Grass cover crops such as rye and oats produce abundant fibrous
root systems that feed soil bacteria and contribute to soil aggregation. Brassica
cover crops such as radish and mustard produce biofumigant compounds that
suppress soil-borne pathogens and pests.

The root systems of cover crops also create channels in the soil that improve
water infiltration and provide pathways for the roots of subsequent cash crops.
When cover crops are terminated and left on the soil surface as mulch, they
provide food and habitat for surface-dwelling organisms and gradually release
nutrients as they decompose. The integration of cover crops into crop
rotations is one of the most effective practices for building and maintaining
a healthy, productive soil food web.

## Anaerobic Pockets and Disease Suppression

Anaerobic conditions in soil, where oxygen is depleted, create pockets that
favor the growth of anaerobic microorganisms, many of which produce compounds
toxic to plant roots. Anaerobic pockets can develop in compacted soils, in
waterlogged areas, or in soils with excessive organic matter that consumes
oxygen faster than it can be replenished. The products of anaerobic
metabolism, including hydrogen sulfide, alcohol, and various organic acids,
can damage or kill plant roots and create entry points for pathogenic
organisms. Managing soil to prevent anaerobic conditions is therefore
important for both plant health and soil food web function.

Disease suppression in healthy soils occurs through several mechanisms
mediated by the soil food web. Competition for resources is perhaps the most
important: a diverse and abundant community of beneficial microorganisms
occupies the ecological niches that pathogenic organisms would otherwise
exploit, making it difficult for pathogens to establish populations above
threshold levels. Antibiosis, the production of antimicrobial compounds by
beneficial organisms, directly inhibits pathogenic species. Predation by
nematodes, microarthropods, and other organisms keeps pathogen populations
in check. And induced systemic resistance, triggered by exposure to
beneficial microorganisms, activates the plant's own defense mechanisms.

The relationship between soil food web health and disease suppression is
well-documented. Soils with high biological diversity and activity consistently
show lower incidence of soil-borne diseases than degraded or biologically
impoverished soils. This is why the transition from conventional to biological
management often produces an initial period of disease problems, as the soil
biology rebuilds, followed by a long-term reduction in disease pressure as the
soil food web matures and natural disease suppression develops.

## Practical Assessment Methods

Assessing the health and composition of the soil food web is essential for
making informed management decisions. Several methods are available, ranging
from simple field observations to sophisticated laboratory analyses. The choice
The choice of method depends on the information needed and the resources
available.

Direct microscopy is the most accessible and informative method for assessing
the soil food web. Using a compound microscope at 400x magnification, a trained
observer can identify and count bacteria, fungal hyphae, protozoa, and
nematodes in a soil sample. The ratios between these groups provide
information about the trophic structure of the food web, the degree of
bacterial versus fungal dominance, and the presence of predatory organisms
that drive nutrient cycling. This method was developed and refined by Elaine
Ingham and her colleagues and is the standard approach used by soil food web
consultants worldwide.

Phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) analysis is a laboratory technique that
quantifies the biomass of different groups of soil microorganisms based on
the signature fatty acids in their cell membranes. PLFA analysis provides a
quantitative measure of total microbial biomass, bacterial-to-fungal ratios,
and the relative abundance of different functional groups including
actinomycetes, mycorrhizal fungi, and gram-positive and gram-negative
bacteria. It is more precise and reproducible than microscopy but requires
specialized laboratory equipment and expertise, making it more expensive and
less accessible for routine monitoring.

Soil respiration rate, measured as CO2 released from a soil sample over a
defined period, provides a general index of overall microbial activity. High
rates indicate active decomposition and nutrient cycling, while low rates
suggest biological inactivity. Respiration is influenced by temperature,
moisture, organic matter, and recent management, so it should be interpreted
in context.

Field indicators of soil food web health include earthworm abundance, soil
aggregation, surface crusting, water infiltration rate, organic matter
content, root health, and the presence or absence of compaction layers. While
these indicators are less precise than laboratory methods, they provide
immediate, practical information that can guide management decisions. The
combination of field observation with periodic laboratory analysis provides
the most comprehensive assessment of soil food web health and the most
reliable basis for adaptive management.
