# Endangered Fungi and Conservation Strategies

## Introduction

Fungi represent one of the most diverse and ecologically vital kingdoms of life
on Earth, yet they remain the most underassessed and underprotected group of
organisms in global conservation efforts. Current estimates suggest there are
between 2.2 and 3.8 million fungal species worldwide, but only approximately
148,000 have been formally described by science. This means that 93 to 96 percent
of fungal diversity remains unknown — a staggering gap in biological knowledge
with profound implications for conservation, ecology, and human welfare. As
habitats disappear faster than species can be documented, an unknown number of
fungal species are likely going extinct without ever being recorded.

The fungal biodiversity crisis is not merely a taxonomic inconvenience; it is an
ecological emergency. Fungi underpin nearly all terrestrial ecosystems through
their roles as decomposers, mutualists, and pathogens. They drive nutrient cycling,
sustain plant productivity, and maintain soil health. Losing undocumented fungal
species means losing the ecosystem services they provide — services upon which
agriculture, forestry, water quality, and carbon sequestration fundamentally
depend. Despite this, fungi have been consistently marginalized in conservation
policy, receiving a fraction of the attention devoted to animals and plants.

Unlike animals, which leave fossil records and population data that can be tracked
over decades, most fungi are invisible for much of their life cycles and leave
virtually no trace when they disappear. The result is a silent extinction crisis
unfolding beneath our feet, one that science is only beginning to comprehend and
science is only beginning to comprehend and that conservation policy has scarcely begun to address. This article surveys the endangered fungi crisis, the factors driving it,
the major threats to fungal diversity, and the conservation strategies that offer
the best hope for preserving this irreplaceable component of Earth's biosphere.

## Why Fungi Are Underdescribed

The profound gap between estimated and described fungal diversity stems from a
combination of biological, historical, and institutional factors. Many fungi lead
cryptic lifestyles, existing primarily as microscopic mycelial networks within
soil, wood, or living host tissues. Their above-ground fruiting bodies are often
ephemeral, appearing for only a few days or weeks under specific environmental
conditions before decomposing or being consumed. This ephemerality makes them
extremely difficult to detect during standard biological surveys designed with
animals and plants in mind.

A deeper problem is the lack of baseline data. For most regions of the world,
there are no comprehensive inventories of fungal species, making it impossible to
establish trends in population size, geographic range, or extinction risk.
Taxonomic uncertainty compounds the problem: many putative species described in
older literature have never been re-examined using modern molecular tools, and it
is unclear how many of these names correspond to real, distinct biological
entities.

Historical bias has also played a major role. Conservation biology developed
around the study of vertebrate animals and flowering plants. Funding agencies,
government agencies, and international bodies have consistently prioritized these
groups, leaving mycology chronically underfunded. The number of professional
fungal taxonomists has been declining for decades as universities phase out
classical taxonomy training in favor of molecular biology and genomics. This
creates a paradox: the need for fungal taxonomic expertise has never been greater,
yet the capacity to provide it is steadily shrinking. Without trained taxonomists
to describe and name species, molecular sequence data accumulates in databases
without being linked to formal biological entities.

## Molecular Revelations and Their Limits

The advent of environmental DNA (eDNA) sequencing has dramatically reshaped our
understanding of fungal diversity, revealing a vast hidden world previously
invisible to science. By extracting and sequencing DNA directly from soil, water,
air, or host tissue samples, researchers can detect fungal species without ever
observing their fruiting bodies. These studies consistently find that fungal
diversity in a given habitat is orders of magnitude higher than traditional
fruiting-body surveys would suggest.

A key concept is the molecular operational taxonomic unit (MOTU), a cluster of
DNA sequences sufficiently similar to be considered the same species. MOTUs
provide a standardized way to quantify and compare fungal diversity across
studies. High-throughput sequencing of ribosomal DNA markers, particularly the
ITS region, has become the standard for fungal community profiling. Single-spore
sequencing and metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) are now providing genomic
insights into species that have never been cultured or observed.

However, molecular tools have significant limitations for conservation. A DNA
sequence in a database is not a described species. Without a formal taxonomic
description, a name, a type specimen, and an assessment of geographic range and
population status, a sequence cannot be evaluated against IUCN Red List criteria.
The gap between sequence-based discovery and formal taxonomy is enormous and
growing: millions of fungal sequences exist in public databases, but the rate of
formal species description has not increased correspondingly. Furthermore, DNA-
based detection is subject to methodological biases. Primer choice, sequencing
depth, and bioinformatic pipelines all influence which taxa are detected. Presence
of DNA does not confirm active presence and reproduction; it may represent dormant
spores, dead biomass, or contamination.

## The Red-Listing Challenge

The IUCN Red List of Threatened Species is the world's most comprehensive
information source on conservation status, yet fungi are dramatically
underrepresented. As of 2025, only a tiny fraction of described fungal species
have been evaluated, and the majority of assessments cover conspicuous,
charismatic macrofungi — the showy mushrooms most likely to be noticed by field
mycologists.

The root of this disparity lies in the IUCN assessment criteria themselves. The
standard criteria rely on quantitative measures such as population size, extent of
occurrence, area of occupancy, rate of decline, and generation time. For most
fungi, these parameters are either unknown or impossible to measure with
confidence. A fungal "population" is not analogous to an animal population: the
visible fruiting bodies represent only a tiny, sporadic fraction of the organism,
which exists primarily as an underground mycelial network of indeterminate extent.
Geographic range, as inferred from fruiting-body records, may dramatically
underestimate or overestimate true distribution. Generation times are equally
problematic — some fungi fruit annually, others only once every decade or more,
and the mycelium may persist for centuries.

Several efforts are underway to adapt Red List assessment for fungi. The IUCN
Species Survival Commission's Fungal Conservation Committee has developed
guidelines emphasizing habitat-based assessments, expert judgment, and proxy
measures of decline such as loss of host tree species or habitat degradation.
National red lists, particularly those maintained in Scandinavian countries, have
pioneered approaches more suited to fungal biology. Nonetheless, the fundamental
mismatch between IUCN criteria and fungal ecology remains a major barrier.

## Major Threats to Fungal Diversity

### Habitat Destruction

Habitat destruction is the single greatest threat to fungal biodiversity worldwide.
Old-growth forests are particularly critical reservoirs, especially for
ectomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi that form intimate, often species-specific associations
with long-lived trees. When old-growth forests are logged or converted to
agriculture, the fungal communities they support are degraded or eliminated. Many
ECM fungi depend on particular tree species and soil conditions that develop over
centuries; once disrupted, the fungi may not recover. Wetlands, grasslands, and
other natural habitats face similar pressures.

### Pollution

Nitrogen deposition from agricultural fertilizers and fossil fuel combustion is
one of the best-documented threats. Elevated nitrogen levels shift fungal community
composition, favoring fast-growing saprotrophic species over slow-growing
mycorrhizal fungi, impairing tree nutrition and reducing forest productivity.
Heavy metal contamination, pesticide application, and herbicide use also affect
fungal communities, often reducing diversity and disrupting functional roles.

### Climate Change

Shifting temperature and precipitation patterns are altering fungal phenology — the
timing of fruiting and spore release — which can decouple fungi from their plant
partners or environmental cues. Range shifts are occurring as species track
suitable climate conditions, but dispersal is limited by spore biology and host
availability. Perhaps most concerning is the potential for climate change to
disrupt plant-fungal mutualisms: if a tree migrates faster than its mycorrhizal
partners, it may establish poorly in its new range, with cascading ecosystem
effects.

### Overharvesting

Commercial and recreational harvesting of wild edible fungi is a growing concern.
Species such as matsutake (*Tricholoma matsutake*) and porcini (*Boletus edulis*
and related species) are harvested at industrial scales for international trade.
While moderate harvesting may not significantly harm populations, intensive
picking — especially when involving soil raking — can damage mycelial networks and
reduce reproductive output. Regulatory frameworks vary widely and are often
inadequate.

### Agricultural Expansion and Monoculture

The global expansion of monoculture agriculture has devastated fungal diversity.
Vast areas of native vegetation have been converted to crop fields, replacing
diverse fungal communities with simplified assemblages dominated by cosmopolitan
species. Tillage disrupts mycelial networks, fungicides kill non-target fungi, and
repeated cultivation of a single crop selects for a narrow suite of associates. The
loss of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) from agricultural soils is particularly
consequential for plant nutrient uptake and soil aggregation.

## The Co-Extinction Problem

One of the most insidious aspects of fungal biodiversity loss is co-extinction: the
disappearance of fungal species as a consequence of the decline or extinction of
their host organisms. Many fungi have narrow ecological niches and depend on
specific plant or animal partners. When a host species declines due to habitat
loss, disease, or climate change, the fungi that depend on it may disappear without
being noticed or documented. This undocumented co-extinction pathway represents a
major and largely invisible form of biodiversity loss.

The chytrid fungi illustrate how poorly we understand fungal diversity and its
ecological consequences. *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis* (Bd), a pathogenic
chytrid, has caused catastrophic amphibian declines worldwide and is responsible
for the extinction or severe decline of hundreds of species. While Bd itself is
not endangered — it is a highly successful invasive pathogen — the crisis it
caused highlights how little we know about chytrid diversity as a whole. The
Chytridiomycota is one of the earliest-diverging fungal lineages and contains
numerous species important in aquatic and soil ecosystems, yet the vast majority
remain undescribed.

Co-extinction is not limited to chytrids. Lichen-forming fungi are among the most
threatened groups due to sensitivity to air pollution and habitat disturbance. Many
wood-inhabiting fungi depend on specific declining tree species. Fungi associated
with rare plants, declining pollinators, or threatened mammals face similar risks.
Because these interdependencies are poorly documented, co-extinction events
frequently occur without any record of the species that were lost.

## Fungal Biodiversity and Ecosystem Function

The relationship between fungal biodiversity and ecosystem function is central to
both ecology and conservation. Decades of research establish that species diversity
in fungal communities supports ecosystem functioning through several mechanisms.
Functional complementarity occurs when different fungal species perform different
roles — some decompose cellulose, others lignin, still others fix nitrogen or
solubilize phosphorus — so that diverse communities process substrates more
efficiently than any single species.

Functional redundancy provides insurance: if one species is lost, others with
overlapping roles can partially compensate. The insurance hypothesis posits that
biodiversity buffers ecosystems against disturbance by ensuring functional roles
are maintained even as composition shifts. This relates to the portfolio effect
from financial theory, where diverse communities are more stable because declines
in some species are offset by increases in others.

The practical implications are clear. Losing fungal diversity degrades ecosystem
services humans depend upon. Reduced decomposer diversity slows organic matter
breakdown, impairing nutrient cycling. Declines in mycorrhizal diversity reduce
plant productivity, compromising both natural vegetation and crops. The Chernobyl
Exclusion Zone provides a dramatic illustration: radiation reduced fungal
diversity, favoring melanized radiation-resistant species, and the simplified
community proved less effective at decomposition and nutrient cycling than the
pre-disaster assemblage. Analogous processes occur worldwide as pollution, habitat
fragmentation, and climate change simplify fungal communities across all ecosystem
types.

## Biodiversity Across Ecosystems

Fungal diversity varies enormously across ecosystem types, each harboring unique
assemblages adapted to local conditions. Tropical forests are the most diverse
terrestrial ecosystems for fungi, with warm temperatures, high humidity, and
enormous plant diversity supporting an extraordinary variety of fungal lifestyles.
Tropical fungal diversity remains the least documented, and rapid deforestation
means species are likely being lost before description.

Temperate and boreal forests are dominated by ectomycorrhizal fungi associated
with oaks, pines, spruces, and birches. Old-growth boreal forests, with massive
accumulations of dead wood, are particularly important reservoirs of wood-inhabiting
fungal species threatened by forestry practices. Grasslands are dominated by
arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) critical for productivity and resilience, and
conversion of native grasslands to cropland has caused significant AMF losses.

Alpine and Arctic ecosystems host specialized communities adapted to cold, short
growing seasons, and nutrient-poor conditions. Many Arctic fungi are associated
with permafrost soils or specific tundra plants, making them vulnerable to
permafrost thaw and warming-driven shrub encroachment. Mangrove forests, coastal
marine environments, deep-sea habitats, deserts, caves, and built environments
each support distinct fungal communities requiring tailored conservation approaches.
Marine fungi play important roles in decomposing coastal and deep-sea detritus,
while desert fungi include remarkable species adapted to extreme desiccation and
temperature fluctuations.

## Conservation Strategies

### Habitat Protection and Forest Management

The most effective conservation strategy for fungi is protecting their habitats.
Because most fungi cannot be conserved ex situ, preserving the ecosystems in which
they naturally occur is paramount. Old-growth forests, wetlands, native grasslands,
and other undisturbed habitats should be prioritized. Within managed forests,
retaining dead wood across all decay classes and diameter categories is critical —
standing dead trees, fallen logs, branches, and stumps each support different
fungal communities. Maintaining diverse age classes of trees ensures that fungal
communities associated with all successional stages, from young stands to
old-growth, are represented.

### Monitoring, Citizen Science, and DNA Barcoding

Effective conservation requires data. Long-term surveys employing standardized
methods, permanent plots, and consistent taxonomic frameworks can detect trends in
fungal diversity over time. Citizen science has emerged as a powerful complement:
platforms such as iNaturalist enable amateur mycologists to submit occurrence data
at scales impossible for professionals alone. Organized fungal forays with proper
voucher collection and expert verification can produce valuable distributional and
phenological data, though many species cannot be identified from photographs.

DNA barcoding using the ITS region accelerates species identification. Reference
libraries such as UNITE link DNA barcodes to named species, enabling more accurate
biodiversity assessments. Barcoding also facilitates detection of cryptic species
that are morphologically similar but genetically distinct, often with different
ecological requirements and conservation statuses. Integrating barcoding into field
surveys, monitoring programs, and herbarium curation enhances conservation capacity.

### Protected Species Designations and Integrative Taxonomy

Several countries have established legal protections for threatened fungi.
Scandinavian nations — Sweden, Finland, and Norway — have been leaders, developing
national red lists and affording legal protection including restrictions on
collecting and habitat disturbance. These designations raise awareness, provide
legal basis for habitat protection, and incentivize targeted conservation action.

Modern fungal conservation requires integrative taxonomy combining morphological,
molecular, ecological, and distributional data. By linking DNA sequences to
vouchered specimens, documented ecological associations, and geographic
distributions, integrative taxonomy bridges the gap between molecular discovery
and formal conservation assessment. This approach demands collaboration among field
mycologists, molecular biologists, ecologists, and bioinformaticians — a model
that funding agencies and institutions should actively support.

## International Policy and Legal Frameworks

Despite their ecological importance, fungi remain conspicuously absent from most
international biodiversity policy frameworks. The Convention on Biological
Diversity (CBD) has historically focused on plants and animals, with fungi
receiving little explicit attention. National biodiversity strategies and action
plans frequently omit fungi entirely or treat them as a minor component.

The IUCN, while increasingly engaged through its Fungal Conservation Committee
and the Global Fungal Red List Initiative, faces structural challenges given the
limitations of standard assessment criteria for fungi. The Kunming-Montreal Global
Biodiversity Framework (2022) represents a potential turning point: its targets
for reducing biodiversity loss, expanding protected areas, and restoring degraded
ecosystems are broadly applicable to fungi, and some language explicitly
acknowledges all kingdoms of life. However, translating these commitments into
specific fungal conservation actions will require dedicated effort.

National red lists for fungi exist in a growing number of countries, with
Scandinavian nations, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Japan among the
leaders. These provide valuable models for fungal threat assessment and demonstrate
that meaningful conservation prioritization is possible even with incomplete data.
International coordination through shared methodologies and data platforms could
greatly accelerate global fungal conservation.

## The Economic Argument for Conservation

Beyond the ecological imperative, there is a strong economic case for conserving
fungal diversity. Fungi are an extraordinary source of bioactive compounds.
Fungal-derived drugs — including penicillin, cyclosporine, statins, and anticancer
compounds — generate over $100 billion in annual pharmaceutical revenue. Every
undescribed species represents a potential source of novel enzymes, pharmaceuticals,
antimicrobials, or other valuable compounds.

The ecosystem services provided by fungal communities are also economically
significant. Mycorrhizal fungi enhance plant nutrient uptake, reducing synthetic
fertilizer needs. Decomposer fungi maintain soil fertility and crop productivity.
Fungal pathogens regulate invasive plants and insect pests, providing natural
biocontrol. Collectively, these services represent billions of dollars in annual
benefits. The cost of losing undiscovered fungal compounds is incalculable — a
species that goes extinct before study may have contained a molecule that could
have treated disease, improved crop yields, or degraded a pollutant.

## The Path Forward

Addressing the fungal biodiversity crisis requires action on multiple fronts.
First, investment in field exploration and taxonomic infrastructure is essential.
Without a dramatic increase in the rate of species description, the conservation
status of the vast majority of fungal species will remain unknown. This means
training more fungal taxonomists, funding herbarium and culture collection
curators, and supporting fieldwork in understudied regions, particularly the
tropics.

Second, molecular tools must be fully integrated with classical taxonomy. DNA
sequencing, metagenomics, and bioinformatics can accelerate species discovery and
provide ecological insights impossible through morphology alone, but these advances
are only valuable when linked to vouchered specimens and formal taxonomic
frameworks. Integrative taxonomy should become the standard.

Third, citizen science must be scaled and professionalized. With appropriate
training, quality control, and data management, millions of fungal observations
generated by amateur naturalists can contribute meaningfully to conservation
monitoring. Fourth, conservation policies at all levels must explicitly account
for the fungal dimension of biodiversity. Protected area designations,
environmental impact assessments, and biodiversity targets should encompass all
kingdoms of life. The Scandinavian model of national fungal red lists should be
adopted more widely.

Fifth, institutional infrastructure must be strengthened. Botanical gardens,
herbaria, and culture collections serve as repositories of fungal diversity
essential for taxonomic research, education, and conservation. Many are
underfunded, and their fungal holdings are often neglected relative to plant
collections. Finally, public awareness of fungal biodiversity and its importance
must be raised. The tools and knowledge needed to conserve fungal diversity exist
or are within reach. What has been lacking is the political will, institutional
commitment, and public engagement to deploy them at the necessary scale. The time
to act is now, before more of this extraordinary kingdom is lost.
