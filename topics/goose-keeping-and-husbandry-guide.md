# Goose Keeping and Husbandry Guide

Geese are among the most self-sufficient and productive livestock a
homesteader or permaculturist can raise. They convert grass and weeds
into meat, eggs, feathers, and [[composting-science-solomon|fertilizer]] with minimal supplemental feed.

Unlike chickens, geese are primarily [[duck-keeping|grazers]]. A flock on good pasture
can meet nearly all its nutritional needs from [[pasture-management|forage]] alone. This makes
them extraordinarily economical to keep — often costing a fraction of
what grain-fed poultry requires.

Domestic geese (Anser anser domesticus) descend from the greylag goose
of Europe and Asia. Some breeds also carry ancestry from the swan goose
(Anser cygnoides) of East Asia. Humans have kept geese for thousands of
years, valued for their vigilance, their down, and their rich meat.

## Botanical and Biological Overview

All domestic geese belong to the family Anatidae, the same family that
includes ducks and swans. They are large-bodied waterfowl with long
necks, strong bills adapted for grazing, and webbed feet.

Geese possess a remarkable digestive system designed for processing
fibrous plant material. Their cecum — a pouch at the junction of the
small and large intestines — ferments cellulose through microbial
action. This allows them to extract nutrients from grasses that many
other poultry cannot efficiently digest.

Their bill is serrated, functioning like a living pair of pruning
shears. Geese grasp plants near the base and tear them with a sharp
sideways head motion. This grazing technique preferentially removes
leafy growth while leaving roots intact.

Adult geese weigh between 10 and 26 pounds depending on breed, with
males (ganders) typically larger than females. Their lifespan can exceed
20 years in protected settings, though productive breeding life is
usually 8 to 12 years.

## History and Cultural Significance

Goose domestication stretches back at least 5,000 years. Archaeological
evidence from Egypt, Mesopotamia, and the Roman Empire reveals geese
kept for meat, fat, and watchdog duties. The Romans famously force-fed
geese to produce an early form of foie gras.

In medieval Europe, goose fat was a primary cooking oil and the birds
served as property guardians. Their loud honking alerted households to
intruders — a role that predated the domestic dog in many rural areas.

The Christmas goose tradition originated in England, where tenant
farmers paid rent to landowners with fat autumn geese. Charles Dickens
immortalized this practice in "A Christmas Carol," cementing the goose
as a symbol of holiday feasting.

Native American peoples hunted wild geese extensively, and several
tribes managed wetland habitats to attract migrating flocks. The
domestic goose arrived in the Americas with European colonists.

## Breeds and Selection

Choosing the right breed depends on your primary goal: meat, eggs,
weed control, ornamental value, or a combination. The following table
summarizes the most common domestic breeds.

| Breed | Mature Weight | Eggs/Year | Temperament | Best Use |
|-------|--------------|-----------|-------------|----------|
| Embden | 20–26 lbs | 30–40 | Calm, hardy | Meat |
| Toulouse | 20–26 lbs | 25–35 | Docile, heavy | Meat, foie gras |
| African | 18–22 lbs | 25–40 | Vocal, alert | Watchdog, meat |
| Chinese | 10–12 lbs | 40–80 | Active, loud | Eggs, weeding |
| Pilgrim | 12–16 lbs | 40–60 | Docile, auto-sexing | Dual purpose |
| Buff | 16–18 lbs | 30–50 | Calm, good mothers | Dual purpose |
| Sebastopol | 10–14 lbs | 25–40 | Gentle, ornamental | Feathers, display |
| Roman Tufted | 10–12 lbs | 25–35 | Small, active | Weeding, small farms |
| Pomeranian | 12–16 lbs | 30–50 | Hardy, tufted | Dual purpose |

For cold climates, Embden and Toulouse are the hardiest choices. For
small homesteads, the Pilgrim is ideal because chicks can be sexed by
color at hatching — males are white, females are gray.

For egg production, the Chinese goose is unmatched, laying up to 80
eggs per year. For purely ornamental purposes, the Sebastopol's curly
feathers make it a living sculpture in the barnyard.

## Housing and Shelter

Geese are remarkably low-maintenance when it comes to housing. A simple
three-sided shelter provides adequate protection from wind, rain, and
predators in most climates.

Allow a minimum of 4 square feet of indoor space per bird. Outdoor runs
should provide 10 to 15 square feet per bird, though geese on open
pasture are happiest and healthiest with much more room.

Bedding should be 4 to 6 inches of straw or wood shavings. Geese sleep
on the ground — they do not use perches. Nest boxes should be roughly
2 feet square, with one box for every 2 to 3 geese, lined with clean
straw.

Keep the shelter dry above all else. Geese tolerate cold well but suffer
in damp, poorly ventilated conditions. Ensure adequate airflow without
creating drafts at ground level.

The shelter must be predator-proof at night. Coyotes, foxes, raccoons,
and stray dogs are the primary threats. Hardware cloth over windows and
a solid latching door are essential.

## Fencing and Predator Protection

A fence 3 to 4 feet high adequately contains most domestic geese because
they are heavy and poor fliers. Woven wire, chain link, or electric
poultry netting all work well.

Electric poultry netting is particularly effective for rotational
grazing because it can be moved easily. Geese learn to respect it
quickly after one or two shocks.

For goslings, additional protection is critical. Young geese are
vulnerable to hawks, owls, cats, and even large rats. Cover brooders
and young-bird runs with bird netting or wire mesh.

During the breeding season, ganders become territorial and will
aggressively defend their mates against perceived threats — including
humans. This protective instinct is valuable for flock security but
requires awareness during daily chores.

## Water Requirements

Geese need water deep enough to submerge their entire head. This is
essential for cleaning their nares (nostrils) after feeding. A simple
bucket or trough works for this purpose.

For swimming, geese appreciate but do not strictly require a pond. A
child's wading pool serves small flocks well. The key rule is no more
than 6 geese per acre of water surface to prevent contamination.
