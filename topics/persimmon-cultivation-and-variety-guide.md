# Persimmon Cultivation and Variety Guide

Persimmons (genus *Diospyros*) are among the most underappreciated fruit trees for permaculture and home orchards. They produce abundant, sweet, nutrient-dense fruit with minimal care, resist most pests and diseases, and thrive across a wide range of climates. The two species most relevant to growers in temperate regions are the Asian persimmon (*Diospyros kaki*) and the American persimmon (*D. virginiana*). A third species, the date plum (*D. lotus*), serves primarily as a rootstock.

## Botanical Overview

Persimmons belong to the family Ebenaceae, which also includes the ebony tree. They are deciduous trees with glossy, dark green leaves that turn spectacular shades of orange, red, and purple in autumn — making them dual-purpose ornamental and food-producing trees.

- **Asian persimmon** (*D. kaki*): Native to China, Japan, and Korea. Trees reach 15–30 feet at maturity. Fruit is large (2–4 inches), ranging from deep orange to red. Hardy to approximately USDA Zone 7 (0°F / -18°C), though some varieties survive in Zone 6 with protection.
- **American persimmon** (*D. virginiana*): Native to the eastern United States. Trees reach 30–60 feet in the wild but are smaller (15–25 feet) in cultivation. Fruit is smaller (1–2 inches) and highly astringent until fully ripe. Extremely cold-hardy, surviving to USDA Zone 4 (-25°F / -32°C).
- **Hybrid persimmons** (D. kaki × D. virginiana): Combines the fruit quality of Asian types with the cold hardiness of American types. The most widely available hybrid is 'Rosseyanka'.

## Astringent vs. Non-Astringent Varieties

The single most important distinction among persimmon varieties is astringency, determined by the presence of soluble tannins in the fruit.

### Non-Astringent (NA) Varieties

Non-astringent persimmons lose their tannins as they develop on the tree and can be eaten while still firm, like an apple. They are sometimes called "Fuyu-type" persimmons.

- **Fuyu** (also called 'Jiro'): The most widely grown non-astringent variety worldwide. Flattened, tomato-shaped fruit with sweet, crisp flesh. Reliable producer, self-fertile. Ripens October–November.
- **Giant Fuyu**: Larger version of standard Fuyu with similar characteristics. Fruit can reach 5+ inches in diameter.
- **Izu**: Smaller tree (10–12 feet), making it ideal for small spaces and container growing. Fruit resembles Fuyu but ripens 2–3 weeks earlier. Good choice for marginal climates.
- **Maru** ('Coffee Cake' or 'Nikita's Gift'): Unique non-astringent variety that develops cinnamon-colored internal streaks when pollinated, giving a spicy, complex flavor. Requires a pollinator.
- **Jiro**: Similar to Fuyu but with slightly earlier ripening. The tree is somewhat more upright in growth habit.
- **Maekawa Jiro**: A bud sport of Jiro with more consistent fruit shape and earlier bearing age.
- **Oku Gosho**: Premium Japanese variety with very large, flattened fruit. Excellent sweet flavor. Less widely available in the West.

### Astringent (A) Varieties

Astringent persimmons contain high levels of soluble tannins and are mouth-puckeringly bitter when firm. They become sweet and delicious only when fully soft — "like a sack of jelly," as the old saying goes. They are sometimes called "Hachiya-type" persimmons.

- **Hachiya**: The classic astringent persimmon. Large, acorn-shaped, deep orange fruit. Outstanding flavor when fully ripe. Excellent for drying (hoshigaki) and baking. Vigorous, upright tree.
- **Tamopan**: Large, oblate fruit with a distinctive constriction near the middle. Very productive and ornamental.
- **Saijo**: Small to medium elongated fruit with exceptional sweetness. The name means "the very best one" in Japanese. Considered one of the finest for fresh eating when fully ripe.
- **Chocolate** (also called 'Maru' — not to be confused with the NA Maru): Astringent when seedless, but develops a chocolate-brown flesh and rich flavor when pollinated. Complex and unique.
- **Sheng**: Large, conical fruit with excellent flavor. Very productive and cold-hardy for an Asian variety.
- **Giombo**: Elongated, light orange fruit. One of the earliest ripening astringent varieties. Very cold-hardy.
- **Ormond**: Large, beautiful fruit. Good for drying. Somewhat less cold-hardy than other Asian varieties.
- **Great Wall**: Very cold-hardy Asian variety, reportedly surviving in Zone 6. Medium-sized, orange-red fruit.

### American Persimmon Varieties

American persimmons are generally smaller and more astringent than Asian types, but selected cultivars offer superior fruit quality:

- **Meader**: Named after plant breeder Elwyn Meader. One of the best American persimmon varieties. Medium-large fruit (1.5–2 inches), good sweetness when fully ripe. Self-fertile, relatively early bearing. Hardy to Zone 4.
- **Prok**: Selected for large fruit size — up to 2.5 inches. Excellent flavor. Productive. Needs a pollinator (American persimmons are generally dioecious).
- **Early Golden**: One of the earliest ripening American varieties (September). Medium-sized fruit. Good for northern growers with short seasons.
- **John Rick**: Very large fruit for an American persimmon. Late ripening (November). Male tree — excellent pollinator.
- **Szukis**: Female tree that is partially self-fertile (unusual for American persimmons). Large, flavorful fruit.
- **Yates**: Very large fruit. Late ripening. Excellent flavor. Requires a male pollinator.
- **Deer Magnet**: As the name suggests, this variety is prolific and attractive to wildlife. Small fruit but incredible productivity. Useful for food plots and permaculture livestock integration.
- **100-42**: A numbered selection from the Kentucky State University breeding program. Excellent fruit quality, reliable.

### Hybrid Varieties

- **Rosseyanka** (D. kaki × D. virginiana): The best-known hybrid. Produces medium-large, attractive orange fruit that is astringent until fully soft, then develops exceptional sweetness with complex caramel and spice notes. Hardy to at least Zone 6, possibly Zone 5 with protection. Self-fertile. Vigorous grower.
- **Nikita's Gift**: Another kaki × virginiana hybrid with large, sweet fruit. Cold-hardy. Pollination from Asian varieties improves fruit quality.

## Pollination Requirements

Understanding pollination is critical for persimmon success:

**Asian persimmons**: Most Asian varieties are female and can produce seedless fruit parthenocarpically (without pollination). However, pollinated fruit tends to be larger, with better flavor and — for some varieties like Maru and Chocolate — distinctly different internal coloration and flavor. A few Asian varieties (notably 'Gailey' and some male selections) are male or perfect-flowered. Planting a pollinator variety is recommended but not strictly necessary for most Asian types.

**American persimmons**: Most American persimmons are dioecious — individual trees are either male or female. You need at least one male tree for every 8–10 female trees to ensure good pollination. Some varieties like 'Meader' and 'Szukis' can produce fruit without a male, but yields improve with cross-pollination. A single male tree can serve a large planting.

**Hybrid persimmons**: Most hybrids are self-fertile but benefit from cross-pollination with either parent species.

**Key takeaway**: If you're planting only one Asian persimmon, choose a self-fertile variety like Fuyu, Izu, or Hachiya. If planting American persimmons, include at least one male tree in your planting.

## Site Selection and Planting

### Climate Requirements

- **Asian persimmons**: Best in USDA Zones 7–10. They need a growing season of at least 150–200 frost-free days. In cooler zones, choose cold-hardy varieties and plant in a warm microclimate (south-facing slope, near a thermal mass wall, or in urban heat islands). Winter chill requirements are relatively low (100–200 hours below 45°F), making them suitable for mild winter climates where other fruit trees struggle.
- **American persimmons**: Extremely adaptable, thriving in Zones 4–9. They handle both extreme cold and hot, humid summers. Native from Connecticut to Florida and west to Iowa and Texas.
- **Hybrids**: Generally Zone 5–8, bridging the gap between the two species.

### Soil Requirements

Persimmons are remarkably tolerant of poor soils but perform best in:

- **pH**: 6.0–7.5 (slightly acidic to neutral)
- **Texture**: Well-drained loam is ideal, but they tolerate sandy and clay soils
- **Drainage**: Critical — persimmons hate waterlogged soils and are susceptible to root rot in persistently wet conditions. Avoid planting in low-lying areas or heavy clay that doesn't drain.
- **Fertility**: They are not heavy feeders. Excessive nitrogen produces vegetative growth at the expense of fruit.

### Sun and Space

- **Sun**: Full sun (6+ hours direct sunlight) for best fruit production. Tolerates partial shade but will produce less fruit.
- **Spacing**: 12–20 feet between Asian persimmon trees, 20–30 feet for American types (they can get large). In permaculture food forests, they serve well in the [[canopy-layer-design-food-forests]] or as tall understory trees.

### Wind Protection

Young trees are vulnerable to wind damage. Provide staking for the first 1–2 years. Mature trees develop strong wood.

### Planting Procedure

1. **Timing**: Plant bare-root trees in late winter or early spring (while dormant). Container-grown trees can be planted in spring or early fall.
2. **Hole preparation**: Dig a hole twice as wide as the root system but no deeper than the root flare. The root flare (where trunk meets roots) must remain at or slightly above soil level.
3. **Soil amendment**: Mix in compost or well-rotted manure at planting, but avoid concentrated fertilizers that can burn young roots. In poor soils, add rock phosphate for phosphorus.
4. **Watering**: Water thoroughly at planting and provide consistent moisture for the first year. Once established, persimmons are remarkably [[drought-tolerant-planting-design]].
5. **Mulching**: Apply 3–4 inches of organic mulch (wood chips, leaves, straw) in a wide ring around the tree, keeping mulch away from the trunk. This retains moisture and suppresses weeds.
6. **Staking**: Stake young trees for the first 1–2 years to protect against wind.

## Care and Maintenance

### Watering

Established persimmon trees are among the most drought-tolerant fruit trees. During the first growing season, water deeply once per week during dry periods. After establishment, supplemental irrigation is only needed during prolonged drought or when fruit is developing. Avoid frequent shallow watering, which promotes shallow roots.

### Fertilization

Persimmons have modest nutritional needs:

- **Year 1**: No fertilizer at planting. Apply a balanced [[organic-fertilizer-sources-guide]] (e.g., 5-5-5) in early spring of the second year, about 1/4 cup scattered in a wide ring around the tree.
- **Mature trees**: Apply a balanced [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof-recipe-solomon]] in early spring as buds swell. A mature tree needs roughly 1–2 lbs of nitrogen per year (adjust based on growth and fruiting). Too much nitrogen produces excessive vegetative growth, delays fruiting, and reduces fruit quality.
- **Compost**: An annual top-dressing of 1–2 inches of well-composted material under the drip line provides slow-release nutrition and improves [[soil-structure-and-aggregate-ecology]].
- **Micronutrients**: Persimmons occasionally show boron deficiency (internal fruit breakdown) and zinc deficiency (small leaves, reduced fruit set). Foliar sprays of micronutrients can address these if symptoms appear.

### Pruning

Persimmons require less pruning than most fruit trees:

- **Training young trees**: Establish a strong [[comparison-central-leader-vs-open-center-fruit-tree-training]] or modified central leader system. Select 3–5 well-spaced scaffold branches starting at 2–3 feet above ground. American persimmons tend to develop a central leader naturally; Asian types may need more guidance.
- **Mature tree pruning**: Prune during dormancy (late winter). Remove dead, diseased, or crossing branches. Thin the canopy to allow light penetration and air circulation. Persimmons bear fruit on current season's growth, so moderate pruning stimulates new fruiting wood.
- **Thinning fruit**: Persimmons sometimes set very heavy crops. Thin fruit to one per shoot when they are about 1 inch in diameter. This improves fruit size, prevents branch breakage, and reduces alternate bearing.
- **Height management**: Persimmons can be kept at 10–12 feet through summer pruning (tip pruning new growth in midsummer) for easier harvest. This is especially useful for Asian persimmons in food forest settings.
