## Cannabis Breeding and Genetics

Cannabis breeding is the deliberate crossing of plants to combine desirable
traits into new varieties. It sits at the intersection of classical Mendelian
genetics, practical horticulture, and modern molecular biology.

The species *Cannabis sativa* L. harbors extraordinary genetic diversity.
Centuries of natural and human selection produced distinct [[cannabis-landrace-and-heirloom-varieties|landrace varieties]]
from Southeast Asia to the Hindu Kush to equatorial Africa.

Modern breeding draws on this diversity to create cultivars with specific
cannabinoid profiles, terpene expressions, flowering times, and disease
resistance. Understanding the genetics behind these traits is essential
for any serious breeding program.

## Taxonomic Varieties of Cannabis

Three major subspecies are recognized within *Cannabis sativa* L.

**Cannabis sativa** grows tall, two to five meters, with long internodes
and narrow leaflets. Originating in equatorial regions, it flowers over
eight to sixteen weeks and is traditionally described as cerebral.

**Cannabis indica** is shorter, one to two meters, bushy with wide
leaflets. It originated in Central Asian highlands like the Hindu Kush
and flowers in six to nine weeks. Traditionally described as sedating.

**Cannabis ruderalis** is small, half a meter to one meter, and
auto-flowering. It evolved in Central and Eastern Europe and Russia,
where short summers forced flowering by age rather than photoperiod.

Modern molecular studies show extensive admixture between these groups.
The commercial sativa/indica labels often fail to predict actual
genetic ancestry or chemical composition.

## Sex Determination and Reproduction

Cannabis is normally dioecious, with separate male and female plants.
Sex is controlled by an X/Y chromosome system — males are XY, females XX.

Female plants produce the cannabinoid-rich flowers valued by growers.
Males produce pollen sacs and are essential for breeding but unwanted in
sinsemilla (seedless flower) production.

Under stress, females can develop male flowers through hermaphroditism.
This genetically influenced response is generally undesirable but is
sometimes deliberately induced to produce feminized pollen.

Controlled breeding requires isolation to prevent unwanted cross-pollination.
Cannabis is wind-pollinated and highly promiscuous — a single stray pollen
grain can compromise an entire breeding generation.

## Genotype versus Phenotype

The genotype is the complete genetic code of the plant, its DNA blueprint.
It determines what the plant *can* become — its potential for cannabinoid
production, growth pattern, and disease resistance.

The [[cannabis-phenotype-and-genotype-guide|phenotype]] is the observable expression of the genotype as shaped by
environment. Two genetically identical clones grown under different
conditions can produce markedly different results.

A single cross can produce plants with dramatically different phenotypes.
This is why clones are preferred for consistent commercial production,
while seeds are necessary for breeding programs.

Understanding this distinction is foundational. Breeders select based on
phenotype but must understand the underlying genotype to predict outcomes
in subsequent generations.

## Chemotype Classification

Traditional sativa/indica taxonomy fails to predict chemical composition.
Molecular studies by Sawler et al. in 2015 demonstrated extensive genetic
admixture between commercially labeled varieties.

Modern chemotaxonomy classifies cannabis by its chemical constituents.
The primary framework uses cannabinoid ratios, specifically THC to CBD.

**Type I** is THC-dominant, with minimal CBD. This is the most common
[[cannabis-chemotype-classification|chemotype]] in recreational markets worldwide.

**Type II** has intermediate THC and CBD levels, often valued for medical
applications seeking balanced effects.

**Type III** is CBD-dominant with minimal THC, used for therapeutic
purposes without significant psychoactive effects.

A multidimensional approach expands this to include dominant terpene
groups, minor cannabinoids like CBG and THCV, and flavonoid profiles.
This provides far more reliable prediction of effects than folk taxonomy.
