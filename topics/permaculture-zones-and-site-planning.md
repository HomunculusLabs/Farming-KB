# Permaculture Zones and Site Planning

Permaculture zones and site planning are methods for arranging the parts of a
homestead, garden, farm, or settlement according to patterns of human attention,
external energies, and landscape form. The method is most often associated with
Bill Mollison and David Holmgren, who developed zones and sectors as practical
spatial tools within permaculture design. Zones describe the intensity and
frequency of human use, while sectors describe incoming forces such as sun,
wind, fire, water, noise, wildlife, and views.

The zone system is commonly represented as a set of rings around a house, but
its practical use is less geometric than relational. A lettuce bed, compost bin,
seedling bench, or rabbit hutch belongs near the household because it is visited
frequently. A woodlot, dam, pasture, or wilderness remnant may lie farther away
because it requires less frequent intervention. The basic proposition is that
landscape elements should be placed where people will actually tend them.

Sector analysis complements zoning by asking what energies enter a site from
outside its human center. A sunny slope, cold winter wind, wildfire path, flood
plain, attractive view, or noisy road can affect design more strongly than
simple distance from the house. The designer maps these forces, then chooses
whether to admit, block, store, deflect, or amplify them. Zones are therefore
about the outward reach of human work, while sectors are about the inward flow
of environmental and social energies.

In mature permaculture practice, zone and sector planning is rarely used alone.
It is combined with slope analysis, water harvesting, access planning, soil
survey, climate interpretation, element analysis, and long-term staging. Its
value lies in reducing wasteful movement and misplaced infrastructure while
making productive relationships obvious. The method is intentionally simple, but
its implications extend from a kitchen garden to a farm, village, or bioregion.

## Core Principles of Zone Planning

The first principle of zone planning is frequency of use. Elements that need
frequent harvesting, feeding, watering, observation, or maintenance are placed
where they can be reached easily. Herbs, salad greens, seedlings, worm bins,
small animal housing, and tools are therefore located close to the kitchen door
or along routes already walked each day.

The second principle is attention matching. A plant or animal should be placed
where the expected level of care is likely to occur. Daily-care elements fail
when placed at the edge of a paddock, while low-care elements become nuisances
when they occupy the most valuable space near the house. Zone planning is a way
of making human behavior part of the design rather than treating it as an
exception to design.

The third principle is efficient movement. A path walked twice a day becomes a
large energy cost over a year. Conversely, a well-placed compost bin, greenhouse,
watering point, or tool shed can save thousands of unnecessary steps. Mollison's
teaching often stresses that small daily efficiencies compound more strongly
than occasional heroic labor.

The fourth principle is progressive development. Holmgren's phrase "starting at
the back door" summarizes the warning against overreach. A small, well-tended
Zone 1 can produce more food than a large neglected paddock. Permaculture site
planning therefore usually begins with the immediate household territory, then
moves outward as systems stabilize and skills accumulate.

The fifth principle is relative location. Zones do not merely place elements by
distance; they also help designers ask what should be next to what. Chickens may
belong near the orchard because they eat fallen fruit and pests, but near enough
to the house that feeding and egg collection are easy. Compost may sit at the
Zone 1 and Zone 2 boundary, where kitchen scraps move outward and finished
fertility moves back inward.

## Zone 0: The Dwelling and Household Center

Zone 0 is the house, dwelling, or principal center of human activity. In some
designs it may be a communal kitchen, workshop, village center, or seasonal
camp. Its importance comes from the concentration of human decisions, tools,
food preparation, heat, shelter, and daily routines. The other zones are planned
in relation to this center.

In permaculture writing, Zone 0 includes much more than architecture. It may
involve passive solar orientation, insulation, thermal mass, roofwater capture,
composting toilets, greywater routing, attached greenhouses, indoor propagation,
porches, and trellised vines. The house is treated as an active element in the
site rather than as an object separate from the landscape.

The edge between Zone 0 and Zone 1 is especially significant. A kitchen door
that opens directly to herbs, salad beds, compost, and water makes daily food
production practical. A poorly placed door, garage, fence, or driveway can make
the most productive garden inconvenient. Site planning often begins by studying
how people actually enter and leave the house.

## Zone 1: Intensive Garden and Daily Care

Zone 1 is the most intensively managed outdoor area. It normally contains the
kitchen garden, culinary herbs, salad greens, seedling areas, small ponds,
compost systems, frequently used tools, cold frames, worm farms, and small
animals requiring daily attention. In Mollison's course material, Zone 1 is the
place visited constantly, often no more than a short walk from the main door.

This zone is designed for convenience and high yield from a small area. Raised
beds, narrow beds, keyhole beds, trellises, vertical structures, herb spirals,
patio containers, cloches, and heavily mulched paths are common. The soil is
usually the most actively improved on the property, receiving compost, mulch,
water, and close observation.

Faires describes the Zone 1 layout as beginning with paths and access. Beds
that require frequent picking are kept narrow enough to reach from both sides.
Larger beds for crops harvested once or twice may move outward toward Zone 2.
Herb spirals, salad beds, and path-side vegetables make use of the space walked
through every day.

Small water features can be highly productive in Zone 1. Mollison noted that a
small pond near a path can yield watercress and provide habitat for frogs that
control slugs. Rain barrels, roofwater tanks, drip irrigation, greywater mulch
basins, and small swales reduce the need to carry water. Because Zone 1 receives
daily attention, leaks, pests, wilting, and nutrient deficiencies are noticed
quickly.

The practical test of Zone 1 is whether the household uses it without making a
special trip. If herbs are too far from the kitchen, they are used less. If the
compost bin is hidden at the back of the property, kitchen scraps may go to
waste. Zone 1 design succeeds when the ordinary act of cooking, walking, or
returning home becomes an act of tending the garden.

## Zone 2: Orchard, Food Forest, and Small Livestock

Zone 2 is a regularly visited but less intensive area. It often contains fruit
trees, berry shrubs, dwarf or semi-dwarf orchards, food forest plantings,
beehives, poultry, rabbits, ducks, larger compost heaps, mulch crops, and
seasonal vegetable beds. It is the bridge between the high-care kitchen garden
and the more extensive farm zones.

The food forest is a common Zone 2 pattern. It uses layered vegetation,
including canopy trees, smaller fruit trees, shrubs, herbaceous perennials,
ground covers, root crops, and vines. Mollison's account of Zone 2 stresses
that it supplies bulk household foods and biological support for the inner
zones. Nitrogen fixers, comfrey, insectary plants, poultry forage, and mulch
species are as important as the fruit crops themselves.

Small livestock often fit well in Zone 2 because they need regular but not
constant care. Chickens can range through orchards, eating fallen fruit and
pests while depositing manure. Ducks can control slugs and snails near ponds and
moist gardens. Rabbits, quail, or poultry housing may be placed at the edge of
Zone 1 so daily care is easy while forage access lies in Zone 2.

Water and fertility move strongly through this zone. Swales, contour paths,
greywater lines, and roofwater overflows may hydrate orchard trees. Composting
systems can be located where raw materials from the house and garden meet
carbon-rich materials from hedges, prunings, and animal bedding. The Zone 1 and
Zone 2 boundary is therefore often a working edge of nutrient cycling.

Zone 2 is also a place for gradual establishment. Young orchards need more
watering, mulching, pruning, and protection than mature food forests. As tree
canopies close, ground covers spread, and animal systems stabilize, the zone
should become less demanding. A mature Zone 2 is not unmanaged, but its daily
workload should be lower than that of Zone 1.

## Zone 3: Broadacre Production and Farm Infrastructure

Zone 3 is the main crop, pasture, and farm production zone. It is visited weekly,
monthly, or seasonally depending on the enterprise. Typical elements include
field crops, staple grains, larger orchards, nut groves, pastures, dams,
windbreaks, grazing animals, barns, firewood lots, and commercial plantings.
It is the scale at which hand gardening gives way to broader farm systems.

Mollison's description of Zone 3 does not treat broadacre farming as an excuse
for monoculture. The recommended patterns include alley cropping, silvopasture,
rotational grazing, strip cropping, mixed orchards, cover crops, perennial
staples, shelterbelts, and water storage. The aim is production that remains
biologically diverse and structurally stable.

Large livestock are usually placed in Zone 3 because they require land area,
fencing, water points, and periodic movement rather than constant handling.
Cattle, sheep, pigs, and goats may be managed through rotational grazing or
seasonal access to orchards and pastures. Laneways, gates, yards, and water
systems must be planned together so that animal movement does not damage soil or
water infrastructure.

Dams and larger water storages often belong in or above Zone 3. When placed high
in the landscape, they can distribute water by gravity to crops, animals, and
fire protection points. Spillways, catchments, access roads, and erosion control
must be considered before planting. In many designs, access and water are the
first permanent structures laid out.

Zone 3 is also a major site for windbreaks, shelterbelts, firewood, and timber.
Coppice woodlots can supply fuel on a rotation, while mixed timber belts provide
long-term construction material and wildlife habitat. These plantings frequently
serve multiple functions: sheltering livestock, slowing wind, marking paddocks,
providing nectar, cycling nutrients, and reducing erosion.

## Zone 4: Managed Forage, Woodlot, and Semi-Wild Land

Zone 4 is an extensive, low-input zone managed occasionally for forage, timber,
water catchment, grazing, hunting, gathering, and ecological buffering. It may
include managed woodlands, rangelands, nut groves, wild berry areas, bamboo,
wetlands, gullies, or seasonal grazing leases. It is neither an intensive farm
area nor untouched wilderness.

The main activity in Zone 4 is opportunistic harvest. People may gather nuts,
berries, mushrooms, wild greens, poles, firewood, seed, craft materials, and
animal fodder. Trails, gates, and simple access tracks are useful, but routine
irrigation, fertilizing, and cultivation are generally inappropriate. Nature
performs most of the management.

Selective forestry is a common Zone 4 practice. Coppicing, pollarding, single
tree selection, protection of veteran trees, retention of snags, and continuous
canopy cover can provide products while conserving habitat. In fire-prone
landscapes, Zone 4 may also contain fuel-reduced corridors, grazed buffers, and
strategic access for fire response.

Zone 4 often contributes genetic resources to the inner zones. Wild or locally
adapted plants observed thriving without care may provide seed, cuttings, or
breeding material for food forests, windbreaks, and restoration plantings. This
function is especially important under climate variability, where locally
adapted genetic diversity may be more valuable than uniform nursery stock.

As a transition zone, Zone 4 mediates between human production and Zone 5
wildness. It can connect wildlife corridors, protect watercourses, and reduce
sharp boundaries between cultivated land and habitat. The designer's task is to
harvest without simplifying the system beyond its capacity to regenerate.

## Zone 5: Wilderness, Reference Ecosystem, and Conservation

Zone 5 is the least managed part of a permaculture site. It is left to natural
processes as far as possible and functions as a reference ecosystem, wildlife
habitat, genetic reservoir, water catchment, and place for observation. Mollison
argued that every design should include some Zone 5, even if only a small
corner, pond edge, tree, or wild strip on an urban lot.

The importance of Zone 5 is not limited to biodiversity conservation. It is also
a teacher. By observing unmanaged succession, leaf litter, predator-prey
relations, water movement, fungal decomposition, and plant association, the
designer learns patterns that can be imitated in managed zones. Zone 5 provides
a baseline against which the health of the cultivated system can be judged.

In large rural properties, Zone 5 may include forest, riparian corridors,
wetlands, rocky slopes, steep gullies, or land that is difficult to farm. In
suburban contexts, it may be a thicket, native meadow, wildlife pond, unmown
edge, or deadwood habitat. Its value lies in setting aside space for non-human
processes rather than in meeting a fixed standard of pristine wilderness.

Management in Zone 5 is intentionally minimal. Possible exceptions include
removing invasive species that threaten the ecosystem, maintaining observation
paths, monitoring ecological change, or responding to outside threats such as
fire. Even these interventions should be restrained. If frequent harvesting,
grazing, planting, or pruning is required, the area is better understood as Zone
4 rather than Zone 5.

Zone 5 also benefits the rest of the property through ecological spillover.
Birds, bats, pollinators, amphibians, predatory insects, and soil organisms may
move from wild habitat into orchards and gardens. Water filtered through intact
vegetation is cleaner and more regular. The outer wild zone therefore supports
inner productivity by remaining largely beyond direct production.

## Sector Analysis and Incoming Energies

Sector analysis maps energies and influences entering the site from outside the
center of management. While zones are often drawn as rings or patches, sectors
are usually drawn as wedges, arrows, or corridors. They may cross all zones and
can override simple distance rules. A close site may be unsuitable for a
greenhouse if it is shaded, windy, or exposed to fire.

The sun sector records seasonal solar paths, shadows, reflected light, and heat
gain. It guides the placement of houses, greenhouses, solar panels, winter
vegetable beds, deciduous shade, and heat-loving crops. In temperate climates,
winter sun access is often more limiting than summer light, so the low winter
sun angle is a critical planning factor.

The wind sector identifies prevailing winds, cold winter winds, hot drying
winds, storm winds, and desirable cooling breezes. Windbreaks can slow damaging
winds, while openings can admit summer ventilation. Dense barriers can create
turbulence if badly designed, so shelterbelts are usually porous, layered, and
placed at a distance from what they protect.

The fire sector is especially important in dry forests, grasslands, shrublands,
and sloping terrain. Fire risk is shaped by wind direction, fuel type, slope,
access, and neighboring land use. Responses include fuel-reduced zones,
fire-resistant plantings, water storage, non-combustible building edges,
multiple escape routes, and careful placement of vulnerable structures.

Water sectors include surface runoff, drainage lines, flood paths, groundwater,
springs, roofwater, and erosion flows. Water is not merely a resource but a
moving force. Swales, ponds, diversion drains, keyline patterns, terraces,
spillways, riparian buffers, and infiltration basins all depend on correct
reading of water movement through the site.

Other sectors may include noise, dust, chemical drift, privacy, crime, wildlife
movement, beautiful views, ugly views, frost, fog, salt spray, and social
traffic. Hemenway summarizes three broad responses: block or screen an unwanted
force, channel or collect a useful force, or open the site to receive as much of
that force as possible.

## Slope, Access, and Water in Site Planning

Slope analysis is closely tied to zones and sectors because gravity affects
water, soil, nutrients, animals, vehicles, fire, and human movement. Holmgren
notes that even a gentle slope creates a downhill force that can either be used
or fought. Good site planning places heavy flows where gravity assists them and
protects soil where gravity could remove it.

Water storage is often most valuable when placed high in the landscape, provided
catchment, soil, and engineering conditions are suitable. A high dam, tank, or
spring box can feed lower gardens and troughs without pumping. Conversely, a
dam placed too low may store water but require energy to move it back uphill.
The same principle applies to compost, mulch, firewood, animal feed, and harvest
loads.

Access design is another primary layer. Roads, gates, paths, animal laneways,
wheelbarrow routes, and service tracks should be established before permanent
planting. Poor access can permanently increase labor, while too much access can
fragment habitat and compact soil. A good path pattern links the elements that
must be linked and avoids inviting unnecessary travel through sensitive areas.

Contour and keyline planning connect slope to water harvesting. Swales and
terraces slow runoff and increase infiltration; diversion drains move excess
water safely; contour paths can serve as access, water spreaders, and firebreaks
when properly designed. The placement of these features often determines the
shape of Zones 2 and 3 more than any abstract circle around the house.

Fire also follows slope. Flames usually move faster uphill, especially when
pushed by wind. Houses sited on ridges, in wind tunnels, or in fire funnels are
classic planning errors in Mollison's teaching. Site planning therefore begins
with safe house placement, reliable access, defensible space, and water before
ornamental or productive details are finalized.

## Mapping and Design Process

A practical zone and sector plan begins with a base map. The map records
boundaries, buildings, roads, paths, slopes, contours, existing vegetation,
watercourses, soil types, utilities, fences, views, neighboring land uses, and
legal constraints. Aerial photographs, survey plans, hand sketches, and digital
maps can all be useful, but direct observation remains essential.

The designer then marks the main center or centers of activity. A single-family
homestead may have one household center, while a farm may have a house, barn,
packing shed, market garden, and livestock yard that each function as partial
centers. Villages and eco-settlements may require multiple overlapping zone
systems rather than one simple pattern.

Next, elements are listed and classified by frequency of attention. Daily crops,
weekly crops, seasonal crops, animals, tools, water points, storage, processing,
firewood, wildlife habitat, and social spaces are sorted by how often they are
used. This step is more reliable than placing elements according to habit,
romance, or the appearance of a diagram.

Sector overlays are then added. Sun, wind, fire, water, views, privacy, wildlife
movement, noise, pollution, and frost are drawn on separate transparent layers
or digital equivalents. The strongest opportunities and hazards are identified.
Compound sectors, such as a direction that brings both hot wind and fire, are
especially important.

Bubble diagrams or rough concept plans follow. At this stage, shapes are loose
and relationships matter more than dimensions. The designer tests whether the
greenhouse is close enough to water and tools, whether animals can move without
crossing gardens, whether compost connects to kitchen and orchard, and whether
windbreaks shade productive winter areas.

Only after relationships are coherent does the plan become more formal. Paths,
roads, fences, tanks, earthworks, orchards, gardens, and buildings are drawn to
scale. Staging plans identify what must happen first. Faires emphasizes a common
implementation order: access, water, and power or energy systems before detailed
planting. This order reduces the risk of removing young plantings to install
infrastructure later.

## Urban, Suburban, Farm, and Community Scales

The zone system adapts to scale. On an urban balcony, Zone 1 may be pots,
seedlings, herbs, and worm compost. Zone 2 may be a courtyard fruit tree, a
community garden plot, or a shared compost area. Zone 5 may be a native planter,
a wild verge, or nearby public habitat observed rather than owned. The logic of
attention still applies even when the land area is tiny.

On suburban lots, the classic sequence often compresses. The whole property may
fall within what a farm designer would call Zone 1 or Zone 2, yet designers can
still distinguish daily herbs, weekly berry patches, seasonal fruit trees, and
unmanaged wildlife edges. Privacy, noise, runoff, pets, neighbors, and municipal
rules often become major sectors.

On farms, the zones may cover hectares. Zone 1 remains compact, while Zone 2 may
hold orchards, poultry, nurseries, and market garden infrastructure. Zone 3 may
include pastures, field crops, dams, and machinery sheds. Zone 4 may be grazing
woodland or rough forage. Zone 5 may be a conservation area, creek corridor, or
ridge forest.

At community scale, zones become multi-nodal. Each household, workshop, school,
market garden, and commons may have its own pattern of use. Holmgren's cellular
interpretation treats the household garden as a settlement cell that reaches an
efficient size, then reproduces through new households or gardens rather than
expanding indefinitely. Links between cells become as important as each cell's
internal layout.

Bioregional planning extends the idea further. Villages, farms, forests,
markets, water catchments, roads, and wild corridors can be understood as a
larger pattern of centers, flows, and sectors. In this context, zone planning is
not a rigid map but a language for asking where human care is effective and
where ecological autonomy should dominate.

## Common Errors and Limitations

A common error is treating zones as fixed concentric circles. Holmgren warns
that zones are never truly concentric in practice. Soil, slope, aspect, access,
fences, buildings, water, legal boundaries, and existing vegetation distort the
pattern. A Zone 1 path may extend like a finger toward a shed; a Zone 5 riparian
corridor may pass close to the house.

Another error is overreach. New landholders often plant distant orchards, large
gardens, or broad fields before the immediate house area functions. Without
water, paths, fertility, fencing, and daily attention, these plantings fail.
Starting small is not a lack of ambition; it is the mechanism by which a system
accumulates competence.

A third error is placing elements by category rather than need. Not all fruit
trees belong in the same zone. A dwarf lemon used daily may belong near the
kitchen, while a commercial nut orchard belongs in Zone 3. A small animal that
needs daily feeding belongs close; hardy grazing animals can be farther away.
The zone label follows the required relationship, not the name of the element.

Sector neglect is equally damaging. A greenhouse in Zone 1 can still fail if it
sits in winter shade or a wind tunnel. A house may be close to a garden but
unsafe if placed in a fire path. A beautiful orchard may be lost if cold air
settles there every spring. Zones must be corrected by the actual energies of
the site.

The framework also has limits. It does not by itself provide detailed knowledge
of soil chemistry, hydrology, engineering, horticulture, animal husbandry, law,
or social governance. It is a placement and thinking tool, not a substitute for
technical expertise. Its strength is that it organizes these other forms of
knowledge into a coherent spatial pattern.
