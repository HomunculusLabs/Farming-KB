# Cattle Husbandry and Permaculture Integration

Cattle are among the most productive and versatile [[holzer-livestock-integration|livestock]] available to
homesteaders, small farmers, and permaculture designers. They convert
pasture and browse that humans cannot digest into high-quality protein
in the form of meat and milk. Their manure distributes fertility across
the landscape. Their [[holistic-grazing-and-pasture-management|grazing]] stimulates root growth in grasses and
promotes pasture diversity.

Integrating cattle into permaculture systems requires careful management
to prevent overgrazing while maximising beneficial functions. When
properly designed, cattle systems produce food, improve soil, manage
vegetation, and generate income from land that might otherwise lie
fallow. This article covers breed selection, grazing management, health
care, housing, dairy production, finishing, and permaculture-specific
integration strategies drawn from Bill Mollison, Sepp Holzer, and
contemporary homesteading practice.

## Beef Cattle Breeds

British breeds tend to be smaller, hardier, and produce well-marbled
meat on grass alone. Angus is the most popular beef breed in North
America, known for excellent marbling, docile temperament, and good
mothering ability. Hereford cattle are red with a distinctive white
face, valued for hardiness and efficient foraging. Shorthorns serve as
true dual-purpose animals, producing both quality meat and adequate milk.

Continental breeds are larger and leaner, selected for rapid growth and
heavy muscling. Charolais cattle are large, white, and fast-growing,
often used as terminal sires in crossbreeding programs. Limousin cattle
produce lean, heavily muscled carcasses and convert feed efficiently.
Simmental cattle are large and dual-purpose, combining good maternal
traits with impressive growth rates.

Heritage and miniature breeds deserve special attention for small-acreage
permaculture systems. Dexter cattle weigh only 600 to 900 pounds, roughly
one-third the feed requirement of standard breeds. They produce both
meat and milk on modest acreage. Highland cattle are extremely
cold-hardy with long shaggy hair coats, thriving on rough terrain and
poor forage that would challenge other breeds.

Galloway and Belted Galloway cattle are Scottish breeds with distinctive
hair coats that shed rain. They produce excellent beef on grass alone
without grain supplementation. Devon cattle are an old dual-purpose
breed valued for hardiness and grass-finishing ability.

## Dairy and Dual-Purpose Breeds

For homesteads seeking milk production, several breeds offer strong
dual-purpose potential. Jersey cows are small in stature but produce
milk with 4 to 5 percent butterfat, ideal for butter and cheese making.
They maintain adequate beef quality in their steer offspring, making them
true dual-purpose animals.

Guernsey cows produce golden-coloured milk with high butterfat content.
They are moderate in size and known for docile temperaments. Dutch
Belted cattle are a rare breed that combines good dairy production with
solid grazing ability and reasonable beef yield from surplus animals.

A family cow of a dairy or dual-purpose breed can provide a gallon or
more of milk per milking, enough for family needs plus a small surplus
for neighbours or value-added products. With quality hay or grass alone,
a cow will continue to produce milk. When forage quality drops, milk
production drops correspondingly.

## Sepp Holzer's Approach to Cattle

Sepp Holzer keeps old and rare cattle breeds at the Krameterhof that
are suited to outdoor, year-round living on alpine pasture. His mixed
herd of approximately 50 animals roams a 25-hectare paddock and includes
European bison, American bison, yak, water buffalo, Scottish Highland
cattle, Hungarian steppe cattle, and Dahomey miniature cattle.

Holzer's priorities are propagating endangered breeds, producing
high-quality food, and keeping livestock happy rather than maximising
yield. All of his breeds are particularly hardy and undemanding, which
makes the daily work of keeping them very low. The diversity of species
in a single paddock creates a resilient, self-regulating system.

A key Holzer principle is that animals with access to lush, diverse
flora including poisonous mushrooms and medicinal plants stay healthy
without routine worming. His district veterinarian examined the livestock
for decades and confirmed the health benefits of this approach.

The critical rule is that animals must choose their own medicine. Never
mix herbs or medicinal plants into feed. Only the animals know what they
need. Holzer summarises this philosophy: food is also medicine, and it
must be varied, nutritious, and free from artificial additives.

Holzer strongly opposes dehorning. He considers it unbelievably painful
and observes that dehorned cattle behave in disturbed ways afterwards.
Without horns, cattle butt each other in the stomach, which can cause
premature or stillbirths in pregnant cows. He views dehorning, tail
docking, and ear cropping as nothing more than mutilation.

## rotational grazing Systems

Rotational grazing is the foundation of productive cattle management.
Properly managed, one cow-calf pair can be sustained on 2 to 5 acres
depending on climate and pasture quality. The goal is to graze paddocks
intensively for short periods, then allow full recovery before regrazing.

Move cattle every 1 to 7 days. Use faster rotations during spring
growth when grass is abundant, and slower rotations through summer and
fall when growth rates decline. Graze to a 4 to 6 inch residual height,
then rest the paddock until 8 to 12 inches of regrowth appears.

Rest periods vary dramatically by season. In spring, 21 to 45 days may
suffice. During summer drought, paddocks may need 45 to 90 or more
days of rest. Stocking density should start conservative and increase
gradually as you learn your land's carrying capacity.

Multi-species grazing improves pasture utilisation and reduces parasite
loads. Cattle prefer tall grasses, sheep graze closer to the ground,
and goats browse shrubs and woody plants. Combining species in a
rotational system creates more uniform use of available forage and
breaks parasite cycles naturally.

## Feeding and Nutrition

The ideal dairy pasture consists of lush clover and alfalfa mixed with
grasses and herbs such as lavender, mustard, rosemary, and sage. This
diversity provides balanced nutrition and reduces the need for mineral
supplementation. Winter hay should be tender green material cut just
after blooming, fed at 2 to 3 pounds per 100 pounds of body weight per
day.

A cow requires approximately two tons of hay over winter. Do not give
more than she can eat between milkings, or she will refuse the older
hay and waste feed. With good quality hay or grass alone, a cow will
maintain milk production. When quality drops, supplementation becomes
necessary.
