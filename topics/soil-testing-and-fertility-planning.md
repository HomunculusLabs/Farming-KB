# Soil Testing and Fertility Planning

Soil testing is the practice of translating a living, mineral, and
chemical system into numbers that can guide fertility decisions. A
laboratory report cannot explain everything about a soil, but it can
reveal pH, nutrient levels, [[fukuoka-textdoc-fertilizer-acidification-trace-element-deficiency]] neglect, and expensive amendments that the
soil does not need.

The traditions represented by Lowenfels, Solomon, Coleman, Jeavons,
Holmgren, Mollison, JADAM, and Fukuoka do not agree on every practice.
Yet together they show that fertility is both measurable and ecological.
Minerals matter, but so do humus, roots, climate, disturbance, and the
grower's willingness to learn from the land.

## Why Soil Testing Matters

Plant symptoms are often ambiguous. Yellowing leaves may indicate
nitrogen deficiency, iron lockout, waterlogging, cold soil, root
disease, or herbicide injury, so a soil test gives the grower a firmer
starting point.

Testing prevents waste. Adding amendments without data can create
nutrient excesses that are harder to correct than deficiencies,
especially phosphorus, salts, and lime.

A soil report also records change through time. Comparing tests across
years reveals whether a fertility program is building reserves,
depleting minerals, or simply moving numbers without improving crops.
