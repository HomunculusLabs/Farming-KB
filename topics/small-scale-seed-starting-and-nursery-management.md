Seed starting is the controlled beginning of a crop. It brings germination,
early root growth, and transplant establishment into a protected setting where
temperature, moisture, light, fertility, and sanitation can be managed more
carefully than in an open bed. For gardeners it extends the season; for market
growers it turns biological uncertainty into a schedule.

A nursery is not merely a place where seedlings wait. It is a production
system that translates seed into field-ready plants. Good nursery management
considers seed biology, potting media, container size, irrigation, airflow,
hardening off, and the labor rhythm of sowing and transplanting. Each decision
affects the plant long after it leaves the tray.

Organic growers often prefer compost-based mixes, but compost must be mature,
fine, and not overly salty. Immature compost can release ammonia, tie up
nitrogen, or carry pathogens. A mix that seems rich to a mature plant may
overwhelm a germinating seed. The nursery rewards moderation.

Potting-on mixes are usually richer than germination mixes. Once seedlings
have true leaves, they need more fertility and structure. Recipes from organic
gardening traditions often include compost, loam, sharp sand, leaf mold, and
small amounts of mineral amendments. The goal is steady growth, not lush
weakness.

Media choice also has ecological consequences. Peat extraction damages
wetlands, while coir travels long distances and may contain salts. Local
compost, leaf mold, and farm-made mixes can reduce dependence on purchased
inputs, but they require testing and consistency. The best medium is
biologically suitable and logistically dependable.
