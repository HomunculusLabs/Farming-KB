# Plant Defense Chemistry and Secondary Metabolites

## Introduction

Unlike animals, plants cannot flee from herbivores or pathogens. Instead, they have evolved an extraordinary arsenal of **secondary metabolites** — chemical compounds that are not directly involved in growth, development, or reproduction, but serve critical ecological functions including defense against herbivores, resistance to pathogens, competition with other plants (allelopathy), attraction of pollinators and beneficial organisms, and protection from abiotic stressors like UV radiation.

These compounds are called "secondary" because they are produced from the [[comparison-companion-planting-reference-vs-jadam-monoculture-critique]] strategies, managing crop stress to optimize phytochemical production (especially in medicinal herbs), and reducing dependence on external pesticide inputs.

## Major Classes of Defense Compounds

### Alkaloids

Alkaloids are nitrogen-containing compounds, typically derived from amino acids. They are among the most potent plant defense chemicals, often acting as neurotoxins that disrupt herbivore nervous systems, inhibit enzyme function, or interfere with DNA replication.

| Alkaloid | Source Plant | Family | Primary Function |
|----------|-------------|--------|-----------------|
| Nicotine | *[[nicotiana-tabacum]]* | Solanaceae | Insect neurotoxin |
| Caffeine | *[[tea-cultivation-and-camellia-sinensis-processing]]* | Rubiaceae, Theaceae | Herbivore deterrent, allelopathic |
| Solanine solanum tuberosum osum* | Solanaceae | Antifungal, insect deterrent |
| Cocaine erythroxylum coca coca* | Erythroxylaceae | Insect feeding deterrent |
| [[papaver-somniferum]] r somniferum* | Papaveraceae | Anti-herbivore, antifungal |
| Strychnine | *Strychnos nux-vomica* | Loganiaceae | Potent vertebrate toxin |
| Atropine atropa belladonna onna* | Solanaceae | Anticholinergic toxin |

Alkaloids often accumulate in specific tissues — leaves, seeds, or roots — and concentrations can vary dramatically with environmental stress. Nightshade-family crops (tomatoes, potatoes, eggplants) contain glycoalkaloids that increase with exposure to light or mechanical damage, which is why potato greening is a safety concern.

### Terpenoids and Terpenes

Terpenoids constitute the largest class of plant [[mycelium-running-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties-species-chart]] and contribute to plant stress responses.

**Coumarins** (e.g., scopoletin, umbelliferone) are found in many plants and have antifungal, antibacterial, and anti-feeding properties. Some coumarins are allelopathic, inhibiting germination of competing species.

**Flavonoids** are a massive subclass with over 6,000 known compounds. They serve as pigments (anthocyanins attract pollinators), UV protectants, antimicrobial agents, and signaling molecules. Key flavonoid subclasses include:

- **Flavones** (apigenin, luteolin) — anti-herbivore, antifungal
- **Flavonols** (quercetin, kaempferol) — UV protection, antioxidant
- **Isoflavones** (genistein, daidzein) — found primarily in legumes; mediate rhizobial symbiosis signaling and have phytoestrogenic effects
- **Tannins** (see below)
- **Anthocyanins** (cyanidin, pelargonidin) — pigment, antioxidant, UV shield

**Lignin** is a complex phenolic polymer that provides structural rigidity to cell walls and creates a physical and chemical barrier against pathogen penetration. Lignified tissues are resistant to most fungal enzymes.

**Tannins** are polyphenolic compounds that bind proteins, making plant tissues unpalatable and difficult to digest. They are classified as:

- **Hydrolyzable tannins** (gallotannins, ellagitannins) — found in oak, sumac, pomegranate
- **Condensed tannins** (proanthocyanidins) — found in legumes, cranberries, tea, cacao

Tannins reduce feed quality for livestock but also have medicinal properties. High-tannin forages can cause bloat reduction in ruminants by binding dietary proteins.

### Glucosinolates

Glucosinolates are sulfur- and nitrogen-containing compounds found almost exclusively in the Brassicaceae (cabbage/mustard family). They are stored separately from the enzyme **myrosinase** in plant tissues; when tissue is damaged (by herbivory), the two mix and glucosinolates are hydrolyzed into toxic breakdown products:

- **Isothiocyanates** (mustard oils) — the pungent compounds in mustard, horseradish, and wasabi; toxic to insects, nematodes, and fungi
- **Nitriles** — less toxic than isothiocyanates but still deterrent
- **Thiocyanates** — goitrogenic compounds
- **Oxazolidine-2-thiones** — goitrogenic, found in cruciferous forages

The glucosinolate-myrosinase system is often called the "mustard bomb" and is one of the best-studied plant defense systems. Different Brassica species produce different glucosinolate profiles, which partly determines their pest and disease resistance spectra. This chemistry is the basis for biofumigation — incorporating [[biofumigation-and-brassica-cover-crops-for-pest-management]] into soil to release isothiocyanates that suppress soilborne pathogens and nematodes.

**Sulforaphane**, an isothiocyanate from broccoli, has potent anticancer properties and is one of the most studied dietary phytochemicals.

### Saponins

Saponins are glycosylated triterpenoids or steroids that have detergent-like properties — they disrupt cell membranes, causing cells to lyse. They are found in many food plants:

- **Tomatoes** (tomatine) — antifungal, insect deterrent
- **Quinoa** — bitter saponins in seed coat (removed by washing before eating)
- **Yucca and soapwort** — traditional soap sources
- **Licorice** (glycyrrhizin) — sweet-tasting saponin
- **Oats** (avenacosides) — antifungal

Saponins also have antifungal, antibacterial, and antiprotozoal activity. Their amphiphilic nature (water-soluble sugar chain + lipid-soluble aglycone) allows them to interact with and disrupt sterol-containing membranes.

### Cyanogenic Glycosides

Cyanogenic glycosides are stored as inactive precursors that release hydrogen cyanide (HCN) upon tissue damage and enzymatic hydrolysis. HCN is a potent inhibitor of cytochrome c oxidase, blocking cellular respiration.

| Amygdalin | Almond, apple seeds, cherry pits | Rosaceae | Gives bitter almond flavor |
| Linamarin | Cassava, lima beans | Euphorbiaceae, Fabaceae | Major food safety concern in cassava |
| Dhurrin | Sorghum | Poaceae | Causes livestock poisoning |
| Prunasin | Wild cherry, chokecherry | Rosaceae | Dangerous to livestock |

Cassav [[manihot-esculenta]] enta*) is a staple food for ~800 million people but contains linamarin; proper processing (peeling, fermentation, cooking) is essential to remove cyanogenic compounds. Breeding programs have developed low-cyanogen varieties.

### Other Notable Defense Compounds

**Benzoxazinoids** are found primarily in grasses (Poaceae), especially maize, wheat, and rye. DIMBOA (2,4-dihydroxy-7-methoxy-2H-1,4-benzoxazin-3(4H)-one) provides resistance against European corn borer and various fungal pathogens. Benzoxazinoids are also allelopathic, explaining the weed-suppressive effect of rye cover crop residues.

**Protease inhibitors** are proteins that inhibit digestive enzymes in insect herbivores. Found in many legumes and solanaceous crops, they reduce the nutritional value of [[plant-tissue-systems-meristematic-ground-vascular-and-dermal]] for insects. Tomato and potato upregulate protease inhibitors systemically after herbivore attack.

**Lectins** are carbohydrate-binding proteins that can be toxic to insects and mammals. Phytohemagglutinin in raw kidney beans is a well-known example — it must be deactivated by cooking.

**Phytoalexins** are antimicrobial compounds synthesized de novo after pathogen attack (see below).

**Cannabinoids** (THC, CBD, etc.) in cannabis are terpenophenolic compounds that serve ecological roles in pest defense and UV protection, in addition to their well-known psychoactive [[ginseng-cultivation-and-medicinal-properties]].

## Constitutive vs. Induced Defenses

### Constitutive Defenses

Constitutive (or "static") defenses are always present, regardless of whether the plant is under attack. These include structural defenses (thorns, spines, trichomes, thick cuticles, lignified cell walls) and pre-formed chemical defenses (stored alkaloids, tannins, glucosinolates, cyanogenic glycosides).

Constitutive defenses represent an ongoing metabolic cost but provide immediate protection. Plants growing in high-herbivory environments (tropical forests, open grasslands) tend to invest more heavily in constitutive chemical defenses than those in low-herbivory environments.

### Induced Defenses

Induced defenses are activated only after the plant detects herbivory or pathogen attack. This allows the plant to allocate resources to defense only when needed, reducing the metabolic cost of maintaining constant chemical protection.

**Phytoalexins** are antimicrobial secondary metabolites synthesized from scratch (de novo) after pathogen recognition. Unlike pre-formed compounds, phytoalexins are not detectable in healthy tissue but accumulate rapidly at infection sites. Examples include:

- **Resveratrol** (grapevines) — antifungal, also valued for human health benefits
- **Camalexin** (Arabidopsis) — broad-spectrum antifungal
- **Glyceollin** (soybean) — induced by Phytophthora infection
- **Capsidiol** (pepper) — antifungal sesquiterpene
- **Phaseollin** (bean) — isoflavonoid phytoalexin
