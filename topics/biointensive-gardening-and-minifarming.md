# Biointensive Gardening and Minifarming

Biointensive gardening, formally known as GROW BIOINTENSIVE (GB), is a
small-scale agricultural system designed to maximize food production in
minimal space while simultaneously building soil fertility. Developed by
John Jeavons at Ecology Action in Willits, California, the method has
been researched and refined continuously since 1972. It is now practiced
in over 150 countries, making it one of the most widely adopted
small-scale sustainable farming systems in the world. The approach
combines elements of traditional European intensive gardening, modern
organic agriculture, and ecological science into a unified methodology
that aims to be truly self-sustaining.

At its core, the biointensive method is built around a closed-loop
principle: everything the plants need is produced within the garden
itself. Unlike many organic methods that rely on imported fertility
inputs such as composted manure from off-site sources or purchased
organic fertilizers, GB grows all of its own compost materials, saves
its own seeds, and produces complete dietary calories within a single
integrated system. This distinguishes it sharply from conventional
agriculture, which depletes soil at rates far exceeding natural
rebuilding, and from many organic approaches that still depend on
external inputs to maintain productivity.

The system is designed around eight interlocking principles that work
synergistically: deep soil preparation through double-digging, close
plant spacing in hexagonal patterns, comprehensive composting, carbon
farming for biomass production, calorie farming for dietary staples,
companion planting, open-pollinated seed saving, and whole system
integration. Proponents claim that when fully implemented, GB can reduce
water consumption by 60 to 80 percent, reduce purchased fertilizer
inputs by 50 percent, and achieve 99 percent less energy use compared
to conventional methods, while producing two to six times higher yields
per square foot.

The practical implications of biointensive agriculture are significant
for global food security. Jeavons estimates that a nutritionally
complete diet for one person, providing approximately 2,500 to 3,000
calories per day, can be grown on as little as 2,800 to 4,000 square
feet (about one-sixteenth to one-tenth of an acre) using the full GB
method. This compares favorably to the 10,000 to 22,000 square feet
required with conventional organic methods and the 33,000 to 77,000
square feet needed with conventional mechanized agriculture. If adopted
widely, the dramatically reduced land requirement could free enormous
areas for ecological restoration, reforestation, and biodiversity
conservation.

## History

The biointensive method traces its lineage through several important
traditions in intensive horticulture. Its most direct predecessor is
the French intensive gardening method, also known as the French
marais system, developed in the market gardens surrounding Paris in
the nineteenth century. French intensive gardeners achieved
extraordinary yields through deep soil preparation, heavy composting,
close spacing, and the use of cold frames and bell jars to extend the
growing season.

The English influence came through Alan Chadwick, a British
horticulturist who emigrated to the United States in the 1960s.
Chadwick synthesized the French intensive method with the biodynamic
principles of Rudolf Steiner. In 1967, he established a student
garden at the University of California, Santa Cruz, where he
demonstrated that deep cultivation, intensive composting, and close
plant spacing could produce extraordinary results. His passionate
advocacy inspired a generation of gardeners.

John Jeavons encountered Chadwick's work at Santa Cruz in the early
1970s and began systematically researching and quantifying the
approach. He founded Ecology Action in 1972 and established a research
farm in Willits, California, to determine the minimum land and
resources needed to feed one person sustainably. Over the following
decades, Jeavons refined the method into the eight-component GROW
BIOINTENSIVE system, published extensively (most notably in "How to
Grow More Vegetables"), and established partnerships worldwide.

## The Eight Principles

The GROW BIOINTENSIVE system consists of eight interlocking
components, each essential to the functioning of the whole. Jeavons
emphasizes that these are not optional enhancements but integral
parts of a system designed to be self-sustaining. Removing any single
component reduces the effectiveness of the entire system.

The first principle is deep soil preparation through double-digging,
which loosens soil to a depth of approximately 24 inches, creating
a well-aerated root zone that dramatically increases the volume of
soil available to plant roots. The second is close plant spacing in
hexagonal patterns, which creates a living mulch that shades the soil,
suppresses weeds, and maximizes photosynthesis per unit area. The
third principle is comprehensive composting, which makes compost the
primary and ideally sole fertility source. Fourth, carbon farming
dedicates approximately 60 percent of growing area to crops that
produce biomass for compost. Fifth, calorie farming focuses on growing
complete dietary calories in minimal space. Sixth, companion planting
intermixes compatible species to reduce pest pressure and improve
growing conditions. Seventh, open-pollinated seed saving adapts
varieties to local conditions and preserves genetic diversity. The
eighth principle is whole system integration, recognizing that all
seven preceding components function synergistically and must be
practiced together for optimal results.

## Bed Preparation (Double-Digging)

Double-digging is the foundational soil preparation technique in the
biointensive method. Conventional tilling typically works only the top
6 to 8 inches of soil. Double-digging opens the soil to approximately
24 inches, which allows roots to penetrate deeper, improves drainage
in heavy clay soils and water retention in sandy soils, increases the
volume of soil available for close plant spacing, and incorporates
compost into two layers rather than just the surface.

The process involves digging a trench one foot deep and one foot wide
across one end of the bed, setting aside the removed topsoil. The
subsoil at the bottom of the trench is then loosened with a spading
fork to another 10 to 12 inches, without inverting the soil layers.
A layer of finished compost is placed in the loosened subsoil. The
next one-foot section is dug, with its topsoil moved into the first
trench on top of the compost. This process continues across the entire
bed, with the soil saved from the first trench filling the last
trench. Standard GB beds are 100 square feet, typically 4 by 25 or
5 by 20 feet, with a width that allows reaching the center from
either side without stepping on the bed.

After the initial double-dig, beds can be maintained with less
labor-intensive tools such as the U-bar, a U-shaped tool with four
to six tines that aerates soil deeply with minimal effort, or the
broadfork, which lifts and aerates without inverting soil. Jeavons
recommends double-digging primarily for new beds and transitioning
to surface cultivation with a U-bar or broadfork for maintenance after
the first year, preserving the microbial networks that develop in
established beds.

A critical consideration is the timing of double-digging. It should be
done when soil is moist but not waterlogged, ideally in fall or early
spring. Working soil that is too wet causes compaction and forms hard
clods when dried; working soil that is too dry requires excessive
effort and may damage soil structure. A simple test is to squeeze a
handful of soil into a ball and then tap it with a finger. If it
crumbles easily, it is ready to work. If it stays in a solid ball or
feels sticky, it is too wet.

## Close Plant Spacing

Close plant spacing is the principle that gives biointensive gardens
their characteristic dense, lush appearance. Plants are arranged in
hexagonal (equilateral triangular) grids rather than conventional rows,
with each plant equidistant from its six nearest neighbors. This
arrangement is the most efficient packing of circles in a plane and
typically allows 20 to 40 percent more plants per unit area than
square grid planting.

The key spacing principle is that plants are placed so their leaves
just touch at maturity. This creates a continuous living canopy that
completely shades the soil surface, minimizing evaporation (reducing
water needs by 60 to 80 percent), naturally suppressing weed
competition, maximizing photosynthesis because all leaf area
intercepts light, and protecting the soil from sun, rain impact, and
wind erosion.

The relationship between close spacing and double-digging is critical.
Without the loosened subsoil created by double-digging, closely spaced
plants would compete too aggressively for nutrients and water in the
compacted upper layer. The deep root zone allows plants to access
resources at different depths, reducing direct competition. GB
strongly favors transplanting over direct seeding, as transplants are
started in flats four to eight weeks before planting out, allowing bed
space to be used continuously and plants to be placed at exact
hexagonal spacing.

Typical biointensive yields are measured in pounds per 100 square foot
bed and are generally two to six times higher per square foot than
conventional row gardening. However, spacing must be carefully
calibrated: plants spaced tighter than recommended compete excessively,
while spacing that is too wide allows weeds to fill gaps and reduces
yields.

## Companion Planting

Companion planting, the sixth principle of the biointensive system,
involves interplanting compatible species to reduce pest pressure,
improve pollination, and maximize space use. GB draws on traditional
companion planting knowledge as well as modern research into plant
interactions, recognizing that diverse plant communities tend to be more
resilient and productive than simplified monocultures.

Strategies include growing nitrogen-fixing legumes alongside heavy-
feeding crops, interplanting aromatic herbs that repel pest insects,
and stacking plants of different heights and root depths to exploit all
available niches. Combined with close spacing and bed preparation,
companion planting creates growing environments that more closely mimic
natural plant communities.

## Composting

Composting is central to the biointensive system and serves as its
primary fertility source. The method emphasizes thermophilic (hot)
composting that produces high-quality finished compost in as little as
three to six weeks. In GB, compost is not an optional amendment but
the foundation of soil health. The system aims to produce enough
compost on-site to sustainably maintain soil health indefinitely,
eliminating the need for imported fertilizers.

The target carbon-to-nitrogen ratio for GB compost piles is
approximately 30:1, achieved by mixing carbon-rich brown materials
(straw, dried leaves, cornstalks) with nitrogen-rich green materials
(fresh green plants, kitchen scraps, legume residues). A properly
built pile should reach 120 to 140 degrees Fahrenheit within 24 to 48
hours and sustain temperatures of 131 to 150 degrees Fahrenheit for
at least three days, which kills most weed seeds and pathogens. The
pile is typically turned two to four times over the course of three
to six weeks until the material is dark, crumbly, and has an earthy
smell.

The GB method recommends dedicating approximately 60 percent of
growing area to compost crops, reflecting the primacy of soil building.
Application rates range from one to two inches for new beds to one-half
to one inch per crop cycle for established beds. The emphasis on
producing all compost materials on-site is the closed-loop principle
that most clearly distinguishes GB from other organic methods that may
import composted manure or purchased fertilizers.

## Calorie Crops

Calorie farming is the principle that focuses on growing complete
dietary calories in minimal space. While many gardening methods
maximize weight or variety of vegetables, GB specifically optimizes
for calories per square foot, the metric that determines whether a
garden can truly sustain human life. Most vegetables are low in
calories; a pound of lettuce provides only about 70 calories. To feed
one person, you would need more than 35 pounds of lettuce daily,
requiring vast areas. Calorie farming addresses this by emphasizing
calorie-dense crops: root crops such as potatoes and sweet potatoes,
grains such as wheat, oats, and corn, protein-rich legumes such as
fava beans and soybeans, and oilseeds such as sunflowers and sesame.

In temperate climates, potatoes are the most calorie-efficient crop,
producing approximately 34,000 calories per 100 square foot bed. Sweet
potatoes yield even more, approximately 40,000 calories per 100 square
feet in warm climates. By comparison, lettuce yields only about 2,000
calories per 100 square feet. Jerusalem artichokes, which are
perennial and highly reliable, produce approximately 30,000 calories
per 100 square feet. These dramatic differences underscore why
biointensive diet design allocates 30 to 40 percent of growing area
specifically to calorie crops.

Growing grains requires additional processing steps including
harvesting, drying, threshing, and winnowing, and Jeavons recommends
dwarf wheat varieties for easier harvesting by hand. Protein
considerations are addressed through complementary grain and legume
combinations that create complete amino acid profiles, along with
vegetable diversity for micronutrients.

## Carbon Farming

Carbon farming is the practice of growing dedicated crops specifically
for producing biomass to feed the compost pile. Approximately 60
percent of the growing area in a fully implemented biointensive system
is devoted to carbon and compost crops, making it the largest single
allocation and the foundation of long-term soil sustainability. This
figure comes from careful calculations: each 100 square foot bed
requires approximately 50 to 100 pounds of finished compost per crop
cycle, and enough biomass must be grown to maintain a positive carbon
balance.

High-biomass carbon crops include rye (15 to 25 pounds of dry biomass
per 100 square feet), sorghum (15 to 30 pounds), Jerusalem artichoke
(20 to 40 pounds), and comfrey (15 to 25 pounds per cutting, with
four to six cuttings per season). Many carbon crops also produce food,
improving the efficiency of the allocation. Grains provide both straw
for carbon and grain for calories; legumes fix nitrogen while also
providing biomass; and sunflowers produce seeds alongside carbon-rich
stalks.

A sustainable biointensive system needs a carbon ratio of approximately
1.5:1 to 2:1, meaning for every unit of carbon removed in harvest, 1.5
to 2 units must be returned to the soil as compost. Jeavons' research
shows that with proper management, GB can build soil at approximately
1 inch of topsoil per year, compared to natural rates of 0.01 to 0.05
inches per year in temperate climates. This soil-building capacity has
significant implications for climate change mitigation, as agricultural
soils globally have lost an estimated 50 to 70 percent of their
original organic carbon content.

Comfrey (Symphytum officinale) deserves special attention as a carbon
crop. Its deep taproot, extending 6 to 10 feet into the subsoil, mines
minerals unavailable to shallow-rooted crops. The leaves contain
approximately 2.5 to 3.0 percent nitrogen on a dry weight basis, making
it one of the few non-leguminous plants with nitrogen levels comparable
to legumes. Russian comfrey, the Bocking 14 cultivar, is sterile and
propagated only by root cuttings, preventing invasive spread.

## Seed Saving

Seed saving is treated in the biointensive system not as an optional
advanced skill but as a fundamental practice for food sovereignty,
local adaptation, and long-term sustainability. GB exclusively uses
open-pollinated varieties because they breed true from seed, can be
improved over time through selection, and maintain genetic diversity
that provides resilience. Hybrid seeds do not breed true and are
therefore unsuitable for the closed-loop approach.

Minimum population sizes must be observed to maintain genetic vigor and
prevent inbreeding depression. Self-pollinating crops such as tomatoes,
lettuce, peas, and beans require saving seed from 10 to 20 plants at
minimum. Cross-pollinating crops such as corn, squash, and brassicas
require 50 to 100 or more plants. Cross-pollinating crops also need
isolation distances ranging from one-quarter mile to one mile to
prevent unwanted crossing, though techniques like staggered planting
times and hand-pollination can reduce these requirements.

Selection criteria emphasized in GB seed saving include plant vigor,
disease resistance, climate adaptation, yield, taste and nutrition,
early maturity, and reliable seed production. Jeavons recommends
dedicating approximately 5 to 10 percent of garden space to seed
production, with an iterative approach where each generation the best
plants are selected and their seeds saved. Over five to ten generations,
locally adapted seeds significantly outperform their commercially
sourced ancestors.

Seeds must be stored under cool, dry, and dark conditions. Below 8
percent moisture content and at temperatures of 32 to 50 degrees
Fahrenheit, most seeds remain viable for several years, though
longevity varies by species.

## Diet Design

Biointensive diet design is the process of planning a complete,
nutritionally adequate vegetarian diet that can be grown entirely
within a biointensive mini-farm. It integrates calorie farming, carbon
farming, and vegetable production into a unified planting plan. The
design principles require complete nutrition (adequate calories,
protein, fats, vitamins, and minerals year-round), minimal area,
closed-loop fertility, seasonal planning, and seed sovereignty.

A typical one-person biointensive diet allocates garden beds roughly
as follows: 30 to 40 percent to calorie crops including potatoes,
grains, and legumes; 10 to 20 percent to special root crops such as
garlic, onions, and leeks; 20 to 30 percent to carbon farming (which
often overlaps with calorie crops); 10 to 20 percent to vegetables for
vitamins, minerals, and variety; and approximately 10 percent to seed
saving and calorie-efficient vegetables.

Seasonal eating is emphasized, with storage crops such as potatoes,
sweet potatoes, onions, garlic, and winter squash providing food for
three to eight months without processing. Year-round harvests are
planned through winter-hardy crops like kale, collards, parsnips, and
Jerusalem artichokes, along with succession planting of quick crops
like radishes, lettuce, and beans for continuous harvest.

Fat is the most difficult nutrient to produce in a small garden.
Sunflower seeds are the best oilseed for small-scale pressing, while
peanuts provide both fat and protein. Protein planning relies on
complementary grain and legume combinations, with soybeans providing
complete protein alone. Scaling considerations show that two people
require approximately 5,000 to 6,000 square feet (benefiting from
economies of scale since paths remain constant), and a family of four
needs approximately 8,000 to 10,000 square feet, about one-fifth to
one-quarter of an acre.

## Yields and Statistics

The resource efficiency claims of the GROW BIOINTENSIVE system are
among its most widely cited features. When practiced completely,
proponents report 60 to 80 percent less water consumption, 50 percent
less purchased fertilizer, and 99 percent less energy use compared to
conventional methods. These gains come from close plant spacing that
shades soil and reduces evaporation, on-site compost production that
eliminates imported fertility, and the elimination of tractor
cultivation and fossil fuel inputs.

Yields per unit area are reported at two to six times higher per square
foot than conventional farming, and up to four times more calories per
unit of area when including calorie crops. These efficiency gains are
cumulative; the more completely the system is implemented, the greater
the returns. The land area required to feed one person illustrates the
contrast starkly: GB requires 2,800 to 4,000 square feet, conventional
organic methods require 10,000 to 22,000 square feet, and conventional
mechanized agriculture requires 33,000 to 77,000 square feet. These
figures represent ideal conditions, and actual results vary
significantly based on climate, soil, skill level, and the degree to
which all eight components are adopted.

## Criticisms and Limitations

Despite its many proponents, the biointensive method has attracted
criticism on several grounds. The most commonly cited limitation is
labor intensity. Double-digging, in particular, is extremely demanding
physically, especially when first establishing beds; a full 100 square
foot bed can require four to eight hours of intensive labor. While
maintenance becomes easier in subsequent years with U-bar or broadfork
cultivation, the initial investment of effort can be prohibitive for
physically limited individuals.

The space requirements, while modest compared to conventional
agriculture, are still significant for urban dwellers. The estimate of
2,800 to 4,000 square feet per person assumes ideal conditions, full
implementation of all eight principles, and skill developed over
multiple growing seasons. Beginners typically achieve lower yields and
may struggle to produce sufficient calories, particularly from grains
that require processing infrastructure (threshing, winnowing,
grinding) that many households lack.

The 60 percent carbon crop allocation means that a majority of the
garden is not directly producing food in any given season, and the
method assumes a vegetarian or near-vegetarian diet, which may not
align with all cultural or dietary preferences. Some agricultural
scientists have also questioned whether the yield claims have been
sufficiently replicated in peer-reviewed research. While Ecology
Action has collected decades of data, controlled replicated trials are
limited. The method was developed in the Mediterranean climate of
Northern California and may require significant adaptation for
tropical, arid, or very cold regions.

## Global Applications

The biointensive method has been particularly impactful in developing
countries where smallholders have limited land, scarce water resources,
and limited access to purchased inputs. Ecology Action has established
demonstration and research sites worldwide, and the method has been
adapted to local conditions in diverse climates from tropical to arid.
Training programs through Ecology Action and partner organizations have
taught the method to thousands of farmers, with reported improvements in
both yields and soil health. The closed-loop nature of the system means
that once established, ongoing costs are minimal, making it economically
sustainable for low-income communities.

In developed countries, biointensive agriculture has found a niche
among backyard gardeners, homesteaders, and community garden
organizations seeking to maximize food production on small plots. The
method's emphasis on soil building and sustainability resonates with
the broader permaculture and regenerative agriculture movements. Urban
practitioners often implement partial versions of the system, focusing
on double-digging, close spacing, and composting while growing a wider
variety of vegetables than a full calorie-focused diet design would
require.
