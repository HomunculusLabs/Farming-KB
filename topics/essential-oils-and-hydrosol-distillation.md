# Essential Oils and Hydrosol Distillation

Essential oils and hydrosols are aromatic plant extracts produced
primarily through steam distillation, one of the oldest forms of
chemical separation known to humanity.

The practice stretches back thousands of years to ancient
Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Indian, and Chinese civilizations, where
aromatic waters and resinous extracts served roles in medicine,
religion, cosmetics, and food preservation.

Today, the global essential oil industry exceeds $11 billion
annually, yet the fundamental chemistry has changed little —
volatile plant compounds are still liberated by steam, condensed,
and separated by density.

What has changed dramatically is our understanding of the
chemistry involved, the precision of modern distillation equipment,
and the scientific evidence supporting (and sometimes challenging)
traditional therapeutic claims.

This article covers the chemistry of essential oils, the
distillation process from lab to home scale, major aromatic plant
families, [[ingham-compost-quality-assessment-visual-microbial-indicators]], storage, safety, and the growing
movement toward small-scale and community-based production.

## Steam Distillation: The Primary Extraction Method

Steam distillation works on a elegant physical principle: when
steam passes through plant material, the volatile aromatic
compounds vaporize at temperatures well below their individual
boiling points.

This is because the steam and essential oil form an azeotropic
mixture — a blend that behaves as a single substance with a
boiling point lower than either component alone.

Typically, the distillation temperature ranges from 100°C
(water's boiling point at sea level) to about 105°C, far below
the 150-300°C boiling points of many essential oil constituents.

This lower temperature is critical because it prevents thermal
degradation of heat-sensitive compounds that would be destroyed
by simple boiling or dry distillation.

The vaporized steam-and-oil mixture travels through a condenser —
a coiled copper or stainless steel tube cooled by circulating
water — where it condenses back into liquid form.
