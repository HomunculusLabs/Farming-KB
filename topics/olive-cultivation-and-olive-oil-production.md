## Olive Cultivation and Olive Oil Production

The olive tree (*Olea europaea* L.) is one of humanity's oldest cultivated plants, with a history spanning over 6,000 years. Native to the Mediterranean basin, this long-lived evergreen produces both table olives and premium cooking oil, forming the cornerstone of Mediterranean agriculture, cuisine, and culture. Modern olive cultivation extends well beyond its ancestral range, thriving in California, South America, Australia, South Africa, and increasingly in regions once considered marginal for production.

## Botanical Overview

- **Family:** Oleaceae (along with ash, lilac, jasmine, and forsythia)
- **Species:** *Olea europaea* (subsp. *europaea* for cultivated olives, subsp. *cuspidata* for wild/feral African olives, subsp. *laperrinei* for Saharan populations)
- **Growth habit:** Evergreen tree, 3–12 m depending on cultivar and management
- **Root system:** Very extensive and deep — roots can penetrate 6–10 m in well-drained soils; lateral roots spread well beyond the canopy drip line
- **Leaves:** Lanceolate, leathery, silvery-green adaxial surface with dense peltate trichomes that reflect light and reduce transpiration
- **Flowers:** Small, white, perfect (hermaphroditic) but often functionally unisexual; borne on paniculate inflorescences of 15–30 flowers
- **Fruit:** Drupe (stone fruit), 0.5–4 g, green maturing to black-purple; composed of skin (epicarp, 1–3% oil), flesh (mesocarp, 70–80% oil by dry weight), and pit (endocarp)
- **Lifespan:** Trees can live and bear fruit for 500–1,000+ years; many ancient groves across the Mediterranean contain trees verified at 1,000–3,000 years old

## History and Cultural Significance

The olive was first domesticated in the eastern Mediterranean — likely the Levant or Crete — around 4000–3000 BCE. Evidence of olive oil pressing dates to ~6000 BCE in the Carmel region of modern Israel. The Minoans of Crete developed sophisticated oil extraction by 2500 BCE, and olive oil became a major trade commodity throughout the ancient world.

In Greek mythology, the olive tree was Athena's gift to Athens, winning her the city's patronage. Olive wreaths crowned Olympic victors. In Abrahamic traditions, the dove returned to Noah's Ark with an olive branch — a symbol of peace that persists to this day. Roman expansion spread olive cultivation across the entire Mediterranean basin, and olive oil served as currency, lamp fuel, soap, medicine, and cosmetic base.

Spain, Italy, and Greece remain the world's top producers, collectively accounting for ~70% of global output. The Mediterranean diet, designated an Intangible Cultural Heritage by UNESCO, places olive oil at its center. Global production now exceeds 3 million metric tons annually, with new plantings expanding in Argentina, Chile, Australia, and the United States.

## Climate Requirements

### Temperature

- **Optimal growing range:** 15–30°C during the growing season
- **Winter chilling requirement:** 200–500 chill hours (7.2°C or below) for reliable flowering; some varieties require as few as 100 hours
- **Extreme cold tolerance:** Most cultivars survive −7°C to −12°C; young trees are damaged at −3°C to −5°C; Mission is the most cold-hardy commercial variety (survives −15°C)
- **Heat tolerance:** Olives are remarkably heat-tolerant; sustained temperatures above 40°C during flowering can reduce fruit set, and above 45°C can cause sunburn on fruit
- **Frost risk:** Spring frosts during flowering (typically April–May in the Northern Hemisphere) cause catastrophic crop loss

### Rainfall

- **Minimum annual rainfall:** 300–400 mm for dryland farming; 600–800 mm optimal for rainfed production
- **Irrigated production:** Supplemental irrigation of 200–500 mm/year dramatically increases yields and oil quality
- **Rainfall timing:** Critical periods are pre-flowering (February–March) for flower induction, and fruit development (May–August); dry harvest season (September–November) is ideal for oil quality

### Elevation and Latitude

- Commercial olives are grown from sea level to 800 m elevation; quality oils often come from 300–600 m where diurnal temperature variation enhances polyphenol accumulation
- Latitudinal range: approximately 30°–45° N and S

## Site Selection and Planting

### Soil Requirements

- **Texture:** Olives tolerate an extraordinary range — sandy loams to clay loams are ideal; heavy clay is acceptable if well-drained
- **Drainage:** Critical. Olives are extremely sensitive to waterlogging; standing water for even 48 hours can cause root asphyxiation and Phytophthora root rot
- **pH:** 5.5–8.5, with optimal range 6.5–7.5; olives are tolerant of moderately alkaline soils
- **Salinity:** Olives are among the most [[salt-tolerant-fodder-species]] fruit crops; EC up to 4–6 dS/m is tolerated (though yields decline above 3 dS/m)
- **Soil depth:** Minimum 60 cm; deeper is far better given the extensive root system

### Spacing

| System | Spacing | Trees/ha | Notes |
|--------|---------|----------|-------|
| Traditional wide | 10 × 10 m | 100 | Large trees, hand harvest |
| Semi-intensive | 7 × 5 m | 285 | Mechanical harvest, moderate vigor |
| Intensive/hedgerow | 4 × 1.5–2 m | 1,250–1,667 | Super-high-density (SHD), harvesters |
| Organic/food forest | 8 × 6 m | 208 | Good for intercropping |

### Pollination

Most olive cultivars are **partially self-sterile** or **self-incompatible**, meaning cross-pollination significantly improves fruit set (15–40% increase in many varieties). Key points:

- Plant at least 2–3 different cultivars for reliable production
- **Pendolino** is an excellent universal pollinator for many Italian and Spanish varieties
- Wind is the primary pollinator; avoid sites with dense windbreaks that block airflow during flowering
- Bees and other insects visit olive flowers but are not essential for pollination
- Flowering typically occurs 4–8 weeks after the last spring frost

### Planting Procedure

1. **Site preparation:** Deep rip or plow to 60–80 cm, correct pH with lime/sulfur, incorporate organic matter if soil organic carbon is below 1.5%
2. **Hole preparation:** 50 × 50 × 50 cm minimum; in heavy soils, consider mounding or planting on ridges to improve drainage
3. **Planting time:** Spring (after frost risk) in cold-winter regions; autumn in mild Mediterranean climates
4. **Tree handling:** Bare-root or container-grown; avoid planting too deep — the root crown should be at or slightly above soil level
5. **Staking:** Essential for the first 2–3 years; use bamboo or wooden stakes 1.5–2 m tall
6. **Irrigation:** Water immediately after planting; provide 10–15 L per tree per week during the first growing season
7. **Weed control:** Maintain a weed-free circle of at least 1 m radius around each young tree for the first 3 years

## Pruning and Training Systems

### Why Prune Olives?

Olives fruit primarily on one-year-old wood. Without pruning, trees become dense, shaded, and biennial-bearing (heavy crop one year, light the next). Proper pruning:

- Maintains light penetration throughout the canopy (critical for flowering and fruit quality)
- Removes dead, diseased, and crossing branches
- Shapes the tree for mechanical access
- Controls tree size and vigor
- Promotes regular annual bearing

### Training Systems

**Vase/Goblet (Vaso):** Traditional Mediterranean system. Open center with 3–4 main scaffold branches radiating outward. Excellent light distribution. Height 3–4 m. Suited to hand harvest. Widely used in traditional groves in Italy, Greece, and Spain.

**Central Leader:** Single trunk with tiered lateral branches. Allows taller, narrower trees. Better suited to mechanical harvesting. Common in newer plantings in Australia and California.

**Spindle/Bush:** Modified central leader with minimal pruning in early years. Good for medium-density plantings (400–600 trees/ha).

**Super-High-Density (SHD) Hedgerow:** 1.5–2.5 m spacing within rows, trees shaped as narrow hedgerows. Harvested by over-the-row straddle machines. Requires low-vigor varieties (Arbequina, Arbosana, Koroneiki). Most productive per hectare but requires significant capital investment. Rapidly expanding globally.

### Pruning Timing

- **Annual light pruning:** Best done immediately after harvest (late autumn/winter) in mild climates; in cold regions, prune in early spring before bud break to avoid frost damage to fresh cuts
- **Major rejuvenation pruning:** Can be done at any time for severely neglected trees; olives respond exceptionally well to heavy pruning and will regrow vigorously from old wood
- **Avoid pruning** during flowering (May–June in Northern Hemisphere) to prevent loss of fruiting wood

### Biennial Bearing Management

Olives are strongly prone to alternate bearing. Management strategies include:

- Moderate pruning after a heavy "on" year to reduce next-year crop load
- Thinning fruit in heavy years (chemical or manual)
- Balanced nitrogen fertilization (excess N exacerbates alternation)
- Irrigation management — deficit irrigation during the "off" year can reduce tree vigor and promote return bloom

## Irrigation and Water Management

Olives are among the most [[drought-tolerant-planting-design]] of all cultivated fruit trees, and dryland (rainfed) groves have been the norm for millennia. However, irrigation transforms yields:

| Management | Water Applied | Yield (kg/tree) | Oil Quality |
|------------|---------------|-----------------|-------------|
| Dryland | 0 mm/year | 5–20 | High polyphenols, lower yield |
| Supplemental | 100–200 mm | 20–40 | Good quality |
| Full irrigation | 400–800 mm | 40–100+ | Moderate polyphenols, high yield |

### Regulated Deficit Irrigation (RDI)

RDI is the standard irrigation strategy for quality olive oil production:

- **Spring (bud break to pit hardening):** Full irrigation to support shoot growth and flowering
- **Summer (pit hardening to veraison):** Reduce to 50–65% of evapotranspiration (ETc); this stress concentrates oil and polyphenols without significantly reducing yield
- **Pre-harvest (veraison to harvest):** Resume moderate irrigation to prevent shrivel, then reduce 2–3 weeks before harvest to concentrate oil
- **[[post-harvest-handling-marketing-mushrooms]]:** Minimal irrigation sufficient to prevent severe water stress

### Drought Survival

Mature olive trees can survive extreme drought (over 12 months without rain) by shedding leaves, reducing transpiration, and drawing on deep soil moisture reserves. However, consecutive years of severe drought cause progressive yield decline, twig dieback, and eventual tree mortality.

## Soil Management and Fertilization

### Macronutrient Needs

- **Nitrogen (N):** 50–150 kg/ha/year depending on tree age, yield, and irrigation. Excess nitrogen promotes vegetative growth at the expense of flowering and increases alternate bearing. Apply in late winter/early spring. Foliar urea sprays (1–2%) are effective for quick correction.
- **Potassium (K):** 80–200 kg/ha/year. Potassium is critical for oil accumulation and fruit quality. Deficiency causes reduced fruit size, premature ripening, and lower oil content. Apply as potassium sulfate (avoid chloride forms).
- **Phosphorus (P):** 20–50 kg/ha/year. Generally less critical than N and K; most Mediterranean soils contain adequate P. [[soil-testing-and-fertility-planning]] every 3–4 years guides applications.
- **Calcium (Ca) and Magnesium (Mg):** Usually adequate in the alkaline soils where olives thrive; monitor through leaf analysis.

### Micronutrient Needs

- **Boron (B):** The most commonly deficient micronutrient in olive production. Deficiency causes flower drop, reduced fruit set, and internal corking. Apply 50–100 g borax or boric acid per mature tree annually, or foliar spray at 0.1–0.2% B at pre-bloom.
- **Iron (Fe):** Deficiency (iron chlorosis) occurs in calcareous/high-pH soils. Treated with iron chelates (Fe-EDDHA) applied to soil or foliar spray.
- **Zinc (Zn) and Manganese (Mn):** Occasional deficiencies; correct with foliar sprays.

### Cover Crops in Olive Orchards

[[cover-crops-and-green-manures]] between tree rows provide multiple benefits:

- **Legumes** (subterranean clover, vetch, pea): Fix nitrogen, improve [[soil-structure-and-aggregate-ecology]]
- **Grasses** (oats, barley, rye): Scavenge residual nitrogen, prevent erosion, add organic matter
- **Mixed stands** are most common; mow or graze in spring before peak water demand
- In dryland groves, cover crops may compete for water — manage by terminating early or planting low-water-demand species
- Resident vegetation (natural weed flora) is acceptable and beneficial for biodiversity when managed by mowing

## Pest and Disease Management

### Key Pests

**Olive Fruit Fly (*Bactrocera oleae*):** The most damaging olive pest worldwide. Larvae feed inside the fruit, causing premature drop, oil quality degradation, and off-flavors (free fatty acid increase). Management: bait sprays (spinosad + protein hydrolysate), mass trapping (pheromone + ammonium bait traps), biological control (parasitoid wasp *Psyttalia concolor*), and cultural practices (early harvest, removal of fallen fruit). Organic growers rely heavily on kaolin clay sprays and mass trapping.

**Olive Moth (*Prays oleae*):** Three generations per year: flower-eating (anthophagous), fruit-piercing (carpophagous), and leaf-mining (phyllophagous). The carpophagous generation causes the most damage. Managed with Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), mating disruption pheromones, and timely insecticide sprays.

**Black Scale (*Saissetia oleae*):** Sap-sucking scale insect that produces honeydew, fostering sooty mold growth. Heavy infestations reduce tree vigor and oil quality. Managed by natural enemies (parasitoid wasp *Metaphycus spp.*), horticultural oil sprays in winter, and avoiding nitrogen excess that promotes soft, scale-susceptible growth.

**Olive Psyllid (*Euphyllura olivina*):** Small sap-sucking insect that causes leaf curling and produces honeydew. Generally a minor pest controlled by natural enemies.

### Key Diseases

**Verticillium Wilt (*Verticillium dahliae*):** The most serious soil-borne disease of olives. Causes wilting, branch dieback, and tree death. No cure once established. Prevention: plant resistant cultivars (Frantoio, Arbequina are relatively tolerant), avoid planting after solanaceous crops, maintain soil health, and use clean nursery stock.

**Olive Leaf Spot / Peacock Spot (*Spilocaea oleaginea*):** Fungal disease causing circular lesions on leaves, premature defoliation, and yield reduction. Favored by wet, cool winters. Management: copper-based fungicide sprays in autumn, resistant cultivar selection, and pruning for air circulation.

**Olive Knot (*Pseudomonas savastanoi pv. oleae*):** Bacterial disease causing rough galls/tumors on branches, twigs, and leaves. Enters through wounds (pruning cuts, frost damage). Management: copper sprays before and after pruning, avoid pruning during wet weather, and use sharp, disinfected tools.

**Anthracnose (*Colletotrichum spp.):** Fungal fruit rot causing mummified fruit and oil quality loss. Most severe in rainy autumns. Management: pre-harvest copper sprays, removal of infected fruit, and resistant cultivar selection.

### Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Approach

Modern olive IPM emphasizes:

1. **Monitoring:** Regular trapping (fruit fly traps, pheromone traps), visual inspection, and degree-day modeling for moth generations
2. **Economic thresholds:** Treat only when pest populations exceed action thresholds, not preventatively
3. **Biological control:** Conserve and augment natural enemy populations; avoid broad-spectrum insecticides
4. **Cultural practices:** Timely harvest, sanitation (remove fallen fruit), balanced nutrition, appropriate pruning for airflow
5. **Targeted chemical use:** When necessary, use the most selective, least disruptive product at the optimal timing

## Harvesting

### Harvest Timing

Optimal harvest timing is a critical determinant of oil quality and depends on the intended product:

- **Early harvest (green fruit, early veraison):** Lower oil yield but highest polyphenol content, most pungent and bitter oil, green, herbaceous flavors. Typically September–October in the Northern Hemisphere.
- **Mid-season (turning color, 50% veraison):** Best balance of oil yield and quality. This is when most premium extra virgin oils are harvested. October–November.
- **Late harvest (fully black):** Maximum oil yield but lower polyphenols, milder flavor, higher free acidity. November–December. Preferred for milder oils and table olives.

### Harvest Methods

| Method | Suitability | Cost | Fruit Damage | Labor |
|--------|-------------|------|--------------|-------|
| Hand picking (combing) | Small groves, table olives | Very high | Minimal | Very high |
| Hand raking onto nets | Traditional groves | High | Low | High |
| Trunk shaker + catch frame | Medium-density | Moderate | Low | Moderate |
| Over-the-row straddle harvester | SHD hedgerow | Low (per kg) | Moderate | Very low |
| Garden blower/air | Under-tree nets | Low | None | Low |

### Post-Harvest Handling

Olives should be pressed within 12–48 hours of harvest to prevent fermentation and free fatty acid increase. During transport and storage before pressing:

- Keep fruit in shallow layers (max 30 cm) in ventilated crates
- Store in cool, shaded areas (10–15°C ideal)
- Avoid crushing or bruising fruit
- Never store in sealed plastic bags (fermentation occurs rapidly)

## Olive Oil Production

### The Extraction Process

**1. Cleaning and Washing:** Leaves, twigs, and debris are removed. Olives are washed in clean water.

**2. Crushing/Milling:** Clean olives are crushed into a homogeneous paste using hammer mills, toothed disc crushers, or stone mills (the latter prized by traditionalists for gentler processing). Modern stainless steel hammer mills are most common.

**3. Malaxation:** The paste is slowly kneaded at 25–28°C for 20–60 minutes. This is the critical step where oil droplets coalesce into larger drops. Temperature control is essential — above 30°C, volatile aroma compounds degrade and the oil cannot be classified as "cold extracted." Some producers use sealed malaxers under inert gas (nitrogen) to prevent oxidation.

**4. Extraction/Centrifugation:** The paste is diluted with warm water and fed into a horizontal centrifuge (decanter), which separates the oil from the water and pomace (solids). Modern three-phase decanters separate oil, water, and pomace simultaneously. Two-phase decanters (preferred for quality) produce oil and a wet pomace, using less water and producing less wastewater.

**5. Final Separation:** The oil is passed through a vertical centrifuge to remove any remaining water and fine particles.

### Olive Oil Classifications

| Grade | Free Acidity | Extraction Method | Characteristics |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Extra Virgin Olive Oil (EVOO)** | ≤ 0.8% | Cold-pressed/extracted (≤ 27°C) | Superior taste, zero defects, high polyphenols |
| **Virgin Olive Oil** | ≤ 2.0% | Mechanical only | Good taste, minor defects |
| **Ordinary Virgin** | ≤ 3.3% | Mechanical only | Lampante quality |
| **Lampante Virgin** | > 3.3% | Mechanical only | Not for consumption; must be refined |
| **Refined Olive Oil** | ≤ 0.3% | Refined + virgin blend | Neutral flavor, lighter color |
| **Olive Pomace Oil** | ≤ 1.0% | Solvent extracted from pomace | Lowest quality; not permitted as "olive oil" in many jurisdictions |

### Key Quality Parameters

- **Free Fatty Acidity (FFA):** Indicator of fruit quality and processing speed. Increases with damaged fruit, delayed processing, and poor storage. Lower is better — premium oils are often 0.1–0.3%.
- **Peroxide Value (PV):** Measures primary oxidation. Fresh oil: < 10 meq O₂/kg. Higher values indicate rancidity onset.
- **Polyphenol Content:** Natural antioxidants that provide bitterness, pungency, and health benefits. Range: 50–800+ mg/kg. Higher in early-harvest oils from certain cultivars (Coratina, Picual, Koroneiki).
- **Fatty Acid Profile:** Olive oil is characterized by high oleic acid (55–83%, a monounsaturated omega-9), moderate linoleic acid (3.5–21%, omega-6), low linolenic acid (≤ 1.0%, omega-3), and low saturated fat (8–14%). High oleic acid content contributes to oxidative stability and health benefits.
- **Sensory Analysis:** Certified tasting panels evaluate oil for fruitiness, bitterness, pungency, and defects (fusty, musty, winey, metallic, rancid). EVOO must have zero defects and positive fruitiness.

## Table Olive Processing

### Green/Spanish Method (California-Style)

1. **Harvest** green olives (September–October)
2. **Cure** in dilute lye (NaOH) solution (1.5–2.5%) for 6–12 hours to neutralize oleuropein (the bitter compound)
3. **Wash** repeatedly to remove lye
4. **Ferment** in brine (8–10% NaCl) for 1–3 months with *Lactobacillus* bacteria
5. **Stabilize** and pack in brine or with acidified herbs

### Natural Black/Greek Method (Kalamata-Style)

1. **Harvest** fully ripe, black olives
2. **Direct brine** in 8–10% salt solution; fermentation takes 5–8 months
3. No lye treatment — slower, but preserves more natural flavor and texture
4. Olives may be lightly slit or bruised to speed brine penetration

### California Black/Oxidized Method

1. **Harvest** green olives
2. **Oxidize** in dilute lye under constant aeration for 5–7 days — this produces the characteristic uniform black color
3. **Cure** in ferrous gluconate solution to fix color
4. **Can** in mild brine; heat-sterilized

## Yield Expectations and Economics

### Yield Timeline

| Tree Age | Yield (kg/tree) | Oil (L/tree) | Notes |
|----------|-----------------|--------------|-------|
| 1–3 years | 0 | 0 | Establishment phase |
| 3–5 years | 2–8 | 0.3–1.2 | First crops, increasing |
| 5–10 years | 10–30 | 1.5–4.5 | Approaching full production |
| 10–25 years | 30–80 | 4.5–12 | Full production |
| 25–50+ years | 50–100 | 7.5–15 | Peak and sustained production |

- Average commercial yields: 3,000–8,000 kg fruit/ha (450–1,200 L oil/ha) for traditional groves
- SHD hedgerow systems: 8,000–15,000 kg/ha (1,200–2,250 L oil/ha)
- Oil extraction rate: 18–25% by weight (kg oil per 100 kg fruit)
- Conversion: ~5–6 kg olives = 1 liter of extra virgin olive oil

### Economic Considerations

- **Establishment cost** (traditional): $3,000–6,000/ha including trees, irrigation, land preparation
- **Establishment cost** (SHD): $12,000–20,000/ha — higher density, trellis, specialized equipment
- **Annual maintenance:** $1,500–3,000/ha (pruning, fertilization, pest management, irrigation)
- **Harvest cost:** $0.30–0.80/kg (hand); $0.05–0.15/kg (mechanical SHD)
- **Milling fee:** $0.15–0.40/kg of fruit at commercial mills
- **Net return:** Highly variable; premium EVOO can fetch $15–40/L retail; bulk commodity oil $3–8/L

## Climate Change Impacts and Adaptation

Climate change presents both challenges and opportunities for olive cultivation:

### Challenges

- **Heat waves** during flowering reduce fruit set; 40°C+ temperatures for consecutive days can cause catastrophic yield loss
- **Drought intensification** in the Mediterranean basin — the region is projected to receive 10–30% less rainfall by 2050
- **Shift in pest pressure:** Olive fruit fly may complete additional generations per year with warmer winters
- **Reduced chill hours** in marginal zones, potentially affecting flowering in some cultivars

### Adaptation Strategies

- **Drought-adapted cultivars:** Selecting for deep root systems and efficient water use (Koroneiki, Picual)
- **Shifting zones:** Expansion into higher elevations and higher latitudes (e.g., southern UK, Pacific Northwest of the USA)
- **Regulated deficit irrigation** to maximize water-use efficiency
- **Agroforestry integration:** Combining olives with livestock or intercropping for diversified income and soil health
- **Soil health management:** Increased organic matter improves water retention, reducing irrigation dependence
- **Heat-tolerant rootstocks:** Breeding programs are developing rootstocks with enhanced thermotolerance

## Role in Permaculture and Food Forest Design

Olives are among the most valuable and versatile trees for permaculture systems in Mediterranean climates:

### Advantages in Permaculture

- **Long-lived, [[low-input-vegetable-gardening]]:** Once established, olives require minimal care and produce for centuries
- **Drought tolerance:** Ideal for water-conscious designs and dryland systems
- **Fire resistance:** Mediterranean olive groves are natural firebreaks — their high moisture content and low resin content resist ignition
- **Biodiversity support:** Mature olive trees provide habitat for birds, lichens, insects; olive groves with cover crops and stone walls are biodiversity hotspots
- **Dual production:** Oil and table olives provide food security and potential income
- **Deep-rooted:** Accesses water and nutrients unavailable to shallow-rooted companion plants

### Companion Planting and Guild Design

- **Understory herbs:** Lavender, rosemary, thyme, oregano share Mediterranean preferences and attract pollinators
- **Leguminous ground covers:** Subclover, vetch, and Lucerne fix nitrogen and suppress weeds
- **[[nitrogen-fixing-bacteria-and-biological-nitrogen-fixation]] shrubs:** Cytisus, Caragana, or coral tree (*Erythrina*) as nurse trees in establishment
- **Bulbs:** Saffron crocus can be grown between olive trees for autumn harvest
- **Livestock integration:** Sheep and poultry can graze cover crops between trees; their manure contributes to soil fertility
- **Silvopasture:** Mature olive groves are excellent for grazing — widely practiced in Spain, Italy, and Greece

## Propagation Methods

### Cuttings (Semi-Hardwood)

The primary commercial propagation method. Semi-hardwood cuttings (15–20 cm, taken in autumn) are treated with rooting hormone (IBA 2,000–5,000 ppm) and placed in mist propagation beds with bottom heat (22–25°C). Rooting percentages vary from 40–90% depending on cultivar — Arbequina roots easily (80–95%), while Picual and Coratina are more difficult (30–60%). Rooting takes 8–16 weeks.

### Grafting/Budding

Used primarily to change cultivars on established trees (topworking) or to combine specific scion/rootstock combinations:

- **Cleft grafting:** On established trees 3+ years old; performed in early spring
- **Chip budding:** In late summer; high success rate for topworking
- **Rootstock options:** Most olives are grown on their own roots; research is ongoing into clonal rootstocks for specific soil and disease resistance

### Seed

Rarely used commercially due to genetic variability and 4–6 year juvenile period before flowering. Olive seeds have deep dormancy requiring warm stratification (3 months at 20°C) followed by cold stratification (2–3 months at 4°C). Germination rates are typically 20–50%. Seed-grown trees are used primarily in breeding programs and reforestation.

### Micropropagation/Tissue Culture

Increasingly important for producing disease-free, genetically uniform planting material at scale. Somatic embryogenesis and organogenesis protocols exist for major cultivars. Adoption is growing rapidly in SHD systems where millions of trees of identical genotype are required.
