# Compost Tea and Biological Extracts

## Introduction

The practice of steeping compost in water to produce a liquid biological
amendment is one of the oldest techniques in agriculture, yet it has
undergone a dramatic scientific renaissance since the late 1990s. What was
once a folk remedy of uncertain efficacy has been rigorously examined and
refined by soil microbiologists, particularly Dr. Elaine Ingham and her
colleagues at the Soil Foodweb Institute. Their work transformed a vague
horticultural tradition into a precisely defined biological technology:
actively aerated compost tea, or AACT.

The central insight is deceptively simple. Healthy soils are living
ecosystems, teeming with billions of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes,
and microarthropods per teaspoon. When intact and diverse, these
communities cycle nutrients, suppress disease, improve soil structure,
and buffer plants against environmental stress. Compost tea extracts and
multiplies these beneficial organisms, delivering them to places where
soil biology has been degraded or is entirely absent.

## Historical Roots

The use of liquid compost preparations extends back centuries. European
farmers long brewed "manure tea" by suspending bags of aged manure in
barrels of water, then using the liquid as a crop drench. In the early
twentieth century, biodynamic agriculture formalized the concept of
biological preparations with Steiner's compost preparations numbered 500
through 508, each designed to bring specific qualities to compost and
soil. While the biodynamic framework is rooted in a spiritual cosmology,
the practical observation that compost biology could be concentrated and
transferred to soils proved valid and inspired further investigation.

In Japan, Masanobu Fukuoka's natural farming emphasized maintaining a
living soil with mulch and diverse vegetation. His demonstration that
fields could be productive for decades without external fertilizer relied
on the soil food web sustaining its own fertility. The Korean Natural
Farming system employs fermented plant juices, fish amino acids, and
indigenous microorganism cultures. These parallel traditions all point
toward the same principle: soil organisms drive plant health, and this
community can be cultivated, concentrated, and applied.

The modern compost tea movement began when soil scientists gained the
tools to quantify microbial communities. The development of direct
microscopy methods by Elaine Ingham allowed researchers to assess not
just total biomass but relative proportions, activity levels, and
diversity. This revealed that traditional compost tea methods, steeping
compost in water for days without aeration, often produced anaerobic
conditions that favored pathogens and phytotoxic compounds. This led
directly to the development of actively aerated compost tea.

## The Soil Food Web Foundation

To understand why compost tea works, one must understand the soil food
web. Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis, in "Teaming with Microbes," describe
this community as a complex food chain beginning with photosynthesis and
cascading through multiple trophic levels. Plants fix carbon from the
atmosphere and exude a significant portion through their roots as sugars,
amino acids, and other compounds. These root exudates feed bacteria and
fungi in the rhizosphere. In return, the microorganisms decompose organic
matter and solubilize mineral compounds, making nutrients available to
plants.

Plants can adjust their exudate composition in response to nutritional
needs, secreting more citric acid when phosphorus is deficient or
producing compounds that recruit beneficial microorganisms. This two-way
communication between plants and soil organisms has been compared to a
chemical language. Compost tea amplifies this communication by increasing
the diversity and density of organisms available to respond to plant
signals.

The relationship between plants and mycorrhizal fungi is particularly
important. As Lowenfels documents in "Teaming with Fungi," an estimated 80
to 95 percent of all terrestrial plants form these symbiotic
relationships. Mycorrhizal fungi extend vast hyphal networks far beyond
the root zone, increasing absorptive surface area by orders of magnitude.
In exchange for plant carbon, they provide water, phosphorus, nitrogen,
and micronutrients. They also produce glomalin, a glycoprotein that binds
soil particles, improving aggregation, water retention, and erosion
resistance.

Above bacteria and fungi are the grazers: protozoa, nematodes, and
microarthropods. A single protozoan can consume ten thousand bacteria per
day, releasing ammonium that feeds plants and nitrifying bacteria. This
nutrient cycling, driven by predation, is far more efficient and
sustainable than synthetic fertilization, which bypasses the soil food
web and often disrupts it.

The soil food web also provides disease suppression through competition,
antibiosis, and induced systemic resistance. When intact and diverse,
diseases are held in check. When disrupted by tillage, chemicals,
compaction, or monoculture, pathogens proliferate and plants become
vulnerable.

## Compost Tea Versus Compost Extract

A critical distinction that Ingham emphasizes is the difference between
compost tea and compost extract. These terms are often used
interchangeably but refer to fundamentally different products.

A compost extract is produced by mixing compost with water and agitating
briefly, typically for just a few minutes. The goal is to extract the
organisms already present without allowing them to multiply. The microbial
diversity mirrors the source compost. Extracts are relatively simple to
produce and carry low risk of anaerobic conditions. However, they do not
significantly increase organism counts beyond what was present in the
compost. Extracts are most useful when transferring a diverse community
from high-quality compost to a new location, such as at transplanting.

Compost tea, by contrast, is a brewed product. Compost is placed in water
with food sources for microorganisms, and the mixture is actively aerated
for 18 to 36 hours. During brewing, the extracted microorganisms feed on
added nutrients and multiply exponentially. A well-brewed tea can contain
billions of bacteria and millions of fungi per milliliter, an increase of
several orders of magnitude over the source compost. This amplification is
what makes compost tea powerful: a small quantity of quality compost can
treat large areas.

The food sources are carefully chosen. Bacterial foods include simple
sugars like molasses for rapid bacterial growth. Fungal foods include
complex carbohydrates such as kelp meal, humic acids, and fish
hydrolysate. By adjusting the ratio of bacterial to fungal foods, the
brewer can produce bacterial-dominated tea for annual crops or
fungal-dominated tea for perennials and trees.

## Actively Aerated Compost Tea Production

The standard method involves several key components: high-quality compost,
clean water, appropriate food sources, adequate aeration, and sufficient
brewing time. Each must be carefully controlled.

The compost is the single most important variable. It must be mature,
aerobic, and biologically diverse. Immature compost contains
incompletely decomposed organic matter that can deplete oxygen during
brewing. Compost that has gone anaerobic may contain human pathogens. The
ideal compost has bacterial biomass of at least 100 to 300 micrograms per
gram, fungal biomass of at least 100 to 300 micrograms per gram, and a
diverse protozoan community. Testing through direct microscopy is
strongly recommended, as visual appearance alone is unreliable. Two
compost piles that look identical can have vastly different microbial
communities depending on their feedstock and curing history.

Water must be free of chlorine and chloramine, which kill beneficial
organisms. Municipal chlorine can be removed by pre-aerating water for
several hours or adding ascorbic acid. Chloramine is more persistent and
may require specialized filtration. Well water or rainwater is preferred.
Water temperature should be between 18 and 25 degrees Celsius for optimal
microbial activity.

Aeration must maintain dissolved oxygen above 6 ppm throughout the brew.
Ingham's guidelines specify that levels should never drop below 5.5 ppm;
if they do, facultative anaerobes dominate and the tea may develop putrid
odors and harmful metabolites. Most commercial brewers are designed for
adequate aeration, but homemade systems using aquarium pumps may be
inadequate for larger volumes. Tall, narrow vessels create better mixing
than wide, shallow ones.

Brewing time is typically 18 to 24 hours for bacterial-dominated teas and
24 to 36 hours for fungal-dominated teas. Overbrewing is a common
mistake: if organisms exhaust their food supply, populations crash and
dissolved oxygen drops. Ingham recommends brewing in late afternoon and
applying first thing the next morning.

## Anaerobic Compost Tea and Fermented Extracts

Anaerobic compost teas represent a fundamentally different approach. Organic
materials are placed in water and allowed to ferment without aeration for
days to weeks. The resulting liquid contains anaerobic and facultatively
anaerobic bacteria and yeasts. Korean Natural Farming makes extensive use
of anaerobic fermentation in preparations like indigenous microorganisms
(IMO) and oriental herbal nutrient.

The scientific evidence for anaerobic teas is more equivocal. Some studies
show benefits, but anaerobic teas can contain phytotoxic compounds such as
organic acids, alcohols, and hydrogen sulfide, particularly when applied
as foliar sprays. Anaerobic conditions also favor potential human
pathogens, making food safety a concern for edible crops.

Proponents argue that anaerobic teas contain unique beneficial compounds
including plant growth hormones and antimicrobial substances. Korean
Natural Farming produces "fish amino acid" by fermenting fish waste in
brown sugar as a nitrogen-rich feed. While these claims lack extensive
controlled validation, long history of use suggests a legitimate role
when applied carefully as soil drenches, diluted adequately, and as
supplements to aerobic tea rather than replacements.

## The Organisms in Compost Tea

A healthy compost tea contains representatives of all major soil food web
groups, though in proportions that differ from soil.

Bacteria are always the most numerous, comprising 70 to 95 percent of
total microbial biomass. In bacterial-dominated tea, targets are 150 to
300 micrograms per milliliter bacterial biomass with 2 to 10 micrograms
fungal biomass. In fungal-dominated tea, these ratios reverse. Bacteria
include Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Streptomyces, Azotobacter, and many
others, performing decomposition, nitrogen fixation, phosphorus
solubilization, antibiotic production, and pathogen competition. Ingham
emphasizes that community diversity is as important as total biomass.

Fungi are primarily saprophytic species decomposing lignin, cellulose, and
chitin. In fungal-dominated teas, visible hyphae give the liquid a cloudy
appearance. Fungi improve soil structure by binding particles into stable
aggregates and are particularly important for perennial crops, orchards,
and forests where the fungal food web component is naturally dominant.
Ingham's research shows the fungal-to-bacterial biomass ratio is a key
indicator of soil suitability: annuals prefer bacterial-dominated soils,
while perennials and trees prefer fungal-dominated soils.

Protozoa, particularly flagellates and amoebae, are critical grazers that
release plant-available nitrogen by consuming bacteria. Testing protocols
specify several thousand flagellates and several hundred amoebae per
milliliter. Ciliates in large numbers can indicate anaerobic conditions.
Protozoan populations are often the limiting factor in tea quality, as
they depend on adequate numbers in the source compost surviving the brew.

## Mechanisms of Plant Benefit

The benefits of compost tea operate through several interconnected
mechanisms, each documented in scientific literature.

Direct nutrient delivery is the first mechanism. Tea contains soluble
nitrogen as ammonium and amino acids, phosphorus, potassium, calcium,
magnesium, sulfur, and micronutrients. While concentrations are low
compared to synthetic fertilizers, these nutrients are biologically
active and less prone to leaching. Living organisms continue cycling
nutrients after application, providing sustained release.

Biological nutrient cycling is the second and perhaps most important
mechanism. When tea organisms colonize soil, they establish or augment the
local food web. Protozoa and nematodes graze on bacteria and fungi,
releasing nutrients in plant-available forms. In a healthy soil food web,
the majority of available nitrogen comes from this grazing activity rather
than applied fertilizer. A single tea application can initiate a
self-sustaining nutrient cycling system, provided the soil food web is
maintained through organic matter inputs and minimal disturbance.

Disease suppression is the third mechanism, operating through competition,
antibiosis, and induced systemic resistance. Beneficial organisms occupy
niches that pathogens would exploit. Bacillus and Pseudomonas produce
antibiotics and enzymes that degrade pathogen cell walls. Foliar
applications have reduced powdery mildew, downy mildew, and botrytis
on grapes, apples, tomatoes, and cucumbers.

Soil improvement is the fourth mechanism. Bacterial exopolysaccharides
and fungal hyphae act as biological glues, binding particles into
aggregates that resist erosion and compaction. Earthworm populations
increase in response to added organic matter, further improving structure.
Over time, repeated applications can transform compacted, lifeless soils
into well-structured, biologically active media.

A fifth mechanism is microbial production of plant hormones. Many tea
organisms produce auxins, gibberellins, and cytokinins that directly
stimulate root and shoot growth. Azotobacter produces auxins and
gibberellins that stimulate root elongation. Pseudomonas produces
siderophores that improve iron availability while depriving pathogens.
These effects explain why treated plants often show more vigorous growth
than nutrient delivery alone can account for.

## Application Methods

Compost tea can be applied as a soil drench, foliar spray, or seed
treatment, each targeting different aspects of plant health.

Soil drenches are the most common and generally most effective method.
Apply at 20 to 50 gallons per acre, diluted for even coverage. Best
results occur when soil temperatures exceed 10 degrees Celsius and
moisture is adequate but not saturated. Applications during active root
growth, in early spring for perennials and at planting for annuals, are
most beneficial. The tea should reach the root zone through drip
irrigation, injection, or direct application. Multiple applications may
be needed for severely degraded soils.

Foliar sprays deliver organisms directly to leaf surfaces for pathogen
suppression. Apply in early morning or late evening to avoid UV damage.
Spray must cover both leaf surfaces, especially undersides where
pathogens colonize. A non-ionic surfactant may improve coverage. Repeat
every 7 to 14 days during disease pressure, with more frequent
applications during wet weather.

Seed treatments involve soaking seeds in bacterial-dominated tea for
several hours before planting. This coats seeds with beneficial organisms
that colonize seedling roots at germination, providing early protection
against damping-off. Seeds should be planted immediately after soaking.

Irrigation application through drip or sprinkler systems allows uniform
coverage of large areas with minimal labor. The system must be compatible
with biological applications: chlorine must be removed, and lines should
be flushed after application.

## Connections to Mycorrhizal Management

While compost tea does not contain significant mycorrhizal populations,
it plays an important supporting role. Mycorrhizal fungi are sensitive to
tillage, which severs hyphal networks, and to high phosphorus fertilizer,
which reduces plant demand for fungal assistance. Compost tea supports
mycorrhizal function by creating biologically active soil environments
that favor colonization. Bacteria and protozoa cycle nutrients in forms
accessible to mycorrhizal fungi, and improved soil structure facilitates
hyphal growth.

In integrated management programs, compost tea and mycorrhizal inoculants
are applied together as complementary treatments. Tea provides broad-
spectrum biological inoculation while mycorrhizal inoculants provide
specialized symbionts that extend root systems. Lowenfels documents cases
where colonization rates increased dramatically with combined application.

Fungal-dominated teas are preferred for perennial systems because they
create conditions favorable to mycorrhizal fungi. Bacterial-dominated
teas can slow mycorrhizal establishment if used excessively. The general
principle is matching tea microbial composition to the plant's preferred
soil food web structure.

## Nutrient Cycling and Tea Biochemistry

Lowenfels and Lewis, in "Teaming with Nutrients," provide a detailed
examination of plant nutrient absorption that illuminates why compost tea
functions differently from conventional fertilizers.

Plants absorb nutrients as ions in solution: nitrate, ammonium,
phosphate, potassium, calcium, and so on. Conventional agriculture
supplies these directly through synthetic fertilizers. This works
short-term but has drawbacks. Excess soluble nutrients leach or
volatilize. Constant soluble supply can reduce root growth and
mycorrhizal investment. High salt concentrations disrupt the soil food
web, creating increasing dependence on external inputs.

Compost tea takes a fundamentally different approach. Rather than
supplying nutrients directly, it supplies the organisms that cycle
nutrients. Bacteria and fungi decompose organic matter, converting
nutrients from unavailable forms into ionic, plant-available forms.
Protozoa and nematodes graze on bacteria and fungi, releasing nutrients
in microbial biomass as plant-available forms. This biological cycling is
slower but more sustained, more efficient, and less prone to losses.

Food sources added during brewing contribute to nutrient content. Molasses
provides potassium and micronutrients. Kelp meal supplies trace minerals
and growth hormones. Fish hydrolysate contributes nitrogen, phosphorus,
and amino acids. Humic acids chelate mineral nutrients, keeping them in
solution. The combination, processed through microbial metabolism during
brewing, produces a biologically complex liquid providing both immediate
nutrient availability and microbial tools for sustained cycling.

## Quality Control and Testing

The biological quality of compost tea can vary enormously. Regular testing
is essential for anyone relying on tea as a primary management tool.

Direct microscopy, advocated by Ingham and the Soil Foodweb Institute,
remains the most practical assessment method. Using a compound microscope
at 400x magnification, a trained operator can estimate bacterial and
fungal biomass, protozoan populations, and community health within
minutes. This allows real-time process adjustment: increasing aeration if
oxygen is low, modifying food sources if fungal biomass is insufficient,
or discarding batches showing anaerobic contamination.

Plate count methods, culturing organisms on nutrient media and counting
colonies, have significant limitations. They detect typically less than
one percent of bacteria and less than ten percent of fungi, biasing
toward fast-growing, easily cultured species. The organisms most
important for soil health, including slow-growing fungi and acidophilic
bacteria, are poorly represented. Direct microscopy provides a more
complete picture.

Molecular methods including DNA sequencing and quantitative PCR are
becoming more accessible. They provide detailed taxonomic composition
data but remain relatively expensive and require specialized expertise.
For most practitioners, direct microscopy offers the best balance of
speed, cost, and actionable information.

Key quality parameters include pleasant earthy smell (never putrid or
sour), dissolved oxygen above 5.5 ppm throughout the brew, appropriate
biomass levels for the intended crop, and adequate protozoan populations.
Regular monitoring allows process refinement over time.

## Common Pitfalls and Troubleshooting

Despite apparent simplicity, compost tea production has many pitfalls.

Anaerobic conditions during brewing are the most frequent problem. If
aeration is inadequate or too much food is added, dissolved oxygen drops
below 5.5 ppm, facultative anaerobes dominate, and the tea develops
foul odors and harmful metabolites. Anaerobic tea should always be
discarded. Solutions include ensuring adequate aeration, using food at
recommended rates, and monitoring dissolved oxygen with a meter.

Poor-quality compost is another common problem. Immature, anaerobic, or
biologically impoverished compost produces poor-quality tea. Testing
before use is the best defense. Vermicompost is often recommended because
it is rich in bacterial diversity and biologically mature, though it
tends to be bacterial-dominated and may need fungal food supplementation.

Chlorinated water kills organisms. Municipal chlorine can be removed by
pre-aeration or ascorbic acid. Chloramine requires specialized filtration.
A simple test: add compost extract to water in a glass; if no foam
develops within minutes, residual chlorine is likely present.

Overfeeding, particularly with molasses, causes bacterial blooms so rapid
they deplete oxygen. Use 1 to 2 tablespoons of unsulfured molasses per 5
gallons, adjusted for the specific system. Never use sulfured molasses
as the preservative is antimicrobial.

Applying at wrong times reduces effectiveness. Avoid midday heat and UV
exposure. Do not apply to saturated soils. Do not combine with synthetic
chemicals. Wait at least two weeks after chemical applications before
applying tea.

## Integration with Broader Soil Management

Compost tea is most effective as one component of a comprehensive
strategy. The soil food web requires multiple inputs and conditions that
tea alone cannot provide.

Organic matter addition through compost, mulch, and cover crops provides
substrate and habitat for soil organisms. Tea inoculates with organisms,
but without organic matter they have nothing to feed on and decline
rapidly. The two practices are synergistic: organic matter feeds
organisms, and organisms decompose organic matter and cycle its nutrients.

Reduced tillage or no-till is essential for maintaining biological
communities. Tillage disrupts fungal hyphae, destroys aggregates, and
oxidizes organic matter. In no-till systems, tea can be applied through
surface drenching or injection without mechanical disturbance, preserving
the networks that tea helps build.

Cover cropping provides living roots during fallow periods, maintaining
root exudate supply that feeds soil organisms. Tea applied at cover crop
termination accelerates decomposition and ensures released nutrients are
captured by beneficial organisms rather than lost to leaching.

Crop rotation and polyculture maintain diversity of root exudates and
organic inputs. Different plant species feed different microbial
communities. Diverse cropping supports diverse soil biology, providing
more robust nutrient cycling and disease suppression.

## The Future of Biological Extracts

Several emerging trends are shaping this field. Defined microbial
inoculants combining specific selected strains are becoming more common.
Unlike compost tea's uncharacterized mixture, defined products contain
known strains with documented functions. While more consistent, they lack
the ecological diversity and resilience of complex communities. The most
promising approach combines defined inoculants with compost tea, using
defined products for specific functions and tea for broader ecological
context.

Molecular tools are increasingly used to characterize tea communities.
DNA methods identify which organisms are present, how abundances change
during brewing, and how conditions affect final composition. This enables
standardized protocols that consistently produce desired characteristics,
reducing variability.

Precision agriculture integration is emerging. Sensors and analytics can
monitor soil biological conditions in real time, applying tea precisely
when and where needed. This improves efficiency, reduces waste, and
ensures biological inputs target situations of greatest impact.

The principles underlying compost tea have implications far beyond
agriculture. The soil food web is central to ecosystem function at every
scale. As climate change, biodiversity loss, and soil degradation
threaten agricultural productivity, biological soil management offers a
path toward more resilient and sustainable food systems.

## Conclusion

Compost tea and biological extracts represent a practical application of
decades of soil microbial ecology research. By extracting, multiplying,
and applying beneficial soil food web organisms, these preparations offer
a powerful tool for restoring degraded soils, suppressing disease,
reducing synthetic input dependence, and building long-term soil health.
The science is well established, methods are accessible at every scale,
and benefits have been demonstrated across diverse crops, climates, and
soil types. Success requires attention to detail in compost selection,
aerobic brewing conditions, and integration with broader practices
including organic matter addition, reduced tillage, cover cropping, and
crop diversity. Compost tea is not merely a product but a practice: a way
of working with soil biology rather than against it, and a step toward
the ecologically informed agriculture that the twenty-first century
demands.
