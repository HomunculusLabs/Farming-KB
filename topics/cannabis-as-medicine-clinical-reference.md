# Cannabis as Medicine: A Clinical Reference

Cannabis has served as a therapeutic agent across cultures for millennia, yet
modern clinical science has only recently begun to unravel the pharmacological
sophistication underlying its medicinal properties. The discovery of the
endocannabinoid system (ECS) in the early 1990s, beginning with the
identification of anandamide by Raphael Mechoulam and colleagues in 1992,
provided a biological framework for understanding why a single plant genus can
exert effects across so many organ systems. The human body produces its own
cannabinoid-like signaling molecules, anandamide and 2-arachidonoylglycerol
(2-AG), which bind to cannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2 distributed throughout
the nervous, immune, cardiovascular, and reproductive systems. Phytocannabinoids
from cannabis mimic and modulate these endogenous pathways, offering a bridge
between plant-based medicine and the body's own regulatory machinery.

Uwe Blesching's *The Cannabis Health Index* (CHI) synthesizes evidence from
hundreds of peer-reviewed studies into an evidence-rating system quantifying
therapeutic confidence across more than 100 conditions. Conditions with the
highest CHI scores include cancer-related symptoms (CHI 100, 50 studies),
multiple sclerosis (CHI 91, 26 studies), and inflammatory diseases broadly
(CHI 79, 28 studies). Even conditions with more modest scores, such as
Alzheimer's disease (CHI 10) or insomnia (CHI 5), reveal intriguing
mechanistic data warranting further investigation. A critical theme across all
therapeutic domains is dose precision: cannabis exhibits biphasic dose-response
characteristics where the same cannabinoid that relieves anxiety at a moderate
dose may provoke it at a high dose. This biphasic behavior, combined with
significant inter-individual variability, makes personalized titration
essential and the concept of the "subjective therapeutic window" arguably the
single most important principle in medical cannabis practice.

## Cannabinoid Pharmacology: THC, CBD, and Beyond

The two most studied phytocannabinoids, tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and
cannabidiol (CBD), have profoundly different pharmacological profiles that
complement one another in clinical practice. THC, whose chemical structure was
elucidated by Mechoulam and Yechiel Gaoni in 1964, binds relatively equally to
both CB1 and CB2 receptors. Through CB1 activation in the central nervous
system, THC produces its characteristic psychoactive effects alongside
analgesic, antiemetic, bronchodilatory, and appetite-stimulating properties.
Through CB2 activation in immune cells, THC modulates inflammatory signaling.
Notably, CB1 receptors are absent from the medulla oblongata, the brain region
regulating respiration, which means cannabinoid-based pain management does not
carry the fatal respiratory depression risk of opioid analgesics.

CBD, by contrast, has a higher affinity for CB2 receptors than CB1 and is
non-psychoactive. Much of CBD's therapeutic influence stems from its ability
to inhibit fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH), the enzyme that degrades
anandamide, thereby extending the activity of the body's endogenous "bliss
molecule." CBD also acts as a negative allosteric modulator at CB1, meaning it
reduces THC's psychoactive intensity while allowing patients to access THC's
other therapeutic properties at higher concentrations. This modulatory
relationship makes CBD an essential component of many clinical protocols.

Beyond THC and CBD, the cannabis plant produces more than 111 cannabinoids and
200 to 250 additional constituents including terpenoids, flavonoids, and other
phytochemicals. (E)-beta-caryophyllene (BCP), a sesquiterpene found both in
cannabis and in common spices such as black pepper (up to 35% BCP), cloves
(7-19%), cinnamon, basil, and oregano, functions as a dietary cannabinoid that
directly activates CB2 receptors without psychoactive effects. BCP has
demonstrated protective activity against inflammation, pain, and cancer in
preclinical models, and its FDA-approved status as a food additive makes it an
accessible adjunct to cannabis-based therapy.

## The Endocannabinoid System and Endogenous Cannabinoids

Anandamide (N-arachidonoylethanolamine, AEA) derives its name from the Sanskrit
word *ananda*, meaning joy or bliss. Discovered in 1992, anandamide binds
primarily to CB1 receptors and modulates pain, appetite, memory, mood, and the
sleep-wake cycle. It is synthesized on demand and rapidly degraded by FAAH,
producing short-lived, localized effects. Genetic variations in FAAH can produce
significant inter-individual differences in endocannabinoid tone. 2-AG,
identified in 1995, is present at higher concentrations, serves as a full
agonist at both CB1 and CB2, and plays a major role in immune function. When
cannabis is consumed, THC binds directly to cannabinoid receptors while CBD
inhibits anandamide degradation, boosting the body's own endocannabinoid levels.
Clinical conditions such as epilepsy (lower anandamide in temporal lobe patients)
and multiple sclerosis (reduced cerebrospinal endocannabinoids) suggest that
endocannabinoid deficiency may underlie certain disease states.

## The Entourage Effect and Whole-Plant Medicine

Blesching emphasizes that cannabis is not a single-molecule medicine but a
complex botanical pharmacopoeia. The entourage effect describes the synergistic
interplay among cannabinoids, terpenes, and other plant constituents that
produces therapeutic outcomes greater than the sum of isolated compounds. THC
and CBD work together: CBD tames THC's psychoactivity while amplifying
therapeutic properties. A low-dose THC-morphine combination produces synergistic
analgesic effects while reducing opiate-related risks. Whole-plant cannabis
extracts have outperformed isolated cannabinoids in breast cancer studies,
delivering broader therapeutic effects alongside equivalent anti-tumor activity.

Terpene profiles further differentiate therapeutic applications. Myrcene-dominant
strains enhance sedative and muscle-relaxant effects; limonene-dominant strains
elevate mood and reduce anxiety; pinene-dominant strains promote alertness and
bronchodilation; linalool-dominant strains support calming and sleep; and
caryophyllene-dominant strains provide anti-inflammatory benefits through CB2
activation. The sativa versus indica distinction, while botanically imprecise,
has practical clinical relevance: sativa strains typically present higher
THC-to-CBD ratios with stimulating effects suited to daytime use, while indica
strains present lower THC-to-CBD ratios with sedating, relaxing properties
suited to evening use and anxiety management.

## Administration Methods and Pharmacokinetics

The route of administration determines onset time, duration, bioavailability,
and the ratio of active compounds reaching systemic circulation. Vaporization
offers the fastest onset (one to five minutes), bioavailability of 10 to 35
percent, and duration of two to four hours, with temperature control allowing
selective extraction: THC vaporizes at 157 degrees Celsius, CBD at 160 degrees,
beta-caryophyllene at 180 degrees, and the optimal broad-spectrum range at
approximately 190 degrees. It is preferred for acute symptoms because rapid
onset allows precise dose titration.

Oral ingestion delivers cannabinoids through the GI tract to the liver, where
first-pass metabolism produces the more psychoactive 11-hydroxy-THC metabolite.
Onset is delayed (30 to 120 minutes) but duration extends to four to eight
hours, ideal for sustained relief. Bioavailability is lowest at four to 20
percent. Sublingual tinctures offer a middle ground with onset of 15 to 45
minutes, bioavailability of 15 to 30 percent, and duration of three to six
hours. Topical application provides localized effects without systemic
psychoactivity. Rectal suppositories achieve the highest bioavailability (50 to
70 percent) with minimal psychoactive effects. Raw cannabis juice delivers
non-psychoactive THCA and CBDA along with concentrated terpenes, allowing
high-dose anti-inflammatory therapy without intoxication.

## Pain Management: Chronic Pain, Neuropathy, and Spasticity

Pain management represents one of the most common reasons patients seek
medical cannabis, supported by evidence across multiple pain subtypes. Chronic
non-malignant pain, defined as non-cancerous pain lasting longer than three
months, affects approximately 17 percent of the population. A 2008 study from
Hadassah-Hebrew University found that oral THC (Dronabinol) provided adequate
response in five of 13 patients with chronic non-malignant pain unresponsive to
conventional pharmacotherapy, leading researchers to conclude that oral THC may
be a valuable option for selected patients.

The endocannabinoid system modulates pain through multiple pathways. CB1
receptors in the central nervous system regulate pain signaling, while CB2
receptors in peripheral immune tissue modulate inflammatory pain. A 2008
Polish study demonstrated that cannabinoid agonists reduce pain sensitivity in
a dose-dependent fashion and that COX-1 inhibitors such as indomethacin may
increase cannabinoid analgesic properties at low dosages, suggesting potential
NSAID-cannabinoid synergy that could allow lower doses of both medication
classes.

The San Diego dose-response study provides critical evidence for the
biphasic nature of cannabinoid analgesia. Using three THC concentrations (2%,
4%, 8%), researchers found that medium doses significantly decreased
capsaicin-induced pain at 45 minutes, while high doses significantly increased
pain perception. This counterintuitive finding underscores that more is not
better in cannabis therapeutics. Cannabinoids also reduce inflammation and can
produce synergy when combined with reduced amounts of opiates, lowering both
adverse effects and habit-forming potential.

Multiple sclerosis provides one of the strongest evidence bases for cannabis in
pain and spasticity management, with a CHI value of 91 from 26 studies. Beyond
spasticity reduction, cannabis improved neuropathic pain, depression, anxiety,
paresthesia, sleep disturbances, and urge incontinence in MS patients. Sativex,
a THC:CBD oromucosal spray, demonstrated sustained efficacy over two years
without evidence of tolerance development. Italian researchers found
significantly reduced endocannabinoid levels in the cerebrospinal fluid of MS
patients, suggesting that cannabinoid deficiency may contribute to disease
pathogenesis.

## Oncology: Anti-Tumor Properties and Symptom Management

The evidence for cannabis in oncology spans two distinct domains: direct
anti-tumor activity and palliative symptom management, with a CHI value of 100
from 50 studies. CBD produces anti-tumor activity by selectively generating
oxidative stress in cancer cells, triggering apoptosis without harming normal
cells. In breast cancer, CBD was identified as the most potent anti-cancer
substance in a 2006 Italian study comparing five cannabinoids to whole plant
extract, and a 2007 San Francisco study found it to be the first nontoxic agent
that significantly decreases metastatic aggressiveness. THC blocks breast cancer
cell cycle progression through CB2 activation, and anandamide was shown to
block human breast cancer cell growth as early as 1998.

In glioblastoma (five-year survival rate of 2 percent), cannabinoids have shown
remarkable preclinical results: the CB2 agonist JWH-133 reduced malignant tumor
size, cannabinoids inhibited tumor blood supply signals, and THC was safely
injected into human brain tumors without psychoactive effects. THC and CBD act
synergistically to inhibit glioblastoma cell growth through reactive oxygen
species induction.

For symptom management, four decades of evidence consistently show cannabinoids
superior to conventional antiemetics for chemotherapy-induced nausea and
vomiting. Nabilone outperformed prochlorperazine in multiple studies. Oral THC
completely prevented vomiting in pediatric chemotherapy patients (Jerusalem,
1995). Inhaled cannabis helped 78 percent of cancer patients unresponsive to
standard antiemetics. Nabilone also effectively treats cancer-induced night
sweats, improving sleep quality while reducing pain, anorexia, and nausea.

## Neurological Conditions: Epilepsy, Alzheimer's, and Neuroprotection

The endocannabinoid system plays a central role in maintaining the
excitatory/inhibitory balance within the central nervous system, making it
directly relevant to seizure disorders. CBD has demonstrated anticonvulsant
properties in animal models and reduced seizure frequency in human trials. The
2005 Leiden/Holland/Rome study of treatment-resistant pediatric patients
receiving oil-based CBD showed that every patient responded positively, with
seizure improvement equal to or greater than 25 percent and clear improvement
in consciousness and spasticity. The 2018 FDA approval of Epidiolex
(cannabidiol) for Lennox-Gastaut syndrome and Dravet syndrome, two severe
treatment-resistant childhood epilepsies, represented a landmark validation.
In Phase 3 trials, patients receiving Epidiolex experienced median seizure
frequency reductions of 37 to 44 percent compared to 17 to 22 percent with
placebo.

CBD's anticonvulsant mechanisms involve multiple pathways: enhancement of
5-HT1A serotonin receptor signaling, positive allosteric modulation of GABA-A
receptors, GPR55 antagonism, adenosine reuptake inhibition, and TRPV1 channel
activation. THC's anticonvulsant effects are mediated primarily through CB1
receptor activation, which reduces glutamate release and neuronal excitability,
though at higher doses THC can be proconvulsant, which is why CBD has become
the primary cannabinoid for epilepsy treatment. Patients with temporal lobe
epilepsy show significantly lower anandamide levels, suggesting endocannabinoid
deficiency as a contributing factor.

In Alzheimer's disease, cannabinoid research has revealed multiple protective
mechanisms. A 2005 Spanish study found that cannabinoid receptors are important
in Alzheimer's pathology and that cannabinoids succeed in preventing the
neurodegenerative process. A 2009 experiment demonstrated that the CB2 agonist
JWH-015 induced removal of native beta-amyloid from frozen human tissue.
Nabilone, administered to an Alzheimer's patient with agitation and aggression
unresponsive to conventional medications, produced dramatic symptom reduction
without side effects. The synthetic cannabinoid WIN55,212-2 prevented
beta-amyloid-induced microglial activation, cognitive impairment, and neuronal
marker loss in rodent models.

Cannabinoids exhibit broad neuroprotective properties documented in U.S.
Patent #6,630,507 ("Cannabinoids as Antioxidants and Neuroprotectants"),
which found that cannabinoids exceed the antioxidant capacity of vitamins C and
E in standard assays. CBD and THC both demonstrate neuroprotective effects
against glutamate-induced neurotoxicity through mechanisms independent of
CB1/CB2 receptor activation. CBD promotes hippocampal neurogenesis, reduces
pro-inflammatory cytokine release from activated microglia, and shifts
microglia from pro-inflammatory (M1) to anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes.
These properties have implications for conditions ranging from stroke and
traumatic brain injury to Parkinson's and Huntington's diseases.

## Psychiatry: Anxiety, PTSD, and Mood Disorders

The ECS modulates anxiety and mood through interactions with GABA, glutamate,
serotonin, dopamine, cortisol, and oxytocin. Its dual capacity to balance GABA
(inhibitory) and glutamate (excitatory) neurotransmission is one of its most
promising features for psychiatric conditions. Low GABA levels are associated
with anxiety and panic, while excessive glutamate contributes to neural
excitation and inflammation. CBD has demonstrated anxiolytic effects without
the sedation and dependence risks of benzodiazepines. A 2005 study found
chronic cannabinoid treatment promoted hippocampal neurogenesis, producing
anxiolytic and antidepressant-like effects. CBD reduced all stress responses
including anxiety, elevated blood pressure, and rapid heart rate in a 2009
Sao Paulo study.

Anxiety disorders affect approximately 20 percent of the U.S. population. The
dose-response relationship is critical: low to moderate THC doses produce
anxiolysis, while higher doses trigger anxiety and panic. Indica-dominant
strains with lower THC-to-CBD ratios are generally preferred. Blesching frames
anxiety as one of the "constricting emotions" alongside fear, anger, guilt,
and depression, contrasting with "expansive emotions" such as bliss, relaxation,
love, and curiosity. Anandamide deficiency may predispose toward constricting
patterns, and cannabinoid therapy combined with practices cultivating expansive
emotions may produce more sustained relief.

PTSD represents a condition where ECS emotional modulation is particularly
relevant. Nabilone is FDA-approved for treating PTSD. An Ottawa study found 72
percent of patients experienced either cessation of nightmares or significant
reduction in intensity. Cannabinoid therapy may modulate memory consolidation
and extinction processes relevant to traumatic memories. Epigenetic research
shows traumatic emotions produce physiological changes transmissible to the next
generation, underscoring the importance of early intervention.

## Inflammation, Autoimmunity, and Immune Modulation

Inflammatory diseases represent one of the strongest evidence bases for
cannabis therapeutics, with a CHI value of 79 from 28 studies. CB2 receptors
on immune cells (B-cells, T-cells, NK cells, macrophages) suppress excessive
inflammatory signaling when activated. CBD modulates the NF-kB pathway, a
master regulator of inflammatory responses, and has demonstrated measurable
therapeutic impact on inflammatory bowel disease, arthritis, periodontitis, and
atherosclerosis. The relationship is bidirectional: cannabinoids suppress
overactive immune responses in autoimmune conditions while supporting immune
competence against infection.

(E)-beta-caryophyllene from common spices provides accessible, non-psychoactive
CB2-mediated immune modulation. BCP ameliorates cisplatin-induced
nephrotoxicity in a CB2-dependent manner. Additional anti-inflammatory spices
include cayenne (peripheral circulation for chronic aches), garlic
(thrombophlebitis), myrrh (arthritis), nigella/black seed (experimentally
induced arthritis), and turmeric (arthritis, psoriasis, atherosclerosis). These
represent an inexpensive complement to cannabis therapy.

## Cardiovascular Health and Cardioprotection

Endocannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2 naturally reside in heart tissue, and the
endogenous cardiac cannabinoid system provides intrinsic cardioprotection
through CB2 activation. Cannabinoids influence vasodilation, cardiac protection,
blood pressure control, and inhibition of endothelial inflammation and
atherosclerosis progression. THC and CBD significantly reduced heart attack
size in mice (2004). THC protects heart cells against hypoxia through nitric
oxide induction (2006). CBD produced substantial cardioprotective effects from
ischemia in rats (2007). CBD calms autonomic stress responses including rapid
heart rate and elevated blood pressure (2009). A 2007 New Zealand meta-analysis
confirmed cannabinoids' positive influence across multiple cardiovascular
parameters. CB2-specific activation via CBD or caryophyllene-containing spices
offers cardioprotection without psychoactive effects.

## Sleep, Appetite, and Metabolic Regulation

The 2004 Farnborough study revealed a self-balancing mechanism between CBD and
THC: dose-dependent THC produces sedative effects while CBD produces alerting
properties. This complementary relationship suggests whole-plant preparations
may provide more balanced sleep effects than isolated cannabinoids. Cannabis
has demonstrated sleep benefits in cancer patients (reducing night sweats),
fibromyalgia (Nabilone superior to amitriptyline), MS (reduced sleep
disturbances alongside reduced spasticity), and PTSD (72 percent nightmare
reduction with Nabilone).

THC stimulates appetite through hypothalamic CB1 activation, ghrelin signaling
potentiation, temporary leptin resistance, enhanced olfactory sensitivity, and
gastric relaxation. These mechanisms have profound implications for cachexia
in cancer, HIV/AIDS, heart failure, COPD, and chronic kidney disease. Dronabinol
is FDA-approved for HIV/AIDS wasting. Blesching notes the irony that the same
ECS mechanism useful for appetite stimulation in wasting conditions can
contribute to metabolic dysfunction when chronically overactivated, with obesity
described as a state of hypothalamic endocannabinoid overactivity.

## Dosing Guidelines and the Therapeutic Window

The therapeutic window concept is the single most important principle in
medical cannabis practice. Unlike most pharmaceuticals that follow a simple
dose-response curve, cannabis cannabinoids frequently display biphasic
(U-shaped) dose-response patterns where excessive doses reverse the desired
therapeutic benefit. The window is governed by three factors: the body's
present endocannabinoid state, the cannabinoid profile in the medicine, and
the form in which it is consumed.

For inhalation, Blesching recommends taking one short to medium inhalation,
waiting five to ten minutes, assessing the effect, and repeating until the
"sweet spot" is found, then stopping. The line between effective dose and
adverse effect is described as very thin. For oral preparations, patients
should use an exact, measured amount (preferably on an empty stomach), wait at
least one hour (two if full stomach), increase by the same measured amount if
needed, and record the total. The most common mistake with oral cannabis is
overconsumption due to impatience with the delayed onset. For tinctures, start
with three to four drops, wait at least one hour between increases, and
re-titrate with each new bottle since most products are not standardized.

Microdosing, defined as sub-threshold doses below the level of noticeable
psychoactive effects, has emerged as a strategy for maintaining therapeutic
benefits including pain relief, mood stabilization, and improved focus without
cognitive impairment. Tolerance develops to many cannabinoid effects over time
through CB1 receptor downregulation, requiring dose adjustments or planned
tolerance breaks of several days to weeks. The San Diego dose-response study
remains the definitive demonstration: medium THC doses reduced pain while high
doses increased it, proving that finding the right dose is often more
important than finding the right strain.

## Safety, Addiction, and Precautions

Between 1997 and 2005, no deaths were attributed to the exclusive use of
cannabis, while the FDA recorded 10,008 deaths from 17 comparable
FDA-approved pharmaceuticals. The median lethal oral THC dose in rats is 800
to 1,900 mg/kg, extrapolated to approximately 14 kilograms of herbal cannabis
for a 70-kilogram human. A 2004 study estimated that 628 kilograms would need
to be smoked in 15 minutes to induce a lethal effect. As former U.S. Surgeon
General Joycelyn Elders stated: "Unlike many of the drugs we prescribe every
day, marijuana has never been proven to cause a fatal overdose."

When dependency occurs, it is psychological rather than physical, unlike
tobacco, alcohol, and heroin. Risk can be mitigated by infusing mindfulness
into the healing process and using raw, non-psychoactive preparations.
Adolescent use warrants particular caution: a Duke University study following
New Zealand residents for 38 years found that while adult cannabis use had no
effect on intelligence, cannabis dependency in adolescents may correlate with
reduced IQ scores later in life, though the study had limitations including
subjective data and small sample size. No long-term studies examining exclusive
cannabis use on fertility, fetal development, or adolescent neurodevelopment
have been conducted.

Common adverse effects include increased appetite, reduced attention span,
dry mouth, and decreased cognitive and motor skills. At higher doses, patients
may experience ataxia, anxiety, slight heart rate increase, and unusual
perceptions. CBD interacts with cytochrome P450 enzymes (particularly CYP3A4
and CYP2C19), which can increase blood levels of co-administered medications
including antiepileptic drugs such as clobazam and valproate. Clinicians should
monitor for drug interactions when cannabis is added to existing medication
regimens, particularly for patients on medications metabolized by the CYP450
system.
