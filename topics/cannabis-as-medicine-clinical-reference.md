## Introduction — Cannabis in the History of Medicine

Cannabis has served as a therapeutic agent across cultures for millennia.
Ancient Chinese, Indian, Egyptian, and Greco-Roman medical texts document its use for pain, inflammation, gastrointestinal
distress, and reproductive health. The Chinese emperor Shen Nung reportedly prescribed cannabis for gout, rheumatism, and malaria
around 2700 BCE.
In nineteenth-century Europe and North America, cannabis tinctures were standard pharmacy items. Queen Victoria's personal
physician, Sir Russell Reynolds, prescribed cannabis for menstrual cramps and wrote in The Lancet that 'when pure and administered
carefully, it is one of the most valuable medicines we possess.'
Prohibition in the twentieth century abruptly halted clinical research. The Controlled Substances Act of 1970 classified cannabis
as Schedule I in the United States, declaring it to have no accepted medical use and a high potential for abuse — a classification
that contradicted millennia of therapeutic practice.
Modern clinical science has only recently begun to unravel the pharmacological sophistication underlying cannabis medicine. The
discovery of the [[endocannabinoid-system]] in the early 1990s provided a biological framework for understanding why a single plant
genus can exert therapeutic effects across so many organ systems.

## The Endocannabinoid System — Biological Foundation

The endocannabinoid system (ECS) is a lipid-signaling network discovered in the early 1990s, beginning with the identification of
[[anandamide]] by Raphael Mechoulam and colleagues in 1992. Anandamide, named from the Sanskrit word for bliss, was the first
endogenous cannabinoid identified.
The ECS comprises three core components: endogenous ligands (anandamide and 2-arachidonoylglycerol), cannabinoid receptors (CB1
and CB2), and the enzymes that synthesize and degrade these ligands (FAAH and MAGL).
CB1 receptors are densely concentrated in the central nervous system, particularly in areas governing memory, motor coordination,
appetite, pain perception, and emotional processing. CB2 receptors are found primarily on immune cells, in the gastrointestinal
tract, and in peripheral tissues involved in inflammation.
The human body produces its own cannabinoid-like signaling molecules — anandamide and 2-AG — which bind to these receptors
distributed throughout the nervous, immune, cardiovascular, and reproductive systems.
Phytocannabinoids from cannabis mimic and modulate these endogenous pathways, offering a bridge between plant-based medicine and
the body's own regulatory machinery. This explains cannabis's remarkably broad therapeutic profile.

## Cannabinoid Pharmacology — THC, CBD, and Minor Cannabinoids

The two most studied phytocannabinoids, [[tetrahydrocannabinol]] (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD), have profoundly different
pharmacological profiles that complement one another in clinical practice.
THC, whose chemical structure was elucidated by Mechoulam and Gaoni in 1964, binds relatively equally to both CB1 and CB2
receptors, initiating simultaneous changes in the central nervous system, immune function, and autonomic regulation.
CBD is non-psychoactive and has greater affinity for CB2 receptors than CB1. Much of its therapeutic influence stems from its
ability to suppress the [[enzyme]] FAAH that breaks down anandamide, keeping the bliss molecule active at higher concentrations and
for longer duration.
CBD also acts as a negative allosteric modulator at CB1, taming THC's psychoactive effects while allowing patients to access THC's
therapeutic powers at higher concentrations. This modulation is central to the concept of whole-plant synergy.
Beyond THC and CBD, cannabis produces over 100 identified cannabinoids. Cannabinol (CBN) forms as THC degrades over time and
demonstrates notable sedative properties. [[cannabigerol]] (CBG) shows promise as an antibacterial agent and intraocular pressure
reducer. Tetrahydrocannabivarin (THCV) may act as an appetite suppressant at low doses.

## The Entourage Effect — Whole-Plant Synergy

[[uwe-blesching]] emphasizes in The [[cannabis-health-index]] that cannabis is not a single-molecule medicine but a complex botanical with
111-plus cannabinoids and 200 to 250 additional constituents including terpenoids.
The entourage effect describes how these compounds work together to produce greater therapeutic efficacy than any isolated
constituent. THC and CBD work together: CBD tames THC's psychoactive effects while amplifying therapeutic properties.
CBD acts as a negative allosteric modulator at CB1, allowing patients to access THC's therapeutic powers at higher concentrations
without overwhelming psychoactivity.
Terpenes play a crucial role in this synergy. Myrcene, the most common cannabis terpene, produces sedating and muscle-relaxing
effects. Limonene elevates mood and may counteract anxiety. Beta-caryophyllene, found in black pepper and cloves, directly
activates CB2 receptors and functions as a dietary cannabinoid.
This complexity explains why patients frequently report that whole-plant cannabis provides better symptom relief than isolated
synthetic cannabinoids like dronabinol or nabilone.

## The Therapeutic Window — Dosing Principles

Blesching identifies the subjective therapeutic window as arguably the single most important principle in medical cannabis
practice. The window is governed by three factors: the body's present endocannabinoid state, the cannabinoid profile in the
medicine, and the form of consumption.
Too little cannabis produces sub-optimal therapeutic effect. Too much can actually increase the very symptoms a patient is
attempting to treat — using cannabis to reduce anxiety but taking too much can rapidly multiply anxiety.
For inhalation, the protocol is straightforward: take one short to medium inhalation, wait five to ten minutes, assess whether the
desired effect was achieved, and repeat if necessary. The line between effective dose and adverse effect is remarkably thin.
For oral ingestion, onset is delayed 45 minutes to 1.5 hours, with longer duration and more body-focused effects. Patients must
resist the common mistake of redosing before the initial dose has taken full effect.
CB1 deficiency states (neurological and emotional conditions) tend to respond better to sativa-dominant strains with higher
THC-to-CBD ratios. CB2 deficiency states (immune and inflammatory conditions) tend to respond better to indica-dominant strains
with more balanced cannabinoid profiles.

## Methods of Administration and Delivery

Different routes of administration produce distinct pharmacokinetic profiles that affect onset time, duration, intensity, and the
ratio of physical to mental effects.
Inhalation — whether smoking or vaporization — provides the fastest onset (minutes) and easiest titration but shortest duration
(two to four hours). Vaporization is preferred in clinical settings because it avoids combustion byproducts while delivering
cannabinoids efficiently.
Oral ingestion produces delayed onset (45 to 90 minutes) but longer duration (six to eight hours) and more intense body-focused
effects. Oral THC metabolizes to 11-hydroxy-THC in the liver, a metabolite roughly five times more potent than delta-9-THC, which
explains why edibles can feel overwhelming even at modest doses.
Sublingual and oromucosal delivery — as used by Sativex — offers a middle ground: onset within 15 to 40 minutes, moderate
duration, and the ability to swallow excess for extended GI absorption.
Topical application delivers cannabinoids to specific localized problem areas with minimal systemic effects, useful for arthritis,
muscle pain, and skin conditions. Transdermal patches provide sustained systemic delivery over many hours.

## Pain Management — Chronic and Neuropathic Pain

Chronic non-malignant pain is one of the most common reasons patients seek medical cannabis. Studies from the Medical University
of Warsaw demonstrate that cannabinoid agonists activate both CB1 and CB2 receptors, reducing pain sensitivity in a dose-dependent
fashion.
Research suggests potential synergistic interactions between cannabinoids and NSAIDs. COX-1 inhibitors like indomethacin may
enhance cannabinoid analgesic properties at low dosages, potentially allowing lower doses of both medication classes.
Neuropathic pain — caused by nerve damage and often resistant to conventional analgesics including opioids — shows particular
responsiveness to cannabinoid therapy. CB1 receptors on pain-signaling pathways in the spinal cord and brain modulate pain
transmission directly.
A case study from Hadassah-Hebrew University Medical Center found that oral dronabinol provided adequate pain relief for five of
thirteen patients with chronic non-malignant pain unresponsive to conventional pharmacotherapy.
Cannabis also offers an alternative to opioid therapy. THC in low-dose combination with morphine produces synergistic analgesic
effects while reducing the total opioid dose required — a strategy with significant implications amid the opioid crisis.

## Multiple Sclerosis and Neurological Conditions

Multiple sclerosis represents one of the strongest evidence bases for medical cannabis, with a CHI confidence value of 91 derived
from 26 studies reviewed by Blesching — one of the largest bodies of evidence for any single condition.
Cannabis reduces spasticity, neuropathic pain, depression, anxiety, paresthesia, sleep disturbances, urge incontinence, and
nocturnal polyuria in MS patients. Research from Perugia, Italy found significantly reduced endocannabinoid levels in the
cerebrospinal fluid of MS patients compared to controls.
Sativex, a whole-plant cannabis extract containing THC and CBD in a one-to-one ratio, is specifically approved for MS spasticity
in over 25 countries. Clinical trials at the Walton Centre and Royal Berkshire Hospital in the UK demonstrated significant
reductions in pain and spasms.
The U.S. federal government itself holds a patent on the neuroprotective properties of cannabinoids for treating diseases
involving inflammation and the nervous system — a remarkable contradiction with Schedule I classification.
Beyond MS, cannabis shows promise for epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, ALS, and Alzheimer's disease through
neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant mechanisms mediated by both CB1 and CB2 receptors.

## Cancer — Palliative Care and Anti-Tumor Research

Cannabis serves two distinct roles in oncology: palliative symptom management and potential direct anti-tumor activity. For
palliative care, the evidence is robust and well-established.
Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) has the longest and strongest evidence base of any cannabis therapeutic
application. The FDA has approved dronabinol and nabilone specifically for CINV unresponsive to conventional antiemetics.
Cannabis also stimulates appetite in cachexia and wasting syndromes, improves sleep quality during treatment, reduces
cancer-related pain, and alleviates anxiety and depression associated with diagnosis and treatment.
The anti-tumor potential is more speculative but increasingly supported by preclinical evidence. Cannabinoids demonstrate
anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects against numerous cancer cell lines in laboratory studies, including breast, brain,
colon, cervical, and bone cancer cells.
