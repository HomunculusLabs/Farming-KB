Ecological sanitation, commonly abbreviated as "ecosan," is a philosophy and set of
practices that treats human excrement and household wastewater not as hazardous
waste to be disposed of, but as a valuable resource to be recovered and recycled.
The core insight is disarmingly simple: the nutrients in human waste — nitrogen,
phosphorus, potassium, and trace minerals — are the same nutrients that plants
need to grow. By safely returning these nutrients to the soil, ecological
sanitation closes a loop that conventional sewerage and septic systems break
wide open, converting a pollution problem into a fertility opportunity.

The conventional approach to sanitation, dominant since the mid-nineteenth
century, mixes human waste with vast quantities of clean drinking water and
flushes it away to centralized treatment plants or septic leach fields. This
model wastes enormous volumes of water (roughly 30 percent of indoor household
use goes to flushing toilets), destroys the nutrient value of the waste, and
creates downstream pollution from treatment plant effluent. Ecological sanitation
offers an alternative: separate waste streams at the source, treat each
appropriately, and cycle the nutrients and water back into productive use.

In practice, ecological sanitation encompasses a family of integrated
technologies: composting toilets that transform humanure into safe soil
amendment, urine-diverting systems that capture liquid fertilizer, graywater
recycling that irrigates landscapes with shower and laundry water, and
[[comparison-constructed-wetlands-vs-bioswales]] that polish wastewater through biological filtration. These
systems can be deployed individually or combined into whole-household designs
that approach true zero-waste water cycling, used in settings ranging from
rural homesteads to eco-villages, national parks, and large-scale
sanitation projects worldwide.

The idea that human waste can be returned to soil is ancient. Chinese and
Japanese farmers practiced the careful collection and composting of "night soil"
for thousands of years, maintaining soil fertility on intensively farmed land
without synthetic fertilizers. Pre-industrial [[fukuoka-european-agriculture-observations]] also relied
on animal and human manure, and the phrase "muck is gold" reflected a practical
understanding of its value. These systems were not without risk — improper
handling of night soil caused disease — but they demonstrate that nutrient
recycling from human waste is not a fringe concept but a historically proven
practice.
## History and Origins

The modern era of sanitation took a different turn. The flush toilet, patented
by Alexander Cumming in 1775 and refined by Thomas Crapper in the late
[[mckenna-club-des-hachischins-and-nineteenth-century-literary-hashish]], offered an apparently clean and convenient way to dispose
of waste by washing it out of sight. Combined with sewer systems pioneered in
London and Paris, the flush toilet became synonymous with civilization itself.
The cost, however, was enormous: nutrient loss from agricultural soils, water
pollution from overloaded treatment plants, and a linear "take-make-dispose"
model that is fundamentally unsustainable.

The ecological sanitation movement emerged in response. In the 1990s,
researchers and practitioners in Sweden, Germany, and Switzerland began
developing urine-diverting toilets and source-separated sanitation systems
under the ecosan banner. The Swedish EPA and organizations like the Stockholm
Environment Institute promoted nutrient recovery as both an environmental
imperative and a development strategy. The United Nations Environment Programme
and the World Health Organization subsequently endorsed ecological sanitation
approaches for expanding sanitation access in the developing world.

Jenkins, a slate roofer from Pennsylvania whose self-published *The Humanure
Handbook* (first edition 1995, fourth edition 2019) made the case for
thermophilic composting of human waste with clarity, humor, and scientific
rigor. Jenkins demonstrated through decades of personal practice and laboratory
analysis that properly managed composting destroys pathogens and produces a safe,
