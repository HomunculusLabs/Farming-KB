## Earthbag Construction and Superadobe Building

Earthbag construction is one of the most accessible, affordable, and resilient
[[garden-hand-tools-solomon]], builders
can create structures that withstand earthquakes, floods, hurricanes, and fire.
The technique requires no specialized skills, no heavy machinery, and no
expensive materials. Fill material typically comes directly from the building
site, making earthbag construction one of the lowest-cost paths to permanent
shelter anywhere in the world.

The method produces walls of extraordinary thermal mass, keeping interiors
cool in summer and warm in winter through passive temperature regulation.
Walls are monolithic and flexible, distributing seismic forces across the
entire structure rather than concentrating them at joints. This flexibility
has been proven in real earthquakes: earthbag buildings in Nepal survived
major seismic events while surrounding conventional structures collapsed
entirely. The technique has been deployed for emergency shelters after
earthquakes, tsunamis, and military conflicts, as well as for permanent homes,
root cellars, garden walls, and community buildings.

Despite its simplicity, earthbag construction produces remarkably beautiful
structures. Curved walls, domes, vaults, and organic shapes are all
straightforward to build. The finished walls, when plastered with earth or
lime, have a sculptural quality that conventional framed construction cannot
match. For homesteaders, permaculture designers, and anyone seeking to build
their own home without a mortgage, earthbag construction represents one of
the most practical paths to owner-built shelter.

## History and Origins

The modern earthbag building technique was developed by Iranian-American
architect Nader Khalili in the 1980s and 1990s at his Cal-Earth Institute in
the Mojave Desert of California. Khalili was inspired by traditional Persian
adobe architecture and the ancient technique of using earth-filled forms for
military fortifications. His insight was to combine the ancient principle of
rammed earth with the modern availability of inexpensive polypropylene bags
and barbed wire as tensile reinforcement.

Khalili trademarked the term "superadobe" for his specific method of using
long tubular bags filled with damp earth, laid in courses with barbed wire
between them. He received approval from NASA for superadobe as a potential
building technique for lunar and Martian habitats, a testament to the method's
structural integrity and minimal material requirements. His Cal-Earth
Institute continues to teach the technique to this day, and his students have
spread earthbag building to every continent.

The basic principle of filling bags with earth for construction predates
Khalili by decades. Military sandbag fortifications have been used since the
19th century, and temporary flood barriers use the same principle. What
Khalili contributed was the systematic development of the technique as a
permanent building method, including the critical innovation of using barbed
wire between courses for tensile reinforcement. This simple addition
transforms a pile of dirt bags into a monolithic structure with genuine
engineering strength.

## Materials

The three essential materials for earthbag construction are bags, fill
material, and barbed wire. All three are inexpensive and widely available.
The total material cost for a small earthbag home of 600 to 800 square feet
can be as low as $2,000 to $5,000, depending on the region and the
finishes chosen. This makes earthbag one of the most affordable building
methods available anywhere in the world.

Bags are most commonly made of polypropylene, the same material used for
sandbags and grain sacks. Polypropylene is strong, resistant to rot, and
relatively inexpensive at $0.10 to $0.50 per bag. Standard sizes are 14 by
18 inches or 18 by 30 inches. Approximately 140 bags are needed per 100
square feet of wall, meaning a thousand bags can build about 700 square feet
of wall area. Burlap and recycled sacks can also be used, though they
degrade faster and must be plastered immediately.

Fill material should ideally come from the building site itself, making it
essentially free. The optimal mix is 25 to 30 percent clay with sand and
gravel aggregate. A simple field test involves dampening the soil, forming a
ball, and dropping it from waist height. The ball should hold its shape on
impact but shatter when stepped on. Too much clay produces shrinkage cracks;
too much sand produces unstable bags. Waste rock, gravel, volcanic scoria,
crushed shells, and any mineral material that compacts tightly can also be
used. Topsoil should be reserved for gardening rather than buried in walls.

Barbed wire serves as the primary tensile reinforcement. Two strands of
standard four-point barbed wire are laid between every course of bags. The
barbs grip both the bags above and below, preventing sliding and providing
the tensile strength that earth alone lacks. This is the key innovation that
transforms stacked bags into a structural wall system capable of resisting
earthquake forces.

## Soil Testing and Mix Design

Proper soil preparation is essential for durable earthbag walls. The ideal
earthbag fill is a well-graded mixture of clay, sand, and gravel. Clay
provides cohesion and binds the fill material together, while sand and
gravel provide compressive strength and reduce shrinkage. A mix with
roughly 25 to 30 percent clay content, 40 to 50 percent sand, and 20 to
30 percent gravel produces excellent results.
