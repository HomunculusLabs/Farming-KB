## Yeast Biology, Ecology, and Fermentation

Yeasts are among the most consequential microorganisms on Earth, yet they
remain poorly understood by most people who interact with them daily. Every
loaf of bread, every glass of wine, every bottle of beer owes its existence
to the metabolic labor of these single-celled fungi. Yeasts have been
domesticated for longer than virtually any other organism — archaeological
evidence from ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt suggests deliberate fermentation
of grain beverages more than 9,000 years ago. Despite this ancient

of mammals to the sap running beneath tree bark.

The scientific and cultural importance of yeasts extends far beyond the
kitchen and the brewery. [[saccharomyces-cerevisiae]] was the first eukaryotic
organism to have its genome fully sequenced, in 1996, and it remains one of
the most important model organisms in molecular biology. Yeasts are workhorses
of the biotechnology industry, producing insulin, hepatitis vaccines, and
industrial enzymes. They are emerging as platforms for biofuel production,
bioremediation of [[fungal-fruiting-body-mechanics-osmotic-pressure]], and
ethanol concentrations that would kill most microorganisms. This
tolerance, combined with their rapid growth and metabolic versatility,
explains why yeasts so frequently dominate sugar-rich environments like
ripening fruit, tree sap flows, and nectar.

## Taxonomic Diversity Across Two Phyla

Yeasts are distributed across both of the major fungal phyla,
Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, with the great majority of described
species belonging to the ascomycetes. Molecular phylogenetics,
particularly analysis of ribosomal RNA genes, has profoundly reshaped
our understanding of yeast evolutionary relationships in recent decades.
It is now clear that ascomycetous yeasts form a sister group to the
euascomycetes (the filamentous ascomycetes that produce fruiting
bodies) rather than representing a more primitive condition.

Among the ascomycetous yeasts, several major taxonomic orders are
recognized. Saccharomycetales contains the commercially most important
genera, including Saccharomyces itself, along with Pichia,
Kluyveromyces, Torulaspora, and Debaryomyces.
Schizosaccharomycetales contains the fission yeasts, most notably
Schizosaccharomyces pombe, which divides by medial splitting rather
than budding and serves as an important research model. The orders
Taphrinales, Pneumocystidiales, and Protomycetales contain more
divergent lineages, including the mammalian pathogen Pneumocystis,
once thought to be a protozoan.
