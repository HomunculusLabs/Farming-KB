## Greenhouse Gardening and Season Extension

**Greenhouse gardening and season extension** encompass the full spectrum of
techniques, structures, and management practices used to protect crops from
adverse weather and extend the period during which fresh food can be grown and
harvested. At its simplest, season extension might involve a few degrees of
frost protection from a spun-fabric row cover draped over a row of lettuce; at
its most sophisticated, it involves fully climate-controlled glasshouses
producing tomatoes and cucumbers through the darkest months of winter. Between
these extremes lies a rich continuum of low-tech, accessible approaches that
have been refined over centuries and that continue to evolve.

The fundamental principle behind all season extension is the creation of a
microclimate more favorable to plant growth than ambient conditions. This may
mean trapping solar heat to raise temperatures, blocking desiccating winds to
reduce moisture loss, or excluding pests to protect vulnerable seedlings. The
greenhouse effect -- the same phenomenon that warms the Earth's atmosphere --
is the physical basis for most protected cultivation: shortwave solar radiation
passes through a transparent cover, warms the soil and plants inside, and the
longwave thermal radiation that the interior emits cannot easily escape back
through the cover. The result is an enclosed space that is consistently warmer
than the surrounding environment, especially during daylight hours.

## History of Protected Cultivation

The history of protected cultivation stretches back to antiquity. The Roman
author Pliny the Elder described the practice of placing translucent sheets of
mica (lapis specularis) over cucumber beds to protect them from cold, and the
Roman emperor Tiberius was reportedly so fond of cucumbers that his gardeners
developed a wheeled cart system to move plants into sunlight by day and
indoors for warmth at night. K. D. White's study of Roman farming documents
that compost, green manures, mixed farming, and crop rotations were standard
knowledge two thousand years ago, establishing an agricultural sophistication
that included early forms of season extension.

The most significant pre-modern system emerged in France. Under Jean-Baptiste
[[la-quintinie]] at the royal potager of Versailles in the 1670s and 1680s,
sophisticated techniques were developed for producing out-of-season produce.
These reached their full expression with the Parisian maraichers (market
gardeners) between 1850 and 1900. Operating on plots of one to two acres within
Paris, the maraichers achieved four to eight harvests per year using 100 to 400
tons of horse manure per acre, glass-covered cold frames, and glass bell jars
called cloches. Hotbeds heated by decomposing manure produced fresh salads in
January. The system declined after 1915 as horses were replaced by automobiles,
but the techniques were documented by writers including Prince [[peter-kropotkin]]
and survived in old gardening books. [[eliot-coleman]] visited [[louis-savier]]'s
maraicher garden near Paris in 1974 and found the nineteenth-century techniques
still alive, adapted with lightweight rail tracks and self-propelled irrigation.

In the English-speaking world, the Victorian era saw elaborate glasshouses for
the wealthy, but the twentieth century brought mass-produced aluminum frames,
polyethylene film coverings, and high tunnel (hoop house) technology, which
dramatically reduced the cost per square foot of protected growing space.

## Types of Protected Structures

Protected cultivation structures exist on a spectrum from simple and temporary
to complex and permanent. The choice of structure depends on the grower's
climate, budget, scale, and production goals.

**Cold frames** are the simplest and most affordable season-extension tools:
bottomless boxes with a transparent lid, typically 3 to 4 feet wide and 6 to 8
feet long, with the back wall 12 to 18 inches tall and the front wall 6 to 10
inches tall to create a south-facing slope. They provide 10 to 20 degrees
Fahrenheit of frost protection and extend the growing season by 4 to 8 weeks
in both spring and fall. More plants are killed by overheating in cold frames
than by cold, making ventilation management critical.

**Low tunnels** (also called quick hoops) are made by bending 9-gauge galvanized
wire or PVC pipe into arches over raised beds and covering them with spun-bonded
row cover fabric or polyethylene film. They provide a few degrees of frost
protection and also serve as insect exclusion barriers.

Quonset-style or gothic-arch structures covered with polyethylene film.
