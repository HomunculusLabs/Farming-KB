    45|# Greenhouse Gardening and Season Extension
    46|
    47|**Greenhouse gardening and season extension** encompass the full spectrum of
    48|techniques, structures, and management practices used to protect crops from
    49|adverse weather and extend the period during which fresh food can be grown and
    50|harvested. At its simplest, season extension might involve a few degrees of
    51|frost protection from a spun-fabric row cover draped over a row of lettuce; at
    52|its most sophisticated, it involves fully climate-controlled glasshouses
    53|producing tomatoes and cucumbers through the darkest months of winter. Between
    54|these extremes lies a rich continuum of low-tech, accessible approaches that
    55|have been refined over centuries and that continue to evolve.
    56|
    57|The fundamental principle behind all season extension is the creation of a
    58|microclimate more favorable to plant growth than ambient conditions. This may
    59|mean trapping solar heat to raise temperatures, blocking desiccating winds to
    60|reduce moisture loss, or excluding pests to protect vulnerable seedlings. The
    61|greenhouse effect -- the same phenomenon that warms the Earth's atmosphere --
    62|is the physical basis for most protected cultivation: shortwave solar radiation
    63|passes through a transparent cover, warms the soil and plants inside, and the
    64|longwave thermal radiation that the interior emits cannot easily escape back
    65|through the cover. The result is an enclosed space that is consistently warmer
    66|than the surrounding environment, especially during daylight hours.
    67|
    68|The economic and nutritional motivations for season extension are compelling.
    69|For commercial growers, extending the harvest season by even a few weeks at
    70|either end can dramatically increase revenue per acre by capturing early- and
    71|late-market premiums. Eliot Coleman's Four Season Farm in Harborside, Maine
    72|(44 degrees north latitude) demonstrated that unheated high tunnels with
    73|interior row covers can produce salad greens and root vegetables from October
    74|through April, turning what was traditionally a dormant period into a
    75|productive and profitable one. For home gardeners, even modest season extension
    76|means more months of fresh food from the backyard and a deeper connection to
    77|the rhythms of the growing year.
    78|
    79|The modern revival of season extension draws on multiple traditions: the
    80|Parisian market gardeners, who fed a major city year-round using hotbeds and
    81|cloches; Chinese and Japanese protected cultivation methods; and the practical
    82|innovations of organic pioneers such as Albert Howard and Lady Eve Balfour.
    83|Contemporary practitioners like Coleman, Steve Solomon, and Sepp Holzer have
    84|synthesized these traditions with new materials -- plastic films, spun-bonded
    85|fabrics, polycarbonate panels -- creating a toolkit accessible to growers at
    86|every scale.

    88|## History of Protected Cultivation
    89|
    90|The history of protected cultivation stretches back to antiquity. The Roman
    91|author Pliny the Elder described the practice of placing translucent sheets of
    92|mica (lapis specularis) over cucumber beds to protect them from cold, and the
    93|Roman emperor Tiberius was reportedly so fond of cucumbers that his gardeners
    94|developed a wheeled cart system to move plants into sunlight by day and
    95|indoors for warmth at night. K. D. White's study of Roman farming documents
    96|that compost, green manures, mixed farming, and crop rotations were standard
    97|knowledge two thousand years ago, establishing an agricultural sophistication
    98|that included early forms of season extension.
    99|
   100|The most significant pre-modern system emerged in France. Under Jean-Baptiste
   101|La Quintinie at the royal potager of Versailles in the 1670s and 1680s,
   102|sophisticated techniques were developed for producing out-of-season produce.
   103|These reached their full expression with the Parisian maraichers (market
   104|gardeners) between 1850 and 1900. Operating on plots of one to two acres within
   105|Paris, the maraichers achieved four to eight harvests per year using 100 to 400
   106|tons of horse manure per acre, glass-covered cold frames, and glass bell jars
   107|called cloches. Hotbeds heated by decomposing manure produced fresh salads in
   108|January. The system declined after 1915 as horses were replaced by automobiles,
   109|but the techniques were documented by writers including Prince Peter Kropotkin
   110|and survived in old gardening books. Eliot Coleman visited Louis Savier's
   111|maraicher garden near Paris in 1974 and found the nineteenth-century techniques
   112|still alive, adapted with lightweight rail tracks and self-propelled irrigation.
   113|
   114|In the English-speaking world, the Victorian era saw elaborate glasshouses for
   115|the wealthy, but the twentieth century brought mass-produced aluminum frames,
   116|polyethylene film coverings, and high tunnel (hoop house) technology, which
   117|dramatically reduced the cost per square foot of protected growing space.
   118|
   119|## Types of Protected Structures
   120|
   121|Protected cultivation structures exist on a spectrum from simple and temporary
   122|to complex and permanent. The choice of structure depends on the grower's
   123|climate, budget, scale, and production goals.
   124|
   125|**Cold frames** are the simplest and most affordable season-extension tools:
   126|bottomless boxes with a transparent lid, typically 3 to 4 feet wide and 6 to 8
   127|feet long, with the back wall 12 to 18 inches tall and the front wall 6 to 10
   128|inches tall to create a south-facing slope. They provide 10 to 20 degrees
   129|Fahrenheit of frost protection and extend the growing season by 4 to 8 weeks
   130|in both spring and fall. More plants are killed by overheating in cold frames
   131|than by cold, making ventilation management critical.
   132|
   133|**Low tunnels** (also called quick hoops) are made by bending 9-gauge galvanized
   134|wire or PVC pipe into arches over raised beds and covering them with spun-bonded
   135|row cover fabric or polyethylene film. They provide a few degrees of frost
   136|protection and also serve as insect exclusion barriers.
   137|
   138|**High tunnels** (also called hoop houses or polytunnels) are larger
   139|Quonset-style or gothic-arch structures covered with polyethylene film.
   140|Coleman's standard high tunnel measures 30 feet wide by 96 feet long, with
   141|sidewalls approximately 6 feet tall and a peak height of 10 to 12 feet. The
   142|gothic arch design is preferred over Quonset hoops because the peaked roof
   143|sheds snow more effectively. Double-layer polyethylene inflated by a small
   144|squirrel-cage blower provides insulation value roughly equivalent to a single
   145|pane of glass while costing a fraction of the price. UV-stabilized 6-mil
   146|greenhouse-grade poly should be replaced every 4 to 6 years. High tunnels
   147|offer the working space and crop height of a greenhouse at a fraction of the
   148|cost.
   149|
   150|**Greenhouses** are permanent structures glazed with glass or polycarbonate
   151|panels on a rigid frame. They offer the greatest level of environmental control
   152|and the longest lifespan. Geoff Hamilton's practical advice holds that "the
   153|biggest you can afford" is the right size, because a greenhouse of any size
   154|will always be full.
   155|
   156|**Walipinis** (from the Aymara word for "place of warmth") are underground or
   157|earth-sheltered greenhouses that use the earth's thermal mass to maintain
   158|stable growing temperatures. By digging the growing area 6 to 8 feet below
   159|grade and covering it with a south-facing glazed roof, the walipini harnesses
   160|passive solar heating combined with geothermal insulation. The earth below the
   161|frost line maintains a constant temperature of approximately 50 to 60 degrees
   162|Fahrenheit year-round. Winter daytime temperatures can reach 60 to 80 degrees
   163|Fahrenheit on sunny days even when outside temperatures are near zero, while
   164|winter nighttime temperatures remain 10 to 25 degrees above outside ambient.
   165|Drainage is the most critical construction consideration -- water pooling in
   166|the excavation is the most common failure mode.
   167|
   168|## Passive Solar Design and Thermal Mass
   169|
   170|Passive solar design is the cornerstone of low-energy season extension.
   171|Structures in the Northern Hemisphere should have their long axis running east
   172|to west, with maximum glazed area facing due south (a slight eastward bias of
   173|10 to 15 degrees can provide earlier morning warming). The glazing angle should
   174|be approximately latitude plus 10 to 15 degrees to capture low-angle winter
   175|sunlight while shedding summer sun and snow.
   176|
   177|Thermal mass is a large area of heat-absorbing material -- stone, brick,
   178|concrete, water -- positioned where sunlight falls on it during the day. These
   179|dense materials absorb solar energy slowly and release it slowly at night,
   180|buffering rapid temperature drops. Water barrels painted black along the north
   181|wall are a common thermal mass strategy due to water's high specific heat
   182|capacity. Sepp Holzer places rocks and boulders throughout growing areas; the
   183|earth walls and floor of a walipini constitute an enormous thermal mass. In
   184|Coleman's unheated high tunnels, the combination of soil thermal mass and
   185|double-layer poly creates temperatures 15 to 30 degrees above outside minimums,
   186|effectively shifting the growing zone by several hardiness zones.

   188|## Four-Season Farming
   189|
   190|Eliot Coleman's Four Season Farm, located at 44.33 degrees north latitude in
   191|Harborside, Maine, represents the most fully developed model of commercial
   192|four-season farming in cold climates. Situated on 40 acres of land originally
   193|part of Scott and Helen Nearing's homestead, the farm operates 12,000 square
   194|feet of greenhouse space, including cold houses (unheated mobile hoop houses)
   195|of 30 by 96 feet and 22 by 48 feet, plus a minimally heated cool house.
   196|
   197|Coleman's winter harvest system is built on three synergistic components:
   198|cold-tolerant crops, a simple structure that provides the protection they need,
   199|and successional planting that matches the crop to the period when it will
   200|experience the conditions it needs. The critical concept is what Coleman calls
   201|the "Persephone period" -- the time of year when daylight is less than 10
   202|hours per day. During this period, plant growth essentially stops regardless
   203|of temperature, so the goal of winter growing is not to stimulate growth but
   204|to protect plants that reached harvestable size before the Persephone period
   205|began, so they can be harvested gradually through the dark months.
   206|
   207|The system is refreshingly uncomplicated in a world of complex technologies.
   208|It relies on well-known components -- high tunnels, row covers, cold-hardy
   209|varieties, and careful planting schedules -- whose synergy is not widely
   210|understood. Winter-grown red lettuces and 'Bull's Blood' beet leaves show
   211|dramatically deeper color than summer-harvested equivalents, and research
   212|confirms that highly colored foods grown under cool conditions are higher in
   213|anthocyanins (valuable antioxidants). The local grower can thus produce a more
   214|nutritious product than large-scale shippers, delivered within 12 to 24 hours
   215|of harvest.
   216|
   217|Coleman's economic targets are $5 per square foot per year for unheated cold
   218|houses (three crops per year) and $10 per square foot per year for minimally
   219|heated cool houses (six crops per year). The farm has achieved $80,000 gross
   220|return per acre per year from field and greenhouse crops combined. Notably,
   221|insects and diseases have not been a significant concern in the winter harvest;
   222|the major pest is the meadow vole, which lives outdoors and must be managed
   223|around the greenhouse perimeter.

   225|## Greenhouse Soil Management and Fertility
   226|
   227|Soil management in protected structures presents both opportunities and
   228|challenges. The enclosed environment allows precise control of inputs, but
   229|intensive cropping and reduced exposure to natural cleansing can lead to soil
   230|exhaustion and pest buildup if not managed carefully.
   231|
   232|Coleman describes his approach as creating a greenhouse soil "almost like
   233|potting soil, but a potting soil attached to the earth." Initial preparation
   234|involves spreading a 2-inch layer of peat moss with ground limestone, tilled
   235|to full tiller-tine depth, to establish the soil structure that is especially
   236|critical in the semi-artificial confines of the greenhouse. Once structure is
   237|established, compost, rock powders, dried seaweed, and alfalfa meal can be
   238|fully effective.
   239|
   240|The "deeper organic" approach goes beyond basic organic certification to
   241|optimize every factor affecting crop quality through specific rock powders or
   242|seaweeds tailored to soil tests, meticulous compost quality, and attention to
   243|trace minerals. The guiding principle comes from Lady Eve Balfour: "Working to
   244|establish optimum soil conditions is the most constructive approach in a
   245|dependable long-range philosophy of agriculture." Coleman distinguishes between
   246|"deep-organic" farmers who mimic natural soil-plant economy to establish
   247|vigorous soil life, and "shallow-organic" farmers who substitute bagged organic
   248|fertilizers for chemical ones while remaining dependent on industrial supply.
   249|
   250|Mobile greenhouses offer a significant soil health advantage. Beds uncovered one
   251|year out of every two are exposed to natural cleansing (rain, sun, wind, snow),
   252|and vigorous leguminous green manures such as cowpeas and black-eyed peas can
   253|occupy the uncovered beds for 10 to 13 months. This eliminates the
   254|soil-sickness problems that historically required permanent greenhouses to
   255|replace 16 inches of soil or resort to steam sterilization.

   257|## Climate Control
   258|
   259|Climate control in protected structures encompasses temperature, humidity,
   260|ventilation, and light management. The specific requirements vary enormously
   261|depending on the structure, the season, and the crops being grown.
   262|
   263|Ventilation is arguably the most important management task. Inadequate
   264|ventilation causes overheating in warm weather and excessive humidity in cool
   265|weather. Coleman's high tunnels use roll-up sidewalls raised to 3 or 4 feet,
   266|allowing hot air to escape while cooler air enters through end wall doors. In
   267|winter, small top vents or louvers at the peak allow moist air to escape
   268|without creating cold drafts at plant level. Automatic vent openers using
   269|temperature-sensitive wax cylinders can automate this process without
   270|electricity.
   271|
   272|For growers who choose to add heat, Coleman's "cool greenhouse" concept
   273|provides an energy-efficient middle ground. The thermostat is set just above
   274|32 degrees Fahrenheit, providing enough heat for continuous crop growth at a
   275|fraction of the cost of maintaining warm-season temperatures, and doubling
   276|crop turnover compared to unheated houses. Double covering with two layers
   277|of plastic separated by an inflation-fan-maintained air gap can lower fuel
   278|consumption by up to 40 percent.
   279|
   280|Humidity management is critical, particularly in winter. Excessive moisture
   281|promotes fungal diseases in the dense canopy of winter greens. Anti-condensate
   282|poly films can improve light transmission and reduce drip damage to crops
   283|below, but the primary tool for humidity control is adequate ventilation and
   284|air circulation.

   286|## Crop Planning for Year-Round Production
   287|
   288|Planning for year-round production requires understanding the interaction
   289|between day length, temperature, and crop growth. During the Persephone period
   290|(days shorter than 10 hours), most plants stop growing, so the winter harvest
   291|strategy is to protect mature plants rather than stimulate new growth. Planting
   292|dates must be calculated backward from the onset of short days to ensure crops
   293|reach harvestable size before growth slows.
   294|
   295|Coleman's mobile greenhouse rotation system illustrates sophisticated crop
   296|planning. In a "four plots, five moves" rotation, a single 1,000-square-foot
   297|greenhouse covers overwinter onions (November through March), early carrots
   298|and beets (March through April), early beans and zucchini (April through May),
   299|summer tomatoes (May onward), then fall cucumbers and late lettuce before
   300|restarting the cycle. The same crop does not return to the same plot more than
   301|once in four years, providing excellent rotation benefits. Crops are harvestable
   302|five weeks earlier and/or five weeks later than unprotected equivalents,
   303|extending the sales season by two and a half months.
   304|
   305|A general seasonal framework for cold-climate year-round production includes
   306|late winter sowing of spinach, lettuce, and Asian greens in cold frames; early
   307|spring hardening off of indoor seedlings; summer production of heat-loving crops
   308|(tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, eggplant, melons, basil) in greenhouses; late
   309|summer and fall succession planting of cold-hardy greens; and winter harvesting
   310|of protected greens and roots. Cover crops fill gaps between cash crops.
   311|
   312|Steve Solomon offers a minimalist counterpoint: the most important
   313|season-extension strategy is proper planting timing rather than elaborate
   314|structures. "Planting too early is the single biggest cause of trouble," he
   315|observes, since growth rate accelerates hugely as soil warms and a crop sown
   316|two weeks later may outperform one sown at the earliest possible date.
   317|
   318|## Cold-Hardy Varieties and Seasonal Selection
   319|
   320|Selecting the right varieties is the first and most important decision a
   321|winter grower makes. Cold hardiness is not a fixed property but a dynamic
   322|condition influenced by acclimation, plant maturity, genetics, growing
   323|conditions, and duration of exposure to cold. Full acclimation typically
   324|requires 2 to 4 weeks of progressively colder temperatures; plants actively
   325|growing in warm conditions cannot instantly switch to a cold-hardy state.
   326|
   327|Coleman's cold-hardiness ratings, based on decades of observation in unheated
   328|high tunnels, categorize winter crops into three tiers. Extremely hardy crops
   329|that survive below 10 degrees Fahrenheit include mache (killing temperature
   330|approximately 5 degrees Fahrenheit), claytonia, minutina, acclimated spinach,
   331|and Siberian-type kales. Very hardy crops surviving 10 to 20 degrees include
   332|tatsoi, mizuna, wild arugula, turnip greens, carrots in the ground, and leeks.
   333|Moderately hardy crops surviving 20 to 28 degrees include cold-hardy lettuces,
   334|endive, escarole, radishes, cultivated arugula, and Swiss chard.
   335|
   336|Within each species, significant variation exists between varieties. 'Giant
   337|Winter' and 'Viroflay' spinach are among the most cold-hardy; 'Red Russian'
   338|and 'White Russian' kale are significantly hardier than curly types; 'Winter
   339|Density,' 'Rouge d'Hiver,' and 'North Pole' lettuces are bred for cold
   340|conditions; and wild arugula (*Diplotaxis tenuifolia*, 'Sylvetta') is
   341|substantially more cold-tolerant than standard arugula. These differences can
   342|mean the difference between a successful winter harvest and total crop loss
   343|during extreme cold events.
   344|
   345|The eating quality of cold-hardy vegetables is dramatically superior during
   346|cooler temperatures -- they reach a higher level of perfection without heat
   347|stress. Winter-grown red lettuces and 'Bull's Blood' beet leaves show
   348|dramatically deeper color than summer-harvested equivalents. Coleman's salad
   349|mix, which includes red lettuces, green lettuces, broadleaf arugula, endive,
   350|claytonia, minutina, spinach, mache, watercress, and beet leaves, contains at
   351|least five times more calcium, four times more iron, twelve times more vitamin
   352|A, and six times more vitamin C than head lettuce salad, according to USDA
   353|nutritional data.

   355|## Irrigation and Water Management in Greenhouses
   356|
   357|Water management in protected structures is fundamentally different from open-
   358|field irrigation. The enclosed environment dramatically reduces evaporation,
   359|and freeze-thaw cycles of winter create unique challenges. Coleman's approach
   360|balances the need for consistent soil moisture with the risks of overwatering
   361|in cold conditions.
   362|
   363|In the winter harvest system, evaporation is minimal from mid-November to
   364|early March due to the low sun angle and cold temperatures. Coleman initially
   365|did not water his greenhouses during this period. However, a pest problem led
   366|to a revision of this practice: when aphids appeared on spinach starting in
   367|early to mid-February, research showed that nitrogen levels in the soil were
   368|the main factor in aphid multiplication, and nitrogen can build up when there
   369|is no rain or irrigation to flush it through. Coleman's solution was to begin
   370|irrigating thoroughly once a week on sunny mornings starting in mid-January.
   371|This flushed out excess nitrogen, the aphid problem disappeared, and the
   372|practice has continued since.
   373|
   374|Evaporation is a cooling process that matters significantly in winter. Water
   375|left on the soil surface can lower soil temperatures and inhibit seed
   376|germination. Managing soil moisture carefully to prevent excess surface water
   377|while maintaining adequate subsurface moisture is a key winter-growing skill.
   378|The freeze-thaw cycle also affects irrigation infrastructure; hoses must be
   379|drained during the coldest periods.
   380|
   381|The moisture that condenses on floating row covers at night creates a
   382|self-regulating micro-irrigation effect: rather than radiating away, condensed
   383|moisture is returned to the soil the next day when it evaporates in the sun.
   384|
   385|## Pest and Disease Management in Enclosed Spaces
   386|
   387|One of the paradoxical advantages of winter growing under cover is that
   388|pest and disease pressure is generally lower than in summer production. The
   389|cold temperatures that challenge crops also suppress most insect pests and
   390|fungal pathogens. Coleman reports that insects and diseases have not been a
   391|significant concern in his winter harvest, with the exception of meadow voles
   392|at tunnel edges. However, heated greenhouses can support year-round
   393|populations of aphids, whiteflies, spider mites, and thrips, and the close
   394|spacing of winter greens production can promote fungal diseases if ventilation
   395|is inadequate.
   396|
   397|An integrated approach to pest management in enclosed spaces includes cultural
   398|practices, biological controls, and environmental management. Coleman's
   399|discovery that regular winter irrigation solved his aphid problem by flushing
   400|excess nitrogen illustrates how soil and water management can address pest
   401|issues without direct intervention. The deep-organic approach emphasizes that
   402|pest problems stem from plant stress, which can be prevented by managing soil
   403|tilth, nutrient balance, organic matter content, crop rotations, and varietal
   404|selection.

   406|## Economics and Scale
   407|
   408|Season extension spans an enormous range of scales, from a single cold frame
   409|on an apartment balcony to multi-acre commercial operations with tens of
   410|thousands of square feet of protected growing space. The economics of season
   411|extension vary accordingly.
   412|
   413|For home gardeners, Steve Solomon offers a deliberately minimalist perspective.
   414|He advises that for most food gardeners, greenhouses, hot frames, heat cables,
   415|and elaborate structures are rarely necessary, and that the investment in
   416|materials should be proportional to the value of the food produced. A small
   417|investment in row cover fabric (typically $20 to $40 for a 50-foot roll) can
   418|provide insect protection and modest frost protection for multiple seasons,
   419|representing excellent value. Solomon encourages gardeners to master the
   420|fundamentals of soil preparation, seed quality, and proper timing before
   421|investing in structures, as these foundational practices yield far greater
   422|returns than any physical season extension device.
   423|
   424|For commercial market gardeners, the economics are more favorable. Coleman's
   425|target of $5 per square foot per year for unheated cold houses and $10 per
   426|square foot for minimally heated cool houses translates to $14,400 to $28,800
   427|annually from a standard 30-by-96-foot high tunnel. Winter salad greens and
   428|root vegetables command premium prices when local production is scarce, and
   429|the ability to offer fresh produce months before or after the outdoor season
   430|builds strong customer loyalty. The mobile greenhouse system amplifies returns
   431|by allowing one structure to protect different crops at different times of
   432|year, producing six distinct crops from a single 1,000-square-foot greenhouse
   433|with each crop harvestable five weeks earlier or later than unprotected
   434|equivalents.

   436|## Sustainable and Low-Tech Approaches
   437|
   438|A defining trend in modern season extension is the emphasis on low-tech,
   439|sustainable approaches that minimize energy inputs and capital costs. Coleman
   440|relies on passive solar gain, crop biology, and careful timing rather than
   441|fossil-fuel heating, with unheated cold houses producing crops through the
   442|Maine winter using only human labor and minimal electricity for inflation
   443|blowers. The mobile greenhouse system reduces capital investment per protected
   444|acre by serving multiple plots with a single structure.
   445|
   446|Sepp Holzer creates diverse microclimates through landscape design: water bodies
   447|moderate temperature extremes through thermal mass; rocks and boulders store
   448|heat during the day and release it at night; thick organic mulch insulates the
   449|ground from freezing. His achievement of growing lemons at 1,100 to 1,500
   450|meters altitude in the Austrian Alps demonstrates the cumulative effect of
   451|combining multiple low-tech strategies.
   452|
   453|The walipini represents a radical low-tech approach: by sinking the growing
   454|area below grade, the earth itself provides insulation and thermal mass,
   455|maintaining year-round growing temperatures with no supplemental heat. Biomass
   456|compost heating systems offer another option, generating temperatures of 140
   457|degrees Fahrenheit through bacterial decomposition with plastic piping to
   458|capture heat for water or space heating.
   459|
   460|Steve Solomon's contribution is his insistence on proportionality: the simplest
   461|effective solution is always preferable. For many gardeners, this means row
   462|covers rather than cold frames, cold frames rather than high tunnels, and high
   463|tunnels rather than heated greenhouses. His preferred method for cold-winter
   464|climates is root cellaring -- storing the harvest rather than growing through
   465|the deepest cold, requiring no structures, no energy, and no maintenance.
