    45|# Greenhouse Gardening and Season Extension
    46|
    47|**Greenhouse gardening and season extension** encompass the full spectrum of
    48|techniques, structures, and management practices used to protect crops from
    49|adverse weather and extend the period during which fresh food can be grown and
    50|harvested. At its simplest, season extension might involve a few degrees of
    51|frost protection from a spun-fabric row cover draped over a row of lettuce; at
    52|its most sophisticated, it involves fully climate-controlled glasshouses
    53|producing tomatoes and cucumbers through the darkest months of winter. Between
    54|these extremes lies a rich continuum of low-tech, accessible approaches that
    55|have been refined over centuries and that continue to evolve.
    56|
    57|The fundamental principle behind all season extension is the creation of a
    58|microclimate more favorable to plant growth than ambient conditions. This may
    59|mean trapping solar heat to raise temperatures, blocking desiccating winds to
    60|reduce moisture loss, or excluding pests to protect vulnerable seedlings. The
    61|greenhouse effect -- the same phenomenon that warms the Earth's atmosphere --
    62|is the physical basis for most protected cultivation: shortwave solar radiation
    63|passes through a transparent cover, warms the soil and plants inside, and the
    64|longwave thermal radiation that the interior emits cannot easily escape back
    65|through the cover. The result is an enclosed space that is consistently warmer
    66|than the surrounding environment, especially during daylight hours.
    67|
    68|The economic and nutritional motivations for season extension are compelling.
    69|For commercial growers, extending the harvest season by even a few weeks at
    70|either end can dramatically increase revenue per acre by capturing early- and
    71|late-market premiums. [[gourmet-medicinal-mushroom-oyster-cultivation-methods]]; and the practical
    82|innovations of organic pioneers such as Albert Howard and Lady Eve Balfour.
    83|Contemporary practitioners like Coleman, [[sepp-holzer]] have
    84|synthesized these traditions with new materials -- plastic films, spun-bonded
    85|fabrics, polycarbonate panels -- creating a toolkit accessible to growers at
    86|every scale.

    88|## History of Protected Cultivation
    89|
    90|The history of protected cultivation stretches back to antiquity. The Roman
    91|author Pliny the Elder described the practice of placing translucent sheets of
    92|mica (lapis specularis) over cucumber beds to protect them from cold, and the
    93|Roman emperor Tiberius was reportedly so fond of cucumbers that his gardeners
    94|developed a wheeled cart system to move plants into sunlight by day and
    95|indoors for warmth at night. K. D. White's study of Roman farming documents
    96|that compost, green manures, mixed farming, and crop rotations were standard
    97|knowledge two thousand years ago, establishing an agricultural sophistication
    98|that included early forms of season extension.
    99|
   100|The most significant pre-modern system emerged in France. Under Jean-Baptiste
   101|La Quintinie at the royal potager of Versailles in the 1670s and 1680s,
   102|sophisticated techniques were developed for producing out-of-season produce.
   103|These reached their full expression with the Parisian maraichers (market
   104|gardeners) between 1850 and 1900. Operating on plots of one to two acres within
   105|Paris, the maraichers achieved four to eight harvests per year using 100 to 400
   106|tons of horse manure per acre, glass-covered cold frames, and glass bell jars
   107|called cloches. Hotbeds heated by decomposing manure produced fresh salads in
   108|January. The system declined after 1915 as horses were replaced by automobiles,
   109|but the techniques were documented by writers including Prince Peter Kropotkin
   110|and survived in old gardening books. Eliot Coleman visited Louis Savier's
   111|maraicher garden near Paris in 1974 and found the nineteenth-century techniques
   112|still alive, adapted with lightweight rail tracks and self-propelled irrigation.
   113|
   114|In the English-speaking world, the Victorian era saw elaborate glasshouses for
   115|the wealthy, but the twentieth century brought mass-produced aluminum frames,
   116|polyethylene film coverings, and high tunnel (hoop house) technology, which
   117|dramatically reduced the cost per square foot of protected growing space.
   118|
   119|## Types of Protected Structures
   120|
   121|Protected cultivation structures exist on a spectrum from simple and temporary
   122|to complex and permanent. The choice of structure depends on the grower's
   123|climate, budget, scale, and production goals.
   124|
   125|**Cold frames** are the simplest and most affordable season-extension tools:
   126|bottomless boxes with a transparent lid, typically 3 to 4 feet wide and 6 to 8
   127|feet long, with the back wall 12 to 18 inches tall and the front wall 6 to 10
   128|inches tall to create a south-facing slope. They provide 10 to 20 degrees
   129|Fahrenheit of frost protection and extend the growing season by 4 to 8 weeks
   130|in both spring and fall. More plants are killed by overheating in cold frames
   131|than by cold, making ventilation management critical.
   132|
   133|**Low tunnels** (also called quick hoops) are made by bending 9-gauge galvanized
   134|wire or PVC pipe into arches over raised beds and covering them with spun-bonded
   135|row cover fabric or polyethylene film. They provide a few degrees of frost
   136|protection and also serve as insect exclusion barriers.
   137|

   139|Quonset-style or gothic-arch structures covered with polyethylene film.
