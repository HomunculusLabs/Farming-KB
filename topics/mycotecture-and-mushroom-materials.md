# Mycotecture and Mushroom-Based Building Materials

Mycotecture — a portmanteau of *mycelium* and *architecture* — is the practice of using fungal mycelium as a primary structural and functional material in construction, design, and manufacturing. Mycelium, the vegetative body of fungi, can colonize organic substrates and bind them into lightweight, durable composites that offer a renewable alternative to conventional materials like expanded polystyrene (EPS) foam, concrete, and engineered wood products.

## Biology of Mycelium as a Building Material

### What Is Mycelium?

Mycelium is a network of thread-like hyphae that constitutes the vegetative growth phase of fungi. Individual hyphae are typically 1–10 μm in diameter but can extend meters in length. As hyphae grow through a substrate, they secrete extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) — composed of polysaccharides, glycoproteins, and glomalin-related proteins — that act as a natural adhesive, binding substrate particles together into a cohesive composite matrix.

### How Mycelium Composites Form

The formation of a mycelium composite involves several biological processes:

- **Hyphal penetration:** Fungal hyphae grow through and around substrate particles (straw, sawdust, hemp hurd, etc.), creating an interlocking network.
- **Enzymatic modification:** Fungi secrete cellulases, ligninases, and other enzymes that partially digest the substrate, modifying its surface chemistry and improving bonding.
- **Extracellular polymer secretion:** Glucans, chitin, and glycoproteins are deposited at hyphal surfaces and interfaces, forming a natural binder.
- **Densification and self-assembly:** As the mycelium colonizes the full substrate volume, the composite achieves structural integrity through the density and connectivity of the hyphal network.
- **Termination and curing:** Heat treatment (typically 80–120°C) kills the fungus, halting growth and stabilizing the material while preserving the [[ganoderma-lucidum]]* (Reishi) | Sawdust, wood chips | Moderate | Dense, hard, high compressive strength | *Pleurotus ostreatus* (Oyster) (Oyster) | Straw, [[fungal-mycelial-networks-nutrient-translocation]]. *Pleurotus* species are preferred for packaging and insulation where rapid growth and lightweight properties are prioritized.

## History and Key Milestones

### Early Concepts and Artistic Exploration

- **1980s–1990s:** The concept of using living fungi as material was explored by artists and designers. Phil Ross, a San Francisco-based artist, began growing *Ganoderma* sculptures in the 1990s, demonstrating that fungal mycelium could be shaped into rigid forms.
- **2006:** Phil Ross filed patents on methods for growing fungal structures and exhibited "Mycotecture" pieces at galleries, establishing the term.
- **2007:** Ecovative Design was founded by Eben Bayer and Gavin McIntyre in Troy, New York, after developing mycelium-based packaging as a class project at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute.

### Commercialization and Scale-Up

- **2010:** Ecovative launched Mushroom® Packaging in partnership with Sealed Air Corporation, replacing EPS foam for Dell computer packaging.
- **2012:** Ecovative received a DARPA contract to develop mycelium-based materials for military applications, including structural composites and insulation.
- **2014:** The Living (an Autodesk studio) created the Hy-Fi installation at MoMA PS1 using mycelium bricks, demonstrating architectural-scale application.
- **2016:** Ecovative launched MycoComposite™ platform, licensing mycelium technology to other manufacturers.
- **2018:** IKEA announced a partnership with Ecovative to replace polystyrene packaging with mycelium-based alternatives.
- **2020:** Biohm, a UK-based startup, began producing mycelium insulation panels for the construction industry.
- **2022–2024:** Multiple startups (NEFFA, MOGU, Mycoworks) entered the market with mycelium leather, tiles, and structural panels. Building code evaluation began in several jurisdictions.
- **2025–2026:** First mycelium-based building projects received preliminary structural certifications in the EU and US; large-scale manufacturing facilities came online in the Netherlands and California.

## Material Properties

### Mechanical Properties

| Property | Typical Range | Comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Compressive strength | 0.1–3.0 MPa | Lower than concrete (20–40 MPa), comparable to cork (1–3 MPa) |
| Tensile strength | 0.05–0.5 MPa | Lower than wood (30–120 MPa parallel to grain) |
| Flexural strength | 0.2–1.5 MPa | Comparable to low-density fiberboard |
| Density | 80–500 kg/m³ | Lightweight; EPS ~25 kg/m³, cork ~120 kg/m³, wood ~500–700 kg/m³ |
| Elastic modulus | 10–300 MPa | Stiffer than EPS, softer than wood |
| Hardness (Shore D) | 40–70 | Comparable to hard rubber |
| Fracture toughness | 0.1–0.5 MPa·m^½ | Moderate; improves with densification |

Key factors affecting mechanical properties:

- **Species selection:** *Ganoderma* produces denser, stronger composites than *Pleurotus*.
- **Substrate type and particle size:** Finer particles and higher lignin content improve bonding and strength.
- **Growth density:** Longer incubation times produce denser mycelial networks and higher strength.
- **Post-processing:** Heat pressing can increase compressive strength by 50–200%.
- **Moisture content at testing:** Fully dried composites are stronger than moist ones.

### Thermal Properties

- **Thermal conductivity:** 0.04–0.07 W/(m·K), comparable to conventional insulation materials (EPS: 0.03–0.04, mineral wool: 0.03–0.04, cork: 0.04). This makes mycelium composites excellent thermal insulators.
- **Specific heat capacity:** ~1,500–2,000 J/(kg·K), higher than most conventional building materials due to high organic content.
- **Thermal degradation:** Begins around 200–250°C; self-extinguishing behavior due to chitin content.

### Fire Resistance

Mycelium composites exhibit notable fire-resistant properties:

- **Ignition temperature:** ~300–350°C (higher than wood at ~250°C)
- **Self-extinguishing:** Chitin and mineral content (especially when using supplemented substrates) provide inherent flame retardancy
- **Smoke production:** Lower than synthetic foams; no toxic halogenated compounds
- **Fire rating:** Can achieve Euroclass D-s1,d0 with mineral additives; standard (unmodified) typically achieves Euroclass E or F
- **Char formation:** Forms a protective char layer that insulates underlying material
