# Soil Mesofauna and Microarthropod Ecology

## Introduction

Soil mesofauna — organisms measuring roughly 0.1 to 2 millimeters — form the critical middle tier of the soil food web. Though invisible to the naked eye, these creatures are among the most abundant animals on Earth. A single square meter of temperate forest soil can contain over 100,000 microarthropods, and nematode densities routinely exceed one million individuals per square meter. Their collective metabolism drives nutrient mineralization, shapes microbial community structure, regulates decomposition rates, and underpins the fertility that sustains all terrestrial ecosystems and agricultural production.

This group encompasses Collembola (springtails), Acari (mites), Nematoda (roundworms), Protista (protozoa), Tardigrada (water bears), Rotifera (wheel animals), and a diversity of lesser-known groups. Despite their small size, their ecological impact is disproportionately large.

## Taxonomic Overview

### Collembola — Springtails

Collembola are hexapods, not true insects, and are among the most ancient terrestrial arthropods with a fossil record extending to the early Devonian. Approximately 9,000 described species exist globally, though the true diversity is estimated at 50,000 or more.

**Morphology:** Springtails range from 0.2 to 10 mm in length. Their defining feature is the furcula — a forked appendage folded beneath the abdomen that snaps forward to propel the animal away from threats. A ventral tube (collophore) aids in water absorption and adhesion to surfaces. Body forms are divided into elongate (arthropleona) and globular (symphypleona) morphs, each associated with different microhabitats.

**Ecological groups:**
- **Epigeic** species live on the soil surface and in [[leaf-litter-microfungal-diversity-tropical-temperate]], feeding on fungal hyphae, spores, and decomposing plant material
- **Hemiedaphic** species occupy the upper mineral soil and litter interface
- **Euedaphic** species are deep-soil specialists with reduced pigmentation, elongated bodies, and reduced furcula — adapted to life in narrow pore spaces

### Acari — Mites

Mites are arachnids rivaling insects in total described species diversity. In soils, two major groups dominate:

**Oribatida (oribatid mites):** Often called "beetle mites" for their hardened, armor-like exoskeletons. Roughly 10,000 described species. They are the dominant microarthropod group in most temperate soils by both abundance and biomass. Oribatids are primarily fungivores and detritivores, with some species feeding on algae, lichens, or dead animal matter. Their slow life cycles — some species take 2–3 years to complete development — make them sensitive to environmental disturbance.

**Mesostigmata (mesostigmatid mites):** A diverse group including many predatory species. Gamasina and Uropodina are the most abundant suborders in soil. Predatory mesostigmatids feed on nematodes, Collembola, other mites, and small insect larvae. *Hypoaspis* and *Stratiolaelaps* species are commercially available as biological control agents for fungus gnat and thrips larvae in greenhouse production.

**Prostigmata:** This group includes tydeid, tarsonemid, and eupodid mites. Feeding habits range from fungivory and algivory to predation and parasitism. *Tyrophagus* species are stored-product pests but also significant decomposers in soil organic matter.

### Nematoda — Roundworms

Nematodes are the most numerically abundant multicellular animals on Earth, with estimated soil densities of 1–10 million per square meter in temperate grasslands and forests. Though many are microscopic (0.1–2 mm), their collective biomass in fertile soils can exceed 100 kg per hectare.

**Trophic groups (after Yeates et al., 1993):**
- **Bacterivores** (feeding on bacteria): *Caenorhabditis*, *Plectus*, *Rhabditis* — the fastest-growing group, completing life cycles in days
- **Fungivores** (feeding on fungal hyphae and spores): *Aphelenchus*, *Aphelenchoides*, *Ditylenchus* — important regulators of mycorrhizal and saprotrophic fungi
- **Plant-parasites** (feeding on root tissue): root-knot (*Meloidogyne*), cyst (*Globodera*, *Heterodera*), lesion (*Pratylenchus*) — major agricultural pests
- **Predators** (feeding on other nematodes): *Mononchus*, *Seinura* — regulate nematode community structure
- **Omnivores** (mixed diet): *Dorylaimus*, *Aporcelaimus* — large-bodied, slow-reproducing, sensitive to disturbance

### Protista — Protozoa

Soil protozoa are single-celled eukaryotes that serve as the primary consumers of bacteria in most soils. Three major groups are recognized:

- **Flagellates:** The smallest and most abundant soil protozoa (typically 3–15 µm). They exploit thin water films in soil pores inaccessible to larger organisms. *Cercomonas*, *Bodo*, and *Heteromita* are common genera. Population densities reach millions per gram of soil.
- **Amoebae:** Includes naked amoebae and testate amoebae (enclosed in shells). Sizes range from 10 to over 200 µm. Testate amoebae like *Assulina* and *Trinema* are useful bioindicators of soil moisture and pH regimes. Amoebae can move through soil pores by cytoplasmic streaming, accessing bacteria in microenvironments.
- **Ciliates:** The largest soil protozoa (20–200+ µm), requiring water-filled pore spaces for movement. Least abundant of the three groups but important in water-saturated soils, wetlands, and the rhizosphere. *Colpoda* is among the most ubiquitous soil ciliate genera.

### Tardigrada — Water Bears

Tardigrades are microscopic animals (0.1–1.5 mm) found in soil, moss, lichen, and freshwater habitats. Approximately 1,300 species are described. Their fame rests on the cryptobiotic abilities of some species — they can survive desiccation, freezing, high radiation doses, and even the vacuum of space by entering a tun state of suspended metabolism.

In soils, tardigrades are primarily predators of nematodes, rotifers, and other microfauna, though some feed on [[plant-cell-membrane-transport-proteins-channels-carriers-and-pumps]] fluids. Eutardigrades dominate terrestrial habitats. Their populations typically peak in moist, organic-rich soils and decline sharply under desiccation or extreme temperatures.

### Rotifera — Wheel Animals

Rotifers are pseudocoelomate animals ranging from 0.1 to 2 mm. Bdelloid rotifers dominate soil communities, particularly in the water film surrounding organic particles and in the rhizosphere. They feed on bacteria, small protozoa, and fungal spores using a ciliated corona that creates feeding currents.

Bdelloid rotifers are notable for their entirely asexual reproduction over millions of years — one of the longest-standing evolutionary puzzles. They can survive desiccation by anhydrobiosis, similar to tardigrades, and are among the most [[radiation-resistant-fungi-and-myco-corrosion]] animals known.

## Ecological Roles

### Nutrient Mineralization and the Microbial Loop

The most significant function of soil mesofauna is their role in nutrient mineralization — the conversion of [[organic-nutrients-vs-synthetic-nutrients]] locked in microbial biomass and organic matter into plant-available inorganic forms.

The process works through what ecologists call the **microbial loop**:

1. Bacteria and fungi immobilize nitrogen and other nutrients from organic matter into their biomass
2. Microfauna (protozoa, bacterivorous nematodes) graze on bacteria, releasing excess nitrogen as ammonium (NH₄⁺) — a process called **trophic-grazing mineralization** or the **microbial loop**
3. Collembola and fungivorous mites graze on fungal hyphae, releasing nutrients that were immobilized in fungal tissue
4. Predatory mites and nematodes regulate populations of all groups below them

This loop is remarkably efficient. Protozoan grazing alone can increase nitrogen mineralization by 20–40% compared to ungrazed microbe-only systems. In the rhizosphere, where root exudates fuel bacterial growth, the protozoan grazing loop is especially active and directly supplies plant roots with mineral nitrogen.

### Decomposition Regulation

Collembola and oribatid mites are primary regulators of fungal decomposition pathways. By selectively grazing fungal hyphae, they:

- Stimulate fungal growth through compensatory regeneration (similar to how grazing herbivory stimulates grass production)
- Shift fungal community composition toward faster-growing, more decomposative species
- Fragment organic litter, increasing surface area for microbial colonization
- Disperse fungal spores through their feces and by carrying propagules on their bodies

Oribatid mites are particularly important in coniferous forest soils, where they dominate the mesofauna and drive the slow, fungi-dominated decomposition characteristic of mor humus formation. Their fecal pellets contribute to the characteristic aggregate structure of these soils.

### Disease Suppression

Several mesofauna groups contribute to natural disease suppression:

- **Fungivorous nematodes** (*Aphelenchus avenae*) consume pathogenic fungi including *Fusarium*, *Rhizoctonia*, and *Verticillium* species
- **Predatory nematodes** (*Mononchus* spp.) feed on plant-parasitic nematodes
- **Predatory mites** (*Hypoaspis miles*, *Stratiolaelaps scimitus*) consume soil-dwelling insect pests including fungus gnat larvae, thrips pupae, and root mealybugs
- **Protozoa** stimulate bacteria that compete with pathogens through competitive exclusion and antibiosis

### Soil Structure Formation

Mesofauna contribute to soil physical structure through several mechanisms:

- **Fecal pellet production:** Oribatid mites and Collembola produce enormous quantities of fecal pellets that act as stable microaggregates. These pellets are rich in organic carbon and resistant to breakdown, forming the building blocks of soil crumb structure.
- **Burrowing and pore creation:** Movement through soil creates and maintains pore networks that facilitate water infiltration, gas exchange, and root penetration.
- **Aggregate stabilization:** Mucopolysaccharide secretions and organic material in fecal pellets act as binding agents between mineral particles.

## Soil Horizon Distribution

Mesofauna communities are not uniformly distributed through soil profiles. Distinct vertical stratification occurs:

**Litter layer (O horizon):** Highest diversity and abundance. Collembola (especially epigeic species), oribatid mites, and prostigmatid mites dominate. Large predatory mesostigmatid mites patrol this zone. Protozoan densities peak in moist litter layers.

**Organic/mineral interface (A horizon):** Rich in hemiedaphic Collembola, fungal-feeding nematodes, and bacterivorous nematodes. Root density is high, making this the primary zone for rhizosphere interactions. Protozoan grazing on bacteria is most intense here due to root exudate stimulation of bacterial growth.

**Deep mineral soil (B horizon):** Euedaphic Collembola (blind, depigmented, with reduced furcula), bacterial-feeding nematodes, and some protozoa. Oribatid mites decline sharply. Community is dominated by small-bodied organisms capable of moving through narrow pore spaces (<60 µm diameter).

**Subsoil (C horizon):** Sparse fauna, dominated by bacteria, some nematodes, and occasionally protozoa in root channels and cracks. Deep-dwelling nematodes (*Xiphinema*, *Longidorus*) may reach these depths.

## Population Dynamics and Seasonal Patterns

Mesofauna populations fluctuate with environmental conditions:

**Temperature:** Most temperate soil mesofauna have activity optima between 15–25°C. Activity declines sharply below 5°C and above 30°C. Oribatid mites are particularly cold-tolerant; some Arctic species remain active at temperatures near freezing.

**Moisture:** Soil moisture is often the limiting factor. Collembola require a thin water film for cuticular respiration and are highly sensitive to desiccation — populations can crash during drought but recover rapidly when moisture returns. Protozoa are similarly moisture-dependent, with flagellates surviving better than ciliates under dry conditions. Tardigrades and bdelloid rotifers are the exception, surviving extended desiccation through anhydrobiosis.

**Seasonal patterns in temperate regions:**
- Spring peak: Collembola and mite populations surge as snowmelt and warming soils activate litter decomposition
- Summer plateau or decline: depending on moisture availability; drought years produce midsummer crashes
- Autumn secondary peak: fresh litter fall supports a second population surge, especially among fungivores
- Winter minimum: many species enter dormancy or reduce metabolic rates; euedaphic species and nematodes remain most active in insulated deeper soil layers

**Land use effects:**
- Conventional tillage reduces Collembola and oribatid mite populations by 50–80% compared to no-till systems
- Organic amendments (compost, manure) stimulate bacterivorous nematode and protozoan populations
- Monoculture cropping reduces mesofauna diversity relative to diverse rotations and polycultures
- Pesticide applications, especially broad-spectrum insecticides and nematicides, cause acute mortality and long-term community disruption
- Cover crops maintain root exudation and soil moisture, supporting mesofauna through fallow periods

## Interactions Within the Soil Food Web

The mesofauna occupy the middle trophic levels of the soil food web, connecting primary decomposers (bacteria and fungi) to higher predators:

```
Plant residues → Bacteria/Fungi → Protozoa/Bacterivorous nematodes/Fungivorous nematodes
                                            ↓
                                    Collembola/Fungivorous mites
                                            ↓
                                    Predatory nematodes/Predatory mites
                                            ↓
                                  Centipedes/Spiders/Carabid beetles (macrofauna)
```

**Key interactions:**

- **Protozoa–bacteria:** The most numerically dynamic interaction in soil. Protozoan grazing prevents bacterial populations from entering stationary phase, maintaining them in exponential growth which maximizes decomposition efficiency and nutrient turnover.

- **Nematodes–fungi:** Fungivorous nematodes regulate mycorrhizal colonization intensity. Moderate grazing can stimulate mycorrhizal hyphal growth, while heavy grazing reduces colonization and can impair plant nutrient uptake. This represents a critical intersection between the bacterial and fungal energy channels.

- **Collembola–mycorrhizae:** Collembola selectively graze ectomycorrhizal mantle hyphae and external [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]] hyphae. In pot experiments, Collembola presence reduced mycorrhizal colonization by 15–40%, but in natural systems, the net effect is often neutral or positive due to compensatory fungal growth and the nutrient mineralization benefits.

- **Mites–Collembola:** Predatory mesostigmatid mites are major predators of Collembola. This predation pressure influences Collembola vertical distribution (moving deeper into soil to avoid surface-active predators) and community composition.

- **Tardigrades/rotifers–nematodes:** Water bears and bdelloid rotifers consume small nematodes, particularly bacterivorous species, adding another regulatory layer to nematode community dynamics.

## Agricultural Significance

### Indicators of Soil Health

Soil mesofauna communities are increasingly used as bioindicators of soil quality:

- **Nematode maturity index (MI):** The ratio of colonizer-persister (c-p) groups, where higher values (more persisters) indicate less disturbed, more stable soils. Conventional agriculture typically scores MI 1.5–2.5, while undisturbed natural systems score 3.0–5.0.

- **Collembola diversity:** Species richness and evenness of Collembola communities correlate with organic matter content, habitat complexity, and the absence of disruptive management practices. Euedaphic species are particularly sensitive indicators.

- **Oribatid mite ratios:** The ratio of oribatid to prostigmatid mites (O:P ratio) increases with soil stability and organic matter. Disturbed soils are dominated by fast-growing prostigmatids.

- **Protozoan:bacterial ratios:** Shifts in protozoan community composition reflect changes in soil moisture regime, pH, and bacterial community structure.

### Benefits to Crop Production

The direct and indirect contributions of mesofauna to agricultural productivity include:

- **Nitrogen mineralization:** Protozoan and nematode grazing on bacteria releases 20–40 kg N/ha/year in temperate cropping systems — nitrogen that would otherwise remain immobilized in microbial biomass.
- **Phosphorus mobilization:** Protozoan grazing releases phosphorus from bacterial cells. Some protozoa also produce phosphatase enzymes.
- **Disease suppression:** Predatory and fungivorous nematodes, combined with predacious mites, reduce populations of plant-parasitic nematodes and soil-borne fungal pathogens.
- **Decomposition acceleration:** Collembola and mite grazing on fungi accelerates litter decomposition by 10–30% compared to microbe-only decomposition.
- **Mycorrhizal regulation:** Moderate fungivory by nematodes and Collembola maintains [[mycorrhizal-networks-and-forest-communication]] in an active, growing state, optimizing nutrient exchange with host plants.

## Conservation and Management

### Practices That Support Mesofauna

1. **Minimize tillage:** No-till and reduced tillage preserve soil structure, pore networks, and vertical stratification that mesofauna depend on. The transition from conventional to no-till typically doubles or triples Collembola and oribatid mite populations within 3–5 years.

2. **Maintain organic matter inputs:** Regular additions of compost, cover crop residues, and organic mulches provide the food base that sustains the entire mesofauna food web. Target 2–4% soil organic matter in arable systems.

3. **Diverse crop rotations and polycultures:** Plant diversity supports mesofauna diversity through varied root exudate profiles, litter quality, and temporal continuity of habitat.

4. **Avoid broad-spectrum pesticides:** Where pest control is necessary, use targeted approaches (biological control, selective pesticides) that spare non-target mesofauna. Entomopathogenic nematodes (*Steinernema*, *Heterorhabditis*) are compatible with most beneficial mesofauna.

5. **Maintain soil moisture:** Mulching, cover cropping, and reducing bare fallow periods prevent desiccation that decimates Collembola and protozoan populations.

6. **Preserve habitat refugia:** Uncultivated field margins, hedgerows, and permanent vegetation provide source populations that recolonize disturbed fields. Buffer strips around waterways serve dual roles as habitat corridors and erosion control.

### Sampling and Monitoring

Several methods are used to assess mesofauna communities:

- **Tullgren funnels (Berlese-Tullgren extractors):** The standard method for extracting Collembola and mites from litter and soil samples. Heat and desiccation drive organisms downward into a collecting fluid. Efficient for arthropods but kills protozoa and nematodes.

- **Baermann funnel extraction:** A modified method using water to extract active nematodes from soil samples over 24–48 hours. Standard for nematode community analysis.

- **Coring and flotation:** Soil cores are processed through density flotation (sucrose or MgSO₄ solutions) to separate mesofauna from mineral particles.

- **[[molecular-methods-fungal-taxonomy-and-diversity]]:** DNA metabarcoding of soil samples increasingly supplements traditional extraction, providing higher taxonomic resolution and detecting rare or cryptic species. However, it cannot yet reliably quantify population densities or assess functional group abundances.

- **Most probable number (MPN) enumeration:** Used for protozoa, particularly in the rhizosphere. Serial dilution cultures are scored for protozoan presence to estimate population densities.

## Climate Change Implications

Rising temperatures and altered precipitation patterns will reshape mesofauna communities:

- **Warming:** Accelerates metabolic rates and decomposition, potentially increasing mesofauna reproductive rates but also increasing respiratory carbon losses. Cold-adapted oribatid mite species face range contraction.
- **Drought:** Extended dry periods favor anhydrobiosis-capable groups (tardigrades, rotifers, some nematodes) over moisture-dependent Collembola and protozoa. Community shifts toward bacterial-dominated energy channels are expected.
- **Increased CO₂:** Elevated atmospheric CO₂ typically increases root exudation and below-ground carbon allocation, stimulating bacterial growth and the protozoan grazing loop. However, effects on higher mesofauna are mediated through complex trophic cascades and remain difficult to predict.
- **Extreme events:** Intense rainfall events can physically displace mesofauna and disrupt pore networks. Heat waves can cause acute mortality in surface-active species.

## Regional and Habitat Comparisons

Mesofauna communities vary dramatically across biomes and land use types:

**Temperate deciduous forests:** The archetype of mesofauna-rich soils. Oribatid mites dominate (often 60–80% of microarthropod abundance), Collembola are diverse (50–100+ species per site), and nematode communities include a full complement of trophic groups. Total microarthropod abundances of 100,000–300,000 per m² are typical. The thick organic horizons provide abundant habitat and food resources.

**Coniferous forests:** Lower overall abundance but unique community composition. Oribatid mites are even more dominant (often 80–95% of microarthropods), and the slow-growing, acid-tolerant species that characterize mor humus are prevalent. Collembola diversity is lower than in deciduous forests. Fungal-feeding nematodes are relatively more important than bacterivores due to the fungi-dominated decomposition pathway.

**Grasslands:** Protozoan and nematode densities often exceed those in forests due to high root exudation and rapid bacterial turnover in the rhizosphere. Collembola and mite abundances are moderate. The nematode community is typically bacterivore-dominated, reflecting the bacterial energy channel. Seasonal fluctuations are more pronounced due to the absence of litter-layer insulation.

**Tropical forests:** Extraordinarily high diversity, especially among Collembola and oribatid mites. Species richness can be 3–5× that of temperate forests, but individual species abundances are lower. Rapid decomposition rates mean nutrient cycling is fast and mesofauna turn over organic matter quickly. Termites (macrofauna) play a larger decomposition role than in temperate systems, partially complementing mesofauna functions.

**Agricultural fields:** Dramatically reduced diversity and abundance compared to natural ecosystems. Tillage, pesticides, monoculture, and periodic fallow disrupt habitat continuity. Bacterivorous nematodes often dominate the reduced community. Collembola and oribatid mite populations may be 80–90% lower than adjacent undisturbed habitats. However, organic farming systems can recover 50–70% of natural mesofauna abundance within a decade of conversion.

**No-till cropland:** A transitional state where mesofauna communities recover substantially. Oribatid mite populations increase 3–5× compared to conventional tillage. Collembola diversity increases. Predatory nematode and mite populations establish, improving natural pest regulation. Surface residue accumulation provides habitat that approaches natural litter-layer conditions.

**Wetlands and riparian soils:** Saturated conditions favor protozoa (especially ciliates), aquatic nematodes, and moisture-tolerant Collembola. Oribatid mites decline but prostigmatid and mesostigmatid mites persist. Decomposition rates are influenced by both aerobic and anaerobic microsites, creating complex mesofauna zonation along moisture gradients.

## Functional Redundancy and Resilience

A remarkable feature of soil mesofauna communities is their functional redundancy — multiple species often perform similar ecological roles. This redundancy confers resilience: if one species declines due to disturbance, others with overlapping functions can partially compensate.

Examples of functional redundancy:
- Several Collembola species (*Folsomia candida*, *Proisotoma minuta*, *Hypogastrura*) occupy similar fungivore niches; loss of one species is buffered by others expanding their populations
- Multiple bacterivorous nematode genera (*Caenorhabditis*, *Plectus*, *Rhabditis*, *Acrobeloides*) occupy overlapping ecological space
- Both protozoan flagellates and small amoebae graze on similar bacterial communities

This redundancy has practical implications: it means that soil management does not need to support every individual species to maintain ecosystem function. Instead, maintaining habitat conditions that allow functional groups to persist is sufficient for continued nutrient cycling, decomposition, and disease suppression. The functional group approach — managing for nematode trophic structure, Collembola life-form diversity, and protozoan abundance — is more tractable than species-level management and aligns well with the tools available to farmers and land managers.

However, functional redundancy has limits. Deep taxonomic losses — the elimination of entire genera or families — can remove unique capabilities. Some oribatid mite species, for example, are the primary agents dispersing specific mycorrhizal fungal spores. Loss of these specialist dispersers could impair mycorrhizal networks even if generalist fungivore species remain. Similarly, large-bodied, slow-reproducing nematode predators (mononchids) and omnivores (dorylaimids) are disproportionately important for top-down regulation of plant-parasitic nematodes, and their loss cannot be compensated by smaller, faster-growing generalist species.

## Mesofauna in Composting Systems

Composting provides an instructive case study of mesofauna succession:

**Early thermophilic phase (55–65°C):** Most mesofauna are absent or restricted to the cooler margins. Thermotolerant bacteria dominate decomposition. Some heat-tolerant nematodes and protozoa survive in insulated microsites.

**Cooling mesophilic phase (25–45°C):** Rapid colonization by bacterivorous nematodes and flagellate protozoa. *Folsomia candida* and other opportunistic Collembola arrive, often in enormous numbers (100,000+ per m²). Bacterial decomposition accelerates as protozoan grazing stimulates bacterial turnover.

**Maturation phase (ambient temperature):** Oribatid mites and diverse Collembola species colonize as the compost stabilizes. [[fungal-communities-water-aquatic-ecosystems]] become more diverse, supporting fungivorous nematodes and mites. Predatory mesostigmatid mites establish as prey populations build. The community gradually shifts from r-selected opportunists to K-selected specialists.

**Finished compost:** A stable community dominated by oribatid mites, diverse Collembola, and a balanced nematode trophic structure. This mature mesofauna community is one indicator of compost quality and biological maturity. When finished compost is applied to soil, it serves as an inoculum, introducing diverse mesofauna to recipient soils.

Vermicomposting (earthworm-driven composting) hosts its own distinctive mesofauna communities. The earthworm gut, castings, and burrow walls (the drilosphere) create unique microhabitats. Protozoan populations in earthworm casts are 3–5× higher than in surrounding soil. Collembola and mites concentrate in the surface layers of vermicompost beds, grazing on the prolific fungal and bacterial communities sustained by earthworm-processed organic matter.

## Conclusion

Soil mesofauna — the springtails, mites, nematodes, protozoa, tardigrades, and rotifers living between soil particles — are the engine room of terrestrial nutrient cycling. Their grazing on bacteria and fungi drives the mineralization that makes soil fertility possible. Their predation structures the soil food web, regulating populations from bacteria to other microarthropods. Their physical activities build and maintain soil structure. Their sensitivity to disturbance makes them invaluable indicators of soil health and ecosystem function.

For farmers, gardeners, and land stewards, the message is clear: soils rich in mesofauna are soils rich in fertility. The practices that support these hidden communities — minimal disturbance, organic matter inputs, diverse plantings, and reduced pesticide use — are the same practices that build resilient, productive agricultural systems. The microscopic animals beneath our feet are not merely present in healthy soils; they are among the principal architects of soil health itself.
