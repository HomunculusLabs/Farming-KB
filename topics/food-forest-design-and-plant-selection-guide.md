## Food Forest Design and Plant Selection Guide

Designing a food forest is among the most ambitious and rewarding projects a
permaculture practitioner can undertake. Unlike a vegetable garden that reaches
full production in a single season, a food forest is a long-term investment in
a living ecosystem that continues increasing in productivity, diversity, and
resilience for decades. The design process demands careful attention to site
conditions, species interactions, succession dynamics, and the relationship
between every element. This guide synthesizes the accumulated wisdom of Robert
Hart, Bill Mollison, David Holmgren, Toby Hemenway, Dave Jacke, and Eric
Toensmeier into a practical framework for designing food forests at any scale.

## The Design Philosophy

Food forest design rests on a fundamental shift in how the grower relates to
the landscape. Conventional agriculture simplifies ecosystems to a single crop
and works constantly to maintain that simplification through plowing, weeding,
spraying, and fertilizing. A food forest does the opposite: it complexifies the
system, filling every ecological niche with useful species so that the system
itself maintains fertility, manages pests, conserves water, and produces food
with minimal human intervention.

Bill Mollison described the forest not as a collection of trees but as a
complex organism whose elements are bound by physical and functional
interconnections. You cannot remove the [[jadam-liquid-fertilizer-nutritional-perspective]]. A well-designed food forest selects as many
multifunctional species as possible.

## Site Analysis and Preparation

Before selecting a single species, the designer must thoroughly understand the
site. A full year of observation is ideal, tracking sun angles through the
seasons, frost pockets, drainage patterns, prevailing winds, existing
vegetation, wildlife corridors, and soil conditions. This observation period
reveals microclimates and subtle patterns that no map or soil test can capture.

### Climate Assessment

Hardiness zone is the starting point but far from sufficient. The designer
should document the actual frost-free period, growing degree days, summer heat
extremes, winter minimum temperatures, precipitation by month, humidity, and
wind exposure. A site in USDA zone 7 that sits in a frost pocket at the bottom
of a slope can be effectively zone 6 for sensitive crops, while a south-facing
slope with good air drainage may support zone 8 species. Wind is one of the
most underestimated factors: persistent wind increases transpiration, cools
soil, damages blossoms, and stunts growth. Windbreaks of fast-growing species
like black locust should be established before or alongside the food forest.

### Soil and Water Mapping

Soil type, depth, drainage, pH, organic matter content, and mineral profile all
influence species selection. A simple percolation test reveals whether the site
suits deep-rooted trees or requires raised beds and drainage amendments. Most
food forest species prefer slightly acidic to neutral soil (pH 5.5 to 7.0),
with notable exceptions like blueberries (pH 4.5 to 5.5). University extension
soil tests provide baseline data and reveal whether contaminants are present.

A sun map tracks sunlight across the site through the year, noting full-sun,
partial-shade, and deep-shade areas. Most fruiting trees need full sun, so the
map determines primary tree placement. Water movement deserves close attention:
swales, berms, and keyline patterns can redirect runoff, turning drainage
problems into assets. Access paths should be planned before planting, with main
paths at least three feet wide and secondary paths branching to individual
guilds.

## The Guild Design Process

A guild is a group of plants, animals, and fungi assembled around a central
productive element so that each member supports the others. Guild design is the
fundamental technique for organizing species within a food forest.

### The Seven Functional Roles

Toby Hemenway identified seven essential roles in any well-designed guild.
Not every role requires a separate species, since the best guild members serve
multiple functions simultaneously, but all roles should be addressed.

The central element is the primary food producer, typically a fruit or nut tree
that anchors the guild. Nitrogen fixers convert atmospheric nitrogen into
plant-available forms through symbiotic relationships with rhizobacteria
(legumes) or actinomycetes (non-legumes). Common choices include white clover
as living ground cover, goumi and seaberry as nitrogen-fixing shrubs, and
black locust or honey locust as canopy nitrogen fixers. These are periodically
coppiced to release fixed nitrogen into the soil surface.
