# Legume Trees and Nitrogen-Fixing Permaculture Species

Legume trees (family Fabaceae, subfamily Faboideae and Mimosoideae) are among the most valuable plants in permaculture design. Through their symbiotic relationship with rhizobial bacteria, they convert atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) into biologically available forms (NH₄⁺), enriching soil fertility while producing food, fodder, timber, fuel, mulch, and nectar. This page provides a comprehensive guide to selecting, establishing, and managing nitrogen-fixing trees in permaculture systems.

---

## How Biological Nitrogen Fixation Works in Trees

### The Rhizobium–Legume Symbiosis

1. **Infection**: Rhizobial bacteria (genera *Rhizobium*, *Bradyrhizobium*, *Sinorhizobium*, *Mesorhizobium*) infect root hairs, triggering nodule formation.
2. **Nodule Formation**: The plant forms root nodules that house the bacteria. In tree legumes, these are typically **indeterminate** nodules (elongated, with a persistent meristem).
3. **Nitrogenase Activity**: Inside nodules, the bacterial enzyme **nitrogenase** catalyzes: N₂ + 8H⁺ + 8e⁻ + 16 ATP → 2NH₃ + H₂ + 16 ADP. This is energetically expensive — the plant supplies carbohydrates from photosynthesis.
4. **Oxygen Regulation**: **Leghemoglobin** (red pigment in active nodules) binds oxygen, maintaining the low-O₂ environment nitrogenase requires while still supplying aerobic respiration.

### Nitrogen Fixation Rates

| Species | Annual N Fixed (kg/ha) | Notes |
|---------|----------------------|-------|
| *Leucaena leucocephala* | 100–500 | Tropical; very high rates with effective rhizobia |
| *Gliricidia sepium* | 50–200 | Tropical; coppices vigorously |
| *Robinia pseudoacacia* | 30–150 | Temperate; deep-rooted |
| *Acacia nilotica* | 20–100 | Arid/semi-arid |
| *Prosopis glandulosa* (mesquite) | 20–60 | Arid; drought-tolerant |
| *Ceratonia siliqua* (carob) | 15–40 | Mediterranean; lower rates |
| *Alnus spp.* (actinorhizal) | 40–200 | Not a legume; *Frankia* symbiosis |

> **Note**: Actual fixation depends on rhizobial effectiveness, soil conditions, tree age, and climate. Young trees fix less than established ones.

## Major Permaculture Legume Trees by Climate

### Tropical and Subtropical Species

#### Leucaena (*Leucaena leucocephala*)

- **Hardiness**: USDA 9b–12; frost-tender
- **Growth**: Extremely fast (2–4 m/year); matures at 3–5 years
- **Uses**: Fodder (18–28% protein), firewood (high BTU), green manure, alley cropping, erosion control
- **N-fixation**: 100–500 kg N/ha/year — among the highest of any tree
- **Nitrogen content**: Foliage contains 3.0–4.5% N on dry weight basis
- **Pitfalls**: Can become invasive; psyllid insect (*Heteropsylla cubana*) can defoliate stands; mimosine toxicity in unadapted ruminants
- **Best varieties**: KX2, K8 (psyllid-resistant, low-mimosine hybrids)
- **Establishment**: Direct seed at 10,000–20,000 seeds/ha or plant seedlings; inoculate with specific *Rhizobium* strain (e.g., CB3060)

#### Gliricidia (*Gliricidia sepium*)

- **Hardiness**: USDA 9b–12
- **Growth**: Fast (2–3 m/year); excellent coppicer
- **Uses**: Living fences, fodder (20–30% protein), shade for coffee/cacao, green manure, rat poison (seeds contain coumarin)
- **N-fixation**: 50–200 kg N/ha/year
- **Advantages**: Tolerates acid soils, drought, and heavy pruning; establishes easily from cuttings
- **Pruning**: Can be cut 4–6 times per year in humid tropics
- **Companion crops**: Excellent overstory for shade-loving crops; used widely in tropical alley cropping with maize, cassava, and taro

#### Calliandra (*Calliandra calothyrsus*)

- **Hardiness**: USDA 10–12
- **Growth**: Fast; multi-stemmed shrub/small tree to 8 m
- **Uses**: Fodder (goats, rabbits), green manure, erosion control on slopes, bee forage
- **N-fixation**: 40–100 kg N/ha/year
- **Advantages**: Shade-tolerant (establishes under other trees), good on steep slopes, palatable to livestock
- **Limitations**: Moderate frost sensitivity; tannin content can reduce protein digestibility if fodder is sole diet

#### Acacia Species

Multiple *Acacia* species serve permaculture roles across climate zones:

- ***Acacia nilotica*** (babul): Arid-zone, nitrogen-fixing, pods for fodder, excellent firewood and charcoal
- ***Acacia senegal***: Source of gum arabic; drought-hardy; provides income from resin
- ***Acacia mangium***: Fast-growing timber for humid tropics; pulpwood and reforestation
- ***Acacia koa***: Hawaiian endemic; premium timber; nitrogen-fixing pioneer on volcanic soils
- **General traits**: Deep taproots, drought tolerance, wildlife habitat; many produce edible seeds or gum

#### Sesbania (*Sesbania grandiflora* — Hummingbird Tree)

- **Hardiness**: USDA 9b–12
- **Growth**: Very fast; reaches 5–8 m in 2 years
- **Uses**: Edible flowers and leaves (high protein), green manure, living fence, pond-edge stabilizer
- **N-fixation**: 60–150 kg N/ha/year
- **Advantages**: Tolerates waterlogging; useful in rice-paddy bunds and flood-prone sites
- **Culinary**: Flowers eaten raw in salads or cooked in curries across Southeast Asia

### Arid and Semi-Arid Species

#### Mesquite (*Prosopis glandulosa*, *P. juliflora*, *P. pallida*)

- **Hardiness**: USDA 6–11 depending on species
- **Growth**: Moderate; deep taproot (documented to 50+ meters) allows survival in extreme drought
- **Uses**: Edible pods (ground into flour — 15–25% sugar, 12–16% protein), firewood (excellent BTU), charcoal, fencing, wildlife habitat
- **N-fixation**: 20–60 kg N/ha/year; less than tropical species but significant for arid systems
- **Soil improvement**: Drops nitrogen-rich leaf litter; increases soil organic matter and nitrogen under canopy ("fertile islands")
- **Pitfalls**: *P. juliflora* is one of the world's worst invasive species in Africa, Australia, and parts of Asia; use native *Prosopis* species when possible
- **Pod processing**: Pods can be milled into sweet, gluten-free flour; traditional food source across the Americas
- **Thorns**: Most species are thorny; thornless cultivars exist for some species

#### Carob (*Ceratonia siliqua*)

- **Hardiness**: USDA 9–11; tolerates 100°C summer heat and -7°C winter cold
- **Growth**: Slow to moderate; long-lived (100+ years); matures at 5–7 years for first pods
- **Uses**: Edible pods (carob/ceratonia — chocolate substitute), fodder, shade, ornamental
- **N-fixation**: 15–40 kg N/ha/year; moderate but consistent
- **Advantages**: Extremely drought-tolerant once established; salt-tolerant; fire-resistant (thick bark); no serious pests
- **Yield**: 80–200 kg pods/tree/year at maturity
- **Nutrition**: Pods are 40–50% sugar, 5–10% protein; contain B vitamins, calcium, iron
- **Propagation**: Typically grafted (seedlings variable and may produce inferior pods); scarification required for seed germination
- **Companion planting**: Understory of Mediterranean herbs (rosemary, thyme, lavender), grapes, olives

### Temperate Species

#### Black Locust (*Robinia pseudoacacia*)

- **Hardiness**: USDA 3–9; very cold-tolerant
- **Growth**: Very fast (1.5–3 m/year); coppices vigorously on 5–10 year rotations
- **Uses**: Premium rot-resistant timber (fence posts last 50+ years in ground), firewood (highest BTU of any North American hardwood), bee forage (major honey source), erosion control
- **N-fixation**: 30–150 kg N/ha/year; most productive temperate N-fixer
- **Wood quality**: Exceptionally hard, rot-resistant, and strong; used for outdoor furniture, decking, and structural posts
- **Pitfalls**: Aggressive spread via root suckers; thorny; toxic leaves and bark (robinetin); can form monocultures
- **Management**: Coppice on 5–8 year cycle for fence posts; pollard for fodder; contain root spread with root barriers or regular mowing
- **Wildlife**: Excellent nectar source; flowers produce premium honey (light, mild, slow to crystallize)

#### Honey Locust (*Gleditsia triacanthos*)

- **Hardiness**: USDA 3–9
- **Growth**: Fast; tall (20–30 m) with open canopy allowing light to understory
- **Uses**: Edible pods (sweet pulp — livestock feed), shade tree, windbreak, living fence (thorny wild type)
- **N-fixation**: 10–30 kg N/ha/year; lower than black locust but still significant
- **Advantages**: Thornless cultivars available (*G. t.* var. *inermis*); open canopy allows intercropping; pods relished by cattle, goats, and wildlife
- **Pod production**: 40–90 kg pods/tree/year; pods contain 13–18% sugar, 12–15% protein
- **Companion planting**: Excellent overstory for pasture, silvopasture systems, or food forest upper canopy

#### Siberian Pea Shrub (*Caragana arborescens*)

- **Hardiness**: USDA 2–7; extremely cold-tolerant (Siberian native)
- **Growth**: Moderate; multi-stemmed shrub to 5 m
- **Uses**: Windbreak, nitrogen fixation, edible seeds (cooked, similar to lentils), chicken forage
- **N-fixation**: 20–80 kg N/ha/year
- **Advantages**: Very hardy, drought-tolerant, nitrogen-fixing, fixes nitrogen in cold soils; tolerates alkaline and saline soils
- **Edibility**: Seeds ~36% protein; young pods edible when cooked; used as a protein crop in cold climates
- **Windbreaks**: Excellent in multi-row windbreak design as middle rows
- **Propagation**: Scarify seeds; direct seed or transplant seedlings; nitrogen-fixing without inoculation in most soils (native rhizobia widespread in temperate zones)

#### Sea Buckthorn (*Hippophae rhamnoides*)

- **Note**: Actinorhizal, not a true legume — fixes nitrogen via *Frankia* bacteria
- **Hardiness**: USDA 3–8; extremely cold-hardy and salt-tolerant
- **Growth**: Moderate; spreading shrub to 4 m; nitrogen-fixing pioneer on poor soils
- **Uses**: Superfood berries (vitamin C, omega fatty acids, antioxidants), erosion control, dune stabilization, windbreak
- **N-fixation**: 15–60 kg N/ha/year
- **Berry production**: Female plants produce 1–5 kg berries/year; requires male pollinator (1:5–8 male:female ratio)
- **Processing**: Berries processed into juice, oil, supplements; oil from seeds and pulp is commercially valuable
- **Pitfalls**: Dioecious (separate male/female plants); spreads via suckers; thorny

#### Autumn Olive (*Elaeagnus umbellata*)

- **Note**: Actinorhizal (*Frankia* symbiosis)
- **Hardiness**: USDA 3–8
- **Growth**: Fast; shrub to 5 m; very drought-tolerant
- **Uses**: Edible berries (high lycopene), wildlife food, erosion control, nitrogen fixation
- **N-fixation**: 20–100 kg N/ha/year; significant
- **Pitfalls**: Highly invasive in eastern North America; check local regulations before planting
- **Berry nutrition**: Very high lycopene content (up to 17× that of tomatoes); also rich in lutein and vitamin C

## Design Integration in Permaculture Systems

### Nitrogen-Fixing Guilds

In food forest design, legume trees serve as the **nutrient-accumulating** element in plant guilds. Common patterns:

```
Canopy (nitrogen-fixer):  Black locust / Honey locust / Mesquite / Acacia
Sub-canopy (food):        Fruit trees / Nut trees / Olive / Citrus
Shrub layer (N-fixer):    Siberian pea shrub / Sea buckthorn / Caragana
Herb layer:               Dynamic accumulators / Comfrey / Herbs
Ground cover (N-fixer):   Clover / Vetch / Alfalfa / Beans
```

### Alley Cropping

Alternating rows of legume trees with annual crops. The trees provide:
- Nitrogen to adjacent crops (especially young trees in establishment phase)
- Mulch from pruned foliage
- Wind protection
- Microclimate modification

**Species**: Leucaena + maize (tropical), Gliricidia + cassava (tropical), Black locust + alley vegetables (temperate)

### Silvopasture

Integrating nitrogen-fixing trees with livestock:

| System | Tree Species | Livestock | Benefit |
|--------|-------------|-----------|---------|
| Tropical silvopasture | Leucaena, Calliandra | Cattle, goats | Protein-rich fodder, shade, soil N |
| Temperate silvopasture | Black locust, Honey locust | Cattle, sheep | Pod drop feed, shade, fence posts |
| Arid silvopasture | Mesquite, Acacia | Cattle, goats | Pods as dry-season fodder, shade |

### Riparian and Erosion Control

- **Sesbania** and **Acacia** species stabilize streambanks and wet areas
- **Black locust** holds steep slopes with deep, aggressive root systems
- **Sea buckthorn** stabilizes coastal dunes and riparian zones

## Establishment and Management

### Inoculation

Many legume trees require **specific rhizobial strains** for effective nodulation:

- **Leucaena**: Requires specific *Rhizobium* (e.g., strain CB3060); many soils lack effective strains
- **Acacia**: Often nodulates with native tropical rhizobia; inoculation less critical but beneficial
- **Robinia**: Nodulates readily with native temperate rhizobia; rarely needs inoculation
- **Caragana**: Nodulates with native rhizobia in cold climates

**Inoculant sources**: Commercial peat-based inoculants, soil from under established trees of same species, or cultured rhizobia from agricultural suppliers.

### Planting

1. **Site preparation**: Clear weeds in a 1 m radius; deep rip compacted soils
2. **Seed treatment**: Scarify hard-coated seeds (hot water soak, nicking, or acid scarification); soak in inoculant slurry
3. **Spacing**: 2–5 m in rows for alley cropping; 5–10 m for scattered food forest elements
4. **Planting depth**: 2–3× seed diameter; transplant seedlings at root collar level
5. **Early care**: Water for first 2–3 dry seasons; mulch heavily; protect from browsing
6. **Weed control**: Critical in first year — mulch ring or manual weeding in 1 m radius

### Pruning and Coppicing

- **Leucaena**: Prune to 50–100 cm height for alley cropping; 4–6 cuts/year in humid tropics
- **Gliricidia**: Coppice at 50 cm; recovers in 2–3 months
- **Black locust**: Coppice on 5–8 year rotation for timber; pollard at 2–3 m for fodder
- **Mesquite**: Minimal pruning; remove lower branches for access and shape
- **General rule**: Never remove more than 50% of leaf area in a single pruning; leave some foliage for photosynthesis to fuel regrowth and continued N-fixation

### Managing Invasiveness

Several legume trees are notorious invaders:
- **Contain root spread**: Install root barriers (30+ cm deep) around black locust
- **Control seed dispersal**: Remove seed pods before maturity on aggressive species
- **Choose native species**: Use locally native *Prosopis* or *Acacia* instead of introduced species
- **Monitor and remove**: Regularly check for unwanted seedlings beyond the planting area
- **Check regulations**: Some species are legally restricted (e.g., *P. juliflora* in many countries)

## Multi-Functional Benefits Beyond Nitrogen

### Timber and Wood Products
- **Black locust**: Fence posts, outdoor lumber, decking, furniture — naturally rot-resistant without treatment
- **Acacia**: Flooring, furniture, charcoal (high-quality), craft wood
- **Mesquite**: Premium BBQ wood and smoking, furniture, artisan crafts

### Food and Fodder
- **Pods**: Mesquite, carob, honey locust — edible for humans and livestock
- **Seeds**: Siberian pea shrub, Acacia (some species) — protein-rich seeds
- **Foliage**: Leucaena, Gliricidia, Calliandra — high-protein fodder for ruminants
- **Flowers**: Sesbania, Black locust — edible flowers for humans; premium nectar for bees

### Ecosystem Services
- **Windbreaks**: Multi-row design with legume trees reduces wind speed 40–60%
- **Wildlife habitat**: Dense foliage, flowers, and pods attract birds, insects, and mammals
- **Soil stabilization**: Deep roots prevent erosion on slopes and streambanks
- **Microclimate**: Shade reduces evapotranspiration; wind protection improves crop yields
- **Carbon sequestration**: Fast-growing legume trees store significant carbon in wood and soil
