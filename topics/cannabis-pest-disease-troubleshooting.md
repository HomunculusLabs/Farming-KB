# Cannabis Pest and Disease Troubleshooting

Cannabis cultivation, whether for recreational or medicinal use, demands
meticulous attention to plant health. Unlike many ornamental or food crops,
cannabis faces unique pest and disease pressures compounded by the fact that
its harvested product is typically consumed via inhalation, vaporization, or
ingestion with minimal processing. Pesticide residues, fungal spores, and
bacterial contamination on flowers represent direct health risks to consumers.
As a result, integrated pest management (IPM) is not merely best practice —
it is an ethical and often legal imperative.

Pests and diseases in cannabis gardens range from nearly invisible arthropods
like spider mites and thrips to devastating fungal pathogens such as Botrytis
cinerea and root rot complexes caused by Pythium and Fusarium species. In
addition to biotic threats, cultivators must contend with abiotic disorders
from nutrient imbalances, temperature extremes, lighting irregularities, and
humidity fluctuations. These environmental stressors frequently serve as the
underlying precondition that invites pest infestations and disease outbreaks.

The diagnostic process can be challenging because many symptoms overlap: leaf
yellowing may indicate nitrogen deficiency, root rot, or spider mite damage,
depending on context and pattern. Effective troubleshooting requires a
systematic approach — observing which plant parts are affected, whether
symptoms are spreading, and what environmental conditions prevail. This guide
provides a structured framework for identifying the most common cannabis pests,
diseases, and disorders, along with proven management strategies grounded in
IPM principles.

## Integrated Pest Management for Cannabis

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is a science-based approach to pest control
that prioritizes prevention and least-harm interventions over reactive chemical
applications. For cannabis, IPM is elevated to a near-absolute necessity.
Because flowers are consumed directly and regulatory bodies increasingly test
for pesticide residues, the margin for chemical error is essentially zero. Many
conventional agricultural pesticides are explicitly prohibited for use on
cannabis, and even approved products must be applied with strict adherence to
pre-harvest intervals.

The IPM hierarchy for cannabis follows a clear order of operations. Prevention
sits at the foundation: maintaining clean grow spaces, selecting resistant
genetics, optimizing environmental conditions, and enforcing strict sanitation.
Monitoring is the second layer, involving regular visual inspection, sticky
trap deployment, and environmental data logging to detect problems at the
earliest stage. Biological controls — predatory insects, beneficial mites, and
microbial antagonists — form the third line of defense, providing ongoing
suppression without chemical inputs. Chemical and biorational interventions
(insecticidal soaps, horticultural oils, approved biopesticides) are deployed
only when biological controls are insufficient and pest populations threaten
crop viability.

The traditional concept of an "economic injury threshold" takes on different
meaning in cannabis. Here, the threshold is driven not solely by yield loss
but by quality degradation. Even modest spider mite activity can contaminate
trichome-rich flower surfaces with webbing and frass, rendering buds
unmarketable. A few powdery mildew spores on a cola can trigger total
rejection by testing laboratories. Cannabis IPM therefore operates at very low
tolerance levels, with the goal of maintaining a near-pest-free environment.

Record-keeping is a critical but often overlooked IPM component. Documenting
pest sightings, environmental conditions, interventions, and outcomes allows
cultivators to identify recurring patterns and refine strategies over
successive grows.

## Spider Mites

Spider mites (Tetranychus urticae), or two-spotted spider mites, are widely
regarded as the single most destructive pest in cannabis cultivation. Their
tiny size — adults measure about 0.5 millimeters — makes early detection
difficult, and their extraordinary reproductive capacity means a small,
overlooked population can explode into a crop-devastating infestation within
days. Spider mites are arachnids, closely related to spiders and ticks.

Identification begins with careful observation of leaf undersides, where
spider mites congregate to feed and reproduce. Early signs include tiny pale
dots or stippling on the upper leaf surface, caused by individual mites
piercing plant cells and draining their contents. As populations grow, fine
silk webbing becomes visible on leaf undersides and eventually spanning entire
branch tips. In severe infestations, leaves turn yellow or bronze, desiccate,
and may drop from the plant.

The spider mite life cycle proceeds through five stages: egg, larva (six
legs), two nymphal instars (eight legs), and adult. Under warm conditions (25
to 30 degrees Celsius), the entire cycle completes in as few as five to seven
days, with females laying up to 20 eggs per day. A single fertilized female
entering a grow room can theoretically give rise to millions of descendants
within a single generation cycle.

Control relies heavily on biological agents. Predatory mites are the
cornerstone: Phytoseiulus persimilis is a specialist predator that reproduces
faster than its prey; Amblyseius californicus tolerates a wider range of
conditions and can survive on pollen when spider mite populations are low;
Neoseiulus fallacis offers cold tolerance for cooler grow environments.
Generalist predators including ladybugs and green lacewings provide
supplementary control. Biorational options include horticultural oils,
insecticidal soaps, azadirachtin and neem oil, and spinosad-based products.
Environmental manipulation is also effective: spider mites thrive in hot, dry
conditions, so raising humidity above 60 percent can suppress population
growth. Between grows, a thorough clean-room protocol — removing all plant
material, sterilizing surfaces, and allowing the space to remain fallow — is
essential to break the cycle.

## Fungus Gnats and Root Damage

Fungus gnats (families Sciaridae and Mycetophilidae) are small, dark-colored
flies commonly encountered in soil and soilless media grows. Adult gnats are
primarily a nuisance, flying lazily around the soil surface, but the larval
stage poses the real threat. Fungus gnat larvae are small, translucent,
white-bodied worms that live in the root zone, feeding on organic matter, root
hairs, and tender root tissue.

The danger lies in root damage from larval feeding. Even moderate larval
populations can prune root hairs and girdle small roots, reducing water and
nutrient uptake. This damage also creates entry points for soilborne pathogens,
particularly Pythium and Fusarium species, which can establish opportunistic
root rot infections. The combination of direct root damage and secondary
pathogen infection makes fungus gnats especially threatening to seedlings and
young clones.

The life cycle proceeds from egg to four larval instars, pupa, and adult,
completing in approximately three to four weeks at typical grow temperatures.
Females lay eggs in moist growing media rich in organic matter, making
overwatered, poorly-draining soils especially prone to infestation.

Control strategies target both life stages. Yellow sticky traps at soil level
capture flying adults and serve as a monitoring tool. For larval control,
biological agents are highly effective: Steinernema feltiae beneficial
nematodes actively hunt larvae, releasing symbiotic bacteria that kill the host
within 24 to 48 hours; the predatory mite Hypoaspis miles feeds on larvae in
the top soil layer; Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (BTI) applied as a soil
drench kills larvae upon ingestion. Cultural controls are equally important —
allowing the soil surface to dry between waterings, and applying sand or
diatomaceous earth as a top-dressing barrier to prevent egg-laying.

## Thrips

Thrips, particularly the western flower thrips (Frankliniella occidentalis),
are a significant pest that often goes unnoticed until populations are well
established. These tiny, slender insects measure only one to two millimeters
and feed by rasping the leaf surface and sucking cell contents, producing
characteristic silver or bronze streaking. Small black fecal spots on damaged
leaves serve as a key diagnostic indicator.

Thrips are problematic because they are efficient vectors for plant viruses,
most notably Tomato Spotted Wilt Virus (TSWV). Their feeding directly affects
trichome integrity on flower surfaces, reducing potency and visual quality.
Most frustratingly, thrips tend to seek shelter inside developing flower buds,
where they are extremely difficult to reach with topical sprays during
flowering.

The life cycle includes egg, two larval instars, a prepupal stage, a pupal
stage (often in soil), and adult, completing in approximately two weeks under
warm conditions. Biological control centers on Amblyseius cucumeris predatory
mites that feed on first-instar thrips larvae, and Orius insidiosus minute
pirate bugs that attack all life stages. Blue sticky traps (more attractive to
thrips than yellow) aid monitoring and population reduction. Spinosad and neem
oil provide chemical backup, though their use during flowering must be carefully
timed to avoid residue contamination.

## Aphids

Several aphid species attack cannabis, including the green peach aphid (Myzus
persicae), cotton/melon aphid (Aphis gossypii), and Phorodon cannabis, the
cannabis aphid. Aphids are small, soft-bodied insects found in dense clusters
on new growth tips and young leaves, feeding on phloem sap and causing leaf
curling, stunting, and general decline.

One of the most insidious aspects of aphid biology is their reproductive
strategy. During the growing season, populations consist entirely of females
that reproduce parthenogenetically — giving live birth to clones already
pregnant when born. A single aphid can rapidly produce hundreds or thousands
within days. Aphid feeding also produces honeydew, a sticky excretion that
promotes sooty mold growth, further reducing photosynthetic capacity and
flower quality.

Control begins with biological agents: ladybugs and green lacewing larvae are
voracious aphid predators, while parasitic wasps in the genus Aphidius lay
eggs inside aphid hosts, producing mummified husks from which new wasps
emerge. Insecticidal soaps and neem oil provide effective contact control, but
during flowering, biological controls are preferred to avoid bud contamination.

## Whiteflies

Whiteflies (family Aleyrodidae) are small, moth-like insects that congregate
on leaf undersides. Adults readily take flight when disturbed, creating a
cloud of white insects around affected plants. Both adults and nymphs feed on
phloem sap, leading to leaf yellowing, wilting, and decline. Whiteflies
produce honeydew promoting sooty mold and have demonstrated resistance to many
insecticides.

The life cycle proceeds through egg, four nymphal instars, pupa, and adult.
Females lay hundreds of eggs in circular patterns on leaf undersides. The
sedentary nymphal stages are vulnerable to parasitoid wasps. Encarsia formosa
lays eggs inside whitefly nymphs, and Delphastus catalinae predatory beetles
feed on all life stages. Yellow sticky traps capture adults, and horticultural
oils can smother eggs and nymphs when applied carefully to avoid phytotoxicity.

## Powdery Mildew

Powdery mildew, caused by Golovinomyces and Podosphaera species, is one of
the most common fungal diseases affecting cannabis. It presents as white to
grayish powdery patches on leaf surfaces, starting on upper surfaces but
spreading to undersides, stems, and eventually flower buds. Unlike many fungal
diseases, powdery mildew does not require free water on leaf surfaces — it
germinates best in moderate humidity with dry leaf surfaces.

The disease cycle begins with airborne conidia landing on susceptible tissue.
The fungus produces haustoria that penetrate epidermal cells to extract
nutrients, then releases new spore chains. Under optimal conditions (20 to 25
degrees Celsius, 50 to 70 percent humidity), the cycle repeats every seven to
ten days, allowing rapid canopy colonization.

Powdery mildew is devastating because it directly affects the harvestable
product. Infected buds are unsightly, unpleasant in taste, and may fail
regulatory testing for mold counts. Fungal mycelium embedded in bud tissue
cannot be removed, meaning infected flowers must be destroyed.

Management integrates genetic, environmental, biological, and chemical
approaches. Selecting less susceptible cultivars is fundamental. Environmental
control focuses on adequate airflow, keeping humidity below 55 percent during
dark periods, and minimizing temperature differentials between day and night.
Biological controls include Trichoderma and Bacillus subtilis products like
Serenade. Potassium bicarbonate sprays raise leaf surface pH unfavorably for
mildew germination. Sulfur vaporizers are effective during vegetative growth
but must stop before flowering. Hydrogen peroxide dilutions and UV-C light
provide additional options.

## Botrytis Bud Rot

Botrytis cinerea, commonly known as gray mold or bud rot, is arguably the most
feared disease in cannabis cultivation. Unlike powdery mildew, Botrytis attacks
the interior of dense flower buds, causing extensive rot before external
symptoms appear. By the time brown, mushy spots or grayish-brown mold growth
are visible, internal damage is often severe.

Botrytis thrives under high humidity (above 60 percent), poor air circulation,
cool temperatures (15 to 20 degrees Celsius), and persistent moisture. Dense,
tightly packed bud structures create microclimates with elevated humidity and
limited airflow. These internal bud environments provide ideal conditions during
late flowering when buds are at their largest.

Once Botrytis is established inside a bud, the affected tissue cannot be
saved. The standard protocol is immediate removal and careful disposal of the
infected bud and surrounding tissue. Preventive measures include selecting
strains with less dense bud structures for humid climates, maintaining robust
canopy airflow, strategic defoliation, aggressive humidity management during
late flowering, and biological preventatives during vegetative growth.

## Root Rot and Damping Off

Root rot and damping off are caused primarily by Pythium, Fusarium, and
Rhizoctonia species. These pathogens attack root systems and lower stems,
causing symptoms from subtle growth reduction to sudden death. Root rot
typically affects older plants showing gradual decline, while damping off
affects seedlings and young clones, causing collapse at the soil line.

Identification involves examining roots, which should be white or cream-colored
and firm in healthy plants. Affected roots turn brown, become slimy, and emit
a foul odor. Above ground, plants show wilting despite adequate moisture,
yellowing lower leaves, stunted growth, and in severe cases, sudden collapse.
Pythium thrives in waterlogged, oxygen-depleted root zones. Fusarium persists
in soil for years and causes vascular wilting. Rhizoctonia produces reddish-
brown stem lesions.

Overwatering is the single most common predisposing factor. Saturated media
displaces oxygen from root pores, creating anaerobic conditions that
suffocate roots and promote Pythium growth. Compromised roots then provide
entry points for Fusarium and Rhizoctonia. Maintaining well-aerated, properly
drained root zones and avoiding chronic overwatering are the key preventive
measures.

Biological controls include Trichoderma species, which colonize roots and
compete with pathogens, and Bacillus subtilis, which produces antifungal
compounds. Hydrogen peroxide root drenches provide temporary relief, and aloe
vera gel containing salicylic acid may stimulate plant immune responses. In
hydroponics, maintaining dissolved oxygen levels, keeping reservoir
temperatures below 22 degrees Celsius, and using enzyme-based cleaning products
all contribute to root health.

## Nutrient Deficiencies and Toxicities

Distinguishing between pest damage, disease symptoms, and nutrient disorders is
a core troubleshooting challenge. Nutrient problems produce patterned symptoms
following predictable rules based on nutrient mobility. Mobile nutrients
(including nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium) can be relocated
from older tissue to new growth, so deficiencies appear first on lower leaves.
Immobile nutrients (calcium, iron, manganese, zinc, boron) cannot be
translocated, so deficiencies appear first on new growth at growing tips.

Nitrogen deficiency manifests as uniform yellowing of lower leaves progressing
upward. Phosphorus deficiency causes purpling of stems and petioles and slows
growth. Potassium deficiency produces characteristic marginal chlorosis and
necrosis — leaf edges turn brown while interior tissue stays green. Calcium
deficiency affects new growth, causing twisted, deformed leaves and increased
bud rot susceptibility. Magnesium deficiency presents as interveinal chlorosis
on older leaves. Iron deficiency causes interveinal chlorosis on the newest
leaves — the opposite pattern from magnesium deficiency.

Nutrient toxicities often result from overzealous feeding. Nitrogen toxicity
causes dark green foliage and downward clawing of leaf tips, and can delay
flowering onset. Excessive EC causes generalized nutrient burn with brown,
crispy leaf tips across the canopy.

The importance of pH in nutrient availability cannot be overstated. Cannabis
in soil performs optimally at pH 6.0 to 7.0; hydroponic systems target 5.5 to
6.5. Outside these ranges, essential nutrients become chemically unavailable
even when abundant — a condition called nutrient lockout. Regular pH
monitoring and adjustment prevents many apparent nutrient problems.

## Environmental Stress Disorders

Environmental stressors represent a distinct category of health problems caused
by suboptimal growing conditions. These are significant because stressed
plants are far more susceptible to pests and diseases. Heat stress (leaf
tacoing, fox-tailing, bleaching) above 30 degrees Celsius reduces plant
defenses. Light stress from excessive intensity causes chlorophyll destruction
and potency loss; light leaks during dark periods disrupt flowering hormones
and can trigger hermaphroditism or re-vegetation. Cold stress slows
metabolism, causing purple stems and slow growth. Root zone temperatures below
15 degrees Celsius dramatically reduce nutrient uptake.

Humidity disorders span a wide spectrum. Low humidity (below 40 percent) during
vegetative growth causes excessive transpiration and tip burn. High humidity
(above 70 percent) during flowering invites Botrytis and powdery mildew. Rapid
humidity fluctuations cause guttation that can harbor pathogens. Managing
humidity is particularly challenging during dark periods in sealed indoor
grows, where transpiration ceases while moisture from media continues to
evaporate.

## Preventive Protocols and Quarantine

The most effective management strategy is preventing problems from ever
entering the grow environment. This "clean room" protocol involves
comprehensive hygiene and biosecurity practices. Quarantine is the first line
of defense — every new plant or clone should be isolated for one to two weeks
and inspected regularly before entering the main grow space. Many devastating
infestations trace to a single infected clone that bypassed quarantine.

Sanitation between grows is critical for breaking pest and disease cycles. The
entire space should be cleaned: all plant material removed, surfaces sterilized
with hydrogen peroxide or diluted bleach, and all equipment cleaned. Grow
tents should be vacuumed in corners and crevices and left fallow for several
days. Air filtration using HEPA filters on intake vents prevents spores and
small insects from entering. Physical screening on vents and doorways provides
additional barriers. Sealed rooms with positive air pressure prevent unfiltered
air entry.

Maintaining standing populations of beneficial organisms — predatory mites in
the canopy, beneficial nematodes in growing media, Bacillus subtilis on leaf
surfaces — creates a biological buffer that intercepts introductions before
they establish. Regular monitoring through inspection, sticky traps, and
environmental logging provides early warning of any breach. When these systems
work together, the grower shifts from reactive crisis management to proactive
prevention and maintenance.
