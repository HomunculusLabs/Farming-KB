# Companion Planting and Polycultures

Companion planting is the practice of growing different plant species in close
proximity for their mutual benefit. The concept is ancient -- indigenous peoples
worldwide developed sophisticated intercropping systems millennia before modern
agriculture -- yet it remains one of the most accessible and immediately
practical techniques available to home gardeners, homesteaders, and farmers
seeking to reduce their dependence on chemical inputs. At its best, companion
planting creates a miniature ecosystem in each garden bed, where plants
support each other through [[arbuscule-isolation-and-nutrient-exchange-mechanisms]], pest control, pollination,
and physical shelter.

The term "polyculture" describes the broader practice of growing multiple
species together as a system, of which companion planting is one expression. A
polyculture might be as simple as interplanting carrots and onions in alternating
rows, or as complex as a seven-layer food forest with dozens of interacting
species. What distinguishes a polyculture from random mixed planting is
intentional design: each species is chosen for the functions it performs within
the community, and the arrangement is planned to maximize beneficial
interactions while minimizing competition.

This article explores the mechanisms behind companion planting, examines the
scientific evidence for and against traditional pairings, details proven
polyculture systems, explains the role of fungi and soil biology in plant
communities, and provides practical guidance for designing companion planting
systems in vegetable gardens, orchards, and integrated landscapes.

## How Companion Planting Works

Companion planting operates through several distinct mechanisms, each
supported by varying levels of scientific evidence. Understanding these
mechanisms allows the gardener to move beyond rote memorization of "good
companion" lists toward thoughtful design based on ecological principles.

### Nutrient Sharing and Nitrogen Fixation

The most well-documented companion mechanism involves nitrogen fixation by
leguminous plants. Legumes host symbiotic Rhizobium bacteria in their root
nodules that convert atmospheric nitrogen gas into ammonium, a form that
plants can absorb. When legumes are interplanted with heavy-feeding crops,
the fixed nitrogen becomes available to neighboring plants through root
exudates, decomposition of legume residues, and mycorrhizal transfer.

White clover used as a living mulch under fruit trees fixes 100 to 200 pounds
of nitrogen per acre per year. Crimson clover, vetch, and field peas provide
similar benefits in vegetable rotations. The critical timing rule is to
terminate legumes before they set seed, because nitrogen moves from leaves to
seeds as pods form. Maximum nitrogen availability occurs at early flowering.

Beyond nitrogen, deep-rooted companion plants serve as "[[comparison-dynamic-accumulators-vs-cover-crops]],"
mining minerals from subsoil that shallow-rooted crops cannot reach. Comfrey
brings up potassium, calcium, and phosphorus from depths of six to ten feet.
Chicory, dandelion, yarrow, and daikon radish perform similar functions. When
these plants drop leaves or are cut for mulch, the minerals become available
to neighboring plants at the soil surface.

### Pest Deterrence and Biological Control

Companion planting for pest control works through several pathways. Some
plants produce [[query-how-do-fungal-volatile-compounds-affect-plant-growth-and-other-microorganisms]] that directly repel insect pests: garlic and
chives exude sulfur compounds that may deter aphids; marigold roots secrete
alpha-terthienyl, which repels root-knot nematodes; rosemary and sage release
aromatic oils that may mask the scent of host plants from pests like carrot
fly.

More important than direct repellence is the indirect pest control provided
by insectary plants -- flowering species that attract predatory insects.
Hoverflies are perhaps the most valuable of these predators. Before laying
eggs, the female hoverfly needs protein from pollen. She feeds from
open-structured flowers where pollen is easily accessible, then lays
individual eggs on colonies of aphids. The hatching larvae devour aphids by
the thousands. Plants that attract hoverflies include marigolds, poppies,
nasturtiums, phacelia, and buckwheat.

Ladybugs, lacewings, parasitic wasps, and predatory bugs provide similar
pest control services. The key to attracting them is continuous bloom from
early spring through late fall, achieved by selecting a diverse mix of
insectary plants with staggered flowering times.

### Physical Support and Spatial Efficiency

Some companion plants provide structural support for others. The classic
example is corn serving as a living trellis for climbing beans in the Three
Sisters system. Tall sunflowers can support cucumbers or climbing beans.
Scarlet runner beans climbing up corn stalks or sunflowers is both productive
and visually striking.

Vertical stacking -- combining tall plants, medium plants, and ground covers
in the same bed -- dramatically increases the productive use of space. A bed
might contain tomatoes (tall), peppers (medium), and lettuce or basil
(low), each occupying a different vertical niche and root zone. This
approach is the garden-scale version of the seven-layer food forest
architecture.
