## Overview

Cinnamon is one of the oldest and most valued spices in human history, derived from the dried inner bark of trees in the genus *Cinnamomum* (family Lauraceae). The spice trade in cinnamon shaped global commerce for millennia, from ancient Egyptian embalming rituals to modern supermarket shelves. Today, cinnamon is cultivated across tropical and subtropical regions, with production concentrated in Sri Lanka, Indonesia, China, Vietnam, and Madagascar.

Four species dominate commercial production:

- **Cinnamomum verum** J. Presl (syn. *C. zeylanicum*) — true cinnamon, Ceylon cinnamon
- **Cinnamomum cassia** (L.) J. Presl — Chinese cassia
- **Cinnamomum burmannii** (Nees & T. Nees) Blume — Indonesian cassia, Korintje
- **Cinnamomum loureiroi** Nees — Vietnamese cassia, Saigon cinnamon

Each species produces bark with a distinct flavor profile, essential oil composition, and coumarin content, leading to different culinary and industrial applications.

## Botanical Description

### Cinnamomum verum (Ceylon Cinnamon)

*C. verum* is an evergreen tree reaching 10–15 m in the wild but maintained at 2–3 m under cultivation through coppicing. Leaves are ovate to elliptic, 7–18 cm long, with three to five prominent lateral veins. Young leaves are reddish, turning glossy dark green at maturity. The characteristic aroma is released when leaves are crushed. Flowers are small, pale yellow to greenish, arranged in paniculate inflorescences. Fruit is a dark purple drupe, approximately 1 cm long, containing a single seed.

The bark is smooth and pale brown on young shoots, becoming rough and fissured on older wood. The inner bark — the spice — is thin, papery, and tightly quilled when dried. Ceylon cinnamon quills are distinguished by their multiple thin layers, light tan color, and fragile, feather-like texture.

### Cinnamomum cassia (Chinese Cassia)

C. cassia is a larger tree, reaching 15–20 m, with thicker, rougher bark. Leaves are leathery, 10–15 cm long, with prominent three-vein patterning. The bark is notably thicker (2–3 mm) than Ceylon cinnamon, producing hard, woody quills that are difficult to grind without industrial equipment. The flavor is stronger, more pungent, and less delicate than true cinnamon, with higher volatile oil content (1–2% vs. 0.5–1% for Ceylon).

### Other Commercial Species

**C. burmannii** produces bark intermediate in thickness between Ceylon and Chinese cassia, with a sweet, mild flavor. It is the most widely exported cassia type, marketed as "Korintje cinnamon" from Indonesia.

**C. loureiroi** produces the highest volatile oil content (2–4%) among commercial cinnamons, with a bold, sweet-spicy flavor. Vietnamese cassia commands premium prices in the US market.

## History and Trade

### Ancient Origins

Cinnamon was among the first spices traded internationally. References appear in Chinese texts as early as 2700 BCE (Shennong Ben Cao Jing), Egyptian hieroglyphics from 1500 BCE (used in embalming and perfumery), and Hebrew scriptures (Exodus 30:23, anointing oil formulation). Arab traders controlled the cinnamon trade for centuries, concealing the true source of the spice behind elaborate origin stories involving cinnamon birds and mythical lands to maintain monopoly pricing.

The exact source of cinnamon remained a closely guarded secret until Portuguese explorers located Ceylon (Sri Lanka) in 1505. The Portuguese established fortified trading posts and controlled production until the Dutch displaced them in 1658. The Dutch East India Company (VOC) monopolized the cinnamon trade for nearly 150 years, systematically managing wild cinnamon stands and restricting cultivation to prevent oversupply.

British control began in 1796, and under colonial administration, cinnamon cultivation was expanded beyond the natural range. Commercial plantations were established in the 19th century, transitioning the industry from wild harvest to managed cultivation.

### Modern Trade

Global cinnamon production exceeds 280,000 metric tons annually (FAO estimates). Indonesia is the largest producer by volume (~40% of global supply), followed by China, Vietnam, Sri Lanka, and Madagascar. Sri Lanka dominates the true cinnamon (C. verum) market, producing approximately 80–90% of the world's Ceylon cinnamon.

The United States is the largest importer, consuming roughly 30% of global production. The US market overwhelmingly favors cassia cinnamon (primarily Indonesian and Vietnamese), while European markets show stronger demand for Ceylon cinnamon, partly due to EU regulations on coumarin content in food products.

## Growing Conditions

### Climate

Cinnamon thrives in tropical and subtropical climates with the following requirements:

| Parameter | Optimal Range | Tolerable Range |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|
| Temperature | 25–30°C | 18–35°C |
| Annual Rainfall | 1,500–2,500 mm | 1,000–3,500 mm |
| Altitude | Sea level–500 m | Up to 1,500 m (Ceylon) |
| Relative Humidity | 70–85% | 50–95% |
| Frost Tolerance | None | None |

Young plants require partial shade (40–60% light reduction) for the first 2–3 years. Mature trees perform best in full sun to partial shade. Consistent moisture is critical during establishment; established trees tolerate seasonal dry periods of 2–3 months.

Wind protection is important, as strong winds cause bark damage and reduce yield. Shelterbelts of taller trees are commonly incorporated into plantation design.

### Soil

Cinnamon is adaptable to a range of soil types but performs best in:

- **Sandy loam to loamy soils** with good drainage
- **Soil pH** of 5.5–6.5 (slightly acidic)
- **Organic matter content** above 2%
- **Well-drained soils** — waterlogging causes root rot and tree death
- **Deep soils** (60+ cm) to accommodate the taproot

Heavy clay soils, saline soils, and waterlogged sites are unsuitable. Cinnamon tolerates moderately low fertility but responds well to organic amendments and balanced fertilization.

## Propagation

### Seed Propagation

Seeds are the primary method for C. verum propagation. Fresh seeds have high viability (80–90%) but lose germination capacity rapidly; seeds should be sown within 2–3 weeks of harvest. Key steps:

1. **Seed collection**: Harvest ripe fruits (dark purple), remove pulp by maceration in water
2. **Seed treatment**: Soak seeds in water for 24 hours; no stratification required
3. **Sowing**: Sow in nursery beds or polybags at 1–2 cm depth; spacing 5 × 5 cm in beds
4. **Germination**: Occurs in 15–30 days at 25–30°C under 40–50% shade
5. **Pricking out**: Transplant seedlings to polybags at the 2–4 leaf stage
6. **Nursery duration**: 8–12 months before field planting

Cassia species are also propagated by seed, though C. cassia shows some recalcitrance and benefits from immediate sowing after harvest.

### Vegetative Propagation

Vegetative methods ensure clonal uniformity and earlier bearing:

- **Cuttings**: Semi-hardwood cuttings (15–20 cm, 2–3 nodes) treated with IBA (2,000–3,000 ppm) root in 60–90 days under mist propagation. Success rates of 60–80% are achievable with C. verum.
- **Air layering (marcotting)**: Applied to 1–2 year old shoots. Rooting occurs in 2–3 months. Layered plants establish faster than seedlings but produce fewer plants per parent tree.
- **Tissue culture**: Micropropagation from nodal and shoot tip explants has been standardized for C. verum on MS medium with BAP and NAA. Used for large-scale planting material production and conservation of elite germplasm.

### Root Suckers

C. verum produces root suckers freely, which can be separated and planted. This is a low-cost propagation method commonly used by smallholders in Sri Lanka but does not ensure genetic uniformity.

## Plantation Establishment

### Site Preparation

- Clear land and remove competing vegetation
- Prepare planting pits 45 × 45 × 45 cm
- Incorporate well-decomposed compost or farmyard manure (5–10 kg per pit)
- Mark out at recommended spacing

### Planting

| System | Spacing | Trees per Hectare |
|--------|---------|-------------------|
| Sole crop | 1.2 × 1.2 m | ~6,900 |
| Sole crop (wider) | 1.5 × 1.5 m | ~4,400 |
| Agroforestry | 3 × 3 m | ~1,100 |
| With shade trees | 2 × 2 m | ~2,500 |

Planting is done at the onset of the monsoon season. Seedlings are removed from polybags with root ball intact and planted at the same depth as in the nursery. Mulch around the base to conserve moisture. Staking may be needed in windy locations.

### Intercropping

Cinnamon integrates well into agroforestry systems. Common intercrops during the establishment phase (first 3–4 years) include:

- **Food crops**: Bananas, pineapples, turmeric, ginger, pepper
- **Shade trees**: Coconut, areca nut, gliricidia, silver oak
- **Cover crops**: Cowpea, mung bean, pigeon pea for nitrogen fixation and soil protection

In mature cinnamon-coconut intercropping systems, cinnamon is planted in the interspaces of coconut palms, utilizing the partial shade. This is a widespread practice in Sri Lanka and Indonesia.

## Plantation Management

### Irrigation

Supplemental irrigation during dry periods increases growth rates and bark yield. Drip irrigation is preferred; young plants require 5–10 liters per plant every 3–5 days during establishment. Mature trees in high-rainfall zones typically do not require irrigation.

### Fertilization

Recommended annual applications per tree (Ceylon cinnamon, Sri Lankan guidelines):

| Age (years) | N (g) | P₂O₅ (g) | K₂O (g) | Organic Matter (kg) |
|-------------|-------|-----------|---------|---------------------|
| 1 | 20 | 30 | 30 | 5 |
| 2 | 40 | 40 | 40 | 5 |
| 3 | 60 | 60 | 60 | 10 |
| 4+ | 80 | 80 | 80 | 10 |

Split applications at the onset of monsoons. Foliar micronutrient sprays (zinc, boron) may be beneficial on deficient soils. Excessive nitrogen promotes vegetative growth at the expense of bark quality.

### Pruning and Coppicing

Cinnamon trees are managed through repeated coppicing:

- **First harvest**: At 2.5–3 years after planting
- **Coppice cycle**: Every 1.5–2 years thereafter
- **Coppice management**: Retain 3–5 shoots per stool; remove weak and overcrowded shoots
- **Stool renewal**: After 10–12 cycles (20–25 years), replanting is recommended as productivity declines

Formative pruning during the first year establishes a straight, unbranched stem of 1.5–2 m, which maximizes bark yield.

### Weed Management

Manual weeding is standard practice. Keep a 1 m weed-free radius around young trees. Mulching with dried leaves or compost suppresses weeds and conserves moisture. Herbicides are generally not recommended in cinnamon cultivation due to shallow root systems and organic market requirements.

## Harvesting and Processing

### Harvesting

Harvesting is timed to coincide with the rainy season when bark peels easily:

1. **Stem selection**: Choose shoots 1.2–2.0 cm in diameter, approximately 2 years old
2. **Cutting**: Cut stems at 15 cm above ground level
3. **Stripping**: Within 24 hours, make longitudinal cuts and peel the bark
4. **Scraping**: Remove the outer corky layer (scrape) with a curved knife

### Quilling

The distinctive quilled form is produced by skilled workers:

1. **Peeling**: Make two longitudinal cuts 30 cm apart and peel the bark strip
2. **Rolling**: Press and roll bark strips by hand to form quills
3. **Stuffing**: Fill small pieces of bark into the quill tubes to maintain round shape
4. **Drying**: Dry quills in shade for 4–7 days (direct sun causes cracking and oil loss)
5. **Grading**: Sort by diameter, length, color, and aroma

Ceylon cinnamon produces the finest quills — thin, multi-layered, and tightly rolled. Cassia bark is typically sold as thick single-layer quills or as broken pieces (chips).

### Grading Standards (Ceylon Cinnamon, Sri Lankan Grades)

| Grade | Quill Diameter (mm) | Characteristics |
|-------|-------------------|-----------------|
| C5 Special | ≤6 | Finest quality, very thin, light color |
| C5 | 6–10 | Thin, uniform, light tan |
| C4 | 10–14 | Medium quality, slightly thicker |
| C3 | 14–18 | Standard export quality |
| M5/H1/H2 | >18 | Lower grades, thick bark |

### Processing Yields

| Product | Yield from Fresh Bark |
|---------|----------------------|
| Dried quills | 25–30% by weight |
| Chips and quillings | 15–20% |
| Essential oil (distillation) | 0.5–1.0% (Ceylon), 1–2% (cassia) |
| Oleoresin | 2–5% |

### Post-Harvest Storage

Dried cinnamon should be stored in cool, dry, well-ventilated conditions:

- **Temperature**: Below 25°C
- **Relative humidity**: Below 60%
- **Shelf life**: 2–3 years (whole quills), 6–12 months (ground)
- **Pest control**: Fumigation may be required for stored products; organic producers use controlled atmosphere storage

Vacuum packaging significantly extends shelf life by preventing volatile oil loss and moisture absorption.

## Essential Oil and Oleoresin Production

### Essential Oil

Cinnamon bark oil and leaf oil are commercially important products:

**Bark oil** is steam-distilled from dried bark or from bark chips and quillings unsuitable for the spice trade. Key constituents:

- *C. verum* bark oil: cinnamaldehyde (60–75%), eugenol (5–10%), linalool
- *C. cassia* bark oil: cinnamaldehyde (85–95%)

**Leaf oil** is distilled from pruned leaves, providing additional income:

- *C. verum* leaf oil: eugenol (70–85%), β-caryophyllene, linalool
- *C. cassia* leaf oil: eugenol (70–80%)

Distillation equipment: Simple field-scale steam distillation units or industrial stainless steel distillers. Bark oil commands prices 10–20× higher than leaf oil.

### Oleoresin

Cinnamon oleoresin is produced by solvent extraction (ethanol or acetone) of ground bark. It contains the full spectrum of volatile and non-volatile flavor compounds. Used extensively in processed food, beverage, and pharmaceutical industries where consistent flavor and color are required.

## Pests and Diseases

### Major Pests

| Pest | Type | Damage | Management |
|------|------|--------|------------|
| *Coleopterus* spp. (cinnamon beetle) | Beetle | Boring into stems and bark | Remove and destroy infested wood; botanical sprays |
| *Xyleborus* spp. (shot hole borer) | Beetle | Tunneling in stems, fungal introduction | Maintain tree vigor; pheromone traps |
| *Helopeltis antonii* (tea mosquito bug) | Mirid bug | Feeding on young shoots, dieback | Neem-based sprays; biological control |
| *Dasineura cinnamomi* (cinnamon gall midge) | Midge | Galls on leaves | Remove galled leaves; parasitoid releases |
| *Pseudococcus* spp. (mealybugs) | Scale | Sap feeding, sooty mold | Natural enemies; horticultural oil |
| Red mites (*Oligonychus* spp.) | Mite | Leaf stippling, defoliation | Sulfur sprays; predatory mites |

### Major Diseases

| Disease | Causal Agent | Symptoms | Management |
|---------|-------------|----------|------------|
| Leaf spot | *Colletotrichum gloeosporioides* | Brown spots with concentric rings | Copper-based fungicides; remove infected leaves |
| Rust | *Puccinia cinnamomi* | Orange pustules on leaf undersides | Remove infected foliage; improve air circulation |
| Root rot | *Phytophthora* spp., *Fusarium* spp. | Wilting, root decay, tree death | Improve drainage; avoid waterlogging; Trichoderma applications |
| Canker | *Corticium* spp. | Bark lesions, stem dieback | Prune affected branches; copper sprays |
| White rot | *Rigidoporus lignosus* | White mycelium at base, wood decay | Remove infected trees; soil solarization |

Integrated pest management (IPM) is standard practice. Biological control agents including *Trichoderma harzianum*, *Beauveria bassiana*, and predatory mites (*Amblyseius* spp.) are widely used in organic cinnamon production.

## Culinary and Medicinal Uses

### Culinary

Cinnamon is used globally in both sweet and savory applications:

- **Baking**: Cinnamon rolls, apple pie, snickerdoodles, breads, cakes
- **Beverages**: Mulled wine, chai, horchata, hot chocolate, coffee flavoring
- **Savory dishes**: Moroccan tagines, Indian curries (garam masala), Mexican mole, Middle Eastern rice pilafs
- **Preserved foods**: Pickling spice blends, fruit preserves, chutneys
- **Confectionery**: Cinnamon candies, chewing gum, chocolate

Ceylon cinnamon is preferred in desserts and delicate dishes for its mild, sweet, complex flavor. Cassia cinnamon is used where bold, assertive cinnamon flavor is desired — in American baking, commercial food processing, and spiced beverages.

### Medicinal and Bioactive Properties

Research has documented numerous bioactive properties of cinnamon and its constituents:

- **Antimicrobial**: Cinnamaldehyde and eugenol show activity against bacteria (*Staphylococcus aureus*, *E. coli*, *Salmonella*), fungi (*Candida albicans*), and viruses
- **Anti-inflammatory**: Polyphenol compounds (proanthocyanidins) reduce inflammatory markers
- **Antioxidant**: Cinnamon ranks among the highest antioxidant spices (ORAC 131,420 μmol TE/100g)
- **Glycemic regulation**: Meta-analyses suggest cinnamon supplementation (1–6 g/day) may lower fasting blood glucose in type 2 diabetics, though evidence is mixed
- **Lipid profile**: Some studies report reductions in total cholesterol, LDL, and triglycerides
- **Neuroprotective**: Cinnamaldehyde and epicatechin show potential in Alzheimer's disease models

### Coumarin Content and Safety

A critical distinction between cinnamon types is coumarin content:

| Species | Coumarin Content (mg/kg) | Safe Daily Intake* |
|---------|-------------------------|---------------------|
| C. verum (Ceylon) | 0.01–0.1 | Effectively unlimited |
| C. cassia (Chinese) | 2,000–7,000 | ~0.5–1 tsp |
| C. burmannii (Indonesian) | 2,000–6,000 | ~0.5–1 tsp |
| C. loureiroi (Vietnamese) | 100–1,500 | 1–3 tsp |

*Based on EFSA tolerable daily intake of 0.1 mg coumarin/kg body weight for a 60 kg adult.

Coumarin is hepatotoxic at high doses and is a known carcinogen in rodents. Ceylon cinnamon is preferred for regular daily consumption, while cassia should be used in moderation.

### Market Trends

- **Organic cinnamon**: Premium of 30–50% over conventional; growing demand in EU and North America
- **Fair trade certification**: Increasing adoption in Sri Lanka and Madagascar smallholder systems
- **Value-added products**: Cinnamon extracts, essential oils, and dietary supplements command higher margins than bulk bark
- **Ceylon cinnamon premium**: True cinnamon commands 3–5× the price of cassia by weight
- **Geographical indication (GI)**: Sri Lanka has obtained GI protection for Ceylon cinnamon in the EU, protecting against adulteration

## Sustainability Challenges

### Soil Degradation

Continuous cinnamon cultivation depletes soil organic matter. Recommended practices include:

- Incorporating leguminous cover crops and green manures
- Returning cinnamon leaf litter to the soil
- Applying compost and organic amendments annually
- Rotating with nitrogen-fixing crops after 10–15 years

### Biodiversity Concerns

Wild *Cinnamomum* populations in Sri Lanka and the Western Ghats face pressure from overharvesting and habitat loss. Conservation efforts include:

- Protected area designation for remaining wild stands
- Germplasm conservation through seed banks and field gene banks
- Promotion of cultivated sources to reduce wild harvest pressure

### Adulteration

Cinnamon is one of the most commonly adulterated spices. Common adulterants include:

- Cassia bark sold as Ceylon cinnamon
- C. tamala (Indian bay leaf) bark
- Added fillers (sawdust, bark from non-cinnamon species)
- Artificial cinnamaldehyde in essential oil

DNA barcoding and HPLC fingerprinting are increasingly used for authentication and quality control.

### Climate Change

Rising temperatures and altered rainfall patterns may shift suitable growing zones. Cinnamon requires consistent moisture; extended droughts reduce yield and bark quality. Research into drought-tolerant rootstocks and irrigation-efficient production systems is ongoing.
