# Hempcrete and Hemp-Lime Construction

Hempcrete is a biocomposite [[fungal-building-material-deterioration]] made from the woody inner core
of the hemp plant (hurd or shiv) mixed with a lime-based binder and water.
Unlike concrete, hempcrete is not used as a structural material in load-bearing
applications but serves as an insulation and infill material around a timber
frame or other structural system. The material has attracted significant
interest in the sustainable building movement because it sequesters carbon
dioxide during the growth of the hemp plant and locks it away for the lifetime
of the building. Hempcrete walls offer excellent thermal insulation, effective
moisture regulation, and a high degree of vapor permeability that creates
healthy indoor environments.

The term "hempcrete" is a colloquial name; the material is more precisely
called "hemp-lime" in technical standards and academic literature. It should
not be confused with hemp-based plastics or hemp fiber-reinforced concrete,
which are entirely different materials. Hempcrete contains no Portland cement
in its traditional formulation, relying instead on lime binders that
carbonate over time, gradually absorbing additional carbon dioxide from the
atmosphere. This dual [[mycorrhizal-fungi-soil-carbon-sequestration-by-type]] — biological during hemp growth
and mineralogical during lime carbonation — makes hempcrete one of the few
[[natural-building-materials-guide]] that can genuinely claim to be carbon-negative over its
lifecycle.

## Hemp Cultivation and Fiber Processing

Industrial hemp ([[comparison-cannabis-sativa-vs-cannabis-indica]] L.) is a variety of cannabis cultivated
specifically for fiber, seed, and hurd production. Unlike drug varieties,
industrial hemp contains less than 0.3% THC by dry weight and is legally
distinguished from marijuana in most jurisdictions. The plant grows rapidly,
reaching heights of 3-5 meters in approximately 100-120 days, making it one of
the fastest-growing terrestrial plants. Hemp cultivation requires minimal
pesticides and herbicides due to the plant's natural resistance to many pests
and its dense canopy, which suppresses weeds effectively.

The stalk of the hemp plant consists of two main components: the long bast
fibers on the exterior and the woody hurd (also called shiv) in the interior.
For hempcrete production, it is the hurd that is used. After harvesting, the
stalks are retted (partially decomposed by moisture and microorganisms) to
separate the bast fibers from the hurd. The hurd is then cleaned, graded by
particle size, and dried to a moisture content of approximately 12-15% before
use in construction. Particle size is important: finer grades produce denser,
stronger hempcrete with lower insulation values, while coarser grades produce
lighter, more insulating material.

A single hectare of industrial hemp can produce enough hurd to build a
moderately sized house. The crop also produces bast fibers (for textiles,
composites, and rope) and seeds (for food, oil, and animal feed), making
hemp cultivation an exceptionally productive use of agricultural land. The
deep root system of hemp also improves soil structure and can remediate
contaminated soils through phytoremediation, though this is not relevant to
the construction-grade hurd supply chain.

## What Hempcrete Is and How It Differs from Concrete

Hempcrete is fundamentally different from conventional concrete in its
composition, structural role, and behavior. Concrete is a mixture of Portland
cement, sand, gravel, and water that cures through hydration to form a hard,
dense, load-bearing material. Hempcrete replaces the cement with lime binder
and the mineral aggregate with hemp hurd. The resulting material is lightweight
(roughly one-seventh the density of concrete), porous, and excellent at
insulating, but it cannot support significant structural loads on its own.

The lime binder in hempcrete is typically a blend of hydrated lime and
hydraulic lime. When mixed with water and hurd, the lime coats the hemp
particles and fills the spaces between them. As the material dries and ages,
the lime undergoes carbonation — a slow chemical reaction with atmospheric
carbon dioxide that converts calcium hydroxide into calcium carbonate. This
carbonation process gradually hardens the material and gives it its
compressive strength. Unlike concrete, which achieves most of its strength
within 28 days, hempcrete continues to harden and carbonate over decades.

The binding mechanism in hempcrete is unique. The lime does not so much
glue the hemp particles together as it forms a mineral matrix around them.
The hemp hurd acts as an aggregate and also as a scaffold that supports the
lime as it carbonates. The porous structure of the hurd allows moisture to
move through the material freely, which is essential for the carbonation
process and for the material's breathability. This porosity is also what
gives hempcrete its excellent insulation properties.

## Historical Development and Modern Revival

The use of hemp in construction has ancient roots. Hemp fiber was used in
mortar mixtures in Roman times and in traditional lime renders across Europe
for centuries. However, modern hempcrete as a distinct building material was
developed in France in the 1990s. French engineer Yves Krehen discovered that
mixing hemp hurd with lime produced a lightweight, insulating material with
remarkable moisture-regulating properties. He patented the method and founded
the company IsoHemp, which continues to produce hempcrete products today.

From France, hempcrete construction spread to the United Kingdom, Germany,
Belgium, the Netherlands, and other European countries. The UK became a
significant center of hempcrete development, with companies like
Lime Technology (now UK Hempcrete) promoting the material and developing
standardized construction details. The first hempcrete building in the UK
was completed in 2000, and hundreds of hempcrete homes have been built there
since. In the United States, hempcrete gained traction more slowly due to
legal restrictions on hemp cultivation, but the 2018 Farm Bill legalized
industrial hemp nationally and accelerated adoption.

International standards have followed the material's growing use. In 2018,
the European Committee for Standardization published technical specifications
for hemp-lime as a building material (EN 16872 and related standards). These
standards cover material properties, [[soil-testing-methods-interpretation]], and design guidance,
providing architects and engineers with the technical basis for specifying
hempcrete in regulated construction. Building codes in the US and other
countries are gradually incorporating hempcrete, often through alternative
materials provisions or specific code amendments.
