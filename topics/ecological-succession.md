# Ecological Succession

## Definition and Overview

Ecological succession is the process of change in the species structure of an ecological community over time. First formally described by Henry Cowles in 1899 and later systematized by Frederic Clements, succession describes the directional, predictable (though not deterministic) sequence of community development following the formation of new habitat or the disruption of an existing one. The concept remains one of the foundational organizing principles of ecology, bridging population biology, ecosystem ecology, and biogeography.

Succession operates on timescales ranging from decades to millennia, depending on the system and the nature of the initiating disturbance. It involves not only changes in plant and animal communities but also fundamental transformations in soil properties, nutrient cycling, microclimate, and energy flow. Two broad categories are recognized:

- **Primary succession**: establishment of communities on entirely new substrates previously lacking life (e.g., lava flows, glacial moraines).
- **Secondary succession**: recovery of communities following disturbance that removes biomass but leaves soil intact (e.g., fire, logging, flooding).

Understanding succession is critical for predicting ecosystem responses to environmental change, managing natural resources, and guiding ecological restoration.

### Historical Development of the Concept

The concept of ecological succession has deep roots in the history of ecology:

- **Eugenius Warming (1895)**: In *Plantesamfund* (Oecology of Plants), Warming described vegetation changes along environmental gradients and recognized the dynamic nature of plant communities, laying conceptual groundwork for succession theory.
- **Henry Cowles (1899)**: His doctoral work on the vegetation of the sand dunes along Lake Michigan established the first formal documentation of successional change, demonstrating that dune communities varied systematically with the age of the land surface.
- **Frederic Clements (1916)**: Clements formalized succession as a unified, deterministic theory in *Plant Succession*, describing it as an orderly, predictable process analogous to the development of an organism — a view that dominated ecology for decades.
- **Henry Gleason (1917, 1926)**: Provided the major counterpoint to Clements, arguing for an individualistic, stochastic view of community assembly that anticipated modern niche and metacommunity theory.
- **Eugene Odum (1969)**: Extended succession theory to ecosystem-level properties, describing predictable changes in productivity, nutrient cycling, and energy flow during ecosystem development in his influential paper "The Strategy of Ecosystem Development."

## Primary Succession

Primary succession occurs on lifeless substrates where no biological legacy persists. Because soil must develop *de novo*, primary succession is typically slower than secondary succession, often requiring centuries to millennia to reach a mature state.

### Bare Rock and Lava Flows

Volcanic eruptions create new land surfaces that serve as natural laboratories for studying primary succession. Lava flows at Mount St. Helens (1980), Kilauea (Hawaiʻi), and historically at Krakatoa (1883) have provided detailed records of colonization sequences. Initial colonizers are typically lithophytic lichens and cyanobacteria that weather rock surfaces through biochemical processes, gradually forming a thin layer of organic matter. Over decades to centuries, mosses, herbaceous plants, and eventually woody species establish as soil depth and nutrient content increase.

### Glacial Retreat

Glacier Bay, Alaska, has served as a paradigmatic chronosequence for primary succession following deglaciation. Research by Chapin et al. (1994) documented a predictable sequence: crustose lichens → foliose lichens and mosses → nitrogen-fixing shrubs (e.g., *Dryas drummondii*) → alder thickets (*Alnus sinuata*) → spruce (*Picea sitchensis*) and western hemlock (*Tsuga heterophylla*) forest. Nitrogen fixation by *Dryas* and *Alnus* is critical, as the young glacial substrates are severely nitrogen-limited. Over approximately 200 years, aboveground biomass increases by orders of magnitude while soil organic matter accumulates to depths exceeding 50 cm.

### Sand Dunes

The Indiana Dunes along Lake Michigan were the site of Henry Cowles's pioneering work. Sand dune succession proceeds from bare sand (stabilized by beach grass, *Ammophila breviligulata*) through shrub stages (willow, cottonwood) to a beech-maple climax forest on stabilized, nitrogen-enriched soils. The Lake Michigan dune chronosequence demonstrates the classic coupling between physical stabilization, soil development, and community change.

## Secondary Succession

Secondary succession occurs when an existing community is disturbed but soil and propagules remain. Because the soil seed bank, residual roots, and nearby source populations persist, recovery is generally faster than primary succession.

### Post-Fire Succession

Fire is one of the most widespread disturbance agents globally. In many forested ecosystems, fire-adapted species resprout from root crowns or germinate from heat-triggered seed banks within weeks of a burn. In boreal forests, serotinous cones of jack pine (*Pinus banksiana*) and black spruce (*Picea mariana*) release seeds onto the mineral soil exposed by fire, initiating a stand-replacement cycle. Herbaceous fire-followers (e.g., *Epilobium angustifolium*, fireweed) colonize rapidly, followed by shade-intolerant hardwoods and eventually the climax conifers.

### Agricultural Abandonment (Old-Field Succession)

Old-field succession is among the best-studied examples of secondary succession. Following cessation of agriculture, fields are typically colonized by annual weeds (e.g., *Ambrosia*, *Chenopodium*), followed by biennial and perennial herbs, then grasses and shrubs (e.g., *Rubus*, *Rosa*), and finally trees. In eastern North America, the typical endpoint is an oak-hickory or beech-maple forest, though the trajectory can be altered by soil fertility, proximity to seed sources, and herbivory. The classic Buell-Small Succession Study in New Jersey has tracked old-field succession since 1958, providing one of the longest continuous records of community change.

### Post-Logging and Flood Recovery

Selective or clear-cut logging initiates secondary succession that may follow different trajectories depending on harvest intensity, soil compaction, and site preparation. In tropical forests, logging gaps are rapidly colonized by pioneer tree species (e.g., *Cecropia*, *Vismia*) that set the stage for gradual reassembly of the original forest community over decades to centuries. River floodplains undergo cyclic succession driven by depositional and erosional processes; pioneer species colonize fresh alluvium, and communities mature until the next flood event resets the cycle.

## Seral Stages and the Chronosequence Concept

### Seral Stages

Each transitional community in a successional sequence is called a **seral stage**. Seral stages progress from early-successional (pioneer) communities through mid-successional communities to the late-successional or climax community. The full sequence of seral stages is termed a **sere**. Key characteristics of seral stages include:

| Property | Early Seral | Mid Seral | Late Seral / Climax |
|---|---|---|---|
| Species diversity | Low | High | Moderate to high |
| Net primary productivity | High | Moderate | Low (balanced) |
| Biomass accumulation | Rapid | Moderate | Near equilibrium |
| Nutrient retention | Low | Increasing | High |
| Community stability | Low | Moderate | High |
| Dominant strategy | Ruderal/r-selected | Mixed | Competitive/K-selected |

### Chronosequences

A **chronosequence** (or space-for-time substitution) is a research design in which sites of different ages since disturbance are compared to infer temporal dynamics. Chronosequences are powerful because they allow researchers to study processes occurring over centuries within a single field season. However, they rest on the critical assumption that sites differ only in age — an assumption frequently violated by spatial variation in soil, topography, and land-use history. Modern approaches combine chronosequences with long-term monitoring and paleoecological records to validate inferred trajectories.

## Pioneer Species and Their Traits

Pioneer species are the first organisms to colonize disturbed or newly formed habitats. They share a suite of adaptive traits that allow them to establish in harsh, resource-poor, or unstable environments:

- **R-selected life history**: high fecundity, small seed size, rapid growth, short generation time, and semelparous reproduction (Grime's ruderal strategy).
- **Wind-dispersed seeds**: effective long-distance dispersal to reach isolated or remote sites (e.g., composites, birch, willow).
- **Nitrogen fixation**: symbiotic associations with *Rhizobium* (legumes) or *Frankia* (actinorhizal plants such as *Alnus*) that alleviate nitrogen limitation.
- **Crust-forming ability**: lichens, mosses, and cyanobacteria (biological soil crusts) that stabilize surfaces, trap organic particles, and fix atmospheric nitrogen and carbon.
- **Tolerance of environmental extremes**: desiccation tolerance, high light requirements, wide temperature tolerance, and tolerance of low-nutrient substrates.
- **Clonal growth**: vegetative propagation via rhizomes, stolons, or root sprouts, enabling rapid space occupation.

Common pioneer taxa include lichens (*Rhizocarpon*, *Xanthoria*), cyanobacteria (*Nostoc*, *Anabaena*), mosses (*Funaria*, *Polytrichum*), herbaceous fire-followers (*Epilobium*, *Senecio*), nitrogen-fixing shrubs (*Dryas*, *Ceanothus*, *Myrica*), and fast-growing trees (*Betula*, *Populus*, *Salix*).

## Facilitation, Tolerance, and Inhibition Models

Connell and Slatyer (1977) proposed three mechanistic models to explain how species interactions drive successional change:

### Facilitation Model

Early-successional species modify the environment in ways that make it more suitable for later species. Examples include nitrogen fixers increasing soil fertility, shade-tolerant species requiring the canopy provided by pioneers, and mosses creating moist microsites for seedling establishment. Facilitation predicts a strict, ordered sequence of species replacements.

### Tolerance Model

Later species can colonize at any time but are outcompeted by early species under the initial environmental conditions. As conditions change (e.g., increasing shade, changing soil chemistry), later species gradually replace earlier ones through preemption of resources. This model does not require positive interactions and predicts a more flexible, overlapping sequence.

### Inhibition Model

Early species establish and inhibit colonization by later species through competition for resources, allelopathy, or physical space occupation. Succession proceeds only when early species are damaged or die, releasing space. This model predicts that the community composition depends heavily on arrival order and that there is no single inevitable endpoint.

These three models are not mutually exclusive; most successional sequences involve elements of all three operating at different stages and among different species pairs.

## Climax Community Concept

### Clementsian Climax

Frederic Clements (1916, 1936) proposed that succession is a deterministic, superorganismal process converging on a single stable endpoint — the **climax community** — determined by regional climate. The climax was viewed as a complex, self-perpetuating entity analogous to a mature organism. Each regional climate supported a single climax formation (e.g., the "beech-maple climax" of the eastern deciduous forest). This framework implied strong predictability and convergence in successional trajectories.

### Gleasonian Individualism

Henry Gleason (1917, 1926) challenged the Clementsian view, arguing that communities are not integrated superorganisms but fortuitous assemblages of individual species, each responding independently to environmental gradients. Under the Gleasonian perspective, there is no single climax — instead, communities are continuously shifting in composition in response to stochastic events, species dispersal, and fine-scale environmental variation.

### Modern Synthesis

Contemporary ecology largely rejects both extremes. The **polyclimax** concept (Tansley, 1935) acknowledges that multiple community types can be stable under a given climate, determined by local soil, topography, disturbance history, and biotic interactions. The **nonequilibrium** paradigm recognizes that many communities never reach a stable state but exist in a dynamic mosaic shaped by recurring disturbance.

### Alternative Stable States

The concept of **alternative stable states** proposes that ecosystems can exist in multiple distinct configurations under the same environmental conditions, with each state maintained by feedback mechanisms. Transitions between states are often triggered by threshold-crossing disturbances and may be difficult or impossible to reverse (hysteresis). Shallow lakes, for example, can exist in either a clear-water macrophyte-dominated state or a turbid phytoplankton-dominated state, with each stabilized by different feedback loops.

## Autogenic vs. Allogenic Succession

Succession can be driven by forces originating within the ecosystem itself or by external forces:

| Type | Driver | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Autogenic** | Internal biotic processes modify the environment, driving community change | Forest canopy closure reducing understory light; litter accumulation changing soil chemistry; nitrogen fixation enriching soil |
| **Allogenic** | External abiotic forces alter the environment independently of the community | Climate change, sea-level rise, tectonic uplift, geomorphic processes (erosion, deposition), volcanic activity, anthropogenic land-use change |

Most successional sequences involve both autogenic and allogenic components. For example, sand dune succession is driven partly by autogenic vegetation stabilization of sand and partly by allogenic changes in lake level, sediment supply, and wind regime.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi and Soil Development

Mycorrhizal fungi play a critical but often underappreciated role in succession. The progressive development of mycorrhizal networks fundamentally shapes plant community trajectories:

- **Early succession**: arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) are often the first to colonize, forming associations with herbaceous pioneers. In severely degraded or primary successional substrates, mycorrhizal inoculum may be absent, limiting the establishment of late-successional species that depend on these symbioses.
- **Mid to late succession**: ectomycorrhizal fungi (ECM) become dominant in boreal and temperate forest successions, forming mutualisms with trees such as pine, spruce, oak, and beech. ECM networks facilitate nutrient and carbon transfer among plants, influence competitive outcomes, and enhance ecosystem-level nutrient retention.
- **Soil development feedbacks**: mycorrhizal fungi contribute to soil aggregation, organic matter turnover, and the weathering of mineral substrates. The transition from AMF-dominated to ECM-dominated fungal communities is often associated with shifts from fast-cycling to slow-cycling nutrient regimes.

Restoration practitioners increasingly recognize the importance of mycorrhizal inoculation when establishing vegetation on severely degraded or mined lands.

## Disturbance Regimes and the Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis

### Disturbance Regimes

Every ecosystem has a characteristic **disturbance regime** — the temporal and spatial pattern of disturbance events. Regimes are described by:

- **Frequency**: how often disturbances occur
- **Severity/intensity**: the magnitude of biomass removal or environmental alteration
- **Extent/scale**: the spatial area affected
- **Type**: fire, flood, windstorm, herbivory, disease, anthropogenic activity

Disturbance regimes select for species traits and life-history strategies adapted to particular disturbance patterns. Fire-adapted ecosystems (e.g., savannas, boreal forests) have species with thick bark, serotinous cones, or resprouting ability, whereas ecosystems with infrequent disturbance (e.g., tropical rainforests) are dominated by shade-tolerant, slow-growing species.

### Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis

Proposed by Joseph Connell (1978), the **intermediate disturbance hypothesis** (IDH) posits that species diversity is maximized at intermediate levels of disturbance. At low disturbance, competitive exclusion by dominant species reduces diversity. At high disturbance, only the most tolerant or rapidly colonizing species persist. At intermediate disturbance, both early- and late-successional species can coexist in a mosaic of patches at different successional stages.

While influential, the IDH has been challenged by more recent theoretical and empirical work suggesting that diversity–disturbance relationships are highly context-dependent and not universally hump-shaped.

## Succession in Specific Ecosystems

### Boreal Forest After Fire

Boreal forests are among the most extensively fire-prone ecosystems on Earth. Stand-replacing crown fires typically occur on 50–200 year return intervals. Post-fire succession follows a well-documented trajectory:

1. **Immediate post-fire (0–5 years)**: herbaceous fire-followers (fireweed, dwarf fireweed) and resprouting shrubs dominate exposed mineral soil.
2. **Shrub/sapling stage (5–30 years)**: deciduous hardwoods (aspen, birch) and conifer saplings establish rapidly in full sunlight.
3. **Young forest (30–80 years)**: canopy closes; shade-intolerant species are outcompeted; conifers (spruce, fir) begin to dominate the overstory.
4. **Mature/old-growth forest (80–250+ years)**: spruce-fir canopy with a moss-dominated understory, coarse woody debris accumulation, and complex vertical structure.

### Intertidal Zones

Rocky intertidal communities exhibit rapid, observable succession driven by the interplay of physical stress, competition, and predation. Classic experiments by Connell (1961) demonstrated that barnacles (*Chthamalus stellatus*) and mussels (*Mytilus*) compete for primary space on intertidal rocks, with disturbance (wave action, predation by *Thais* snails) resetting the successional clock and maintaining diversity.

### Volcanic Islands: Surtsey and Krakatoa

**Krakatoa** (Indonesia): The catastrophic 1883 eruption sterilized the island, providing one of the earliest documented cases of primary succession on a volcanic substrate. By 1886, blue-green algae and ferns had colonized the ash. Within 50 years, a dense grassland and shrubland had developed, and by the 1930s, forest cover was established. The Krakatoa succession is notable for the rapid role played by animal dispersers (bats, birds) in bringing plant propagules.

**Surtsey** (Iceland): Formed by a volcanic eruption in 1963–1967, Surtsey has been intensively studied as a primary succession site. Colonization began with bacteria, fungi, and lichens on lava surfaces. Seabird colonies, once established, dramatically accelerated soil development through guano deposition, enabling the establishment of higher plants. By 2020, vascular plant species numbered over 80, with a developing bird community and invertebrate fauna.

### Sand Dune Succession

Coastal sand dune systems exhibit succession driven by the interplay of sand burial, salt spray, and nutrient limitation. The classic zonation pattern from beach to inland includes:

1. **Beach and foredune**: sparse vegetation of *Ammophila* and *Cakile*; heavy sand movement.
2. **Backdune and dune grassland**: increasing species richness; grasses, herbs, and low shrubs.
3. **Shrub thicket**: *Myrica*, *Rhus*, *Prunus* maritima.
4. **Maritime forest**: *Pinus rigida*, *Quercus* spp., *Ilex opaca* on stabilized, humus-rich soils.

### Tropical Forest Gap Dynamics

In species-rich tropical forests, succession often operates at the scale of individual treefall gaps rather than entire landscapes. When a large canopy tree falls, it creates a gap of 50–1000 m² with dramatically increased light availability at the forest floor. This gap phase initiates a micro-successional sequence:

- **Gap colonizers** (0–5 years): fast-growing, shade-intolerant pioneers such as *Cecropia*, *Piper*, *Trema*, and *Miconia* rapidly fill the gap.
- **Building phase** (5–30 years): shade-tolerant saplings that recruited before gap formation or established shortly after begin to overtop the pioneers.
- **Mature phase** (30–100+ years): the gap closes, shade-tolerant climax species dominate, and the gap gradually becomes indistinguishable from the surrounding forest.

Gap dynamics theory, developed by Hubbell and others, suggests that tree species diversity in tropical forests is partly maintained by a shifting mosaic of gaps at different stages of regeneration, each favoring different species.

## Applied Ecology

### Restoration Using Succession Principles

Ecological restoration explicitly leverages succession to accelerate the recovery of degraded ecosystems. Key principles include:

- **Assisting natural regeneration** by removing barriers to succession (e.g., invasive species control, reducing grazing pressure).
- **Jump-starting succession** through site preparation, soil amendment, and targeted planting of early- and mid-successional species that create favorable conditions for late-successional species.
- **Using nurse plants** to facilitate the establishment of target species in harsh environments (common in arid and tropical restoration).
- **Applying nucleation**: establishing small patches of vegetation that serve as colonization foci and gradually expand outward.

### Agroforestry Succession

Agroforestry systems mimic natural successional processes to create productive, resilient agricultural landscapes:

- **Successional agroforestry** (syntropic agriculture, Ernst Götsch's method) intentionally plants a sequence of species that replicate natural succession — fast-growing pioneers that shade and mulch the soil, followed by mid-successional fruit trees and timber species, and eventually climax-type canopy species.
- **Shade coffee and cacao systems** incorporate canopy trees that simulate forest structure, providing habitat for biodiversity while maintaining crop production.
- **Alley cropping and silvopastoral systems** integrate trees with crops or livestock in configurations that progressively build soil and ecological function.

### Assisted Natural Regeneration (ANR)

ANR is a cost-effective restoration approach that protects and enhances natural successional processes rather than establishing plantations. Techniques include:

- Protecting degraded areas from fire, grazing, and further disturbance
- Enrichment planting of key species (e.g., nitrogen fixers, late-successional trees)
- Soil conservation measures (contour bunds, erosion control)
- Community-based fire management and governance

ANR has been successfully applied in tropical forest restoration across Southeast Asia, West Africa, and Latin America, often achieving forest cover recovery at a fraction of the cost of conventional reforestation.

## Modern Research Directions

### Climate Change and Succession Trajectories

Climate change is altering successional dynamics in several ways:

- **Range shifts**: as temperatures and precipitation patterns change, species are migrating poleward and to higher elevations, disrupting historical successional sequences. Novel species assemblages are forming with unknown trajectories.
- **Altered disturbance regimes**: increased wildfire frequency and severity in western North America, intensifying hurricanes in the Atlantic, and permafrost thaw in the Arctic are all resetting successional clocks more frequently and in more extreme ways.
- **Drought stress**: prolonged droughts can arrest succession at early stages, prevent the establishment of late-successional species, and increase tree mortality in mature forests.
- **CO₂ fertilization**: elevated atmospheric CO₂ may favor fast-growing, early-successional species over slow-growing climax species, potentially altering competitive hierarchies.

### Novel Ecosystems

Novel ecosystems are emerging as climate change, species invasions, and land-use transformation create conditions without historical analog. Defined by Hobbs et al. (2013), novel ecosystems are characterized by:

- New species combinations resulting from biotic homogenization and invasion
- Altered disturbance regimes and abiotic conditions
- Changed ecosystem function relative to historical baselines
- Resistance to restoration to historical conditions

The recognition of novel ecosystems raises important questions about restoration targets. If historical communities cannot be restored, should practitioners aim for functional, self-sustaining novel ecosystems? This debate is one of the most active areas in contemporary restoration ecology.

### Long-Term Ecological Research

Long-term ecological research (LTER) networks have been instrumental in documenting successional dynamics over multi-decadal timescales. Sites such as Hubbard Brook (New Hampshire), Andrews Experimental Forest (Oregon), and Luquillo (Puerto Rico) have provided critical insights into the role of legacies, stochasticity, and climate in shaping successional trajectories that short-term studies cannot capture.

## Summary

Ecological succession remains a central concept in ecology, integrating community dynamics, ecosystem function, and environmental change. While early deterministic models (Clements) have been largely superseded by more nuanced frameworks recognizing stochasticity, multiple pathways, and nonequilibrium dynamics, the core insight — that communities change directionally through time in response to both biotic interactions and environmental gradients — remains robust. Contemporary research increasingly focuses on how global change is altering successional trajectories, the role of microbial communities in driving succession, and the practical application of successional theory in ecosystem restoration and sustainable land management.
### Key Terms and Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Sere** | The complete sequence of communities from pioneer to climax in a given environment |
| **Seral stage** | An individual community within a successional sequence |
| **Climax** | The theoretical endpoint of succession; a stable, self-replacing community |
| **Pioneer species** | The first species to colonize a disturbed or newly formed habitat |
| **Chronosequence** | A space-for-time substitution comparing sites of different ages |
| **Disturbance** | Any event that disrupts community structure and resource availability |
| **Legacy** | Organisms, propagules, or soil modifications that persist after disturbance |
| **Resilience** | The rate at which a community recovers to its pre-disturbance state |
| **Resistance** | The ability of a community to withstand disturbance without significant change |
| **Hysteresis** | When the recovery path differs from the disturbance path, leading to alternative states |
