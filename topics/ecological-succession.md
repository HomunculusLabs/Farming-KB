## Introduction to Ecological Succession

Ecological succession is the process by which biological communities of plants, animals, fungi, and microorganisms change in structure and species composition over time. It is one of the most fundamental concepts in ecology, describing how barren rock becomes forest, how abandoned farmland returns to woodland, and how disturbed ecosystems reorganize.
In permaculture design, understanding succession is essential because it provides the temporal framework within which all living systems operate. Designers do not merely observe succession — they actively accelerate, guide, and harvest it. David Holmgren describes succession as both an ecological model and a design framework for creating productive, resilient landscapes.
Succession operates across multiple scales simultaneously. Microbial communities succeed one another in the soil within days. Plant communities shift over years and decades. Forest ecosystems transform over centuries. Permaculture practitioners work at all these scales, from managing soil food web succession through compost to designing food forests that evolve over generations.

## Primary Succession — Life on Bare Ground

Primary succession begins on surfaces devoid of life — lava flows, glacial moraines, freshly exposed rock, or severely degraded land. Pioneer species are the first colonizers: lichens, mosses, cyanobacteria, and hardy annual plants that tolerate extreme conditions.
Lichens and mosses begin soil formation through physical and chemical weathering, trapping dust and organic debris. Cyanobacteria in biological soil crusts fix atmospheric nitrogen, providing the first inputs of this essential nutrient.
In permaculture, understanding primary succession helps rehabilitate severely degraded sites. Designers accelerate this process by introducing pioneer plants like daikon radish (breaking compacted subsoil) and nitrogen-fixing legumes (building fertility rapidly).

## Secondary Succession — Recovery After Disturbance

Secondary succession occurs on sites where an existing community has been disturbed but some soil biology and seed bank remain — abandoned farmland, cleared forests, burned areas. This is the most common context for permaculture design.
Fire plays a critical role in many ecosystems as a natural pulse that resets succession. Indigenous Australians practiced firestick farming for millennia, using controlled burns to manage landscapes. The initial floristic composition model describes how disturbance leads to simultaneous regeneration of all species present.

## The Four-Phase Adaptive Cycle

Holmgren draws on C.S. Holling's adaptive cycle model: exploitation (rapid pioneer colonization with high energy flows), conservation (mature system building complex structures), release (disturbance breaks down accumulated structures), and reorganization (system reassembles with new compositions).
Holmgren extends classical theory with the concept of pulsing — healthy ecosystems alternate between accumulation and release rather than progressing linearly. Grasslands pulse rapidly with grazing and fire cycles measured in years. Forests pulse slowly with century-scale intervals. In permaculture, planned disturbance through rotational grazing, coppicing, and controlled burning mimics these natural pulses productively.

## Fungal Succession in Decomposition

Fungal communities undergo predictable succession during decomposition. On freshly fallen wood, primary decomposers (fast-growing species like Stereum) colonize first. Secondary decomposers follow, processing more complex materials. Tertiary decomposers complete the process on recalcitrant substrates.
Paul Stamets organizes decomposer mushrooms into three functional groups: primary decomposers (first on fresh material), secondary decomposers (relying on metabolic products of primary fungi alongside bacteria), and tertiary decomposers (slowly breaking down the most resistant humic compounds).
The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio drops from ~200:1 to 30:1 during wood decomposition. White-rot basidiomycetes with ligninase systems become prominent in middle stages, while slow-growing species dominate as recalcitrant humus forms.

## Bacterial-to-Fungal Soil Succession

Soil ecosystems undergo predictable microbial succession from bacterial-dominated to fungal-dominated communities. After disturbance, bacteria colonize first, thriving on simple nutrients from disrupted organic matter. Bacterial-dominated soils favor annual crops and vegetables.
As ecosystems mature without disturbance, fungi gradually increase in dominance. Fungal-dominated soils support perennials, shrubs, and trees. The transition typically takes five to thirty years in undisturbed soils.

## Mycorrhizal Succession in Forest Ecosystems

Mycorrhizal fungi show concentric rings around maturing trees. Outer rings contain early-stage fungi (Thelephora terrestris, Laccaria laccata), while inner rings host late-stage species with greater enzyme capabilities. Communal mycorrhizal networks provide ecosystem stability through carbon exchange. After severe disturbance, loss of these networks significantly slows recovery.

## Pioneer Species and Accelerating Succession

Pioneer species perform critical services: stabilizing water flow, providing shelter, generating mulch, fixing nitrogen, and improving soil structure. Common pioneers include thistles, bracken, daikon radish, clover, and fast-growing nitrogen-fixing trees like black locust, alder, and tagasaste.
Bill Mollison emphasizes that pioneers mine nutrients from deep soil layers and bring them to the surface through leaf fall. Unlike natural succession unfolding over centuries, permaculture designers place all successional elements in a single planting, compressing centuries into five to fifteen years.
Toby Hemenway describes how fast-growing nitrogen fixers serve as nurse plants while sheet mulching suppresses pioneer weeds and builds soil. Chop-and-drop mulching converts pioneer biomass into soil organic matter. This planned transition is the essence of directed succession.

## Stacked Planting and System Evolution

A well-designed stacked planting installs the full successional array at once. Short-term yields arrive in years 1-3 (annual vegetables, herbs), medium-term yields in years 3-15 (berries, fodder, pole timbers), and long-term yields over 15-50 years (fruit, nuts, timber).
Mollison identifies three system phases: Stage A (establishment — fencing, planting, soil building), Stage B (semi-hardy — introducing livestock as system stabilizes), and Stage C (mature — self-reliant with diverse marketable yields). Nucleus development — starting small and expanding — is the most successful approach.

## Disturbance, Fire, and the Serial Mosaic

Almost all landscapes show evidence of past disturbance and recovery, creating a patchwork of different successional stages (serial mosaic). Fire, flood, windthrow, and pest outbreaks create local disturbances that renew the system.
Post-fire mycorrhizal succession depends critically on fire intensity. Minimal soil damage preserves residual communities as inoculum sources. Severe damage eliminates networks, requiring colonization from distant refugia. Planned disturbance through rotational grazing, coppicing, and controlled burning creates productive pulses that maintain landscape diversity.

## Soil Food Web Diagnostics and Agricultural Sustainability

Jeff Lowenfels emphasizes that weeds are indicators of soil food web condition. Annual weeds (pigweed, lamb's quarter, purslane) signal bacterial-dominated disturbed soils. Biennial/perennial weeds (thistles, bindweed) indicate transitional soils. Woodland species indicate mature fungal-dominated soils.
Holmgren argues conventional agriculture is inherently unsustainable because it fights natural succession, locking land in the earliest stage. Perennial polycultures represent a managed middle successional stage. Converting requires managing the bacterial-to-fungal transition over two to three years through sheet mulching with woody layers, reduced tillage, and fungal-rich compost.

## Social and Economic Succession

Holmgren applies succession thinking beyond ecology: civilizational pulses follow the same accumulation-and-release pattern. Periods of growth alternate with contraction and reorganization. Gradual reforms fail in conservation phases; only release phases through crisis enable significant reorganization. Economic succession manifests in the transition from extractive to regenerative enterprises.

## Design Principles for Working with Succession

Effective design works with succession by: observing existing pioneers to reveal soil conditions, identifying the appropriate climax community, installing earthworks first, planting the full sequence at once, and managing transitions through pruning and chop-and-drop. Zone analysis organizes space while succession organizes time — a well-designed site has multiple successional stages simultaneously in different zones.
Seasonal management follows rhythms: spring for pioneer planting and compost application, summer for monitoring competition and pruning, autumn for chop-and-drop and cover crops, winter for planning the next year's interventions.
