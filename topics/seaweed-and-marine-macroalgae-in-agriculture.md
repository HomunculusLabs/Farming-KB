# Seaweed and Marine Macroalgae in Agriculture

## Overview

Seaweed and marine macroalgae represent one of the oldest and most versatile inputs in agriculture, with a documented history of use spanning thousands of years. Coastal communities in Europe, Asia, and the Pacific Islands have long recognized the value of hauling beach-cast kelp and wrack onto fields as a soil amendment. Modern agricultural science has validated these traditional practices and revealed that seaweed provides far more than simple mineral nutrition — it delivers a complex suite of bioactive compounds including plant hormones, polysaccharides, amino acids, and antimicrobial agents that act as biostimulants, triggering systemic improvements in plant growth, stress tolerance, and disease resistance.

The global seaweed industry has grown dramatically, with production exceeding 35 million tonnes annually as of the mid-2020s, the vast majority from aquaculture rather than wild harvest. While the largest share goes to human food and hydrocolloid extraction (carrageenan, agar, alginates), the agricultural sector is the fastest-growing market segment. Seaweed extracts are now formulated as liquid concentrates, soluble powders, and granular meals, sold under dozens of brand names and applied across conventional and organic farming alike.

## Biology and Classification of Agricultural Seaweeds

Marine macroalgae are classified into three major groups based on their pigment composition:

### Brown Algae (Phaeophyceae)

Brown algae are the most commercially important group for agriculture. They are almost exclusively marine, ranging from tiny filamentous forms to the giant kelps that form underwater forests reaching 60 meters in length. Key genera used in agriculture include:

- **Ascophyllum nodosum** (knotted wrack) — Harvested from cold North Atlantic shores, particularly Norway, Iceland, Scotland, and Atlantic Canada. This is the single most important species for agricultural biostimulant production. Its slow growth in cold, turbulent intertidal waters produces exceptionally high concentrations of cytokinins, alginates, fucoidans, mannitol, and polyphenols. Most commercial liquid kelp extracts are derived from *A. nodosum* processed through alkaline hydrolysis or cold-press extraction.
- **Ecklonia maxima** (sea bamboo) — A large subtidal kelp native to South African waters. Notable for its exceptionally high auxin content, making extracts from this species particularly effective for stimulating root development in cuttings and transplants.
- **[[laminaria-digitata]]** and **L. saccharina** (oarweed and sugar kelp) — Widely distributed across North Atlantic coasts. *L. digitata* is rich in laminarin (a beta-glucan polysaccharide with elicitor properties) and is used both whole as a soil amendment and as a source material for extract production.
- **Sargassum spp.** — A tropical and subtropical brown alga that can form massive floating mats. Used extensively in East Asian agriculture both as a direct field amendment and as a compost ingredient. *Sargassum* is notable for its high alginic acid and fucoidan content.

- **Durvillaea potatorum** (bull kelp) — A southern Australian species harvested for kelp meal production, valued for its high mineral content and moderate hormone levels.

Brown algae derive their characteristic olive-brown color from the carotenoid fucoxanthin, which masks the underlying chlorophyll pigments. Their cell walls contain alginic acid and fucoidan, polysaccharides not found in terrestrial plants, which contribute significantly to the biostimulant activity of seaweed extracts.

### Red Algae (Rhodophyceae)

Red algae are less commonly used in agriculture but play important roles in specific applications:

- **Gracilaria, Gelidium, and Pterocladia** species — Primarily harvested for agar and carrageenan extraction. The residues from hydrocolloid processing (seaweed waste) can be composted or processed into agricultural amendments rich in galactans and minera  lvarezii** (elkhorn sea moss) — Cultivated extensively in tropical waters for carrageenan. Farm waste from this species is increasingly being evaluated as a slow-release fertilizer and soil conditioner.
- **Porphyra / Pyropia** (nori) — The most valuable cultivated seaweed globally by market price, though almost entirely used for human food. Waste material from nori processing contains bioactive peptides and minerals with potential agricultural value.

Red algae contain unique sulfated galactans (agar and carrageenan) in their cell walls, and many species produce antimicrobial compounds that may contribute to [[aact-disease-suppression-mechanisms-ingham]] when red algal residues are applied to soil.

### Green Algae (Chlorophyceae)

Green algae are the least used group in mainstream agriculture but have niche applications:

- **[[ulva-lactuca]]** (sea lettuce) — A common intertidal green alga with rapid growth rates. Used as a biofertilizer in some coastal farming systems, particularly in Southeast Asia. High in protein and potassium relative to brown algae.
- **Caulerpa, Enteromorpha, and Chaetomorpha** species — Occasionally used as compost feedstock or direct soil amendments in coastal regions. *Enteromorpha* (now reclassified ulva intestinalis alis*) blooms can become problematic eutrophication indicators, but harvested biomass can be repurposed as a nitrogen-rich amendment.

Green algae lack the complex polysaccharides (alginates, fucoidans) that make brown algae so valuable as biostimulants, but they compensate with higher nitrogen and protein content, making t green manure crops o green manure crops in their nutrient profile.

## Nutritional Composition

The mineral and organic composition of seaweed varies enormously by species, harvest location, season, water temperature, and processing method. The following represents typical ranges for the most commonly used agricultural species:

### Macronutrients (dry weight basis)

| Element | Typical Range | Notes |
|---------|--------------|-------|
| Nitrogen (N) | 1.0–5.0% | Varies by species; green algae highest |
| Phosphorus (P) | 0.1–0.5% | Relatively low compared to animal manures |
| Potassium (K) | 2.0–12.0% | Often the most abundant macronutrient |
| Calcium (Ca) | 0.5–3.5% | Important for cell wall integrity |
| Magnesium (Mg) | 0.3–1.5% | Central to chlorophyll molecule |
| Sulfur (S) | 0.5–2.0% | Present as sulfated polysaccharides |

### Micronutrients and Trace Elements

Seaweed absorbs minerals directly from seawater, which contains all known elements in solution. This gives seaweed a uniquely broad trace element profile:

- **Iron (Fe):** 50–3,000 ppm — crucial for chlorophyll synthesis and electron transport
- **Manganese (Mn):** 10–200 ppm — activates numerous enzymes including those in the photosynthetic water-splitting complex
- **Zinc (Zn):** 20–400 ppm — essential for auxin metabolism and enzyme function
- **Copper (Cu):** 5–100 ppm — component of plastocyanin and lignin-forming enzymes
- **Boron (B):** 20–200 ppm — critical for cell wall formation and pollen tube growth
- **Molybdenum (Mo):** 0.5–10 ppm — cofactor for nitrate reductase and nitrogenase
- **Cobalt (Co):** 0.5–10 ppm — component of vitamin B12, important for rhizobial [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]]

In addition, seaweed contains measurable amounts of selenium, iodine, nickel, vanadium, chromium, and silicon — elements rarely found in conventional NPK fertilizers but increasingly recognized as beneficial for plant and animal nutrition.

### Organic Compounds of Agricultural Significance

The biostimulant activity of seaweed goes far beyond its mineral content. The key [[query-what-are-the-main-bioactive-compounds-in-medicinal-plants]] include:

**Plant Hormones:**
- **Cytokinins** — Present at 10–400 ppb in *A. nodosum* extracts, cytokinins promote cell division, delay leaf senescence, enhance chlorophyll retention, and improve fruit set and size. Seaweed-derived cytokinins are considered the primary driver of the yield-enhancing effects observed in many field trials.
- **Auxins** — Particularly abundant in *Ecklonia maxima*, auxins stimulate root initiation, lateral root formation, and vascular differentiation. The auxin:cytokinin ratio in seaweed extracts is species-dependent and influences whether the extract promotes root growth (high auxin) or shoot growth (high cytokinin).
- **Abscisic acid (ABA)** — Present at low levels, ABA priming from seaweed extracts has been shown to improve [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] and reduce water loss through regulated stomatal closure.
- **Gibberellins** — Found in lower concentrations than cytokinins and auxins, gibberellins in seaweed contribute to stem elongation, seed germination, and overcoming dormancy.

**Carbohydrates and Polysaccharides:**
- **Alginic acid** — A linear copolymer of mannuronic and guluronic acids, alginic acid improves soil structure by acting as a flocculating agent that binds soil particles into stable aggregates. It also enhances water-holding capacity and can chelate micronutrients, keeping them available in alkaline soils.
- **Laminarin** — A beta-1,3-glucan found in brown algae that acts as an elicitor of plant defense responses. When applied as a foliar spray, laminarin triggers the plant's [[endophytic-mycorrhizal-induced-systemic-resistance]] resistance (ISR) pathways, priming the plant to respond more rapidly and strongly to pathogen attack.
- **Fucoidan** — A sulfated fucose polymer with demonstrated antimicrobial, antiviral, and antioxidant properties. In agricultural applications, fucoidan contributes to disease suppression and may modulate soil microbial communities.
- **Mannitol** — A sugar alcohol that serves as a compatible solute and osmoprotectant. Mannitol in seaweed extracts can improve plant tolerance to salinity and drought stress by helping maintain cellular water balance.

**Other Bioactive Compounds:**
- **Polyphenols (phlorotannins)** — Unique to brown algae, phlorotannins are antioxidant compounds that protect the alga from UV damage and herbivory. In agricultural use, they contribute to the [[mycelium-running-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties-species-chart]] of seaweed extracts and may inhibit soil-borne pathogens.
- **Betaines** — Osmoprotective compounds including glycine betaine and homarine that help plants cope with salt stress, drought, and temperature extremes.
- **Amino acids and peptides** — Seaweed contains all essential amino acids, some in free form. These serve as readily absorbable nitrogen sources and chelating agents for micronutrients in foliar applications.
- **Vitamins** — B-group vitamins (B1, B2, B12), vitamin C, vitamin E, and provitamin A (beta-carotene) are present in measurable quantities and contribute to the overall biostimulant effect.

## Seaweed Products and Processing Methods

The form in which seaweed is delivered to plants significantly affects its efficacy. Processing methods determine which bioactive compounds are preserved or destroyed.

### Whole Seaweed and Kelp Meal

Whole dried and milled seaweed (kelp meal) is the least processed form and retains the widest spectrum of naturally occurring compounds. *Ascophyllum nodosum* meal is the most common product, typically dried at low temperatures (below 60°C) to preserve hormone activity, then milled to a coarse powder.

- **Application rate:** 1–3 kg per 10 m² as a soil amendment, or 1–2 cups per cubic foot of potting mix
- **Nutrient analysis:** Approximately 1–2% N, 0.2–0.5% P, 3–8% K, plus full trace element spectrum
- **Advantages:** Slow-release nutrition, soil structure improvement, full spectrum of bioactive compounds, long shelf life (2+ years when stored dry)
- **Limitations:** Low NPK compared to synthetic fertilizers, variable composition between batches, bulky to transport

Kelp meal is particularly valuable as a compost activator (added at 1–2% by volume to compost piles) and as a component of organic potting mixes. The physical structure of milled seaweed improves soil aeration and water retention, while the slow mineralization of algal tissue provides a sustained release of nutrients over 2–4 months.

### Liquid Kelp Extracts

Liquid extracts are the most popular and widely studied form of seaweed biostimulant. Two primary extraction methods are used commercially:

**Alkaline Hydrolysis (KOH extraction):**
- The traditional method where dried kelp is heated with potassium hydroxide solution at 60–80°C for several hours
- Solubilizes alginates, fucoidans, laminarin, and other polysaccharides
