# Four-Season Farming and Winter Harvest

Four-season farming, as developed by Eliot Coleman at Four Season Farm in
Harborside, Maine (44.33 degrees N), is a comprehensive system for
producing fresh vegetables year-round in cold climates without
supplementary heat. The approach synthesizes decades of practical
experimentation with insights from the Parisian maraicher tradition,
British organic pioneers, and modern soil science. The system proves that
cold-hardy vegetables, combined with simple protected-cultivation
structures and carefully calibrated planting schedules, can provide
harvestable crops through the depths of winter even in USDA Zone 5, where
outdoor temperatures regularly fall to minus 20 degrees Fahrenheit.

The system rests on three interlocking components: cold-hardy vegetable
varieties that tolerate freezing temperatures; succession planting from
late summer through late fall to provide continual harvest; and protected
cultivation using unheated greenhouses with inner row-cover layers to
create a "twice-tempered" microclimate. The synergy of these three
well-known components produces results greater than the sum of their
parts, enabling growers to harvest fresh greens 52 weeks per year from a
small farm in coastal Maine.

Coleman's broader philosophy, which he calls "deep organic" farming, goes
beyond organic certification to optimize every factor affecting crop
quality, from soil biology and mineral nutrition to variety selection and
harvest timing. The guiding principle comes from Lady Eve Balfour:
"Working to establish optimum soil conditions is the most constructive
approach in a dependable long-range philosophy of agriculture." This
philosophy views the farm as a human-managed microcosm of the natural
world, where fertility is maintained by biological processes of living
soil organisms feeding on organic matter.

The economic case is compelling. Winter-harvested greens command premium
prices, typically three to ten times that of equivalent summer produce,
because they arrive during a period of natural scarcity. Coleman has
achieved gross returns of $80,000 per acre per year, with unheated cold
houses targeting $5 per square foot per year across three crops and
minimally heated cool houses targeting $10 per square foot across six.
The system requires minimal infrastructure beyond standard hoop houses,
row covers, and compost-making capacity, making it accessible to
small-scale growers worldwide.

## Historical and Philosophical Foundations

Coleman's practice draws from multiple traditions. The most significant
is the nineteenth-century Parisian maraicher system, where market
gardeners produced vegetables year-round using deep, heavily manured beds
protected by cloches and glass frames, reportedly achieving four to six
times conventional production on the same land area. British organic
pioneers Lady Eve Balfour, F. H. King, and Albert Howard also shaped his
thinking. Coleman purchased his farm in 1968 from Scott and Helen
Nearing, whose homesteading classic "Living the Good Life" had inspired
his interest in organic farming.

Coleman distinguishes between "deep organic" and "shallow organic"
farming. Deep organic farmers reject chemicals and seek better methods by
mimicking natural systems, using deep-rooting legumes, green manures,
and composts to establish vigorous soil life. Shallow organic farmers
reject chemicals but replace them with bagged organic fertilizers and
natural pest-control products, remaining dependent on an industrial
supply network. The difference is one of life views: shallow organic
regards the natural world as inadequate, while deep organic understands
it as "elegant, impeccably designed, smooth-functioning systems that must
be studied and nurtured."

## Protected Cultivation: The Double-Layer System

The technical foundation of winter harvest is the combination of an
outer greenhouse layer and an inner row-cover layer, creating a
twice-tempered climate. The outer structure consists of standard
plastic-covered gothic-style hoop houses oriented east-west to maximize
light capture during the low-angle winter sun. Inside, floating row-cover
material is supported by wire wickets at approximately 12 inches above
the soil, spaced every 4 feet along 30-inch-wide growing beds.

This double coverage produces a dramatic temperature differential. When
outdoor temperatures drop to minus 15 degrees Fahrenheit, the temperature
under the inner layer drops only to 15 to 18 degrees above zero on
average. The key insight is that cold-hardy vegetables do not need warm
temperatures; they need protection from wind and desiccation. The
double-layer system blocks wind while maintaining elevated humidity,
providing the stable environment that keeps crops alive through the
coldest months.

Covering choices involve important tradeoffs. A single layer maximizes
light input, the most limiting factor in winter. Two layers with air
inflation add approximately 4 degrees Fahrenheit of warmth but reduce
light transmission by roughly 10 percent. Coleman generally uses
single-layer covering, reserving double covering only for December 15
through February 15, when new seedlings need extra warmth. Anti-drip
coatings on the plastic cause condensed moisture to form a thin film
rather than droplets, reflecting heat radiating from the soil back
downward at night. Snow accumulation on the structures serves as both
risk (structural load) and benefit (insulation), with gothic arches
shedding snow effectively while the blanket of snow adds thermal mass.

## Moveable Greenhouses

One of Coleman's most innovative contributions is the moveable greenhouse
system, a revival of an old European practice dating to 1898. Rather than
keeping a greenhouse permanently fixed over one plot, structures are
relocated along tracks, allowing multiple plots to benefit from protected
growing conditions throughout the year. Coleman calls this "the best new
gardening idea of the twenty-first century" or more accurately, a
"rediscovered new idea."

The moveable system creates "the equivalent of 14 months per year."
Summer crops grow in open field while the greenhouse occupies its winter
position. Meanwhile, winter greens are sown outdoors where the greenhouse
will move. In mid-to-late October, the greenhouse slides over the
established winter crops. Because winter crops were established two
months before needing protection and summer crops continued growing two
months longer than a fixed greenhouse would allow, the effective growing
season is dramatically extended.

The soil-health benefits are equally significant. Permanent greenhouses
create a rain shadow that prevents natural rainfall from reaching soil,
leading to salt accumulation and soil structure degradation. When moved,
the exposed soil receives rain, snow, and cover crops for months,
leaching accumulated salts and allowing beneficial organisms to
recolonize. For one year out of every two, growing beds are uncovered
and exposed to sun, rain, wind, and snow, preventing the buildup of
pests, diseases, and excess nutrients that plague permanent greenhouses.

Coleman's preferred mobility system uses pipe skids made from
off-the-shelf galvanized pipe. Three 16-foot lengths connect with
plastic decking inserts, and temporary steel ski tips bolt to the front
for sliding. A 48-foot house moves with a small farm tractor. Structures
slide between two alternate sites, each uncovered for one year out of
two, with ground anchors securing houses once positioned.

## Crop Selection and Cold Hardiness

Approximately thirty different vegetables have been grown in Coleman's
winter-harvest greenhouses. Leafy greens form the largest category,
including arugula, beet greens, chard, chicory, claytonia, collards,
endive, escarole, kale, lettuce, mache, minutina, mizuna, mustard
greens, pak choi, sorrel, spinach, tatsoi, and watercress. Root crops
include carrots, radishes, and turnips, while alliums such as leeks and
scallions round out the selection. The eating quality of these crops is
dramatically superior during cooler months, as many become sweeter, more
tender, and more flavorful after cold exposure. This is not a limitation
but one of winter growing's greatest advantages.

Cold hardiness is not fixed but dynamic, influenced by acclimation, plant
maturity, genetics, growing conditions, and exposure duration. The
acclimation process occurs in two stages: between 35 and 50 degrees
Fahrenheit, plants accumulate sugars and cryoprotective compounds; below
freezing, more profound changes occur including membrane lipid
modification and antifreeze protein production. Full acclimation requires
two to four weeks of progressively colder temperatures, which is why
Coleman emphasizes establishing winter crops well before the first hard
freeze.

The most cold-tolerant crops, surviving below 10 degrees Fahrenheit
under double protection, include mache, claytonia, minutina, Siberian
kale, and acclimated spinach. A second tier of very hardy crops
surviving 10 to 20 degrees includes tatsoi, mizuna, wild arugula, turnip
greens, carrots, and leeks. Moderately hardy crops in the 20 to 28
degree range include cold-hardy lettuce, endive, escarole, radishes, and
Swiss chard. Successful winter growing requires specific variety
selection: Coleman prefers 'Winter Bloomsdale' and 'Giant Winter'
spinach, 'Vates' and 'Winterbor' kale, 'Vit' mache, and 'Giant Winter'
claytonia.

## Planting Schedules and the Persephone Period

The central concept governing winter planting is the "Persephone months,"
when days are shorter than ten hours. Named after the Greek myth in which
Demeter makes the earth barren during Persephone's absence, this period
causes plant growth to slow dramatically. At Four Season Farm (44.33
degrees N), the Persephone period runs from November 5 to February 5. At
higher latitudes it extends further; at 32 degrees N, days never fall
below ten hours.

The practical implication is that crops must reach near-maturity before
the ten-hour-day threshold. A crop requiring 40 days to mature for a
September 1 harvest needs approximately 80 days in a minimally heated
cool house and 120 days in an unheated cold house for midwinter harvest.
The cutoff for new sowings at Four Season Farm is approximately October
31; after that, seeds germinate but seedlings "just sit there waiting
expectantly" until the ten-hour day returns in February.

Coleman frames the calendar using the traditional eight-point
agricultural calendar. August 1 (Lughnasadh) marks the "second spring,"
when winter harvest planting begins. September is the peak planting
month. November 1 (Samhain) marks the end of outdoor harvest. February 2
(Imbolc) signals accelerating growth as light returns. Precise sowing
dates are far more critical for fall than spring planting, because day
length is contracting and temperatures falling. Coleman advises growers
to keep careful notes on seed-to-harvest days and prepare a day-by-day
planting schedule specific to their location.

## Succession Planting and Crop Rotation

Succession planting means sowing vegetables multiple times during a
season to provide continual harvest. During midwinter, vigorous regrowth
on cut-and-come-again crops provides the harvest, while late-fall and
winter-sown crops slowly reach productive size. Coleman aims to never
leave a greenhouse bed unplanted: within 24 hours after a crop is
harvested, residues are removed, soil is re-prepared with compost and
amendments, and the bed is replanted.

Crop rotation follows several principles rather than a fixed schedule.
Heavy feeders should be followed by lighter feeders or nitrogen-fixing
crops. Each bed should be part of a planned rotation considering nutrient
demands and disease vulnerabilities. A typical cold house rotation might
include winter-harvested carrots, baby new potatoes in mid-March,
Charantais melons in late May, and fall-harvested spinach before the
house moves to its alternate position.

As the year-round system matured, green manure crops are grown less
frequently. The tradeoff is explicit: the organic matter from a green
manure is lost, but the organic matter from root residues and leaves of
harvested crops is gained, along with the financial return. When cover
crops are used, Coleman favors vigorous legumes like cowpeas and
black-eyed peas, turned under three to four weeks before the subsequent
crop. Red clover undersown into standing tomatoes is another traditional
technique for fitting cover crops into the rotation without sacrificing
commercial production.

## Soil Management and Fertility

Soil management is the biological foundation of the four-season system.
The defining feature is the 30-inch-wide raised bed, adapted from the
French intensive method. Beds are initially double-dug to 24 inches or
loosened with a broadfork, with generous compost additions creating
"something approaching potting soil attached to the earth." Close
spacing in a hexagonal or offset grid maximizes production per unit area.

For ongoing bed preparation between succession crops, Coleman designed
the "Tilther," an electric tiller working only the top 2 inches of soil,
based on weed research showing that weed seeds rarely germinate from
deeper. Before tilling, the soil is aerated with a broadfork, which
loosens without inverting, preserving natural layering and microbial
communities. The bed surface is then leveled with a 30-inch Austrian hay
rake and lightly firmed with a mesh-surfaced roller for precision seeder
wheels.

Compost is the single most important fertility input. Coleman produces it
from mowed forage crops supplemented with vegetable wastes, seaweed,
clay, and livestock manure, turned twice during summer using a manure
spreader that chops, aerates, and windrows in a single pass. For winter
access to compost during frozen months, he erects a temporary
plastic-covered A-frame over one windrow each fall, keeping rain and
snow off while preventing more than surface freezing.

Beyond compost, Coleman employs organic amendments tailored to specific
soil needs. His standard between-crop greenhouse fertilizer is alfalfa
meal; for crops following heavy feeders like spinach, additional nitrogen
from dried crab wastes may be necessary. Mineral amendments including
rock phosphate, greensand, and boron are applied based on soil tests. He
cautions against pushing crops with high-analysis fertilizers, advocating
"low-test" amendments for most crops.

## Winter Greens and Salad Production

Winter greens are the economic backbone of the four-season system.
Spinach is the most important winter crop: it continues producing new
leaves all winter (unlike kale, which stops during the coldest months);
demand always exceeds supply; and its timing fits the mobile greenhouse
rotation perfectly. Sown outdoors during the second and third weeks of
September, each bed provides four harvests at approximately monthly
intervals from Thanksgiving through late March. Coleman devotes nearly
half of greenhouse space to winter spinach, harvested as individual
leaves for better regrowth and quality.

Mache continues growing right through winter regardless of temperature
and is at its best December through February. Unlike most winter greens,
it is harvested as whole plants, making succession sowings crucial for
steady supply. Claytonia is exceptionally cold-hardy and retains crisp
texture after hard freezes, while arugula adds peppery contrast. The
salad mix includes red and green lettuces, broadleaf and wild arugula,
endive, narrow-stem chard, claytonia, minutina, spinach, mache,
watercress, and beet leaves, containing at least five times more calcium,
four times more iron, twelve times more vitamin A, and six times more
vitamin C than head lettuce.

Quality standards are rigorous: leaves should be no more than three
inches long, easily fitting on a fork. Overgrown leaves go to the
braising mix or are sold bunched. The braising mix comprises hardy Asian
greens cut to four inches maximum, including spinach, chard, mizuna,
mibuna, tatsoi, and pak choi, sold as an Asian salad or braising mix.
One successful value-added product was the "Stir-Fry Pak," containing a
whole pak choi, tatsoi, radish, turnip with greens, leek, and carrot in
one bag, which sold out by mid-December.

## Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling

Winter harvesting presents unique challenges. After a below-zero night,
crews wait until interior temperatures rise above freezing, typically
achieving a harvest window from 10:00 AM to 3:00 PM. Cutting is done
with small sharp Victorinox knives, with leaves placed in food-grade
five-gallon buckets. The key efficiency factor is minimizing hand travel
between cutting point and container; Coleman estimates 40 percent of a
harvester's time is spent moving the hand to the bucket. Crews wear
neoprene gloves, and Coleman favors a vest over a parka for arm mobility.

Inner covers must be replaced immediately after harvesting. Once
temperatures drop below freezing, row-cover fabric freezes to itself and
becomes impossible to reposition without tearing. On rare occasions when
sunlight is insufficient to warm houses above freezing, a propane
salamander heater provides temporary heat, needed on average only once
per winter.

Meticulous cleanliness is essential. The washing area is scrubbed after
each harvest. Each salad ingredient is washed separately for quality
control, then spin-dried and mixed in a custom rotating barrel. Winter
greens have exceptional shelf life: properly handled spinach, kale, and
mache maintain marketable quality for two to three weeks near freezing,
far exceeding summer-harvested equivalents. This extended shelf life is a
significant advantage for direct-market farmers selling at weekly winter
markets.

## Pest Management and Plant Health

Remarkably, insects and diseases have not been significant concerns in
winter harvest. Cold temperatures are inhospitable to most insect pests,
and the absence of outdoor host plants limits reinfection. This natural
pest exclusion is an often-overlooked advantage of winter growing. The
humid air inside double-covered greenhouses can favor fungal diseases
such as gray mold and downy mildew, managed through adequate plant
spacing, avoidance of overhead watering, and ventilation on warm days.

The primary pest is the meadow vole (Microtus pennsylvanicus), a
vegetarian mammal that must eat its weight daily and breeds prolifically,
tunneling directly into greenhouses. After years of failed baited-trap
strategies (voles learned to avoid baits), Coleman developed the
breakthrough unbaited box trap: a wooden box with mouse-sized holes at
each corner, containing snap traps at each entrance. Voles naturally dart
into small dark holes and are caught. The first captured vole scents the
box, and subsequent voles show no aversion, making this system
impressively effective year-round.

Coleman's broader pest philosophy is nonconfrontational, viewing pests
as symptoms of inadequate growing conditions rather than enemies. When
aphids appeared on greenhouse spinach, research showed the root cause was
excess nitrogen accumulating in unwatered soil. Weekly irrigation on
sunny mornings starting in mid-January flushed the excess nitrogen and
eliminated the problem entirely, without any pesticide application.

## Marketing and Economics

Coleman's marketing centers on "fresh" and "local" as key advantages.
Produce shipped from afar is at least a week old; Coleman delivers
within 12 to 24 hours of harvest. Winter-harvested crops also offer
nutritional superiority: highly colored foods grown under cool conditions
are higher in anthocyanins, valuable antioxidants. His first commercial
winter crops in 1995 were priced $2 per pound above California organic
mixes; local customers bought his supply while the competition sat
unsold.

Four Season Farm sells through a farm stand with perennial flower beds,
local stores, and restaurants. Consistent year-round supply is a
powerful advantage; winter months, when competitors have nothing, are
particularly valuable for loyalty. Coleman identifies winter harvesting
as a logical step for CSA farms: rather than adding members, increase
the months of supply to existing customers. A winter CSA share provides
steady income during months when many farms have no production.

The economic model allows a five-year ramp-up to target gross returns.
Current focus is improving net income through better tools and more
efficient harvesting. Coleman envisions a "small-farm revival" led by
growers who will improve upon these systems, transforming the
traditionally least profitable season into one of the most valuable.

## Tools and Innovation

Coleman's tool philosophy views any purchased tool as a starting point
for modification. The collineal hoe, developed through systematic
experimentation, features a narrow blade aligned with the handle at 70
degrees, cutting weeds far more efficiently than standard designs. The
Tilther, his custom electric tiller for greenhouse beds, works only the
top 2 inches of soil, preserving structure and avoiding dormant weed
seeds, while producing minimal noise and no fumes suitable for enclosed
use.

Johnny's Selected Seeds sells a human-powered Greens Harvester with a
cloth basket collector behind a bow-saw frame. Coleman has also envisioned
a battery-powered hand harvester for smaller growers: light enough for
one-hand use, adjustable cutting height, and wide enough to cover half a
30-inch bed. He encourages growers to apply innovative spirit to farm
tools, arguing that the future of small farming must be invented by
practitioners themselves.
