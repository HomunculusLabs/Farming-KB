## Four-Season Farming and Winter Harvest

Four-season farming, as developed by Eliot Coleman at Four Season Farm
in Harborside, Maine (44.33°N), is a comprehensive system for producing
fresh vegetables year-round in cold climates without supplementary heat.

The system rests on three interlocking components: cold-hardy vegetable
varieties that survive and improve in freezing temperatures; succession
planting from late summer through fall to ensure continual harvest
readiness; and protected cultivation using unheated hoop houses with
inner row-cover layers to create a "twice-tempered" microclimate.

The synergy of these three components produces results greater than the
sum of their parts, enabling growers to harvest fresh greens 52 weeks
per year from a small farm in coastal Maine.

## The Persephone Period and Day-Length Thresholds

Coleman borrowed the "Persephone months" from Greek mythology to
describe the period when day length drops below 10 hours and plant
growth essentially ceases, regardless of temperature.

At Coleman"s Maine latitude, this period extends from approximately
November 10 to January 30, roughly 11 weeks. Crops do not die but stop
producing new growth, shifting to a maintenance mode. At 38°N the
period is perhaps 6 to 8 weeks; at 30°N, day length never drops below
10 hours.

All the growth that sustains the winter harvest must occur between
August 1 and mid-November at Coleman's latitude. Crops that reach
near-maturity before the Persephone months provide reliable harvests.

## Cold-Hardy Vegetables and Their Adaptations

The biological foundation is a curated selection of vegetables that not
only survive freezing but improve in eating quality when exposed to
cold. Cold converts starches to sugars, concentrating flavor compounds.

Key winter crops include spinach, which continues producing new leaves
all winter; mache (lamb's lettuce), a true winter annual that thrives in
cold; claytonia (miner's lettuce), with succulent leaves that resist
freezing damage; and tatsoi, an Asian green forming compact rosettes.

Other reliable producers include kale, leeks, arugula, endive,
escarole, radicchio, and scallions. Carrots overwinter in the ground
and undergo cold sweetening, becoming exceptionally sweet "candy
carrots." Coleman argues cold-hardy vegetables reach their highest
perfection precisely because they are grown without heat.

## The Double-Layer Protection System

The technical foundation is a two-layer protection approach. An outer
layer of greenhouse plastic on a gothic-style hoop house, combined with
an inner layer of floating row cover on wire wickets, creates what
Coleman calls a "twice-tempered" climate.

The greenhouse shifts the growing environment approximately one and a
half hardiness zones warmer. The inner row-cover layer adds another zone
and a half. In Zone 5 Maine, the microclimate under the inner layer is
effectively Zone 8, a shift of three full hardiness zones.

The system uses no heating, no thermal mass, no buried insulation, no
fans or pumps. The soil itself serves as the heat-storage medium,
absorbing solar energy during the day and radiating it back at night.

The gothic-style (pointed-arch) hoop design sheds snow more
effectively than Quonset profiles and provides greater headroom at the
sides of the tunnel.

## Succession Planting for Winter Harvest

Succession planting for winter is fundamentally different from spring.
In spring, day length increases and growth accelerates. In fall, day
length contracts, temperatures drop, and each successive planting takes
progressively longer to reach maturity.

Coleman designates August 1 as the start of the "second spring," the
beginning of the planting season for winter crops. This reflects the
biological reality that winter crops must be well-established before the
shortest days arrive.

Crops sown on August 1 experience approximately 14 hours of daylight,
warm soil for rapid germination, and two to three months of declining
but still adequate day length. A crop requiring 40 days when sown
September 1 may require 80 days from a midwinter sowing.
