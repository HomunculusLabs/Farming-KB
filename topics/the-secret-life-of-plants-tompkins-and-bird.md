---# The Secret Life of Plants

In 1973, journalist Peter Tompkins and former CIA intelligence officer
Christopher Bird published *The Secret Life of Plants*, a sprawling
investigation into the idea that plants possess sensory abilities,
emotional responses, and perhaps even consciousness. The book became
an international bestseller, translated into dozens of languages, and
provoked fierce debate that continues to this day. It gathered
experiments from [[cleve-backster]]'s polygraph research in New York City, Soviet
electrophysiology at the Timiryazev Academy in Moscow, and the
mystical [[brassinosteroid-physiological-mechanisms-cell-elongation-division|Mechanisms]] differ from what [[rudolf-steiner]]. Bird's
exposure to Steiner's [[fukuoka-western-eastern-philosophy-agriculture]], ecology, and
alternative science was at its peak. Tompkins and Bird did not claim
to be scientists. Their method was journalistic: they traveled the
world, interviewed researchers, and synthesized findings from
disparate fields into a single narrative. This approach was both the
book's greatest strength and its most serious weakness. It connected
ideas that mainstream science kept in separate compartments, but it
also failed to distinguish rigorously between replicated experiments
and anecdotal claims.

## Cleve Backster and Primary Perception

The most famous experiments in the book belong to Cleve Backster
(1924-2013), America's foremost polygraph examiner. In February 1966,
working late in his polygraph school near Times Square, Backster
attached electrodes from a lie detector to the leaf of a Dracaena
massangeana. His initial question was simple: would watering the
plant produce any measurable change in its electrical resistance?

As the plant absorbed water, the galvanometer traced not the expected
steady decrease in resistance but an active, sawtooth pattern
resembling the emotional response of a human subject. Intrigued,
Backster decided to threaten the plant. Dunking a leaf in his hot
coffee produced no reaction, but the moment he formed the mental image
of burning the leaf with a match, the recording pen registered a
dramatic upward sweep before he could even reach for the matches.

Backster called this phenomenon "primary perception" and spent the
next decade developing increasingly elaborate experiments. He
demonstrated that plants appeared to recognize individuals who had
harmed other plants, identifying a "culprit" from among six
blindfolded students. He claimed plants maintained a connection with
their keeper regardless of distance, showing synchronized reactions
from rooms, buildings, and even hundreds of miles away.

Backster's work was never replicated to the satisfaction of
mainstream science. Horowitz, Lewis, and Gasteiger published a study
in *Science* in 1975 that failed to find any evidence of the Backster
effect using automated equipment that eliminated human involvement.
Critics argued that the results were artifacts of electrostatic
interference, unconscious experimenter bias, or selective
interpretation of ambiguous galvanometer traces.

## Marcel Vogel and Plant-Human Communication

Marcel Vogel (1917-1991), a research chemist at IBM in Los Gatos,
California, provided the most scientifically credible investigation
of plant-human communication described in the book. Vogel had spent
fifteen years building Vogel Luminescence into a leader in the field,
developing the red color for television screens and black light
inspection kits. At IBM, he worked on magnetics, optic-electrical
devices, and liquid crystal systems, holding patents crucial to
computer information storage.

Vogel first encountered Backster's work when a student handed him an
Argosy magazine article titled "Do Plants Have Emotions?" His
initial reaction was to throw it away, convinced Backster was a
charlatan. Days later he retrieved it and attempted replication.
When his three student teams failed, Vogel tried the experiments
himself and succeeded, demonstrating that plants anticipated having
leaves torn and reacted with even greater alarm to threats than to
actual harm.

Vogel developed a precise protocol for plant-human communication. He
attached electrodes to philodendron leaves using a mucilaginous paste
of agar with karri gum and salt to eliminate electromagnetic noise.
He found that the quality of interaction depended critically on the
emotional bond between the experimenter and the plant. Positive
mental attitude and a sensitivity analogous to deep meditative states
produced the strongest responses. Vogel could wipe a leaf with a
cloth and observe a response; he found that individual leaves had
distinct "personalities," with those of higher water content being
more responsive.
