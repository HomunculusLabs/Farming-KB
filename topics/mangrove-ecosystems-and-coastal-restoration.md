# Mangrove Ecosystems and Coastal Restoration

## Introduction

Mangroves are a diverse group of [[biodiversity-of-fungi-ecosystem-services-agriculture]] including coastal protection, [[dighton-fungi-climate-change-feedback]], coastal development, and aquaculture expansion has made mangrove loss one of the most pressing conservation challenges of the 21st century. This page examines mangrove biology, ecology, threats, restoration science, and their integration with permaculture and agroforestry systems.

## Mangrove Biology and Species Diversity

### Taxonomic Overview

Mangroves are not a single taxonomic group but rather an ecological guild—approximately 70–80 true mangrove species (obligate halophytes) belonging to roughly 20 families, along with 60–100 mangrove associates. True mangrove species are those restricted to the intertidal zone, while associates can also occur in non-mangrove habitats.

The major families include:

- **Rhizophoraceae** — Red mangroves (*Rhizophora* spp.), the iconic stilt-rooted mangroves of the Indo-Pacific and Atlantic
- **Avicenniaceae** — Black mangroves (*Avicennia* spp.), widely distributed with pneumatophore root systems
- **Arecaceae** — Nypa palm (*Nypa fruticans*), a mangrove palm of South and Southeast Asia
- **Sonneratiaceae** — *Sonneratia* spp., with pencil-like pneumatophores and showy flowers
- **Combretaceae** — *Laguncularia* (white mangrove) and *Conocarpus* (buttonwood)
- **Meliaceae** — *Xylocarpus* spp. (cannonball mangroves) and *Ceriops* spp.
- **Lythraceae** — *Bruguiera* spp., characteristic of the Indo-West Pacific

### Physiological Adaptations

Mangroves have evolved a remarkable suite of adaptations to survive in the harsh intertidal environment:

**Salt Management.** Different species employ different strategies:
- **Salt excretion** — *Avicennia* and *Aegiceras* possess specialized salt glands on their leaves that actively secrete concentrated salt crystals, which are then washed away by rain or wind
- **Salt exclusion** — *Rhizophora* and *Bruguiera* use ultrafiltration at the root level, excluding up to 90–97% of salt from seawater before it enters the xylem
- **Salt accumulation** — Some species tolerate high internal salt concentrations by compartmentalizing salts in vacuoles or older leaves

**Root System Adaptations.** Oxygen-poor, waterlogged soils demand specialized root architectures:
- **Stilt roots (prop roots)** — *Rhizophora* species develop arching aerial roots from the trunk and branches, providing both structural support and gas exchange surfaces
- **Pneumatophores** — *Avicennia* and *Sonneratia* produce vertical root extensions (snorkel-like projections) up to 30 cm tall, covered in lenticels for atmospheric oxygen uptake
- **Knee roots** — *Bruguiera* forms looped roots that emerge from the substrate, exposing lenticels to the air
- **Buttress roots** — *Xylocarpus* and other species develop broad, plank-like root extensions for stability

**Vivipary.** Many mangroces, particularly Rhizophoraceae, exhibit cryptovivipary or true vivipary. Embryos germinate while still attached to the parent tree, developing into elongated seedlings (propagules) 20–60 cm long. When released, these buoyant propagules can float for weeks in ocean currents before rooting in suitable substrate. This adaptation dramatically increases establishment success in the intertidal zone.

### Zonation Patterns

Mangrove forests display distinctive zonation along environmental gradients, driven primarily by tidal inundation frequency, salinity, and substrate stability:

1. **Seaward fringe** — Dominated by *Rhizophora* spp. and *Avicennia marina*, receiving daily tidal inundation; high wave energy zone
2. **Mid-zone** — Mixed stands of *Bruguiera*, *Ceriops*, *Sonneratia*; moderate inundation frequency
3. **Landward zone** — *Xylocarpus*, *Lumnitzera*, *Excoecaria* (blind-your-eye mangrove); infrequent inundation, lower salinity, transition to terrestrial vegetation
4. **Riverine mangroves** — Along estuarine channels, zonation is less pronounced; species diversity is often highest here
5. **Basin mangroves** — Inland depressions with stagnant or slowly flushing water; typically dominated by *Avicennia*

Zonation is not rigid—overlap and gradation are common, and the pattern varies significantly between the Indo-West Pacific (highest diversity, up to 40+ species per site) and Atlantic-East Pacific (lower diversity, 3–10 species per site).
