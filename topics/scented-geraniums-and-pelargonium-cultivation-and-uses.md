# Scented Geraniums and Pelargonium Cultivation and Uses

Scented geraniums—botanically *Pelargonium* species and their hybrids—are among the most versatile and underappreciated plants in the home garden and [[essential-oils-and-hydrosol-distillation]], culinary applications, medicinal properties, and ornamental value.

## Taxonomy and Origins

The genus *Pelargonium* comprises over 200 species, with the majority indigenous to the Cape Floristic Region of South Africa. The name derives from Greek *pelargos* (stork), referring to the elongated seed capsule that resembles a stork's bill—contrasting with *Geranium* (from *geranos*, crane). The confusion between the two genera dates to the 18th century when Linnaeus initially grouped them together.

The most important species for scent and cultivation include:

- **P. graveolens** — Rose-scented, the primary source of geranium essential oil
- **P. crispum** — Lemon-scented, compact growth habit
- **P. tomentosum** — Peppermint-scented, large fuzzy leaves
- **P. capitatum** — Apple-scented, low spreading form
- **P. odoratissimum** — Apple/nutmeg scent
- **P. fragrans** — Nutmeg-scented
- **P. denticulatum** — Pine-scented, finely cut foliage
- **P. quercifolium** — Oak-leaf scented geranium, distinctive lobed leaves
- **P. grossularioides** — Coconut-scented, low growing
- **P. citrosum** — Citronella-scented (marketed as mosquito repellent, though evidence is mixed)

## Scent Profiles and Popular Cultivars

Scented geraniums are primarily categorized by their fragrance. Hundreds of cultivars exist, each with a unique aromatic profile. The scent comes from glandular trichomes on the leaf surface that release volatile oils when brushed or crushed.

### Rose Group
The most commercially significant group. Rose-scented geraniums (*P. graveolens* and hybrids) produce geranium oil used in perfumery, aromatherapy, and skincare.

- **'Rober's Lemon Rose'** — Classic rose with lemon undertone
- **'Attar of Roses'** — Intense, sweet rose fragrance
- **'Old Fashioned Rose'** — Deep, true rose scent

### Citrus Group
Bright, uplifting aromas that complement culinary uses.

- **'Mabel Grey'** (*P. crispum* × *P. fulgidum*) — Strong lemon, one of the most intense
- **'Prince of Orange'** — Sweet orange, as close to actual citrus as any geranium
- **'Lemon Fancy'** — Bright, clean lemon
- **'Orange Fizz'** — Orange soda-like effervescence

### Mint Group
Cool, refreshing scents with larger, often fuzzy leaves.

- **'Peppermint'** (*P. tomentosum*) — Large velvety leaves, strong menthol
- **'Chocolate Mint'** — Mint with cocoa undertones
- **'Grapefruit Mint'** — Mint-citrus hybrid character

### Fruit and Spice Group
Warm, sweet, and spicy aromas.

- **'Apple'** (*P. odoratissimum*) — Crisp green apple
- **'Nutmeg'** (*P. fragrans*) — Warm baking spice
- **'Coconut'** (*P. grossularioides*) — Sweet tropical coconut
- **'Ginger'** — Spicy, warm ginger root
- **'Strawberry'** — Sweet berry-like fragrance
- **'Licorice'** — Anise-like

### Herbal and Woody Group
Complex, layered aromatics.

- **'Camphor'** — Medicinal, eucalyptus-like
- **'Pine'** (*P. denticulatum*) — Resinous forest scent
- **'Lavender'** — Soft floral-herbal blend
- **'Rosemary'** — Savory evergreen character

## Cultivation

### Climate and Hardiness

Scented pelargoniums are tender perennials hardy only in USDA Zones 9–11 (roughly 20°F / -7°C minimum). In cooler climates they are grown as annuals or overwintered indoors. They thrive in Mediterranean climates with warm days, cool nights, low humidity, and minimal frost.

### Light Requirements

Full sun (6–8 hours) produces the most compact growth and highest essential oil concentration. In hot climates (above 90°F / 32°C), afternoon shade prevents leaf scorch and stress. Indoor plants need the brightest south- or west-facing window available, or supplemental grow lights.

### Soil

Well-drained soil is non-negotiable. Scented geraniums are highly susceptible to root rot in heavy, waterlogged soils.

- **[[raised-beds-vs-in-ground-vs-containers]]**: Excellent option in clay-heavy soils

### Watering

Allow the top inch of soil to dry between waterings. Overwatering is the most common cause of death. In containers, water thoroughly until it drains from the bottom, then empty saucers. During winter dormancy indoors, reduce watering significantly—water only when leaves show slight wilt.

### Fertilization

These are light feeders. Excess nitrogen produces lush but scentless foliage.

- **In-ground**: Side-dress with compost in spring; no additional fertilizer typically needed
- **Containers**: Balanced [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] at the expense of essential oil production

### Propagation

#### Stem Cuttings (Primary Method)
1. Take 3–4 inch cuttings from non-flowering stems in spring or late summer
2. Remove lower leaves, leaving 2–3 nodes
3. Dip cut end in rooting hormone (optional but improves success rate)
4. Stick into moist perlite, sand, or [[cannabis-watering-schedule-and-frequency]]
