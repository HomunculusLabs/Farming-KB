# Scented Geraniums and Pelargonium Cultivation and Uses

Scented geraniums—botanically *Pelargonium* species and their hybrids—are among the most versatile and underappreciated plants in the home garden and permaculture landscape. Despite their common name, they are not true geraniums (*Geranium* spp., cranesbills) but belong to the Geraniaceae family alongside their distant cousins. Native primarily to southern Africa, these aromatic plants have been cultivated for centuries for their [[essential-oils-and-hydrosol-distillation]], culinary applications, medicinal properties, and ornamental value.

## Taxonomy and Origins

The genus *Pelargonium* comprises over 200 species, with the majority indigenous to the Cape Floristic Region of South Africa. The name derives from Greek *pelargos* (stork), referring to the elongated seed capsule that resembles a stork's bill—contrasting with *Geranium* (from *geranos*, crane). The confusion between the two genera dates to the 18th century when Linnaeus initially grouped them together.

The most important species for scent and cultivation include:

- **P. graveolens** — Rose-scented, the primary source of geranium essential oil
- **P. crispum** — Lemon-scented, compact growth habit
- **P. tomentosum** — Peppermint-scented, large fuzzy leaves
- **P. capitatum** — Apple-scented, low spreading form
- **P. odoratissimum** — Apple/nutmeg scent
- **P. fragrans** — Nutmeg-scented
- **P. denticulatum** — Pine-scented, finely cut foliage
- **P. quercifolium** — Oak-leaf scented geranium, distinctive lobed leaves
- **P. grossularioides** — Coconut-scented, low growing
- **P. citrosum** — Citronella-scented (marketed as mosquito repellent, though evidence is mixed)

## Scent Profiles and Popular Cultivars

Scented geraniums are primarily categorized by their fragrance. Hundreds of cultivars exist, each with a unique aromatic profile. The scent comes from glandular trichomes on the leaf surface that release volatile oils when brushed or crushed.

### Rose Group
The most commercially significant group. Rose-scented geraniums (*P. graveolens* and hybrids) produce geranium oil used in perfumery, aromatherapy, and skincare.

- **'Rober's Lemon Rose'** — Classic rose with lemon undertone
- **'Attar of Roses'** — Intense, sweet rose fragrance
- **'Old Fashioned Rose'** — Deep, true rose scent

### Citrus Group
Bright, uplifting aromas that complement culinary uses.

- **'Mabel Grey'** (*P. crispum* × *P. fulgidum*) — Strong lemon, one of the most intense
- **'Prince of Orange'** — Sweet orange, as close to actual citrus as any geranium
- **'Lemon Fancy'** — Bright, clean lemon
- **'Orange Fizz'** — Orange soda-like effervescence

### Mint Group
Cool, refreshing scents with larger, often fuzzy leaves.

- **'Peppermint'** (*P. tomentosum*) — Large velvety leaves, strong menthol
- **'Chocolate Mint'** — Mint with cocoa undertones
- **'Grapefruit Mint'** — Mint-citrus hybrid character

### Fruit and Spice Group
Warm, sweet, and spicy aromas.

- **'Apple'** (*P. odoratissimum*) — Crisp green apple
- **'Nutmeg'** (*P. fragrans*) — Warm baking spice
- **'Coconut'** (*P. grossularioides*) — Sweet tropical coconut
- **'Ginger'** — Spicy, warm ginger root
- **'Strawberry'** — Sweet berry-like fragrance
- **'Licorice'** — Anise-like

### Herbal and Woody Group
Complex, layered aromatics.

- **'Camphor'** — Medicinal, eucalyptus-like
- **'Pine'** (*P. denticulatum*) — Resinous forest scent
- **'Lavender'** — Soft floral-herbal blend
- **'Rosemary'** — Savory evergreen character

## Cultivation

### Climate and Hardiness

Scented pelargoniums are tender perennials hardy only in USDA Zones 9–11 (roughly 20°F / -7°C minimum). In cooler climates they are grown as annuals or overwintered indoors. They thrive in Mediterranean climates with warm days, cool nights, low humidity, and minimal frost.

### Light Requirements

Full sun (6–8 hours) produces the most compact growth and highest essential oil concentration. In hot climates (above 90°F / 32°C), afternoon shade prevents leaf scorch and stress. Indoor plants need the brightest south- or west-facing window available, or supplemental grow lights.

### Soil

Well-drained soil is non-negotiable. Scented geraniums are highly susceptible to root rot in heavy, waterlogged soils.

- **[[garden-soil-terminology-glossary]]**: Sandy loam amended with perlite or coarse sand; pH 6.0–7.0
- **Containers**: Potting mix with 30–40% perlite or pumice; terracotta pots preferred for drainage
- **[[raised-beds-vs-in-ground-vs-containers]]**: Excellent option in clay-heavy soils

### Watering

Allow the top inch of soil to dry between waterings. Overwatering is the most common cause of death. In containers, water thoroughly until it drains from the bottom, then empty saucers. During winter dormancy indoors, reduce watering significantly—water only when leaves show slight wilt.

### Fertilization

These are light feeders. Excess nitrogen produces lush but scentless foliage.

- **In-ground**: Side-dress with compost in spring; no additional fertilizer typically needed
- **Containers**: Balanced liquid fertilizer (5-5-5 or similar) at half strength every 4–6 weeks during the growing season
- **Avoid**: High-nitrogen fertilizers that promote vegetative growth at the expense of essential oil production

### Propagation

#### Stem Cuttings (Primary Method)
1. Take 3–4 inch cuttings from non-flowering stems in spring or late summer
2. Remove lower leaves, leaving 2–3 nodes
3. Dip cut end in rooting hormone (optional but improves success rate)
4. Stick into moist perlite, sand, or [[seed-starting-methods-comparison]] mix
5. Maintain high humidity (humidity dome or plastic bag) and bright indirect light
6. Roots form in 2–4 weeks; transplant when roots are 1–2 inches long

#### Seed
Most scented cultivars don't come true from seed. Species pelargoniums (*P. tomentosum*, *P. crispum*) can be grown from seed, which requires a light pre-chill (stratification) for 2–4 weeks at 40°F before sowing. Germination is irregular over 2–6 weeks at 70°F.

### Pruning and Maintenance

Regular pinching encourages bushy, compact growth. Pinch stem tips every few weeks during the growing season. Remove spent flower stalks to direct energy into foliage production (the leaves, not flowers, are the primary harvest).

Hard prune in early spring before new growth begins, cutting back by one-third to one-half. This rejuvenates leggy plants and stimulates fresh basal growth.

### Overwintering

In cold climates, bring plants indoors before first frost. Options include:

1. **As houseplants**: Place in bright windows; reduce watering and stop fertilizing
2. **Dormant storage**: Prune back hard, store in cool (45–55°F), dark basement; water monthly to prevent complete desiccation
3. **Take cuttings**: Root fresh cuttings in fall to overwinter as smaller plants

## Pests and Diseases

### Common Pests
- **Whiteflies**: Tiny white insects on leaf undersides; yellow sticky traps and insecticidal soap
- **Aphids**: Clustered on new growth; jet of water or neem oil
- **Spider mites**: Fine webbing, stippled leaves; increase humidity, miticide sprays
- **Mealybugs**: White cottony masses at leaf nodes; alcohol swab treatment
- **Caterpillars**: Occasional chewing damage; handpick or Bt spray

### Common Diseases
- **Root rot** (*Pythium*, *Phytophthora*): Caused by overwatering; improve drainage immediately
- **Botrytis blight**: Gray mold in humid, poorly ventilated conditions; improve airflow
- **Pelargonium rust**: Orange pustules on leaf undersides; remove affected leaves, avoid overhead watering
- **Edema**: Corky bumps on leaves from inconsistent watering; stabilize watering schedule

## Culinary Uses

Scented geranium leaves are edible and widely used in cooking, baking, and beverage-making. The leaves themselves are not typically eaten whole due to texture, but they impart flavor through infusion.

### Sweet Applications
- **Jellies**: Rose-scented geranium jelly is a classic, pairing beautifully with toast, scones, or as a glaze for meats
- **Cakes and cookies**: Line cake pans with leaves before pouring batter; the cake absorbs the aroma
- **Sugar**: Layer fresh leaves with granulated sugar in a sealed jar for 1–2 weeks for scented sugar
- **Ice cream and sorbets**: Infuse leaves into cream bases
- **Syrups**: Simple syrup infused with leaves for cocktails, lemonade, or dessert drizzles
- **Fruit salads**: Toss minced leaves with fresh fruit for aromatic complexity

### Savory Applications
- **Butter**: Blend minced leaves into softened butter for herb breads or vegetables
- **Vinegar**: Steep leaves in white wine or champagne vinegar for 2–3 weeks
- **Marinades**: Add leaves to marinades for chicken, fish, or lamb
- **Stuffing**: Include in poultry stuffing alongside traditional herbs

### Beverage Uses
- **Tea**: Fresh or dried leaves steeped 5–7 minutes; rose, lemon, and mint varieties are favorites
- **Cocktails**: Muddled leaves in gin-based drinks; rose geranium gimlet is a modern classic
- **Lemonade and sangria**: Float leaves in cold beverages
- **Kombucha secondary fermentation**: Add leaves during the flavoring stage

### Best Culinary Varieties
Rose, lemon, peppermint, apple, and nutmeg scents are the most versatile in the kitchen. Avoid varieties with camphor or pine notes for culinary use unless specifically desired.

## Medicinal and Aromatic Properties

### Geranium Essential Oil

Rose-scented pelargoniums (*P. graveolens*, *P. x asperum*) are the commercial source of geranium essential oil, produced primarily in Egypt, China, Reunion Island, and South Africa. The oil contains:

- **Citronellol** — Rose-like, insect-repellent
- **Geraniol** — Floral, antimicrobial
- **Linalool** — Calming, anti-inflammatory
- **Formic acid esters** — Contribute to the characteristic scent

### Traditional and Research-Backed Uses

- **Skin care**: Geranium oil is astringent, balancing for oily and combination skin, and promotes wound healing. Used in commercial skincare formulations for centuries.
- **Anxiety and stress**: Aromatherapy studies show geranium oil inhalation reduces anxiety scores in clinical settings. The linalool and geraniol content modulate GABA receptors.
- **Antimicrobial activity**: Geranium oil shows broad-spectrum activity against *Staphylococcus aureus*, *E. coli*, and *Candida albicans* in vitro. Not a replacement for antibiotics but useful in topical preparations.
- **Insect repellent**: Citronellol and geraniol content provides moderate mosquito repellency. The "mosquito plant" (*P. citrosum*) is heavily marketed for this, but research shows it must be crushed to release oils—simply growing it nearby provides negligible protection.
- **Hemostatic properties**: Traditionally used to slow bleeding; the astringent tannins constrict blood vessels.
- **Menstrual discomfort**: Traditional use as a uterine tonic; limited clinical evidence supports mild analgesic effects.

### Safety Considerations
Geranium essential oil is generally recognized as safe (GRAS) for external use and as a food flavoring. Avoid internal use of concentrated oil without professional guidance. Not recommended during pregnancy in therapeutic doses. May cause contact dermatitis in sensitive individuals—patch test before topical application.

## Companion Planting and Permaculture Roles

### Companion Planting Benefits

- **Tomatoes**: The strong scent may help mask tomato plants from whiteflies
- **Cabbage family**: Scented geraniums planted near brassicas can deter cabbage white butterflies
- **Roses**: Despite not being true geraniums, they pair aesthetically and may help deter Japanese beetles (anecdotal)
- **Pest confusion**: The diverse volatile compounds released by scented pelargoniums can interfere with pest insect host-finding behavior through sensory masking

### Permaculture Functions

- **Edge plants**: Thrive at garden edges and in transitional zones between cultivated and wild areas
- **Container polyculture**: Combine multiple scent varieties in large containers for aromatic diversity
- **Pollinator support**: Small flowers attract beneficial insects including syrphid flies, parasitic wasps, and small native bees
- **Living mulch**: Low-growing varieties like coconut geranium (*P. grossularioides*) can serve as ground cover in mild climates
- **Aromatic garden design**: Scented geraniums form the backbone of sensory and therapeutic gardens

### Guild Considerations

Scented pelargoniums pair well in guilds with:
- Rosemary, lavender, and thyme (Mediterranean companion guild)
- Strawberries (share similar soil and sun preferences)
- Alliums (complementary pest-repellent profiles)
- Nasturtiums and calendula (edible flower guild)

## Harvesting and Preservation

### Leaf Harvest
Harvest leaves at any time during the growing season. For maximum essential oil concentration, harvest in mid-morning after dew has dried but before the heat of the day volatilizes the oils. Young, fully expanded leaves have the best balance of flavor and tenderness.

### Drying
- Air dry in a single layer on screens in a warm, dark, well-ventilated area
- Leaves dry in 3–7 days depending on humidity
- Store dried leaves in airtight glass jars away from light
- Dried leaves retain scent for 6–12 months; for best quality, use within 6 months

### Freezing
Freeze whole leaves in ice cube trays with water or oil for convenient later use in cooking. This preserves the fresh aroma better than drying.

### Oil and Butter Infusions
- **Infused oil**: Pack a jar with fresh leaves, cover with neutral oil (grapeseed, sunflower), steep 2–4 weeks in a cool dark place. Strain and use within 3 months.
- **Compound butter**: Blend minced leaves into softened butter, roll into a log, wrap in wax paper, and freeze for up to 6 months.

## Regional Cultivation Notes

### Mediterranean and Dry Climates (Zones 9–11)
Scented geraniums are ideally suited to Mediterranean conditions. Plant in well-drained sandy or rocky soil in full sun. They can survive on minimal irrigation once established, making them excellent candidates for xeriscaping and water-wise gardens. In these climates, they can be planted directly in the ground as permanent landscape elements.

### Temperate Climates (Zones 7–8)
In regions with mild but freezing winters, scented geraniums can be grown in the ground during frost-free months but must be protected or brought indoors before temperatures drop below 30°F (-1°C). Heavy mulching with straw or frost cloth can provide brief protection down to 25°F (-4°C), but extended freezes are fatal. [[container-growing-vegetables-herbs]] simplifies the transition indoors.

### Cold Climates (Zones 6 and below)
Container growing is essential. Plants spend May–October outdoors and October–April indoors. Select compact varieties for indoor growing — large specimens like *P. tomentosum* need significant space. South-facing windows with supplemental light from a simple LED grow strip are sufficient to keep plants healthy through winter. The dormant storage method (dark, cool basement, monthly watering) works well for growers without adequate indoor light.

### Tropical and Subtropical Climates (Zones 12+)
High humidity is the primary challenge. Scented geraniums can struggle with fungal issues in constantly moist, warm conditions. Ensure maximum airflow, avoid overhead watering, and choose disease-resistant varieties. Raised beds with very gravelly, fast-draining soil are essential. Coconut and apple-scented varieties tend to perform better in humid conditions than rose types.

## Landscaping and Design Uses

### Container Combinations
Scented geraniums pair beautifully with other [[drought-tolerant-planting-design]] plants in mixed containers:
- Rose geranium + trailing rosemary + ornamental oregano
- Lemon geranium + lavender + thyme (Mediterranean theme)
- Peppermint geranium + creeping jenny + variegated mint
- Coconut geranium + sweet alyssum + bacopa (soft, tropical look)

### Border and Edge Plantings
Low-growing varieties (coconut, apple, nutmeg) make excellent border plants at 8–12 inches tall. Taller varieties (rose, peppermint) provide mid-border structure at 18–24 inches. Their textured, often lobed or ruffled foliage adds visual interest even when not in bloom.

### Scented Gardens and Sensory Pathways
Plant scented geraniums along pathways where they'll be brushed against, releasing their fragrance. Position rose-scented varieties near seating areas, lemon varieties near entry points, and peppermint near dining spaces. This intentional placement maximizes the sensory experience of moving through the garden.

## Historical and Cultural Significance

Scented pelargoniums arrived in Europe from South Africa in the mid-17th century via the Dutch East India Company. By the 18th century, they were among the most sought-after plants in English and French gardens. The Victorian era saw an explosion of cultivar development, with over 300 varieties documented by the late 1800s.

The French town of Grasse became the center of geranium oil production in the 19th century, and geranium oil remains a staple of the perfumery industry. It serves as a more affordable substitute for rose otto in many fragrance formulations.

In traditional Cape Dutch and Afrikaner culture, rose geranium leaves were placed in cupboards and linen chests to repel moths and scent clothing—a practice that continues today.

## Troubleshooting Guide

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Yellowing lower leaves | Overwatering or poor drainage | Reduce watering, check drainage, repot if needed |
| Leggy, sparse growth | Insufficient light | Move to brighter location, pinch tips |
| Brown leaf edges | Salt buildup or low humidity | Flush soil with water, increase humidity |
| White powdery patches | Powdery mildew | Improve airflow, treat with potassium bicarbonate spray |
| No scent | Low light or excessive nitrogen | Increase light, reduce fertilization |
| Wilting despite moist soil | Root rot | Check roots—brown/mushy roots need immediate repotting into fresh dry mix |
| Sticky leaves | Aphids or scale | Inspect closely, treat with insecticidal soap |
| Leaves turning red | Cold stress or phosphorus deficiency | Move to warmer location, check soil pH |

## Summary

Scented geraniums are remarkably multifunctional plants that bridge the gap between ornamental gardening, culinary arts, herbal medicine, and [[permaculture-design-course-notes]]. Their drought tolerance, pest-repelling properties, ease of propagation, and extraordinary aromatic diversity make them invaluable in any garden setting. Whether grown in a kitchen window, a [[permaculture-guild-design-method]], or a dedicated aromatic garden, pelargoniums reward the grower with fragrance, flavor, and beauty throughout the growing season—and with proper overwintering, for many years beyond.
