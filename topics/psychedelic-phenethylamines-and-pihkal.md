The phenethylamine family is distinguished from the tryptamine family
(lsd, psilocybin, dmt) by its fundamental chemical architecture. Where
tryptamines are built on an indole ring system, a fused bicyclic structure
containing a benzene ring joined to a pyrrole nitrogen, phenethylamines are
based on a simpler phenyl ring connected to an amine group through a short
carbon chain. Despite this structural difference, both families converge on
many of the same neural targets, particularly the serotonin 5-HT2A receptor,
which is widely regarded as the primary mediator of classical psychedelic
effects. This convergence is one of the most fascinating facts in psychopharmacology:
molecules with very different shapes can produce remarkably similar subjective
experiences by engaging the same receptor systems.

No single individual did more to map the phenethylamine territory than
Alexander "Sasha" Shulgin, an American chemist who, together with his wife Ann
Shulgin, synthesized and self-tested hundreds of novel psychedelic compounds
over a career spanning five decades. The culmination of this work was PIHKAL:
A Chemical Love Story, published in 1991, which documented 179 phenethylamine
compounds along with their synthesis procedures, dosages, and qualitative
effects as reported by Shulgin and a circle of trusted volunteers. PIHKAL
stands as arguably the most extensive first-person exploration of psychedelic
chemistry ever published, and its influence on subsequent research, [[adverse-effects-and-harm-reduction-psilocybin]] considerations, cultural impact, and modern research
directions. It draws primarily from PIHKAL itself, its companion volume TIHKAL,
and Hamilton Turner's Essential Psychedelic Guide, which offers valuable
comparative perspectives on the subjective effects of these compounds.

## Alexander Shulgin and the PIHKAL Project

Alexander Theodore Shulgin (1925-2014) was a uniquely positioned figure in the
history of psychopharmacology. He earned his PhD in biochemistry from the
University of California, Berkeley in 1954 and subsequently worked as a
research chemist at Dow Chemical, where he developed the first commercially
viable biodegradable insecticide and gained a reputation for brilliant
synthetic work. His interest in psychoactive substances was kindled in the
1960s when he experienced mescaline and was struck by the disconnect between
the richness of the subjective experience and the relative simplicity of the
molecule responsible. This encounter launched what would become a lifelong
investigation into the relationship between molecular structure and
consciousness.

Shulgin's credentials gave him an unusual degree of legitimacy and access. He
maintained a DEA research license permitting him to legally synthesize and study
controlled substances, an arrangement that persisted for decades. His laboratory
behind his home in Lafayette, California, became a legendary site in psychedelic
history, equipped with glassware, reagents, and an extraordinary personal
library of chemical literature.

Central to the PIHKAL project was Shulgin's partnership with his wife Ann
Shulgin, a psychotherapist who brought a complementary perspective to the work.
While Sasha handled synthesis and analytical chemistry, Ann contributed
psychological insight and helped design the bioassay protocols. Their
collaboration extended beyond the laboratory into a shared life philosophy
that viewed psychedelic exploration as a legitimate path to understanding
consciousness. Together, they developed a systematic methodology: synthesize a
compound based on hypothesized structure-activity relationships, begin with a
very low "threshold" dose, carefully titrate upward in subsequent sessions, and
meticulously document the subjective effects using a standardized descriptive
framework.

PIHKAL, which stands for "Phenethylamines I Have Known and Loved," was
published in 1991 by Transform Press. The book is divided into two parts: an
autobiographical love story between Sasha and Ann, and a detailed chemical
compendium documenting 179 phenethylamine compounds. For each compound, Shulgin
provided the chemical name and structure, step-by-step synthesis procedures,
dosage information, duration, qualitative commentary, and footnotes on related
compounds. The book was followed in 1997 by TIHKAL ("Tryptamines I Have Known
and Loved"), which applied the same format to the tryptamine family.
