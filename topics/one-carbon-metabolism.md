## Overview

One-carbon metabolism is the cellular network that transfers single-carbon units between folate cofactors, amino acids, nucleotide synthesis, and methylation reactions.
It is not a single pathway, but a set of linked cycles that distribute carbon atoms at different oxidation states.
The network connects dietary folate, vitamin B12, vitamin B6, riboflavin, choline, betaine, serine, glycine, and methionine to central cell functions.
Its products support DNA synthesis, RNA synthesis, methylation, phospholipid production, redox balance, detoxification, and embryonic development.
Because these processes are fundamental, disturbances in one-carbon metabolism can appear as anemia, developmental defects, neurological disease, cancer vulnerability, or altered drug response.
The term is most often used in biochemistry, nutrition, oncology, pharmacology, toxicology, and epigenetics.
A useful definition is that one-carbon metabolism is the economy of transferable methyl, methylene, methenyl, formyl, and related one-carbon groups in cells.

## Historical Background

The study of one-carbon metabolism emerged from work on folate deficiency, anemia, and nucleotide synthesis in the [[s-adenosylmethionine]] established a biochemical explanation for the transfer of methyl groups to DNA, proteins, phospholipids, and small molecules.
Cancer chemotherapy also shaped the field because antifolate drugs demonstrated that blocking folate-dependent reactions could selectively harm rapidly dividing cells.
Later molecular biology added an epigenetic dimension by connecting methyl donor supply to DNA and histone methylation.
Modern systems biology now treats the pathway as a compartmentalized flux network rather than a simple vitamin chart.

## Folate-Mediated Carbon Transfer

Folate cofactors are central carriers in one-carbon metabolism.
Dietary folates and synthetic folic acid are converted into reduced tetrahydrofolate forms that can accept and donate one-carbon groups.
Tetrahydrofolate can carry carbon as 5,10-methylene-THF, 5-methyl-THF, 10-formyl-THF, 5,10-methenyl-THF, and other interconvertible forms.
These forms are not interchangeable in function; each feeds particular reactions with particular oxidation requirements.
5,10-methylene-THF is used by thymidylate synthase to convert dUMP into dTMP for DNA synthesis.
10-formyl-THF contributes carbon atoms to the purine ring during de novo purine biosynthesis.
5-methyl-THF donates a methyl group to homocysteine in the vitamin B12-dependent methionine synthase reaction.
The enzyme methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase, commonly abbreviated MTHFR, helps determine how much folate flux is directed toward methylation rather than nucleotide synthesis.

## The Methionine Cycle

The methionine cycle translates one-carbon input into methylation capacity.
Methionine is converted by methionine adenosyltransferase into S-adenosylmethionine, commonly called SAM.
SAM is the major methyl donor used by methyltransferase enzymes throughout the cell.
After donating its methyl group, SAM becomes S-adenosylhomocysteine, or SAH.
SAH is hydrolyzed to homocysteine and adenosine, but accumulation of SAH can inhibit many methyltransferases.
Homocysteine can be remethylated back to methionine through methionine synthase using 5-methyl-THF and methylcobalamin.
In liver and kidney, betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase provides a folate-independent remethylation route using betaine derived from choline.
The SAM-to-SAH relationship is often called methylation potential, although actual methylation depends on enzyme expression, substrates, compartments, and chromatin state.

## Transsulfuration and Redox Balance

Homocysteine can also leave the methionine cycle through transsulfuration.
Cystathionine beta-synthase condenses homocysteine with serine to form cystathionine in a vitamin B6-dependent reaction.
Cystathionine gamma-lyase then converts cystathionine into cysteine, alpha-ketobutyrate, and ammonia.
Cysteine is a limiting precursor for glutathione synthesis in many contexts.
Glutathione is a major antioxidant, redox buffer, and conjugating molecule used in cellular defense.
This link means one-carbon metabolism affects redox biology even when the immediate topic is methylation.
High SAM can stimulate cystathionine beta-synthase, encouraging excess homocysteine to flow toward sulfur metabolism.
The network therefore balances remethylation, methyl-group use, sulfur amino acid metabolism, and oxidative-stress response.

## Mitochondrial One-Carbon Metabolism

Mitochondria are major producers of one-carbon units.
Serine, glycine, sarcosine, dimethylglycine, and related substrates can feed mitochondrial folate reactions.
Mitochondrial serine hydroxymethyltransferase and the glycine cleavage system are important sources of one-carbon flux in many tissues.
Mitochondria can export formate to the cytosol, where it supports purine synthesis, thymidylate synthesis, and other folate-dependent reactions.
This arrangement lets cells connect energy metabolism, amino acid catabolism, and biosynthesis.
Rapidly proliferating cells often increase serine uptake and mitochondrial folate flux to support [[nitrous-oxide]] can inactivate vitamin B12-dependent methionine synthase, creating acute disruption in susceptible patients.
Some detoxification processes consume methyl donors or glutathione, indirectly linking xenobiotic metabolism to one-carbon and sulfur pathways.
These examples show why the network is important in both therapy and adverse-effect risk.

## Research Significance

Cancer biology studies one-carbon metabolism because proliferating cells need nucleotide synthesis, redox control, and methylation capacity.
Developmental biology studies it because early embryos depend on folate availability and epigenetic programming.
Neuroscience studies it because B12, folate, methylation, and neurotransmitter-related reactions intersect in the nervous system.
Microbiology studies it because microbes have folate pathways that can be targeted by selective drugs.
Aging research considers the pathway because methylation patterns, homocysteine, mitochondrial function, and nutrient status change with age.
Metabolomics now allows researchers to measure folate species, methionine-cycle intermediates, serine flux, formate production, and isotope-labeled carbon transfer.
The result is a field that bridges classical nutrition and molecular systems biology.

## Common Misconceptions

One-carbon metabolism is often reduced to methylation, but methylation is only one output of the network.
High homocysteine is a useful clue, not a complete diagnosis of pathway function.
MTHFR genotype does not determine health by itself and should not be interpreted without diet, clinical context, and biochemical markers.
Folate and folic acid are related but not identical; their effects depend on dose, metabolism, and clinical context.
More methyl donors are not always better because nucleotide synthesis, redox balance, and cell proliferation may respond differently.
Pathway charts can make flux look linear, while real cells use reversible reactions, compartments, feedback regulation, and competing demands.
The most accurate view is of an adaptable metabolic hub that integrates nutrition, growth, defense, and gene regulation.
