## Mushroom Contamination Identification and Troubleshooting

Contamination is the single greatest challenge in [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities|mushroom cultivation]].

Competing organisms — molds, bacteria, viruses, and insect pests — can destroy entire crops before a single fruiting body appears.

The nutrient-rich environments that support mycelial growth are equally inviting to opportunistic invaders.

Prevention through proper technique is far more effective than treatment after contamination occurs.

This article covers the full spectrum of contamination threats in mushroom cultivation, from aggressive competitor molds to insect pests and viral diseases.

## Trichoderma — The Green Mold Threat

[[trichoderma-harzianum|Trichoderma species]] are the most feared contaminants in mushroom cultivation worldwide.

They are aggressive, fast-growing, and produce enormous quantities of spores that aerosolize and colonize new [[substrate-preparation|substrate]]s within hours.

Trichoderma harzianum biotype 4 is specifically adapted to mushroom compost and has caused devastating losses in commercial [[grain-spawn-preparation|grain spawn]] facilities.

The mold initially appears as white [[mycelium-colonization|mycelium]] that rapidly turns green as sporulation begins. On malt [[agar-culture-and-isolation|agar]], colonies display bright green with woolly texture.

Trichoderma thrives in slightly acidic conditions, on wet casings, and in the presence of undecomposed woody tissue or potting soil in casing layers.

It can parasitize mushroom mycelium directly. It produces cellulolytic and chitinolytic enzymes that degrade fungal cell walls.

Once established, it spreads at an explosive rate through airborne spores. Control centers on raising pH, lowering humidity, and removing diseased mushrooms.

Air exchange of 3–5 changes per hour at 85–92% humidity is recommended. Mites are major vectors of Trichoderma spores and must be controlled simultaneously.

A single sciarid fly can carry millions of Trichoderma spores on its body, making integrated insect control a critical component of mold prevention.

## Dactylium — Cobweb Mold

Dactylium produces a fine, grayish-white cobweb-like mycelium that spreads rapidly across the casing layer.

It resembles mushroom mycelium but grows faster and more wispy, making early detection difficult for inexperienced cultivators.

The mold is common in fruiting chambers with high humidity and poor air exchange. It envelops developing primordia, suffocating them before maturity.

Under magnification, Dactylium spores are two-celled, approximately 20 microns in length.

The mold spreads primarily through airborne spores and contaminated equipment.

It is especially aggressive when CO2 is elevated and fresh air is insufficient — conditions that also favor mushroom growth during early pinning.

Treatment in early stages includes spraying with 3% hydrogen peroxide solution.

Covering affected areas with table salt works because Dactylium is highly sensitive to sodium chloride.

Lowering room temperature slows its growth significantly. Spores are killed at 115–122°F (46–50°C) for 30 minutes, so proper pasteurization eliminates this threat.

## Verticillium — Dry Bubble Disease

Verticillium fungicola is the most common fungal disease parasitizing commercial mushroom crops worldwide.

It causes deformed caps, split stems, and characteristic sclerotia-like balls of amorphous whitish mycelia on severely infected primordia.

On Agaricus, slightly infected mushrooms show brown spots on the basal stem and developing caps. Afflicted mushrooms bend toward the infected side.

Mature diseased mushrooms display downy grayish mycelium on the cap. The stem splits vertically, resembling a peeled banana.

The dry, leathery texture distinguishes dry bubble from wet bubble caused by Mycogone. This textural difference is the key diagnostic feature.

On Psilocybe cubensis, parasitized caps become plane early with swollen, hollow stems.

Transmission occurs through human handlers, sciarid flies, water splash, and casing material inoculum.

Strict hygiene before veil break is essential. Remove infected mushrooms promptly to prevent spore spread throughout the growing room.

## Mycogone — Wet Bubble Disease

Mycogone perniciosa causes wet bubble disease, producing distorted, spongy masses of tissue on the casing surface.

Unlike the dry, leathery texture of Verticillium, Mycogone produces soft, wet, rotting tissue.

Infected mushrooms leak amber liquid and emit a strong sour odor that is diagnostic for this disease.

The disease thrives in overly wet casing conditions and spreads through water splash during watering.

Insect vectors and contaminated tools also transmit Mycogone between growing beds and cultivation batches.

Control requires reducing watering frequency, improving air circulation, and removing infected mushrooms promptly.

Casing pasteurization eliminates soil-borne Mycogone inoculum before it can cause infection.

## Other Competitor Molds

Penicillium appears as bluish-green colonies on grain spawn, compost, and casing soil. Humans are the most frequent carriers.

Aspergillus species produce green, yellow, or black colonies. A. fumigatus causes "Worker's Lung Disease" from contaminated compost. Respiratory protection is mandatory.

Neurospora (pink mold) produces rapid, tenacious aerial growth that overtakes cultures in days. Its heat-resistant spores survive standard pasteurization.

Chaetomium (olive green mold) indicates poor composting or insufficient pasteurization. It degrades cellulose and causes significant crop losses.

Geotrichum (lipstick mold) thrives on concrete and inhibits primordia formation.

Mucor and Rhizopus (black pin molds) grow extremely fast and elevate substrate temperature to 95–104°F through metabolic heat.

Scopulariopsis (white plaster mold) indicates insufficient pasteurization.

Sepedonium produces yellow-white mold with heat-resistant spores and a sweet nauseous odor.

Botrytis (brown mold) is less virulent and often contained by healthy mushroom mycelium. Its presence still warrants improved hygiene.

## Bacterial Pathogens and Blotch Diseases

Pseudomonas tolaasii causes bacterial blotch — brown, sunken lesions on mushroom caps.

The bacterium thrives in wet conditions with standing water on the cap surface. Poor air circulation worsens the problem.

An interesting contrast: Pseudomonas putida actually stimulates primordia formation in some strains. Not all bacteria in cultivation are harmful.

Bacterial contamination of grain spawn presents as slimy, sour-smelling wet spots caused by Bacillus species surviving insufficient sterilization.

These thermophilic Bacillus bacteria survive brief boiling temperatures. Grain spawn requires pressure sterilization at 15 PSI (121°C).

Bacterial blotch is managed through environmental control rather than chemical treatment.

Eliminating standing water on mushroom surfaces and avoiding condensation drip are the most effective preventive measures.

## Viral Diseases — La France and Die-Back

Viral diseases are infrequent but devastating when they occur.

La France disease causes mycelium to lose vigor and produces small, deformed mushrooms. The virus spreads via spores and contaminated tools.

There is no cure once a viral infection is established. The only option is strict hygiene and starting over with verified clean stock.

Virus-infected mycelium may appear normal initially but progressively weakens with each flush. Declining yields across flushes is the hallmark diagnostic.

Prevention centers on certified virus-free spawn, sterilizing tools between uses, and never reusing substrate from suspect crops.
