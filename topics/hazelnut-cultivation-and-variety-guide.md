## Hazelnut Cultivation and Variety Guide

The hazelnut (also called filbert or cobnut) is a versatile, long-lived nut-producing shrub or small tree in the genus *Corylus* (family Betulaceae). Cultivated for thousands of years, hazelnuts rank among the most important nut crops worldwide, prized for their rich flavor, high nutritional value, and adaptability to diverse growing systems — from commercial orchards to permaculture-food-waste-cyclingod-forests-design-course.

## Species Overview

Four species are of primary interest to growers in temperate regions:

- **[[corylus-maxima]]** — The giant filbert, native to southeastern Europe and western Asia. Larger in stature than *C. avellana* with proportionally larger nuts enclosed in fringed husks. Many ornamental varieties (such as the purple-leafed filbert and the contorted hazel) derive from this species or from hybrids with *C. avellana*. Kentish cobnuts, widely grown in southern England, are primarily selections of *C. avellana* with some *C. maxima* ancestry.

- **Corylus americana** — The American hazelnut, native to eastern and central [[cervantes-wind-protection-outdoor-cannabis]] is also important. While moderate wind facilitates pollination, sustained strong winds (>40 km/h / 25 mph) during bloom can damage catkins and reduce pollen viability. In exposed sites, permanent windbreaks of conifers or other tall species should be established on the prevailing wind side.

## Soil Requirements

Hazelnuts are adaptable to a range of soil types but perform best on deep, well-drained loams with a pH of 5.5–7.0. They are somewhat tolerant of clay soils provided drainage is adequate; waterlogging kills roots rapidly and promotes Phytophthora root rot, which can devastate plantings in heavy, poorly drained ground. Sandy soils are acceptable with irrigation and mulching to retain moisture. Rocky or shallow soils (less than 60 cm / 24 in to bedrock or hardpan) restrict root development and reduce vigor and yield.

The root system is extensive and fibrous, with most feeder roots concentrated in the top 60 cm (24 in) of soil but individual roots capable of penetrating much deeper in favorable conditions. This shallow, spreading root system makes hazelnuts relatively drought-sensitive during establishment but also responsive to surface mulching and irrigation.

## Site Selection

Key considerations for hazelnut site selection:

- **Air drainage**: Plant on slopes or elevated sites to avoid frost pockets. Cold air flows downslope; low-lying areas accumulate cold and increase frost risk to emerging flowers. Even a 1–2 m elevation difference can mean the difference between a full crop and complete loss on a frosty spring night.
- **Wind exposure**: Moderate wind is beneficial for pollination but strong, sustained winds damage catkins and desiccate young plants. Windbreaks on the prevailing wind side are advisable in exposed sites. Avoid planting on exposed ridges or hilltops.
- **Sunlight**: Full sun is required for maximum nut production, though *C. americana* tolerates partial shade and is often found as an understory shrub in its native habitat (with reduced yield). For nut production, a minimum of 6–8 hours of direct sunlight is recommended.
- **Spacing**: In commercial orchards, plants are typically spaced 4.5–6 m (15–20 ft) apart in rows 5–6 m (16–20 ft) apart, yielding approximately 300–500 plants per hectare. ## Pollination Requirements

Hazelnuts are obligately outcrossing and self-incompatible. Every planting requires at least two genetically distinct cultivars for nut production, and three or more are preferable for reliable cross-pollination and extended bloom overlap.

- **Wind pollination**: Male catkins release millions of pollen grains, which are carried by wind to receptive female stigmas. No insects are involved. Pollen can travel hundreds of meters on wind, but effective pollination drops off sharply beyond 22 m (75 ft).
- **Timing is critical**: Pollen shed and female receptivity must overlap. Some cultivars shed pollen very early (January in mild climates) while females of other cultivars may not be receptive until late February or March. Always verify bloom timing charts from your regional extension service when selecting pollinizer combinations. A common mistake is planting two cultivars that bloom at different times.
- **Distance**: Pollinizers should be planted within 18–22 m (60–75 ft) of each other. Intermixed planting (alternating cultivars in rows, or planting every third tree as a pollinizer) maximizes pollination success.
- **Incompatibility groups**: European hazelnuts use a sporophytic self-incompatibility system with multiple S-allele groups. Compatible cultivars must differ in their incompatibility group. For example, Barcelona is incompatible with other Group 1 cultivars but compatible with Groups 2, 3, etc. Consult local nursery catalogs or extension publications for specific compatibility charts.
