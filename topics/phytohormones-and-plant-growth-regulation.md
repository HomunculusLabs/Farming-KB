## Phytohormones and Plant Growth Regulation

Phytohormones (plant hormones) are naturally occurring [[query-how-do-phytohormones-regulate-plant-growth-and-stress-responses]], development, and responses to environmental stimuli at extremely low concentrations. Unlike animal hormones, which are typically produced in specialized glands, phytohormones can be synthesized in virtually any plant cell and often act locally or are transported to distant sites. Understanding phytohormone biology is fundamental to improving crop yields, managing plant stress, and developing sustainable growing practices.

## Overview of Phytohormone Classes

Plants produce a diverse array of hormone classes, each with distinct but overlapping functions. The classical "five major" hormones — auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, ethylene, and abscisic acid — were identified through most of the 20th century. Since the 1990s, additional classes including brassinosteroids, jasmonates, salicylic acid, strigolactones, and peptide hormones have been recognized as essential regulators.

A single plant cell may respond to dozens of hormones simultaneously, and the observed phenotype typically reflects the integrated output of multiple signaling pathways interacting through complex crosstalk networks.

## Classical Phytohormones

### Auxins

**Chemistry and Biosynthesis:**
Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) is the primary natural auxin in most plants. IAA is synthesized primarily from the amino acid tryptophan via two major pathways: the TAA/YUC (tryptophan aminotransferase/YP-flavin monooxygenase) route, which predominates in Arabidopsis, and the IAM (indole-3-acetamide) pathway used by some pathogenic bacteria. Non-tryptophan-dependent pathways also exist.

**Physiological Roles:**
- **Apical dominance:** Auxin produced by the shoot apex suppresses lateral bud outgrowth, maintaining a single dominant stem. Removing the apex (decapitation) redistributes auxin and allows lateral buds to grow — a principle exploited in pinching and topping cannabis plants.
- **Phototropism and gravitropism:** Auxin redistributes asymmetrically in response to light or gravity, causing differential cell elongation on one side of the stem. The Cholodny-Went hypothesis remains the foundational model.
- **Root initiation:** Auxin accumulation at wound sites promotes adventitious root formation. This is the basis for rooting powders containing synthetic auxins like IBA (indole-3-butyric acid) and NAA (naphthaleneacetic acid).
- **Vascular differentiation:** Auxin flow from shoots to roots (polar auxin transport) patterns vascular tissue development, including xylem and phloem differentiation.
- **Fruit development:** Auxin application can induce parthenocarpic (seedless) fruit set in tomatoes, cucumbers, and other crops.

**Polar Auxin Transport:**
Auxin moves directionally through plant tissues via efflux carriers of the PIN-FORMED (PIN) family and influx carriers of the AUX/LAX family. PIN proteins cycle between the plasma membrane and endosomes, allowing dynamic redistribution of auxin flow in response to developmental or environmental cues. The synthetic auxin transport inhibitor TIBA (2,3,5-triiodobenzoic acid) is used experimentally to study this process.

**Synthetic Auxins in Agriculture:**
- **2,4-D:** Selective broadleaf herbicide used in cereal crops and pastures
- **MCPA:** Similar to 2,4-D, used in grain crops
- **Dicamba:** Broadleaf herbicide, increasingly used with herbicide-tolerant crops
- **NAA:** Used for fruit thinning, prevention of pre-harvest fruit drop
- **IBA:** Rooting hormone for cuttings

### Gibberellins

**Chemistry and Biosynthesis:**
Gibberellins (GAs) are diterpenoid acids. Over 130 GA compounds (GA₁ through GA₁₃₆) have been identified, but only a subset are bioactive — primarily GA₁, GA₃, GA₄, and GA₇. Biosynthesis occurs in plastids (ent-kaurene production), the endoplasmic reticulum (oxidation steps), and the cytosol (final hydroxylation to bioactive forms). The key enzyme GA 20-oxidase is a major regulatory step.

**Physiological Roles:**
- **Stem elongation:** Gibberellins stimulate cell division and cell elongation in the intercalary meristems of stems. The famous "Green Revolution" semi-dwarf wheat and rice varieties carry mutations in GA signaling (e.g., the *sd1* gene in rice, *Rht* genes in wheat) that reduce response to GA, producing shorter, sturdier plants less prone to lodging.
- **Seed germination:** GA produced by the embryo diffuses to the aleurone layer of cereal seeds, where it induces expression of α-amylase and other hydrolytic enzymes that mobilize stored starch. This discovery led to the development of the malt brewing industry.
- **Bolting and flowering:** GA promotes the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth in long-day plants and in biennials that require vernalization. GA application can substitute for cold treatment in some species.
- **Fruit development:** GA promotes fruit set and enlargement in grapes (used commercially to produce seedless Thompson grapes with larger berries), apples, and citrus.

**GA Signaling:**
Bioactive GAs bind to the GID1 receptor, promoting interaction with DELLA repressor proteins. DELLA proteins are then targeted for ubiquitin-mediated degradation by the SCF complex, relieving repression of GA-responsive genes. The DELLA proteins serve as a central hub integrating multiple hormone and environmental signals.

**Applications in Growing:**
- GA₃ (gibberellic acid) is commercially available and used to break dormancy in seeds, promote uniform germination, increase fruit size in grapes, and prevent rind creasing in citrus
- Prohexadione-calcium is a GA biosynthesis inhibitor used to reduce shoot growth in apples and ornamentals
- Paclobutrazol and uniconazole inhibit GA biosynthesis and are used for height control in nursery stock

### Cytokinins

**Chemistry and Biosynthesis:**
Cytokinins are N⁶-substituted adenine derivatives. The most common natural cytokinins are zeatin (trans- and cis-isomers) and isopentenyladenine (iP). Biosynthesis occurs primarily via the MEP (methylerythritol phosphate) pathway, with dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP) transferred to ADP/ATP by isopentenyl transferases (IPT). Cytokinin nucleotides are activated by removal of ribose and phosphate groups by LOG (LONELY GUY) enzymes.

**Physiological Roles:**
- **Cell division:** Cytokinins were originally identified as factors promoting cytokinesis (cell division) in tobacco tissue culture, combined with auxin. The auxin:cytokinin ratio determines organogenesis — high auxin:cytokinin promotes root formation, while low auxin:cytokinin promotes shoot formation.
- **Shoot initiation:** Cytokinins promote shoot apical meristem activity and leaf formation. Overproduction of cytokinin leads to excessive shoot branching, while cytokinin deficiency causes stunted shoot growth.
- **Delay of senescence:** Cytokinins retard leaf senescence by maintaining chlorophyll and protein content. This "stay-green" effect has commercial applications in cut flowers and foliage.
- **Nutrient mobilization:** Cytokinins regulate source-sink relationships, promoting nutrient movement toward cytokinin-rich tissues such as young leaves and developing seeds.

**Cytokinin Signaling:**
The phosphorelay signaling pathway involves membrane-localized histidine kinase receptors (CRE1/AHK4, AHK2, AHK3), histidine phosphotransfer proteins (AHPs), and type-B response regulators (ARRs) that activate gene expression. Type-A ARRs serve as negative feedback regulators.

**Practical Applications:**
- Kinetin and benzyladenine (BA, 6-BA) are used in tissue culture for shoot multiplication
- BA is applied to apples to improve fruit shape and reduce russeting
- Forchlorfenuron (CPPU) is used to enlarge kiwifruit and grapes
- Cytokinin-containing biostimulants are marketed as anti-senescence sprays

### Ethylene

**Chemistry and Biosynthesis:**
Ethylene (C₂H₄) is the simplest plant hormone — a gaseous hydrocarbon produced from the amino acid methionine via S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) and the cyclic intermediate 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC). The key enzyme ACC synthase (ACS) is encoded by a multigene family and is the primary regulatory step. ACC oxidase (ACO) catalyzes the final conversion to ethylene and is oxygen-dependent.

**Physiological Roles:**
- **Fruit ripening:** Ethylene is the "ripening hormone" responsible for the climacteric rise in respiration that accompanies ripening in fruits like bananas, tomatoes, apples, and mangoes. The commercial practice of shipping bananas green and gassing them with ethylene at destination exploits this biology.
- **Abscission:** Ethylene promotes the separation of leaves, flowers, and fruits from the plant by activating cell wall-degrading enzymes (polygalacturonases, cellulases) in the abscission zone.
- **Flower senescence:** Ethylene accelerates the aging of cut flowers. Carnations, orchids, and petunias are particularly ethylene-sensitive.
- **Triple response:** In dark-grown seedlings, ethylene inhibits stem elongation, promotes horizontal growth (diageotropism), and induces an apical hook — a set of responses that helps seedlings push through soil.
