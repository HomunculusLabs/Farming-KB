## Mushroom Dyes and Fungal Pigments

Mushroom dyes represent one of the most ancient and still one of the least explored branches of natural color. While plant dyes have dominated the textile colorant world for millennia, fungi offer a remarkable palette of hues — from vivid reds and oranges to rare blues and greens — that are difficult or impossible to achieve with plant materials alone. The chemistry behind fungal pigments is diverse, involving anthraquinones, quinones, terphenyls, and other compound classes that produce colors with excellent lightfastness and wash-fastness when properly applied.

## History of Fungal Dyeing

The use of fungi for dyeing textiles is ancient but poorly documented compared to plant-based dye traditions. Archaeological evidence suggests that fungal pigments were used by indigenous peoples across multiple continents:

- **European traditions**: Alpine communities in Slovenia, Austria, and Italy used species of *Dermocybe* (now reclassified within *Cortinarius*) to dye wool for traditional folk costumes. Records from the 18th and 19th centuries document the use of *Cortinarius sanguineus* to produce brilliant reds and *[[cortinarius-violaceus]]* for purples.
- **Scandinavian practices**: Finnish and Swedish dyers historically used *[[pisolithus-tinctorius]]* (dyer's puffball) and *Hydnellum suaveolens* to produce golden yellows and olive greens on wool and linen.
- **Indigenous [[stamets-reishi-ganoderma-lucidum-cultivation]]* (reishi) extracts as components in lacquer and pigment mixtures, and certain *Trametes* species were employed in paper dyeing.
- **Modern revival**: The systematic study of mushroom dyes began with Miriam Rice in the 1970s, whose pioneering work at the Mendocino Art Center in California established fungal dyeing as a recognized craft. Her books, notably *Mushrooms for Color* (1980), inspired a global community of fungal dyers.

## Pigment Chemistry

### Anthraquinones

Anthraquinones are the most important class of fungal dye pigments. They are [[lichenized-fungi-taxonomy-diversity]]) and contributes yellow-orange color.
- **Trametic acid**: Isolated from *Phaeolus schweinitzii*, contributes yellow-brown tones.

Anthraquinone-based fungal dyes generally exhibit excellent lightfastness, often superior to their plant-derived counterparts. This is attributed to the stability of the quinone ring system and its ability to form stable complexes with metal mordants.

### Polyporic Acid

Polyporic acid is a tetrahydroxyquinone found in species such as *Hapalopilus nidulans* and *Sarcodon* species. It is a pH-sensitive pigment that can produce striking purples and magentas on wool and silk. The compound is structurally similar to henna (lawsone) but produces cooler tones. In alkaline conditions, polyporic acid shifts toward red; in acidic conditions, it tends toward purple-violet.

### Atromentin and Terphenyl Compounds

Atromentin and related terphenyl compounds are produced by *Hydnellum*, *Sarcodon*, and *Thelephora* species. These are complex aromatic molecules that yield yellows, oranges, and browns. Atromentin itself is a bis-benzylated derivative of 4,4'-dihydroxybiphenyl. The color range can be modulated by mordants and by varying the extraction pH.

### Xylindein

Xylindein is a naphthoquinone pigment produced by *Chlorociboria* species. It is responsible for the striking blue-green color of wood colonized by these fungi — commonly known as "green oak" or "green stained wood" and historically prized by woodworkers for inlay and marquetry. Xylindein is poorly soluble in water, making it challenging to extract for textile dyeing, but it can be dissolved in ethanol or acetone for application to wood or paper.

### Badione A

Badione A is a bis-naphthoquinone pigment found in *Cortinarius* and *Dermocybe* species, particularly those producing warm reds and oranges. It is structurally related to the hypericin family of compounds and contributes to the depth and warmth of colors from these fungi.

### Solvent Extraction

For pigments that are poorly water-soluble (such as xylindein from *Chlorociboria*):

1. **Ethanol extraction**: Soak the fungal material in 70-95% ethanol for 24-48 hours. This is effective for many naphthoquinone and terphenyl pigments.
2. **Acetone extraction**: Useful for chlorociboria and other stubborn pigments. Acetone extracts are fast but evaporate quickly.
3. **Application**: Solvent-extracted pigments are best applied to wood, paper, or as paints rather than used in standard textile dye baths. They can be mixed with linseed oil or other binders for wood finishing.

### Fermentation Extraction

A traditional and low-energy-futures-in-permaculture approach:

1. Submerge mushroom material in water at room temperature.
2. Allow the mixture to ferment for 1-4 weeks in a warm location. Indigenous fermentation may produce different color results than sterile extraction.
3. The fermented dye bath often produces richer, more complex colors than hot extraction, particularly for yellow-producing species.

## Mordanting

Mordants are metal salts that create a chemical bridge between the fiber and the dye molecule, improving color fastness and often modifying the hue. The choice of mordant significantly affects the final color.

### Common Mordants for Fungal Dyes

| Mordant | Chemical | Typical Use (on wool) | Color Effect |
|---------|----------|-----------------------|--------------|
| Alum (potassium aluminum sulfate) | KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O | 15% WOF | Brightens colors; standard for most fungal dyes |
| Iron (ferrous sulfate) | FeSO₄·7H₂O | 2-5% WOF | Saddens/darkens colors; shifts yellows to olive-green, reds to purple |
| Copper (copper sulfate) | CuSO₄·5H₂O | 2-4% WOF | Shifts yellows to green, adds blue-green tones |
| Chrome (potassium dichromate) | K₂Cr₂O₇ | 1-3% WOF | Deepens and enriches colors; shifts to warmer tones |
| Tin (stannous chloride) | SnCl₂·2H₂O | 1-2% WOF | Brightens dramatically; can shift to pink/magenta; use sparingly |
| Tannin (oak gall, sumac) | Tannic acid | 10-20% WOF | Pre-mordant for cellulose fibers; enhances dye uptake |
| Cream of tartar | KHC₄H₄O₆ | 5-8% WOF | Often combined with alum; softens colors, improves evenness |
