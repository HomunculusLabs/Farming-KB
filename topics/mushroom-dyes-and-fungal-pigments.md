# Mushroom Dyes and Fungal Pigments

Mushroom dyes represent one of the most ancient and still one of the least explored branches of natural color. While plant dyes have dominated the textile colorant world for millennia, fungi offer a remarkable palette of hues — from vivid reds and oranges to rare blues and greens — that are difficult or impossible to achieve with plant materials alone. The chemistry behind fungal pigments is diverse, involving anthraquinones, quinones, terphenyls, and other compound classes that produce colors with excellent lightfastness and wash-fastness when properly applied.

## History of Fungal Dyeing

The use of fungi for dyeing textiles is ancient but poorly documented compared to plant-based dye traditions. Archaeological evidence suggests that fungal pigments were used by indigenous peoples across multiple continents:

- **European traditions**: Alpine communities in Slovenia, Austria, and Italy used species of *Dermocybe* (now reclassified within *Cortinarius*) to dye wool for traditional folk costumes. Records from the 18th and 19th centuries document the use of *Cortinarius sanguineus* to produce brilliant reds and *Cortinarius violaceus* for purples.
- **Scandinavian practices**: Finnish and Swedish dyers historically used *Pisolithus tinctorius* (dyer's puffball) and *Hydnellum suaveolens* to produce golden yellows and olive greens on wool and linen.
- **Indigenous North American use**: Several Pacific Northwest tribes used *Phaeolus schweinitzii* to dye basketry materials and cedar bark. The Haida and Tlingit peoples reportedly used fungal pigments in ceremonial contexts.
- **East Asian traditions**: Chinese textile artisans used *Ganoderma lucidum* (reishi) extracts as components in lacquer and pigment mixtures, and certain *Trametes* species were employed in paper dyeing.
- **Modern revival**: The systematic study of mushroom dyes began with Miriam Rice in the 1970s, whose pioneering work at the Mendocino Art Center in California established fungal dyeing as a recognized craft. Her books, notably *Mushrooms for Color* (1980), inspired a global community of fungal dyers.

## Pigment Chemistry

### Anthraquinones

Anthraquinones are the most important class of fungal dye pigments. They are polycyclic aromatic compounds characterized by a three-ring structure with two carbonyl groups. Key anthraquinones in fungal dyes include:

- **Emodin**: Found in many *Cortinarius* and *Dermocybe* species. Produces yellow to orange-red hues. Closely related to emodin found in rhubarb and buckthorn.
- **Physcion**: A methylated derivative of emodin, contributing orange tones.
- **Dermorubin**: Found in *Dermocybe sanguinea*; produces deep reds.
- **Parietin**: Present in some *Xanthoria* lichens (lichenized fungi) and contributes yellow-orange color.
- **Trametic acid**: Isolated from *Phaeolus schweinitzii*, contributes yellow-brown tones.

Anthraquinone-based fungal dyes generally exhibit excellent lightfastness, often superior to their plant-derived counterparts. This is attributed to the stability of the quinone ring system and its ability to form stable complexes with metal mordants.

### Polyporic Acid

Polyporic acid is a tetrahydroxyquinone found in species such as *Hapalopilus nidulans* and *Sarcodon* species. It is a pH-sensitive pigment that can produce striking purples and magentas on wool and silk. The compound is structurally similar to henna (lawsone) but produces cooler tones. In alkaline conditions, polyporic acid shifts toward red; in acidic conditions, it tends toward purple-violet.

### Atromentin and Terphenyl Compounds

Atromentin and related terphenyl compounds are produced by *Hydnellum*, *Sarcodon*, and *Thelephora* species. These are complex aromatic molecules that yield yellows, oranges, and browns. Atromentin itself is a bis-benzylated derivative of 4,4'-dihydroxybiphenyl. The color range can be modulated by mordants and by varying the extraction pH.

### Xylindein

Xylindein is a naphthoquinone pigment produced by *Chlorociboria* species. It is responsible for the striking blue-green color of wood colonized by these fungi — commonly known as "green oak" or "green stained wood" and historically prized by woodworkers for inlay and marquetry. Xylindein is poorly soluble in water, making it challenging to extract for textile dyeing, but it can be dissolved in ethanol or acetone for application to wood or paper.

### Badione A

Badione A is a bis-naphthoquinone pigment found in *Cortinarius* and *Dermocybe* species, particularly those producing warm reds and oranges. It is structurally related to the hypericin family of compounds and contributes to the depth and warmth of colors from these fungi.

## Dye Extraction Methods

### Water Extraction (Hot Simmer)

The most common method for extracting water-soluble pigments from dye mushrooms:

1. **Preparation**: Clean the mushrooms to remove dirt and debris. Fresh mushrooms can be used whole or chopped; dried mushrooms should be crumbled or ground. A typical ratio is 100% weight of fiber (WOF) to 200-400% weight of dried mushroom material.
2. **Soaking**: Cover the mushroom material with water and let it soak for several hours or overnight. This softens the tissue and begins pigment extraction.
3. **Simmering**: Bring the pot to a gentle simmer (approximately 80-90°C / 175-195°F) and maintain for 1-2 hours. Avoid vigorous boiling, which can break down some pigments. Stir occasionally.
4. **Straining**: Strain the dye liquid through a fine mesh strainer or cheesecloth. Return the mushroom material to the pot for a second extraction if the color remains strong.
5. **Dyeing**: Add pre-mordanted fiber to the dye bath and simmer gently for 45-90 minutes. Turn the fiber occasionally to ensure even dyeing.
6. **Cooling and rinsing**: Allow the fiber to cool in the dye bath for best color uptake. Rinse in cool water until the rinse runs clear, then dry away from direct sunlight.

### Alkaline Extraction

Some pigments, particularly those in *Hapalopilus* and *Sarcodon* species, respond well to alkaline extraction:

1. Follow the standard hot water extraction procedure.
2. Add ammonia (household strength, approximately 5%) to the dye bath in small increments while stirring. The color may shift dramatically — polyporic acid turns from dull brown to vivid purple or magenta.
3. Monitor the pH; a range of 8-10 is typical for alkaline extraction.
4. Use caution: ammonia vapors are irritating. Work in a ventilated area.

### Solvent Extraction

For pigments that are poorly water-soluble (such as xylindein from *Chlorociboria*):

1. **Ethanol extraction**: Soak the fungal material in 70-95% ethanol for 24-48 hours. This is effective for many naphthoquinone and terphenyl pigments.
2. **Acetone extraction**: Useful for chlorociboria and other stubborn pigments. Acetone extracts are fast but evaporate quickly.
3. **Application**: Solvent-extracted pigments are best applied to wood, paper, or as paints rather than used in standard textile dye baths. They can be mixed with linseed oil or other binders for wood finishing.

### Fermentation Extraction

A traditional and low-energy approach:

1. Submerge mushroom material in water at room temperature.
2. Allow the mixture to ferment for 1-4 weeks in a warm location. Indigenous fermentation may produce different color results than sterile extraction.
3. The fermented dye bath often produces richer, more complex colors than hot extraction, particularly for yellow-producing species.

## Mordanting

Mordants are metal salts that create a chemical bridge between the fiber and the dye molecule, improving color fastness and often modifying the hue. The choice of mordant significantly affects the final color.

### Common Mordants for Fungal Dyes

| Mordant | Chemical | Typical Use (on wool) | Color Effect |
|---------|----------|-----------------------|--------------|
| Alum (potassium aluminum sulfate) | KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O | 15% WOF | Brightens colors; standard for most fungal dyes |
| Iron (ferrous sulfate) | FeSO₄·7H₂O | 2-5% WOF | Saddens/darkens colors; shifts yellows to olive-green, reds to purple |
| Copper (copper sulfate) | CuSO₄·5H₂O | 2-4% WOF | Shifts yellows to green, adds blue-green tones |
| Chrome (potassium dichromate) | K₂Cr₂O₇ | 1-3% WOF | Deepens and enriches colors; shifts to warmer tones |
| Tin (stannous chloride) | SnCl₂·2H₂O | 1-2% WOF | Brightens dramatically; can shift to pink/magenta; use sparingly |
| Tannin (oak gall, sumac) | Tannic acid | 10-20% WOF | Pre-mordant for cellulose fibers; enhances dye uptake |
| Cream of tartar | KHC₄H₄O₆ | 5-8% WOF | Often combined with alum; softens colors, improves evenness |

### Mordanting Process (for Protein Fibers)

1. Dissolve the mordant in hot water.
2. Fill a dye pot with enough water for the fiber to move freely.
3. Add the mordant solution and the wetted fiber to the pot.
4. Bring to a gentle simmer and hold for 1 hour, stirring occasionally.
5. Turn off the heat and allow the fiber to cool in the mordant bath.
6. Rinse lightly or proceed directly to dyeing.

### Safety Note on Mordants

Iron, copper, and chrome mordants are toxic and should be handled with care. Use dedicated equipment (never use cooking pots for mordanting), wear gloves, and work in a well-ventilated area. Dispose of spent mordant baths responsibly — never pour them down drains or into waterways. Alum is the safest mordant and is suitable for most applications.

## Fiber Compatibility

Different fibers accept fungal dyes differently due to their chemical structure:

- **Wool**: The ideal fiber for fungal dyeing. The protein structure readily bonds with mordants and dye molecules. Rich, deep colors with excellent fastness.
- **Silk**: Excellent dye uptake; produces luminous, jewel-toned colors. More delicate than wool; lower dyeing temperatures (70-80°C) are recommended.
- **Alpaca, mohair, and other protein fibers**: Generally dye well, similar to wool. Mohair tends to produce slightly brighter colors due to its smooth fiber surface.
- **Cotton**: Poor affinity for fungal dyes without extensive pre-treatment. Requires both a tannin mordant and an alum mordant (a two-step process) for adequate color uptake. Colors tend to be lighter and less fast than on protein fibers.
- **Linen**: Similar to cotton; improved uptake with tannin pre-mordanting. Historically used with fungal dyes in Scandinavian traditions.
- **Hemp and bamboo**: Moderate uptake with appropriate pre-mordanting. Generally produce paler shades than wool.

## Sustainable Harvesting

Ethical collection of dye mushrooms is essential to protect fungal populations and their ecological roles:

- **Never collect rare species**: Species like *Cortinarius violaceus* and *Cortinarius sanguineus* are not common in all areas. Learn to identify local populations before harvesting.
- **Collect fallen or senescent fruiting bodies when possible**: Old, dried, or insect-damaged mushrooms can still produce excellent dyes and do not affect spore dispersal.
- **Leave some behind**: Never harvest the entire fruiting body population in an area. A good rule is to take no more than 25% of what you find.
- **Avoid disturbing the mycelium**: Cut mushrooms at the base rather than pulling them from the substrate. The mycelial network is the living organism and is essential for soil health.
- **Grow your own**: Several dye mushroom species can be cultivated at home (see below), reducing pressure on wild populations.
- **Be aware of regulations**: Some areas restrict or prohibit mushroom collection on public lands. Check local regulations before foraging.

## Safety Precautions

- **Identification is critical**: Never consume or handle mushrooms you cannot positively identify. Some toxic species resemble dye mushrooms. Learn from experienced mycologists or use reliable field guides.
- **Ventilation**: Simmering mushrooms releases volatile compounds. Work in a well-ventilated area, especially when using ammonia for alkaline extraction.
- **Protective equipment**: Wear gloves when handling mordants, and use dedicated dye pots that are never used for food preparation.
- **Spore allergies**: Some people are sensitive to mushroom spores. If you experience respiratory irritation during collection or processing, wear a mask or work outdoors.
- **Toxic species**: A few dye-producing species are also toxic (e.g., some *Cortinarius* species contain orellanine). Do not allow children or pets near the dye workspace. Label all materials clearly.

## Modern Applications

### Art and Craft

Fungal dyes have experienced a renaissance in the slow fashion and fiber arts movements. Artists use mushroom-dyed yarns for weaving, knitting, and felting. The subtle, complex colors produced by fungi are valued for their natural warmth and depth, which synthetic dyes often fail to replicate.

### Woodworking

Xylindein from *Chlorociboria* species has been used for centuries to create green-stained wood for marquetry, inlay, and decorative woodworking. The pigment penetrates wood fiber deeply and is extremely lightfast, making it superior to many synthetic wood stains.

### Scientific and Industrial Research

Fungal pigments are being investigated for several high-tech applications:

- **Organic photovoltaics**: Xylindein and related naphthoquinones have been studied as light-absorbing materials in organic solar cells due to their strong visible-light absorption and chemical stability.
- **Antimicrobial textiles**: Some fungal pigments, particularly anthraquinones, exhibit antimicrobial properties. Research is exploring their use in medical textiles and food packaging.
- **Cosmetics**: Fungal pigments are being evaluated as alternatives to synthetic colorants in makeup and personal care products, driven by consumer demand for natural ingredients.
- **Biodegradable colorants**: As the textile industry seeks to reduce its environmental impact, fungal pigments offer a renewable, biodegradable alternative to petroleum-derived dyes.

## Growing Dye Mushrooms at Home

Several dye mushroom species can be cultivated, providing a sustainable source of pigment material:

### *Pisolithus tinctorius* (Dyer's Puffball)

- **Method**: Can be grown on sterilized substrate (straw, wood chips, or compost) using spore slurry or inoculated grain spawn.
- **Substrate**: Prefers sandy, well-draining soil with organic matter. Often found in association with eucalyptus and pine.
- **Yield**: Produces large fruiting bodies with substantial pigment content.
- **Difficulty**: Moderate; reliable fruiting requires some experimentation with substrate and conditions.

### *Pycnoporus cinnabarinus* (Cinnabar Polypore)

- **Method**: Grows well on hardwood sawdust (oak, beech) supplemented with bran.
- **Substrate**: Hardwood sawdust blocks or logs.
- **Yield**: Good pigment yield; the bright orange fruiting bodies are easy to spot and harvest.
- **Difficulty**: Easy to moderate for cultivators familiar with mushroom growing.

### *Ganoderma lucidum* (Reishi)

- **Method**: Widely cultivated for medicinal purposes; the same cultivation methods apply for dye production.
- **Substrate**: Hardwood sawdust, supplemented with wheat bran or rice bran. Grows well on oak logs.
- **Yield**: Moderate; the tough, woody fruiting bodies require extended simmering for full pigment extraction.
- **Difficulty**: Easy; reishi is one of the most commonly cultivated mushrooms worldwide.

### *Laetiporus sulphureus* (Chicken of the Woods)

- **Method**: Can be cultivated on inoculated hardwood logs (oak, walnut).
- **Substrate**: Fresh hardwood logs, 30-60 cm long, inoculated with plug or sawdust spawn.
- **Yield**: Variable; good for yellow dyes but yield depends on the specific strain and growing conditions.
- **Difficulty**: Moderate; outdoor log cultivation requires patience (1-2 years to fruiting).

### Tips for Home Cultivation

- Start with species that are easy to grow (reishi, oyster mushrooms with dye potential).
- Maintain sterile technique during spawn preparation to avoid contamination.
- Experiment with different substrates to find what produces the most pigment-rich fruiting bodies.
- Even failed crops can be useful — substrate colonized by mycelium can sometimes be used as a dye material.
- Join local mycological societies or online communities for support and spore exchange.

## Troubleshooting Common Issues

| Problem | Likely Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------------|----------|
| Color is too pale | Insufficient mushroom material | Increase WOF ratio to 300-400% or use a second extraction |
| Color is uneven | Uneven mordanting or poor fiber movement | Ensure fiber can move freely in the dye bath; stir frequently |
| Color washes out | Inadequate mordanting | Re-mordant the fiber; increase mordant concentration |
| Color fades in sunlight | Inherently fugitive pigment or no mordant | Choose anthraquinone-rich species; always use a mordant |
| Dye bath smells bad | Fermentation of mushroom material | This is normal for some species (e.g., *Phaeolus*); ventilate well |
| Fiber is damaged | Temperature too high for delicate fibers | Lower dyeing temperature for silk (70-80°C) |
| No color development | Wrong pH for the pigment | Try adjusting pH with vinegar (acid) or ammonia (alkaline) |
| Color is muddy/dull | Iron contamination in water or equipment | Use clean, dedicated equipment; test water for iron content |

## Getting Started: Recommended Beginner Projects

1. **Pisolithus tinctorius yellow on wool**: Abundant, easy to find, forgiving to work with. Use alum mordant for bright gold.
2. **Phaeolus schweinitzii gold-to-green**: Readily available on conifer stumps. Experiment with iron mordant for olive green.
3. **Hapalopilus nidulans purple**: Stunning magenta/purple with ammonia modification. A dramatic demonstration of pH-sensitive fungal pigments.
4. **Cortinarius semisanguineus orange-red**: For those with access to these beautiful mushrooms, the rich orange-red on alum-mordanted wool is one of the finest natural reds available.
