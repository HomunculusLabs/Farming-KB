# Trap Cropping and Push-Pull Pest Management

## Overview

Trap cropping and push-pull strategies are ecologically-based pest management techniques that manipulate insect behavior and plant chemistry to protect crops without reliance on synthetic pesticides. These methods exploit the natural preferences of pest insects—drawing them toward decoy plants (trap crops) or repelling them from the main crop while attracting them to a perimeter (push-pull)—reducing pest pressure on the target harvest. Together they form a cornerstone of integrated pest management (IPM) and have been validated across diverse cropping systems on every inhabited continent.

## What Is Trap Cropping?

A trap crop is a plant species (or cultivar) that is grown specifically to attract pest insects away from the main cash crop. The trap crop is more attractive to the pest than the primary crop, concentrating pest populations where they can be monitored, suppressed, or destroyed. The concept is ancient—Roman agricultural writers described planting sacrificial plants to protect vineyards—but modern trap cropping is grounded in chemical ecology, insect behavioral science, and evolutionary biology.

### Mechanisms of Attraction

Pest insects locate host plants through a hierarchy of cues:

- **Volatile organic compounds (VOCs)**: Plants emit specific blends of volatile chemicals that herbivorous insects detect with their antennae. Trap crops often produce higher concentrations of attractive volatiles than the main crop. For example, European corn borer moths are drawn to the sesquiterpene β-caryophyllene released by certain trap plant species at concentrations 10–100× higher than standard maize.
- **Visual cues**: Color, shape, and size influence landing and oviposition choices. Many pests preferentially land on taller, more prominent plants, which is why perimeter trap crops are often planted before the main crop to establish a height advantage.
- **Oviposition stimulants**: Surface chemicals, trichome density, and leaf texture can trigger egg-laying behavior. The Indian mustard cultivar 'PI 447697' produces glucosinolate profiles that serve as potent oviposition stimulants for diamondback moth, far exceeding those of neighboring cabbage.
- **Nutritional suitability**: Some pests show a strong feeding preference for trap crops that contain higher levels of specific nutrients—sugars, amino acids, or secondary compounds that serve as feeding stimulants. Colorado potato beetles preferentially feed on certain Solanum species due to their glycoalkaloid profiles.

## Types of Trap Crops

### Perimeter Trap Cropping

The trap crop is planted as a border entirely surrounding the main crop. This is the simplest and most widely adopted configuration. The border intercepts pests moving into the field from overwintering sites or adjacent habitats. Perimeter trap cropping works well for pests that colonize fields from edges and are strongly attracted to the trap species.

**Classic example**: In Indian cotton farming, pigeonpea (*Cajanus cajan*) is planted as a border around cotton fields. Cotton bollworm (*Helicoverpa armigera*) strongly prefers pigeonpea for oviposition, and the border plants concentrate 80–90% of the egg masses. Farmers can then apply targeted controls only to the pigeonpea border, reducing pesticide use on cotton by 50–70%.

### Row Interception Trap Cropping

Trap crop rows are planted at intervals within the main crop—typically every 4–10 rows, depending on pest mobility. This approach intercepts pests that have already penetrated the field edge. It is more effective for highly mobile pests that quickly disperse throughout a field.

**Example**: In crucifer vegetable production in Kenya, Indian mustard is planted every 5–7 rows within cabbage fields. Diamondback moth populations concentrate on the mustard, and farmers can apply Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) sprays only to the mustard rows.

### Sequential Trap Cropping

A trap crop is planted earlier than the main crop so that it reaches the attractive growth stage before the primary crop emerges. This temporal mismatch is critical for pests that are highly mobile during specific phenological windows.

**Example**: In California tomato production, early-planted alfalfa strips attract lygus bugs (*Lygus hesperus*) before tomatoes flower. The bugs establish on the alfalfa and remain there through the critical fruit-setting period.

### Multiple-Crop Trap Cropping Systems

Complex systems use two or more trap crop species targeting different pest complexes simultaneously. This is increasingly common in diversified vegetable operations.

**Example**: A New England vegetable farm might use blue Hubbard squash as a perimeter trap crop for squash bugs and cucumber beetles, while interplanting nasturtiums to attract aphids away from brassicas within the same field.

## Push-Pull Technology

Push-pull pest management is an elaboration of trap cropping that adds an active repellent ("push") component to the main crop while simultaneously maintaining an attractive trap crop ("pull"). The dual strategy creates a directional behavioral gradient that drives pests away from the protected crop and concentrates them in a manageable area.

### The Push Component

The push uses stimuli that deter pests from the main crop:

- **Intercropped repellent plants**: Species that emit volatile compounds unattractive or repellent to the target pest. In African cereal systems, silverleaf desmodium (*Desmodium uncinatum*) intercropped between maize rows repels stemborer moths—specifically, desmodium releases (E)-β-ocimene and several other volatile terpenoids that disrupt stemborer host-finding behavior.
- **Synthetic repellents**: Application of compounds that mask crop volatiles or actively repel pests. Methyl salicylate dispensers have been used to repel certain aphid species from apple orchards.
- **Visual disruption**: Reflective mulches, UV-absorbing films, or colored markers that disorient pest insects. Aluminum-coated plastic mulch repels aphids and thrips from pepper and tomato fields.
- **Mechanical barriers**: Floating row covers and insect netting function as physical push components when used in combination with trap crops.

### The Pull Component

The pull uses the same principles as conventional trap cropping—an attractive plant that lures pests away from the main crop. In push-pull systems, the pull crop is typically planted as a perimeter border.

**In the African push-pull system**: Napier grass (*Pennisetum purpureum*) is planted as a border around maize fields. Stemborer moths are attracted to Napier grass volatiles and preferentially oviposit on it. When larvae hatch and bore into the grass stems, the grass produces a sticky sap that traps and kills them—a built-in mortality mechanism unique to this grass species.

### The African Push-Pull Success Story

The most extensively validated push-pull system was developed by the International Centre of Insect Physiology and Ecology (ICIPE) in collaboration with Rothamsted Research and local African farming communities. Targeting cereal stemborers (*Chilo partellus* and *Busseola fusca*) and the parasitic weed Striga (*Striga hermonthica*), the system has been adopted by over 100,000 smallholder farmers across East Africa.

**Components**:
- **Push**: Silverleaf desmodium (*Desmodium uncinatum*) intercropped between maize rows
- **Pull**: Napier grass (*Pennisetum purpureum*) or Brachiaria grass as a border crop

**Mechanism**:
1. Desmodium releases volatile chemicals that repel stemborer moths from maize
2. Desmodium root exudates trigger suicidal germination of Striga seeds, depleting the soil seed bank
3. Napier grass at the border attracts stemborer moths for oviposition
4. Napier grass stems produce a gummy substance that kills boring larvae before they can develop
5. Desmodium is a legume that fixes nitrogen, improving soil fertility for the maize crop

**Results**: Adoption of push-pull technology has produced maize yield increases of 2–3× compared to monoculture, while virtually eliminating Striga infestation in treated fields. The system also provides high-quality livestock fodder (both Napier grass and desmodium), diversifying farm income.

## Design Principles

### 1. Know Your Pest Biology

Effective trap cropping requires detailed knowledge of the target pest's:
- **Host plant preference hierarchy**: Which plants does the pest rank above the main crop?
- **Flight range and dispersal behavior**: How far does the pest move, and in what pattern?
- **Phenology**: When does the pest emerge, mate, and oviposit relative to crop stages?
- **Density-dependent behavior**: Does the pest avoid overcrowded trap crops?

A trap crop that is attractive at the wrong growth stage or to the wrong pest species will fail entirely. Blue Hubbard squash, for example, is an excellent trap crop for squash bugs but provides no benefit against squash vine borer, which has different host preferences.

### 2. Synchronization Is Critical

The trap crop must reach its most attractive stage before or concurrent with the main crop's vulnerable stage. If the main crop emerges and becomes attractive before the trap crop, pests will colonize the cash crop directly. Solutions include:

- Planting the trap crop 2–4 weeks earlier than the main crop
- Using transplants for the trap crop while direct-seeding the main crop
- Selecting fast-growing cultivars for the trap crop
- Using multiple successions of the trap crop to extend the attractive period

### 3. Scale and Proportion

The ratio of trap crop to main crop area must be large enough to intercept a significant proportion of the pest population but small enough to remain economically viable. Research suggests:

- **Perimeter trap crops**: Typically 10–20% of total field area
- **Row-interception trap crops**: Every 4th to 10th row
- **Highly mobile pests**: May require larger trap crop areas (up to 30%)
- **Sedentary pests**: Can be managed with smaller trap crop areas (5–10%)

### 4. Trap Crop Management

Trap crops require active management to remain effective:

- **Monitor pest populations** on trap crops at least twice weekly using sweep nets, visual inspection, or pheromone traps
- **Destroy or treat** trap crop insects before they complete development and can move to the main crop
- **Remove trap crop residues** promptly after the main crop's vulnerable period ends to prevent the trap crop from becoming a pest breeding reservoir
- **Rotate trap crop species** between seasons to prevent pest adaptation and avoid buildup of trap crop-specific diseases

### 5. Economic Considerations

The cost-benefit analysis of trap cropping must account for:
- Seed, planting, and management costs of the trap crop
- Any revenue from the trap crop (fodder, grain, or secondary harvest)
- Reduced pesticide application costs on the main crop
- Yield protection value
- Labor for monitoring and managing the trap crop

In most documented cases, trap cropping is cost-effective when pesticide applications are reduced by 40% or more, which is achievable in well-designed systems. The push-pull system in Africa provides additional economic returns through fodder production and nitrogen fixation that offset trap crop costs entirely.

## Combination with Other IPM Strategies

### Biological Control Integration

Trap crops create concentrated pest populations that are ideal targets for biological control agents:

- **Conservation biological control**: Parasitoid wasps such as *Trichogramma* spp. can be released onto or near trap crops where host eggs are concentrated, increasing parasitism efficiency. Parasitism rates of 70–90% have been documented on trap crops vs. 10–30% on scattered hosts in the main crop.
- **Predator support**: Diverse trap crop borders provide habitat for generalist predators (lady beetles, lacewings, ground beetles) that spill over into the main crop.
- **Microbial pesticides**: Applications of Bt, entomopathogenic nematodes, or entomopathogenic fungi are more efficient on concentrated pest populations in trap crops, requiring less product for equivalent control.

### Pheromone-Based Enhancement

Mating disruption pheromones can be applied to trap crops to further concentrate pests and reduce their reproductive success. In cotton systems, pheromone dispensers placed in pigeonpea trap crop borders have reduced mating success of pink bollworm by 90% compared to untreated controls.

### Cultural Control Synergy

Trap cropping synergizes with:
- **Crop rotation**: Breaks pest cycles that trap cropping alone might not interrupt
- **Tillage timing**: Destroying overwintering sites in combination with trap cropping provides multi-season pest suppression
- **Sanitation**: Removing crop residues after harvest prevents carryover that could overwhelm trap crops in subsequent seasons

## Limitations and Challenges

### Pest Adaptation

Pests can adapt to trap cropping over time. Diamondback moth populations in some regions have shifted preference from Indian mustard to the main cabbage crop after 8–10 seasons of continuous mustard trap cropping. Rotation of trap crop species and integration with other control methods mitigates this risk.

### Secondary Pest Outbreaks

Trap crops that attract large pest populations may support populations of natural enemies that later disperse and provide biological control of other pests—a benefit in most cases. However, trap crops can also harbor diseases or non-target pests that spread to the main crop. This risk is managed by selecting trap crop species that do not share pathogens with the main crop.

### Spatial Requirements

Small or intensively managed gardens may lack space for dedicated trap crops. In these situations, interplanting repellent companion plants (a simplified push strategy) or using sacrificial plants within the garden bed can provide partial protection.

### Climate and Environmental Variability

Unusual weather patterns can disrupt the synchrony between trap crop attractiveness and pest phenology. Warm springs may accelerate pest emergence before trap crops are established, while cool conditions may delay trap crop growth. Contingency plans—such as pre-started trap crop transplants—help buffer against climatic variability.

### Knowledge Intensity

Trap cropping demands more ecological knowledge, monitoring effort, and adaptive management than conventional pesticide programs. This knowledge barrier is a significant adoption constraint, particularly in regions with limited agricultural extension services. Successful adoption programs (such as the push-pull technology in East Africa) have relied heavily on farmer field schools and peer-to-peer learning networks.

## Emerging Research Directions

### Semiochemical-Based Trap Cropping

Advances in analytical chemistry have identified the specific volatile compounds responsible for pest attraction to trap crops. Synthetic formulations of these compounds—dispensed from lures or slow-release matrices—can enhance trap crop attractiveness by 3–10×. Research at Rothamsted Research has demonstrated that methyl anthranilate dispensers combined with blue Hubbard squash increase squash bug attraction by 400% compared to the trap crop alone.

### Genetic Engineering of Trap Crops

Transgenic trap crops expressing Bt toxins or RNAi targeting pest genes are under development. These "suicidal trap crops" would attract and kill pests in a single step, eliminating the need for separate pest management actions on the trap crop. Ethical and regulatory considerations currently limit deployment.

### Remote Sensing and Precision Trap Cropping

Drone-based multispectral imaging can detect pest hotspots in trap crops at early stages, enabling precisely targeted interventions. Machine learning models trained on trap crop monitoring data can predict pest pressure and optimize trap crop placement and management timing.

### Multi-Trophic Push-Pull Systems

Next-generation push-pull systems incorporate plants that simultaneously manipulate pest behavior (push/pull), support natural enemies (banker plants), and enhance soil biology. These multi-functional plant mixtures aim to create self-regulating pest management systems that require minimal external input.

## Global Case Studies

### India: Cotton-Pigeonpea System

In Gujarat and Maharashtra states, pigeonpea border trap crops around Bt cotton have reduced pink bollworm and American bollworm populations by 60–80% compared to Bt cotton alone. The system has extended the effective lifespan of Bt cotton by reducing selection pressure for resistance, as surviving pests concentrate on the non-Bt pigeonpea where they can be managed without selecting for Bt resistance genes.

### Kenya: Push-Pull for Smallholder Cereals

Over 100,000 farming households in Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, and Ethiopia have adopted the desmodium-maize-Napier grass push-pull system. Average maize yields increased from 1.0 t/ha to 2.5–3.5 t/ha, while Striga parasitism was reduced from 40–80% of plants to less than 5%. Farmer income increased by an average of $500–$800 per household per year through combined grain and fodder sales.

### United States: Squash-Pumpkin Systems

Blue Hubbard squash perimeter trap crops around commercial pumpkin and summer squash fields in the northeastern US have reduced squash bug damage by 70–90% and cucumber beetle populations by 50–70%. The system is now recommended by multiple university extension services and has been adopted on an estimated 15,000+ acres.

### China: Rice-Sesbania Push-Pull

In southern China, sesbania (*Sesbania cannabina*) planted as a border around rice paddies attracts rice stem borers for oviposition. The trap crop is then removed before larvae can complete development. Combined with light traps that capture adult moths, the system has reduced stem borer damage by 60–75% while decreasing insecticide applications by 3–4 per season.

## Summary

Trap cropping and push-pull technology represent some of the most effective and well-validated ecological pest management strategies available. Their success depends on understanding pest biology, careful planning of crop timing and spatial arrangement, and active monitoring and management. When properly implemented, these strategies can reduce pesticide dependence by 50–90%, increase crop yields by 2–3×, and provide additional benefits through soil improvement, fodder production, and biodiversity conservation. The ongoing development of semiochemical-enhanced systems, precision monitoring technologies, and multi-trophic designs promises to make these approaches even more effective and accessible to farmers worldwide.
