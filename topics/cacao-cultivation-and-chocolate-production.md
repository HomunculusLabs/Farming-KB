
## Introduction

*Theobroma cacao*, commonly known as cacao or cocoa, is an evergreen understory tree native to the Amazon basin and the foothills of the Andes. Belonging to the family Malvaceae, the genus name *Theobroma* translates to "food of the gods," reflecting the deep [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]] and northern South America. Trees are susceptible to most major diseases, which has led to declining cultivation. Criollo accounts for less than 5% of global production. The beans are large, plump, and pale-colored when fermented. Well-known Criollo-derived types include Porcelana and Chuao from Venezuela.

### Forastero

Forastero is the most widely grown variety, originating in the Amazon basin and now dominant in West Africa (over 80% of world production). It is hardy, disease-resistant, and high-yielding, but produces beans with a more robust, somewhat harsher flavor often described as "basic" or "bulk" cacao. The Nacional subtype from Ecuador (also called Arriba) is a notable exception, producing a sought-after floral and fruity flavor profile. Forastero beans are smaller and flatter than Criollo, with deeper purple coloration.

### Trinitario

Trinitario is a natural hybrid between Criollo and Forastero, originating in Trinidad after a catastrophic Criollo die-off in the eighteenth century. It combines the disease resistance and vigor of Forastero with the superior flavor characteristics of Criollo. Trinitario accounts for roughly 10–15% of global production and is widely grown in the Caribbean, Central and South America, and increasingly in Southeast Asia. Many single-origin and fine-flavor chocolates are made from Trinitario beans.

### Modern Genetic Classification

Contemporary research using molecular markers has identified 10 distinct genetic clusters: Amelonado, Contamana, Criollo, Curaray, Guiana, Iquitos, Marañon, Nanay, Nacional, and Purús. This classification reflects the species' complex evolutionary history in the upper Amazon and provides a more accurate framework for breeding and conservation programs.

## Ideal Growing Conditions

Cacao is strictly tropical, requiring specific environmental conditions for optimal growth and production:

### Climate

- **Temperature:** 25–28°C (77–82°F) annual mean. Growth ceases below 15°C, and temperatures above 35°C cause leaf scorch and flower drop. Optimal day–night differential is 5–7°C.
- **Rainfall:** 1,500–2,500 mm annually, ideally distributed throughout the year with no more than 3 consecutive dry months. Below 1,200 mm, irrigation becomes essential.
- **Humidity:** 70–80% relative humidity year-round. Low humidity during flowering reduces pod set.
- **Wind:** Cacao is extremely wind-sensitive. Shelterbelts or shade trees must provide wind protection. Persistent winds exceeding 15 km/h cause leaf desiccation and branch breakage.

### Altitude

Optimal elevation is 0–600 m above sea level, though some fine-flavor cacaos are grown up to 1,200 m (e.g., in parts of Ecuador and Papua New Guinea). Higher elevations produce slower bean maturation and potentially more complex flavors but increase frost risk.

### Soil

Cacao prefers deep, well-drained, loamy soils with:

- pH 5.0–7.0 (optimum 6.0–6.5)
- High organic matter content (3–5% or more)
- Good water-holding capacity but no waterlogging (cacao roots are highly susceptible to Phytophthora)
- Adequate calcium, magnesium, and potassium; micronutrients (zinc, boron, iron) are commonly limiting
- Volcanic soils and alluvial soils are generally favorable

### Shade

In its natural habitat, cacao is an understory tree. Cultivated cacao benefits from 25–50% shade, particularly during the first 3–4 years. Shade trees (commonly Inga spp., Gliricidia sepium, Erythrina spp., or fruit trees like banana and plantain) reduce temperature stress, protect from wind, improve soil through [[leaf-litter-microfungal-diversity-tropical-temperate]], and can provide additional income. Some modern full-sun production systems achieve higher yields but require intensive irrigation, fertilization, and pest management.

## Propagation Methods

### Seed Propagation

Seeds must be planted fresh — viability drops rapidly after 2 weeks. Seeds are extracted from ripe pods, the pulp is partially removed (or left on to aid germination), and sown in nursery bags or beds at 2–3 cm depth. Germination occurs in 7–14 days. Seed-grown trees exhibit high genetic variability, which is undesirable for commercial production but can be useful in breeding programs. Seed propagation is primarily used for rootstock production.
