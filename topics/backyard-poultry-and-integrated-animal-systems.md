# Backyard Poultry and Integrated Animal Systems

In a well-designed homestead or small farm, animals are not separate from the
garden — they are woven into every zone, performing essential functions that
would otherwise require human labour, fossil fuels, or purchased inputs.
Chickens scratch and fertilize garden beds. Ducks patrol orchards for slugs.
Rabbits generate manure for compost. Goats clear brush and produce milk. The
permaculture principle of "integrating rather than segregating" finds perhaps
its most direct expression in the integration of small livestock into food
production systems.

The industrial model of animal agriculture isolates livestock in massive
concentrated facilities, feeds them grain grown on distant fields, and
treats their manure as a waste problem. The homestead model inverts this
relationship entirely. Animals live where their services are needed. Their
manure becomes fertilizer, not pollution. Their foraging replaces pesticides
and herbicides. Their presence converts low-value vegetation — weeds, insect
pests, kitchen scraps, fallen fruit — into high-value protein: eggs, meat,
and milk. The key insight, articulated by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren
from the earliest days of permaculture, is that every animal should perform
multiple functions, and every function should be supported by multiple
elements. A chicken is not just an egg machine. It is a soil builder, a pest
controller, a compost turner, a weed manager, and a source of meat and
feathers.

This article covers the design and management of integrated animal systems on
the homestead scale, with emphasis on chickens as the most versatile and
widely kept poultry species, but extending to ducks, rabbits, quail, pigeons,
and the principles that govern all successful animal integration.

## The Philosophy of Animal Integration

The permaculture approach to animals begins with a fundamental question:
what functions do we need performed on this site, and which animals can
perform them? This is the opposite of the conventional approach, which asks:
what animals do I want to keep, and how do I house and feed them? In a
permaculture design, animals are introduced to serve specific ecological
functions, and their housing, feeding, and management are designed around
those functions.

David Holmgren articulated this as the principle of using "nature's
services" — the work that living organisms perform for free — to replace
energy-intensive technologies. His classic example is the chicken tractor:
the natural scratching action of a fowl cultivates soil instead of a tractor
or rotary hoe. At the same time as it cultivates, the chicken's foraging
for insects replaces toxic insecticides, and its manure replaces
energy-intensive fertilizers. The comparison reveals that the functions of
a tractor are better understood than the functions of a fowl, but the fowl
performs all of them simultaneously, at no energy cost, while producing food.

Bill Mollison placed animal integration within a broader framework of
nutrient cycling. In nature, nutrients cycle continuously through living
systems. On a conventional farm, nutrients are imported as fertilizer and
exported as produce, creating linear flows that deplete both the farm and the
environments where fertilizers are mined or manufactured. Animals are the
primary mechanism for closing nutrient loops on a homestead: they eat plant
material and kitchen scraps, concentrate nutrients in their manure, and
return those nutrients to the soil where they feed the next cycle of plant
growth.

The sequential introduction of animals reflects the maturation of the food
production system. Chickens can be introduced after one to three years, once
the garden and orchard are established enough to withstand their scratching.
Pigs may follow after three to seven years, when the food forest can
withstand rooting. Sheep and cattle require the most mature systems and
are introduced only after seven to fifteen years. This patience prevents
young plantings from being destroyed by animals before they can establish.

## Chickens: The Most Versatile Homestead Animal

Chickens are the gateway animal for most homesteaders, and for good reason.
They are relatively easy to keep, they produce eggs year-round, they convert
a wide variety of feed sources into protein, and they perform multiple
ecological functions simultaneously. A flock of chickens provides eggs, meat,
feathers, fertilizer, pest control, and weed management from a modest
investment of space and labour.

Their manure is high in nitrogen, with an N-P-K ratio of approximately
1.5-1.0-0.5 when fresh. Deposited directly on garden beds or incorporated
into compost, it adds significant fertility. In deep bedding systems — where
chickens are housed on a thick layer of carbon material such as straw,
leaves, or wood chips — the combination of manure and carbon generates large
quantities of compost through microbial decomposition. The heat generated by
this decomposition even provides winter warmth for the flock.

As pest controllers, chickens are remarkably effective. They eat slugs,
snails, grasshoppers, beetles, and many garden pests. Their scratching
behaviour uncovers insect eggs and larvae that would otherwise hatch into
the next generation of pests. In orchards, chickens reduce codling moth and
other pest populations by eating fallen fruit that serves as breeding
habitat. Free-ranging chickens significantly reduce tick and flea populations
around the homestead. They also eat many weed seeds before they can set,
disturb weed seedlings through scratching, and consume certain weeds entirely
including chickweed, purslane, and dandelion leaves.

Breed selection matters enormously. Good foraging breeds include Rhode
Island Reds, Plymouth Rocks, Wyandottes, Sussex, and Australorps. These
breeds actively seek out insects and vegetation, reducing the need for
supplemental feed. Dual-purpose breeds such as Orpingtons and Plymouth
Rocks provide both respectable egg production and meat yield. Leghorns and
other light breeds are the most prolific egg producers but are less hardy
and less inclined to forage. Heavy breeds don't fly, are better mothers,
and lay brown eggs, but they are less effective foragers. Heritage breeds are
more likely to go broody (sit on eggs to hatch them), which is essential for
self-replacing flocks that don't depend on purchased chicks.

## Chicken Tractors and Mobile Systems

The chicken tractor is perhaps the most iconic permaculture tool for animal
integration. It is a portable, floorless enclosure that concentrates chickens
on specific areas of the garden or pasture, allowing them to perform their
ecological functions in a controlled and targeted manner. Rather than
building a permanent coop with a fixed run, the tractor moves across the
landscape, converting each site it passes over.

The concept is simple but the design implications are profound. A chicken
tractor eliminates the problems associated with permanent poultry housing:
manure buildup, odour, parasite loads from contaminated ground, and the need
to manually collect and spread fertilizer. It provides chickens with
continuous access to fresh forage, improving their diet and reducing feed
costs. And it allows the gardener to deploy chicken labour precisely where
it is needed: preparing beds before planting, cleaning up after harvest, or
controlling pests in specific areas.

Standard designs hold four to twelve birds, with approximately one square
metre per bird as a minimum floor area. The enclosure must be tall enough
for birds to stand comfortably and for the caretaker to access the interior
for feeding, watering, and egg collection. A height of sixty to ninety
centimetres at the low end sloping to 120 centimetres is typical. The
floorless base is essential — birds must have direct soil contact for
scratching and manure deposition. Predator protection requires secure wire
mesh on all sides and overhead, with wire buried fifteen to thirty
centimetres into the ground to prevent digging predators. A solid-roofed
shelter section at one end provides protection from rain and sun. The unit
must be light enough for one person to move regularly; wheels, skids, or
PVC pipe runners facilitate relocation.

The most common small design is an A-frame, with a triangular house and a
totally enclosed triangular pen covered in chicken wire. Rectangular pens
are another common design, with the house taking up one-third of the space
and a square cutout allowing chickens to access their run. The chicken
door should be thirty centimetres high, incorporated into the peak of the
roof, where the height deters predators.

Moving frequency depends on the application and the season. For intensive
garden bed preparation, daily moves of one to two metres are ideal. For
lighter soil conditioning, moves every two to three days may suffice. The
key principle is observation: move the tractor before bare soil appears and
before manure accumulates heavily enough to burn plants. In spring, the
tractor prepares beds by scratching in cover crops and depositing fertilizer
ahead of heavy-feeding crops. In summer, it follows behind established crops
to control weeds and pests. In autumn, it cleans up crop residues and
deposits manure before winter cover crops are planted.

The chicken tractor concept extends readily to other species. Duck tractors
are effective in orchards for slug control, as ducks forage more aggressively
near wet areas. Rabbit tractors provide manure for gardens while rabbits
graze on pasture, and they are quieter than chickens, making them suitable
for urban settings where noise is a concern. Guinea fowl tractors provide
excellent tick and insect control over larger areas.

## Ducks: The Gentle Pest Controllers

Ducks are the most versatile waterfowl for the homestead, and in many
respects they outperform chickens for integrated pest management. While
chickens scratch destructively through mulch and can damage garden beds,
ducks waddle through the garden eating slugs, snails, and grubs without
disturbing plants. They are the gentlest and most versatile poultry species
for homestead integration.

A duck's diet includes algae and weeds from ponds, slugs, snails, grubs,
soft greens, grasses, water plants, small tree greens, and grains. Their
manure fertilizes both water and soil, creating a nutrient cycle between
pond and garden that is difficult to achieve with any other livestock.
With adequate water, a grassy yard, and a forage garden, ducks require
minimal supplemental feed, making them more self-sufficient than chickens
in many situations.

The ideal setup places ducks in an enclosed area around a small pond with
an island for nesting. A stocking rate of approximately twenty-five ducks
per acre of pond surface is recommended. A forage garden around the pond
provides additional feed. Duck manure feeds the organisms that control
algae and helps grow pond plants, creating a self-regulating aquatic
ecosystem. During the growing season, ducks can be released into the garden
to eat slugs and pests once plants are large enough to withstand their
gentle foraging. Their water source must be kept separate from other
animals, as ducks rapidly foul any water they access.

Housing requirements are modest: four square feet per duck in a three-sided
shelter near the pond during the day, with a barn room and straw bed at
night. Unlike chickens, many duck breeds can fly, so one wing's long
feathers should be clipped when they first grow and after each moult.
The clipping must not be done during moulting, when the blood feathers
are vulnerable and cutting can cause fatal bleeding.

Ducks need more niacin than other poultry, which fresh greens or peas
usually provide. Their diet should include wheat as the primary grain,
supplemented with oats, hard-boiled eggs or liquid milk for protein, and
crushed eggshells or seashells for calcium. Grit in the form of sand and
small stones is essential for digestion. They are social creatures and
should be kept in groups of at least two. Females have a loud, raspy
quack while males are quieter or nearly silent, making sex determination
by sound straightforward.

Egg production breeds include the Khaki Campbell (the most popular and
first domestic duck breed) and Indian Runners, which lay nearly as many
eggs as chickens. Meat breeds include Muscovy, Rouen, and Pekin. Among
dual-purpose heritage breeds, the Saxony is probably the best combination
of foraging ability and egg production.

## Rabbits, Quail, and Other Small Livestock

Small livestock are especially practical for urban and suburban homesteaders
because they are legal in most jurisdictions (including places where chickens
are banned), they are quiet, and they require minimal space. In the
permaculture zone system, they fit in Zone 1 near the house or at the edge
of Zone 2, where their waste feeds the garden and compost system.

Rabbits are among the most space-efficient meat producers. They provide
meat, manure, and fur. Unlike other livestock, they can never be allowed to
roam gardens freely because they will eat virtually everything and multiply
uncontrollably. Their housing consists of a hutch with a mesh floor so
droppings fall through into a worm bin below, eliminating cleanup entirely
while producing vermicompost. Meat breeds such as New Zealand (the most
common and efficient), California (large, fast-growing, and calm), and
Champagne d'Argent (excellent meat-to-bone ratio) reach processing weight
in ten to twelve weeks. With intensive breeding — a doe bred every six
weeks and weaned at five weeks — a single breeding pair can produce three
hundred pounds of meat per year. In winter, rabbits housed in the
greenhouse provide both warmth and manure for the garden.

Pigeons are easy keepers that produce squabs (young pigeons, considered a
gourmet meat) and eggs. A breeding pair produces approximately twelve
squabs per year. They mate for life, need minimal care, and can fly free
during the day to forage on seeds and grain from the garden, fertilizing
everything before returning to the coop at night. A walk-in coop of at
least six by eight feet, seven feet tall, with elevated nesting boxes
attached to the walls, accommodates three or more breeding pairs.

Quail require very little space and mature rapidly. Coturnix quail reach
egg-laying age at six weeks and can be processed for meat at eight to ten
weeks. They are efficient converters of feed to protein and are well-suited
to small urban systems where even a few chickens would be impractical.

## Korean Natural Farming Poultry Methods

Dr. Cho Han-Kyu's Korean Natural Farming system offers a radically different
approach to poultry management that eliminates chemical feed, artificial
heating, and manual waste removal. The system produces healthy chickens and
eggs while generating fertilizer for crops and rehabilitating the
environment, and it challenges many assumptions embedded in conventional
poultry care.

The foundation is the soil floor. Cho insists that the floor must be
soil-based, not concrete, because separating chickens from soil causes
disease. Direct soil contact allows natural behaviours — dusting, pecking,
scratching — and exposure to diverse microorganisms that support immune
function. The floor is carpeted with rice straw chopped to three
centimetres (seven centimetres thick for layers, three for broilers) and
treated with indigenous microorganisms collected from the local
environment. Water is sprayed one to two times weekly during dry periods.

The most remarkable feature is the approach to waste. Chicken faeces are
not removed unless needed for compost. Microorganisms on the living floor
break down faeces continuously, resulting in zero odour, zero waste removal,
and the production of valuable compost. The decomposing floor also generates
heat that supplements the chickens' environment during cooler months. The
chickens themselves feed on the fermented floor products, closing the
nutrient loop entirely. This living floor system simultaneously serves as
feed producer, fertilizer factory, and waste treatment plant.

Cho's feeding protocol begins with whole brown rice grains and bamboo
leaves for hatchlings rather than commercial starter feed, arguing that
soft, over-nutritious commercial feed prevents chicks from developing
strong digestive organs. Fresh green grass comprises approximately one-third
of adult feed, with floor fermented products making up seven to ten percent.
Feed is given once daily, two hours before sunset. Any material except
mineral matter can serve as feed, making the system highly adaptable to
local conditions.

## Feeding and Nutrition

One of the most compelling aspects of integrated animal systems is the
potential to dramatically reduce or eliminate dependence on purchased feed.
Free-ranging chickens obtain twenty to fifty percent of their diet from
foraging, depending on the season, breed, and available habitat. In a
well-designed permaculture system with dedicated forage areas, food forest
drop, and kitchen scrap cycling, the proportion of self-produced feed can
be much higher.

Forage plants for poultry include a remarkably diverse list: alfalfa,
amaranth, autumn olive, barley, buckwheat, chickweed, chicory, clover,
comfrey, corn, cucumber, currant, dandelion, elderberry, fava beans, fennel,
fruit and nut trees, honey locust, oak, oats, quinoa, Russian olive, rye,
Siberian pea shrub, stinging nettle, sunflower, Swiss chard, vetch, and
wheat. A dedicated poultry forage paddock planted with a mix of these
species provides significant nutrition during the growing season.

Homemade poultry feed can be formulated to meet nutritional requirements
while using on-farm ingredients. For chicks, a mixture of thirty percent
grain (finely ground wheat with some corn and oats), twenty percent protein
(fish meal, meat meal, yogurt, cottage cheese, worms, bugs, and grubs), and
fifty percent greens (alfalfa meal, alfalfa leaves, or fresh chopped
lettuce) provides balanced nutrition. Adults need less protein — fifteen
percent versus twenty percent for chicks — and more greens at fifty-five
percent of the total ration. Supplements include wheat germ, sunflower
seeds, linseed meal, sand for grit, and ground shells from mussels, snails,
oysters, or eggshells for calcium.

Black soldier fly larvae represent one of the most promising innovations
in homestead-scale poultry feed production. Black soldier fly colonies
consume food scraps and manure, converting waste into high-quality insect
protein that chickens relish. A well-managed colony can produce enough
larvae to substantially supplement a small flock's protein needs while
processing organic waste that would otherwise require composting or
disposal.

## Housing and Flock Management

A permaculture coop differs from conventional designs in several important
ways. It is typically set on stilts so that manure drops through slats in
the floor, either into a swale for direct distribution or into a waiting
wheelbarrow for composting. Inside, roosting poles are placed at least
eighteen inches from the wall and low enough for chickens to fly up to.
Nesting boxes — approximately thirty by thirty centimetres, one box for
every two hens — can be attached to the outside of the coop with a lid,
allowing egg collection without entering the coop and disturbing the flock.

The minimum space requirement is four square feet per bird in the coop and
ten square feet in the run. Deep bedding systems start with at least six
inches of carbon material — straw, leaves, wood chips — with additional
material added regularly. The bedding is cleaned out annually, yielding a
large volume of rich compost. In winter, deep bedding generates heat through
microbial decomposition, reducing the need for artificial heating. Clean
water, grit, and oyster shell for calcium must always be available.

Winter management requires particular attention in cold climates. Chickens
need protection from wind and wet more than from cold. A light on a timer
providing fourteen to sixteen hours of total light maintains winter egg
production, as hens naturally reduce laying when daylight hours decrease.
Sprouted grains and fermented feed improve winter nutrition. Feed stores
should be built up in autumn in anticipation of reduced foraging. The deep
bedding system provides warmth, and a well-insulated coop with adequate
ventilation (but not drafts) keeps the flock comfortable through cold snaps.

Health management in integrated systems is largely preventive. Disinfecting
the coop before introducing new birds, keeping bedding clean and dry,
providing fresh water daily, separating old birds from young to prevent
disease transmission, and maintaining adequate space and nutrition
addresses most common health problems. Cannibalism is prevented by
adequate space and nutrition rather than debeaking. A grit supply and
cleanliness prevent worms and parasites. Chickens need vitamin D, and
in northern winters where sunlight is scarce, cod liver oil may be
necessary.

## Integration with Garden Cycles

The power of integrated animal systems emerges when livestock management is
synchronized with the garden's annual cycle. Before planting, chickens run
on beds for two to four weeks, clearing residual crops, eating weed seeds,
and depositing manure. A two to three week rest period follows, allowing the
manure to mellow before planting. During the growing season, portable
electric fencing restricts chickens to specific areas — paths, borders, and
designated forage zones — without giving them access to actively growing
vegetable beds. After harvest, chickens clean up crop residues, eat remaining
vegetables and weed seeds, and fertilize the soil for the next season.

In orchards, the chicken-orchard integration is one of the most productive
and low-maintenance combinations in permaculture. Chickens eat fallen fruit
(reducing disease carryover between seasons), control insect pests, eat weed
seeds, and fertilize fruit trees with their manure. Chicken tractors that
fit between tree rows allow targeted deployment without over-concentrating
manure around tree trunks. In young orchards, only bantams or very small
chickens should be allowed to forage, as larger birds can damage young
trees. As the orchard matures, a stocking rate of fifty to one hundred
chickens per acre is appropriate.

The overall goal is a system where animals do the work that would otherwise
require human labour and fossil fuel energy. Chickens spread compost by
scratching. Pigs turn piles by rooting. Geese mow grass. Ducks eat pests.
The more functions each animal performs, and the more functions are served by
multiple animals, the more resilient and self-maintaining the system becomes.
