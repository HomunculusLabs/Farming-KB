## Overview

In a well-designed homestead, animals are not separate from the garden — they are woven into every zone. Chickens scratch and fertilize garden beds while ducks patrol orchards for slugs and geese clip grass beneath fruit trees.
Rabbits generate rich manure for compost and quail produce eggs in minimal space. Pigeons forage independently across wilder areas of the property. Each species fills a different ecological niche.
The permaculture principle of "integrating rather than segregating" finds its most direct expression in small livestock. Every animal performs multiple functions, and every function is supported by multiple elements.
This article covers the design and management of integrated animal systems on the homestead scale, extending from chickens as the cornerstone species to ducks, geese, rabbits, quail, and pigeons.

## The Permaculture Principle of Animal Integration

The industrial model isolates livestock in massive facilities, feeds them grain from distant fields, and treats manure as waste. The homestead model inverts this entirely — animals live where their services are needed.
Manure becomes fertilizer. Foraging replaces pesticides and herbicides. Each animal element serves multiple functions simultaneously, reducing external inputs while producing food and fertility.
Mollison and Holmgren articulated this from the earliest days: a chicken is a soil builder, pest controller, compost turner, weed manager, and source of meat and feathers — not just an egg machine.
Cycling wastes through animals captures more stored energy than direct composting alone. Feeding kitchen scraps to a hen produces meat and eggs plus nearly as much fertilizer as composting would yield.
A duck is a slug-hunting patrol that protects lettuce and strawberries without chemical input. A goose is a grass clipper, fertilizer spreader, and watchdog all at once.

## Chickens — The Cornerstone Species

Chickens are the most versatile homestead poultry. Their scratching cultivates soil, replacing tractor work. Their insect foraging replaces toxic insecticides. Their manure replaces purchased fertilizers.
Mollison observed that a fowl performs all the functions of a tractor simultaneously, at no energy cost, while producing food. A flock of 50-60 birds prepares seedbeds and controls pests in a single pass.
Hens begin laying at 18-22 weeks with peak production in years one and two, declining 15-20 percent annually thereafter. With 14-16 hours of light, hens maintain year-round laying.
Egg color does not affect nutrition or taste — it is purely a breed characteristic. Brown, white, blue, and green eggs are nutritionally identical.
Chickens in forage systems show remarkable longevity. Mollison reported birds laying until death at six years, often passing at roost having laid the day before.

## Chicken Breeds for the Homestead

Breed selection depends on climate, space, and purpose. Dual-purpose breeds suit most homesteads because they provide eggs and meat while maintaining hardiness and foraging ability.
Egg specialists include Leghorns (280-320 white eggs, flighty but efficient) and Rhode Island Reds (250-300 brown eggs, hardy and adaptable). Sussex are calm foragers producing 250+ brown eggs.
Heavy dual-purpose breeds include Brahmas (150-200 brown eggs, 10-12 lb, gentle giants) and Orpingtons (200-280 brown eggs, cold-hardy and broody). These breeds also produce substantial meat.
Bantams suit small spaces — Silkies produce 100-120 tiny eggs and make excellent broody mothers for hatching other species' eggs. Their calm temperament makes them ideal for families with children.
For seasonal production, Mollison recommended Dorkings for winter, heavy breeds for spring, Leghorns for summer, and Wyandottes or Silkies for brooding duties throughout the year.

## Coop Design and Housing

Good housing balances predator protection, ventilation, and comfort. Allow 3-4 square feet per bird inside and 8-10 square feet in outside runs, with free-range access as the ideal.
Nesting boxes should be 12x12x12 inches, one per 3-4 hens, in a dark quiet corner. Roosts need 8-10 inches per bird at 2-4 feet height using 2-inch rounded bars for comfortable gripping.
Ventilation is critical for respiratory health — provide openings near the roofline above roost height with cross-ventilation if possible. Never seal the coop completely in winter.
Moisture control matters more than heat. Ammonia from accumulated droppings damages respiratory tracts long before cold becomes problematic for the birds.
In Korean Natural Farming, the chicken house uses deep bedding inoculated with indigenous microorganisms. Lactobacillus cultures sprayed regularly control odor and accelerate decomposition.

## Chicken Tractor Systems

A chicken tractor is a portable, floorless enclosure that houses chickens over a patch of ground. Mollison championed tractors as stacking functions — cultivating, fertilizing, and debugging simultaneously.
A standard tractor holds 8 layers or 16 broilers in a 4x8 foot frame with wire mesh, a weatherproof roof section, wheels or skids, and hanging feed and water containers.
Seasonal positioning follows the garden calendar: spring over future garden beds, summer through orchard alleys, autumn over harvested beds, winter near compost piles.
The concept extends beyond chickens. Duck tractors work in orchards for slug control. Rabbit tractors deposit manure precisely. Guinea fowl tractors control ticks along field edges.
Hemenway describes Jerome Osentowski's Colorado greenhouse where chickens provide body heat, manure, and CO₂ for a passive solar greenhouse — tractor principles applied to a permanent structure.


## Day-Range and Free-Range Systems

Day-ranging releases poultry from a secure night house to forage freely during daylight, then closes them in at dusk for predator protection. This combines the best of free-range and confined management.
Unlike tractors, day-ranged birds choose their own foraging areas. They naturally seek out the richest food — compost piles, recently turned soil, and fallen fruit in the orchard understory.
Stocking density for day-range is lower: 50-100 birds per acre of range. Overstocking leads to bare ground, parasite buildup, and damage to desirable vegetation across the range.
Fencing can be permanent or portable. Five-strand electric fencing at 4-6 inch spacing excludes ground predators while allowing birds to range freely within the defined boundary.
The night house should be positioned centrally within the range area. Birds naturally return to roost at dusk, making the evening lock-up routine straightforward and reliable for the caretaker.
Day-range systems suit properties too large for tractor management but too small for full pasture operations. They require less daily labour while providing similar foraging benefits.

## Feed and Forage for Poultry

Mollison emphasised that chickens are omnivores adapted to scratching for insects and seeds, not grazers. Designing insect production systems reduces or eliminates the need for grain supplements.
Compost piles teem with black soldier fly larvae, earthworms, and invertebrates. Chickens with compost access obtain 30-50 percent of protein from insects alone.
Deep mulch under fruit trees harbours beetles and slugs; scratching accelerates decomposition and pest removal. Fruit fallen from trees provides carbohydrate energy while breaking pest cycles.
Concentrated feed follows stage of life: starter (20-24% protein, 0-8 weeks), grower (15-18%, 8-18 weeks), and layer (16%, 18+ weeks). Provide free-choice oyster shell and grit once laying begins.
Scratch grains should be limited to 10 percent of diet as treats. Water must be clean and fresh — 1-2 cups per bird daily, with apple cider vinegar (1 tbsp/gal) added weekly for gut health.

## Poultry Forage — Trees and Shrubs

Mollison compiled one of the most comprehensive poultry forage species lists in permaculture literature, spanning trees, shrubs, vines, herbaceous plants, and root crops.
He valued mulberry (Morus alba) at 17 percent protein — equal to a double crop of grain. Large cuttings of 1.5 inch diameter root in autumn and produce full trees the following year.
Leguminous trees provide critical summer seed and pod forage: black locust for high-protein pods, honey locust for edible pods and timber, carob for 68% sugar content, mesquite for drought resistance.
For winter nut storage, black walnut and Persian walnut keep 12 months. Oak acorns of almost all species are edible for poultry — they were recommended in UK wartime rations.
Elderberries, mulberries, and Lycium species provide berries from late summer through winter. Mollison noted poultry can survive all year on just three Coprosma species in New Zealand.

## Poultry Forage — Herbaceous Plants and Roots

Perennial herbaceous forage sustains poultry through drought. Comfrey provides protein and minerals when grasses fail. Chicory's deep taproot keeps it green through dry spells.
Clovers are critical — white clover for direct grazing, lucerne for high-protein hay. Mollison recommended seeding pastures with 10-15 species including grasses, legumes, and forbs.
Root crops extend winter forage dramatically. Jerusalem artichoke produces tubers chickens root up. On a half-acre, Mollison calculated 5,000 pounds of forage at higher protein value than wheat.
Nut-grass and sour-grass yield shallow tubers utilized by birds — pest species in gardens but valuable poultry forage. Chickens also husk and thresh grain sheaves hung above their range.
This labour-saving strategy eliminates the need for threshing and bagging equipment, letting the birds process grain directly from sheaf to stomach.

## Flock Behavior and Social Dynamics

Chickens establish a pecking order within days. Ideal flock size is 50-60 birds with about five roosters to prevent wandering. Four 60-bird flocks of different breeds run on one acre.
Introducing new birds requires care: quarantine two weeks, then integrate at night by placing newcomers on the roost. Existing birds wake to find strangers present, reducing territorial aggression.
Roosters serve multiple functions beyond fertilization: alerting hens to food, warning of aerial predators, and breaking up fights. Aggressive roosters should be culled promptly.
For seasonal egg production, maintain different breed flocks selected for their laying peaks: heavy breeds for winter and spring, light breeds for summer, Silkies for brooding.
Understanding these dynamics prevents most behavioral problems and allows the designer to work with the birds' natural instincts rather than against them.


## Urban and Suburban Poultry

Keeping poultry in urban settings is feasible with careful design. Bantam chickens, quail, and rabbits suit small lots where space and noise are constraints on animal husbandry.
Many municipalities allow 4-6 hens without roosters. Ducks are often permitted where chickens are not, since they are quieter and produce fewer complaints from nearby neighbours.
In small spaces, vertical integration maximizes production: hanging grain sheaves for forage, stacked rabbit hutches over worm bins, and chicken tractors rotating through raised beds.
Manure management is critical at small scale. Deep bedding in a compact coop processes manure into compost without odor. A two-bird flock produces enough fertility for a substantial vegetable garden.
Neighbour relations matter: share eggs regularly, keep coops clean, and choose quiet breeds. Muscovy ducks and bantam chickens are least likely to generate noise complaints.
Quail are the ideal urban poultry — virtually silent, producing eggs in cages the size of a bookshelf, and processing kitchen waste into protein with minimal space or maintenance requirements.

## Ducks — Orchard Guardians

Ducks are exceptional slug hunters and fly catchers, invaluable in orchards and moist gardens. They forage aggressively near wet areas and are gentler on plants than scratching chickens.
Indian runner ducks are the classic orchard duck — upright, active foragers patrolling systematically between crop rows. Muscovy ducks are heavier, excellent fly catchers, and quiet for suburbs.
Ducks eat snails carrying liver fluke, protecting sheep and other grazing livestock on shared pasture. In integrated systems they follow sheep through paddocks cleaning up parasites.
For water, ducks need only a child's wading pool or shallow pond. They do not require swimming water to stay healthy, but head-dipping access for cleaning nostrils is essential.
Holzer values naturally hatched ducks on the Krameterhof: mallards, Indian runners, crested ducks, and Muscovies, all adapting to local conditions through natural selection over generations.

## Geese — Living Lawn Mowers

Geese convert grass directly into manure and meat without soil damage from heavier foragers. Mollison valued them as the most efficient grass converters among poultry species.
In orchards they clip short swards beneath fruit trees, preventing invasive grasses that weaken trees through root competition. Their droppings concentrate fertility where vegetation was consumed.
Geese thrive in arid areas with adequate food and water. The main limitation is not climate but denudation by inappropriate livestock — geese offer a lighter-touch alternative.
A few geese maintain grass beneath an entire orchard while serving as territorial watchdogs alerting the farm to intruders. They produce meat, fat, and feathers while performing vegetation management.

## Rabbits, Quail, and Other Small Livestock

Rabbits generate some of the richest manure available — high in nitrogen and phosphorus, applied directly to beds without composting and never burning plants. Rabbit tractors deposit manure precisely.
Quail are the most space-efficient poultry, producing eggs and meat in minimal area. Mollison noted they yield most of the year if age classes are kept mixed — ideal for urban settings.
Pigeons raised in secure elevated lofts are largely immune from predation and forage independently across wilder areas. They require minimal management once established.
Guinea fowl are outstanding tick hunters, valuable in Lyme disease areas. They are rangy and semi-wild, best suited to larger properties where their ranging habit is an asset.
All these smaller species combine with chickens and ducks in diversified systems, each filling a different ecological niche while contributing unique products and services to the homestead.

## Poultry-Orchard Integration

Integrating poultry into orchards is one of the most mutually beneficial permaculture strategies. Chickens and ducks range through tree rows, controlling weeds and pests while fertilizing soil.
Before planting, release poultry to dig out slugs and clean the ground. They consume slugs enthusiastically but leave most seeds undisturbed — preparing the site for subsequent planting.
Chickens with plum and cherry trees sharply reduce crawling and flying pests, including soil-dwelling species that re-emerge annually without intervention.
Dead animals hung in the run produce larvae that drop and are eaten before hatching — eliminating fly problems while providing high-protein feed from what was a disposal problem.
The Faires system places the coop on the Zone 1-2 border with runs divided into succession-planted pens. Each pen includes a flip-log revealing pill bugs and worms when turned over.

## Rotational Grazing and Paddock Systems

Rotational grazing prevents overstocking, parasite buildup, and pasture degradation. Mollison advocated paddocks of 200-500 square metres with 2-4 week rotation cycles.
Allow 30+ days rest between rotations for pasture recovery and parasite cycle interruption. Use portable electric fencing for flexible boundaries and mobile housing in each paddock.
Follow poultry with cattle, sheep, or pigs to harvest remaining forage and break parasite cycles through cross-species grazing — one of the most effective parasite management strategies.
On the Krameterhof, Holzer manages all land as a paddock system where livestock stay out year-round. Animals with fulfilled natural needs remain healthy without supplementary feeding.
The system moves in a circle, making additional feeding unnecessary because the animals work for themselves, harvesting their own food while fertilizing the land they graze.

## Korean Natural Farming for Poultry

Dr. Cho Han-Kyu's KNF offers a complete alternative to conventional management using indigenous microorganisms, fermented plant extracts, and local feed — no antibiotics or commercial feed required.
The KNF chicken house uses deep bedding inoculated with IMO cultures. Lactobacillus improves digestion and gut health. Fermented plant extract and mixed compost provide nutritional supplements.
A key insight: any material except mineral matter can serve as feed. Whole brown rice replaces commercial starter. Bamboo leaves follow after days. Rice husk reaches 25% of feed by six months.
KNF housing emphasizes natural ventilation, deep bedding, and odor elimination through microbial management. The result is housing close to the home without nuisance, supporting Zone 1-2 integration.
This approach dramatically reduces input costs while producing healthy birds — a complete system that replaces the entire conventional poultry management toolkit with biological processes.

## Health Management and Disease Prevention

Prevention is more effective and economical than treatment. The four pillars are biosecurity, nutrition, ventilation, and sanitation. Quarantine new birds for two weeks before flock introduction.
Coccidiosis is the most common young bird disease (3-8 weeks), caused by Eimeria protozoa with bloody diarrhea and lethargy. Medicated starter prevents outbreaks; herbal options include oregano oil and garlic.
Marek's disease is a herpesvirus with up to 80 percent mortality unvaccinated. Vaccination at hatch is the single most important preventive measure. The virus persists through feather dander.
Respiratory diseases demand good ventilation above all. Infectious bronchitis and Newcastle disease spread faster in damp, ammonia-laden conditions. Never sacrifice air quality for warmth.
External parasites include mites and lice — check vent areas and under wings regularly. Treat with diatomaceous earth or permethrin. Internal parasites require periodic deworming in free-range flocks.

## Predator Protection Strategies

Predation is the most common cause of flock loss. Different predators require different defenses, and a layered approach provides the best protection for the homestead flock.
Aerial predators (hawks, owls) need covered runs with hardware cloth or netting overhead. Digging predators (foxes, coyotes) require buried hardware cloth 12-18 inches or a 2-foot wire apron.
Climbing predators (raccoons, mink) demand hardware cloth rather than chicken wire plus carabiner latches on all doors. Lock birds in every night at dusk — most attacks occur at dawn and dusk.
Motion-activated lights near the coop deter nocturnal predators. Guardian dogs or geese provide an additional behavioral deterrent layer for larger properties.
Holzer uses thorny hedge barriers as living fencing: multiflora rose and dog rose create dense borders that shelter from aerial attack while providing rose hips as supplementary food for the flock.

## Nutrient Cycling and Soil Building

Poultry manure is one of the richest available — approximately 1.5 percent nitrogen plus phosphorus and potassium. Fresh chicken manure must be composted before use on growing plants.
The most efficient cycling happens when birds work manure directly into soil through tractor systems that concentrate birds on specific areas for 1-2 weeks of intensive scratching.
In orchards, poultry convert fallen fruit into fertilizer, breaking pest cycles while recycling nutrients. A single laying hen produces approximately 40 pounds of manure per year.
Rabbit manure applies directly without composting since it will not burn plants. Combined with poultry manure in compost, the two create balanced fertility for the entire garden.
Deep bedding in coops acts as slow-release compost: birds scratch manure into carbon-rich bedding where thermophilic decomposition proceeds gradually, producing humus-rich finished material.


## Poultry Manure Composting Methods

Fresh poultry manure is too hot for direct garden application due to high nitrogen. Composting transforms it into safe, stable fertility that builds soil rather than burning tender plants.
The simplest method is deep bedding: add carbon material continuously to the coop floor. Birds scratch and mix manure with carbon. Remove the entire bedding mass every 6-12 months as finished compost.
Hot composting at C:N ratio of 25-30:1 reaches thermophilic temperatures (130-150°F) that kill pathogens and weed seeds. Turn the pile when temperatures drop below 120°F for best results.
Composting takes 6-8 weeks in active hot piles or 6-12 months in passive deep bedding. The finished product is dark, crumbly humus with balanced nutrients and a pleasant earthy smell.
Vermicomposting poultry manure produces the highest-quality compost. Pre-compost for 2 weeks to reduce heat, then add worms. The vermicompost is rich in plant-available nutrients and beneficial microbes.
A single hen's annual manure output of 40 pounds becomes approximately 120-160 pounds of finished compost when mixed with carbon bedding — enough to fertilize 50-100 square feet of intensive garden beds.

## Water Systems for Poultry

Clean water is the most critical resource for poultry health. Hens need 1-2 cups daily; ducks require more in heat. Contaminated water spreads disease rapidly through the entire flock.
Gravity-fed systems from rainwater tanks reduce daily labour. Nipple drinkers keep water clean and prevent spillage. For ducks, shallow troughs allow head-dipping essential for respiratory health.
In winter, prevent freezing with heated bowls or submersible heaters. Moving water freezes less readily than still, so simple recirculating systems help in cold climates.
Enrich weekly with apple cider vinegar (1 tbsp/gal) for gut health or add electrolytes during heat stress. Avoid medicating water unless treating a specific diagnosed condition.
Plan water access in every paddock and tractor location. Multiple stations reduce competition and ensure all birds drink adequately, especially in large or multi-species flocks.

## Breeding and Incubation

Breeding your own poultry closes the production loop entirely. Naturally hatched birds retain maternal instincts and reliable breeding pairs establish through natural selection over generations.
Silkie bantams and Wyandottes are excellent broody breeds. A single Silkie can hatch and raise other breeds' chicks, functioning as a living incubator that eliminates need for equipment.
For incubator use, maintain 99.5°F and 50-55% humidity for 18 days, then 65% for the final three. Turn eggs three times daily until day 18. Candle at day 7 and 14 to remove non-viable eggs.
Holzer strongly prefers natural hatching. Birds that fail to brood are accepted as genetic selection while successful mothers balance losses. Over generations the flock adapts to local conditions.
This approach produces stock uniquely suited to the specific site — hardier, more self-reliant, and better adapted than any commercially purchased birds could be.


## Egg Collection and Storage

Collect eggs at least twice daily — morning and evening — to ensure freshness and reduce breakage or predation risk. More frequent collection in extreme heat prevents spoilage and quality loss.
Fresh unwashed eggs store at room temperature for up to two weeks in moderate climates. The natural bloom (cuticle) on the shell protects against bacteria. Washing removes this protective layer.
For longer storage, refrigerate at 40°F (4°C) where quality maintains for 3-5 months. Point the narrow end down to keep the yolk centered. Date-stamp cartons for proper rotation.
Water glassing (sodium silicate) preserves eggs for 6-12 months without refrigeration. Submerge clean unwashed eggs in a solution of one part water glass to ten parts water in a sealed container.
Freezing eggs extends storage indefinitely. Crack and scramble whole eggs, then freeze in ice cube trays (one egg per cube) or labelled freezer bags with the egg count noted on each bag.
Pickled eggs keep for 3-4 months refrigerated. Use a basic brine of vinegar, salt, and spices. This traditional preservation method produces a protein-rich food that requires no cooking to enjoy.

## Seasonal Management Calendar

Spring: position tractors over future garden beds. Release ducks into the orchard to clean overwintering slugs before new growth. Start chicks on deep bedding under heat lamps or broody hens.
Summer: rotate through orchard alleys and garden paths every 1-2 weeks. Geese clip grass beneath fruit trees. Quail production peaks. Provide extra water and shade during heat waves.
Autumn: position tractors over harvested beds to clean residues. Collect and store acorns, walnuts, and chestnuts for winter feed. Cull older hens below economic production levels.
Winter: concentrate birds near compost and protected areas. Supplement light to 14-16 hours for continued egg production. Feed stored nuts and acorns. Ensure water does not freeze.
In milder climates, tractors continue across fallow ground through winter. Deep bedding in coops provides insulation while generating compost for spring application.

## Economics and Small-Scale Production

Mollison calculated 400 chickens as break-even for pasture operations; 300 is workable. Ideal flock size is 50-60 birds. Four 60-bird flocks of different breeds run productively on one acre.
A well-managed forage system reduces purchased feed by 30-50 percent by designing so animals harvest their own food rather than requiring daily feeding from purchased bags.
Beyond direct products, poultry provides economic value through pest control, fertilization, and soil preparation. These indirect values often exceed the direct value of eggs and meat.
Pastured eggs command 2-3 times supermarket prices. Direct marketing through farm-gate sales and farmers' markets captures retail rather than wholesale margins.
A diversified operation selling eggs, meat birds, and breeding stock across multiple channels provides the most resilient income stream for small-scale producers.


## Record-Keeping and Flock Performance

Track egg production daily per flock to identify declining performance early. A simple wall chart or notebook records eggs collected, feed consumed, and any health observations worth noting.
Monitor feed conversion ratio (pounds of feed per dozen eggs) to assess forage system effectiveness. Well-designed forage systems produce eggs at 2-3 lbs feed per dozen versus 4+ lbs with no forage.
Weigh a sample of birds monthly to track growth and body condition. Rapid weight loss signals disease, inadequate feed, or predator stress requiring immediate investigation and response.
Annual flock performance review informs next season's breeding and culling decisions. Replace underperforming breeds and expand those showing the best adaptation to local conditions.
Good records also support the economics of the operation by revealing true costs of production per dozen eggs or per pound of meat, enabling informed pricing and marketing decisions for the small producer.

## Designing Your Integrated Animal System

Begin with zone planning. Position poultry housing on the Zone 1-2 edge — close for daily care but far enough to prevent odor. Connect the coop to garden and orchard through rotating paddocks.
Start with chickens as the foundational species. Add ducks for orchard areas, geese for grass management, rabbits for concentrated manure, and quail for space-constrained areas.
Design for redundancy: every function supported by multiple elements, every element serving multiple functions. A hedgerow provides windbreak, predator protection, forage, and bee habitat.
A pond provides water, duck habitat, irrigation reserves, and fire protection. A compost system accepts animal manure and produces feed (insects) while generating fertility for gardens.
Observe and adapt over years. Mollison's advice: "The problem is the solution." A chicken destroying mulch signals a redesign need — perhaps wire barriers or timed access rather than continuous ranging.

## Summary

Integrated animal systems are among the most powerful permaculture design strategies. By positioning livestock where services are needed most, the homesteader eliminates external inputs of fertilizer and pesticide.
The concepts developed by Mollison, Hemenway, Cho, Holzer, and Faires cover breed selection, coop design, forage species, rotational grazing, health management, and economics in comprehensive detail.
The key principle throughout is integration: every animal serves multiple functions, every function is supported by multiple elements, and the system produces more than the sum of its parts.
A well-designed integrated animal system builds soil fertility, controls pests, produces protein, and creates resilience — all while reducing dependence on external inputs and purchased feeds.
