# Backyard Poultry and Integrated Animal Systems

In a well-designed homestead or small farm, animals are not separate from the
garden — they are woven into every zone, performing essential functions that
would otherwise require human labour, fossil fuels, or purchased inputs.
Chickens scratch and fertilize garden beds. Ducks patrol orchards for slugs.
Rabbits generate manure for compost. Goats clear brush and produce milk. The
permaculture principle of "integrating rather than segregating" finds perhaps
its most direct expression in the integration of small livestock into food
production systems.

The industrial model of animal agriculture isolates livestock in massive
concentrated facilities, feeds them grain grown on distant fields, and
treats their manure as a waste problem. The homestead model inverts this
relationship entirely. Animals live where their services are needed. Their
manure becomes fertilizer, not pollution. Their foraging replaces pesticides
and herbicides. Their presence converts low-value vegetation — weeds, insect
pests, kitchen scraps, fallen fruit — into high-value protein: eggs, meat,
and milk. The key insight, articulated by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren
from the earliest days of permaculture, is that every animal should perform
multiple functions, and every function should be supported by multiple
elements. A chicken is not just an egg machine. It is a soil builder, a pest
controller, a compost turner, a weed manager, and a source of meat and
feathers.

This article covers the design and management of integrated animal systems on
the homestead scale, with emphasis on chickens as the most versatile and
widely kept poultry species, but extending to ducks, rabbits, quail, pigeons,
and the principles that govern all successful [[mollison-animal-integration-permaculture-design]].

## The Philosophy of Animal Integration

The permaculture approach to animals begins with a fundamental question:
what functions do we need performed on this site, and which animals can
perform them? This is the opposite of the conventional approach, which asks:
what animals do I want to keep, and how do I house and feed them? In a
permaculture design, animals are introduced to serve specific ecological
functions, and their housing, feeding, and management are designed around
those functions.

David Holmgren articulated this as the principle of using "nature's
services" — the work that living organisms perform for free — to replace
energy-intensive technologies. His classic example is the [[comparison-chicken-tractor-vs-free-range]]:
the natural scratching action of a fowl cultivates soil instead of a tractor
or rotary hoe. At the same time as it cultivates, the chicken's foraging
for insects replaces toxic insecticides, and its manure replaces
energy-intensive fertilizers. The comparison reveals that the functions of
a tractor are better understood than the functions of a fowl, but the fowl
performs all of them simultaneously, at no energy cost, while producing food.

Bill Mollison placed animal integration within a broader framework of
[[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]]. In nature, nutrients cycle continuously through living
systems. On a conventional farm, nutrients are imported as fertilizer and
exported as produce, creating linear flows that deplete both the farm and the
environments where fertilizers are mined or manufactured. Animals are the
primary mechanism for closing nutrient loops on a homestead: they eat plant
material and kitchen scraps, concentrate nutrients in their manure, and
return those nutrients to the soil where they feed the next cycle of plant
growth.

The sequential introduction of animals reflects the maturation of the food
production system. Chickens can be introduced after one to three years, once
the garden and orchard are established enough to withstand their scratching.
Pigs may follow after three to seven years, when the food forest can
withstand rooting. Sheep and cattle require the most mature systems and
are introduced only after seven to fifteen years. This patience prevents
young plantings from being destroyed by animals before they can establish.

## Chickens: The Most Versatile Homestead Animal

Chickens are the gateway animal for most homesteaders, and for good reason.
They are relatively easy to keep, they produce eggs year-round, they convert
a wide variety of feed sources into protein, and they perform multiple
ecological functions simultaneously. A flock of chickens provides eggs, meat,
feathers, fertilizer, pest control, and weed management from a modest
investment of space and labour.
