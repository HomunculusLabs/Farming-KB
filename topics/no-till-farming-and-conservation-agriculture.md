# No-Till Farming and Conservation Agriculture

No-till farming is an agricultural system that avoids mechanically inverting
or aggressively disturbing the soil profile, instead relying on biological
processes, surface mulches, and carefully designed crop rotations to maintain
soil structure, fertility, and health. Rather than plowing, disking, or
rototilling the land before planting, no-till practitioners leave the soil
intact and introduce seeds through minimal surface disturbance methods such as
direct drilling, broadcasting into mulch, or using small hand tools to create
narrow planting slits. The approach represents a fundamental philosophical and
practical departure from thousands of years of conventional agriculture, one
that recognizes soil as a living ecosystem whose biological networks are
destroyed by mechanical disruption.

## Historical Development of Tillage and Its Critics

The practice of turning the soil before planting is ancient. The moldboard plow
allowed farmers to invert the topsoil completely, burying surface residues and
weeds while bringing subsoil to the surface. In the eighteenth century, the
English lawyer and farmer Jethro Tull (1674 to 1741) further codified intensive
tillage by inventing a mechanical seed drill and actively promoting the idea
that soil should be thoroughly loosened before planting. He had observed that
vegetables grew better in loosened soil and concluded, erroneously, that plant
roots possessed tiny mouths that consumed soil particles.

Believing that finer soil particles would be easier for roots to ingest, he
developed a horse-drawn hoe to break up the earth. His writings later caught
the attention of [[comparison-nutrient-cycling-vs-nutrient-dense-gardening]], is severely disrupted each time
the soil is turned. Populations of beneficial organisms are reduced, their
habitat is destroyed, and the ecological relationships that drive healthy soil
function are set back.

## The Soil Biology of No-Till Systems

The scientific basis for no-till farming rests on an understanding of soil as
a biological system rather than an inert substrate. In undisturbed soils,
fungal hyphae form extensive networks that connect plant roots to distant
nutrient sources, particularly phosphorus and micronutrients that are otherwise
inaccessible. Mycorrhizal fungi extend the effective root zone of their host
plants by orders of magnitude and serve as conduits for [[fungal-ecosystem-processes-overview]], described the three
primary roles that fungi play in soil: as pathogens detrimental to plant
growth, as mycorrhizae that help plants obtain nutrients and provide defense,
and as endophytes that improve plant nutrition and defend against herbivory.
All three roles are disrupted by tillage. Pathogenic fungi may proliferate
briefly after tillage as their competitors are destroyed, creating disease
flushes that farmers then treat with fungicides, further damaging the soil
biology.

Earthworms are particularly vulnerable to tillage. Their burrows create
macropores that allow water to infiltrate rather than run off, and their casts
enrich the soil with available nutrients. Research has consistently shown that
earthworm populations increase under mulch and decrease under tillage. Bill
Mollison, in the Permaculture Designers Manual, cited extension trials in
Nigeria that recorded eleven percent better water infiltration on low-tillage
and mulched plots compared to conventionally tilled ones. Under mulch, soil
temperature extremes are buffered, remaining cooler by day and warmer at night,
conditions that favor both root growth and microbial activity.
