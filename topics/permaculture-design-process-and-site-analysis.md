# Permaculture Design Process and Site Analysis

Permaculture design is a systematic yet [[allegro-womans-part-in-the-creative-process]] for creating
regenerative landscapes that produce food, conserve resources, and build
ecological resilience. Unlike conventional landscaping or agriculture,
which typically imposes a predetermined layout onto a site, permaculture
design begins with deep observation of the land itself and works outward
from what the site reveals. The process draws on established frameworks
like SADIM and OBREDIMET, analytical tools such as zone and sector
mapping, and creative methods like [[hemenway-random-assembly-design-technique]] and [[holmgren-pattern-thinking-and-pattern-language]].
Whether applied to a suburban backyard or a broad-acre farm, the design
process follows the same fundamental sequence: observe thoroughly,
analyze rigorously, design holistically, implement in phases, and adapt
continuously. The result is a landscape where water, soil, plants,
animals, and human structures function as an integrated whole rather
than a collection of isolated elements.

The design process is not a rigid formula but a structured framework
that guides creative decision-making. Bill Mollison, who codified
permaculture with David Holmgren in the 1970s, emphasized that there

## The Ethics Foundation

Every permaculture design rests on three ethical pillars that serve as
the ultimate filter for all decisions. Earth Care means that the
design must enhance rather than degrade the ecological health of the
site. People Care requires that the design serve real human needs for
food, shelter, community, and meaningful livelihood. Fair Share
extends the ethic beyond the site boundary, recognizing that surplus
should be distributed and that no design should consume resources at
the expense of neighbors or future generations. These ethics are not
abstract ideals but practical evaluation criteria. When a designer
faces a choice between two placement options for a water tank, for
example, the ethics help determine which option better serves the
whole system.

The ethics connect directly to the design process because they prevent
the designer from optimizing one element at the expense of others. A
design that maximizes food production but degrades soil health fails
the Earth Care test. A design that is ecologically pristine but
produces nothing edible fails the People Care test. Keeping all three
ethics in view throughout the process ensures balanced outcomes. The
twelve design principles articulated by David Holmgren provide more
specific guidance within this ethical framework, covering everything
from catching and storing energy to using small and slow solutions.

## SADIM: The Core Framework

SADIM (Survey, Analyse, Design, Implement, Maintain) is the most
widely taught design process framework in permaculture. Its linear
structure makes it accessible for beginners while remaining flexible
enough for experienced designers to adapt. The Survey phase involves
gathering all available information about the site: physical
characteristics like topography, soil, and water; climate data
including temperature, rainfall, and wind patterns; existing
infrastructure such as buildings, fences, and utilities; and client
goals, values, budget, and timeline. Legal constraints like zoning
ordinances, easements, and water rights are researched during this
phase as well.

The Analyse phase transforms raw survey data into understanding. The
designer identifies sector energies (the ways that sun, wind, water,
fire, and other external forces move across the site), develops zone
placement logic based on frequency of use, and maps soil and water
conditions. Opportunities and constraints emerge from this analysis.
The Design phase uses the analytical understanding to create concept
plans, detailed planting schemes, and implementation schedules.
[[permaculture-principles]] are applied throughout, ensuring that each
element serves multiple functions and that elements support one another.

Implementation follows a specific priority sequence: earthworks and
water systems first, then infrastructure and access, then perennial
plantings, and finally annual crops and ground covers. This sequence
reflects the principle of designing from [[permaculture-principle-design-from-patterns-to-details]] and
ensures that foundational systems are in place before dependent
elements are added. The Maintain phase acknowledges that permaculture
systems are dynamic, not static. Monitoring, pruning, harvesting, and
adapting are ongoing responsibilities that continue for as long as
the system exists.

## OBREDIMET: An Expanded Process

OBREDIMET (Observe, Boundaries, Resources, Evaluate, Design,
Implement, Maintain, Evaluate and Tweak) extends the SADIM framework
with two important additions. The Observe phase emphasizes extended
engagement with the site across seasons. Where SADIM's Survey phase
can be completed relatively quickly, OBREDIMET's Observe phase
recommends a minimum of one full year of seasonal observation before
committing to major earthworks or permanent plantings. This longer
timeframe reveals patterns that a single-season survey would miss:
where frost pockets form, how water moves during different rainfall
intensities, which areas dry out first in summer, and how wildlife
uses the landscape.

The Boundaries phase asks the designer to define all the limits of
the project: physical boundaries (property lines, soil depth, water
availability), legal boundaries (zoning, building codes, covenants),
financial boundaries (budget, ongoing maintenance costs), temporal
boundaries (how quickly the design needs to be productive), and
personal boundaries (the skills, energy, and time available). Naming
these constraints early prevents the designer from creating plans that
look beautiful on paper but cannot be implemented.

The Resources phase inventories everything available to the project:
on-site materials like timber, stone, and soil; biological resources
like existing plants, seeds, and wildlife; human resources including
skills, labor, and community connections; and financial resources.
Crucially, this inventory distinguishes between resources that already
exist, resources that are readily available, and resources that would
need to be imported. A good design maximizes use of existing and
available resources while minimizing dependence on imported inputs.
