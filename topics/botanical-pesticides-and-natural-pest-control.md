# Botanical Pesticides and Natural Pest Control

Botanical pesticides are pest management materials derived from plants,
including oils, extracts, powders, fermented preparations, and volatile
repellents. In organic farming and gardening they are usually considered part of
natural pest control, a broader field that also includes soil health, sanitation,
mechanical barriers, traps, beneficial insects, trap crops, resistant varieties,
and microbial or fungal biocontrol agents. Their value lies not only in their
natural origin but in how narrowly and intelligently they can be integrated into
a living agroecosystem.

The topic bridges several traditions. Organic gardening sources emphasize daily
observation, hand removal, clean cultivation, companion planting, and the
maintenance of a diverse predator community. Permaculture sources frame pest
management as a design problem, asking how plant diversity, edge, habitat,
water, animals, and trap crops can keep herbivores below damaging levels.
Korean Natural Farming and JADAM add farm-made inputs, fermented attractants,
herbal extracts, soaps, wetting agents, sulfur, and microbial solutions that can
be prepared at low cost from local materials.

Modern horticultural examples, especially cannabis and greenhouse vegetable
production, show both the usefulness and the limits of botanical controls.
Neem, pyrethrum, insecticidal soap, hot pepper, garlic, citrus oils, and
diatomaceous earth can suppress aphids, mites, whiteflies, thrips, fungus
gnats, caterpillars, and other pests. Yet many of these materials also injure
beneficial insects, irritate workers, alter flavor near harvest, or fail when
applied at the wrong time, pH, humidity, or coverage.

Natural pest control therefore is not a rejection of intervention. It is a
hierarchy of interventions. The grower first prevents pest outbreaks by growing
healthy plants in biologically active soil, minimizing stress, rotating crops,
maintaining clean growing spaces, and attracting natural enemies. Direct sprays
or dusts are then used when monitoring shows that pest numbers are rising faster
than the ecosystem can contain them. This approach is commonly described as
integrated pest management, or IPM.

## Definition and scope

A botanical pesticide is commonly defined by origin rather than by toxicity. The
active compounds may be extracted from leaves, seeds, flowers, roots, bark, or
whole plants, and may act as repellents, antifeedants, growth regulators,
contact poisons, smothering oils, or physical barriers. Neem seed oil,
pyrethrum flower extract, hot pepper spray, garlic extract, wormwood tea,
cinnamon oil, citrus oil, and JADAM herb solution are all botanical in this
broad sense.

Natural pest control is broader than botanical pesticide use. It includes any
strategy that reduces pest damage without relying on synthetic broad-spectrum
pesticides as the central tool. It may be ecological, cultural, mechanical,
biological, microbial, or botanical. Yellow sticky cards, row covers, clean
compost handling, irrigation management, predator habitat, fungal pathogens of
insects, trap crops, diatomaceous earth, and beer traps for slugs all belong to
this wider category.

## Soil health and plant resistance

A recurring claim across the source traditions is that unhealthy plants are
more attractive to pests. Excess nitrogen, weak roots, poor mineral balance,
compaction, drought stress, inadequate aeration, immature compost, and damaged
microbial communities can all produce soft, lush, or physiologically stressed
growth. Such growth is easier for sucking insects, mites, fungi, and bacteria to
exploit.

Compost and humus are central because they provide slow nutrient release,
moisture buffering, cation exchange, and microbial diversity. Jeavons notes that
mature compost contains large microbial populations, including organisms that
produce antibiotics or suppress disease. KNF similarly relies on indigenous
microorganisms and lactic acid bacteria to occupy ecological niches that might
otherwise be taken by pathogens.

Balanced nutrition is also important. Calcium strengthens cell walls and is
frequently associated in natural farming systems with resistance to penetration
by insects and pathogens. Over-fertilization, especially with soluble nitrogen,
can create a flush of tender tissue. Coleman's spinach aphid example shows that
removing excess nitrogen can be more effective than killing aphids after they
arrive.

## Soaps, oils, and wetting agents

Insecticidal soaps and oil-based sprays are among the simplest natural controls.
Soap breaks down the waxy coating of soft-bodied insects such as aphids, mites,
whiteflies, thrips, and mealybugs, causing dehydration. Oils can smother eggs,
larvae, and adults by coating respiratory surfaces. Both require direct contact
and thorough coverage, especially on leaf undersides.

KNF-derived soap-water recipes use natural biodegradable soap, water, and often
a small amount of cooking oil to improve adhesion. Applications are made in the
early morning or evening to reduce leaf burn and are repeated after rain. Hot
pepper or garlic may be added as repellents, particularly against chewing
insects and some mammals.

JADAM wetting agent is a more formalized soap-like input made from canola oil,
potassium hydroxide, and water. It functions as a surfactant, allowing herbal
solutions and sulfur sprays to spread, wet pest bodies, and penetrate protective
surfaces. JADAM stresses the importance of soft water, since hard water can make
the wetting agent clot and lose effectiveness.

## Neem and azadirachtin

Neem products come from Azadirachta indica, a drought-tolerant tropical tree
long used in India and Southeast Asia for medicine, stored grain protection,
livestock care, and crop pest management. The principal insecticidal compound in
neem seed products is azadirachtin, although whole cold-pressed oil contains a
wider set of active and emulsifying constituents.

Neem acts mainly as an antifeedant, growth regulator, and reproduction
disruptor. Treated larvae may stop feeding, fail to molt, or fail to mature
into fertile adults. This makes neem slower than contact poisons but useful for
breaking pest life cycles. It is especially associated with control of spider
mites, fungus gnats, aphids, whiteflies, thrips, caterpillars, leafminers,
mealybugs, nematodes, and root maggots.

Cannabis sources distinguish neem powder from neem oil. Neem powder made from
leaves contains azadirachtin, nutrients, and trace elements, and is most useful
against young insects. Cold-pressed neem oil from seeds is preferred for spider
mites and broad foliar use. Heat-processed oil is considered weaker because
azadirachtin and associated compounds are degraded.

Application details matter. Neem mixtures are prepared shortly before use,
often in water below pH 7 with a spreader-sticker or emulsifier. The sprayer
must be agitated to keep oil suspended. Leaf undersides are targeted because
mites, whiteflies, and aphids often shelter there. In soil drenches, neem can
act systemically for a period after root uptake, helping with fungus gnat larvae
or root-feeding pests.

## Pyrethrum and other botanical insecticides

Pyrethrum is extracted from flowers of pyrethrum chrysanthemums. Its active
pyrethrins, cinerins, and jasmolins act as fast contact nerve poisons. In
horticulture it is valued for quick knockdown of aphids, whiteflies, spider
mites, and flying insects, but it is broad-spectrum and can kill beneficials as
well as pests.

Natural pyrethrum breaks down quickly in air and light, often within hours.
Cannabis guidance therefore recommends application just before lights are turned
off, with fans temporarily stopped when appropriate, to extend contact time.
Water below pH 7 and a spreader-sticker improve performance. Pyrethrum should
not be mixed with sulfur, lime, copper, soaps, or high-pH materials, which can
make it ineffective or increase plant injury.

Synthetic pyrethroids such as permethrin and cypermethrin are chemically
related but more persistent and more disruptive. They are generally treated
separately from botanical pyrethrum in organic systems because they are
broad-spectrum, non-selective, and more likely to select for resistant mites and
insects. Many sources reserve them, if used at all, for last-resort situations.

## Diatomaceous earth and mineral controls

Diatomaceous earth is not botanical, but it is commonly discussed with natural
pest control because it is a mineral material with mechanical action. It is made
of fossilized diatom shells rich in silica. The sharp microscopic particles
abrade waxy insect coatings and can damage digestive tracts when ingested,
leading to dehydration and death.

In cannabis cultivation, food-grade or agricultural-grade diatomaceous earth is
used as a soil amendment, topdressing, foliar dust, or perimeter barrier. It can
suppress fungus gnat larvae and adults, aphids, spider mites, slugs, snails,
ants, beetles, and ground stages of thrips. It may also add trace minerals and
improve some physical properties of soil mixes.

The safety distinction between food-grade and pool-grade material is critical.
Pool-grade diatomaceous earth has been heat-treated and may contain crystalline
silica that is hazardous if inhaled. Even food-grade material is a respiratory
irritant as dust, so masks, eye protection, calm conditions, and careful
application are needed.

## KNF preparations and behavioral traps

Korean Natural Farming pest control begins with plant vigor rather than direct
poisoning. Indigenous microorganisms, fermented plant juice, fish amino acid,
lactic acid bacteria, oriental herbal nutrient, brown rice vinegar, calcium, and
seawater are applied according to plant stage to support balanced growth. These
inputs are understood to make plants less attractive and more resistant.

Cho's aromatic insect attractant uses fermented liquids to exploit insect
behavior. Rice wine and diluted fermented plant or fruit juices are placed in
PET bottle traps at the height of fruits or leaves. Sweet fermented aromas draw
moths, butterflies, and other insects into the bottle, where they drown. Timing
is decisive; traps are installed during egg-laying periods before larvae are
already feeding in the crop.

The fluorescent insect attractant uses light reflected by a zinc sheet to draw
night-flying insects into a basin of water. A few drops of kerosene or similar
material break surface tension so insects drown. As with aromatic traps, height,
season, and reproductive stage of the crop determine effectiveness.

## JADAM natural pesticide system

JADAM natural pesticide is built around three core components: JADAM wetting
agent, JADAM sulfur, and JADAM herb solution. The wetting agent provides
coverage and penetration. The sulfur functions mainly as a germicide against
powdery mildew, downy mildew, canker, anthracnose, and other diseases. The herb
solution provides insect control and, according to JADAM, nutrient value.

JADAM herb solution is commonly made by boiling pesticidal plants in soft water.
Recommended plants include Jerusalem artichoke, ginkgo, Korean pasque flower,
red spider lily, pokeweed, water pepper, bracken, foxglove, tobacco, hot pepper,
garlic, mugwort, ginger, and neem. Strong aromatic materials may also be
extracted into oil. Stored solutions must remain sterile and clear; bottles that
inflate or ferment are considered to have lost potency.

The standard JADAM mixing order avoids clotting. Water is added first, then the
wetting agent is diluted into it, followed by sulfur if used, and then the herb
solution. Each ingredient is dispersed separately before the next is added. The
finished liquid is filtered to protect spray equipment. Concentrations are
adjusted by crop, weather, and pest severity rather than treated as universal.

## Beneficial insects and biological control

Beneficial insects are central to natural pest control because they provide
continuous, self-renewing suppression. Lady beetles consume aphids, scale, and
mealybugs. Lacewing larvae feed on aphids, thrips, whiteflies, and small
caterpillars. Hoverfly larvae can consume large numbers of aphids, while adults
need accessible pollen and nectar. Ground beetles hunt slugs, caterpillars, and
soil pests at night.

Parasitoids are often less visible but highly effective. Trichogramma wasps
attack eggs of moths and butterflies. Aphidius wasps parasitize aphids. Chalcid
wasps and Encarsia formosa attack whiteflies and scale insects. In cannabis and
greenhouse whitefly control, Encarsia formosa is released after toxic residues
have been washed from plants and repeated at intervals so that parasite
populations track the pest population.

Predatory mites, especially Phytoseiulus and other phytoseiids, are important
against spider mites in greenhouses, orchards, and indoor crops. Their success
requires suitable humidity, limited dust, and avoidance of sprays that kill
mites indiscriminately. Broad-spectrum botanicals can undermine these releases
if applied at the wrong time.

## Insectary plants and habitat design

Insectary plants supply nectar, pollen, shelter, overwintering sites, and
alternative prey for beneficial organisms. Small, shallow, open flowers are
especially valuable because many parasitoid wasps and hoverflies have short
mouthparts. Dill, fennel, coriander, yarrow, calendula, marigold, alyssum,
daisies, nasturtium, poppies, and Queen Anne's lace are common examples.

Habitat structure is as important as flower choice. Mulches, green manures,
perennial grasses, beetle banks, hedgerows, log piles, stone edges, wild areas,
water dishes, and ponds all provide refuge. A small uncultivated area can allow
predators to survive cultivation, harvest, and crop rotation. Without refuge,
beneficial insects may arrive too late to prevent pest outbreaks.

Companion planting works through several mechanisms. Some plants repel pests by
odor or root exudates, as with garlic, aromatic herbs, and French marigold
against some nematodes. Others attract natural enemies, as marigolds,
nasturtiums, and open flowers attract hoverflies. Mixed plantings can also
camouflage host plants from pests that locate food by sight.

## Trap crops, barriers, and physical methods

Trap crops are plants that attract pests more strongly than the main crop. They
may be planted around the perimeter, upwind of pest movement, or within the
crop. Once pests concentrate on the trap crop, the grower can hand-pick, vacuum,
remove the plant, release biological controls, or apply a targeted spray.

Common examples include nasturtium for aphids and cabbage white butterflies,
radish for flea beetles, chervil for slugs, Blue Hubbard squash for squash bugs
and vine borers, mustard for harlequin bugs, and marigold for nematode
management. Effectiveness depends on planting the trap crop early enough and
keeping its area small enough that it does not become a pest nursery.

Physical exclusion remains one of the least toxic forms of pest control.
Netting protects fruit and brassicas from birds or butterflies. Row covers
protect seedlings, cucurbits, melons, and other vulnerable transplants until
they are established. Fences, barriers, and fruit cages are often more reliable
against vertebrate pests than any spray.

## Fungal, bacterial, and microbial biocontrol

Microbial controls occupy a middle ground between living ecology and formulated
pesticide. Bacillus thuringiensis var. israelensis, or Bti, is used against
fungus gnat and fly larvae in mushroom production. It is mixed into substrate or
injected into problem areas rather than sprayed on mature mushrooms. Other Bt
strains are used against caterpillars in vegetable and cannabis systems.

Fungal biocontrol can be valuable against insects. The fungus Verticillium
lecanii, also known as Lecanicillium or Cephalosporium in older references, is
used against whiteflies and can complement Encarsia formosa. Entomopathogenic
fungi require suitable humidity and careful timing, so they are often better in
greenhouses than in dry open fields.

JADAM microbial solution and KNF lactic acid bacteria are not single-target
commercial biopesticides, but they are part of microbial pest and disease
management. They aim to occupy leaf, soil, and rhizosphere niches, support plant
health, and suppress pathogens through competition, organic acids, enzymes, and
microbial diversity.

## Crop examples

In cannabis, common targets include spider mites, aphids, whiteflies, thrips,
fungus gnats, caterpillars, powdery mildew, and root pests. Neem oil is widely
used in vegetative growth and early flowering but is avoided near harvest to
protect flavor and aroma. Pyrethrum may be used for quick knockdown, while
insecticidal soap and diatomaceous earth provide contact and mechanical control.
Beneficial mites, parasitoids, sticky traps, sanitation, and climate control
complete the IPM program.

Whitefly management illustrates integration. The grower monitors by shaking
branches and inspecting leaf undersides, then uses yellow sticky traps at canopy
height. Damaged leaves are removed. Neem, homemade sprays, or pyrethrum may be
applied at intervals when necessary. Encarsia formosa or Verticillium lecanii
can then provide biological control, but residues from toxic sprays must be
removed before parasitoids are released.

Mushroom farms require a different emphasis because the crop is a fungus and
fruitbodies absorb contaminants. Pest management centers on clean substrates,
clean rooms, isolated batches, timely harvest before gills spread, sticky traps,
vinegar and soap traps, Bti against larvae, and biological predators in fruiting
rooms. Direct pesticide sprays on mature mushrooms are avoided.

## Safety, residues, and environmental effects

Worker safety is a primary concern even with natural materials. Pyrethrum dusts
and aerosols can be hazardous when inhaled, and aerosol synergists such as
piperonyl butoxide may add toxicity. Neem oil can irritate eyes and skin.
Diatomaceous earth dust irritates lungs and eyes. Caustic materials used to make
JADAM wetting agent or sulfur require serious handling precautions during
preparation.

Crop safety is also important. Sprays can clog stomata, slow plant growth, burn
tender leaves, or increase humidity-related disease. Seedlings and unrooted
cuttings are more sensitive than established plants. Many sources recommend
spraying in early morning, evening, or just before lights-off, and testing a
small area before treating the whole crop.

Environmental safety includes pollinators and aquatic organisms. Many botanical
insecticides are toxic to fish, aquatic invertebrates, bees, parasitoids, and
predatory mites. They should not be sprayed into ponds, onto open flowers, or
across insectary strips without need. The safest pesticide is often the one not
used because monitoring showed that predators were already controlling the pest.

## Resistance and rotation

Resistance develops when pest populations are repeatedly exposed to the same
mode of action. Neem has multiple effects but still should not be the sole tool
used over many generations. Pyrethrum and pyrethroids can select resistant
insects and mites, especially where sublethal doses allow survivors to recover.
Even soaps and oils can become less effective if coverage is poor and only
protected individuals survive.

Rotation reduces this risk. A grower might combine sanitation, pruning,
beneficial insects, soap, neem, microbial control, trap crops, and occasional
pyrethrum rather than spraying neem every few days indefinitely. JADAM recipes
also recommend varying herbal solutions. Diatomaceous earth offers a mechanical
mode of action that does not create biochemical resistance, but it still must be
used carefully to avoid harming beneficials.

## Integration into IPM

Integrated pest management begins with identification. The grower must know
whether the problem is aphids, whiteflies, spider mites, thrips, fungus gnats,
slugs, caterpillars, mites, molds, bacteria, or nutrient stress mimicking pest
damage. Treatment without identification often kills beneficial organisms while
leaving the actual cause unchanged.

The second step is prevention: soil health, compost, balanced fertility, crop
rotation, resistant varieties, clean planting material, proper irrigation,
ventilation, spacing, and sanitation. The third step is monitoring with visual
inspection, sticky traps, leaf counts, soil checks, and crop records. Only after
these steps does the grower choose direct action.

Direct action proceeds from least disruptive to more disruptive methods.
Hand-picking, pruning, washing, barriers, trap crops, sticky traps, and habitat
adjustment come first. Biological controls and microbial controls follow when
conditions suit them. Botanical sprays, soaps, oils, sulfur, or mineral dusts
are then applied selectively, with attention to beneficial insects, crop stage,
weather, pH, water quality, and harvest interval.
