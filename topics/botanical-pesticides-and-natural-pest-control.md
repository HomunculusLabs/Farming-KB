## Botanical Pesticides and Natural Pest Control

Botanical pesticides are pest management materials derived directly from plants, including seed oils,
leaf extracts, flower powders, fermented preparations, and volatile repellents. Unlike synthetic chemical
pesticides, botanical insecticides generally break down rapidly in the environment, leave minimal toxic
residues, and can be produced on-farm at very low cost.
In organic farming, permaculture, and natural farming systems, botanical pesticides sit within a broader
philosophy of ecological pest management. Healthy soils produce healthy plants that resist pest pressure
naturally. Diverse plantings attract beneficial predators. Sanitation, crop rotation, and mechanical
barriers form the first line of defense. Botanical sprays are a last resort, not the foundation.
This page covers the major botanical pesticide families, their preparation and use, the JADAM
ultra-low-cost pesticide system, Korean Natural Farming approaches, ecological design principles for
integrated pest management, and practical guidance for building a complete natural pest control program
on a small farm or homestead.

## Neem — The Village Pharmacy

The neem tree (Azadirachta indica) is native to the dry forests of India and Burma and has been called
'the village pharmacy' for its extraordinary range of uses. Its seeds and leaves contain azadirachtin, a
complex limonoid compound that disrupts insect hormone systems, preventing moulting, feeding, and
reproduction while remaining relatively non-toxic to mammals, birds, and most beneficial insects.
Neem oil is pressed from seeds and used as a foliar spray or soil drench. It controls aphids, whiteflies,
thrips, fungus gnats, spider mites, and caterpillars. It also acts as a fungistat against powdery mildew
and rust. Neem must be mixed fresh before each use with water below pH 7 and a spreader-sticker agent to
keep the solution emulsified.
Beyond pest control, neem wood is tougher than teak and resists termites. The leaves repel stored-grain
insects when layered in granaries. Neem leaf dust baths control ectoparasites on poultry. The tree
coppices readily, produces high-calorific firewood, and its leaf litter raises acidic soil pH toward
neutral.
Neem is planted in permaculture systems to overhang ponds, where falling berries provide passive mosquito
and fly control. It thrives in arid zones with as little as 130 mm annual rainfall, though seed viability
lasts only 2-3 weeks and seedlings are killed by frost.

## Pyrethrum — The Ancient Insecticide Daisy

Pyrethrum is extracted from the flower heads of Chrysanthemum cinerariifolium, the Dalmatian
chrysanthemum, native to the Balkan Peninsula. Six naturally occurring pyrethrin esters work together to
paralyze insects by disrupting nervous system function. Pyrethrum provides rapid knockdown of flying and
crawling insects while being essentially non-toxic to mammals.
The Dalmatian strain contains the highest pyrethrin concentration. The Persian chrysanthemum (C.
coccineum) is more cold-hardy but less potent. Flowers are harvested on warm sunny days, dried upside
down in the dark, and stored whole in airtight containers away from light. Grind only when ready to use.
Pyrethrum dissipates within hours in sunlight and UV exposure. Apply in the evening for maximum
persistence. Do not mix with sulfur, lime, copper, or soaps, all of which destroy pyrethrins. It is toxic
to fish, amphibians, and beneficial insects, so target applications carefully and avoid spraying near
ponds or flowering insectary plants.

## Rotenone, Derris, and Traditional Botanicals

Derris species produce rotenone, a potent insecticide long used in traditional Asian agriculture. Fukuoka
documented the use of derris powder alongside pyrethrum and fowl for vegetable pest control in
pre-chemical Japan. Rotenone is highly toxic to fish and aquatic organisms, making it unsuitable near
waterways.
Several other plants have strong insecticidal or repellent properties. Garlic and chili pepper sprays
repel aphids and mites. Wormwood (Artemisia absinthium) tea repels fleas, slugs, beetles, and weevils but
is allelopathic and should not be planted near crops. Tobacco leaf extracts contain nicotine, a potent
broad-spectrum insecticide.
Japanese traditional farmers also used hot pepper extracts and ginkgo fruit preparations for insect
control. Korean Natural Farming and JADAM systems have formalized many of these traditional preparations
into systematic, repeatable recipes using locally available plants.

## The JADAM Natural Pesticide System

JADAM (Korean for 'nature-loving people') developed an ultra-low-cost natural pesticide system that costs
approximately 1/50th of commercial organic pesticides and about $7-9 per acre per year. The core insight
is that pathogens and pests differ only in size — a germicide at higher concentration becomes an
insecticide.
The system rests on four ingredients. JADAM Wetting Agent (JWA) is a natural surfactant that provides
coating, penetration, and delivery — without JWA, there is no pesticide. JADAM Sulfur (JS) is a germicide
prepared from sulfur without heating in just 20 minutes. JADAM Herb Solution (JHS) is made by steeping
selected insecticidal herbs in water. JADAM Microorganism Solution (JMS) provides biological pest
suppression.
Different combinations target different pests. For powdery mildew and fungus: JWA plus JS. For aphids and
mites: JWA plus Jerusalem artichoke JHS. For moths: stronger JWA with more JHS. An all-purpose formula
combines all four. Every recipe uses soft water and requires a mixture test before full application.
The JADAM Bongha Village proof demonstrated that a 320-acre rice farming community using JADAM natural
pesticide four times per season spent only $9 per acre. The system avoids resistance because it works
through multiple modes of action simultaneously.

## Korean Natural Farming Pest Management

Dr. Cho Han Kyu's Korean Natural Farming (KNF) approach to pest management begins with soil health.
Indigenous microorganisms (IMOs) collected from local forests decompose organic matter, cycle nutrients,
and produce antibiotic substances that suppress pathogens. A diverse soil microbiome prevents any single
pathogen from dominating.
KNF uses fermented plant juices, labana (fermented mixed compost), and other inputs at specific plant
growth stages to maintain plant health and vigor. Healthy, well-nourished plants are inherently more
resistant to pest and disease pressure. Physical traps and biological methods are employed only when
necessary.
Soap water sprays are the primary direct pest intervention in KNF. They are inexpensive, easy to prepare,
and effective against soft-bodied insects. Aromatic insect attractants draw beneficial predators to the
growing area. Fluorescent insect attractants trap specific flying pests. Daily observation and early
intervention prevent minor issues from becoming outbreaks.

## Fukuoka and the Philosophy of Non-Intervention

Masanobu Fukuoka's approach to pest management was radical in its simplicity: do nothing. He argued that
modern agriculture creates the conditions for pest outbreaks through monoculture, soil disruption, and
chemical dependency, then treats the symptoms with pesticides that further degrade the system.
Fukuoka described a remarkable spider phenomenon in his rice fields. After harvest, millions of spiders
appeared, covering the stubble with webs 'as though with silk.' After a day or two, the young spiders
sailed away on the wind. This natural pest control cycle is still not fully understood by science, yet it
functioned perfectly without any human intervention.
He once scattered wood ash on his fields, thinking it harmless. Days later, every spider was gone — the
ash had destroyed the web strands. This illustrated how even 'harmless' interventions can disrupt complex
ecological relationships that provide invisible pest control services.
Fukuoka's stem borer experiments showed that untreated fields with many withered stalks actually yielded
more than insecticide-sprayed fields. The borers naturally thinned weak plants, allowing survivors more
sunlight and resources. Chemical intervention eliminated this beneficial thinning and often reduced
yields.

## Jeavons and Biointensive Pest Prevention

John Jeavons' GROW BIOINTENSIVE approach begins with the insight that most insects target sick plants.
Healthy soil with diverse microbial populations produces healthy plants that resist pests naturally.
Synthetic fertilizers produce lush but weak growth that actually attracts insect attack.
Companion planting is central to biointensive pest control. Plants like wormwood, garlic, onions, and
marigolds repel specific pest species when interplanted with vulnerable crops. Composite flowers such as
daisies, yarrow, and fennel produce nectar and pollen that attract ladybugs, lacewings, parasitic wasps,
and hover flies.
Certain herbs benefit the entire garden. Stinging nettle increases essential oil content in neighboring
herbs and promotes humus formation. Chamomile contains growth hormones and combats damping-off disease.
Valerian stimulates phosphorus activity and disease resistance. Dandelion increases aromatic quality of
all herbs.
Crop rotation is companion planting 'over time' and one of the simplest, most effective pest prevention
strategies. Many pest insects are crop-specific and cannot persist when their host plants are absent for
a season. Combined with interplanting, rotation creates a continuously shifting environment that confuses
pests.

## Mushroom Cultivation and Pest Control

Tradd Cotter's work on organic mushroom farming highlights a unique constraint: mushrooms bioaccumulate
everything from their environment, making synthetic pesticide contamination a serious food safety
concern. Mushroom growers must rely on sanitation, biological controls, and carefully selected natural
interventions.
Fungus gnats are the most common mushroom pest. Their larvae feed on mycelium and damage fruiting bodies.
Biological control with Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (Bti) applied to casing layers targets gnat
larvae without affecting the mushroom crop. Sticky yellow traps catch flying adults and serve as
population monitoring tools.
Competitive molds like Trichoderma (green mold) are controlled through strict sanitation, proper
pasteurization of substrate, and maintaining optimal environmental conditions. Spot treatment with
agricultural lime and dilute bleach addresses isolated contamination. In the laboratory, contaminated
cultures must be removed immediately to prevent spread.

## Ecological Design for Pest Management

Permaculture design integrates pest management into the whole farm system rather than treating it as an
isolated problem. Insectary plants — species that provide pollen, nectar, shelter, and alternative prey
for beneficial insects — are placed throughout the growing area, not confined to separate borders.
Trap crops are plants deliberately grown to attract pest insects away from main crops. Mollison
recommended planting small clumps of attractive host plants at strategic points, then either harvesting
the concentrated pests or treating only those plants with botanical sprays, minimizing total pesticide
use.
Water features stocked with small fish and predatory insects provide mosquito control. Neem trees planted
to overhang ponds drop berries that suppress aquatic insect larvae. Duck and poultry integration provides
mobile pest patrol, with birds consuming large quantities of insects, slugs, and larvae while fertilizing
the soil.
Windbreaks and hedgerows create habitat corridors for predatory insects, birds, and bats. A diverse farm
landscape with multiple vegetation layers supports more predator species than a simplified monoculture,
creating a self-regulating pest management system that requires progressively less intervention over
time.

## Preparing and Applying Botanical Sprays

Fresh preparation is essential for most botanical pesticides. Neem oil must be emulsified just before
spraying and applied within hours. Pyrethrum loses potency rapidly once ground and exposed to light.
JADAM herb solutions are used within 1-2 days of preparation. Never store leftover spray mixtures.
Water quality matters more than most growers realize. JADAM requires soft water, tested by checking for
clear foam when JWA is mixed. High pH water destroys pyrethrins and reduces neem effectiveness. Use
rainwater or test and adjust municipal water before mixing botanical sprays.
Spray timing significantly affects results. Early morning, late evening, and cloudy days provide the
humidity needed for spray droplets to remain on foliage long enough to be effective. Dry, sunny, windy
conditions cause rapid evaporation and poor coverage. Fine-particle nozzles produce smaller droplets that
coat leaf surfaces more thoroughly.
Always conduct a small patch test before full application. Mix a small quantity, spray a few plants, and
check results after 2-3 days. Some plants are sensitive to particular botanicals, and concentration
errors can cause foliar burn. Start with lower concentrations and increase only if needed.

## Botanical Pesticides and Resistance Management

One of the greatest advantages of botanical pesticides is their complex chemistry. Neem oil contains over
100 active compounds that attack insects through multiple pathways simultaneously. Pyrethrum contains six
pyrethrin esters working together. This chemical complexity makes it extremely difficult for pests to
develop resistance.
Synthetic pesticides typically contain a single active ingredient that selects for resistant individuals.
Over generations, the pest population becomes dominated by resistant individuals, and the pesticide
becomes ineffective. This has driven an escalating arms race of increasingly toxic chemicals at
increasing cost.
JADAM pesticide specifically avoids resistance development because its multiple ingredients act through
diverse mechanisms. Even so, best practice is to rotate between different botanicals — neem, pyrethrum,
garlic, chili — and combine botanical sprays with cultural, mechanical, and biological controls.

## Safety and Environmental Considerations

Botanical does not mean harmless. Rotenone is highly toxic to fish and aquatic life. Pyrethrum is lethal
to bees, fish, and beneficial insects on contact. Neem can harm aquatic organisms. Even JADAM
preparations, while safe for humans and compliant with organic regulations, should be handled with care
and kept away from waterways.
Synthetic pyrethroids — permethrin, cypermethrin, deltamethrin — are laboratory-made analogs of natural
pyrethrins that persist much longer in the environment. They are broad-spectrum, non-selective contact
insecticides that kill beneficial insects alongside pests. Their persistence increases environmental risk
compared to natural pyrethrum.
Always wear protective clothing and a mask when spraying any pesticide, including botanicals. Avoid
spraying during bloom when bees are foraging. Maintain buffer zones around ponds, streams, and water
features. Read and follow all preparation and application guidelines carefully.

## Stored Food Protection with Botanicals

Neem leaves have been used for centuries in India to protect stored grains from insect damage. Research
confirms that neem compounds repel and disrupt stored-grain insects including weevils, beetles, and
moths. Layer dried neem leaves between grain batches in storage containers.
Pyrethrum powder dusted onto stored grain provides contact insecticide protection without contaminating
food. Derris powder offers similar protection. Diatomaceous earth, while not a botanical, complements
botanical grain protectants by physically abrading insect exoskeletons, causing dehydration.
In permaculture systems, multipurpose trees like neem serve double duty: providing fresh botanical
pesticide material during the growing season and dried leaf material for grain storage after harvest.
This integration eliminates the need for purchased storage fumigants.

## Livestock and Botanical Pest Control

Neem products extend beyond crop protection into livestock health. Dried neem leaves in poultry dust
baths control ectoparasites like mites and lice. Neem oil applied to cattle rubbing posts provides
continuous relief from biting flies. Hessian sacks soaked in neem oil and tied to posts keep pigs free of
lice.
Mollison described neem oil mixed with vegetable oils as an effective coat treatment for horses. The same
compounds that disrupt insect hormone systems in crop pests work against livestock ectoparasites. Indian
beech (Pongamia pinnata) seed oil offers similar benefits and can be used interchangeably with neem for
livestock applications.
Free-range poultry integration provides dual pest control benefits: birds consume insects directly while
their manure fertilizes the soil. Chickens, ducks, and guinea fowl all contribute. The key is selecting
breeds that forage without damaging crops and timing their access to prevent destruction of young
seedlings.

## Seasonal Pest Management Calendar

Spring is the critical prevention window. Build soil health with compost, microbial inoculants, and cover
crops. Set up insectary plantings before pest populations build. Install physical barriers like row
covers on vulnerable crops. Scout early and often — catching aphid or mite colonies in their first week
prevents exponential growth.
Summer demands vigilance and rapid response. Monitor crops daily for early signs of damage. Have
botanical spray ingredients prepared and ready. Apply preventive JADAM microorganism sprays to suppress
disease. Release beneficial insects when pest populations first appear. Use trap crops to intercept
migrating pests.
Fall focuses on cleanup and preparation. Remove crop residues that harbor overwintering pests. Plant
cover crops that disrupt pest life cycles. Compost all infected material at high temperatures. Collect
and dry botanical pesticide plants for winter storage. Prepare JWA, JS, and JHS for next season.
Winter is for planning and education. Review pest records from the past season. Map pest hotspots and
plan rotation accordingly. Order insectary plant seeds. Study and practice new botanical spray recipes.
Maintain spray equipment and calibrate nozzles.

## Economics and Self-Reliance

One of the strongest arguments for botanical pesticides is economic independence from industrial inputs.
Chemical pesticide prices have increased dramatically, with JADAM reporting that products costing $10 a
decade ago now cost $100. Commercial organic pesticides are often even more expensive than chemical
alternatives.
Neem trees produce seed for oil within 5-7 years and continue producing for decades. Pyrethrum daisies
are perennial and can be harvested annually. JADAM ingredients cost pennies per application. Korean
Natural Farming inputs are made entirely from locally collected materials. This self-reliance insulates
small farmers from price volatility.
The knowledge itself is a form of capital. Once a grower understands the principles — how to identify
pest problems early, how to prepare botanical sprays, how to design insectary plantings — the cost of
pest management drops to near zero. This knowledge cannot be taken away by market forces or supply chain
disruptions.

## Integrating Multiple Approaches

No single botanical pesticide or cultural practice provides complete pest management. Effective natural
pest control integrates multiple strategies in a layered defense. Soil health and plant nutrition form
the foundation. Companion planting, crop rotation, and insectary habitat create the ecological
infrastructure.
Physical barriers — row covers, netting, sticky traps, copper tape — prevent pest access without any
chemical intervention. Biological controls — beneficial insects, microbial sprays, poultry integration —
provide living pest management that operates continuously. Botanical pesticides are the targeted
intervention when other layers prove insufficient.
The sequence matters. Start with the least disruptive method and escalate only as needed. Remove pest
habitat first. Then deploy physical barriers. Release beneficial insects. Apply preventive microbial
sprays. Only if problems persist should concentrated botanical sprays be used. This hierarchy minimizes
collateral damage to beneficial organisms.

## Research and Future Directions

Research into botanical pesticides continues to expand. Current areas include optimizing extraction
methods for maximum active compound yield, identifying new insecticidal plant species from traditional
knowledge, developing formulations for better shelf life, and understanding complex synergies between
multiple botanical compounds.
The JADAM system represents a significant innovation in making botanical pest control accessible and
affordable at any scale. Its emphasis on ultra-low cost and on-farm production directly addresses the
economic barriers that prevent many small farmers from adopting organic pest management.
Advances in molecular biology are revealing how botanical compounds interact with insect physiology at
the cellular level, potentially enabling more targeted formulations. Citizen science networks are
documenting traditional pest management knowledge from diverse farming cultures before it is lost to
industrialization.
The convergence of traditional ecological knowledge with modern scientific methods offers particularly
promising avenues. Many time-tested practices align well with contemporary understanding of ecosystem
function, and combining these perspectives yields insights that neither approach achieves alone.
