# Mycorrhizal Inoculation and Inoculum Production

Mycorrhizal inoculation is the deliberate introduction of beneficial
mycorrhizal fungi into soil or growing media to establish or restore
the symbiotic relationship between these fungi and plant roots. The
practice has grown from a niche academic technique into a mainstream
component of sustainable agriculture, horticulture, forestry, and
ecological restoration. Mycorrhizal fungi associate with approximately
95 percent of all plant species, extending the effective root surface
area by 700 to 1,000 times and providing plants with water, phosphorus,
nitrogen, and micronutrients in exchange for carbohydrates. Despite
their ubiquity in natural ecosystems, these fungi are often depleted or
absent in agricultural soils due to tillage, chemical inputs, fallow
periods, and the use of sterile potting media. Inoculation bridges this
gap, reintroducing the fungal partners that plants evolved alongside
over hundreds of millions of years.

## Why Inoculate?

The case for mycorrhizal inoculation rests on several well-documented
benefits. Plants colonized by [[dighton-fungal-drought-tolerance-plant-water-relations]] by extending the absorptive surface deep into the
soil profile. They provide biological protection against soil-borne
pathogens through physical barriers, competitive exclusion, and
biochemical signaling that primes the plant's own immune defenses. The
fungi also produce glomalin, a glycoprotein that binds soil particles
into stable aggregates, improving soil structure, water infiltration,
and long-term carbon sequestration.

Inoculation is most critical in disturbed or artificial growing
environments: newly tilled fields, sterilized potting mixes, mined or
eroded soils, urban landscapes, and commercial nursery operations.
Even in apparently healthy soils, inoculation can boost performance by
introducing more effective or diverse fungal strains than those already
present. The economic argument is straightforward for high-value crops
where the cost of inoculant is small relative to the value of yield or
quality improvements.

## Types of Mycorrhizal Fungi Used in Inoculation

Three major categories of mycorrhizal fungi are relevant to inoculation
practices, each associated with different plant groups and requiring
distinct handling.

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) are the workhorses of agricultural
inoculation. They penetrate root cells to form arbuscules, the
tree-like exchange organs where carbon and nutrients flow between plant
and fungus. AMF associate with the vast majority of crops, vegetables,
grasses, and many ornamentals. Key genera used in commercial products
include Rhizoglomus (formerly Glomus), Rhizophagus, Funneliformis,
Gigaspora, and Scutellospora. Most commercially available inoculants
are AMF-based, reflecting their broad host range and agronomic
importance.

Ectomycorrhizal fungi (ECM) form a sheath around root tips without
penetrating cells. They are essential for conifers, oaks, birches,
beeches, and many other forest trees. Commercial ECM inoculants
typically feature Pisolithus (a genus that produces conspicuous fruiting
bodies), Laccaria, Suillus, and Rhizopogon. ECM inoculation is
primarily used in forestry, reforestation, mine reclamation, and
