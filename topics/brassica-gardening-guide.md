# Brassica and Cole Crop Gardening

The brassica family, Brassicaceae, encompasses some of the most
nutritious and productive vegetables available to home gardeners and
market farmers alike. Known collectively as cole crops, this diverse
group includes broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, kale,
collards, kohlrabi, turnips, radishes, and a wide array of Asian
greens such as bok choy, mizuna, and tatsoi.

What unites these crops is their shared ancestry in the wild mustard
Brassica oleracea, a plant that early farmers transformed through
centuries of selective breeding into the strikingly different
vegetables we recognize today. Despite their visual diversity, all
brassicas share fundamental growing requirements: cool weather, rich
fertile soil, and vigilant pest management.

Nutritionally, brassicas are rich in vitamin C, vitamin K, folate,
iron, calcium, and sulfur-containing glucosinolates studied for
potential anti-cancer properties. Kale and collards are particularly
nutrient-dense. In the garden, brassicas fill the spring and fall
shoulder seasons when warm-weather crops are not yet planted or have
been killed by frost. However, they are also among the most
challenging crops to grow organically due to specialized insect pests
and persistent diseases like clubroot.

## Botanical Background and the Refinement Scale

One of the most illuminating ways to understand brassicas is through
Steve Solomon's concept of the "refinement scale." All domesticated
brassicas derive from wild Brassica oleracea, but centuries of
selection have pushed different varieties along a continuum from wild
to highly refined.

At the bottom of this scale sits kale, the brassica closest to its
wild ancestor. Kale retains the robust vigor, cold hardiness, and pest
resistance of its wild forebear. It grows on a tall central stalk and
produces leaves from a crown, requiring relatively modest soil
fertility and minimal intervention.

Moving up the scale, collards are a non-heading type slightly less
vigorous than kale. Giant kohlrabi, grown for its swollen stem, is
essentially a low-demand fodder crop. Cabbage represents a dramatic
refinement: a kale whose stalk has been compressed into a tight head
with almost no space between leaves.

Brussels sprouts take this compression further, forming miniature
cabbages at each leaf joint along a tall stem. Broccoli was bred from
a more aggressive but less cold-hardy wild strain that naturally
produced larger flower clusters. At the top of the scale sits
cauliflower, the most refined and demanding of all, with a
particularly weak root system intolerant of heavy or compacted soils.

This scale has practical implications for gardeners. New growers
should start at the bottom with kale and collards, which are forgiving
of imperfect conditions. As soil improves and skills develop, they can
gradually attempt more demanding crops like cabbage, broccoli, and
finally cauliflower.

## Soil Requirements and Site Selection

Brassicas are heavy feeders that require rich, fertile soil with plenty
of organic matter. They prefer a slightly alkaline soil pH between
6.5 and 7.0, and all brassicas need more calcium than most other
vegetable families. Where soil is acidic, broadcasting five pounds of
finely ground agricultural lime per 100 square feet before planting is
standard practice.

The soil should be firm and well-consolidated rather than fluffy and
loose. This contrasts with root crops like carrots, which prefer loose
soil for unimpeded root growth. Firm soil provides the stability that
heading brassicas need to develop compact, dense heads.

In organic systems, preparing a brassica bed begins the previous
season. Rather than applying fresh manure just before planting, which
produces soft disease-prone growth, experienced growers manure the
preceding crop. Alternatively, two to three handfuls of blood, fish,
and bone meal per square yard provide balanced nutrition at planting
time.

Brassicas benefit from following legumes in crop rotation. The nitrogen
released by decomposing pea and bean root nodules provides natural
fertility. In raised beds, Solomon recommends concentrating extra
complete organic fertilizer or strong compost directly under each
planting station to support the heavy feeding demands of refined
brassicas like broccoli and cauliflower.

## Starting Seeds and Transplanting

Most brassicas are raised in seed trays or seedbeds and transplanted
rather than direct-sown. This approach produces strong, stocky plants
better able to withstand clubroot and other soil-borne problems. It
also allows the gardener to control growing conditions during the
critical early weeks.

Seeds should be started 6 to 8 weeks before the intended transplant
date. Sow one-quarter inch deep in sterile seed-starting mix, and
maintain a germination temperature of 70 to 75 degrees Fahrenheit.
Seeds typically emerge within 5 to 10 days under these conditions.

Transplant seedlings when they have developed 2 to 4 true leaves,
usually at 4 to 6 weeks of age. For maximum clubroot resistance, pot
on to 4-inch containers and grow until roots fill the pot, about 6
weeks total. By this stage, the root system is robust enough to
resist infection even in contaminated soil.

Hardening off is essential before transplanting. Gradually expose
seedlings to outdoor conditions over 7 to 10 days by increasing their
time outside, starting with just a few hours in a sheltered location.
Brassica seedlings that are not properly hardened off may bolt or
fail to establish.

For spring plantings, transplant broccoli and cabbage 2 to 4 weeks
before the last expected frost. Cauliflower needs 2 to 3 weeks
before last frost but must be harvested before summer heat arrives.
Brussels sprouts should be started early to ensure a long enough cool
season. Kale, the most cold-tolerant, can be set out 4 to 6 weeks
before last frost.

Fall plantings are often more successful than spring ones. Start seeds
indoors in June or July and transplant into the garden in July or
August, roughly 6 to 8 weeks before the first fall frost. Fall brassicas
face less pest pressure and produce better quality heads, especially
cauliflower and Brussels sprouts.

## Crop Profiles: Broccoli and Cauliflower

Broccoli is one of the most rewarding brassicas for home gardeners
because it offers an extended harvest. The central head matures in 60
to 90 days from transplant, depending on variety. Cut the head when
florets are still tight and of good green-blue color, leaving 5 to 6
inches of stem.

After removing the central head, smaller side shoots continue
developing for 4 to 6 weeks, providing a prolonged secondary harvest.
Regular picking of side shoots encourages continued production. Popular
varieties include Belstar, Green Magic, and the heirloom Di Cicco,
which is especially prolific with side shoots.

Broccoli requires rich soil and consistent moisture for tight, compact
heads. Space plants 18 to 24 inches apart. Heat-tolerant varieties
like Green Magic and Premium Crop perform better in warmer climates.
In areas with short cool seasons, choosing a fast-maturing variety is
critical.

Cauliflower is widely considered the most demanding brassica to grow.
It requires the most precise conditions of any cole crop, with
sensitivity to heat and temperature fluctuations that can cause the
curd to button or rice. For this reason, cauliflower is most reliable
as a fall crop in most growing zones.

Heads mature in 55 to 85 days from transplant. The curd must be
blanched to keep it white: tie outer leaves above the head when curds
reach 1 to 2 inches in diameter, excluding sunlight. Self-blanching
varieties eliminate this step. Harvest about 2 weeks after tying, when
the head is full size but before curds begin to separate.

Cauliflower varieties now include colorful options beyond white.
Graffiti produces purple heads, Cheddar offers orange curds rich in
beta-carotene, and Veronica forms an intricate green romanesco. These
colored types do not require blanching. Space cauliflower 18 to 24
inches apart and ensure constant growth, as any check from drought,
nutrient deficiency, or temperature stress causes tiny button heads
instead of a full curd.

## Crop Profiles: Cabbage and Brussels Sprouts

Cabbage is one of the oldest and most widely grown vegetables, with
remarkable diversity in head shape, color, and seasonality. Heads
mature in 60 to 110 days depending on the type. Early varieties like
Golden Acre and Copenhagen Market mature in 60 to 70 days with
smaller heads, while late storage types like January King and Storage
No. 4 need 100 to 110 days but produce large, dense heads.

Red cabbage varieties such as Ruby Perfection add color to the
garden. Savoy cabbages with crinkled leaves are especially cold-hardy
with milder flavor. Space early cabbages 12 to 18 inches apart and
large types 18 to 24 inches.

Cabbage is among the best-storing cole crops. Fall-grown heads store
for up to 2 months under cold, moist conditions of 32 to 40 degrees
Fahrenheit at 90 to 95 percent relative humidity. Harvest when heads
are solid and firm, heavy for their size, with no soft spots. Cut at
the base with a sharp knife, leaving a few wrapper leaves for
protection.

Brussels sprouts have the longest growing season of any brassica,
requiring 90 to 120 days from transplant to harvest. The plants form
tall stems, 2 to 3 feet tall, with small cabbage-like sprouts
developing in the leaf axils. Sprouts are harvested from the bottom
of the stem upward, beginning when they reach 1 to 1.5 inches in
diameter.

Flavor improves dramatically after frost. Cold temperatures convert
starches to sugars, transforming what can be bitter little spheres
into sweet, nutty morsels. In mild climates, the entire plant can be
left in the ground and harvested over 2 to 3 months. In cold regions,
the whole stem can be cut and hung upside down in a cool shed for
gradual harvest.

Popular varieties include Jade Cross, Long Island Improved, Diablo,
and Gustus. Space plants 18 to 24 inches apart and stake them in
windy locations. Remove lower leaves as sprouts develop to direct
energy upward and improve air circulation around the ripening sprouts.

## Crop Profiles: Kale, Kohlrabi, and Asian Greens

Kale is the most cold-hardy and vigorous of all garden brassicas.
It survives to 10 degrees Fahrenheit unprotected and its flavor
sweetens after frost as starches convert to sugars. Steve Solomon has
observed kale resume growing after thawing from an overnight low of
21 degrees, as long as the soil itself has not frozen.

Two distinct species of kale are commonly grown. Brassica oleracea
kale forms a tall central stalk with leaves emerging from a crown.
This is the most vigorous type in most conditions. Brassica napus kale,
known as Siberian kale, forms a rosette pattern similar to lettuce.
Some gardeners prefer its milder flavor for raw salads.

Popular oleracea kale varieties include Lacinato (also called
Dinosaur kale), Winterbor, Red Russian, and Vates. Harvest outer
leaves continuously, leaving the central growing point intact for
months of production from a single planting. This cut-and-come-again
approach makes kale exceptionally productive per square foot.

Kohlrabi is grown for its swollen stem, which develops above ground
into a crisp, mild vegetable with a turnip-like flavor. It must be
harvested young at 2 to 3 inches in diameter, or the globe becomes
woody and tough. Giant kohlrabi, grown as a fodder crop, can reach
volleyball size. Sow successionally for continuous harvest through the
season.

Asian brassicas deserve wider attention from home gardeners. Many are
faster-growing, less demanding, and less pest-prone than their Western
counterparts. Mizuna, mibuna, tatsoi, and bok choy (pak choi) mature
in 30 to 45 days and tolerate a range of conditions. They are
excellent choices for filling gaps between larger brassica plantings
and for extending the harvest season.

## Successional Planting for Year-Round Harvest

With careful planning, brassicas can provide harvests across all four
seasons. This successional approach requires advance planning, as
summer and autumn crops are sown in spring, winter crops are sown in
late spring, and spring crops are sown in midsummer.

The spring gap from March through May is filled by overwintered spring
cabbage and sprouting broccoli. As these finish, summer cabbage and
early calabrese come into production. During summer, June through
August, summer cabbage, calabrese, and early kohlrabi peak. Begin
sowing autumn and winter types during this period.

Autumn, from September through November, brings autumn cabbage, late
calabrese, and the first Brussels sprouts after frosts. Kale becomes
harvestable. Winter, December through February, is when brassicas
truly earn their place. Winter cabbage, Brussels sprouts at their
sweetest, kale, and sprouting broccoli carry the garden through the
leanest months.

A written planting calendar is essential for avoiding gaps or over-
production. Start by marking your average first and last frost dates,
then work backward from each crop's maturity date to determine sowing
times. Many gardeners find it helpful to draw a simple chart showing
which beds are planted to which crop in each month.

For continuous kale harvest, start the main crop about three months
before first frost. A late fill-in sowing six weeks before first frost
produces smaller but frost-hardy plants. Cauliflower is safest
scheduled for autumn maturation to avoid heat damage.

## Pest Management

Brassicas attract a specialized suite of insect pests that can quickly
devastate unprotected plantings. Prevention and early intervention are
more effective than reactive treatments, making an integrated approach
essential.

Cabbage white butterflies are among the most visible brassica pests.
The adult butterflies lay yellow eggs on the undersides of leaves, and
the resulting green caterpillars chew holes in foliage and bore into
developing heads. Hand-picking caterpillars and egg clusters works in
small plantings, but floating row covers are the most effective
prevention for larger gardens.

Cabbage loopers cause similar damage but are distinguished by their
characteristic looping motion when moving. They share the same
management strategies as cabbage worms. The biological insecticide
Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), applied to leaf surfaces, effectively
kills both types of caterpillar while remaining safe for beneficial
insects and humans.

Diamondback moth larvae feed on leaf undersides and can develop
resistance to Bt over time. Rotating control methods is important:
where Bt has been used repeatedly, switch to spinosad. Row covers
remain effective against diamondback moth regardless of resistance.

Flea beetles are tiny jumping insects that shotgun small holes in
brassica leaves, particularly damaging to young seedlings. Floating
row covers on seedlings provide the best protection. Kaolin clay
(Surround WP) sprayed on leaves creates a physical deterrent. Plants
generally outgrow the damage once established.

Cabbage aphids cluster in heads and leaf crevices, forming dense
gray-green colonies that are difficult to wash out of tight heads like
cauliflower. Spray with insecticidal soap or strong water jets.
Encourage ladybugs, lacewings, and parasitic wasps by planting
flowering companion plants nearby.

Cabbage root fly larvae attack roots, stunting or killing plants.
Brassica collars, which are discs of felt, cardboard, or carpet
placed around each plant stem at soil level, provide a simple physical
barrier. Clean up crop residue at season's end to reduce overwintering
pest populations.

An interesting observation from Steve Solomon suggests that cabbage
worms show relatively little interest in kale compared to refined
brassicas. The presence of large kale plants in the garden may
divert moth attention away from more vulnerable crops, effectively
acting as a trap crop.

## Disease Prevention

Clubroot is the most serious disease affecting brassicas. Caused by
the soil-borne protist Plasmodiophora brassicae, it causes roots to
swell into distorted clubs that cannot function properly. Once
established in soil, clubroot can persist for decades. There is no
cure, making prevention through crop rotation and soil management the
only viable strategy.

Management begins with a minimum four-year rotation between brassica
plantings in any given bed. Liming the soil to maintain pH above 6.5
creates conditions less favorable to the pathogen. Growing seedlings in
pots until their roots fill a 4-inch container produces plants robust
enough to resist infection even in contaminated soil.

Choosing resistant varieties helps, though resistance can break down
under high pathogen pressure. Some Brussels sprouts and cabbage
varieties carry clubroot resistance genes, but cauliflower remains the
most susceptible and should be avoided in areas with known clubroot
problems. Good drainage also discourages the disease.

Downy mildew, caused by Peronospora parasitica, appears as gray-
purple fungal growth on leaf undersides. It thrives in cool, damp
conditions and overcrowded plantings. Adequate spacing, good air
circulation, and removal of affected leaves are the primary organic
controls.

Black rot, caused by Xanthomonas campestris, produces V-shaped
yellow-brown lesions from leaf margins. Avoid overhead watering and
working with wet plants. Alternaria leaf spot causes dark brown
concentric rings on leaves, promoted by warm, wet conditions. Ring
spot is most common on Brussels sprouts and cabbage in autumn. For
all three, crop rotation and removal of infected debris are the
primary organic controls.

## Nutrition and Fertility

Brassicas are among the heaviest feeders in the vegetable garden,
with particularly high nitrogen demands. In organic systems, this
requires thoughtful fertility management that provides steady nutrition
without producing the soft, sappy growth that attracts aphids and
fungal disease.

Before planting, work 2 to 3 inches of finished compost into the bed
along with a balanced organic fertilizer. Blood, fish, and bone meal
applied at two handfuls per square yard provides a slow-release source
of nitrogen, phosphorus, and calcium. For refined brassicas, Solomon
recommends concentrating an extra half cup of complete organic
fertilizer directly under each planting station.

A nitrogen side-dressing applied about 3 weeks after transplanting
supports the rapid growth phase. One pound of a nitrogen source per
100 feet of row is a common recommendation. Pelleted poultry manure
works well for this purpose in organic systems.

Trace minerals are particularly important for brassicas. Boron
deficiency causes brown heart in cauliflower and internal browning
in turnips. Molybdenum deficiency leads to whiptail, a condition
where leaves become narrow and distorted. Seaweed meal provides a
broad spectrum of trace minerals including both boron and molybdenum.

A liquid feed of comfrey or nettle tea applied every 2 weeks during
active growth supports steady development. Comfrey is especially
valuable because its deep roots mine potassium and trace minerals from
subsoil layers. However, avoid excess nitrogen overall, which produces
soft, sappy growth susceptible to aphids, frost damage, and fungal
diseases.

## Harvesting and Storage

Proper harvest timing directly affects both eating quality and storage
potential. Each brassica crop has specific indicators of readiness, and
learning to recognize them is key to getting the best from the garden.

For broccoli, harvest the terminal head while florets are still tight
and of good green-blue color. If florets begin to separate or show
yellow petals, the head is overmature. Cut with 5 to 6 inches of stem.
Smaller side heads will develop after the central head is removed,
providing weeks of additional harvest.

Cabbage is ready when heads are solid and firm, heavy for their size
with no soft spots. Squeeze the head gently to check density. Cut at
the base with a sharp knife, leaving wrapper leaves for protection.
Fall-grown cabbage generally stores better than spring cabbage.

Cauliflower requires attention to blanching. Tie outer leaves over
the curd when it reaches 1 to 2 inches in diameter. Harvest about 2
weeks after tying, when the head is full size but before curds begin
to separate. Delay causes the curd to rice, losing its smooth texture.

Brussels sprouts are harvested from the bottom of the stem upward as
individual sprouts reach firmness at about 1 inch in diameter. Lower
sprouts mature first. All sprouts should be harvested before the first
severe freeze. The whole plant can be left in the ground in mild
climates and harvested over 2 to 3 months.

Kale is harvested by picking outer leaves from the bottom up, leaving
the central growing point intact. This cut-and-come-again method
yields continuous harvests from a single planting. Frost sweetens the
flavor, making winter kale the most desirable.

Most brassicas store best under cold, moist conditions of 32 to 40
degrees Fahrenheit at 90 to 95 percent relative humidity. Perforated
plastic bags in the refrigerator or cold cellar work well. Cabbage
stores for up to 2 months, Brussels sprouts for 3 weeks, and broccoli
for only 1 week. Freeze any surplus promptly, blanching before freezing
to preserve quality and color.

## Companion Planting and Intercropping

Companion planting strategies can help reduce brassica pest pressure
and make efficient use of garden space. The evidence for many companion
planting claims is mixed, but several partnerships have practical value
in the brassica garden.

French marigolds planted between brassica rows may help mask the
distinctive cabbage scent that attracts white butterflies. Thyme, dill,
and mint planted nearby are also reported to deter egg-laying, though
the effect is variable and should not be relied upon as a sole control
method.

More reliably, fast-maturing catch crops like lettuce and radishes can
be interplanted between brassica rows during the early weeks of growth.
The brassicas start small and occupy little space, allowing the catch
crops to mature and be harvested before the brassicas need the full
growing area. This makes efficient use of space and soil.

Nasturtiums are sometimes planted near brassicas as a trap crop for
aphids. For weed suppression between rows, mulching with compost or
biodegradable plastic reduces cultivation needs and helps maintain
soil moisture.

## Seed Saving Challenges

Saving brassica seed presents unique challenges that make it one of
the more difficult crops for home seed savers. All Brassica oleracea
varieties cross-pollinate freely via bees, making isolation essential
for varietal purity. A half-mile separation may work for low-quality
seed, but reliable isolation requires greater distances.

All brassicas are biennials, meaning they must pass through a cold
period (vernalization) before flowering is triggered. In cold
climates, this means overwintering plants and allowing them to flower
in their second year. Where the ground freezes, large kale plants must
be dug up carefully in late autumn and stored in damp soil in a root
cellar, then transplanted outside in spring.

Population size is critical. Saving kale seed from fewer than six
plants causes rapid inbreeding depression. For refined brassicas like
Brussels sprouts and cauliflower, the gene pool should be at least
100 plants, with 200 being better. This is why productive
open-pollinated Brussels sprouts are essentially unavailable.

Tomatoes, by contrast, are self-pollinating and need only 50 feet of
isolation, making seed saving straightforward. Brassica seed saving
demands significant space, planning, and plant numbers that put it
beyond the reach of most home gardeners. Those interested in seed
saving are best advised to start with kale, which has the lowest
minimum population requirements.

For Asian brassicas like bok choy and tatsoi, cross-pollination
risks extend to other Brassica rapa species including turnips and
rutabagas, requiring careful planning about which related crops are
flowering simultaneously in the garden or neighborhood.
