## Pepper Cultivation and Variety Guide

Peppers belong to the genus Capsicum within the nightshade family
Solanaceae, a group that also includes tomatoes, potatoes, eggplants,
and tobacco. Native to the Americas, peppers have been cultivated for
over six thousand years and today rank among the most important vegetable
crops worldwide. The genus encompasses five domesticated species and
dozens of wild relatives, producing an extraordinary range of flavors,
colors, shapes, and heat levels. From the mild crunch of a bell pepper to
the searing intensity of a Carolina Reaper, peppers offer something for
every palate and every climate.

[[cannabis-harvest-timing-visual-cues]]. Gardeners in temperate climates must plan
carefully, often starting seeds indoors eight to ten weeks before the last
expected frost and employing season-extension techniques to maximize the
relatively short growing window.

The diversity within Capsicum is remarkable. Sweet bell peppers and hot
chile peppers are not different genera or even different species in most
cases; a single gene controls the presence of capsaicin, the compound
responsible for heat. This means that the same species, Capsicum annuum,
produces both the mildest bell pepper and some of the hottest jalapeño
variants. Understanding this genetic relationship helps gardeners
appreciate why cross-pollination between sweet and hot peppers planted
near each other can produce unexpected results in the next generation of
saved seed, though the current season's fruit is unaffected.

## Botanical Background and Species

The genus Capsicum includes approximately thirty-five recognized species,
of which five have been domesticated. Each domesticated species occupies
a distinct geographic origin and offers unique characteristics for
cultivation and culinary use. Capsicum annuum is by far the most widely
grown, encompassing bell peppers, jalapeños, poblanos, serranos,
cayennes, and hundreds of other varieties. It originated in Mexico and

challenging outside tropical and subtropical regions.

Capsicum frutescens includes the tabasco pepper and several African and
Asian cultivars. These plants tend to be compact and highly productive,
bearing small, upright-facing fruits. Capsicum baccatum, native to the
Andean region of South America, produces the aji peppers popular in
Peruvian and Bolivian cuisine. They are distinguished by distinctive
yellow or green spots on the corolla and often have a fruity, berry-like
flavor with moderate heat. Finally, Capsicum pubescens is a high-altitude
species from the Andes that produces large, fleshy peppers with black
seeds and purple flowers. The rocoto pepper is the best-known example,
and it thrives in cool conditions that would stunt other species, though
it cannot tolerate heat above thirty degrees Celsius.

## History and Domestication

Archaeological evidence places pepper domestication in the region that is
now Mexico roughly six thousand to ten thousand years ago, making peppers
one of the oldest cultivated crops in the Americas. The earliest
domesticated forms were likely small, berry-like fruits similar to modern
chiltepins, selected gradually for larger size, less pungency, and
different fruit colors. By the time European explorers arrived in the
late fifteenth century, peppers had spread throughout the Americas, from
the southwestern United States through Central America and into South
America.

Christopher Columbus encountered peppers in the Caribbean and mistakenly
called them "peppers" because their pungency reminded him of black pepper,
Piper nigrum, from Asia. This linguistic confusion persists today, though
the two plants are entirely unrelated. Columbus and subsequent Spanish
and Portuguese traders carried Capsicum seeds across the globe. Peppers
