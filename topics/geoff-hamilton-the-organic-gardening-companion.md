# Geoff Hamilton — The Organic Gardening Companion

Geoff Hamilton (1936–1996) was one of Britain's most beloved gardening
broadcasters and the presenter of BBC Television's *Gardeners' World*
for seventeen years. His book *The Organic Gardening Companion*, later
revised and expanded by his son Nick Hamilton, stands as one of the most
practical and comprehensive guides to chemical-free gardening ever
published. Unlike many organic gardening texts that lean heavily on
philosophy, Hamilton's approach is relentlessly practical: every
recommendation is grounded in decades of hands-on experience at his
Barnsdale garden in Rutland, England. The book covers soil management,
vegetable growing, fruit cultivation, ornamental gardening, greenhouse
management, propagation, pest and disease control, and garden design
within a unified organic framework.

## Organic Gardening Principles

Hamilton defines organic gardening as working with nature rather than
against it, recycling natural materials to maintain soil fertility, and
encouraging natural methods of pest and disease control rather than
relying on chemicals. The approach is fundamentally less involved than
chemical gardening because it leverages natural systems that have
sustained life for hundreds of millions of years.

The chemical versus organic debate is clear. The purely chemical gardener
uses soil merely as a means of anchoring plant roots and holding
artificial fertilizers. While this produces excellent short-term results,
the long-term consequences are disastrous: organic matter is not
replaced, soil organisms die out, and the soil becomes hard, airless,
and unproductive. The organic gardener, by contrast, builds a balanced
ecosystem of pests and predators where no single species gains dominance.

The first and most important principle is to feed the soil rather than
the plant. The soil teems with millions of microorganisms that release
nutrients from organic matter as plants require them. By maintaining
soil fertility through regular additions of compost and manure, plants
draw on a steady reservoir of nutrients without risk of overdose.

## The Four Phases of Soil Management

Hamilton divides soil management into four sequential phases that provide
a systematic framework for building and maintaining fertile soil. This
structured approach ensures that soil health is addressed comprehensively,
from initial assessment through to specialized crop nutrition.

Phase one is soil analysis. Every gardener starting on new ground should
have soil professionally tested, particularly for pH. Home testing kits
for nutrient levels are not accurate enough for reliable diagnosis; they
indicate trends but no more. Professional analysis provides exact
chemical data and specific fertilizer recommendations. Annual pH testing
is sufficient for most gardeners once the initial assessment is complete.

Phase two involves soil conditioners. The best materials for general
soil improvement and replacement of plant nutrients removed by previous
crops are manure and compost. These improve drainage in heavy soils and
water retention in light soils, while feeding the [[cannabis-cultivar-microbial-community-effects]]
that sustains plant health.

Phase three addresses pH correction through liming. Most garden plants
thrive at pH 6.5, though individual crops have specific preferences.
Acid soils are made more alkaline by adding lime gradually, never in
large single doses. Excessive lime chemically locks up trace elements,
making them unavailable to roots.

Phase four covers [[hamilton-concentrated-organic-fertilizer-products]] fertilizers for correcting
specific [[hamilton-nutrient-deficiencies-and-treatment]] identified in the first phase. This
targeted approach ensures that plants receive exactly what they need
without disrupting the broader soil ecosystem.

## Soil Types and Their Management

Hamilton identifies five main soil types, each requiring different
management techniques. Clay soils are heavy and cold, difficult to work
when wet but excellent for moisture and nutrient retention once amended
with organic matter. Clay should be dug in autumn, leaving the surface
rough through winter so frost action breaks down clods.

Sandy soils are light and free-draining, warming quickly in spring but
losing nutrients rapidly through leaching. Constant additions of organic
matter are essential, and mulching between plants reduces evaporation.
Sandy soils benefit enormously from regular applications of compost,
manure, and green manure crops.

Silt soils are neither gritty nor sticky, smooth and silky to the
touch, and should be dug during autumn to benefit from winter weather
breakdown. Chalk soils are pale, free-draining, and alkaline, often
appearing hungry-looking with yellowing foliage. Peat soils are dark,
moisture-retentive, and acidic, requiring no lime but potentially
needing [[hamilton-soil-drainage-improvement]].

## Composting and Organic Matter

Composting is the cornerstone of Hamilton's organic system. It transforms
vegetable and animal remains into humus-rich material that improves soil
structure, increases water-holding capacity, feeds soil organisms, and
supplies plant nutrients. The traditional compost heap alternates layers
of green nitrogen-rich materials (grass clippings, kitchen scraps, young
weeds) with brown carbon-rich materials (straw, autumn leaves, woody
prunings, cardboard).

Hamilton is refreshingly realistic about compost production: a normal
garden will not produce enough compost alone, so supplementary organic
matter must be sourced. Well-rotted farmyard manure is the gold standard,
followed by [[staycare-spent-mushroom-compost-bioremediation]], composted bark, and coir.

Worm composting receives special attention. Research on the effects of
earthworms on waste materials demonstrated that worms can be harnessed
to produce some of the finest soil conditioners available. Worm-worked
compost is rich in plant-available nutrients released slowly as
required by plants, preventing short-term toxicity. Worms break organic
matter into smaller granules, enabling soil microbes to work on a greater
surface area, and produce enzymes that make bacteria more efficient.
