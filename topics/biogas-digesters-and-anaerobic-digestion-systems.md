# Biogas Digesters and Anaerobic Digestion Systems

## Overview

Anaerobic digestion (AD) is a series of biological processes in which microorganisms break down organic material in the absence of oxygen, producing **biogas** (primarily methane and carbon dioxide) and **digestate** (a nutrient-rich residue used as fertilizer). Biogas digesters are the engineered vessels — ranging from simple household units to industrial-scale facilities — that host these processes.

The technology serves a dual purpose: **[[jadam-local-material-resource-cycling]].

## History

- **Ancient origins:** Decomposition of organic matter in sealed containers producing flammable gas was observed as early as the 10th century BCE in Assyria, though not understood scientifically.
- **1776:** Alessandro Volta discovered methane gas emanating from marsh sediments, noting its flammability.
- **1808:** Sir Humphry Davy confirmed methane production from cattle [[agricultural-waste-substrates]], sewage treatment, and food waste. Germany became the global leader in farm-scale biogas electricity generation, with over 10,000 plants by the 2010s.
- **21st century:** Growing interest in small-scale and community digesters for climate resilience, [[off-grid-electricity-basics]] energy in developing nations.

## The Four Stages of Anaerobic Digestion

Anaerobic digestion is a multi-stage microbial process. Each stage is carried out by a distinct group of microorganisms:

### 1. Hydrolysis

Complex organic polymers (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids) are broken down into soluble monomers (sugars, amino acids, fatty acids) by **hydrolytic bacteria**. This is often the rate-limiting step, especially for lignocellulosic feedstocks like crop residues.

- Enzymes involved: cellulases, amylases, proteases, lipases
- Typical substrates: cellulose, hemicellulose, starch, proteins
- Retention time impact: Lignin-rich materials slow hydrolysis significantly

### 2. Acidogenesis (Acid Formation)

Acidogenic (fermentative) bacteria convert the monomers from hydrolysis into short-chain volatile fatty acids (VFAs), alcohols, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide.

- Primary products: acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, ethanol, H₂, CO₂
- Ammonia is released from protein degradation
- pH drops during this stage (to ~5.0–6.0) before rising again

### 3. Acetogenesis

Acetogenic bacteria convert the longer-chain VFAs and alcohols into **acetic acid**, along with additional hydrogen and CO₂. This stage is thermodynamically challenging and depends on low partial pressure of hydrogen (maintained by methanogens).

- Key conversion: propionate → acetate + CO₂ + H₂
- Interspecies hydrogen transfer is critical — methanogens consuming H₂ drive acetogenesis forward

### 4. Methanogenesis

**Methanogenic archaea** convert acetic acid, hydrogen, and CO₂ into methane (CH₄) and carbon dioxide. Two primary pathways:

| Pathway | Reaction | Methanogen Group | Contribution |
|---------|----------|-----------------|--------------|
| Acetoclastic | CH₃COOH → CH₄ + CO₂ | *Methanosarcina*, *Methanothrix* | ~60–70% of methane |
| Hydrogenotrophic | CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O | *Methanobacterium*, *Methanospirillum* | ~30–40% |

Methanogens are the most sensitive group in the digester — they are slow-growing, sensitive to pH (optimum 6.8–7.5), temperature, and inhibitory compounds.

## Types of Biogas Digesters

### Fixed-Dome (Chinese Model)

The most widely built small-scale digester worldwide. An underground brick or concrete dome serves as both the fermentation chamber and gas storage.

- **Construction:** Underground masonry or concrete; the expanding gas displaces digestate into an outlet chamber
- **Capacity:** 6–12 m³ (household) to 50+ m³ (community)
- **Advantages:** No moving parts, low cost, long lifespan (15–25 years), all underground (space-efficient, temperature-buffered)
- **Disadvantages:** Gas pressure varies with volume stored; construction quality is critical (gas leaks are common); difficult to inspect or repair
- **Cost:** $300–$1,500 for household scale

### Floating-Drum (Indian Model / KVIC)

A cylindrical digester tank with a floating gas holder (steel or fiberglass drum) that rises and falls with gas production.

- **Construction:** Underground or above-ground brick/concrete tank; mild steel or FRP drum
- **Capacity:** 6–10 m³ typical for household
- **Advantages:** Constant gas pressure (weight of drum); easy to inspect gas volume visually; proven design
- **Disadvantages:** Steel drum corrodes and requires painting/replacement every 5–8 years; more expensive than fixed-dome; drum can get stuck
- **Cost:** $500–$2,000 for household scale

### Plug-Flow Reactor (PFR)

A long, narrow, horizontal tank in which feedstock flows through as a "plug." Common on [[hamilton-greenhouse-heating-and-temperature-control]] (climate-dependent); large land area; slow startup
- **HRT:** 40–60+ days

## Operating Parameters

### Temperature

| Regime | Range | HRT | Pros | Cons |
|--------|-------|-----|------|------|
| Psychrophilic | <20°C | 60–100+ days | No heating needed | Very slow, low yield |
| Mesophilic | 35–40°C | 15–30 days | Stable, tolerant, common | Moderate pathogen kill |
| Thermophilic | 50–57°C | 10–20 days | Fast, high pathogen kill | Less stable, more energy input |

Mesophilic operation is standard for small-scale systems due to stability and lower heating requirements.
