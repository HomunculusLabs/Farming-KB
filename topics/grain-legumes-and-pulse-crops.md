## Nutritional Profile

Pulses are nutritional powerhouses, offering a combination of macronutrients and micronutrients that few other crop groups can match.

### Macronutrients

- **Protein**: Pulses contain 20–35% protein by dry weight, making them the primary protein source for over a billion people worldwide. Unlike cereals, pulses are rich in the amino acid lysine, which is limiting in grains.
- **Carbohydrates**: 55–65% complex carbohydrates, primarily starch with a significant proportion of resistant starch that acts as a prebiotic fiber.
- **Fat**: Most pulses are very low in fat (1–5%), with the notable exception of lupins and soybeans (which sit at the boundary of pulse/oilseed classification).
- **Dietary Fiber**: 15–30% fiber content, including both soluble (pectins, gums) and insoluble (cellulose, hemicellulose) fractions.

### Micronutrients

- **Iron**: 3–7 mg per 100 g dry weight; a critical source in vegetarian diets, though bioavailability is limited by phytic acid.
- **Zinc**: 2–4 mg per 100 g; important for immune function and growth.
- **Folate**: 150–400 μg per 100 g; pulses are among the richest plant sources of folate, essential during pregnancy.
- **Potassium**: 800–1,400 mg per 100 g; supports cardiovascular health.
- **B Vitamins**: Thiamine (B1), riboflavin (B2), niacin (B3), and B6 in meaningful quantities.
- **Antioxidants**: Phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and tannins contribute to anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective effects.

### Anti-Nutritional Factors

Raw pulses contain several compounds that reduce nutrient bioavailability. Traditional processing methods effectively mitigate these:

- **Phytic acid**: Binds iron, zinc, and calcium. Reduced 40–80% by soaking (12+ hours) and fermentation.
- **Trypsin inhibitors**: Interfere with protein digestion. Destroyed by cooking at 100°C for 10+ minutes.
- **Lectins (phytohemagglutinins)**: Can cause gastrointestinal distress. Heat-labile; eliminated by thorough cooking (especially critical in red kidney beans).
- **Oligosaccharides (raffinose, stachyose)**: Cause flatulence because humans lack α-galactosidase to digest them. Reduced by soaking, sprouting, and fermentation.
- **Tannins**: Reduce protein digestibility. Mostly concentrated in seed coats; reduced by dehulling.

## Biological Nitrogen Fixation

The single most important agronomic feature of grain legumes is their ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) through a symbiotic relationship with rhizobia bacteria (*Rhizobium*, *Bradyrhizobium*, *Sinorhizobium*, and *Mesorhizobium* species).

### The Nodulation Process

1. **Signal Exchange**: Legume roots exude flavonoid compounds that attract compatible rhizobia and induce their *nod* (nodulation) genes.
2. **Infection**: Rhizobia attach to root hairs and form an infection thread that penetrates root cortical cells.

The nitrogen benefit extends beyond the pulse crop itself. When pulse residues (roots, stems, leaves, and any unharvested seed) decompose, they release nitrogen that becomes available to subsequent crops. This "nitrogen credit" typically ranges from 20–80 kg N/ha depending on species, biomass production, and management practices.

### Inoculation Practices

- **Chickpea**: Requires specific *Mesorhizobium ciceri* strains; native populations are often inadequate in new fields.
- **Lupin**: Requires *Bradyrhizobium lupini*; highly specific and often limiting in temperate soils.
- **Cowpea**: Highly promiscuous — nodulates with many native tropical rhizobia; rarely needs inoculation.
- **Common Bean**: Problematic — often poorly nodulated even with inoculation due to rhizobial strain specificity and soil factors.
- **Fava Bean**: Nodulates readily with native *Rhizobium leguminosarum* bv. *viceae* in most temperate soils.

## Cool-Season Grain Legumes

### Chickpea (*Cicer arietinum*)

Chickpeas are the second most widely grown pulse globally (after dry beans), with India alone producing over 70% of the world supply. Two main types exist:

- **Desi**: Small, dark-colored seeds with rough coats. Grown primarily in South Asia and Ethiopia. Higher fiber and antioxidant content. Better [[permplant-carob-drought-tolerant-food-tree]] planting design among grain legumes, owing to their deep taproot system (often exceeding 1.5 m) and phenological escape strategy — they flower and set seed during the cool season, maturing before summer drought. Optimal temperature: 18–29°C. Soil pH: 6.0–8.0 (tolerates alkaline soils better than most legumes). Sensitive to waterlogging, frost, and high humidity (which promotes *Ascochyta* blight).
