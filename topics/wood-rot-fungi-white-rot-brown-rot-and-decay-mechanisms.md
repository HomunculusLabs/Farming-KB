## Wood-Rot Fungi — White Rot, Brown Rot, and Decay Mechanisms

Wood-rot fungi are the primary biological agents that decompose woody
biomass in terrestrial ecosystems. These filamentous organisms,
predominantly basidiomycetes with some ascomycetes, possess enzyme
systems capable of breaking down lignocellulose.

Without wood-rot fungi, dead timber would accumulate indefinitely,
locking nutrients away from living organisms. An estimated ten percent
of terrestrial net primary production passes through fungal wood
decay pathways each year.

## Wood Composition and Lignocellulose Structure

Wood is composed of three major structural polymers. Cellulose
accounts for roughly forty to fifty percent of wood mass, forming
linear chains of beta-1,4-linked glucose units in crystalline
microfibrils of extraordinary tensile strength.

Hemicellulose constitutes twenty to thirty percent and includes
branched polysaccharides such as xylan, glucomannan, and arabinan
that cross-link cellulose microfibrils.

Lignin makes up the remaining twenty to thirty percent and is the
most recalcitrant component. It is an irregular, three-dimensional
phenylpropanoid polymer that fills spaces between cellulose and
hemicellulose, providing compressive strength and chemical resistance.

Its random structure contains no regularly repeating bonds that
enzymes can easily target. This recalcitrance is the primary reason
lignocellulosic biomass persists in the environment.

## Classification of Wood Decay Types

Wood-rot fungi are classified into three principal decay types based
on their biochemical strategies. White rot, brown rot, and soft rot
represent fundamentally different approaches to extracting energy
from lignocellulose.

These categories reflect a continuum rather than rigid boundaries.
Some species produce overlapping decay patterns, leaving mottled
white and brown discoloration in colonized wood.

Bear's head (Hericium abietis) causes white rot in the core of a
stump while the outer regions become brown. Understanding the decay
type is essential for identification and practical applications.

## White Rot Decay — Mechanisms and Enzymes

White rot fungi are the only organisms capable of substantial lignin
degradation. They employ an oxidative enzymatic system centered on
lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, and laccase.

These enzymes generate radical intermediates that nonspecifically
attack lignin's aromatic structure, eventually mineralizing it to
carbon dioxide. The degraded wood becomes lighter, soft, and fibrous.

Simultaneous white rot removes all cell wall components at roughly
equal rates. Selective white rot preferentially removes lignin while
leaving cellulose intact, as in Ceriporiopsis subvermispora.

Genomic evidence suggests brown rot fungi evolved from white rot
ancestors through gene loss, shedding ligninolytic peroxidase genes
in favor of more efficient cellulose-targeting mechanisms.

## Brown Rot Decay — Fenton Chemistry and Cellulose Attack

Brown rot fungi generate hydroxyl radicals through the Fenton
reaction to depolymerize cellulose non-enzymatically. The reaction
proceeds as Fe(II) plus H2O2 yielding Fe(III) plus hydroxyl radical.

Hydroxyl radicals are among the most powerful oxidants known and
cleave cellulose chains non-specifically. This rapidly reduces the
degree of polymerization of crystalline cellulose into amorphous
oligomers that conventional cellulases can further hydrolyze.

Brown rot fungi leave behind a brown, cracked residue of modified
lignin. The characteristic cubical cracking pattern is a diagnostic
field feature. Only about seven percent of wood-decay fungi are
brown rot species.

They also produce oxalic acid, which lowers the local pH to between
two and four, solubilizing iron needed for the Fenton reaction and
creating optimal conditions for non-enzymatic decay.

## Soft Rot Decay — Ascomycete Strategy

Soft rot fungi, primarily ascomycetes, dominate wood decomposition
in extreme environments where basidiomycetes cannot thrive. These
include waterlogged wood, arid conditions, and preservative-treated
timber.

They produce cellulase complexes that create characteristic
cylindrical cavities within the secondary cell wall. Type I creates
cavities aligned with cellulose microfibrils, while Type II causes
general cell wall erosion from the lumen outward.

Soft rot fungi are slower decomposers but their importance has been
historically underestimated. They contribute significantly to
decomposition in tropical forests and aquatic environments.

## Key White Rot Genera and Species

Phanerochaete chrysosporium is the model white-rot organism, first
shown to degrade lignin and polymeric dyes. It produces lignin
peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, and hydrogen peroxide-generating
enzymes under nitrogen-limited conditions.

Trametes versicolor often surpasses P. chrysosporium in degradation
rate and extent. Pleurotus species produce versatile ligninolytic
systems; P. ostreatus achieves high decolorization efficiency.

Ganoderma species including reishi (G. lucidum) and artist conk
(G. applanatum) are potent white rotters. Ceriporiopsis subvermispora
is valued for selective lignin degradation in biopulping.

Bjerkandera adusta reduces toxicity of textile dyes. Irpex lacteus
achieves ninety to one hundred percent removal of reactive dyes
through manganese peroxidase-dependent mechanisms.

## Key Brown Rot Genera and Species

Serpula lacrymans, the dry rot fungus, is the most economically
destructive brown rot species in the built environment. It produces
extensive rhizomorphs that pipe water through masonry for many metres.

Meruliporia incrassata causes catastrophic building damage in North
America. A single established mycelium can colonize an entire home
without producing a single spore.

Laetiporus sulphureus, the chicken of the woods, is a conspicuous
brown rot species on standing trees and fallen timber. Fomitopsis
species include the agarikon (F. officinalis) of old-growth forests.

Gloeophyllum species are important brown rotters of coniferous wood.
Phaeolus schweinitzii causes distinctive cubical rot in conifer roots.

## Ligninolytic Enzyme Systems in Detail

Lignin peroxidase (LiP) oxidizes non-phenolic aromatic structures
that constitute approximately ninety percent of lignin bonds. It uses
hydrogen peroxide as a co-substrate and veratryl alcohol as a redox
mediator produced simultaneously by the fungus.

Manganese peroxidase (MnP) oxidizes Mn(II) to Mn(III), which diffuses
into the lignin matrix to oxidize phenolic compounds. This enzyme is
unique to white rot fungi and essential for complete mineralization.

Laccase is a multicopper oxidase that attacks phenolic subunits
directly. With chemical mediators, it extends activity to non-phenolic
structures, dramatically expanding its substrate range.

Hydrogen peroxide generation is critical for all peroxidase activity.
White rot fungi produce H2O2 through glyoxal oxidase, aryl alcohol
oxidase, and methanol oxidase.

Regulation responds to nutrient limitation. Nitrogen starvation
triggers ligninolytic gene expression, linking enzyme production to
secondary metabolism.

## Cellulase Enzyme Systems

Cellulases are multi-enzyme complexes that hydrolyze beta-1,4
glycosidic bonds in cellulose. Three major classes work
synergistically: endoglucanases attacking internal bonds,
exoglucanases cleaving cellobiose from chain ends, and
beta-glucosidases hydrolyzing cellobiose to glucose.

Trichoderma species are the most prolific known cellulase sources.
Hemicellulases target the heterogeneous hemicellulose fraction,
including xylanases, mannanases, and arabinanases.

Brown rot fungi rely on cellulases after Fenton chemistry has
depolymerized crystalline cellulose. White rot fungi produce
cellulases alongside their ligninolytic complement.

Regulation is tightly controlled by carbon availability. Readily
metabolizable carbon sources repress cellulase expression through
carbon catabolite repression.

## Fungal Succession in Decomposing Wood

Fungal communities undergo predictable successional changes during
decomposition. Early colonizers are fast-growing opportunists with
broad substrate ranges that arrive through airborne spores or insect
vectors.

Boddy and Rayner identified twelve basidiomycete species dominating
oak canopies, with distinct pioneer and secondary colonizers.
Secondary species replace pioneers as resource quality changes.

Combative species such as Stereum hirsutum maintain territory through
chemical antagonism. As decomposition progresses, communities shift
toward species adapted to more degraded substrates.

Nutrient immobilization occurs as fungi accumulate nitrogen and
phosphorus. At eighty percent mass loss, nitrogen immobilization of
forty-three to one hundred thirty-nine milligrams per gram has been
measured, making decaying wood a biological hotspot.

Wood-boring beetles inoculate wood with fungal spores during
egg-laying, establishing mutualistic insect-fungus relationships.

## Cord-Forming Fungi and Resource Translocation

Cord-forming basidiomycetes connect and translocate resources across
the forest floor through aggregated hyphal structures. Strands
develop as hyphal assemblages serving as exploratory structures.

Rhizomorphs are larger, waterproofed structures enclosing a central
pipe for water and nutrient transport. They can extend for many
metres, piping water from wet soil to dry wood.

Radiotracer studies show nutrients scavenged at foraging fronts are
translocated to support growth. Mycelial networks are continuously
remodeled in response to nutritional cues.

This gives filamentous fungi a decisive advantage over
unit-restricted decomposers such as bacteria. Regressing mycelium
redistributes biomass from exhausted zones to active fronts.

Cord-forming species like Serpula lacrymans bridge dry gaps between
moist resources, enabling colonization of structurally isolated wood.

## Wood Decay in Forest Carbon Cycling

Wood represents a substantially different carbon pool from leaf
litter due to its high C:N ratio, lignin content, and physical
structure. In Douglas fir forests, tree biomass constitutes
seventy-eight to seventy-nine percent of standing stock.

Coarse woody debris can persist for decades to centuries. White and
brown rot fungi produce fundamentally different carbon signatures.

Brown rot leaves more recalcitrant lignin residues that accumulate as
stable soil organic matter. White rot more completely mineralizes
woody carbon to CO2 but produces less residual organic matter.

Mycorrhizal fungi can reduce litter decomposition rates through
nutrient competition with decomposer communities. Stable isotope
signatures reveal that fungal species using more decomposed material
show higher nitrogen-15 values.

## Indoor Wood Decay and Building Deterioration

Wood-decay fungi colonizing buildings represent a significant threat
to built structures. Serpula lacrymans, the dry rot fungus, causes
extensive property damage across temperate regions.

In advanced decay, basidiomes as large as surfboards appear on beam
surfaces, producing massive spore quantities. Individual fruiting
bodies measuring six square metres have been documented.

Historical impact has been catastrophic. The average Royal Navy ship
life fell from twenty-five years to twelve years in the eighteenth
century due to dry rot. The Queen Charlotte was rebuilt before
launching at a cost equivalent to roughly 2.5 billion dollars today.

Possible biblical references appear in Leviticus 14:33-53, describing
a fretting leprosy of the house with greenish or reddish hollow
streaks in walls.

## Spalted Wood and Aesthetic Values

When a tree is wounded, it becomes a battleground for competing
fungi whose territorial struggles produce spalted wood. Spalting
encompasses white rot bleaching, zone lines from fungal territorial
boundaries, and pigmentation from fungal metabolites.

Zone lines are dark melanin bands marking borders between competing
colonies. Each colony produces antagonistic compounds to defend its
territory through chemical warfare.

Spalted wood is highly valued by woodworkers for unique patterns that
cannot be artificially replicated. The most prized spalted wood comes
from maple, birch, and beech colonized by multiple fungal species.

Key pigmentation species include Scytalidium, Trametes, and
Chlorociboria. Controlled spalting enables commercial production of
decorative wood.

## Wood Decay Fungi in Bioremediation

The enzymatic capabilities of wood-decay fungi extend far beyond wood
to a remarkable range of environmental pollutants. White rot fungi
are the most effective known biological agents for degrading
recalcitrant contaminants.

Their nonspecific ligninolytic systems oxidize PAHs, PCBs, textile
dyes, explosives, pesticides, and pharmaceutical residues. The same
radical chemistry attacking lignin also breaks bonds in pollutants.

Phanerochaete chrysosporium achieves complete decolorization of
multiple synthetic dyes. Trametes versicolor removes ninety-five to
ninety-eight percent of reactive textile dyes.

Brown rot fungi contribute through Fenton chemistry, degrading PAHs
and chlorinated compounds that resist enzymatic attack. Environmental
factors including nitrogen limitation, agitation, and pH strongly
influence outcomes.

## Implications for Mushroom Cultivation

Many commercially important mushrooms are lignicolous species.
Shiitake, oyster, lion's mane, reishi, and maitake all employ
wood-decay strategies in their life cycles.

Understanding decay type informs substrate selection. White rot
species such as shiitake preferentially degrade lignin, making them
suited to sawdust and log substrates.

Brown rot species require formulations emphasizing accessible
cellulose. This explains why different species perform differently on
identical substrates.

Pasteurization and sterilization protocols must account for the
successional biology of the target species. Spawn rates and
supplementation strategies are optimized by understanding enzymatic
capabilities.

## Research Frontiers and Emerging Discoveries

Some wood-decay fungi participate in direct extracellular electron
transfer, analogous to bacterial processes. Phanerochaete can
transfer electrons to humic acids, extending metabolic reach into
anoxic zones where oxygen-dependent peroxidases cannot function.

Genomic studies confirm the gene loss hypothesis for brown rot
evolution. Comparative genomics shows absent or truncated
ligninolytic peroxidase genes in brown rot lineages.

Metatranscriptomics reveals how fungal communities coordinate
enzymatic activities during decomposition. Different species express
complementary enzyme sets, functioning as collective decomposition
consortia.

Climate change is altering fungal distribution and activity. Warmer
temperatures accelerate decomposition, while changing moisture shifts
competitive balances between white rot, brown rot, and soft rot.

Enzyme engineering is expanding practical applications. Directed
evolution of laccases and peroxidases aims to create biocatalysts
with enhanced stability for industrial bioremediation.
