# Wood-Rot Fungi — White Rot, Brown Rot, and Decay Mechanisms

Wood-rot fungi are the primary biological agents responsible for
decomposing woody biomass in terrestrial ecosystems. These filamentous
fungi, predominantly basidiomycetes and some ascomycetes, possess
specialized enzyme systems capable of breaking down lignocellulose —
the complex structural polymer that gives wood its strength and
resistance to degradation.

Their activity drives global carbon cycling by returning the carbon
locked in trees back to the atmosphere and soil. Without wood-rot
fungi, dead timber would accumulate indefinitely, sequestering
nutrients and disrupting ecosystem function. An estimated ten percent
of terrestrial net primary production passes through fungal wood
decay pathways each year.

## Wood Composition and Lignocellulose Structure

Wood is composed of three major structural polymers. Cellulose
accounts for roughly forty to fifty percent of wood mass and forms
linear chains of beta-1,4-linked glucose units organized into
crystalline microfibrils of extraordinary tensile strength.
Hemicellulose constitutes twenty to thirty percent and is a
heterogeneous group of branched polysaccharides including xylan,
glucomannan, and arabinan that cross-link cellulose microfibrils.

Lignin makes up the remaining twenty to thirty percent and is the
most recalcitrant component. It is an irregular, three-dimensional
phenylpropanoid polymer that fills the spaces between cellulose and
hemicellulose, providing compressive strength and resistance to
microbial attack. Its random structure, formed through radical
coupling of phenylpropanoid units, contains no regularly repeating
bonds that enzymes can easily target.

This recalcitrance is the primary reason lignocellulosic biomass
persists in the environment. No single enzyme can cleave all the
bond types in lignin, requiring a coordinated suite of oxidative
enzymes that only certain fungi possess.

## Classification of Wood Decay Types

Wood-rot fungi are classified into three principal decay types based
on their biochemical strategies and the residual materials they
produce. White rot, brown rot, and soft rot represent fundamentally
different approaches to the same challenge: extracting energy and
nutrients from lignocellulose.

These categories reflect a continuum rather than rigid boundaries.
Some species produce overlapping decay patterns, leaving mottled
white and brown discoloration in colonized wood. Understanding the
decay type is essential for identifying the responsible fungus,
predicting structural consequences, and harnessing fungal enzymes
for practical applications.

## White Rot Fungi — Lignin Degraders

White rot fungi are a polyphyletic group of basidiomycetes and some
ascomycetes characterized by their unique ability to degrade all
major components of wood, including lignin, cellulose, and
hemicellulose. They are named for the bleached white appearance of
colonized wood, resulting from selective removal of brown lignin
pigments.

White rot fungi are the only organisms on Earth capable of
substantial lignin mineralization. Phylogenomic analyses suggest
that the ancestral condition of Agaricomycetes was likely white rot,
with brown rot and ectomycorrhizal lifestyles evolving later through
loss of lignin-degrading enzyme gene families.

Key genera include Phanerochaete, Trametes, Pleurotus, Bjerkandera,
Ceriporiopsis, and Ganoderma. White rot is found across diverse
fungal lineages, particularly within the orders Polyporales,
Hymenochaetales, Agaricales, and Russulales.

### Selective Versus Simultaneous White Rot

White rot fungi employ two principal decay strategies that exist on
a continuum. Selective white rot preferentially removes lignin and
hemicellulose while leaving cellulose largely intact, producing a
fibrous, bleached residue. This strategy is more common in species
like Phanerochaete chrysosporium and Ceriporiopsis subvermispora.

Simultaneous white rot degrades all wood components at comparable
rates, resulting in uniform erosion and eventual complete
decomposition. This pattern is typical of Trametes versicolor and
many other common polypores.

The selective strategy has attracted considerable industrial interest
for biopulping, because it removes lignin while preserving cellulose
fibers for paper production. Both strategies depend on the same core
ligninolytic enzyme systems, with differences in timing, regulation,
and enzyme ratios.

## Brown Rot Fungi — Cellulose Specialists

Brown rot fungi represent a convergently evolved strategy found in
roughly seven percent of all mushroom-forming fungi. Of these brown
rot species, approximately seventy percent are polypores. Rather than
degrading lignin, brown rot fungi primarily attack cellulose and
hemicellulose, leaving the brownish lignin behind as a residual
material.

The result is characteristically checkered, cubical cracking and
shrinking in wood — a pattern commonly observed on downed conifer
trees in forest ecosystems. Brown rot fungi are particularly
important in coniferous and boreal forests, where they function as
the dominant decomposers of conifer timber.

Notable brown rot genera include Serpula, Gloeophyllum, Piptoporus,
Postia, Laetiporus, Phaeolus, and Fomitopsis. The dry rot fungus
Serpula lacrymais is perhaps the most economically significant brown
rot species, causing extensive damage to buildings worldwide.

### The Fenton Reaction Mechanism

Brown rot fungi employ a fundamentally different biochemical strategy
from white rot species. Rather than producing ligninolytic enzymes,
they generate hydroxyl radicals through the Fenton reaction. This
non-enzymatic oxidative system depolymerizes cellulose by producing
highly reactive hydroxyl radicals from hydrogen peroxide and ferrous
iron.

The Fenton reaction converts crystalline cellulose into more
accessible, amorphous oligomers that can then be further hydrolyzed
by conventional cellulase enzymes. Brown rot fungi also produce
oxalic acid, which lowers the pH of colonized wood and helps
solubilize iron for the reaction.

This two-stage process — initial oxidative depolymerization followed
by enzymatic hydrolysis — is critical because it allows brown rot
fungi to rapidly reduce the degree of polymerization of wood
cellulose without needing to produce the complex ligninolytic enzyme
systems of white rot species.
