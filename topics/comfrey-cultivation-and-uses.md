# Comfrey Cultivation and Uses

## Introduction and Botany

Comfrey is a perennial herb in the family Boraginaceae, native to Europe and temperate regions of western Asia. The genus name *Symphytum* derives from the Greek *symphyo*, meaning "to unite," referring to its traditional reputation for knitting bones and healing wounds. The common name likely comes from the Latin *con firma*, "made firm."

Plants form a large basal rosette of hairy, lanceolate leaves that can reach 30–60 cm in length. The mature plant produces bell-shaped flowers in pendent clusters, typically white, purple, pink, or cream depending on the species and cultivar. Flowering occurs from late spring through midsummer. The root system is extensive: a deep, branching taproot penetrates 1.5–2 m or more into the subsoil, with thick, dark, mucilaginous roots that are difficult to eradicate once established.

Comfrey's primary value in horticulture and permaculture stems from this deep root system, which mines minerals from subsoil horizons and deposits them in above-ground biomass. Combined with rapid vegetative growth—producing multiple leaf harvests per season—comfrey functions as an on-site fertilizer factory and biomass source.

## Species and Varieties

### *Symphytum officinale* (Common Comfrey)

The wild European species. Variable in flower color (white to purple). Seed-producing and potentially invasive in disturbed soils. Less vigorous vegetative growth than cultivated hybrids. Still widely used in traditional herbal medicine.

### *Symphytum x uplandicum* (Russian Comfrey)

A naturally occurring hybrid between *S. officinale* and *S. asperum*. Sterile triploid; produces negligible viable seed. This sterility is a significant advantage in garden settings because it eliminates self-seeding and reduces invasive spread. Most cultivated comfrey varieties sold for permaculture and garden use belong to this hybrid group.

### Bocking Cultivars

In the 1950s, Lawrence D. Hills at the Henry Doubleday Research Association (HDRA) in Bocking, Essex, conducted trials on numerous comfrey strains. He selected and named several for specific qualities:

| Cultivar | Sterility | Primary Use | Notable Traits |
|----------|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| **Bocking 14** | Sterile | Biomass/fertilizer | Highest potash content; most vigorous growth; preferred for compost and liquid feed |
| **Bocking 4** | Sterile | Biomass/fertilizer | Good yield; slightly less vigorous than 14; reliable performer |
| **Bocking 1** | Sterile | Biomass | Good general-purpose variety |
| **Bocking 2** | Sterile | Ornamental | Attractive violet flowers; lower biomass yield |
| **Bocking 4** | Sterile | Medicinal | Higher protein and allantoin content in leaves |

Bocking 14 is the standard recommendation for permaculture systems focused on fertilizer production. Its leaves have been measured at approximately 3.1% nitrogen, 1.1% phosphorus, and 6.7% potassium on a dry-weight basis, though these figures vary with soil conditions and harvest timing.

## Cultivation Requirements

### Soil

Comfrey tolerates a wide range of soil types, from heavy clay to sandy loam. It performs best in deep, moisture-retentive, fertile soils with a pH between 6.0 and 7.5. Extremely waterlogged soils can cause root rot. Poor, shallow soils will still support comfrey but with reduced biomass output.

The plant's deep taproot makes it drought-tolerant once established, though consistent moisture produces the greatest leaf yields. Comfrey is one of the few productive plants that can be sited in partial shade (as little as 4–5 hours of direct sun) while still producing useful biomass, though full sun yields maximum production.

### Sun Exposure

- **Full sun** (6+ hours): Optimal biomass production
- **Partial shade** (4–6 hours): Acceptable yields; useful for under-planting fruit trees
- **Full shade**: Poor growth; not recommended

### Planting and Establishment

1. Prepare the planting site by removing perennial weeds. Comfrey does not compete well with established grass or weed mats during its first year.
2. Dig a hole 15–20 cm deep and incorporate well-rotted manure or compost.
3. Place root cuttings or crown divisions horizontally, 2–5 cm below the soil surface.
4. Space plants 60–90 cm apart in all directions (0.5–1 m² per plant).
5. Water thoroughly after planting and keep moist for the first growing season.
6. Do not harvest during the first year to allow the root system to establish.

## Propagation Methods

### Crown Division

Divide established plants in early spring or autumn. Lift the entire crown and root mass, then cut into sections each containing at least one growing bud and a portion of root. Replant immediately. This is the simplest method and provides transplants that will reach full production within one growing season.

Each established comfrey plant can typically be divided into 4–8 viable sections depending on root mass.

### Root Cuttings

The most common propagation method for Bocking cultivars:

1. Dig up a portion of root from an established plant in spring or autumn.
2. Cut the root into sections 2–5 cm long. Thicker roots (pencil diameter or greater) give better results.
3. Lay cuttings horizontally in a nursery bed, 2–5 cm deep, 15 cm apart.
4. Cover with soil and keep moist. Shoots emerge in 3–6 weeks.
5. Thin or transplant seedlings when they reach 10–15 cm tall.

Root cuttings can be taken at any time during the dormant season. Each 2 cm section of root is potentially viable, which is the same trait that makes comfrey difficult to eradicate from sites where it is no longer wanted.

### Offset Planting (Thongs)

A "thong" is a section of root about 5–8 cm long with a bud attached. Plant vertically with the bud 2 cm below the surface. This method produces a plant slightly faster than plain root cuttings but requires more careful handling.

### Seed

Only practical for *S. officinale* (the fertile species). Sow in spring, barely covering the seed, and keep at 15–20°C. Germination is irregular and can take 1–3 months. Seed-grown plants are genetically variable. This method is not used for Bocking cultivars because they are sterile.

## Growth Habits and Lifecycle

Comfrey emerges in early spring, often one of the first perennials to break dormancy. Growth is rapid: a mature Bocking 14 plant produces its first cuttable leaves within 4–6 weeks of emergence. Under good conditions, 4–5 leaf harvests per season are possible.

The flowering stalk rises from the center of the rosette to 60–120 cm, bearing the characteristic pendent bells. Cutting flower stalks back redirects energy into leaf production, increasing biomass yield. Some gardeners allow flowering once per season to support pollinators, then cut subsequent stalks.

Growth slows in late summer and autumn. Above-ground growth dies back with hard frost. The plant overwinters as a dormant crown and root system.

### Yield Estimates

| Condition | Leaves per harvest (fresh) | Harvests per year | Annual total (fresh) |
|-----------|---------------------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Poor soil, partial shade | 0.5–1 kg | 2–3 | 1–3 kg |
| Average garden soil, full sun | 1–2 kg | 3–4 | 3–8 kg |
| Rich soil, full sun, irrigated | 2–3 kg | 4–5 | 8–15 kg |

Dry weight is approximately 15–20% of fresh weight.

## Medicinal Uses

### Active Constituents

- **Allantoin** (0.3–1.0%): A cell-proliferant that promotes wound healing and tissue regeneration. Stimulates fibroblast activity and epithelial cell migration in damaged tissue.
- **Mucilage** (high): Provides a soothing, protective layer on mucous membranes and skin.
- **Rosmarinic acid**: Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant.
- **Tannins**: Mild astringent properties.
- **Silica**: Supports connective tissue health.
- **Vitamin B12**: Present in the leaves (unusual for a plant), though the amounts are not nutritionally significant for humans.

### Traditional Applications

External use of comfrey has a long documented history across European herbal traditions. The primary historical uses are:

- **Wound healing**: Crushed leaves or poultices of root applied to cuts, abrasions, and minor burns. The allantoin content accelerates tissue repair.
- **Bone and joint injuries**: Comfrey was historically called "knitbone." Poultices were applied to fractures and sprains. Clinical studies have shown that topical comfrey preparations reduce pain and improve mobility in osteoarthritis of the knee and acute ankle sprains, though the number of rigorous trials remains limited.
- **Bruises and inflammation**: Applied as a compress or ointment for contusions, insect bites, and inflammatory skin conditions.
- **Skin conditions**: Comfrey-infused oils and salves used for eczema, psoriasis, and dry skin, primarily due to the emollient mucilage content.

### Preparations

- **Comfrey oil/salve**: Dried leaves or root macerated in carrier oil (olive, sunflower) for 4–6 weeks, then strained and combined with beeswax.
- **Poultice**: Fresh leaves bruised and applied directly to the affected area.
- **Compress**: Strong decoction of root soaked into a cloth and applied warm.
- **Comfrey cream**: Commercial preparations (available in some countries) standardized to allantoin content.

### Important Safety Warning: Pyrrolizidine Alkaloids

Comfrey contains hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs), primarily symphytine and lycopsamine. These compounds cause veno-occlusive disease of the liver and are potentially carcinogenic. The PA content varies by plant part and variety:

| Plant Part | PA Concentration (approx.) |
|------------|---------------------------|
| Root | 0.1–0.4% (highest) |
| Mature leaves | 0.01–0.03% |
| Young leaves | Lower than mature |

**Regulatory status**: Several countries (Australia, Germany, Canada) have restricted or banned the sale of oral comfrey products. The US FDA issued an advisory against internal use in 2001.

**Practical guidance for gardeners**:
- Do not consume comfrey internally in any form (tea, salad, capsule).
- External use of leaf preparations on unbroken skin for limited periods is the generally accepted practice among herbalists who still work with comfrey.
- Root preparations contain higher PA concentrations and carry greater risk even externally.
- Pregnant and nursing women, children, and individuals with liver disease should avoid all comfrey use.
- PA-free comfrey cultivars have been developed but are not widely available.

## Garden Uses

### Comfrey Tea (Liquid Fertilizer)

One of the most practical uses for comfrey in the garden. The high potassium and moderate nitrogen content make comfrey tea particularly valuable for fruiting and flowering plants.

**Method**:
1. Harvest fresh comfrey leaves (1–2 kg).
2. Pack into a container (bucket or barrel) and weigh down with a stone or board.
3. Cover with water (optional: some practitioners prefer to let the leaves decompose without added water, producing a thicker concentrate).
4. Cover loosely to exclude insects but allow gas escape.
5. Ferment for 3–6 weeks. The result is a dark, foul-smelling liquid.
6. Dilute 1:10 to 1:15 with water before application. Use as a soil drench every 2 weeks during the growing season.

The odor is significant—place the brewing container away from living areas and downwind. Sealed anaerobic systems with gas-release valves reduce the smell.

**Typical NPK comparison (approximate, dry weight basis)**:

| Material | N | P | K |
|----------|---|---|---|
| Comfrey leaves | 3.1% | 1.1% | 6.7% |
| Farmyard manure | 0.6% | 0.2% | 0.5% |
| Garden compost | 1.5% | 0.5% | 1.0% |
| Well-rotted farmyard manure | 0.6% | 0.7% | 0.8% |

Comfrey is not a complete fertilizer substitute—it is relatively low in phosphorus and trace minerals compared to a balanced organic fertilizer program—but its potassium content exceeds most other on-farm biomass sources.

### Mulch

Fresh comfrey leaves spread 5–10 cm thick around plants provide a nitrogen- and potassium-rich mulch that breaks down within 2–4 weeks. Particularly effective around:

- Tomatoes, peppers, and other fruiting vegetables
- Potatoes (apply after hilling)
- Fruit bushes and trees
- Curcurbits

The leaves decompose quickly enough that they rarely need to be incorporated. In no-till systems, comfrey mulch is one of the most effective ways to feed crops without disturbing soil structure.

### Compost Activator

Adding comfrey leaves to compost piles accelerates decomposition. The high nitrogen and moisture content feed thermophilic bacteria. Layer comfrey leaves between carbon-rich materials (straw, cardboard, dry leaves) at roughly 1 part comfrey to 3–5 parts carbon material. This is comparable in effect to adding fresh manure or a commercial compost starter.

### Dynamic Accumulator

The term "dynamic accumulator" describes plants whose deep roots mine minerals from subsoil horizons and bring them into biological cycling through leaf drop and decomposition. Comfrey is one of the most widely cited dynamic accumulators in permaculture literature.

Minerals accumulated in above-ground biomass (dry weight basis):

| Mineral | Concentration | Comparison to Average Soil |
|---------|--------------|--------------------------|
| Potassium (K) | 6.7% | Very high |
| Nitrogen (N) | 3.1% | High |
| Calcium (Ca) | 2.0% | Moderate-high |
| Phosphorus (P) | 1.1% | Moderate |
| Iron (Fe) | Trace | Variable |
| Silica (Si) | Moderate | Moderate |

**Caveat**: The dynamic accumulator concept, while useful in permaculture design, is based partly on limited data. The mineral content of comfrey varies significantly with soil type, depth, and local geology. On potassium-deficient soils, comfrey leaves may not be particularly potassium-rich. The deep roots certainly access subsoil minerals unavailable to shallow-rooted plants, but the extent of net nutrient contribution to surface soils depends on how the biomass is managed.

## Companion Planting

Comfrey integrates well into diverse planting schemes:

- **Under fruit trees**: The standard permaculture guild placement. Comfrey's deep taproot does not compete significantly with tree roots in the topsoil zone, while the leaf biomass feeds the tree through mulch and decomposition.
- **Around nitrogen-fixing plants**: Pair comfrey with leguminous plants (beans, peas, clover, elaeagnus) to capture both fixed nitrogen and mined minerals.
- **Berry bushes**: Comfrey planted between and around currant, gooseberry, and blueberry bushes provides potassium-rich mulch.
- **Avoid planting near**: Root vegetables (carrots, parsnips) in the same bed, as comfrey's root system may physically interfere with root crop development.

## Pest Resistance

Comfrey is remarkably pest-free. Few significant insect pests or diseases affect established plants:

- **Comfrey rust** (*Melampsorella symphyti*): The most common disease. Causes orange pustules on the undersides of leaves. Generally cosmetic; does not kill the plant. Remove affected leaves and destroy them (do not compost).
- **Slugs and snails**: May feed on young shoots in spring but generally prefer other plants.
- **Blackfly/aphids**: Occasionally found on flower stalks. Not a significant problem for biomass production.

The hairy leaves are a natural deterrent to many leaf-feeding insects. Comfrey can serve as a trap crop in some systems, though this use is not well documented.

## Invasive Potential

This is a critical consideration for gardeners and land managers:

- ***S. officinale*** (common comfrey): Produces viable seed and can spread aggressively. Not recommended for gardens.
- ***S. x uplandicum*** (Bocking cultivars): Sterile triploids that do not set viable seed. Spread is limited to vegetative propagation by root fragments.

**Eradication is extremely difficult.** Any fragment of root left in the soil can regenerate. Mechanical cultivation (rototilling) chops and distributes root pieces, worsening the problem. If removal is necessary:

1. Repeatedly cut the foliage to exhaust root reserves (takes 2+ years).
2. Dig out all root material to a depth of 30+ cm.
3. Cover the area with light-excluding material (black plastic, thick cardboard) for at least one full growing season.
4. Do not put any comfrey root or leaf material in compost if there is any risk of live root fragments.

Site comfrey carefully from the start. Permanent bed positions (along fences, under trees, in dedicated fertility strips) are preferable to mixed annual vegetable beds where soil disturbance is frequent.

## Harvesting Techniques

- **First year**: Do not harvest. Allow the plant to establish its root system.
- **Subsequent years**: Begin harvesting when the plant reaches 30–45 cm tall. Cut leaves 5 cm above the crown to avoid damaging the growing point.
- **Frequency**: Every 4–6 weeks during the growing season, typically 4–5 cuts per year.
- **Timing**: Cut before flowering for maximum nutrient content. Flowering redirects nutrients to reproductive structures.
- **Final cut**: Stop harvesting 4–6 weeks before the first expected hard frost to allow the plant to build root reserves for winter.
- **Tools**: Shears, sickle, or machete. Wear gloves; the leaf hairs can irritate skin.

## Overwintering

Comfrey is extremely cold-hardy. Established plants survive winter temperatures to -30°C or lower. Above-ground growth dies back with hard frost. The crown and root system remain dormant until spring.

No special winter protection is needed in temperate climates. In regions with very mild winters, comfrey may remain semi-evergreen, continuing slow growth through the cooler months.

A late-autumn mulch of autumn leaves or straw around the crown can help retain soil moisture and moderate soil temperature fluctuations, but this is optional for established plants.

## Common Problems and Solutions

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---------|-------|----------|
| Poor establishment | Competition from weeds, dry soil | Keep weed-free and watered during first year |
| Yellowing leaves | Nitrogen deficiency (rare), waterlogging | Improve drainage; add compost |
| Rust spots on leaves | Comfrey rust fungus | Remove affected leaves; improve air circulation |
| Slow growth | Shallow or compacted soil, insufficient sun | Relocate to better site; break up compacted layers |
| Rotting crown | Waterlogged soil | Improve drainage; do not plant in low-lying boggy areas |
| Failure to establish from cuttings | Root pieces too small, planted too deep, or too dry | Use pencil-thick root cuttings 2–5 cm long; keep moist |

## Uses in Permaculture Food Forests

Comfrey occupies the herb layer in food forest design. Its placement in the classic seven-layer model is as a nutrient-cycling support species rather than a direct food crop.

**Design considerations**:

- Plant comfrey in a ring at the drip line of fruit trees (1–2 m from the trunk). This positions the plant where it accesses tree-root-zone nutrients without significant competition.
- Use comfrey as biomass corridors between tree rows. A 30–60 cm wide strip of comfrey between fruit trees provides a steady supply of mulch material that can be cut and thrown onto the tree root zone.
- Integrate comfrey into swale berms and on-contour plantings where its deep roots help stabilize soil.
- Plant comfrey near compost toilets or animal pens where nutrient loading is high. The plant will absorb excess nitrogen and potassium, reducing leaching.

**Yield in food forests**: A mature Bocking 14 plant in a food forest guild typically produces 2–5 kg of fresh leaf material per harvest, 4–5 times per season. A single row of 10 plants spaced 75 cm apart can produce 80–250 kg of fresh biomass annually—enough to mulch a substantial orchard.

## Integration with No-Till Gardens

Comfrey is one of the most useful plants in no-till and minimal-till systems because it provides fertility without requiring soil disturbance:

1. **Sheet mulch ingredient**: Layer comfrey leaves directly onto beds when building new sheet mulch (lasagna) gardens. The leaves decompose quickly, contributing nitrogen and potassium to the soil profile.
2. **Chop-and-drop mulch**: Cut comfrey in place and let it decompose where it falls, or carry cut material to crop beds. No digging or turning required.
3. **Nutrient cycling**: In permanent bed systems, comfrey planted at the edges of beds produces a continuous supply of fertility that is carried onto the beds as mulch.
4. **Soil structure improvement**: Comfrey roots penetrate compacted subsoil layers, creating channels that improve water infiltration and root penetration for subsequent crops. This is a slow process but beneficial over years.
5. **Living mulch pathways**: Comfrey planted in permanent pathways between raised beds produces mulch that is easily raked onto adjacent beds, while suppressing pathway weeds through dense shade.

**Practical no-till layout**: Plant comfrey in a dedicated "fertility hedge" along one side of the garden, spaced 60–75 cm apart. Harvest leaves with a sickle or shears and distribute as mulch across beds. This separates the vigorous perennial comfrey from annual crop roots while maintaining easy access.

## Summary

Comfrey is a high-biomass perennial with few equals in the temperate garden for on-site nutrient cycling. Its deep taproot, rapid growth, and high potassium content make it the standard dynamic accumulator in permaculture design. The Bocking cultivars (particularly Bocking 14) provide sterile, non-invasive options that eliminate self-seeding while maximizing biomass production.

The primary cautions are the hepatotoxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids that make internal use inadvisable, and the difficulty of eradicating established plants. Both are manageable with informed site selection and external-only use of the herb.

For growers focused on building soil health, reducing off-farm fertilizer inputs, and integrating perennial support species into food production systems, comfrey remains one of the most productive and versatile plants available.
