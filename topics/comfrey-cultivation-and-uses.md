# Comfrey Cultivation and Uses (Symphytum spp.)

## Overview

Related topics: [[moringa-oleifera-cultivation-uses-and-nutrition]] [[baobab-cultivation-ecology-and-uses]] [[lupin-cultivation-and-uses]]
Comfrey (Symphytum spp.) is one of the most versatile and productive
perennial plants in temperate agriculture and permaculture systems.

Often called "knitbone" for its traditional wound-healing properties,
comfrey serves simultaneously as a dynamic mineral accumulator, a
high-yielding biomass crop, a potent liquid fertilizer source, and a
valuable pollinator plant.

Its deep taproot penetrates subsoil layers up to three meters, mining
minerals that are unavailable to shallow-rooted garden vegetables.

The leaves concentrate these minerals — particularly potassium, calcium,
and magnesium — at levels that rival commercial organic amendments.

No single plant better embodies the permaculture principle of each
element serving multiple functions.

## Botanical Profile and Species

Comfrey belongs to the Boraginaceae family, which includes borage and
forget-me-nots.

The genus Symphytum contains approximately 35 species native to Europe
and western Asia.

Two species dominate cultivation: [[symphytum-officinale]] (common comfrey)
and Symphytum x uplandicum (Russian comfrey), a naturally occurring
hybrid of S. officinale and S. asperum.

Common comfrey grows 60-120 cm tall with broadly lanceolate leaves
covered in bristly hairs.

The bell-shaped flowers range from cream to purple and appear in
nodding cymes from late spring through summer.

Russian comfrey is generally larger and more vigorous, producing
greater biomass per cutting.

The root system is the defining feature: a thick, deep taproot that
can penetrate clay subsoils and shatter compacted layers over time.

All parts of the plant contain mucilage, tannins, allantoin, and
pyrrolizidine alkaloids.

## Bocking Cultivars and Selection

The Henry Doubleday Research Association (HDRA), now Garden Organic,
developed the Bocking series of sterile Russian comfrey cultivars in
the 1950s under Lawrence Hills.

Bocking 14 is the most widely grown cultivar worldwide, prized for its
high leaf yield, sterility, and superior potassium content.

Because Bocking 14 produces no viable seed, it cannot become an
invasive weed — a critical advantage for managed landscapes.

Other Bocking cultivars (1, 2, 4, 7, and others) were developed for
specific traits such as early growth or particular mineral profiles.

Bocking 4 has slightly higher protein content and is sometimes
preferred for livestock forage applications.

Common comfrey (S. officinale) sets viable seed and can self-sow
aggressively, making it less suitable for garden use despite equal
fertilizer value.

For most homestead and permaculture applications, Bocking 14 remains
the standard recommendation.

## History and Ethnobotany

Comfrey has been used medicinally since antiquity, with records from
Dioscorides and Pliny the Elder describing its wound-healing properties.

The common name "comfrey" derives from the Latin "confervere," meaning
to grow together — a reference to its reputation for healing broken
bones.

Traditional European herbalism employed comfrey poultices for sprains,
bruises, and skin ulcers for centuries.

Lawrence Hills pioneered modern comfrey cultivation in the 1950s,
establishing trials at the HDRA that demonstrated its value as a
fodder crop and fertilizer source.

Hills documented yields of up to 100 tonnes of fresh leaf per hectare
per year under intensive management.

Soviet agricultural researchers independently studied comfrey as a
protein-rich cattle fodder during the same period.

The plant's transition from medicinal herb to permaculture staple
accelerated with [[bill-mollison]]'s writings in the 1970s and 1980s.

## Dynamic Accumulator Properties

Comfrey's deep taproot mines minerals from subsoil layers that are
entirely inaccessible to most garden vegetables, whose roots seldom
penetrate below 30-60 cm.

The accumulated minerals are concentrated in the leaf tissue at levels
that make comfrey leaves a potent nutrient source when decomposed.

Potassium levels in comfrey leaf tissue reach 3-7% on a dry weight
basis — comparable to commercial potassium fertilizers.

Calcium, magnesium, iron, and silica are also present in significant
concentrations.

Nitrogen content ranges from 1.5-3% dry weight, making comfrey leaves
effective "green" material for composting.

The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of fresh comfrey leaves is approximately
12:1 to 15:1, ideal for rapid decomposition.

When comfrey leaves are used as mulch or compost input, these mined
minerals become bioavailable to other plants in the root zone.

This nutrient pumping effect is the cornerstone of comfrey's role in
permaculture nutrient cycling strategies.

## Comfrey Tea and Liquid Fertilizer

Comfrey tea is one of the most potent homemade liquid fertilizers
available to organic gardeners.

The high potassium concentration makes it especially valuable for
fruiting crops such as tomatoes, peppers, and fruit trees.
