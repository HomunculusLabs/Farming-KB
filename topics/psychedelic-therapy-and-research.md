## Psychedelic Therapy and Research

Psychedelic therapy represents one of the most remarkable stories in the history
of medicine — a field that showed extraordinary promise in the 1950s and 1960s,
was shut down for decades by political forces, and has now re-emerged as one of
the most exciting frontiers in psychiatry. Unlike conventional psychiatric
medications, which are typically taken daily to manage symptoms, psychedelic
therapy uses a small number of carefully guided sessions with compounds like
psilocybin, MDMA, and ketamine to produce transformative psychological
experiences that can lead to lasting relief from conditions such as
post-traumatic stress disorder, treatment-resistant depression, end-of-life
anxiety, and substance use disorders.

By 1947, Sandoz was distributing LSD as "Delysid" to researchers worldwide,
and the compound quickly attracted interest from psychiatrists. British
psychiatrist Humphry Osmond, working in Saskatchewan, Canada, coined the term
"psychedelic" in 1954 — from the Greek psyche (mind) and delos (manifesting) —
to replace "psychotomimetic" (mimicking psychosis), which he felt
mischaracterized the experience. In 1956, Hofmann isolated psilocybin and
psilocin from Psilocybe mexicana mushrooms collected by R. Gordon Wasson in
Mexico. Wasson's 1957 Life magazine article "Seeking the Magic Mushroom"
introduced psilocybin to Western audiences and ignited public fascination.
