# Pig Husbandry and Permaculture Integration

Pigs are among the most versatile and productive livestock a homesteader
or small farmer can raise. Their intelligence, rapid growth, omnivorous
appetite, and powerful rooting behavior make them uniquely valuable beyond
simple meat production. In permaculture design, pigs function as living
tractors, land clearers, waste recyclers, and orchard sanitizers, turning
inputs that would otherwise become problems — food scraps, crop residues,
weedy brush, and fallen fruit — into high-quality protein and fertile
manure. A well-managed pig operation can transform marginal land into
productive pasture, reduce pest pressure in orchards, and close nutrient
loops that would otherwise require fossil-fueled machinery or purchased
fertilizer.

The relationship between humans and domestic pigs stretches back roughly
9,000 years, with independent domestication events in the Near East and
China. Modern breeds reflect centuries of selection for diverse purposes:
lard production, bacon, lean meat, foraging ability, and mothering
instincts. Heritage breeds in particular retain the hardiness, foraging
skill, and disease resistance that make them well-suited to pasture-based
and permaculture systems, unlike commercial breeds optimized for
confinement feeding.

This article covers the full scope of pig husbandry from a permaculture
perspective: breed selection, housing and fencing, nutrition and forage
systems, breeding and farrowing, health management, processing, and the
many ways pigs can be integrated into broader farm ecosystems. Whether you
are raising a single feeder pig for the freezer or managing a breeding
herd on rotational pasture, the principles here emphasize working with the
pig's natural behaviors rather than against them.

## Breed Selection for Homesteads and Small Farms

Choosing the right breed is the first and most consequential decision a
pig keeper makes. Heritage breeds are overwhelmingly recommended for
permaculture and homestead systems because they retain the traits that
matter most outside confinement: foraging ability, hardiness, good
mothering instincts, disease resistance, and the ability to thrive on
diverse diets including pasture and food waste.

Berkshire pigs are perhaps the most celebrated heritage breed for meat
quality. Known as Kurobuta in Japan, Berkshire pork is heavily marbled
with dark, richly flavored flesh. Berkshires are good mothers, moderate in
size, and adaptable to pasture systems. Their black coloration provides
some sunburn protection, though shade remains essential.

Tamworths are long-bodied, red-gold pigs bred originally as bacon-type
animals. They are exceptional foragers, capable of thriving on rough
pasture and woodland browse. Their long snouts make them particularly
effective rooters, which is an asset for land-clearing work but requires
careful management around desirable plantings. Tamworths are hardy, good
mothers, and active animals that benefit from ample space.

Large Black pigs are docile, excellent foragers, and produce high-quality
lard-type pork. Their black skin resists sunburn, and their calm
temperament makes them easy to handle. Gloucestershire Old Spots share
similar qualities and were traditionally raised in orchards on fallen
fruit, making them a natural choice for silvopasture integration.

Red Wattle hogs are fast-growing, hardy, and disease-resistant, with
distinctive fleshy wattles hanging from their jaws. They are critically
rare, and keeping them serves both a production and a conservation
purpose. Durocs, while more common in commercial systems, are a
reasonable terminal sire for crossbreeding programs, offering fast growth
and good feed efficiency with reddish-brown coloring.

For lard production, breeds like the Mulefoot (a rare, syndactyl breed
with a single fused hoof) and the Guinea Hog (a small, hardy African-
derived breed) offer compact animals well-suited to small acreage.
Kunekune pigs, a New Zealand breed, are among the smallest domestic pigs
and are notable for their ability to fatten on grass alone, making them
ideal for very small homesteads where grain feeding is undesirable.

Commercial breeds such as Landrace, Hampshire, and Pietrain are generally
not recommended for pasture-based systems. They have been selected for
confinement conditions, maximum lean yield on grain-heavy diets, and
large litter sizes that require intensive management. They tend to be less
hardy, less effective at foraging, and more prone to stress-related
health problems on pasture.

## Housing and Shelter Systems

Pig housing on permaculture homesteads should prioritize mobility,
natural ventilation, and protection from temperature extremes. Pigs
cannot sweat and are extremely susceptible to heat stress, making shade
and wallowing access non-negotiable in warm climates. Conversely, young
piglets require ambient temperatures of 85-95 degrees Fahrenheit during
their first week of life, dropping gradually to 70 degrees by weaning
age.

The simplest and most effective housing for pasture-based systems is the
moveable hoop house or A-frame shelter. These structures can be dragged
or skidded to fresh ground as pigs are rotated between paddocks,
preventing the accumulation of manure and mud in any single location. A
minimum of eight to ten square feet of shelter space per pig is
recommended, with deep bedding of straw or hay that pigs will burrow into
during cold weather. Carbon should be added regularly to maintain dry,
composting bedding that generates warmth through microbial activity.

Farrowing housing requires more attention. Individual farrowing pens of at
least five by seven feet provide space for the sow and her litter.
Farrowing rails — horizontal bars mounted inside the pen — prevent the
sow from accidentally lying on piglets while still allowing them to nurse.
Heat lamps or heated creep areas give newborn piglets the warmth they
need without overheating the sow. In pasture-based systems, farrowing
huts with deep straw bedding can substitute for indoor crates, though
predation protection becomes an additional consideration.

Wallows or mud areas are essential for thermoregulation during warm
months. Pigs lack functional sweat glands and rely on behavioral cooling:
wallowing in mud, seeking shade, and reducing activity during the hottest
parts of the day. A wallow can be as simple as a low spot in the pasture
that retains water, or a designated shallow basin refilled regularly. The
mud also serves as sunblock and insect repellent.
