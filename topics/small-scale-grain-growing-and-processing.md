# Small-Scale Grain Growing and Processing

Grains are the foundation of human civilization. For roughly ten thousand
years, cereals -- wheat, rice, barley, corn, oats, rye, and their
lesser-known cousins -- have provided the caloric backbone of human diets
across every inhabited continent. Today, most people source their grain
through industrial supply chains: massive monocultures, combine harvesters,
and centralized milling operations that strip grains of their bran and germ
before shipping flour that has already begun to oxidize. But a growing
movement of homesteaders, permaculturists, and small-scale farmers is
rediscovering what was once common knowledge: that grains can be grown,
harvested, processed, and milled at home, producing food of vastly superior
nutrition and flavor while building soil health and reducing dependence on
distant supply chains.

This article covers the complete cycle of small-scale grain production,
from selecting varieties and preparing beds through harvesting, threshing,
winnowing, milling, and storage. It draws on the work of Masanobu Fukuoka,
Bill Mollison, John Jeavons, Sepp Holzer, and Nicole Faires, among others,
to present a practical guide for anyone who wants to grow their own bread.

## Why Grow Your Own Grain

The case for home grain production rests on several interconnected
arguments. Nutritionally, freshly milled whole-grain flour retains the full
spectrum of vitamins, minerals, oils, and enzymes that commercial flour
loses during processing and storage. The wheat germ, comprising just three
percent of the kernel by weight, contains most of the grain's vitamin E,
B vitamins, healthy fats, and antioxidants. In commercial whole wheat
flour, these germ oils begin oxidizing within days of milling, and the
resulting flour has lost 50 to 90 percent of its vitamin E by the time it
reaches the consumer. Freshly milled flour tastes demonstrably better --
complex, slightly sweet, and nutty -- because the volatile flavor compounds
have not had time to degrade.

From a self-sufficiency standpoint, grains are unmatched in their caloric
density and storability. Whole grain berries, properly dried and stored in
rodent-proof containers, keep for six to twelve months at room temperature
and longer in cool conditions. A quarter acre of grain can produce 1,300
pounds of rice or wheat using Fukuoka's methods, enough to feed a person
for much of the year. By contrast, producing the same caloric value from
potatoes requires three times the land area, dairy six times, and beef
fifty times. For homesteaders serious about food sovereignty, grains are
the most land-efficient calorie source available.

Grains also serve essential ecological functions. Their massive root
systems -- wheat roots reach three to four feet deep -- break compacted
soil layers, contribute enormous amounts of carbon to the soil through
root exudates and root turnover, and the straw remaining after harvest is
invaluable as mulch, animal bedding, or compost material. Many grains
double as cover crops, fitting neatly into rotations with vegetables and
legumes while breaking disease cycles and suppressing weeds. In
permaculture design, grains are typically placed in Zone 3, as crops that
require less frequent attention than vegetables but still benefit from
regular management.

## Choosing Grain Varieties for the Homestead

The choice of which grains to grow depends on climate, soil, intended use,
and the scale of one's operation. The following overview covers the major
options for temperate climates, with notes on tropical and arid
adaptations.

### Wheat

Wheat is the most versatile grain for home growing and the one most
homesteaders start with. Winter wheat varieties are planted in autumn and
harvested the following summer, while spring wheat is planted in early
spring for a late summer harvest. Winter wheat generally yields better
because its root systems develop over winter, giving the plants a head
start.

Heritage varieties like Turkey Red (a hard red winter wheat brought to
Kansas by Mennonite immigrants from Crimea in the 1870s) and Wrens
Abruzzi (a soft red winter wheat) offer excellent flavor and adaptability
that modern commercial breeds, bred for machine harvesting and uniform
processing, often lack. Hard red wheats produce high-protein flour ideal
for bread, while soft white wheats make tender pastry flour. For the home
miller, growing both types provides flexibility.

On a small scale, expect yields of 5 to 15 pounds of grain per 100 square
feet, with experienced biointensive growers like John Jeavons achieving
up to 21 pounds per 100-square-foot bed. At the upper end, two 26-pound
crops in an eight-month period from just 100 square feet can yield
approximately one pound loaf of bread per week for the entire year.

### Rye

Rye is the most cold-hardy of all cereal grains and will grow in poor,
sandy, or acidic soils where wheat struggles. It germinates at
temperatures as low as 34 degrees Fahrenheit and can be planted later in
autumn than any other grain. Rye's extensive root system and
allelopathic properties make it an excellent cover crop for weed
suppression, and its early spring growth provides the first green forage
in northern climates. For eating, rye produces dense, flavorful breads
(pumpernickel and traditional sourdough rye) and can also be used for
whiskey and animal feed.

Sepp Holzer's "Brandroggen" (fire rye) is an ancient cereal variety that
demonstrates the potential of heritage grains. Holzer has championed the
growing of ancient cereals as part of his integrative farming approach,
noting that older varieties often have deeper root systems, better disease
resistance, and richer nutritional profiles than modern selections.

### Oats

Oats have the highest protein content of common grains at 12 to 14
percent and produce excellent animal bedding in addition to food. Hull-less
varieties like Tibetan and Buffalo eliminate the tedious dehulling step
that makes processing oats challenging. Oats are a cool-season crop, best
planted in early spring or early autumn, and yield 50 to 100 bushels per
acre on a field scale, or 3 to 8 pounds per 100 square feet in garden
beds. Oat straw is considered the premier bedding material for livestock
and poultry.

### Barley

Barley adapts to a wider range of soil conditions than wheat and matures
earlier. It serves dual purposes as both a food grain and a malting grain
for brewing. Mollison lists sowing rates of 65 to 120 kilograms per
hectare with yields of 2,700 to 3,500 kilograms per hectare. Fukuoka
developed an extensive barley cultivation system as part of his rice-barley
rotation, broadcasting barley seed over ripening rice in autumn and
harvesting it the following May. For home brewers, growing one's own
barley opens the door to truly local beer and connects grain production to
the fermented foods tradition.

### Corn

Corn is the easiest grain to harvest by hand and produces the highest
yields per unit area among common grains -- 10 to 25 pounds per 100 square
feet for sweet corn, and even higher for field corn grown for flour and
meal. Corn is wind-pollinated, so plantings should be in blocks of at
least four rows rather than long single rows to ensure adequate
pollination. For grain corn, dent varieties (so named because the kernels
dent as they dry) are preferred over sweet corn, as they produce harder,
drier kernels that store well and grind into excellent cornmeal. Heritage
dent varieties like Bloody Butcher, Reid's Yellow Dent, and Oaxacan Green
produce cornmeal with far more flavor and nutritional complexity than
commercial yellow cornmeal.

### Alternative Grains

Several less common grains deserve consideration for the homestead.
Buckwheat, despite its name, is not a cereal grass but a relative of
rhubarb. It matures in just 10 to 13 weeks, produces no gluten, and serves
as an excellent late-season cover crop that bees love. Its flowers attract
pollinators in abundance, and the grain makes nutritious pancakes and
noodles. Mollison notes that buckwheat yields 3,800 to 4,000 kilograms per
hectare -- competitive with wheat -- and is bee-pollinated, making it an
excellent green manure crop.

Quinoa, native to the Andes, is a complete protein containing all nine
essential amino acids. It grows at high altitudes, tolerates salty soils,
and is drought-resistant once established. Mollison lists yields of 1,800
to 2,700 kilograms per hectare from over 1,000 known varieties. Quinoa
requires processing to remove the bitter saponin coating from its seeds,
but this is easily done by rinsing in water.

Amaranth, like buckwheat, is not a true cereal grass but produces
grain-like seeds with 12 to 16 percent protein. It is extremely
drought-tolerant and produces broad edible leaves that are among the most
nutritious greens available. Sorghum thrives in heat and drought, produces
very high biomass for compost, and can be used for grain, syrup, or animal
forage.

## No-Till and Natural Farming Methods

The most important innovation in small-scale grain growing over the past
century came from Masanobu Fukuoka, who demonstrated on his farm in Ehime
Prefecture, Japan, that rice and barley can be grown in continuous annual
rotation on the same field without plowing, chemical fertilizers,
pesticides, or compost preparation. His method consistently produced
yields of approximately 22 bushels each of rice and barley per quarter
acre -- competitive with conventional methods while requiring a fraction
of the labor and energy input.

### Fukuoka's Core Insight

Fukuoka's fundamental observation was that plowing is not merely
unnecessary but actively destructive. He compared the effect to kneading
bread dough: rather than loosening the soil, tilling breaks it into finer
and finer particles that compact and harden. He observed that the roots of
grasses and cover crops work the soil to twelve inches or more -- deeper
than any plow -- and that when these roots die, they add humus, allowing
microbes to proliferate and earthworms to appear. As he wrote: "Let the
grasses plow the topsoil and the trees work the deeper layers."

This insight led him to develop what he called "direct-seeded, no-tillage,
winter grain/rice succession in a clover cover." The system works as
follows.

### The Annual Cycle

In autumn, white clover and winter grain (barley or wheat) are broadcast
over the ripening heads of rice before the fall harvest. The standing rice
supports the germinating clover and grain, and the clover begins to
establish as a living ground cover. After the rice harvest, the rice straw
is scattered uncut over the entire field as mulch.

During winter, seed rice is formed into clay pellets (to protect from
birds and rodents) and scattered over the straw mulch before the new year.
With the winter grain growing and the rice seed already sown, there is
nothing left to do until harvesting the winter grain.

In late May, the winter grain is harvested, dried, and threshed. All
straw is scattered uncut over the field. The field is then flooded for
four to five days to weaken the clover and give the rice shoots a chance
to break through the cover. After flooding, the field is drained, the
clover recovers and continues to fix nitrogen, and the cycle repeats.

### Why It Works

Several mechanisms make this system self-sustaining. The white clover
fixes nitrogen, eliminating the need for fertilizer. The uncut straw
mulch suppresses weeds, retains moisture, moderates soil temperature, and
feeds soil organisms as it decomposes. The clay pellets protect rice seed
from predation while allowing it to germinate when conditions are right.
The brief flooding disrupts weed competition without the need for
herbicides. And the continuous root activity of the grain-clover rotation
maintains soil structure without mechanical cultivation.

Fukuoka calculated the energy efficiency of his system compared to
conventional methods. A farmer eating a 2,000-calorie-per-day diet can
produce 1,300 pounds of rice on a quarter acre using his methods.
Traditional animal-powered farming uses at least five times as many
calories for the same results, and tractor farming uses at least ten
times as many. Human-powered no-till grain production is, by this
measure, the most energy-efficient food production method known.

### Permanent Raised Beds

For dryland grains (wheat, rye, oats, barley), permanent raised beds
offer an excellent no-till approach. Beds are formed once and maintained
annually by adding compost to the surface. Walking paths between beds
become compacted while the beds themselves stay loose and well-aerated.
Raised beds improve drainage in wet climates, warm faster in spring, and
allow salt to leach downward away from plant roots. Cover crops between
grain cycles maintain soil health, and well-mulched beds can be planted
in succession throughout the season.

John Jeavons's GROW BIOINTENSIVE system uses intensively managed raised
beds to achieve remarkable grain yields. His approach emphasizes double-
digging the beds initially (a one-time investment of labor), then
maintaining them with surface compost applications. Ecology Action's
highest wheat yield is approximately 21 pounds per 100-square-foot bed,
using about 10 inches of water for the whole season with compost grown
on-site.

## Harvesting Grain by Hand

Harvesting is the most labor-intensive part of home grain production, but
it is also deeply satisfying work that connects the grower to agricultural
traditions stretching back millennia. The goal is to cut the grain when
it is in the late dough stage -- far past the milk stage, still slightly
dentable when pressed with a thumbnail -- and then let it dry to "dead
ripe" either in the field or under cover.

### Cutting

For very small areas, a sickle suffices. For larger plots, the scythe is
the tool of choice. A scythe consists of a large blade swung low to the
ground with a long handle called a snath. Proper technique involves
holding the nibs (handgrips) with arms straight, twisting at the waist
while stepping forward, and keeping the top of the snath down to prevent
the blade from going into the ground. A cradle attachment can be added
to the snath to collect cut grain for depositing into piles.

The grain can be left in windrows (long rows) if it is not fully ripe, or
gathered into piles if it is dead ripe and ready for immediate shocking.

### Sheaving and Shocking

A sheave is a large handful of stalks tied near the top with barren
tillers (secondary stalks from the base) using a binder's knot -- a
technique where stalks are twisted together and tucked under the band
going around the sheave. Several sheaves are leaned against each other
with another sheave on top to form a shock. The shock should be large
enough that your hands meet on the other side when you reach around it.

Grain dries in the shocks during fall. When no green stems remain and
corn stalks sound hollow when tapped, the grain is ready for threshing.
Alternatively, sheaves can be brought into a dry barn for protection from
rain.

## Threshing and Winnowing

### Threshing

Threshing separates grain kernels from their stalks. For most grains, a
hand flail is used -- a wooden handle with a swingle (swinging piece)
attached by a loose ring. The grain is spread on a hard floor and struck
with the flail, then flipped with a pitchfork and flailed again.

An important caveat: oats and barley cannot be hulled by flailing. Their
hulls are too tightly attached and must be removed by steaming and
grinding in a mill. For wheat and rye, flailing works well, and the
process is best done in winter when other farm work is minimal.

On a very small scale, threshing can be done by placing grain heads in a
pillowcase and beating it against a hard surface, or by driving over
grain laid on a tarp with a car or truck (a technique used by some
homesteaders with appropriately clean wheels).

### Winnowing

Winnowing separates the chaff (husks and debris) from the grain using
wind. The traditional method involves tossing grain upward with a basket
or winnowing tray on a breezy day and letting the wind carry away the
lighter chaff while the heavier grain falls back into the basket. A
pitchfork can be used similarly for larger quantities. For beans, grain
can be poured back and forth between two tubs on a windy day.

Chaff is not waste -- it makes excellent livestock feed or can be added
to compost piles. Straw should be saved for animal bedding, mulch, or
compost carbon.

## Milling Grain at Home

Home milling is where the homesteader reaps the nutritional rewards of
growing their own grain. A whole grain kernel contains three parts: the
bran (14 percent by weight, providing fiber, B vitamins, minerals, and
protein), the germ (3 percent, containing healthy fats, vitamin E, B
vitamins, and antioxidants), and the endosperm (83 percent, mostly starch
and protein including gluten). Commercial white flour removes the bran
and germ entirely. Commercial whole wheat flour includes them but the
oils in the germ begin oxidizing immediately, which is why it must be
refrigerated and still loses quality within weeks.

### Stone Mills Versus Steel Burr Mills

Stone mills crush grain between two circular stones -- one stationary and
one rotating with grooves that move grain from center to edge. Their
advantages include lower milling temperatures (preserving heat-sensitive
nutrients and enzymes), broader particle size range that produces more
flavorful flour, quieter operation, and the ability to mill oily grains
and seeds. Disadvantages include slower speed and the need for periodic
stone dressing. Notable models include the Mockmill ($250-300, attaches
to KitchenAid mixers), the WonderMill Junior Deluxe ($300-350, hand crank
with excellent build quality), and the Komo Classic ($500-700, electric
with stone burrs).

Steel burr mills use toothed steel plates instead of stones. They mill
faster than stone, offer adjustable coarseness from coarse meal to fine
flour, and are easier to clean. However, they generate higher milling
temperatures that can degrade some nutrients, produce less consistent
flour texture, and steel plates can wear over time.

### What You Can Mill

Beyond wheat, home mills handle a remarkable variety of grains and seeds.
Rye produces flavorful bread flour. Corn, when ground coarsely, makes
polenta and grits; ground finely, it produces cornmeal for breads,
muffins, and coatings. Oats can be flaked using the roller attachment on
some mills. Spelt, an ancient wheat relative, mills beautifully and is
often tolerated by people with wheat sensitivity. Buckwheat, amaranth,
and quinoa all grind into nutritious flour for pancakes, porridge, and
baking.

Fresh flour should be used within one to three days for maximum nutrition,
or within one to two weeks if stored in a cool, dark place. Beyond that,
the germ oils begin to oxidize noticeably. This is why milling on demand
is the whole point -- you mill only what you need, when you need it.

## Integrating Grains into Polyculture Systems

Permaculture design integrates grains into diverse polyculture systems
rather than growing them as monocultures. Mollison's Permaculture
Designers Manual describes several approaches to integrated grain
production that work at homestead scale.

### Duck-Rice-Fish Polyculture

Perhaps the most elegant integration is the traditional Asian rice paddy
system that combines rice, ducks, and fish in a single water management
unit. Ducks eat insects and weeds while fertilizing the rice with their
droppings. Fish (typically carp or tilapia) consume mosquito larvae and
organic debris while adding further fertility. After harvest, ducks
continue to forage in the paddy, cleaning up remaining grain and pests.
Fukuoka described this system as one of his key innovations, noting that
it eliminates the need for synthetic nitrogen while producing multiple
yields from the same water area.

### Broadacre No-Tillage Sequences

Mollison describes a system where rye and wheat are broadcast into
soybean crops when the leaves begin to fall -- the falling leaves hide
the seed from birds. Soybeans and other legumes are then broadcast into
the stubble of oats, barley, wheat, or rye after grain harvest. Peas are
planted after corn, and green peas are followed by corn. This creates a
continuous no-tillage rotation where each crop's residue protects the
next crop's seed and maintains soil cover.

### Tree-Based Grains

Several tree species produce edible grains or grain-like seeds. Oak acorns
from the white oak group are relatively low in tannins and, after
leaching, can be ground into nutritious flour for breads and porridges.
Chestnuts serve as a starchy staple in many temperate regions and can be
dried and ground into flour. Mesquite pods, native to arid North America,
produce sweet, protein-rich flour that has been a staple food of
indigenous peoples for thousands of years.

### Aquatic Grains

Wild rice (Zizania spp.) grows naturally in shallow lakes and slow-moving
streams across northern North America. Mollison describes wild rice
polycultures combining rice with taro and water chestnut. Great water
grass (Glyceria maxima) and floating manna grass (Glyceria fluitans)
produce edible seeds that can be ground into flour for breads and
puddings. These aquatic systems integrate naturally with duck and fish
production.

## Seed Saving for Grains

Saving grain seed is straightforward and essential for the self-sufficient
grower. Unlike hybrid vegetables, most grain varieties are open-pollinated
and breed true from saved seed. Select the best seed heads -- unbroken,
healthy, and representative of the traits you want to encourage. Let seed
dry in the shock for at least one month until totally dry. Thresh and
ensure no leaves or twigs remain. Store in rodent-proof containers in a
cool, dry place.

Fukuoka went further, arguing that the practice of saving and selecting
the "best" seed actually weakens crops over time by narrowing genetic
diversity. He preferred to allow natural selection to operate, saving
seed from the general population rather than from individual superior
plants. His "happy hill" rice strain emerged not from deliberate breeding
but from allowing nature to select the plants best adapted to his
particular conditions over decades of natural farming.

## Storage and Pest Management

All grains must be stored in rodent-proof containers in the house rather
than in sheds or outbuildings, where temperature fluctuations and
moisture encourage mold and pest problems. Grain must be thoroughly dry
before packing -- moisture content below 14 percent is essential for
long-term storage. Adding bay leaves to containers helps deter some
insect pests. Food-grade diatomaceous earth can be mixed with stored grain
to kill crawling insects without affecting the grain's edibility. Stored
grain should be checked periodically for mold, insect activity, or signs
of rodent intrusion.

Once ground, flour must be used within days for peak nutrition. This is
not a drawback of home milling but rather its greatest advantage: the
assurance that every loaf of bread is made from the freshest possible
flour, carrying the full nutritional payload that the grain intended.

## The Bigger Picture: Grains and Food Sovereignty

Growing grain at home is, in one sense, a practical skill. But it also
represents something deeper: a reclamation of food sovereignty that
industrial agriculture has steadily eroded. When you grow your own grain,
you control the variety, the cultivation method, the processing, and the
quality of one of humanity's most fundamental foods. You are no longer
dependent on a supply chain that prioritizes shelf life over nutrition,
transport efficiency over flavor, and uniformity over diversity.

The energy argument is compelling. Fukuoka's no-till system produces 590
kilograms of rice on a quarter acre using only human labor powered by a
2,000-calorie daily diet. This represents a positive energy return --
the farmer gets more calories out than they put in -- something no
tractor-powered system can claim. In a world increasingly concerned
with the carbon footprint of agriculture, low-input grain production
represents not just a hobby but a genuine alternative.
