## Pyrethrum and Pyrethrin — Natural Insecticides

## Overview

Pyrethrum is one of the oldest and most widely used botanical insecticides in the world. Derived from the flowers of the Dalmatian chrysanthemum (*Chrysanthemum cinerariifolium*, formerly *Tanacetum cinerariifolium*), pyrethrum has been employed as an insecticide for over 150 years. The active insecticidal compounds, collectively called **pyrethrins**, are six esters found in the flower's achenial hairs (oil glands). Pyrethrins are valued in organic agriculture for their broad-spectrum insecticidal activity, rapid knockdown effect, and low mammalian toxicity.

## Botanical Background

### The Pyrethrum Daisy

- **Scientific name**: *Chrysanthemum cinerariifolium* (Trevir.) Vis.
- **Family**: Asteraceae
- **Common names**: Dalmatian chrysanthemum, Dalmatian pellitory, pyrethrum daisy
- **Origin**: Dalmatian coast of Croatia, Montenegro, and surrounding Balkan regions
- **Other species**: *C. coccineum* (Painted daisy, Persian chrysanthemum) — lower pyrethrin content but more ornamental
- **Hardiness**: USDA Zones 3–7; prefers well-drained, calcareous soils

The plant is a perennial herb growing 30–60 cm tall with deeply divided, silvery-green leaves and white-petaled flowers with yellow centers. The highest pyrethrin concentrations are found in the flower heads, specifically in the achenial hairs on the surface of the seeds (achenes) within the flower.

### Synergists — Piperonyl Butoxide (PBO)

Because insects can rapidly metabolize pyrethrins, commercial formulations almost always include **piperonyl butoxide (PBO)** as a synergist. PBO inhibits cytochrome P450 enzymes in the insect, preventing metabolic detoxification of pyrethrins and increasing their potency 5–20 fold.

**Important organic certification note**: PBO is a synthetic compound and is **not permitted** in most organic certification programs (USDA NOP, EU organic). Organic growers must use pure pyrethrin formulations without PBO. This means higher [[spider-mites-cannabis]] (moderate efficacy)

### Resistance and Limitations

Several important limitations should be understood:

1. **Resistance**: Repeated use of pyrethrins has led to resistance development in several pest species, particularly diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella), greenhouse whitefly, and two-spotted spider mite. Resistance mechanisms include enhanced P450 activity (which is why PBO is added), target site insensitivity (kdr mutations), and increased excretion.

2. **Broad-spectrum toxicity**: Pyrethrins kill [[comparison-predatory-mites-vs-ladybugs-biocontrol]], lady beetles, lacewings, and parasitic wasps. This makes them a poor choice for [[beneficial-insect-habitat]] activity.

3. **Short residual activity**: While this is an advantage for residue concerns, it means repeated applications (every 2–5 days) may be needed for sustained pest control.

4. **Phytotoxicity**: Some formulations can cause leaf burn on sensitive plants, particularly at high concentrations or in hot weather.

### Application Guidelines

| Factor | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Timing | Apply in early morning or evening to protect pollinators and reduce UV degradation |
| Concentration | 0.5–2.0% pyrethrin in commercial formulations; follow label rates |
| Coverage | Thorough coverage of both upper and lower leaf surfaces is essential — pyrethrins have no systemic activity |
| Frequency | Every 3–7 days for heavy infestations; rotate with other modes of action |
| pH of spray water | Slightly acidic (pH 5.5–7.0) for maximum stability; alkaline water accelerates degradation |
| Tank mixing | Compatible with most other organic insecticides; do not mix with alkaline materials like Bordeaux mixture |
| PHI (pre-harvest interval) | Typically 0 days — can be applied up to day of harvest in most formulations |
| REI (re-entry interval) | Usually 12–24 hours |
