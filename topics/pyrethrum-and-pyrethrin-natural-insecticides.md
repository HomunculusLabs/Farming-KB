# Pyrethrum and Pyrethrin — Natural Insecticides

## Overview

Pyrethrum is one of the oldest and most widely used botanical insecticides in the world. Derived from the flowers of the Dalmatian chrysanthemum (*Chrysanthemum cinerariifolium*, formerly *Tanacetum cinerariifolium*), pyrethrum has been employed as an insecticide for over 150 years. The active insecticidal compounds, collectively called **pyrethrins**, are six esters found in the flower's achenial hairs (oil glands). Pyrethrins are valued in organic agriculture for their broad-spectrum insecticidal activity, rapid knockdown effect, and low mammalian toxicity.

## Botanical Background

### The Pyrethrum Daisy

- **Scientific name**: *Chrysanthemum cinerariifolium* (Trevir.) Vis.
- **Family**: Asteraceae
- **Common names**: Dalmatian chrysanthemum, Dalmatian pellitory, pyrethrum daisy
- **Origin**: Dalmatian coast of Croatia, Montenegro, and surrounding Balkan regions
- **Other species**: *C. coccineum* (Painted daisy, Persian chrysanthemum) — lower pyrethrin content but more ornamental
- **Hardiness**: USDA Zones 3–7; prefers well-drained, calcareous soils

The plant is a perennial herb growing 30–60 cm tall with deeply divided, silvery-green leaves and white-petaled flowers with yellow centers. The highest pyrethrin concentrations are found in the flower heads, specifically in the achenial hairs on the surface of the seeds (achenes) within the flower.

### Growing Pyrethrum Daisies commercially

| Parameter | Optimal Range |
|---|---|
| Soil pH | 6.5–7.5 (slightly alkaline preferred) |
| Soil type | Well-drained, sandy loam; rocky/limestone soils work well |
| Annual rainfall | 400–600 mm (drier conditions concentrate pyrethrins) |
| Temperature | 15–25°C growing season; cold winters for vernalization |
| Altitude | 600–1500 m (higher altitude = higher pyrethrin content) |
| Plant spacing | 30–45 cm between plants, 60–90 cm between rows |
| Harvest | Full flower stage, before seed set; typically 2–3 harvests per season |

Major commercial production occurs in:
- **Tanzania** (historically the world's largest producer, ~70% of global supply)
- **Kenya** (highland regions, particularly Molo and Nakuru districts)
- **Australia** (Tasmania — significant production since the 1990s)
- **Ecuador, Rwanda, and Papua New Guinea** (smaller but growing production)
- **Croatia and neighboring Balkan countries** (historic origin, limited modern production)

## Pyrethrin Chemistry

### The Six Pyrethrin Esters

Pyrethrins are a group of six closely related esters formed from the combination of two cyclopropane carboxylic acids (pyrethric acid and chrysanthemic acid) with three alcohols (pyrethrolone, cinerolone, and jasmolone):

| Compound | Acid component | Alcohol component | Relative toxicity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pyrethrin I | Chrysanthemic acid | Pyrethrolone | High |
| Pyrethrin II | Pyrethric acid | Pyrethrolone | Moderate |
| Cinerin I | Chrysanthemic acid | Cinerolone | Moderate |
| Cinerin II | Pyrethric acid | Cinerolone | Low |
| Jasmolin I | Chrysanthemic acid | Jasmolone | Low |
| Jasmolin II | Pyrethric acid | Jasmolone | Lowest |

Pyrethrin I is generally considered the most potent insecticide, while Pyrethrin II contributes significantly to the knockdown effect. Commercial pyrethrum extracts typically contain 25–50% total pyrethrin content by weight.

### Mechanism of Action

Pyrethrins act on the nervous systems of insects through multiple pathways:

1. **Sodium channel modulation**: Pyrethrins bind to voltage-gated sodium channels in insect nerve membranes, keeping them in an open state. This causes prolonged nerve excitation, leading to uncontrolled repetitive firing of neurons.

2. **Rapid knockdown**: The initial paralysis ("knockdown") is caused by this hyperexcitation of the peripheral nervous system. Affected insects become paralyzed within seconds to minutes of exposure.

3. **Lethal effect**: With sufficient exposure, the hyperexcitation leads to total nervous system failure and death. However, many insects can recover from knockdown if the dose is sublethal, as some species possess detoxification enzymes ([[cytochrome-p450-enzymes-structure-function-pharmacology]] monooxygenases) that metabolize pyrethrins.

4. **Repellent activity**: At sublethal concentrations, pyrethrins also act as effective repellents, making them useful in mosquito coils, flea collars, and animal sprays.

### Synergists — Piperonyl Butoxide (PBO)

Because insects can rapidly metabolize pyrethrins, commercial formulations almost always include **piperonyl butoxide (PBO)** as a synergist. PBO inhibits cytochrome P450 enzymes in the insect, preventing metabolic detoxification of pyrethrins and increasing their potency 5–20 fold.

**Important organic certification note**: PBO is a synthetic compound and is **not permitted** in most organic certification programs (USDA NOP, EU organic). Organic growers must use pure pyrethrin formulations without PBO. This means higher application rates may be needed, and efficacy may be somewhat reduced compared to conventional formulations.

### Environmental Fate and Degradation

One of pyrethrin's greatest advantages is its rapid environmental breakdown:

- **UV photodegradation**: Pyrethrins degrade rapidly in sunlight, with a half-life of 10–12 hours in direct sun. This means they leave minimal residue on crops and in the environment.
- **Soil degradation**: Half-life in soil is typically 1–2 days. Microbial degradation is the primary pathway in soil.
- **Water**: Pyrethrins have low water solubility and bind moderately to soil particles. They are not considered groundwater contaminants.
- **Temperature sensitivity**: Pyrethrins degrade more slowly in cool, dark conditions — this is relevant for storage and for indoor applications.

## Agricultural Applications

### Target Pests

Pyrethrins are effective against a broad range of insect pests:

**Chewing insects:**
- Caterpillars (cabbage looper, imported cabbageworm, tomato hornworm)
- Colorado potato beetle larvae
- Japanese beetle adults
- Mexican bean beetle

**Sucking insects:**
- Aphids
- Whiteflies
- Thrips
- Leafhoppers
- [[spider-mites-cannabis]] (moderate efficacy)

**Other pests:**
- Fleas and ticks (on animals)
- Mosquitoes
- Flies (house flies, stable flies)
- Ants
- Cockroaches

### Resistance and Limitations

Several important limitations should be understood:

1. **Resistance**: Repeated use of pyrethrins has led to resistance development in several pest species, particularly diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella), greenhouse whitefly, and two-spotted spider mite. Resistance mechanisms include enhanced P450 activity (which is why PBO is added), target site insensitivity (kdr mutations), and increased excretion.

2. **Broad-spectrum toxicity**: Pyrethrins kill [[beneficial-insects-guide]] as readily as pests. They are highly toxic to bees (though short residual activity means evening application can mitigate harm), predatory mites, lady beetles, lacewings, and parasitic wasps. This makes them a poor choice for [[integrated-pest-management-ipm]] management programs during periods of [[beneficial-insect-habitat]] activity.

3. **Short residual activity**: While this is an advantage for residue concerns, it means repeated applications (every 2–5 days) may be needed for sustained pest control.

4. **Phytotoxicity**: Some formulations can cause leaf burn on sensitive plants, particularly at high concentrations or in hot weather.

### Application Guidelines

| Factor | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Timing | Apply in early morning or evening to protect pollinators and reduce UV degradation |
| Concentration | 0.5–2.0% pyrethrin in commercial formulations; follow label rates |
| Coverage | Thorough coverage of both upper and lower leaf surfaces is essential — pyrethrins have no systemic activity |
| Frequency | Every 3–7 days for heavy infestations; rotate with other modes of action |
| pH of spray water | Slightly acidic (pH 5.5–7.0) for maximum stability; alkaline water accelerates degradation |
| Tank mixing | Compatible with most other organic insecticides; do not mix with alkaline materials like Bordeaux mixture |
| PHI (pre-harvest interval) | Typically 0 days — can be applied up to day of harvest in most formulations |
| REI (re-entry interval) | Usually 12–24 hours |

## Safety Profile

### Mammalian Toxicity

- **Oral LD₅₀ (rat)**: ~1500–2000 mg/kg (low toxicity — Category IV)
- **Dermal LD₅0 (rabbit)**: >2000 mg/kg (very low toxicity)
- **Inhalation LC₅₀**: Low toxicity
- **Skin and eye irritation**: Mild to moderate irritation possible with concentrated formulations
- **Sensitization**: Pyrethrins are known human sensitizers and can cause allergic contact dermatitis, particularly in people with chrysanthemum allergies (compositae allergy)
- **Antidote**: No specific antidote; treatment is supportive. Pyrethrins are rapidly metabolized and excreted in mammals.

### Aquatic Toxicity

Pyrethrins are **highly toxic to fish and aquatic invertebrates**:
- Fish LC₅₀: 0.5–25 ppb (parts per billion) depending on species
- Daphnia LC₅₀: 0.1–1.0 ppb
- This extreme aquatic toxicity means pyrethrins must NEVER be applied near waterways, ponds, or drainage areas
- Even small amounts of drift or runoff can cause fish kills

### Avian Toxicity

- Low toxicity to birds (LD₅₀ > 1000 mg/kg for most species)
- Birds metabolize pyrethrins rapidly and are generally not affected by field-level exposures

### Bee Toxicity

- Highly toxic to bees on direct contact (LD₅₀: 0.02–0.2 μg/bee)
- Residual toxicity is short (hours) due to UV degradation
- **Best practice**: Apply after sunset when bees have returned to hives; residual will degrade before morning foraging

## Pyrethrins vs Synthetic Pyrethroids

A critical distinction that is often confused:

| Property | Pyrethrins (natural) | Synthetic Pyrethroids |
|---|---|---|
| Source | *C. cinerariifolium* flower extract | Chemical synthesis |
| Examples | Pyrethrin I & II, cinerin I & II | Permethrin, bifenthrin, cypermethrin, deltamethrin |
| Environmental persistence | Hours to 1–2 days | Days to months |
| Aquatic toxicity | Very high | Extremely high (often 10× higher) |
| Mammalian toxicity | Low | Low to moderate |
| Bee toxicity | High (contact), low residual | Very high, longer residual |
| Organic certification | Generally permitted (without PBO) | Never permitted |
| Resistance management | Moderate resistance issues | Severe resistance in many species |
| Cost | Higher (agricultural raw material) | Lower (synthetic production) |
| Knockdown speed | Very fast | Fast to moderate |

Synthetic pyrethroids were developed starting in the 1950s–1970s to overcome pyrethrins' instability and short residual. While they share a similar mode of action, the enhanced persistence of pyrethroids creates greater environmental concerns, particularly regarding aquatic ecosystems and beneficial insect populations. In organic agriculture, only natural pyrethrins are permitted.

## Integrated Pest Management Considerations

### When Pyrethrins Are a Good Choice

- **Emergency pest knockdown**: When pest populations exceed economic thresholds and rapid control is needed
- **Pre-harvest clean-up**: Low PHI allows application close to harvest
- **Greenhouse/nursery use**: Effective in enclosed spaces with controlled application
- **Organic certification**: One of the few broad-spectrum insecticides permitted in organic systems
- **Flea and tick control**: Highly effective for companion animals (use veterinary-approved formulations)
- **Indoor pest control**: Low mammalian toxicity makes them suitable for indoor use against ants, roaches, and flies

### When Pyrethrins Are a Poor Choice

- **During active pollinator foraging**: High bee toxicity requires careful timing
- **Near waterways**: Extreme aquatic toxicity
- **As a standalone control method**: Broad-spectrum activity kills beneficials and leads to secondary pest outbreaks
- **For mite control**: Limited efficacy; can actually flare mite populations by eliminating predators
- **For long-term pest management**: Short residual means no lasting protection
- **For resistance-prone pests**: Diamondback moth and whitefly have significant pyrethrin resistance; use alternative approaches

### Resistance Management Strategies

1. **Rotate modes of action**: Alternate pyrethrins with insecticides that have different modes of action (spinosad, Bt, neem, insecticidal soaps)
2. **Threshold-based applications**: Only spray when pest counts exceed economic injury levels
3. **Spot treatments**: Treat only infested areas rather than blanket spraying
4. **Use selective alternatives first**: Try Bt (for caterpillars), insecticidal soaps (for soft-bodied pests), or selective insecticides before resorting to broad-spectrum pyrethrins
5. **Preserve refuges**: Maintain untreated areas to sustain susceptible pest populations that dilute resistance genes
6. **Monitor resistance**: If control efficacy declines, switch to an alternative product

## Home Extraction and Preparation

While commercial pyrethrin products are widely available, some small-scale growers cultivate pyrethrum daisies and prepare their own insecticidal extracts.

### Growing for Home Production

1. Start seeds indoors 6–8 weeks before last frost; stratify seeds at 4°C for 2 weeks for better germination
2. Transplant to well-drained, slightly alkaline soil in full sun
3. Space plants 30–45 cm apart
4. Harvest flower heads at full bloom; dry in shade (direct sun degrades pyrethrins)
5. Store dried flowers in airtight containers in a cool, dark place; pyrethrin content is stable for 6–12 months when properly dried and stored

### Homemade Pyrethrum Dust

1. Grind dried pyrethrum flower heads to a fine powder using a mortar and pestle or electric grinder
2. Dust directly onto plants or apply with a puff duster
3. Apply in early morning when dew is present for better adhesion
4. Reapply after rain or heavy dew
5. Typical dilution: 50% pyrethrum powder + 50% inert carrier (talc, clay, or [[diatomaceous-earth-cannabis]])

### Homemade Pyrethrum Extract

1. Soak 100 g of dried pyrethrum flowers in 1 liter of water for 24 hours
2. Add 1 teaspoon of liquid soap as a surfactant (not detergent)
3. Strain through a fine cloth or coffee filter
4. Apply as a foliar spray, covering all plant surfaces
5. Use within 12 hours — homemade extracts degrade rapidly
6. Add 2–3 drops of vegetable oil per liter to improve coverage and persistence

**Safety note**: Even homemade preparations are toxic to fish and bees. Exercise the same precautions as with commercial products.

## Regulatory Status

- **USDA National Organic Program (NOP)**: Pyrethrins are permitted (Synthetic substances allowed for crop pest management — §205.601). PBO is NOT permitted in organic production.
- **EU Organic Regulation (2018/848)**: Pyrethrins are permitted as a plant protection product. PBO is restricted.
- **Codex Alimentarius**: Pyrethrins are permitted in organic production with maximum residue levels specified for various crops.
- **EPA registration**: Pyrethrins are registered as a broad-spectrum insecticide with established tolerances for food crops.
- **Maximum Residue Limits (MRLs)**: Generally 0.5–1.0 ppm on most crops, though many countries have MRLs at or near the limit of detection.

## Storage and Shelf Life

- **Concentrated formulations**: Store at 2–8°C in opaque, airtight containers; shelf life of 2–3 years
- **Ready-to-use sprays**: Shelf life of 1–2 years at room temperature
- **Dry pyrethrum powder**: Store in airtight, light-proof containers; shelf life of 6–12 months
- **Degradation indicators**: Loss of characteristic odor, darkening of the extract, reduced efficacy
- **Never store near**: Oxidizing agents, strong bases, or heat sources

## Cultural and Historical Significance

### Early History

- Pyrethrum's insecticidal properties were known to ancient civilizations in the Caucasus region and Persia, where crushed chrysanthemum flowers were used to kill body lice and stored grain insects
- The earliest written records come from Armenian traders in the 17th century, who called the plant "Insect Powder"
- The plant was formally introduced to European science in 1828 by Dr. J. J. Paul, a Russian-German physician working in the Balkans, who published observations on its use as an insecticide by Dalmatian peasants

### Commercial Development

- Commercial production began in Dalmatia (Croatia) in the 1840s, with dried flowers exported across Europe as "Dalmatian insect powder"
- Japanese production of *C. cinerariifolium* began in the 1880s and became a major industry by the 1920s, with Japan supplying most of the world's pyrethrum until WWII
- East African production (Kenya, Tanzania) was established by British colonial agricultural programs in the 1920s–1930s and rapidly became dominant due to ideal highland growing conditions
- During WWII, pyrethrum was strategically important as the primary insecticide for military use — protecting troops from lice-borne typhus and controlling malaria mosquitoes
- Australian production in Tasmania began in the 1990s and has grown to supply a significant share of the global market, with advanced agronomic practices achieving higher pyrethrin yields per hectare

### Modern Market

The global pyrethrum market is valued at approximately $200–250 million USD annually, with steady demand driven by:
- Growth in organic agriculture (organic sector requires natural pyrethrins)
- Increasing restrictions on synthetic insecticides (EU regulations, California Prop 65, etc.)
- Public health applications (mosquito control, bed bug treatments)
- Veterinary and companion animal products (flea/tick sprays, shampoos, collars)
- [[post-harvest-handling-marketing-mushrooms]] grain protection and stored product pest management

Supply volatility remains a challenge, as pyrethrum production is concentrated in a few developing countries and is subject to weather variability, labor costs, and competing crops.

## Formulation Types

### Emulsifiable Concentrates (EC)

The most common commercial formulation. Pyrethrin extract is dissolved in an organic solvent with emulsifiers, allowing it to be diluted in water for foliar spraying. Typical pyrethrin concentration: 2–20%.

### Wettable Powders (WP)

Dry powder formulations containing pyrethrin, inert carriers, and surfactants that suspend in water. Less common than ECs but preferred by some organic growers who want to avoid petroleum-based solvents.

### Dusts

Ready-to-use dry powders for direct application. Used in indoor settings, on pets, and in stored grain facilities. Often combined with diatomaceous earth for enhanced physical insecticidal action.

### Aerosols

Pressurized spray cans for household and greenhouse use. Typically contain 0.1–0.5% pyrethrins, sometimes with PBO.

### Microencapsulated Formulations

Newer formulations that encapsulate pyrethrin molecules in microscopic polymer shells, slowing their release and extending residual activity from hours to several days. These offer a promising middle ground between pyrethrins' rapid degradation and the environmental persistence problems of synthetic pyrethroids.

### Combination Products

Many commercial products combine pyrethrins with other organic insecticides:
- **Pyrethrin + insecticidal soap**: Soap improves coverage and penetrates insect cuticle, enhancing pyrethrin uptake
- **Pyrethrin + neem oil**: Combines rapid knockdown with neem's growth-disrupting and repellent effects
- **Pyrethrin + kaolin clay**: Clay provides a physical barrier while pyrethrin offers contact kill
- **Pyrethrin + garlic extract**: Garlic enhances repellent activity and may mask host plant volatiles

## Impact on Specific Beneficial Insects

Understanding pyrethrin's effects on key beneficial organisms is essential for IPM decision-making:

| Beneficial Organism | Pyrethrin Toxicity | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Honey bees (Apis mellifera) | Very high (contact) | LD₅₀ ~0.07 μg/bee; residual toxicity ~4 hours |
| Bumble bees (Bombus spp.) | Very high (contact) | More vulnerable than honey bees due to smaller colony sizes |
| Lady beetles (Coccinellidae) | High | Both adults and larvae are susceptible on contact |
| Lacewings (Chrysopidae) | High | Eggs are less susceptible; larvae and adults highly vulnerable |
| Parasitic wasps (Braconidae, Ichneumonidae) | High | Very small size makes them especially vulnerable to fine sprays |
| Syrphid flies (hoverflies) | High | Adults killed on contact; larvae (aphid predators) also susceptible |
| Predatory mites (Phytoseiidae) | Moderate to high | Pyrethrin applications can cause mite flare-ups by eliminating predators |
| [[ground-beetles-carabidae-and-biological-pest-control]] (Carabidae) | Moderate | Less exposed to foliar sprays due to ground-dwelling habit |
| Spiders | Low to moderate | Many spiders are tolerant; web-dwelling species are relatively protected |
| Earthworms | Low | Contact toxicity is low; soil applications at label rates are generally safe |

This comprehensive kill sheet underscores why pyrethrin should be a tool of last resort in IPM programs, not a routine spray. Every application sets back biological control by days to weeks.

## Key Takeaways

- Pyrethrins are naturally occurring insecticidal esters extracted from Dalmatian chrysanthemum flowers
- They provide rapid knockdown and kill of a broad spectrum of insect pests
- Low mammalian toxicity and rapid environmental degradation make them relatively safe for use on food crops
- High toxicity to bees (on contact), fish, and aquatic invertebrates requires careful application practices
- Short residual activity means frequent reapplication but also minimal crop residue
- Broad-spectrum activity kills beneficial insects alongside pests — not ideal for IPM programs
- Resistance is a growing concern in several pest species; rotation and threshold-based application are essential
- Only natural pyrethrins (without PBO) are permitted in organic certification programs
- The distinction between natural pyrethrins and synthetic pyrethroids is critical for organic growers and environmental stewardship
- Pyrethrum has a rich commercial history spanning nearly 200 years, from Dalmatian peasant insect powder to a globally traded agricultural commodity
- Modern formulation technology (microencapsulation, combination products) is extending pyrethrin's utility while maintaining its environmental advantages
